Lessons

1. Moisture, Ash, and Protein Determination

Moisture, ash, and protein are the three most basic tests you will do daily in a flour mill. Moisture tells you how much water is in the flour. Too much moisture (above 14%) makes flour clump and spoil quickly. Too little (below 12%) means you are selling water weight that is not there, and you lose money. The standard method uses a moisture meter, like a Dickey-john or a Kett meter. Take a 10-gram sample, put it in the meter, and read the percentage. In Africa, where humidity varies from 30% in the Sahel to 90% on the coast, you must test every batch. If moisture is high, run the flour through a dryer or blend with drier flour.

Ash content measures mineral residue after burning. It tells you how much bran got into the flour. Take 5 grams of flour, put it in a crucible, and burn it in a muffle furnace at 550Â°C for 6 hours. The remaining ash is weighed. White flour should have ash below 0.6%. Whole wheat flour can be 1.5% or more. In many African mills, ash is used to check the efficiency of the sifting process. If ash is too high, your sieves are worn or torn.

Protein is measured by the Kjeldahl method or a near-infrared (NIR) analyzer. Kjeldahl is slow but accurate: digest 1 gram of flour with sulfuric acid and a catalyst, distill, and titrate. NIR is fast and used in large mills. Protein content for bread flour should be 11-13%. For biscuits, 8-10%. In Africa, where hard wheat is often imported, you may blend local soft wheat with imported hard wheat to hit the target. Always record results in a logbook. If protein drops below 10%, your bread will not rise well. Adjust by adding vital wheat gluten or blending with higher-protein wheat.

2. Falling Number and Alpha-Amylase Activity

The Falling Number test measures how much alpha-amylase enzyme is active in your flour. Too much enzyme means the starch breaks down too fast, and your bread will be sticky inside. Too little means the bread will be dry and have a poor crust. The test is simple: you mix 7 grams of flour with 25 ml of water in a special tube, shake it, and put it in a boiling water bath. A stirrer moves up and down. The time it takes for the stirrer to fall to the bottom is the Falling Number, measured in seconds.

A Falling Number of 250-300 seconds is ideal for most bread. If it is below 200, the flour has too much enzyme (often from sprouted wheat). You can fix this by blending with low-enzyme flour or adding fungal alpha-amylase inhibitor. If it is above 400, the flour has too little enzyme. You can add malted barley flour or fungal alpha-amylase to correct it. In Africa, rain during harvest can cause sprouting, so test every incoming wheat lot. A farmer may not know his wheat is sprouted, but the Falling Number will tell you.

You do not need a fancy lab. A basic Falling Number kit costs about $1,500. Many millers in Kenya and Nigeria use it. If you cannot afford one, you can do a simple sedimentation test: mix flour and water, and see how fast the starch settles. But the Falling Number is the standard. Always calibrate your water bath to 100Â°C at your altitude. In Johannesburg (1,750 m), water boils at 95Â°C, so you must adjust the test time or use a pressure bath. Record every result. If you see a sudden drop, stop and check your wheat supply.

3. Gluten Quality and Rheology

Gluten is the protein network that gives bread its structure. But not all gluten is equal. You can have strong gluten that stretches like rubber, or weak gluten that tears easily. The two main tests are the Gluten Index and the Farinograph (covered in Lesson 5). For a simple hand test, take 10 grams of flour, add 5 ml of water, and knead into a small dough ball. Wash it under running water to remove the starch. What is left is wet gluten. Weigh it. Good bread flour should have 25-35% wet gluten. Then stretch it. If it stretches thin without breaking, it is strong. If it breaks quickly, it is weak.

The Gluten Index machine gives a number. A sample of wet gluten is centrifuged through a sieve. The percentage that stays on top is the Gluten Index. For bread, you want 60-90%. Below 60% means weak gluten, good for biscuits or cakes. Above 90% means very strong gluten, good for blending but too tough for some products. In Africa, local wheat often has weak gluten. You can improve it by blending with imported hard wheat or adding ascorbic acid (vitamin C) at 50-100 ppm.

Rheology is the study of how dough flows and stretches. The Extensograph measures how far dough stretches before breaking. A good bread dough stretches 15-20 cm. If it stretches only 10 cm, it is too stiff. If it stretches 30 cm, it is too slack. You can adjust with dough conditioners like sodium metabisulfite (to relax) or calcium peroxide (to strengthen). Always test your flour with the actual recipe you will use. A flour that works for baguettes may fail for pan bread. Keep a record of every test. If you change wheat suppliers, test again.

4. Particle Size and Color Analysis

Particle size affects how fast flour absorbs water and how it feels. Fine flour (less than 100 microns) makes smooth dough and white bread. Coarse flour (over 200 microns) feels gritty and is used for whole wheat or semolina. You can measure particle size with a set of sieves. Stack sieves with openings of 500, 250, 150, and 75 microns. Shake 100 grams of flour through them for 10 minutes. Weigh what stays on each sieve. For white flour, at least 70% should pass through the 150-micron sieve. If not, your mill rolls are worn or your sifter is overloaded.

Color analysis is important because customers judge flour by its whiteness. A simple method is the Pekar test: place a sample of flour on a glass plate, press it smooth, and compare it to a standard color card. You can also use a colorimeter that gives L* (lightness), a* (red-green), and b* (yellow-blue) values. For white flour, L* should be above 90. If L* is low, you have too much bran or your wheat was heat-damaged. In Africa, many markets prefer creamy white flour, not pure white. So you may need to adjust your bleaching or add a small amount of fine bran to meet local preference.

Color is also affected by carotenoids (yellow pigments). If you want a very white flour, you can add benzoyl peroxide (50 ppm) to bleach it. But in some countries, bleaching is banned. Instead, you can store flour for 2-3 weeks to let natural oxidation occur. Always test color under consistent lighting. A north-facing window is best. Never judge color under fluorescent lights, which can make flour look blue. Record your results and compare with your customer's standard. If a customer rejects your flour, check particle size and color first.

5. FarinoGraph and MixoLab Testing

The Farinograph is a machine that mixes flour and water and measures the resistance. It tells you how much water the flour absorbs and how long it takes to develop gluten. You put 300 grams of flour in the mixing bowl, turn on the machine, and add water until the dough reaches a standard consistency (500 Farinograph Units). The amount of water you added is the water absorption. For bread flour, it should be 60-65%. If it is 55%, the flour is weak or has low protein. If it is 70%, the flour is very strong.

The Farinograph also gives you development time (how long to mix), stability (how long the dough stays strong), and mixing tolerance index (how much it weakens). Good bread flour has a development time of 4-8 minutes, stability of 8-15 minutes, and MTI below 30 BU. If stability is only 3 minutes, your dough will collapse in the oven. You can fix this by adding ascorbic acid or vital wheat gluten. In Africa, many small mills cannot afford a Farinograph ($10,000). The MixoLab is a cheaper alternative ($3,000). It uses 50 grams of flour and gives similar information in 20 minutes.

If you have no machine, you can do a simple mix test by hand. Mix 100 grams of flour with 60 ml of water. Knead and note how long it takes to become smooth and elastic. If it takes more than 10 minutes, the flour is weak. If it never becomes smooth, the gluten is poor. Always test with your actual production water. Hard water (high calcium) makes dough stiffer. Soft water makes it slack. Adjust your water addition accordingly. Keep a log of every Farinograph or MixoLab result. Compare with your baking results. After a few months, you will know exactly what numbers give you good bread.

6. Interpreting Test Results for Quality Control

You have run all the tests: moisture, ash, protein, Falling Number, gluten, particle size, color, and Farinograph. Now you must interpret them together. No single test tells the whole story. For example, high protein (13%) sounds good, but if the Falling Number is 150 (too much enzyme), your bread will still be sticky. So you need a quality control (QC) chart. Set a target range for each test. For bread flour: moisture 13-14%, ash 0.5-0.6%, protein 11-13%, Falling Number 250-300, wet gluten 28-32%, water absorption 60-65%, stability 8-12 minutes. If any result falls outside, flag it.

When a result is out of range, you must decide: accept, blend, or reject. If moisture is 15%, you can blend with dry flour (12%) at a 1:1 ratio to get 13.5%. If protein is 10%, blend with 14% protein wheat at 1:1 to get 12%. If Falling Number is 180, blend with 400 at 1:1 to get 290. Always calculate the blend using the Pearson square method. For example, to get 12% protein from 10% and 14%: difference between 14 and 12 is 2 parts of 10% flour; difference between 12 and 10 is 2 parts of 14% flour. So 50:50. Write down every blend you make. If you cannot fix it, sell it as biscuit flour or animal feed.

In Africa, your biggest challenge is consistency. The wheat you get from local farmers varies from truck to truck. So test every incoming load. Keep a sample of each load for 3 months. If a customer complains about bread, you can trace back to the wheat. Train your staff to record results in a simple notebook or Excel sheet. Use a traffic light system: green (good), yellow (blend), red (reject). Post the chart on the wall. Every miller who follows this will reduce complaints and waste. Remember: quality control is not about expensive machines. It is about discipline and record-keeping.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Flour Quality Analysis and Testing

For millers and bakers across Africa, flour quality is not an academic exercise â it is the difference between a repeat customer and a returned consignment. Whether you are supplying a commercial bakery in Lagos, a pasta plant in Nairobi, or a chapati processor in Dar es Salaam, your customers judge consistency by what happens in their mixing bowl, on their sheeter, or in their fryer. This practical reference consolidates the core tests you will use daily in a milling laboratory, explains what the numbers mean for your business, and gives you a working checklist for quality control (QC).

In most African mills, the laboratory is the early-warning system for the entire production line. A single out-of-specification batch of wheat â or a mis-set mill â can produce flour that bakers reject after one trial. The tests below are not optional extras; they are the commercial language through which you negotiate with buyers, defend your brand, and reduce claims.

Why these tests matter commercially

  • Moisture drives yield and shelf life. Every 1% excess moisture is paid for at wheat price but sold as water â and it shortens storage life in humid tropical conditions.
  • Ash indicates extraction rate and milling efficiency. Higher ash means more bran contamination, which affects colour, water absorption, and customer specifications.
  • Protein determines end-use suitability: bread, biscuit, noodle, or chapati. Protein is the single biggest driver of flour price and customer selection.
  • Falling Number protects against sprout-damaged wheat, which produces sticky, gummy dough that ruins bread crumb and machinery.
  • Gluten quality and rheology predict how dough behaves on the line â critical for high-speed bakeries and mechanised chapati or noodle plants.
  • Particle size and colour influence customer perception and end-product appearance.
  • Farinograph and Mixolab provide the mixing tolerance, water absorption, and starch behaviour data that bakers need to set their processes.

2. Falling Number and Alpha-Amylase Activity

The Falling Number (FN) test measures the viscosity of a flour-water slurry after gelatinisation. A high FN (e.g., 350â400 s) indicates low alpha-amylase activity and sound, un-sprouted wheat. A low FN (below 200 s) signals pre-harvest sprouting or excessive amylase, producing sticky dough, gummy crumb, and poor bread volume. In Africa, where rain at harvest can cause sprouting in wheat from Ethiopia, Kenya, and South Africa, FN is a critical intake test. Millers often blend low-FN wheat with sound wheat to bring the finished flour into spec. For bread, target 250â350 s; for biscuits, 250â300 s; for noodles, 300â400 s.

3. Gluten Quality and Rheology

Wet gluten content is determined by washing dough under running water or saline, then weighing the residue. Dry gluten and gluten index (using a Glutomatic) give a more complete picture: the gluten index measures gluten strength and elasticity. A high gluten index (>80) indicates strong, elastic gluten suitable for bread; a low index (<50) indicates weak, extensible gluten better for biscuits or chapati. In African markets, chapati and flatbread producers often prefer moderate gluten strength, while bread bakeries demand strong gluten with good mixing tolerance.

4. Particle Size and Colour Analysis

Particle size distribution is measured by sieving or laser diffraction. Finer flour generally absorbs water faster and produces smoother dough, but excessively fine flour can cause dusting and handling issues. Colour is measured with a colourimeter (L*, a*, b* values) or by comparison to a standard. Brightness (L*) is a key customer specification: bread flour typically L* â¥ 92, while whole wheat is lower. In many African markets, customers equate whiter flour with higher quality, so colour control directly affects marketability.

5. Farinograph and MixoLab Testing

The Farinograph measures dough resistance during mixing, giving water absorption, development time, stability, and degree of softening. A stable dough (stability >8 min) is essential for high-speed bakeries. The MixoLab extends this by measuring protein and starch behaviour during heating and cooling, predicting baking performance. For African bakeries using local wheat blends or imported wheat, these instruments help set the correct water addition and mixing time, reducing waste and improving consistency.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Laboratory and Milling Hazards

Flour dust is highly combustible. In enclosed spaces such as laboratory sieving areas, mill rooms, and pneumatic conveying systems, a dust cloud can ignite with a single spark, causing a devastating secondary explosion. Always use explosion-proof electrical fittings, keep dust extraction systems maintained, and prohibit smoking or open flames. When handling chemicals for Kjeldahl digestion (concentrated sulfuric acid, sodium hydroxide) or ash muffle furnaces at 550â600Â°C, wear acid-resistant gloves, face shields, and heat-resistant aprons. Never add water to concentrated acid. In tropical African laboratories without reliable ventilation, ensure fume hoods or local exhaust ventilation are functional before digestion. Store wheat and flour away from moisture and pests; mycotoxin (aflatoxin, DON) risks increase with poor storage. Train all staff on emergency shutdown and first aid for acid burns and dust explosions.

6. Interpreting Test Results for Quality Control

QC interpretation is where technical data becomes business decisions. Establish specification limits for each product and use statistical process control (SPC) to monitor trends. If moisture rises above 14.5%, investigate tempering or dryer performance immediately. If FN drops below 250 s, segregate the wheat and blend with sound stock. If protein falls below customer specification, consider blending with higher-protein wheat or adjusting mill extraction. Record every result in a batch log and issue a certificate of analysis with each delivery. In African markets where trust is built on consistency, a well-documented QC system is a competitive advantage.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Calibrate moisture meter and oven daily; verify against a reference sample.
  2. Test wheat moisture and protein at intake before unloading; reject or segregate out-of-spec loads.
  3. Run Falling Number on every wheat lot during rainy harvest periods; target 250â350 s for bread flour.
  4. Check ash and protein on finished flour at least every 4 hours; record against specification.
  5. Perform gluten index or wet gluten test on each production run; adjust blending if outside range.
  6. Use Farinograph or MixoLab weekly to verify dough behaviour and water absorption.
  7. Measure colour and particle size for customer-critical orders; retain samples for traceability.
  8. Review QC trends weekly with production and sales; issue certificate of analysis with every dispatch.
