1. Blast Furnace Design and Operation

A blast furnace is a tall, vertical shaft furnace used to smelt iron ore into molten iron. In Africa, small-scale blast furnaces are often built from local clay or steel drums lined with firebrick. The furnace is charged from the top with alternating layers of iron ore, coke (or charcoal), and limestone. A hot air blast (often preheated to 600â1000Â°C) is blown into the bottom through tuyeres. The coke burns, producing carbon monoxide that reduces the iron ore to iron. The molten iron and slag collect at the bottom and are tapped periodically. Key design parameters include the furnace height (typically 5â30 meters for industrial units, but 1â3 meters for small-scale), the diameter of the hearth, and the number of tuyeres. In Africa, charcoal is often used instead of coke because of its availability, but it is weaker and can crush under the burden, so the furnace must be shorter. Operation requires careful control of the charge ratio: for every 1 kg of iron ore, you need about 0.5â1 kg of coke and 0.2â0.5 kg of limestone. The blast volume should be around 100â200 mÂ³ per minute per square meter of hearth area. Temperature zones: preheating (200â800Â°C), reduction (800â1100Â°C), and melting (1200â1600Â°C). Tapping is done every 2â4 hours. Safety: always wear leather aprons, face shields, and gloves. Never stand in front of the tap hole. In Nigeria, the Ajaokuta Steel Plant uses large blast furnaces, but small foundries in Kenya and Tanzania use cupola-like furnaces for cast iron. Regular maintenance includes checking for cracks in the refractory lining and replacing tuyeres when they burn out.

2. Electric Arc Furnace Fundamentals

An electric arc furnace (EAF) uses high-voltage electric arcs to melt scrap metal or directly reduced iron. It is common in South Africa, Egypt, and Nigeria for steel production. The furnace is a large cylindrical vessel with a removable roof through which three graphite electrodes descend. When the electrodes touch the scrap, an arc forms, generating temperatures up to 3000Â°C. The furnace is lined with refractory bricks, usually magnesite or dolomite, because the slag is basic. A typical EAF for a small African foundry might have a capacity of 1â5 tonnes. Power supply is critical: a 1-tonne furnace needs about 500â800 kVA. The transformer steps down high voltage to low voltage (200â600 V) but high current (10,000â50,000 A). Charging is done by opening the roof and dropping scrap in a bucket. Melting takes 30â60 minutes. After melting, you add alloying elements and tap the steel into a ladle. Slag is removed by tilting the furnace. In Africa, EAFs are often used because they can melt locally available scrap, reducing dependence on imported coke. However, electricity supply can be unreliable, so many operators use diesel generators as backup. Key operating parameters: arc length (controlled by electrode position), power input (adjusted by transformer taps), and foamy slag practice (injecting carbon and oxygen to create a foamy slag that shields the arcs and reduces heat loss). Safety: never touch electrodes or water-cooled cables. Wear arc-rated clothing. In South Africa, companies like Scaw Metals use large EAFs, while small workshops in Ghana use 1-tonne units.

3. Reverberatory Furnace Principles

A reverberatory furnace is a rectangular or cylindrical furnace where the charge is melted by heat reflected from the roof and walls. It is used for melting copper, tin, and aluminum, and for refining metals. In Africa, it is common in small-scale copper smelting in Zambia and the DRC. The furnace has a shallow hearth, a curved roof, and a chimney at one end. Fuel (coal, oil, or gas) is burned in a firebox at one end, and the hot gases pass over the charge and exit through the chimney. The roof is made of high-alumina bricks to withstand temperatures up to 1600Â°C. The hearth is lined with magnesite or chrome-magnesite bricks. The charge is introduced through doors on the side. Melting occurs primarily by radiation from the roof, so the roof must be kept hot. A typical small reverberatory furnace might be 2 m long, 1 m wide, and 1 m high, with a capacity of 500 kg. Fuel consumption is high: about 1â2 kg of coal per kg of metal melted. To improve efficiency, preheat the combustion air using a recuperator. The slag is tapped from a notch at the side, and metal is tapped from a lower notch. In Zambia, the Nkana smelter uses large reverberatory furnaces for copper. For a learner, key points: keep the roof clean, maintain a slightly oxidizing atmosphere to burn off sulfur, and avoid overcharging. Safety: wear heat-resistant gloves and face shields. Never open the charging door while the burner is on full blast.

4. Flash Smelting Furnace Technology

Flash smelting is a pyrometallurgical process for smelting sulfide ores, especially copper and nickel. It was developed in Finland but is used in Africa at large mines like Kansanshi in Zambia and Tenke Fungurume in the DRC. In flash smelting, dry concentrate is injected with oxygen-enriched air or pure oxygen into a hot furnace. The particles ignite and burn instantly, generating their own heat. The furnace is a tall, vertical shaft with a reaction chamber at the top and a settler at the bottom. The reaction shaft is lined with chrome-magnesite bricks, and the settler is lined with magnesite-chrome bricks. Temperatures reach 1300â1400Â°C. The products are a matte (molten metal sulfide) and a slag. The matte is tapped and further processed, while the slag is discarded or reprocessed. Key design parameters: oxygen enrichment (typically 30â70% oxygen), concentrate feed rate (e.g., 50â100 tonnes per hour for large units), and furnace pressure (slightly negative to prevent gas leakage). For small-scale operations, flash smelting is not practical because it requires fine, dry concentrate and sophisticated gas handling. However, the principles are useful to understand. In Africa, flash smelting is used at the Kansanshi smelter in Zambia, which produces about 300,000 tonnes of copper per year. Safety: the main hazards are sulfur dioxide gas and molten material. Always wear self-contained breathing apparatus when working near the reaction shaft. The off-gas is rich in SO2 and is captured to make sulfuric acid.

5. Crucible and Cupola Furnaces

Crucible furnaces are simple: a clay-graphite or silicon carbide crucible is placed inside a furnace heated by coke, oil, or gas. The metal is melted in the crucible, not in contact with the fuel, so it is clean. They are ideal for melting small amounts (5â50 kg) of aluminum, brass, bronze, and precious metals. In Africa, crucible furnaces are common in artisanal gold and jewelry workshops in Ghana, Mali, and South Africa. A typical furnace is a steel drum lined with firebrick, with a hole for the crucible and a lid. Fuel is coke or LPG. Melting times: 30â60 minutes for aluminum, 1â2 hours for copper alloys. Temperature control is by adjusting air supply. Crucibles must be preheated to avoid cracking. Cupola furnaces are vertical shaft furnaces used for melting cast iron. They are like small blast furnaces but simpler. The cupola is charged with alternate layers of coke, limestone, and scrap iron. Air is blown through tuyeres near the bottom. The coke burns, melting the iron, which trickles down and is tapped continuously. A typical cupola is 1â3 m tall and 0.5â1 m in diameter, melting 1â5 tonnes per hour. In Africa, cupolas are used in foundries in Nigeria, Kenya, and Zimbabwe. Key operating parameters: coke-to-iron ratio (1:8 to 1:12), blast rate (80â150 mÂ³/min per mÂ²), and tuyere size. Safety: cupolas produce carbon monoxide, so good ventilation is essential. Never tap molten iron into a wet ladleâit will explode.

6. Refractory Materials and Furnace Linings

Refractory materials are heat-resistant materials used to line furnaces. They must withstand high temperatures, thermal shock, chemical attack from slag and gases, and mechanical wear. In Africa, common refractories include fireclay bricks (for temperatures up to 1200Â°C), high-alumina bricks (up to 1600Â°C), magnesite bricks (basic, for steel furnaces), and chrome-magnesite bricks (for copper and nickel furnaces). For small-scale furnaces, locally made clay bricks mixed with sawdust or rice husks can be used, but they have lower performance. The lining is installed in layers: an insulating layer (e.g., diatomaceous earth) against the steel shell, then a firebrick layer, then a working lining of high-alumina or magnesite bricks. The working lining is the sacrificial layer that contacts the melt. Mortar must match the brick type: fireclay mortar for firebricks, magnesite mortar for magnesite bricks. Expansion joints are needed to allow for thermal expansion. In Africa, refractory suppliers include Vesuvius in South Africa and local brick makers in Nigeria. Maintenance: inspect linings after every campaign. Cracks can be patched with refractory cement. A lining typically lasts 6â24 months depending on operation. For a small foundry, a 1-tonne cupola might need 200â300 firebricks. Safety: always wear a dust mask when cutting or grinding refractories; some contain crystalline silica. When installing, ensure bricks are dry and free of moisture to prevent steam explosions during first heat-up.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Molten Metal, Off-Gas & Refractory Failure

Molten metal and matte contact with water causes violent steam explosions. In African smelters with seasonal humidity and monsoon/rainy-season water ingress, all furnace floors, launders, and tapholes must be dry, inspected, and covered before tapping. Sulphur dioxide from sulphide smelting is lethal at 100 ppm and immediately dangerous above 500 ppm â flash and reverberatory furnace off-gas systems must have continuous SOâ monitoring, functioning scrubbing or acid plants, and site-wide alarm systems. Refractory lining failure (hot spots, breakthrough) is the leading cause of catastrophic furnace incidents; thermal imaging and regular lining thickness surveys are mandatory, not optional. Never bypass water-cooling, pressure, or gas-detection interlocks. Zambia's OSHA and South Africa's Mine Health and Safety Act (MHSA) both require documented risk assessments for all hot-work and tapping operations. A single molten-metal splash incident can halt production for months â the business case for safety discipline is absolute.
