Lessons

1. Determining Harvest Maturity

Determining when to harvest avocados is critical because they do not ripen on the tree. They must be mature enough to ripen properly after picking. In Africa, varieties like Hass, Fuerte, and Pinkerton are common. For Hass, maturity is reached when the fruit's dry matter content is at least 24%. You can test this by weighing a sample of fruit, drying them in an oven or sun, and re-weighing. A simpler field test is to check the skin color: Hass turns from green to dark purple or black, but some varieties remain green. Another method is to gently scratch the skin near the stem; if the color underneath is pale yellow, it's mature. Also, the fruit should be firm but not rock-hard. In Kenya, harvest seasons vary: Hass is often ready from March to September, while Fuerte from February to May. In South Africa, the season runs from April to October. Always check with local extension officers for exact timing in your area. Harvest too early and the fruit will shrivel and not ripen; too late and it may drop or rot. Use a refractometer to measure dry matter if available, but for small-scale farmers, the scratch test and calendar are practical. Remember, avocados are climacteric; they continue to ripen after harvest. So, maturity, not ripeness, is what you look for. Train your pickers to recognize these signs to ensure consistent quality.

2. Harvesting Tools and Techniques

Harvesting avocados requires the right tools and careful techniques to avoid bruising, which reduces market value. The main tools are a picking pole with a cutting hook and a catching bag, a clean sharp knife or pruning shears, and clean plastic crates or bins. For tall trees, use a pole 3-4 meters long with a hook and a small basket at the end to catch the fruit. For lower branches, hand-pick by holding the fruit and cutting the stem with a knife, leaving a short stub (about 5 mm) to prevent rot. Never pull the fruit off; that damages the stem end and causes early spoilage. Always harvest in the cool of the day, early morning or late afternoon, to reduce heat stress. Wear soft gloves to avoid scratching the skin. In Africa, many farmers use ladders, but ensure they are stable. Climb carefully and never overreach. Sanitize tools between trees with a 10% bleach solution to prevent disease spread. Collect fruit in clean crates; do not drop them into buckets. A good picker can harvest 200-300 fruits per hour. After picking, keep fruit in the shade immediately. Techniques vary by region: in Kenya, some farmers use long poles with nets; in South Africa, mechanical harvesters are used on large farms. For smallholders, manual picking is fine. Remember, avocados bruise easily; a bruise may not show immediately but will cause dark spots later. So, handle each fruit like an egg. Proper harvesting sets the stage for good post-harvest life.

3. Field Handling and Collection

After harvesting, how you handle avocados in the field directly affects their shelf life. As soon as a fruit is picked, it starts to lose moisture and quality. So, move fruit to shade within 30 minutes. Do not leave them in the sun; temperatures above 30C cause rapid softening. Use clean, smooth-sided plastic crates or bins; never use sacks or baskets that can bruise the fruit. Stack crates no more than three high to avoid crushing. If you are collecting from several trees, use a central collection point in the shade. In Africa, many farms use a simple shade structure made of poles and palm fronds. For collection, use a tractor with a trailer or a pickup, but ensure the bed is lined with foam or soft material. Avoid driving over rough roads; vibrations cause bruising. If you must transport by headload or bicycle, use padded containers. Field heat is a major enemy; if possible, pre-cool fruit by moving them to a cool room within 2 hours. For smallholders, a well-ventilated shed works. Also, never mix fruit from different trees or harvest dates; keep batches separate for traceability. Remove any damaged or diseased fruit immediately; they release ethylene and speed up ripening of healthy fruit. Weigh and record each batch. In Kenya, collection centers often use a simple scale and record book. Proper field handling reduces post-harvest losses, which can be up to 30% in Africa if not done well. So, train your workers to be gentle and quick.

4. Post-Harvest Cleaning and Grading

Cleaning and grading avocados after harvest is essential to meet market standards and get a better price. Start by removing any leaves, twigs, or soil. Do not wash avocados unless they are very dirty; washing can spread disease and remove the natural bloom that protects the fruit. If you must wash, use clean water with a small amount of food-grade detergent, then rinse and dry gently with a soft cloth. For grading, sort by size, shape, and skin condition. In Africa, common grades are: Extra Class (perfect, uniform), Class I (good quality, slight defects), and Class II (fair quality, more defects). Use a sizing ring or a simple caliper to measure diameter. For Hass, sizes range from 60 mm (small) to 80 mm (large). Remove any fruit with cuts, bruises, sunburn, or insect damage. These can be sold locally or processed. Grading tables should be clean and well-lit. Workers should wear gloves. In Kenya, the Horticultural Crops Development Authority (HCDA) sets standards. For export, fruits must meet stricter criteria. After grading, pack fruit into clean crates or boxes. Label each box with grade, size, and harvest date. Proper grading reduces waste and ensures customer satisfaction. Remember, a uniform box of avocados sells faster. Also, keep graded fruit in a cool place. This step adds value and opens doors to supermarkets and exporters.

5. Packaging and Storage

Packaging and storage are key to maintaining avocado quality after harvest. For packaging, use clean, ventilated plastic crates or cardboard boxes. For local markets, 4 kg or 10 kg crates are common. For export, use 4 kg cartons with fruit arranged in a single layer or in trays. Line boxes with soft paper or foam to prevent movement. Do not overfill; leave space for air circulation. In Africa, many farmers use wooden boxes, but these can splinter and damage fruit. Plastic crates are better because they are reusable and easy to clean. For storage, avocados should be kept at 5-7C for short-term (up to 2 weeks) or 2-4C for longer storage (up to 4 weeks). However, most smallholders lack refrigeration. In that case, store in a cool, dark, well-ventilated room. You can use a charcoal cooler: a double-walled structure filled with charcoal, kept wet, which lowers temperature by 5-10C. This is a low-cost technology used in Kenya and Tanzania. Also, keep fruit away from strong-smelling substances like onions or chemicals; avocados absorb odors. Relative humidity should be 85-90%. If you have a refrigerator, set it at 5C. For ripening, keep at 20-25C for 3-5 days. Never store avocados with apples or bananas; they produce ethylene and speed ripening. Proper storage reduces weight loss and maintains firmness. Always check for spoiled fruit weekly and remove them. With good packaging and storage, you can extend shelf life and sell when prices are better.

6. Transportation to Market

Transporting avocados to market requires care to prevent bruising and overheating. The best transport is a refrigerated truck, but in Africa, many farmers use open trucks, pickups, or even bicycles. If using an open truck, cover the load with a white tarpaulin to reflect sun, but leave sides open for ventilation. Never pile crates more than 4 high. Secure them with ropes to prevent shifting. Avoid traveling during the hottest part of the day; early morning or evening is best. For long distances, use a refrigerated truck set at 5-7C. In Kenya, exporters use refrigerated containers from Nairobi to Mombasa port. For local markets, a pickup can work if you pack crates tightly and drive slowly on rough roads. Use air suspension if available. Also, plan your route to avoid potholes. If you are transporting by bicycle or motorbike, use padded boxes and go slowly. Time is critical: the shorter the journey, the better. For export, avocados must reach the port within 24-48 hours of harvest. Always keep records of transport conditions. Upon arrival, unload immediately into a cool place. In South Africa, the avocado industry uses specialized cold chain logistics. For smallholders, group transport can reduce costs. Remember, rough handling during transport causes bruises that lead to rot. So, train drivers and loaders to be gentle. Proper transportation ensures your avocados arrive fresh and fetch a good price.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling of Avocado in Africa



Avocado has become one of Africa's most bankable export crops. Kenya, Tanzania, South Africa, Ethiopia, Rwanda, Uganda, and Zimbabwe all ship significant volumes to the EU, UK, Middle East, and increasingly to intra-African markets. But the margin between profit and loss is decided in the 72 hours after the fruit leaves the tree. A single mishandled consignment picked immature, bruised in the field, held too long at ambient temperature can turn a premium export lot into a rejected container or a low-value processing grade. This Practical Reference consolidates the seven lessons in this module into a single field-and-packhouse decision guide, with the numbers, tools, and checks that determine whether your avocado crop earns export, regional, or processing prices.





1. Determining Harvest Maturity


Maturity is the single most important quality decision. Immature fruit never ripens properly, has poor oil content, and is the leading cause of export rejection. For 'Hass', the internationally traded standard is a minimum of 24% dry matter (DM); for 'Fuerte', 22% DM is commonly accepted. In Kenya, KEPHIS enforces a minimum maturity index before export certification. South Africa's SAAGA and the Peruvian and Chilean export industries use similar DM thresholds.


Field methods: (a) dry matter test microwave or oven-dry a representative sample; (b) count-back method harvest at a set number of days after full bloom (varies by altitude and cultivar); (c) visual and feel fruit fills the pedicel cavity, skin darkens slightly on 'Hass'. Never rely on size alone. A large but immature fruit is a low-value fruit.

2. Harvesting Tools and Techniques


Avocado is a climacteric fruit it ripens after harvest and is easily bruised. The harvest crew's technique directly determines packout percentage and shelf life. Use sharp, curved harvesting clippers or pole-mounted cutters for tall trees. Cut the pedicel (stem) to about 510 mm, leaving a short stem to reduce stem-end rot and water loss. Never pull, twist, or drop fruit.


Field hygiene: disinfect clippers between trees with 70% alcohol or a 1% sodium hypochlorite solution, especially where anthracnose or stem-end rot is present. Harvest in the cool of the day early morning or late afternoon. Avoid harvesting wet fruit; surface moisture encourages fungal infection during storage.




SAFETY WARNING Tree Climbing and Pole Work


Falls from avocado trees and ladders are a leading cause of serious injury on African avocado farms. Always use a stable three-point ladder, never climb with a full picking bag, and never work a pole cutter directly beneath live power lines. Wear a helmet, non-slip boots, and eye protection. A single injury during harvest can cost a smallholder more than the entire season's crop value train every picker before the season starts.




3. Field Handling and Collection


The field is where most quality is lost. Fruit must never be placed in direct sun, never dumped into sacks, and never left in heaps. Use ventilated plastic field crates (typically 1020 kg capacity) lined with soft material. Stack crates no more than three high. Move fruit to the collection point within 2 hours of picking.


Collection points should be shaded, with a hard, clean floor not bare soil. Weigh and record each picker's lot separately so that traceability is maintained and quality problems can be traced back to a specific block or crew.



4. Post-Harvest Cleaning and Grading


Cleaning removes field dust, latex, and fungal spores. Use clean, cool water (ideally 1215 C) with a registered food-grade sanitiser. Do not soak fruit a quick dip or spray is sufficient. Dry gently with soft cloths or forced air. Never scrub the skin.


Grading sorts fruit by size, shape, skin condition, and defects. Export grade requires uniform shape, clean skin, no bruising, no insect damage, and correct maturity. Reject fruit goes to regional markets, processing (oil, guacamole, pulp), or animal feed. Accurate grading protects your brand and prevents a single bad fruit from downgrading an entire carton.

5. Packaging and Storage


Pack fruit into clean, vented cartons of 4 kg (export) or 10 kg (regional). Use moulded pulp or plastic trays to prevent movement. Label with farm code, harvest date, block, and grade this is now a market requirement in the EU under due-diligence rules.


Pre-cool fruit to 57 C within 6 hours of harvest. Optimum storage for 'Hass' is 57 C at 9095% relative humidity for 24 weeks, depending on maturity. Do not store avocado with ethylene-producing fruit such as bananas, mangoes, or tomatoes it accelerates ripening and shortens shelf life. For smallholders without cold rooms, harvest only what can be sold or moved within 48 hours.



6. Transportation to Market


Transport is the final quality gate. Use refrigerated trucks for export and long-haul regional movement; for short domestic runs, use covered, well-ventilated vehicles. Never transport avocado in open pickups under direct sun. Stack cartons on pallets, not directly on the truck floor, and secure loads to prevent shifting. Maintain the cold chain every break in temperature costs shelf life. For airfreight, deliver to the airport within the pre-cooling window and ensure the freight forwarder maintains the cold chain from packhouse to aircraft.



Quick Reference Checklist



  • Test maturity before harvest: 'Hass' 24% dry matter, 'Fuerte' 22% never harvest on size alone.

  • Harvest cool: pick early morning or late afternoon; never harvest wet fruit.

  • Cut, don't pull: leave a 510 mm stem; disinfect clippers between trees.

  • Field crates only: no sacks, no heaps, no direct sun; move to shade within 2 hours.

  • Clean gently: cool, sanitised water; no soaking, no scrubbing.

  • Grade accurately: separate export, regional, and processing fruit before packing.

  • Pre-cool fast: 57 C within 6 hours; store at 9095% RH; keep away from bananas and mangoes.

  • Protect the cold chain: refrigerated or covered transport; palletise; secure the load; label every carton.
