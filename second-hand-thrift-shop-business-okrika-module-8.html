Lessons

1. Separating Business and Personal Money

Many okrika sellers mix business and personal money. This is the fastest way to kill your business. You buy stock with money meant for rent, or you spend sales money on personal needs, and soon you cannot restock. The fix is simple: separate your money. Open a separate mobile money wallet or bank account for the business. Use a different SIM card if possible. Every sale goes into that account. Every business expense comes out of it. Your personal money stays in your personal wallet. If you must take money for personal use, record it as 'owner's draw' and subtract it from profit. For example, if you sell 20 dresses at 50 birr each, that's 1,000 birr. Deposit all 1,000 birr into the business account. Then pay yourself a fixed salary, say 300 birr per week. This way, you always know how much the business has. At the end of each day, count your cash and compare with your sales record. Any difference must be explained. This discipline helps you qualify for loans and grow. Start today: get a separate wallet or account. Label it 'Okrika Business'. Never mix.

2. Tracking Daily Sales and Expenses

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Every day, write down your sales and expenses. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Wave or Keeper. At the end of each sale, record: date, item sold, quantity, price, total. For expenses, record: date, what you bought (stock, transport, rent, airtime), amount. For example: '15/06/2025 - Sold 3 shirts at 100 birr = 300 birr'. '15/06/2025 - Bought 10 shirts from supplier = 500 birr'. At the end of the day, total your sales and expenses. Subtract expenses from sales to get daily profit. Keep receipts for all expenses. If you use mobile money, the transaction history is your record. At the end of the week, review your notebook. You will see which items sell fast and which don't. You will also see if you are spending too much on transport or airtime. This information helps you make better decisions. For example, if you see that you spend 200 birr per day on transport, consider buying in bulk once a week. Tracking takes only 10 minutes a day but can double your profit.

3. Budgeting for Restocking and Overhead Costs

A budget is a plan for your money. Without it, you may spend all your profit on small things and have nothing to restock. Start by listing your monthly overhead costs: rent for your stall or storage, transport to buy stock, airtime and data, packaging bags, and any helper's salary. Add them up. For example: rent 1,000 birr, transport 500 birr, airtime 200 birr, bags 100 birr, helper 300 birr. Total overhead = 2,100 birr per month. Next, estimate your monthly sales. If you sell 300 items at average 80 birr, that's 24,000 birr. Subtract overhead: 24,000 - 2,100 = 21,900 birr. From this, you need to restock. Aim to spend at least 50% of your sales on new stock: 12,000 birr. That leaves 9,900 birr for profit and savings. But remember, sales vary. So set a restocking budget: decide how much you will spend each week, say 3,000 birr. Stick to it. If you see a great deal, you can adjust, but always keep enough for overhead. Write your budget on paper and keep it where you see it daily. Review it weekly and adjust based on actual sales.

4. Simple Profit Calculation and Reinvestment

Profit is what remains after you subtract all business expenses from your sales. Many sellers think all the money in their pocket is profit, but that's wrong. You must subtract the cost of goods sold (what you paid for the items), transport, rent, airtime, and any other business expense. For example, you buy 10 dresses for 500 birr total. You sell them for 1,000 birr. Your sales = 1,000 birr. Your cost of goods = 500 birr. Other expenses (transport, airtime) = 100 birr. Profit = 1,000 - 500 - 100 = 400 birr. That 400 birr is your true profit. Now, reinvestment: use part of your profit to buy more stock or improve your business. A good rule is to reinvest at least 50% of profit. So from 400 birr, reinvest 200 birr into new stock. The remaining 200 birr can be your personal income or savings. If you reinvest consistently, your business grows. For example, if you start with 10 dresses and reinvest 200 birr each week, after a month you might be buying 20 dresses. Track your profit weekly. If profit is low, find ways to reduce costs or increase prices slightly. Never eat your seed capital.

5. Using Mobile Money and Cash Handling Safely

Mobile money is safe and convenient, but you must use it wisely. In many African countries, services like M-Pesa, Airtel Money, MTN Mobile Money, and Tigo Pesa are common. Register for a business account if possible. Always confirm the transaction SMS before handing over goods. For example, if a customer sends 500 birr, wait for the message that says 'You have received 500 birr from...' before you give the item. Beware of fake messages. Scammers send fake SMS. Check your balance by dialing the code (e.g., *144# for some networks). For cash, keep it in a safe place. Do not carry all your cash at once. Use a lockable cash box at your stall. If you must carry money, split it between pockets or use a money belt. At the end of the day, deposit excess cash into your mobile money or bank account. Keep small change for customers. Count your cash before you leave the market. If you have helpers, set clear rules: only you handle money. If you accept mobile money, have a dedicated phone for business. Never share your PIN. If you suspect fraud, report immediately to your network provider.

6. Planning for Slow Seasons and Emergencies

Every business has slow seasons. For okrika, sales may drop during rainy seasons, holidays, or when customers have no money. You must plan ahead. During good months, save at least 10% of your profit in a separate savings account or mobile money savings. For example, if you make 5,000 birr profit in a good month, save 500 birr. After six good months, you have 3,000 birr for slow times. Also, build an emergency fund for unexpected costs: sickness, theft, or urgent restocking. Aim for at least one month's overhead costs. If your overhead is 2,000 birr per month, save 2,000 birr as emergency fund. During slow seasons, reduce expenses: buy less stock, negotiate lower rent if possible, and focus on selling existing inventory. You can also run promotions like 'buy 2 get 1 free' to clear stock. If you have a mobile money savings account, you can earn small interest. Avoid borrowing from loan sharks. Instead, join a savings group (like a SACCO or chama) where you can access small loans at fair rates. Plan for emergencies by keeping a list of contacts: supplier, landlord, mobile money agent. Review your plan monthly. With a plan, slow seasons won't kill your business.

<h2>Practical Reference: Managing Money and Daily Operations in an Okrika Business</h2>

<p>In the second-hand clothing trade known across much of Africa as <strong>okrika</strong>, <em>mitumba</em>, or <em>salaula</em> daily cash flow is fast, informal, and easily blurred with household spending. A trader can sell well on Saturday and still be broke by Wednesday. This practical reference gives you the systems, numbers, and habits that separate a business that survives from one that collapses. It is written for the African market: mobile money, bale-based restocking, market-day rhythms, and family pressure included.</p>

<p>Treat every lesson here as an operating rule, not a suggestion. The traders who grow are not the ones who sell the most they are the ones who <strong>know where the money went</strong>.</p>

<h3>1. Separating Business and Personal Money</h3>
<p>The single biggest killer of small okrika businesses is mixed money. When the same pocket, the same mobile wallet, or the same tin holds both school fees and restocking cash, you cannot tell whether you are making a profit. Open a dedicated mobile money wallet (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money, Orange Money, Wave whichever is dominant in your market) and use it <strong>only</strong> for business. Pay yourself a fixed weekly or monthly salary from it. If you must keep cash, use two separate boxes and label them.</p>

<h3>2. Tracking Daily Sales and Expenses</h3>
<p>You do not need accounting software. You need consistency. Record every sale and every expense the same day, at the same time for example, at closing. A simple exercise book with columns for Date, Item, Sales, Expenses, and Balance is enough. If you have a smartphone, a free Google Sheet or a mobile-money statement screenshot folder works just as well.</p>

<h3>3. Budgeting for Restocking and Overhead Costs</h3>
<p>In okrika, restocking is your biggest single cost often a full bale purchased outright. Budget it as a percentage of sales, not as "whatever is left." A workable split for most small traders is <strong>4050% restock, 1525% overheads, 10% savings, 10% reinvestment, and the rest as owner pay</strong>. Adjust to your reality, but write the percentages down and stick to them for at least three months before changing them.</p>

<h3>4. Simple Profit Calculation and Reinvestment</h3>
<p>Profit is not the money in your pocket at the end of the day. It is:</p>
<p style="background:#f1faee;padding:10px;border-left:4px solid #2a9d8f;"><strong>Profit = Total Sales (Cost of Goods Sold + Overheads + Owner Salary)</strong></p>
<p>If you sold KSh 60,000 of clothes that cost KSh 27,000, paid KSh 12,000 overheads, and took KSh 9,000 salary, your profit is KSh 12,000. Of that, KSh 6,000 goes to savings and KSh 6,000 to reinvestment for example, buying a better-graded bale, adding a second display rack, or renting a stall at a busier market one extra day per week. Reinvestment is what turns a stall into a shop.</p>

<h3>5. Using Mobile Money and Cash Handling Safely</h3>
<p>Mobile money is now the default payment channel for many African traders. Use it deliberately:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use a <strong>business-registered SIM/wallet</strong> where available (e.g., M-Pesa Business, MoMo Agent/Business).</li>
<li>Keep your PIN private never share it, even with family.</li>
<li>Confirm every incoming payment before releasing goods. Fraudsters use fake SMS alerts.</li>
<li>Withdraw or transfer large cash the same day. Do not carry bale money home in a handbag.</li>
<li>Keep a written or screenshot record of every mobile transaction it is your audit trail.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:2px solid #e63946;background:#fdecea;padding:14px;border-radius:6px;margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Cash and Mobile Money Fraud</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Never release goods on the basis of an SMS alone. Fraudsters send fake "You have received KSh X" messages, or reverse transactions after collection. <strong>Always open your own mobile money app or dial the official balance-check code</strong> and confirm the credit in your own account before handing over clothes. Do not share your PIN, do not accept "help" from strangers at the agent till, and do not keep large cash at the stall overnight. In 2022, mobile money fraud was one of the most commonly reported cybercrimes across East Africa (Communications Authority of Kenya, 2023).</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Planning for Slow Seasons and Emergencies</h3>
<p>Okrika demand is seasonal. Sales spike before school opening, Christmas, and Eid; they fall in the deep rainy season and post-festival weeks. Plan for this by building a <strong>slow-season fund</strong> equal to at least one month of overheads plus one bale. Keep it in a separate mobile wallet or a locked savings account that you cannot easily withdraw from. Emergencies illness, theft, a flooded stall should be covered by the same fund, not by dipping into restocking money.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li> Business and personal money kept in <strong>separate wallets or boxes</strong> no mixing.</li>
<li> Every sale and expense recorded <strong>the same day</strong>, in a book or app.</li>
<li> Restock budget set at <strong>4050% of sales</strong>, written down, not guessed.</li>
<li> Owner salary paid as a <strong>fixed amount</strong>, not random pocket withdrawals.</li>
<li> At least <strong>10% of monthly sales</strong> moved to a slow-season / emergency fund.</li>
<li> Every mobile money payment <strong>verified in your own app</strong> before goods leave the stall.</li>
<li> Profit calculated monthly: <em>Sales (COGS + Overheads + Owner Salary)</em>.</li>
<li> Reinvestment decision made <strong>monthly</strong>, not only when things break.</li>
</ol>
