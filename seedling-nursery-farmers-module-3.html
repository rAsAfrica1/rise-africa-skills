3. Propagation Techniques

Growing seedlings from seed is the heart of the nursery business. This module covers seed sourcing, sowing, germination conditions, and the vegetative propagation methods that command premium prices.

Module 3 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 3.1 — Seed sourcing — quality, germination rates, storage

Seed quality determines everything downstream. Cheap seed = poor germination = unhappy customers.

Seed sourceGermination rateCostBest for
Certified hybrid seed (from agro-dealer)90–98%HighCommercial, high-value crops
Open-pollinated certified80–95%MediumReliable, saved seed allowed
Local market seed50–80%LowOnly for low-value crops
Saved seed (from own crops)VariableFreeNon-hybrid varieties only

What to check when buying seed:

  • Packaging date. Should be under 12 months old for most vegetables.
  • Company label. Reputable breeder names — Syngenta, Bayer, East-West Seed, Seed Co.
  • Germination rate on packet. Should be 85%+ for commercial seed.
  • Appearance. Uniform size, no discolouration, no mould.
  • Storage conditions at the shop. Cool, dark, dry. Not in a hot shelf under the sun.

Seed storage at home:

  • Sealed container (glass jar or airtight bag)
  • Cool, dark place (under 20°C if possible)
  • Silica gel packet to absorb moisture
  • Label with purchase date
  • Use within 12 months (2 years for some crops)
Never buy seed from an unlabelled bulk bin. You cannot verify age, origin, or germination. The small saving is not worth the risk of a failed crop.
Practical exercise 3.1: Visit 3 agro-dealers. Price 3 tomato hybrid varieties. Buy one packet each. Do your own germination test — plant 100 seeds, count how many sprout in 10 days.

Lesson 3.2 — Sowing — depth, spacing, timing

Sowing correctly determines your germination rate.

CropSeed depthGermination tempDays to germinate
Tomato0.5 cm20–28°C6–10 days
Cabbage0.5–1 cm15–22°C6–10 days
Onion0.5 cm15–22°C10–14 days
Pepper0.5 cm22–28°C7–14 days
Kale0.5–1 cm15–22°C6–10 days
Lettuce0.3 cm (barely covered)15–22°C5–10 days
Maize / corn2–3 cm20–30°C5–8 days

Sowing method:

  1. Fill trays with media, level off, firm gently.
  2. Water the tray before sowing.
  3. Place 1–2 seeds per cell (or scatter in bed).
  4. Cover with a thin layer of media.
  5. Water gently with fine rose.
  6. Cover with plastic or newspaper until germination (keeps humidity high).
  7. Remove cover as soon as first seedlings appear.
The depth rule: Sow at a depth of 2–3× the seed's diameter. Too shallow = dries out. Too deep = cannot reach light. When in doubt, shallower is better.
Practical exercise 3.2: Sow 1 tray of tomato seeds. Note the date. Check daily for germination. Count emergence rate over 10 days.

Lesson 3.3 — Germination conditions

Germination is triggered by three conditions: moisture, warmth, and oxygen. Get all three right and most seeds will sprout.

FactorIdeal rangeEffect if wrong
MoistureDamp media, not soakedToo dry: seeds dry out. Too wet: seeds rot.
Temperature20–28°C (varies by crop)Too cold: slow or no germination. Too hot: seeds cook.
OxygenLoose media, good drainageCompact media = no oxygen = rot.
LightDark for most seedsSome seeds (lettuce) need light. Most do not.

Speeding up germination:

  • Soak seeds overnight before sowing (not all crops — check).
  • Use a germination box. A tray covered with plastic or glass keeps humidity high.
  • Warmth. Place germination area in the warmest part of the nursery.
  • Consistent moisture. Mist lightly daily — do not let media dry out.
Practical exercise 3.3: Set up a germination box. Sow 100 seeds of any crop. Cover with plastic. Check daily. Remove cover as soon as first sprouts appear. Log germ date and rate.

Lesson 3.4 — Vegetative propagation — cuttings, grafting, budding

Not all seedlings come from seed. Some come from cuttings, grafting, or budding — and these command 3–10× the price per plant.

MethodWhat it isBest forPrice premium
CuttingsTake a stem or leaf and root itCassava, sweet potato, roses, many ornamentals2–3× seed
GraftingJoin a scion to a rootstockMango, avocado, citrus, tomato (disease resistance)5–10× seed
BuddingInsert a bud into rootstockCitrus, roses, stone fruit5–10× seed
LayeringRoot a stem while attached to plantSome shrubs, guava, mango3–5× seed
DivisionSeparate suckers or offsetsBanana, pineapple, agave2–3× seed

Why graft?

  • Rootstocks can resist soil diseases that kill seed-grown plants.
  • Grafted trees produce fruit years earlier.
  • Fruit quality matches the parent tree exactly.
  • Farmers pay premium prices for grafted trees because they save 3–5 years.
The premium rule: A tomato seedling sells for $0.15. A grafted mango tree sells for $5–$15. Same nursery, same land, same labour. Grafting is where the money is in a mature nursery.
Practical exercise 3.4: Buy 20 rootstock seedlings of mango or avocado. Watch a grafting tutorial online. Practise one graft. Aim for 50% success at first — you will improve.

Lesson 3.5 — Layering and division

Layering is a low-tech propagation method: you bend a low branch to the ground, cover the middle with soil, and let it root while still attached to the parent. After 6–12 weeks, you cut it free as a new plant.

Best for: guava, mango, some citrus, jasmine, bougainvillea.

Division is separating suckers, offshoots, or clumps from a parent plant. Each piece becomes a new plant.

Best for: banana, pineapple, agave, aloe, sugarcane, chrysanthemum, daylilies.

Steps for layering:

  1. Choose a low, flexible branch.
  2. Make a small cut on the underside (wound it).
  3. Bend the branch to ground level.
  4. Cover the wounded section with soil.
  5. Hold in place with a peg or stone.
  6. Keep damp for 6–12 weeks.
  7. Once roots form, cut from parent and transplant.

Steps for division:

  1. Wait until plant is dormant or after harvest.
  2. Dig up the parent clump carefully.
  3. Separate with a clean knife or fork.
  4. Each piece must have roots and shoots.
  5. Replant immediately at same depth.
  6. Water well.
Practical exercise 3.5: Try layering on any low-branching plant in your garden. Try division on any clumping plant. Even one success teaches the technique.

Lesson 3.6 — Tissue culture basics (advanced)

Tissue culture is high-tech propagation: taking a tiny piece of plant tissue and growing it in a sterile lab into many identical plants. Rare in African nurseries, but growing.

What it does:

  • Produces thousands of identical plants from one parent
  • Free from disease (unlike traditional cuttings)
  • Rapid multiplication of new varieties

Common tissue-cultured crops: banana, cassava, potato, strawberry, date palm, ornamental orchids.

Why most nurseries should not start here:

  • Requires a proper lab ($5,000–$50,000)
  • Requires trained technicians
  • Slow turnaround (weeks per cycle)
  • Waste rates high for beginners

If you want to enter tissue culture:

  1. Start with a mature, profitable nursery first.
  2. Partner with a university or research lab.
  3. Train one person in the technique.
  4. Start with one crop (usually banana or cassava).
  5. Scale slowly over 3–5 years.
The tissue culture rule: Do not attempt tissue culture in your first 3 years of nursery business. Master seeds, cuttings, and grafting first. These techniques are enough to build a $50,000+/year nursery.
Practical exercise 3.6: If you are interested in tissue culture, visit one African lab that does it. Ask about costs, output, and requirements. If not, note this as a 5-year goal.
