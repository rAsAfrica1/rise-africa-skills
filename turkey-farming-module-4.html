1. Common Turkey Diseases

Turkeys in Africa face several diseases that can wipe out your flock quickly if you don't act fast. The most common is Newcastle disease, which causes sneezing, coughing, twisting of the neck, and sudden death. It spreads through droppings, contaminated feed, and even your shoes. There is no cure, so vaccination is your only protection. Another killer is fowl pox, which shows as scabs on the head, legs, and mouth. It spreads slowly but can kill young poults. Blackhead disease is a big problem where turkeys share space with chickens. It causes yellow droppings, drooping wings, and darkening of the head. Chickens carry the parasite but don't get sick, so never keep turkeys and chickens together. Coccidiosis is caused by a tiny parasite in dirty water and wet litter. It leads to bloody droppings and weakness. Treat with amprolium in drinking water for 5 days. CRD (chronic respiratory disease) causes rattling breath and swollen eyes; use tylosin or doxycycline as directed by a vet. To prevent these, keep your turkey house dry, clean feeders daily, and isolate any new birds for 3 weeks. In Africa, many farmers lose birds because they don't recognize early signs. Check your flock every morning: look for birds that stand apart, have pale combs, or refuse to eat. If you see these signs, call a vet or agricultural extension officer immediately. Remember, dead birds should be buried deep or burned, never eaten or sold. With good management, you can reduce disease outbreaks by up to 80%.

2. Vaccination Protocols

Vaccination is the cheapest insurance for your turkeys. In Africa, the most important vaccines are for Newcastle disease (ND), fowl pox, and sometimes fowl typhoid. Start with ND vaccine at day 1 for poults, given as a drop in the eye or nostril. Repeat at 3 weeks and again at 8 weeks. After that, booster every 3 months if you keep breeders. For fowl pox, vaccinate at 4 weeks by wing web stab. Use a two-pronged needle to prick the wing web; check for scabs 7-10 days later to confirm it worked. Store vaccines in a cooler with ice packs, never in direct sunlight. Reconstitute only with the provided diluent, and use within 2 hours. Use clean syringes and boil them for 10 minutes before use. Never use disinfectants on syringes because they kill the vaccine. For Newcastle, you can also use a drinking water route, but add skim milk powder (2g per litre) to protect the virus. Withdraw water for 2 hours before giving the vaccine so birds are thirsty. Give enough waterers so every bird drinks. After vaccination, birds may look slightly sick for a day; that's normal. Keep records: date, vaccine name, batch number, and number of birds. In many African countries, the government or NGOs provide free ND vaccines. Contact your local veterinary office. If you cannot afford all vaccines, prioritize Newcastle because it kills fastest. Never vaccinate sick birds; wait until they recover. A good protocol can save 90% of your flock from deadly outbreaks.

3. Parasite Control

Parasites are silent thieves that steal your turkeys' feed and health. The two main types are internal (worms) and external (lice, mites, fleas). Internal worms like roundworms and tapeworms cause weight loss, dull feathers, and diarrhea. You can deworm every 3 months with levamisole or albendazole. Give the correct dose: usually 1 ml per 5 kg body weight orally. For a 10 kg turkey, give 2 ml. Use a syringe without needle and squirt into the beak. After deworming, collect and burn droppings for 24 hours to prevent reinfection. For external parasites, check under wings and around the vent. Lice and mites cause itching, feather loss, and restlessness. Dust birds with permethrin powder or spray with a solution of 2 ml permethrin per litre of water. Treat the house too: spray walls, roosts, and floor. Repeat after 10 days to kill newly hatched eggs. Keep litter dry; wet litter breeds coccidia and flies. Use wood shavings or chopped straw, and stir it daily. Add diatomaceous earth to dust baths; it cuts parasites naturally. Rotate your turkeys to fresh pasture if you free-range, and don't overcrowd. For coccidiosis, use amprolium in water for 5 days as a treatment, and consider a coccidiostat in feed for prevention. In Africa, many farmers use local plants like neem or tobacco leaves as repellents, but these are not as reliable as modern dewormers. Always follow withdrawal periods: for meat, stop dewormers 14 days before slaughter. Keep a deworming calendar and stick to it. Healthy turkeys gain weight faster and lay more eggs.

4. Biosecurity Practices

Biosecurity means keeping germs out of your turkey farm. It is the cheapest way to prevent disease. Start with a fence around your compound to keep out wild birds and visitors. Wild birds carry Newcastle and avian influenza. Use a footbath at the entrance of each pen. Fill it with 2% sodium hypochlorite (bleach) or 1% formalin. Change the solution daily. Only you and essential workers should enter. Wear dedicated boots and overalls that you leave at the farm. Never wear the same clothes to the market and then to your turkeys. Keep turkeys away from chickens, ducks, and other poultry. Chickens carry blackhead and coccidia without getting sick. If you must keep both, house them in separate buildings at least 50 metres apart. Quarantine new birds for 3 weeks in a separate pen. Watch for signs of illness before mixing. Clean and disinfect feeders and drinkers daily. Use a brush and soap, then rinse with bleach solution. Store feed in sealed containers to keep out rats and wild birds. Rats carry salmonella and leptospirosis. Control rodents with traps, not poison, because turkeys may eat poisoned rats. Remove dead birds immediately. Burn or bury them 1 metre deep with lime. Compost manure away from the turkey house. Limit visitors; if they must come, give them boots and a coat. After handling birds, wash your hands with soap. If you visit another farm, change clothes and shower before returning to your turkeys. These steps reduce disease risk by over 90%. Good biosecurity saves money on drugs and prevents heartbreak.

5. Recognizing Sick Birds

You must spot sick turkeys early to save them and the flock. A healthy turkey is alert, active, and has bright eyes and red wattles. It eats and drinks well, and its droppings are firm with a white cap. A sick turkey often stands apart with drooped wings and a dull, pale head. It may close its eyes or have a runny nose. Check for these signs every morning and evening. Look at the droppings: green or yellow watery droppings mean infection; bloody droppings mean coccidiosis; white pasty droppings mean pullorum. Listen for sneezing, coughing, or rattling breath. Watch for head shaking or twisting of the neck, which suggests Newcastle. Check the legs and feet for swelling or scabs, a sign of fowl pox or bumblefoot. Feel the crop: if it is hard and full, the bird may have impacted crop; if it is soft and squishy, it may have sour crop. A sick bird often stops eating but may drink a lot. Take its temperature if you can; normal is 40-41C. If you find a sick bird, isolate it immediately in a warm, quiet pen. Give it clean water with electrolytes and vitamins. Do not medicate blindly; call a vet or extension officer. If the bird dies, do not eat it. Send the body to a lab if possible for diagnosis. Keep a notebook to record symptoms, dates, and treatments. Over time, you will learn to spot problems before they spread. In Africa, where vet access is limited, your eyes are your best tool. Early action saves the flock.

6. Working with Veterinarians

A good veterinarian is your best partner in turkey farming. In many African countries, you can find vets through the Ministry of Agriculture, veterinary schools, or private clinics. Some also work for NGOs. Start by building a relationship before you have an emergency. Invite a vet to visit your farm once a year for a health check. That way, when disease strikes, they know your setup. Keep a record book with all treatments, vaccinations, and deaths. This helps the vet make a quick diagnosis. When you call a vet, describe the symptoms clearly: how many birds are sick, their age, what you see in droppings, and when it started. If the vet cannot come, you can send photos or short videos via WhatsApp. Many vets now offer phone consultations. For laboratory tests, collect fresh droppings or a dead bird in a plastic bag on ice. Do not freeze. The vet may prescribe antibiotics, dewormers, or vaccines. Always follow the dosage and withdrawal period. Never buy drugs from unlicensed sellers; they may be fake. If you cannot afford a private vet, contact your local government veterinary officer. They often provide free or subsidized services. Some African countries have community animal health workers who can help with basic treatments. Attend farmer field days and workshops where vets teach. Learn to give injections yourself for simple cases, but always ask for guidance first. A vet can also help you design a vaccination schedule and biosecurity plan. Remember, vets are busy; be polite and pay promptly if private. Good vet relationships lead to healthier flocks and higher profits.

<h2>Practical Reference: Turkey Health Management</h2>

<p>In African turkey production whether you are running a 200-bird backyard enterprise in Ogun State, a 2,000-bird semi-commercial unit in Kiambu County, or a 10,000-bird integrated operation in Mpumalanga disease is the single largest destroyer of margin. A brooding poult that dies at day 10 costs you the poult price, the feed consumed, the labour, the housing space, and the sale you never made. A flock hit by Newcastle or fowl cholera at week 12 can wipe out a full production cycle's profit in 72 hours.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference lesson is not a veterinary textbook. It is a working desk document: the numbers you need to budget for health, the signs that tell you to call a vet <em>today</em> rather than next week, the vaccination and parasite schedules that actually work in African conditions, and the biosecurity habits that separate profitable farms from farms that close within three years. Keep it printed. Keep it near the brooder house. Update it with your own vet's advice.</p>

<p><strong>Business first:</strong> every shilling, naira, cedi, or rand you spend on prevention returns multiples of what you would spend on treatment and treatment often fails anyway. Prevention is the cheapest input on the farm.</p>

<h3>The Six Lessons in One Page</h3>
<p>This reference module covers six areas. Each is treated in detail in its own lesson; here we compress them into the working knowledge you need on a Monday morning when something looks wrong.</p>

<p><strong>1. Common turkey diseases.</strong> The big four in African turkey flocks are Newcastle disease, fowl cholera (<em>Pasteurella multocida</em>), blackhead disease (<em>Histomonas meleagridis</em>), and colibacillosis (<em>E. coli</em>). Newcastle is the most economically devastating; blackhead is the most turkey-specific and is often misdiagnosed as coccidiosis. Coccidiosis and haemorrhagic enteritis round out the top six.</p>

<p><strong>2. Vaccination protocols.</strong> There is no single African schedule, but most commercial and semi-commercial farms follow a core set: Newcastle (HB1/La Sota or killed), fowl pox, and in some regions haemorrhagic enteritis and cholera bacterin. Vaccines must be stored cold a broken cold chain is worse than no vaccine, because it wastes money and gives false confidence.</p>

<p><strong>3. Parasite control.</strong> Internal parasites (roundworms, caecal worms, tapeworms) and external parasites (lice, mites, fleas) are chronic margin-killers. They rarely kill outright but they suppress growth, reduce feed conversion, and open the door to secondary infection. Rotate anthelmintics and treat for mites on a schedule, not only when you see them.</p>

<p><strong>4. Biosecurity practices.</strong> Biosecurity is the cheapest medicine on the farm. Footbaths, all-in/all-out batches, rodent control, restricted visitor access, and clean feed and water storage prevent more disease than any drug you can buy.</p>

<p><strong>5. Recognizing sick birds.</strong> Turkeys hide illness until it is advanced. The early signs reduced feed intake, huddling, ruffled feathers, watery droppings, snicking, head shaking are the ones that matter. Train every worker to report these daily.</p>

<p><strong>6. Working with veterinarians.</strong> A good vet is a business partner, not an emergency expense. Establish a relationship before you have a crisis, agree on a health plan, and use the vet for diagnosis and prescription, not for guessing.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Daily walk-through:</strong> Observe every pen at feeding time. Count birds, check droppings, listen for snicking or gasping, and watch for huddling or ruffled feathers. Record anything unusual.</li>
<li><strong>Footbath at every entrance:</strong> Keep a working disinfectant footbath at the gate and at each house door. Refresh the solution at least every 23 days, or sooner if dirty.</li>
<li><strong>All-in/all-out:</strong> Where possible, batch birds by age. Clean and rest the house for at least 714 days between batches. Do not mix ages in one house.</li>
<li><strong>Cold chain check:</strong> Confirm vaccine fridge temperature (28C) every morning. Never use a vaccine that has been warm, frozen, or past expiry.</li>
<li><strong>Quarantine new birds:</strong> Isolate any new or returning bird for at least 21 days, away from the main flock, before introduction.</li>
<li><strong>Rodent and wild bird control:</strong> Keep feed in closed bins, repair holes, clear vegetation around houses, and net open sides to keep wild birds out.</li>
<li><strong>Parasite calendar:</strong> Deworm on a fixed schedule (e.g. every 68 weeks) and treat for mites/lice at least quarterly. Rotate products to avoid resistance.</li>
<li><strong>Vet relationship:</strong> Have a named veterinarian, a written health plan, and a phone number on the wall. Call early not after half the flock is dead.</li>
</ol>
