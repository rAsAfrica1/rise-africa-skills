1. Combination Tanning

Combination tanning means using two or more tanning agents together to get the best properties of each. In Africa, many tanneries use a combination of vegetable tanning and chrome tanning. For example, first tan with 5% wattle extract (based on pelt weight) for 2 hours, then add 2% chrome powder (25% basicity) and drum for another 4 hours. This gives leather that is firm like vegetable-tanned but more heat-resistant and softer like chrome-tanned. You can also combine aluminium with vegetable tannins for washable leather. Use a drum that rotates at 8-12 rpm. The process: after bating, you pickle the pelt with salt and acid (pH 2.5-3.0). Then add the first tanning agent, drum for 2-4 hours, then add the second. Check penetration by cutting a small piece and looking for a uniform colour. If the centre is white, drum longer. After tanning, basify with sodium bicarbonate to pH 3.8-4.2 for chrome combination, or pH 4.5 for vegetable-aluminium. Then wash and fatliquor with 6% sulphated oil. This technique is good for goatskins and sheepskins, common in Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa. It reduces chrome use, which cuts costs and waste. A typical 100 kg pelt batch uses 5 kg wattle extract and 2 kg chrome powder. You need a drum, pH meter, and weighing scale. Always add chemicals slowly through the drum door to avoid spots. Combination tanning is a practical way to upgrade your leather without buying expensive new equipment.

2. Chrome-Free Tanning

Chrome-free tanning uses alternatives like glutaraldehyde, oxazolidine, or vegetable tannins to avoid chromium, which can harm workers and the environment. In Africa, where chrome waste can pollute water sources, this is important. A common method: after pickling, use 4% glutaraldehyde (50% solution) and drum for 6 hours at 30Â°C. Then add 2% aluminium sulphate and drum for 2 hours. Basify with sodium bicarbonate to pH 4.0. This gives a white leather that is soft and heat-resistant up to 80Â°C. Another option is using 8% mimosa extract with 2% aluminium. For 100 kg of pelt, use 4 kg glutaraldehyde and 2 kg aluminium sulphate. You need a drum, pH meter, and thermometer. The process takes about 8 hours. Check penetration by cutting a piece and looking for uniform colour. If the centre is not tanned, add more glutaraldehyde and drum for 2 more hours. After tanning, wash with 200% water (based on pelt weight) for 10 minutes. Then fatliquor with 5% sulphated oil and 2% lecithin. Chrome-free leather is popular for baby shoes, car interiors, and export to Europe. In Ethiopia and South Africa, some tanneries get premium prices for chrome-free leather. The main challenge is that glutaraldehyde is more expensive than chrome, but you save on waste treatment. Always wear gloves and goggles because glutaraldehyde can irritate skin. Store chemicals in a cool, dark place. This method is a step toward greener leather.

3. Enzyme-Assisted Processes

Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions. In leather making, they replace harsh chemicals in soaking, dehairing, and bating. For example, use 0.5% protease enzyme (based on pelt weight) in the soaking drum. This breaks down blood and dirt, so you use less salt and water. For dehairing, use 1% alkaline protease with 2% lime and 1% sodium sulphide. The enzyme digests hair roots, so the hair falls off easily. Drum for 4-6 hours at 30Â°C. Then rinse. For bating, use 0.3% pancreatic enzyme (like trypsin) at pH 8.0 and 35Â°C for 2 hours. This softens the pelt and removes non-structural proteins. You need a drum, pH meter, thermometer, and weighing scale. For 100 kg of pelt, use 0.5 kg protease for soaking, 1 kg for dehairing, and 0.3 kg for bating. Enzymes work best at specific temperatures and pH; if too hot, they stop working. Always add enzymes after adjusting pH and temperature. In Africa, enzymes are used in Kenya and Nigeria to reduce pollution. They cut chemical oxygen demand (COD) in wastewater by up to 50%. They also save water because you can soak for 2 hours instead of 12. Store enzymes in a cool place (below 25Â°C) and use within 6 months. This technique is affordable and eco-friendly. Start with a small trial batch of 10 kg to learn the correct doses.

4. Microwave and Ultrasound in Tanning

Microwave and ultrasound can speed up tanning and improve chemical penetration. Ultrasound uses sound waves above 20 kHz to create tiny bubbles that push chemicals into the pelt. In a drum, you can fit an ultrasonic transducer (like a 40 kHz, 100W unit) to the side. For 100 kg of pelt, after pickling, add tanning agents and turn on ultrasound for 30 minutes instead of 2 hours of drumming. This saves time and energy. Microwave works differently: you apply microwaves (2.45 GHz) to the pelt for short bursts (30 seconds on, 30 seconds off) for 10 minutes. This heats the pelt internally and speeds up tanning. But be careful: too much microwave can cook the pelt. Use a domestic microwave (800W) for small batches (up to 5 kg). For larger batches, you need a industrial microwave. Both methods are not common in Africa yet, but they are being tested in South Africa and Egypt. You need a generator for ultrasound and a microwave oven. The main benefit is faster processing: vegetable tanning can go from 24 hours to 4 hours. However, equipment is expensive. Start with ultrasound if you have a drum; it is cheaper than microwave. Always follow safety: keep water away from electrical parts, and do not put metal in microwave. For a small tannery, ultrasound can be a good investment if you want to increase output without buying more drums.

5. Nanotechnology in Leather

Nanotechnology uses particles smaller than 100 nanometres to improve leather properties. In tanning, you can use nano-silica, nano-clay, or nano-titanium dioxide. For example, add 1% nano-silica (based on pelt weight) to the tanning drum along with your tanning agent. The nanoparticles fill gaps in the leather fibres, making the leather stronger and more water-resistant. For 100 kg of pelt, use 1 kg nano-silica powder. Mix it with 10 litres of water before adding to the drum. Drum for 2 hours after tanning. Nano-clay (like montmorillonite) at 2% improves heat resistance and reduces flammability. Nano-titanium dioxide at 0.5% gives UV protection, so leather doesn't fade in sunlight. These nanoparticles are available from chemical suppliers in South Africa and Nigeria. You need a drum, weighing scale, and personal protective equipment (mask, gloves, goggles) because nanoparticles can be inhaled. The main challenge is that nanoparticles are more expensive than regular chemicals. But you use very little, so cost per batch is low. In Ethiopia, some tanneries use nano-silica to make high-quality shoe upper leather. Always disperse nanoparticles well in water before adding to the drum; otherwise they clump and don't work. After tanning, wash the leather thoroughly to remove loose particles. This is an advanced technique, but it can give your leather a premium edge.

6. Automation and Control Systems

Automation in tanning means using sensors and controllers to monitor and adjust drum conditions automatically. For a small African tannery, you can start with a simple pH controller and a temperature sensor. For example, install a pH probe in the drum and connect it to a dosing pump that adds acid or base to keep pH at 3.8 during tanning. Cost: about $500 for a basic system. You also need a temperature controller that turns on a heater or chiller to keep the drum at 30Â°C. For 100 kg batches, this ensures consistent results. More advanced: use a programmable logic controller (PLC) to run the drum speed, chemical addition, and timing. A PLC can be programmed to add 2% chrome at 30 minutes, then 1% at 60 minutes, and so on. This reduces human error. You can buy used PLCs from South Africa or Kenya for $300-$800. You also need a drum with a variable speed motor (8-12 rpm). Automation saves labour and chemicals. For example, a pH controller can cut chrome use by 10% because you add only what is needed. It also improves quality: every batch is the same. Start with a pH meter and a timer; then add temperature control. Always calibrate sensors weekly with buffer solutions. Keep a logbook of all batches. In Nigeria, some tanneries use simple automation to export to Europe. It is an investment, but it pays off in fewer rejects and happier customers.

Ultrasound (20â40 kHz) accelerates diffusion of tanning agents into the pelt, cutting process time by 30â50% and improving chemical uptake uniformity. Microwave is used mainly for drying and finishing, not bulk tanning, because of uneven energy distribution and collagen damage risk at high power. â  SAFETY WARNING â CHROME AND ALDEHYDE HANDLING

Chrome tanning agents contain Cr(III) which can oxidise to carcinogenic Cr(VI) under alkaline or high-temperature conditions. Aldehyde tanning agents (glutaraldehyde, oxazolidine, formaldehyde) are respiratory sensitisers and skin irritants. Mandatory controls: (1) enclosed dosing and local exhaust ventilation at all addition points; (2) nitrile gloves, goggles and respirators for all operators; (3) pH monitoring to keep tanning floats below pH 4.2 during chrome offer; (4) never mix chrome and aldehyde liquors in the same drum without validated procedure; (5) Cr(VI) testing on finished leather must be below 3 mg/kg for EU compliance. Train all staff on SDS and emergency spill procedures before any trial.




Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Define the target market first: automotive, footwear, upholstery or leather goods â each dictates Ts, Cr(VI), formaldehyde and colour-fastness limits.

  2. Validate Ts before scaling: shrinkage temperature must exceed 85 Â°C for chrome-free and 95 Â°C for combination routes intended for upper leather.

  3. Control pH at every addition: log pH before and after each chemical offer; drift beyond Â±0.2 pH units predicts uneven tanning.

  4. Run a 3-batch trial before full production: compare chemical consumption, area yield, Ts and Cr(VI) against your baseline recipe.

  5. Install sensors before automation: pH, temperature and flow sensors deliver most of the value; full SCADA can follow.

  6. Budget for effluent treatment: advanced tanning reduces but does not eliminate chrome, aldehyde and sulfide loads; confirm your WWTP capacity.

  8. Train and protect workers: no advanced tanning trial should start without SDS review, PPE issue and ventilation verification.
