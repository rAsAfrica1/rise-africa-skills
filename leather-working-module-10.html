Module 10

🧰 Leather Types and What Each Is For

A buyer does not ask for "leather". They ask for full-grain, or top-grain, or a split, or suede, or nubuck, or vegetable-tanned tooling leather, and each of those words carries a different price. This module teaches you what those names actually mean in the anatomy of the hide, how to tell them apart with your hands and a knife cut, and how to match a leather to a product so that neither of you is disappointed six months later.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Locate the grain layer and the reticular layer in a cut edge of leather
  • Describe what splitting physically does to a hide and when in the process it can happen
  • Explain exactly what buffing removes and what it costs the hide
  • Identify vegetable, chrome and combination tannage from colour, feel and known properties
  • Select a thickness range for a given product using the commercial reference table
  • Declare a leather's type, tannage and thickness accurately in a sale

The six lessons

1. The Grain Layer: Where the Price Lives 2. Splitting: One Hide, Two Materials 3. Buffing, Corrected Grain and Nubuck 4. Reading the Tannage in Your Hand 5. Matching Leather to Product 6. Selling by Name: Honest Declaration and Its Price
Lesson 10.1~12 min

The Grain Layer: Where the Price Lives

In this lesson
  • Locate the grain layer and the reticular layer in a cut edge of leather
  • Explain why full-grain leather carries the highest price on anatomy, not on finishing skill
  • Judge a piece of leather by its cut cross-section instead of by its surface alone

Every price difference between leather types in this module comes from one fact about the animal, and you already met it earlier in the course. Learn to see it in a cut edge and you will never be sold a lower grade at a higher price again.

A raw hide has three layers. The epidermis is the thin outer layer carrying the hair, it contains no useful collagen and it is deliberately destroyed and removed in the beamhouse. The hypodermis, the flesh side, is fat and loose connective tissue and it is fleshed away. Between them sits the corium, and the corium is the leather. It is almost entirely interwoven collagen fibre.

Now the part that decides money. The corium is not one material. It divides into two zones.

The upper zone, sitting just under where the epidermis was, is the grain or papillary layer. Its collagen fibres are fine, densely packed and tightly woven. This is the layer that carries the natural surface of the animal, the pores and the fine grain pattern a buyer looks for. The industry describes it as the key to good leather quality, and that is not marketing language, it is a description of fibre architecture.

Below it, making up the bulk of the thickness, is the reticular layer. Its fibres are thicker and coarser, more loosely but very strongly interwoven. The source describes it as fibrous, strongly interwoven but coarse. This is where the leather gets its bulk strength and tear resistance.

So one hide contains a thin, fine, beautiful surface layer and a thick, strong, coarse body layer. The two do different jobs and they are worth different money.

Full-grain leather is the piece that still has that grain layer intact, with only light finishing applied, deliberately preserving the natural un-buffed surface. It is the highest-value visual and tactile grade because the tightly woven papillary layer is still there and still visible.

Top-grain is the same grain piece with light buffing or correction to even out minor defects. Slightly less natural, slightly more uniform, and it sits just below full-grain in value.

Everything else in this module is a description of what happens when the grain layer is damaged, sanded away, split off, or was never there.

Here is the sentence to carry with you. Grain quality is set on the living animal and at flaying, and it is never restored by tanning. A scar, a tick bite, a brand mark or a knife score into the grain layer is a permanent loss of the finest and most valuable collagen in the hide. No chemical, no drum, no finishing table brings it back.

That is why the whole first half of this course was about live animals, flaying and curing. It was not preliminary material. It was the value.

Now the practical skill: reading a cut edge.

Take any piece of leather and cut a clean edge with a sharp knife. Do not tear it. Hold the cut edge to good light, preferably daylight, and look at the cross-section with your eye close.

On a full-grain or top-grain piece you will see a distinct, denser, finer band along the top few tenths of a millimetre, then a visibly coarser, more open fibre body below it. Two textures, one on top of the other. Run your thumbnail along the cut edge and you will often feel the difference as well as see it.

On a split you will see one texture the whole way through, coarse and open, with no fine band at the top, because the fine band was cut off and sold to someone else.

This single test, which costs you a knife and thirty seconds, tells you more about a piece of leather than the surface does, because a surface can be coated, embossed, pigmented and printed to look like anything. A cross-section cannot lie about which layer of the animal it came from.

Do this on every leather you are offered before you buy, and do it on your own production before you grade it. A workshop that grades its own leather honestly by cross-section builds a reputation. A workshop that sells a split as full-grain sells it once.

Splitting: One Hide, Two Materials

In this lesson
  • Describe what splitting physically does to a hide and when in the process it can happen
  • Explain why the flesh split can never be sold as grain leather no matter how it is finished
  • Decide whether splitting makes commercial sense for your own thickness and product range

Splitting is dividing a hide horizontally, through its thickness, into two or more layers. It is done with a band-knife splitting machine in most operations, and it follows the anatomical division you learned in the last lesson: the grain layer above, the reticular bulk below.

Understand first why anyone does it. A cattle hide is often far thicker than the product needs. A wallet wants leather at two to four ounces, that is 0.8 to 1.6 mm. A raw thick hide can be several times that. You have two choices: buy a thinner animal, or split the hide you have and get two saleable pieces instead of one thick one you cannot use.

Splitting therefore does two jobs at once. It brings leather to a precise, uniform working thickness, and it produces a second piece of material from what would otherwise be wasted bulk.

When does it happen? Two points in the process are both normal practice.

Splitting a limed pelt in the beamhouse is common for thicker hides, to give a more uniform working thickness before tanning. This means every later chemical step, tanning, retanning, dyeing, fatliquoring, is working on a thinner and more even piece, which usually means faster and more even penetration.

Splitting after tanning is the other route, used to bring already-tanned leather to a precise final thickness or to separate a grain split from a flesh split at a later stage, depending on the tannery's equipment and workflow.

Wherever it happens, the anatomical rule holds without exception. Only the piece that keeps the grain layer can become full-grain or top-grain leather. The flesh-side piece is a split. It has no natural grain surface, it never had one, and it is priced accordingly.

This is the point where beginners lose money, so let it be clear. You cannot finish a split into full-grain leather. You can emboss a grain pattern onto it. You can pigment it heavily and make it smooth and uniform. You can make it look, at arm's length, very like a grain leather. What you cannot do is give it the fine, tightly woven papillary collagen it never contained, and that layer is where the wear resistance, the surface durability and the ageing character of good leather come from. A heavily finished split will wear through and crack at flex points in a way full-grain will not.

So the honest way to sell a split is as a split, at a split's price, into a product where a split does the job well. There are plenty of those. Linings. Lower-cost goods. Suede, which is the subject of the next lesson. Bag interiors. Backing pieces. A split is a real, useful, saleable material. It is simply not the same material as grain leather, and a workshop that pretends otherwise loses the customer permanently the first time the product fails.

Now the commercial decision for your own workshop.

Splitting needs a splitting machine, and that is capital. This course has no equipment prices for you, and it will explain why in module 12. What you can reason about now is whether splitting fits your product range at all.

If you make heavy goods, belts, straps, saddlery, structural leathercraft, at six to fifteen ounces and above, you want thickness. Splitting a hide down thin destroys the very property those products are sold on. You may need no splitting at all.

If you make small goods, wallets at two to four ounces, linings, light bag panels, then most raw hide is far too thick for you, and every hide you buy carries thickness you are paying for and cannot use. Either buy from a supplier who splits, or work out whether the volume you handle justifies the machine.

And if you are running the tannery rather than the craft workshop, splitting is a way to get two revenue streams from one animal, but only if you have a buyer for the split. A pile of unsold splits in the corner is not a second revenue stream. It is stock you paid to create.

One last practical note. Splitting also removes the thickness variation across the animal, and you already know that a hide is thicker at the butt and thinner at the belly. Splitting evens that out mechanically. That is genuinely useful for a cutter who wants every panel of a bag to behave the same. It does not, however, change the fibre density difference between butt and belly. A split belly piece is still looser than a split butt piece of the same thickness.

Buffing, Corrected Grain and Nubuck

In this lesson
  • Explain exactly what buffing removes and what it costs the hide
  • Distinguish nubuck from suede by which layer was abraded
  • Read a heavy pigmented finish as evidence about what happened upstream

Buffing uses a rotating emery or abrasive cylinder to remove minor grain irregularities from the leather surface. That is the neutral description. Here is the honest one: buffing is sanding away a small amount of the most valuable collagen layer in the hide in order to hide a defect.

It works. Sometimes it is the right commercial decision. But it is a one-way door. A buffed hide is a corrected-grain hide, corrected grain is worth less than full grain, and there is no process anywhere in the trade that reverses it.

So three leather types come out of the buffing decision, and telling them apart is a real, saleable skill.

Top-grain is the light end. The grain piece has been lightly buffed or corrected to even out minor defects, then finished. Most of the natural grain character survives. The buyer gets a more uniform, blemish-free surface than raw full-grain provides, and pays a mid-to-high price for it.

Corrected-grain is the heavy end. The grain piece has been more heavily buffed and sanded, and then given a heavier pigmented finish to hide the defects that buffing alone did not remove. The natural surface is largely gone and an artificial one has been built on top. This is the standard route for lower-cost footwear and goods, and for anything wanting a very uniform, wipe-clean surface. It is a real product with a real market. It is not a premium product.

Nubuck is the interesting one, because it is buffing used deliberately rather than defensively. Nubuck is a full-grain leather, lightly buffed on the grain side itself to raise a fine, short nap while keeping the grain-layer strength. You are not sanding the grain away to hide something. You are roughening its very surface to create a velvety hand-feel, on a piece good enough that it did not need correcting.

Do not confuse nubuck with suede. This is the single most common leather-type confusion in the trade and it is worth money to get right.

  • Nubuck is the grain side of a grain leather, lightly napped. The grain layer is still there underneath, so it keeps grain-layer strength.
  • Suede is a split, or sometimes a grain piece, finished with a napped brushed surface on the flesh side. There is no grain layer beneath it.

Both feel soft and velvety to a hand. They are not the same material, they do not wear the same, and they should not carry the same price. Nubuck is used for casual footwear and goods wanting a suede-like look with more grain-layer strength than true suede.

Now the finish types that sit on top, from lightest to heaviest, because they interact directly with the buffing decision.

Aniline finishing applies a transparent film containing dye but no pigment, so the natural grain shows through fully. It is used only on the best-quality, most defect-free grain, because there is nothing to hide a fault behind.

Semi-aniline adds a small quantity of ultra-fine pigment, enough to mask minor blemishes while still showing most of the natural grain.

Pigmented and heavier finishes use progressively harder film layers, with the hardest top coat providing wear resistance. This is the standard route for lower-grade or heavily corrected grain, where an opaque, resistant surface both hides defects and stands up to wear.

Put those two ladders side by side, the buffing ladder and the finishing ladder, and you have a reading skill. Aniline on full-grain is a badge of a defect-free hide managed correctly from flaying onward. A heavy pigmented finish on heavily corrected grain is very often, though not always, covering for defects earlier in the chain.

Use this when you buy leather. Use it more ruthlessly when you look at your own.

If you find yourself reaching for a heavier finish batch after batch, the finishing table is not your problem. Go back up the chain. The money was lost at flaying, at curing, at liming, and the finish is only where you noticed. Buffing and pigment are the cost of a defect, not the cure for it.

One practical caution. Buffing produces fine leather dust. Chrome-tanned leather dust carries chromium, and chrome-bearing solid waste is a waste-management question in its own right, covered in module 12. Buff with dust extraction or in open air, wear a mask, and do not let the dust collect in a corner of an enclosed workshop.

Reading the Tannage in Your Hand

In this lesson
  • Identify vegetable, chrome and combination tannage from colour, feel and known properties
  • State the shrinkage temperatures that separate the main tannages and what they mean in use
  • Refuse to sell a leather into a use its tannage cannot survive

A customer asks whether your leather will stand up to being repeatedly soaked and dried in a hot climate. The honest answer depends almost entirely on the tannage, and you should be able to give it in one sentence.

Start with what you can see and feel.

Vegetable-tanned leather carries the tannin's own colour: cream to dark brown, and it darkens further with age and light exposure. It is generally firm. It holds a stamped or carved design better than most chrome leather, and that firmness is exactly why it is the standard material for belts, straps, saddlery, structural leathercraft and tooling or carving work. If a piece of leather is cream or tan coloured, firm under the thumb, and takes a clean stamped impression, you are very probably holding vegetable-tanned leather.

Chrome-tanned leather starts pale, and before finishing it is the blue-grey material the whole trade calls wet-blue. Because the tanned fibre itself is not strongly coloured, it can be dyed almost any colour. It is generally softer and more supple than vegetable leather of the same thickness. Chrome tannage accounts for roughly 85 percent of world leather production by the estimate in the sourced industry guide, and it is the majority of the world's footwear, garment, upholstery and bag leather.

Combination-tanned leather is chrome-tanned then vegetable-retanned, or the reverse. It exists to get chrome's wet-heat resistance and colour flexibility together with vegetable tannage's fuller, firmer handle. If you meet a leather that feels firmer than you would expect for something dyed a strong colour, that is your likely candidate.

Now the property that actually decides fitness for use, and it is a number.

Shrinkage temperature is the temperature at which tanned collagen starts to shrink and degrade when it is wet and heated. It is the single clearest technical difference between tannages, and the sourced figures are these.

  • Vegetable-tanned leather: around 85 degrees Celsius.
  • Chrome-tanned leather: over 100 degrees Celsius.
  • Glutaraldehyde, so-called wet-white tannage: around 75 degrees Celsius.
  • Alum, aluminium tannage: in the range 65 to 85 degrees Celsius.
  • Cod-oil, chamois tannage: around 50 degrees Celsius.

Read that list as a fitness-for-purpose ladder, not as trivia.

In practice, vegetable leather's lower wet-heat tolerance means it is less forgiving of hot, wet conditions and generally not the first choice for footwear that will be repeatedly wetted and dried, or worn in a hot working environment. Chrome leather tolerates that cycle better. This is not an argument that chrome is a better material. It is an argument that the two materials belong in different products.

So when the customer asks about repeated soaking in the heat, you now have a real answer: for that use, chrome-tanned or combination-tanned leather is the safer specification, and if all you make is vegetable-tanned leather, say so and direct them to a product your leather is actually good at. You will lose one sale and keep a customer.

The other named tannages are worth recognising even though you will probably never run them. Glutaraldehyde or wet-white tannage is often used as a chrome-free pre-tannage or full tannage, giving light-coloured leather with good perspiration resistance but comparatively poor shape retention. Alum tannage was historically important and is still used for some speciality and decorative leathers. Cod-oil or chamois tannage produces very soft, stretchy, highly water-absorbent leather, traditionally used for chamois cloth-type leathers rather than structural goods. Syntans, the synthetic tanning agents, are usually used in combination with or as a retanning agent alongside chrome or vegetable tannage, not as a tannage on their own.

No African-specific production or cost data for any of these alternative tannages was retrieved for this course. They are named here so you can correctly identify unfamiliar leather in the market, not taught as production routes you should be costing.

One honest gap you should know about. No quantified African market-share breakdown by leather type was retrieved for this course, so nobody can tell you from this material what proportion of African-processed leather is full-grain versus split, or chrome versus vegetable. If you want to know what your own market is actually full of, you will have to walk it and count. That is a genuinely useful afternoon.

Lesson 10.5~12 min

Matching Leather to Product

In this lesson
  • Select a thickness range for a given product using the commercial reference table
  • Choose hide regions for pattern pieces according to butt and belly fibre behaviour
  • State honestly what the thickness table is and is not

This lesson turns everything so far into a purchasing and cutting decision. Three questions answer it: how thick, from which part of the animal, and which tannage.

Start with thickness, because it is the one you can measure.

Thickness is quoted in millimetres or in ounces. Ounce here is a leather-trade unit meaning weight per square foot at a standard thickness, not an everyday weight. The commercial conversion is one ounce is about 0.40 mm, rising roughly linearly to sixteen ounces at about 6.40 mm. Learn a few by heart: 2 oz is 0.8 mm, 4 oz is 1.6 mm, 8 oz is 3.2 mm, 12 oz is 4.8 mm.

Typical thicknesses by product, from the same commercial reference:

  • Footwear uppers 1 to 7 oz, that is 0.4 to 2.8 mm. Soles and heels 13 oz and above.
  • Wallets and thin small goods 2 to 4 oz, 0.8 to 1.6 mm.
  • Handbags and purses 6 to 12 oz, 2.4 to 4.8 mm.
  • Belts and straps 6 to 15 oz and above, 2.4 to 6.4 mm and above.
  • Upholstery 2 to 9 oz, 0.8 to 3.6 mm.

Now be honest about what that table is. It is commercial trade convention from a leathercraft supplier reference. It is not an industrial standard and it is not an African source. Use it to select and split leather sensibly and to talk to customers in the language they use. Do not quote it as a specification in a contract, and always confirm by feel and by test-flexing a sample before you commit a whole hide to a cutting plan.

Second question: which part of the animal.

The corium is not uniform across one animal. The butt, the rear and back section, has thick, densely woven fibre and gives the strongest and most valuable leather. The belly is thinner, more loosely woven, and stretches far more readily.

That gives a cutting rule you can apply immediately. Lay the pattern pieces that need strength and shape-holding on the butt and back: straps, structural bag panels, belt blanks, sole components. Reserve the belly and the looser areas for pieces that benefit from drape and flexibility: gussets, linings, softer panels.

A cutter who ignores this and cuts a load-bearing strap from belly leather has not made a design choice. They have made an anatomical mistake, and that strap will stretch and fail sooner than the identical pattern cut from the butt. The customer will not blame the cutting. They will blame your leather.

Third question: which tannage, which you covered in the last lesson. Firm structural goods and anything tooled or carved lean vegetable. Soft, brightly coloured, or repeatedly wetted goods lean chrome. A leather that needs both firmness and colour leans combination.

Now put the three questions together on a real product.

Say you are making a men's belt. Thickness: 6 to 15 oz range, so aim around 8 to 10 oz, that is 3.2 to 4.0 mm. Region: butt, without argument, because a belt is a load-bearing strap that must not stretch. Tannage: vegetable, both for firmness and because if you want to stamp or tool a pattern into it, vegetable leather holds the impression best. Type: full-grain or top-grain, because the belt's surface is the product and a split will crack at the buckle fold.

Say instead you are making a soft shoulder bag with a gathered body. Thickness: lower end of the handbag range, 6 to 8 oz or thinner if the design is very soft. Region: butt for the strap and the base panel, which carry the load, belly for the gussets and soft side panels, which want drape. Tannage: chrome, if the customer wants colour. Type: grain leather for the outside, and a split is perfectly honest for the lining.

Notice that the second product uses three different parts of the hide for three different jobs. That is what an experienced cutter does, and it is also how you get more saleable product out of the same animal.

One gap you must fill yourself. No sourced average African cattle hide, sheepskin or goatskin area was retrieved for this course. That means no one can tell you from this material how many square feet a typical hide of yours will yield, so you cannot plan a cutting layout from a book. Measure twenty of your own hides, write down each area, and take the average. From that day forward you can turn a per-hide price into a per-square-foot price, which is the conversion that all of your costing depends on.

Selling by Name: Honest Declaration and Its Price

In this lesson
  • Declare a leather's type, tannage and thickness accurately in a sale
  • Explain the commercial argument for honest grading rather than the moral one
  • Set up a simple type-and-grade record for every batch you sell

This lesson is about the sentence you say when you hand a piece of leather to a buyer. It sounds like a soft topic. It is not. It is the difference between a workshop that gets repeat orders and one that sells to each customer once.

Start with what a full, honest declaration contains. Four things.

  1. Type: full-grain, top-grain, corrected-grain, split, suede or nubuck.
  2. Tannage: vegetable, chrome, or combination.
  3. Thickness: in millimetres and in ounces, with the range across the piece, not just the best spot.
  4. Grade: the grade you assigned it, on whatever grading system you and your buyer both use.

That is the whole declaration and it takes ten seconds to say. Most small workshops say none of it, and then wonder why buyers negotiate them down. A buyer who has to work out what he is holding assumes the worst, because assuming the worst is how buyers survive.

Now the honest reason to declare correctly, and it is a commercial reason, not a sermon.

The quality checkpoint at the end of your process is this: correct grain classification honestly declared, and correct thickness for the intended product. Both of those are things the customer discovers eventually. Thickness they discover the moment they put a gauge on it or cut it. Grain classification they discover the first time a product made from your split cracks at a fold that full-grain would have survived.

When that happens, you do not lose one sale. You lose the customer, and you lose everyone the customer talks to, which in a district trade is everyone.

Against that, look at what honest grading buys you. The clearest single figure in this course connecting grade to price is from Sagana Tannery in Kenya: after equipment rehabilitation, hides processed to the top quality grade commanded up to a 20 percent price premium over standard wet-blue. Be careful with that figure. It is a single plant-level result, it is pre-2000, and it is unverified as a current premium anywhere. It is not a promise that your top grade will fetch 20 percent more. What it does show, from a real African tannery, is that a grade premium is a real thing that real buyers really pay, and that it is worth chasing.

At national scale the same logic showed up in Kenya as a projected 1.75 million US dollars a year in additional foreign exchange from just a 5-percentage-point improvement in the proportion of hides reaching the top wet-blue grade, on a million-hide annual export volume. Again, this is an old programme projection and not a current price. The lesson is the shape of it: small percentage improvements in grade, multiplied across volume, are where the money in this trade actually sits.

And be realistic about the pace. When a Kenyan improvement programme in the Kiambu and Nairobi target area measured grade I share before and after, cattle hides went from 45.6 percent to 46 percent, goatskins from 47.6 to 48 percent, sheepskins from 44.3 to 45 percent. That is a gain of roughly half a percentage point to one percentage point over the measured period. Defect reduction is a slow, compounding improvement, not a one-season fix. Do not promise yourself a transformation. Promise yourself a record and a direction.

Which brings us to the record you should be keeping.

For every batch of leather you finish, write down: the batch number, the intake grade of the hides that went into it, the tannage used, the type and grade of finished leather that came out, the thickness range, the area, and who bought it and at what price.

That is one line in a book per batch. Within a year it is the single most useful dataset your business owns, because it connects input quality to output value in your own market, with your own hides. No document written anywhere else can give you that, and no consultant can sell it to you.

One more thing to record, and it costs nothing. When a customer rejects a piece or complains about a product, write down what they said and which batch it came from. Complaints are free quality-control data delivered to your door by the only inspector whose opinion decides your income.

Finally, the classification table in this module is a selection and identification tool. It is not a description of what African leather production currently consists of, because no quantified African market-share breakdown by leather type was retrieved for this course. Teach yourself your own market by walking it, counting what is on the shelves, and asking what it sold for.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Which layer of the hide actually becomes leather?

The epidermis carries the hair and holds no useful collagen; the hypodermis is fat and loose tissue. The corium, almost entirely interwoven collagen, is the leather, and both other layers are removed in the beamhouse.

2. What is the difference between the grain layer and the reticular layer?

The two zones of the corium have genuinely different fibre architecture, which is why the piece keeping the grain is worth more than the piece that does not.

3. A supplier shows you a smooth, even, perfect-looking leather surface. What should you do before believing it is full-grain?

A surface can be coated, embossed and pigmented to look like anything. A cross-section shows which layer of the animal the piece came from and cannot be dressed up the same way.

4. Why can tanning not repair a brand mark or a flay cut in the grain?

Tanning cross-links the collagen that is there. It cannot recreate collagen that was burned, cut or bitten away, which is why hide-improvement work happens before the tannery, not in it.

5. What is top-grain leather?

Top-grain keeps the grain layer but has been lightly corrected for uniformity, so it sits just below full-grain, which receives only light finishing and keeps the natural un-buffed surface.

6. What does splitting a hide physically do?

Splitting is a horizontal division through the thickness, done with a band-knife machine, giving a grain piece above and a flesh split below.

7. Can a flesh split be finished into full-grain leather?

You can make a split look like grain leather at arm's length, but a heavily finished split wears through and cracks at flex points differently, because the layer that resists that wear was cut off.

8. A workshop makes only heavy belts and saddlery at 8 to 14 oz. What is the likely value of buying a splitting machine?

Splitting matters most for workshops whose products need far less thickness than a raw hide has. A heavy-goods workshop may need no splitting at all.

9. Why is a pile of unsold splits not a second revenue stream?

Two revenue streams from one animal is only real when both pieces sell. Otherwise splitting has converted usable thickness into unsold inventory.

10. Does splitting remove the difference between butt and belly leather?

Splitting controls thickness, not fibre density. The butt is thick and densely woven and the belly thinner and more loosely woven, and that weave difference survives splitting.

11. What is the real cost of buffing a hide?

Buffing removes minor grain irregularities with an abrasive cylinder, but it does so by removing the fine papillary collagen that makes grain leather valuable. There is no way back.

12. How do nubuck and suede differ?

Both feel velvety, but one still has the fine, tightly woven grain layer underneath and one does not. They wear differently and should not carry the same price.

13. Why is aniline finishing used only on the best hides?

There is nothing opaque in an aniline finish to mask a blemish. Semi-aniline adds a little ultra-fine pigment for that reason, and heavy pigmented finishes hide much more.

14. Your workshop finds itself using heavy pigmented finishes on most batches. What does that most likely tell you?

A finish is a visible statement of how much natural grain quality survived the whole process. Reaching for heavier cover repeatedly is a signal to look upstream, because that is where the value was lost.

15. Why should buffing dust from chrome-tanned leather be controlled?

Chrome-bearing solid waste, including shavings and dust, is a hazardous-waste question in its own right and not only a liquid effluent problem. Do not let it accumulate in an enclosed workshop.

16. What is shrinkage temperature?

It is the clearest single technical difference between tannages, and it decides whether a leather will survive repeated wetting and heat in a product.

17. A customer wants boots for a hot workplace where they will be soaked and dried repeatedly. Which tannage suits better?

Chrome leather tolerates the hot-wet cycle better. If your workshop only makes vegetable leather, the honest move is to say so and steer the customer to a product your material is genuinely good at.

18. You handle a leather dyed a strong bright colour that feels firmer than you expect. What is it most likely to be?

A bright colour points to a pale chrome starting material, since vegetable leather can only be dyed on top of its own cream-to-brown ground. Unexpected firmness points to a vegetable retannage on top.

19. Why is vegetable-tanned leather the standard choice for tooled and carved work?

The firmness that limits vegetable leather in soft, drapey products is exactly the property that makes it hold an impression, which is a real product differentiation to lean into.

20. What is a syntan normally used for?

Synthetic tannins are listed among the standard retanning materials along with vegetable tannins, resins and filling agents, used to adjust a leather's character rather than to tan it from scratch.

21. A belt blank at 10 oz is roughly how thick in millimetres?

One ounce is about 0.40 mm, so 10 oz is about 4.0 mm. That sits comfortably in the 6 to 15 oz belt and strap range.

22. Where on the hide should a load-bearing bag strap be cut from?

Cutting a load-bearing strap from belly leather is an anatomical mistake, not a design choice. The strap will stretch and fail sooner, and the customer will blame your leather.

23. What is the honest status of the product thickness table in this lesson?

It is genuinely useful for selecting leather and talking to customers, but it should not be quoted as a specification in a contract, and a sample should always be felt and test-flexed first.

24. Why can this course not tell you how many square feet your hides yield?

It is a flagged gap in the reference material. Your own measured average is what converts a per-hide price into the per-square-foot price all your costing runs on.

25. A soft gathered shoulder bag uses which parts of the hide?

An experienced cutter uses different regions for different jobs in one product. That is both better engineering and more saleable output from the same animal.

26. What four things belong in an honest leather declaration?

Those four answer everything a buyer needs to decide whether the leather suits their product, and stating them plainly stops the buyer assuming the worst.

27. What is the honest status of the 20 percent grade premium figure from Sagana Tannery?

It is the clearest single sourced figure connecting grade to price, but it is old and plant-specific. Use it as evidence that chasing grade pays, not as a number to plan revenue on.

28. Why is selling a split as full-grain a poor commercial decision, not just a dishonest one?

A split has no fine papillary layer beneath the surface and wears differently at flex points. The deception has a delivery date, and in a district trade the reputation damage costs far more than the one sale.

29. What does the Kiambu and Nairobi before-and-after data teach about defect reduction?

Cattle hides moved from 45.6 to 46 percent grade I, goatskins 47.6 to 48, sheepskins 44.3 to 45. Real, measurable, incremental. Plan for a direction and a record rather than a transformation.

30. Why is your own batch book more valuable than any table in this course?

Grading records linking intake grade to finished type, grade and price are the single most useful internal dataset a small operation can build, and customer complaints are free quality data feeding straight into it.

Module 10 capstone

Build your own leather sample board, the reference tool you will use for the rest of your working life. Step 1: collect at least six different pieces of leather, from your own production, from offcuts, from other workshops and from the market. Try to get one you believe is full-grain, one corrected-grain, one split or suede, one nubuck, one vegetable-tanned and one chrome-tanned. Step 2: cut each one to a hand-sized rectangle and cut one clean edge on each so you can see the cross-section. Step 3: for each piece, look at the cut edge in good light and write down whether you can see a fine, dense grain layer at the top, or whether the whole thickness is coarse and open. Step 4: measure the thickness in millimetres in three places on each piece and convert to ounces at one ounce being about 0.40 mm. Step 5: flex each piece and scratch the surface lightly with your thumbnail, and write down what happens. Step 6: label each piece with your best judgement of type, thickness and probable tannage, and name one product it suits and one it does not. Step 7: take the board to a buyer or an experienced tanner and ask them to correct you. Write their corrections on the labels. That board is now worth more than any table in this course.
