1. Storage Conditions: Temperature, Humidity, and Ventilation

Sweet potatoes are living roots that continue to breathe after harvest. To store them well, you must control three things: temperature, humidity, and ventilation. The ideal storage temperature is 13â16Â°C (55â60Â°F). Below 10Â°C, roots get chilling injuryâthey turn dark, hard, and taste bad. Above 20Â°C, they sprout and rot quickly. In most African homes without cooling, aim for the coolest shaded room, often on the ground floor or in a pit. Humidity should be 85â90%. If air is too dry, roots shrivel; too wet, they grow mould. You can hang a wet cloth or place a basin of water in the storage room to raise humidity. Ventilation is critical: air must move around each root to carry away heat and moisture. Never pile roots more than 30 cm deep. Use slatted wooden crates or woven baskets that allow airflow. In a traditional room, stack crates with gaps between them. Check stored roots every week. Remove any that are soft, sprouting, or mouldy immediatelyâone rotten root can spoil many others. For long-term storage, cure roots first: keep them at 30Â°C and 90% humidity for 4â7 days. This heals cuts and thickens the skin. In rural areas, a simple pit storage works: dig a pit 1 m deep, line with dry grass, add roots, cover with grass and soil, and leave a small vent. But pits can get too hot; monitor with a thermometer. If you sell fresh roots, store only 2â4 weeks. For longer, process into flour or chips. Always keep roots out of direct sunlight and away from onions, which release gases that cause sprouting.

2. Traditional and Modern Storage Structures

African farmers have stored sweet potatoes for generations using simple, low-cost structures. The most common traditional method is pit storage. Dig a pit 1â1.5 m deep and 1 m wide in well-drained soil. Line the bottom and sides with dry grass or straw. Fill with cured, healthy roots. Cover with a layer of grass, then soil, leaving a small opening for air. Pits keep roots cool and humid for 2â3 months. Another traditional method is the clamp: a cone-shaped pile of roots on a bed of straw, covered with more straw and soil, with a vent stick in the top. In West Africa, farmers use raised platforms under shade trees. For modern storage, you can build a simple ventilated room. Use mud bricks or timber. Make slatted shelves 30 cm apart. Install vents near the floor and roof for cross-ventilation. A small solar-powered fan (12V, 10W) can move air. For larger scale, use evaporative coolers: a double-walled chamber with wet sand between walls. As water evaporates, it cools the inner chamber to 15â18Â°C. This works well in dry areas. You can also use plastic crates (60x40x30 cm) stacked with 5 cm gaps. Never use airtight containers like drumsâroots will suffocate and rot. In markets, many vendors use woven baskets lined with banana leaves. For a family, a clay pot storage works: put roots in a large unglazed clay pot, cover with a damp cloth, and keep in a shaded corner. Modern cold rooms are expensive but effective for cooperatives. A 10-tonne cold room at 13Â°C and 90% humidity can store roots for 6 months. If you cannot afford one, share with neighbours. Always clean and disinfect structures between harvests to prevent disease build-up.

3. Preventing Storage Rots and Pests

Storage rots and pests can destroy up to 50% of your sweet potato harvest. The main culprits are fungi (like Fusarium and Rhizopus) and bacteria that cause soft rot. Insects include sweet potato weevils and moths. Prevention starts at harvest. Never harvest when soil is wetâwet roots rot faster. Use a fork or hoe carefully to avoid cuts. Cure roots immediately: keep at 30Â°C and 90% humidity for 4â7 days. Curing heals small wounds and thickens skin, making it harder for rot to enter. After curing, sort roots. Remove any with cuts, bruises, or signs of disease. Store only perfect roots. Keep storage area clean. Remove all plant debris and old roots. Wash crates with soap and water, then spray with a 10% bleach solution (1 part bleach to 9 parts water). Let dry. For weevils, use a simple trap: cut a sweet potato into pieces, place in a jar with a lid that has small holes, and bury it in the storage pile. Weevils enter and get trapped. Replace weekly. You can also dust roots with wood ashâit repels insects and absorbs moisture. For fungal rot, good ventilation is key. Never pile roots more than 30 cm deep. If you see a rotten root, remove it immediately and destroy it (burn or feed to animals). Do not compost it near storage. For long-term storage, consider treating roots with a natural fungicide: mix 1 tablespoon of baking soda in 1 litre of water and dip roots for 1 minute, then dry. Some farmers use neem leaf extract: soak 1 kg of neem leaves in 10 litres of water for 24 hours, strain, and spray on roots. Store in a cool, dark place. Check weekly. If rot spreads, sell or process remaining roots quickly. Never store roots with onions, garlic, or applesâthey release ethylene gas that speeds sprouting and rot.

4. Processing into Flour, Chips, and Puree

Processing sweet potatoes adds value and reduces waste. Three common products are flour, chips, and puree. For flour: wash and peel roots. Grate or slice thinly. Dry in the sun on a clean mat or rack for 2â3 days until brittle (moisture below 10%). In rainy weather, use a solar dryer or oven at 60Â°C for 6â8 hours. Grind dried slices in a hammer mill or blender. Sift to remove coarse particles. Store flour in airtight containers in a cool, dark place. It lasts 6 months. Use it to make porridge, bread, or ugali. For chips: wash, peel, and slice roots into 2â3 mm rounds or strips. Blanch in boiling water for 2 minutes (optional, keeps colour). Dry in sun for 2â3 days or in a dehydrator at 60Â°C for 4â6 hours until crispy. Fry in hot oil (170Â°C) for 2â3 minutes until golden. Salt and pack in sealed bags. Chips sell well in shops and markets. For puree: boil peeled roots until soft (20â30 minutes). Mash or blend with a little water. For a smooth puree, pass through a sieve. Add lemon juice (1 tablespoon per kg) to prevent browning. Pack in clean jars or freeze. Puree is used in baby food, soups, and sauces. You can also make sweet potato juice: blend raw roots with water, strain, and add sugar or honey. For all processing, hygiene is critical. Wash hands, use clean utensils, and sterilise jars by boiling. Label products with date. Start small: 10 kg of fresh roots yields about 2â3 kg of flour or 4 kg of chips. Sell to local shops, schools, or at markets. Processing turns a perishable crop into a business.

5. Developing Value-Added Products

Value addition means turning raw sweet potatoes into products that sell for more. Beyond flour and chips, you can make many items. Sweet potato bread: mix 1 part sweet potato flour with 2 parts wheat flour, add yeast, salt, water, and sugar. Bake as normal bread. It is moist and nutritious. Sweet potato jam: boil puree with sugar (1:1 ratio) and lemon juice until thick. Jar it. Sweet potato crisps: slice thin, fry, and flavour with chilli, salt, or honey. Sweet potato porridge mix: blend flour with roasted groundnuts and sugar. Pack in 500g bags. Sweet potato animal feed: dry and grind vines and roots for poultry or pigs. Sweet potato starch: grate roots, soak in water, strain, and let starch settle. Dry and sell to textile or food industries. For all products, focus on packaging. Use clean, sealed bags or jars with labels showing weight, ingredients, and expiry date. A simple logo and phone number help. To develop a product, test with neighbours. Ask what they like and what price they would pay. Start with one product. For example, make 20 kg of sweet potato crisps. Sell at a local market or school. If it sells, scale up. You can also add value by making ready-to-cook mixes: sweet potato pancake mix or chapati mix. Another idea: sweet potato flour for baby porridgeâmix with soya and maize flour. This is a big market. For preservation, use solar drying or vacuum sealing if available. In Africa, many organisations offer training and small grants for value addition. Join a cooperative to share equipment like dryers, mills, and sealers. Remember: quality and consistency matter. If your first batch is good, customers will return. Keep records of costs and sales. A simple notebook works. Value-added products can double or triple your income from the same plot.

6. Marketing and Distribution Channels

You have grown and processed sweet potatoes. Now you must sell them. Marketing starts with knowing your customer. Who will buy? Local families, schools, restaurants, shops, or exporters? Each wants different things. Families want fresh roots or flour in small packs. Schools want cheap, nutritious food. Restaurants want consistent quality and regular supply. Shops want packaged products with labels. Exporters want large volumes and specific varieties. For fresh roots, sell at local markets, roadside stands, or to traders. Price depends on season. In harvest season, prices drop. Store roots and sell later when prices rise. For processed products, go to supermarkets, health food shops, and bakeries. Offer free samples. Use social media: WhatsApp groups, Facebook Marketplace, or Instagram. Post photos of your products with prices. For distribution, start local. Use a bicycle or motorbike to deliver. Partner with other farmers to share transport costs. For larger scale, join a cooperative that aggregates products and negotiates better prices. You can also sell to institutions: schools, hospitals, prisons. They buy in bulk. Write a simple proposal: what you sell, price, and delivery. For export, you need certification and packaging standards. Start with local markets first. Branding matters: give your product a name, like 'Mama's Sweet Potato Flour'. Use a clean label with your phone number. Keep records: who bought, how much, and when. This helps you plan. Attend trade fairs and farmer markets. Network with other processors. Many NGOs and government programmes help farmers find markets. Also consider online platforms like Twiga Foods or Mkulima Young in East Africa. For any channel, be reliable. Deliver on time. If you promise 50 kg of flour, deliver 50 kg. Good reputation spreads. Reinvest profits into better packaging or equipment. Marketing is not just sellingâit is building relationships.

Practical Reference: Storage, Processing, and Value Addition for Sweet Potato

Sweet potato is one of Africa's most versatile food and cash crops. Yet across sub-Saharan Africa, post-harvest losses for roots range between 20% and 40% according to FAO, with the bulk occurring during storage and handling. For an agribusiness, every percentage point saved from spoilage is money straight to the bottom line. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the operating numbers, structures, and market pathways needed to turn sweet potato roots into reliable revenue â from the curing shed to the flour mill to the supermarket shelf.

Treat this module as your field manual. It is written for African conditions: smallholder aggregation, humid coastal zones, dry savanna storage, and markets where fresh roots compete with processed products. Where you see a table, use it to plan. Where you see the warning box, treat it as a hard rule.

1. Storage Conditions: Temperature, Humidity, and Ventilation

Unlike Irish potato or grains, sweet potato roots are living tissue that continues to respire after harvest. They have no dormancy period that protects them well, and they are thin-skinned and easily wounded. The single most important post-harvest intervention is curing â holding roots at high temperature and high humidity for several days so that wounds heal (suberize) and a protective periderm forms. Curing can cut storage losses dramatically.

After curing, roots should be held cool and well-ventilated. Ideal cured storage conditions are 12â15 Â°C with 85â90% relative humidity and continuous air movement. Below about 10 Â°C, roots suffer chilling injury â they develop hard cores, pitting, and internal breakdown, and they rot faster when returned to warm conditions. Above about 20 Â°C, respiration and sprouting accelerate and shelf life collapses. High humidity prevents shrivelling (weight loss), while ventilation removes the heat and ethylene that build up in a closed pile.

In most of tropical Africa, ambient temperatures sit above the ideal range for long storage, so practical storage horizons are shorter: 2â8 weeks for cured roots under good natural conditions, versus 4â10 months in a properly controlled cold room. This is why processing is not optional for a serious business â it is the only way to capture value from surplus roots when fresh markets are saturated.

2. Traditional and Modern Storage Structures

Traditional African storage takes many forms: pit storage in East Africa, in-ground clamps, woven granaries, and simple shaded sheds. These work for short-term household use but typically fail for commercial volumes because they trap moisture, allow rodent entry, and offer no temperature control. Modern options range from improved ventilated sheds (low cost, 4â8 weeks) to evaporative coolers (charcoal-lined double walls, useful in dry zones) to full cold rooms (high capital, 4â10 months).

Choose based on your market window and capital. A trader selling within three weeks can use a well-built ventilated shed. An exporter or processor who needs steady throughput should budget for a cold room or a processing line â often the processing line pays back faster than the cold room in African conditions.

3. Preventing Storage Rots and Pests

Sweet potato storage rots are dominated by Fusarium, Ceratocystis fimbriata (black rot), and soft rots caused by Erwinia bacteria. The entry point is almost always a wound. Prevention therefore starts in the field and at harvest:

  • Cure immediately. Hold roots at 28â30 Â°C and 85â95% RH for 4â7 days. This is the highest-return step you can take.
  • Handle like eggs. Use padded crates, never throw or drop roots. Discard all cut, cracked, or weevil-damaged roots before storage.
  • Do not wash before storage. Brush off dry soil only. Washing introduces water-borne bacteria into wounds.
  • Sanitise the store. Remove previous crop residues, sweep, and where permitted use a dilute bleach wash on surfaces and pallets.
  • Control rodents and weevils. Use traps, mesh screens on vents, and keep the store dry. Sweet potato weevil (Cylas spp.) is the most damaging pest and is controlled mainly through field sanitation and prompt harvesting.
  • Inspect weekly. Remove any rotting root immediately â one infected root can spread to the whole pile.

4. Processing into Flour, Chips, and Puree

Processing converts perishable roots into storable, transportable, higher-margin products. The three core products are:

  • Flour: Wash, peel, slice (2â3 mm), dry to 10â12% moisture, mill, and sift. Yield is roughly 25â30% of fresh root weight. Flour keeps 6â12 months in sealed, moisture-proof packaging and is blended into bread, porridge, and baby food.
  • Chips / crisps: Slice, blanch, fry or oven-dry, salt, and package. This is the highest-margin snack line but requires consistent oil quality and packaging to avoid rancidity.
  • Puree: Steam or boil, mash, and either use fresh (short shelf life, cold chain required) or aseptic-pack. Puree is the base for juice, jam, and infant food and is increasingly used by food processors as a substitute for imported puree.

Key processing economics: drying is the bottleneck. A solar dryer with 50â100 kg fresh capacity per batch is the realistic entry point for a small enterprise. Drying to the correct moisture is non-negotiable â under-dried flour moulds within weeks; over-dried chips burn and lose colour.

5. Developing Value-Added Products

The African market opportunity is not in selling raw roots â it is in selling branded, consistent, packaged products to urban consumers, schools, and food processors. High-potential lines include:

  • Orange-fleshed sweet potato (OFSP) flour for bread, mandazi, and porridge â rich in beta-carotene, with strong demand from nutrition programmes.
  • OFSP puree as a partial wheat substitute in commercial bakeries (10â40% substitution is technically proven).
  • Snack chips in retail packs for urban supermarkets and kiosks.
  • Infant and complementary foods â high margin but requires food-safety certification.
  • Animal feed from peels and rejects â a zero-waste revenue stream.

Winning in value addition is about consistency, packaging, and certification, not just processing. Register with your national bureau of standards, get a nutrition analysis, and design packaging that survives African transport and humidity.

6. Marketing and Distribution Channels

Map your channels deliberately. Fresh roots move through rural assembly markets, urban open markets, supermarkets, and institutional buyers (schools, hospitals, prisons). Processed products move through supermarkets, health-food shops, e-commerce, export, and institutional feeding programmes. Aggregator co-operatives and contract farming arrangements reduce your risk on both input and output sides.

Practical pricing rule: calculate your cost of production per kg of finished product, add storage/processing loss, packaging, transport, and a 20â30% margin. Do not price against fresh roots â price against the substitute product you are replacing (wheat flour, imported puree, potato crisps).
