1. Common Goat Diseases and Symptoms

As a goat farmer in Africa, you must know the common diseases that threaten your herd. The first is Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR), a viral disease that causes high fever, mouth ulcers, and diarrhea. It spreads quickly and can kill up to 90% of unvaccinated goats. Another major killer is pneumonia, often caused by bacteria. Symptoms include coughing, rapid breathing, and nasal discharge. Goats with pneumonia often stand with their heads stretched forward. You can treat early cases with antibiotics like oxytetracycline, but prevention through good ventilation is key.

Parasites are a constant battle. Haemonchus contortus, the barber's pole worm, sucks blood from the stomach. Signs include pale gums, bottle jaw (swelling under the jaw), and weakness. You can check for anemia by pressing your goat's gum; if it stays pale for more than 2 seconds after you press, the goat is anemic. Coccidiosis is another common disease, especially in young goats. It causes bloody diarrhea and weight loss. Keep bedding dry and use coccidiostats like amprolium in drinking water.

Foot rot is a bacterial infection that causes lameness. The hoof becomes swollen and smells bad. You can treat it by trimming the hoof and applying zinc sulfate or copper sulfate footbaths. Orf, also called sore mouth, is a viral disease that causes scabs on the lips and mouth. It usually heals on its own in 2-3 weeks, but you should isolate affected goats to prevent spread. Mastitis is an infection of the udder, common in dairy goats. The udder becomes hot, hard, and painful. Strip the udder and treat with intramammary antibiotics.

Always observe your goats daily. Look for changes in appetite, behavior, and droppings. A healthy goat is alert with bright eyes and a shiny coat. Early detection saves money and lives. If you see multiple goats sick at once, call a veterinarian immediately. Keep a notebook to record symptoms and treatments so you learn what works on your farm.

2. Vaccination and Deworming Schedules

Vaccination is the cheapest insurance for your goats. In Africa, the most important vaccine is for PPR. Give the first dose at 3 months old, then a booster 3-4 weeks later. After that, vaccinate once a year. If you live in an area with outbreaks, vaccinate every 6 months. The vaccine costs about $0.50 per dose and protects your goat for life. You can get it from your local veterinary office or agricultural supplier. Always keep vaccines cold in a cooler with ice packs. Never use expired vaccines.

Deworming should be strategic, not random. Over-deworming causes worms to become resistant. Use the FAMACHA method to decide which goats need deworming. Check the lower eyelid color: if it's pale white or pink, deworm. If it's red, don't. In Africa, common dewormers are albendazole, ivermectin, and levamisole. Rotate between different groups every year to prevent resistance. Deworm at the start of the rainy season and again mid-season when worms are most active. For goats kept in dry areas, you may only need to deworm twice a year.

Young goats need special attention. Deworm them at 6 weeks old, then every 4-6 weeks until they are 6 months old. Pregnant does should be dewormed 2-4 weeks before kidding to reduce worm eggs in their feces, which protects the kids. But avoid deworming in the first 3 months of pregnancy as it can cause abortion. Always follow the dosage on the label. Underdosing is worse than not deworming because it allows worms to survive and develop resistance.

Keep a record of every vaccination and deworming. Write the date, product name, and dosage. This helps you stay on schedule and track which goats are due. If you have a small herd, you can team up with neighbors to buy vaccines in bulk and share costs. Many African countries have government-subsidized vaccines for PPR and other diseases. Ask your extension officer about programs in your area. Remember, prevention is always cheaper than treatment.

3. Parasite Control Strategies

Parasites are the number one health problem for goats in Africa. They include internal worms, external ticks, mites, and lice. The key to control is integrated management: you cannot rely on drugs alone. Start with pasture management. Rotate your grazing areas so that goats don't graze on the same land for more than 7 days. This breaks the worm life cycle because larvae die after a few weeks without a host. If you have limited land, cut and carry forage from different areas. Avoid grazing goats on wet, marshy ground where worms thrive.

Nutrition plays a huge role. Goats with good body condition can tolerate some worms without getting sick. Feed a balanced diet with enough protein and minerals. Copper and selenium are especially important for immune function. You can use a mineral block or loose minerals. Also, provide clean water. Goats are browsers, not grazers, so let them eat leaves, shrubs, and weeds. This reduces exposure to worm larvae that live on grass near the ground. If you must graze, do it in the morning after dew has dried, as larvae are less active then.

For external parasites, regular inspection is key. Check your goats every week, especially around the ears, under the tail, and between the legs. Ticks can transmit diseases like heartwater. Use acaricides (tick sprays) like amitraz or cypermethrin. Spray or dip your goats every 2-3 weeks during the rainy season. For mites and lice, use ivermectin injections or pour-on products. But be careful: some goats are sensitive to ivermectin, especially certain breeds. Always test on a few goats first.

Biological control can help. Ducks and chickens eat ticks and fly larvae. If you keep poultry with your goats, they will reduce tick numbers. Also, plant repellent plants like neem or tephrosia around the goat house. You can make a homemade spray by boiling neem leaves in water and spraying it on goats. However, this is not as strong as commercial products. Use it as a supplement, not a replacement. Finally, keep bedding dry and clean. Damp bedding breeds parasites. Replace bedding weekly and compost it. Composting kills many parasite eggs.

4. Hoof Care and Trimming

Goat hooves grow continuously, just like fingernails. In the wild, goats wear them down by walking on rocky ground. But on most farms, especially in soft soil or confined areas, hooves overgrow. Overgrown hooves cause pain, lameness, and make it hard for goats to walk to feed and water. They also trap dirt and bacteria, leading to foot rot. You should trim your goats' hooves every 6-8 weeks. If you have rocky terrain, you might trim every 3-4 months. Check hooves monthly to see if trimming is needed.

You need the right tools: a sharp pair of hoof trimmers (also called hoof shears), a hoof knife, and a rasp. You can buy these at agricultural supply stores for about $15-30. Always clean the tools with disinfectant before and after use to prevent spreading disease. To trim, secure the goat in a standing position. You can use a milking stand or have a helper hold the goat. Lift one leg and bend it at the knee. The goat may struggle, so be patient and calm. Trim small pieces at a time. Cut the overgrown wall and sole until the hoof is level with the sole. Do not cut too deep into the quick (the pink tissue inside). If you see blood, you've cut too far. Apply a disinfectant and wrap the hoof.

The sole should be slightly concave, not flat. The heel should be trimmed to match the toe. After trimming, the hooves should look like two even toes. If the goat has foot rot, you will smell a foul odor and see black, necrotic tissue. In that case, trim away all dead tissue, then soak the hoof in a 10% zinc sulfate solution for 10 minutes. Repeat daily for 5 days. You can also use copper sulfate. For severe cases, give an antibiotic injection like oxytetracycline.

Prevention is better than trimming. Keep the goat house dry. Mud and wet bedding soften hooves and make them prone to rot. Provide a hard, dry standing area. If your goats are on soft ground, create a concrete or gravel area where they can walk. Regular exercise on hard ground helps wear hooves naturally. Also, feed a balanced diet. Biotin deficiency can cause weak hooves. Some farmers give biotin supplements, but it's expensive. Better to provide a good mineral mix. Finally, cull goats with chronic hoof problems. They are a liability.

5. First Aid for Goats

First aid can save your goat's life before the vet arrives. Every goat farmer should have a basic first aid kit. It should include: antiseptic solution (like iodine or chlorhexidine), antibiotic powder, bandages, gauze, scissors, tweezers, a thermometer, a syringe (without needle), and a drench gun. Also keep a bottle of activated charcoal for poisonings, and a small bottle of epinephrine for allergic reactions. Store everything in a clean, dry box. Check expiration dates every 6 months.

The most common emergencies are wounds, bloat, and poisoning. For wounds, first stop bleeding by applying pressure with a clean cloth. Then clean the wound with saline or boiled cooled water. Apply antiseptic and cover with a bandage. If the wound is deep or dirty, give an antibiotic injection (e.g., penicillin) to prevent infection. Change the bandage daily. For bloat, which happens when goats eat too much lush grass or grain, you need to act fast. The left side of the abdomen will be swollen and tight. Give 20-30 ml of vegetable oil orally to break up foam. Then massage the rumen. If the goat is in severe distress, you may need to pass a stomach tube. Call a vet immediately.

Poisoning is common when goats eat toxic plants like lantana, oleander, or wilted cherry leaves. Symptoms include salivation, vomiting, and convulsions. If you suspect poisoning, give activated charcoal: mix 1-2 grams per kg of body weight in water and drench. Do not induce vomiting. Keep the goat calm and in a dark, quiet place. For snake bites, keep the goat still and apply a cold compress. Do not cut the wound. Give an antihistamine if available. Transport to a vet as soon as possible.

Other emergencies include dystocia (difficult birth), mastitis, and heat stress. For dystocia, if the doe has been in labor for more than 2 hours without progress, check the position of the kid. If it's not coming correctly, you may need to gently pull. Use lubricant. If you can't correct it, call for help. For heat stress, move the goat to shade, pour cool water on its body, and offer electrolytes. For mastitis, strip the udder frequently and give intramammary antibiotics. Always record what you did and when. This helps the vet and helps you learn.

6. Biosecurity on the Farm

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out of your farm. It is the cheapest and most effective health strategy. The first rule is quarantine. Any new goat you bring to the farm must be isolated for at least 30 days. Keep it at least 10 meters away from your herd. Feed and water it last, and wash your hands and change clothes after handling it. Watch for signs of disease. Only introduce it to the herd after 30 days if it is healthy. This simple step prevents many outbreaks.

Limit visitors. Diseases can enter on shoes, clothes, and hands. If you must have visitors, provide them with clean boots or shoe covers. Keep a footbath with disinfectant (like 2% sodium hypochlorite) at the entrance to your goat house. Change the disinfectant every 3 days. Do not let visitors touch your goats unless necessary. If they do, have them wash hands before and after. Also, keep wild animals out. Birds, rodents, and stray dogs can carry diseases. Use mesh wire on windows and doors. Store feed in sealed containers to prevent rodents.

Practice all-in, all-out management if possible. This means bringing in a group of goats, raising them together, and then selling or slaughtering them all at once before bringing in new ones. This breaks disease cycles. If you can't do that, at least keep age groups separate. Young goats are more susceptible. Do not mix kids with adults unless they are with their mothers. Also, keep different species separate. Chickens, ducks, and pigs can carry diseases that affect goats. If you have mixed farming, use separate houses and equipment.

Clean and disinfect regularly. Remove manure daily. Scrub water and feed troughs weekly with soap and water, then disinfect. Use a disinfectant like Virkon or bleach. Let them dry in the sun. Sunlight kills many germs. Keep the goat house dry and well-ventilated. Damp, stuffy houses breed disease. Finally, vaccinate and deworm on schedule. A healthy goat is less likely to get sick. If you see a sick goat, isolate it immediately. Do not wait. Treat it and monitor the rest of the herd. If multiple goats get sick, call a vet. Biosecurity is a habit, not a one-time task.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Health and Disease Management for Goat Enterprises

In African goat farming, animal health is not a veterinary expense line it is a revenue protection strategy. A single disease outbreak can wipe out 3060% of a herd's market value within weeks, destroy breeding stock you spent years building, and close off buyers who demand consistent supply. This practical reference gives you the field-ready numbers, schedules, and decision rules you need to keep your herd productive and your business bankable.

Treat this module as your farm's operating manual. Every table, checklist, and diagram below is designed so you can print it, laminate it, and pin it in your goat house. In low-resource African settings where veterinary services may be hours away, the farmer who recognises symptoms early and acts on a schedule is the farmer who stays in business.

SAFETY WARNING Drug Residues and Human Health

Never sell or slaughter an animal before the withdrawal period stated on the dewormer or antibiotic label has passed (commonly 728 days for meat, longer for milk). Residues in meat and milk can harm consumers and will get your animals rejected at formal markets and abattoirs. Store all drugs locked away from children, use gloves when handling pour-ons and sprays, and never use human antibiotics on goats. Dispose of needles and expired drugs safely do not dump them in water sources.

3. Parasite Control Strategies

Blanket deworming is now failing across Africa because of drug resistance. The business-smart approach is targeted selective treatment (TST) combined with pasture and nutrition management.

  • Use FAMACHA scoring to treat only anaemic animals cuts drug costs and slows resistance.
  • Rotate pastures where land allows: graze goats after cattle, and rest paddocks 3060 days.
  • Zero-graze or cut-and-carry systems break the parasite cycle entirely ideal for intensive dairy goat units.
  • Feed well. A goat on good protein gains resilience to worms; poor nutrition multiplies losses.
  • Rotate drug families annually (e.g. benzimidazoles macrocyclic lactones imidazothiazoles) and dose by accurate weight.
  • Quarantine and treat all new animals before they join the herd.

4. Hoof Care and Trimming

Limping goats do not graze, do not breed, and do not fetch top market price. In wet African highlands, hoof overgrowth and foot rot are among the most underrated profit killers.

  • Inspect hooves every 68 weeks; trim in dry weather on a clean surface.
  • Use sharp hoof shears or a trimming knife; cut small amounts never into the quick (pink tissue).
  • Trim the sole and walls level so the animal stands flat.
  • Treat foot rot with a zinc sulphate or copper sulphate footbath plus antibiotic if severe.
  • Keep pens dry; wet, manure-filled floors are the number one cause of hoof disease.

5. First Aid for Goats

Your first-aid kit is a business asset. Keep it stocked and accessible:

  • Antiseptic (povidone-iodine), wound spray, and clean gauze
  • Antibiotic eye ointment and oral rehydration salts
  • Hoof shears, sharp scissors, disposable gloves
  • Thermometer (normal goat temperature: 38.540.0 C)
  • Dewormer, tick spray, and a notebook to record treatments

Emergency rules: isolate the sick animal, record its temperature and symptoms, give oral fluids if diarrhoeic, and call your vet or extension officer. Do not guess with antibiotics wrong drugs cost money and create resistance.

6. Biosecurity on the Farm

Biosecurity is cheaper than treatment. Simple practices stop 80% of disease entry:

  • Quarantine all new goats for 2130 days in a separate pen.
  • Control visitors no entry without clean boots or footbath.
  • Separate age groups: kids, weaners, and adults in different pens.
  • Isolate sick animals immediately; never share feed or water troughs.
  • Clean and disinfect pens, troughs, and equipment weekly.
  • Keep a farm record book: births, deaths, treatments, sales buyers and lenders want this.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. FAMACHA-check the whole herd every 23 weeks in the rainy season; treat only scores 35.
  2. Vaccinate all kids against PPR at 3 months and booster annually before the rains.
  3. Deworm strategically never blanket-treat; rotate drug families each year.
  4. Trim hooves every 68 weeks and keep pens dry to prevent foot rot.
  5. Quarantine every new animal for 2130 days before mixing with the herd.
  6. Keep a stocked first-aid kit and record every treatment with dates and withdrawal periods.
  7. Isolate any sick goat immediately and call a vet if fever, diarrhoea or coughing spreads.
  8. Maintain a farm record book for health, breeding and sales it protects your market access and creditworthiness.
