Lessons

1. Parts of the Body

In Shona, the body is called 'muviri'. Start by learning the head, 'musoro'. Your eyes are 'maziso', ears 'nzeve', nose 'mhuno', and mouth 'muromo'. For the torso, 'mudumbu' is the stomach, 'chipfuva' is the chest, and 'musana' is the back. Arms are 'maoko', hands 'maoko' (same word), fingers 'minwe', and legs 'makumbo'. Feet are 'tsoka', and toes are 'zvigunwe'. When you visit a clinic in Harare or Bulawayo, the nurse might ask, 'Unorwadziwa kupi?' meaning 'Where does it hurt?' You can reply, 'Musoro unorwadza' (My head hurts) or 'Mudumbu unorwadza' (My stomach hurts). Practice pointing to each part on your own body as you say the word aloud. For example, touch your head and say 'musoro', touch your eyes and say 'maziso'. This method helps you remember faster. Also, learn the plural forms: 'maziso' (eyes), 'nzeve' (ears), 'maoko' (arms/hands), 'makumbo' (legs). In everyday conversation, you might hear 'Ane maziso akanaka' (She has beautiful eyes). To ask about someone's health, you can say, 'Muviri wako wakanaka here?' (Is your body well?). If you are in a rural area and need to explain a pain to a traditional healer, knowing these words is crucial. For instance, 'Baba, musoro wangu unorwadza uye ndine chizungu' (Father, my head hurts and I feel dizzy). Remember that Shona uses prefixes to show possession: 'wangu' means 'my', 'wako' means 'your', 'wake' means 'his/her'. So 'musoro wangu' is 'my head', 'musoro wako' is 'your head'. Practice with a friend: one points to a body part, the other names it in Shona. Do this for 10 minutes daily. Within a week, you will know all the major parts. This vocabulary is the foundation for describing any health issue, so master it first.

2. Common Illnesses and Symptoms

In Shona, common illnesses include 'chirwere' (disease), 'mukana' (flu), 'chikosoro' (cough), 'fivha' (fever), and 'kurwadziwa' (pain). Symptoms like headache are 'musoro unorwadza', stomach ache 'mudumbu unorwadza', and nausea 'kurutsa'. If you have diarrhea, say 'ndine manyoka'. For malaria, the word is 'marariya', and you might say 'ndine marariya' (I have malaria). A runny nose is 'mhuno inoyerera', and a sore throat is 'huro inorwadza'. When you feel weak, say 'ndine usimbe'. To ask someone if they are sick, say 'Uri kurwara here?' (Are you sick?). They might reply, 'Hongu, ndiri kurwara' (Yes, I am sick). In many African communities, people use herbal remedies like 'moringa' or 'mupfuti' for various ailments. For example, 'mupfuti' (moringa) is used to boost immunity. But it's important to know when to go to a clinic. If you have a fever above 38C, go to the nearest health center. In Shona, 'fivha' means fever. You can say 'Ndine fivha' (I have a fever). Also, learn to describe duration: 'Ndakarwara kwemazuva maviri' (I have been sick for two days). 'Mazuva' means days. If you have a cough, say 'Ndine chikosoro'. For a cold, 'ndine dzihwa'. When you visit a pharmacy, you can ask for medicine: 'Ndinoda mushonga wechikosoro' (I need medicine for a cough). Remember that 'mushonga' means medicine. Common symptoms also include 'kurwadziwa nemusoro' (headache), 'kurwadziwa nemudumbu' (stomach ache), and 'kunzwa kuneta' (feeling tired). Practice these phrases with a partner. For instance, one person acts sick and says, 'Ndine fivha nechikosoro', and the other responds with advice: 'Enda kuchipatara' (Go to the hospital). This will help you communicate effectively when you or someone else is ill.

3. Visiting a Doctor or Pharmacy

When you visit a doctor in Zimbabwe, you might go to a 'chipatara' (hospital) or 'kiriniki' (clinic). A pharmacy is 'khemisiti' or 'chitoro chemishonga'. At the reception, you will be asked, 'Unoda kuona chiremba here?' (Do you want to see a doctor?). You can reply, 'Hongu, ndinoda kuona chiremba' (Yes, I want to see a doctor). The doctor is called 'chiremba' or 'dhokotera'. A nurse is 'mukoti'. When you see the doctor, they may ask, 'Uri kunzwa sei?' (How are you feeling?). You can say, 'Ndiri kunzwa kurwadziwa nemusoro' (I am feeling a headache). If you need medicine, the doctor will write a prescription, 'chinyorwa chemishonga'. You then take it to the pharmacy. At the pharmacy, say, 'Ndine chinyorwa chemishonga' (I have a prescription). The pharmacist might ask, 'Unoda mushonga uyu here?' (Do you want this medicine?). You can ask about dosage: 'Ndinofanira kunwa mushonga kangani pazuva?' (How many times a day should I take the medicine?). The answer might be 'Katatu pazuva' (Three times a day). Also, learn to say 'mangwanani' (morning), 'masikati' (afternoon), and 'manheru' (evening) to specify times. If you have allergies, say 'Ndine allergy kune penicillin' (I am allergic to penicillin). For payment, you might ask, 'Mushonga uyu unodhura zvakadii?' (How much does this medicine cost?). In rural areas, you might visit a 'n'anga' (traditional healer) first. But for emergencies, go to the clinic. Remember to bring your 'chitupa' (ID) and 'kadhi rehutano' (health card). If you don't understand, say 'Ndapota, dzokorora' (Please repeat). Practice these phrases with a friend to build confidence. Role-play a visit to the doctor: one plays the patient, the other the doctor. This will prepare you for real situations.

4. Describing Pain and Discomfort

To describe pain in Shona, use the verb 'kurwadza' (to hurt). For example, 'Musoro unorwadza' means 'My head hurts'. You can specify the type of pain: 'kurwadza kwakanyanya' (severe pain), 'kurwadza kushoma' (mild pain), 'kurwadza kunopisa' (burning pain), 'kurwadza kunotonhora' (dull pain). If the pain is sharp, say 'kurwadza kunocheka'. For a throbbing pain, 'kurwadza kunorova'. To say 'I feel pain in my chest', say 'Ndine kurwadziwa muchipfuva'. If you have a stomach ache, 'Mudumbu unorwadza'. For a headache, 'Musoro unorwadza'. You can also say 'Ndiri kurwadziwa' (I am in pain). To ask someone where it hurts, say 'Unorwadziwa kupi?' The answer might be 'Ndiri kurwadziwa musana' (I have pain in my back). If you feel dizzy, say 'Ndine chizungu'. For nausea, 'Ndine kurutsa'. If you have a fever, 'Ndine fivha'. To describe the intensity, use 'zvakanyanya' (very much) or 'zvishoma' (a little). For example, 'Musoro unorwadza zvakanyanya' (My head hurts very much). If the pain is getting worse, say 'Kurwadziwa kuri kuwedzera'. If it's getting better, 'Kurwadziwa kuri kupera'. You can also use similes: 'Kurwadziwa kunenge kuchibaya' (The pain is like stabbing). Practice by describing different scenarios: a headache, a backache, a toothache ('zino rinorwadza'). If you have a toothache, say 'Zino rangu rinorwadza'. To say 'I cannot sleep because of the pain', say 'Handikwanise kurara nekuda kwekurwadziwa'. These phrases will help you communicate clearly with a doctor or nurse. Remember to use gestures to show where it hurts. Combine words with pointing for effective communication.

5. Healthy Habits and Advice

In Shona, healthy habits are called 'tsika dzakanaka dzehutano'. To stay healthy, you should 'kudya zvinovaka muviri' (eat nutritious food), 'kunwa mvura yakawanda' (drink plenty of water), and 'kurovedza muviri' (exercise). Eat fruits and vegetables like 'muriwo' (vegetables), 'muchero' (fruit), and 'nyama' (meat) in moderation. Drink at least 8 glasses of water daily. In Shona, water is 'mvura'. Say 'Ndinonwa mvura yakawanda' (I drink a lot of water). Exercise is 'kurovedza muviri'. You can say 'Ndinoro vedza muviri mangwanani' (I exercise in the morning). Sleep is 'kurara'. Aim for 7-8 hours of sleep. Say 'Ndinorara maawa masere' (I sleep eight hours). Avoid alcohol and smoking: 'Usanwa doro' (Don't drink alcohol) and 'Usaputa fodya' (Don't smoke). To give advice, use 'unofanira' (you should). For example, 'Unofanira kudya michero' (You should eat fruits). To ask for advice, say 'Ndinofanira kuita sei kuti ndive neutano hwakanaka?' (What should I do to be healthy?). A common response is 'Idya zvinovaka muviri uye urove muviri' (Eat nutritious food and exercise). Also, washing hands is 'kugeza maoko'. Say 'Ndinogeza maoko' (I wash my hands). To prevent illness, 'Dzivirira chirwere' (Prevent disease). You can say 'Ndinoda kudzivirira chirwere' (I want to prevent disease). In many African communities, people use 'mishonga yechivanhu' (traditional medicine) alongside modern medicine. But always consult a professional. Practice these phrases with a friend. For instance, one says 'Ndine fivha' and the other advises 'Unofanira kunwa mvura yakawanda uye kuzorora' (You should drink plenty of water and rest). This will help you give and receive health advice in Shona.

6. Emergency Phrases

In an emergency, every second counts. Learn these Shona phrases to call for help. 'Ndapota, ndibatsireiwo!' means 'Please help me!' If someone is injured, shout 'Akakuvara!' (He/She is injured!). For a fire, 'Moto!' (Fire!). If someone is drowning, 'Ari kunyura!' (He/She is drowning!). To call an ambulance, say 'Fonera ambulance' (Call an ambulance). The emergency number in Zimbabwe is 994 for ambulance, 995 for fire, and 999 for police. But in rural areas, you might need to transport the person to the nearest clinic. Say 'Ndinoda kuti tiende kuchipatara' (I want us to go to the hospital). If someone is unconscious, 'Akapera simba' (He/She is unconscious). For bleeding, 'Ari kubuda ropa' (He/She is bleeding). To ask for a first aid kit, 'Ndinoda bhokisi rekurwara' (I need a first aid kit). If someone has a seizure, 'Ari kugwinha' (He/She is having a seizure). To say 'Call a doctor', 'Fonera chiremba'. If you are lost, 'Ndarasika' (I am lost). For a snake bite, 'Akarumwa nenyoka' (He/She was bitten by a snake). To say 'I need help urgently', 'Ndinoda rubatsiro nekukurumidza'. Practice these phrases with a partner. Role-play scenarios: one acts as the victim, the other as the rescuer. For example, victim: 'Ndapota, ndibatsireiwo! Ndine kurwadziwa muchipfuva!' Rescuer: 'Zorora, ndichafonera ambulance.' Also, learn to give directions: 'Kuchipatara kuri kure here?' (Is the hospital far?). If you need to describe the emergency, say 'Pane tsaona' (There is an accident). Remember to stay calm and speak clearly. These phrases can save lives.

<h2>Practical Reference: Health &amp; Body Language for Shona-Speaking Markets</h2>

<p>For agribusiness operators, farm managers, extension officers, and traders working across Zimbabwe and the wider Shona-speaking belt, health vocabulary is not academic. It is operational. When a worker reports <em>musoro unorwadza</em> (headache) at 6 a.m., when a customer at a rural pharmacy asks for <em>mushonga wechirwere chemudumbu</em> (medicine for stomach illness), or when you must explain a workplace injury to a clinic nurse, the right Shona phrase protects productivity, reduces downtime, and builds trust with the communities your business depends on.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in the Health and Body module into a single working document. Use it as a laminated field card, a staff induction handout, or a quick-lookup page for supervisors who manage mixed-language teams. Every term is given in Shona with an English gloss, and where relevant, a note on the business scenario in which it is most useful.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Never rely on a single phrase in a medical emergency. If a worker is unconscious, bleeding heavily, has swallowed pesticide, or reports chest pain, call emergency services immediately (<strong>993</strong> for ambulance in Zimbabwe, <strong>112</strong> from mobile networks across much of Southern Africa) and use the phrase <em>"Ndapota, batsirai! <h3>Visiting a Doctor or Pharmacy</h3>

<p>In rural Zimbabwe, the first point of care is often a village health worker or a rural clinic, not a doctor. The pharmacy counter at a growth point is a commercial interaction as much as a health one. The following phrases keep that interaction efficient.</p>

<ul>
<li><em>Ndinoda kuona chiremba.</em> I need to see a doctor.</li>
<li><em>Ndinoda mushonga wechirwere ichi.</em> I need medicine for this illness.</li>
<li><em>Mushonga uyu unonwiwa kangani pazuva?</em> How many times a day is this medicine taken?</li>
<li><em>Ndinorwadziwa pano.</em> I have pain here (point to the site).</li>
<li><em>Ndine fivha kubva nezuro.</em> I have had a fever since yesterday.</li>
<li><em>Ndinoda bara rechirwere.</em> I need a sick note / medical certificate.</li>
</ul>

<p>For agribusiness managers, the sick note (<em>gwaro rekurwara</em> or <em>bara rechirwere</em>) matters for payroll and insurance. Train supervisors to request it politely and to log the clinic name, date, and diagnosis category. This protects both the worker and the business in any compensation dispute.</p>

<h3>Describing Pain and Discomfort</h3>

<p>Shona pain description is often metaphorical. Workers may say <em>musoro unorova</em> (my head is beating) for a throbbing headache, or <em>dumbu rinopisa</em> (my stomach is burning) for gastritis. Learn the metaphors, not just the dictionary words.</p>

<ul>
<li><em>Inorwadza zvikuru.</em> It hurts a lot.</li>
<li><em>Inorwadza zvishoma.</em> It hurts a little.</li>
<li><em>Inorova.</em> It is throbbing / beating.</li>
<li><em>Inopisa.</em> It is burning.</li>
<li><em>Ndine chizungu.</em> I feel dizzy.</li>
<li><em>Ndinonzi ndinorutsa.</em> I feel like vomiting.</li>
<li><em>Handikwanise kufamba.</em> I cannot walk.</li>
<li><em>Handikwanise kusimuka.</em> I cannot stand up.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Healthy Habits and Advice</h3>

<p>Health messaging in Shona lands better when it is framed around work capacity, not abstract wellness. A supervisor who says <em>"Geza maoko nguva dzose kuti usarwara uye usarasikirwe nebasa"</em> ("Always wash your hands so you don't get sick and lose work") is more persuasive than a generic hygiene poster.</p>

<ul>
<li><em>Geza maoko nguva dzose.</em> Wash your hands regularly.</li>
<li><em>Nwa mvura yakawanda.</em> Drink plenty of water.</li>
<li><em>Rara nguva yakakwana.</em> Sleep enough.</li>
<li><em>Dya zvokudya zvine hutano.</em> Eat healthy food.</li>
<li><em>Shandisa mosquito net.</em> Use a mosquito net.</li>
<li><em>Enda kuchipatara kana uchinge usina kunaka.</em> Go to the clinic if you feel unwell.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Emergency Phrases</h3>

<p>These phrases should be posted at every workstation, weighbridge, and packhouse. They are short enough to memorise under stress.</p>

<ul>
<li><em>Batsirai!</em> Help!</li>
<li><em>Ndapota, fonera ambulance.</em> Please call an ambulance.</li>
<li><em>Munhu uyu akakuvara.</em> This person is injured.</li>
<li><em>Munhu uyu haafeme.</em> This person is not breathing.</li>
<li><em>Ndoda first aid kit.</em> I need the first-aid kit.</li>
<li><em>Ndoda chiremba nekukurumidza.</em> I need a doctor urgently.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li>Post a laminated ShonaEnglish body-part and emergency phrase card at every workstation and first-aid point.</li>
<li>Train at least one supervisor per shift in basic first aid and the emergency Shona phrases above.</li>
<li>Keep a written, professionally translated consent card in the first-aid kit do not improvise consent language.</li>
<li>Record all injury and illness reports using both the Shona term given by the worker and the English clinical term.</li>
<li>Verify the nearest clinic, pharmacy, and ambulance contact number for each operating site, and update quarterly.</li>
<li>Include health vocabulary in staff induction so new hires can report symptoms accurately from day one.</li>
<li>Audit water, sanitation, and PPE provision against the illness profile in Table 2 each season.</li>
<li>Review sick-note and compensation procedures annually with a qualified HR or legal advisor.</li>
</ol>
