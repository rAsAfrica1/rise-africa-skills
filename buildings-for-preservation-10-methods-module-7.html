1. Sources of Damp: Rising Damp, Leaks, and Condensation

Damp is the enemy of any building. In Africa, we deal with three main types: rising damp, leaks, and condensation. Rising damp happens when ground water climbs up through porous walls by capillary action. You'll see a tide mark up to 1.5 meters high, white salt deposits, and peeling paint. It's common in older buildings without a damp-proof course (DPC). Leaks come from broken pipes, cracked roofs, or faulty gutters. They are often sudden and localized. Condensation occurs when warm, moist air meets a cold surface, like a cold wall or window. In humid areas like the coast, condensation can cause mould and mildew. To identify the source, check for patterns: rising damp is continuous along the base, leaks are patchy and may drip, condensation appears on cold surfaces and disappears when ventilated. Use a moisture meter (cost about $30) to measure. A simple test: tape a square of aluminium foil to the wall for 24 hours. If moisture appears on the outside, it's condensation; if on the inside, it's rising damp or a leak. Fixing the source is the first step before any waterproofing.

2. Installing Damp-Proof Courses

A damp-proof course (DPC) is a barrier that stops moisture from rising up through walls. In Africa, we use materials like bitumen felt, plastic sheets (polyethylene), or slate. For existing buildings, you can install a DPC by cutting a horizontal slot into the wall about 150mm above ground level, using a angle grinder or masonry saw. The slot should be about 12mm wide and deep enough to go through the wall. Then insert a DPC membrane, such as a 0.5mm thick polyethylene sheet, and seal it with mortar. For new construction, place the DPC between the foundation and the first course of bricks. Ensure it's continuous and overlaps at corners. Cost: about $2 per meter for polyethylene. Tools: angle grinder, mortar, trowel, DPC membrane. Safety: wear goggles and gloves. After installation, replaster the wall. This method can last 20-30 years if done correctly. If you can't cut a slot, you can inject a chemical DPC (silane/siloxane) into holes drilled every 150mm. This is more expensive but less invasive.

3. Using Waterproof Membranes and Coatings

Waterproof membranes and coatings are your second line of defence after fixing the source. For roofs, use bituminous membranes (torch-on) or EPDM rubber. For walls, cementitious coatings (like Thoroseal) are popular in Africa because they are affordable and easy to apply. First, clean the surface: remove loose paint, dirt, and mould with a wire brush and water. Let it dry. Mix the coating according to the instructions (usually 1 part water to 3 parts powder). Apply two coats with a brush or roller, waiting 4-6 hours between coats. Coverage: 1kg covers about 1 square meter per coat. For extra protection, embed a fibreglass mesh into the first coat. For below-ground walls, use a bituminous coating or a dimpled drainage membrane. For flat roofs, use a polyurethane coating. Cost: cementitious coating is about $5 per kg; bitumen is $3 per kg. Always apply when rain is not expected for 24 hours. Temperature should be between 10C and 35C. Ventilate the area. These coatings can last 5-10 years if maintained.

4. Improving Drainage Around Buildings

Water pooling around your foundation is a major cause of damp. Improve drainage by creating a slope away from the building. The ground should fall at least 150mm over the first 3 meters (a 5% slope). If you have a garden, dig a swale (a shallow ditch) 300mm deep and 600mm wide, lined with gravel, to channel water away. For downspouts, extend them at least 1.5 meters from the wall using plastic pipes. Install a French drain: dig a trench 300mm wide and 600mm deep around the perimeter, fill with 20mm gravel, and lay a perforated pipe at the bottom. Cover with geotextile fabric to prevent clogging. Slope the pipe 1% towards a soakaway or storm drain. In areas with heavy rain, consider a soakaway pit: a 1m x 1m x 1m hole filled with rubble, allowing water to seep into the ground. Cost: about $50 for a 10-meter French drain. Tools: shovel, wheelbarrow, level, gravel. Clean gutters regularly to prevent overflow. These simple steps can reduce damp significantly.

5. Ventilation Strategies to Reduce Moisture

Good ventilation removes moist air before it causes condensation. In Africa, many buildings rely on natural ventilation, but you can improve it. Install air bricks (also called vents) in walls, especially in kitchens, bathrooms, and laundry areas. A 215mm x 65mm air brick provides about 1000mm of free area. Place them low and high to create cross-ventilation. For rooms with poor airflow, use extractor fans: a 100mm fan can move 80 cubic meters per hour. Cost: $20-$50. In humid coastal areas, use dehumidifiers or ceiling fans to circulate air. Open windows for at least 15 minutes a day, even in the rainy season. For roofs, ensure eaves are ventilated with soffit vents. If you have a crawl space, install vents every 2 meters. Avoid blocking vents with furniture or plants. For traditional buildings, use thatch or breathable materials. A simple trick: place a bowl of salt or charcoal in damp corners to absorb moisture. But ventilation is more effective. Regular maintenance: check vents for blockages, clean fans, and ensure doors have gaps for air movement.

6. Practical: Applying a Waterproof Coating to a Wall

In this practical, you will apply a cementitious waterproof coating to a damp wall. Tools: wire brush, trowel, bucket, measuring jug, mixing paddle, paintbrush, roller, gloves, goggles. Materials: cementitious coating powder (e.g., Thoroseal), water, fibreglass mesh (optional). Step 1: Prepare the surface. Remove all loose paint, dirt, and efflorescence with a wire brush. Rinse with water and let dry. Step 2: Mix the coating. Add 1 part clean water to 3 parts powder in a bucket. Mix with a paddle until smooth and lump-free. Let it sit for 5 minutes. Step 3: Apply the first coat. Dampen the wall with water. Apply a thin, even coat using a brush or roller. Work it into the surface. If using mesh, embed it while the coating is wet. Step 4: Wait 4-6 hours (or as per instructions) until the first coat is touch-dry. Step 5: Apply the second coat at right angles to the first. Step 6: Cure the coating. Keep it damp for 24 hours by misting with water. Avoid rain for 48 hours. Clean tools with water immediately. Cost: for a 10m wall, you need about 20kg of coating, costing $100. This coating will stop damp and protect the wall for years.

<h2>Practical Reference: Applying a Waterproof Coating to a Wall</h2>

<p>Waterproofing a wall is one of the fastest ways for an African building contractor, maintenance company, or property owner to stop money leaking out of an asset. Damp walls destroy paint, weaken plaster, rust reinforcement, damage stock stored indoors, and force repeated repairs that eat into rental income and resale value. In many African markets, buildings are the largest single investment a family, small business, or landlord will ever make. Protecting that investment from moisture is therefore not just a technical task it is a commercial discipline. This practical reference gives you a field-ready method for applying a waterproof coating to a wall, with the checks, materials, and quality controls that determine whether the job lasts two years or ten.</p>

<p>Before any coating is opened, remember this principle: <strong>waterproofing is 20% product and 80% preparation</strong>. A cheap coating on a well-prepared wall will outperform an expensive coating on a dirty, cracked, or damp surface. In hot, humid, and rainy African climates, walls face intense cycles of wetting and drying, high UV, dust, and in coastal cities like Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, or Lagos, salt-laden air. The method below is designed for those conditions and for contractors working with limited water, power, and equipment.</p>

<h2>Why This Job Matters Commercially</h2>
<p>In most African cities, waterproofing failures are the number one cause of premature building maintenance. A wall that keeps damp forces repainting every 1218 months instead of every 57 years. In commercial buildings, damp walls reduce rentable value, fail health inspections, and in food or pharmaceutical storage, can lead to total stock loss. Contractors who master waterproofing win repeat clients because they solve a visible, painful problem. The practical method below is written so a small team with basic tools can deliver a professional, warrantable result.</p>

<h2>Step-by-Step Application Method</h2>

<h3>Step 1: Diagnose the moisture source</h3>
<p>Never coat a wall until you know why it is wet. Rising damp, a leaking pipe, a cracked roof, or condensation each require a different fix. Coating over an active leak traps water and causes the coating to blister and fail. Use a moisture meter and observe the damp pattern: rising damp is usually up to about 1.5 m from the base and has a tide mark; leaks are localized and appear after rain or use; condensation is heaviest on cold surfaces and in kitchens and bathrooms.</p>

<h3>Step 2: Prepare the surface</h3>
<p>Remove all loose material, paint, and efflorescence. Repair cracks and honeycomb with non-shrink repair mortar. Rinse the wall and allow it to dry. The surface should be sound, clean, and slightly damp but not wet when applying cementitious coatings always follow the manufacturer's instruction. For acrylic or polyurethane coatings, the surface must be dry.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Apply the primer</h3>
<p>Apply the specified primer by brush or roller. This improves adhesion and seals the substrate. Allow it to dry according to the data sheet, typically 26 hours in African heat.</p>

<h3>Step 4: Apply the first coat</h3>
<p>Apply the waterproof coating in a uniform layer using a stiff brush or roller. Work the material into pores and corners. For cracks and joints, embed alkali-resistant mesh tape into the first coat while it is still wet. Allow the first coat to cure per the manufacturer's instructions.</p>

<h3>Step 5: Apply the second coat</h3>
<p>Apply the second coat at right angles to the first to ensure full coverage. Total dry film thickness should meet the manufacturer's specification, often 12 mm for cementitious systems. Do not thin the product unless the data sheet allows it.</p>

<h3>Step 6: Protect and cure</h3>
<p>Protect the coating from rain and direct sun during curing. In hot climates, curing may require dampening or a curing membrane. Once cured, apply a protective render, screed, or tile finish where required. Do not allow traffic or backfill against the wall until fully cured.</p>

<h3>Step 7: Improve drainage and ventilation</h3>
<p>Coating alone is not enough. Ensure the ground slopes away from the wall, gutters and downpipes discharge away from the base, and the wall has adequate ventilation. This addresses the source of moisture and extends the coating's life.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:14px; margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE STARTING</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Waterproofing products can contain cement, crystalline silica, isocyanates, solvents, and biocides. Inhaling dust or vapor can cause serious lung damage, and skin contact can cause severe chemical burns or allergic reactions. Always wear chemical-resistant gloves, sealed goggles, a dust mask or respirator, and protective boots. Work in a well-ventilated area. Never mix products from different manufacturers unless approved. Keep children and animals away from the work area. Store chemicals away from food, water sources, and direct sunlight. If a product enters the eyes, rinse with clean water for at least 15 minutes and seek medical help immediately. In hot African conditions, schedule work for early morning or late afternoon to reduce heat stress and rapid drying.</p>
</div>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Diagnose first:</strong> Confirm the moisture source rising damp, leak, or condensation before buying any coating.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare properly:</strong> Remove loose material, repair cracks, clean, and allow the wall to reach the correct moisture content.</li>
<li><strong>Use the right primer:</strong> Match the primer to the coating system and apply evenly.</li>
<li><strong>Two coats, cross-hatched:</strong> Apply the second coat at right angles to the first for full coverage.</li>
<li><strong>Reinforce weak points:</strong> Embed mesh tape at cracks, joints, and corners.</li>
<li><strong>Control curing:</strong> Protect from rain and direct sun; follow the manufacturer's curing time.</li>
<li><strong>Fix drainage and ventilation:</strong> Slope ground away from the wall and ensure gutters and vents work.</li>
<li><strong>Document and warrant:</strong> Photograph each stage, record batch numbers, and give the client a written maintenance schedule.</li>
</ul>
