1. Pediatric Cardiac Arrest: Differences from Adults

When a child's heart stops, it is usually not because of a heart attack like in adults. In children and infants, cardiac arrest often happens because of a lack of oxygen. This can be from choking, drowning, suffocation, or a severe allergic reaction. The heart may slow down and stop after breathing has already stopped. This means that for children and infants, rescue breaths are very important. You must give breaths along with chest compressions.

Another big difference is the cause. In adults, the most common cause is a heart rhythm problem called ventricular fibrillation. In children, it is more often a breathing problem. So, if you see a child collapse, check if they are breathing. If not, start CPR immediately. Do not wait for an AED. But if an AED is available, use it as soon as possible.

The technique also differs. For adults, you use two hands on the center of the chest. For children, you may use one or two hands, depending on the child's size. For infants, you use two fingers or two thumbs. The depth of compressions is also different: for adults, at least 2 inches; for children, about 2 inches; for infants, about 1.5 inches. The rate is the same for all: 100 to 120 compressions per minute.

In Africa, many children die from preventable causes like drowning in open water, choking on food like groundnuts, or respiratory infections. Knowing these differences can save a life. Remember: children are not small adults. Their bodies respond differently. Always prioritize breathing and use the correct technique. If you are alone, give 1 minute of CPR before calling for help. If you have a phone, put it on speaker and call emergency services while you start CPR.

2. CPR for Children (1 year to puberty)

If a child is unresponsive and not breathing, start CPR. First, check for safety. Tap the child and shout, "Are you okay?" If no response, shout for help. If you are alone, start CPR first for 1 minute, then call emergency services. If you have a phone, call on speaker while you begin.

Place the child on a firm, flat surface. Tilt the head back slightly and lift the chin to open the airway. Give 2 rescue breaths. Each breath should last about 1 second. Watch for the chest to rise. If it doesn't, re-tilt the head and try again. For a child, you can use your mouth to cover their mouth, or mouth-to-nose if needed. If you have a pocket mask or face shield, use it.

Next, start chest compressions. For a child, place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, between the nipples. If the child is large, you can use two hands. Push down hard and fast, at least one-third the depth of the chest, about 2 inches (5 cm). Let the chest come back up fully between compressions. Do 30 compressions at a rate of 100 to 120 per minute. Then give 2 more rescue breaths. Continue this cycle of 30 compressions and 2 breaths.

If you are trained and willing, you can do compression-only CPR, but for children, breaths are very important. If you have an AED, use it as soon as it arrives. Keep going until the child starts breathing, or until help arrives. In Africa, where emergency services may be far, you might need to continue CPR for a long time. If others are around, take turns to avoid fatigue. Remember: push hard, push fast, and don't stop.

3. CPR for Infants (under 1 year)

For infants under 1 year, CPR is similar but with key differences. First, check responsiveness. Tap the sole of the foot and shout. If no response, shout for help. If alone, give 1 minute of CPR before calling for help.

Place the infant on a firm, flat surface. Open the airway by tilting the head back slightlydo not overextend. Give 2 rescue breaths. For an infant, cover both the mouth and nose with your mouth. Give gentle puffs, just enough to see the chest rise. Each breath should last 1 second. If the chest doesn't rise, re-tilt the head and try again.

For chest compressions, use two fingers (index and middle) on the center of the chest, just below the nipple line. Alternatively, you can use two thumbs with hands encircling the chest. Push down about 1.5 inches (4 cm), or one-third the depth of the chest. Rate: 100 to 120 compressions per minute. After 30 compressions, give 2 breaths. Continue the cycle.

If you have an AED, use it. Many AEDs have pediatric pads or a switch for children under 8 years. If not available, use adult pads, placing one on the front and one on the back of the infant. In Africa, where infants may be carried on the back, always ensure the surface is firm. If you are alone and have a phone, call emergency services on speaker while you work. Keep going until the infant breathes or help arrives. Remember: gentle breaths, firm compressions, and don't stop.

4. AED Use on Children and Infants

An AED (Automated External Defibrillator) is a device that can shock the heart back into a normal rhythm. It is safe to use on children and infants. If a child is unresponsive and not breathing, start CPR immediately. Send someone to get an AED if available. If you are alone, do 1 minute of CPR first, then get the AED.

When the AED arrives, turn it on and follow the voice prompts. For children 1 to 8 years old, use pediatric pads if available. These pads are smaller and may reduce the energy dose. If pediatric pads are not available, use adult pads. Place one pad on the upper right chest and the other on the lower left side, as shown on the pads. For infants under 1 year, use pediatric pads if possible. If not, use adult pads, but place one on the front of the chest and one on the back. Ensure the pads are not touching each other.

Make sure no one is touching the child when the AED analyzes the rhythm or delivers a shock. The AED will tell you to stand clear. After the shock, immediately resume CPR, starting with 30 compressions. If no shock is advised, continue CPR. The AED will re-analyze every 2 minutes. Keep going until the child breathes or help arrives.

In Africa, AEDs may be found in airports, large hotels, or shopping malls. If you don't have one, continue CPR. Do not delay CPR to look for an AED. If you have a phone, call emergency services and ask for an AED. Remember: AEDs are simple to usejust follow the prompts.

5. Choking Relief for Children and Infants

Choking is a common emergency in children. It happens when food or an object blocks the airway. For a child (1 year to puberty), if they can cough forcefully, encourage them to keep coughing. If they cannot breathe, cough, or speak, act fast. Give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. Then give 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver). To do this, stand behind the child, wrap your arms around their waist, make a fist with one hand, place it just above the navel, and grab it with the other hand. Pull inward and upward sharply. Repeat cycles of 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object is out or the child becomes unresponsive.

For infants under 1 year, never use abdominal thrusts. Instead, sit down and place the infant face-down on your forearm, supporting the head. Give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades. Then turn the infant face-up and give 5 chest thrusts using two fingers on the center of the chest, just like in CPR. Repeat until the object is coughed up or the infant becomes unresponsive.

If the child or infant becomes unresponsive, start CPR immediately. Check the mouth for the object before giving breaths, but only if you can see it. Do not do a blind finger sweep. In Africa, common choking hazards include groundnuts, bones, and small toys. Always supervise children while eating. If you are alone, call for help after 1 minute of choking relief. Remember: back blows and chest thrusts for infants, back blows and abdominal thrusts for children.

6. Special Considerations: Drowning and Trauma

Drowning is a major cause of death in children in Africa, especially near open water. If a child is drowning, get them out of the water safely. Do not put yourself at risk. Once out, check if they are breathing. If not, start CPR immediately. For drowning, rescue breaths are very important because the problem is lack of oxygen. Give 2 breaths first, then start compressions. If you are alone, give 1 minute of CPR before calling for help. If you have a phone, call on speaker. Continue CPR until the child breathes or help arrives. Do not try to drain water from the lungsit wastes time.

Trauma, such as a fall or road accident, can also cause cardiac arrest in children. If a child is unresponsive after trauma, check for severe bleeding. If there is bleeding, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth. If the child is not breathing, start CPR. But if you suspect a neck or spine injury, avoid moving the head and neck. Open the airway using a jaw thrust instead of a head tilt. If you are alone, call for help first, then start CPR. If you have an AED, use it. Trauma can cause internal bleeding, so keep the child warm and monitor breathing.

In both drowning and trauma, hypothermia can occur. Remove wet clothes and cover the child with a blanket. In Africa, where help may be far, you might need to continue CPR for a long time. If others are present, take turns. Remember: for drowning, start with breaths; for trauma, control bleeding and protect the spine. Always prioritize your safety and the child's breathing.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: CPR and AED for Children and Infants

In African communities and businesses, children are often present at markets, shops, schools, places of worship, and family compounds. A child who collapses from drowning, choking, severe malaria-related seizure, or trauma needs immediate, correct action. This practical reference gives you the numbers, sequences, and decision points you need to act with confidence. It is designed for shop owners, teachers, security staff, drivers, and community responders who may be the first person on scene.

Pediatric resuscitation is not simply adult CPR scaled down. Children usually suffer asphyxial arrest (lack of oxygen first), not sudden cardiac arrest from a primary heart rhythm problem. This changes everything: ventilation matters more, and you must act fast because irreversible brain damage begins within 34 minutes. In many African settings, ambulance response can exceed 2030 minutes. Your early CPR and AED use is not a bridge to help it is the help.

1. Pediatric Cardiac Arrest: Differences from Adults

Adults typically collapse from a sudden heart rhythm problem (ventricular fibrillation). Children usually arrest because their breathing stopped or became inadequate drowning, choking, smoke inhalation, severe pneumonia, or prolonged seizure. This is called asphyxial arrest. Because the heart is still receiving some oxygen at the start, early rescue breaths can be life-saving. In adults, compression-only CPR is acceptable for bystanders; in children, compressions plus ventilations are strongly preferred.

2. CPR for Children (1 year to puberty)

Check responsiveness by tapping the shoulders and shouting. If unresponsive and not breathing normally (or only gasping), start CPR immediately. Send someone to call for help and fetch an AED but do not delay compressions while waiting.

Compressions: Place the child on a firm, flat surface. Kneel beside them. Put the heel of one hand on the lower half of the breastbone (sternum), or use two hands if the child is larger or you need more depth. Keep your arms straight and shoulders directly above your hands. Push hard and fast about 100120 compressions per minute, roughly 5 cm deep, allowing full chest recoil. Count aloud: "one and two and three and"

Breaths: After 30 compressions, give 2 rescue breaths. Tilt the head back slightly, lift the chin, pinch the nose, and make a seal over the mouth. Breathe for about 1 second just enough to see the chest rise. If the chest does not rise, reposition the head and try again. Do not over-inflate; stomach inflation causes vomiting and aspiration.

Continue 30:2 until the child breathes normally, an AED arrives, or another trained rescuer takes over. If you have a second trained rescuer, switch to 15:2 and change compressor every 2 minutes to avoid fatigue.

3. CPR for Infants (under 1 year)

Infants are fragile. Handle them gently but act decisively. Place the infant on a firm surface a table, a mat, or your forearm if no flat surface is available. Check for response by tapping the sole of the foot.

Compressions: Imagine a line between the nipples. Place two fingers just below that line, on the lower half of the sternum. Alternatively, use the two-thumb encircling technique if you have two rescuers: both thumbs side by side on the sternum, hands encircling the chest and supporting the back. Compress about 4 cm (1.5 inches) roughly one-third of the chest depth at 100120 per minute.

Breaths: Cover the infant's mouth and nose with your mouth to create a seal. Give 2 gentle puffs, each about 1 second, just enough to see the chest rise. Infants have small lungs; a normal adult breath is far too much. Use 30:2 for a single rescuer, 15:2 with two rescuers.

SAFETY WARNING Do Not Delay or Improvise

Never shake an infant or child to check responsiveness this can cause brain or spinal injury. Never practice compression depth on a conscious person. Do not use adult AED pads on an infant if pediatric pads or an attenuator are available; if they are not, adult pads may be used with one pad on the front and one on the back. Do not give abdominal thrusts to an infant under 1 year use back blows and chest thrusts only. If you suspect a spinal injury (fall, road traffic crash), avoid tilting the head; use a jaw-thrust to open the airway. In malaria-endemic areas, a child who has just had a seizure and is now unresponsive may be post-ictal check breathing for up to 10 seconds before starting CPR.

4. AED Use on Children and Infants

An AED (Automated External Defibrillator) analyses the heart rhythm and delivers a shock if needed. It is safe and effective for children. Use it as soon as it arrives do not wait until CPR has been running for a set time. Turn it on and follow the voice prompts.

Dry the chest if wet (drowning is common in African communities near rivers, dams, and wells). Remove any medication patches. Do not place pads over an implanted device (you will feel a lump under the skin) place them at least 8 cm away. During analysis and shock delivery, ensure no one touches the child. Resume CPR immediately after the shock.

5. Choking Relief for Children and Infants

Choking is a leading cause of accidental death in young children. In African markets, small groundnuts, hard sweets, and plastic bottle caps are common culprits. If the child is coughing forcefully, encourage them to keep coughing. If the cough is silent or weak, or the child cannot breathe, speak, or cry, act immediately.

Child (1 year to puberty): Give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, leaning the child forward. If the object does not come out, give 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich manoeuvre): stand behind the child, make a fist just above the navel, and pull inward and upward. Alternate 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object is expelled or the child becomes unresponsive. If they become unresponsive, start CPR.

Infant (under 1 year): Never give abdominal thrusts. Place the infant face-down along your forearm, supporting the head, with the head lower than the chest. Give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades. Turn the infant face-up and give 5 chest thrusts using two fingers on the lower half of the sternum. Alternate 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts. If the infant becomes unresponsive, start CPR.

6. Special Considerations: Drowning and Trauma

Drowning: In Africa, drowning often occurs in rivers, lakes, wells, and water drums around the home. The priority is to remove the child from the water safely and start rescue breaths as soon as possible. Give 5 initial rescue breaths before starting compressions this is different from standard CPR and reflects the asphyxial nature of drowning. Dry the chest before applying AED pads.

Trauma: Road traffic crashes, falls, and burns are common. If you suspect a spinal injury, avoid moving the neck. Use a jaw-thrust to open the airway instead of a head-tilt. Control severe bleeding with direct pressure before starting CPR if the child has a pulse. If the child is unresponsive and not breathing, CPR takes priority over bleeding control but if you can do both, do so.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Check safety is the scene safe for you, the child, and bystanders?
  2. Check response tap shoulders (child) or sole of foot (infant) and shout.
  3. Shout for help send someone to call emergency services and fetch an AED.
  4. Check breathing for no more than 10 seconds. Gasping is not normal breathing.
  5. Start CPR 30 compressions : 2 breaths. Child: 5 cm deep, 12 hands. Infant: 4 cm deep, 2 fingers or 2 thumbs.
  6. Use the AED as soon as it arrives pediatric pads if available; dry the chest; follow voice prompts.
  7. Continue until the child breathes normally, help takes over, or you are physically exhausted.
  8. Handover tell the arriving team what happened, how long the child was down, and what you did.
