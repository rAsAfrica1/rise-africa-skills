1. Lesson 1: Selecting and Preparing the Greenhouse Site for Maximum Profit

Welcome to Module 5. This lesson focuses on the critical first step in greenhouse vegetable farming: choosing and preparing your site. In Africa, where climate variability, pest pressure, and market access can make or break a farm, site selection is not just about soil and sun. It is a business decision that affects your costs, yields, and ability to sell. By the end of this lesson, you will know how to evaluate a potential site, prepare the land, and set up basic infrastructure that saves money and boosts productivity. First, consider your market. Are you selling to local hotels, supermarkets, or informal markets? Your site should be within 30 to 60 minutes of your target buyers to reduce transport costs and keep produce fresh. If you plan to export, proximity to an airport or major highway is essential. Also, check for reliable water. Greenhouse vegetables like tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers need consistent irrigation. A borehole, river, or municipal supply that does not dry up in the dry season is non-negotiable. Test water quality: high salinity or heavy metals will harm your crops. Next, evaluate the land itself. A gentle slope of 1 to 2 percent is ideal for drainage. Avoid low-lying areas that flood. The soil should be loamy, well-drained, and rich in organic matter. If the soil is poor, you can still use raised beds or grow bags, but that adds cost. Test soil pH: most vegetables prefer 6.0 to 6.8. If pH is too low, add agricultural lime; too high, add gypsum or organic matter. Also, check for previous land use. If the field grew tomatoes or peppers before, it may harbor soil-borne diseases like bacterial wilt. Solarize the soil by covering it with clear plastic for 4 to 6 weeks during the hot season. Now, clear the site. Remove all weeds, rocks, and debris. Do not burn residues; instead, compost them to add organic matter. Plow or dig to a depth of 30 cm to break hardpan. If you are using a greenhouse, level the ground carefully. A 1 percent slope inside the greenhouse helps drainage but prevents waterlogging. Install a perimeter drain if the site is flat. For the greenhouse structure, choose a location with good sunlight but protection from strong winds. In many African regions, winds can exceed 40 km per hour. Plant a windbreak of fast-growing trees like Grevillea or Casuarina 10 to 15 meters away. Do not plant tall trees too close, as they shade the greenhouse. Orient the greenhouse east-west to capture maximum sunlight in winter and reduce overheating in summer. If you are in a hot area, consider a shade net of 30 to 50 percent on the roof. Before building, mark the corners with pegs and string. Ensure the area is rectangular. For a 8m by 20m greenhouse, you will need about 160 square meters. Clear a 1-meter border around it for access and ventilation. The floor inside should be either soil, gravel, or concrete. Gravel is cheap and drains well, but concrete is easier to clean and reduces weeds. However, concrete is expensive. A compromise is to use gravel with a weed mat underneath. Now, think about water infrastructure. Install a tank or reservoir at least 2 meters above ground for gravity-fed drip irrigation. If you use a pump, place it on a raised platform to avoid flooding. Drip irrigation saves 40 to 60 percent water compared to overhead sprinklers and reduces foliar diseases. For a 8m by 20m greenhouse, you need about 1,000 to 1,500 liters per day in peak season. So, a 5,000-liter tank gives you 3 to 5 days of buffer. Also, install a filter to prevent clogging. Next, plan for electricity. If you have grid power, great. If not, solar panels can run a small pump and fans. A 300-watt solar kit with a battery costs about 300 to 500 USD and pays back in one or two seasons by reducing diesel costs. For security, fence the site with chain-link or thorny bushes. Theft of pumps, pipes, and produce is common. Also, keep a record of all costs: land preparation, greenhouse kit, water system, and labor. A typical 8m by 20m greenhouse in Kenya or Nigeria costs between 1,500 and 3,000 USD for a basic galvanized steel frame with UV-treated polythene. You can reduce costs by using locally available materials like bamboo or timber, but these last only 2 to 3 years. Galvanized steel lasts 10 to 15 years. Now, let us talk about soil preparation inside the greenhouse. If you use native soil, add 20 to 30 kg of well-rotted manure per square meter. Mix it into the top 20 cm. Also add 100 grams of triple superphosphate per square meter if phosphorus is low. For raised beds, make them 1 meter wide and 20 cm high. Leave 40 cm paths between beds. This improves drainage and makes harvesting easier. If you use grow bags, fill them with a mix of 60 percent cocopeat, 30 percent compost, and 10 percent perlite. This is expensive but gives excellent results and avoids soil diseases. For a low-cost option, use 50 percent topsoil, 40 percent compost, and 10 percent sand. Sterilize the mix by solarizing it under plastic for 2 weeks. Finally, test your plan by growing a small nursery of seedlings before full planting. This helps you catch problems early. Remember, a well-chosen and prepared site reduces labor, water, and pesticide costs by up to 30 percent. That directly increases your profit margin. In the next lesson, we will cover greenhouse construction and climate control. But for now, take action: evaluate your site using the checklist I will provide in the summary. Write down your water source, soil test results, and distance to market. Then, prepare the land step by step. Your future harvests depend on this foundation. Make it strong.

2. Lesson 2: Building and Managing the Greenhouse Structure for African Conditions

In Lesson 1, you selected and prepared your site. Now, you will learn how to build or assemble a greenhouse that withstands African weather and maximizes vegetable yields. This lesson is practical and business-focused. We will cover frame materials, covering, ventilation, cooling, and maintenance. By the end, you will know how to choose the right structure for your budget and climate, and how to manage it daily for profit. First, choose the frame. The three main options are galvanized steel, timber, and bamboo. Galvanized steel is the best for longevity and strength. It lasts 10 to 15 years and resists termites and rot. A typical 8m by 20m steel greenhouse kit costs 1,500 to 3,000 USD in Kenya, Nigeria, or South Africa. Timber is cheaper, around 500 to 1,000 USD, but lasts only 3 to 5 years and needs treatment against termites. Bamboo is the cheapest, 200 to 500 USD, but lasts 1 to 2 years and is weak against wind. For a business, steel is usually the best investment because it reduces replacement costs and protects your crops from collapse. If you cannot afford steel, use treated eucalyptus poles and replace them every 3 years. Next, the covering. UV-treated polyethylene film is standard. It should be 200 microns thick for durability. Cheaper films (150 microns) tear easily and last only 1 to 2 years. Good film lasts 3 to 5 years. In hot areas, use a film with 30 to 50 percent shade. In cool highlands, use clear film to trap heat. Also, consider insect netting on the sides. A 40-mesh net keeps out whiteflies, aphids, and thrips, which transmit viruses. This can reduce pesticide costs by 50 percent. Install the film tightly. Loose film flaps in wind and tears. Use wiggle wire or battens to secure it. Now, ventilation. Greenhouses overheat quickly in Africa. Without ventilation, temperatures can reach 45 degrees Celsius, which stops tomato and pepper production. You need roof vents and side vents. A simple design is a roll-up side curtain. You can open it manually in the morning and close it in the evening. For a 8m by 20m greenhouse, you need at least 20 percent of the floor area as vent area. That is 32 square meters. So, if you have two side vents of 20m by 1m each, that is 40 square meters. Good. Also, install a ridge vent at the top. Hot air rises and escapes. If you cannot afford automatic vents, use manual pulleys. Check vents daily. On hot days, open them by 8 AM. On cool days, open by 10 AM. Close by 4 PM to trap heat for the night. In very hot regions like northern Nigeria or Senegal, you may need a fan. A 50-watt exhaust fan can move enough air for a small greenhouse. Solar-powered fans are ideal. They cost 200 to 400 USD but save on electricity. Also, use shade nets. A 50 percent black shade net over the roof reduces temperature by 5 to 8 degrees. You can also paint the roof with whitewash (lime) to reflect sunlight. Now, cooling. Besides ventilation and shade, you can use evaporative cooling. This works best in dry heat. Install a pad of honeycomb material on one side and a fan on the opposite side. Water drips down the pad, and the fan pulls air through it. This cools the air by 5 to 10 degrees. However, it uses water, about 20 to 40 liters per hour. In humid areas, it is less effective. For most African smallholders, a combination of shade net, roll-up sides, and ridge vent is enough. Next, the floor. As mentioned, gravel with weed mat is good. But if you have disease problems, concrete is better. You can also use raised beds with plastic mulch. Mulch reduces weeds, conserves water, and keeps fruit clean. Black plastic mulch is cheap and effective. For tomatoes, use red mulch, which increases yields by 10 to 15 percent. Now, irrigation system. Drip irrigation is essential. You need a main line, sub-main, and drip lines. For a 8m by 20m greenhouse, you need about 10 drip lines of 20m each, spaced 80 cm apart. Each line has emitters every 30 cm. The cost is 150 to 300 USD. Install a filter and a fertilizer injector. A simple venturi injector costs 30 USD and lets you apply water-soluble fertilizers through the drip. This saves labor and improves nutrient use. Also, install a timer. A battery-operated timer costs 50 USD and ensures you never forget to irrigate. Now, climate management. Keep a daily log of temperature and humidity. Use a max-min thermometer (5 USD) and a hygrometer (10 USD). Ideal temperature for tomatoes is 20 to 27 degrees Celsius. For peppers, 20 to 25. For cucumbers, 22 to 28. If temperature exceeds 30, open vents and turn on fan. If humidity exceeds 80 percent, increase ventilation to prevent fungal diseases like powdery mildew and botrytis. If humidity is below 40 percent, mist the paths or use a fogger. Now, maintenance. Check the film monthly for tears. Patch with clear tape. Check bolts and wires for rust. Tighten loose parts. Clean the roof film every 2 weeks with a soft brush and water to remove dust. Dust reduces light by 20 to 30 percent. Also, check the insect net for holes. Repair with needle and thread. Keep the area around the greenhouse free of weeds, as they harbor pests. Now, business considerations. A well-built greenhouse costs more upfront but gives higher yields and lower pesticide costs. For example, a farmer in Uganda who invested 2,500 USD in a steel greenhouse with drip and netting harvested 3 times more tomatoes than in open field and sold at a premium because of fewer blemishes. The payback period was 1.5 years. If you use cheap bamboo, you may save 2,000 USD initially, but you will replace it every 2 years and lose crops when it collapses in a storm. That is a bad business decision. Also, consider government subsidies. Many African countries offer cost-sharing for greenhouse kits. Check with your local agriculture office. In Kenya, the National Agriculture and Rural Inclusive Growth Project supports greenhouse farming. In Nigeria, the Anchor Borrowers Programme provides loans. Now, let us summarize the steps. 1. Choose frame material based on budget and lifespan. 2. Install UV-treated film and insect net. 3. Ensure 20 percent vent area with roll-up sides and ridge vent. 4. Add shade net if temperatures exceed 30 degrees. 5. Install drip irrigation with filter and injector. 6. Keep daily climate records. 7. Maintain structure weekly. 8. Explore subsidies and loans. By following these steps, you will have a greenhouse that protects your crops and increases your profit. In the next lesson, we will cover seedling production and transplanting. But for now, review your current or planned greenhouse. Does it have enough ventilation? Is the film tight? Do you have a drip system? If not, make a plan to upgrade. Your vegetables will thank you with higher yields and better quality.

3. Lesson 3: Seedling Production and Transplanting for High Survival and Uniform Growth

Welcome to Lesson 3. You have a greenhouse and a water system. Now, you need healthy seedlings. Seedlings are the foundation of your crop. Weak seedlings lead to poor yields, disease, and wasted money. In this lesson, you will learn how to produce strong seedlings in a nursery, harden them, and transplant them correctly. This is practical, Africa-focused, and business-oriented. First, choose the right varieties. For greenhouse production in Africa, select hybrid varieties that are disease-resistant and high-yielding. For tomatoes, look for varieties resistant to tomato yellow leaf curl virus, bacterial wilt, and fusarium. Examples include Padma, Tylka, and Anna. For peppers, use varieties like California Wonder or local hybrids resistant to bacterial spot. For cucumbers, use parthenocarpic varieties that do not need pollination, such as Beit Alpha. Buy certified seed from reputable suppliers. Cheap seed from informal markets often has low germination and carries diseases. A 10-gram packet of hybrid tomato seed costs 20 to 50 USD but gives you 2,000 to 3,000 seedlings. That is enough for 0.2 hectares. The investment is worth it. Next, prepare the nursery. You can use a raised nursery bed or seedling trays. Trays are better because they reduce root damage and disease. Use 200-cell trays for tomatoes and peppers, and 50-cell trays for cucumbers. Fill the trays with a sterile growing medium. You can make your own mix: 60 percent cocopeat, 30 percent compost, and 10 percent perlite. Or use 50 percent topsoil, 40 percent compost, and 10 percent sand. Sterilize the mix by solarizing it under clear plastic for 2 weeks. Do not use soil from a field that grew tomatoes or peppers, as it may contain wilt pathogens. If you use a nursery bed, make it 1 meter wide and 20 cm high. Add 5 kg of manure per square meter and 100 grams of triple superphosphate. Cover with a thin layer of sterile soil. Now, sowing. Sow seeds in the trays, one seed per cell. For tomatoes, sow 0.5 cm deep. For peppers, 1 cm. For cucumbers, 2 cm. Water gently with a fine rose can. Keep the nursery moist but not waterlogged. Cover with a clear plastic sheet or a shade net to maintain humidity and temperature. For tomatoes, ideal germination temperature is 20 to 25 degrees Celsius. For peppers, 25 to 30. For cucumbers, 25 to 30. If it is too cold, use a small heater or place trays in a warm spot. If too hot, use shade net. Germination takes 5 to 10 days. Once seedlings emerge, remove the cover and provide light. If you use a greenhouse nursery, ensure good ventilation. Now, seedling management. Water daily, preferably in the morning. Avoid overhead watering in the evening, as it promotes damping-off disease. Use a fungicide drench if you see damping-off. A cheap option is to mix 1 gram of metalaxyl per liter of water and drench the trays. Also, fertilize. Start with a weak solution of water-soluble fertilizer (NPK 20-20-20) at 0.5 grams per liter, one week after emergence. Apply every 3 days. Increase to 1 gram per liter after 2 weeks. Do not over-fertilize, as it burns roots. For organic growers, use compost tea or liquid seaweed. Now, hardening. Hardening is the process of adapting seedlings to outdoor conditions. Start 7 days before transplanting. Reduce watering by 30 percent. Increase ventilation. If seedlings are in a shaded nursery, expose them to more sunlight gradually. For the last 2 days, do not water at all. This makes the stems tough and reduces transplant shock. Also, spray with a weak solution of calcium nitrate (2 grams per liter) to prevent blossom end rot later. Now, transplanting. Transplant when seedlings have 4 to 6 true leaves. For tomatoes, this is 3 to 4 weeks after sowing. For peppers, 4 to 5 weeks. For cucumbers, 2 to 3 weeks. Transplant in the late afternoon or on a cloudy day to reduce stress. Water the seedlings thoroughly 2 hours before transplanting. This helps the root ball hold together. Make a hole in the bed or grow bag. Place the seedling so that the root ball is level with the soil. For tomatoes, you can bury the stem up to the first leaves, as they will develop roots. For peppers and cucumbers, do not bury too deep. Firm the soil around the roots. Water immediately with a starter solution. A good starter solution is 1 gram of NPK 20-20-20 per liter of water. Apply 200 ml per seedling. This gives a quick boost. Now, spacing. Proper spacing reduces disease and increases yield. For tomatoes in a greenhouse, use 60 cm between plants and 80 cm between rows. That gives about 2 plants per square meter. For peppers, 45 cm by 60 cm. For cucumbers, 40 cm by 100 cm. If you use grow bags, place two plants per bag for tomatoes, one for cucumbers. Do not overcrowd. Overcrowding reduces airflow and light, leading to fungal diseases and small fruit. Now, after transplanting. Keep the soil moist for the first week. If the sun is intense, use a shade net for 3 days. Check for transplant shock: wilting, yellowing, or stunted growth. If shock occurs, water with a weak fertilizer solution and shade. Also, watch for pests. Cutworms can cut seedlings at the base. Use a collar of cardboard or a toothpick around the stem. Aphids and whiteflies can transmit viruses. Use yellow sticky traps (10 per greenhouse) and neem oil spray. Now, business tips. Producing your own seedlings saves money. A tray of 200 tomato seedlings costs 5 to 10 USD to produce, but buying them costs 20 to 40 USD. Also, you can sell extra seedlings to other farmers. A small nursery business can add 20 to 30 percent to your income. Keep records of germination rates and seedling vigor. If a variety performs poorly, do not use it again. Also, time your transplanting to match market demand. For example, in Kenya, tomatoes planted in January fetch high prices in April. In Nigeria, peppers planted in March get good prices in June. Now, common mistakes. 1. Using old seed. Always check the expiry date. 2. Overwatering. This causes damping-off. 3. Not hardening. Seedlings die after transplanting. 4. Planting too deep or too shallow. 5. Skipping starter solution. 6. Ignoring pests in the nursery. 7. Transplanting in hot midday. Avoid these. Now, let us summarize. Choose hybrid seed. Use sterile media. Sow correctly. Manage water, fertilizer, and pests. Harden for 7 days. Transplant with starter solution. Space properly. Monitor after transplanting. By following these steps, you will get 95 percent survival and uniform growth. That means higher yields and better prices. In the next lesson, we will cover crop nutrition and irrigation management. But for now, practice making your own seedling mix and sowing a small trial. Measure your germination rate. If it is below 85 percent, adjust your method. Your profit starts with a strong seedling.

4. Lesson 4: Crop Nutrition and Irrigation Management for Maximum Yield and Quality

Welcome to Lesson 4. Your seedlings are in the ground. Now, you must feed and water them correctly. This lesson covers crop nutrition and irrigation management for greenhouse vegetables in Africa. Poor nutrition and irregular watering are the top reasons for low yields and poor fruit quality. By the end, you will know how to create a fertigation schedule, choose fertilizers, and manage water efficiently. This is a business lesson: every gram of fertilizer and every liter of water must give a return. First, understand plant needs. Greenhouse tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers need 16 essential nutrients. The main ones are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), and magnesium (Mg). Nitrogen drives leaf growth. Phosphorus supports roots and flowers. Potassium improves fruit size, color, and shelf life. Calcium prevents blossom end rot. Magnesium is needed for chlorophyll. In African soils, nitrogen and phosphorus are often low. Potassium may be adequate but is depleted by intensive farming. So, you must fertilize. The best method is fertigation: applying water-soluble fertilizers through the drip system. This gives nutrients directly to the roots and saves 30 to 50 percent compared to broadcasting. For a 8m by 20m greenhouse with 160 tomato plants, you need about 1 to 2 kg of NPK 20-20-20 per week during vegetative growth, and 2 to 3 kg per week during fruiting. But do not guess. Use a soil test or leaf analysis. A soil test costs 20 to 50 USD and tells you exactly what to add. That is a good investment. Now, let us build a simple fertigation schedule for tomatoes. Week 1 after transplant: apply 0.5 grams of NPK 20-20-20 per liter, 200 ml per plant, every 3 days. Week 2 to 4: increase to 1 gram per liter, 500 ml per plant, every 2 days. Week 5 to 8 (flowering): switch to NPK 15-15-30 or 12-12-36. Apply 1.5 grams per liter, 700 ml per plant, every 2 days. Also add calcium nitrate at 1 gram per liter once a week to prevent blossom end rot. Week 9 to 12 (fruiting): use NPK 10-10-40 or 15-5-30. Apply 2 grams per liter, 1 liter per plant, every day. Also add magnesium sulfate (Epsom salt) at 0.5 grams per liter once a week. For peppers, reduce nitrogen slightly and increase potassium. For cucumbers, use a balanced formula with more calcium. Now, organic options. If you want to grow organic, you can use compost tea, vermicompost, and liquid seaweed. However, organic fertilizers are less concentrated, so you need more volume. A good organic schedule: apply 2 liters of compost tea per plant per week, plus 100 grams of rock phosphate per square meter at planting. Also, use wood ash for potassium. But be careful: wood ash raises pH. Do not use more than 100 grams per square meter. Now, irrigation. Greenhouse vegetables need consistent moisture. Too dry, and plants wilt and drop flowers. Too wet, and roots rot and diseases spread. The best method is drip irrigation with a timer. For tomatoes, apply 2 to 4 liters per plant per day, depending on temperature and growth stage. In hot weather (above 30 degrees), increase to 5 liters. In cool weather (below 20 degrees), reduce to 1.5 liters. For peppers, 1.5 to 3 liters. For cucumbers, 3 to 5 liters. Always water in the morning. This allows foliage to dry before evening, reducing fungal diseases. Also, check soil moisture. Use a simple tensiometer (30 USD) or just stick your finger 5 cm into the soil. If it is dry, water. If it is soggy, skip a day. Now, water quality. Test your water for salinity (EC) and pH. Ideal EC is 0.5 to 1.5 mS/cm. If EC is above 2.0, you may need to leach the soil with extra water. Ideal pH is 5.5 to 6.5. If pH is too high, add acid (like citric acid) to the irrigation water. If too low, add potassium hydroxide. But be careful. Now, mulching. Mulch reduces evaporation by 50 percent and suppresses weeds. Use black plastic mulch for tomatoes and peppers. For cucumbers, use straw mulch. Mulch also keeps fruit clean and reduces rot. Now, monitoring. Keep a weekly log of fertilizer applied, water used, and plant growth. Measure plant height, leaf color, and fruit size. If leaves are yellow, you need more nitrogen. If leaf edges are brown, you need more potassium. If fruit has a black bottom, you need more calcium. If leaves are purple, you need more phosphorus. Adjust your fertigation accordingly. Now, common problems and solutions. 1. Blossom end rot: caused by calcium deficiency and irregular watering. Solution: add calcium nitrate and maintain even moisture. 2. Fruit cracking: caused by uneven watering. Solution: water consistently. 3. Leaf curl: caused by high temperature or virus. Solution: increase ventilation and control whiteflies. 4. Stunted growth: caused by poor nutrition or root damage. Solution: check EC and pH, and apply a balanced fertilizer. 5. Wilting: caused by root rot or bacterial wilt. Solution: remove affected plants and solarize soil. Now, business tips. Fertilizer is expensive. Buy in bulk with other farmers to get discounts. Store in a dry place. Do not mix calcium with phosphate or sulfate fertilizers, as they precipitate and clog drippers. Always add fertilizers to water, not water to fertilizers. Use a fertilizer injector or a simple venturi. Also, keep a budget. For a 8m by 20m greenhouse, you will spend about 100 to 200 USD per season on fertilizers and 50 to 100 USD on water (if you pay for it). Your yield should be 1,500 to 2,500 kg of tomatoes. At 1 USD per kg, that is 1,500 to 2,500 USD. So, fertilizer is only 5 to 10 percent of revenue. Do not skimp. Now, let us summarize. Test your soil and water. Use fertigation with a schedule. Adjust for growth stage. Water consistently in the morning. Mulch. Monitor plants weekly. Fix problems early. By doing this, you will get uniform, high-quality fruit that sells for a premium. In the next lesson, we will cover pest and disease management. But for now, create a fertigation calendar for your next crop. Write down the dates and amounts. Stick to it. Your plants will reward you with a heavy harvest.

5. Lesson 5: Integrated Pest and Disease Management for Greenhouse Vegetables

Welcome to Lesson 5, the final lesson in Module 5. You have a greenhouse, healthy seedlings, and a fertigation plan. Now, you must protect your crop from pests and diseases. In Africa, greenhouse vegetables face constant threats: whiteflies, aphids, thrips, spider mites, and diseases like powdery mildew, bacterial wilt, and tomato yellow leaf curl virus. If you do not manage them, you can lose 50 to 100 percent of your crop. This lesson teaches integrated pest management (IPM). IPM combines cultural, biological, and chemical methods to control pests safely and economically. By the end, you will know how to scout, prevent, and treat problems. First, prevention is the cheapest and most effective strategy. Keep your greenhouse clean. Remove weeds inside and around the structure. Weeds host whiteflies and aphids. Use a weed mat or gravel floor. Do not allow workers to enter with dirty boots or tools. Use a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance. Also, use insect netting on all vents and doors. A 40-mesh net keeps out most pests. If you find a hole, repair it immediately. Next, crop rotation. Do not grow tomatoes, peppers, or cucumbers in the same soil season after season. Rotate with cereals like maize or sorghum, or with legumes like beans. This breaks disease cycles. If you cannot rotate, solarize the soil. Cover the soil with clear plastic for 4 to 6 weeks during the hot season. This kills many pathogens and nematodes. Now, scouting. Walk through your greenhouse twice a week. Look at the underside of leaves, growing tips, and flowers. Use a hand lens (10x) to see small pests. Check for: whiteflies (small white flies that fly up when disturbed), aphids (small green or black insects clustered on new growth), thrips (tiny slender insects that cause silvery streaks), spider mites (tiny red or brown dots with fine webbing), and diseases like powdery mildew (white powdery spots on leaves), downy mildew (yellow patches on top, gray fuzz below), and bacterial wilt (sudden wilting of green plants). Keep a record of what you find and where. Now, cultural controls. Remove and destroy infected plants immediately. Do not compost them. Burn or bury them. Prune lower leaves of tomatoes to improve airflow. Stake plants to keep fruit off the ground. Avoid overhead watering. Use drip irrigation. Keep humidity below 80 percent by ventilating. If you see whiteflies, use yellow sticky traps. Place 10 to 20 traps per greenhouse. They cost 1 to 2 USD each and last 2 to 3 weeks. Also, use blue sticky traps for thrips. Now, biological controls. You can buy beneficial insects like Encarsia formosa for whiteflies, and Phytoseiulus persimilis for spider mites. These are available in Kenya, South Africa, and Egypt. They cost 20 to 50 USD per introduction. They work best when pest populations are low. Also, use neem oil, which is available in most African countries. Mix 5 ml of neem oil per liter of water with a few drops of soap. Spray in the evening to avoid leaf burn. Neem controls aphids, whiteflies, and thrips. Another option is Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) for caterpillars. Now, chemical controls. Use chemicals only as a last resort. Choose pesticides that are approved for vegetables and have a short pre-harvest interval. Always read the label. Wear protective clothing. Do not spray in the morning when bees are active. Spray in the evening. Rotate chemicals to prevent resistance. For whiteflies, use imidacloprid or thiamethoxam. For aphids, use dimethoate or pirimicarb. For thrips, use spinosad or abamectin. For spider mites, use abamectin or bifenazate. For powdery mildew, use sulfur or potassium bicarbonate. For bacterial wilt, there is no cure. Remove infected plants and solarize soil. For viral diseases, control the vector (whiteflies or aphids). Remember, chemicals are expensive and can harm beneficial insects. Use them sparingly. Now, let us build an IPM plan for tomatoes. Week 1: Scout. If no pests, do nothing. Week 2: Scout. If whiteflies appear, put yellow sticky traps. If more than 5 whiteflies per trap per week, spray neem oil. Week 3: Scout. If aphids appear, spray soapy water (5 ml soap per liter). If severe, use imidacloprid. Week 4: Scout. If powdery mildew appears, spray potassium bicarbonate (5 grams per liter). Week 5: Scout

Why Precision Matters for Profit

Greenhouse vegetable production in Africa operates on thin margins. A farmer in Kenya, Nigeria, or South Africa who misjudges irrigation volume by 20% can lose an entire tomato crop to blossom-end rot. A grower who applies the wrong pesticide concentration can destroy beneficial insects, trigger resistance, and lose market access. The difference between a profitable season and a loss often comes down to knowing the right number at the right time.

Chemicals used in greenhouse vegetable production can be hazardous to human health and can leave illegal residues on food. Follow these rules without exception:

  • Always wear gloves, a face mask, long sleeves, and boots when mixing or applying any pesticide â even "organic" products.
  • Never apply a pesticide closer to harvest than the pre-harvest interval (PHI) stated on the label. This is a legal requirement and a food safety issue.
  • Never mix two or more pesticides unless the label explicitly permits tank mixing.
  • Store all chemicals in a locked cabinet, away from food, feed, and children.
  • Dispose of empty containers by triple-rinsing and puncturing, then follow local regulations â never burn or dump near water sources.
  • If you feel dizzy, nauseous, or have blurred vision after spraying, stop immediately, move to fresh air, and seek medical help. Bring the product label with you.

Source: WHO (2019), Recommended Classification of Pesticides by Hazard and Guidelines to Classification; FAO/WHO (2016), International Code of Conduct on Pesticide Management.
