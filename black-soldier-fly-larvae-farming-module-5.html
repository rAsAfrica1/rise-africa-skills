1. Substrate Selection and Preparation

Black soldier fly larvae eat almost any organic waste, but for good growth you need the right mix. In Africa, common substrates include market waste (fruit and vegetable peels), brewery waste, chicken manure, pig manure, and food scraps from hotels. The best substrate is a mix: 60% fruit and vegetable waste, 30% manure, and 10% dry matter like sawdust or rice husks. This gives the right balance of moisture, protein, and fibre. Avoid oily waste, meat, and bones because they smell bad and attract pests. Also avoid citrus peels in large amountsthey slow larval growth.

Before feeding, prepare the substrate. Chop large pieces into smaller bits (25 cm) so larvae can eat faster. If using fresh manure, let it dry in the sun for 12 days to reduce moisture and kill harmful bacteria. For fruit and vegetable waste, remove plastic, glass, and stones. Mix everything in a large basin or on a tarpaulin. Add water if the mix is too dryit should feel like a wrung-out sponge. A simple test: squeeze a handful; only a few drops should come out. If it drips, add dry material like sawdust or crushed maize cobs.

Fermentation is optional but helps. You can let the mixed substrate sit in a covered bucket for 12 days. This starts microbial breakdown and makes it more digestible for larvae. But don't let it rotif it smells like rotten eggs, it's too far gone. In hot African climates, ferment for only 24 hours. For small-scale farmers, you can feed fresh substrate directly, but fermentation reduces odours and flies.

Always wear gloves when handling manure. Wash your hands and tools after. Store prepared substrate in a shaded area, covered with a tarp to keep flies and rain out. Use within 2 days to prevent mould. A good substrate should have a pH between 6 and 8. You can test with a cheap pH strip from a pharmacy. If too acidic (below 6), add a little wood ash; if too alkaline (above 8), add more fruit waste. Proper preparation is the first step to healthy larvae.

2. Feeding Rates and Frequency

Feeding your larvae correctly is the difference between a good harvest and a failed one. The rule of thumb: larvae eat about 23 times their own body weight per day. But you don't feed based on their weight; you feed based on the surface area of your bin and the age of the larvae. For a standard 1 m rearing bin, start with 500 g of substrate per day for the first week after hatching. Increase to 1 kg per day in the second week, and up to 2 kg per day in the third and fourth weeks. If you feed too little, larvae crawl away looking for food. If you feed too much, the substrate heats up, smells, and kills larvae.

Feed once a day, preferably in the morning when it's cool. In hot weather (above 30C), feed in the late afternoon instead. Spread the substrate evenly over the surfacedon't dump it in one corner. Larvae need to access it easily. If you see larvae clustering on top of the feed, they are hungry; add a little more next time. If you see leftover feed after 24 hours, reduce the amount. A simple rule: feed only what they can finish in 24 hours. For a 1 m bin with 10,000 larvae (about 100 g of eggs), start with 500 g per day and adjust.

You can also feed every two days, but then double the amount. However, daily feeding is better because it keeps moisture and temperature stable. In rainy season, feed less because substrate stays wet longer. In dry season, feed more but add water. Always keep a record: date, amount fed, and how much is left. This helps you learn your bin's needs. If you are feeding manure, it's already wet, so don't add extra water. For fruit waste, you may need to add water.

Never feed mouldy or rotting substrateit can make larvae sick. If you see black spots or a sour smell, remove that feed immediately. Also, don't feed more than 5 cm deep; larvae need air. If the layer is too thick, they suffocate. For large farms, use a wheelbarrow to measure feed. A wheelbarrow holds about 50 kg. So for 100 m of bins, you might feed 50100 kg per day. Start small and scale up as you learn.

3. Optimal Larval Density

Larval density means how many larvae you keep in a given space. If you put too many, they compete for food and space, grow slowly, and many die. If you put too few, you waste space and substrate. The ideal density for black soldier fly larvae is 12 larvae per square centimetre. For a 1 m bin, that means 10,00020,000 larvae. A good starting point for a beginner is 10,000 larvae per square metre. This is roughly the number that hatches from 10 grams of eggs. So if you buy 10 g of eggs, you can fill one 1 m bin.

How do you know your density? You can estimate by weighing. One thousand mature larvae weigh about 100150 g. So if you have 1 kg of larvae, that's roughly 7,00010,000 larvae. For a 1 m bin, aim for 11.5 kg of larvae at the start. As they grow, they get heavier, so you may need to split into two bins. A simple rule: when larvae cover the entire surface and you can't see the substrate, it's time to split. Also, if you see larvae crawling up the walls trying to escape, they are too crowded.

Density affects growth. At low density (0.5 larvae/cm), larvae grow faster and bigger, but you use more bins. At high density (3 larvae/cm), growth slows and mortality increases. For maximum profit, keep density at 1.5 larvae/cm. This gives a good balance. In Africa, where space can be limited, many farmers use stacked bins. If you stack, make sure each bin has enough depth (at least 15 cm) and ventilation. Don't stack more than 4 bins high.

To manage density, you can split bins when larvae are 10 days old. Use a scoop to move half the larvae and substrate to a new bin. Always use clean tools. If you are raising larvae for chicken feed, you can keep higher density (2 larvae/cm) because you want many small larvae. For fish feed, you want larger larvae, so keep lower density (1 larvae/cm). Monitor weekly: if larvae look small and pale, they are too crowded. Increase feed and split. If they are large and dark, density is good.

4. Monitoring Larval Growth

Monitoring growth helps you catch problems early and harvest at the right time. You need to check your larvae at least every two days. Start by looking at the bin: are larvae active? Healthy larvae are light brown to cream, shiny, and move quickly. If they are dark, sluggish, or not moving, something is wrong. Use a small spoon to dig into the substrate and find larvae. Measure 10 larvae with a ruler. Record their length. A well-fed larva grows from 1 mm at hatching to 2025 mm in 1418 days. If after 10 days they are less than 10 mm, they are not growing well.

Weigh a sample. Take 100 larvae and weigh them on a kitchen scale. At day 10, 100 larvae should weigh about 57 g. At day 15, they should weigh 1520 g. If they weigh less, increase feeding or check temperature. Keep a notebook with dates, average length, and weight. This is your growth chart. Also, check for pupae. When larvae turn black and hard, they are pupating. That means they are ready to harvest or let them become flies. For feed, harvest when they are white and plump, before they turn black.

Check the substrate. If it looks dry, add water. If it smells bad, remove old feed. If you see mould, reduce moisture and add dry material. Also, look for pests: ants, cockroaches, or rats. Ants can carry away larvae. Put a barrier of ash or oil around the bin legs. For rats, use traps or cover bins with wire mesh. Record any pest sightings. Temperature is key: larvae grow best at 2530C. If it's too hot (above 35C), move bins to shade and add water. If too cold (below 20C), cover bins with a blanket or move to a greenhouse.

At day 1418, larvae will be ready to harvest. You can tell because they stop eating and start crawling away to find a dry place to pupate. This is called 'self-harvesting'. You can set up a ramp with a bucket at the end; larvae will crawl up and fall in. Weigh your harvest. A good yield is 1520% of the substrate weight. So 10 kg of feed should give 1.52 kg of larvae. If you get less, review your feeding and density. Keep records for each batch to improve.

5. Managing Moisture in Rearing Bins

Moisture is the most critical factor in larval rearing. Larvae need a moist environmentabout 6070% moisture. If it's too dry, they dry out and die. If it's too wet, the substrate becomes anaerobic, smells, and breeds harmful bacteria. The substrate should feel like a wrung-out sponge. To test, squeeze a handful: only a few drops of water should come out. If it drips, it's too wet. If it crumbles, it's too dry. In Africa, where temperatures can be high, moisture evaporates quickly, so you may need to add water daily.

How to add water? Use a watering can with a fine rose, or a spray bottle. Sprinkle evenly over the surface. Do not pour water directlyit can drown larvae. For a 1 m bin, add 0.51 litre of water if the substrate is dry. In hot dry season, you might need 2 litres per day. In rainy season, you may not need to add water at all, but cover bins to prevent rain from flooding them. If you use fresh fruit and vegetable waste, it already has high water content (8090%), so you may not need extra water. Manure is also wet. Dry substrates like sawdust or rice husks need more water.

Drainage is important. Drill small holes (5 mm) in the bottom of your bin, or tilt the bin so excess water can run out. Place a tray underneath to catch leachate. Leachate is a dark liquid that smells; it can be used as liquid fertiliser for plants, diluted 1:10 with water. If you see standing water in the bin, add dry material like sawdust or crushed charcoal. Also, increase ventilation by stirring the substrate gently with a stick. This helps air get in and reduces smell.

Check moisture daily. Pick up a handful of substrate from different spots. If it's too dry, add water. If too wet, add dry material and mix. In very humid areas, use raised bins and keep them in shade. In dry areas, keep bins in shade and cover with a wet sack. Never use chlorinated waterit kills beneficial microbes. Use rainwater, well water, or let tap water sit for 24 hours. If you see white mould, it's too wet and not enough air. Remove mouldy patches and add dry material. With good moisture, larvae will grow fast and healthy.

6. Common Larval Health Issues

Even with good care, larvae can get sick. The most common problem is black soldier fly larvae turning black and dying. This is usually caused by too much moisture, lack of oxygen, or bacterial infection. If you see many black larvae, remove them immediately. Then check moisture: if too wet, add dry material and stir. Also, reduce feeding for a day. If the problem continues, clean the bin with a mild bleach solution (1 tablespoon per litre of water), rinse well, and start fresh with new substrate.

Another issue is larvae crawling out of the bin. This means they are stressed. Causes include too much heat, too little food, or overcrowding. Check temperature: if above 35C, move bins to shade and add cool water. If food is scarce, increase feeding. If overcrowded, split the bin. Also, check for pests like ants. Ants can attack larvae. Put a barrier of wood ash or used engine oil around the bin legs. If you see small flies or maggots of other flies, your substrate is too wet or rotting. Remove old feed and add fresh.

Mould is a common problem. White or green mould grows on wet substrate. It can kill larvae. To prevent, keep moisture at 6070% and stir the substrate daily. If mould appears, remove the mouldy part and add dry material like sawdust. You can also add a little lime (calcium carbonate) to raise pH and reduce mould. But don't overdo it1 teaspoon per kg of substrate. If larvae are pale and soft, they may have a nutrient deficiency. Add more protein-rich substrate like manure or fish waste (but fish waste smells, so use sparingly).

Viral and fungal diseases are rare but possible. If you see larvae with white spots or fuzzy growth, isolate that bin and destroy the larvae. Disinfect tools with bleach. Always wash hands after handling. To keep larvae healthy, maintain clean bins, proper moisture, and good ventilation. Rotate bins: after harvest, clean with soap and water, then sun-dry for a day. Use new substrate each cycle. If you buy eggs, get them from a reputable supplier. Sick larvae can wipe out a whole batch, so act fast. Keep records of any disease outbreak and what you did. That way you learn for next time.

<h3>Substrate Selection and Preparation</h3>

<p>Your substrate determines your larval growth rate, your final protein content, and your production cost. In Africa, the most commercially viable substrates are market waste (fruit and vegetable), brewery waste, abattoir waste, and cassava peels. Each has different moisture, pH, and nutrient profiles.</p>

<p>The rule is simple: <strong>if it was once food, it can feed larvae</strong>. But not all substrates are equal. Fruit and vegetable waste produces fast growth but lower protein. Brewery waste and abattoir waste produce slower growth but higher protein. The best commercial approach is to blend substrates to balance cost, availability, and protein output.</p>

<p>Preparation matters as much as selection. Chop or blend substrate to 2-5 cm pieces. This increases surface area and speeds up consumption. For high-fibre substrates like cassava peels, ferment for 24-48 hours with a lactic acid bacteria starter or simply allow natural fermentation. This breaks down cellulose and makes nutrients more accessible to larvae.</p>

<p>Overfeeding is the most common mistake among new producers. Excess substrate heats up, becomes anaerobic, and kills larvae. Underfeeding slows growth and extends your cycle, increasing labour cost per kilogram.</p>

<h3>Monitoring Larval Growth</h3>

<p>You cannot manage what you do not measure. Weigh a sample of 50 larvae every 3 days. Record average weight. Healthy larvae should gain 15-25% body weight per day during peak growth. If weight gain drops below 10%, check moisture, temperature, and substrate quality.</p>

<p>Also monitor bin temperature. It should stay between 26-30C. Above 35C, larvae stop feeding and may die. Below 20C, growth slows dramatically. In hot African climates, shade your bins and avoid direct sunlight. In cooler highland areas, use black bins to absorb heat.</p>

<h3>Managing Moisture in Rearing Bins</h3>

<p>Moisture is the silent killer in BSF production. Too dry, and larvae cannot feed. Too wet, and you get anaerobic conditions, ammonia, and death.</p>

<p>The target is <strong>60-70% moisture</strong> in the substrate. Test by squeezing a handful. It should hold together but not drip. If it drips, add dry substrate (sawdust, rice husk, or dry frass). If it crumbles, add water or fresh wet substrate.</p>

<p>Drainage is critical. Every bin needs holes at the bottom or a slight slope to allow excess leachate to escape. In Africa, where humidity can be high during rainy season, increase drainage and reduce substrate moisture by 5-10%.</p>

<h3>Common Larval Health Issues</h3>

<p><strong>Black soldier fly larvae are hardy</strong>, but they are not invincible. The most common problems in African production systems are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Ammonia toxicity:</strong> Caused by anaerobic substrate. Symptoms include dark colour, sluggish movement, and death. Fix by draining, aerating, and adding dry substrate.</li>
<li><strong>Fungal infection:</strong> White or green mould on substrate. Caused by excess moisture and poor ventilation. Remove affected substrate and increase airflow.</li>
<li><strong>Parasitic wasps:</strong> Small wasps that lay eggs in larvae. Common in open systems. Use fine mesh screens over bins.</li>
<li><strong>Bacterial soft rot:</strong> Larvae become soft, dark, and die rapidly. Caused by <em>Serratia</em> or <em>Pseudomonas</em>. Remove affected larvae and disinfect bins.</li>
<li><strong>Nutritional deficiency:</strong> Slow growth, pale colour. Usually caused by low-protein substrate. Blend with brewery waste or abattoir waste.</li>
</ul>

<li><strong>Substrate:</strong> Chop to 2-5 cm, ferment high-fibre material 24-48 hours, never use fresh manure without treatment.</li>
<li><strong>Moisture:</strong> Target 60-70%. Squeeze test holds together, no drip. Adjust with dry substrate or water.</li>
<li><strong>Density:</strong> 2-5 larvae/cm depending on scale. Never exceed 7 larvae/cm.</li>
<li><strong>Feeding:</strong> Feed twice daily during peak growth. Remove uneaten substrate after 24 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Temperature:</strong> Keep bins at 26-30C. Shade in hot climates, insulate in cool highlands.</li>
<li><strong>Monitoring:</strong> Weigh 50 larvae every 3 days. Expect 15-25% daily weight gain.</li>
<li><strong>Drainage:</strong> Every bin must drain. No standing leachate.</li>
<li><strong>Hygiene:</strong> Wear gloves. Wash hands. Disinfect bins between cycles.</li>
</ol>

<p><strong>Bottom line:</strong> The producers who make money in BSF are the ones who treat it as a manufacturing process, not a hobby. Control your inputs, measure your outputs, and never stop improving your substrate mix and density management. The market for insect protein in Africa is growing. Your job is to produce consistently, at low cost, at scale.</p>
