1. Using a Manual or Motorised Grinder to Make Tomato Puree

Start with fully ripe, red tomatoes. Wash them in clean water, then remove the green stem and any bruised or rotten spots. You can leave the skin and seeds if you will sieve later, but for a smooth paste, many processors blanch the tomatoes first: put them in boiling water for 2 minutes, then immediately into cold water. The skins will slip off easily. Cut the tomatoes into quarters.

If you are using a manual grinder, clamp it firmly to a table. Feed the tomato pieces into the hopper a few at a time. Turn the handle steadily. The puree will come out through the holes, while skins and seeds stay behind if you use a fine screen. For a motorised grinder, plug it in and switch on. Feed tomatoes gradually; do not overload. Most small motors handle 23 kg per minute. If the machine slows, stop and remove some pulp.

After grinding, you may want an even smoother puree. Pass the pulp through a sieve or a muslin cloth. This removes remaining seeds and skin bits. Collect the puree in a clean bucket or pot. Work in small batches: 5 kg of tomatoes yields about 4 litres of puree, depending on variety and ripeness.

Keep everything clean. Wash the grinder parts with warm soapy water after each use, then rinse and dry. Dirty equipment causes spoilage. If you are processing many tomatoes, grind in the morning when it is cool. The puree should be used immediately or cooked down right away. Do not let it sit for hours, as it will ferment. With practice, you can grind 20 kg of tomatoes in under an hour using a motorised machine.

2. Cooking Tomato Puree to Remove Water and Thicken

Pour your fresh tomato puree into a heavy-bottomed pot. Stainless steel or enamel is best; aluminium can give a metallic taste. Do not fill the pot more than halfway, because the puree will splatter as it boils. Place on a medium fire. Stir often with a long wooden spoon to prevent burning at the bottom. As it heats, water evaporates. You will see steam rising and the volume slowly dropping.

After about 30 minutes of simmering, the puree will start to thicken. Reduce the fire to low. Continue stirring every few minutes. The goal is to remove enough water so that the paste holds its shape. For every 10 litres of puree, you want to end up with about 3 to 4 litres of thick paste. This takes 1.5 to 2 hours on a steady low fire. If you rush with high heat, the bottom will scorch and ruin the taste.

To test if it is thick enough, dip a spoon into the paste and turn it over. If the paste coats the spoon and does not run off quickly, it is close. Another sign is when you can draw a line through the paste in the pot and it stays open for a second or two. At this stage, the paste is ready for preservatives or for hot filling.

Remember that cooking also sterilises the paste. Keep a lid partly on to prevent splashing but allow steam to escape. If you are cooking outdoors, use a windbreak to keep the fire steady. Never leave the pot unattended. Stir, stir, stir. With practice, you will learn the right consistency by sight and feel. When done, the paste should be thick, glossy, and deep red.

3. Adding Preservatives: Salt, Sugar, and Natural Acids

Preservatives are essential to keep your tomato paste from spoiling. The three main ones are salt, sugar, and natural acids like lemon juice or vinegar. Add them after the puree has cooked down and thickened, but while it is still hot. For every 4 litres of thick paste, add 2 tablespoons of salt (about 40 grams). Salt draws out moisture and discourages mould and bacteria. Do not add too much or the paste will be too salty to use in cooking.

Sugar balances the acidity and improves flavour. Add 1 tablespoon of sugar (about 15 grams) for the same 4 litres. Some people prefer a sweeter paste, but too much sugar can cause browning during storage. Natural acids are the most important preservative. Lemon juice or vinegar lowers the pH, making it hard for spoilage organisms to grow. Add 4 tablespoons of lemon juice (about 60 ml) or 3 tablespoons of white vinegar (about 45 ml) per 4 litres. Stir well.

You can also use citric acid powder if you have it: 1 teaspoon per 4 litres. Always taste after adding. The paste should taste slightly tangy, not sour. If you are selling, keep a consistent recipe so customers trust your product. Write down your measurements.

After adding preservatives, simmer for another 5 minutes to blend the flavours and dissolve the salt and sugar. Then proceed to hot filling. Never add preservatives to cold paste; they will not mix properly. Also, do not use artificial preservatives like sodium benzoate unless you are trained and licensed. Natural acids and salt are safe, cheap, and effective for small-scale production.

4. Testing Consistency: The Spoon and Plate Test

Before you fill your jars, you must check that your tomato paste is thick enough. The spoon test is simple. Dip a clean, dry spoon into the hot paste. Lift it out and turn it upside down. If the paste sticks to the spoon and does not drip off within 5 seconds, it is thick enough. If it runs off like water, cook it longer. For a more precise test, use the plate test. Put a tablespoon of paste on a white plate. Tilt the plate. If the paste slides slowly and leaves a trail, it is ready. If it flows quickly, it is too thin.

Another method is the cold water test. Drop a small amount of paste into a glass of cold water. If it sinks and holds together, it is thick. If it disperses and clouds the water, it needs more cooking. Remember that paste thickens as it cools. So test while hot, but aim for a consistency that is slightly thinner than what you want in the final jar. When cooled, it will set more.

Why does consistency matter? Thin paste is harder to sell because customers feel cheated. It also spoils faster because extra water encourages bacteria. Thick paste has a longer shelf life and better taste. For commercial sales, aim for a paste that can be spread with a knife without running. If you are making sauce rather than paste, you can stop earlier, but for paste, the spoon and plate test is your guide.

Keep a small notebook to record how long you cooked each batch and how it tested. Over time, you will know exactly when to stop. Do not rely on time alone; the initial water content of tomatoes varies. Always test before filling.

5. Hot Filling into Jars, Bottles, or Sachets

Hot filling is the secret to long-lasting tomato paste. The paste must be filled into containers while it is still boiling hot, at least 85C. This kills any mould or bacteria inside the container and creates a vacuum seal as it cools. First, sterilise your jars, bottles, or sachets. For glass jars, wash them in hot soapy water, then boil them in a large pot for 10 minutes. Keep them hot until filling. For plastic bottles or sachets, wash with hot water and let them dry completely. Do not boil plastic unless it is heat-resistant.

Use a clean ladle or a funnel to fill the containers. Leave a small headspace: about 1 cm for jars and bottles, and 2 cm for sachets. This allows for expansion and helps form a vacuum. Fill one container at a time and seal immediately. For jars, screw on the lid tightly. For bottles, use a cap or cork. For sachets, use a heat sealer or tie the top tightly with a string. Work quickly so the paste does not cool down before sealing.

As the filled containers cool, you will hear a pop or see the lid pull down. That means the seal is good. If a lid does not pop, store that container in the fridge and use it first. After filling, wipe any spills with a clean cloth. Label each container with the date and batch number. Hot filling is not difficult, but it requires organisation. Set up your filling station before you start cooking. Have all containers ready, a ladle, a funnel, and clean cloths. Never fill cold paste into cold jars; it will spoil within days.

6. Sealing and Labelling Your Tomato Paste for Sale

Sealing is the final step to protect your tomato paste from air and contaminants. For glass jars, check that the lid is tight. You can also dip the lid in melted wax to create an extra seal, but this is optional. For bottles, use a crown cap or a screw cap. If you use corks, soak them in hot water before inserting to make a tight fit. For sachets, a heat sealer is best. If you do not have one, fold the top over and seal with a hot iron, or tie tightly with a rubber band and then tape. The goal is an airtight seal.

After sealing, let the containers cool completely. Then test the seal: press the centre of the lid. If it does not move, the vacuum is good. For sachets, squeeze gently; if air escapes, reseal. Store your sealed paste in a cool, dark place. A cupboard away from the stove is fine. Properly sealed and hot-filled paste can last 6 to 12 months. Once opened, keep it in the fridge and use within 2 weeks.

Labelling is important for selling. Your label must include: product name (Tomato Paste), net weight (e.g., 250g), ingredients (tomatoes, salt, sugar, lemon juice), your business name and contact, and the production date and expiry date. You can print labels at a local print shop or write neatly with a permanent marker. Use a clean, attractive design. In many African markets, customers trust a label that shows a phone number and a batch number.

Finally, package your containers in crates or boxes for transport. Do not stack heavy containers on top of sachets. Keep records of how many you made and sold. Good sealing and labelling turn a homemade paste into a professional product that can be sold in shops, markets, and to restaurants.

<h2>Practical Reference: Processing Tomatoes into Paste</h2>

<p>Tomato paste is one of the highest-value ways to turn a glut of fresh tomatoes into a product that travels, stores, and sells. In Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, and across East and West Africa, demand for tomato paste is steady year-round even when fresh tomato prices crash during harvest. This lesson gives you the practical steps, numbers, and checks to produce paste that is safe, consistent, and sellable, using tools you can source locally: a manual or motorised grinder, a cooking pot, jars, bottles, or sachets, and simple preservatives.</p>

<p><strong>Business first:</strong> your margin comes from three things buying tomatoes at harvest peak when prices are lowest, removing water (which reduces weight and transport cost), and packaging in a format your customers already buy. A 1 kg jar of paste can retail for several times the value of the 56 kg of fresh tomatoes that went into it, provided you control consistency and safety.</p>

<h3>Step 1: Grinding Tomatoes into Puree</h3>

<p>Wash tomatoes in clean water, remove stems and any spoiled fruit, then cut larger tomatoes to fit your grinder. A manual grinder (hand-crank or mill) works for 1030 kg batches and needs no electricity useful in rural areas or during power cuts. A motorised grinder or blender processes 50200 kg per hour and gives a finer, more uniform puree, which cooks faster and yields smoother paste.</p>

<p>For a finer paste, grind twice, or pass the puree through a sieve to remove skins and seeds. Removing skins and seeds improves texture and reduces bitterness, but many small producers in West Africa keep skins for higher yield and darker colour decide based on your target market. If you sieve, expect 1020% loss in weight but a premium product.</p>

<h3>Step 2: Cooking the Puree to Remove Water and Thicken</h3>

<p>Cooking does two jobs: it evaporates water to concentrate solids, and it pasteurises the puree, killing spoilage organisms. Use a wide, heavy-bottomed pot (stainless steel or enamel, never bare aluminium for acidic tomato) so heat spreads evenly and steam escapes fast. Stir continuously with a wooden or stainless paddle to prevent burning burnt paste is bitter and unsellable.</p>

<p>Target: reduce the puree to roughly one-third of its original volume for a thick paste (about 2530% total solids). A typical batch of 30 kg fresh puree yields 810 kg of paste after 4590 minutes of simmering. Add a pinch of citric acid or lemon juice early to protect colour and flavour.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Adding Preservatives Salt, Sugar, and Natural Acids</h3>

<p>Preservatives in tomato paste are not a substitute for proper cooking and sealing they are insurance. Use them at the right levels so the product tastes good and stays safe.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Salt:</strong> 1.52.5% of paste weight (1525 g per kg). Salt lowers water activity and improves taste. Above 3% it becomes too salty for most markets.</li>
<li><strong>Sugar:</strong> 13% of paste weight to balance acidity and improve browning and mouthfeel. Optional; skip if your market prefers a savoury profile.</li>
<li><strong>Natural acids:</strong> citric acid (0.20.5%), lemon juice (12 tbsp per kg), or vinegar (12%). Acids lower pH below 4.6, which is the critical safety threshold for botulism prevention in canned tomato products.</li>
</ul>

<p>Add salt and sugar near the end of cooking so you can taste and adjust. Add acid early for colour protection, or at the end if you want a fresher flavour.</p>

<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Botulism Risk</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;">Tomato paste with pH above 4.6 and sealed without proper heat treatment can support <em>Clostridium botulinum</em>, which causes a life-threatening illness. Always acidify to pH 4.6 (use a pH strip or meter), hot-fill at 8590C, and never sell jars that have bulging lids, off-smells, or mould. If in doubt, discard never taste-test a suspect jar. This is not negotiable for a food business; one incident can destroy your brand and put lives at risk.</p>
</div>

<h3>Step 4: Testing Consistency Spoon and Plate Test</h3>

<p>You don't need a laboratory to know when paste is ready. Use these two field tests:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Spoon test:</strong> dip a clean spoon into the paste, lift it out, and hold it sideways. Ready paste clings to the spoon and falls in a slow, thick ribbon not a thin stream. If it drips like water, keep cooking.</li>
<li><strong>Plate test:</strong> place a teaspoon of paste on a white plate and tilt it. Ready paste holds its shape and does not release a watery ring. If liquid separates, cook longer; if it's stiff and dry, add a little water or oil to adjust.</li>
</ul>

<p>For consistency across batches, weigh a known volume: 100 ml of finished paste should weigh roughly 125135 g for a good commercial paste (about 2530% solids). Track this in a simple notebook your customers will notice when batch 5 tastes different from batch 4.</p>

<h3>Step 5: Hot Filling into Jars, Bottles, or Sachets</h3>

<p>Hot filling is the single most important step for shelf life. Fill containers while the paste is still at 8590C. The heat sterilises the inside of the container and creates a vacuum as it cools, which seals out air and microbes.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Jars and bottles:</strong> wash, rinse, and heat in an oven or hot water before filling. Fill to within 1 cm of the rim, wipe the rim clean, and cap immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Sachets:</strong> use food-grade laminated sachets and a heat sealer. Fill hot, expel air, and seal immediately. Sachets are popular in Nigeria and Ghana for single-use cooking portions and have lower packaging cost than glass.</li>
<li><strong>Containers:</strong> never reuse containers that held non-food products, and never use containers with damaged rims or lids.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Step 6: Sealing and Labelling for Sale</h3>

<p>Sealing must be airtight. For jars, check the lid "pop" as it cools a concave lid that doesn't flex means a good vacuum. For bottles, use new caps with intact liners. For sachets, test the seal by squeezing gently; if it leaks, re-seal or discard.</p>

<p>Labelling is your legal and commercial obligation. Include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Product name (e.g. "Tomato Paste Concentrated")</li>
<li>Net weight in grams or kilograms</li>
<li>Ingredients list in descending order</li>
<li>Batch number and production date</li>
<li>Best-before date (typically 1218 months for properly hot-filled paste)</li>
<li>Your business name, address, and contact</li>
<li>Storage instructions ("Store in a cool, dry place. Refrigerate after opening.")</li>
</ul>

<p>Check your national regulator NAFDAC in Nigeria, FDA Ghana, KEBS in Kenya for specific labelling and registration requirements before you sell. Registration builds trust and opens doors to supermarkets and export buyers.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li>Buy tomatoes at harvest peak; sort out spoiled fruit before grinding.</li>
<li>Grind to a smooth puree; sieve if your market wants a premium texture.</li>
<li>Cook in a wide, heavy pot, stirring constantly to avoid burning.</li>
<li>Add salt (1525 g/kg), sugar (optional), and acid (citric, lemon, or vinegar) at the right stage.</li>
<li>Confirm readiness with the spoon and plate test; target 2530% solids.</li>
<li>Hot-fill at 8590C into clean jars, bottles, or sachets; seal immediately.</li>
<li>Check seals after cooling; discard any that failed.</li>
<li>Label with product name, weight, ingredients, dates, and your contact and register with your national food authority.</li>
</ol>
