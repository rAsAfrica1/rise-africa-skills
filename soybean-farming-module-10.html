1. Determining Harvest Maturity

Soybean harvest maturity is not just about the calendar. You need to check the crop itself. The best time to harvest is when 95% of the pods are brown or tan and the seeds rattle inside. The leaves have mostly dropped, and the stems are dry but not brittle. If you wait too long, pods split open and seeds fall to the ground. If you harvest too early, seeds are green, small, and have poor oil content. In Africa, where many farmers grow soybean in rain-fed conditions, maturity can vary by field and even within a field. Walk your field and sample a few plants from different spots. Squeeze a pod: if the seed is hard and makes a sound, it's ready. A moisture meter is ideal: grain moisture should be between 13% and 15% for harvest. If you don't have a meter, bite a seed: it should crack, not be chewy. In northern Nigeria, harvest often starts in late October to early November; in Zambia, it's May to June. Plan to harvest as soon as the crop is ready, because delays increase losses. If you have a lot of acreage, you may need to harvest in stages. Also, check the weather forecast: if rain is coming, harvest immediately, even if slightly immature, because wet pods lead to mold and poor quality. For smallholders, hand harvesting with sickles is common, but it's slow. If you can, hire a combine harvester or a contractor. The key is to monitor your crop daily once it starts turning brown. Don't rely on neighbours; every field is different. Use a simple moisture tester or the bite test. Harvest when the majority of pods are dry and seeds are firm. This ensures maximum yield and quality.

2. Combine Settings and Harvest Efficiency

If you use a combine harvester, correct settings are critical to avoid losses. Start with the reel speed: it should be about 10-20% faster than the ground speed. If the reel is too fast, it will beat the pods and cause shattering. For soybean, the cylinder speed should be around 400-600 rpm, and the concave clearance should be wide enough to avoid cracking seeds but narrow enough to thresh all pods. A good starting point is 10-15 mm clearance. The fan speed should be adjusted so that chaff is blown out but seeds are not. Too much air blows seeds out the back; too little leaves chaff in the grain tank. Check the grain tank sample: if you see cracked seeds, reduce cylinder speed or increase clearance. If you see unthreshed pods, tighten clearance or increase speed. Ground speed should be slow enough to allow the header to pick up all pods, but fast enough for efficiency. In Africa, many farmers use small combine harvesters or contractors. Before harvesting, level the field and remove obstacles. Make sure the header is set low to catch low pods, but not so low that it picks up soil. For soybean, the header height should be about 5-10 cm above the ground. If the crop is lodged, you may need to adjust the reel to lift the plants. Always monitor losses behind the combine: place a 1m x 1m sheet on the ground and count seeds. More than 4-5 seeds per square foot means you are losing too much. Adjust settings accordingly. Also, check for header loss: pods left on the stem. If you find many, slow down or adjust the reel. Remember, a well-adjusted combine can reduce losses from 10% to less than 3%. Take time to calibrate. If you are hiring a contractor, insist on a test run and check the grain tank and ground losses. Efficiency saves money and increases your harvest.

3. Minimizing Harvest Losses

Harvest losses in soybean can be as high as 20% if you are not careful. The main causes are shattering, header loss, and threshing loss. Shattering happens when pods are overly dry and brittle. To reduce it, harvest when moisture is 13-15%, not lower. If you must harvest at lower moisture, do it in the early morning or late evening when humidity is higher, reducing shattering. For hand harvesting, use a sharp sickle and cut close to the ground, then gently place plants on a tarpaulin. Don't throw them. For combine harvesting, adjust the reel speed and height. Also, ensure the header is not too low to avoid picking up stones and soil. Another major loss is from lodging (plants fallen over). If your crop is lodged, you may need to harvest in one direction or use a pickup reel. In Africa, many smallholders harvest by hand and then thresh by beating with sticks. This can cause seed damage and loss. Instead, use a small thresher or a mechanical thresher. If beating, do it on a clean tarpaulin and gently. After threshing, winnow to remove chaff. Losses also occur during transport: use tight bags and don't overload. Store in a dry place. To measure losses, use a 1m x 1m frame. Count seeds on the ground before harvest (pre-harvest loss), during harvest (header loss), and after (threshing loss). Aim for total losses below 5%. For every 1% loss, you lose about 20 kg per hectare if yield is 2 tons. So, 5% loss is 100 kg. That's money lost. Simple steps: harvest at right moisture, adjust combine, use tarpaulins, handle gently. Also, train your workers. In many African communities, sharing a combine or thresher with neighbours can reduce costs and improve efficiency. But ensure the machine is clean to avoid mixing varieties. By minimizing losses, you increase your income and food security.

4. Drying and Storage of Soybeans

After harvest, soybean moisture is often 15-18%. For safe storage, you need to dry it to 12% or less. If you store at higher moisture, mold grows and seeds rot. In Africa, sun drying is common. Spread beans on a clean tarpaulin or concrete floor in thin layers (5-10 cm) and stir every hour. It takes 2-3 days of good sunshine to reach 12%. If you have a moisture meter, test daily. Without a meter, bite a seed: it should shatter, not be soft. Another method is using a solar dryer or a mechanical dryer. For small quantities, a simple solar dryer with a black plastic sheet can work. Never dry on bare ground; it picks up soil and stones. Also, avoid drying on roads where vehicles may run over. Once dry, clean the beans by winnowing or using a sieve to remove chaff, dirt, and broken seeds. Then store in clean, dry bags. Use new or well-cleaned jute or polypropylene bags. Never use bags that previously held fertilizer or chemicals. Store in a cool, dry, well-ventilated place. Raise bags on pallets or wooden planks to avoid moisture from the floor. Keep away from walls. Stack bags no more than 10 high to avoid crushing. If you have many bags, leave space between stacks for air circulation. For long-term storage, you can use hermetic bags (like PICS bags) or metal silos. These prevent insects and mold. Check stored beans every month for signs of insects or moisture. If you see weevils, you can sun-dry again or use approved storage insecticides like Actellic. But always follow safety instructions. In humid areas, consider using airtight containers. Proper drying and storage can keep soybeans for 6-12 months without quality loss. This allows you to sell when prices are better, not immediately after harvest when prices are low. So invest time in drying and storage.

5. Grain Quality Standards and Grading

Soybean quality determines its price and use. Buyers look for specific standards: moisture content, foreign matter, broken seeds, and damaged seeds. In most African markets, the standard for Grade 1 soybean is: moisture max 12%, foreign matter max 1%, broken seeds max 2%, and damaged seeds max 3%. Grade 2 allows moisture up to 14%, foreign matter up to 2%, broken up to 5%, damaged up to 5%. Grade 3 is lower. To get a good price, aim for Grade 1. To achieve this, start with a clean harvest. Remove all stones, dirt, and plant parts. Use a sieve or winnowing. Broken seeds come from rough threshing or poor combine settings. Adjust your thresher to minimize breakage. Damaged seeds include those with insect holes, mold, or discoloration. These reduce quality and can cause rejection. After drying, take a sample to a local extension officer or buyer to test. Many cooperatives have moisture meters and grading charts. If you are selling to a processor, they may have specific requirements. For example, some want large seeds for food, others want high oil content for crushing. Know your market. Also, avoid mixing old and new beans; it lowers quality. Store different varieties separately. When bagging, label with date and variety. If you are in a group, consider collective grading and marketing to get better prices. In Nigeria, the Nigerian Agricultural Quarantine Service sets standards; in Kenya, KEBS. But even without formal grading, you can improve by cleaning and drying properly. Remember, a clean, dry product always fetches more. Also, never use chemicals directly on the grain unless approved and correctly applied. Some farmers use storage pesticides, but they must be food-safe. Always read labels. By meeting quality standards, you can access better markets, including export. So, take the extra step to grade your soybean.

6. Transportation and Marketing Preparation

After harvest, drying, and grading, you need to transport and market your soybean. Proper preparation ensures you get the best price. First, package your beans in clean, strong bags. Use 50 kg or 100 kg bags, depending on the market. For export, 50 kg is common. Make sure bags are sealed and labeled with your name, variety, and weight. If you are selling to a cooperative or processor, they may provide bags. Otherwise, buy new polypropylene bags. Avoid overfilling; leave a small space for tying. Before transport, arrange for a vehicle. For small quantities, use a pickup or a lorry. For larger, hire a truck. Ensure the vehicle is clean and dry. Cover with a tarpaulin to protect from rain and dust. If traveling long distances, avoid delays because heat and humidity can affect quality. In Africa, poor roads can cause bruising and breakage. So, pack bags tightly to reduce movement. Also, consider the timing: market prices are usually lowest at harvest and rise later. If you can store safely, sell 3-6 months later when prices are higher. But you need to monitor market trends. Join a farmer group or cooperative to access market information and better prices. Many cooperatives aggregate and negotiate with buyers. Also, get a moisture meter or have your beans tested before sale. Buyers will test and may reject if moisture is above 12%. For marketing, know your buyer: local traders, processors, or export companies. Each has different requirements. For export, you may need certification (e.g., phytosanitary certificate) and meet aflatoxin standards. Aflatoxin is a poison from mold; proper drying and storage prevent it. If you suspect mold, don't sell for food. Also, keep records of your production and sales for future planning. Finally, consider value addition: cleaning, grading, or even processing into oil or feed can increase income. But start with proper transportation and marketing preparation. With good planning, you can turn your soybean into a profitable venture.

Practical Reference: Harvesting & Post-Harvest Handling of Soybean in Africa

Soybean is one of Africa's fastest-growing cash and food crops, driven by demand from poultry feed millers, edible oil processors, and smallholder nutrition programmes. Yet the gap between potential yield and marketable grain often comes down to what happens in the two weeks around harvest. Poor timing, wrong combine settings, inadequate drying, or bad storage can cut saleable value by 20â40% and destroy the premium that processors pay for quality grain. 1. Determining Harvest Maturity

Harvest too early and you get green, shrivelled seed with low oil content and poor germination. Harvest too late and pods shatter, seed splits, and you lose 5â15% before the combine reaches the field. The commercial trigger is simple: 95% of pods are brown or tan, and seed moisture has dropped to 13â15%. At this point, seed in the pod rattles when shaken. In most African growing zones â from Nigeria's Benue State to Zambia's Eastern Province â this window is 7â10 days wide. After that, shatter loss accelerates sharply, especially in indeterminate varieties.

If leaves have dropped and stems are dry but pods are still green, you may be looking at a variety with delayed maturity â check with your seed supplier. 3. Minimizing Harvest Losses

Losses in African soybean harvest typically run 8â20% combined. The biggest culprits are shatter at the header, unthreshed pods, and cracked seed from over-threshing. To cut losses:

  • Harvest at the right moisture. 13â15% is the sweet spot. Below 12% shatter risk rises; above 16% you get clogging and mould.
  • Harvest early morning or late afternoon. Dew reduces shatter. Avoid the hottest part of the day.
  • Slow down. A 20% reduction in ground speed can cut header loss by half.
  • Check behind the combine every 2 hours. Use a 1 mÂ² frame and count seeds on the ground. More than 4 seeds/mÂ² means adjust settings.
  • Use a stripper header or air reel where available â these reduce shatter significantly in dry conditions.
  • Train operators. A well-trained combine operator can save 3â5% of the crop, which on a 5-tonne crop is 150â250 kg â real money.

4. Drying and Storage of Soybeans

After harvest, soybean must be dried to 12â13% moisture for safe storage of up to 6 months, and to 11â12% for longer storage or seed. In humid African zones â the Guinea Savannah, the Congo Basin, coastal West Africa â natural sun drying on tarpaulins can take 3â5 days. In drier zones like the Sudan Savannah or Zambia's plateau, 2â3 days is often enough.

Key rules:

  • Never dry on bare ground â soil, stones, and animal droppings contaminate the grain and downgrade it at the buying centre.
  • Spread to 5 cm depth and turn every 2 hours for even drying.
  • Use a moisture meter. Cheap handheld meters cost $30â80 and pay for themselves in one season.
  • Cool grain before storage. Hot grain in bags causes condensation and mould.
  • Use hermetic storage (PICS bags, GrainPro, or metal silos) to control insects without chemicals. This is now the gold standard across Africa.
  • Stack bags on pallets, 10 cm off the floor and 20 cm from walls. Never stack against a damp wall.

Soybean dust is highly combustible and can cause severe respiratory irritation. Always wear a dust mask and eye protection when handling, cleaning, or bagging grain. Never smoke or use open flames near grain dust. If you use phosphine fumigation tablets for storage insect control, they are highly toxic â only trained and licensed operators should apply them, and the store must be sealed, signed, and kept locked for the full fumigation period (typically 5â7 days). For smallholder storage, hermetic bags (PICS/GrainPro) are the safer alternative and are approved across the continent.

6. Transportation and Marketing Preparation

Your grain's value is set at the point of sale, and buyers discount heavily for poor presentation. Before you load:

  • Clean the grain. Remove stones, chaff, and broken seed. A simple winnowing or a small cleaner can move you from Grade 3 to Grade 1.
  • Use clean, dry bags. Never use bags that previously held fertiliser or chemicals. Use new or properly washed polypropylene bags of 50 kg or 100 kg.
  • Label each bag with your name, farm, date, variety, and moisture content. This builds trust with processors and can earn a premium.
  • Transport in covered vehicles. Rain or damp tarpaulins can add 2â3% moisture in transit and cause mould within days.
  • Deliver at the right moisture. Buyers test on arrival. If your grain is above 13%, you may be rejected or heavily discounted.
  • Aggregate with neighbours to meet minimum volumes (often 5â20 tonnes) required by processors and to negotiate better prices.
  • Know your market. Feed millers want high protein; oil processors want high oil. Test your grain and target the buyer who values your specific quality.
