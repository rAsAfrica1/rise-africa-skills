1. Solar Irradiance Basics and Measurement Units

Solar irradiance is the power of sunlight hitting a surface, measured in watts per square meter (W/mÂ²). When you leave a solar panel in the sun, the irradiance determines how much power it can produce. In Africa, peak sun hours are often 5 to 7 kWh/mÂ² per day, meaning you get the equivalent of 5 to 7 hours of full sunshine. This is called peak sun hours (PSH). For a dam site, you need to know the average daily irradiance to size your pump. A simple tool is a solar pathfinder or a smartphone app like Sun Surveyor. But the most accurate way is to use a pyranometer, which costs around $200-$500. If you can't afford one, you can use satellite data from free sources like NASA POWER or Global Solar Atlas. These give you monthly averages for your exact location. For example, a site in Kenya might have 6.5 PSH in January and 4.5 PSH in July. You need to design for the worst month to ensure water pumping even in cloudy conditions. Remember, irradiance changes with time of day and season. So always use long-term averages, not just one day's reading.

2. Using Pyranometers and Satellite Data for Dam Sites

A pyranometer measures global horizontal irradiance (GHI). It's a flat sensor that you place level on the ground. For dam sites, you need to measure at the same tilt as your solar panels. A cheaper option is a reference cell, which is a small solar panel that outputs a signal proportional to irradiance. You can buy one for under $100. To use it, connect it to a multimeter and note the short-circuit current. But for most small-scale projects, satellite data is enough. Go to Global Solar Atlas (globalsolaratlas.info) and enter your dam's coordinates. It gives you GHI, direct normal irradiance (DNI), and diffuse irradiance. For pumping, GHI is most important because most pumps use flat panels. If your dam is in a valley, satellite data might overestimate due to shading from hills. So you must do a site visit. Use a compass and a clinometer to measure horizon angles. If a hill blocks the sun before 9 AM or after 3 PM, you lose pumping hours. Also, check for dust and bird droppingsâthey reduce irradiance by 5-10%. Clean panels weekly. For a 1 kW pump, you need about 4-5 kWh/mÂ²/day. In the Sahel, you might get 6 kWh/mÂ²/day, but in coastal Cameroon, only 4.5. Always add a 20% safety margin.

3. Estimating Solar Energy Potential at the Dam

To estimate energy potential, you need three things: average daily irradiance in peak sun hours (PSH), total solar panel capacity in kilowatts (kW), and system efficiency. The formula is: Energy (kWh/day) = PSH Ã Panel kW Ã Efficiency. Efficiency for a solar pump system is typically 50-60% because of losses in cables, controller, motor, and pump. So if you have 5 PSH and a 1 kW panel, you get 5 Ã 1 Ã 0.55 = 2.75 kWh/day. That energy can pump about 2,750 liters of water to a 10-meter head (assuming pump efficiency of 50%). But you need to know your dam's head and required flow. For example, if you need 10,000 liters per day from a 20-meter deep dam, you need more panels. Use a pump sizing tool like the one from Lorentz or Grundfos. They have free online calculators. Also, consider the tilt angle. In Africa, tilt panels at latitude minus 10 degrees for year-round performance. For a dam in Zimbabwe (latitude 20Â°S), tilt at 10Â° north. This maximizes winter output. If you can't adjust tilt, use a fixed tilt equal to your latitude. Remember, energy yield drops on cloudy days. So design for the worst month. In many parts of Africa, July is cloudy. Use July PSH for design. If July PSH is 4.0, then your daily energy is 4.0 Ã 1 Ã 0.55 = 2.2 kWh. That's your reliable yield.

4. Seasonal and Daily Irradiance Variations

Solar irradiance changes every hour and every month. In Africa, the dry season usually has clear skies and high irradiance, while the rainy season brings clouds and lower irradiance. For example, in Ghana, March has about 6.0 PSH, but August drops to 4.0 PSH. This means your pump will deliver less water in August. You must plan for this. If your dam is for irrigation, you need water most in the dry season, which is good because that's when irradiance is highest. But for domestic water supply, you need water all year. So size your system for the worst month. Daily variation: from 6 AM to 6 PM, irradiance follows a bell curve. Peak is around noon. A solar pump starts working when irradiance reaches about 200 W/mÂ², which is around 7 AM. It stops when irradiance falls below that, around 5 PM. So you get about 10 hours of pumping, but not at full power. The effective peak sun hours (PSH) is the area under the curve. In practice, you can use a data logger to record irradiance every 10 minutes. If you can't, use the monthly average from NASA POWER. Also, consider the harmattan in West Africaâdust reduces irradiance by up to 30% in December-February. So clean panels daily during that period. For a dam, you can also use a floating solar pump, which benefits from cooling and less dust.

5. Shading Analysis and Mitigation Strategies

Shading can kill your solar pump's performance. Even a small shadow on one panel can reduce output by 50% or more. At a dam site, common shaders are trees, hills, buildings, and even tall grass. You must do a shading analysis before installing panels. The simplest tool is a Solar Pathfinder, which costs about $300. It has a dome that reflects the horizon, and you overlay a sun path chart for your latitude. You can also use a smartphone app like Sun Surveyor or Solar Shading. Walk around the proposed panel location and note any obstacles. For each obstacle, measure its height and distance. Then calculate the shadow angle: angle = arctan(height/distance). If the angle is greater than the sun's elevation at any time between 9 AM and 3 PM, you have shading. For example, a 10-meter tree 20 meters away gives an angle of 26.6Â°. In winter at noon, the sun might be at 40Â° elevation, so no shading. But in the morning, the sun might be at 20Â°, so shading occurs. To mitigate, cut trees, move panels, or use micro-inverters or power optimizers. For a dam, you can also install panels on a floating platform in the water, which avoids land shading. If shading is unavoidable, oversize the array by 30% to compensate. Also, keep panels clean and free of bird droppingsâthose are small but persistent shaders.

6. Calculating Daily Energy Yield for Pumping

To calculate daily energy yield, you need the solar array's rated power (W), the average daily peak sun hours (PSH), and the system's overall efficiency. The formula is: Daily energy (Wh) = Array power (W) Ã PSH Ã Efficiency. For a solar pump, efficiency is typically 0.5 to 0.6. So a 1000 W array with 5 PSH and 0.55 efficiency gives 1000 Ã 5 Ã 0.55 = 2750 Wh/day. That's 2.75 kWh. Now, how much water can you pump? Use the formula: Water volume (mÂ³/day) = (Energy (kWh) Ã 367 Ã Pump efficiency) / Total head (m). Pump efficiency is about 0.5 for small pumps. So with 2.75 kWh and 20 m head: (2.75 Ã 367 Ã 0.5) / 20 = 25.2 mÂ³/day. That's 25,200 liters. But you must also consider the flow rate. A pump might be rated at 10 mÂ³/h, but it only runs when irradiance is high enough. So actual daily volume is lower. Use a pump controller with maximum power point tracking (MPPT) to maximize yield. Also, account for cable losses: keep cables short and thick. For a 24V system, use at least 4 mmÂ² cable for runs up to 20 m. If you need more water, add more panels or reduce head. For a dam, you can also use a storage tank to store water during peak sun and use it later. Always monitor your system with a simple energy meter to see actual yield and adjust as needed.

Practical Reference: Solar Resource Assessment for Dam-Based Pumping in Africa

This practical reference consolidates the field-ready numbers, measurement methods, and decision rules you need to size a solar pumping system at a dam site. It is written for the agribusiness operator, irrigation manager, or cooperative technician who must turn solar irradiance data into a reliable daily water yield â and into a bankable investment case.

The core equation is simple: Water pumped = Solar energy captured Ã Pump efficiency Ã· Total head. Every variable in that equation is measurable, and every measurement has a cost. In African dam sites, the dominant uncertainties are (1) seasonal irradiance swings, (2) dust and shading losses, and (3) the mismatch between peak sun hours and the dam's actual water demand curve.

Measurement: Pyranometers vs Satellite Data

A pyranometer measures global horizontal irradiance directly. A calibrated Class A or Class B pyranometer (ISO 9060) costs USD 800â3,000 and requires cleaning, levelling, and annual recalibration. For most dam pumping projects in Africa, a 12-month ground measurement campaign is not justified unless the project exceeds roughly 500 kWp or is financed by a lender requiring bankable data.

Satellite-derived data (Global Solar Atlas, NASA POWER, PVGIS) is free, covers all of Africa at 1 km to 250 m resolution, and has typical uncertainty of 4â8% for GHI. For a 10â100 kWp irrigation pump, this is sufficient. The practical rule: use satellite data for design, and use a pyranometer only if you need to validate performance or resolve a dispute with a contractor.

Seasonal and Daily Variations: The Business Risk

In the Sahel, the harmattan (NovâFeb) brings dust that can cut irradiance by 10â25%. In Central Africa, the rainy season can reduce daily irradiation by 30â40% for weeks. For a dam-based irrigation scheme, this matters because the dry season â when you need water most â is also when dust and haze are highest in some regions, while in others it is the clearest.

Daily variation follows a bell curve. A fixed-tilt array in Africa typically produces 80â90% of its daily energy between 09:00 and 15:00 solar time. If your irrigation schedule requires water before 09:00 or after 16:00, you must either add battery storage (expensive) or a larger array plus a water storage tank (cheaper and more robust). For dam sites, the tank is almost always the better business decision.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Electrical and Structural Hazards at Dam Sites

Solar pumping installations at dams combine high-voltage DC arrays, open water, and often steep or unstable banks. Before any installation or maintenance: (1) isolate and lock out the DC array â DC arcs do not self-extinguish and are lethal; (2) never work alone on a floating or bank-mounted array; (3) verify that the pump intake and any floating structure are anchored against sudden dam level changes and flood surges; (4) use IP68-rated junction boxes and marine-grade cable for any submerged run; (5) confirm that the array mounting can withstand local wind loads, which at exposed dam sites can exceed 120 km/h. Failure to observe these precautions has caused fatalities in African solar pumping projects.

Shading Analysis and Mitigation

At dam sites, shading sources are predictable: dam walls, spillway structures, trees on the bank, and â critically â the array shading itself. A single row of modules can shade the row behind it in the early morning and late afternoon. The standard mitigation is row spacing of at least 2.5 times the module height for fixed-tilt systems at low latitudes.

Use a horizon scanner or the free Sun Path chart from the University of Oregon Solar Radiation Monitoring Laboratory to map obstructions. In the field, a simple clinometer and compass, combined with a sun-path diagram for your latitude, will identify shading losses within 30 minutes. If shading exceeds 10% of annual energy, relocate the array or raise the mounting height â do not accept the loss.
