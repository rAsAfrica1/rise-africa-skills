1. Growth and Development of the Foal

A newborn foal should stand within 1-2 hours and nurse within 2-4 hours. If it doesn't, call a vet immediately. In the first month, a foal gains 0.5-1 kg per day. By 6 months, it should weigh about 45-50% of its mature weight. Measure weight using a weigh tape around the heart girth; if you don't have one, estimate by heart girth in centimeters: weight (kg) (heart girth cm)^2.6 / 11,000. For example, a foal with a 100 cm girth weighs about 100^2.6/11000 110 kg. Record weight weekly. Height at withers also increases rapidly: about 1 cm per week for the first 3 months. Use a measuring stick or a marked wall. Monitor growth rate; if a foal gains less than 0.4 kg/day after the first month, increase feed or check for parasites. In Africa, rapid growth in hot climates can cause stress; provide shade and fresh water. The foal's legs are long at birth, making them appear awkward. This is normal. By 3 months, the foal will start eating solid food, but milk is still the main diet until 4-6 months. Observe the foal's body condition: ribs should be felt but not seen. If ribs are visible, the foal is too thin; if ribs cannot be felt, it's too fat. Adjust feed accordingly. Keep records of weight and height to spot problems early. A healthy foal is alert, curious, and plays frequently. If it lies down a lot or seems dull, it may be sick. Deworm every 4-6 weeks starting at 2 months of age using a product like ivermectin at 0.2 mg/kg body weight. Also, ensure the foal gets colostrum in the first 6 hours for immunity. Without colostrum, the foal is at high risk of infection. If the mare rejects the foal, milk her and bottle-feed the colostrum. In Africa, many smallholders have local breeds; these may grow slower but are hardy. Adjust expectations accordingly. Focus on steady, consistent growth rather than maximum speed.

2. Nutritional Management of the Growing Foal

From birth to 3 months, the foal gets most nutrition from mare's milk. A good mare produces 10-20 liters of milk per day. To support this, feed the mare 2-3 kg of concentrate daily plus good hay and pasture. If the mare is thin, increase feed. The foal will start nibbling at 1-2 weeks. Provide a creep feedera small pen with feed that only the foal can enter. Use a creep feed with 16-18% protein, 0.8-1.0% calcium, and 0.5-0.7% phosphorus. A simple mix: 50% crushed maize, 30% soybean meal, 15% wheat bran, 5% mineral-vitamin premix. Add salt at 0.5%. Feed 0.5-1 kg per day at 2 months, increasing to 2-3 kg by 6 months. Split into 2-3 meals. Always provide clean water. In hot African climates, foals drink 20-30 liters per day. If pasture is poor, give good-quality hay (e.g., lucerne or Rhodes grass) free-choice. Avoid sudden feed changes; introduce new feed over 7-10 days. Watch for diarrhea; if it occurs, reduce concentrate and add probiotics. Weigh the foal monthly to adjust feed: a 6-month-old foal should eat about 1-1.5% of its body weight in concentrate plus hay. For a 200 kg foal, that's 2-3 kg concentrate. If the foal is too thin, increase by 0.5 kg per day. If too fat, reduce. Minerals are critical: calcium and phosphorus in a 2:1 ratio. A lack causes bone problems. Provide a mineral block or loose minerals. In Africa, many soils are deficient in selenium and copper; use a supplement with 0.3 ppm selenium and 10 ppm copper. Also, add vitamin E. If the foal is orphaned, use a mare milk replacer (e.g., Foal Lac) at 10-20% of body weight per day, divided into 6-8 feedings. Never feed cow's milk alone; it causes diarrhea. Gradually introduce solid feed by 2 weeks. By 6 months, the foal should eat 2-3 kg concentrate and 2-3 kg hay daily. Adjust based on body condition. Keep feed troughs clean to prevent mold. Aflatoxin in maize can kill foals. Buy from trusted suppliers.

3. Handling and Early Training of Foals

Start handling the foal within the first 24-48 hours. Wear gloves and move slowly. Approach the mare first, then the foal. Touch the foal all over: head, neck, back, legs, and hooves. This makes future trimming and vet care easier. Use a soft cotton rope halter; don't use a chain. Put it on and off several times a day for short periods. Reward with gentle scratching. By 1 week, teach the foal to lead with a butt rope. Stand on the foal's left side, hold the halter with your right hand and a rope behind its rump with your left. Ask it to walk forward; never pull. Use a calm voice. Practice for 5-10 minutes daily. By 1 month, the foal should tie quietly for 10 minutes. Use a quick-release knot. Never leave a tied foal unsupervised. Pick up feet daily: start with front, then back. Hold each foot for a few seconds, then release. Gradually increase to 30 seconds. This prevents kicking later. By 3 months, introduce grooming with a soft brush. Also, teach the foal to stand for the farrier; have the farrier visit monthly just to handle the feet. In Africa, many foals are not handled until weaning, which makes them wild. Invest time daily. Use a small pen or stable for training. Avoid shouting or hitting; it creates fear. If the foal resists, stay calm and patient. Use food rewards like a handful of concentrate. By 6 months, the foal should walk, trot, and stop on command. Also, get it used to a trailer: park a trailer in the yard, feed the foal near it, then inside. This reduces stress when transporting. Handle the foal's ears and mouth; this helps with deworming and dental care. Introduce clippers and spray bottles. For African conditions, also get the foal used to flies and heat; apply fly spray and provide shade. Socialize the foal with other horses, but avoid aggressive ones. A well-handled foal becomes a safe adult horse. Spend 15-20 minutes daily. Consistency is key. If you have multiple foals, handle each individually. Record progress. If a foal is difficult, seek help from an experienced handler. Never use force; it damages trust.

4. Weaning Methods and Timing

Weaning is the process of separating the foal from the mare. The best time is 4-6 months, when the foal is eating 1-2 kg of concentrate daily and can survive without milk. In Africa, wean during the cool dry season to reduce stress. Never wean before 3 months; the foal's immune system is not ready. Methods: abrupt separation, gradual separation, and fence-line weaning. Abrupt: move the mare to a distant paddock where the foal cannot see or hear her. This causes stress and possible injury. Not recommended. Gradual: separate for increasing periods over 1-2 weeks. Start with 1 hour, then 2, then 4, then overnight. Finally, separate permanently. This is kinder. Fence-line: put the mare and foal in adjacent paddocks with a strong fence. They can see and touch but not nurse. After 3-5 days, move the mare further away. This is the best method for smallholders. Whichever method, reduce the mare's feed 2 days before weaning to lower milk production and prevent mastitis. Check her udder daily for heat or hardness; if present, milk her out and call a vet. For the foal, provide a clean, safe pen with hay and water. Feed the same concentrate it was eating before weaning. Offer small meals 3-4 times daily. Monitor the foal's weight; some loss is normal (up to 5% in first week). If it loses more than 10%, consult a vet. The foal should be dewormed and vaccinated before weaning. After weaning, keep the foal in a small group with other weanlings for company. In Africa, many farmers wean at 8-12 months, which is too late; it drains the mare and reduces her fertility. Aim for 6 months. If the mare is pregnant again, wean by 5 months. Use a halter and lead rope to move the foal gently. Never chase. After weaning, the mare may call for 2-3 days; this is normal. Give her plenty of water and hay. Watch for mastitis. The foal may also call; provide distractions like toys or a companion. Within a week, both settle. Record weaning date and weight. If you have limited space, wean one foal at a time. Always ensure the foal has been eating solid feed well before weaning. A foal that is not eating enough will struggle. Weaning is stressful but necessary for the mare's health and the foal's independence.

5. Health Care and Vaccination Schedules

Foals need a strong health program. First, ensure they get colostrum within 6 hours of birth. If not, give 1 liter of frozen colostrum or plasma. Vaccinate the mare 4-6 weeks before foaling with tetanus, influenza, and herpes (EHV-1/4). This boosts colostrum quality. For the foal: at 3 months, start vaccinations. Give tetanus toxoid (1 ml IM) and influenza (1 ml IM). Repeat in 4 weeks. At 6 months, give rabies (2 ml IM) and African horse sickness (AHS) if in endemic areas (1 ml SC). AHS vaccine is live; give only in areas where the disease occurs. Also, deworm: at 2 months, use fenbendazole (5 mg/kg) or ivermectin (0.2 mg/kg). Repeat every 4-6 weeks until 6 months, then every 8 weeks. In Africa, ticks are a major problem. Use tick grease or spray with amitraz every 2 weeks. Check for tick-borne diseases like piroplasmosis; signs are fever, jaundice, and colic. Treat with imidocarb (2.2 mg/kg SC). Also, watch for diarrhea. In foals, diarrhea can be caused by rotavirus, Salmonella, or parasites. If diarrhea lasts more than 24 hours, call a vet. Give oral electrolytes. For respiratory disease, vaccinate against strangles (Streptococcus equi) at 4 months and 6 months (1 ml IM). Also, deworm for bots in autumn. Hoof care: trim every 4-6 weeks. If the foal is lame, check for abscess. In hot climates, provide shade and fresh water to prevent heat stress. Vaccinate against anthrax in endemic areas (1 ml SC annually). Keep records. A simple notebook with dates and products. If you cannot afford all vaccines, prioritize tetanus and rabies. Tetanus kills; rabies is zoonotic. Also, test for equine infectious anemia (EIA) annually; in many African countries, it's required for movement. Use sterile needles and dispose safely. Never reuse needles. For deworming, rotate products to prevent resistance. Use a weigh tape to dose accurately. Underdosing leads to resistance. If a foal is sick, isolate it. Common signs: dullness, not eating, fever (over 38.5C), diarrhea, cough. Take temperature rectally with a digital thermometer. Normal is 37.5-38.5C. If fever, call a vet. Keep a first-aid kit: bandages, antiseptic, scissors, thermometer, gloves. In Africa, many vets are far; learn basic skills. Attend a training workshop. Prevention is cheaper than treatment.

6. Preventing Developmental Orthopedic Diseases

Developmental orthopedic diseases (DOD) are bone and joint problems in growing foals. Common types: osteochondrosis (OCD), physitis, angular limb deformities, and contracted tendons. Causes include rapid growth, unbalanced nutrition, genetics, and trauma. To prevent DOD, control growth rate. Aim for moderate, steady growth, not maximum. A foal should gain 0.5-0.8 kg per day, not more. Feed a balanced diet with correct calcium:phosphorus ratio (2:1). Avoid high-energy feeds like straight maize; they cause rapid growth. Use a concentrate with 16% protein and added minerals. Provide free-choice hay. Do not overfeed. Keep the foal's body condition score at 5 out of 9 (ribs easily felt, not visible). Exercise is important: allow free movement in a paddock daily. Confinement increases DOD risk. In Africa, many foals are kept in small pens; provide at least 100 square meters per foal. If not possible, hand-walk for 30 minutes daily. Avoid strenuous exercise like lunging or chasing. Also, avoid excessive trauma: don't let foals run on hard, rocky ground. Check hooves regularly. Angular limb deformities: if the foal's legs are crooked, consult a vet early. Mild cases correct with trimming. Severe cases need surgery. Contracted tendons: if the foal walks on its toes, provide a soft bed and gentle stretching. If it cannot stand, call a vet. Physitis: swollen growth plates above the hooves. Rest and reduce feed. In all cases, early detection is key. Weigh and measure the foal monthly. If growth is too fast, reduce concentrate by 20%. If too slow, increase by 10%. Also, ensure copper and zinc levels are adequate. In Africa, many soils are deficient; supplement with 10 mg copper and 40 mg zinc per kg feed. Selenium is also needed: 0.3 ppm. Vitamin E: 100 IU per day. Avoid excess phosphorus; it causes bone weakness. Use a mineral premix designed for horses. Do not feed dog or chicken feed. If DOD occurs, work with a vet. X-rays may be needed. Treatment includes rest, corrective trimming, and sometimes surgery. But prevention is far cheaper. Keep records of growth and feed. If a foal has a history of DOD, adjust its diet and exercise. Genetics play a role; avoid breeding from affected horses. In Africa, many local breeds are hardy, but crossbreeds may be more susceptible. Monitor closely. Share knowledge with other farmers. A healthy foal is a sound future horse.

<h2>Practical Reference: Foal Development and Weaning</h2>

<p>In African horse breeding operationsfrom stabled Warmblood studs in the Western Cape to pastoralist herds in the Sahel and commercial Thoroughbred farms in Kenya and Zimbabwethe foal's first six months decide lifetime soundness, sale value, and training potential. This practical reference condenses the operational essentials for stud managers, grooms, and farm owners into a business-first framework: measurable milestones, feeding costs, labour requirements, and risk controls that protect your investment.</p>

<p>Foals are a high-value, high-risk asset class. A single case of septic joint infection, contracted tendon, or developmental orthopedic disease (DOD) can eliminate a year's profit on that animal. The protocols below are designed for practical African conditions: variable forage quality, seasonal rainfall, limited veterinary access in remote areas, and the need to keep costs per foal predictable.</p>

<h3>1. Growth Benchmarks and Business Targets</h3>

<p>Monitor growth monthly using a weight tape and height stick. In African conditions, where pasture quality varies sharply between rainy and dry seasons, monthly data is your early-warning system for nutritional gaps. A foal that falls below 0.7 kg average daily gain (ADG) in the first three months is a candidate for intervention before DOD develops.</p>

<h3>2. Nutritional Management of the Growing Foal</h3>

<p>Mare's milk alone supports growth for the first 810 weeks, but by 2 months the foal requires supplementary feed. In African studs, the cheapest reliable creep feed is a 1618% crude protein pellet formulated with locally available ingredients: maize, soybean or sunflower meal, and a mineral-vitamin premix with balanced calcium and phosphorus (target Ca:P ratio 1.52:1). Avoid high-starch rations; they increase DOD risk.</p>

<p>Feed costs dominate foal budgets. A practical creep ration in Southern Africa costs roughly USD 0.450.65 per kg. A foal consuming 1.5 kg/day from month 2 to month 6 will cost approximately USD 120180 in concentrate over that perioda small price against the USD 2,00010,000+ value of a sound yearling.</p>

<h3>3. Handling and Early Training</h3>

<p>Foals should be handled daily from birthnot to "break" them, but to build the handler's safety and the foal's tractability. In African commercial studs, labour is often the largest variable cost; efficient handling routines reduce injury claims and staff turnover. Aim for 510 minutes of quiet, structured contact per day: haltering, leading, picking up feet, and standing for the farrier.</p>

<p>By 3 months, every foal should: lead on a halter without pulling, allow all four feet to be picked up, stand tied briefly under supervision, and load into a trailer or race. These are saleable skills. A foal that loads calmly at 6 months saves hours of labour at yearling prep and reduces the risk of injury to handlers and the animal.</p>

<h3>4. Weaning Methods and Timing</h3>

<p>Weaning is the single most stressful event in a foal's first year. In African conditions, weaning at 46 months is standard, but timing must align with forage availability and weather. Avoid weaning during the peak of the dry season when pasture is scarce, or during extreme heatstress compounds and growth stalls.</p>

<p><strong>Recommended method: gradual (fence-line) weaning.</strong> Separate the foal from the mare but keep them within sight and touch of a fence for 57 days. This reduces cortisol spikes, lowers the risk of gastric ulcers, and maintains feed intake. Abrupt weaningcommon in low-input systemsincreases respiratory disease and DOD risk.</p>

<p>After weaning, group foals of similar age and size. Provide high-quality hay and the same creep feed they were eating before weaning. Monitor body condition weekly for the first month; a drop of more than 0.5 body condition score is a red flag.</p>

<h3>5. Health Care and Vaccination Schedules</h3>

<p>Core vaccinations in most African countries include tetanus, equine influenza, and African horse sickness (AHS) where endemic (South Africa, parts of Namibia and Botswana). AHS vaccination is strictly regulated and must be administered by a state veterinarian in South Africa. Rabies vaccination is recommended in areas with wildlife contact.</p>

<p>Deworming should begin at 23 months, with faecal egg counts guiding treatment intervals. Over-deworming is a growing problem; use targeted selective treatment to preserve anthelmintic efficacy and reduce costs.</p>

<h3>6. Preventing Developmental Orthopedic Diseases (DOD)</h3>

<p>DODincluding osteochondrosis, physitis, and angular limb deformitiesis the leading cause of yearling sale rejection and lost athletic potential. Prevention is cheaper than treatment. Key controls:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Balanced minerals:</strong> Copper, zinc, and calcium-phosphorus balance are critical. Supplement according to NRC guidelines, not guesswork.</li>
<li><strong>Moderate growth:</strong> Do not push for maximum weight gain. Rapid growth is a primary DOD trigger.</li>
<li><strong>Exercise:</strong> Free movement in paddocks is protective. Confinement increases DOD risk.</li>
<li><strong>Regular farrier care:</strong> Every 46 weeks from 3 months of age.</li>
<li><strong>Monitoring:</strong> Monthly weight and height data; radiograph at 912 months if DOD is suspected.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="font-size:14px;line-height:1.7;">
<li> Ensure foal receives colostrum within 2 hours of birth; test IgG at 1824 hours.</li>
<li> Introduce creep feed by 2 weeks; target 0.20.5 kg/day by 1 month.</li>
<li> Record monthly weight and height; intervene if ADG falls below 0.7 kg/day.</li>
<li> Begin daily handling from birth: halter, lead, pick up feet, stand tied.</li>
<li> Deworm at 23 months based on faecal egg count, not calendar.</li>
<li> Wean gradually at 46 months, avoiding dry-season or heat stress.</li>
<li> Vaccinate per national protocol (tetanus, influenza, AHS where endemic).</li>
