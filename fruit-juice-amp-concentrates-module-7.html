1. What Is a Fruit Concentrate? Benefits and Uses

A fruit concentrate is simply fruit juice with most of the water removed. If you start with 10 litres of fresh mango juice and remove 7 litres of water, you end up with about 3 litres of thick, syrupy concentrate. That concentrate still contains all the natural sugars, acids, colours, and flavours of the original fruitjust in a smaller volume. In Africa, where mangoes and pineapples often rot in the field during harvest season, concentrates solve two big problems: they reduce spoilage and make transport cheaper. A 20-litre bucket of concentrate can make 100 litres of ready-to-drink juice, so you can sell far more product from the same harvest.

Concentrates are used in three main ways. First, for juice sales: you mix concentrate with clean water, add sugar if needed, and sell as fresh juice. Second, as ingredients for other businesses: bakeries, ice cream makers, and yoghurt producers buy concentrate to flavour their products. Third, for home use: a spoonful of pineapple concentrate can sweeten tea or porridge. Because concentrate is thick and acidic, it resists spoilage better than fresh juice. If you keep it in a clean, airtight container in a cool, dark place, it can last 36 months without refrigeration. That means you can process fruit during the glut and sell all year round.

To make concentrate, you need a few basic tools: a juicer or blender, a large pot or a solar dryer, a fine mesh strainer or muslin cloth, clean glass bottles or food-grade plastic containers, and a refractometer if you want to measure sugar levels (Brix). A simple refractometer costs about $20$40 and helps you keep quality consistent. The key is to remove water without burning the juice. You can do this by slow boiling, vacuum evaporation, or solar drying. Each method has trade-offs in cost, speed, and flavour retention.

One important note: concentrate is not the same as squash or cordial. Squash has added sugar and preservatives; concentrate is pure fruit. When you sell concentrate, you are selling a raw ingredient that your customers can dilute to their own taste. This makes it a flexible product for both households and small businesses.

2. Evaporation Methods: Open Pot, Vacuum, and Solar

Evaporation is the heart of concentrate making. You need to remove water from juice without destroying flavour, colour, or nutrients. Three methods work in an African setting: open pot boiling, vacuum evaporation, and solar evaporation. Each has a different cost and quality outcome.

Open pot boiling is the simplest and cheapest. Use a wide, heavy-bottomed stainless steel pot. Pour in fresh juicemango, pineapple, or a mix. Heat over a medium fire, stirring constantly with a wooden spoon to prevent burning. Bring to a gentle simmer (about 9095C) and keep stirring. Water will rise as steam. For every 10 litres of juice, you can expect to remove 67 litres of water in 23 hours. The concentrate is ready when it coats the back of the spoon or when a drop on a cold plate stays thick. The downside: heat can dull the fresh flavour and darken the colour. To reduce this, keep the temperature below boiling and stir often. Never let it scorchburnt concentrate tastes bitter and cannot be fixed.

Vacuum evaporation is faster and preserves flavour better. You need a vacuum pump and a sealed vessel, which can cost $200$500. The juice boils at 4060C under vacuum, so delicate flavours survive. This method is best if you plan to sell to bakeries or ice cream makers who demand high-quality concentrate. In many African cities, you can rent time on a vacuum evaporator at a food processing incubator or university lab. If you cannot afford your own, consider a shared facility.

Solar evaporation uses the sun. Build a solar dryer with a black tray inside a glass-covered box. Pour juice into the tray at 12 cm depth. The sun heats the juice and water evaporates through vents. On a hot day (3035C), 10 litres of juice can reduce to 3 litres in 68 hours. Solar evaporation is slow but free and retains more nutrients than open boiling. However, it risks contamination from dust and insects, so cover the tray with a fine mesh. Also, if the weather turns cloudy, the juice can ferment. Use solar evaporation only for small batches and when you have reliable sunshine.

Choose your method based on your budget, batch size, and target market. For home sales, open pot works. For business-to-business, vacuum is best. For a low-cost, off-grid option, solar is viable with care.

3. Making Mango and Pineapple Concentrates

Mango and pineapple are two of Africa's most abundant fruits, and both make excellent concentrates. The process is similar but each fruit has quirks. Follow these steps for a 10-litre batch of fresh juice, which will yield about 3 litres of concentrate.

For mango concentrate: choose ripe but firm mangoesoverripe fruit gives a dull flavour. Wash, peel, and cut the flesh away from the stone. Blend the flesh with a little water (1 cup water per 4 cups mango) to help extraction. Strain through a muslin cloth or fine sieve to remove fibre. You should get about 67 litres of thick juice from 10 kg of mangoes. Pour the juice into a pot and simmer gently, stirring constantly. Mango juice burns easily because of its natural sugars. Keep the heat low and stir every 30 seconds. After 23 hours, the volume will drop to about 23 litres. The concentrate should be thick like honey. Add a pinch of citric acid (1/4 teaspoon per litre) to brighten the flavour and help preservation. Bottle hot into clean glass jars.

For pineapple concentrate: select ripe pineapples with a sweet aroma. Peel, remove the eyes, and cut into chunks. Blend without adding waterpineapple is juicy. Strain to remove pulp. You should get 78 litres of juice from 10 kg of fruit. Pineapple juice is more acidic than mango, so it foams during boiling. Use a tall pot to avoid overflow. Simmer gently, skimming off foam with a spoon. After 23 hours, you will have 23 litres of concentrate. Pineapple concentrate is very tart; you may add sugar later when reconstituting. Do not add sugar during concentrationit can cause crystallisation. Bottle hot.

For both fruits, test the concentrate by cooling a spoonful. It should be thick and syrupy, not watery. If it is still thin, continue simmering. Record your yield: for every 10 litres of juice, you should get 2.53.5 litres of concentrate. If you get less, you may have boiled too hard and lost flavour. If more, it is under-concentrated and will spoil faster. Store in sterilised bottles with airtight lids. Label with fruit type and date. Use within 6 months.

4. Packaging and Storing Concentrates

Concentrate is a high-value product, so packaging must protect it from air, light, moisture, and contamination. Poor packaging ruins months of work. Follow these practical steps for small-scale African producers.

First, sterilise your containers. Glass bottles or jars are best because they do not react with acid. Wash them with hot soapy water, rinse, then place in an oven at 120C for 15 minutes or boil in water for 10 minutes. Let them dry upside down on a clean cloth. For plastic, use food-grade PET or HDPE containers. Never use old cooking oil bottlesthey harbour bacteria and smells. If you are selling to businesses, use 1-litre or 5-litre food-grade buckets with tight lids.

Second, fill hot. Pour concentrate into containers while it is still hot (above 80C). This creates a vacuum seal as it cools, killing most microbes. Leave 1 cm headspace. Seal immediately with a new lid. For glass jars, you can use a simple metal lid that pops down when sealed. For plastic buckets, use a rubber gasket lid. After sealing, turn the jar upside down for 2 minutes to sterilise the lid, then turn upright.

Third, label clearly. Include: fruit type (e.g., Mango Concentrate), date of production, batch number, and instructions for reconstitution (e.g., Mix 1 part concentrate with 4 parts water). If you sell to other businesses, add your contact details and a best before date 6 months from production. Use waterproof labels or write directly on the lid with a permanent marker.

Fourth, store properly. Keep in a cool, dark placea cupboard away from the stove, or a cellar. Ideal temperature is 1020C. Do not expose to direct sunlight; UV light destroys colour and vitamins. Once opened, store in the refrigerator and use within 2 weeks. If you see mould, a fizzy smell, or a changed colour, discard the whole batch. Do not scoop out mould and use the resttoxins spread invisibly.

For transport to markets, pack bottles in crates with newspaper or straw to prevent breakage. If you are selling at a distant market, consider using 5-litre plastic jerrycans instead of glass to reduce weight and breakage. Always keep a sample from each batch so you can trace any quality problem.

5. Reconstituting Concentrates for Juice Sales

Reconstituting means adding water back to concentrate to make ready-to-drink juice. This is where you turn a thick syrup into a sellable beverage. The key is to get the ratio right so your juice tastes good and your costs are controlled. A standard ratio for most fruit concentrates is 1 part concentrate to 4 parts clean water. So 1 litre of concentrate makes 5 litres of juice. But this varies by fruit and customer preference. Mango concentrate is sweet, so you might use 1:5. Pineapple is tart, so 1:4 or even 1:3 if you want a strong taste.

Start by measuring. Use a clean measuring jug. Pour 1 litre of concentrate into a 10-litre bucket. Add 4 litres of safe water. Stir well with a long spoon. Taste. If it is too thick, add more water 100 ml at a time. If too thin, add more concentrate. For a consistent product, use a refractometer to check Brix. Most ready-to-drink juices are 1012 Brix. Your concentrate might be 6070 Brix. So you need to dilute until you hit 1012 Brix. Without a refractometer, rely on taste and a simple rule: the juice should taste like fresh fruit, not like syrup.

Next, adjust sweetness. Concentrate has natural sugars, but customers often expect a sweeter juice. Add sugar syrup, not dry sugar, to avoid gritty texture. Make syrup by dissolving 1 kg of sugar in 1 litre of hot water. Cool it, then add to the juice. Start with 100 ml of syrup per 5 litres of juice. Taste and adjust. You can also add a pinch of citric acid (1/4 teaspoon per 5 litres) to brighten flavour. For pineapple, a squeeze of fresh lime helps.

Finally, chill and serve. If you are selling from a cooler, keep the juice at 4C or add ice. For immediate sale, pour into clean cups or bottles. If you are bottling for later, pasteurise by heating to 75C for 15 seconds, then hot-fill into sterilised bottles. Label with date and keep refrigerated. Sell within 3 days if unpasteurised, or 2 weeks if pasteurised. Always calculate your cost: if 1 litre of concentrate costs you $2 to make, and you get 5 litres of juice, your cost per litre is $0.40. Add cups, labour, and transport, then set a price that gives you at least 50% profit.

6. Selling Concentrates to Other Businesses

Selling concentrate to other businesses is often more profitable than selling juice to consumers. You sell in bulk, you don't need to bottle or chill, and you get repeat orders. Your customers include bakeries, ice cream makers, yoghurt producers, hotels, restaurants, and other juice vendors. They buy concentrate because it saves them time, reduces waste, and gives consistent flavour year-round.

To start, identify your local buyers. Visit bakeries and ask if they use fruit flavourings. Many use artificial syrups; your natural concentrate is a premium upgrade. Ice cream makers love mango and pineapple concentrate because it adds real fruit taste. Yoghurt producers can stir it in for fruit-on-the-bottom cups. Hotels and restaurants use it for cocktails, smoothies, and sauces. Make a simple list of 10 potential buyers within 20 km of your production site.

Next, prepare samples. Put 200 ml of concentrate in a clean, labelled bottle. Include a one-page sheet with: fruit type, Brix level (e.g., 65 Brix), ingredients (100% fruit, no preservatives), shelf life (6 months unopened), and reconstitution ratio (1:4). Offer a free sample so they can test it in their own kitchen. Follow up after 3 days. Ask for feedback on taste, thickness, and packaging. Be ready to adjust your productsome bakers want a thicker concentrate, some want it less sweet.

Price your concentrate fairly. Calculate your cost per litre: fruit, labour, fuel, bottles, and labels. For example, if 10 kg of mangoes costs $5, and you get 3 litres of concentrate, your raw material cost is $1.67 per litre. Add $0.50 for packaging and $0.50 for labour and fuel, total $2.67. Sell at $4$5 per litre to a business. That gives you 5080% profit. Offer a discount for bulk orders (e.g., 10 litres for $40).

Deliver reliably. Agree on a delivery scheduleweekly or bi-weekly. Use clean, food-grade containers. Always include a batch number and date. Ask for payment on delivery or within 7 days. Keep records of each sale. As you grow, you can buy larger equipment and hire help. Remember: one bakery that buys 5 litres per week is worth more than 50 one-time customers. Build relationships, be consistent, and your concentrate business will grow.

<h3>Understanding Concentration Levels and Yield</h3>

<p>The single most important number in concentrate production is <strong>Brix</strong> the percentage of soluble solids, mostly sugars, in the liquid. Fresh mango juice typically measures 1418 Brix; pineapple 1216 Brix. A concentrate is usually standardised to 6070 Brix for shelf stability and transport efficiency. That means you are removing roughly 7580% of the water. For every 100 litres of fresh juice at 15 Brix, you can expect around 2025 litres of 6065 Brix concentrate, depending on evaporation efficiency and losses.</p>

<h3 style="color: #b71c1c; margin-top: 0;"> SAFETY WARNING: Aflatoxin and Mycotoxin Risk in Fruit Concentrates</h3>
<p style="color: #b71c1c; margin-bottom: 0;">Never use mouldy, bruised, or overripe fruit for concentrate production. Mangoes and other tropical fruits are highly susceptible to <em>Aspergillus</em> moulds that produce aflatoxins carcinogenic compounds that are <strong>not destroyed by boiling or evaporation</strong>. Aflatoxin-contaminated concentrate can cause liver damage and is illegal for sale in most markets. Always sort fruit before processing, discard any fruit with visible mould or soft rot, and test finished concentrate if you suspect contamination. In Kenya, KEBS requires aflatoxin testing for fruit products; in Nigeria, NAFDAC enforces similar limits. The cost of testing (USD 2050 per sample) is far less than the cost of a recall or legal action.</p>
</div>

<h3>Making Mango and Pineapple Concentrates: Step-by-Step</h3>

<p><strong>Mango concentrate:</strong> Select ripe but firm mangoes (varieties like Apple, Ngowe, or Kent). Wash, peel, and remove stones. Pulp the fruit using a pulper or blender, then press through a fine sieve to remove fibre. Heat the juice to 8590C for 23 minutes to inactivate enzymes (this prevents browning). Then evaporate at 6070C under vacuum, or 8085C in an open pot with constant stirring, until Brix reaches 6065. Add citric acid to adjust pH to 3.54.0. Hot-fill into sterilised glass bottles or food-grade plastic containers, seal, and invert for 2 minutes to sterilise the lid.</p>

<p><strong>Pineapple concentrate:</strong> Use ripe pineapple with 1216 Brix. Peel, core, and juice. Pineapple juice contains bromelain, an enzyme that can cause bitterness if overheated so heat to 90C for 30 seconds only, then cool rapidly before evaporation. Evaporate to 6065 Brix. Pineapple concentrate darkens easily, so vacuum evaporation is strongly preferred. Adjust pH to 3.23.8 with citric acid. Hot-fill and seal as above.</p>

<h3>Packaging and Storage</h3>

<p>Concentrates are hygroscopic they absorb moisture from the air, which can cause fermentation. Use airtight, food-grade containers. Glass is best for small batches; high-density polyethylene (HDPE) drums or jerrycans work for bulk. Fill containers to the top to minimise headspace. Store in a cool, dark, dry place below 25C. Properly packaged 6065 Brix concentrate can last 612 months unopened; once opened, refrigerate and use within 24 weeks. Never store concentrate in metal containers unless they are food-grade stainless steel acidic concentrate reacts with iron and aluminium.</p>

<h3>Reconstituting for Juice Sales</h3>

<p>To make ready-to-drink juice from concentrate, dilute with clean, potable water. A typical ratio is 1 part concentrate to 45 parts water, depending on desired Brix (usually 1012 Brix for drinking). Add citric acid to taste, and optionally add sugar, preservatives (if legal in your market), or natural flavours. For commercial sale, pasteurise the reconstituted juice at 85C for 1530 seconds, then hot-fill into bottles or sachets. Label clearly with production date, expiry date, and ingredients. In many African markets, sachet juice at 200500 ml is the highest-volume format.</p>

<h3>Selling Concentrates to Other Businesses</h3>

<p>Your best customers are not always consumers they are other businesses. Juice bars, hotels, restaurants, bakeries, ice-cream makers, and soft-drink bottlers buy concentrate in bulk because it saves them storage space and labour. Approach them with a clear value proposition: consistent quality, reliable supply, and competitive price per litre of finished juice. Offer 5-litre, 20-litre, and 200-litre options. Provide a specification sheet with Brix, pH, packaging date, and shelf life. For export, you will need food safety certification (HACCP, ISO 22000) and compliance with destination country regulations.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Sort fruit carefully</strong> discard mouldy or bruised fruit to avoid aflatoxin contamination.</li>
<li><strong>Measure Brix</strong> with a refractometer before and after evaporation; target 6065 Brix for most concentrates.</li>
<li><strong>Control temperature</strong> use vacuum or solar evaporation for best colour and flavour; avoid prolonged boiling.</li>
<li><strong>Adjust pH</strong> to 3.24.0 with citric acid for safety and stability.</li>
<li><strong>Hot-fill and seal</strong> containers immediately to prevent microbial growth.</li>
<li><strong>Store below 25C</strong> in a dark, dry place; use airtight, food-grade containers.</li>
<li><strong>Label every batch</strong> with production date, Brix, pH, and expiry date.</li>
<li><strong>Test for aflatoxin</strong> if you suspect contamination; keep records for regulators and buyers.</li>
