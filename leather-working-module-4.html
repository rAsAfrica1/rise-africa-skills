Module 4

🧰 Curing the Raw Hide

A freshly flayed hide is a piece of meat with hair on it, and it starts to rot immediately. This module gives you the clock you are racing, the two preservation routes - salt and drying - with every sourced figure for each, the storage rules that keep a cured hide saleable for months, and the safety rule that matters most in this whole trade: never take a hide from an animal that died of an unknown cause, because untreated hides have carried anthrax to the people handling them.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • State how quickly curing must begin after flaying in tropical conditions
  • Explain the two mechanisms by which salt preserves a hide
  • Describe the two-stage piling sequence used in practice
  • Set up frame drying correctly, with sourced spacing and timing
  • Explain curing damage as a collagen-fibre failure rather than a cosmetic one
  • State the absolute rule on hides from animals that died of unknown causes

Lesson 4.1~12 min

The Clock You Are Racing

In this lesson
  • State how quickly curing must begin after flaying in tropical conditions
  • Explain why heat is the variable that governs the whole curing window
  • Plan a slaughter day so no hide waits for a decision

Everything in this module exists to answer one question. How do you stop bacterial putrefaction long enough to get the hide to a tannery in a condition the tannery can still use?

Start by seeing the hide honestly. A green hide - green meaning freshly flayed and unpreserved - is not a material. It is a piece of meat with hair on it. It carries residual blood, flesh, fat and a great deal of moisture, and the bacteria that will destroy it are already on it and already multiplying. Nothing is waiting for you to be ready.

The figure to build your whole day around is this. A green hide should go into curing within about 4 hours of flaying in tropical conditions.

Four hours. Not four hours of working time. Four hours on the clock, from the moment the hide comes off the carcass.

Why so short? Because ambient heat accelerates bacterial growth in the residual blood, flesh and moisture on the hide, and tropical African temperatures are, for this purpose, close to worst-case. The same hide in a cold climate has a much longer window. You do not have that window. A hide left green through a hot afternoon is a hide already losing value before anyone has done anything wrong except wait.

That sentence is worth pinning up somewhere. Most hide value lost to putrefaction is not lost through a mistake. It is lost through a delay that nobody thought of as a mistake at the time. The animal was slaughtered at eleven. The salt was in the store and the store key was with someone at the market. The hide sat in the sun until four. Nobody did anything wrong, and the hide is now second grade at best.

Drying rather than salting does not stop the clock either. If sun or air drying is your chosen method, the hide needs to reach a safe moisture level within 2 to 3 days to avoid putrefaction risk. And there is an outer limit worth knowing precisely because it is so often misused: a hide can tolerate 3 to 4 days of high ambient temperature without noticeable damage. That is an outer limit, not a target. Treat it the way you treat the depth of water you can wade through, not the way you treat a normal path.

So you have three time figures and they mean three different things.

About 4 hours from flaying to the start of curing is the deadline you plan your day around.

Two to three days is the period within which a drying hide must reach safe moisture.

Three to four days of high ambient temperature is the absolute outer tolerance before noticeable damage, quoted so you understand how little margin there is behind the deadline, not so you can use it.

Now turn it into a working plan, because a deadline you have not planned for is just a thing you will miss.

Decide the curing method before the animal is killed, not after. This sounds obvious and is almost never done. The salt, the frame, the space and the labour must all be present before the knife goes in.

Weigh out the salt in advance if you are salting, because salting is done as a percentage of the hide's green weight and you cannot weigh out 35 percent of a weight you have not measured yet. Have the scale ready.

Have the curing surface ready and clean. A hide laid flesh-down on dirty ground while you fetch things is picking up contamination and staying warm.

And give one named person the job of the clock. On a day with several animals, hides get forgotten in exactly the way one hide never does. Somebody writes the flaying time for each hide on a board, and somebody checks the board.

One last thought that connects this lesson to your money. In Module 3 you learned to grade defects and you learned that flaying damage is the most avoidable defect in the trade. Curing delay is its twin. Both are free to prevent. Both cost you the full price of a downgraded hide. And curing delay has one cruel extra property: for the first day or so, a hide that has begun to rot looks almost exactly like a hide that has not.

Lesson 4.2~12 min

Salt Curing - How Much and Why

In this lesson
  • Explain the two mechanisms by which salt preserves a hide
  • State the sourced salt percentages for wet salting, brining and dry salting
  • Calculate a salt quantity from a hide's green weight

Salt is sodium chloride, and it preserves a hide in two ways at once.

First, it draws water out of the hide. Bacteria need water to grow, and salt pulls it out of the tissue by simple osmosis. Second, at high concentration salt has a direct antibacterial action of its own. So salting is not just drying by another route. It is drying plus a hostile chemical environment.

Remember from Module 2 that the corium can absorb and hold water, salt, lime, acid, tannin and oil. Curing is the first member of that family: you are forcing salt into the fibre network in order to force water out of it. The same network, a different substance, a different purpose.

There are two salting methods.

Wet salting is the dominant one. Salt is rubbed into the flesh side of the hide, or the hide is immersed in strong brine. The sourced figures are these. Standard wet-salting practice uses 30 to 40 percent salt on green hide weight. A reduced, short-term regime uses about 25 percent. Where brining is used, the brine itself is made up to about 33 percent salt solution, which is saturated.

Dry salting means the hides are salted and then dried. It uses roughly 10 percent less salt than wet salting, because the subsequent drying step removes additional water that the salt would otherwise have had to draw out. Note the wording carefully: 10 percent less salt than wet salting, not 10 percentage points off the percentage figure.

Now, what actually happens inside the hide while it cures? Three sourced figures describe it, and together they let you predict what your scale will read.

The hide absorbs roughly 13 to 17 percent of its own weight in salt.

The salt draws out approximately 20 percent of the hide's water content.

Together these produce an overall weight loss of 10 to 20 percent from green weight to preserved hide.

That last figure is the one that matters commercially, and it is also the one that starts arguments. If you buy a hide green and sell it cured, it will weigh less. That is not shrinkage you did wrong. It is water you deliberately removed, and it is the whole point of the operation. If you are buying or selling by weight, agree in advance whether the price is on green weight or cured weight, because 10 to 20 percent is a large enough difference to destroy a margin.

Now the arithmetic, done step by step, because this is a calculation you will do for the rest of your working life.

Suppose you have weighed a green hide and it reads 25 kilograms. Twenty-five is used here only as a round number to show the method. No sourced average green weight for African cattle hides, sheepskins or goatskins was available for this course, so you must weigh your own hides rather than assume a typical weight.

You choose wet salting at 35 percent, which sits in the middle of the 30 to 40 percent range.

Salt needed = 25 kilograms multiplied by 0.35 = 8.75 kilograms of salt for that one hide.

Now take that 8.75 kilograms to your own salt supplier and price it. That gives you a real, local, current cost of curing one hide, before any other input. Do it again at 30 percent and at 40 percent, and you will see the range of what curing costs you: at 30 percent it is 7.5 kilograms, at 40 percent it is 10 kilograms.

That spread of 2.5 kilograms of salt per hide, across a hundred hides, is 250 kilograms of salt. This is why the percentage is a decision, not a detail.

And the counter-lesson, which matters more. Salt is a real, budgetable input cost, not something free that you sprinkle on. But it is also the cheapest insurance in your entire business. Under-salting to save money is how you convert a first-grade hide into a rotting one, and you will lose the whole hide to save a few kilograms of salt. When you are unsure, the answer is not to economise on the salt.

One practical caution to end on. Salt must reach every part of the flesh side, especially the thick areas, the folds around the shanks and the edges. Bacteria do not care that you salted 90 percent of the hide well. They will start where you missed.

Lesson 4.3~12 min

Piling, Storage Life and Testing the Cure

In this lesson
  • Describe the two-stage piling sequence used in practice
  • State how long correctly cured hides can be stored
  • Judge salt quality and cure adequacy against sourced checkpoints

Applying salt is not the same as curing a hide. A curing regime is not finished the moment the salt goes on, and this lesson covers what happens over the days and months that follow.

The practical sequence used in the African hide-improvement training material runs in two piling stages, each of about 5 days. The hide is stacked flesh-to-flesh with salt between layers, left to cure for the first period, then commonly re-piled and re-salted for a second period of similar length.

Why two stages rather than one longer one? Because the first period is when the salt does most of its water-drawing work, and by the end of it the pile has released a large amount of brine and the salt distribution has changed. Re-piling and re-salting resets the contact between fresh salt and the hide, and it gives you a second look at every hide in the stack. If something is going wrong - a dry patch, a fold that stayed unsalted, a hide that is slipping hair - you find it on day five instead of on the day you try to sell it.

Stack flesh-to-flesh, not flesh-to-hair. Salt belongs against the flesh side, which is where the water and the residual tissue are.

Now, how long does a correctly cured hide last?

Well-cured, wet-salted hides can be stored for about 2 to 3 months under humid conditions, or up to about 6 months with additional preservative treatment.

There is also a short-term, no-salt holding method, and it is usable for only about 4 to 6 weeks.

That last figure teaches something important about how to think. "No salt" is not the same as "no preservation plan". It is a much shorter plan. Somebody using a short-term method is not being careless as long as they know they have four to six weeks and have a buyer inside that window. They are being careless if they think time is not running.

Now the quality control, and this is where a professional separates from an amateur.

Salt quality matters and it is checkable without a laboratory. Usable curing salt should be 94 to 95 percent sodium chloride, with a grain size of about 2 to 3 millimetres.

The grain size figure is one you can actually inspect between your fingers, and it has a reason on both sides. Too fine a grain cakes and does not penetrate evenly - it forms a crust on the surface and leaves the tissue underneath unsalted. Too coarse a grain penetrates too slowly, and slowly is exactly what you cannot afford in the first hours.

For the purity, you will need the supplier's specification or a test, but you can at least ask the question. A salt sold for road use, or a very dirty industrial salt, is not the same product as one at 94 to 95 percent sodium chloride, and asking a supplier what their salt assays at tells them you are a buyer who knows what they are doing.

Then there are the two figures that describe a properly cured hide rather than the salt. A properly cured hide should show an ash content of not less than about 27 percent on a water-remaining basis, with an associated salt-saturation target of not less than about 26 percent.

Be honest about what those two are for. They are quality-control checkpoints that an inspector or a buyer uses with the means to test. They are not a home recipe, and you cannot measure ash content in a village workshop without equipment. Learn them as what a good cure looks like when it is tested, so that when a buyer's inspector quotes a figure at you, you know what they are measuring and why. Do not pretend you are hitting them if you have no way to test.

What you can check without a laboratory is a short list, and it is worth writing on the wall of your store.

  • Is the flesh side fully covered, including folds, shanks and edges?
  • Is brine draining away from the pile rather than sitting under it?
  • Does the pile smell of salt and hide, or does it smell of rot?
  • Does hair pull out easily when you tug it? Hair slip means bacterial action has begun.
  • Are the hides off the floor?

That last one belongs to the next lesson, and it applies to salted and dried hides equally.

Lesson 4.4~12 min

Sun and Air Drying Without Ruining the Hide

In this lesson
  • Set up frame drying correctly, with sourced spacing and timing
  • List the storage clearances that protect hides from insects and damp
  • Explain why a folded wet hide is ruined at the fold

Drying removes water directly instead of binding it with salt. It is the lowest-cost preservation method and the traditional method across much of rural Africa, and it requires no purchased input at all. That is a real advantage and this lesson takes it seriously.

But drying is far less forgiving of delay or poor technique than salting, and the reason is straightforward. Salt starts working the moment it touches the flesh side. Drying works only as fast as the air and sun can carry water away, and until the hide reaches a safe moisture level - within 2 to 3 days, from Lesson 1 - the bacteria are still working too. With salting you are fighting rot with a chemical. With drying you are simply racing it.

The first rule is the one most often broken, and breaking it costs you the hide.

Hides must be stretched on a frame or pegged flat. Never folded or piled while wet.

A folded wet hide rots at the fold before it dries. Air cannot reach the inside of the fold, moisture stays there, and the temperature inside a pile stays high. The defect shows up later as a hole or a weak crease line - and it shows up late, after the hide looks dry and acceptable everywhere else. Somebody folds a hide to carry it home and does not think of it as a decision at all. It is one of the most expensive decisions on the whole day.

Now the frame itself, with the sourced figures.

Frame drying takes about 7 days under the conditions described in the source. Frame sizing given for cattle hides is roughly 9 feet by 10 feet for large hides and 9 feet by 8 feet for smaller breeds. Leave at least 1 foot of clearance between adjacent frames.

That clearance figure is not a tidiness rule. Frames packed edge to edge block the air movement that is doing all the work. If you have ten frames in a row with no gaps, the frames in the middle are drying far more slowly than the ones on the ends, and it is the middle hides that will fail.

Then the storage clearances, which apply to salted and dried hides alike and which are the cheapest protection in this module.

Drying racks, storage racks and stacked hides should be at least 15 centimetres above the floor, to protect against white ants and termites. Pallets used for stacked storage should give at least 6 inches, about 15 centimetres, of clearance above the ground - the same figure arriving from a second direction, which should tell you it is a real rule and not an arbitrary one.

And stored hides held more than about 10 days may need insecticide treatment against insect damage.

Read that last one as a planning figure. If your hides move within ten days, insects are a housekeeping problem. If they sit for a month, insects become a treatment decision, and that is a cost and a chemical you need to plan for rather than discover.

Now the honest part, and it is the kind of honesty that is worth more than a false answer.

Shade and air-suspension drying versus full sun drying are treated in the regional literature as two distinct traditional methods, alongside dry salting and wet salting. The optimal choice depends on climate and infrastructure in each area.

No single, universal best traditional preservation method for all African conditions was identified in the sources available for this course. The sourced material is explicit that the right choice is country- and region-specific.

That is not a gap to be embarrassed about. It is the correct answer. A humid coastal district and a hot dry inland district are not the same drying problem, and anyone who tells you there is one best method for the whole continent is selling something. What you should do instead is find out what works where you are, from the people who have been doing it there longest, and then test it yourself against the deadlines in Lesson 1.

Here is a fair test you can run. Cure two similar hides on the same day, one in full sun and one in shade or air suspension, and record temperature, time to dry, and the condition of each at one month. Do it twice more across different seasons. After three rounds you will know more about drying in your own district than any document could have told you, and you will know it about your own climate rather than somebody else's.

Lesson 4.5~12 min

Curing Failure Cannot Be Undone Later

In this lesson
  • Explain curing damage as a collagen-fibre failure rather than a cosmetic one
  • Predict which later process steps a badly cured hide will fail at
  • Connect curing quality to collection rates and to what buyers pay

This lesson exists because of one persistent hope that costs small operators real money. The hope is that a hide which is not quite right can be rescued further down the line - that the beamhouse will clean it up, or that a good tannage will hide the problem.

It will not. A hide that putrefies partially before curing, or that is cured with too little salt, too coarse a grain, or dried while folded, carries damage that no beamhouse or tanning step downstream can undo.

Understand why, because the reason makes it memorable rather than a rule to obey.

Go back to Module 2. Leather is a three-dimensional interwoven network of collagen fibres, and its strength comes from that architecture. Bacterial putrefaction is not dirt on a surface. It is bacteria digesting collagen. When bacterial action has begun breaking down collagen fibre, it has taken apart the structure that was going to be the product. Nothing downstream builds collagen back.

So curing failure is a collagen-fibre failure, not merely a cosmetic one. That single sentence is the whole lesson.

Now, what does it look like in practice, further down the line? Three things, and they arrive at different moments.

Permanently weakened leather. The hide may look acceptable on the outside and still tear, split or fail under tension where the fibre was digested. This one hides until the product is in use.

Patchy grain looseness. The grain layer separates and moves over the layer beneath it, giving a loose, wrinkled surface where the hide should be tight. It appears during processing and it cannot be corrected.

Hair that will not grip properly for chemical unhairing. This is a subtle one and it surprises people. If bacterial action has already loosened the hair, the unhairing chemistry does not behave as expected in the beamhouse, and you get uneven results across a hide or across a batch. A curing failure has become a beamhouse problem that looks like a chemistry problem, and you will spend a week adjusting a liming recipe that was never the fault.

That last point is worth generalising, because it will save you many wasted weeks. When something goes wrong in the beamhouse and every setting seems correct, ask what condition the hide arrived in. A tannery cannot chemically correct its way out of a raw material that was damaged before it arrived.

This is also exactly why buyers pay premiums for well-cured hides before any tanning has begun. They are not paying for appearance. They are paying to avoid the invisible risk of buying a batch that will fail in their drums, and they have all been caught before.

Now the wider point, and it is the one that makes curing a business subject rather than a technical one.

Correct curing is not only a quality lever. It is a collection-rate lever.

Remember the figures from the start of the course. In one Ethiopian target area, sheepskin and goatskin collection ran at about 95 percent of expected production, while cattle-hide collection at the same time ran at about 37 percent of expected production. The source material connects that difference partly to how consistently each type of raw material was being properly cured and brought to market.

Think about what that gap actually means physically. It is not that the cattle existed and their hides were graded badly. It is that most of those hides never entered the value chain at all. They rotted, they were discarded, they were used for something else, or nobody collected them because they were not worth collecting by the time anyone came.

So a hide cured badly enough to be rejected, or badly enough to rot in storage, is not merely downgraded. It is permanently lost from the value chain. Nobody gets any value from it - not you, not the tannery, not the country.

And that connects straight back to Module 1's central argument. Africa holds a far larger share of the world's animals than of the world's hides and skins. A large part of that gap is hides lost to poor flaying, poor curing, disease and poor collection systems before value can be added at all. Curing is one of the biggest single levers on that gap, it requires no advanced chemistry, no imported equipment and no capital, and it is available to every person handling a hide anywhere in the region.

Of all the things this course will teach you, this is the one with the widest reach and the lowest cost.

Lesson 4.6~12 min

Anthrax - The Hide That Can Kill the Person Handling It

In this lesson
  • State the absolute rule on hides from animals that died of unknown causes
  • Describe how anthrax has reached people through untreated hides
  • Identify which authority sets hide movement, treatment and medical rules

This lesson is the most important one in the module and possibly in the course. Read it before you buy a single hide.

Anthrax is a real, documented hazard for people who handle untreated hides, and it kills. It is caused by a bacterium, Bacillus anthracis, which forms extremely durable spores.

Start with the property that makes it different from every other contamination risk you will meet. Anthrax spores can remain viable in soil for many years, and they are correspondingly persistent on and in improperly treated animal products such as untreated hides.

That means the hazard does not fade with time the way fresh blood contamination might. An old, dried, untreated hide from an infected animal can still be dangerous. A hide that has sat in a store for a year, looks clean, smells of nothing and handles well, is not made safe by having been there a year.

Now the documented case, because this is not theory.

In 2007, a drum maker in Connecticut, in the United States, contracted cutaneous anthrax from handling an untreated goat hide sourced from Guinea, in West Africa. His workplace and his home both tested positive for anthrax contamination. One of his children is believed to have contracted a secondary case from environmental contamination in the home.

Read that again slowly, and note four things.

The hide came from West Africa. The person infected never went near the animal. The contamination did not stay at the workplace - it travelled home on him. And a child who never handled a hide at all is believed to have been infected by that contamination.

That is what an untreated hide from an unknown animal is capable of. Not a theoretical risk in a textbook. A documented transmission event, from an African hide, to a man working with his hands, and then to his family.

Now the fact that removes any comfort from feeling well afterwards. Symptoms usually occur within seven days of exposure, but documented cases have shown onset as early as one day and as late as more than two months after exposure. So a worker who feels fine a few days after handling a suspect hide is not necessarily in the clear, and "I handled one of those last month and nothing happened" is not evidence of anything.

Here is the absolute rule, and it is the rule this whole module has been building towards.

Never accept, buy or process a hide or skin from an animal that died suddenly, was found dead, was not inspected before or after death, or whose cause of death is unknown.

Not at a discount. Not as a favour to a neighbour. Not because it looks clean, because it will look clean.

This is not excessive caution. It is the single control measure that removes almost the entire anthrax risk from a small tannery's raw-material intake, and it costs nothing except refusing a cheap, suspect hide. Everything else in occupational safety is about reducing a risk you have accepted. This one removes the risk at the door.

Notice too that the same rule protects your money. As Module 3 said, an animal that died unexpectedly and uninspected will usually have been poorly bled, poorly flayed and badly cured, because nobody was managing the process professionally. The suspect hide is also, nearly always, a bad hide. You are refusing a poor purchase as well as a hazard.

Beyond that intake rule, the recognised control measures for people who handle hides, furs, wool, hair or bristles are these. Adequate ventilation and dust control where hides are processed - dust is how spores move into the air you breathe. Decontamination procedures: removing contaminated clothing and washing with soap and water promptly after suspected exposure. And for anyone whose occupation regularly involves imported or untreated animal hides, routine pre-exposure vaccination is a recognised administrative control in the occupational-safety guidance.

Now be clear about what this course cannot give you.

A specific vaccination or medical protocol for African tannery and hide-trading workers must come from your national veterinary or public-health authority. This course does not supply one, and no document written elsewhere should be treated as one.

Requirements about moving, treating, importing or exporting hides, and about what must be reported when an animal dies suddenly, are set by national authorities and differ by country. The kind of authority to ask is your national veterinary services or livestock department, together with the public health authority for the human-health side. Ask them directly, get the answer in writing if you can, and record the date and the name of the person who gave it.

Do that before you open, not after something happens. It is one phone call, and it is the cheapest insurance in this trade.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. How soon should a green hide go into curing in tropical conditions?

Ambient heat accelerates bacterial growth in the residual blood, flesh and moisture on the hide. Tropical temperatures make this close to worst-case, so the window is hours, not days.

2. If you choose sun or air drying instead of salting, what is the deadline?

Drying removes water rather than binding it, but bacteria do not pause while it happens. Until the moisture level is safe, putrefaction is still running.

3. How should the figure of 3 to 4 days of high ambient temperature be used?

It describes tolerance, not practice. Planning to it means running with no margin at all on a material that is already rotting.

4. Why must you weigh a hide before curing it with salt?

Every salting figure in this module is a percentage on green weight. Without the green weight you are guessing at the salt, and guessing low is what ruins hides.

5. What makes curing delay especially dangerous compared with other defects?

The damage is bacterial breakdown of collagen fibre inside the hide. By the time it is visible or smellable, the loss has already happened and cannot be undone.

6. What are the two ways salt preserves a hide?

Bacteria need water to grow, so removing it starves them, and the salt environment itself is hostile to them. Salting is drying plus a chemical defence, not drying alone.

7. What is the standard salt quantity for wet salting?

A reduced, short-term regime uses about 25 percent, and where brining is used the brine is made up to about 33 percent saturated solution.

8. A green hide weighs 20 kilograms and you wet salt at 35 percent. How much salt?

20 multiplied by 0.35 equals 7. Do this calculation from your own weighed hide every time, because no sourced average African hide weight exists to use instead.

9. Your cured hide weighs noticeably less than it did green. What has happened?

The hide absorbs 13 to 17 percent of its weight in salt while the salt draws out about 20 percent of its water. Agree in advance whether trade is on green or cured weight.

10. Why is under-salting a false economy?

Salt is a real budgetable cost, but it is the cheapest insurance in the business. Bacteria start wherever the salt did not reach, including folds, shanks and edges.

11. How is the piling stage of salt curing carried out in the sourced practical sequence?

The re-pile resets fresh salt against the hide after the first period has drawn out brine, and it gives you a second inspection of every hide in the stack.

12. How long can well-cured, wet-salted hides be stored under humid conditions?

Salt buys you months, not permanence. A short-term, no-salt holding method by contrast lasts only about 4 to 6 weeks.

13. What is wrong with using very fine-grained salt for curing?

The sourced grain size is about 2 to 3 millimetres. Too coarse a grain has the opposite problem: it penetrates too slowly, and slowly is what you cannot afford in the first hours.

14. What should you do with the ash content and salt saturation figures?

Ash content of not less than about 27 percent and salt saturation of not less than about 26 percent are inspection checkpoints requiring equipment. Claiming them without testing is dishonest and easily caught.

15. You tug the hair on a salted hide and it pulls out easily. What does that tell you?

Hair slip means the hair root structure is breaking down, which is the same bacterial action that damages the collagen. It is one of the checks you can make without any equipment.

16. Why must a wet hide never be folded or piled?

Air cannot reach the inside of a fold, moisture stays there and the temperature inside a pile stays high. The defect appears late, after the rest of the hide looks acceptable.

17. How long does frame drying take under the conditions described in the source?

It is an expectation to check your own drying against, since climate, humidity and air movement all change the real figure in your district.

18. Why leave at least a foot of clearance between adjacent drying frames?

Air movement is the whole mechanism of drying. A row of frames with no gaps is a row where the hides in the middle are still wet while the end ones are done.

19. What is the minimum clearance above the floor for drying racks, storage racks and stacked hides?

The same figure appears in the sourced guidance twice, once as 15 centimetres and once as at least 6 inches of pallet clearance. It is the cheapest defect prevention available.

20. Which traditional preservation method is best across Africa?

A humid coastal district and a hot dry inland district are different drying problems. Find what works where you are, then test it yourself against the curing deadlines.

21. Why can a partially putrefied hide not be rescued in the beamhouse or the tannery?

Curing failure is a structural failure of the fibre network that Module 2 described, not dirt on a surface. Nothing downstream restores it.

22. Which of these is a beamhouse symptom of a curing failure that is easily mistaken for a chemistry fault?

If bacterial action has already loosened the hair, unhairing chemistry does not behave as expected. Operators can spend a week adjusting a liming recipe that was never at fault.

23. In the Ethiopian target area studied, what were the collection rates?

The wide gap between the two shows collection efficiency is a solvable, learnable problem, and the source connects it partly to how consistently each type was properly cured.

24. What is the correct way to describe a hide that rots in storage?

This is different from a graded defect. A downgraded hide still earns something. A lost hide is a large part of the gap between Africa's share of the world's animals and its share of the world's hides.

25. Why do buyers pay a premium for well-cured hides before any tanning has begun?

They are not paying for appearance. They are paying to avoid weakened leather, grain looseness and unhairing problems that only surface once their own money has been spent.

26. What is the absolute rule on hide intake?

It is the single control measure that removes almost the entire anthrax risk from a small tannery's intake, and it costs nothing but refusing a cheap, suspect hide.

27. Why does age not make an untreated hide safe?

Unlike a fresh blood contamination risk, the hazard does not fade with time. A hide that has sat in a store for a year and looks clean is not made safe by having been there.

28. What does the documented 2007 case show?

The man never went near the animal, and the hazard travelled home with him. It is a documented transmission event from an African hide, not a theoretical scenario.

29. You handled a suspect hide five days ago and feel well. What does that mean?

The wide and variable incubation range is exactly why past uneventful experience with suspect hides is not evidence that they are safe.

30. Who sets the rules on hide movement, treatment and worker vaccination?

No document written elsewhere is your rule. Ask the authority directly, get the answer in writing where you can, and record the date and the name of the person who gave it.

Module 4 capstone

Cure two hides or skins by two different methods, measure everything, and write the comparison. Step 1: arrange to obtain two similar fresh hides or skins on the same day from the same slaughter point, and confirm before you take either of them that both animals were slaughtered under inspection and that the cause of death is known and safe - if you cannot confirm that, do not take the hide and do not do this project with it. Step 2: weigh each hide green, before anything is done to it, and write the weight and the exact time of flaying. Step 3: cure the first hide by wet salting, weighing out your salt as a measured percentage of that hide's own green weight, and record which percentage you chose and why. Step 4: cure the second hide by frame drying, building or borrowing a frame, and record the time it went on the frame. Step 5: record the ambient temperature morning and afternoon, and the time each hide reached its cured state. Step 6: weigh both hides again when cured and calculate the percentage weight loss for each, showing your arithmetic. Step 7: store both for one month, off the floor on a raised rack, inspect weekly, and write down any smell, hair slip, insect damage or mould you find. Step 8: write two pages comparing cost, time, labour, storage behaviour and what a buyer said about each hide.
