Lessons

1. From One Welder to a Workshop

Moving from a one-man operation to a workshop means you stop being the only person who welds and start being the person who runs the business. In most African cities, the first step is to rent a small spaceoften a 20ft by 30ft room or a covered yard in an industrial area. Expect to pay between $150 and $400 per month depending on location and whether you have three-phase power. You need at least one 3-phase 415V supply for a 250-amp AC/DC TIG welder and a plasma cutter. If three-phase is not available, a 7.5kVA generator will run a 200-amp inverter welder but will cost about $0.30 per hour in fuel.

Your first hire should be an apprentice, not a qualified welder. An apprentice costs $80$150 per month and can cut, grind, and hold pieces while you weld. This frees you to take on two jobs at once. As you win more work, add a second welder. Pay a skilled aluminium TIG welder $300$500 per month in most African markets. Keep your own hands on the torch for complex jobs, but let your team handle simple frames and repairs.

Organise your workshop into three zones: a cutting and grinding bay, a welding bay with good ventilation, and a finishing and assembly area. Buy or build steel tables at 900mm height. Use firebricks to line your welding table. Keep a 5kg CO2 fire extinguisher within reach. Label everythingargon cylinders, filler rods (4043 and 5356), and consumables. A clean shop reduces mistakes and impresses clients who visit.

Track your jobs on a whiteboard or notebook. Write down the client name, job description, agreed price, and deadline. At the end of each week, calculate your revenue, material costs, and labour costs. If your profit is less than 30% of revenue, raise your prices or cut waste. A workshop that does not track numbers will grow broke even as it grows busy.

2. Buying Equipment as You Grow

Do not buy everything at once. Buy equipment when a specific job or a steady stream of work justifies it. Start with a 200-amp AC/DC TIG welder with a foot pedal and a #17 torch. A good used machine from brands like Miller, Lincoln, or ESAB costs $800$1,500. A new Chinese equivalent (e.g., Everlast or Jasic) costs $500$900 and works fine for most aluminium jobs up to 6mm thick. You also need a plasma cutter for clean cutsa 40-amp unit costs $400$700 and will cut up to 10mm aluminium.

As your workload grows, add a second TIG welder so two people can work at once. Then buy a MIG welder with a spool gun for thicker aluminium (above 6mm). A 250-amp MIG with spool gun costs $1,200$2,000. This machine pays for itself on jobs like truck beds, boat hulls, and large gates. Next, invest in a 3-ton hydraulic press brake and a sheet metal shear. These let you bend and cut your own aluminium sheets, saving you 2030% on material costs. A used press brake costs $2,000$4,000; a shear costs $1,500$3,000.

Buy argon cylinders outright instead of renting. A 50-litre cylinder costs $200$300, and refills cost $40$60. You need at least two cylinders so you never run out mid-job. Buy filler rods in bulk10kg boxes of 4043 (for general work) and 5356 (for stronger, marine-grade joints). A 10kg box costs $80$120. Keep a stock of tungsten electrodes (2% lanthanated, 1.6mm and 2.4mm), ceramic cups, collets, and gas lenses. These consumables are cheap but running out stops work.

Maintain your equipment. Blow out dust from welder internals every month. Check torch cables for cracks. Replace worn contact tips on the MIG. A well-maintained machine lasts 10 years; a neglected one lasts two. Keep a simple logbook for each machine: date of purchase, service dates, and repairs. When you need a loan or grant, this logbook proves you are serious.

3. Training and Hiring

Hiring the right person is harder than welding. Do not hire based on a certificate alone. Test every candidate with a practical job: give them two pieces of 3mm aluminium, a TIG welder, and 30 minutes. Ask them to make a butt weld and a T-joint. Then cut the weld in half and inspect for penetration, porosity, and cracks. A good welder produces a clean, consistent bead with no holes. A bad welder makes a pretty bead that breaks under load. Pay for the test$10$20 is fair.

When you hire, start with a one-month trial. Pay a fair daily rate: $10$20 per day for a skilled welder, $5$8 for a helper. After one month, if they show up on time, follow safety rules, and produce good work, offer a monthly salary. Add a bonus for jobs completed ahead of schedule or under material budget. This motivates without raising fixed costs.

Train your team every week. Spend one hour on Friday afternoon on a specific skill: how to set the AC balance for clean aluminium, how to avoid burn-through on thin sheet, how to tack without distortion. Use scrap metal for practice. Show them a good weld and a bad weld side by side. Explain why one fails. Let them practice while you watch and correct. This weekly training costs you one hour of production but doubles your teams value in six months.

Document your own methods. Write down your settings for common jobs: 3mm aluminium sheet, 1.6mm tungsten, 70% AC balance, 120 amps, 15 CFH argon. Write down your jig designs and clamping tricks. Put this in a notebook or a WhatsApp group for your team. When you hire a new person, they learn from your notes, not from trial and error. This saves time and reduces costly mistakes. A team that learns together stays together and makes you money.

4. Certification and Quality Standards

Certification opens doors to bigger contracts. In most African countries, the national standards bodysuch as KEBS in Kenya, SON in Nigeria, or SABS in South Africaoffers welding certification. The common international standard is ISO 9606 for welder qualification. To get certified, you need to pass a test on specific joints and positions. For aluminium, you will likely test on 3mm and 6mm plate, butt and T-joints, in flat and horizontal positions. The test costs $100$300 and takes one day. Prepare by practicing the exact test pieces for two weeks.

Once you are certified, your business can bid on government and NGO contracts that require proof of quality. You can also certify your workshop to ISO 9001, but that is expensive and not necessary at first. Instead, create your own quality control (QC) system. For every job, record the welders name, the machine settings, the filler rod used, and the date. Take photos of critical welds. If a client complains, you can trace the problem. This simple system shows professionalism and wins repeat business.

Quality also means using the right materials. Never substitute 4043 filler for 5356 on a marine job5356 is stronger and more corrosion-resistant. Never weld dirty aluminium; clean it with a stainless steel brush dedicated to aluminium only. Use acetone or methylated spirits to remove oil and grease. Keep your tungsten sharp and your gas flow correct (1015 CFH for most jobs). A clean, consistent weld is your best advertisement.

Offer a warranty on your work. For structural jobs, offer a one-year warranty against defective welds. For decorative jobs, offer six months. Put it in writing. This builds trust and justifies a higher price. If a weld fails, fix it for free and learn why it failed. Your reputation is worth more than any single job. Word of mouth in African business communities travels fastboth good and bad.

5. Finding Larger Contracts

Larger contracts come from three places: construction companies, manufacturing firms, and government tenders. Start with construction. Visit building sites and ask for the site manager. Show them photos of your workaluminium windows, doors, railings, and roofing. Offer a free sample: make a small window frame and give it to them. If they like it, they will call you for the next project. A single apartment block can need 50100 aluminium windows, worth $5,000$15,000 in fabrication and installation.

Manufacturing firms need aluminium jigs, machine guards, trolleys, and workbenches. Visit factories that make food products, cosmetics, or furniture. Ask to speak to the maintenance manager. Offer to make a prototype of a jig they need. Charge for materials but not labour for the first one. If it works, you become their go-to welder. These contracts are repeat businessmonthly orders of $500$2,000.

Government tenders are published on national procurement websites and in newspapers. You need a registered business, a tax compliance certificate, and often a certification. Start with small tendersschool desks, hospital beds, or fencing for a local clinic. Bid competitively but do not underprice. Include your material costs, labour, transport, and a 20% profit margin. Attend the tender opening to learn how others bid. After a few losses, you will know the right price.

To win larger contracts, you must be reliable. Deliver on time. If you promise a job in two weeks, finish in 12 days. Communicate daily with the client. Send photos of progress. When you finish, clean the site and remove all scrap. A happy client gives you a reference letter. Use that letter in your next bid. Keep a folder of reference letters, photos, and certificates. This is your portfolio. In Africa, relationships matter as much as price. Be honest, be on time, and be easy to work with.

6. A 12-Month Growth Plan

Month 13: Stabilise your one-man operation. Track every job and cost. Save 20% of profit. Buy a second argon cylinder and a stock of 4043 and 5356 filler rods. Practice TIG on 3mm and 6mm aluminium until you can weld clean beads in flat and horizontal positions. Write down your settings. Open a separate bank account for the business. Register your business name with the local authority.

Month 46: Hire your first apprentice. Train them on cutting, grinding, and clamping. Buy a plasma cutter. Start offering a one-year warranty on structural work. Visit 10 construction sites and 5 factories. Give out 20 business cards and a simple flyer with photos of your work. Aim for your first contract over $1,000. Save 30% of profit for equipment.

Month 79: Hire a skilled welder. Buy a MIG welder with spool gun if you are doing jobs above 6mm. Get certified to ISO 9606 or the national equivalent. Create a QC logbook. Start bidding on small government tenders. Increase your prices by 10% if you are fully booked. Move to a larger workshop if you have outgrown your space. Aim for monthly revenue of $3,000$5,000.

Month 1012: Buy a press brake and shear if you are bending and cutting your own sheets. Hire a second welder or a dedicated finisher. Apply for a business loan or grant using your logbooks and reference letters. Target one large contract worth $10,000 or more. Offer training to other welders for a fee$50 per day per student. By month 12, your goal is to have a team of 35 people, a certified workshop, and a waiting list of clients. Review your year: what worked, what did not, and what you will change. Then write your next 12-month plan. Growth is a habit, not an event.

<h2>Practical Reference: Scaling an Aluminium Welding Business in Africa</h2>

<p>Scaling from a single welder working under a shade net to a registered workshop with staff, equipment, and repeat contracts is one of the hardest transitions in African metal fabrication. This reference module consolidates the core decisions you will face over a 12-month growth cycle, with numbers grounded in real African market conditions.</p>

<h2>Hiring and Training Economics</h2>

<p>Hiring is where most growing workshops either stall or explode. The rule: hire when you have three months of confirmed pipeline for the new person, not when you feel busy. In many African markets, apprentices cost less than the value they add within 69 months but only if you train them on aluminium specifically, not just steel.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#a5281b;"> SAFETY WARNING Aluminium Welding Hazards</p>
<p style="margin:0;color:#7b1e13;">Aluminium welding produces ozone, aluminium oxide fumes, and intense UV reflection off bright surfaces. In hot, poorly ventilated African workshops, this causes <strong>metal fume fever, respiratory damage, and arc eye within hours</strong>. Never weld aluminium without: (1) local exhaust ventilation or cross-flow fans, (2) a P2/P3 respirator when welding inside, (3) auto-darkening helmet rated for aluminium AC TIG, (4) leather gloves and long sleeves bare skin under bright light leads to severe UV burns. Aluminium is also highly reflective; keep the work area free of shiny surfaces that bounce arc light into other workers' eyes.</p>
</div>

<h2>Certification and Quality Standards</h2>

<p>Certification is the single biggest gate between small jobs and formal contracts. In aluminium, the relevant standards are typically <strong>ISO 9606-2</strong> (welder qualification for aluminium) and <strong>ISO 3834</strong> (quality requirements for fusion welding). In practice, most African clients construction firms, NGOs, mining suppliers ask for either a certified welder on staff or evidence of a documented QC system. You do not need full ISO certification on day one. Start by:</p>

<ul>
<li>Documenting your welding procedure specifications (WPS) for the aluminium jobs you do most.</li>
<li>Keeping a weld log: job number, material, filler, current, welder, date.</li>
<li>Sending your best welder for a qualification test at a recognised body (KEBS in Kenya, SABS in South Africa, Ghana Standards Authority, etc.).</li>
<li>Building a simple QC file that you can show to any tender committee.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Finding Larger Contracts</h2>

<p>Larger contracts come from visibility and readiness, not from cold calling. In African markets, the most reliable channels are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Subcontracting</strong> to established fabrication firms who already have contracts but need overflow capacity.</li>
<li><strong>NGO and donor projects</strong> often require only business registration, tax compliance, and a bank account.</li>
<li><strong>County / municipal tenders</strong> published in national procurement portals (e.g., Kenya's PPIP, Ghana's PPA).</li>
<li><strong>Construction and mining suppliers</strong> usually demand ISO 9606-2 welders and safety compliance.</li>
<li><strong>Repeat commercial clients</strong> restaurants, hotels, signage companies, and agro-processors need aluminium work continuously.</li>
</ul>

<p>Before you chase any large contract, verify you can finance the materials. Most tender losses for small workshops are not technical they are cash-flow failures while waiting for payment.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>

<ol>
<li>Register the business (name, TIN, bank account) before approaching formal clients.</li>
<li>Keep 3 months of working capital separate from equipment money.</li>
<li>Buy AC/DC TIG first aluminium capability is the differentiator.</li>
<li>Hire only when you have confirmed pipeline, not when you feel busy.</li>
<li>Train every new hire on aluminium safety before they touch a torch.</li>
<li>Qualify at least one welder to ISO 9606-2 within 12 months.</li>
<li>Maintain a weld log and QC file from day one tenders require it.</li>
<li>Never accept a contract you cannot finance through to payment.</li>
</ol>
