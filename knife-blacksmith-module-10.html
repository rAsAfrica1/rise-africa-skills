1. Sourcing Steel and Materials in African Markets

Welcome to Module 10, Lesson 1. This lesson focuses on the practical and business-critical task of sourcing steel and materials for knife blacksmithing in African markets. Whether you are in Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, or a smaller town, your ability to find consistent, affordable, and workable steel determines your product quality, production speed, and profit margin. We will cover where to look, how to identify good steel, how to negotiate, and how to build a reliable supply chain. First, understand that Africa is rich in scrap steel. Vehicle leaf springs, coil springs, axles, bearings, and agricultural implements are everywhere. These are often high-carbon or medium-carbon steels that make excellent knives. Your first stop should be auto repair garages, scrap yards, and metal fabrication workshops. Build relationships with the owners. Bring a small gift or buy them tea. Explain that you need specific parts, not just any scrap. Leaf springs from trucks and buses are ideal for heavy choppers and machetes. Coil springs from cars work well for smaller blades. Bearings can be forged into punches, chisels, and small knives. Axles are tough but can be used for tools. Always ask for clean, rust-free pieces when possible. Rust is not a dealbreaker, but heavy rust means more grinding and less usable steel. Next, learn to identify steel types without a lab. The spark test is your best friend. Take a sample to a grinder in the dark or shade. Press the steel against the wheel. Observe the sparks. High-carbon steel produces bright, dense, short sparks with many bursts. Medium-carbon steel has fewer bursts and longer sparks. Low-carbon steel (mild steel) produces long, straight, yellow sparks with few bursts. You want medium to high carbon for knives. If you cannot grind, use a file test. A good knife steel will resist a file. If the file bites deeply and easily, it is likely mild steel. If it skates or bites lightly, it has more carbon. Another test is the quench test. Heat a small piece to non-magnetic, quench in oil, then try to break it in a vise. If it snaps with a fine grain, it is hardenable. If it bends, it is likely low carbon. Now, consider buying new steel. In many African cities, you can find steel merchants who sell flat bars, round bars, and sheets. Ask for EN9, EN45, 5160, 1075, 1080, or 1095. These are common spring and tool steels. If they do not know these names, ask for spring steel or high-carbon steel. Some merchants will let you test with a file. Always buy a small sample first, forge it, heat treat it, and test the edge. Do not buy a ton of unknown steel. Build a relationship with one or two suppliers who can consistently provide the same steel. Consistency is more important than getting the cheapest price. Your customers will notice if one knife holds an edge and the next does not. For handles, source local hardwoods. In West Africa, look for iroko, mahogany, ebony, and camwood. In East Africa, consider mvule, mahogany, and olive wood. In Southern Africa, use kiaat, tamboti, and leadwood. Visit timber yards, furniture makers, and sawmills. Ask for offcuts and shorts. These are often sold cheaply or given away. Dry the wood properly. Green wood will crack and shrink. Air-dry for at least six months, or kiln-dry if available. You can also use bone, horn, and antler from local butchers and farmers. Buffalo horn, cow horn, and goat horn are excellent. Clean them well, boil them to remove fat, and dry them. For adhesives, epoxy is best, but it can be expensive. Some smiths use local resins, but they are not as strong. If you use epoxy, buy in bulk to reduce cost. For pins and rivets, use brass, copper, or mild steel rods. You can find these at metal merchants or make them from welding rods. For sheaths, use locally tanned leather. Visit tanneries or leather workers. Cowhide is common. Vegetable-tanned leather is best because it molds well and lasts. If you cannot find it, chrome-tanned leather works but is harder to tool. You can also use canvas, nylon, or wood for sheaths, but leather is preferred by customers. Now, let us talk about business. Your sourcing strategy must match your production volume. If you are a one-person shop making 10 knives a month, you can rely on scrap. If you want to make 100 knives a month, you need consistent suppliers. Start by mapping your local area. List all auto garages, scrap yards, metal merchants, timber yards, and tanneries. Visit them. Take notes. Ask for prices. Negotiate. In many African markets, prices are negotiable. Do not accept the first price. Build relationships. Offer to make a small knife or repair a tool in exchange for steel. Barter is common and respected. Also, consider forming a cooperative with other smiths. Buy steel in bulk together. Share transport costs. This lowers your unit cost. You can also pool resources to buy a truckload of leaf springs. Another option is to contact large companies. Mining companies, construction firms, and farms often discard worn-out springs, drill rods, and cables. Write a letter or visit their maintenance department. Explain that you are a local blacksmith and you can recycle their scrap. Some will sell cheaply or donate. Always be professional. Wear clean clothes. Bring a sample of your work. Show them you are serious. Keep records. Write down every purchase: date, supplier, type of steel, weight, price. This helps you track costs and quality. If a supplier gives you bad steel, you will know. If a supplier is reliable, you can reward them with more business. Finally, think about storage. Steel rusts. Keep it dry. Store under a roof or cover with oil. Wood needs to be kept dry and away from termites. Use a raised platform. Horn and bone need to be kept clean and dry. Label everything. You do not want to mix up your high-carbon steel with mild steel. Use paint or a marker to code your steel. In summary, sourcing is not just about finding materials. It is about building a network, testing quality, negotiating prices, and ensuring consistency. Do this well, and your knives will be better, your costs lower, and your business stronger. 2. Forge Design and Fuel Efficiency for African Conditions

Welcome to Module 10, Lesson 2. This lesson is about building and running a forge that works in African conditions. Whether you are in a rural area with limited electricity or a city with access to gas, your forge is the heart of your shop. A good forge must be hot enough to weld and forge high-carbon steel, efficient with fuel, safe, and affordable. We will cover solid fuel forges, gas forges, and hybrid designs. We will also discuss fuel choices, insulation, airflow, and maintenance. First, understand your fuel options. In Africa, common fuels are charcoal, coal, coke, and gas. Charcoal is widely available but burns fast and produces less heat than coal. It is also expensive in bulk. Coal is better but may be hard to find. Coke is ideal but often imported and costly. Gas (LPG or propane) is clean and easy but requires a tank, regulator, and burner. In many cities, gas is available. In rural areas, charcoal or coal is more practical. Your choice depends on cost, availability, and your production volume. For a solid fuel forge, you need a firepot, tuyere, air supply, and table. The firepot can be made from a car brake drum, a truck rim, or a thick steel plate. A brake drum is excellent because it is deep and durable. Cut a hole in the side for the tuyere. The tuyere is a pipe that brings air into the fire. It should be made of thick steel or cast iron. Angle it slightly downward so ash does not clog it. The air supply can be a hand-crank blower, a vacuum cleaner, a hair dryer, or a bellows. In Africa, many smiths use a car heater blower or a small fan. You can also use a bicycle pump or a bag bellows. The key is to control airflow. Too much air wastes fuel and burns steel. Too little air means low heat. Install a gate valve or a simple flap to adjust air. The table around the firepot can be made of mud, bricks, or steel. Mud is cheap and insulates well. Bricks are durable. Steel is portable. Build a table that is comfortable to work at. The center should be at your waist height. For a gas forge, you need a forge body, insulation, burner, and regulator. The body can be a steel pipe, a bucket, or a drum. Line it with ceramic fiber blanket (kaowool) and coat it with refractory cement. This insulation keeps the heat in and the outside cool. A single burner is enough for a small forge. For larger forges, use two or three burners. The burner can be a simple pipe with a hole for gas and a hole for air. You can buy a venturi burner or build a blown burner with a fan. Gas forges are clean and fast. They reach welding heat in minutes. But they require gas, which can be expensive. They also produce carbon monoxide, so you must work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Now, let us talk about efficiency. Fuel is a major cost. You want to get the most heat from every bag of charcoal or every liter of gas. For solid fuel forges, the biggest waste is too much air. When you are not forging, turn off the air. Do not let the fire roar. Cover the fire with a lid or bricks to reduce burn rate. Use dry fuel. Wet charcoal or coal wastes heat. Store fuel under cover. Use a deep firepot. A deep firepot concentrates heat and uses less fuel. A shallow firepot spreads heat and wastes fuel. Also, use the right size fire. Do not build a huge fire for a small knife. Build a fire just big enough for your work. For gas forges, insulation is key. A well-insulated forge will reach temperature with less gas. Use ceramic fiber blanket at least 1 inch thick. Coat it with refractory cement to protect it from flux and mechanical damage. Use a firebrick for the floor. Keep the forge door closed when not inserting steel. Use a proper regulator. Too much gas pressure wastes fuel. Adjust the pressure to just enough to reach your desired temperature. Another efficiency tip is to batch your work. Instead of heating one knife at a time, heat several pieces and forge them in sequence. This keeps the forge hot and reduces idle time. Also, preheat your steel if you can. In cold weather, steel takes longer to heat. Keep your steel near the forge. For solid fuel, you can also make charcoal yourself. This is a business opportunity. You can burn wood in a pit or drum to make charcoal. This reduces your fuel cost. However, it is labor-intensive and produces smoke. Check local regulations. In some areas, making charcoal is illegal due to deforestation. Use sustainable wood sources. Plant trees. Use prunings and dead wood. For coal, you can sometimes find local coal deposits. In Nigeria, South Africa, and Mozambique, coal is mined. You can buy coal from suppliers. It is often cheaper than charcoal. Coke is better but harder to find. You can also mix fuels. Some smiths start with charcoal and then add coal. Now, let us discuss safety. Forges are hot. They cause burns and fires. Always wear leather gloves, an apron, and safety glasses. Keep a bucket of water or sand nearby. Do not wear synthetic clothes. They melt. Work in a well-ventilated area. Solid fuel forges produce smoke and carbon monoxide. Gas forges produce carbon monoxide. Never work in a closed room. Build your forge in a shed with open sides or under a roof with good airflow. Keep children and animals away. Store fuel safely. Charcoal and coal should be kept dry and away from ignition sources. Gas cylinders should be kept upright and outside the work area. Check for leaks with soapy water. Never use a damaged hose or regulator. Now, let us talk about maintenance. Clean your forge regularly. Remove ash and clinker. Clinker is melted impurities that block airflow. Use a poker to break it up and remove it. Check your tuyere and air supply for blockages. Lubricate blowers and fans. Replace worn parts. For gas forges, check the burner and insulation. If the ceramic fiber is damaged, replace it. If the refractory cement cracks, patch it. Keep a spare burner and hose. Finally, think about your forge layout. You want your anvil, vise, and tools within easy reach. You want your fuel and water bucket nearby. You want good lighting. You want a clear path to the exit. A good layout saves time and prevents accidents. In summary, a good forge is efficient, safe, and suited to your local conditions. Whether you use charcoal, coal, or gas, you can build a forge that works for you. Test your forge. Adjust it. Improve it. Your forge is your most important tool. Treat it well. 3. Heat Treating and Tempering African Steels

Welcome to Module 10, Lesson 3. This lesson is about heat treating and tempering knives made from African steels. Heat treating is the most important step in knife making. It determines whether your knife holds an edge, resists breaking, and lasts. If you get it wrong, even the best forged blade will be useless. If you get it right, a simple leaf spring can become a superb knife. We will cover normalizing, hardening, quenching, tempering, and testing. We will focus on steels commonly found in Africa: leaf springs (5160, EN45), coil springs (5160, 9260), bearings (52100), files (W1, 1095), and agricultural steels. First, understand the basics. Steel is iron with carbon. The amount of carbon and other alloys determines how it responds to heat treatment. High-carbon steel (0.6% to 1.5% carbon) can be hardened. Low-carbon steel (less than 0.3% carbon) cannot. You need to know your steel. If you are using scrap, test it. The spark test and file test from Lesson 1 will help. If you are buying new steel, ask for the grade. If you cannot get the grade, assume it is medium to high carbon and treat it accordingly. Before hardening, you must normalize. Normalizing relieves stress from forging and refines the grain. Heat the blade to non-magnetic (about 750 to 800 degrees Celsius, or cherry red in dim light). Hold it there for a few minutes. Then let it cool in still air. Do not quench. Do this two or three times. This makes the steel softer, easier to grind, and prepares it for hardening. If you skip normalizing, your blade may warp or crack during quenching. After normalizing, you can grind the blade to its final shape. Leave the edge thick, about the thickness of a coin. You will sharpen it after heat treating. Now, hardening. This is the process of heating the steel to its critical temperature and quenching it to make it hard. The critical temperature is when the steel becomes non-magnetic. Heat the blade slowly and evenly. Use a forge, a kiln, or a charcoal fire. Do not overheat. If the steel gets too hot, it will burn and become useless. Watch the color. In dim light, non-magnetic is cherry red. In bright light, it is a dull red. Use a magnet to test. When the magnet no longer sticks, you are at temperature. Hold for a minute or two to ensure even heating. Then quench. The quench medium depends on the steel. For simple high-carbon steels like 1095, W1, and files, water or brine is best. Brine is water with salt (about 10% salt). It cools faster than water. For alloy steels like 5160, EN45, and 52100, oil is best. Use warm oil (about 50 to 60 degrees Celsius). Canola oil, sunflower oil, or commercial quenching oil works. Do not use motor oil. It is toxic and has inconsistent properties. For leaf springs, oil is safer because it reduces the risk of cracking. For bearings, oil is also good. For files, water or brine is good. The quench should be done quickly. Move the blade from the fire to the quench in one second. Submerge the blade vertically, edge down, or at a slight angle. Do not move it around too much. Let it cool until it is warm to the touch, then remove it. Do not quench the handle area if you can avoid it. You want the handle to be soft and tough. You can use a torch to heat only the blade, or you can quench the whole blade and then temper the handle later. After quenching, the blade is hard but brittle. It will snap if you bend it. You must temper it. Tempering reduces hardness and increases toughness. Heat the blade in an oven or a tempering forge to a specific temperature. For knives, the range is 180 to 220 degrees Celsius. The exact temperature depends on the steel and the intended use. For a kitchen knife, you want about 200 degrees Celsius. For a chopper, you want about 220 degrees Celsius. For a small carving knife, you want about 180 degrees Celsius. Hold the blade at that temperature for one to two hours. Then let it cool in air. You can do this two or three times. This is called tempering cycles. It improves toughness. If you do not have an oven, you can use a charcoal fire. Heat the blade until it turns a specific color. Straw yellow is about 180 degrees Celsius. Bronze is about 200 degrees Celsius. Purple is about 220 degrees Celsius. Blue is about 250 degrees Celsius. For knives, you want straw to bronze. Do not go to blue. That is too soft. Use a pan of sand or ash to hold the blade. Heat the pan slowly. Watch the colors. This is called tempering by color. It is not as precise as an oven, but it works. Now, let us talk about testing. After tempering, test the blade. First, check for hardness. Use a file. A good knife should skate a file. If the file bites, the blade is too soft. You can re-harden and re-temper. Second, check for toughness. Put the blade in a vise and bend it. A good knife should bend slightly and return to true. If it snaps, it is too hard. Temper it again at a higher temperature. Third, check edge retention. Cut cardboard, rope, or wood. A good knife should cut many times without dulling. If it dulls quickly, it is too soft. Harden it again. Fourth, check for cracks. Look at the blade under good light. If you see cracks, the blade is scrap. Start over. Now, let us talk about common problems. Warping is common. It happens when the blade is unevenly heated or quenched. To prevent it, normalize well. Heat evenly. Quench straight. If it warps, you can straighten it while it is still warm, using a vise or a hammer. But be careful. It may break. Cracking is also common. It happens when the steel is too hot, or the quench is too fast, or the steel has too much carbon. To prevent it, do not overheat. Use the right quench medium. Normalize before hardening. If it cracks, you cannot fix it. Start over. Soft spots happen when the blade does not cool fast enough. This can be due to insufficient quench volume, or the blade touching the bottom of the quench tank, or the quench medium being too hot. Use a large quench tank. Move the blade around slightly. Use enough oil. Now, let us talk about African steels specifically. Leaf springs are often 5160 or EN45. They are forgiving. They harden well in oil. They temper well. They make excellent choppers and camp knives. Coil springs are similar. They can be 5160 or 9260. They are also forgiving. Bearings are 52100. They are high-carbon and high-chromium. They are less forgiving. They need careful heating and oil quenching. They make excellent small knives and tools. Files are W1 or 1095. They are simple high-carbon steels. They harden in water or brine. They are brittle. They need tempering. They make good small knives. Agricultural steels, like plow discs and harrow tines, are often medium-carbon. They may not harden fully. Test them. If they do not harden, use them for tools or decorative pieces. Finally, think about business. Heat treating is a skill. It takes practice. Do not sell a knife until you have tested it. Your reputation depends on it. Keep records. Write down the steel type, the hardening temperature, the quench medium, the tempering temperature, and the results. This helps you repeat success. If you are making many knives, consider building a heat treating kiln. A kiln gives precise temperature control. It reduces guesswork. It improves consistency. You can build a kiln from firebricks and a propane burner. Or you can buy a small electric kiln. It is an investment. But it pays off. In summary, heat treating is the heart of knife making. Learn it. Practice it. Master it. Your knives will be better, your customers happier, and your business stronger. In the next lesson, we will cover finishing, sharpening, and handle making.

4. Finishing, Sharpening, and Handle Making

Welcome to Module 10, Lesson 4. This lesson covers finishing, sharpening, and handle making. These steps turn a rough forged blade into a beautiful, functional knife that customers will pay good money for. In Africa, where competition can be high, fit and finish set you apart. A knife that looks good and feels good sells faster and for a higher price. We will cover grinding, sanding, polishing, sharpening, handle materials, handle shaping, and finishing. First, grinding. After heat treating, your blade is hard. You need to grind the bevels and sharpen the edge. Use a belt grinder if you have one. If not, use a file and sandpaper. A belt grinder is faster and more precise. You can build one from a motor, a belt, and some wheels. Or you can buy a small one. Use a coarse belt (60 to 120 grit) to set the bevel. Then move to finer belts (220, 400, 800). Keep the blade cool. Dip it in water often. If the blade gets too hot, it will lose its temper. You will see the color change. If that happens, you must re-temper. For a file, use a new, sharp file. Draw file the bevels. Then use sandpaper on a flat surface. Start with 120 grit, then 220, 400, 800, 1000, 1500. You can use water or oil as a lubricant. This is called wet sanding. It gives a better finish. For polishing, use a buffing wheel with polishing compound. Or use a fine sandpaper and then a cloth with metal polish. You can also use a local abrasive like ash or fine sand. But commercial products are better. Now, sharpening. A sharp knife is safe and effective. A dull knife is dangerous. Sharpen the edge at a 15 to 20 degree angle per side. Use a sharpening stone. In Africa, you can find natural stones or buy synthetic stones. Use a coarse stone to set the edge, then a fine stone to polish it. Use water or oil as a lubricant. Finish with a leather strop. A strop is a piece of leather glued to a board. Apply polishing compound. Draw the blade across the strop, edge trailing. This removes the burr and makes the edge razor sharp. Test the edge by cutting paper or shaving arm hair. If it does not cut, keep sharpening. Now, handle making. The handle is as important as the blade. It must be comfortable, durable, and attractive. You can use wood, horn, bone, antler, or synthetic materials. In Africa, hardwoods are abundant. Use iroko, mahogany, ebony, camwood, mvule, olive, kiaat, tamboti, or leadwood. Dry the wood well. Cut it into blocks. You can also use cow horn, buffalo horn, or goat horn. Clean and boil the horn to remove fat. Dry it. You can use bone from cows or camels. Clean it well. Boil it. Dry it. For a full tang knife, you need two handle scales. For a hidden tang knife, you need a block. Shape the handle to fit the tang. Use epoxy to glue the scales. Drill holes for pins. Use brass, copper, or mild steel pins. Peen the pins to hold the scales. For a hidden tang, insert the tang into a hole in the block. Use epoxy. You can also use a ferrule or a bolster. A bolster is a piece of metal between the blade and the handle. It adds strength and beauty. You can make a bolster from brass, copper, or steel. Shape it to fit. Now, shaping the handle. After gluing, the handle is blocky. You need to shape it. Use a file, a rasp, or a grinder. Shape it to fit your hand. A good handle is comfortable. It should not have sharp edges. It should be slightly oval or contoured. Test it by holding it. If it feels good, continue. If not, adjust. Sand the handle with 120, 220, 400, 800, 1000 grit. Then polish it. You can use oil, wax, or varnish. For wood, use linseed oil, tung oil, or beeswax. For horn, use a light oil. For bone, use wax. Polish with a cloth. Now, let us talk about sheaths. A knife needs a sheath. It protects the knife and the user. Leather is best. Use vegetable-tanned cowhide. Cut the leather to shape. Wet it. Mold it around the knife. Stitch it with waxed thread. Use a saddle stitch. It is strong. You can also use rivets or snaps. For a cheaper sheath, use canvas or nylon. But leather sells better. You can decorate the sheath with tooling, stamping, or beads. In Africa, customers appreciate traditional designs. You can add a belt loop. Make sure the knife fits snugly. It should not fall out. But it should be easy to draw. Now, let us talk about business. Finishing and handle making are where you add value. A knife with a beautiful handle and a sharp edge can sell for two or three times more than a rough knife. Take your time. Do not rush. Your customers will notice. Also, consider branding. Put your mark on the blade. You can stamp it with a punch or etch it with acid. A brand builds trust. It tells customers who made the knife. Use a simple logo. Register your business if you can. Keep records of your sales. Know your costs. Calculate your price. Do not undercharge. Many African smiths undercharge because they do not count their time. Count your time. Pay yourself. If you do not, you will burn out. Now, let us talk about tools. You do not need expensive tools. You can make many of them. A belt grinder can be made from a motor and a belt. A buffer can be made from a motor and a wheel. A strop is just leather on wood. A vise is essential. A drill is essential. A hacksaw is essential. A file is essential. Sandpaper is essential. Epoxy is essential. Pins are essential. You can buy these locally or online. If you cannot afford a belt grinder, use files and sandpaper. It takes longer, but it works. Now, let us talk about quality control. Before you sell a knife, check it. Check the edge. Is it sharp? Check the handle. Is it comfortable? Is it secure? Check the sheath. Does it fit? Is it strong? Check the finish. Are there scratches? Is the blade clean? If you find a problem, fix it. Do not sell a defective knife. Your reputation is everything. In summary, finishing, sharpening, and handle making are the steps that turn a blade into a product. Do them well. Take pride in your work. Your customers will come back. In the next lesson, we will cover pricing, marketing, and selling knives in African markets.

5. Pricing, Marketing, and Selling Knives in African Markets

Welcome to Module 10, Lesson 5. This lesson is about turning your knife making skill into a profitable business. You can make the best knives in Africa, but if you cannot price them right, market them well, and sell them consistently, you will not survive. We will cover costing, pricing, marketing, sales channels, customer service, and growth. First, costing. You must know how much it costs to make each knife. This includes materials, fuel, labor, and overhead. Materials: steel, wood, horn, epoxy, pins, leather, thread, sandpaper, belts, polishing compound. Fuel: charcoal, coal, gas, electricity. Labor: your time. Decide an hourly rate. Even if you are a one-person shop, pay yourself. If you do not, you will not know if you are profitable. Overhead: rent, tools, maintenance, transport, phone, internet, taxes, licenses. Add all these up. Divide by the number of knives you make per month. That is your overhead per knife. Now, add your profit margin. A common margin is 30 to 50 percent. If your knife costs 1000 shillings to make, sell it for 1500 to 2000 shillings. Do not be afraid to charge what you are worth. Many African smiths undercharge. They think customers will not pay. But customers will pay for quality. If your knife is better, charge more. Now, pricing. There are different pricing strategies. Cost-plus pricing is what we just described. Market pricing is based on what competitors charge. Value-based pricing is based on what the customer is willing to pay. For custom knives, use value-based pricing. A custom knife for a hunter or a chef can sell for a high price. For simple knives, use market pricing. Check what other smiths charge. Check what imported knives cost. If you can make a better knife for less, you have an advantage. But do not compete only on price. Compete on quality, service, and story. Now, marketing. You need to let people know you exist. Start with your local community. Tell everyone you meet. Give business cards. Put up posters. Attend local markets, fairs, and festivals. Set up a booth. Bring your knives. Let people hold them. Demonstrate cutting. Show them the quality. Use social media. Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, TikTok. Post photos and videos of your work. Show the process. Show the finished knife. Show happy customers. Use hashtags. Join local business groups. Network with other artisans. Partner with leather workers, wood workers, and retailers. Now, sales channels. You can sell directly to customers. You can sell to shops. You can sell online. You can sell at markets. You can sell to tourists. You can sell to hotels, restaurants, and safari companies. Each channel has pros and cons. Direct sales give you the best price. But you have to find customers. Shops give you volume. But they take a cut. Online gives you reach. But you need shipping and payment. Markets give you exposure. But you need to be there. Tourists pay well. But they are seasonal. Hotels and restaurants buy in bulk. But they may want credit. Start with direct sales and markets. Then add shops and online. Now, customer service. Treat every customer like a king or queen. Answer questions. Be polite. If a customer is not happy, fix it. Replace the knife or refund the money. A happy customer tells others. An unhappy customer tells everyone. Keep a record of your customers. Send them a message when you have a new knife. Offer them a discount for repeat business. Now, growth. As your business grows, you will need help. Hire an apprentice. Teach them your skills

<h3>1. Identifying steel before you buy it</h3>

<p>Most African knife smiths work from a mix of sources: vehicle leaf springs, coil springs, bearing races, files, machete blanks, rebar, and imported flat bar. Each has a different carbon content and therefore a different heat-treat recipe. Using the wrong steel wastes charcoal, time, and reputation. Before you buy a batch of scrap, run three cheap tests.</p>

<p><strong>Spark test.</strong> Touch the metal to a running grinder in a shaded area and watch the spark stream (see diagram). Long straight orange sparks with few bursts mean low carbon good for handles and guards, poor for a cutting edge. Short bright yellow sparks with many star bursts mean high carbon this is your blade steel. Dull red short sparks mean cast iron or stainless, which will not harden in a simple forge.</p>

<p><strong>File test.</strong> A new mill file should skate across hardened high-carbon steel and bite into mild steel. After you quench a test coupon, the file tells you in two seconds whether the quench worked.</p>

<p><strong>Break test.</strong> Cut a small coupon, heat to non-magnetic, quench in your oil or water, then snap it in a vise. High-carbon steel snaps with a fine grey grain; mild steel bends and tears. This is the cheapest quality control you own.</p>

<h3>2. Measuring your process so quality repeats</h3>

<p>You cannot sell the same knife twice if every knife is a surprise. Four measurements remove most guesswork.</p>

<p><strong>Temperature by colour and magnet.</strong> A magnet is the cheapest heat-treat tool you can own. Steel becomes non-magnetic at roughly 770 C, which is close to the correct hardening temperature for most simple carbon steels. Use colour as a backup: cherry red in daylight is roughly 800 C. Do not rely on colour alone at dusk your eyes lie.</p>

<p><strong>Hardness by file.</strong> After quench, a file should skate. If it bites, your steel was too cold, your quench too slow, or your steel is low carbon.</p>

<p><strong>Edge geometry by gauge.</strong> A cheap vernier caliper or even a marked card lets you record spine thickness and edge angle. A kitchen knife and a bush knife need different geometry; write down what works for each product line.</p>

<p><strong>Yield by weight.</strong> Weigh your starting bar and your finished knife. The ratio tells you your true material cost per knife, including the steel lost to scale and grinding.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #b03a2e; background:#fdecea; padding:14px 16px; margin:20px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#8c1d13;"> SAFETY WARNING QUENCH OILS, METAL DUST, AND GRINDING</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#3a1a15;">Used engine oil and unknown waste oils are commonly used as quenchants in African workshops. They contain heavy metals, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and other carcinogens, and they produce toxic smoke when hot steel enters them. Work outdoors or under strong ventilation, stand upwind, and never lean over the quench tank. Wear eye protection and a dust mask when grinding metal dust and grinding sparks cause eye injuries and lung damage. Keep a bucket of dry sand or a fire extinguisher within reach; oil quench fires are common and must never be put out with water. Wash hands before eating or smoking. If you can afford it, switch to clean quenching oil or warm water for suitable steels.</p>
</div>

<h3>3. Business records that make you money</h3>

<p>A knife smith who cannot answer "what does this knife cost me?" is working for free and does not know it. Keep three simple records a notebook is enough.</p>

<p><strong>Material log.</strong> Every time you buy or collect steel, write the date, source, weight, cost, and spark-test result. This stops you from re-testing the same batch and lets you trace a failed blade back to its source.</p>

<p><strong>Job card.</strong> For each knife or batch, record: product type, start and finish date, steel used, fuel used, hours worked, and final price. After twenty knives you will know your real cost per product line. This is the single most powerful tool for raising your prices with confidence.</p>

<p><strong>Customer and price list.</strong> Record who buys what, at what price, and how they heard about you. This tells you which market channel market stall, hardware shop, restaurant, tourist lodge, online pays best. Most smiths underprice wholesale and overprice retail; the records show you where the margin really is.</p>

<p>Link these records to the pricing and marketing lesson in this course. When you know your cost, you can quote a wholesale price that still protects your margin, and you can negotiate from data instead of hope.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Test before you buy:</strong> spark, file, and break test every new batch of scrap steel.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a magnet at the forge:</strong> non-magnetic means you are at hardening temperature.</li>
<li><strong>Quench in clean oil or water, outdoors:</strong> never lean over the tank, never quench in unknown waste oil without ventilation.</li>
<li><strong>File-test every blade after quench:</strong> if the file bites, redo the heat treat before finishing.</li>
<li><strong>Weigh input and output:</strong> record steel yield per knife to know true material cost.</li>
<li><strong>Write a job card for every knife or batch:</strong> date, steel, fuel, hours, price.</li>
<li><strong>Review your price list monthly:</strong> adjust for charcoal and steel price changes.</li>
<li><strong>Wear eye protection and a dust mask:</strong> grinding and forging dust cause permanent injury.</li>
</ul>
