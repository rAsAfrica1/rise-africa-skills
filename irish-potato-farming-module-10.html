1. Principles of Potato Storage

Potatoes are living tubers that continue to breathe and lose moisture after harvest. To store them well, you must slow down this breathing without killing the tuber. The goal is to keep potatoes firm, sprout-free, and disease-free for as long as possible. The three key factors are temperature, humidity, and ventilation. For table potatoes, aim for 47C and 9095% relative humidity. For processing potatoes (chips and fries), store at 710C to avoid too much sugar buildup. Never store potatoes in direct sunlight or in hot roomsthey turn green and produce solanine, which is toxic.

Before storage, potatoes need to cure. Curing heals cuts and thickens the skin. Spread potatoes in a shaded, well-ventilated area at 1520C and 8590% humidity for 1014 days. This reduces weight loss and rot. After curing, gradually lower the temperature to the target storage range over 23 weeks. Sudden temperature drops can cause black spot or sweetening.

Sort potatoes before storage. Remove any with cuts, bruises, or signs of disease. One rotten potato can spoil many others. Store only healthy, mature tubers. In Africa, many farmers use traditional stores like pits, clamps, or raised cribs. These work if kept dry and ventilated. For small quantities, use jute sacks in a dark, cool room. Never use airtight plastic bagsthey trap moisture and cause rot.

Regular inspection is critical. Check stored potatoes every two weeks. Remove sprouts, soft spots, or mouldy tubers immediately. Keep rodents and insects out. With good practices, table potatoes can store for 36 months, and processing potatoes for 48 months. Proper storage means less waste and more income.

2. Storage Facility Design and Ventilation

A good storage facility protects potatoes from heat, cold, rain, and pests while allowing air to move. In Africa, you can build a simple but effective store using locally available materials. The basic design is a raised, slatted structure with a roof. The floor should be at least 30 cm above ground to prevent moisture and rodents. Use wooden slats spaced 23 cm apart for walls and floor so air can pass through. Cover the roof with grass thatch, iron sheets, or tarpaulin to block sun and rain. Leave openings at the bottom and top for ventilation. A store of 2 m x 3 m x 2 m can hold about 1,000 kg of potatoes.

Ventilation is the key to preventing heat and moisture buildup. Hot air rises and escapes through top vents, pulling fresh air in from bottom vents. This natural convection works well if the store is not too wide. For larger stores, you may need a small fan. In humid areas, use a dehumidifier or increase ventilation. Never block vents with sacks or debris. Air must flow around every potato. Stack potatoes no more than 1.5 m high to avoid crushing and to allow air circulation. Use crates or mesh bags instead of piling loose.

For bulk storage, consider a forced-air system. A small fan (50100 W) connected to a duct under the pile can push air through. This is useful for curing and cooling. If you cannot afford a fan, rely on passive ventilation and keep the store shaded. In very hot regions, dig a pit store 1 m deep, line it with straw, and cover with a ventilated lid. Pit stores keep potatoes cool but must be kept dry.

Always monitor temperature and humidity. Hang a thermometer and hygrometer inside the store. If temperature rises above 10C for table potatoes, increase ventilation. If humidity drops below 85%, sprinkle water on the floor (not on potatoes). With good design, you can store potatoes for months with minimal loss.

3. Managing Temperature and Humidity

Temperature and humidity control is the difference between a good harvest and a rotten one. Potatoes are 80% water, so they lose weight and quality if humidity is too low. If humidity is too high, mould and bacteria grow. The ideal range is 9095% relative humidity for table potatoes and 9598% for processing potatoes. You can measure humidity with a hygrometer (costs about $1020). If you don't have one, watch for signs: potatoes shrink and feel soft when humidity is low; they feel wet and develop mould when humidity is high.

Temperature control starts at harvest. After curing, lower the temperature slowly. For every 1C drop, potatoes produce less heat and breathe slower. But do not go below 4C for table potatoesthat causes chilling injury and sweetening. For processing potatoes, keep at 710C to avoid sugar accumulation that makes fries dark. In hot African climates, you may need a cool store. A simple evaporative cooler (charcoal cooler) can lower temperature by 510C. Build a double-walled box with wet sand or charcoal between walls. Air passing through the wet layer cools down. This works well in dry areas.

Monitor temperature daily. Use a max-min thermometer. If temperature rises, increase ventilation. If it falls too low, close vents or add insulation. In cold highland areas, potatoes can freeze if stored below 0C. Insulate with straw or move potatoes to a warmer room.

Humidity control: If too dry, place shallow pans of water on the floor or wet the floor. If too wet, increase ventilation and reduce the amount of potatoes per room. Never sprinkle water directly on potatoesthat invites rot. For small quantities, store potatoes in perforated plastic bags or jute sacks. Jute breathes and helps regulate humidity. Check stored potatoes weekly. Remove any that are soft, sprouting, or mouldy. With careful management, you can keep potatoes fresh for 46 months.

4. Disease and Pest Control in Storage

Even healthy-looking potatoes can carry hidden diseases. The most common storage diseases are late blight, dry rot, soft rot, and black scurf. Late blight causes brown, greasy patches and a foul smell. Dry rot creates sunken, wrinkled spots with white or pink mould. Soft rot turns potatoes into a smelly, wet mass. Black scurf leaves black spots on the skin. These diseases spread fast in warm, humid conditions. The best control is prevention: store only clean, undamaged potatoes; cure properly; keep temperature and humidity in the right range; and remove infected tubers immediately.

Pests in storage include potato tuber moth, rats, mice, and ants. Tuber moth larvae tunnel into potatoes, leaving frass (sawdust-like waste). To control moths, use pheromone traps or store potatoes in mesh bags with neem leaves. Neem leaves repel insects. For rats and mice, keep the store clean, seal cracks, and use traps or cats. Avoid poisons that can contaminate potatoes. Ants can be deterred by placing ashes or diatomaceous earth around the store.

Good hygiene is your best defence. Before storing a new crop, clean the store thoroughly. Remove all old potatoes, dust, and debris. Wash walls and floor with a disinfectant (e.g., bleach solution: 1 part bleach to 10 parts water). Let it dry completely. Use clean crates or bags. Never mix old and new potatoes. Inspect stored potatoes every two weeks. Wear gloves and remove any rotten, sprouting, or diseased tubers. Put them in a sealed bag and dispose of them away from the store. Do not compost diseased potatoesburn or bury them deep.

If you see signs of disease, lower temperature to 4C (for table potatoes) to slow spread. Improve ventilation to reduce moisture. For tuber moth, you can use Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) spray, which is safe. In Africa, many farmers use wood ash to dust potatoesit reduces rot and insect damage. With strict sanitation and monitoring, you can keep storage losses below 10%.

5. Processing Potatoes: Chips, Fries, and Starch

Processing turns potatoes into higher-value products. Chips, fries, and starch are the most common. Start with clean, mature potatoes. Wash them thoroughly to remove soil. Peel if desired (peeling reduces yield but improves texture). For chips (crisps), slice potatoes thinly (12 mm) using a mandoline or sharp knife. Soak slices in cold water for 30 minutes to remove excess starchthis makes them crispy. Dry them with a clean cloth. Fry in vegetable oil at 170180C for 23 minutes until golden. Remove and drain on paper towels. Salt immediately. For fries, cut potatoes into 1 cm thick strips. Blanch in hot water (80C) for 5 minutes, then dry. Fry at 160C for 3 minutes, then increase to 180C for 2 minutes until golden. For frozen fries, blanch, dry, and freeze on trays before packing.

For starch, grate or blend raw potatoes with water. Strain through a fine cloth or sieve. Let the liquid settle for 46 hours. A white layer of starch forms at the bottom. Pour off water, add fresh water, stir, and settle again. Repeat 23 times until water is clear. Spread the starch on a tray and dry in the sun or a low oven (50C). Break into powder. Store in airtight containers. About 10 kg of potatoes yields 11.5 kg of starch.

In Africa, processing can be done at home or in small workshops. Use a grater, blender, or even a clean cement block to crush potatoes. For frying, use a deep pan or a local fryer. Maintain oil temperature with a thermometer. Change oil regularlyold oil makes dark, unhealthy products. Package chips and fries in airtight bags or containers. Label with date. Starch can be used in baking, as a thickener, or sold to industries. With simple equipment, you can add value and earn more from your potato harvest.

6. Value-Added Potato Products

Value-added products let you sell potatoes at a higher price and reduce waste. Beyond chips and fries, you can make potato flour, crisps, mashed potato powder, and even animal feed. Potato flour is made by drying peeled, sliced potatoes and grinding them into powder. Use a solar dryer or sun-dry on racks for 23 days until brittle. Grind in a mill. Potato flour is gluten-free and can be mixed with wheat flour for baking. It sells for 23 times the price of fresh potatoes.

Mashed potato powder: Cook potatoes, mash them, and dry the mash on trays at 60C until completely dry. Grind into powder. To use, add hot water and milk. This is convenient for hotels and schools. Crisps (chips) can be flavoured with local spices like chili, paprika, or salt and vinegar. Package in small sachets for sale. Frozen fries are in high demand in citiesblanch, fry lightly, freeze, and pack. You need a freezer, but you can rent space in a cold room.

Animal feed: Small or damaged potatoes can be boiled and mixed with bran or maize to feed pigs and chickens. Never feed raw green potatoesthey are toxic. Cook them well. This reduces waste and feeds your livestock.

To succeed, focus on quality and hygiene. Use clean equipment, wear gloves, and package attractively. Label your products with a name and date. Sell to local shops, schools, restaurants, and markets. Start smalltest the market with 5 kg of chips. Calculate your costs: 1 kg of fresh potatoes costs about $0.50, and 1 kg of chips sells for $510. With consistent quality, you can build a brand. In Africa, potato processing is a growing business. Join a cooperative or train others to increase volume. Always keep records of your costs and sales to know your profit.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Storage and Processing of Potatoes in Africa


This reference distils the operational essentials from the seven lessons in the Storage and Processing module. It is written for the working potato grower, trader, or small processor who needs numbers, not theory. Every figure is drawn from published research or official extension guidance, and every recommendation is framed around one question: does it protect or increase the value of the crop?


Potato is a perishable, high-moisture crop typically 7580% water and in sub-Saharan Africa post-harvest losses of 2040% are common where storage and handling are poor. In Kenya, Rwanda, Uganda, and Ethiopia, potatoes are among the top food-security crops, yet the gap between farm-gate price at harvest and off-season price can be two to three times. That gap is the business opportunity. Storage, grading, and processing are the tools that capture it.



Storage fundamentals at a glance


Potato tubers are living tissue. After harvest they respire, transpire, and, if wounded, heal. Storage is simply the management of three variables temperature, humidity, and air movement to slow respiration and prevent disease. The optimum for table potatoes is 48C with 9095% relative humidity; for processing potatoes destined for chips or fries, 710C is preferred to limit reducing-sugar accumulation that causes dark fry colour. In most of tropical Africa, ambient temperatures of 1830C mean that without ventilation, evaporative cooling, or refrigeration, storage life is measured in weeks, not months.



The single most cost-effective intervention for smallholders is a well-ventilated, shaded, raised-floor store often called a diffused-light store or improved ventilated store. Diffused-light storage keeps tubers in light bright enough to suppress sprouting but not so bright that greening (chlorophyll and solanine accumulation) becomes a problem. This is a proven, low-cost technology used across Kenya, Ethiopia, and Rwanda.





Design and ventilation principles


A functional store does four jobs: keeps rain and sun off the crop, moves air through the pile, excludes rodents and insects, and allows inspection. The raised-floor design tubers on slatted pallets or in crates 3040 cm off the ground is essential in humid climates. Air must move through the gaps, not just over the top. A ridge vent or chimney at the highest point lets warm, moist air escape, while low-level inlets draw in cooler night air. In the diagram above, the two inlet arrows and the chimney create a stack effect: as warm air rises, it is replaced by cooler air from below.


For larger operations, forced-air ventilation with a thermostat-controlled fan is standard. The rule of thumb from commercial storage engineering is 0.050.1 m of air per second per tonne of potatoes during the curing phase, dropping to 0.020.04 m/s/t during long-term holding. Over-ventilation dries and shrivels tubers; under-ventilation causes heat build-up and condensation, which drives soft rot.

Managing temperature and humidity


Curing is the first step and is often skipped. After harvest, hold tubers at 1520C and 8590% RH for 1014 days. This heals cuts and thickens the skin, reducing subsequent rot. Then cool gradually no faster than 0.5C per day to the target holding temperature. Rapid cooling causes condensation and black spot.


Humidity control in tropical stores is mostly about preventing condensation, not adding moisture. The danger point is when warm daytime air cools at night and water condenses on tubers. Good insulation under the roof and continuous ventilation at dusk reduce this. A simple sling psychrometer or a cheap digital thermo-hygrometer (US$1020) is a worthwhile investment; record readings twice daily.



Disease and pest control in storage


The main storage diseases are bacterial soft rot (Pectobacterium spp.), dry rot (Fusarium spp.), late blight (Phytophthora infestans), and silver scurf. The main pests are potato tuber moth (Phthorimaea operculella), which is severe in warm, unrefrigerated stores across Africa, and rodents. The most effective controls are cultural: harvest in dry weather, avoid bruising, cure properly, remove diseased tubers before storage, and keep the store clean between seasons. Tuber moth can be managed with diffused-light storage, pheromone traps, and the biological control Bacillus thuringiensis; chemical fumigants are rarely justified for smallholders and carry residue and safety risks.




SAFETY WARNING Fumigation and chemical treatment of stored potatoes

Fumigants such as phosphine and chloropicrin are highly toxic. They must only be applied by trained, licensed operators using approved respiratory protection, in sealed stores, with clear warning signage and a mandatory re-entry interval. Never enter a fumigated store without self-contained breathing apparatus. In many African countries, on-farm fumigation is restricted or prohibited; check with your national plant protection authority. For smallholder stores, use non-chemical methods diffused light, pheromone traps, Bt, and strict sanitation instead.




Processing: chips, fries, and starch


Processing turns a bulky, perishable tuber into a storable, higher-margin product. The three main value chains are crisps (chips), French fries, and starch. For crisps and fries, the critical quality parameter is reducing sugar content: below 0.1% fresh weight gives light golden colour; above 0.3% gives dark, bitter product and elevated acrylamide. This is why storage temperature for processing potatoes must stay at 710C, and why reconditioning (holding at 1518C for 23 weeks before frying) is used to lower sugars.


For starch, the process is simpler and less quality-sensitive: wash, grind, extract, settle, dry. Small-scale starch production can be profitable where there is a market for food-grade or industrial starch, but margins are thin and water use is high. In Africa, the strongest small-processor opportunities are crisps and frozen fries for urban markets, and dried potato products (flour, flakes, dried chips) for institutional buyers and off-season supply.

Value-added products and business logic


The business case for processing rests on three advantages: price stability, reduced waste, and market access. A grower who sells only fresh tubers at harvest is a price-taker. A grower who stores for three months and sells graded potatoes, or who processes into crisps, captures the off-season premium and the processing margin. In Kenya, the National Potato Council and CIP have supported small processor groups producing crisps and fries for supermarkets and schools. In Rwanda and Uganda, dried potato products and flour are growing segments.


Start small. A single fryer, a hand slicer, a solar dryer, and good packaging can be enough to test a market. Reinvest profits into cold storage or a larger dryer. Track three numbers weekly: cost per kg of raw tubers, yield per kg of product, and selling price per kg. If the margin is under 30%, fix the process before scaling.



Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Harvest dry, handle gently. Bruising is the entry point for most storage rots.

  2. Cure for 1014 days at 1520C and 8590% RH before cooling.

  3. Cool slowly no more than 0.5C per day to 48C (table) or 710C (processing).

  4. Ventilate continuously with raised floors and a ridge vent; avoid condensation at dusk.

  5. Inspect weekly and remove diseased or sprouting tubers immediately.

  6. Never mix seed, table, and processing potatoes in the same store.

  7. For processing, test reducing sugars before frying; recondition at 1518C if needed.

  8. Keep records of temperature, humidity, weight loss, and margins data drives better decisions.
