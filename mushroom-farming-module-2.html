Lessons

1. Fungal Anatomy and Classification

Mushrooms are fungi, not plants. They lack chlorophyll and cannot make their own food. Instead, they digest dead organic matter like straw, sawdust, or cottonseed hulls. A mushroom has two main parts: the mycelium, a network of fine white threads that grows inside the substrate, and the fruiting body, which is the part you eat. The fruiting body includes the cap (pileus), gills (lamellae) under the cap, and stem (stipe). Some have a ring (annulus) or cup (volva) at the base. In Africa, the most commonly cultivated mushrooms are oyster (Pleurotus spp.) and button (Agaricus bisporus). Oyster mushrooms are easier for beginners because they grow on pasteurized straw and need less controlled conditions. Button mushrooms require compost and cooler temperatures. Classification: Kingdom Fungi, Phylum Basidiomycota (most edible mushrooms), Class Agaricomycetes, Order Agaricales. Oyster mushrooms belong to the genus Pleurotus, with species like P. ostreatus (grey oyster) and P. pulmonarius (phoenix oyster). Knowing the difference matters: oyster mushrooms have decurrent gills (running down the stem) and no volva, while deadly Amanita species have a volva and white spores. Always identify mushrooms correctly. For farming, buy certified spawn from reputable suppliers like those in Kenya, Nigeria, or South Africa. Never use wild spawn unless you are trained. The anatomy affects how you handle them: the mycelium is sensitive to heat and contamination, so keep your workspace clean. The fruiting body develops only when conditions are right, which we will cover later. In this lesson, focus on the basics: mycelium = vegetative part, fruiting body = reproductive part. Mushrooms reproduce via spores produced on gills. Each mushroom can release millions of spores, but in farming we propagate via mycelium on grain or sawdust. Understanding this prevents mistakes like exposing spawn to direct sunlight or mixing species.

2. Spore Germination and Mycelium Growth

Spores are like tiny seeds, but they are microscopic and produced in huge numbers. When a spore lands on a suitable substrate with moisture, it germinates and sends out a hypha (a single thread). Hyphae branch and fuse to form mycelium, the white fuzzy network you see on bread mould or on a colonized substrate. In mushroom farming, we usually skip spores and start with spawnmycelium grown on grain or sawdust. Spawn is made by inoculating sterile grain with a pure culture. You can buy spawn from suppliers or make your own using tissue culture from a fresh mushroom. For beginners, buying spawn is safer. Spore germination requires: moisture (not soaking wet), nutrients (like sugars from grain), and a temperature around 24-28C for oyster mushrooms. Darkness is not required but light can inhibit some species. Mycelium growth is the vegetative phase. It needs oxygen, so substrates must not be compacted. It also produces heat and CO2. In a bag, you will see condensation and the substrate turning white. Healthy mycelium is thick, white, and smells like mushrooms. Contaminated mycelium may be green (Trichoderma), black (Aspergillus), or slimy (bacteria). To grow mycelium successfully: pasteurize substrate (e.g., straw in hot water at 65-80C for 1 hour), drain, cool, then mix with spawn at 5-10% of wet substrate weight. Pack into bags or bottles with breathable filters. Incubate in a dark room at 25C. Oyster mycelium fully colonizes a 5 kg bag in 10-14 days. Button mushrooms take longer (3-4 weeks). During colonization, do not open bagscontamination risk. Check daily for temperature (should not exceed 30C) and contaminants. If you see green, remove the bag immediately. Mycelium growth is the foundation: strong mycelium means good yields. Weak mycelium from old spawn or poor substrate will give few mushrooms. In Africa, many farmers use rice straw, wheat straw, or maize cobs. Chop to 2-5 cm pieces. Always use clean water. This stage is about patience and hygiene.

3. Fruiting Body Development

Once mycelium has fully colonized the substrate, it needs a trigger to form fruiting bodies. This is called pinning. Pins are tiny mushroom primordia that emerge from the mycelium. They look like small white dots or bumps. For oyster mushrooms, pins appear within 3-5 days after you introduce fresh air and light. The fruiting body develops in stages: pinhead, young mushroom, mature mushroom. The cap expands, gills become visible, and spores are released. In oyster mushrooms, the fruiting body is shelf-like with a short stem. In button mushrooms, it has a distinct cap and stem with gills that start pink then turn brown. Development requires: high humidity (85-95%), fresh air exchange (FAE), light (for direction, not photosynthesis), and a slight drop in temperature (e.g., from 25C to 18-22C for oysters). CO2 levels must be low; if too high, you get long stems and small caps (leggy mushrooms). For button mushrooms, a casing layer (peat moss + limestone) is added after colonization to induce fruiting. The casing must be kept moist. Fruiting bodies grow quickly: oyster mushrooms can double in size every 24 hours. Harvest before spores drop, usually when the cap edges are still slightly curled down. If you wait too long, spores drop and the mushroom becomes tough and messy. In Africa, many farmers grow oysters in simple humid rooms or even under trees with shade nets. You can use a bucket with holes or hanging bags. The key is to monitor daily: check humidity with a hygrometer (aim 85-90%), mist walls and floor (not directly on mushrooms), and open vents for FAE. If pins turn brown and dry, humidity is too low. If stems are long and thin, increase FAE. Fruiting happens in flushes: you get a group of mushrooms, then a rest period, then another flush. Oyster mushrooms typically give 3-4 flushes over 2-3 months. Proper development ensures good market quality. Handle mushrooms gentlythey bruise easily.

4. Substrate Colonization

Substrate is the material mushrooms grow on. It provides nutrients and a place for mycelium to spread. Common substrates in Africa: rice straw, wheat straw, maize stover, cottonseed hulls, sawdust (hardwood, not softwood), coffee husks, banana leaves, and water hyacinth. Each has different properties. Straw is cheap and widely available. Sawdust needs supplementation with bran (10-20%) for better yields. Cottonseed hulls are excellent but may be expensive. Before use, substrate must be pasteurized or sterilized to kill competitors. Pasteurization: soak chopped straw in hot water at 65-80C for 1-2 hours. You can use a drum with a fire underneath. Sterilization: for sawdust, use a pressure cooker or steam for 2-3 hours at 121C. After heating, drain excess water. The substrate should be at field capacity: squeeze a handful, and only a few drops come out. Moisture content around 65-70%. Then cool to below 30C before adding spawn. Mix spawn thoroughly at 5-10% of wet weight. For a 10 kg bag, use 500 g to 1 kg spawn. Pack into bags or bottles. Make sure bags have filter patches for gas exchange. Colonization: place in a dark room at 24-28C. Do not stack bags too high; heat builds up. Check daily. White mycelium should spread from the spawn grains. After 10-14 days for oysters, the bag should be completely white. If you see any green, black, or pink patches, remove that bag. A sour smell means bacterial contamination. Successful colonization is when the substrate is firm and white. At this point, the bag is ready for fruiting. In Africa, many farmers use the 'bottle technique' for small-scale: fill plastic bottles with substrate, sterilize, inoculate, and colonize. This reduces contamination. Another method is the 'bag log' used for shiitake. For oysters, the simplest is the 'hanging bag' method: colonized bags are hung and slit to allow mushrooms to grow out. Always keep records: date of spawning, temperature, and observations. Colonization is the silent phase; it determines your yield. If contamination occurs, clean the area with 70% alcohol or bleach solution. Never reuse contaminated substrate.

5. Environmental Triggers for Fruiting

Mushrooms fruit when environmental conditions change. The main triggers are: temperature drop, fresh air exchange (FAE), light, and humidity. For oyster mushrooms, a drop from 25C to 18-22C signals fruiting. In nature, this happens after rain or at night. In your grow room, you can use a fan or open vents to lower temperature and CO2. CO2 levels should be below 800 ppm for oysters. High CO2 causes long stems and small caps. Light: mushrooms need light to know which way to grow, but not for photosynthesis. A 12-hour light cycle from a fluorescent bulb or indirect sunlight is enough. Direct sunlight will dry and kill pins. Humidity: keep relative humidity at 85-95%. Use a hygrometer. Mist the floor and walls, not the mushrooms. If you mist mushrooms directly, they can get bacterial blotch. FAE: exchange air 4-6 times per day. You can use a small fan on a timer or manually open vents. For button mushrooms, the triggers are different: a drop to 16-18C, high humidity, and a casing layer. The casing layer (peat moss + limestone) is applied after colonization and kept moist. It provides a reservoir of moisture and bacteria that stimulate fruiting. In Africa, many farmers use simple evaporative cooling: hang wet sacks on windows, or use a fan blowing over a water tray. In dry areas, you may need a humidifier. In humid areas, you may need a dehumidifier to prevent mould. Monitor daily: check temperature (min-max thermometer), humidity, and CO2 (if you can afford a meter). Adjust by opening/closing vents, turning on fans, or misting. Fruiting usually starts 3-7 days after triggering. Pins appear as small white dots. Do not disturb them. If pins dry out, they abort. If you see pins, maintain conditions. Harvest within 2-4 days. After harvest, clean up debris and let the substrate rest for 7-10 days before the next flush. The environmental triggers are the same for most gourmet mushrooms, but check species-specific needs. For example, shiitake needs a cold shock and soaking. Oysters are more forgiving. Always keep a log of what works.

6. Harvesting Stages and Indicators

Harvesting at the right time gives you the best quality and shelf life. For oyster mushrooms, harvest when the cap edges are still slightly curled down and before they flatten or turn upward. The gills should be clean and white to cream. If you wait until spores drop, the mushrooms become messy and less attractive. Spores can also cause allergies. For button mushrooms, harvest when the cap is closed (buttons) or slightly open (flats). The veil should be intact for buttons. If the veil breaks and gills are exposed, they are still edible but have shorter shelf life. For shiitake, harvest when the cap is 70-80% open and the edges are still curled in. For milky mushrooms, harvest when caps are convex. Indicators to watch: size (varies by species; oysters 5-15 cm across), colour (should be uniform), firmness (firm, not spongy), and moisture (dry surface but not cracked). Harvest by twisting and pulling gently, or cutting with a clean knife at the base. Do not pull out the substrate. For oyster mushrooms, you can harvest the whole cluster at once. Use clean hands and tools. After harvest, place mushrooms in a clean container. Do not wash them; just brush off debris. Store in a paper bag or perforated plastic bag in a cool place (4-8C). They last 5-7 days. For market, grade by size and appearance. In Africa, fresh mushrooms sell quickly in urban markets, hotels, and restaurants. You can also dry them for longer storage. Harvest in the morning when it's cool. After harvesting, remove any remaining stems and debris from the substrate. This reduces contamination. Then let the substrate rest for 7-10 days. During rest, keep humidity lower (70-80%) and stop misting. After rest, you can trigger another flush by repeating the environmental triggers. Typically, you get 3-4 flushes. The first flush is the largest. Record your yields: weight per bag, number of mushrooms, and any issues. This helps you improve. Always handle mushrooms gentlythey bruise easily. Use a sharp knife to cut the stem cleanly. If you see any mould on harvested mushrooms, discard them. Proper harvesting ensures repeat customers and good income.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Mushroom Biology & Life Cycle for African Growers

This practical reference distils the biology you need to run a commercial mushroom operation in Africa. It is written for the grower, not the mycologist: every section links back to yield, contamination control, cost, and market timing. Use it alongside the seven lessons in this module as a field manual you can pin to the wall of your growing house.

In most African markets, the highest-margin species for small and medium enterprises are oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus ostreatus, P. pulmonarius, P. eryngii), shiitake (Lentinula edodes) in cooler highland zones, and button mushrooms (Agaricus bisporus) where cold-chain and compost infrastructure exist. The biology below applies broadly; species-specific notes are flagged.

1. Anatomy and Classification What Matters Commercially

Mushrooms are fungi in the phylum Basidiomycota (most cultivated species) or Ascomycota (e.g. morels, some medicinal fungi). The parts you manage are the mycelium (the vegetative network that eats substrate), the primordia (pinheads), and the fruiting body (the marketable product). The fruiting body has a cap, gills or pores, a stem, and often a ring or volva. For grading and pricing, cap size, stem length, and colour uniformity are the traits buyers pay for.

Classification drives your production plan: Pleurotus species are primary decomposers that grow on pasteurised agricultural waste a competitive advantage in Africa where cottonseed hulls, maize cobs, rice straw, banana leaves, and sawdust are cheap. Agaricus requires composted substrate and tighter climate control, a higher-capex route. Know your species before you build.

2. Spore Germination and Mycelium Growth

Spores germinate into hyphae that fuse to form dikaryotic mycelium. In practice, growers skip spores and buy spawn grain or sawdust colonised by proven mycelium. Spawn quality is the single largest determinant of first-flush yield. Store spawn at 26 C, out of direct sun, and use within the supplier's window.

Mycelium grows radially, extending 210 mm per day under good conditions. It secretes enzymes that break down cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. This is why substrate particle size and moisture matter: too fine and it compacts anaerobically; too coarse and colonisation is slow and contamination wins the race.

3. Substrate Colonisation The Make-or-Break Phase

Colonisation is a race between your mycelium and competitors (green mould Trichoderma, bacteria, and insect pests). Win it with three levers: clean spawn, correct moisture (6575% for most Pleurotus substrates), and pasteurisation or sterilisation. In African smallholder systems, hot-water pasteurisation at 6070 C for 6090 minutes, or lime (calcium hydroxide) soaking, are low-cost and effective. Steam sterilisation is preferable for sawdust and supplemented substrates.

Maintain substrate temperature at 2228 C for Pleurotus; Lentinula prefers 2024 C. Keep the colonisation room dark or dimly lit, with passive or filtered air exchange. Full colonisation typically takes 1428 days depending on species, substrate, and spawn rate (use 510% spawn by wet weight for reliable results).

4. Fruiting Body Development and Environmental Triggers

When mycelium has fully colonised the substrate, it needs a trigger to shift from vegetative growth to reproduction. The classic triggers are:

  • Temperature drop (cold shock) e.g. from 26 C to 18 C for oyster mushrooms.
  • Fresh air exchange lowering CO below 8001,000 ppm.
  • Light 5001,000 lux for 12 hours a day; essential for normal cap and stem development.
  • Humidity 8592% RH at the pinning surface.
  • Mechanical disturbance some growers scratch or "ruffle" the casing or bag surface.

Pinheads appear within 37 days of triggering. Over the next 35 days they enlarge into harvestable fruiting bodies. Poor triggers produce long stems, small caps, and low market value a direct revenue loss.

5. Harvesting Stages and Indicators

Harvest timing determines shelf life, price, and the size of the next flush. For Pleurotus, harvest when the cap margin is still slightly inrolled and before spores are released (spore drop causes allergies and reduces shelf life). For Agaricus, harvest at the "closed veil" stage for premium fresh market, or "open veil" for processing. For shiitake, harvest when the cap is 7080% expanded and the edge is still slightly curled.

Twist and pull, or cut with a clean knife at the base. Handle with clean gloves; bruising is a major post-harvest loss. Cool to 4 C within 2 hours of harvest where cold chain exists; otherwise sell within 24 hours and use evaporative cooling or shade to extend shelf life.

SAFETY WARNING Do not eat unidentified mushrooms.

Africa has many wild fungi, some of which are deadly (e.g. Amanita phalloides and related species). Never consume a wild mushroom unless it has been identified by a qualified mycologist. Never sell wild-collected mushrooms to the public. Always keep spawn, cooked mushrooms, and cleaning chemicals separate from wild specimens. Grow only from certified spawn of known edible species. Wash hands and tools after handling substrate and before handling food.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Buy certified spawn from a reputable supplier; record batch and date.
  2. Pasteurise or sterilise substrate 6070 C for 6090 min, or lime soak, or steam.
  3. Target 6575% substrate moisture; squeeze test: a few drops, not a stream.
  4. Use 510% spawn by wet weight; mix thoroughly and pack evenly.
  5. Colonise in the dark at 2228 C; keep the room clean and pest-free.
  6. Trigger fruiting with cold shock, fresh air, light (5001,000 lux), and 8592% RH.
  7. Harvest before spore drop; handle with clean gloves; cool fast.
  8. Track yield per bag and per flush; cull contaminated bags immediately and away from the grow room.
