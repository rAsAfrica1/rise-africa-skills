1. Phase Feeding Programs for Growing-Finishing Pigs

Phase feeding means changing the diet as pigs grow, instead of feeding one mix from weaning to market. Pigs need different nutrient levels at different weights. A common program in Africa uses three phases: nursery (8â20 kg), grower (20â50 kg), and finisher (50â90 kg). Each phase has a different crude protein (CP) target: nursery 20â22%, grower 16â18%, finisher 14â16%. You also adjust lysine, the first limiting amino acid. For example, nursery diet needs 1.2â1.3% lysine, grower 0.9â1.0%, finisher 0.7â0.8%. If you feed a high-protein diet too long, you waste money and the pig excretes excess nitrogen. If you feed a low-protein diet too early, the pig grows slowly and may become fat.

To build a phase feeding program, start with locally available ingredients. In many African countries, you can use maize, sorghum, or cassava chips for energy; soybean meal, sunflower cake, or fish meal for protein; and wheat bran or rice bran for fibre. For a 100 kg batch of nursery diet, you might use 55 kg maize, 25 kg soybean meal, 10 kg fish meal, 7 kg wheat bran, and 3 kg of a mineral-vitamin premix plus lysine and methionine. For grower, use 65 kg maize, 20 kg soybean meal, 10 kg wheat bran, 2 kg fish meal, and 3 kg premix. For finisher, use 70 kg maize, 15 kg soybean meal, 10 kg wheat bran, 2 kg premix, and 3 kg limestone or bone meal.

Weigh pigs every two weeks to decide when to switch phases. A simple hanging scale or a livestock weigh tape works. If average weight is below the target for the phase, delay the switch by one week. If pigs are growing faster, switch earlier. Always mix feeds thoroughly on a clean concrete floor or in a drum mixer. Store in dry, ventilated bags away from sunlight. Never use mouldy maize â it causes aflatoxin poisoning. Clean water must be available at all times. With good phase feeding, you can save 10â15% on feed cost and reach market weight in 5â6 months.

2. Diets for Lean Growth and Carcass Quality

Lean growth means more muscle and less fat. Consumers in Africa often prefer lean pork, and lean carcasses fetch higher prices. To produce lean pigs, you must balance energy and protein. Too much energy (from maize, cassava, or oil) makes pigs fat. Too little protein slows muscle growth. The key is the lysine to energy ratio. For a 50 kg pig, aim for 0.9% lysine and 14 MJ digestible energy per kg feed. For a 70 kg pig, 0.75% lysine and 13.5 MJ DE/kg. You can increase lean growth by adding synthetic lysine, methionine, and threonine. These amino acids are cheap and allow you to lower crude protein without hurting performance. For example, replace 2% soybean meal with 0.2% L-lysine and 0.05% DL-methionine.

Use ingredients that support lean growth. Good protein sources include soybean meal, fish meal, and cottonseed meal (limit to 5% because of gossypol). Avoid high-fat ingredients like palm kernel cake or full-fat soy if you want lean carcasses. Instead, use maize or sorghum as the main energy source. Add 1â2% vegetable oil only if pigs need extra energy in cold weather. Minerals matter too: calcium and phosphorus for bone, and zinc for skin and muscle. A typical lean finisher diet: 68 kg maize, 18 kg soybean meal, 8 kg wheat bran, 3 kg fish meal, 1 kg limestone, 1 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, 0.5 kg premix. Mix well.

Slaughter at the right weight. Pigs grown too heavy (over 100 kg) deposit more fat. In most African markets, slaughter at 90â100 kg live weight. Use a weigh tape or scale. If you sell to a butcher, ask for feedback on carcass fat. Adjust the diet if pigs are too fat: reduce energy by 5% and increase lysine by 0.1%. If too lean, add 2% maize. Always provide clean water and keep pigs comfortable â heat stress reduces lean growth. With proper formulation, you can achieve 55â60% lean meat yield.

3. Feeding Strategies for Lactating Sows to Maximize Milk Output

A lactating sow produces 5â8 litres of milk per day, which takes a lot of energy and protein. If she does not eat enough, she loses body condition and milk drops, and piglets wean lighter. The goal is to feed 2 kg of feed for maintenance plus 0.4 kg per piglet she is nursing. For a sow with 10 piglets, that is 2 + (10 x 0.4) = 6 kg per day. Feed a diet with 16â18% crude protein, 0.9â1.0% lysine, and 14 MJ DE/kg. Use highly digestible ingredients: maize, soybean meal, fish meal, and wheat bran. Avoid too much fibre â it fills the gut but gives little energy. Add 3â5% vegetable oil to increase energy density without bulk.

Feed the sow at least twice a day, and give water freely. A sow drinks 20â30 litres daily when lactating. If she is hot, she eats less. In tropical Africa, feed early morning and late evening when it is cool. Wet feeding â mixing feed with water to a porridge â can increase intake by 10â15%. Provide a creep feed for piglets from 7 days old so they start eating solid food and reduce demand on the sow. Keep the farrowing pen clean and dry. If the sow is too thin (you can see her backbone), increase feed by 0.5 kg/day. If too fat, reduce energy but do not drop below 4 kg/day.

A good lactating sow diet for a 100 kg batch: 60 kg maize, 22 kg soybean meal, 8 kg wheat bran, 5 kg fish meal, 2 kg vegetable oil, 1 kg limestone, 1 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, 0.5 kg premix. Mix and store in a cool dry place. Never feed mouldy feed â it can cause abortion or poor milk. After weaning, feed the sow a maintenance diet (2â2.5 kg/day) until she is mated again. Good lactation feeding gives heavier piglets and shorter weaning-to-service interval.

4. Gestating Sow Diets for Fetal Development and Body Condition

A gestating sow needs less feed than a lactating sow, but the diet must be balanced for fetal growth and her own body condition. Overfeeding makes her fat and causes farrowing problems. Underfeeding makes piglets small and weak. A common target is 2.0â2.5 kg of feed per day for a 150â200 kg sow. The diet should have 12â14% crude protein, 0.5â0.6% lysine, and 12â13 MJ DE/kg. Use more fibre than in lactation â 10â15% wheat bran or rice bran is fine. Fibre keeps the sow satisfied and prevents constipation. Add 1â2% vegetable oil only if she is too thin.

Body condition is your best guide. Use a body condition score (BCS) from 1 (very thin) to 5 (very fat). Aim for 3. Check by feeling the backbone and hip bones â they should be covered but not buried. If BCS is below 3, increase feed by 0.5 kg/day. If above 3.5, reduce by 0.5 kg/day. Weigh or condition-score sows every two weeks. In the last month of gestation, increase feed slightly (by 0.5 kg/day) because piglets grow fast. But do not overfeed â too much energy in late gestation can reduce milk production later. Provide clean water always.

A typical gestation diet for 100 kg: 65 kg maize, 15 kg soybean meal, 15 kg wheat bran, 2 kg fish meal, 1 kg limestone, 1 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, 0.5 kg premix. Mix well. If you cannot get soybean meal, use 10 kg sunflower cake and 5 kg fish meal, but add 0.1% lysine. Never feed mouldy maize â aflatoxin causes stillbirths. Keep sows in groups if possible, but feed individually to control intake. A good gestation feeding programme gives strong piglets and healthy sows that breed back quickly.

5. Diets for Weanling Pigs: Managing the Weaning Transition

Weaning is the most stressful time in a pig's life. At 3â4 weeks old, piglets are suddenly separated from the sow, moved to a new pen, and switched from milk to solid feed. Their digestive system is not fully developed, so they often stop eating, lose weight, and get diarrhoea. The goal is to get them eating a highly digestible diet quickly. Use a nursery diet with 20â22% crude protein, 1.2â1.3% lysine, and 15 MJ DE/kg. Ingredients must be very digestible: cooked maize, soybean meal, fish meal, skim milk powder (if available), and cooked cereals. Avoid high-fibre ingredients like wheat bran or sunflower cake â they cause gut problems. Add 5â10% dried whey or 3â5% vegetable oil for energy. Include 0.3% salt and a good nursery premix with zinc oxide (for diarrhoea control) and organic acids.

Feed small amounts many times a day â 4 to 6 times â for the first week. Use shallow feeders and clean them daily. A typical first nursery diet for 100 kg: 50 kg cooked maize, 20 kg soybean meal, 10 kg fish meal, 10 kg dried whey, 5 kg vegetable oil, 2 kg limestone, 1.5 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, 1 kg premix. Mix with a little water to make a wet mash for the first 2 days â this encourages eating. Always provide fresh water. Keep the pen warm (28â30Â°C) and dry. If piglets have diarrhoea, reduce feed by 20% for one day and add electrolytes to water.

After 7â10 days, gradually switch to a cheaper grower diet over 5 days. Weigh piglets weekly. A good weanling programme gives 300â400 g average daily gain and less than 5% mortality. If you cannot afford fish meal, use 15 kg soybean meal and 5 kg skim milk powder, but add 0.2% lysine and 0.1% methionine. Never use mouldy feed â it kills weaners fast.

6. Formulating Diets for Organic and Specialty Pork Production

Organic and specialty pork is a growing market in Africa, especially for export or high-end local sales. Organic means no synthetic pesticides, no antibiotics, no GMO feed, and outdoor access. Specialty pork includes free-range, hormone-free, or high-omega-3 pork. To formulate organic diets, you must use certified organic ingredients. Common ones: organic maize, organic soybean meal, organic sunflower cake, and organic fish meal (from sustainable sources). You cannot use synthetic lysine or methionine. Instead, rely on protein blends: 20% fish meal, 20% soybean meal, and 10% sunflower cake can meet amino acid needs. For energy, use organic maize or organic cassava chips. Add organic minerals like limestone, bone meal, and sea salt. A typical organic grower diet: 60 kg organic maize, 20 kg organic soybean meal, 10 kg organic fish meal, 5 kg organic wheat bran, 3 kg limestone, 1 kg bone meal, 0.5 kg sea salt, 0.5 kg organic premix.

For high-omega-3 pork, feed 5â10% flaxseed or 3â5% fish oil in the last 4 weeks before slaughter. This increases omega-3 in the meat. For free-range systems, pigs get some nutrients from pasture, so you can reduce feed by 10â20%. But you must still provide a balanced diet â pasture alone is not enough. Always check local organic certification rules. In many African countries, you can get certified through organisations like IFOAM or local organic networks. Keep records of all feed ingredients and sources.

Specialty pork often sells for 20â50% more than conventional pork. But feed costs are higher â organic maize can cost 30% more than regular. So you must calculate your margin. Use a simple feed formulation spreadsheet or app like Feed Formulation for Pigs (available on Google Play). Test small batches first. Never use antibiotics or synthetic growth promoters. Health comes from good hygiene, clean water, and balanced nutrition. With proper formulation, you can produce organic pork that meets export standards and earns premium prices.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Formulating Diets for Specific Production Goals

In African pig production, feed represents 65â75% of total production cost. Whether you run a 20-sow backyard unit in Uganda or a 500-sow commercial operation in South Africa, the difference between profit and loss often comes down to how precisely you match diets to the pig's stage of production. This practical reference consolidates the key formulation targets, ingredient strategies, and management decisions for each production phase â with locally available feed resources in mind.

Phase feeding is not a luxury for large farms. It is a cost-control tool. Every time you over-formulate protein or energy for a pig that does not need it, you are burning money. Every time you under-formulate for a lactating sow, you are losing piglets and delaying rebreeding. The tables and checklists below give you actionable numbers to work with.

Phase Feeding Programs for Growing-Finishing Pigs

The principle is simple: as pigs grow, their daily protein deposition rate slows relative to their feed intake. A 15 kg weaner needs roughly 1.25% total lysine in the diet; a 90 kg finisher needs only about 0.70%. If you feed one diet throughout, you either overfeed nutrients early or underfeed them late â both cost money.

For African producers, a practical compromise is a three-phase program: weaner (7â25 kg), grower (25â60 kg), and finisher (60â100+ kg). Where feed mixing capacity allows, split the grower phase into early and late for better precision. Diets for Lean Growth and Carcass Quality

Lean growth is driven by lysine and energy. The ratio matters more than the absolute level. A common mistake in African pig production is feeding high-energy maize-heavy diets without balancing amino acids â the result is fat deposition, poor carcass grading, and lower prices at slaughter. For lean gain, keep the lysine-to-energy ratio above 0.75 g lysine per MJ DE during the grower phase. Synthetic lysine, where available and affordable, allows you to reduce soybean meal inclusion and cut costs without sacrificing lean deposition.

Feeding Strategies for Lactating Sows to Maximize Milk Output

A lactating sow producing 8â10 kg of milk daily has the highest nutrient demand of any pig on the farm. If she cannot eat enough to meet that demand, she mobilises body reserves, loses condition, and delays return to oestrus. In tropical conditions, heat stress suppresses appetite further. Practical strategies include:

  • Feed 2.0â2.5 kg/day in late gestation, increasing to 2.5â3.0 kg at farrowing.
  • Ramp up to ad libitum (5â7 kg/day) by day 7 of lactation.
  • Use high-energy, high-lysine diets (1.0â1.1% total lysine, 14.0â14.5 MJ DE/kg).
  • Feed 3â4 times daily during hot weather; wet feeding improves intake.
  • Ensure constant access to clean water â sows drink 20â40 litres/day when lactating.

Gestating Sow Diets for Fetal Development and Body Condition

Gestating sows should be fed restrictively â typically 1.8â2.2 kg/day of a diet containing 13â14% crude protein and 0.55â0.65% lysine. Body condition scoring (BCS) is your primary management tool. Target a BCS of 3.0â3.5 on a 5-point scale. Thin sows (BCS â¤2) need extra feed; fat sows (BCS â¥4) face farrowing difficulties and reduced milk yield. In the last 3â4 weeks of gestation, increase feed by 0.5â1.0 kg/day to support fetal growth and mammary development.

Diets for Weanling Pigs: Managing the Weaning Transition

Weaning is the single most stressful event in a pig's life. At weaning, the piglet loses sow milk, changes environment, mixes with strangers, and must learn to eat dry feed. The result is a growth check, often 3â7 days of near-zero gain. To minimise this, use a creep feed from day 10â14 of lactation, and continue the same diet for 5â7 days post-weaning. The weaner diet must be highly digestible: cooked cereals, skim milk powder or whey, fish meal, and spray-dried plasma (if available). Avoid high levels of soybean meal â it can trigger gut inflammation in young pigs.

Formulating Diets for Organic and Specialty Pork Production

Organic and specialty pork markets are growing in South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria, driven by urban consumers willing to pay a premium. Organic standards prohibit synthetic amino acids, growth promoters, and most conventional feed additives. This means you must rely on high-quality protein sources: soybean meal (non-GMO certified), fish meal, lucerne meal, and legumes. Energy comes from organic maize, sorghum, or cassava. Mineral supplementation must use approved sources. Expect 20â30% higher feed costs â but also 30â50% higher selling prices if you can certify and market effectively.

Maize and groundnut cake â staple energy and protein sources in many African pig diets â are highly susceptible to Aspergillus flavus mould and aflatoxin contamination, especially in humid tropical conditions. Aflatoxin B1 levels above 20 ppb in pig feed cause liver damage, reduced growth, immunosuppression, and can be fatal to young pigs. Never feed mouldy, discoloured, or musty-smelling maize or groundnut cake. Test suspect batches with aflatoxin test kits (available from ILRI and national agricultural research stations). Store all feed ingredients in dry, ventilated conditions on pallets, never directly on the floor. Use a mould inhibitor (e.g., propionic acid) in mixed feed stored longer than two weeks.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Match diet to phase: Never feed one formulation across all growth stages â use at least three phases (weaner, grower, finisher).
  2. Check lysine first: Lysine is the first limiting amino acid in African pig diets. Balance it before adding expensive protein sources.
  3. Body condition score sows: Target BCS 3.0â3.5. Adjust feed by 0.3â0.5 kg/day per half-point deviation.
  4. Maximise lactation intake: Feed lactating sows 3â4 times daily; wet feed in hot weather; ensure 20â40 L clean water daily.
  5. Creep feed early: Introduce creep feed at day 10â14 of lactation to prepare piglets for weaning.
  6. Test for aflatoxin: Every batch of maize, groundnut cake, or cottonseed cake should be visually inspected and periodically lab-tested.
  7. Calculate cost per kg gain, not cost per bag: A cheaper feed with poor FCR can cost more than a premium feed with efficient conversion.
  8. Keep records: Track feed intake, weight gain, and feed cost per pig. Without records, you are guessing.
