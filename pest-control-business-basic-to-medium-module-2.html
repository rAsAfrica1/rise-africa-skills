1. Identifying Common Household Pests: Ants, Bedbugs, Fleas, and Flies

Ants are the most common household pest in Africa. In Nigeria, the sugar ant (Camponotus maculatus) and the black crazy ant (Paratrechina longicornis) invade kitchens and food stores. They follow pheromone trails, so you must clean with soapy water to destroy the scent. Use gel baits containing fipronil or hydramethylnon placed near trails. For every 10 ants you see, there are 100 in the nest. Locate nests by following trails at night with a torch. Treat nests with insecticidal dust (e.g., 0.5% permethrin) applied with a hand duster.

Bedbugs (Cimex lectularius) hide in mattress seams, bed frames, and cracks. They feed at night and leave itchy bites in lines. In African cities like Nairobi and Lagos, bedbug infestations are rising in hostels and apartments. To detect, inspect seams with a torch and look for black fecal spots, shed skins, and live bugs. Treat by steaming at 70Â°C, then apply a residual insecticide like deltamethrin. Repeat after 10 days to kill newly hatched nymphs.

Fleas (Ctenocephalides felis) are common where pets and livestock live. They lay eggs in dust and cracks. Larvae feed on organic matter. Adults jump onto hosts. In rural areas, fleas transmit plague and murine typhus. Control by treating pets with fipronil spot-on, washing bedding in hot water, and spraying floors with a mixture of permethrin and insect growth regulator (IGR) like methoprene. Vacuum daily and dispose of the bag.

Flies (Musca domestica and Chrysomya putoria) breed in rotting organic waste, animal dung, and open latrines. A single fly can carry over 100 pathogens. In markets, fly populations explode within days. Use fly traps with ultraviolet light and sticky boards. For large infestations, apply residual sprays on resting surfaces (walls, ceilings) using lambda-cyhalothrin. Sanitation is key: cover waste bins, clean animal pens daily, and use fly screens on windows. A combination of bait (imidacloprid) and traps reduces populations by 80% in two weeks.

2. Identifying Common Agricultural Pests: Locusts, Fall Armyworms, and Weevils

Locusts (Schistocerca gregaria) are devastating in the Sahel and Horn of Africa. A single swarm can cover 100 kmÂ² and eat 100,000 tonnes of crops per day. In 2020, East Africa suffered the worst outbreak in 70 years. Identify hoppers (wingless nymphs) by their bright yellow and black bands. They march in bands. Adults are pink when immature and yellow when mature. Control by spraying concentrated insecticide (e.g., fenitrothion or malathion) from the ground using motorized knapsack sprayers. For large swarms, aerial spraying is needed. Report sightings to local agricultural offices immediately.

Fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda) attacks maize, sorghum, and rice. The larvae have a white inverted Y on the head and four dark spots on the second-to-last segment. They feed inside the whorl, leaving ragged holes and moist sawdust-like frass. In Ghana, yield losses reach 50% if untreated. Scout fields twice a week. For small farms, handpick larvae and apply sand or wood ash into the whorl. For larger farms, spray with emamectin benzoate or chlorantraniliprole early morning or late evening. Rotate crops with legumes to break the cycle.

Weevils (Sitophilus zeamais and Callosobruchus maculatus) destroy stored grains. The maize weevil bores into kernels, leaving hollow seeds and flour dust. In West Africa, up to 30% of stored maize is lost after six months. Detect by sieving grain and looking for adults (small, dark brown, snout). Control by drying grain to below 13% moisture, storing in hermetic bags (e.g., Purdue Improved Crop Storage bags), and mixing with diatomaceous earth at 1 kg per 100 kg grain. For cowpea weevils, use airtight containers and add a few drops of neem oil.

Integrated pest management (IPM) combines cultural, biological, and chemical methods. For locusts, early detection is critical. For armyworms, plant early and use resistant varieties. For weevils, proper storage is 90% of the solution. Always wear protective gear when handling pesticides. Train farmers to identify pests correctly to avoid unnecessary spraying.

3. Understanding Termite Behavior and How to Detect Infestations

Termites are social insects with three main types: subterranean (Macrotermes), drywood (Cryptotermes), and dampwood. In Africa, subterranean termites cause the most damage to buildings and crops. They build mud tubes from soil to wood, which protect them from predators and drying out. A mature colony can contain 2 million termites. Workers are blind, soft-bodied, and white. Soldiers have large mandibles. Reproductives (alates) swarm after rain to start new colonies.

Detect subterranean termites by looking for mud tubes on foundation walls, wooden posts, and trees. Tap wood; if it sounds hollow, termites are inside. Probe with a screwdriverâif it sinks easily, the wood is eaten out. In fields, look for termite mounds and damaged crops with hollow stems. Drywood termites leave tiny fecal pellets (frass) that look like sawdust. They nest inside furniture, roof timbers, and door frames. Swarmers (winged termites) are often mistaken for flying ants; termites have straight antennae and a thick waist.

Control subterranean termites by trenching and treating soil with termiticide (e.g., imidacloprid or fipronil) at 0.5% concentration. Dig a trench 30 cm deep around the building, pour the solution, and backfill. For active infestations, drill holes into infested wood and inject insecticide or foam. Bait stations containing hexaflumuron are effective but slow (3-6 months). For drywood termites, fumigation with sulfuryl fluoride is best, but localised treatment with borate sprays works for small infestations.

Prevent termites by keeping wood away from soil, ensuring proper drainage, and using treated timber. In rural areas, remove dead trees and stumps near homes. For crops, plant resistant varieties like cassava and maize with hard stems. Regular inspections every 3 months save money. A small mud tube today can become a $2,000 repair in two years. Train your clients to recognise early signs.

4. Rodent Behavior: Rats and Mice in Urban and Rural Settings

In Africa, the most common rodents are the brown rat (Rattus norvegicus), black rat (Rattus rattus), and house mouse (Mus musculus). Brown rats live in burrows near ground level, especially in rubbish dumps and sewers. Black rats are excellent climbers and live in roofs and attics. Mice nest in wall voids and furniture. All three are nocturnal, cautious, and neophobic (fear new objects). They memorise routes and avoid traps for days.

Signs of infestation: droppings (rat droppings are 1-2 cm, mouse droppings 3-6 mm), gnaw marks on wood, plastic, and wires, greasy rub marks along walls, and urine stains under UV light. In rural areas, rats damage stored maize, groundnuts, and rice. A single rat eats 25 g of grain per day and contaminates 10 times more. In cities, rats spread leptospirosis, salmonellosis, and plague. They also cause electrical fires by gnawing wires.

Control requires a three-step approach: sanitation, exclusion, and population reduction. Remove food and water sources. Store grain in metal silos or airtight containers. Seal cracks larger than 6 mm with steel wool and cement. Trim vegetation near buildings. For trapping, use snap traps baited with peanut butter or dried fish. Place traps along walls, not in open spaces. Set traps for 3-5 days without setting them to overcome neophobia. Use 10-20 traps for a small house.

Rodenticides: use anticoagulants like bromadiolone or difenacoum in tamper-resistant bait stations. Never use loose bait where children and pets can access. In rural areas, use 2-3 bait stations per 100 mÂ². Replace bait every 3 days until consumption stops. For large infestations, combine trapping and baiting. Always wear gloves and a mask when handling rodents or cleaning droppings. Disinfect with 10% bleach solution. After control, monitor monthly with tracking powder or chew cards.

5. Mosquito Breeding Sites and Control Strategies in African Climates

Mosquitoes breed in standing water. In Africa, the main vectors are Anopheles gambiae (malaria), Aedes aegypti (dengue, Zika, yellow fever), and Culex quinquefasciatus (filariasis). Anopheles breed in sunlit, clean water like rice paddies, puddles, and tyre tracks. Aedes breed in small containers: discarded tyres, buckets, flower pots, and water storage jars. Culex breed in polluted water: pit latrines, septic tanks, and blocked drains.

A female mosquito lays 100-300 eggs. Anopheles eggs hatch in 2-3 days; larvae become adults in 7-14 days depending on temperature. In hot African climates (30-35Â°C), the cycle can complete in 7 days. This means you must eliminate breeding sites weekly. A single bottle cap of water can produce 50 mosquitoes. In dry season, Aedes eggs survive up to 6 months and hatch when rain returns.

Control strategies: Source reduction is the most effective. Conduct weekly clean-up campaigns. Empty and scrub water containers every 7 days. Cover water storage tanks with tight lids. Fill puddles with soil or sand. Clear blocked drains. For rice paddies, use intermittent irrigation (alternate wet and dry cycles). For large water bodies, apply larvicides like temephos (1% sand granules) at 1 kg per hectare. Use Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti) for organic control.

Adult control: Indoor residual spraying (IRS) with lambda-cyhalothrin or bendiocarb every 6-12 months. Long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) treated with deltamethrin. For outdoor biting, use spatial repellents or fogging with malathion at dusk. In urban areas, fogging reduces adult populations by 70% but must be repeated every 3 days. Community participation is critical. Train households to do a weekly 'mosquito hunt'âa 10-minute check for standing water. In African climates, the rainy season requires daily vigilance. A professional pest control business can offer monthly larval surveys and treatment contracts.

6. Life Cycles of Key Pests and Timing Your Control Measures

Every pest has a life cycle: egg, larva/nymph, pupa (some), and adult. Timing control to the weakest stage saves money and reduces chemicals. For mosquitoes, the larval stage is easiest to kill because they concentrate in water. Apply larvicide every 7 days in warm weather. For flies, target larvae in breeding sitesâremove waste every 3 days to break the cycle. For bedbugs, eggs hatch in 6-10 days, so repeat insecticide after 10 days to kill nymphs before they lay eggs.

Termites: subterranean termites have a gradual life cycle (egg, nymph, adult) with no pupa. The queen lays 1,000 eggs per day. Control is best during swarming season (after first rains) when colonies are vulnerable. Use bait stations then. For drywood termites, treat before swarmers emerge (usually spring). Fall armyworm: eggs hatch in 2-4 days, larvae feed for 14-21 days, then pupate in soil for 7-14 days. Spray when larvae are small (first 3 days) because older larvae hide in whorls. Scout twice weekly and treat when 5% of plants show damage.

Locusts: eggs hatch in 10-20 days. Hopper bands are flightless and easy to spray. Once they become adults, they fly and are hard to control. So time your intervention when hoppers are 2-3 weeks old. Weevils: complete life cycle in 4-6 weeks in stored grain. Females lay 300 eggs. Control by fumigation or hermetic storage before adults emerge. For rodents, they breed year-round, with a gestation of 21-23 days. A pair can produce 1,000 offspring in a year. Control must be continuous, not one-off.

Create a pest calendar for your area. In West Africa, mosquito breeding peaks in rainy season (June-September). Armyworm outbreaks occur in early maize season. Termite swarms happen after first heavy rains. Train your clients to record pest sightings. Use pheromone traps for monitoring. Always rotate active ingredients to prevent resistance. For example, alternate fipronil and imidacloprid for termites. Timing is 80% of successful pest control. A well-timed treatment costs half as much as a reactive one.

Never apply pesticides in occupied rooms without following label re-entry intervals. In many African countries, only licensed operators may purchase and apply restricted chemicals (e.g., fumigants, organophosphates). Using unregistered products or exceeding label rates can cause poisoning, kill non-target species, and void your insurance. Always wear the PPE specified on the label, store chemicals locked away from children and food, and keep a written record of every application. If a client reports illness after treatment, stop work and contact your national pesticide authority immediately.

Quick Reference Checklist â Before Every Job

  1. Identify the pest correctly. Confirm species (or at least genus) before selecting chemical or method. Wrong ID = wasted chemical and unhappy client.
  2. Confirm the life stage present. Larvae, nymphs, and adults may need different products. Timing beats volume.
  3. Inspect for breeding sites. Standing water, refuse, cracks, stored grain, and dark harbourages. Treat the source, not just the symptom.
  4. Check the label and legal status. Is the product registered in your country for this pest and this site? Is your licence valid for it?
  5. Prepare PPE and equipment. Gloves, mask, goggles, boots, calibrated sprayer. No exceptions.
  6. Warn the client in writing. Re-entry time, what to remove (food, pets, bedding), and what to expect (e.g., more ants for 3â5 days after baiting).
  7. Schedule the follow-up visit. Bedbugs 10â14 days; fleas 7â10 days; rodents 7 and 14 days. Book it before you leave.
  8. Document everything. Product name, batch, rate, area treated, technician name, date. This protects you legally and builds the client file for renewals.
