7. Marketing and Selling

You can produce the best blocks in the district — but if no one knows, you will not sell them. This module shows you where builders buy blocks, how to get on their list, and how to build a reputation that makes customers come to you.

Module 7 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 7.1 — Who buys blocks, and where they look

Customer typeHow they buyWhat they need from you
Individual homeownerDirect, small ordersTrust, delivery, pricing
Small contractorDirect, regular ordersReliability, quality, credit terms
Hardware shopWholesale, resellsBulk discount, consistent supply
Large contractor / developerTender, contractDocumentation, capacity, quality reports
GovernmentTender processRegistration, compliance, formal invoicing

Where each looks:

  • Individuals: word of mouth, hardware shops, Facebook marketplace
  • Contractors: Yellow pages, WhatsApp contractor groups, hardware shop recommendations
  • Hardware shops: they find you — as long as you are visible in the market
  • Large contractors: tenders, referrals from architects, previous client lists
  • Government: public tender announcements
Practical exercise 7.1: List 10 potential customers by type. For each, note where they currently buy blocks and how you would reach them. This is your first 10-customer target list.

Lesson 7.2 — Getting the first 10 customers

Your first 10 customers almost never come from advertising. They come from direct outreach and building relationships.

Fastest paths:

  1. Visit 10 active construction sites. Introduce yourself to the site supervisor or foreman. Bring a sample block. Leave your contact. Do this every week.
  2. Visit 10 hardware shops. Offer them a wholesale price. Some will buy; some will refer.
  3. Attend 3 local building contractor meetings. Most districts have regular meetings. Ask around.
  4. Post on local Facebook groups. One clear post with a photo, price, and delivery promise. Not spam — a proper listing.
  5. Partner with a truck owner. Every load moved into the area could be from you. Give the driver 5% of every sale he brings.
  6. Do one free sample job. Build a small wall or pave a path for free at a local school or church. In return: photos, a testimonial, and word of mouth.
The 5-minute rule: Reply to every enquiry within 5 minutes. Speed of response is the single biggest reason customers choose one supplier over another. Slow repliers lose sales to whoever answers first.
Practical exercise 7.2: Visit 10 construction sites this week. Leave your card or number with every supervisor. Follow up in 3 days on each. Aim for 2 trial orders in the first month.

Lesson 7.4 — Customer service and repeat business

Repeat customers are the engine of a brick business. A contractor who buys 10,000 blocks a year is worth $8,500 in revenue — every year, for years.

What drives repeat business:

  • On-time delivery. No excuses. Arrive when you promised.
  • Consistent quality. Every block, every batch, the same.
  • Fast response. Answer calls and messages within 5 minutes.
  • Fair pricing. Same customer, same price, every time.
  • Honest problems. When something goes wrong, say it first.
  • Small extras. Free delivery on large orders, small discount on first repeat, a gift at Christmas.

Loyalty rewards that work:

ProgramHow it works
Volume tiersOrder 10,000 blocks this quarter → 5% off next order
Referral bonus$50 credit for every new customer brought in
Annual giftSmall branded gift to top 10 customers each year
Priority deliveryLoyal customers jump the queue in peak season
The loyalty rule: Your top 5 customers should be known by name. Call them once a month. Ask how the last batch performed. This single practice puts you ahead of 95% of competitors.
Practical exercise 7.4: Start a customer list. Every buyer gets recorded. Track how many are repeat customers after 6 months. Aim for 60%+ repeat rate.

Lesson 7.5 — Handling competition

Every brick market has competition. There are two ways to compete: price or value.

Price wars destroy everyone. Every producer cuts a little more, margins thin, quality drops, and eventually someone goes out of business. Do not start price wars.

Compete on value instead:

  • Better quality. Consistent dimension, tested strength, clean finish.
  • Reliable delivery. On-time every time. Even if the competition is cheaper, they are not if they are late.
  • After-sales service. Replace problems without argument. Answer questions. Provide simple advice.
  • Flexibility. Small orders welcome. Emergency deliveries accepted. Non-standard sizes on request.
  • Reputation. The best blocks in the district, known by name.

What to do when a competitor undercuts you:

  1. Do not cut your price. Hold the line.
  2. Talk to your regular customers. Remind them why they buy from you.
  3. Emphasise reliability — many customers will pay 10% more for a producer who is never late.
  4. Focus on premium products (pavers, decorative blocks) where price wars do not exist.
  5. If a customer leaves for a cheaper producer, keep them on your contact list. They often come back after a bad experience.
The competition rule: Never compete on price alone. Once you race to the bottom, everyone loses. Compete on reliability, quality, and service — the three things that are hardest to copy.
Practical exercise 7.5: Identify your top 3 competitors. Note their prices, strengths, and weaknesses. Find your specific advantage — the one thing you do better than all three.

Lesson 7.6 — Growing the brick business

Once your yard is profitable, you have three growth paths.

1 — Scale volume. Add machines, more workers, larger yard. Take larger contracts. Revenue grows, margins stay similar.

2 — Add premium products. Pavers, kerbstones, decorative blocks, coloured finishes. Smaller volumes, higher margins. Boost average margin by 10–15 percentage points.

3 — Open a second yard. Replicate the model in a different district. Requires a trained manager and proven cash flow.

Growth pathInvestmentTime to breakevenRisk
Add machines$1,500–$4,0003–6 monthsMedium
Add premium products$200–$8001–3 monthsLow
Second yard$5,000–$15,0006–12 monthsHigh

The safest growth path for most producers: premium products first. Low investment, quick return, low risk. Then scale volume. Then second yard.

The growth rule: One change at a time. Never add machines and a second yard in the same quarter. You will not know which one is working or which one is bleeding money.
Practical exercise 7.6 — Capstone: Write your one-page brick business growth plan. Current monthly production, next 12-month target, growth path, investment needed, and expected profit. Post it at your yard. Review quarterly.
