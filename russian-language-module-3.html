Lessons

1. Numbers 0-20

In Russian, numbers 0-20 are the foundation for telling time, dates, and prices. Start with 0-10: nol (0), odin (1), dva (2), tri (3), chetyre (4), pyat (5), shest (6), sem (7), vosem (8), devyat (9), desyat (10). Practice saying your phone number or the price of a kilo of tomatoes at your local market. For example, if a kilo of onions costs 15 rubles, you say 'pyatnadtsat rubley'. Note that 'odin' changes to 'odna' for feminine nouns (e.g., odna minuta) and 'odno' for neuter (e.g., odno yabloko).

From 11 to 19, the pattern is number + 'nadtsat': odinnadtsat (11), dvenadtsat (12), trinadtsat (13), chetyrnadtsat (14), pyatnadtsat (15), shestnadtsat (16), semnadtsat (17), vosemnadtsat (18), devyatnadtsat (19). For 20, say 'dvadtsat'. To ask 'How much?' in Russian, say 'Skolko stoit?' (How much does it cost?). When bargaining in a market in Nairobi or Lagos, knowing numbers helps you avoid overpaying. For example, if a vendor says 'sto rubley' (100 rubles), you can respond with 'dvadtsat' (20) as a counteroffer.

Practice counting aloud daily: count steps, cars, or coins. Use a language app like Duolingo or Memrise to drill numbers. Write numbers in a notebook and say them in Russian. For listening practice, search YouTube for 'Russian numbers 0-20 pronunciation'. Remember that stress can change meaning: 'sorok' (40) vs 'soroka' (magpie). But for 0-20, stress is usually on the last syllable for 11-19, and on the first for 0-10. With consistent practice, you'll master these numbers in a week.

2. Numbers 21-100 and Beyond

Once you know 0-20, building numbers 21-100 is straightforward. For 21-29, combine 'dvadtsat' (20) with the unit: dvadtsat odin (21), dvadtsat dva (22), etc. For 30, say 'tridtsat'; 40 is 'sorok'; 50 is 'pyatdesyat'; 60 is 'shestdesyat'; 70 is 'semdesyat'; 80 is 'vosemdesyat'; 90 is 'devyanosto'; 100 is 'sto'. Notice that 40 ('sorok') is irregularit doesn't follow the pattern of other tens. Memorize it early.

For numbers like 35, say 'tridtsat pyat' (30 and 5). For 99, say 'devyanosto devyat'. When you reach 100, you can combine: sto odin (101), sto dvadtsat tri (123). For hundreds, use 'dvesti' (200), 'trista' (300), 'chetyresta' (400), 'pyatsot' (500), etc. Thousands: 'tysyacha' (1000), 'dve tysyachi' (2000). For example, 'dve tysyachi devyatnadtsat' is 2019.

In practical situations like buying a bus ticket from Kigali to Kampala, you might hear 'sorok tysyach' (40,000). Practice by reading prices aloud: a bag of maize flour might cost 'shestdesyat pyat rubley' (65 rubles). Use a Russian number chart or flashcards. For listening, search YouTube for 'Russian numbers 1-100'. Try to count backwards from 100 to 0. Write random numbers and say them in Russian. With daily practice, you'll handle any number up to 1000 within two weeks.

3. Telling Time: Hours and Minutes

Telling time in Russian uses the 24-hour clock in formal situations, but the 12-hour clock is common in speech. To ask 'What time is it?', say 'Kotoryy chas?' (literally 'Which hour?'). The answer uses 'chas' (hour) for 1, 'chasa' for 2-4, and 'chasov' for 5-20. For minutes, use 'minuta' (1), 'minuty' (2-4), 'minut' (5-20). For example, 3:05 is 'tri chasa pyat minut' (three hours five minutes). For 'past' and 'to', use 'posle' (after) and 'do' (before). So 3:15 is 'pyatnadtsat minut posle trekh' (15 minutes after three).

In everyday conversation, you might hear 'polovina' for half past: 'polovina chetvertogo' is 3:30 (half of the fourth hour). For 3:45, say 'bez pyatnadtsati chetyre' (without 15, four). To say 'at' a time, use 'v': 'v tri chasa' (at 3 o'clock). For example, 'My vstrechaemsya v shest chasov vechera' (We meet at 6 PM). Note that 'utra' (morning), 'dnya' (afternoon), 'vechera' (evening), and 'nochi' (night) can be added: 'v sem chasov utra' (at 7 AM).

Practice by setting your phone to Russian time format and reading the time aloud every hour. Use a clock face and say the time in Russian. For listening, search YouTube for 'Russian telling time'. When scheduling a meeting with a Russian speaker, confirm: 'Vo skolko?' (At what time?). If you're in Lagos and need to call Moscow, remember the time differenceMoscow is 2 hours ahead of Lagos in winter. Always double-check times to avoid confusion.

4. Days of the Week and Months

The Russian week starts on Monday: ponedelnik (Monday), vtornik (Tuesday), sreda (Wednesday), chetverg (Thursday), pyatnitsa (Friday), subbota (Saturday), voskresenye (Sunday). Notice that 'voskresenye' also means 'resurrection'. To say 'on Monday', use 'v ponedelnik'. For 'on Mondays' (repeated), say 'po ponedelnikam'. Practice by writing your weekly schedule in Russian: 'V ponedelnik ya rabotayu' (On Monday I work).

The months are: yanvar (January), fevral (February), mart (March), aprel (April), may (May), iyun (June), iyul (July), avgust (August), sentyabr (September), oktyabr (October), noyabr (November), dekabr (December). To say 'in January', use 'v yanvare'. For example, 'Moy den rozhdeniya v marte' (My birthday is in March). Note that months are not capitalized in Russian unless at the start of a sentence.

In practical contexts, you might need to say the day and date: 'Segodnya ponedelnik, pyatoye maya' (Today is Monday, the fifth of May). To ask 'What day is it today?', say 'Kakoy segodnya den?' For 'What month is it?', say 'Kakoy segodnya mesyats?' Practice by keeping a diary in Russian, writing the day and month each morning. Use a calendar with Russian names. For listening, search YouTube for 'Russian days of the week song'. Knowing days and months helps you make appointments and understand schedules, whether in Nairobi or Moscow.

5. Dates and Ordinal Numbers

In Russian, dates use ordinal numbers (first, second, third) for the day, and the month is in the genitive case. For 'the first of May', say 'pervoye maya'. The ordinal numbers for days 1-31 are: pervyy (1st), vtoroy (2nd), tretiy (3rd), chetvertyy (4th), pyatyy (5th), shestoy (6th), sedmoy (7th), vosmoy (8th), devyatyy (9th), desyatyy (10th), odinnadtsatyy (11th), dvenadtsatyy (12th), trinadtsatyy (13th), chetyrnadtsatyy (14th), pyatnadtsatyy (15th), shestnadtsatyy (16th), semnadtsatyy (17th), vosemnadtsatyy (18th), devyatnadtsatyy (19th), dvadtsatyy (20th), dvadtsat pervyy (21st), etc. For 30th: tridtsatyy; 31st: tridtsat pervyy.

When writing a date, Russians use day.month.year: 05.05.2025. To say 'on the fifth of May', say 'pyatogo maya'. The ending '-ogo' is used for dates. For example, 'Ya rodilsya pyatogo maya' (I was born on the fifth of May). To ask 'What's the date today?', say 'Kakoye segodnya chislo?' The answer: 'Segodnya pyatoye maya' (Today is the fifth of May). Note that 'chislo' means 'date' or 'number'.

Practice by writing today's date in Russian each morning. Use a calendar and say the date aloud. For listening, search YouTube for 'Russian dates and ordinals'. When filling out forms in Russian, you'll need to write the date in the correct format. For example, a visa application might ask for 'data rozhdeniya' (date of birth). Knowing ordinals also helps with floors: 'pervyy etazh' (first floor). With practice, you'll master dates in a week.

6. Practical: Making Appointments and Schedules

Making appointments in Russian requires combining numbers, time, and dates. Start with a polite request: 'Mozhno s vami vstrechatsya?' (Can I meet with you?). Suggest a time: 'Davayte vstrechaemsya v sreda v tri chasa' (Let's meet on Wednesday at 3 o'clock). To confirm, say 'Vam udobno?' (Is it convenient for you?). If you need to reschedule, say 'Izvinite, mozhno perenesti?' (Sorry, can we reschedule?). Use 'v' for days and times: 'v ponedelnik v desyat chasov utra' (on Monday at 10 AM).

For scheduling, you might say: 'U menya vstrecha v chetverg v chetyre chasa' (I have a meeting on Thursday at 4 o'clock). To ask about someone's schedule, say 'Kogda vy svobodny?' (When are you free?). If you're busy, say 'Ya zanyat' (I'm busy). For a phone call, say 'Ya pozvonyu vam zavtra v sem chasov vechera' (I'll call you tomorrow at 7 PM). Remember to use the 24-hour clock in formal writing: 'v 15:00' (at 15:00).

Practice by role-playing with a friend: one person suggests a time, the other accepts or declines. Use a planner and write your appointments in Russian. For example, 'Vtornik, 10:00 - vstrecha s klientom' (Tuesday, 10:00 - meeting with client). For listening, search YouTube for 'Russian making appointments'. In African business contexts, punctuality is valued, so always confirm times. If you're in Accra and scheduling with a partner in Moscow, note the time difference (Moscow is 3 hours ahead of Accra in winter). Double-check dates to avoid mistakes. With practice, you'll handle appointments confidently.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Making Appointments and Schedules in Russian

For African agribusiness professionals trading with Russia whether negotiating grain contracts in Moscow, coordinating fertilizer shipments through St. Petersburg, or hosting Russian buyers at a cocoa cooperative in Ghana the ability to schedule meetings, confirm delivery windows, and set deadlines in Russian is a direct commercial asset. This lesson consolidates the numbers, time expressions, weekdays, months, and ordinal dates covered earlier in this module into practical, business-ready phrases you can use immediately.

Russian business culture places heavy emphasis on punctuality and written confirmation. A verbal "we'll meet next week" carries far less weight than a specific date and time stated clearly in Russian. Mastering these structures reduces costly misunderstandings, especially when coordinating across time zones between Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, and Moscow (MSK, UTC+3).

The most common structure in Russian business scheduling follows this pattern: + [verb] + [time/date] ("Let's...") or + [infinitive] + [time]? ("Is it possible to...?"). For formal written communication, ("I propose") opens the sentence.

Example 1 Proposing a meeting:

Example 2 Asking availability:

Example 3 Rescheduling:
, . ?

Example 4 Confirming a delivery window:
.

Notice the preposition (on) used with weekdays: (on Tuesday), (on Wednesday). For months, use + ordinal genitive: (in March). For specific dates, the ordinal takes genitive case: (on the fifteenth of March).

Russian uses both the 12-hour and 24-hour clock. In formal business correspondence, the 24-hour format dominates: 14:30 is read as or (half past two, literally "half of the third").

SAFETY WARNING Time Zone Confusion

Russia spans 11 time zones (UTC+2 to UTC+12). Moscow Time (MSK, UTC+3) is the reference for most federal business. When scheduling with Russian partners, always state the time zone explicitly e.g., (Moscow time). A meeting stated as "14:00" without a time zone can be misinterpreted by up to 9 hours across Russian regions, and by 3+ hours relative to West Africa (WAT, UTC+1), East Africa (EAT, UTC+3), or Southern Africa (SAST, UTC+2). For agribusiness, a misread delivery window can mean spoiled perishables, missed port slots, or failed cold-chain handoffs. Confirm all appointments in writing with both the local time and the time zone abbreviation.
