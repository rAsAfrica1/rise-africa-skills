1. Module 12 Lesson 1: Assessing Water Sources and Quality for Irrigation in Africa

Welcome to Module 12, Lesson 1. As an irrigation technician, your first job on any site is not to install pipes or pumps. It is to understand the water. In Africa, water sources vary enormously: rivers, boreholes, dams, canals, rainwater harvesting tanks, and even treated wastewater. Each source has different reliability, cost, and quality implications. This lesson gives you a practical framework for assessing water sources before you recommend or design any irrigation system. Start with quantity. You need to know how much water is available during the driest month, not the wettest. Ask the farmer or farm manager for historical records. If none exist, conduct a simple yield test. For a borehole, measure the static water level, then pump at a known rate for at least two hours and record the drawdown. Calculate the safe yield as the pumping rate that keeps the water level stable. For a river or canal, measure the cross-section and velocity using a float and a stopwatch. Multiply cross-sectional area by velocity to get flow in cubic metres per second. Convert to litres per hour. Compare this to the peak crop water requirement. A rule of thumb: one hectare of maize in a semi-arid area needs about 5 to 7 millimetres per day, which is 50,000 to 70,000 litres per hectare per day. If your source cannot supply that, you must plan for storage or deficit irrigation. Next, assess reliability. A borehole may be reliable but expensive to pump. A river may be free but seasonal. A dam may be reliable but subject to evaporation and siltation. In many parts of Africa, groundwater is the most reliable source, but over-abstraction is a real risk. As a technician, you must advise on sustainable yield. Never recommend a pump that exceeds the safe yield of the borehole. For surface water, consider the dry season minimum flow. If the river dries up for three months, you need storage or an alternative source. Rainwater harvesting is excellent for supplementary irrigation, especially for high-value crops like vegetables. A typical roof of 100 square metres can collect 80,000 litres per year in a region with 800 millimetres of rainfall. But you need tank capacity to store it. Now, water quality. This is often ignored, but it can make or break your system. Test for salinity, sodicity, pH, and specific ions. Electrical conductivity is the quickest measure of salinity. Most crops tolerate up to 1.5 decisiemens per metre without yield loss. Above 3 decisiemens per metre, only salt-tolerant crops like barley, date palm, and cotton will survive. Sodium adsorption ratio indicates sodicity. If SAR is above 9, the soil can become sodic, losing structure and infiltration. You also need to check for iron, manganese, and carbonates. Iron above 0.3 milligrams per litre can clog drip emitters. Manganese above 0.1 milligrams per litre causes black slime. Carbonates above 100 milligrams per litre can precipitate and block drippers. In Africa, many boreholes have high iron and manganese. You must test before installing drip. If levels are high, you need aeration and filtration, or you should choose sprinkler or surface irrigation instead. Also test for biological contamination if using wastewater or open canals. E. coli and other pathogens can contaminate crops, especially vegetables eaten raw. If you must use such water, advise filtration, chlorination, or drip irrigation that keeps water off the leaves. Finally, consider the cost of water. Even if water is free, pumping costs money. Calculate the energy cost per cubic metre. For a diesel pump, one litre of diesel can pump about 30 to 50 cubic metres to a height of 10 metres. At current prices, that is a significant cost. Solar pumping has high upfront cost but low running cost. As a technician, you must present a simple cost-benefit analysis to the farmer. In summary, assess quantity, reliability, quality, and cost. Do this before you design anything. A good irrigation system starts with a good water source assessment. In the next lesson, we will look at pump selection and maintenance.

2. Module 12 Lesson 2: Pump Selection, Installation, and Maintenance for African Farms

Welcome to Lesson 2. Pumps are the heart of most irrigation systems. In Africa, you will encounter centrifugal pumps, submersible pumps, helical rotor pumps, and solar-powered pumps. Your job is to match the pump to the water source, the irrigation system, and the farmer's budget and skills. Start with the basics. Every pump has a performance curve: flow rate on the horizontal axis and head on the vertical axis. Head is the total dynamic head, which includes static lift, friction losses, and pressure required at the emitters. Static lift is the vertical distance from the water level to the pump outlet. Friction losses depend on pipe diameter, length, and fittings. Pressure required depends on the irrigation method: drip needs 1 to 2 bar, sprinklers need 2 to 4 bar, and surface irrigation needs almost none. You must calculate total dynamic head accurately. If you undersize the pump, it will not deliver enough pressure. If you oversize it, you waste energy and may damage the system. For boreholes, submersible pumps are common. They are efficient and can lift water from deep depths. But they are expensive and require a generator or grid connection. Solar submersible pumps are increasingly popular because they have no fuel cost. However, they only work when the sun shines. You need a tank to store water for cloudy days or for night irrigation. A typical solar pump for a 1-hectare drip system might be 1 to 2 kilowatts, with a 10,000-litre tank. For surface water like rivers and dams, centrifugal pumps are common. They are cheap and easy to maintain. But they must be primed and placed close to the water level. Suction lift should not exceed 7 metres at sea level, and less at higher altitudes. In many African farms, farmers place pumps on the riverbank and run suction hoses into the water. This works, but you must use a foot valve to keep the suction line primed. Otherwise, the pump loses prime and burns out. Helical rotor pumps are good for low-flow, high-head applications, such as pumping from shallow wells with high lift. They are positive displacement pumps and can handle sandy water better than centrifugal pumps. But they are more expensive and require more maintenance. Now, installation. Always use the correct pipe size. A pump with a 2-inch outlet should not be connected to a 1-inch pipe. That increases friction and reduces flow. Use a pipe size that keeps velocity below 1.5 metres per second on the discharge side and below 1 metre per second on the suction side. Install a non-return valve on the discharge line to prevent backflow. Install a pressure gauge and a flow meter if possible. These help you monitor performance and detect problems early. For electrical connections, use proper cable sizes and waterproof joints. In Africa, power fluctuations are common. Install a surge protector or a voltage stabiliser. For diesel pumps, ensure the fuel tank is clean and the fuel filter is changed regularly. Maintenance is critical. A pump that is not maintained will fail, often at the worst time. Create a maintenance schedule with the farmer. Daily: check for leaks, unusual noises, and vibrations. Weekly: check oil levels, clean air filters, and inspect suction lines. Monthly: check impeller wear, replace worn seals, and test pressure and flow. Annually: overhaul the pump, replace bearings, and check the motor insulation. For solar pumps, clean the panels weekly and check the controller settings. Keep a logbook. Record running hours, fuel consumption, and any repairs. This helps you predict failures and plan replacements. Common problems and solutions: If the pump loses prime, check for air leaks in the suction line or a worn foot valve. If flow drops, check for clogged impellers, worn wear rings, or a blocked discharge line. If the pump overheats, check for low voltage, a clogged cooling system, or excessive load. If the motor trips, check for overload, phase imbalance, or a short circuit. In many African countries, pump repair services are limited. Train the farmer or a local technician to do basic maintenance. Keep spare parts on hand: seals, bearings, impellers, and filters. A spare pump is a good investment for critical operations. Finally, consider the energy source. Diesel is expensive and polluting. Grid electricity is cheap but unreliable in many rural areas. Solar is clean and reliable but has high upfront cost. Hybrid systems, such as solar with a diesel backup, are often the best compromise. As a technician, you must advise on the most cost-effective option for the specific farm. In the next lesson, we will cover irrigation scheduling and water management.

3. Module 12 Lesson 3: Irrigation Scheduling and Water Management for Smallholder and Commercial Farms

Welcome to Lesson 3. Irrigation scheduling is the decision of when and how much to irrigate. It is the most important factor in water productivity. Over-irrigation wastes water, leaches nutrients, and waterlogs the soil. Under-irrigation stresses crops and reduces yields. In Africa, where water is often scarce and expensive, good scheduling is essential. Start with crop water requirements. The reference evapotranspiration, ETo, is the water lost from a well-watered grass surface. You can estimate ETo using a simple pan evaporimeter or a local weather station. In many parts of Africa, ETo ranges from 3 to 8 millimetres per day. Crop evapotranspiration, ETc, is ETo multiplied by a crop coefficient, Kc. Kc varies by crop and growth stage. For maize, Kc is about 0.3 at emergence, 1.2 at tasselling, and 0.5 at maturity. For tomatoes, Kc is 0.6 at transplanting, 1.15 at fruiting, and 0.8 at harvest. You can find Kc values from FAO publications or local research. Once you know ETc, you can calculate the irrigation requirement. If ETc is 5 millimetres per day, that is 5 litres per square metre per day, or 50,000 litres per hectare per day. But you must account for rainfall and soil moisture. Use a simple soil moisture balance. Add rainfall and irrigation, subtract ETc and deep percolation. Keep the soil moisture between field capacity and permanent wilting point. For most crops, irrigate when 50 to 60 percent of available water is depleted. You can measure soil moisture with a tensiometer, a gypsum block, or a simple feel method. The feel method: take a handful of soil, squeeze it. If it forms a ball and leaves a slight moisture mark on your palm, it is at field capacity. If it crumbles, it is dry. Train farmers to use this method. It costs nothing and works well. For drip irrigation, you can irrigate daily or every second day. Apply small amounts frequently. For sprinkler, irrigate every 3 to 7 days, depending on soil type. For surface irrigation, you need to manage the flow rate and duration carefully to avoid runoff and deep percolation. Now, consider the irrigation system capacity. If your system can deliver 10,000 litres per hour, and your crop needs 50,000 litres per day, you need 5 hours of pumping per day. But you should not run the pump continuously. Allow time for maintenance and for the soil to absorb water. Also, consider the peak demand. In hot, dry periods, ETc can double. Your system must be able to meet peak demand, or you must accept some yield loss. A good rule is to design for 80 percent of peak demand and use deficit irrigation during extreme periods. Deficit irrigation is deliberately applying less water than full requirement. It can save water with minimal yield loss, especially for crops like maize, sorghum, and wheat. But it requires careful monitoring. For high-value crops like vegetables and fruits, full irrigation is usually more profitable. Water management also includes reducing losses. Conveyance losses in open canals can be 30 to 50 percent. Lining canals with concrete or plastic reduces losses. For pipes, check for leaks regularly. A single leak of 1 litre per minute wastes 1,440 litres per day. That is enough to irrigate 100 square metres of vegetables. Mulching reduces evaporation from the soil surface. A good mulch of crop residues can reduce evaporation by 30 to 50 percent. It also suppresses weeds and improves soil health. In Africa, mulching is a low-cost, high-impact practice. You should also consider the timing of irrigation. Early morning or late evening irrigation reduces evaporation losses. Avoid irrigating in the heat of the day. Also, avoid irrigating when it is windy, as sprinkler uniformity suffers. For drip, timing is less critical, but still avoid the hottest hours. Finally, keep records. A simple notebook or a mobile phone app can track irrigation dates, amounts, and crop performance. This data helps you refine the schedule over time. It also helps with budgeting and planning. In the next lesson, we will look at maintenance of irrigation systems, including drip, sprinkler, and surface systems.

4. Module 12 Lesson 4: Maintenance and Troubleshooting of Drip, Sprinkler, and Surface Irrigation Systems

Welcome to Lesson 4. A well-designed irrigation system will fail if it is not maintained. In Africa, maintenance is often neglected because of lack of skills, spare parts, or time. As a technician, you must build maintenance into every system you install. This lesson covers the main maintenance tasks and troubleshooting for drip, sprinkler, and surface irrigation. Start with drip irrigation. Drip is efficient but sensitive to clogging. The three main causes of clogging are physical particles, chemical precipitates, and biological growth. Physical particles include sand, silt, and clay. You must filter the water. Use a sand filter for open water sources and a screen or disc filter for clean water. Screen filters should be 120 to 200 mesh. Clean them regularly. If the pressure drop across the filter exceeds 0.5 bar, clean or replace the filter element. Chemical precipitates include calcium carbonate, iron, and manganese. If your water has high iron, you need aeration and settling before filtration. You can also inject acid to dissolve precipitates. Use food-grade hydrochloric acid or sulfuric acid at a concentration that brings the water pH to 2 for 30 minutes. Flush the system afterwards. Be careful with acid. Wear protective gear. Biological growth includes algae and bacteria. Algae grow in open water sources and in drip lines exposed to sunlight. Cover the water tank and bury or cover the mainlines. Inject chlorine at 1 to 2 parts per million continuously, or 10 to 20 parts per million for 30 minutes weekly. Chlorine kills bacteria and algae. But do not mix chlorine with acid. It produces toxic gas. For drip lines, flush the laterals regularly. Open the end caps and let water run for 2 to 3 minutes. Do this weekly or after every fertigation. Fertigation is injecting fertiliser through the drip system. It is efficient but can cause clogging if not done properly. Use fully soluble fertilisers. Flush the system with clean water after each fertigation. Now, sprinkler irrigation. Sprinklers are more robust than drip but still need maintenance. Check for worn nozzles, broken risers, and leaking joints. Replace worn nozzles because they affect uniformity. A worn nozzle can increase flow by 20 percent and reduce pressure. Check the pump pressure. Sprinklers need a specific pressure to operate correctly. If pressure is too low, the pattern is poor. If too high, the droplets are too fine and drift. Clean the sprinkler heads regularly. In dusty areas, nozzles can clog. Use a small brush or compressed air. For impact sprinklers, check the arm and bearing. Replace worn parts. For gear-driven sprinklers, check the gearbox oil. Also, check the risers. In Africa, animals and tractors often damage risers. Use flexible risers or protect them with guards. Surface irrigation includes furrow, basin, and border irrigation. It is cheap but inefficient if not managed well. Maintenance includes levelling the land, repairing bunds, and cleaning canals. Land levelling is critical. Uneven land causes waterlogging in low spots and drought in high spots. Use a laser level or a simple wooden level. Repair bunds and banks before each irrigation season. Clean canals and ditches of weeds and silt. Weeds block flow and increase seepage. Silt reduces capacity. Also, manage the flow rate. Too high a flow causes erosion. Too low a flow causes deep percolation at the head of the field and drought at the tail. Use a simple flow meter or a weir to measure flow. Troubleshooting common problems. If the system pressure is low, check for a clogged filter, a closed valve, a worn pump impeller, or a leak in the mainline. If pressure is high, check for a closed valve downstream or a blocked lateral. If flow is uneven, check for clogged emitters, a broken lateral, or a pressure difference. If the pump cycles on and off, check the pressure tank or the pressure switch. If the pump runs but no water comes, check for a lost prime, a closed valve, or a blocked suction line. If the motor overheats, check for low voltage, a clogged cooling fan, or an overload. Keep a maintenance log. Record every inspection, repair, and replacement. This helps you track performance and plan budgets. Train the farmer to do daily checks: look for leaks, listen for unusual noises, and check pressure gauges. Weekly: clean filters, flush drip lines, and inspect sprinklers. Monthly: check pump oil, test water quality, and calibrate the system. Annually: overhaul the pump, replace worn parts, and test the entire system. In Africa, spare parts can be hard to find. Keep a stock of common spares: emitters, filters, nozzles, seals, and bearings. Work with local suppliers to ensure availability. Also, consider using locally made or adapted parts. For example, in some countries, farmers make their own drip lines from locally available pipes. These may not be as efficient, but they are affordable and repairable. Finally, document the system. Create a simple manual with diagrams, maintenance schedules, and troubleshooting steps. Leave a copy with the farmer. This empowers them to take ownership. 5. Module 12 Lesson 5: Business Skills for Irrigation Technicians in Africa

Welcome to Lesson 5. You have the technical skills. Now you need the business skills to succeed as an irrigation technician. In Africa, the demand for irrigation services is growing, but competition is also growing. To stand out, you must be professional, reliable, and business-minded. This lesson covers costing, pricing, marketing, customer service, and record-keeping. Start with costing. You must know your costs before you can set a price. Costs include direct costs and indirect costs. Direct costs are materials and labour for a specific job. Materials include pipes, fittings, pumps, filters, emitters, and tanks. Labour includes your time and that of any helpers. Indirect costs are overheads: transport, tools, phone, internet, office rent, and insurance. You must calculate your hourly rate. Add up your annual indirect costs and divide by the number of billable hours you expect to work. For example, if your annual overheads are 5,000 dollars and you work 1,000 billable hours per year, your overhead rate is 5 dollars per hour. Then add your direct labour rate. If you pay yourself 10 dollars per hour, your total labour rate is 15 dollars per hour. For materials, add a markup of 10 to 20 percent to cover waste, storage, and handling. For pumps and tanks, a markup of 15 to 25 percent is common. But be transparent. Show the farmer the material costs and your markup. Trust is important. Now, pricing. You can price by the job, by the hour, or by the hectare. For small jobs, hourly pricing is simple. For larger jobs, a fixed price is better for the farmer. For irrigation design and installation, price per hectare. In many African countries, a drip system for one hectare costs between 2,000 and 5,000 dollars, depending on the source and quality. A sprinkler system costs 1,500 to 3,000 dollars. A surface irrigation system costs 500 to 1,500 dollars. These are rough estimates. You must calculate your own costs. When quoting, be clear about what is included and what is not. Include a written contract. The contract should specify the scope of work, the price, the payment schedule, the timeline, and the warranty. A simple one-page contract is enough. For payment, ask for a deposit of 30 to 50 percent before you start. This covers materials. Ask for progress payments as you complete milestones. Keep the final 10 percent until the farmer is satisfied and the system is tested. This protects you and the farmer. Marketing is essential. In Africa, word of mouth is the most powerful marketing. Do good work and ask for referrals. Also, use local radio, WhatsApp groups, and farmer cooperatives. Attend agricultural shows and field days. Demonstrate your systems. Show farmers how much water and money they can save. Create a simple business card or flyer. Include your name, phone number, and a list of services. If you have a smartphone, take photos of your installations. Show before and after. Use these in your marketing. Customer service is your competitive advantage. Answer your phone. Return calls. Arrive on time. Communicate clearly. If you cannot do a job, say so. If you make a mistake, fix it. Do not argue. A satisfied customer will tell others. An unsatisfied customer will tell everyone. Also, offer after-sales service. A maintenance contract is a good way to build recurring income. Offer to inspect the system every three months. Charge a fee for this service. It is good for the farmer and good for your business. Record-keeping is critical. Keep records of every job: date, customer, materials used, labour hours, and payment. Keep receipts for all purchases. Keep a separate bank account for your business. This makes it easier to track profit and pay taxes. At the end of each month, calculate your profit. If you are not making a profit, you are not in business. You are doing charity. Adjust your prices or reduce your costs. Also, manage your cash flow. Irrigation is seasonal. You may have busy periods and slow periods. Save money during busy periods to cover slow periods. Consider offering other services during the off-season, such as pump repair, plumbing, or solar installation. Finally, invest in yourself. Take courses on new technologies: solar pumping, drip irrigation, fertigation, and soil moisture monitoring. Join professional associations. Network with other technicians. Share knowledge. The more you learn, the more you earn. In summary, treat your irrigation business like a business. Know your costs. Price fairly. Market yourself. Serve your customers. Keep records. And always keep learning. This concludes Module 12. You now have the practical skills to assess water sources, select and maintain pumps, schedule irrigation, maintain systems, and run a successful business. Go out and make a difference for African farmers.

<p style="margin:0; color:#7f0000;">Never recommend untreated wastewater, greywater, or surface water downstream of open defecation or livestock pens for irrigating crops eaten raw (lettuce, tomato, herbs, salad onion). Faecal coliform and helminth egg contamination causes diarrhoeal disease and is a documented cause of cholera and typhoid outbreaks in East and Southern Africa. If a client insists on using such water, restrict it to non-food crops, or require treatment (settling ponds plus filtration, or chlorination) and a minimum 10-day withholding period before harvest. Always wear gloves when handling filters, screens, and emitters from suspect sources, and wash hands with soap before eating. Full guidance: WHO (2006) <em>Guidelines for the Safe Use of Wastewater, Excreta and Greywater in Agriculture</em>.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist On Every Farm Visit</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Confirm the water source and test it.</strong> Take EC, pH, and a visual turbidity reading. If the source is surface water used for salad crops, request a faecal coliform test before recommending drip.</li>
<li><strong>Measure the static water level and lift.</strong> Note the vertical distance from water surface to pump and from pump to the highest emitter. Add 20% for friction losses before selecting a pump.</li>
<li><strong>Check the filter and flush the lines.</strong> A blocked 120-mesh screen is the number one cause of uneven drip. Show the farmer how to flush the submain and laterals this is a paid maintenance visit, not a favour.</li>
<li><strong>Walk the whole block with the farmer.</strong> Look for wet patches (leaks), dry patches (clogged emitters), and erosion (over-irrigation). Photograph and tag each problem.</li>
<li><strong>Record the irrigation schedule.</strong> Write down the current run time, days per week, and the crop stage. Compare against the crop water requirement in Table 2 and adjust in writing.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate the quote on site.</strong> Use Table 2 for area, pump, and cost. Add 15% for transport, 10% for contingency, and a separate line for a service contract.</li>
<li><strong>Leave a written service agreement.</strong> State the visit frequency, what is covered (filter cleaning, emitter inspection, pump check), and the fee. Business-first: a maintenance contract is recurring income.</li>
<li><strong>File the data.</strong> Keep a photo, a GPS point, and the water test result for every farm. This becomes your reference library for the next client in the same catchment.</li>
</ol>
