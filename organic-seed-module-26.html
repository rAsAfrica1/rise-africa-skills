Risk Management - The Disasters You Cannot Prevent But Can Survive

🧠 Brain Opener

Every seed business faces risks. Drought. Pests. Price drops. Buyers who do not pay. Certification loss. You cannot prevent all of them. But you can plan so that one of them does not destroy you.

  • What are the five main risks for a seed business?
  • How do you mitigate each?
  • What is the biggest mistake in risk management?
🎁 Reveal a strong answer

The five risks are production (weather, pests, disease), market (price, demand), financial (cash flow, debt), regulatory (certification, law changes), and personal (health, family). Mitigate with diversification, insurance, savings, and backup plans. The biggest mistake is pretending the risk does not exist.

📖 Chipo Survives the Drought

Chipo had planted two hectares of tomato seed for the second season. Her first season had been perfect. She had grown 15 kilograms and sold them all. She had used the profit to buy better foundation seed and hire help.

Then the drought came.

By week 4 the rain had not arrived. By week 6 the young plants were wilting. By week 8 half the field was dead.

But Chipo had prepared. She had three things in place.

First, water. She had installed a small solar pump the season before. It could irrigate one hectare from her borehole. It was expensive - 600 dollars - but she had paid for it out of first-season profits. The pump saved 0.6 hectares of her crop.

Second, savings. She had kept 400 dollars of her first-season profit in a mobile money account. This was enough to buy water for the remaining 0.4 hectares from a neighbour with a borehole.

Third, diversification. She had planted half a hectare of amaranth on a different plot. Amaranth is more drought-tolerant than tomato. Her amaranth harvest was unaffected.

At the end of the season, Chipo harvested 6 kilograms of tomato seed (down from 15) plus 40 kilograms of amaranth seed.

Her revenue: tomato seed at 80 dollars per kg = 480 dollars. Amaranth seed at 70 dollars per kg = 2,800 dollars. Total: 3,280 dollars.

Her neighbour, who had planted all tomato on all his land, harvested 1 kilogram of tomato seed. Revenue: 80 dollars. He lost everything else.

Chipo did not beat the drought. She survived it. Three small preparations turned a disaster into a small setback.

💡 The Big Idea

Plan for the season that goes wrong

Every 5 years, a seed farmer has a bad season. The question is whether they survive it. The answer is preparation: water, savings, and diversification. Three things you can build in a good season that will keep you alive in a bad one.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

The five risks

Production risk. Drought, flood, pests, disease, fire. The seed farmer cannot prevent these. Can only prepare.

Market risk. Price drops, buyer loss, unsold stock. Can be hedged by contracts and diversification.

Financial risk. Cash flow gaps, unpaid invoices, over-borrowing. Managed with reserves and conservative planning.

Regulatory risk. Loss of certification, change of seed law, new tax. Managed with relationships and compliance.

Personal risk. Illness, family emergency, land dispute. Managed with insurance and document preparation.

2

The three-level mitigation strategy

Level 1 - Prevention. Buy irrigation. Build savings. Diversify crops. Use contracts. Take insurance. Choose drought-tolerant varieties. These reduce the chance of disaster.

Level 2 - Preparation. Have a cash reserve. Have a backup water source. Have a second buyer. Have records that prove your business. These reduce the impact when disaster strikes.

Level 3 - Response. Have a plan for what you will do when the disaster hits. Which crops to abandon. Which buyers to call. Which costs to cut. Who to borrow from. Having a plan means you do not panic.

3

Diversification - the single best risk reduction

Diversification means not putting all your eggs in one basket. For a seed farmer, that means: multiple crops, multiple plots (some with different rainfall), multiple buyers, and multiple income streams (seed, training, consultancy).

Aim for: no single crop producing more than 50 percent of revenue, no single buyer accounting for more than 40 percent of sales, and at least one income stream not affected by weather.

The seed farmer who plants only one crop for one buyer is gambling. The seed farmer who plants three crops for five buyers is running a business.

🛠 Hands-On - Write Your Risk Register

You need

  • A notebook
  • A pen
  1. List every risk you face across five categories: production, market, financial, regulatory, personal.
  2. Rate each risk: low, medium, high.
  3. For each high risk, write one prevention action and one preparation action.
  4. For each medium risk, write one action.
  5. Estimate the cost of each action.
  6. Prioritize the top three by impact and cost.
  7. Do those three first.
  8. Review the register every 6 months.
Why it works: A written risk register converts vague worry into specific action.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Zimbabwe 2019 drought

Zimbabwe 2019 drought destroyed 60 percent of smallholder harvests. Farms with irrigation, savings, and diversified crops survived with 30 to 40 percent losses. Farms with none of these lost everything. The difference was preparation, not luck.

Kenya coffee price collapse

When coffee prices collapsed in 2019, Kenyan farmers with organic certification and specialty buyers survived. Farmers selling to commodity buyers saw their income drop 40 percent. Certification and contracts are risk mitigation.
