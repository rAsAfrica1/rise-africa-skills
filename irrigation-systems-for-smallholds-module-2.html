1. Understanding Soil Texture and Structure

Soil texture is the mix of sand, silt, and clay particles. Sand feels gritty, silt feels like flour, and clay feels sticky when wet. To test your soil, put a handful in a jar with water, shake it, and let it settle. Sand sinks first, then silt, then clay. After 24 hours, you can measure the layers. A loam soilâabout 40% sand, 40% silt, and 20% clayâis best for most crops because it holds water and drains well. In many parts of Africa, soils are sandy (like in the Sahel) or clayey (like in parts of the Congo Basin). Sandy soils drain fast but need frequent watering. Clay soils hold water but can become waterlogged. Structure is how soil particles stick together into crumbs. Good structure means roots can grow and water moves easily. You can improve structure by adding compost or manure. For example, add 2-3 handfuls of well-rotted manure per square meter before planting. Avoid tilling wet soil because it destroys structure. A simple test: dig a small hole, fill it with water, and see how fast it drains. If it drains in less than 30 minutes, your soil is too sandy; if it takes more than 2 hours, it's too clayey. Knowing your soil helps you choose irrigation methods. Sandy soils need drip irrigation or frequent small waterings. Clay soils can use furrow irrigation but avoid overwatering. In Africa, many smallholders have sandy soils, so adding organic matter is key. Mulching with crop residues also protects structure and reduces evaporation.

2. Soil Moisture Basics: Field Capacity and Wilting Point

Field capacity is the amount of water soil holds after excess water has drained away. Think of a sponge after you squeeze itâit's still wet but not dripping. Wilting point is when soil is so dry that plants can no longer pull water from it and they wilt permanently. The difference between field capacity and wilting point is called available water capacity (AWC). For sandy soil, AWC is about 5-10% by volume; for loam, 15-20%; for clay, 18-25%. To check your soil moisture, squeeze a handful. If it forms a ball and leaves a wet outline on your hand, it's near field capacity. If it crumbles and won't hold together, it's near wilting point. You can also use a simple tensiometer, a tool that measures how tightly water is held. In Africa, you can make a DIY tensiometer with a ceramic cup, a tube, and a vacuum gauge, but buying one for $20-$50 is easier. For smallholders, a cheaper method is to use a soil auger or a spade to dig 15 cm deep and feel the soil. Irrigate when soil at root depth feels dry but before plants wilt. For most crops, irrigate when 50% of available water is used. For example, if your loam soil holds 20% AWC, irrigate when it drops to 10%. This avoids stressing plants and saves water. In sandy soils, check moisture every 2-3 days; in clay, every 5-7 days. Overwatering wastes water and leaches nutrients. Underwatering reduces yields. A simple rule: if you can't squeeze a ball, it's time to water.

3. Determining Infiltration Rates

Infiltration is how fast water enters the soil. If water runs off instead of soaking in, you're wasting water and causing erosion. To test infiltration, use a tin can with both ends removed. Push it 5 cm into the soil, pour in 2.5 cm of water, and time how long it takes to disappear. Do this three times. The rate is the depth divided by time. For example, if 2.5 cm soaks in in 10 minutes, that's 0.25 cm per minute. Sandy soils infiltrate at 2.5 cm/hour or more; loams at 1-2 cm/hour; clays at less than 0.5 cm/hour. If your rate is very low, you may need to improve soil structure with compost or use drip irrigation. High infiltration (sandy) means you need to water more often but in smaller amounts. In Africa, many soils are crusted or compacted from overgrazing or plowing. To improve infiltration, add organic matter, avoid tilling wet soil, and plant cover crops like cowpeas or lablab. You can also make small basins around plants to hold water. For furrow irrigation, the furrow length and slope affect infiltration. A slope of 0.5-2% is ideal; steeper slopes cause runoff. If infiltration is too fast, you can add clay or compost. If too slow, add sand or gypsum. A simple infiltration test helps you decide how long to run your irrigation. For example, if your soil takes in 1 cm/hour and you need to apply 3 cm of water, you must run your sprinkler for 3 hours. Without this test, you might overwater or underwater. Always test before planting and after heavy rains.

4. Crop Water Requirements and Evapotranspiration

Crop water requirement is the amount of water a crop needs from planting to harvest. It depends on the crop, climate, and growth stage. Evapotranspiration (ET) is water lost from soil evaporation and plant transpiration. In hot, dry areas, ET can be 5-8 mm per day; in humid areas, 2-4 mm per day. To estimate ET, use a simple pan evaporimeter: a shallow pan filled with water. Measure how much water evaporates in 24 hours. Multiply by a crop factor (0.6-1.2 depending on crop and stage) to get crop water use. For example, if pan evaporation is 6 mm and your maize is at tasseling (factor 1.1), crop water use is 6.6 mm. That means you need to apply 6.6 liters per square meter per day. For a 10 m x 10 m plot, that's 660 liters daily. In Africa, many smallholders grow maize, sorghum, millet, cowpea, and vegetables. Maize needs 500-800 mm total; sorghum 400-600 mm; cowpea 300-500 mm. Critical periods: maize needs most water at tasseling and silking; sorghum at booting and flowering; vegetables throughout. You can reduce ET by mulching, which cuts evaporation by 30-50%. Also, water early morning or evening to reduce evaporation. Drip irrigation saves water by applying it directly to roots. If you don't have a pan, use a simple rule: if the top 5 cm of soil is dry, irrigate. But for better accuracy, keep a daily record of rainfall and irrigation. A rain gauge costs $5-$10. Knowing crop water needs helps you avoid over- or under-irrigation, which can reduce yields by 20-50%.

5. Identifying Critical Irrigation Periods for Common Crops

Critical irrigation periods are when water shortage causes the biggest yield loss. For maize, the most critical stage is tasseling and silking (about 60-70 days after planting). Water stress then can cut yields by 50%. Next is grain filling. For sorghum, critical periods are booting (when the head emerges) and flowering. For millet, it's flowering and grain filling. For cowpea, it's flowering and pod filling. For tomatoes, it's flowering and fruit set; for onions, it's bulb formation. In Africa, many smallholders grow these crops. To manage critical periods, plan your irrigation schedule. For example, if you grow maize in a dry area, ensure you have enough water during tasseling. If water is scarce, prioritize that stage. You can use a simple calendar: mark planting date, then count days to critical stages. For maize, tasseling at 60 days, silking at 65 days. For sorghum, booting at 50 days, flowering at 60 days. For cowpea, flowering at 40 days, pod fill at 55 days. During these periods, check soil moisture daily. If the top 10 cm is dry, irrigate. Apply enough water to wet the root zone (about 30 cm deep). For sandy soil, water every 2-3 days; for clay, every 5-7 days. Avoid waterlogging, which can also reduce yields. In many parts of Africa, rainfall is erratic, so supplemental irrigation is key. Even small amounts (10-20 mm) at critical stages can make a big difference. For example, a 10 mm irrigation at maize tasseling can increase yields by 20%. Use mulch to conserve moisture. If you have a drip system, run it daily during critical periods. Keep records to learn your crop's needs.

6. Simple Tools for Soil Moisture Monitoring

You don't need expensive equipment to monitor soil moisture. The cheapest tool is your hand. Dig 15 cm deep with a spade, take a handful of soil, and squeeze. If it forms a ball and leaves a wet outline, it's at field capacity. If it forms a ball but no outline, it's slightly dry. If it crumbles, it's near wilting point. Do this every 2-3 days. A second tool is a soil auger or a metal pipe to take samples at different depths. A third tool is a tensiometer, which measures soil water tension. You can buy one for $20-$50 or make a simple one with a ceramic cup, a tube, and a vacuum gauge. Place it at root depth (15-30 cm). Readings: 0-10 centibars (wet), 10-30 (moist), 30-60 (dry, irrigate), above 60 (too dry). A fourth tool is a gypsum block, which works like a tensiometer but is cheaper. A fifth tool is a simple moisture meter that you push into the soil; these cost $10-$20 but can be inaccurate. For smallholders, the hand feel method plus a rain gauge is enough. A rain gauge costs $5-$10 and helps you track rainfall. You can also use a calendar to record irrigation and rainfall. In Africa, many farmers use a stick or a machete to check moisture. Push a clean metal rod into the soil; if it goes in easily, soil is moist; if hard, dry. Another method: use a bottle with a cloth wick buried in the soil; check if the cloth is wet. Combine methods for accuracy. Always check before and after irrigation. Keep a notebook. This helps you learn your soil and crops. Over time, you'll know exactly when to irrigate without tools.

Practical Reference: Soil, Water & Crop Relationships for Smallholder Irrigation

This practical reference distils the six lessons of the module into a working tool you can keep in the field, in the pump shed, or on the office wall. Every number here is meant to be used: to size a pump, schedule a cycle, price a service, or decide whether a field is worth irrigating at all. In African smallholder systems, water is rarely free â it costs fuel, labour, time, and sometimes conflict. Treating soil, water, and crop as one managed system is the difference between a farm that survives the dry season and one that buys maize at retail in February.

Work through the sections in order: soil first (it holds the water), then moisture limits (they define how much is available), then infiltration (it defines how fast you can apply), then crop demand (it defines how much to apply), then timing (it defines when), and finally monitoring (it tells you whether you were right).

1. Soil Texture and Structure: The Storage Tank

Texture is the proportion of sand, silt, and clay. It sets the upper limit of how much water your soil can hold and how fast it drains. Structure â how those particles clump into aggregates â determines whether that potential is realised. A compacted sandy loam behaves like a poorer soil than its texture suggests.

2. Field Capacity and Wilting Point: The Working Range

Field capacity (FC) is the water left after free drainage; wilting point (PWP) is the water held so tightly that plants can no longer extract it. The difference is the plant-available water capacity (PAWC). This is the number that matters commercially: it tells you how many millimetres of water your soil can bank between irrigations.

Never operate a petrol, diesel, or electric pump on wet ground or with wet hands. In many smallholder schemes, pumps are wired with exposed joints and no earth leakage protection â this kills. Always use an RCD/earth-leakage breaker on electric pumps, keep fuel away from the pump exhaust, and never refuel a hot engine. When using furrow irrigation on steep slopes, never open a head ditch alone; flash floods and bank collapse have drowned farmers. Children must be kept clear of open water, sumps, and pump belts at all times.

5. Critical Irrigation Periods

Not all water is equal. Stress at flowering and grain-filling costs far more yield than stress during vegetative growth. Schedule scarce water to protect the critical stages first.

  • Maize: tasselling to silking (weeks 8â11) â most sensitive; then grain fill.
  • Tomato: flowering and fruit set; also fruit enlargement for market size.
  • Onion: bulb formation â stress here slashes marketable yield.
  • Beans (common): flowering and pod fill.
  • Rice (upland/lowland): panicle initiation to flowering.
  • Irish potato: tuber initiation and tuber bulking.

6. Simple Tools for Soil Moisture Monitoring

You do not need a $2,000 probe. A hand-feel test, a tensiometer in a representative bed, or a simple gypsum block will tell you when to irrigate. For smallholders, the most reliable and cheapest method is the feel and appearance method, calibrated to your own soil. Combine it with a fixed schedule and a rain gauge, and you have a working irrigation decision-support system.
