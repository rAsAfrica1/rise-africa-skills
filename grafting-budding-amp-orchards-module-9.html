1. Choosing the best site for an orchard: soil, water, and sun

Before you plant a single tree, the site you choose will decide whether your orchard thrives or fails. Start by checking the soil. A simple test: dig a hole one foot deep and fill it with water. If the water drains within 10-15 minutes, the soil is too sandy; if it stays for hours, it's too clayey. The best soil is loamyâdark, crumbly, and full of organic matter. You can also squeeze a handful of moist soil; if it forms a ribbon that breaks easily, it's good. Avoid waterlogged spots; fruit trees like mango, avocado, and citrus will die if their roots sit in water. If your only land is poorly drained, consider making raised beds or planting on mounds.

Water is critical. You need a reliable source nearbyâa river, well, borehole, or piped supplyâbecause young trees need watering twice a week in dry season. A mature tree can survive on rainfall, but during establishment, you must irrigate. Also, check for a slope; a gentle slope (2-5%) helps drainage and prevents frost pockets. Avoid steep slopes unless you build terraces. Sunlight: fruit trees need at least 6-8 hours of direct sunlight daily. Too much shade reduces flowering and fruit quality. Watch how shadows move across your land; if trees or buildings cast shade for more than half the day, find another spot.

Windbreaks matter. Strong winds can break branches, knock off flowers, and dry out soil. If your area has strong winds, plant a living fence of fast-growing trees like Grevillea or Casuarina on the windward side, but keep them at least 10 meters from your orchard to avoid competition. Also, check for previous land use: if the field grew tomatoes, peppers, or potatoes, it may carry nematodes or diseases that harm fruit trees. A cover crop of beans or maize for one season can help clean the soil. Finally, consider access. You'll need to bring in manure, tools, and harvest fruit. A path or road nearby saves labour. Mark your site boundaries and take a soil sample to your local extension officer for testingâmany government offices do this cheaply. Remember, a good site reduces pests, diseases, and labour, while a bad site costs you money and time for years.

2. Land preparation with hand tools and small machines

Once you've chosen your site, you need to prepare the land. The goal is to remove weeds, loosen soil, and create a good seedbed for your young trees. If your plot is small (1-2 acres), hand tools are enough. Start by clearing bushes and tall grasses using a slasher or machete. For tough roots, use a hoe or mattock. Do not burn everything; you can compost the weeds or use them as mulch later. If you have stubborn perennial weeds like couch grass or blackjack, dig them out completelyâthey will compete with your trees for water and nutrients.

Next, plough or dig the soil. For hand tools, use a fork jembe or spade to turn the top 30 cm (12 inches) of soil. This is hard work, so do it in small sections. If you can access a small two-wheel tractor (power tiller), it can plough an acre in a few hours. For larger areas, hire a tractor with a disc plough. But avoid deep ploughing that brings up poor subsoil. After ploughing, harrow to break clods and level the land. If your soil is acidic (common in high rainfall areas), apply agricultural lime at 200-400 kg per acre during ploughing. If it's sandy, add well-rotted manure or compost at 5-10 tonnes per acre to improve water holding.

Mark your planting holes. For grafted fruit trees, dig holes 60 cm wide and 60 cm deep (2 ft x 2 ft). Keep topsoil and subsoil separate. Mix topsoil with 1-2 buckets of compost or manure and a handful of rock phosphate or bone meal. This gives roots a strong start. If you're planting on a slope, make a small basin on the downhill side to catch water. For windbreaks, prepare a strip around the orchard. Finally, ensure you have a water source ready for irrigation. Land preparation is best done at the end of the dry season so you can plant at the start of rains. A well-prepared field saves you weeding and watering later.

3. Spacing and planting design for mango, avocado, and citrus

Spacing determines how many trees you plant per acre and how they grow. Too close, and trees compete for light, water, and nutrients; too far, and you waste land. The right spacing depends on the tree species, rootstock, and your management. For mango, standard spacing is 10 m x 10 m (about 40 trees per acre). But with modern dwarf rootstocks, you can plant 5 m x 5 m (160 trees per acre). In Africa, many farmers use 8 m x 8 m for grafted mangoes on vigorous rootstocks. Avocado: 7 m x 7 m (about 80 trees per acre) for vigorous types, or 5 m x 5 m for dwarf varieties like 'Hass' on clonal rootstock. Citrus: 5 m x 5 m (160 trees per acre) for oranges, lemons, and tangerines. Limes can be closer at 4 m x 4 m.

Planting design also matters. The square system (trees in straight rows both ways) is easiest for management and intercropping. The rectangular system (closer in rows, wider between rows) allows more trees and easier machinery access. The quincunx (diamond) system fits more trees per acre but complicates weeding. For smallholders, the square system is best. Always plant in straight lines using a string and pegs. Leave a 3-4 m border around the orchard for windbreaks and access. If you plant on a slope, run rows across the slope (contour) to reduce erosion. For pollination, some avocado and citrus need cross-pollination. Plant two or more varieties in alternating rows. For example, avocado 'Hass' with 'Fuerte'. Mangoes are mostly self-pollinating, but mixing varieties can improve yield.

Consider the mature size of trees. Mango can reach 10-15 m tall, so don't plant under power lines or near buildings. Avocado and citrus are smaller (5-10 m). Also, think about sunlight: plant tall trees on the south side (in southern hemisphere) so they don't shade shorter ones. If you intercrop, leave enough space between rows for crops like beans or vegetables. A good design includes a permanent cover crop or mulch between rows. Finally, record your layout on paperâthis helps you remember where each variety is and plan future operations. Proper spacing and design will give you healthy trees and easier harvest for years.

4. Planting grafted trees correctly: depth, mulch, and watering

Planting a grafted tree correctly is the difference between a tree that fruits in 3 years and one that dies in 3 months. Start with healthy nursery trees: at least 60 cm tall, with a straight stem and no root damage. Plant at the beginning of the rainy season when soil is moist. Dig a hole 60 cm x 60 cm x 60 cm. Keep topsoil and subsoil separate. Mix topsoil with 1-2 buckets of compost or well-rotted manure and a handful of phosphate fertilizer (like DAP or rock phosphate). Do not add fresh manureâit burns roots.

Depth is critical. The graft union (the bump where the scion was joined to the rootstock) must be 15-20 cm above the soil line. If you bury it, the scion will root and you lose the benefits of the rootstock. If it's too high, roots dry out. Place the tree in the hole, spread roots gently, and backfill with the topsoil mixture. Firm the soil with your hands to remove air pockets. Water immediately with 10-20 litres per tree. Then mulch around the base with dry grass, straw, or leaves, but keep mulch 10 cm away from the trunk to prevent rot. Mulch conserves water, suppresses weeds, and adds organic matter.

Watering is essential for the first two years. In dry weather, water twice a week with 10-20 litres per tree. In rainy season, reduce to once a week if no rain. A simple drip irrigation system using a bucket with a small hole works well. After planting, stake the tree if it's in a windy area. Use a strong stick and tie with soft clothânot wire. Do not fertilize immediately; wait 4-6 weeks, then apply a small amount of nitrogen (like urea) 15 cm from the trunk. Keep the area around the tree weed-free. Watch for pests like termites and borers; treat with wood ash or neem extract. With proper planting, your grafted tree will establish quickly and start bearing fruit in 3-5 years.

5. Intercropping with beans, groundnuts, or vegetables for extra income

Intercropping means growing crops between your young fruit trees. It gives you income while trees are maturing, controls weeds, and improves soil. But not all crops are suitable. Choose legumes like beans, groundnuts, cowpeas, or soybeansâthey fix nitrogen from the air into the soil, feeding your trees. Vegetables like tomatoes, onions, cabbage, and leafy greens also work well, but avoid crops that compete heavily for water and nutrients, like maize or sugarcane. Also, avoid crops that share pests with fruit trees; for example, tomatoes and peppers can carry nematodes that attack citrus and avocado.

When to intercrop? During the first 2-3 years after planting, when tree canopies are small. As trees grow, shade increases and intercrops fail. So plan to phase out intercropping by year 4. Plant your intercrops in rows between the tree rows, leaving at least 1 meter around each tree so you don't disturb roots. For beans, plant 2-3 seeds per hole, spaced 30 cm apart. Groundnuts: 30 cm x 10 cm. Vegetables like kale or spinach can be transplanted. Always rotate crops each season to prevent disease. For example, beans then cabbage then groundnuts.

Intercropping can earn you extra money. A quarter-acre of beans can yield 100-200 kg, worth $100-$300. Groundnuts: 200-300 kg per acre, worth $150-$400. Vegetables can bring even more if you have market access. But you must manage competition. Water your intercrops and trees together; never let intercrops dry out the trees. Fertilize intercrops lightlyâtoo much nitrogen makes trees grow leaves instead of fruit. Also, use mulch between rows to conserve moisture. If you have livestock, you can grow fodder like lablab or sweet potato vines. Remember, intercropping is not permanent; it's a bridge to full orchard production. By the time trees bear fruit, you'll have earned income and improved your soil. Always check local markets before planting to ensure you can sell your harvest. And keep records of costs and yields to know which intercrop is most profitable for you.

6. Hands-on: Designing a 1-acre orchard layout on paper

In this lesson, you will design a 1-acre orchard layout on paper. This is a practical exercise that you can do with a pencil, ruler, and graph paper. First, know that 1 acre is about 4,046 square meters, or roughly 63 m x 64 m. Draw a square to represent your acre. Mark the north direction (important for sun). Now decide what you want to plant. For example, a mix of mango, avocado, and citrus. Let's say: 20 mango trees, 20 avocado trees, and 40 citrus trees. Use the spacing rules from Lesson 3: mango 8 m x 8 m, avocado 7 m x 7 m, citrus 5 m x 5 m.

Start by drawing a border of 3 meters around the edge for windbreaks and access. Then divide the remaining area into blocks. For mango: 20 trees at 8 m x 8 m means each tree needs 64 mÂ². 20 trees need 1,280 mÂ². That's a block about 32 m x 40 m. For avocado: 20 trees at 7 m x 7 m = 49 mÂ² each, total 980 mÂ², block about 28 m x 35 m. For citrus: 40 trees at 5 m x 5 m = 25 mÂ² each, total 1,000 mÂ², block about 25 m x 40 m. Add these areas: 1,280 + 980 + 1,000 = 3,260 mÂ². Plus border (3 m wide around 63 m x 64 m = about 760 mÂ²). Total 4,020 mÂ², close to 1 acre. So it fits.

Now draw the blocks on your paper. Place tall mangoes on the south side (if you are in southern hemisphere, south is sunny; if northern, north is sunny) so they don't shade shorter trees. Put citrus on the north side. Leave paths 2 m wide between blocks for wheelbarrows. Mark where you will plant windbreaks (e.g., Grevillea every 3 m along the border). Also mark a water tank or tap point. Then draw the intercrop area: you can plant beans in the spaces between young trees for the first 2 years. Show rows of beans 50 cm apart. Finally, label each tree with a number and variety. This plan will guide your planting. Keep it in a safe place and refer to it when you buy seedlings. You can also use it to calculate costs: 80 trees at $2 each = $160, plus manure, tools, etc. This exercise ensures you don't waste space or plant too closely. A good plan saves money and gives you a productive orchard.

Practical Reference: Planning and Establishing Your Orchard

An orchard is a long-term business asset. A mango or avocado tree can produce for 30â50 years, and citrus for 20â30 years. That means the decisions you make in the first three months â where you plant, how you prepare the land, how you space the trees, and how you manage the young orchard â will determine your income for decades. This practical reference lesson walks you through the six core skills in this module, with a business-first focus: every choice is linked to yield, cost, and market access.

1. Choosing the Best Site: Soil, Water, and Sun

Site selection is the highest-leverage decision you will make. A poorly sited orchard cannot be fixed with fertilizer or irrigation. Before you clear a single tree, test three things: soil, water, and sun.

Soil. Most fruit trees â mango, avocado, and citrus â need deep, well-drained soil at least 1.5 m deep. Waterlogging kills avocado faster than any pest. Dig a 60 cm Ã 60 cm Ã 60 cm pit and fill it with water. If the water has not drained within 24 hours, the site is unsuitable for avocado and risky for citrus. Ideal soil pH is 5.5â6.5 for avocado, 5.5â7.5 for mango, and 5.5â6.5 for citrus. In much of sub-Saharan Africa, soils are acidic (pH 4.5â5.5); agricultural lime corrects this, but it takes 3â6 months to act, so apply it before planting.

Water. A mature mango tree needs 1,000â1,500 mm of rainfall per year; avocado needs 1,000â1,600 mm; citrus needs 900â1,200 mm. If your area receives less, you must plan irrigation. Critically, avocado and citrus are sensitive to saline water (EC above 1.5 dS/m causes leaf burn and yield loss). Test your water source before investing in a pump.

Sun. Fruit trees need full sun â at least 6â8 hours per day. Avoid sites shaded by buildings, mature trees, or steep hillsides facing away from the sun. In East and Southern Africa, north-facing slopes (in the southern hemisphere) or south-facing slopes (in the northern hemisphere) receive more sun and are warmer, which reduces frost risk for avocado.

2. Land Preparation with Hand Tools and Small Machines

Land preparation is where many smallholders overspend or underspend. The goal is simple: remove competition, correct soil chemistry, and create a planting medium that roots can colonize quickly.

Hand tools. For 1â2 acres, hand tools are viable and cost-effective. You need: a slasher or machete (clearing), a hoe (weeding and basin construction), a mattock (breaking hardpan), a shovel (digging planting holes), and a wheelbarrow (moving compost and mulch). A digging bar is essential in rocky or dry soils.

Small machines. For 3+ acres, hire a tractor with a disc plough and harrow. In Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, tractor hire costs roughly USD 40â80 per acre. A brush cutter (USD 150â300) replaces 5â10 laborers for clearing. A water pump (USD 200â500) is essential if you irrigate.

Business tip: Do not deep-plough. Fruit tree roots need undisturbed subsoil structure. Plough only the top 20â30 cm, then dig individual planting holes. This saves fuel, preserves soil biology, and reduces erosion.

4. Planting Grafted Trees Correctly

Grafted trees are expensive â USD 2â5 each in most African nurseries. Poor planting kills that investment. Follow these steps:

Depth. The graft union must sit 15â20 cm above soil level. If buried, the scion can root, and the rootstock's benefits (disease resistance, dwarfing) are lost. This is the single most common planting error.

Mulch. Apply a 10 cm layer of dry grass, straw, or crop residues in a 1 m ring around the tree. Keep mulch 10 cm away from the trunk to prevent termite and rodent damage. Mulch conserves moisture, suppresses weeds, and adds organic matter.

Watering. Water immediately after planting with 10â20 liters. Continue watering twice per week for the first 3 months, then weekly for the first year. In dry areas, a drip line or a 20-liter jerrycan with a small hole is a low-cost solution.

6. Hands-On: Designing a 1-Acre Orchard Layout on Paper

Before you plant, draw your orchard on paper. Use graph paper or a simple grid. Mark the following:

  • Boundary and access paths (3 m wide).
  • Tree positions at your chosen spacing (e.g., 8 m Ã 8 m for mango).
  • Water source and irrigation lines.
  • Intercrop rows between tree rows.
  • Compost and tool storage area.
  • Windbreak on the prevailing wind side (e.g., grevillea or casuarina).

A well-designed 1-acre orchard layout saves time, reduces labor, and prevents costly replanting. Bring your drawing to the next practical session for review.
