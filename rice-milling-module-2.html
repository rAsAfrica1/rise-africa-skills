1. Classification of Rice Varieties

Rice is broadly classified into two species: Oryza sativa (Asian rice) and Oryza glaberrima (African rice). In Africa, O. glaberrima is native to West Africa and is known for its hardiness, but it shatters easily and has lower yield. Most commercial rice in Africa is O. sativa, which includes indica and japonica types. Indica rices are long, slender grains that cook fluffy and separate; they are grown in the lowland tropics and are the most common in Africa. Japonica rices are short, round grains that cook sticky; they are grown in temperate regions and high-altitude areas like Ethiopia and Madagascar. A third group, javanica, is intermediate. You can also classify by ecology: lowland (paddy) rice grows in flooded fields; upland rice grows on dry land, often in rotation with other crops. Lowland rice yields more but needs water control. Upland rice yields less but is important for food security in hilly areas. Another classification is by grain length: long (over 6.6 mm), medium (5.56.6 mm), and short (less than 5.5 mm). Grain length affects milling: long grains break more easily. Also, glutinous (waxy) rice is sticky when cooked and is used for special foods. Aromatic rices like Basmati and Jasmine are long-grain and prized for smell. In Africa, local varieties like Nerica (New Rice for Africa) combine African and Asian traits for drought tolerance and higher yield. Knowing these classes helps you set your mill correctly: long-grain rice needs gentler handling to reduce breakage.

2. Physical Properties of Paddy

Paddy is rice still in its husk. Its physical properties determine how you mill it. Key properties include grain dimensions (length, width, thickness), weight (1000-grain weight), hardness, moisture content, and husk content. Length ranges from 5 to 10 mm, width from 2 to 4 mm. A typical 1000-grain weight is 2030 grams. Hardness affects breakage: harder grains resist cracking. Moisture content is critical: ideal for milling is 1214%. Too dry (below 12%) makes grains brittle and they shatter; too wet (above 14%) makes them sticky and clog the mill. You can test moisture with a simple moisture meter or by biting a grainif it shatters, it's too dry; if it chews, it's too wet. Husk content is about 20% of paddy weight. After milling, you get about 6065% white rice, 20% husk, 10% bran, and 5% germ and losses. Grain shape matters: long slender grains break more easily than short round ones. Also, chalkiness (white opaque spots) weakens grains and lowers milling yield. You can check chalkiness by holding grains against light. Foreign matter like stones, straw, and dirt should be removed before milling to prevent damage. In Africa, many farmers thresh by hand, leaving more foreign matter, so cleaning is essential. Use a simple screen or winnowing to remove light matter. Proper storage keeps moisture stable; store paddy in dry, ventilated bags off the floor. These physical properties guide your mill settings: adjust roller clearance based on grain size, and set husker speed to avoid cracking.

3. Chemical Composition of Rice Kernels

A rice kernel is mostly starch (about 7580% of dry weight), with protein (78%), fat (23%), and small amounts of fibre, vitamins, and minerals. Starch is the main energy source. It comes in two forms: amylose and amylopectin. Amylose makes rice firm and separate; amylopectin makes it sticky. Long-grain indica rices have high amylose (2230%), while short-grain japonica have low amylose (020%). This affects cooking and milling: high-amylose rice tends to break more during milling because it is less flexible. Protein content is higher in the bran and germ, so white rice has less protein than brown rice. Fat is mostly in the bran and germ; it can go rancid if stored too long, so bran should be removed quickly. Vitamins and minerals (B vitamins, iron, zinc) are concentrated in the bran and germ. Milling removes these, which is why white rice is less nutritious. In Africa, where rice is a staple, this matters for nutrition. You can reduce nutrient loss by milling less (e.g., brown rice) or by fortifying white rice. The chemical composition also affects milling: high-fat rice can clog rollers if not cleaned. High-moisture rice has more free water, making it softer and harder to mill without breakage. To test composition simply, you can't do lab tests, but you can observe: rice with a lot of bran (darker) has more nutrients. Aromatic rice has volatile compounds (like 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline) that give smell. These compounds are lost during milling if you over-polish. So, for aromatic varieties, mill gently and avoid excessive polishing.

4. Differences Between Indica and Japonica Rice

Indica and japonica are the two major subspecies of Asian rice. Indica rices are grown in tropical and subtropical lowlands, including most of Africa. They have long, slender grains, and when cooked, they are fluffy and separate. Japonica rices are grown in temperate regions and high altitudes, like Ethiopia and Madagascar. They have short, round grains and cook sticky and moist. Indica plants are usually taller, tiller more, and are less resistant to cold. Japonica plants are shorter, have fewer tillers, and tolerate cold better. In milling, indica grains are longer and more prone to breaking because they are slender. Japonica grains are shorter and rounder, so they break less. Indica rice usually has higher amylose, making it firmer; japonica has lower amylose, making it stickier. This affects milling: indica may need slower roller speeds to reduce breakage. Japonica may need less polishing because it is already sticky. In Africa, indica is dominant because it suits the climate. However, Nerica varieties are interspecific crosses between African rice (O. glaberrima) and Asian rice (O. sativa), often with indica traits. They combine drought tolerance with higher yield. When you mill, check the grain type: if it's long and slender, treat it as indica; if short and round, treat it as japonica. Adjust husker clearance: indica needs wider clearance to avoid cracking; japonica can take tighter clearance. Also, cooking quality differs: consumers in West Africa prefer long-grain indica for dishes like jollof rice, while some East African markets prefer japonica for sticky dishes. Know your market to choose varieties and milling settings.

5. Impact of Variety on Milling Yield

Milling yield is the amount of white rice you get from paddy. It varies by variety. Typically, you get 6065% head rice (whole grains) and 510% broken grains, plus bran and husk. But some varieties give higher yields. For example, long-grain indica rices often have lower head rice yield because they break more easily. Short-grain japonica rices tend to have higher head rice yield because they are rounder and stronger. Nerica varieties can give good yields if milled properly. Other factors: grain hardness, chalkiness, and moisture content. Hard, translucent grains mill better than soft, chalky ones. Chalky grains break easily. Moisture content: if too dry, grains shatter; if too wet, they mash. So, adjust moisture to 1214% before milling. Also, the milling process itself: a two-step process (husking then polishing) gives higher yield than a one-step. In Africa, many small mills use single-pass machines, which cause more breakage. You can improve yield by using a rubber-roll husker instead of a steel husker, and by setting the roller clearance correctly. For long-grain rice, use a clearance of about 0.50.8 mm; for short-grain, 0.30.5 mm. Also, screen the paddy to remove immature grains, which break easily. Immature grains are lighter and can be removed by winnowing. By choosing the right variety and milling carefully, you can increase head rice yield by 510%. That means more income. For example, if you mill 100 kg of paddy, a good yield gives 60 kg head rice; a poor yield gives 50 kg. At $1 per kg, that's $10 difference. So, variety matters.

6. Grain Grading and Specifications

Grain grading sorts milled rice by quality. It affects price. Grades are based on head rice percentage, broken rice percentage, foreign matter, discoloration, and moisture. In many African markets, grades are: Grade 1 (premium): at least 95% head rice, no foreign matter, no discolored grains. Grade 2: 8094% head rice, minimal foreign matter. Grade 3: 6079% head rice, some broken and foreign matter. Below that is off-grade. To grade, you need simple tools: a sieve to separate broken from head rice, a scale to weigh, and your eyes. Take a 100-gram sample. Remove foreign matter (stones, straw). Weigh the head rice (grains at least 75% of original length). Calculate percentage. For example, if you have 85 grams head rice, that's 85%. Also check discoloration: yellow or black grains lower grade. Moisture should be 1214%; test with a moisture meter or by feel. In Africa, many millers sell to local traders who set their own standards. But for export or formal markets, you need to meet standards like those of the West African Rice Development Association (WARDA) or international ISO. For example, Grade 1 rice must have less than 0.1% foreign matter. To improve grade, you can use a destoner to remove stones, a paddy separator to remove immature grains, and a color sorter (if available) to remove discolored grains. Even without machines, you can hand-pick. Grading also involves labeling: put rice in clean bags, label with grade, weight, and date. This builds trust and gets better prices. Remember: consistent grading leads to repeat customers.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Rice Varieties, Grain Characteristics and Milling Decisions

In African rice milling, the variety of paddy that enters your mill determines your recovery rate, your broken-grain percentage, your energy cost per tonne, and ultimately your margin. A miller who understands grain characteristics can negotiate better paddy prices, set the right machine settings, and reduce post-harvest losses that routinely reach 1530% across sub-Saharan Africa. This practical reference consolidates the key technical knowledge from the module into a working tool for mill managers, procurement officers, and agribusiness entrepreneurs.

Rice in Africa is not one product. It is a spectrum: local Oryza glaberrima landraces, improved Oryza sativa varieties from Asia, and new interspecific crosses such as NERICA (New Rice for Africa). Each behaves differently in the mill. Your job is to match the grain to the machine, and the machine to the market.

1. Classification of Rice Varieties What Matters at the Mill Gate

African millers typically receive three categories: local African rice (O. glaberrima), Asian rice (O. sativa, indica and japonica), and interspecific NERICA lines. For milling decisions, classify by grain shape (long, medium, short) and by endosperm type (indica vs japonica). Long-grain indica dominates West African imports and local production in Nigeria, Senegal, Mali, and Tanzania. Japonica is grown in cooler highlands (e.g., Madagascar, Rwanda) and behaves differently it is stickier and more prone to breakage at low moisture.

2. Physical Properties of Paddy Your First Quality Screen

Before you buy, measure moisture, bulk density, thousand-grain weight, and foreign matter. Moisture above 14% causes rubbery kernels that smear in the whitener; below 12% causes brittle kernels that shatter. In African conditions, where sun-drying is dominant, moisture variability between bags is the single largest cause of inconsistent milling yield.

3. Chemical Composition of Rice Kernels

Milled white rice is roughly 7580% starch, 68% protein, and less than 1% fat. The bran removed during whitening contains most of the fat, fibre, vitamins, and minerals which is why bran goes rancid quickly in tropical heat and must be sold or processed fast. For a miller, composition translates into two commercial facts: (1) the more you polish, the more you lose in weight and nutrients; (2) bran is a revenue stream (animal feed, oil extraction) that many African mills discard.

4. Indica vs Japonica Practical Differences

Indica grains are long, slender, and mill to separate, fluffy cooked rice preferred across most of West and East Africa. Japonica grains are short and round, mill to sticky rice, and are more sensitive to moisture during milling. In practice, do not run indica and japonica through the same machine settings without recalibration: japonica needs a wider roller gap and gentler whitening to protect head rice. NERICA lines often behave closer to indica but with variable grain dimensions test each lot before setting the mill.

5. Impact of Variety on Milling Yield

Milling yield (head rice recovery) ranges from 55% to 68% in well-run African mills, versus 6872% in advanced Asian mills. Variety alone can account for a 510 percentage point difference. Long slender indica tends to break more than medium grain; chalky or immature grains shatter; and locally parboiled paddy mills differently from raw paddy. Parboiling widely practised in Nigeria, Mali, and Senegal raises head rice yield and nutrient retention but requires different drying and tempering schedules.

SAFETY WARNING Rice Dust and Aflatoxin Risk

Rice milling generates fine dust that is a respiratory hazard and, in enclosed spaces, a dust-explosion risk. Always operate with adequate ventilation, wear N95 or equivalent dust masks, and never use open flames near milling areas. Separately: paddy stored above 14% moisture in humid African conditions is highly susceptible to Aspergillus flavus and aflatoxin contamination. Aflatoxin is a carcinogen and is not destroyed by milling. Reject mouldy, musty-smelling paddy at intake and never blend it with good stock. Test suspect lots before sale aflatoxin-contaminated rice can destroy your brand and expose you to legal liability.

6. Grain Grading and Specifications

Buyers from local traders to exporters grade milled rice by head rice percentage, broken percentage, moisture, foreign matter, and colour. Common East African and West African market grades require >60% head rice, <5% foreign matter, and 1314% moisture. Export grades to ECOWAS or COMESA markets are stricter. Build a simple intake and outturn log: variety, moisture at intake, head rice at outturn, and broken percentage. Within one season you will know which varieties and which suppliers give you the best margin.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test moisture at intake reject above 14% or below 12% unless you can recondition.
  2. Identify variety (indica, japonica, NERICA, local) and record it in the intake log.
  3. Measure foreign matter and immature grains before agreeing a price.
  4. Recalibrate roller gap and whitening pressure for each variety change.
  5. Never mix varieties in one milling batch it lowers head rice recovery.
  6. Capture bran separately; it is a saleable by-product, not waste.
  7. Record head rice and broken percentage at outturn for every lot.
  8. Reject mouldy or musty paddy aflatoxin risk is non-negotiable.
