1. Introduction and Why It Matters

A business plan is your bakery's roadmap. Without it, you are baking blindly. In Africa's daily bread market, competition is fierce. A plan helps you buy flour at the best price, set a profit margin, and know when to expand. For example, if you sell 200 loaves a day at 10 birr each, that's 2,000 birr daily. But your costsflour, yeast, salt, firewood, labourmight be 1,600 birr. That leaves 400 birr profit. Without a plan, you might not notice that your profit is shrinking because flour prices rose. The five stages of a business plan are: 1) Market Research, 2) Production Plan, 3) Financial Plan, 4) Marketing Plan, and 5) Action Plan. Each stage builds on the last. Many small bakeries fail because they skip research and jump to baking. They don't know their customers or competitors. A plan forces you to answer: Who will buy? At what price? How many? What will it cost? Where will you sell? When will you break even? In Kenya, a woman named Grace started a bakery with no plan. She baked 100 loaves daily but sold only 60. The rest went stale. After writing a simple plan, she reduced to 70 loaves, added mandazi, and increased profit by 30%. Your plan doesn't need to be fancy. A notebook and pen are enough. But you must write it down. This module will guide you through each stage with practical steps, local examples, and tools you can use today. By the end, you will have a one-page business plan ready to guide your daily bread sales.

2. Tools and Materials

To create a business plan, you don't need a computer. A simple exercise book, a pen, and a calculator (or your phone) will do. But you also need information. Gather these tools: 1) A notebook for your plan. 2) A separate notebook for daily records (sales, expenses). 3) A measuring tape to check your workspace. 4) A clock or watch to time baking. 5) A list of suppliers with prices. 6) A map of your area showing potential customers (schools, offices, bus stops). For market research, you need to talk to people. Prepare a simple survey: ask 20 people how many loaves they buy daily, what price they pay, and where they buy. Use tally marks. For financial planning, you need current prices: a bag of flour (50 kg) costs about 1,800 birr in Ethiopia, 2,500 shillings in Kenya, or 1,200 naira in Nigeria. Yeast, salt, sugar, and fuel also have prices. Write them down. For production planning, measure your oven capacity. How many loaves can you bake per hour? A traditional clay oven might bake 30 loaves per batch. A gas oven might do 60. You also need a scale to weigh dough for consistent loaf sizes. For marketing, you need samples to give potential customers. For the action plan, you need a calendar to set deadlines. All these tools are inexpensive and locally available. If you have a smartphone, you can use free apps like Google Sheets or a simple notes app. But paper works just as well. The key is to record everything. Without records, your plan is just guesses. With records, you can see what works and adjust.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these five stages to build your business plan. Stage 1: Market Research. Spend one week talking to 30 potential customers. Ask: How many loaves do you buy daily? What price? Where? Note their answers. Also visit 5 competitors. What do they sell? At what price? What are their busy hours? Write a summary: 'I will sell 150 loaves daily at 10 birr each to workers and students in my area.' Stage 2: Production Plan. Decide how many loaves to bake daily. If demand is 150, bake 160 to allow for spoilage. Calculate ingredients: For 160 loaves, you need 50 kg flour, 1 kg yeast, 1 kg salt, 2 kg sugar, and fuel. Write the steps: mix, knead, proof, bake. Time each step. For example, mixing 30 minutes, kneading 45 minutes, proofing 1 hour, baking 30 minutes. Plan your schedule: start at 4 AM to finish by 7 AM. Stage 3: Financial Plan. List startup costs: oven (5,000 birr), mixer (2,000), trays (500), initial ingredients (3,000), rent (1,500 per month). Total: 12,000 birr. Then monthly costs: flour (1,800 x 30 = 54,000), yeast (200 x 30 = 6,000), fuel (300 x 30 = 9,000), labour (2,000), rent (1,500). Total monthly cost: 72,500 birr. Revenue: 160 loaves x 10 birr x 30 days = 48,000 birr. Waitthat's a loss! So you must adjust: either raise price, reduce costs, or sell more. Maybe sell at 12 birr: 160 x 12 x 30 = 57,600. Still loss. So you need to sell 200 loaves at 12 birr: 72,000. Close. Add mandazi or cakes to increase revenue. This is why the financial plan is crucial. Stage 4: Marketing Plan. How will customers know you? Use signs, word of mouth, free samples. Offer a loyalty card: buy 10 loaves, get 1 free. Partner with local tea shops. Stage 5: Action Plan. Set deadlines: by next Monday, buy flour. By Wednesday, test recipes. By Friday, start selling. Review weekly and adjust.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: No market research. Many bakers assume they know what customers want. Fix: Talk to at least 30 people. Ask about price, quantity, and preferences. In Tanzania, a baker made only white bread, but customers wanted brown. He lost sales. After surveying, he added brown bread and sales rose 40%. Mistake 2: Underestimating costs. You forget to include fuel, transport, or spoilage. Fix: Track every expense for one month. Add 10% for unexpected costs. For example, if you think flour costs 1,800 birr, add 200 for transport. Mistake 3: Overestimating sales. You bake 200 loaves but sell 100. Fix: Start small. Bake 50 loaves for one week. If you sell all, increase by 20 next week. In Uganda, a baker baked 300 loaves on day one and lost half to spoilage. He then started with 80 and grew steadily. Mistake 4: Ignoring competition. If three bakeries sell at 8 birr, you cannot sell at 15. Fix: Visit competitors. Note their price, quality, and location. Differentiate: offer fresher bread, longer hours, or delivery. Mistake 5: No record keeping. Without records, you don't know profit. Fix: Use a simple notebook. Write daily sales and expenses. At week's end, subtract expenses from sales. If profit is low, find why. Mistake 6: Mixing personal and business money. Fix: Keep separate tins or accounts. Pay yourself a fixed salary. Mistake 7: Giving up too soon. Many bakeries take 3-6 months to become profitable. Fix: Review your plan monthly. Adjust prices, products, or marketing. Stay committed. Mistake 8: Not adapting. If a new competitor opens, you must respond. Fix: Add valueoffer sliced bread, home delivery, or bulk discounts. These fixes are simple but require discipline. Write them in your plan as 'What I will do if...' scenarios.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in a bakery is not just about fire and knives; it's also about food safety and financial safety. For food safety: always wash hands before handling dough. Keep raw dough away from baked bread. Store flour in dry, airtight containers to prevent weevils. Use clean water. Bake bread to an internal temperature of 90C to kill bacteria. Cool bread on racks, not on the floor. For fire safety: keep a bucket of sand or water near the oven. Never leave the oven unattended. Wear oven mitts. Keep children away from hot surfaces. For personal safety: lift heavy flour bags with your legs, not your back. Take breaks to avoid fatigue. For financial safety: keep business money separate from personal money. Use a lockable box or mobile money account. Record every transaction immediately. Don't lend business money to friends. Best practices: 1) Weigh ingredients accurately. Inconsistent loaves lose customers. 2) Maintain a cleaning schedule: sweep, wipe, and sanitise daily. 3) Train your workers on hygiene and safety. 4) Have a backup supplier for flour and yeast. 5) Keep a fire extinguisher if possible. 6) Label containers to avoid mixing salt and sugar. 7) Set aside 10% of profit for emergencies. 8) Review your business plan every three months. 9) Network with other bakers to share tips and bulk-buy ingredients. 10) Comply with local health regulations. In many African countries, you need a food handler's permit. Get it. It builds trust. Safety and best practice protect your health, your customers, and your business. They are not optional; they are part of your daily bread.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to write your own one-page business plan. Follow these steps and fill in the blanks. Use a notebook. Step 1: Market Research. Talk to 10 people today. Ask: How many loaves do you buy daily? What price? Where? Record answers. Then visit 3 competitors. Note their price, location, and busy hours. Write a sentence: 'I will sell ____ loaves daily at ____ birr each to ____ (customer type) in ____ (location).' Step 2: Production Plan. Decide daily production. If you plan to sell 100 loaves, bake 110. List ingredients for 110 loaves: flour (35 kg), yeast (0.7 kg), salt (0.7 kg), sugar (1.4 kg), fuel (enough). Write your schedule: start time, mixing, kneading, proofing, baking, cooling. Step 3: Financial Plan. Calculate startup costs: oven, mixer, trays, initial ingredients, rent, permits. Total: ____ birr. Calculate monthly costs: flour, yeast, salt, sugar, fuel, labour, rent, transport. Total: ____ birr. Calculate monthly revenue: loaves per day x price x 30. If revenue is less than costs, adjust price or quantity. Aim for at least 20% profit. Step 4: Marketing Plan. List 3 ways to attract customers: e.g., free samples, loyalty card, signboard. Write your unique selling point: 'My bread is ____ (fresher, cheaper, larger).' Step 5: Action Plan. Set deadlines: By ____ (date), buy ingredients. By ____, test recipe. By ____, start selling. By ____, review sales. Write your plan on one page. Keep it where you can see it daily. Review weekly. Adjust as needed. This exercise turns your dream into a concrete plan. Do it now, and you are on your way to a successful daily bread business.

<h2>Practical Reference: The Business Plan for Daily Bread Sales &amp; Bakery Production</h2>

<p>This practical reference is the working toolkit for the module <strong>The Business Plan: Five Stages</strong>. It is written for African bakery entrepreneurs from a one-oven home baker in Kumasi or Kano to a small commercial bakery supplying kiosks in Nairobi or Lusaka. The five stages of a business plan (Concept, Feasibility, Planning, Execution, Review) are not academic exercises; they are the difference between a bakery that feeds a family for a decade and one that closes within two rainy seasons. Use this document alongside the lessons in this module as a field manual, not a theory text.</p>

<p>Bread is one of the most reliable daily-consumed products in Africa. Demand is stable, margins are thin, and competition is local. A disciplined business plan is therefore not optional it is the operating system of your bakery. Every number you write down should be traceable to a real cost, a real customer, or a real supplier.</p>

<h3>Stage 1: Concept Defining Your Bakery</h3>
<p>Before you bake a single loaf for sale, write one paragraph answering: what will you sell, to whom, at what price, and why will they buy from you? In most African markets, this means choosing between white bread, brown bread, rolls, or a mix. Your concept should be specific enough to test. "I will bake 200 loaves of white bread daily for kiosk resellers in my ward" is a concept. "I will start a bakery" is not.</p>

<h3>Stage 2: Feasibility Testing the Numbers</h3>
<p>Feasibility is arithmetic, not optimism. You must calculate your cost per loaf, your break-even point, and your realistic daily sales volume. Most bakery failures in Africa come from underestimating ingredient cost volatility (flour, yeast, sugar, cooking oil) and overestimating daily sales in the first three months.</p>

<h3>Stage 3: Planning The Written Plan</h3>
<p>Your plan should cover operations, marketing, finance, and risk. In the African context, three risk items dominate: (1) flour price shocks driven by import dependency, (2) power or fuel interruptions, and (3) seasonal demand shifts around school terms and religious festivals. Build contingency into your plan, not just your hopes.</p>

<h3>Stage 4: Execution Baking and Selling</h3>
<p>Execution is where plans meet reality. Keep a daily production log: loaves baked, loaves sold, loaves returned, cash collected, cash outstanding from resellers. In many African markets, credit sales to kiosks are the norm but credit without a ledger kills bakeries. Track every borrower, every amount, and every due date.</p>

<h3>Stage 5: Review Measuring and Adjusting</h3>
<p>Review weekly, not annually. Compare actual sales, costs, and margins against your plan. Adjust prices, recipes, or routes accordingly. A bakery that reviews weekly survives; one that reviews "when things get bad" usually does not.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#b00020;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING Food Safety and Fire Risk</p>
<p style="margin:0;color:#7a0016;font-size:14px;">Bakery work in small African operations carries two serious risks. <strong>First, food safety:</strong> bread sold for daily consumption must be baked to an internal temperature of at least 90C and cooled on clean, raised surfaces never on the floor. Contaminated bread can cause widespread illness and will destroy your business reputation permanently. <strong>Second, fire and burn risk:</strong> wood-fired and gas ovens in poorly ventilated rooms are a leading cause of burns and fires in small bakeries. Keep a fire extinguisher or sand bucket within 3 metres of the oven, never leave a lit oven unattended, and ensure children are kept away from the baking area at all times. Comply with your national food safety authority (e.g., NAFDAC in Nigeria, FDA Ghana, KEBS in Kenya).</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Concept written:</strong> One paragraph stating product, customer, price, and reason to buy.</li>
<li><strong>Cost per loaf calculated:</strong> Flour, yeast, sugar, oil, fuel, labour, packaging, transport, depreciation.</li>
<li><strong>Break-even point known:</strong> Minimum loaves per day to cover all fixed and variable costs.</li>
<li><strong>Cash flow forecast for 12 months:</strong> Including a bad month for flour price rises.</li>
<li><strong>Supplier list with at least two options per input:</strong> Never depend on one flour miller.</li>
<li><strong>Daily production and sales log in use:</strong> Written, not remembered.</li>
<li><strong>Credit ledger for resellers:</strong> Name, amount, date, due date, signature or thumbprint.</li>
<li><strong>Food safety and fire safety measures in place:</strong> Clean cooling racks, extinguisher or sand, ventilation, restricted access.</li>
</ol>
