1. Why Aluminium Is Different from Steel

Aluminium is not just a softer steel. If you treat it like steel, you will fail. Steel is heavy and strong; aluminium is light and conducts heat about five times faster. That means when you weld aluminium, the heat runs away from the weld pool quickly. You need more amps and a faster travel speed. Steel welds at around 1,500C; aluminium melts at about 660C, but its oxide layer melts at over 2,000C. That oxide skin forms instantly and must be broken before you can weld. With TIG, you use AC current to clean the oxide. With MIG, you need a push technique and a spool gun or a teflon liner because aluminium wire is soft and birds-nests easily.

Aluminium also does not change colour when it gets hot. Steel glows red before it melts; aluminium stays silver until it suddenly collapses. You must learn to judge the weld pool by its fluidity and sound, not by colour. Porosity is a big enemy. Aluminium absorbs hydrogen when molten, so you must clean the metal with a stainless steel brush dedicated to aluminium, then wipe with acetone or alcohol. Do not use the same brush on steel.

For a beginner in Africa, the most common aluminium jobs are window frames, door frames, roofing sheets, and repairs on cooking pots or water tanks. These are often thin materials, 1mm to 3mm. You cannot weld thin aluminium with a stick welder. You need TIG or MIG. A basic inverter TIG machine with AC/DC capability costs around $500 to $800 new. A MIG with a spool gun is similar. If you cannot afford that yet, start by practising on scrap with a rented machine or borrow one. The key difference is preparation: clean, clean, clean. Then use a stainless steel wire brush, then weld hot and fast. That is the aluminium way.

2. The Market for Aluminium Welding

In most African cities, aluminium is the material of choice for windows, doors, shop fronts, and roofing. Why? It does not rust in the rain, it is light to transport, and it looks modern. Steel rusts; wood rots; aluminium lasts. That means there is constant demand for welders who can work with it. Look around your town: new buildings, renovated shops, and even informal settlements are using aluminium roofing sheets and window frames. The market is not just big factories; it is small workshops and individual homeowners.

Your customers will come from three main groups. First, construction contractors who need window and door frames fabricated and installed. Second, homeowners and landlords who need repairs or custom pieces like gates, burglar bars, or carports. Third, other businesses like restaurants, bars, and shops that need signage, counters, or display stands. Each group has different needs. Contractors want speed and consistency; homeowners want a fair price and a neat finish; businesses want durability and a design that attracts customers.

To tap this market, you do not need a big shop. You can start from a small rented space or even your backyard. But you must be visible. Make a signboard with your phone number. Visit construction sites and introduce yourself to foremen. Offer to do a small repair for free or at cost to show your skill. Word of mouth is powerful. In many African communities, one satisfied customer brings five more. Also, consider mobile welding: a small generator, a TIG machine, and a grinder in a pickup or on a motorbike can take you to the customer. That saves them transport and saves you rent.

The competition may be other welders, but many of them only know steel. If you master aluminium, you can charge more. Aluminium welding is seen as specialised. So market yourself as the aluminium specialist. Use WhatsApp to share photos of your work. Join local business groups on Facebook. The demand is there; you just need to position yourself to capture it.

3. Your Customers and What They Buy

Knowing your customer is as important as knowing your torch. In aluminium welding, your customers are buying solutions, not just welds. A homeowner whose aluminium window is broken wants it fixed quickly and neatly, without damaging the wall or the glass. A contractor building a new office block wants 50 window frames delivered on time, all identical, and ready to install. A restaurant owner wants a new aluminium counter that looks good and can handle heat and spills. Each of these customers has a different definition of quality and urgency.

Start by listening. Ask what the piece is for, where it will be installed, and how long it needs to last. A window frame in a coastal town needs to resist salt air; a roofing sheet in a hot, dry area needs to reflect heat. Your welding must be strong enough for the load. For example, a gate needs deep penetration; a decorative panel can have smaller, neater welds. Also ask about finish: do they want the weld ground smooth and painted, or is a clean TIG bead acceptable? Many customers prefer the look of a stacked TIG bead on aluminium because it shows craftsmanship.

Pricing is part of understanding the customer. Some customers are price-sensitive; others value speed and quality. You can offer tiers: a basic repair, a standard fabrication, and a premium finish. For example, a simple window frame repair might be $20; a custom-made aluminium door might be $150; a full shop front could be $500 or more. Always give a written quote that lists materials, labour, and any extras like installation or transport. That builds trust.

Finally, remember that customers talk. If you do a good job, they will refer you. If you do a bad job, they will tell everyone. So under-promise and over-deliver. Confirm the deadline, then finish a day early. Clean up the site. That is how you build a reputation as the go-to aluminium welder in your area.

4. Costing Your Work

If you do not know your costs, you cannot know your profit. Many welders in Africa undercharge because they forget to count everything. Let us break it down. First, materials: aluminium rods, wire, gas, and filler. For TIG, a 1kg spool of 4043 filler wire costs about $15. Argon gas cylinder refill is around $50 and lasts for maybe 10 hours of welding. For MIG, you need a spool gun and wire, similar cost. Then there is electricity or generator fuel. A small TIG machine draws about 3kW; if you run it for 4 hours a day, that is 12kWh. At $0.15 per kWh, that is $1.80 per day. If you use a generator, fuel might be $5 per day.

Next, consumables: tungsten electrodes ($2 each, last a few hours), nozzles, collets, and a stainless steel brush ($5). You also need grinding discs, cutting discs, and PPE like gloves and a helmet. These add up. Then there is your time. How much is your hour worth? If you are just starting, maybe $5 per hour. As you gain skill, $10 to $20 per hour. Do not forget overhead: rent for your workshop, transport to buy materials, phone data, and taxes. If you work from home, still count a portion of your electricity and water.

Let us do an example. You make a simple aluminium window frame. Materials: 2 metres of aluminium profile at $8 per metre = $16. Filler wire and gas: $3. Electricity: $0.50. Consumables: $1. Labour: 1 hour at $10 = $10. Overhead: $2. Total cost = $32.50. If you charge $50, your profit is $17.50. That is a 35% margin. But if you forgot the gas and electricity, you might think you made $30 profit, when really you made less. So always write down every cost. Use a notebook or a simple phone app. At the end of each job, record actual costs. After a month, you will know your true costs and can price better.

5. Pricing for Profit

Pricing is not guessing. It is a strategy. You want to cover costs, pay yourself, and grow. The first rule: never price based on what the other guy charges. He might be losing money. Instead, use a simple formula: (Materials + Labour + Overhead) + Profit Margin. Your profit margin should be at least 20% to 30% for small jobs, and 15% to 20% for large contracts. But you also need to consider the market. If you are the only aluminium welder in town, you can charge more. If there are many, you must compete on quality and speed, not just price.

Value-based pricing is powerful. For example, a customer needs a broken aluminium door fixed urgently because they are losing business. That repair is worth more to them than $50. You can charge $80 and they will happily pay if you do it same day. On the other hand, a homeowner who is not in a hurry might negotiate. So ask about urgency. Also, offer packages: a basic repair with a 1-month guarantee, or a premium repair with a 1-year guarantee. Charge 20% more for the premium. Many customers will choose the premium because they want peace of mind.

Do not forget to factor in risk. Aluminium welding can go wrong; you might burn a hole or get porosity. Build a small buffer into your price, maybe 10%, to cover rework. Also, consider payment terms. For large jobs, ask for 50% deposit upfront, 50% on completion. That protects you from non-payment. For small jobs, cash on delivery. If a customer wants credit, charge a little more or require a signed agreement.

Finally, review your prices every three months. Costs change: gas prices go up, electricity tariffs increase. If you have not raised prices in a year, you are likely earning less. Keep a simple price list for common jobs: window frame per metre, door per unit, repair per hour. That makes quoting fast and consistent. Remember, profit is not greed; it is what keeps your business alive and your family fed.

6. The Records That Make You Money

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Records are not paperwork for its own sake; they are your business memory. Start with a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone. For every job, record: date, customer name, contact, description of work, materials used, hours worked, total cost, price charged, and profit. Also note any problems: did you run out of gas? Did the customer change the design? That helps you quote better next time. At the end of each week, add up your income and expenses. At the end of the month, calculate your profit. If you are not making a profit, you are not in business; you have a hobby.

Keep separate records for materials and tools. Know how much aluminium profile you have in stock. Know when your gas cylinder is nearly empty. That prevents last-minute trips that waste time and fuel. Also, record your machine maintenance: when you changed the tungsten, when you cleaned the liner, when you serviced the generator. A well-maintained machine lasts longer and welds better. If you have employees, record their hours and pay. That avoids disputes.

For taxes, many African countries have a small business tax regime. Even if you are informal, keeping records helps you know if you are growing. You can also use records to get a loan or grant. Banks and microfinance institutions want to see cash flow. A simple profit and loss statement for six months can open doors. And when you want to raise your prices, your records show you exactly why: because your costs went up.

Finally, use records to learn. Which jobs are most profitable? Maybe custom gates earn more than window repairs. Then focus on marketing those. Which customers pay late? Maybe you need to ask for deposits. Your records will tell you. So buy a notebook today. Number the pages. Write every job. It is the simplest habit that separates a struggling welder from a successful aluminium business owner.

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-weight:bold; color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Aluminium welding hazards</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-size:13px; color:#222;">Aluminium welding produces <strong>ozone and nitrogen oxides</strong> far more readily than steel welding, plus fine <strong>aluminium oxide fume</strong> and, if the base metal contains magnesium, <strong>magnesium oxide fume</strong> that can cause metal fume fever. <strong>Never weld in a closed room without forced extraction and open ventilation.</strong> Aluminium is also highly reflective to UV arc eye can occur even at a distance, so screen off the work area and warn bystanders. Store gas cylinders upright and chained, never in a vehicle cab. When grinding aluminium, use a dedicated disc never one previously used on steel, to avoid contamination and sparks igniting fine aluminium dust.</p>

<h3>4. The Records That Make You Money</h3>

<p>For every job, keep a one-page job card. It should record: client name and contact, date, alloy and thickness, filler and gas used, machine settings, welder name, weld lengths, any defects found and how they were fixed, photos before and after, materials issued from stock, hours worked, and the final invoice number. This is not bureaucracy it is your evidence when a client disputes a price, your costing data for the next quote, and the record that lets you see which types of work actually make profit. In many African markets it is also what separates you from informal competitors when bidding for NGO, government or corporate contracts that require traceability.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.6;">
<li><strong>Identify the alloy and thickness</strong> before quoting 6061, 5052, 5083 and castings all weld differently.</li>
<li><strong>Run a test coupon</strong> on scrap of the same alloy before touching the customer's material.</li>
<li><strong>Cost every job with all seven lines</strong>: wire, gas, power, consumables, labour, transport, then add 2035% markup.</li>
<li><strong>Never quote from memory</strong> use your own historical job cards to price the next one.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilate and extract</strong> before striking an arc; check that fume is moving away from your face.</li>
<li><strong>Screen the arc</strong> aluminium's reflectivity means bystanders can be injured without looking directly at the weld.</li>
<li><strong>Complete a job card</strong> with photos and settings for every job, no exceptions.</li>
<li><strong>Invoice in writing</strong> with a reference number, and record the date payment was received.</li>
</ol>
