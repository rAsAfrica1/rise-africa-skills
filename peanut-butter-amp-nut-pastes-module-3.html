1. Cleaning Groundnuts: Removing Stones, Dirt, and Bad Nuts

Cleaning groundnuts is the first and most important step in making good peanut butter. Dirty nuts can spoil the taste and even make people sick. Start by spreading your raw groundnuts on a clean tarpaulin or a large wooden tray in a thin layer. Pick out stones, sticks, and any foreign matter by hand. This is best done in small batches so you don't miss anything. A simple trick is to run your fingers through the nuts and feel for hard objects. For larger amounts, use a sieve or a locally made wire mesh to shake out dirt and small stones. You can also use a fan or the wind to blow away light chaff and dust. After dry cleaning, wash the nuts in clean water if they are very dirty. Soak them for 5-10 minutes, rub them between your hands, then rinse until the water runs clear. Spread them out to dry in the sun for 1-2 days, or use a solar dryer. Never roast wet nuts; they must be completely dry. Next, remove bad nuts: discolor, shriveled, mouldy, or insect-damaged nuts. Mouldy nuts can contain aflatoxins, which are dangerous. Discard them immediately. A good rule: if in doubt, throw it out. For consistent quality, do this cleaning step every time, even if the nuts look clean. Aim to remove at least 99% of foreign matter. A 50 kg bag of raw groundnuts typically yields about 45 kg of clean nuts after removing stones, dirt, and bad nuts. This step takes time but ensures a safe and tasty final product. Remember, clean nuts make clean peanut butter.

2. Roasting Methods: Pan Roasting, Sand Roasting, and Oven Roasting

Roasting brings out the flavour and makes peeling easier. There are three common methods: pan roasting, sand roasting, and oven roasting. Pan roasting: Use a heavy-bottomed pan, like a cast iron or thick aluminium pot. Heat it over medium fire. Add a single layer of clean, dry groundnuts. Stir constantly with a wooden spoon to avoid burning. Roast for 10-15 minutes until they are golden brown and smell nutty. This method is good for small batches (1-2 kg). Sand roasting: Heat clean, dry sand in a large pot or pan. The sand should be hot but not smoking. Add groundnuts and stir continuously. The sand helps distribute heat evenly and prevents burning. Roast for 8-12 minutes. Then separate the nuts from the sand using a sieve. This method is faster and works well for larger batches (5-10 kg). Oven roasting: Preheat an oven to 160-180C. Spread groundnuts in a single layer on a baking tray. Roast for 15-20 minutes, stirring every 5 minutes. This method is less labour-intensive but requires an oven. For all methods, the key is constant stirring and watching closely. The nuts will darken and release oil. Test by taking a few out, cooling, and tasting. They should be crunchy, not soft. Over-roasting makes them bitter and oily; under-roasting makes peeling hard and flavour weak. After roasting, cool the nuts completely before peeling. Roasting also kills bacteria and reduces moisture, which helps the peanut butter last longer. For a 10 kg batch, pan roasting takes about 45 minutes, sand roasting 30 minutes, and oven roasting 25 minutes. Always roast in a well-ventilated area.

3. Determining Perfect Roast: Color, Aroma, and Taste Tests

Knowing when groundnuts are perfectly roasted is a skill you can learn with practice. You need to check three things: colour, aroma, and taste. Colour: Raw groundnuts are pale, whitish-yellow. As they roast, they turn light brown, then golden brown, and finally dark brown. The perfect roast is a uniform golden brown. If they are still pale, they need more time. If they are dark brown or black, they are over-roasted and will taste bitter. Aroma: Raw nuts smell earthy. As they roast, they release a rich, nutty aroma. When you can smell that strong, pleasant nutty smell, they are close to done. If you smell a burnt or acrid smell, they are over-roasting. Taste: The most reliable test. Take a few nuts out, let them cool for a minute (they are hot!), and taste. Perfectly roasted nuts are crunchy, slightly sweet, and have a deep nutty flavour. If they taste raw or bland, roast longer. If they taste bitter or burnt, you've gone too far. Another test: rub a nut between your fingers. The skin should come off easily. If the skin sticks, they need more roasting. Also, listen for a crackling sound as they roast; when the crackling slows down, they are nearly done. For consistent results, roast in small batches and check every 2 minutes after the first 10 minutes. Record the time and temperature that works for your setup. Different groundnut varieties and sizes roast at different rates. For example, small nuts roast faster than large ones. Always preheat your pan or oven. After roasting, spread the nuts on a cool tray to stop the cooking process. Do not pile them up, as they will continue to cook from residual heat. Perfect roast is key to great peanut butter.

4. Peeling Groundnuts: Manual and Mechanical Methods

After roasting and cooling, the next step is peeling off the skins. Peeling is important because skins can make peanut butter bitter and gritty. There are manual and mechanical methods. Manual peeling: This is the most common for small-scale producers. Rub the cooled nuts between your hands or on a rough surface like a woven basket or a clean piece of burlap. The skins will flake off. For larger quantities, put nuts in a sack and beat them gently with a wooden stick, then winnow. Another manual method is to rub them on a wire mesh. This takes time: one person can peel about 2-3 kg per hour. It's labour-intensive but requires no special equipment. Mechanical peeling: If you produce larger volumes, consider a groundnut peeler. These machines range from simple hand-cranked models to motorised ones. A small motorised peeler can process 50-100 kg per hour. They work by rubbing the nuts against a rotating drum or abrasive surface. You can find these machines from agricultural suppliers or fabricators. They cost between $200 and $1000, depending on capacity. When using a mechanical peeler, adjust the spacing so it doesn't break the nuts. Broken nuts are fine for peanut butter, but whole nuts look better if you sell them. After peeling, you must separate the skins from the nuts. This is done by winnowing (see next lesson). Peeling efficiency: manual methods remove about 80-90% of skins; mechanical methods can remove 95% or more. Some skins may remain, especially in the crevices. You can rub the nuts again or use a fan to blow away loose skins. Don't worry if a few skins remain; they won't ruin the peanut butter. But too many skins make it bitter. After peeling, use the nuts immediately or store them in an airtight container to prevent them from absorbing moisture. Peeling is easier when nuts are roasted and cooled completely. If nuts are still warm, skins stick.

5. Cooling and Winnowing to Remove Skins

After roasting, groundnuts must be cooled completely before peeling. Cooling stops the cooking process and makes skins brittle and easy to remove. Spread the hot nuts in a single layer on a clean, dry tray or mat. Do not pile them up, as trapped heat will cause them to steam and become soft. Let them cool for 20-30 minutes at room temperature. You can speed cooling by using a fan, but avoid blowing dust onto the nuts. Once cooled, the skins will be loose and flaky. Now, winnowing: This is the process of separating the skins from the nuts. The traditional method is to toss the nuts in the air using a winnowing basket or a flat tray. The wind blows away the lighter skins while the heavier nuts fall back. Do this on a windy day or use a fan. Stand with your back to the wind, hold the basket, and toss the nuts gently. The skins fly away, and the nuts land back in the basket. Repeat until most skins are removed. For small quantities, you can also use a hair dryer on cool setting or an electric fan. Another method is to pour the nuts from one container to another in front of a fan. The skins blow away. For larger scale, use a mechanical winnower, which is a machine that uses air to separate skins. These are available from agricultural equipment suppliers. After winnowing, check for any remaining skins. You can rub the nuts again and winnow once more. Aim to remove at least 95% of skins. Remaining skins are okay but can add bitterness. Winnowing also removes any small stones or debris that were missed. Do this in a well-ventilated area, as the dust can be irritating. Wear a mask if you are sensitive. After winnowing, your nuts are ready for grinding into peanut butter. Store them in a clean, dry container. If you are not grinding immediately, keep them in an airtight container in a cool, dry place. They will stay fresh for up to 2 weeks. Proper cooling and winnowing ensure a smooth, tasty peanut butter.

6. Quality Control: Ensuring Consistent Roast and Cleanliness

Quality control is what separates a good peanut butter from a great one. You need to ensure every batch is consistent in roast and cleanliness. Start by setting standards. For roast, decide on a target colour (e.g., golden brown) and aroma. Use a colour chart or sample photos. Train yourself to recognise the perfect roast by sight, smell, and taste. For cleanliness, aim for zero foreign matter, stones, or bad nuts. Implement checks at each step. After cleaning, inspect a handful of nuts for stones or dirt. After roasting, check colour and taste. After peeling and winnowing, check for remaining skins. Keep a record of times, temperatures, and batch sizes. This helps you repeat success. Use simple tools: a thermometer for oven roasting, a timer, and a scale. For pan roasting, use a timer and your senses. Consistency also means uniform roasting. If you roast unevenly, some nuts will be burnt and others raw. To avoid this, roast in small batches, stir constantly, and use a single layer. For large batches, use a mechanical roaster, like a drum roaster, which rotates for even roasting. Cleanliness also means hygiene. Wash your hands, wear clean clothes, and keep your work area clean. Use clean water for washing. Store nuts in clean, covered containers. Avoid contamination from pests. If you find mouldy nuts, discard them and clean the area. Aflatoxins are a serious health risk. Test for moisture: good roasted nuts should have less than 6% moisture. You can use a moisture meter or the salt test: put a nut in a saturated salt solution; if it floats, moisture is too high. For small producers, simply ensure nuts are dry and crunchy. Finally, do a final quality check before grinding. Taste a few nuts. They should be crunchy, nutty, and free of off-flavours. If they are not perfect, adjust your process. Consistent quality builds customer trust and allows you to charge a better price. Keep a notebook and review your notes regularly.

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Cleaning, Roasting, and Peeling Groundnuts for Nut Paste Production</h2>

<p>In African groundnut-processing businesses from small kiosks in Kampala and Kumasi to mid-scale plants in Dakar and Lilongwe the quality of your final peanut butter is decided long before the jar is filled. Cleaning, roasting, and peeling are the make-or-break steps. Get them right and you produce a smooth, sweet, shelf-stable paste that commands premium prices. Get them wrong and you get bitter, gritty, mouldy product that loses customers and may breach food-safety standards.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference is written for the operator who must deliver consistent output every single day, often with limited equipment and unpredictable raw material. It covers stone and dirt removal, three roasting methods, how to judge a perfect roast without a lab, manual and mechanical peeling, cooling and winnowing, and the quality-control habits that keep your brand trusted.</p>

<h3>1. Cleaning Groundnuts: Removing Stones, Dirt, and Bad Nuts</h3>

<p>Raw groundnuts from smallholder farms typically arrive with soil, stones, plant debris, shrivelled kernels, and critically mouldy or discoloured nuts. Aflatoxin-producing moulds (<em>Aspergillus flavus</em> and <em>A. parasiticus</em>) thrive on damaged and improperly dried kernels, so cleaning is a food-safety step, not just a cosmetic one.</p>

<p>Work in sequence: <strong>destoning sieving/screening manual sorting aspiration</strong>. A simple vibrating sieve or a locally fabricated rotary drum removes fine soil and chaff. Destoning can be done with a gravity separator (aspirated table) or, at micro scale, by flotation in a salt bath or by hand-picking on a tilted tray. Manual sorting on a light-coloured table under good light is the cheapest and most reliable way to remove discoloured, shrivelled, and mouldy kernels the ones most likely to carry aflatoxin.</p>

<p>Aspiration (a fan or blower) removes light chaff and dust. For food businesses, always keep a "reject" container and record the percentage removed this is your first quality metric.</p>

<h3>2. Roasting Methods: Pan, Sand, and Oven Roasting</h3>

<p>Roasting develops flavour, reduces moisture (improving shelf life), loosens skins for peeling, and inactivates some enzymes and microbes. The three practical methods in African processing are pan roasting, sand roasting, and oven roasting.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Pan roasting:</strong> A heavy cast-iron or steel pan over a controlled fire or gas burner. Best for small batches (25 kg). Requires constant stirring. Fast, cheap, but operator-dependent.</li>
<li><strong>Sand roasting:</strong> Clean, dry river sand is heated in a pan or drum, then groundnuts are added. Sand distributes heat evenly and prevents scorching. Common in West Africa. Must use clean sand and sieve it out afterwards.</li>
<li><strong>Oven roasting:</strong> A tray oven or convection oven at 160180 C for 1525 minutes. Best for consistency and larger batches. Requires trays loaded in a single layer for even roasting.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. Determining Perfect Roast: Colour, Aroma, and Taste Tests</h3>

<p>Under-roasted nuts produce a raw, "green" taste and poor texture. Over-roasted nuts turn bitter, dark, and can develop burnt notes that ruin the paste. The target is a uniform, golden-brown kernel with a nutty, sweet aroma and no bitterness.</p>

<p>Three practical tests, all requiring no laboratory:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Colour test:</strong> Break a few kernels. The inside should be an even light golden-brown. Dark brown or black centres mean over-roast; pale cream means under-roast.</li>
<li><strong>Aroma test:</strong> The roast should smell sweet and nutty, not acrid or smoky. If you sneeze or the air burns your eyes, the batch is scorching.</li>
<li><strong>Taste test:</strong> Chew one cooled kernel. It should be crisp, sweet, and free of bitterness. If it sticks to your teeth, it is under-roasted.</li>
</ul>

<p>Record roast time, temperature, and batch weight for every run. Consistency comes from repeatable records, not memory.</p>

<h3>4. Peeling Groundnuts: Manual and Mechanical Methods</h3>

<p>Peeling removes the papery seed coat (testa). Skins left on contribute bitterness and a gritty texture, and can darken the paste. Two routes:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Manual peeling:</strong> Rub cooled nuts between palms, in a woven basket, or with a wooden paddle. Suitable for 520 kg/day. Labour-intensive but low capital.</li>
<li><strong>Mechanical peeling:</strong> A groundnut peeler/dehuller (often a rubber-roller or abrasive-drum machine) handles 100500 kg/hour. Available from local fabricators and Indian/Chinese suppliers. Requires correct roller gap; too tight crushes kernels.</li>
</ul>

<p>Peel immediately after cooling. Warm nuts peel more easily, but hot nuts crush. Aim for the "warm but handleable" window roughly 510 minutes after removing from heat.</p>

<h3>5. Cooling and Winnowing to Remove Skins</h3>

<p>Cooling is not optional. Hot nuts continue to roast internally ("carry-over roasting") and become over-roasted and bitter. Spread roasted nuts in a thin layer (23 cm) on clean trays or mats in a shaded, ventilated area for 1020 minutes. Never cool in a sealed container condensation causes sogginess and mould risk.</p>

<p>Winnowing separates loosened skins from kernels. Traditional winnowing uses a woven tray or basket tossed in a breeze. Mechanical winnowers use a fan and vibrating screen. In humid conditions, aspiration alone may not remove skins; a light rub before winnowing helps.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020;background:#fdecec;border-radius:8px;padding:14px 18px;margin:18px 0;">
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Aflatoxin and Mould Risk</p>
<p style="margin:0;color:#5a1010;">Never use mouldy, discoloured, or shrivelled groundnuts. Aflatoxin is a potent carcinogen and is not destroyed by normal roasting or boiling. Reject any batch with visible mould, musty smell, or yellow-green discolouration. Store raw nuts below 8% moisture in dry, ventilated conditions. Wear a dust mask during cleaning and winnowing to avoid inhaling mould spores and dust. If in doubt, throw the batch out a lost batch is cheaper than a lost customer or a health incident.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Quality Control: Ensuring Consistent Roast and Cleanliness</h3>

<p>Quality control in groundnut processing is a set of simple, recorded habits. For every batch, record: supplier, date received, moisture (using a cheap moisture meter or the salt-and-bottle method), weight in, weight after cleaning, roasting method, roast time and temperature, colour score, taste score, peeling yield, and final kernel weight.</p>

<p>Use a colour reference chart (a set of roasted kernel samples from pale to dark) to train staff. Hold a daily 5-minute taste panel with 23 staff. Reject any batch that fails colour or taste. Clean all equipment between batches to prevent cross-contamination and rancid oil build-up.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Inspect incoming raw nuts; reject mouldy, discoloured, or damp batches immediately.</li>
<li>Destone, sieve, and hand-sort on a light table under good light.</li>
<li>Weigh and record every batch before and after cleaning.</li>
<li>Roast using the chosen method; record time, temperature, and batch size.</li>
<li>Test colour, aroma, and taste; reject any batch that fails.</li>
<li>Cool in a thin layer for 1020 minutes; never pack hot.</li>
<li>Peel within the warm window; winnow to remove skins.</li>
<li>Record final yield and clean all equipment before the next batch.</li>
</ol>
