1. Common Weeds in Onion Fields

Weeds are the biggest thief in your onion field. They steal water, nutrients, and sunlight, and they can cut your harvest by half if you ignore them. In Africa, the most common weeds in onion fields include pigweed (Amaranthus), blackjack (Bidens pilosa), goosegrass (Eleusine indica), nutgrass (Cyperus rotundus), and striga (Striga hermonthica). These weeds grow fast and compete fiercely with onions, especially in the first 6 weeks after transplanting.

Pigweed grows tall and wide, shading young onion leaves. Blackjack has sticky seeds that stick to your clothes and tools, spreading quickly. Goosegrass is a grassy weed that looks like young onion seedlings but grows faster. Nutgrass is a sedge with underground nuts that survive even after you pull the plant. Striga attaches to onion roots and sucks nutrients, causing stunting and yellowing.

You must identify these weeds early. Walk your field every 3 days. Pull out any weed you see before it flowers. A single pigweed plant can produce 100,000 seeds. If you let one weed go to seed, you will fight it for years. Learn to recognize seedlings: pigweed has red stems and oval leaves; blackjack has yellow flowers and black seeds; goosegrass has finger-like seed heads.

In Africa, many farmers also struggle with spear grass (Imperata cylindrica) and couch grass (Cynodon dactylon). These have deep roots and spread underground. You cannot control them by hand weeding alone. You need to combine methods. For example, in Kenya, farmers use mulching with dry grass to smother these weeds. In Nigeria, crop rotation with maize or cowpea helps break the weed cycle.

Always keep your field clean. Remove weeds from paths and borders. Don't let weeds grow around your water source. Use a hoe or hand fork to uproot them. If you see nutgrass, dig out the underground nuts and burn them. Never put weeds with seeds into your compost. Burn or feed them to livestock. Remember, weed control is not a one-time job. It is a continuous battle from planting to harvest.

2. Cultural Weed Control: Crop Rotation and Mulching

Cultural weed control means using farming practices to stop weeds from growing. Two of the best methods are crop rotation and mulching. They are cheap, safe, and work well in African conditions.

Crop rotation: Do not plant onions in the same bed season after season. Weeds that like onions will build up. Instead, rotate with crops that have different growth habits. For example, plant maize or sorghum after onions. These tall crops shade the ground and reduce weed growth. Or plant cowpea or groundnut, which cover the soil quickly and smother weeds. In Ethiopia, farmers rotate onions with faba beans, which also add nitrogen to the soil. A good rotation is: onions maize cowpea onions. This breaks the life cycle of weeds like pigweed and blackjack.

Mulching: Mulch is any material you spread on the soil surface to block sunlight. Weeds need light to grow. Without light, their seeds cannot sprout. Use dry grass, straw, rice husks, or dry leaves. Spread a layer 510 cm thick around your onion plants. Do not pile mulch against the onion stems, as this can cause rot. Mulching also keeps soil moist and cool, which onions love. In Tanzania, farmers use dry banana leaves as mulch. In Senegal, they use peanut shells. You can also use black plastic mulch, but it is expensive. If you use organic mulch, make sure it is free of weed seeds. Do not use fresh grass that has seeds.

Another cultural method is planting cover crops like mucuna or lablab between onion rows. These smother weeds and can be cut and left as mulch. Also, plant onions at the right spacing (10 cm between plants, 30 cm between rows) so they form a canopy that shades the ground. A dense onion crop competes better with weeds.

Finally, solarization: In hot African regions, you can cover moist soil with clear plastic for 46 weeks. The sun heats the soil to 5060C, killing weed seeds near the surface. This is good for small plots. Combine rotation and mulching for best results. They reduce your weeding work and improve soil health.

3. Mechanical Weed Control: Cultivation and Hand Weeding

Mechanical weed control means using tools or your hands to remove weeds. It is the most common method in Africa because it is cheap and effective if done correctly. The two main methods are cultivation (using hoes or cultivators) and hand weeding.

Cultivation: Use a hoe or a hand cultivator to disturb the soil and cut weeds. Do this when weeds are small, before they flower. For onions, be careful: onion roots are shallow. Do not hoe too deep or you will damage the bulbs. Shallow cultivation of 23 cm is enough. In rows, use a wheel hoe or a push cultivator. In small plots, use a hand hoe. The best time to cultivate is when the soil is dry and weeds are tiny (less than 5 cm tall). If you cultivate when soil is wet, you may spread weed seeds or create mud that compacts the soil. In West Africa, farmers use the 'azada' hoe for shallow weeding. In East Africa, the 'jembe' hoe is common. Sharpen your hoe regularly for easy cutting.

Hand weeding: This is pulling weeds by hand or with a hand fork. It is best for weeds that grow close to onion plants, where a hoe cannot reach. Do it when weeds are young and the soil is moist (after rain or irrigation). Pull gently so you don't uproot the onions. For nutgrass, you must dig out the underground nuts. Wear gloves to protect your hands. In many African countries, women and children do hand weeding, but it is labor-intensive. A family can weed 1 acre in 23 days if the weeds are small. If you wait until weeds are big, it takes twice as long.

Timing is critical. The first weeding should be 23 weeks after transplanting. The second 45 weeks after. After that, onions start to bulb and their leaves cover the ground, reducing weed growth. But you may still need one more weeding at 78 weeks. Never let weeds go to seed. After weeding, remove the weeds from the field. Do not leave them in rows, as they can re-root. Dry them and use as mulch or burn them. Combine cultivation and hand weeding for best results. Mechanical control is hard work, but it is safe and improves your yield.

4. Herbicide Selection and Application

Herbicides are chemicals that kill weeds. They can save labor, but they are dangerous if used wrongly. You must choose the right herbicide for onions and apply it correctly. In Africa, many farmers use herbicides like pendimethalin (Stomp), oxyfluorfen (Goal), and fluazifop-p-butyl (Fusilade). Always read the label and follow instructions.

Pre-emergence herbicides: These are applied before weeds come up. Pendimethalin is common. Apply it to the soil after transplanting onions but before weeds appear. It stops weed seeds from germinating. Use 23 liters per hectare in 200300 liters of water. Spray evenly on the soil surface. Do not spray on onion leaves. If it rains within 2 hours, you may need to reapply. Oxyfluorfen is another pre-emergence herbicide, but it can damage onions if too much is used. Use 0.51 liter per hectare.

Post-emergence herbicides: These kill weeds that are already growing. Fluazifop-p-butyl (Fusilade) kills grassy weeds like goosegrass but not broadleaf weeds. Apply when grasses are 1015 cm tall. Use 12 liters per hectare. For broadleaf weeds, you can use oxyfluorfen as a directed spray, but avoid contact with onion leaves. Always use a shield or nozzle to direct spray to weeds.

Safety first: Wear gloves, boots, a mask, and long sleeves. Never eat or drink while spraying. Mix chemicals in a bucket, not in a drinking water container. Spray in the morning or evening when there is no wind. Do not spray when it is raining or about to rain. Keep children and animals away. Store herbicides in a locked place, away from food.

Calibration: You must know how much herbicide to use. Measure your field. For a 10 m x 10 m plot (100 m), you need 2030 ml of pendimethalin in 23 liters of water. Use a knapsack sprayer. Test the sprayer with water first to see how much it covers. Do not overuse herbicide; it wastes money and harms the soil. Rotate herbicides with different modes of action to prevent resistant weeds. If you are not sure, ask an extension officer. Herbicides are not a replacement for good farming; combine them with cultural and mechanical methods.

5. Integrated Weed Management Strategies

Integrated Weed Management (IWM) means using many methods together to control weeds. No single method works perfectly. By combining cultural, mechanical, biological, and chemical methods, you get better control and reduce costs. IWM is the smart way for African onion farmers.

Start with prevention. Use clean seed. Onion seed can contain weed seeds. Buy certified seed or clean your own. Clean your tools and boots before entering the field. Keep field edges and irrigation canals free of weeds. If you see a new weed, remove it immediately. Do not let weeds produce seeds.

Combine cultural and mechanical methods. Rotate onions with maize, cowpea, or sorghum. Mulch with dry grass or rice husks. Plant cover crops like mucuna. Use hand weeding and shallow cultivation at the right times. For example, in Kenya, farmers combine mulching with hand weeding and get 80% weed control. In Nigeria, rotating onions with maize reduces striga by half.

Add biological control where possible. Some insects eat weeds. For example, the flea beetle (Altica spp.) eats pigweed. You can also use living mulches like clover, which suppress weeds. But be careful not to introduce invasive species.

Use herbicides wisely as a backup. Apply pre-emergence herbicide after transplanting. Use post-emergence only when needed. Always rotate herbicides. Do not rely on one chemical.

Monitor your field every week. Keep a notebook. Record which weeds are present and which methods worked. Adjust your plan. For example, if nutgrass is a problem, focus on digging out the nuts and solarization. If striga is a problem, rotate with cowpea and apply nitrogen fertilizer.

IWM saves money. A study in Ethiopia showed that farmers using IWM got 30% more onions than those using only hand weeding. It also protects your health and the environment. Start small. Try one new method each season. Share what you learn with your neighbors. Together, you can beat weeds.

6. Preventing Weed Seed Production

The most important rule in weed management is: never let a weed produce seeds. One weed plant can produce thousands of seeds that will plague you for years. Preventing seed production is cheaper and easier than fighting new weeds later.

Know when weeds flower. Pigweed flowers 46 weeks after germination. Blackjack flowers in 68 weeks. Goosegrass flowers in 57 weeks. Nutgrass produces seeds and underground nuts. You must remove weeds before they flower. Walk your field every 34 days during the first 8 weeks. Pull out any weed that is about to flower. Do not just cut the top; pull the whole plant, including roots.

If you see a weed with flowers or seeds, do not leave it on the soil. Put it in a sack and remove it from the field. Burn it or feed it to livestock. Do not compost weeds with seeds. Compost piles may not get hot enough to kill seeds. If you use manure, make sure it is well-rotted. Fresh manure can contain weed seeds.

Clean your equipment. After working in a weedy area, clean your hoe, boots, and tractor tires before moving to a clean field. Seeds stick to mud. Also, clean your onion seed. Winnow or sieve to remove weed seeds. Some farmers soak onion seed in salt water; weed seeds float and can be skimmed off.

Control weeds along borders and paths. These are seed factories. Mow or slash them before they flower. In irrigation canals, remove weeds regularly. If you have livestock, keep them out of weedy areas, as they can spread seeds in their dung.

Use mulching to block light and stop seed germination. A thick mulch of 10 cm prevents most weed seeds from sprouting. Solarization also kills seeds near the surface. For nutgrass, you must dig out the nuts and destroy them. Do not just pull the leaves; the nuts will regrow.

Finally, keep records. Note where weeds are worst. Next season, plant a smother crop there or solarize the soil. Preventing seed production is a habit. Once you master it, your weed problem will shrink every year. Your onions will grow stronger and you will harvest more.

<h2>Practical Reference: Weed Management in African Onion Fields</h2>

<p>Weed management is one of the highest-return investments an onion grower can make. Onions are shallow-rooted, slow to establish, and poor competitors. Research across sub-Saharan Africa shows uncontrolled weeds can cut marketable bulb yields by <strong>5080%</strong>, and the losses are not just tonnage they are grade losses. Weedy onions bulb later, size unevenly, and store poorly, which directly reduces the price you receive at the farm gate or market. This module gives you a practical, business-first reference for planning weed control as a cost line, not an afterthought.</p>

<p>Treat weed control like any other input decision: estimate the cost, estimate the yield protection, and calculate the return. A well-timed hand weeding costing US$60120 per hectare can protect several tonnes of marketable bulbs worth US$6002,000 or more, depending on market prices. That is a return on investment most other farm inputs cannot match.</p>

<h2>Practical Decision Guide: What to Do, When</h2>

<p>The critical period of weed competition in onion is roughly <strong>28 weeks after emergence</strong>. This is the window where every weed costs you the most yield. Plan your labour and inputs around this window.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Before planting:</strong> Prepare a stale seedbed irrigate, allow weeds to germinate, then destroy them with shallow cultivation or a non-selective burn-down before transplanting.</li>
<li><strong>At transplanting:</strong> Apply a pre-emergence herbicide if registered and affordable, or plan your first hand weeding within 1014 days.</li>
<li><strong>24 weeks:</strong> First hand weeding or mechanical cultivation. Keep tools shallow (23 cm) to avoid onion root damage.</li>
<li><strong>46 weeks:</strong> Second weeding. This is the peak competition period do not delay.</li>
<li><strong>68 weeks:</strong> Third weeding if needed. After this, onion canopy begins to shade out late weeds.</li>
<li><strong>After 8 weeks:</strong> Spot-remove weeds that produce seed, especially nutsedge and pigweed, to protect future seasons.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Integrated Weed Management: The Business Case</h2>

<p>No single method wins. Integrated Weed Management (IWM) combines cultural, mechanical, and chemical tools to reduce cost and slow resistance. For African onion growers, the most profitable combinations are:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Crop rotation</strong> with cereals (maize, sorghum, millet) or legumes to break weed cycles, especially nutsedge and Striga.</li>
<li><strong>Mulching</strong> with dry grass, rice straw, or plastic where affordable suppresses weeds and conserves moisture.</li>
<li><strong>Mechanical cultivation</strong> shallow hoeing or inter-row cultivation at 23 cm depth, timed to the critical period.</li>
<li><strong>Hand weeding</strong> the backbone of smallholder control; train workers to remove weeds before they seed.</li>
<li><strong>Herbicides</strong> used strategically, not as a substitute for good agronomy.</li>
<li><strong>Preventing seed production</strong> one pigweed plant can produce over 100,000 seeds. Removing weeds before flowering is the cheapest long-term investment you can make.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>

<ul>
<li> Prepare a stale seedbed and destroy the first weed flush before transplanting.</li>
<li> Plan three weedings: 24 weeks, 46 weeks, and 68 weeks after emergence.</li>
<li> Keep all cultivation shallow (23 cm) to protect onion roots and bulbs.</li>
<li> Apply pre-emergence herbicide only if registered in your country and label rate is followed.</li>
<li> Wear full PPE when handling or spraying any herbicide.</li>
<li> Never let weeds go to seed remove flowering weeds immediately.</li>
