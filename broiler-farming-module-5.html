1. Common Broiler Diseases and Prevention

Broilers in Africa face several common diseases that can wipe out your flock if you are not careful. The most frequent are Newcastle disease, infectious bursal disease (Gumboro), coccidiosis, chronic respiratory disease (CRD), and fowl typhoid. Newcastle causes coughing, gasping, twisted necks, and sudden death. Gumboro affects young birds, causing watery droppings, depression, and a swollen vent. Coccidiosis is a parasite that damages the gut, leading to bloody droppings and poor growth. CRD causes sneezing, swollen eyes, and rattling sounds. Fowl typhoid leads to yellow diarrhoea and sudden deaths.

Prevention starts with buying chicks from a reputable hatchery that vaccinates against Marek's disease. Keep different age groups separate. Clean and disinfect the brooder house before chicks arrive using a product like Virocid or glutaraldehyde. Provide clean water and feed, and avoid overcrowdingallow 10 birds per square metre for heavy breeds. Control wild birds and rodents that carry disease. Use footbaths with disinfectant at the entrance of each pen.

Vaccination is key for Newcastle and Gumboro. In many African countries, you can buy vaccines from agrovet shops. Follow the hatchery's vaccination schedule. For coccidiosis, use anticoccidial drugs in feed or water, such as amprolium, or vaccines like Paracox. Rotate drugs to prevent resistance. If you see sick birds, isolate them immediately and consult a vet. Dead birds should be buried deep or burned to prevent spread.

Good nutrition also boosts immunity. Ensure feed has enough vitamins A, D, and E, and minerals like selenium. Stress from heat, cold, or poor ventilation weakens birds. Provide shade, fans, or evaporative cooling in hot climates. Keep litter dry to reduce ammonia and respiratory problems. Record all treatments and vaccinations. By following these steps, you can prevent most diseases and keep your flock healthy.

2. Vaccination Programs and Administration

Vaccination is your best defence against killer diseases like Newcastle and Gumboro. In Africa, you can buy vaccines from agrovet shops or government veterinary offices. Common vaccines include Newcastle (Hitchner B1, La Sota), Gumboro (intermediate or intermediate-plus), and fowl pox. Store vaccines in a fridge between 2-8C. Never freeze them. Carry them in a cool box with ice packs to the farm.

A typical broiler vaccination schedule: Day 1: Marek's (done at hatchery). Day 7: Newcastle + IB via eye drop or drinking water. Day 14: Gumboro via drinking water. Day 21: Newcastle (La Sota) via drinking water. Day 28: Gumboro booster if needed. Adjust based on local disease pressure. Always check expiry dates.

Administering vaccines correctly is critical. For eye drops, reconstitute the vaccine with the provided diluent. Hold the chick gently, put one drop in the eye, and wait for it to blink. For drinking water, withdraw water for 1-2 hours so birds are thirsty. Mix the vaccine with cool, clean water (no chlorine) and add skim milk powder (2g per litre) to protect the virus. Provide enough drinkers so all birds get a dose within 1-2 hours. Do not use metal containers.

For injections, use a sterile needle and syringe. For example, some vaccines are given under the wing (subcutaneous) or in the breast muscle. Change needles often to avoid lumps. After vaccination, give vitamins and electrolytes to reduce stress. Handle used vials and needles safelyburn or bury them. Record the vaccine name, batch number, and date. If you see reactions like swelling, it's usually mild and resolves. Never vaccinate sick birds; wait until they recover.

3. Biosecurity Protocols for Disease Control

Biosecurity means keeping germs away from your birds. It is the cheapest and most effective way to prevent disease. Start with a perimeter fence around your farm. Limit visitorsonly essential workers enter. Keep a logbook of all visitors and vehicles. Provide boots and overalls for workers, and a footbath with fresh disinfectant (e.g., 2% caustic soda or quaternary ammonium) at every entrance. Change disinfectant daily.

Clean and disinfect the poultry house between flocks. Remove all litter, scrape and scrub with detergent, then apply disinfectant. Use a pressure washer if possible. Let the house dry for at least 3 days before bringing new chicks. Use a flame gun or insecticide to kill mites and lice. Keep feed in sealed bins to prevent rodents and wild birds. Store feed off the floor on pallets.

Control traffic: all-in, all-out is bestone age group per site. If you must have multiple ages, work from youngest to oldest. Never borrow equipment from other farms. If you do, disinfect it first. Keep wild birds out with netting. Drain stagnant water to prevent mosquitoes. Quarantine new birds for 2 weeks before mixing. Isolate sick birds immediately.

Personal hygiene: workers should shower and change clothes before entering. Do not keep poultry at home or visit live bird markets. If you visit a market, stay away from your flock for 24 hours. Use dedicated boots and clothing. Dispose of dead birds properlyburn or bury deep with lime. Compost litter away from the house. Regular rodent control: set traps and bait stations. Keep grass short around houses to remove hiding places. Train workers on these rules and post them visibly. Biosecurity is a daily habit, not a one-time task.

4. Parasite and Pest Control

Parasites and pests steal feed, spread disease, and stress your birds. The main external parasites are mites, lice, fleas, and ticks. Internal parasites include coccidia (a protozoan) and worms (roundworms, tapeworms). Flies and beetles can also carry disease. Rodents eat feed and damage buildings.

For external parasites, inspect birds weeklypart the feathers around the vent and under wings. Look for tiny moving specks or scabs. Treat with approved insecticides like permethrin or cypermethrin sprays or dusts. Apply to litter, walls, and cracks. Repeat after 7-10 days to kill newly hatched eggs. For mites, you may need to treat birds individually with a drop of ivermectin or fipronil. Always follow withdrawal periods.

Coccidiosis is controlled with anticoccidials in feed or water. Common drugs: amprolium (e.g., 1g per 2 litres of water for 5 days), toltrazuril (e.g., 7mg per kg body weight for 2 days), or sulphonamides. Rotate drugs to prevent resistance. Vaccines (e.g., Paracox) are available but expensive. Keep litter drycoccidia thrive in wet conditions. Use raised feeders and drinkers to reduce spillage.

For worms, deworm birds at 4-6 weeks if they have access to soil or if you see worms in droppings. Use levamisole or albendazole in water or feed. Repeat after 2 weeks. Flies: use fly traps, baits, and insecticides. Keep manure dry and remove it regularly. For rodents, use traps and anticoagulant baits (e.g., brodifacoum) placed in tamper-resistant boxes away from birds. Never use rat poison in feed areas. Beetles: use diatomaceous earth or insecticide sprays. Always read labels and observe withdrawal periods. Integrate control: keep houses clean, repair cracks, and monitor weekly.

5. Recognizing Signs of Illness and Mortality Management

Early detection saves flocks. Learn normal behaviour: active, eating, drinking, and clean vent. Sick birds show signs like huddling, drooping wings, ruffled feathers, closed eyes, and reluctance to move. They may gasp, cough, sneeze, or have a swollen head. Check droppings: normal is greyish-brown with white cap. Abnormal: watery, green, bloody, or yellow. Also watch for sudden increase in deaths.

Do a daily walk-through. Look at birds, listen for abnormal sounds, and check feeders and drinkers. Pick up a few birds and feel their breastshould be plump. Feel the cropshould be full after feeding. Check legs for swelling or scales. Examine eyes and nostrils for discharge. Record any abnormalities. If mortality exceeds 0.5% per day, act immediately.

When you find a sick bird, isolate it in a separate pen. Provide warmth, easy access to water with electrolytes, and soft feed. Call a vet or extension officer. If many birds are sick, collect samples (blood, swabs, droppings) for diagnosis. Do not sell or eat sick birds. For mortality, remove dead birds promptly. Do not leave them in the pen. Wear gloves and wash hands after handling.

Dispose of dead birds by burning in an incinerator or burying deep (at least 1 metre) with lime. Never feed dead birds to other animals or throw them in rivers. Composting is an option: layer carcasses with litter and cover; turn regularly. Keep a mortality record: date, number, age, and signs. This helps identify patterns. If you suspect a notifiable disease like avian influenza, report to authorities immediately. Quarantine the farm and stop all movement. Proper disposal prevents spread and protects neighbours.

6. Antibiotic Stewardship and Residue Avoidance

Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria. They do not work on viruses like Newcastle or Gumboro. Using antibiotics unnecessarily leads to resistant bacteria, which are harder to treat. It also leaves residues in meat, which can harm consumers and cause export rejections. As a broiler farmer, you must use antibiotics responsibly.

Only use antibiotics when prescribed by a vet for a confirmed bacterial infection, such as CRD, fowl cholera, or colibacillosis. Never use them as a routine feed additive for growth promotionmany countries have banned this. Common antibiotics: oxytetracycline, amoxicillin, tylosin, and enrofloxacin. Note that enrofloxacin is banned in some countries for poultry. Always check local regulations.

When using antibiotics, follow the dosage and duration exactly. Do not underdoseit fails to kill bacteria and promotes resistance. Do not overdoseit wastes money and increases residues. Use the correct route: water, feed, or injection. For water medication, calculate the total water consumption for the day and mix the correct amount. Provide fresh medicated water daily. Never mix antibiotics with vaccines or other drugs without vet advice.

Observe withdrawal periods strictly. Withdrawal period is the time after the last dose before slaughter, so drugs leave the body. For example, oxytetracycline has a withdrawal of 5-7 days; amoxicillin 1-2 days; tylosin 5 days. Check the label. If you sell before withdrawal, meat will have residues. Buyers may test and reject your birds. Keep records of all antibiotic use: date, drug, dose, reason, and withdrawal end date.

Alternatives to antibiotics: improve biosecurity, use probiotics, prebiotics, organic acids, and vaccines. These reduce the need for antibiotics. If you must use antibiotics, work with a vet. Never share antibiotics between farms. Dispose of expired drugs safelydo not flush them. By being a good steward, you protect your birds, your customers, and your business.

<h2>Practical Reference: Health Management for Broiler Farming in Africa</h2>

<p>Broiler farming in Africa sits at the intersection of rising protein demand, thin margins, and real disease pressure. A well-run health programme is not a cost centre it is the single largest lever on feed conversion, mortality, and marketable output. This practical reference consolidates the core actions a commercial or semi-commercial broiler operation must master: disease prevention, vaccination, biosecurity, parasite control, early illness detection, and responsible antibiotic use.</p>

<p>Because African production environments vary widely from open-sided houses in Uganda to environmentally controlled sheds in South Africa the principles below are framed around what is controllable at farm level. Where regional data exists, it is cited so you can benchmark your own performance.</p>

<h3>1. Common Broiler Diseases and Prevention</h3>

<p>The diseases that most frequently hit African broiler flocks fall into three groups: viral (Newcastle disease, infectious bursal disease/Gumboro, infectious bronchitis), bacterial (colibacillosis, fowl cholera, necrotic enteritis), and protozoal (coccidiosis). Newcastle disease remains the single most reported poultry disease across sub-Saharan Africa, with outbreaks documented in Nigeria, Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia.</p>

<p>Prevention is built on three pillars: (1) all-in/all-out stocking with proper downtime between batches, (2) vaccination timed to maternal antibody decay, and (3) litter and water management that denies pathogens a foothold. In practice, the most common failure point is not the vaccine it is the water. Chlorine demand from organic matter, biofilm in drinker lines, and inconsistent dosing render many vaccination and medication efforts ineffective.</p>

<p>For coccidiosis, the choice between ionophore anticoccidials in feed and live coccidiosis vaccines depends on your production system. In high-challenge open houses, a coccidiosis vaccine followed by a clean litter programme often reduces reliance on in-feed drugs. In controlled sheds, rotation of anticoccidial classes remains common but must be managed to avoid resistance.</p>

<h3>2. Vaccination Programs and Administration</h3>

<p>A workable broiler vaccination programme in most African contexts includes:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Day 1 (hatchery):</strong> Marek's disease (if not already done at hatchery), infectious bronchitis (IB) via spray or eye drop.</li>
<li><strong>Day 710:</strong> Newcastle disease (ND) + IB often a combined vaccine via eye drop or drinking water.</li>
<li><strong>Day 1418:</strong> Infectious bursal disease (Gumboro) intermediate strain via drinking water.</li>
<li><strong>Day 2124:</strong> ND booster (depending on regional challenge and vaccine type).</li>
</ul>

<p>Administration matters as much as the vaccine. Drinking-water vaccination requires: clean water with no chlorine or sanitizer residual (add skim milk powder at 23 g/litre to neutralise), enough drinker space so every bird drinks within 12 hours, and withdrawal of water for 12 hours before vaccination to ensure thirst. Eye-drop vaccination is more reliable per bird but labour-intensive. Spray vaccination suits mass application but requires correct droplet size and house closure.</p>

<p>Cold chain is non-negotiable. In many African settings, the weakest link is transport from the agrovet to the farm. Use a vaccine carrier with ice packs, never freeze reconstituted vaccine, and discard any vial that has been out of the cold chain for more than a few hours.</p>

<h3>3. Biosecurity Protocols for Disease Control</h3>

<p>Biosecurity is the cheapest disease control you will ever buy. It has three layers: isolation, traffic control, and sanitation.</p>

<p><strong>Isolation:</strong> Keep birds away from wild birds, rodents, and other poultry. In free-range or open-sided systems, this is difficult but can be improved with netting, roof overhangs, and feed storage that excludes birds and rodents.</p>

<p><strong>Traffic control:</strong> Limit visitors. If they must enter, provide boots and coveralls. A simple footbath with a quaternary ammonium or phenolic disinfectant at every house entrance reduces pathogen load dramatically. Vehicles and crates must be washed and disinfected between farms.</p>

<p><strong>Sanitation:</strong> All-in/all-out with a minimum 1014 day downtime. Dry clean, then wet clean, then disinfect, then fumigate if possible. Litter should be removed and replaced. Water lines should be flushed and descaled. Feed bins should be emptied and cleaned.</p>

<p>In African conditions, where many farms are small and multi-age, the practical compromise is to concentrate biosecurity at the house level: dedicated boots and clothing per house, hand-washing stations, and a strict rule that no one enters a house after visiting another poultry farm that day.</p>

<h3>4. Parasite and Pest Control</h3>

<p>External parasites mites (especially red mite), lice, and fleas cause irritation, anaemia, and reduced feed intake. Internal parasites (roundworms, tapeworms) are more common in older birds and free-range systems but can appear in broilers on contaminated litter. Coccidiosis is the most economically significant internal parasite in broilers.</p>

<p>Control measures:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use approved acaricides/insecticides for premise treatment between batches. Rotate active ingredients to avoid resistance.</li>
<li>Keep litter dry. Damp litter is the single biggest driver of coccidiosis and fly populations.</li>
<li>Control rodents and beetles (lesser mealworm) which can carry pathogens and parasites.</li>
<li>Use coccidiostats in feed or coccidiosis vaccination, matched to your challenge level.</li>
<li>For free-range or semi-intensive systems, routine deworming may be needed consult a vet for approved products and withdrawal times.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Recognizing Signs of Illness and Mortality Management</h3>

<p>Early detection is a business skill. The first signs are usually behavioural and easy to miss if you only walk the house once a day. Train yourself and your staff to look for:</p>

<ul>
<li>Birds huddling or crowding near heat sources (chilling or fever).</li>
<li>Reduced feed and water intake the earliest reliable indicator.</li>
<li>Ruffled feathers, drooping wings, closed eyes.</li>
<li>Respiratory signs: snickering, coughing, gasping, swollen sinuses.</li>
<li>Diarrhoea, pasty vents, or blood in droppings (coccidiosis).</li>
<li>Lameness, reluctance to move, or twisted necks (Newcastle).</li>
</ul>

<p>Mortality management: remove dead birds immediately, record daily mortality, and investigate any spike above 0.1% per day after the first week. Post-mortem examination by a vet or trained technician is the fastest way to identify the cause. Dispose of carcasses by incineration, deep burial, or rendering never by feeding to other poultry or dumping near the house.</p>

<h3>6. Antibiotic Stewardship and Residue Avoidance</h3>

<p>Antibiotic resistance is a global food-safety issue, and African poultry value chains are increasingly under scrutiny from export markets and domestic regulators. The business case for stewardship is straightforward: residues cause rejections, and resistant infections cause higher mortality and higher treatment costs.</p>

<p>Rules for responsible use:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use antibiotics only on veterinary prescription, never as a routine growth promoter.</li>
<li>Match the antibiotic to the diagnosis do not treat blindly.</li>
<li>Follow the withdrawal period strictly. For most antibiotics used in broilers, this is 510 days, but check the specific product label.</li>
<li>Never use human-critical antibiotics (e.g., colistin, fluoroquinolones) unless no alternative exists and a vet has confirmed sensitivity.</li>
<li>Record every treatment: date, product, dose, reason, withdrawal date.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:12px 16px; margin:16px 0; border-radius:4px;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Antibiotic Residues</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Selling birds before the withdrawal period ends is illegal in most African countries and can cause serious harm to consumers, including allergic reactions and antibiotic resistance. If you cannot verify the withdrawal date, do not sell the birds. Residue violations can lead to farm closure, fines, and loss of buyer contracts. Always keep treatment records and verify withdrawal times with your vet or the product label.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Vaccinate on time, every time.</strong> Cold chain, correct dose, clean water, and no chlorine residual.</li>
<li><strong>Keep litter dry.</strong> Damp litter drives coccidiosis, flies, and respiratory disease.</li>
<li><strong>Footbath and boots at every house.</strong> No exceptions for staff or visitors.</li>
<li><strong>All-in/all-out with 1014 days downtime.</strong> Clean, disinfect, and rest the house.</li>
<li><strong>Check water and feed intake daily.</strong> A drop is your earliest warning.</li>
<li><strong>Record mortality daily.</strong> Investigate any spike above 0.1% per day after week one.</li>
<li><strong>Never use antibiotics without a vet and a diagnosis.</strong> Respect withdrawal periods.</li>
<li><strong>Control rodents and wild birds.</strong> They are the most common entry point for disease.</li>
</ol>
