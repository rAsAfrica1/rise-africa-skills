Lessons

1. L1

Before you touch a single bolt, you must plan. Engine removal is not a race; it is a careful process. First, park the vehicle on a flat, hard surface like a concrete slab. If you don't have concrete, use thick wooden planks under the wheels to prevent sinking. Engage the handbrake and chock the wheels. Disconnect the battery: remove the negative terminal first, then the positive. This prevents accidental sparks. Next, drain all fluids. Place a clean container under the oil drain plug (usually a 17mm or 19mm bolt) and remove it. Let the oil drain completelyit may take 15 minutes. Do the same for the coolant: open the radiator drain cock or remove the lower hose. Be careful, coolant is toxic. Also drain the power steering fluid if equipped. Label and bag every bolt you remove. Use zip-lock bags and a permanent marker. Write where they came from, e.g., 'exhaust manifold bolts'. This saves hours later. Take photos with your phone before disconnecting anything. In rural Africa, you may not have a phone, so sketch the layout on paper. Note the firing order of spark plug wires. Remove the hood (bonnet) if it helps accesstwo people can lift it. Remove the radiator and fan shroud. Keep the radiator upright to avoid bending fins. Disconnect the throttle cable, choke cable, and any electrical connectors. Use masking tape to label wires. If the engine is seized or you plan to convert it, you still need to remove it carefully. Work methodically. A clean removal makes the conversion easier. Remember, you are not just pulling an engine; you are preparing it for a new life as an irrigation pump.

2. L2

You will need a hoist or a strong lifting frame. In many African workshops, a simple A-frame made from 4-inch steel pipes or hardwood logs works well. The frame must support at least 500 kg. Use a chain hoist or a 2-ton come-along. Never use rope that can stretch or snap. Attach the hoist to the engine lifting eyes or use a lifting chain bolted to the cylinder head boltsremove two head bolts and screw in lifting eyes. If you don't have lifting eyes, use a thick nylon sling rated for 1 ton. Position the hoist directly above the engine. Take up the slack slowly. Remove the engine mount boltsusually two on each side, 14mm or 17mm. Also unbolt the transmission bell housing from the engine block. Support the transmission with a jack or wooden blocks so it doesn't hang. Remove the starter motor (two or three bolts). Disconnect the exhaust pipe from the manifold. Now, lift the engine slowly. Watch for anything still attached: wires, hoses, linkages. Have a helper guide the engine as it comes out. Once clear, lower it onto a sturdy pallet or old tires. Never let it drop. If you are working alone, use a chain fall and go slowly. After removal, mount the engine on a stand or a heavy wooden bench. This is your preparation station. Clean the engine with a wire brush and diesel. Remove the clutch and flywheel if you don't need themthey are not needed for a pump. Keep the flywheel if you plan to use a belt drive; it provides momentum. But for direct coupling, remove it. Also remove the alternator, starter, and air conditioning compressor. You won't need them. Keep the water pump if it is in good condition; otherwise, you will replace it. The goal is a bare engine block with cylinder head, ready for the next steps.

3. L3

Once the engine is out, strip it down to the long block. Start by removing the intake and exhaust manifolds. These are held by 12mm or 14mm bolts. Soak them with penetrating oil like WD-40 if they are rusty. Wait 10 minutes, then turn slowly. If a bolt snaps, you will need to drill it outavoid that. Next, remove the valve cover (usually 10mm bolts). Inspect the rocker arms and pushrods. If they are worn, replace them. Remove the cylinder head bolts in the correct sequence: start from the outside and work inward in a spiral pattern. Use a breaker bar. Once the head is off, check the head gasket for cracks. Clean the block deck with a scraper and sandpaper. Now remove the oil pan (10mm or 12mm bolts). Clean out sludge with diesel and a brush. Remove the oil pump and pickup tube. For a pump application, you might keep the oil pump to circulate oil, but often you will block it off. Remove the timing cover and timing chain or belt. If the engine is a pushrod type, remove the camshaft. If overhead cam, remove the cam caps evenly. Keep all valvetrain parts in orderuse a cardboard template to store pushrods and rockers. Remove the pistons and connecting rods if you plan to rebuild. For a pump, you may keep the pistons to maintain compression, but if the engine is worn, a rebuild is wise. Hone the cylinders if needed. Replace all gaskets and seals. This is the time to install new bearings, piston rings, and a new timing chain. A clean, well-sealed engine will run cooler and last longer. Work in a shaded area to avoid overheating. Use a torque wrench for all critical bolts. If you don't have one, borrow or buy a cheap oneit is essential.

4. L4

A car engine is designed to run at variable speeds, but an irrigation pump needs a steady speed. You must remove or modify the governor. Most car engines have a mechanical governor inside the distributor or carburetor. For a pump, you will likely convert to a fixed throttle setting. Remove the carburetor and intake manifold. You will replace the carburetor with a simple throttle body or a fixed venturi. If you keep the carburetor, wire the throttle open to a set positionabout 1500 to 2000 RPM for a small pump. But better: remove the distributor and install a electronic ignition system or a magneto. You need a reliable spark. For diesel engines, you will adjust the injection pump to a fixed rack setting. This is more complex; consider a diesel if you have one because they are more fuel-efficient and have more torque. For petrol engines, remove the mechanical fuel pump and install an electric fuel pump (low pressure, 3-5 psi). You will also need to remove the radiator fan and replace it with an electric fan or a direct-coupled fan. But for a pump, you may not need a fan if the engine runs at low speed and has a large water tank. Remove the air filter housing; you will install a simple mesh filter. Now, the most important part: the output shaft. You need to connect the engine's crankshaft to the pump. Remove the flywheel and clutch. The crankshaft flange will have 6 or 8 bolt holes. You will bolt a pulley or a coupling to this flange. For a belt drive, use a double-groove pulley. For direct coupling, use a flexible coupling like a Lovejoy coupling. Align it perfectlymisalignment causes vibration and bearing failure. Use a dial indicator if available, or a straight edge. Also, remove the power steering pump and air conditioning compressorthey rob power. Block off any vacuum lines. The engine should now be a bare, self-contained power unit. Test it on a stand with fuel and ignition before mounting the pump.

5. L5

Preparation is everything. After stripping the engine, you must clean and inspect every part. Use a parts washer or a bucket of diesel and a stiff brush. Scrape off old gaskets with a putty knife. Do not gouge the aluminum surfaces. For the cylinder head, check for warping with a straight edge and feeler gauge. If it is warped more than 0.05mm, have it resurfaced at a machine shop. Check the cylinder walls for scoring. If there are deep scratches, you need to rebore and install oversize pistons. That is expensive. For a pump, a slightly worn engine can still work, but it will burn oil. If you can afford it, do a rebuild. Replace all bearings: main bearings, rod bearings, and cam bearings. Use plastigauge to check clearance. Install new piston rings. Hone the cylinders with a ball hone to create a cross-hatch pattern. This helps rings seal. Clean the block thoroughly with soap and water, then dry with compressed air. Oil the cylinder walls immediately to prevent rust. Now, assemble the engine with new gaskets. Use a torque wrench in the correct sequence. For the cylinder head, torque in three steps: 30 Nm, 60 Nm, then 90 Nm, following the manual. If you don't have a manual, search online for your engine model. For a typical 4-cylinder, head bolts are torqued to about 80-100 Nm. Install a new timing belt or chain. Set the timing marks perfectly. Install the oil pump and new oil filter. Fill with 15W-40 diesel engine oil (for diesel) or 20W-50 for petrol. Prime the oil system by cranking the engine with the coil disconnected until oil pressure builds. Then start it. Let it idle for 20 minutes, then change oil and filter again. This removes assembly debris. Now the engine is ready for the pump coupling. Remember, a clean engine is a reliable engine.

6. L6

You have removed and prepared the engine. Now you must connect it to the pump. But before that, build a sturdy base. Use a steel frame made from 50mm angle iron. Weld or bolt it together. The base must be level and strong enough to handle vibration. Mount the engine on rubber isolators to reduce vibration. Now, align the engine output shaft with the pump input shaft. If using a belt drive, mount the pump on a sliding rail so you can tension the belts. Use a double V-belt for reliability. Align the pulleys with a straight edgethey must be parallel and in line. For direct coupling, use a flexible coupling. The two shafts must be aligned within 0.05mm. Use a dial indicator to check. If you don't have one, use a feeler gauge between the coupling halves. Rotate the engine by hand and check for binding. Now, connect the plumbing. The pump inlet should have a strainer and a foot valve. Use a 2-inch suction hose for a 2-inch pump. The discharge line should have a check valve and a gate valve. For irrigation, you might use lay-flat hose or PVC pipes. Ensure all connections are tightair leaks on the suction side will stop the pump from priming. Prime the pump by filling the casing with water. Start the engine and let it run at 1500 RPM. Adjust the throttle until the pump delivers the desired flow. For a typical 5 HP engine, you can expect about 500 liters per minute at 10 meters head. Monitor temperature and oil pressure. After 30 minutes, check for leaks and tighten bolts. Now you have a working irrigation pump. Maintain it: change oil every 100 hours, clean the air filter, and check belts. This engine will serve you for years if treated well.

<h2>Practical Reference: Engine Removal and Preparation</h2>

<p>This practical reference supports the module <strong>Engine Removal and Preparation</strong> (Lessons L1L6) in the course <em>Convert a Car Engine to an Irrigation Water Pump</em>. It is written for African agribusiness operators who want to move from a donated or salvaged petrol or diesel car engine to a working, revenue-generating irrigation pump. The focus is business-first: every step here is about reducing downtime, protecting your capital, and getting water to crops or livestock faster so the unit pays for itself.</p>

<p>In most African markets from Kenya's Kiambu vegetable belt to Nigeria's Kano River irrigation scheme, from Ghana's Ashanti tomato farms to Zambia's Chisamba block farms a converted car engine pump can cost 4060% less than an imported purpose-built pump of similar flow. But that saving only materialises if the engine is removed, cleaned, and prepared correctly. A rushed removal leads to cracked mounts, damaged wiring looms, bent crankshaft pulleys, and oil leaks that turn a profitable asset into a workshop liability.</p>

<h3>Why preparation determines profitability</h3>

<p>An engine that has been sitting in a scrap vehicle is a bundle of unknowns: sludge in the sump, corroded water jackets, brittle rubber seals, and a timing system that may be one cold start away from failure. For irrigation duty, the engine will run for long hours at relatively steady load often 610 hours per day during dry season. That duty cycle is kinder than stop-start driving in one sense, but it exposes cooling and lubrication weaknesses far faster. Preparation is therefore not cosmetic; it is the difference between a pump that runs a full season and one that fails in week three.</p>

<h3>Step-by-step removal and preparation</h3>

<p><strong>L1: Vehicle assessment and documentation.</strong> Before touching a spanner, record the engine model, displacement, fuel type, and any visible leaks. Photograph the engine bay from four angles. Note whether the vehicle has a mechanical or electronic fuel system this determines whether the conversion will need a simple carburettor setup or an ECU and wiring harness. In African conditions, mechanical diesel engines (e.g., Toyota 1HZ, Isuzu 4JB1) are often preferred for irrigation because they tolerate fuel quality variation and have no ignition system to fail.</p>

<p><strong>L2: Safe isolation and fluid drainage.</strong> Disconnect the battery negative terminal first. Drain engine oil, coolant, and fuel into labelled containers for recycling or safe disposal. In many African towns, used oil can be sold to informal recyclers, which offsets some costs. Remove the radiator, fan, and belts. Keep all bolts in labelled bags a missing 10 mm bolt can cost hours of improvisation later.</p>

<p><strong>L3: Disconnection of ancillaries.</strong> Label every wire, hose, and linkage before removal. Use masking tape and a permanent marker. Photograph each connection. Remove the alternator, starter motor, power-steering pump (if present), air-conditioning compressor, and exhaust manifold. For irrigation use, the alternator may be retained to charge a small battery for starting, while the air-conditioning compressor is usually discarded.</p>

<p><strong>L4: Engine extraction.</strong> Attach a certified engine crane or a rated chain block to the lifting eyes. Never use the cylinder head bolts or manifolds as lifting points. Lift slowly, checking for forgotten hoses or wiring. Two people minimum; in many African workshops, a third person acts as a spotter. Lower the engine onto a sturdy pallet or engine stand.</p>

<p><strong>L5: Cleaning and inspection.</strong> Pressure-wash the engine after sealing all openings with tape or rags. Remove the sump and inspect for sludge and metal particles. Remove the cylinder head and check for warping, valve seat recession, and gasket failure. Check the crankshaft pulley for runout, the timing belt or chain for wear, and the water pump for play. Replace all rubber seals, hoses, and the thermostat as a matter of course they are cheap relative to a mid-season failure.</p>

<p><strong>L6: Preparation for pump coupling.</strong> Decide the drive method: direct coupling to a centrifugal pump via a flexible coupling, or belt drive to a pump mounted alongside. For most smallholder and medium-scale irrigation, belt drive with a 1:1 or 1.5:1 ratio is simpler and allows the pump to be positioned away from engine heat. Fabricate a steel base frame from 50505 mm angle iron, with the engine and pump bolted on adjustable slotted mounts for belt tensioning. Fit a radiator or a heat exchanger, and ensure the cooling fan is retained or replaced with an electric fan.</p>

<h4 style="color:#b00020; margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING Engine Removal and Preparation</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;">Never work under a suspended engine supported only by a hydraulic jack. Use rated stands, a chain block, or an engine crane with a locking pin. Fuel lines may still contain pressurised fuel relieve pressure before disconnecting. Used engine oil and coolant are hazardous: wear nitrile gloves and eye protection, and dispose of fluids through a licensed recycler, not into drains or farmland. In hot African conditions, coolant can be above 90C even after the engine has been off for some time allow full cooling before opening the radiator cap. Petrol engines produce flammable vapours; no smoking or open flames within 10 metres of the work area.</p>

<h3>Business and Africa context</h3>

<p>For an African agribusiness, the conversion decision should be driven by cash flow, not by technical curiosity. A pump that irrigates one hectare of high-value vegetables tomatoes, onions, kale can generate several thousand dollars of revenue per season. The capital cost of a converted engine pump is often recovered within one or two seasons, especially where fuel is available and spare parts for common Japanese and Indian engine families are stocked locally. Key business considerations include:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Fuel logistics:</strong> Diesel engines are more fuel-efficient per kW but require a reliable diesel supply. In remote areas, petrol may be easier to obtain in small quantities.</li>
<li><strong>Spare parts:</strong> Choose an engine family with a strong local parts presence Toyota, Isuzu, Mitsubishi, Perkins, and Lister are common across much of Africa.</li>
<li><strong>Service skills:</strong> Train at least one operator in daily checks and one mechanic in overhaul procedures. The <em>Rise AFRICA Skills</em> pathway is designed around this.</li>
<li><strong>Water source:</strong> Match pump head and flow to the actual borehole, river, or canal. Over-specifying wastes fuel; under-specifying burns clutches and belts.</li>
<li><strong>Regulation:</strong> Check local water abstraction rules. In some countries, permits are required for irrigation pumping above a threshold.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li>Record engine model, fuel type, and displacement before removal; photograph all connections.</li>
<li>Disconnect battery negative first; drain oil, coolant, and fuel into labelled containers.</li>
<li>Label every wire, hose, and linkage with tape and marker; bag and label all bolts.</li>
<li>Use rated lifting equipment on the engine lifting eyes only never on manifolds or head bolts.</li>
<li>Clean the engine after sealing openings; inspect sump, head, timing system, and water pump.</li>
<li>Replace all rubber seals, hoses, thermostat, and gaskets as standard do not reuse old ones.</li>
<li>Fabricate a rigid base frame with slotted mounts for belt tensioning and pump alignment.</li>
<li>Test-run the engine on the frame before coupling the pump; check for leaks, vibration, and overheating.</li>
</ol>
