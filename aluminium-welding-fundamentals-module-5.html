1. How MIG Works on Aluminium

MIG welding aluminium uses a continuous wire electrode fed through a gun, with argon shielding gas. The wire melts into the joint, forming a weld. Unlike steel, aluminium conducts heat away fast and melts at about 660Â°C, so you need higher heat input and faster travel speeds. The wire is softer and can tangle easily, so a spool gun or push-pull system helps. In Africa, many workshops use portable MIG sets like the Telwin or Deca, often powered by a generator. Set your machine to DCEP (electrode positive) for best penetration. Use a contact tip slightly larger than the wire, e.g., 1.2mm tip for 1.0mm wire, to avoid binding. Keep your stick-out about 10-15mm. Push the gun away from the weld pool (forehand) to get good cleaning action from the argon. Argon gas flow should be 12-18 litres per minute. If you hear a popping sound, your wire feed or gas flow is off. Clean the aluminium with a stainless steel brush before welding to remove oxide. Practice on 3mm thick 6061 plates. Set voltage around 20-22V and wire speed to match. Listen for a steady sizzling sound like frying bacon. If the wire stubs into the plate, increase voltage or reduce wire speed. If the wire balls up, reduce voltage or increase wire speed. Keep a consistent travel angle of 10-15 degrees. For a 3mm fillet weld, use about 120-150 amps. Remember, aluminium has no colour change when hot, so use a good helmet with a clear lens. Always wear leather gloves and a respirator; aluminium oxide fumes are harmful. In rural areas, ensure your generator can supply at least 5kVA for a 200A MIG set. Practice on scrap until you get a smooth, rippled bead without burn-through.

2. Spool Guns and Push-Pull Systems

Aluminium wire is soft and can buckle when pushed through a standard MIG torch. That's why spool guns and push-pull systems exist. A spool gun has a small spool of wire mounted directly on the gun, so the wire only travels a short distance. This eliminates feeding problems. Spool guns are common in African workshops because they are cheaper than push-pull systems. A typical spool gun like the Miller Spoolmate 100 or a generic 200A model costs around $200-$400. They work well for short welds and repairs. However, the spool is small (usually 0.5kg or 1kg), so you refill often. For longer production runs, a push-pull system is better. It has a torch with a small motor in the handle that pulls the wire, while the main feeder pushes. This allows using a standard 15kg spool on the machine. Push-pull torches like the Binzel or Tregaskiss are more expensive ($800+) but save time. When using a spool gun, set wire feed speed on the gun, not the machine. Keep the spool tension light so the wire doesn't birdnest. Use a contact tip one size larger than the wire, e.g., 1.2mm tip for 1.0mm wire. Clean the drive rolls regularly; aluminium wire leaves residue. In dusty environments, cover the spool when not in use. For both systems, use a liner made for aluminium, like a Teflon or nylon liner. Never use a steel liner; it will shave the wire. If you use a push-pull, adjust the tension so the wire doesn't slip but isn't crushed. Test by feeding wire onto a piece of wood; it should come out straight, not coiled. In Africa, where power can be unreliable, spool guns are often preferred because they draw less from the main feeder. But if you weld thick aluminium (over 6mm) regularly, invest in a push-pull. Always keep spare contact tips, nozzles, and drive rolls. A typical spool gun kit includes a spare tip and nozzle. Practice feeding wire without welding to get the feel. Remember, the gun should be held at a slight angle, and you should not drag the spool gun; push it forward.

3. Wire Types and Diameters

Choosing the right aluminium wire is critical. The most common wires are 4043 and 5356. 4043 (5% silicon) is softer, feeds better, and gives a shiny weld. It's great for general fabrication and castings. 5356 (5% magnesium) is stronger, harder, and feeds worse but gives better corrosion resistance and anodising colour match. For African workshops, 4043 is often the go-to because it's easier to find and cheaper. Wire diameters: 0.8mm, 1.0mm, and 1.2mm. For thin aluminium (1-3mm), use 0.8mm or 1.0mm. For thicker sections (3-6mm), use 1.2mm. The wire must match the base metal. For 6061, use 4043 or 5356. For 5052, use 5356. Never use steel wire in an aluminium MIG. Store wire in a dry place; moisture causes porosity. Buy wire from a reputable supplier; cheap wire often has inconsistent diameter, causing feed issues. In Africa, you can find 4043 wire at welding supply shops in major cities, but in rural areas, you may need to order online. A 1kg spool of 1.0mm 4043 costs about $15-$20. A 0.5kg spool for a spool gun costs $10-$15. Check the wire for bends or kinks before loading. If the wire is rusty or dirty, discard it. Use a wire cutter to snip the end at a 45-degree angle before feeding. Set drive roll tension so the wire deforms slightly but doesn't flatten. For 1.2mm wire, use a 1.4mm contact tip. For 1.0mm wire, use a 1.2mm tip. Keep a log of your settings for each wire type and thickness. Practice on scrap of the same alloy. If you get porosity, try a different wire or clean the base metal better. Remember, aluminium wire work-hardens if bent repeatedly, so avoid sharp bends in the liner. When switching from steel to aluminium, change the liner, drive rolls, and contact tip. Never mix.

4. Shielding Gas Selection

For MIG welding aluminium, you need 100% argon. Argon provides good cleaning action and stable arc. Do not use CO2 or argon-CO2 mixes; they cause black soot and poor welds. In Africa, argon is available in cylinders from suppliers like Afrox, BOC, or local gas companies. A typical cylinder is 10-20 litres, filled to 200 bar. Rent or buy a cylinder; refills cost about $50-$80. Flow rate: 12-18 litres per minute (LPM). Too low, and you get porosity; too high, and you get turbulence that draws in air. Use a flowmeter, not just a regulator. For thick aluminium (over 6mm), you can use a helium-argon mix (25-75% helium) to get more heat and better penetration. Helium is expensive and less common in Africa, but some suppliers stock it. If you use helium mix, increase flow rate to 20-25 LPM. Always purge the line before welding: open the valve, set flow, and let gas run for 5 seconds. Check for leaks with soapy water. In windy conditions, use a windbreak; even a slight breeze can blow away shielding gas. For outdoor work, increase flow to 20 LPM and use a larger nozzle. Keep the nozzle clean; spatter can block gas flow. Replace the nozzle if it's damaged. Use a gas lens kit for better coverage. In rural areas, you may need to transport cylinders carefully; secure them upright. Never use a cylinder without a regulator. When finished, close the cylinder valve and bleed the line. Store cylinders in a cool, ventilated area. If you run out of argon, don't substitute with CO2; stop and get argon. Practice on scrap to see the effect of flow rate: start at 12 LPM, then 18 LPM, and note the weld appearance. You want a clean, shiny bead with no porosity. If you see black spots, increase flow or check for leaks. Remember, argon is heavier than air, so it settles; in a pit, ensure ventilation.

5. Machine Settings

Setting your MIG machine for aluminium is about balancing voltage and wire feed speed. Start with the manufacturer's chart, but fine-tune by ear and eye. For 3mm aluminium, set voltage at 20-22V and wire speed at 6-8 m/min (or 300-400 inches per minute). For 6mm, use 23-25V and 8-10 m/min. For 1.5mm, use 17-19V and 4-6 m/min. These are starting points; adjust based on your machine. Use the 'sizzle' test: a steady frying sound means good. If it pops, increase voltage or decrease wire speed. If it stubs, increase voltage or decrease wire speed. If it burns through, decrease voltage or increase travel speed. Set the inductance (if available) to a medium-high setting for smoother arc. For spool guns, set wire speed on the gun. For push-pull, set on the main feeder. Always check polarity: DCEP (electrode positive) for aluminium. Some machines have a switch; set it to MIG. Use a contact tip one size larger than wire. Stick-out: 10-15mm. Travel angle: push 10-15 degrees. Travel speed: about 30-40 cm/min for 3mm. For a fillet weld, aim the wire at the root. For butt welds, leave a small gap (0-1mm) for penetration. Practice on scrap of the same thickness. Keep a notebook with settings for each job. In Africa, voltage fluctuations can affect settings; use a stabiliser or generator with AVR. If your machine has a digital display, note the numbers. If not, mark the dials. Always test on scrap before welding the actual part. Clean the base metal with a stainless brush. Preheating thick aluminium (over 10mm) to 100-150Â°C helps. Use a temperature crayon or infrared thermometer. After welding, clean the weld with a stainless brush and water to remove smut. Remember, aluminium welds cool fast; don't quench. Let it air cool. If you get porosity, check gas flow, wire cleanliness, and base metal. If you get cracks, try 5356 wire or preheat. Practice makes perfect.

6. Running Beads and Common Faults

Running a bead on aluminium requires steady hands and correct settings. Start with a push angle (forehand) of 10-15 degrees. Keep a consistent travel speed. For a straight bead on 3mm plate, use 20V, 6 m/min wire speed, and 15 LPM argon. The bead should be smooth with fine ripples. If you see a ropey bead, your travel speed is too fast or voltage too low. If the bead is too wide and flat, voltage is too high or travel too slow. Common faults: 1) Porosity (holes in weld) â caused by low gas flow, dirty wire, or moisture. Fix: increase gas to 15-18 LPM, clean wire, use fresh spool. 2) Burn-through â too much heat. Fix: reduce voltage, increase travel speed, use thicker material or backing. 3) Spatter â too much voltage or wrong gas. Fix: use 100% argon, reduce voltage. 4) Wire stubbing â wire hits plate and stops. Fix: increase voltage or decrease wire speed. 5) Birdnesting â wire tangles at drive rolls. Fix: reduce drive roll tension, use Teflon liner, don't kink wire. 6) Lack of fusion â travel too fast or voltage too low. Fix: slow down, increase voltage. 7) Cracking â wrong wire or too much restraint. Fix: use 5356 wire, preheat, or redesign joint. 8) Black smut â normal on aluminium; clean with stainless brush and water. For practice, run beads on scrap, then cut and etch to see penetration. In Africa, use a hacksaw and file to cut, then dip in caustic soda (careful!) to etch. Always wear PPE. If you get a fault, adjust one variable at a time. Keep a log. Watch videos of skilled welders to see technique. Practice daily for at least 30 minutes. Remember, aluminium welding is less forgiving than steel, but with patience, you can master it. Start with thin material and work up. Use a grinder to clean up bad welds and try again. Don't be afraid to ask for help from experienced welders in your area.

Practical Reference: MIG Welding Aluminium

This reference lesson is written for the working welder, fabricator, or workshop owner who needs to set up a MIG (metal inert gas) machine on aluminium and get paid work out the door. Across Africa â from aluminium window and door fabricators in Nairobi and Lagos to truck body builders in Durban and irrigation tank makers in the Sahel â MIG welding aluminium is increasingly the fastest route to strong, clean joints on 3 mm to 12 mm material. But aluminium does not behave like mild steel. It conducts heat roughly five to six times faster, its oxide layer melts at around 2,072 Â°C (well above the 660 Â°C melting point of the base metal), and its wire is soft and easily tangled. This lesson gives you the numbers, settings, and checks that keep a small fabrication business profitable: fewer reworks, less wire waste, and consistent bead quality.

1. Why Aluminium Needs Its Own MIG Setup

Aluminium's high thermal conductivity means heat is pulled away from the joint almost as fast as you add it. Welders must therefore use higher current and travel speeds than on steel of the same thickness, and often a "push" travel angle (10â15Â°) with a slightly longer arc. The tenacious AlâOâ oxide layer must be broken up by electrode-positive (DCEP) operation, which gives the cleaning action that removes oxide ahead of the puddle. This is why aluminium MIG is almost always run on DCEP, unlike many steel applications that can use DCEN for penetration. For a workshop, the practical consequence is: your machine must be capable of stable spray or pulsed-spray transfer, and your wire feed must be short and low-friction â hence the spool gun.

2. Spool Guns vs Push-Pull Systems

Aluminium wire (especially 1.2 mm and below) is soft and has low columnar strength. Pushing it down a 3â4 m torch liner causes birdnesting, erratic feed, and porosity from stop-start travel. Two solutions dominate in African workshops:

  • Spool gun: a small 100â200 mm spool (0.45 kg) mounted on the gun itself. Cheap, simple, ideal for short beads and site work. Wire length is short, so feeding is reliable. Downside: small spool means frequent changes, and the gun is heavier.
  • Push-pull system: a feed motor in the torch handle pulls wire while the main feeder pushes. This allows longer torches (up to 6â8 m) and larger spools (up to 7 kg), which suits production benches and robotic cells. It costs more and needs a matched torch and feeder.

For most small and medium African fabricators doing windows, gates, truck bodies, and tanks, a spool gun on a 250â350 A machine is the best value. Move to push-pull only when you have continuous production runs or long reach requirements.

3. Wire Types and Diameters

Choosing the right filler is the single biggest determinant of crack-free, serviceable welds. The most common aluminium MIG wires are 4043 (Al-5% Si) and 5356 (Al-5% Mg). 4043 is more forgiving, feeds well, and gives good bead appearance; 5356 gives higher strength and better anodising colour match, but is stiffer and more prone to cracking if used on 6xxx-series heat-treatable alloys without care. 5183 is a higher-magnesium alternative for 5xxx and 7xxx alloys where 5356 is marginal.

Argon-helium mixes (e.g. 75% Ar / 25% He, or 50/50) increase heat input and penetration on thick sections (10 mm+), but helium is expensive and less available in many African markets. Aluminium welding produces high levels of ozone and metal fume, and the intense UV from a DCEP arc is stronger than on steel. Always use a powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR) or at minimum a fume extractor at the arc, plus a welding helmet with a shade of at least 10â11 (higher for high-current spray). Aluminium dust and fines are combustible â keep the workshop clean and store wire away from ignition sources. Argon and helium are asphyxiants: never weld in a confined tank or truck body without forced ventilation and a gas monitor. In many African markets, local exhaust ventilation is the cheapest way to meet workplace safety obligations and keep skilled welders healthy and productive.

6. Running Beads and Common Faults

On aluminium, use a push angle of 10â15Â° and a slight side-to-side weave on vertical-up. Keep a short arc and move fast enough to stay ahead of the heat. Common faults and fixes:

  • Porosity (black specks / pinholes): usually gas flow too low, wrong gas, dirty base metal, or too long stick-out. Clean with a stainless brush dedicated to aluminium, increase flow to 16â20 L/min, check for leaks.
  • Birdnesting / wire tangle: liner too long or dirty, drive rolls too tight, spool brake too tight. Switch to a spool gun or push-pull, use a Teflon liner, and set drive roll tension just enough to feed without deforming the wire.
  • Burn-through on thin sheet: too much current, too slow travel, or poor fit-up. Drop one wire diameter, use pulsed MIG, and increase travel speed.
  • Lack of fusion / cold lap: too little current or too fast travel. Increase WFS and voltage, slow down, and ensure the arc is on the leading edge of the puddle.
  • Cracking: wrong filler (e.g. 5356 on certain 6xxx restraint joints), or too much heat input. Switch to 4043 where strength allows, or preheat thick sections to 80â120 Â°C.
