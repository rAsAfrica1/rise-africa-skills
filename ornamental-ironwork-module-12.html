Lessons

1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Many skilled welders in Africa produce beautiful gates, window grills, and railings, but struggle to make a profit because they don't price correctly or find enough customers. This module will teach you how to calculate a fair price that covers your costs and gives you a living, and how to market your work so people know about you. Without these skills, you might work hard all day and still have no money at the end of the month.

Pricing is not just guessing. You must know the cost of your materials, your time, your transport, your electricity or gas, and your overheads like rent and tools. Then you add a profit margin. For example, if a gate uses 20 kg of steel at 1,500 Naira per kg, that's 30,000 Naira for material. If it takes you 8 hours at 1,000 Naira per hour, that's 8,000 Naira. Add 2,000 Naira for welding rods, gas, and electricity. Add 10% for wear and tear on your machine. Your total cost is 44,000 Naira. Add 30% profit (13,200 Naira) and your price is 57,200 Naira. If you had charged 40,000 Naira, you would have lost money.

Marketing is how you get customers. In your community, word of mouth is powerful. Tell everyone you meet what you do. Keep a simple photo album on your phone of your finished work. Show it to potential clients. Put a sign on your workshop with your phone number. Use WhatsApp to share photos with friends and family. Ask happy customers to refer you. In many African cities, Facebook Marketplace and local buy-and-sell groups are free and effective. You can also partner with building contractors, architects, and hardware stores; they often need ironwork and can refer clients to you.

Remember, your reputation is your best marketing. Do quality work, finish on time, and treat customers fairly. A single satisfied customer can bring you five more. This module will give you the tools to price confidently and market smartly, so your skill turns into a sustainable business.

2. Tools and Materials

To price and market your ornamental ironwork, you need a few simple tools and materials. You don't need expensive software. A notebook and pen are essential for recording costs and quotes. A basic calculator helps you add up numbers quickly. A tape measure is needed to measure gates, windows, and railings accurately. A smartphone is very useful: you can take photos of your work, use a calculator app, and access the internet for marketing. If you don't have a smartphone, a simple camera or even printed photos work.

For pricing, you need to know the current prices of your raw materials. Visit your local steel supplier regularly and write down prices for square tubes, round bars, flat bars, angle iron, and welding rods. Prices change, so update your list every month. Also note the cost of paint, primer, and any decorative elements like scrolls or castings. Keep receipts for everything you buy. That way, you can calculate your exact material cost per job.

For marketing, you need a way to show your work. A photo album on your phone is best. Take clear pictures in good light, from different angles. If you can, print a few photos to stick on your workshop wall or to carry with you. Business cards are helpful: you can print simple ones with your name, phone number, and what you do. In many African markets, you can get 100 cards printed for a small fee. A signboard for your workshop is also important; it should be visible and include your phone number.

You also need a way to communicate with customers. A phone is essential. If you don't have a personal phone, consider getting a cheap one with a local SIM card. WhatsApp is widely used for sending quotes and photos. For record-keeping, a simple exercise book works: write down each job, the customer's name, the price agreed, and the date. This helps you track your income and follow up on payments. With these basic tools, you can start pricing and marketing professionally.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Calculate your material cost. For each job, list every piece of steel, welding rod, paint, and consumable you will use. Measure lengths and weights. Multiply by current unit prices. For example, a window grill might need 5 meters of 12mm square tube at 800 Naira per meter = 4,000 Naira, plus 1 kg of welding rods at 1,200 Naira, plus 0.5 liter of paint at 1,500 Naira. Total material = 6,700 Naira. Always add 10% for waste and offcuts.

Step 2: Calculate your labour cost. Decide on a fair hourly rate. In many African countries, a skilled welder might charge 800 to 1,500 Naira per hour. Estimate how many hours the job will take. Include time for measuring, cutting, welding, grinding, painting, and installation. If a gate takes 10 hours, at 1,000 Naira per hour, labour = 10,000 Naira. Don't forget your helper's wages if you have one.

Step 3: Add overhead costs. These are expenses that don't change per job: rent for your workshop, electricity, gas, machine maintenance, transport to buy materials and to deliver. Estimate a monthly overhead, say 20,000 Naira, and divide by the number of jobs you do per month, say 10 jobs, so 2,000 Naira per job. Add this to your cost. Also add transport for that specific job, like fuel for delivery.

Step 4: Add profit. This is your income. A common profit margin is 20% to 50% of total cost. If your total cost is 20,000 Naira, a 30% profit is 6,000 Naira, so your price is 26,000 Naira. Be consistent. Don't undercharge just to win a job; you'll go out of business. For special designs or rush jobs, charge more.

Step 5: Write a clear quote. Include your business name, date, customer details, description of work, materials, dimensions, price, and payment terms (e.g., 50% deposit, balance on delivery). Give the quote in person or via WhatsApp. Follow up after a few days. If the customer negotiates, you can adjust slightly but never below cost. Keep a copy for your records.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Forgetting to include all costs. Many welders only count steel and welding rods, ignoring electricity, gas, transport, and wear on tools. Fix: Use a checklist. Write down every single expense, even small ones like grinding discs and paint brushes. Add a 10% contingency for unexpected costs.

Mistake 2: Undercharging to beat competitors. This is common but dangerous. If you charge too little, you won't cover your costs, and you'll soon run out of money for materials. Fix: Know your costs and stick to a minimum price. Compete on quality and service, not on being the cheapest. Explain to customers why your work is better: stronger welds, better finish, on-time delivery.

Mistake 3: Not tracking time. If you don't know how long a job takes, you can't price labour accurately. Fix: Use a notebook to record start and finish times for each job. After a few jobs, you'll know your average speed. Add 20% extra time for delays.

Mistake 4: Ignoring marketing. Many skilled welders sit and wait for customers. Fix: Be proactive. Tell everyone you meet about your work. Post photos on WhatsApp status, Facebook, and Instagram. Ask satisfied customers for referrals. Visit construction sites and introduce yourself to contractors. Print simple flyers and distribute them in your neighbourhood.

Mistake 5: Poor record-keeping. Without records, you can't tell if you're making a profit. Fix: Keep a simple cash book. Record every sale and every expense. At the end of the month, subtract expenses from income to see your profit. If you're not making a profit, adjust your prices or cut costs.

Mistake 6: Not asking for a deposit. Customers may disappear after you've bought materials. Fix: Always ask for a 50% deposit before starting work. This covers your material costs and shows the customer is serious. For large jobs, ask for progress payments. Put the payment terms in writing.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in pricing and marketing might not seem obvious, but it's about protecting your business and your health. First, never compromise on safety to save money. Buy proper safety gear: goggles, gloves, apron, and boots. These are business expenses, not optional. If you get injured, you can't work, and you lose income. Include the cost of safety equipment in your overheads.

Second, be honest and transparent with customers. Give clear quotes and stick to them. If there are changes, discuss and agree on a new price before doing the work. Don't surprise the customer with hidden charges. This builds trust and repeat business. Also, be honest about delivery times. If you can't finish by a certain date, say so. Under-promise and over-deliver.

Third, handle money safely. Keep your cash in a safe place or bank. In many African countries, mobile money is a secure option. Ask for deposits via mobile money so you have a record. Don't carry large amounts of cash late at night. If you employ helpers, pay them fairly and on time. This keeps your team motivated and reduces theft.

Fourth, maintain your tools and equipment. A well-maintained welder is safer and lasts longer. Schedule regular maintenance and budget for it. A broken machine means lost jobs. Also, keep your workshop clean and organised. This prevents accidents and makes a good impression on customers who visit.

Fifth, protect your reputation. In the age of social media, one unhappy customer can damage your name. If a problem arises, fix it quickly and politely. Apologise if you're wrong, and offer a solution. A satisfied customer will tell others. Finally, keep learning. Attend workshops, watch videos, and ask other welders about pricing and marketing. The market changes, so your prices and methods should adapt. By following these best practices, you build a sustainable business that supports you and your family.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to put what you've learned into practice. This exercise has three parts: pricing a real job, creating a marketing plan, and making a quote.

Part 1: Pricing a job. Choose a simple item you can make, like a small window grill (1m x 1m) or a garden gate (2m x 1m). If you don't have a real order, pretend you do. Measure the materials needed. Visit your local steel supplier and record current prices. Write down: steel cost, welding rods, paint, electricity/gas, transport, and any other consumables. Calculate your total material cost. Then estimate the time you'll take in hours. Decide on an hourly rate (e.g., 1,000 Naira). Multiply to get labour cost. Add overhead per job (e.g., 2,000 Naira). Add 30% profit. Write the final price. Compare with what others charge in your area. Is your price competitive? If not, adjust your costs or profit margin.

Part 2: Marketing plan. Write a simple plan for the next month. List 5 things you will do to get customers. For example: (1) Take 10 good photos of your best work and save them on your phone. (2) Post one photo on WhatsApp status every day for a week. (3) Print 50 flyers and give them to neighbours and local shops. (4) Visit two construction sites and introduce yourself to the foreman. (5) Ask three past customers for referrals. Set a goal: how many new customers do you want? Write down how you will track results.

Part 3: Write a quote. Using the price from Part 1, write a professional quote. Include your business name, your phone number, the date, customer name, description of work, dimensions, materials, total price, and payment terms (50% deposit, balance on delivery). Write it neatly or type it on your phone. Show it to a friend or family member and ask for feedback. If you have a real customer, give them the quote and follow up after three days. Keep a copy for your records. By completing this exercise, you'll have a real price, a marketing plan, and a quote ready to use.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Pricing and Marketing Ornamental Ironwork in Africa

This practical reference is the working document for the module Pricing and Marketing in the Ornamental Ironwork course. It is built for African artisans, workshop owners and apprentices who need to turn craft skill into a profitable business. The ornamental ironwork market across the continent is growing: from gate and grille fabrication in rapidly urbanising cities to decorative balustrades, security doors and custom furniture. Yet many skilled fabricators undercharge, under-market and under-document their work. This reference gives you the numbers, the tools and the checklists to fix that.

Ornamental ironwork sits in a sweet spot. It is a durable, high-value product with strong demand in African housing, hospitality and commercial construction. According to the UN-Habitat World Cities Report, Africa's urban population will nearly double by 2050, creating massive demand for housing and building components. Every new home, lodge, shopfront and office block is a potential customer for gates, railings, grilles and decorative metalwork. The artisan who prices correctly and markets deliberately captures a larger share of that value.

SAFETY WARNING: Pricing and Marketing Site Visits

When visiting client sites to measure, quote or install ornamental ironwork, you enter uncontrolled environments. Always wear closed safety boots, a hard hat where overhead work is happening, and high-visibility clothing on active construction sites. Never climb unfinished structures or lean ladders against unsecured walls to take measurements. Carry a first-aid kit in your vehicle. In areas with live electrical wiring, confirm isolation before drilling or welding. Heat, dust and traffic are real hazards on African urban sites treat every site visit as a safety-critical task, not just a sales opportunity.

Quick Reference Checklist: Pricing and Marketing Your Ironwork

  1. Cost every job fully material, consumables, labour, transport, overhead and profit margin. Never quote from memory.
  2. Know your break-even price the minimum you can charge without losing money. Write it down and never go below it.
  3. Photograph every finished job before, during and after. These photos are your marketing asset for years.
  4. Ask every satisfied customer for a referral one happy client can bring three more. Make it a habit, not an afterthought.
  5. Keep a simple quote book or spreadsheet record what you quoted, what you won, and why you lost. Review monthly.
  6. Maintain a WhatsApp Business profile with catalogue, location, hours and a clear description of your services.
  7. Build relationships with builders, architects and hardware stores these are your highest-value repeat customers.
  8. Review your prices every quarter steel and fuel prices move. Your quotes must move with them.

Putting It Together

Pricing and marketing are not separate from your craft they are part of it. An ornamental ironwork business in Africa succeeds when the artisan treats every gate, grille and balustrade as both a product and a marketing asset. Price with discipline. Market with consistency. Document everything. The demand is there: Africa's cities are building, and they need skilled metalworkers who can deliver quality on time at a fair price. Your job is to be that artisan and to run the business like a business.

Use the tables, checklist and links in this reference as your working toolkit. Return to them before every quote and every marketing push. Over time, the numbers will become second nature, and your workshop will grow from a job shop into a brand.
