1. Using NRC and BR-CORTE tables for nutrient requirements

To feed cattle correctly, you need to know how much energy, protein, minerals, and vitamins they require each day. Two widely used references are the NRC (National Research Council) tables from the United States and the BR-CORTE tables from Brazil. Both provide requirements based on animal weight, age, sex, and expected growth rate. For example, a 250 kg growing steer gaining 1 kg per day may need 2.5 kg of TDN (total digestible nutrients) and 0.7 kg of crude protein according to NRC. BR-CORTE, designed for tropical cattle like Zebu, often gives slightly different values because it accounts for heat stress and lower-quality forages. In Africa, where many cattle are Zebu or crosses, BR-CORTE is often more accurate. To use these tables, first identify your animal's body weight and target daily gain. Then find the corresponding row in the table for energy (TDN or ME) and protein (CP or MP). Adjust for environmental factors like temperature if needed. For instance, if you are in a hot region like northern Kenya, increase energy requirements by 10-15% because cattle pant and sweat, using extra energy. Always use a scale or weigh tape to get accurate weights. If you don't have a scale, use a heart girth tape: measure the circumference just behind the front legs and the body length from shoulder to pin bone, then apply the formula: weight (kg) = (heart girth cm)^2 x body length cm / 10840. Once you have the requirements, compare them to the nutrient content of your available feeds. If your feed is deficient, you can add supplements like cottonseed cake or molasses. Remember, these tables are guides; observe your cattle's body condition and adjust. A body condition score of 3 (on a 1-5 scale) is ideal for breeding, while growing steers should be around 3.5. Keep records of feed intake and weight gain to fine-tune your rations. For smallholder farmers, the BR-CORTE tables are often more accessible and tailored to tropical breeds. You can find them online or in extension manuals. Always use the most recent version, as requirements are updated with new research.

2. Determining dry matter intake in cattle

Dry matter intake (DMI) is the amount of feed (excluding water) that a cow or steer eats in a day. It is the foundation of any ration because if the animal doesn't eat enough, it won't get the nutrients it needs, no matter how good the feed is. DMI is affected by body weight, feed quality, and environmental conditions. As a rule of thumb, cattle consume 2% to 3% of their body weight in dry matter daily. For example, a 300 kg steer on good quality forage might eat 6 to 9 kg of dry matter. However, if the forage is low quality (high fiber, low protein), intake drops to maybe 1.5% of body weight. To determine DMI accurately, you can use prediction equations. The NRC (2000) equation for growing cattle is: DMI (kg/day) = (Body weight^0.75 x (0.2435 x NEg - 0.0466 x NEg^2 - 0.1128)) / NEg, where NEg is net energy for gain in Mcal/kg. That's complex, so a simpler field method is to offer a known amount of feed and weigh the leftovers. For example, give 10 kg of hay and if 2 kg is left, DMI is 8 kg. But this only works if you can weigh feed. In Africa, many farmers use visual estimates: a standard 20-litre bucket holds about 5 kg of chopped maize stover, but this varies. Better to use a hanging scale. Another method is to use the 'fill unit' system: 1 kg of concentrate equals 1 fill unit, while 1 kg of straw equals 3 fill units. A 300 kg steer can consume about 8 fill units per day. So if you feed 2 kg of concentrate (2 fill units), you can feed 2 kg of straw (6 fill units) to reach 8. This helps balance rations. Environmental heat also reduces DMI: for every 1C above 25C, intake drops by about 0.5%. So in hot climates, feed during cooler hours (early morning, evening) to maximize intake. Also, ensure fresh water is always available; cattle drink 3-5 litres per kg of dry matter consumed. High-protein feeds and good palatability increase intake. Monitor body condition and adjust. If cattle are losing weight, they are not eating enough. Use a simple record sheet to track daily feed offered and refusals. Over time, you'll learn your herd's typical intake. Remember, DMI is the first thing to check when formulating a ration: if you formulate for 8 kg DMI but the animal only eats 6 kg, the nutrient density must be higher. So always start with a realistic DMI estimate.

3. Energy systems: TDN, ME, NE for maintenance and gain

Energy is the most important nutrient for cattle, driving growth, milk production, and body maintenance. In feed formulation, we use three main energy systems: TDN (Total Digestible Nutrients), ME (Metabolizable Energy), and NE (Net Energy). TDN is the simplest and most common in Africa. It is expressed as a percentage or in kg. For example, maize grain has about 80% TDN, while maize stover has only 50%. A 250 kg steer gaining 1 kg/day needs about 2.5 kg of TDN per day. TDN is calculated from digestible crude protein, fiber, nitrogen-free extract, and fat (fat is multiplied by 2.25 because it has more energy). However, TDN overestimates energy for low-quality feeds because it doesn't account for energy lost in urine and methane. That's where ME and NE come in. ME is the energy available after digestion and urine losses. It is measured in megacalories (Mcal) per kg of dry matter. For example, good quality lucerne hay has about 2.2 Mcal ME/kg, while poor grass hay has 1.6 Mcal ME/kg. NE is the energy actually used by the animal for maintenance (NEm) and gain (NEg). The relationship is: NE = ME x efficiency (0.6 for maintenance, 0.4 for gain). So if a feed has 2.0 Mcal ME/kg, it provides 1.2 Mcal NEm and 0.8 Mcal NEg. To formulate, you need to meet both maintenance and gain requirements. Maintenance is the energy needed just to keep the animal alive; gain is for growth. For a 300 kg steer, maintenance requirement is about 5.5 Mcal NEm/day, and to gain 1 kg, you need an additional 3.5 Mcal NEg/day. So total NE requirement is 9.0 Mcal/day. If your feed has 1.2 Mcal NEm and 0.8 Mcal NEg per kg, you would need 5.5/1.2 = 4.6 kg for maintenance and 3.5/0.8 = 4.4 kg for gain, total 9 kg dry matter. But that assumes the feed is balanced. In practice, you mix feeds to match the energy density. For example, combine 2 kg maize (1.6 Mcal NEm/kg) with 6 kg hay (0.9 Mcal NEm/kg) to get 2*1.6 + 6*0.9 = 8.6 Mcal NEm, close to the 9.0 needed. Always check that protein and minerals are adequate. Energy deficiencies cause poor growth, weight loss, and infertility. Excess energy makes cattle fat, which is costly and can cause calving problems. Use TDN for quick field calculations, but for precise rations, use ME and NE. Most African extension materials provide TDN values for local feeds, so start there.

4. Protein systems: CP, DIP, UIP, metabolizable protein

Protein is essential for muscle growth, milk production, and overall health. In cattle nutrition, we talk about crude protein (CP), degradable intake protein (DIP), undegradable intake protein (UIP), and metabolizable protein (MP). CP is simply the total nitrogen content multiplied by 6.25. For example, cottonseed cake has 40% CP, while maize stover has only 4%. But not all CP is used equally. DIP is the portion broken down by rumen microbes. These microbes need DIP to grow and digest fiber. UIP (also called bypass protein) escapes the rumen and is digested in the small intestine. MP is the actual protein absorbed by the animal, coming from both microbial protein and UIP. A common mistake is to feed only CP without considering DIP and UIP. For a growing steer, you need about 12-14% CP in the diet. But if all of it is DIP, the microbes may not have enough energy to use it, and excess DIP is wasted as urea in urine. On the other hand, if you feed too much UIP and not enough DIP, the microbes starve, and fiber digestion drops. The ideal ratio for growing cattle is about 60-70% DIP and 30-40% UIP of total CP. For example, if you need 0.8 kg CP per day, aim for 0.5 kg DIP and 0.3 kg UIP. Common DIP sources are urea, soybean meal, and fresh grass. UIP sources include fishmeal, blood meal, and heat-treated soybean meal. In Africa, cottonseed cake and sunflower cake are good sources of both DIP and UIP. To calculate MP, use the NRC equation: MP (g/day) = 0.64 x microbial CP (g) + 0.8 x UIP (g). Microbial CP depends on rumen available energy and DIP. A simple rule: microbes produce about 130 g of microbial CP per kg of TDN consumed. So if a steer eats 3 kg TDN, microbes produce 390 g CP. Then add UIP: if you feed 0.3 kg UIP, that's 300 g, but only 80% is digestible, so 240 g MP. Total MP = 390 + 240 = 630 g. The requirement for a 250 kg steer gaining 1 kg/day is about 600 g MP, so this diet works. In practice, balance energy and protein together. If you increase energy, microbes need more DIP. A good strategy is to feed a basal forage plus a concentrate mix that provides both DIP and UIP. For example, mix 2 kg maize (energy) with 0.5 kg cottonseed cake (protein) and 0.1 kg urea (DIP). Always provide minerals. Monitor weight gain and adjust. If cattle are not growing, check protein first, then energy.

5. Mineral and vitamin supplementation guidelines

Minerals and vitamins are needed in small amounts but are critical for health, growth, and reproduction. In many African grazing systems, cattle suffer from deficiencies, especially of phosphorus, calcium, zinc, copper, and selenium, as well as vitamins A and D. A lack of these can cause poor growth, weak bones, infertility, and even death. The most common deficiency in Africa is phosphorus, because tropical soils and forages are low in it. A growing steer needs about 0.2% phosphorus in the diet. If your forage has only 0.1%, you must supplement. A simple way is to provide a mineral lick. For example, a good mineral mix for cattle might contain: 8% phosphorus, 12% calcium, 0.5% zinc, 0.2% copper, 0.1% selenium, and 0.05% iodine. You can buy commercial mineral blocks or make your own. A homemade mix: 40% bone meal (phosphorus and calcium), 30% salt, 20% limestone (calcium), 5% trace mineral premix, and 5% molasses for palatability. Offer free-choice; cattle will eat 30-50 g per day. But be careful with selenium: too much is toxic. Never exceed 0.3 ppm in the total diet. For vitamins, vitamin A is often deficient in dry-season forages. Cattle need about 2,200 IU of vitamin A per kg of dry matter. If grazing dry grass, supplement with a vitamin A injection (500,000 IU per animal every 2-3 months) or add a vitamin premix to the feed. Vitamin D is made from sunlight, so cattle on pasture usually get enough, but housed cattle may need supplementation. Vitamin E works with selenium; a deficiency causes white muscle disease. Provide 20-30 IU of vitamin E per kg dry matter. In areas with high rainfall, copper can be deficient due to soil leaching. A copper bolus given every 6 months can help. Also, salt (sodium and chloride) is often lacking; provide iodized salt free-choice. When using mineral supplements, always read the label for elemental levels. For example, copper sulfate is 25% copper, so 10 g provides 2.5 g copper. Mix thoroughly into feed to avoid toxicity. If you see symptoms like poor growth, stiff joints, or reproductive failures, consult an extension officer or vet for a mineral analysis. Regular supplementation is cheap and gives big returns. Remember, minerals are not a substitute for energy and protein; they are complementary. Always provide clean water; minerals increase water intake.

6. Adjusting requirements for environmental factors

Cattle requirements change with the environment. Heat, cold, humidity, wind, and mud all affect how much energy and feed an animal needs. In Africa, heat stress is the biggest issue. When temperatures rise above 25C, cattle eat less and use more energy to cool themselves. For every 1C above 25C, dry matter intake drops by about 0.5%, and maintenance energy requirement increases by about 2%. For example, a steer in northern Nigeria where it is 35C would need 20% more energy for maintenance and would eat 5% less feed. To adjust, you must increase the energy density of the diet. If the normal diet has 60% TDN, you might need 70% TDN. You can do this by adding more maize or molasses. Also, feed during cooler hours (early morning or evening) to encourage intake. Provide shade and plenty of water. In cold environments, like the highlands of Ethiopia, cattle need more energy to stay warm. Below 15C, maintenance energy increases by 1% per degree drop. For a 300 kg steer at 5C, that's a 10% increase. So you might add 0.5 kg of maize per day. Humidity also matters: high humidity makes heat stress worse because cattle cannot pant effectively. Wind helps cool cattle, so in hot, windy areas, heat stress is less. Mud in wet seasons increases energy needs by up to 30% because cattle expend energy walking through it. In such conditions, provide a dry resting area and increase energy by 10-15%. Altitude affects water intake and can cause stress; at high altitudes, cattle may need more energy for breathing. When adjusting, always use the NRC or BR-CORTE environmental adjustment factors. For example, NRC has an equation: NE requirement = maintenance NE x (0.8 + 0.2 x (20 - temperature)/10) for cold, and for heat, multiply by 1.1 to 1.3. A simpler rule: for each 5C above 25C, increase energy by 10%. For each 5C below 15C, increase by 5%. Protein requirements also increase slightly under heat stress because cattle lose more nitrogen through sweat and urine. Increase CP by 1-2 percentage points. Minerals like potassium and sodium are lost in sweat, so provide extra salt and a good mineral mix. Always monitor body condition and adjust based on actual performance. If cattle are losing weight, they need more energy. If they are too fat, reduce. Keep records of temperature and feed intake to learn your local patterns. In Africa, where climate varies widely, local adjustment is key to profitable feeding.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Nutrient Requirements and Feeding Standards for Cattle and Steers

In African beef and dairy operations, feed is the single largest variable costoften 6075% of total production cost. Getting nutrient requirements right is not an academic exercise; it is the difference between a profitable feedlot and one that bleeds money through overfeeding, underfeeding, or wasted supplements. This Practical Reference consolidates the key standards from the NRC (National Research Council) and BR-CORTE systems, adapted for tropical and subtropical African conditions. It is designed as a working tool for feed formulators, feedlot managers, and extension officers who need to make fast, defensible decisions.

Two nutrient requirement systems dominate practice in Africa: the NRC (2016, 8th revised edition) and the Brazilian BR-CORTE (2016, 3rd edition). The NRC is the global default, widely used in Southern and East Africa. BR-CORTE is increasingly popular in Lusophone Africa and tropical regions because its equations were developed on Bos indicus (Zebu) cattle under tropical conditionsexactly the genetics and environment found across much of the continent. Where the two differ, BR-CORTE often predicts lower maintenance energy requirements for Zebu cattle, reflecting their superior heat tolerance and lower metabolic overhead. Using NRC values for Zebu in hot climates can overestimate requirements by 1015%, leading to unnecessary feed costs.

1. Using NRC and BR-CORTE Tables for Nutrient Requirements

Both systems provide tables and equations for maintenance and gain requirements based on body weight (BW), average daily gain (ADG), and physiological state. The core maintenance energy equation in NRC (2016) for beef cattle is:

NEm (Mcal/d) = 0.077 MBW0.75 (where MBW = metabolic body weight in kg)

BR-CORTE (2016) uses a similar structure but with adjusted coefficients for Zebu: NEm = 0.070 BW0.75 for Bos indicus, reflecting a lower maintenance requirement. For a 400 kg Zebu steer, NRC predicts 0.077 4000.75 9.7 Mcal NEm/d, while BR-CORTE predicts 8.8 Mcal NEm/da 9% difference that translates directly into feed cost savings when using BR-CORTE for Zebu cattle.

For gain, both systems use the equivalent of NEg (net energy for gain), which depends on ADG and mature weight. In practice, you calculate total NE requirement = NEm + NEg, then match it to the NE concentration of your diet (Mcal/kg DM). 2. Determining Dry Matter Intake (DMI) in Cattle

DMI drives everything: it sets the ceiling on nutrient supply. In African conditions, DMI is often limited by forage quality, heat stress, and water availability. NRC (2016) predicts DMI for growing cattle using:

DMI (kg/d) = BW0.75 (0.2435 NEm 0.0466 NEm2 0.1128) / NEmdiet

BR-CORTE uses a simpler approach based on body weight and diet energy concentration. For tropical forages, actual DMI is often 1.82.2% of BW for growing cattle, compared with 2.53.0% for high-concentrate feedlot diets. In the African context, where Brachiaria, Panicum, and maize stover dominate, DMI prediction must be adjusted downward by 1020% when forage NDF exceeds 60% or when ambient temperature exceeds 30C.

3. Energy Systems: TDN, ME, NE for Maintenance and Gain

African feed formulators commonly work with TDN (Total Digestible Nutrients) because it is the basis of many local feed tables and is easier to measure than NE. The conversion is:

DE (Mcal/kg) = TDN (%) 0.04409
ME (Mcal/kg) = DE 0.82 (for roughages) or 0.90 (for concentrates)
NEm (Mcal/kg) = ME 0.72 (roughages) or 0.78 (concentrates)

In practice, a diet with 65% TDN provides approximately 2.87 Mcal DE/kg, 2.35 Mcal ME/kg, and 1.69 Mcal NEm/kg (roughage-based). For a 400 kg steer gaining 1.0 kg/d requiring 14.2 Mcal NE/d (NRC), the required DMI from this diet would be 14.2 / 1.69 8.4 kg DM/dabout 2.1% of BW, which is achievable but tight. This is why African feedlots often need to increase energy density with maize, molasses, or protected fats.

4. Protein Systems: CP, DIP, UIP, Metabolizable Protein

Crude protein (CP) alone is insufficient. The modern approach separates DIP (Degradable Intake Protein)used by rumen microbesand UIP (Undegradable Intake Protein)bypass protein absorbed in the small intestine. Together with microbial protein, these supply Metabolizable Protein (MP).

NRC (2016) and BR-CORTE (2016) both use MP systems. For a 400 kg steer gaining 1.0 kg/d, MP requirement is approximately 600700 g/d. Rumen microbes typically supply 6080% of MP when DIP is adequate (roughly 1013% of diet DM as DIP). In African conditions, where true protein sources like soybean meal are expensive, urea can supply DIP cheaplybut only if energy is adequate. UIP sources include cottonseed cake, brewers' grains, and fish meal.

Practical rule: Ensure DIP is at least 60% of CP for forage-based diets, and 5055% for high-concentrate diets. If using urea, never exceed 1% of diet DM or 30% of total CP.

5. Mineral and Vitamin Supplementation Guidelines

African soils and forages are frequently deficient in phosphorus (P), calcium (Ca), zinc (Zn), copper (Cu), selenium (Se), and cobalt (Co). A free-choice mineral supplement is non-negotiable in most production systems. Key guidelines:

  • Ca:P ratio: Maintain 1.5:1 to 2:1 for growing cattle. Tropical grasses are often low in P, so dicalcium phosphate or bone meal is essential.
  • Trace minerals: Provide 3050 ppm Zn, 810 ppm Cu, 0.10.2 ppm Se, and 0.1 ppm Co in the total diet.
  • Vitamins: Vitamin A (2,2003,000 IU/kg DM) is critical where green forage is scarce. Vitamin E (1525 IU/kg DM) supports immunity. Vitamin D is rarely deficient under sunlight.
  • Salt (NaCl): Provide 0.250.5% of diet DM, or free-choice.

SAFETY WARNING: Urea Toxicity

Urea is a cost-effective DIP source but is highly toxic if misused. Never feed urea to starving cattle, never exceed 1% of total diet DM, and always adapt animals over 57 days. Symptoms of ammonia toxicity include staggering, excess salivation, bloat, and death within 3060 minutes. Keep vinegar (5% acetic acid) on hand as an emergency treatment (24 litres per adult animal) and contact a veterinarian immediately. In African smallholder settings, urea should only be used in total mixed rations (TMR) or properly formulated supplementsnever as a top-dress on forage.

6. Adjusting Requirements for Environmental Factors

African cattle face heat stress, humidity, and seasonal feed variability. The NRC (2016) provides adjustment factors:

  • Heat stress: For every 1C above 25C, increase maintenance energy requirement by 24% (up to 35C). Above 35C, DMI drops by 1030%.
  • Cold stress: Below 15C, increase NEm by 1% per degree (relevant in highland Ethiopia, Kenya, and South Africa).
  • Mud and rain: Increase maintenance by 1020% in wet, muddy conditions.
  • Altitude: Above 1,500 m, increase energy requirements by 510% due to increased respiratory and cardiac work.
  • Parasites and disease: Subclinical infections can increase maintenance by 1025%. Budget for deworming and vaccination as part of the feeding programme.

In practice, a 400 kg steer in the Sahel at 38C may need 2030% more maintenance energy than NRC tables predict for temperate conditions. This is why local calibrationusing BR-CORTE or locally derived equationsis essential for profitability.
