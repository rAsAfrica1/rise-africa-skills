1. Crop Yield Records

Crop yield records tell you exactly how much food or cash crop you harvested from each field. Without them, you are guessing. Start by dividing your farm into plots. Give each plot a name or number, like "Plot A - maize near river" or "Plot 3 - beans on hill." When you harvest, weigh the crop. Use a simple hanging scale (up to 100 kg) or a bathroom scale. For maize, weigh in kilograms. For example, if Plot A gave 850 kg of maize from 0.5 hectares, write that down. Also record the date of harvest and the crop variety. If you sold some, note the price per kg. Keep a notebook with columns: Date, Plot, Crop, Weight (kg), Area (ha), and Notes (e.g., "rained heavily in July"). At the end of the season, calculate yield per hectare: weight divided by area. 850 kg 0.5 ha = 1,700 kg/ha. Compare plots. Maybe Plot B gave only 1,200 kg/ha because you planted late. That tells you to plant earlier next year. If you grow multiple crops, keep separate pages for each. For tubers like cassava, weigh in bags or basins, then convert to kg using a standard (e.g., 1 basin = 20 kg). For vegetables, record in crates or bunches. Always record losses: if 50 kg of tomatoes spoiled before sale, write it. This helps you plan better storage. At the end of the year, add all yields per crop. If you harvested 3,000 kg of maize from 2 hectares, your average is 1,500 kg/ha. Share these records with your family or cooperative. They help you decide which crop to expand and which to drop. Without yield records, you cannot know if you are improving. Start today with a simple exercise book and a pen. Even a phone note works, but paper is more reliable in rural areas. Make it a habit after every harvest.

2. Livestock Birth and Mortality Records

Keeping track of births and deaths in your herd or flock is critical for knowing if your livestock business is growing or shrinking. For each animal, record the date of birth, sex, and dam (mother) and sire (father) if known. Use a notebook with one page per animal or a simple table. For example, if your goat "Mama" gave birth to twins on 12 March 2024, write: Date: 12/03/2024, Dam: Mama, Kids: 2 (1 male, 1 female). Ear tags or neck collars with numbers help you identify individuals. If you cannot afford tags, use names or descriptions like "white spot on left ear." For mortality, record the date, animal ID, and suspected cause. If a chicken died on 5 April 2024, write: Date: 05/04/2024, ID: Hen 7, Cause: unknown (possibly Newcastle). This helps you spot disease outbreaks. If three piglets die in one week, you know to call a vet. Also record vaccinations and treatments. For example, if you dewormed all goats on 1 May 2024, note it. At the end of each month, count your animals. Start with 20 goats, 5 died, 8 born, so you have 23. If deaths are higher than births, your herd is shrinking. You need to improve feed, housing, or health. For cattle, record calving intervals: if a cow gives birth every 18 months instead of 12, you lose money. Keep a summary sheet: total births, total deaths, and current herd size. Share with your cooperative or vet. Even smallholders can do this. Use a wall calendar to mark birth dates. For poultry, record eggs hatched and chicks that survive to 4 weeks. If only 30 out of 50 chicks survive, you have a 60% survival rate. That tells you to improve brooding. These records turn guesswork into facts. They help you cull poor breeders and keep productive ones. Start with one species and expand.

3. Milk and Egg Production Logs

Daily milk and egg records show you which animals are paying for their feed and which are not. For milk, record the amount from each cow or goat at each milking. Use a graduated bucket or a simple measuring jug. If you milk twice a day, record morning and evening. For example: Cow "Nyota" gave 8 litres morning, 6 litres evening on 10 June 2024. Total 14 litres. Write this in a table with columns: Date, Animal ID, Morning (L), Evening (L), Total (L), Notes (e.g., "feed changed"). At the end of the week, add totals per animal. If Nyota gave 70 litres and another cow gave 40 litres, you know Nyota is your best. If a cow's milk drops suddenly, check for mastitis or poor feed. For eggs, record daily collection per hen house or per flock. If you have 50 layers, collect eggs each morning. Write: Date: 10/06/2024, Eggs collected: 42, Broken: 2, Notes: "hot weather." Calculate laying percentage: eggs collected number of hens 100. 42 50 100 = 84%. If it drops below 70%, check feed, water, or disease. For goats, milk yield is smaller, maybe 1-2 litres per day. Still record it. Use a simple notebook kept in the milking shed. At the end of the month, total milk and eggs. If you sold 300 litres of milk at 50 KES per litre, that's 15,000 KES. Compare with feed costs. If feed cost 10,000 KES, you made a profit. If not, adjust. For eggs, if you sold 1,000 eggs at 15 KES each, that's 15,000 KES. These logs help you decide which animals to keep and which to sell. They also help you predict future production. For example, if a cow is pregnant, she will dry off in 2 months. Plan for that. Keep records simple but consistent. Even a chalkboard works. The key is to write daily, not from memory.

4. Feed and Input Usage Records

Feed and input records track what you put into your farm so you can calculate costs and efficiency. For livestock, record every bag of feed, amount given per animal or group, and cost. For example, if you buy 50 kg of dairy meal for 2,500 KES, write: Date: 01/07/2024, Item: Dairy meal, Quantity: 50 kg, Cost: 2,500 KES. Then record daily feeding: Cow Nyota gets 4 kg per day. At the end of the week, she ate 28 kg. If she gave 70 litres of milk, you used 0.4 kg of feed per litre. That's your feed conversion. If another cow uses 0.6 kg per litre, she is less efficient. For crops, record seeds, fertiliser, and pesticides. If you planted 10 kg of maize seed on 1 hectare, write it. If you applied 100 kg of DAP fertiliser, record date and amount. For example: Date: 15/04/2024, Input: DAP, Quantity: 100 kg, Plot: A, Cost: 6,000 KES. Also record labour if you pay workers. At the end of the season, add all input costs per plot. If Plot A cost 20,000 KES in inputs and gave 1,700 kg of maize sold at 30 KES/kg = 51,000 KES, your gross margin is 31,000 KES. If Plot B cost 15,000 KES and gave 1,200 kg = 36,000 KES, gross margin is 21,000 KES. Plot A is better. Keep receipts in a folder or envelope. For homemade feed, record ingredients: 10 kg maize bran, 5 kg cotton seed cake, etc. This helps you adjust rations. For water, if you irrigate, record hours pumped or litres used. Even simple records like "watered for 2 hours" help. These records stop waste. If you see that you are overfeeding a low-producing cow, reduce her feed. If you see that fertiliser gave a good yield increase, buy more next year. Without input records, you cannot know your true profit. Start with a notebook and a pen. Write every purchase and every use. Review monthly. Share with your cooperative to compare prices. Good records turn farming into a business.

5. Field and Plot Identification

If you cannot identify your fields clearly, your records are useless. Every plot needs a unique name or number that everyone on the farm understands. Start by drawing a simple map of your farm on paper. Mark boundaries, roads, and water sources. Then divide into plots. Give each a name: "Plot 1 - near homestead," "Plot 2 - behind mango tree," "Plot 3 - riverbank." Write these names on wooden stakes or painted stones at the corner of each plot. Use the same names in all your records. For example, if you harvest maize from Plot 2, write "Plot 2" in your yield record. If you apply fertiliser to Plot 1, write "Plot 1." Never use vague terms like "the big field" or "the one near the road." If you have many small plots, number them systematically: Plot 1, Plot 2, etc. For livestock, identify pens or houses: "Cattle shed A," "Goat pen 2," "Chicken house 1." Use the same IDs in birth, mortality, and feed records. For example, if a goat dies in Goat pen 2, write that. This helps you trace problems. If you rent land, record the owner's name and plot size. Measure plot size accurately. Use a tape measure or a GPS app on a smartphone. If you don't have either, pace the length and width. For a rectangular plot, area = length width. If it's irregular, divide into rectangles. Write the size in hectares or acres. For example, Plot 1 is 0.5 ha, Plot 2 is 0.3 ha. This is essential for calculating yield per hectare. Update your map each season if you rotate crops. Keep a master list of all plots with their names, sizes, and current crop. Post it in your store or house. Train your family or workers to use the same names. If you have a cooperative, share your plot map. This prevents confusion when sharing equipment or selling produce. Good identification makes all other records reliable. Without it, you might mix up yields or inputs. Start today: draw your map, name your plots, and use those names consistently. It takes one hour but saves months of confusion.

6. Using Production Records for Decision Making

Production records are only useful if you use them to make decisions. At the end of each season or month, sit down with your records and ask simple questions. Which crop gave the highest yield per hectare? Which cow gave the most milk? Which plot had the most pest damage? For example, if your records show that Plot A gave 1,700 kg/ha of maize and Plot B gave 1,200 kg/ha, you know Plot A is better. Next season, plant more of Plot A and improve Plot B with compost or better seed. If your milk records show that Cow Nyota gave 14 litres per day and Cow Tembo gave 8 litres, you might sell Tembo and keep Nyota's heifer. If your feed records show that you spend 60% of your income on feed, you need to find cheaper feed or reduce waste. Use records to set targets. If you harvested 1,500 kg/ha last year, aim for 2,000 kg/ha this year. Write that target down. Then plan: use improved seed, apply manure, plant on time. For livestock, if your mortality rate is 20%, aim for 10%. Vaccinate, improve housing. Records also help you get loans. Banks and cooperatives want to see your production history. If you can show three years of yield records, you are more likely to get credit. Share records with extension officers. They can advise you based on facts, not guesses. For example, if your egg production drops in the hot season, an officer might suggest cooling the hen house. At the end of the year, calculate your profit per enterprise. If maize gave 50,000 KES profit and beans gave 30,000 KES, focus on maize. But also consider risk: if maize fails, beans might save you. Use records to diversify wisely. Keep a simple ledger: income minus costs. Review monthly. Make one change at a time. If you change too much, you won't know what worked. Start with one decision: which plot to fertilise first? Your records will tell you. Without records, you guess. With records, you know. That is the difference between a farmer and a businessperson.

<h2>Practical Reference: Production Records in African Agribusiness</h2>

<p>Production records are the financial engine room of any African farm or agribusiness. Without them, decisions on what to plant, which animals to cull, how much feed to buy, or which plot is profitable are guesses. With them, a farmer or agribusiness manager can negotiate credit, plan cash flow, benchmark against peers, and prove traceability to buyers. This practical reference lesson gives you the formats, benchmarks, and decision rules you need to keep production records that work in real African conditions low-cost, field-ready, and business-focused.</p>

<h3>1. Crop Yield Records</h3>
<p>Record yield per plot, not per farm. A 2-hectare farm with one plot yielding 4 t/ha and another yielding 1.2 t/ha has a management problem, not a fertility problem. Use a simple field notebook or a free app such as <strong>ODK Collect</strong> or <strong>Kobo Toolbox</strong>. Record: plot code, crop, variety, planting date, harvest date, area (ha), total harvest (kg), and yield (kg/ha). Weigh at least 10% of the harvest to calibrate visual estimates.</p>
<p>Benchmark: smallholder maize in East Africa averages 1.52.5 t/ha; with improved seed and fertiliser, 46 t/ha is achievable. If your plot is below 1.5 t/ha, investigate soil, seed, or timing before expanding area.</p>

<h3>2. Livestock Birth and Mortality Records</h3>
<p>For each animal, record tag number, dam, sire (if known), date of birth, sex, and birth weight. Record every death with date, cause (if known), and age. Calculate:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Calving/kidding/lambing rate</strong> = (number of live births number of females exposed) 100</li>
<li><strong>Mortality rate</strong> = (number of deaths average herd size) 100</li>
<li><strong>Weaning rate</strong> = (number weaned number born alive) 100</li>
</ul>
<p>Targets: cattle calf mortality below 10%; goat/kid mortality below 15%; poultry mortality below 5% per month. If mortality exceeds these, review vaccination, housing, and feed before buying more stock.</p>

<h3>3. Milk and Egg Production Logs</h3>
<p>Milk: record daily yield per cow (litres), morning and evening, plus lactation number and days in milk. Egg: record daily collection per house or flock (number and weight), cracked eggs, and mortality.</p>
<p>Benchmarks: crossbred dairy cow in East Africa: 815 litres/day; indigenous cow: 25 litres/day. Layer hen: 250300 eggs/year (about 7080% lay rate). If your cow drops below 8 litres/day for a week, check feed, water, and health.</p>

<h3>4. Feed and Input Usage Records</h3>
<p>Record every input: date, type (feed, fertiliser, seed, vaccine), quantity, unit cost, supplier, and which plot or animal group received it. This gives you cost per kg of milk, per egg, or per kg of maize. Without this, you cannot know if you are profitable.</p>
<p>Example: if a dairy cow eats 6 kg of concentrate per day at KES 45/kg, that is KES 270/day. At 12 litres/day and KES 50/litre, milk revenue is KES 600/day feed is 45% of revenue. If feed cost exceeds 60% of milk revenue, renegotiate feed supply or improve forage.</p>

<h3>5. Field and Plot Identification</h3>
<p>Every field must have a permanent code (e.g., F01, F02) and a sketch map or GPS point. Record plot size, soil type, slope, and previous crop. This allows you to compare yields across seasons and plots. In communal land, agree codes with neighbours to avoid confusion.</p>

<h3>6. Using Production Records for Decision Making</h3>
<p>Records are only useful if you act on them. Use this simple monthly review:</p>
<ol>
<li>Compare actual yield/milk/eggs against target.</li>
<li>Identify the worst-performing plot or animal.</li>
<li>Calculate cost per unit of output.</li>
<li>Decide: keep, cull, replant, or change input.</li>
<li>Write the decision and review next month.</li>
</ol>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong> Never use veterinary drugs, pesticides, or fertilisers without recording the product name, batch number, dose, date, and withdrawal period. In many African countries, selling milk or meat before the withdrawal period ends is illegal and dangerous. Keep a separate <em>chemical and drug register</em> and store it away from feed and food. If you cannot read the label, do not use the product.

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Every field has a permanent code (F01, F02) and a sketch map or GPS point.</li>
<li> Every animal has a tag number linked to its dam and birth date.</li>
<li> Crop yield recorded per plot in kg and converted to kg/ha.</li>
<li> Daily milk and egg records kept, including cracked eggs and mortality.</li>
<li> All feed, seed, fertiliser, and drugs recorded with date, quantity, and cost.</li>
<li> Monthly review compares actual vs. target and identifies worst performer.</li>
<li> Withdrawal periods for drugs and chemicals strictly observed and recorded.</li>
<li> Records stored safely (paper in a dry box, digital backed up weekly).</li>
</ul>

Start with one plot and one animal group. Record for one season. Then use the data to make one decision. That is how record keeping becomes profitable.</p>
