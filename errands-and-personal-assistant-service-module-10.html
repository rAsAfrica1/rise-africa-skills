1. Personal Safety When Running Errands

When you run errands for clients, your personal safety comes first. Always plan your route before leaving. Use Google Maps or a local app like Waze to check for high-crime areas. In cities like Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg, avoid known hotspots such as certain informal settlements or isolated roads. Share your live location with a trusted friend or family member using WhatsApp or Google Maps location sharing. Keep your phone charged and carry a power bank. If you use public transport, sit near the driver or conductor. In a matatu or danfo, avoid sitting by the window if you carry valuables. When walking, keep your bag close to your body and avoid displaying expensive items like smartphones or jewellery. Trust your instincts: if a place feels unsafe, leave immediately.

Carry a whistle or personal alarm. In South Africa, you can buy a rape whistle for under R50. In Kenya, a small pepper spray costs about KES 500. Learn how to use it safely. If you are confronted by a robber, do not resist. Hand over your phone or cash. Your life is worth more than any errand. Memorise emergency numbers: police 999 in Kenya, 10111 in South Africa, 112 in Nigeria. Save them on your phone as 'Police'. If you drive, keep doors locked and windows up in traffic. Do not pick up hitchhikers. Park in well-lit areas. When meeting a new client for the first time, meet in a public place like a mall or cafÃ© during daylight. Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return. If you feel threatened, call for help loudly. Many African communities have neighbourhood watch groups or WhatsApp security groups. Join one in your area. Finally, stay sober and alert. Never run errands under the influence of alcohol or drugs. Your safety is your responsibility.

2. Securing Client Homes and Belongings

When you have access to a client's home, you are trusted with their property. Always lock doors and windows when you leave, even if you are just stepping out to the bin. In many African cities, burglaries happen during the day when homes are empty. Use deadbolts if available. If the client has an alarm system, learn how to arm and disarm it. Common systems in Africa include Paradox, DSC, and local brands like Securex in Kenya. Get the code and test it with the client present. Never share the code with anyone. If you clean or organise, do not move valuable items like jewellery, cash, or documents unless instructed. If you must move them, take a photo of the original location and put them back exactly. Do not invite friends or family into the client's home. This is a serious breach of trust and can lead to dismissal or legal action.

If you notice anything unusual, like a broken window or a strange car parked outside, do not enter. Call the client and the police. When you leave, double-check that all doors are locked and windows closed. If the client has a safe, do not attempt to open it. If you are given a key, keep it on a secure keyring and never label it with the client's address. If you lose a key, inform the client immediately so they can change locks. In some African countries, it is common to have a security guard at the gate. Introduce yourself and show your ID. Build a good relationship with guards; they can be your eyes and ears. If you are responsible for receiving deliveries, verify the delivery person's identity before opening the door. Use a peephole or a chain. Never let strangers inside. If you are away for more than a day, ask a neighbour to keep an eye on the property. Document everything: take photos of the home before and after your visit. This protects you if the client claims something is missing. Always act as if the client is watching, because trust is hard to build and easy to destroy.

3. Handling Cash and Valuables Safely

Many errand jobs involve handling cash. Whether you are paying bills, buying groceries, or depositing money, you must keep it safe. Never carry large amounts of cash in a visible way. Use a money belt or an inside pocket. In Africa, mobile money is king. Use M-Pesa in Kenya, MTN Mobile Money in Uganda, Ghana, and Cameroon, or EcoCash in Zimbabwe. For example, M-Pesa allows you to send up to KES 150,000 per day. Encourage clients to pay you via mobile money or bank transfer instead of cash. If you must carry cash, split it into different pockets. Do not count money in public. If you need to withdraw cash from an ATM, do it during daylight in a busy area. Use ATMs inside banks or malls. Cover the keypad when entering your PIN. If someone suspicious is nearby, cancel the transaction and leave. After withdrawing, put the money away immediately and do not linger.

When paying for goods, keep receipts. For valuable items like laptops or jewellery, use a secure bag. If you are transporting a valuable item for a client, consider using a ride-hailing service like Uber or Bolt instead of public transport. In South Africa, you can use a courier service like PostNet for high-value items. Always get a tracking number. If you are robbed, do not fight. Give up the cash or valuables. Report to the police within 24 hours and get a case number. This is needed for insurance claims. For cash deposits, go directly to the bank. Do not stop for other errands. If you use a bank, count the money in front of the teller and get a receipt. For mobile money, confirm the transaction on your phone. Never share your PIN or password. If a client gives you a cheque, deposit it immediately. Keep a record of all transactions in a notebook or app like Excel. At the end of the day, reconcile the cash. If there is a shortage, report it immediately. Honesty is your best policy. If you follow these steps, you will build a reputation as a trustworthy errand professional.

4. Emergency Procedures and First Aid Basics

Emergencies can happen anytime. You must know what to do. First, memorise emergency numbers: Police 999 (Kenya), 10111 (South Africa), 112 (Nigeria), 999 (Uganda), 112 (Ghana). Ambulance: 999 (Kenya), 10177 (South Africa), 112 (Nigeria). Fire: 999 (Kenya), 10111 (South Africa). Save these on your phone. If you are in a car accident, stop, check for injuries, and call for help. Do not move an injured person unless there is immediate danger like fire. If you are trained in first aid, help. If not, keep the person calm and wait for paramedics. For bleeding, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth. For burns, cool with running water for 10 minutes. Do not apply oil or toothpaste. For choking, give five back blows between the shoulder blades. For unconsciousness, check breathing. If not breathing, start CPR if trained. Push hard and fast in the centre of the chest at 100-120 compressions per minute. In Africa, many community health workers offer free first aid training. Organisations like the Red Cross and St John Ambulance run courses for around KES 3,000 or R500. Take one.

If you are caught in a fire, stay low and crawl under smoke. Feel doors before opening. If hot, do not open. Use a wet cloth to cover your nose. If you are in a building with a fire extinguisher, use the PASS method: Pull, Aim, Squeeze, Sweep. If you are robbed, do not resist. After the incident, call the police and your client. If you are locked out, call a locksmith. Keep a spare key with a trusted neighbour. For medical emergencies, know the nearest hospital. In Nairobi, Kenyatta National Hospital; in Lagos, Lagos University Teaching Hospital; in Johannesburg, Charlotte Maxeke Hospital. If you have a chronic condition like asthma, carry your inhaler. If you are allergic to bee stings, carry an EpiPen. Always carry a small first aid kit in your bag: bandages, antiseptic, painkillers, gloves, and a whistle. Check it every month. If you are with a client who collapses, call for help, check breathing, and start CPR if needed. Stay calm. Panic makes things worse. Your quick actions can save a life.

5. Background Checks and Trust Building

Trust is the foundation of errand and personal assistant work. Clients need to know you are reliable and honest. To build trust, start with a professional background check. In many African countries, you can get a police clearance certificate. In Kenya, it is called a Certificate of Good Conduct from the Directorate of Criminal Investigations (DCI). It costs KES 1,050 and takes about two weeks. In Nigeria, you get a Police Character Certificate from the Nigeria Police Force. In South Africa, a police clearance certificate from SAPS costs around R150. These certificates show you have no criminal record. Get one and keep it updated. Also, provide references from previous clients or employers. Ask them to write a short letter or be available for a phone call. If you have worked in a home, a reference from that client is powerful. Create a simple CV that includes your skills, experience, and contact details. Use a professional photo. If you have a smartphone, create a LinkedIn profile or a WhatsApp Business profile with your services.

When you meet a new client, be on time. Dress neatly. Bring your ID, police clearance, and references. Be honest about your skills and limitations. If you cannot do something, say so. Do not make promises you cannot keep. During the first meeting, ask questions about the job: what errands, how often, what hours, and what payment. Discuss boundaries: what you will and will not do. For example, you will not handle large sums of cash without a receipt. Write a simple contract. Even a handwritten one signed by both parties is better than nothing. Include the scope of work, payment terms, and confidentiality clause. Never share client information with others. If you are on social media, do not post about your client's home or belongings. If you make a mistake, admit it immediately and offer a solution. Clients appreciate honesty. Over time, you can ask for a testimonial. Positive reviews on Google or Facebook can bring more work. Join professional groups like the Errand Association of Kenya or similar in your country. Network with other errand professionals. They can refer clients to you. Always follow through on your commitments. If you say you will be there at 9 AM, be there at 8:45 AM. Trust is built in small actions. Once you have it, protect it fiercely.

6. Insurance Coverage for Errand Professionals

As an errand professional, you face risks: accidents, theft, damage to client property, or injury. Insurance protects you. In Africa, there are affordable options. For personal accident cover, you can get a policy from companies like Britam, Jubilee, or UAP in Kenya. A basic personal accident policy might cost KES 5,000 per year and cover up to KES 1 million for death or disability. In Nigeria, Leadway Assurance and AXA Mansard offer similar plans. In South Africa, Discovery and Old Mutual have personal accident cover. If you use a car or motorbike for errands, you need motor insurance. Third-party insurance is mandatory in many countries. In Kenya, it costs about KES 5,000 per year. Comprehensive cover is more expensive but covers your own vehicle. If you carry goods for clients, get goods-in-transit insurance. This covers loss or damage while you are transporting items. For example, if you are carrying a laptop and it is stolen, the insurance pays. In South Africa, Santam and Hollard offer this. In Nigeria, AIICO and Custodian offer it. If you handle cash, consider a money insurance policy. This covers cash while in transit or on your premises. It is often part of a business insurance package.

For errand professionals who work inside client homes, you need public liability insurance. This covers damage you accidentally cause to client property, like breaking a vase. It also covers injury to a third party on the client's property. In Kenya, APA Insurance offers public liability for small businesses starting at KES 10,000 per year. In Ghana, Enterprise Insurance and SIC offer similar. If you employ others, you need employer's liability insurance. This covers you if your employee is injured on the job. In many African countries, this is required by law if you have staff. To get insurance, contact a broker or go directly to an insurance company. You will need your ID, KRA PIN (Kenya), or equivalent tax number. Compare quotes. Ask about exclusions. For example, some policies do not cover theft from an unlocked car. Read the fine print. Keep your receipts. If you make a claim, report to the police within 24 hours and get a case number. Then notify your insurer. They will send a loss adjuster. Be honest. If you lie, your claim will be denied. Insurance may seem like an extra cost, but one accident could ruin you. A small monthly premium is worth the peace of mind. Talk to other errand professionals about their insurance. Join a SACCO or cooperative that offers group insurance at a discount. For example, in Kenya, Safaricom's M-KOPA offers insurance for informal workers. Always keep your policy documents safe and know your policy number.

Practical Reference: Safety, Security, and Risk Management for Errand and Personal Assistant Professionals

In the African errand and personal assistant (PA) sector, trust is the currency of the business. Clients hand over house keys, vehicle access, bank cards, and confidential documents to someone they may have met only once. A single safety failure â a stolen laptop, a cash shortfall, an accident on a delivery run â can end a promising business overnight. This practical reference module covers the operational controls that protect you, your client, and your enterprise. It is written for working professionals in Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, Johannesburg, Kigali, and beyond, where cash remains king, informal settlements complicate navigation, and formal insurance is often misunderstood.

Treat this lesson as a reference you return to when onboarding a new client, reviewing your pricing for risk, or training a new team member. Every control here has a cost â in time, money, or convenience â and every control has a business justification.

1. Personal Safety When Running Errands

Your body is your primary business asset. In many African cities, errand professionals navigate high-density markets, unlit streets, and unpredictable public transport. Practical controls include: sharing live location with a dispatcher or family member; avoiding fixed cash-carrying routines; wearing closed shoes and carrying minimal visible valuables; and using verified ride-hailing rather than street taxis for late jobs. In Nairobi and Lagos, motorbike (boda/bike) transport is fast but risky â insist on helmets, avoid overloaded bikes, and never accept a rider without a reflective vest at night.

2. Securing Client Homes and Belongings

When you hold keys, you hold liability. Best practice: use a key log with signed handover, never label keys with the client's address, and return keys immediately after each job unless a written retainer covers storage. Photograph the state of a home or vehicle before and after service. For deliveries, use tamper-evident bags and require a recipient signature or photo. In shared compounds common across West and East Africa, confirm the gatekeeper or security guard's identity before entering.

3. Handling Cash and Valuables Safely

Cash errands remain common where card penetration is low. Split large sums across two locations on your person, use a crossbody bag with a zipped internal pocket, and never count money in public. Issue a written receipt for every cash handover and reconcile the same day. For valuables such as jewellery or documents, use a sealed envelope with a serial number and obtain the client's signature at both ends. Consider mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money) for amounts above your comfort threshold, but confirm transaction fees and reversibility with the client beforehand.

4. Emergency Procedures and First Aid Basics

Every errand professional should carry a basic first aid kit and know how to respond to road accidents, burns, and fainting. In Africa, ambulance response can be slow; identify the nearest clinic or hospital on your regular routes. Save emergency numbers: police, fire, and a trusted colleague. For incidents involving a client's property, document with photos and a written statement within 24 hours. Report theft to police and obtain a case number â insurers in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa typically require it.

5. Background Checks and Trust Building

Trust is built through verifiable process. For staff you hire, conduct identity verification, referee checks, and where possible a police clearance certificate. For clients, verify their identity and address before accepting high-value errands. Use a simple service agreement that outlines scope, liability limits, and data handling. In Ghana and Nigeria, the Data Protection Act and NDPR respectively govern how you store client information â keep records encrypted and never share client details on social media.

6. Insurance Coverage for Errand Professionals

Many errand businesses operate uninsured, exposing themselves to catastrophic loss. Relevant cover includes public liability (injury or damage to third parties), goods-in-transit (client items in your care), and personal accident cover for you and your staff. In South Africa, the Compensation Fund covers workplace injuries; in Kenya, WIBA requires employers to insure staff. Premiums vary, but a basic public liability policy can cost less than a single lost client relationship. Always disclose the nature of your work â misrepresentation voids claims.
