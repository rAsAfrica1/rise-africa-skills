1. Identifying Available Biomass: Sawdust, Rice Husks, Maize Cobs, and More

Before you start making briquettes, you need to find raw materials. In Africa, you have many options. Sawdust is common at sawmills. Rice husks come from rice mills. Maize cobs are found after harvest. Other materials include coconut shells, sugarcane bagasse, and even waste paper. Each material burns differently. Sawdust gives high heat but needs binding. Rice husks are abrasive and have high ash, but they are free. Maize cobs must be dried and crushed.

Start by looking around your area. Visit local sawmills. They often pile sawdust and consider it waste. Rice mills produce heaps of husks. During maize harvest, collect cobs from farms. You can also use coffee husks from coffee processing plants. In cities, check markets for vegetable waste, but that is wet and better for compost, not briquettes.

Test each material for moisture and burn quality. Squeeze a handful. If it holds shape, it is too wet. Dry it in the sun. Burn a small sample. Good material lights easily and leaves little ash. Sawdust from hardwood is better than softwood. Rice husks need a binder like clay or cassava starch. Maize cobs must be charred or carbonized first.

Mix materials to improve quality. For example, 70% sawdust and 30% rice husks make a good briquette. You can also add binder: 5-10% cassava starch by weight. Always wear a mask when handling dusty materials. Store samples in labeled bags. Keep a notebook of where you got each material and how it performed. This helps you choose the best sources.

Remember, consistency is key. If you switch materials, your briquette quality changes. So build relationships with suppliers who can give you the same material regularly. Start small: collect 50 kg of one material and make test briquettes. Once you know what works, scale up. Your goal is to find abundant, cheap, and dry biomass close to your workshop.

2. Talking to Sawmills, Farms, and Markets to Get Free or Cheap Materials

Many people give away biomass because they see it as waste. Your job is to ask. Start with sawmills. Go in person. Ask for the manager. Explain that you make briquettes and want their sawdust. Offer to clear it for free. Most sawmills pay to dispose of sawdust, so they will be happy. Bring a sack and a broom. If they agree, set a regular pickup day.

Farms are another source. After harvest, maize cobs and stalks are left in fields. Ask farmers if you can collect them. Offer a small payment or a bag of briquettes in exchange. Rice farmers can give you husks from their milling. Visit during harvest season. Be polite and explain your project. Farmers appreciate help clearing fields.

Markets produce waste like coconut shells, banana peels, and cardboard. Talk to market leaders. Offer to take organic waste away. But be careful: wet waste rots quickly. Only take dry materials like coconut shells or paper. You can also ask restaurants for waste paper and cardboard.

When you talk to suppliers, be clear and consistent. Say: I need 100 kg of sawdust every week. Can you provide it? If they say yes, shake hands and confirm the priceoften free. If they want money, negotiate. Offer to pay a small fee, like 500 shillings per sack, if it saves you transport.

Keep a list of contacts. Note names, phone numbers, and what they supply. Call ahead before you go. Bring a small gift like sugar or tea to build goodwill. Over time, they will trust you and may even deliver. Remember, you are solving their waste problem. So be confident. Start with three suppliers: one sawmill, one farm, one market. That gives you a steady flow of materials.

3. How to Test Moisture Content Without Expensive Equipment

Moisture content is critical. Wet biomass makes poor briquettesthey crumble and smoke. You need materials with less than 15% moisture. You can test without a moisture meter. Use these simple methods.

First, the squeeze test. Take a handful of sawdust or rice husks. Squeeze hard. If it sticks together in a ball and feels damp, it is too wet. If it falls apart easily, it is dry enough. For maize cobs, break one. If it snaps cleanly, it is dry. If it bends, it is wet.

Second, the salt test. Put a teaspoon of salt in a dry bottle. Add a spoonful of your biomass. Shake well. If the salt sticks to the material, moisture is high. If it stays separate, moisture is low. This works because salt absorbs moisture.

Third, the oven method. If you have a kitchen oven, weigh a small sample (say 100g). Dry it at 105C for 2 hours. Weigh again. The weight loss is moisture. For example, if it goes from 100g to 85g, moisture is 15%. You can use a solar dryer instead: put sample in sun for a day and weigh.

Fourth, the plastic bag test. Put material in a clear plastic bag. Seal it and leave in sun for an hour. If water droplets form inside, moisture is high. If not, it is dry.

Always test before buying or collecting. If too wet, spread it on a tarpaulin in the sun for 2-3 days. Turn it daily. For rice husks, you may need 4-5 days. Store dry material under cover. Remember, dry material saves you money on binders and gives stronger briquettes. Teach your suppliers this test so they deliver dry material.

4. Storing Raw Materials to Prevent Rot, Mold, and Pests

Once you have dry biomass, you must store it properly. Damp storage ruins everything. Mold grows in 2-3 days if moisture is above 20%. Pests like rats and insects also attack stored materials. Follow these steps.

First, choose a dry, raised area. A concrete floor is best. If not, use wooden pallets to keep materials off the ground. Cover with a tarpaulin or plastic sheet. Leave space for air to flow. Do not store against wallsleave a gap.

Second, use breathable sacks. Polypropylene sacks (like rice bags) are good. Do not use airtight plastic bags for long-term storagethey trap moisture. Fill sacks only 3/4 full. Tie them loosely. Stack sacks on pallets, not more than 5 high.

Third, keep different materials separate. Sawdust, rice husks, and maize cobs have different moisture levels. Mixing them can cause sweating. Label each sack with the date and material type. Use oldest first.

Fourth, control pests. Sprinkle wood ash or diatomaceous earth around the storage area. This deters insects. For rats, use traps or a cat. Never use poison near biomassit can contaminate your product. Keep the area clean. Remove any spilled material.

Fifth, monitor weekly. Open a sack and feel the material. If it feels damp, spread it in the sun again. If you see mold (white or green patches), discard that sack. Moldy material is unsafe to burnit releases toxins.

Sixth, for large quantities, build a simple shed with a raised floor and a tin roof. Ensure good ventilation. In humid regions, add a solar dryer or a dehumidifier if you can afford it. But for most small producers, sun-drying and proper sacks are enough.

Remember, storage is not just about keeping materialit is about keeping it dry. A well-stored sack of sawdust can last 6 months. A poorly stored one lasts 2 weeks. So invest time in storage. It saves money and keeps your briquettes strong.

5. Transporting Materials on a Small Budget: Bicycles, Motorbikes, and Pickups

Transport can eat your profits. But you have options. For small quantities, use a bicycle. A bicycle can carry 50-100 kg if you balance sacks on the back and front. Use a strong rack. Tie sacks with rope. Go slow on rough roads. This is free if you own a bike, or you can rent one for 2,000 shillings per day.

For medium quantities, use a motorbike (boda boda). A motorbike can carry 200-300 kg. Agree on a price before loading. In many African cities, a boda boda trip of 5 km costs 5,000-10,000 shillings. If you have regular pickups, negotiate a daily rate. For example, 15,000 shillings for two trips.

For large quantities, hire a pickup truck. A small pickup (like a Toyota Hilux) can carry 1,000-1,500 kg. Cost: 50,000-100,000 shillings per trip, depending on distance and fuel. Share the cost with other producers if possible. Or buy your own used pickup for 3-5 million shillingsit pays off if you transport daily.

Always plan your route. Combine pickups from multiple suppliers in one trip. For example, go to the sawmill, then the farm, then the market. This saves fuel. Use sacks, not loose materialloose material blows away and is hard to load.

Negotiate transport prices. Tell the driver you will be a regular customer. Ask for a discount. If you use a bicycle or motorbike, consider buying your own. A used bicycle costs 50,000-100,000 shillings. A used motorbike costs 500,000-1,000,000 shillings. With regular work, it pays back in 6 months.

Safety first. Do not overload. Secure loads with ropes. Wear a helmet on motorbikes. For pickups, cover the load with a tarpaulin to keep it dry. Avoid transporting during rain. If you must, use plastic sheets.

Finally, consider location. If your workshop is near suppliers, you save on transport. So choose a site close to sawmills or farms. Even if rent is higher, transport savings can be bigger.

6. Negotiating Prices and Building Long-Term Supplier Relationships

Negotiation is not about winningit is about creating a fair deal that lasts. Start by knowing the market price. Ask other briquette makers what they pay. For sawdust, many sawmills give it free if you clear it. For rice husks, some mills charge 1,000-2,000 shillings per sack. For maize cobs, farmers may give them free after harvest.

When you meet a supplier, be friendly. Introduce yourself and your business. Explain that you need a steady supply. Ask: What do you do with this waste now? If they pay for disposal, offer to take it for free. If they sell it, ask for a bulk discount. For example, if they ask 2,000 per sack, offer 1,500 for 10 sacks.

Use the good for both approach. Say: I can pick up every week. That saves you storage space. Can you give me a lower price? Or offer a trade: I will give you 5 kg of briquettes for every 100 kg of sawdust. This shows you are partners.

Build trust with small gestures. Bring a soda or a snack. Remember their names. Pay on time if you owe money. Never cheat on quantity. If you promise to pick up on Monday, be there. Over time, they will prioritize you.

Put agreements in writing, even if simple. Write: Supplier X will provide 200 kg of sawdust every Friday at 1,000 shillings per sack. Both sign. This prevents misunderstandings.

When you grow, offer incentives. For example, if a supplier delivers 1,000 kg in a month, give a bonus of 5,000 shillings. Or give them a free briquette stove. This locks in loyalty.

Always have a backup supplier. If one fails, you can turn to another. But do not play them against each otherthat destroys trust.

Finally, think long-term. A good supplier is worth more than a cheap price. If you treat them well, they will warn you about price changes, give you the best material, and even help you find new sources. So invest in relationships. They are your business backbone.

<h2>1. Identifying Available Biomass: Sawdust, Rice Husks, Maize Cobs, and More</h2>
<p>Before you buy a single machine, walk your district and map every biomass source within 30 km. The best feedstock is free or near-free, dry, and already aggregated in one place. In East and West Africa the highest-volume sources are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sawdust</strong> from timber yards and furniture workshops. Often burned or dumped. Best carbonised or pressed directly.</li>
<li><strong>Rice husks</strong> from rice mills in Nigeria, Tanzania, Uganda, Senegal, Mali. High silica (1520% ash), so blend with sawdust or carbonise.</li>
<li><strong>Maize cobs</strong> from maize mills and farms. Excellent density and calorific value when carbonised.</li>
<li><strong>Groundnut shells</strong> Senegal, Nigeria, Malawi. Good binder-friendly feedstock.</li>
<li><strong>Coconut and palm kernel shells</strong> Ghana, Cte d'Ivoire, Tanzania. High energy, good for pellets.</li>
<li><strong>Charcoal dust</strong> from charcoal traders and markets. Cheap binder-rich feedstock.</li>
<li><strong>Bagasse</strong> from sugar mills. Wet; needs drying and often blending.</li>
<li><strong>Water hyacinth and banana waste</strong> high moisture, low density, but useful in blends.</li>
</ul>

<h2>2. Talking to Sawmills, Farms, and Markets to Get Free or Cheap Materials</h2>
<p>Most suppliers see biomass as waste, not product. Your job is to make removal easy and cheap for them. Approach with a clear proposal:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sawmills:</strong> Offer to clear sawdust weekly. Bring your own bags and labour. Many will pay you to remove it, or give it free.</li>
<li><strong>Rice and maize mills:</strong> Mills pay to dump husks and cobs. Offer scheduled collection. Sign a simple 612 month agreement at a fixed low price.</li>
<li><strong>Markets:</strong> Charcoal sellers produce dust daily. Buy at 1020% of charcoal price or exchange for finished briquettes.</li>
<li><strong>Farms:</strong> Cobs and stalks are left after harvest. Pay per tonne at farm gate, or offer to clear fields free.</li>
</ul>
<p>Always ask: <em>How much is produced per week? Where is it dumped? Who else takes it? What would make collection easier for you?</em> Record names, phone numbers, and volumes in a supplier logbook.</p>

<h2>3. How to Test Moisture Content Without Expensive Equipment</h2>
<p>Moisture above 15% causes mold, poor pressing, and crumbling briquettes. You do not need a lab meter. Use these field methods:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hand squeeze test:</strong> Squeeze a handful. If it holds shape and drips water &gt;30%. If it holds shape but no drip 2030%. If it crumbles and springs back &lt;15%. Good for pressing.</li>
<li><strong>Salt shaker test:</strong> Put a spoon of salt in a sealed jar with the sample. If salt clumps after 1 hour, moisture is high.</li>
<li><strong>Oven / solar dry test:</strong> Weigh 100 g sample. Dry in oven at 105C for 23 hours (or solar dryer for a day). Reweigh. Moisture % = (wet weight dry weight) wet weight 100.</li>
<li><strong>Microwave test:</strong> Weigh 100 g, microwave in 2-minute bursts, reweigh until stable. Same formula.</li>
</ul>
<p>Target 1015% moisture for pellets, 812% for briquettes before pressing. Test every new batch and every new supplier.</p>

<h2>4. Storing Raw Materials to Prevent Rot, Mold, and Pests</h2>
<p>Poor storage destroys more biomass than poor pressing. Follow these rules:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Raise off the ground:</strong> Use wooden pallets or stones. Never store directly on soil.</li>
<li><strong>Keep dry:</strong> Use tarpaulins, a ventilated shed, or a simple roof. Cover during rains.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilate:</strong> Leave gaps for airflow. Stack in shallow layers (max 1.5 m high) to avoid heat build-up.</li>
<li><strong>Separate batches:</strong> Keep wet and dry feedstock apart. Never mix moldy material with clean.</li>
<li><strong>Pest control:</strong> Sprinkle wood ash or diatomaceous earth around stacks. Use traps, not poisons, near feedstock.</li>
<li><strong>FIFO:</strong> First In, First Out. Use oldest stock first. Record date and supplier on each pile.</li>
</ul>

<h2>5. Transporting Materials on a Small Budget: Bicycles, Motorbikes, and Pickups</h2>
<p>Transport can double your feedstock cost if unplanned. Match the vehicle to volume and distance:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bicycle / cargo trike:</strong> 50150 kg per trip. Best under 5 km. Cost: near zero. Use for sawdust bags from nearby mills.</li>
<li><strong>Motorbike / boda-boda:</strong> 100300 kg with trailer. 515 km. Cost: USD 28 per trip. Ideal for rice husks and cobs.</li>
<li><strong>Pickup / small truck:</strong> 13 tonnes. 1550 km. Cost: USD 2080 per trip. Use for bulk contracts.</li>
<li><strong>Shared transport:</strong> Combine loads with other traders to split cost. Negotiate return loads to avoid empty trips.</li>
</ul>
<p>Rule of thumb: transport cost should not exceed 15% of your finished briquette selling price. If it does, find a closer source or increase load density.</p>

<h2>6. Negotiating Prices and Building Long-Term Supplier Relationships</h2>
<p>Your goal is not the lowest one-off price it is a reliable supply at a fair, predictable price. Practical tactics:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Start with a free trial:</strong> Offer to clear a supplier's waste for one month free. Prove reliability first.</li>
<li><strong>Sign simple written agreements:</strong> Volume, price, collection schedule, quality standards (moisture, contamination). One page is enough.</li>
<li><strong>Pay on time, every time:</strong> Reputation spreads. Suppliers prioritise reliable buyers.</li>
<li><strong>Offer value beyond cash:</strong> Free briquettes for their home, help with transport, or a small monthly retainer.</li>
<li><strong>Diversify:</strong> Never depend on one supplier. Aim for at least three per feedstock type.</li>
<li><strong>Review quarterly:</strong> Adjust prices with inflation and volume. Keep the relationship long-term.</li>
</ul>

<li>Mapped all biomass sources within 30 km (sawmills, farms, mills, markets).</li>
<li>Tested moisture of every new batch using hand squeeze or oven method (target &lt;15%).</li>
<li>Confirmed feedstock is free of sand, plastic, stones, and mold.</li>
<li>Signed or verbally agreed a collection schedule with at least 3 suppliers.</li>
<li>Storage is raised, dry, ventilated, and pest-protected (FIFO in use).</li>
<li>Transport cost per tonne calculated and kept below 15% of selling price.</li>
<li>Safety gear (N95 mask, gloves, eye protection) available and used.</li>
<li>Supplier logbook updated with names, volumes, prices, and contact details.</li>
</ol>
