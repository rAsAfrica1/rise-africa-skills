1. Sourcing and Preparing African Steel for Knife Blades

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on the single most important raw material decision you will make as a knife blacksmith in Africa: where your steel comes from and how you prepare it before the forge. Your reputation, your edge retention, and your profit margin all begin here. Many beginners waste money buying new flat bar from importers when excellent blade steel is sitting in scrap yards, farms, and workshops within a few kilometres of their smithy. The goal is not to be cheap. The goal is to be smart, consistent, and profitable. Start by understanding the three main sources of steel available across most African markets. First, recycled vehicle leaf springs. These are usually 5160 or equivalent medium carbon spring steel, tough, forgiving, and ideal for machetes, bush knives, and heavy choppers. Second, coil springs from cars and trucks, often similar steel but round in section, good for forging down into blades with a little more work. Third, files and rasps, which are high carbon, sometimes 1095 or W1 equivalent, excellent for small hunting knives, skinners, and carving tools, but more brittle if overheated. You will also find ball bearings, bearing races, and old plough discs. Plough discs are common in agricultural areas and make superb cleavers and kitchen knives because they are already thin and abrasion resistant. Your first business task is to build a supplier network. Visit mechanics, panel beaters, farms, and transport depots. Offer to buy scrap by weight or to trade a finished knife for a steady supply. A simple arrangement works well: one good kitchen knife for twenty kilograms of leaf spring. Keep a notebook with names, phone numbers, steel types, and prices. In many countries mobile money makes payment easy, so budget for airtime and small deposits. Always ask about the history of the steel. A leaf spring that has been in a fire may have lost its temper, but that does not matter because you will re-forge and re-harden it. What matters is that it is not galvanised, painted, or plated. Galvanised steel releases toxic zinc fumes when heated. Never forge galvanised metal. Strip paint and rust outdoors with a wire brush or a weak acid bath, then rinse and dry. Next, learn to identify steel with simple tests. The spark test is free and fast. Touch the steel to a grinding wheel in a dark area. Low carbon steel gives long yellow sparks with few bursts. Medium carbon like leaf spring gives shorter orange sparks with more forks. High carbon like a file gives bright yellow-white sparks with many small explosions near the end. The magnet test helps too. Most knife steels are magnetic, but austenitic stainless is weakly magnetic. The quench test is the most reliable. Forge a small sample, heat it to non-magnetic, quench it in warm oil, then strike it with a hammer. If it snaps cleanly with a fine grain, it will harden. If it bends, it is low carbon and better for fittings or practice. Once you have your steel, prepare it properly. Cut leaf springs into manageable billets with an angle grinder or a hacksaw. Anneal them if possible by heating to red and burying them in dry wood ash or vermiculite overnight. This softens the steel and saves your drill bits and files. Clean the surface to bare metal before forging. Remove any cracks or deep pits. Forge at a bright orange to yellow heat, never at a sparking white heat, because that burns the carbon. In Africa, charcoal is the most common fuel. Build a deep fire with hard wood charcoal, use a clay or metal tuyere, and control your air with a simple hand crank or hair dryer. A good forge fire has a small hot spot, not a huge flame. Keep your anvil or anvil substitute secure. A section of railway line works well. Mount it on a hardwood stump buried in the ground. Your hammer should be around 800 grams to 1.5 kilograms for most work. Heavier hammers cause fatigue and mistakes. Remember that your time is money. A blacksmith who spends three hours hunting for steel earns nothing. A blacksmith with a stack of prepared billets earns every hour. Aim to keep at least twenty kilograms of known steel ready to forge. Store it indoors, oiled, and labelled. Finally, think about the market. In many African towns, the demand is for working knives: kitchen knives for women who cook over charcoal, machetes for farmers, skinners for butchers, and utility knives for artisans. Stainless steel is prized for kitchen knives because it resists rust, but carbon steel holds a better edge and is easier to forge. You can offer both. Start with carbon steel because it is available and cheap. As your skills grow, consider buying new 1084 or 15N20 flat bar from a specialist supplier for consistent results. But never wait for perfect steel. Start with what you have, learn its behaviour, and improve. Your homework is to visit three scrap sources this week, collect at least ten kilograms of steel, run a spark test on each piece, and forge one simple blade from your best billet. Record everything in a notebook. That notebook becomes your business asset. In the next lesson we will cover forge welding and billet preparation for pattern-welded blades, but none of that matters if you cannot source and prepare clean, known steel. Master this first. Your future customers will feel the difference in every cut.

2. Forge Welding and Layered Steel for African Bladesmiths

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 2. Forge welding is the art of joining two pieces of steel at high heat with pressure. It is the foundation of pattern-welded Damascus, but it is also a practical repair and construction skill. In Africa, forge welding lets you turn small scraps into large billets, combine hard and tough steels, and create blades that stand out in a crowded market. This lesson is practical. We will cover fire management, flux, stacking, welding, and the business case for layered steel. First, understand what forge welding requires. You need clean steel, high heat, flux, and pressure. The steel must be at welding temperature, which is a bright yellow-white, just below sparking. At that temperature the surface becomes plastic and, with flux, the oxide layer is dissolved so the two surfaces can bond. If the steel is too cold, it will not stick. If it is too hot, it burns and becomes useless. In a charcoal forge, you control heat by air blast and fuel depth. Use a deep fire with a small, focused hot spot. A hair dryer on low speed works well. Do not use high speed because it blows ash and creates an oxidizing fire. Build your fire with hard charcoal, light it, and wait until the coals are red hot with no flames. Then place your billet in the hot spot and cover it with more charcoal. Watch the colour. In daylight, welding heat looks like a bright yellow that hurts to look at. At night it is almost white. If you see sparks, you are too hot. Pull it out immediately. Flux is essential. Borax is the most common flux. In many African markets you can buy borax as a laundry booster or from a chemical supplier. Mix it with a little water to make a paste, or use it dry. Some smiths add a pinch of salt or fine sand, but borax alone works well. Apply flux as soon as the steel reaches a dull red. It will bubble and glaze the surface. Reapply flux each time you return the billet to the fire. Without flux, scale forms and the weld fails. Now, stacking. For a simple layered billet, start with two pieces of steel: one high carbon like a file, one medium carbon like a leaf spring. Clean both to bare metal. Stack them flat. Wire them together tightly with mild steel wire. Do not use galvanised wire. Heat the stack to a dull red and apply flux. Then bring it to welding heat. When it is ready, take it to the anvil and strike it firmly with a hammer. Start with light taps to set the weld, then heavier blows. Work from the centre outward. Do not hit so hard that you split the layers. If the weld does not take, reheat, re-flux, and try again. Sometimes a failed weld is just a dirty surface. Cut it apart, clean it, and start over. Once you have a solid billet, you can draw it out, fold it, and weld again. Each fold doubles the layers. Three folds give you eight layers. Five folds give you thirty-two. Seven folds give you one hundred and twenty-eight. But more layers is not always better. For a working knife, sixteen to thirty-two layers is plenty. Too many layers can make the steel brittle and the pattern muddy. The pattern comes from the contrast between the steels. If both are similar, you will not see much. Use a high carbon steel that etches dark and a medium carbon or nickel steel that stays bright. In Africa, old files and leaf springs give good contrast. You can also add a thin strip of mild steel for a bright line. After welding, normalize the billet. Heat it to non-magnetic and let it cool in air. Do this three times. This relieves stress and refines the grain. Then you can forge your blade. Remember that forge welding is hot, dirty, and tiring work. Take breaks. Drink water. Wear safety glasses, leather gloves, and a leather apron. Never wear synthetic clothing near the forge. Now, the business case. Pattern-welded knives sell for two to five times the price of a plain carbon knife in many African markets. Tourists, collectors, and gift buyers love the look. But you must be honest. Do not call it Damascus if it is not. Call it layered steel or pattern-welded steel. Explain the process. Show your customers the billet before you forge it. Tell them how many layers. That story adds value. You can also use forge welding to make san-mai blades: a hard core with soft sides. This gives a knife that holds an edge and resists breaking. It is a premium product for hunters and chefs. To make san-mai, stack a thin high carbon core between two thicker pieces of mild or low carbon steel. Weld the edges shut, then forge the billet out into a blade. The core stays hard and the sides stay tough. This is an excellent product for the African bush because it survives rough use. Your homework is to make one three-layer billet from a file and a leaf spring. Weld it, draw it out, and forge a small utility knife. If the weld fails, try again. Keep a record of your fire, flux, and hammer technique. In the next lesson we will cover heat treating and tempering, which is where your blade gets its soul. But without a solid weld, heat treating cannot save a bad billet. So practice until your welds are clean and strong. Your customers will pay for quality. Your reputation depends on it.

3. Heat Treating and Tempering Knives in an African Workshop

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 3. Heat treating is the moment of truth. A blade that is forged perfectly but heat treated badly is useless. A blade that is forged roughly but heat treated well will cut and last. In this lesson we cover normalizing, hardening, tempering, and testing, all with tools and fuels available in Africa. First, understand the goal. You want a hard edge that resists wear and a tough spine that resists breaking. That means you need to harden the steel and then temper it back. Hardening makes the steel brittle. Tempering makes it tough. You cannot skip either step. Before hardening, normalize your blade. Normalizing means heating the steel to non-magnetic, which is around 750 to 800 degrees Celsius for most carbon steels, and then letting it cool in still air. Do this three times. Normalizing refines the grain and relieves stress from forging. If you skip it, your blade may warp or crack during hardening. To check for non-magnetic, use a small magnet on a wire. When the steel stops attracting the magnet, it is at the right temperature. In a charcoal forge, this is a dull red to cherry red in dim light. Do not overheat. If the steel sparkles, it is too hot. Now, hardening. You need to quench the steel fast enough to harden it. The quench medium depends on the steel. For water-hardening steels like 1095 or W1, use warm water. For oil-hardening steels like 5160 or 1084, use warm oil. In Africa, you can use old engine oil, vegetable oil, or even palm oil. Warm oil is better than cold oil because it cools slightly slower and reduces cracking. Heat your oil to around 50 to 60 degrees Celsius. You can test it with a piece of paper. If it sizzles, it is ready. Do not use motor oil that contains additives if you can avoid it, because it produces toxic smoke. Use a deep metal container with a lid. Quench outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. To harden, heat the blade evenly to non-magnetic. Hold it at that temperature for a minute or two to ensure the core is hot. Then plunge it into the quench medium edge first, or point first for a dagger. Move it around gently to break the vapour barrier. Do not swirl it violently. Hold it under the surface until it stops steaming, usually ten to twenty seconds. Then pull it out and check for warping. If it warped, you can straighten it while it is still warm, around 200 degrees Celsius, using a hammer and anvil or a vice. Be careful. It is hot. After quenching, the blade is glass hard. It will snap if you bend it. You must temper it immediately. Tempering means reheating the blade to a lower temperature, usually between 180 and 250 degrees Celsius, and holding it there for one to two hours. This reduces hardness slightly and increases toughness. In an African workshop, you can temper in a kitchen oven, a charcoal fire, or a bed of hot sand. The most reliable method is a kitchen oven with a thermometer. If you do not have one, use a charcoal fire and watch the oxide colours. Clean the blade to bare metal. Heat it slowly. Straw yellow appears around 200 degrees Celsius. Bronze appears around 230. Blue appears around 300. For most knives, you want a straw to bronze colour on the edge and a blue or purple colour on the spine. This gives a hard edge and a tough back. To achieve this, you can use a torch or a hot piece of steel to draw the temper. Heat the spine first, let the colours run down to the edge, then quench the whole blade in water to stop the process. This is called differential tempering. It is a traditional technique that works well. Alternatively, temper the whole blade at 200 degrees for one hour, then let it cool slowly. For a tougher blade, temper at 230 degrees. For a harder blade, temper at 180 degrees. Test your blade after tempering. First, check the edge with a file. A tempered blade should resist the file. If the file bites, the blade is too soft. Second, do a brass rod test. Lightly press the edge against a brass rod. If it chips, it is too hard. If it rolls, it is too soft. Third, cut with it. Cut rope, cardboard, or hide. A good knife will slice cleanly and hold its edge. Now, the business side. Heat treating is where you can differentiate yourself. Many local knives are soft because they are not properly hardened. If you can produce a knife that holds an edge, word will spread. Farmers will pay more for a machete that does not dull after one day. Butchers will pay more for a skinner that keeps cutting. Chefs will pay more for a kitchen knife that slices tomatoes without crushing them. You can also offer a sharpening service and a warranty. Tell your customers to bring the knife back if it chips or rolls. Then you can adjust your heat treatment. Keep records. Write down the steel type, the quench medium, the tempering temperature, and the result. Over time you will develop a recipe for each steel you use. Finally, safety. Quenching oil can catch fire. Keep a lid nearby to smother flames. Do not quench in water if the steel is oil-hardening because it may crack. Wear safety glasses and gloves. Do not temper in a kitchen oven that you use for food unless you clean it thoroughly afterwards. Your homework is to heat treat three blades from the same steel. Temper one at 180, one at 200, and one at 230 degrees. Test each one and record the results. Find the temper that works best for your market. In the next lesson we will cover handle materials and construction, which is where your knife becomes a product that people want to buy.

4. Handles, Sheaths, and Finishing for the African Market

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 4. A blade without a handle is just a piece of steel. A handle makes it a tool. A sheath makes it a product. In this lesson we cover handle materials, construction, finishing, and sheaths, all with an eye on the African market. Your goal is to make a knife that is comfortable, durable, and attractive, using materials that are locally available and affordable. Start with handle materials. In Africa you have access to some of the best handle materials in the world. Hardwoods like ebony, African blackwood, mukwa, mahogany, and wild olive are dense, beautiful, and durable. Bone and horn from cattle, goats, and kudu are traditional and strong. Leather from cowhide is easy to work and widely available. You can also use synthetic materials like micarta, which you can make yourself from cloth and resin, or G10, which you can buy from suppliers. For a working knife, wood and bone are excellent. For a premium knife, consider stabilized wood, which is wood impregnated with resin under vacuum. You can build a simple vacuum chamber with a jar and a hand pump. Stabilized wood resists moisture and cracking. It also takes a high polish. But do not let perfect materials stop you. A simple hardwood handle with a good finish will outsell a fancy handle with a bad fit. The key is fit and finish. The handle must fit the hand. It must be smooth. It must not have sharp corners. It must be sealed against moisture. Now, construction. There are three main handle types: full tang, hidden tang, and stick tang. Full tang means the steel runs the full length of the handle. It is the strongest and easiest to make. Hidden tang means the steel runs into the handle but is covered by wood or bone. It is lighter and allows more shaping. Stick tang means the steel is narrow and goes into a hole drilled in the handle. It is traditional but weaker. For African working knives, full tang is best. It survives hard use. To make a full tang handle, cut your handle material into two scales. Sand the blade tang flat. Roughen it with sandpaper or a file. Mix two-part epoxy. In Africa you can buy epoxy from hardware stores or use a local resin. Apply epoxy to the tang and the scales. Clamp them together with C-clamps or a vice. Use pins to reinforce the handle. Brass rod, copper tube, or mild steel rod works well. Drill holes in the tang and scales, insert the pins, and peen them over. Wait for the epoxy to cure, usually twenty-four hours. Then shape the handle with a file, rasp, and sandpaper. Start with coarse grit and work up to fine. Sand in one direction to avoid scratches. Once the handle is shaped, finish it. For wood, use boiled linseed oil, tung oil, or a mixture of beeswax and oil. Warm the oil and rub it into the wood. Let it dry and repeat. For bone and horn, sand to a high grit and polish with a buffing wheel or a cloth. For a waterproof finish, use polyurethane or epoxy. But remember that shiny finishes can be slippery. A slightly textured finish is better for a working knife. Now, sheaths. A knife without a sheath is dangerous and hard to sell. Leather is the best material. You can buy cowhide from local tanneries or traders. Vegetable-tanned leather is best because it holds shape and can be tooled. To make a simple pouch sheath, cut two pieces of leather slightly larger than the blade. Wet the leather and mould it around the blade. Let it dry. Then sew the edges with a saddle stitch. Use waxed thread. Punch holes with an awl. For a stronger sheath, add a welt, which is a strip of leather between the layers at the edge. This protects the stitching from the blade. Add a belt loop and a retaining strap. You can also make sheaths from kydex, which is a thermoplastic. Heat it in an oven or with a heat gun and press it around the blade. Kydex is waterproof and durable, but it is not traditional. For the African market, leather is preferred. It looks good, it is quiet, and it can be repaired. You can also decorate leather with stamping, carving, or beadwork. Beadwork is a strong cultural element in many African communities. A beaded sheath can command a higher price. Now, finishing the blade. After heat treating, the blade will have scale and oxide. Clean it with sandpaper or a belt grinder. Start with 120 grit and work up to 400 or 600 for a working finish. For a polished finish, go up to 1000 or 2000. You can also etch the blade in acid to bring out the pattern if it is layered steel. Use ferric chloride or a mixture of vinegar and salt. Be careful with acid. Wear gloves and goggles. Neutralize with baking soda and water. Then oil the blade to prevent rust. For carbon steel, use a thin coat of mineral oil, camellia oil, or even cooking oil. For a long-lasting finish, you can blue or parkerize the blade, but that requires chemicals. A simple oiled finish is fine. Now, the business. Your handle and sheath are what customers see first. They will pick up a knife and feel the handle. If it is rough or uncomfortable, they will put it down. If it is smooth and balanced, they will want it. Take time to finish. Do not rush. A good finish can add fifty percent to your price. Also, think about packaging. A simple cardboard box with your name and a care card adds value. A leather sheath with your stamp adds value. A story about the steel and the maker adds value. In Africa, trust is everything. If you sell a knife that works, people will come back. If you sell a knife that fails, they will tell everyone. So make every knife as if your reputation depends on it, because it does. Your homework is to make one complete knife from start to finish: forge, heat treat, handle, sheath, and sharpen. Then give it to a friend or family member to use for one week. Ask for honest feedback. In the next lesson we will cover sharpening, maintenance, and customer care, which is how you keep customers for life.

5. Sharpening, Maintenance, and Building a Knife Business in Africa

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 5. You have forged, heat treated, handled, and sheathed your knife. Now you need to sharpen it, maintain it, and sell it. This lesson is about the business of blacksmithing. It covers sharpening, maintenance, pricing, marketing, and customer care, all tailored to the African market. First, sharpening. A dull knife is a dangerous knife because you have to force it. A sharp knife cuts cleanly and safely. There are many sharpening systems, but the simplest and most effective is a set of water stones or oil stones. In Africa you can find natural stones in riverbeds or buy synthetic stones from markets. A combination stone with coarse on one side and fine on the other is ideal. You can also use sandpaper on a flat surface like glass or tile. For a working edge, start with 400 grit, then 1000, then 3000 if you have it. For a polished edge, go up to 6000 or use a leather strop with polishing compound. To sharpen, maintain a consistent angle. For most knives, 15 to 20 degrees per side is good. Use a marker to colour the edge bevel. Then sharpen until the marker is gone. That tells you that you are hitting the edge. Raise a burr on one side, then the other. Then remove the burr with light passes. Finish with a strop. Do not use a grinder for sharpening unless you are very skilled, because it can overheat the edge and ruin the temper. If you must use a grinder, keep a cup of water nearby and dip the blade often. Now, maintenance. Teach your customers how to care for their knives. Carbon steel rusts. Tell them to dry the blade after use and oil it lightly. Do not put it in a dishwasher. Do not leave it in a wet sheath. Sharpen it regularly with a honing rod or a stone. If the handle is wood, oil it occasionally. If the sheath is leather, treat it with wax or oil. Provide a small care card with every knife. This shows professionalism and reduces complaints. Now, pricing. This is where many blacksmiths fail. They charge too little because they are afraid. But if you charge too little, you cannot buy better materials, you cannot invest in tools, and you cannot support your family. Price your work based on three things: materials, time, and profit. Materials include steel, handle material, sheath leather, epoxy, sandpaper, and fuel. Time includes forging, heat treating, finishing, and sharpening. Profit is what you pay yourself and reinvest. A simple working knife might take four hours to make. If you want to earn the equivalent of ten dollars per hour, that is forty dollars. Materials might be five dollars. So your price is forty-five dollars. But you must also consider what the market will pay. In many African markets, a good working knife sells for ten to thirty dollars. A premium knife sells for fifty to two hundred dollars. You need to find your niche. If you make a knife that is better than the imported competition, you can charge more. If you make a cheap knife, you will always be poor. So focus on quality. Now, marketing. You do not need a big budget. You need a story and a network. Tell people what you do. Carry a knife with you. Show it to farmers, butchers, chefs, and shopkeepers. Let them hold it. Let them cut with it. Word of mouth is the strongest marketing in Africa. You can also use social media. Take photos of your work. Post them on Facebook, Instagram, or WhatsApp. Join local business groups. Attend markets and trade fairs. Partner with leather workers, bead workers, and wood carvers. You can also approach tourist lodges, safari companies, and gift shops. They often look for authentic local products. Offer them a wholesale price. Make sure your knives are clearly marked with your name or logo. A simple stamp or etching is enough. Now, customer care. When a customer buys a knife, give them your phone number. Tell them to call if there is a problem. If a knife chips or breaks, repair or replace it. This builds trust. It also gives you feedback. If a knife fails, you learn what went wrong. Maybe the heat treatment was off. Maybe the steel was bad. Maybe the handle was too thin. Fix it and improve. Keep a record of every knife you sell. Write down the date, the steel, the handle material, the price, and the customer name. This helps you track your business and follow up. Now, growth. As your skills improve, you can hire an apprentice. Train them in the same methods. Pay them fairly. You can also diversify. Make kitchen knives, hunting knives, machetes, sickles, hoes, and tools. Make sheaths, belts, and bags. Offer sharpening services. Teach workshops. The more you diversify, the more resilient your business becomes. But do not lose focus on quality. A blacksmith who makes ten bad knives will fail. A blacksmith who makes one excellent knife will succeed. Finally, think about your legacy. In many African cultures, blacksmithing is a respected trade. You are not just making knives. You are making tools that feed families, build homes, and protect people. You are preserving a skill that has been passed down for generations. Take pride in that. Your homework is to sharpen five knives to a shaving edge, write a care card, and sell one knife to a stranger. Record the price, the customer feedback, and what you learned. Then set a goal for the next month: how many knives will you make, how much will you earn, and how will you improve? Write it down. Share it with a friend. Then get back to the forge. Your future is in your hands and in your hammer.

<h3>Why Steel Identification Is a Business Skill, Not Just a Craft Skill</h3>

<p>Every hour you spend forging an unknown steel into a blade that fails at the quench is an hour you cannot bill. In African markets where finished knives sell from KES 800 to KES 15,000 (approximately USD 6115), your margin depends on three variables: the cost of your input steel, the yield of usable blades per kilogram, and your rejection rate. A bladesmith who can reliably identify medium-carbon steel from low-carbon scrap will cut rejection rates dramatically and can charge premium prices for consistent quality.</p>

<p>The most common blade-worthy scrap in African workshops comes from vehicle leaf springs (typically 5160 or equivalent medium-carbon spring steel), railway clips, worn hand files (often 1.01.2% carbon), and agricultural implement tines. Galvanised sheet, stainless kitchenware, and low-carbon construction rod are frequent traps they look like steel, feel like steel, but will not hold an edge.</p>

<h4 style="margin-top:0; color:#7a1f10; font-family:Arial;"> SAFETY WARNING Galvanised and Coated Steel</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom:0; font-family:Arial; font-size:14px; color:#3b1a12;">Never heat, cut, weld, or forge galvanised steel, chrome-plated parts, or painted scrap indoors or without forced ventilation. Heating zinc coatings releases zinc oxide fumes that cause <strong>metal fume fever</strong> a flu-like illness with fever, chills, and chest tightness that can require medical attention. WHO and CDC occupational health guidance classifies zinc oxide fume exposure as a serious respiratory hazard. If you suspect galvanising, test with a drop of muriatic acid (bubbling = zinc present) outdoors, and if confirmed, either reject the piece or strip the coating mechanically outdoors with a grinder and full PPE before any thermal work. Symptoms appearing 412 hours after exposure require immediate medical assessment.</p>
</div>

<h3>The Spark Test: Your Fastest Field Tool</h3>

<p>The spark test is the single most valuable free skill in a scrap-based African bladesmith shop. Grind a sample against a bench grinder or angle grinder in a darkened area and read the spark stream:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Low carbon (rebar, mild steel):</strong> long, straight, straw-yellow sparks with few forks. Will not harden.</li>
<li><strong>Medium carbon (leaf spring, tines):</strong> orange sparks with medium feathery bursts and moderate forking. Excellent blade stock.</li>
<li><strong>High carbon (files, bearings):</strong> short, dense, bright yellow-white sparks with many fine forks close to the wheel. Best for edge-holding blades, but needs careful normalising.</li>
<li><strong>Cast iron:</strong> dull red, short sparks that break into small dots. Unforgeable reject.</li>
</ul>

<p>Pair the spark test with a magnet test (all carbon steels are magnetic) and a fracture test on a thin offcut: medium and high carbon steel shows a fine, silvery-grey grain; low carbon shows a coarse, fibrous grey fracture. Record every batch in a simple notebook source, date, spark signature, quench result. Within six months you will have a personal steel database worth more than any textbook.</p>

<h3>Normalising: The Step Most African Bladesmiths Skip</h3>

<p>Salvaged steel has a chaotic internal structure from years of service stress, bending, and unknown heat history. Before you shape a blade, normalise the stock: heat to non-magnetic (roughly 760800C, judged by colour cherry red in dim light) and allow to cool in still air. Repeat three times. This refines grain, relieves stress, and dramatically reduces the risk of cracking during quench. For files and other very high-carbon scrap, this step is non-negotiable.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist Steel Intake and Shop Safety</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Identify before you buy:</strong> spark test, magnet test, and fracture test every new batch. Reject rebar, galvanised sheet, and unknown painted metal.</li>
<li><strong>Record every batch:</strong> source, date, cost per kg, spark signature, and quench result in a shop notebook.</li>
<li><strong>Normalise 3 before shaping</strong> any salvaged steel, especially files and springs.</li>
<li><strong>Test quench on an offcut</strong> from every new batch before committing a full blade.</li>
<li><strong>Never heat galvanised or chrome-plated steel</strong> without outdoor stripping and full respiratory PPE.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilate your forge:</strong> work outdoors or with cross-ventilation; zinc and paint fumes are the top workshop health hazard.</li>
<li><strong>Wear eye and hearing protection</strong> during all grinding spark testing and shaping produce high-velocity fragments.</li>
<li><strong>Track your yield:</strong> calculate blades produced per kg and your rejection rate monthly. If rejection exceeds 15%, revisit your steel sourcing.</li>
</ol>
