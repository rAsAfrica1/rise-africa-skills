Lessons

1. L1

Welcome to the first lesson of converting a car engine into an irrigation water pump. Before you touch any tools, you need to understand what you're doing and why safety comes first. A car engine is a powerful machine that can run for hours, but when you convert it to run a water pump, you're adding new risks like water, electricity, and moving belts. In Africa, many farmers use this conversion to irrigate crops during dry seasons. For example, a small 1.5-liter engine from a Toyota Corolla can pump up to 500 liters per minute if set up correctly. But without proper safety, you could lose a finger, suffer burns, or even die from electrocution.

Start by wearing the right gear. You need safety goggles to protect your eyes from flying metal chips, heavy leather gloves to handle hot parts, and steel-toe boots to protect your feet from falling engine parts. Never wear loose clothing or jewelry that can get caught in belts or pulleys. Work in a well-ventilated area because engine exhaust contains carbon monoxide, a deadly gas you cannot see or smell. If you're working indoors, open all doors and windows and use a fan to blow fumes away. Always have a fire extinguisher nearbydry powder type is best for fuel and electrical fires. Keep a bucket of sand or water for emergencies, but never use water on an electrical fire.

Before starting any work, disconnect the battery. Remove the negative terminal first, then the positive. This prevents accidental sparks that could ignite fuel vapors. If the engine has a radiator, let it cool completely before opening the caphot coolant can spray out and cause severe burns. Drain all fuel from the tank and lines into a safe container. Petrol is highly flammable; store it away from your work area. Use a fuel siphon pump, not your mouth, to avoid poisoning.

Lift the engine using a proper engine hoist or a sturdy chain block rated for at least 500 kg. Never use makeshift ropes or weak straps. Place the engine on a solid workbench or concrete floor. If you're working on the ground, put down wooden planks to prevent the engine from sinking into mud. Keep children and animals away from the work area. In rural areas, it's common for kids to be curious, so set up a fence or barrier.

Finally, plan your conversion. You'll need a water pump that matches the engine's power. A typical car engine produces 50-100 horsepower. For irrigation, you need a centrifugal pump with a flow rate of 200-1000 liters per minute depending on your field size. Write down all steps and gather tools: wrenches, screwdrivers, a hacksaw, a welding machine, and pipe fittings. In the next lessons, we'll go step by step. Remember, safety is not optionalit's the difference between a successful project and a disaster.

2. L2

Now that you understand general safety, let's focus on personal protective equipment (PPE) and safe work practices specific to engine conversion. In Africa, many workshops lack proper PPE, but you can improvise without compromising safety. For eye protection, use clear safety glasses or a face shield when grinding or cutting metal. If you can't afford new ones, visit a local hardware store and ask for used but intact goggles. For hand protection, leather gloves are best for handling hot engine parts and sharp edges. Avoid rubber gloves near moving partsthey can get caught. For your feet, wear closed leather boots with steel toes if possible. If not, thick-soled work boots will do. Never wear sandals or flip-flops.

Hearing protection is often overlooked. An engine running at 2000 RPM produces about 90 decibels of noise, which can damage your hearing over time. Use earplugs or earmuffs. You can buy foam earplugs cheaply at a pharmacy. If you don't have them, cotton wool works as a temporary measure but is not as effective. Respiratory protection is also key. When welding or grinding, you create fumes and dust. Wear a dust mask or a respirator with P2 filters. For painting or using solvents, use a respirator with organic vapor cartridges.

Safe work practices include keeping your work area clean and organized. Tripping over tools or hoses causes many accidents. Put tools back on a rack or in a toolbox after use. Wipe up oil spills immediately with sawdust or sandoil is slippery and can cause falls. Never work alone if possible. Have a buddy who can help in an emergency and knows where the first aid kit is. Your first aid kit should include bandages, antiseptic cream, burn gel, and eye wash. Check it monthly and replace expired items.

When working with electricity, such as wiring the pump motor or using power tools, ensure all circuits are grounded and protected by circuit breakers. In many African homes, wiring is not up to code. Use a residual current device (RCD) plug adapter for any power tool. Never touch electrical equipment with wet hands or while standing in water. If you're using a generator, place it outside and away from windows to avoid carbon monoxide buildup.

Finally, learn to lift safely. Engine parts are heavya cylinder head can weigh 20 kg. Bend your knees, keep your back straight, and lift with your legs. If something is too heavy, get help or use a hoist. Don't twist your body while lifting. Practice these habits until they become second nature. In the next lesson, we'll cover tool safety and maintenance.

3. L3

Tools are your best friends, but they can also be dangerous if used incorrectly. In this lesson, you'll learn how to select, use, and maintain tools safely for engine conversion. Start with hand tools: wrenches, sockets, screwdrivers, pliers, and hammers. Always use the correct size wrench for a bolt. A loose wrench can slip and skin your knuckles. For stubborn bolts, use a penetrating oil like WD-40 and let it soak for 10 minutes. Never use a cheater bar (a pipe over the wrench) unless it's designed for ityou can break the wrench or round the bolt. When using a screwdriver, match the tip to the screw head. A Phillips screwdriver for a Phillips screw, flat for flat. Don't use a screwdriver as a chisel or pry bar.

Power tools save time but require extra caution. An angle grinder is common for cutting metal. Always wear a face shield and gloves. Check the disc for cracks before use. Never remove the guard. Hold the grinder with both hands and let the tool reach full speed before touching the metal. Don't force itlet the disc do the work. When cutting, position yourself so sparks fly away from you and not toward flammable materials. A bench grinder is useful for sharpening tools, but keep the tool rest close to the wheel (within 2 mm) to prevent the work from being pulled in. Wear goggles.

A welder is essential for fabricating brackets and mounts. Arc welding (stick) is common in Africa because it's affordable. Use a welding helmet with a dark lens (shade 10-13) to protect your eyes from arc flash. Wear leather gloves, a leather apron, and closed shoes. Work in a dry areanever weld in the rain or on wet ground. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. After welding, let the metal cool before touching it. Chipping slag can fly, so wear goggles.

A drill press is safer than a hand drill for precise holes. Clamp your work securely. For hand drills, use sharp bits and let the drill do the work. Don't force it. When drilling metal, use a cutting fluid or oil to keep the bit cool. A hacksaw is for cutting pipes and bolts. Use a blade with the right teeth per inch (TPI): 18 TPI for thick metal, 24 TPI for thin. Apply light pressure and let the saw cut. Replace dull blades.

Maintain your tools. Clean them after use, oil moving parts, and store them in a dry place. Rusty tools are unsafe. Sharpen chisels and drill bits. Check power cords for cuts or exposed wiresreplace if damaged. Never use a tool with a cracked handle. In the next lesson, we'll focus on fuel and fire safety.

4. L4

Fuel is the lifeblood of your engine, but it's also the most dangerous element in your workshop. Petrol (gasoline) and diesel are both flammable, but petrol is far more volatile. Its vapors can ignite from a spark, a hot surface, or even static electricity. In Africa, many people store fuel in jerrycans in their homesthis is extremely risky. Always store fuel in approved containers with tight caps, away from living areas and ignition sources. Keep only small amounts (less than 20 liters) on hand. Use a funnel when pouring to avoid spills. Never smoke or use a phone near fuel.

Before you start working on the fuel system, disconnect the battery and drain the fuel tank into a safe container. Use a hand-operated siphon pump. Do not use your mouth to start a siphonpetrol is toxic and can cause poisoning. If you spill fuel on your skin, wash it off immediately with soap and water. If you spill it on the ground, cover it with sand or sawdust and dispose of it properly. Never wash fuel down a drain or into a riverit pollutes water and kills fish.

When working on the carburetor or fuel lines, keep a fire extinguisher within reach. A dry powder extinguisher is best for fuel fires. If a fire starts, don't panic. Turn off the fuel source if safe to do so. Aim the extinguisher at the base of the fire, not the flames. Sweep side to side. If the fire is too big, evacuate and call for help. Never throw water on a petrol fireit will spread the flames.

Welding near fuel tanks is extremely dangerous. Even an empty tank can contain explosive vapors. Before welding on a tank, it must be thoroughly cleaned and purged with water or inert gas. Better yet, remove the tank entirely. If you must weld near fuel lines, disconnect and cap them, and move them away. Use a fire blanket to protect nearby flammable materials.

Static electricity can ignite fuel vapors. When filling containers, touch the nozzle to the container to equalize charge. Avoid sliding containers across plastic surfaces. Wear cotton clothing, not synthetic, to reduce static buildup. Ground the engine block when working on fuel systems. In dry climates, static is a real hazard.

Finally, know the signs of fuel poisoning: dizziness, headache, nausea, confusion. If you inhale too many vapors, move to fresh air immediately. If symptoms persist, seek medical help. In the next lesson, we'll cover mechanical hazards like rotating parts and heavy loads.

5. L5

An engine has many moving parts that can crush, cut, or amputate. In this lesson, you'll learn how to avoid mechanical hazards. The most dangerous parts are the fan, belts, pulleys, and the flywheel. Never put your hands near these while the engine is running. Before starting any engine, ensure all guards are in place. If guards are missing, fabricate them from sheet metal or wire mesh. A simple fan guard can be made from a 1-inch wire mesh bent around the fan. Secure it with bolts.

When the engine is running, keep loose clothing, long hair, and jewelry away. Tie back long hair and wear fitted clothing. Never reach over a running engine. If you need to adjust something, shut off the engine and wait for all parts to stop moving. The flywheel can continue spinning for a few seconds after shutdown. Use a stick or tool to check if it's stoppednever your hand.

Belts and pulleys transmit power to the pump. Check belt tension regularly. A loose belt can slip and burn, while a tight belt can damage bearings. Use a belt tension gauge or press the belt with your thumbit should deflect about 10-15 mm. Replace cracked or glazed belts. When installing a belt, never force it over a pulley with a screwdriveryou can damage the belt or injure yourself. Use a belt tensioner tool.

Heavy components like the engine block, cylinder head, and pump can weigh over 100 kg. Use proper lifting techniques and equipment. A chain hoist rated for 500 kg is ideal. Never lift alone if the object is heavier than 20 kg. Use a trolley or wheelbarrow to move heavy parts. Keep your feet clear when lowering loads. Wear steel-toe boots.

Hydraulic systems, if you use a hydraulic pump, operate at high pressure. A pinhole leak can inject fluid into your skin, causing serious injury. Never use your hand to check for leaksuse a piece of cardboard. If injured, seek medical help immediately. Depressurize the system before working on it.

Rotating shafts and couplings must be guarded. A simple guard can be made from a metal box with a hinged lid. Ensure the guard is secure and cannot be removed without tools. Lubricate moving parts regularly to reduce wear and noise.

Finally, practice lockout/tagout. Before maintenance, disconnect the battery, remove the key, and attach a tag that says "Do not start." Keep the key in your pocket. This prevents someone from accidentally starting the engine while you're working. In the next lesson, we'll cover emergency procedures and first aid.

6. L6

Even with all precautions, accidents can happen. This lesson teaches you how to respond to emergencies and provide basic first aid. The first rule is: don't become a victim yourself. If someone is electrocuted, don't touch them until the power is off. If they're trapped in machinery, turn off the engine before helping. Call for helpin many African villages, you may not have an ambulance, so have a plan. Know the nearest clinic or hospital and keep a phone charged. Save emergency numbers.

For cuts and bleeding, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth or bandage. Elevate the wound if possible. If bleeding is severe, use a tourniquet only as a last resortit can cause tissue damage. For burns, cool the area with clean running water for at least 10 minutes. Do not apply butter, oil, or toothpaste. Cover with a sterile dressing. For chemical burns from battery acid, flush with plenty of water and seek medical help. For eye injuries, flush with water for 15 minutes and cover with a clean pad.

For fractures, immobilize the injured area with a splint. Use a board or stick and wrap with cloth. Don't try to straighten a broken bone. For head injuries, keep the person still and monitor for confusion, vomiting, or unequal pupils. For heat exhaustion, move the person to a cool place, give water, and loosen clothing. For heat stroke (hot dry skin, high temperature), cool rapidly with wet cloths and fanning, and get medical help.

Fire emergencies: know your extinguisher. For electrical fires, use CO2 or dry powder. For fuel fires, use dry powder, foam, or sand. Never use water on fuel or electrical fires. If fire is small, aim at the base. If it's large, evacuate and call for help. Have a fire drill planpractice escaping from your workshop. Keep exits clear.

Spills: for fuel spills, turn off all ignition sources, ventilate, and absorb with sand or sawdust. For oil spills, use absorbent pads or cat litter. For battery acid spills, neutralize with baking soda and rinse with water. Dispose of all waste properlydon't dump into rivers.

Finally, keep a well-stocked first aid kit. Include: assorted bandages, sterile gauze, adhesive tape, antiseptic wipes, antibiotic ointment, burn gel, eye wash, scissors, tweezers, gloves, CPR mask, and a first aid manual. Check it monthly. Take a first aid course if possiblemany Red Cross societies in Africa offer free training. Remember, quick and correct action can save a life. In the next module, we'll start the actual conversion process.

<h2>Practical Reference: Engine-to-Pump Conversion Field Data, Specs & Safety Benchmarks</h2>

<p>
This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the technical figures, tolerances, and safety benchmarks you will rely on when converting a small petrol or diesel car engine into a stationary irrigation pump. It is written for the African smallholder and emerging commercial farming context, where the engine is usually sourced from a written-off saloon car (Toyota Corolla 4A-FE, Nissan Sunny, VW Polo, Peugeot 504, etc.), a motorcycle, or a light truck, and where the pump must run reliably on borehole, river, or canal water with minimal grid dependence. Every figure here is intended to help you quote a job, buy the right parts at the local market, and avoid the two most common causes of failure: cavitation and overheating. Treat this module as your bench book keep it beside the engine stand.
</p>

<p>
<strong>Business framing:</strong> a converted engine pump that delivers 3060 m/hour at 2030 m head can irrigate 12 hectares of horticulture per day and is typically hired out at KES 1,5003,000 (or USD 1225) per day in East Africa, or sold as a package at USD 4001,200 depending on engine condition. Your margin lives in correct matching of impeller, pulley ratio, and suction lift. Get these wrong and you burn fuel, wear the engine, and lose the client.
</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:12px 16px; margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING CARBON MONOXIDE &amp; MECHANICAL HAZARDS</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">
Never run a converted car engine in an enclosed, unventilated pump house, shed, or grain store. Petrol and diesel engines produce <strong>carbon monoxide (CO)</strong>, an odourless gas that kills within minutes. In African farm settings, pump houses are often built from mud, timber, or corrugated iron with no cross-ventilation this is a lethal combination. Position the engine outdoors, at least 3 m from any wall or opening, with the exhaust piped away from workers and livestock. Additionally: (1) never touch the belt or pulley while the engine is running a 100 mm pulley at 2,000 rpm will amputate fingers; (2) fit a guard over all belts and couplings; (3) secure the engine to a concrete plinth of at least 100 kg to prevent walk-off; (4) disconnect the spark plug lead before any maintenance; (5) never prime a hot pump with cold water thermal shock cracks castings. Follow the donor vehicle's OEM service manual for all torque and fluid specifications.
</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist (Field Card)</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Match power to duty:</strong> confirm engine kW pump shaft power required at your design flow and head (add 20 % margin for belt losses).</li>
<li><strong>Suction lift 7 m</strong> (at sea level; reduce by 0.1 m per 100 m of altitude). Keep the pump as close to the water as possible.</li>
<li><strong>Pipe sizing:</strong> suction pipe one size larger than discharge; keep suction line short, straight, and free of high points.</li>
<li><strong>Foot valve &amp; strainer:</strong> fit a cleanable strainer with at least 2 the pipe area; inspect weekly in sandy or silty water.</li>
<li><strong>Alignment &amp; tension:</strong> pulleys aligned within 0.5 mm; belt deflection 1015 mm at mid-span under thumb pressure.</li>
<li><strong>Cooling &amp; lubrication:</strong> retain the radiator and fan; check oil and coolant daily; never run without a functional cooling system.</li>
<li><strong>Guarding:</strong> belt, pulley, and coupling guards fitted before first start; no loose clothing, no open sandals.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilation:</strong> engine outdoors or in a cross-ventilated shelter; exhaust piped away from people and animals.</li>
<li><strong>Logbook:</strong> record hours, fuel, flow, and repairs this data is your pricing and maintenance tool.</li>
</ol>
