Lessons

1. Pond Preparation and Fertilization

Before you stock any fry, you must prepare the pond to produce natural food. Start by draining the pond completely and removing any debris, sticks, or dead leaves. If the pond bottom is muddy, scrape off the top 23 cm of silt and spread it on the banks to dry. This silt can carry diseases and predators. After draining, apply agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) at 200300 kg per hectare. Lime raises the pH and kills harmful organisms. Spread it evenly over the dry bottom and the dykes. Let the pond dry for 35 days until the soil cracks slightly. This cracks expose pests and disease agents to sunlight.

Next, fill the pond with clean water from a well or stream that does not contain wild fish. Use a fine mesh screen (1 mm) over the inlet pipe to keep out eggs and small fish. Fill to a depth of 6080 cm for nursery ponds. After filling, you must fertilize the water to grow phytoplankton and zooplankton the natural food for fry. Use fresh animal manure (chicken, cow, or pig) at 5001000 kg per hectare. Put the manure in sacks and hang them at several points in the pond, or spread it evenly. Alternatively, use compost made from grass and kitchen waste. For faster results, add 2030 kg per hectare of urea and 1015 kg per hectare of triple superphosphate. These chemical fertilizers dissolve quickly and cause a green algae bloom within 35 days.

Check the water colour after 3 days. A healthy pond looks light green to brownish-green. If it is clear, add more fertilizer. If it turns dark green or blue-green, too much fertilizer was used stop and add fresh water. You should see tiny animals (zooplankton) swimming in the water by day 5. Test by dipping a white plate into the pond; if you see small specks moving, the pond is ready. Stock fry only after the zooplankton population peaks, usually 57 days after fertilizing. Never stock fry into a pond that has not been fertilized they will starve. Also, ensure the pond has no leaks and the water temperature is between 2530C, which is ideal for tilapia and catfish fry.

2. Stocking Density and Acclimation of Fry

Stocking density determines how fast your fry grow and how many survive. For tilapia, stock 2030 fry per square metre in a nursery pond. That means a 100 square metre pond holds 2,0003,000 fry. For catfish, stock 50100 fry per square metre because they are more tolerant of crowding. If you stock too many, the water will foul quickly and growth will be slow. If you stock too few, you waste pond space. Always count your fry before stocking. Use a small cup or net to scoop them from the holding tank and count a sample (e.g., 100 fry), then estimate the total by volume. Do not guess accurate numbers prevent overstocking.

Acclimation is critical. Fry are sensitive to sudden changes in water temperature and pH. If you dump them straight into the pond, they may die from shock. First, float the plastic bag or container holding the fry on the pond surface for 1520 minutes. This lets the water temperature inside the bag slowly match the pond temperature. Next, every 5 minutes, add a small cup of pond water to the bag. Do this for 34 times. This gradually adjusts the fry to the ponds pH and hardness. After 20 minutes, gently release the fry into the pond at several points, not all in one spot. Use a soft net or your hand to scoop them out never pour them from a height.

Stock early in the morning or late evening when the water is cool. Avoid stocking during the hottest part of the day. Also, do not feed the fry for 24 hours before stocking this reduces stress and waste. After stocking, monitor the pond for any dead fry floating. If you see many dead fry within 24 hours, the acclimation was too fast or the water quality is poor. In that case, test the pH (should be 6.58.5) and temperature. For African catfish, you can stock at higher densities, but you must provide hiding places like PVC pipes or plastic crates to reduce cannibalism. Always keep a record of the number of fry stocked and the date. This helps you calculate survival rate later.

3. Natural Food Production and Supplemental Feeding

Fry need tiny food particles they can swallow. Natural food phytoplankton (microscopic plants) and zooplankton (tiny animals) is the best first food. To keep natural food growing, you must maintain a green water colour. Add manure or compost every week at 100200 kg per hectare. If the water becomes clear, fertilize again. You can also add agricultural by-products like rice bran or maize bran at 2030 kg per hectare to stimulate plankton. However, do not over-fertilize, or the water will become toxic. A simple test: put your hand 20 cm deep into the pond. If you cannot see your fingers, the water is too thick stop fertilizing and add fresh water.

After 710 days, the fry will need supplemental feed because natural food alone cannot support high densities. Start with finely ground feed. For tilapia fry, use a powder made from fish meal, soybean meal, and maize flour. A simple recipe: mix 40% fish meal, 30% soybean meal, 20% maize flour, and 10% vitamin premix. Grind it into a fine powder (0.20.5 mm). For catfish fry, use 50% fish meal, 30% soybean meal, and 20% blood meal. Feed the fry 45 times a day, broadcasting the powder evenly over the pond surface. Use 1020 grams per 1,000 fry per day. Increase gradually as they grow.

You can also make a simple feed from locally available materials. Dry and grind termites, ants, or small insects. Mix with cassava flour or rice bran. Another option is to boil and mash chicken eggs the yolk is excellent for very young fry. For the first two weeks, feed only what the fry can eat in 10 minutes. Remove any uneaten feed to avoid fouling the water. After two weeks, switch to a slightly larger pellet (0.51 mm). You can buy commercial starter feed if available, but it is expensive. Many African farmers use a mixture of rice bran, fish meal, and brewery waste. Always store feed in a dry, cool place to prevent mould. Mouldy feed kills fry. Monitor growth weekly if fry are not growing, increase feed or check water quality.

4. Predator and Competitor Control in Ponds

Predators and competitors can wipe out your fry overnight. Common predators include frogs, snakes, birds, dragonfly larvae, water beetles, and wild fish. Competitors like tadpoles and small wild fish eat the same food as your fry, slowing their growth. The first step is to screen all water inlets and outlets with fine mesh (1 mm) to keep out eggs and small fish. Before filling the pond, dry it completely and apply lime to kill any remaining pests. After filling, inspect the pond daily for frogs and their eggs. Frogs lay eggs in jelly-like clusters remove them immediately. Dragonfly larvae (also called water tigers) are fierce predators. They hide in weeds and debris. Keep the pond edges clean and remove excess vegetation. You can also add a few tilapia adults (not fry) to eat dragonfly larvae, but be careful they may eat your fry too.

Birds like herons and kingfishers can eat many fry. Cover the pond with a net or thin wire mesh if birds are a problem. For snakes, clear tall grass around the pond and set simple traps. In Africa, many farmers use a fence made of palm fronds or mosquito netting around the pond. If you see water beetles or backswimmers, net them out manually. You can also use a light trap at night hang a lantern over a bucket of water with a little kerosene. Insects fall in and die. For tadpoles, remove them with a fine net. Do not use chemicals to kill predators because they will also kill your fry.

Competitors like wild tilapia or mosquito fish can enter through the water supply. Always filter incoming water. If you find wild fish in the pond, you must drain and restart. Prevention is better than cure. Also, keep the pond free of excess mud and weeds where predators hide. Check the pond every morning for signs of predators missing fry, disturbed water, or tracks on the banks. Train a local child or worker to watch for birds and frogs during the day. A simple scarecrow or reflective tape can keep birds away. Remember, a single frog can eat dozens of fry per day. So act fast. If you have a serious predator problem, consider building a raised pond or using a greenhouse-like structure with netting.

5. Water Quality Monitoring in Nursery Ponds

Fry are very sensitive to water quality. You must check the water every day, preferably in the morning before feeding. The most important parameters are temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, and ammonia. For temperature, use a simple thermometer. Tilapia and catfish fry grow best at 2730C. If the water is too cold (below 20C), growth stops. If too hot (above 35C), fry become stressed. You can control temperature by adjusting pond depth deeper ponds stay cooler. In hot African afternoons, add fresh water to cool the pond. For pH, use a pH test kit or strips. The ideal range is 6.58.5. If pH is too low (acidic), add agricultural lime at 100200 kg per hectare. If too high (alkaline), add fresh water or organic matter like cow dung.

Dissolved oxygen (DO) is critical. Fry need at least 5 mg/L. You can test DO with a meter, but if you dont have one, watch for fry gasping at the surface. That means low oxygen. To increase oxygen, stop feeding, add fresh water, or use a small pump or aerator. In an emergency, you can splash water with a bucket. Ammonia is a silent killer. It comes from fish waste and uneaten feed. High ammonia (above 0.5 mg/L) burns fish gills. To reduce ammonia, stop feeding for a day, add fresh water, and increase natural filtration. You can also add zeolite or activated carbon if available. A simple ammonia test kit is cheap and worth buying.

Other things to monitor: water colour (should be green, not brown or black), transparency (use a Secchi disk a white plate on a string should be visible at 3040 cm depth), and salinity (for tilapia, 010 ppt is fine). Keep a daily record in a notebook. Write down the date, time, temperature, pH, and any observations. This helps you spot trends. For example, if pH drops every morning, you know the pond needs lime. If you see foam or bubbles that dont break, that means too much organic matter reduce feeding and add fresh water. Always have a backup water source. In Africa, many ponds suffer from low oxygen at dawn because algae consume oxygen at night. So check early morning DO. If you cannot afford test kits, use your senses: smell for rotten eggs (hydrogen sulphide), look for dead fish, and watch fry behaviour. Healthy fry swim actively and eat well.

6. Sampling and Growth Assessment

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Sampling tells you how fast your fry are growing, how many are surviving, and whether you need to adjust feeding or water quality. Sample your pond every 710 days. Use a fine mesh seine net (23 mm mesh) or a small cast net. Pull the net slowly through the water at several spots. Collect at least 3050 fry for a representative sample. Do not sample in the heat of the day early morning is best. Handle fry gently with wet hands or a soft net. Count the number of fry you catch and record it. Then measure their length. Use a ruler or a piece of graph paper. Measure from the tip of the snout to the end of the tail fin (total length). For accuracy, measure 20 fry individually and calculate the average.

Weighing is more accurate than length for growth. Use a small digital scale (0.1 g precision). Weigh a group of 10 fry together, then divide by 10 to get average weight. Record the weight in grams. Compare with previous samples. For tilapia, fry should gain about 0.51 gram per week in a well-managed pond. For catfish, growth is faster 12 grams per week. If growth is slow, check: (1) Are you feeding enough? Increase feed by 10% if needed. (2) Is water quality poor? Test pH, ammonia, and oxygen. (3) Is stocking density too high? If average weight is low but survival is high, you may need to reduce density by selling or moving some fry. (4) Are predators eating the feed? Check for competitors.

Survival rate is calculated by dividing the number of fry harvested by the number originally stocked, then multiplying by 100. For example, if you stocked 2,000 fry and harvested 1,400, survival is 70%. Good survival for tilapia is 7090%; for catfish, 6080%. If survival is below 50%, investigate predators, water quality, or disease. Keep a simple record sheet: date, average length, average weight, number sampled, and notes. This record helps you plan when to harvest. Fry are ready to move to grow-out ponds when they reach 510 grams (about 58 cm). At that size, they can escape most predators and eat larger pellets. Do not keep them in the nursery too long, or they will become stunted. Sampling also lets you check for diseases look for red spots, frayed fins, or abnormal behaviour. If you see sick fry, isolate them and treat the pond with salt (35 kg per 100 square metres) or potassium permanganate (24 mg/L).

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Fingerling Grow-Out in Ponds

This practical reference consolidates the seven lessons in the Fingerling Grow-Out in Ponds module into a single field-ready guide for African fish farmers. It is written for the operator who must prepare a pond, stock fry, feed them, protect them, monitor water, and sample growth all while keeping costs low and survival high. In African tilapia and catfish nursery systems, fingerling grow-out is the stage where most losses occur and where the highest return on management effort is realised: a 1015% improvement in survival can double net margins on a single production cycle. Use this reference alongside the individual lessons as a decision-support tool, not a replacement for them.

1. Pond Preparation and Fertilization

Prepare the pond 714 days before stocking. Drain, dry to crack the bottom (25 days), and remove debris. Apply agricultural lime (CaCO) at 200500 kg/ha if soil pH is below 6.5. Fill to 0.81.0 m depth using a screened inlet (250500 m mesh) to exclude wild fish and predator eggs. Fertilize to establish a phytoplankton bloom: apply well-decomposed poultry manure at 5001,000 kg/ha, or NPK 20-10-10 at 50100 kg/ha, split over two applications 34 days apart. Target a green water colour (Secchi depth 2540 cm). In Africa, where commercial fertilizers are costly, integrated manure from on-farm poultry or cattle is the most profitable option, provided it is fully composted to avoid oxygen depletion.

2. Stocking Density and Acclimation of Fry

Stock at 2040 fry/m for tilapia (Oreochromis spp.) and 50100 fry/m for African catfish (Clarias gariepinus) in earthen nursery ponds. Higher densities require aeration and daily water exchange. Acclimate fry before release: float the transport bag for 1520 minutes, then gradually add pond water over 2030 minutes until temperatures equalise (within 12 C). Release fry at dusk or on a cloudy day to reduce thermal and predation stress. In Africa, where hatcheries may be several hours away, always transport fry in oxygenated bags at 2025 C and avoid feeding for 24 hours before transport.

3. Natural Food Production and Supplemental Feeding

Natural food (zooplankton, insects, algae) supplies 3050% of fingerling nutrition in well-fertilized ponds. Maintain the bloom with weekly fertilization (manure or inorganic) and monitor Secchi depth. Supplement with a fine, high-protein feed (3545% crude protein) starting 35 days after stocking. Feed 58% of body weight per day, split into 34 meals. In Africa, locally formulated feeds using fishmeal, soybean meal, maize bran, and vitaminmineral premix can reduce feed costs by 2030% compared to imported pellets, provided the formula is balanced.

SAFETY WARNING Chemical and Manure Handling

Agricultural lime, NPK fertilizers, and raw manure can cause severe skin burns, eye damage, and respiratory irritation. Always wear gloves, goggles, and a dust mask when applying. Never use fresh (uncomposted) manure it depletes oxygen and introduces pathogens. Store chemicals away from children, livestock, and water sources. If lime contacts skin or eyes, rinse immediately with clean water for 15 minutes and seek medical attention. In Africa, where farm chemical regulation varies, follow national guidelines and keep a first-aid kit at the pond site.

4. Predator and Competitor Control in Ponds

Predators (birds, frogs, snakes, dragonfly larvae, wild fish) and competitors (tadpoles, snails, unwanted tilapia) can reduce survival by 2050%. Control measures: screen all inlets and outlets with 250500 m mesh; maintain a 3050 cm freeboard to deter frogs; use bird nets or reflective tape over the pond; remove aquatic weeds that harbour predators; and stock only sex-reversed or monosex tilapia to prevent unwanted reproduction. In African ponds, the most common predator is the African fish eagle and the monitor lizard physical exclusion is more cost-effective than chemical control.

5. Water Quality Monitoring in Nursery Ponds

Monitor daily: dissolved oxygen (DO) 4 mg/L (critical below 2 mg/L), temperature 2630 C for tilapia and 2428 C for catfish, pH 6.58.5, ammonia < 0.5 mg/L, and Secchi depth 2540 cm. Use a simple DO meter or test kit; in low-resource settings, observe fish behaviour gasping at the surface at dawn indicates low DO. Aerate or exchange water if DO drops. In Africa, where electricity is unreliable, solar-powered aerators or manual splashing at dawn are practical alternatives.

6. Sampling and Growth Assessment

Sample weekly using a cast net or seine net. Weigh 3050 fish individually, record mean weight, and calculate specific growth rate (SGR) and feed conversion ratio (FCR). Target SGR of 35%/day for tilapia and 46%/day for catfish. Adjust feeding based on sample weights. If growth is below target, check water quality, feed quality, and stocking density. In African markets, fingerlings are sold at 510 g; reaching this size in 68 weeks is the profitability benchmark.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Prepare pond 714 days ahead: drain, dry, lime, fill with screened water, and fertilize to a green bloom (Secchi 2540 cm).
  2. Acclimate fry properly: float bags 1520 min, gradually mix pond water, release at dusk; never feed 24 h before transport.
  3. Stock at recommended densities: 2040 fry/m tilapia; 50100 fry/m catfish; use monosex or sex-reversed tilapia.
  4. Feed a 3545% protein diet: 810% body weight/day in week 1, reducing to 45% by week 8; split into 34 meals.
  5. Control predators and competitors: screen inlets/outlets, maintain freeboard, remove weeds, and use bird nets.
  6. Monitor water daily: DO 4 mg/L, pH 6.58.5, ammonia < 0.5 mg/L, temperature 2630 C (tilapia) or 2428 C (catfish).
  7. Sample weekly: weigh 3050 fish, calculate SGR and FCR, and adjust feeding accordingly.
  8. Keep records: stocking date, feed used, water parameters, mortality, and final fingerling weight this drives profit decisions.
