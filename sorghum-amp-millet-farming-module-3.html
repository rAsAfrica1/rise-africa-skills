1. Clearing and preparing land with hand tools vs. oxen vs. tractor

Before you plant sorghum or millet, you must clear the land. The method you choose depends on your budget, land size, and available labour. Hand tools are cheapest but slowest. A hoe (jembe) and machete (panga) can clear 0.1 hectare in about 10 days of work. Use a slasher to cut tall grass first, then dig out roots and stumps. Burn or compost the debris. This method suits small plots under 0.5 hectare.

Oxen ploughing is faster and cheaper than a tractor for medium farms. A pair of trained oxen with a mouldboard plough can prepare 0.5 hectare in one day. You need two people: one to guide the oxen and one to lead. Oxen cost about $500 to buy or $20 per day to hire. They work best on flat, stone-free land. Keep the plough depth at 1520 cm. After ploughing, harrow to break clods.

Tractors are fastest for large areas. A 50-horsepower tractor with a disc plough can prepare 1 hectare in 23 hours. Hiring costs $30$50 per hectare. But tractors compact wet soil and cannot work on steep slopes or in rocky fields. If you use a tractor, plough when soil is moist but not waterlogged. Follow with a disc harrow to level the seedbed.

For all methods, clear the field 24 weeks before planting. Remove all perennial weeds like couch grass. If you use hand tools, dig deeply to break hardpan. With oxen or tractor, plough along the contour on slopes to reduce erosion. After clearing, leave the field to settle before making ridges or beds. Always wear boots and gloves to avoid cuts and snake bites. Choose the method that matches your resources, but remember: proper clearing saves weeding time later.

2. Making ridges and flat planting beds for sorghum and millet

Sorghum and millet grow well on either ridges or flat beds, but the choice depends on your soil and rainfall. Ridges are best for heavy clay soils and areas with poor drainage. Flat beds suit sandy soils and dry regions where moisture conservation is key. Both methods need a fine, level seedbed.

To make ridges by hand, use a hoe to pull soil into mounds 2030 cm high and 6075 cm apart. For sorghum, ridges should be 75 cm apart; for millet, 60 cm. Make ridges along the contour on slopes. This slows runoff and traps water. You can also make ridges with an ox-drawn ridger. A pair of oxen can form 1 hectare of ridges in one day. Tractor-drawn ridgers do 1 hectare in 12 hours. Ridges should be firm, not loose, so they don't collapse in rain.

Flat beds are simpler. After ploughing, harrow to break clods. Then level the soil with a rake or a wooden board. Beds should be 12 meters wide and as long as you like, with 30 cm paths between them for walking and weeding. Flat beds are ideal for millet in dry areas because they allow water to spread evenly. But in heavy rain, flat beds can waterlog. If your area gets more than 600 mm of rain, use ridges.

For both methods, add organic matter before planting. Spread 23 tonnes of manure per hectare and mix it into the top 15 cm. If you use ridges, place manure in the furrows before pulling soil over. For flat beds, broadcast manure and rake it in. Make sure the bed is free of large stones and roots. Plant immediately after making beds to avoid moisture loss. A good seedbed gives strong germination and uniform stands.

3. Testing soil pH and organic matter using simple field kits

Soil pH tells you if your soil is too acidic or too alkaline for sorghum and millet. These crops grow best at pH 5.5 to 7.5. You can test pH with a simple field kit that costs $10$20. The kit includes a test tube, a colour chart, and a powder or liquid reagent. To test, take a handful of soil from 15 cm deep in five spots across your field. Mix them in a clean bucket. Put one spoon of soil in the test tube, add the reagent, shake, and wait for the colour to change. Compare with the chart. If pH is below 5.5, add wood ash or lime (12 tonnes per hectare). If above 7.5, add compost or gypsum.

Organic matter is the dark, rotted plant and animal material in soil. It holds water and nutrients. You can test it without a kit. Take a glass jar, fill it one-third with soil, add water to the top, and shake hard. Let it settle for 24 hours. The top layer is organic matter (floating or dark). If it is less than 2 cm thick, your soil is poor. Add 23 tonnes of manure or compost per hectare. Another simple test: squeeze moist soil in your hand. If it holds together and stains your fingers dark, organic matter is good. If it crumbles and looks pale, it is low.

For a more accurate organic matter test, use a field colorimeter kit ($30$50). It measures the darkness of a soil solution. But the jar test is free and works well. Test your soil every year before planting. Keep records of pH and organic matter for each field. If pH is wrong, you waste fertilizer. If organic matter is low, your crops suffer in drought. Fix these before planting. Add manure, compost, or cover crops like cowpea to build organic matter. Healthy soil grows healthy sorghum and millet.

4. Applying manure and compost before planting

Manure and compost are the cheapest fertilizers for sorghum and millet. They add nutrients and improve soil structure. Apply them 24 weeks before planting. Fresh manure can burn seedlings, so use well-rotted manure that is at least 3 months old. Compost is ready when it is dark, crumbly, and smells like earth.

For a small plot, spread 23 tonnes of manure per hectare. That is about 23 wheelbarrows per 100 square meters. Spread it evenly and mix it into the top 15 cm with a hoe. If you make ridges, put manure in the furrow before pulling soil over. For flat beds, broadcast and rake in. A good rate for sorghum is 5 tonnes per hectare if your soil is very poor. For millet, 34 tonnes per hectare is enough. Do not apply more than 10 tonnes per hectare; too much manure can cause lodging (falling over).

Compost can be made from crop residues, animal dung, kitchen waste, and ash. Build a pile 1.5 m wide and 1.5 m high. Turn it every 2 weeks. It will be ready in 23 months. Apply compost at 24 tonnes per hectare. You can also make liquid manure: put 1 part manure in 10 parts water, let it sit for 1 week, and water your seedbed with it. This gives a quick nutrient boost.

If you have no manure, plant a cover crop like cowpea or groundnut before sorghum. These legumes fix nitrogen. Plough them under 2 weeks before planting sorghum. This adds 4060 kg of nitrogen per hectare. Combine manure with mineral fertilizer if you can afford it: use half the recommended NPK and half manure. Always apply manure before the rains start, so nutrients soak in. Never leave manure in a heap in the field; spread it. This protects nutrients from sun and rain.

5. Choosing the right planting date based on local rains

The best planting date for sorghum and millet is when the rains are steady. If you plant too early, seeds rot or seedlings dry out. If you plant too late, you lose yield to drought or pests. In most of Africa, the first reliable rains come in late May to early June in the Sahel, and November to December in southern Africa. But you must use your own local knowledge.

Start by watching the rains. Plant after 2030 mm of rain has fallen and the soil is wet 15 cm deep. Do not plant after a single light shower. Wait for at least two good rains within a week. For sorghum, plant 23 weeks after the first rains. For millet, plant 12 weeks after the first rains because it is more drought-tolerant. In dry areas with short rains, plant early maturing varieties as soon as the soil is moist.

You can also use the onset of the rainy season. In West Africa, the 'mango rains' or 'planting rains' come in late May. In East Africa, the long rains start in MarchApril. In Southern Africa, the first rains come in November. But these dates vary. Talk to older farmers and extension officers. Keep a record of when you planted and how the crop did. After 23 years, you will know your best date.

If you have irrigation, you can plant earlier or later. But most smallholders rely on rain. So prepare your land before the rains. Have your seed and manure ready. When the rains come, plant immediately. Do not wait for the soil to dry. For sorghum, plant 45 cm deep. For millet, 23 cm deep. Space sorghum 75 cm between rows and 25 cm between plants. Millet 60 cm between rows and 20 cm between plants. Plant when the soil is moist, not wet. If you plant in dry soil, the seed will not germinate. If you plant in waterlogged soil, the seed will rot. Aim for a planting date that gives your crop 90120 days of rain.

6. Intercropping sorghum with cowpea or groundnut for extra income

Intercropping means growing two or more crops together on the same field. Sorghum with cowpea or groundnut is a classic African combination. It gives you two harvests, reduces risk, and improves soil. Cowpea and groundnut are legumes that fix nitrogen from the air. They add 2040 kg of nitrogen per hectare to the soil. This helps the next sorghum crop.

To intercrop, plant sorghum in rows 75 cm apart. Between two sorghum rows, plant one row of cowpea or groundnut. Space cowpea 30 cm apart within the row. For groundnut, space 20 cm apart. Plant both crops at the same time or plant the legume 12 weeks after sorghum. This reduces competition for light and water. Use a 1:1 or 2:1 ratio (two sorghum rows to one legume row). Do not plant too dense. If you plant too many legumes, they compete with sorghum for water.

Cowpea is better for dry areas. It matures in 6090 days, earlier than sorghum. You can harvest cowpea leaves as vegetables. Groundnut needs more rain (500600 mm) and prefers sandy soil. It matures in 90120 days. Both crops give extra income. A hectare of intercropped sorghum and cowpea can yield 1.5 tonnes of sorghum and 0.5 tonnes of cowpea. At market prices, that is an extra $200$300 per hectare. Groundnut can give 0.8 tonnes per hectare, worth $400$500.

Intercropping also reduces pests. Cowpea attracts natural enemies of sorghum pests. Groundnut shades the soil, reducing weeds. But you must manage carefully. Weed early. Use a hoe to remove weeds between rows. Do not apply herbicide that kills broadleaf plants, as it will kill cowpea and groundnut. If you use fertilizer, apply it to the sorghum rows only. After harvest, plough the legume residues into the soil. This adds organic matter. Intercropping is a smart way to get more from your land and improve soil for future crops.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Preparing Your Land for Sorghum & Millet

Land preparation is the single largest cost line in dryland cereal production across the Sahel, East Africa, and Southern Africa and the one where smallholder margins are won or lost. A farmer who spends three weeks hand-clearing a hectare before the first rains has already lost part of the planting window. A farmer who hires a tractor too early on sandy soil loses moisture to evaporation. This lesson gives you the decision rules, cost logic, and field measurements you need to prepare land that pays for itself.

1. Clearing and Primary Tillage: Hand Tools vs. Oxen vs. Tractor

Your choice of tillage power is a business decision driven by three variables: field size, soil type, and access to cash or credit at the right moment. Sorghum and millet are hardy, but they respond to good seedbed preparation especially pearl millet on sandy soils and sorghum on heavier loams.

Hand tools (hoe/jembe, machete, hand-pushed ripper). Viable for 0.251 hectare. Labour requirement is roughly 1525 person-days per hectare for clearing and 2030 for first tillage. Cost is your own labour or hired labour at prevailing daily rates. Advantage: you can till precisely when soil moisture is right, and you avoid compaction. Disadvantage: it consumes the planting window.

Oxen/donkey plough. The workhorse of the 15 hectare farm. A well-trained ox pair with a mouldboard or ard plough can prepare 0.51 hectare per day, depending on soil and stoniness. This is the cheapest mechanised option per hectare in most of Africa, but it requires animal ownership or hire, feed, and a plough. Oxen work best on light-to-medium soils; heavy clay or rocky fields will break equipment and animals.

Tractor. Economical above roughly 5 hectares, or where a contractor charges per hectare. Tractor ploughing can reach 24 hectares per day. The risks are real: compaction on wet soils, plough pans from repeated shallow passes, and a narrow hiring window when every farmer in the district wants the same tractor. Book early and confirm the contractor's rate per hectare in writing.

2. Ridges vs. Flat Planting Beds

Ridges (tied ridges or open ridges) are the default on poorly drained or crusting soils, and on slopes where water erosion is a risk. They concentrate moisture in the furrow and reduce runoff. Flat planting is simpler and cheaper, and works well on level, well-drained sandy soils typical of pearl millet zones.

For sorghum on heavier soils, ridges 7590 cm apart with 2030 cm height are standard. For millet on sandy soils, flat beds with a light hand-hoe furrow are often enough. Tied ridges ridges with cross-ties every 12 m are a proven water-harvesting technique in the Sahel and can lift yields substantially in dry years.

3. Testing Soil pH and Organic Matter with Simple Field Kits

Sorghum tolerates pH 5.58.5; pearl millet prefers pH 5.57.5. Below pH 5.5, aluminium toxicity and phosphorus lock-up cut yields. Above pH 8.0, iron and zinc deficiencies appear. Organic matter below 1% means your soil has little water-holding capacity and will respond strongly to manure.

Field kits (colour-comparison pH kits, and the "jar/water" or loss-on-ignition field tests) give you a working number in 20 minutes. Take 1015 sub-samples in a "W" pattern across the field, mix them in a clean bucket, and test the composite. Do this before you buy lime, gypsum, or extra manure it prevents wasted input spend.

4. Applying Manure and Compost Before Planting

Manure and compost are the highest-return inputs on most African smallholder cereal fields, because they fix both fertility and water-holding capacity at once. Apply 25 t/ha of well-rotted manure 24 weeks before planting, incorporated into the top 1015 cm. Fresh manure burns seedlings and carries weed seeds never plant into fresh manure. Compost should be dark, crumbly, and odourless.

In dry areas, combine manure with micro-dosing of mineral fertiliser (a bottle-cap per planting station) for a stronger response. This is a proven ICRISAT/FAO recommendation for sorghum and millet.

SAFETY WARNING Manure and Compost Handling

Fresh manure and poorly composted material carry E. coli, Salmonella, and parasitic worms that cause serious human illness. Always: (1) compost manure for at least 46 weeks until it heats through; (2) wear gloves and boots when handling; (3) wash hands and tools with soap after work; (4) never apply fresh manure to vegetables or crops eaten raw; (5) keep children and animals away from manure heaps. If you feel unwell after handling manure, seek medical care and mention the exposure.

5. Choosing the Right Planting Date Based on Local Rains

Planting date is the highest-leverage decision in dryland cereal farming. Sorghum and millet need 60120 days depending on variety. The rule: plant when 2030 mm of rain has fallen within 13 days and the forecast shows continued moisture not on the first isolated shower. Early planting into a false start is the most common cause of crop failure in the Sahel.

Use local rainfall records, extension service advisories, and the national meteorological service seasonal forecast. For millet, plant slightly earlier than sorghum on sandy soils; for sorghum on heavier soils, wait until the soil is reliably wet to 20 cm.

6. Intercropping Sorghum with Cowpea or Groundnut for Extra Income

Intercropping sorghum with a legume is one of the most reliable ways to raise total farm income per hectare while improving soil nitrogen. Cowpea and groundnut both fix nitrogen and give you a second cash crop grain, fodder, and a marketable legume without extra land.

Standard pattern: alternate rows of sorghum and legume, or sorghum in rows with legume between. Keep sorghum spacing at 7590 cm between rows and 2030 cm within rows; plant the legume in the inter-row. Do not overcrowd competition for water in dry years can reduce sorghum yield. In good years, the legume more than compensates.
