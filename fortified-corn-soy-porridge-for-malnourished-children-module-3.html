Lessons

1. Cleaning corn and soybeans by winnowing and washing

Start by spreading your corn and soybeans on a clean tarpaulin or woven mat in the sun. Pick out stones, dirt clods, insects, and any mouldy or discoloured grains. Mouldy grains are dangerousthey can contain poisons that survive cooking. For 10 kg of corn, you should spend at least 15 minutes hand-sorting. Do the same for soybeans: remove shrivelled, green, or black beans.

Next, winnow to remove lighter chaff and dust. On a windy day, pour the grains slowly from one basin to another from a height of about 1 metre. The wind blows away husks and dust while heavier grains fall into the lower basin. If there is no wind, use a fan or gently blow with your mouth. Repeat 34 times until no chaff remains.

Washing removes remaining dirt and some surface germs. Put grains in a large basin, cover with clean water, and rub them between your hands for 23 minutes. The water will turn cloudy. Pour off the dirty water and repeat with fresh water until the water stays clearusually 3 rinses. For soybeans, soaking is not needed for this porridge, but washing is essential because soybeans often carry soil bacteria.

After washing, drain the grains in a colander or sieve. Spread them on a clean mat or tray to dry in the sun for 24 hours, or until they are dry to the touch. Wet grains will stick together during roasting and can grow mould. If you are processing 20 kg, split the work into smaller batches of 5 kg so that every grain is properly cleaned. Always wash your hands and use clean toolsdirty hands can recontaminate the grains. Clean grains are the foundation of safe, healthy porridge.

2. Roasting soybeans to remove beany taste and anti-nutrients

Raw soybeans contain anti-nutrients like trypsin inhibitor that block protein digestion and cause stomach upset. Roasting destroys these anti-nutrients and removes the strong beany taste that children dislike. You will need a dry frying pan, a large pot, or a clay pot. A metal pan heats faster, but stir constantly to avoid burning.

Measure your cleaned, dried soybeans. For a standard batch, use 2 kg of soybeans. Heat your pan over medium firenot high, because high heat burns the outside while the inside stays raw. When a drop of water sizzles and disappears immediately, the pan is ready. Add the soybeans in a single layer, about 23 cm deep. Do not overcrowd; if you have more, roast in batches.

Stir continuously with a wooden spoon or paddle for 1520 minutes. The beans will first crackle, then darken to a golden brown. You will see small brown spots and smell a nutty aroma. That is the sign they are done. If you see black spots or smoke, the heat is too highreduce the fire and keep stirring. Raw soybeans are hard; roasted ones are brittle and easy to break between your teeth.

After roasting, spread the beans on a clean tray to cool for 1015 minutes. They must be completely cool before milling, or they will clog the mill. Roasted soybeans can be stored in an airtight container for up to 2 weeks in a dry place. Always roast soybeans separately from corn because they need different times and temperatures. Properly roasted soybeans are safe, digestible, and give the porridge a rich, nutty flavour that masks the beany taste. Never skip this stepit is critical for child health.

3. Roasting corn for better flavor and digestibility

Roasting corn improves its flavour, makes it easier to digest, and reduces cooking time later. You will need whole dried corn kernelsnot fresh corn. Clean and dry them as you learned in Lesson 1. Use a large dry pan, pot, or a traditional clay baking pot. A wide pan gives more surface area and roasts faster.

Measure 3 kg of dried corn. Heat your pan over medium fire. Test the heat by sprinkling a few drops of waterthey should sizzle and evaporate in 2 seconds. Add the corn in a layer no deeper than 3 cm. Stir constantly with a wooden spoon. The corn will start to pop slightly and turn a deeper yellow. After 1012 minutes, you will see light brown patches. Keep stirring for another 58 minutes until most kernels are golden brown and smell like roasted maize. Total roasting time is 1520 minutes.

Do not let the corn turn black or dark brownthat means burnt, and it will make the porridge bitter. If you hear loud popping, that is normal, but reduce heat slightly. Burnt corn can also form harmful compounds. When done, pour the corn onto a clean mat or tray to cool completely. It should be cool to the touch before milling, about 2030 minutes.

Roasted corn is lighter and crushes more easily than raw corn. It also has a sweeter, nuttier taste that children prefer. Store roasted corn in a dry sack or airtight container for up to 1 month. Always roast in a well-ventilated areasmoke from roasting can irritate your eyes and lungs. If you are roasting both corn and soybeans, do corn first, then soybeans, because soybeans leave an oily residue that can burn. With practice, you will know the right colour and smell by heart.

4. Using a local mill to grind corn and soybeans into flour

After roasting and cooling, you need to grind the corn and soybeans into fine flour. You can use a local hammer mill, a disc mill, or a traditional grinding stone. Most African villages have a mill that charges a small fee per kilogramoften 5001000 shillings or 0.501 USD per 10 kg. Call ahead to make sure the mill is clean and not used for other products like fish or spices, which can leave smells.

Before milling, mix your roasted corn and roasted soybeans in the correct ratio. For a standard fortified porridge, use 70% corn and 30% soybeans by weight. For example, for 10 kg of flour, use 7 kg roasted corn and 3 kg roasted soybeans. Mix them well in a basin so that every scoop has both grains. This ensures each serving has balanced protein and energy.

Take your mixture to the mill. Ask the miller to set the grinder to a fine settinglike for maize meal, not coarse like for animal feed. Feed the grains slowly into the hopper. If the mill is too fast, the flour will be hot and may clog. You may need to pass the flour through the mill twice to get a fine texture. The first pass breaks the grains; the second makes it powdery. For 10 kg, milling takes about 1015 minutes.

Collect the flour in a clean, dry sack or bucket. Do not use a sack that previously held chemicals or dirty produce. Immediately after milling, spread the flour on a clean tray to cool for 10 minutesmilling generates heat. Then pack it into airtight containers or double plastic bags. Label with the date. Freshly milled flour should be used within 23 months. If you cannot mill immediately, store the roasted grains in a dry place for up to 2 weeks. Never grind wet grainsthey will ruin the mill and spoil the flour.

5. Sieving flour to get the right texture for porridge

Sieving removes coarse particles, hulls, and any lumps from your milled flour. The right texture is important: too coarse and the porridge will be gritty and hard for a sick child to swallow; too fine and it may become gluey. You will need a fine-mesh sieve or a traditional woven sieve. A 1 mm mesh is idealabout the size of a mosquito net.

Place your sieve over a large clean basin. Add flour a few cups at a time. Shake the sieve gently side to side, or tap it with your hand. Do not press the flour through with your fingersthat forces coarse bits through. The fine flour falls into the basin; the coarse bits stay in the sieve. For 10 kg of milled flour, sieving takes about 2030 minutes. Work in a clean, draft-free area so dust does not blow away.

What to do with the coarse bits? You can re-mill them, or if you have no mill, you can use them for animal feed or compost. Do not throw them into the porridgethey will not cook soft and can cause choking. The sieved fine flour should be soft like baby powder. Test by rubbing a pinch between your fingers: it should feel smooth, not sandy.

After sieving, you may want to blend different batches to ensure consistency. Mix the sieved flour well with a clean spoon. Then pack immediately into airtight containers. Sieving also helps remove any small stones or metal bits that might have come from the millalways check the sieve for foreign objects. If you are making porridge for a malnourished child, the flour must be fine enough to cook into a smooth, drinkable liquid. A good test: mix 2 tablespoons of flour with 1 cup of waterit should form a smooth paste with no lumps. If lumps form, sieve again or blend in a dry mill. Proper sieving makes the porridge smooth, digestible, and safe.

6. Preventing contamination during milling and packaging

Contamination can happen at any stepfrom dirty hands to unclean mills to poor storage. Your goal is to keep the flour free from dirt, germs, insects, and chemicals. Start with personal hygiene: wash your hands with soap and water before handling grains or flour. Wear a clean apron and tie back long hair. Do not smoke, eat, or chew while working.

At the mill, check that the equipment is clean. Ask the miller to run a small handful of your grain through first and throw it awaythis cleans out old flour from previous customers. If the mill was used for peanuts, fish, or spices, the smell can transfer to your flour. In that case, find another mill or clean the mill with a dry cloth and run a batch of plain corn through it first. Never use a mill that has milled poisonous seeds like castor beanstraces can be deadly.

During milling, keep your collection sack or bucket clean and off the ground. Place it on a clean mat or table. Do not let the flour touch the floor, which may have animal droppings or chemicals. After milling, cool the flour completely before packaging. Hot flour creates condensation inside the container, which leads to mould. Use food-grade plastic bags, clean jerrycans, or airtight buckets. Double-bagging is best: put flour in a small bag, twist it closed, then place it in a larger bag.

For packaging, work on a clean table. Use a scoop that is only for flournot for chemicals or animal feed. Fill bags to the top, press out air, and seal tightly with a knot or heat sealer. Label each bag with the date and contents. Store in a cool, dry, dark place off the floor, like on a shelf or pallet. Keep away from soap, kerosene, or pesticides. Check weekly for insects or moisture. If you see clumps or smell mould, throw the flour awaydo not feed it to children. Clean tools and careful storage protect the health of the children you are feeding.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Cleaning, Roasting, and Milling Grains for Fortified Corn-Soy Porridge

For African food processors, school feeding programmes, and smallholder cooperatives, the quality of fortified corn-soy porridge is decided long before the first scoop reaches a child's bowl. Cleaning, roasting, and milling are the operations where you either build a product that passes inspection, stores well, and earns repeat orders or where you lose margin to rejects, spoilage, and customer complaints. This Practical Reference gives you the operating parameters, data, and checkpoints to run these steps profitably at small to medium scale.

The business logic is simple: every kilogram of grit, stone, or mouldy kernel that enters the mill is a kilogram you paid for but cannot sell. Every batch of poorly roasted soybean carries anti-nutrients that reduce protein digestibility and a beany taste that kills repeat purchase. Every over-coarse or over-fine flour either clogs the porridge line or fails the texture spec your institutional buyer wrote into the contract. Treat these three steps as cost centres you are optimising, not chores.

1. Cleaning Corn and Soybeans by Winnowing and Washing

Cleaning is the cheapest quality intervention in the entire line. Winnowing removes light chaff, dust, and immature kernels; washing removes soil, stones, and surface microbial load. In most African settings, a combination of a locally fabricated winnower or a simple basket winnow followed by a wash tank is enough to reach the 1% foreign matter spec that buyers expect.

Winnow on a day with steady, low wind, or use a mechanical fan. Feed grain slowly and let the heavy fraction fall into a clean container. Repeat two to three passes. Then wash in clean water ideally with a 12 minute agitation and 23 rinse cycles until the water runs clear. Do not soak overnight; excess moisture slows roasting and promotes mould.

2. Roasting Soybeans to Remove Beany Taste and Anti-Nutrients

Raw soybeans contain trypsin inhibitors, lectins, and lipoxygenase enzymes that reduce protein digestibility and create the beany, grassy off-flavour that children reject. Roasting inactivates these. The key is to roast enough to destroy anti-nutrients but not so much that you burn protein quality and flavour.

Use a drum roaster, sand roaster, or a well-stirred open pan. Aim for a uniform golden-brown colour, a nutty aroma, and a crisp kernel that cracks rather than bends. The most reliable field test is the "no raw centre" check: split a bean and confirm the interior is the same colour as the surface. A more rigorous test is the urease test, which measures residual urease activity as a proxy for anti-nutrient destruction.

SAFETY WARNING ROASTING AND MILLING

  • Never roast in an enclosed room without ventilation. Carbon monoxide and smoke from open fires and drum roasters can be lethal. Use a chimney, hood, or outdoor roasting bay.
  • Keep a fire extinguisher and a bucket of sand near the roaster. Hot grain and chaff ignite easily.
  • Do not enter or reach into a running mill. Isolate power and lock out before clearing jams. Mill belts and hammers cause severe injury.
  • Wear a dust mask and eye protection during milling and sieving. Flour dust is a respiratory and explosion hazard in confined spaces.
  • Never use pesticide-treated storage bags or containers for food grain. Use food-grade liners only.

3. Roasting Corn for Better Flavour and Digestibility

Corn roasting is lighter than soybean roasting. The goal is to drive off excess moisture, develop a toasted flavour, and slightly gelatinise starch so the porridge cooks faster and has better mouthfeel. Over-roasting makes the flour dark and bitter and reduces the appeal of the final porridge, especially for young children.

Roast in small batches no more than 1015 kg per batch in a drum roaster and stir continuously. Stop when the kernels are light golden and smell toasted, not burnt. Cool immediately by spreading in a thin layer on a clean, dry surface. Do not bag warm grain; condensation causes mould.

4. Using a Local Mill to Grind Corn and Soybeans into Flour

Most African processors use a local hammer mill or plate mill. The critical business decisions are: (a) the corn-to-soy ratio, (b) the mill setting, and (c) hygiene between batches. A common fortified porridge blend is 7080% corn and 2030% soybean by weight, depending on the target protein content and buyer specification.

Mill soybeans first or use a dedicated mill if possible, because residual oil from soy can rancidify subsequent corn batches. If sharing a mill, purge with a small quantity of corn and discard the purge. Set the mill to a medium-fine grind coarse enough to avoid clogging but fine enough to cook into a smooth porridge.

5. Sieving Flour to Get the Right Texture for Porridge

Sieving does two jobs: it removes oversized grits that would settle in the porridge, and it standardises particle size so every batch cooks the same way. Use a 500710 micron sieve for standard porridge; finer sieves ( 500 micron) give a smoother product for infant feeding. Oversized material is returned to the mill never discarded. This is a direct margin recovery step.

Record the sieve size and the percentage of oversize on each batch sheet. If oversize exceeds 10%, adjust the mill setting or replace worn hammers/plates. Consistent texture is one of the strongest drivers of repeat purchase in institutional feeding programmes.

6. Preventing Contamination During Milling and Packaging

Contamination at this stage comes from three sources: dirty equipment, dirty hands and clothing, and dirty packaging. Implement a simple HACCP-style control: clean the mill and sieving area before and after each batch; require clean aprons, hairnets, and handwashing; use food-grade, sealed packaging with a batch code and production date.

Store finished flour off the floor on pallets, in a dry, ventilated room, away from fuel, pesticides, and livestock. Target 12% moisture and use within the shelf life stated on the label typically 36 months in tropical conditions, shorter in humid coastal areas.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Weigh and sample every incoming batch; record moisture and foreign matter.
  • Winnow 23 passes, then wash until rinse water is clear; drain well.
  • Roast soybeans at 120150C for 1525 min; confirm no raw centre and nutty aroma.
  • Roast corn at 100130C for 1015 min; stop at light golden, toasted aroma.
  • Cool grain to ambient temperature before milling; never bag warm grain.
  • Mill at the correct corn:soy ratio and purge shared mills between products.
  • Sieve to spec (500710 micron); return oversize to mill; record oversize %.
  • Package in food-grade sealed bags with batch code, date, and weight; store off floor, dry, and ventilated.
