🧠 Brain Opener

A chatbot is not magic. It is a system with rules, memory, prompts, and a plan for what happens when things go wrong. You can build one for your school, your family, or a small business.

  • What is the difference between a chatbot and a prompt?
  • What should a chatbot do when it does not know the answer?
  • How do you test a chatbot before launching it?
🎁 Reveal a good answer

A prompt answers one question. A chatbot handles a conversation - many questions, sometimes in a sequence, sometimes off-topic. When a chatbot does not know, it should say so honestly, then offer to help in another way. You test a chatbot by playing every kind of user you can imagine before real users try it.

📖 Simba Builds a School Bot

Simba was seventeen. His school had 1,200 students. Students were always asking the same ten questions - fees, timetables, uniform rules, sports fixtures, exam dates, teacher contacts, lunch menu, library hours, term dates, admissions.

The deputy headmistress spent two hours a day answering these questions. She was exhausted.

Simba offered to build a chatbot. He had three weeks.

Week one: he collected every question the school got in one month. He found the top fifty questions. Then he wrote answers to each one, checked by a teacher.

Week two: he wrote the system prompt. His chatbot had a role: You are Simba School helpful assistant. It had a task: Answer questions about the school using the information I give you. It had constraints: Never guess. If you do not know, say so and give the office phone number. It had format: short, friendly, one sentence, no emojis.

He tested the chatbot himself. Then he asked ten students to try to break it. They asked in Shona, in slang, in code. They asked about other schools, about history, about football teams. The chatbot stayed on topic. When it did not know, it apologised and gave the office number.

Week three: he deployed the bot on the school website. He watched the first 200 messages. Only three times did the bot give a wrong answer. He fixed those three cases in the system prompt.

At the end of the month, the deputy headmistress reported saving 90 minutes a day. She used the time to actually work with students.

Simba had not written any code. He had written a well-designed prompt, tested it, and deployed it. That was the whole bot.

💡 The Big Idea

A chatbot is a system, not a prompt

A chatbot is a prompt wrapped in a system. The system collects input, decides whether to use memory, calls the model, checks the output against rules, and returns an answer. The prompt is the brain. The system is the body. Both matter.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

The five parts of a chatbot system

Input handler: collects what the user typed. Context: gathers any relevant history or facts. Prompt: sends the input, context, and rules to the model. Output filter: checks the response for safety, tone, and accuracy. Fallback: what happens when the model fails or the question is out of scope.

2

Rules and memory

Rules are the chatbot boundaries. What topics it handles. What tone. What it never says. Rules go in the prompt. The clearer the rules, the better the behaviour.

Memory is what the chatbot remembers from earlier in the conversation. Short-term memory is the last few messages. Long-term memory is stored facts about the user or the topic. A well-designed chatbot uses memory carefully - enough to be helpful, not so much that it feels intrusive.

3

Testing - the part everyone skips

Before you launch a chatbot, you must test it against real users. Not your friends - real users. Different ages, different moods, different languages. Give them tasks. Watch where the bot fails. Fix the prompt. Test again.

The most common chatbot failures are: giving wrong information, refusing to answer reasonable questions, and going off-topic. All three are fixed by better prompts and better fallbacks. None are fixed by ignoring the problem.

🛠 Hands-On - Build a Simple School Bot

You need

  • A phone or laptop with AI access
  • A list of 20 common questions with verified answers
  • A document to write the system prompt
  1. Pick one narrow topic (school info, clinic hours, a small business).
  2. Collect 20 common questions and their verified answers.
  3. Write the system prompt: role, task, constraints, knowledge, format.
  4. Include a rule: If you do not know, say so and give this phone number.
  5. Test with 10 different users - different ages, different languages.
  6. Record every failure. Fix the prompt. Test again.
  7. Deploy and monitor the first 100 messages.
Why it works: The narrow scope is the secret. A bot that answers 20 questions perfectly beats a bot that half-answers 1,000.

🎮 Game - Break the Bot

3 to 12 players - 20 min

One player acts as the chatbot (using a prepared prompt). Everyone else tries to break it - asking off-topic questions, in different languages, with trick questions. The chatbot scores 1 point for each valid answer and 0 for each failure. The class scores 1 point for each failure. Highest score wins.

🤖 AI-Era Skill

A chatbot needs a system prompt. Test the prompt before building anything else.

Try this prompt: "You are a helpful assistant for a small school. You answer questions about fees, timetables, and rules. If you do not know the answer, say so and give the office number: 077-000-0000. Keep answers to one sentence. Do not use emojis. Do not answer questions unrelated to the school."

Send twenty varied questions. Count how many stay on topic, how many go off-topic, how many wrongly guess. Fix the prompt until failures drop below 10 percent.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Zimbabwe health helpline chatbot

A Zimbabwean health helpline launched a chatbot for common questions about clinics and medicines. It handles 30 percent of queries without a human. Answers are checked by nurses every week. When it does not know, it forwards the user to a real person. The bot is narrow, verified, and careful.

Kenyan university admissions bot

A Kenyan university built a chatbot for admissions enquiries. It was tested by 100 prospective students before launch. It failed 40 percent of the time in the first version. The team fixed the prompt and re-tested until failures dropped to 8 percent. Now it handles 2,000 queries per day.
