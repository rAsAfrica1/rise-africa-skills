Module 7

Health, Parasites and Disease in Fattening Cattle

Health problems in a fattening pen are concentrated in the first month, and most of them are preventable or cheap to fix if you catch them early. This module gives you an arrival and quarantine protocol, a working knowledge of ticks and tick-borne disease, parasite control, lameness, and the feeding-related emergencies of bloat and acidosis. It ends with your legal duties: reporting notifiable disease, movement permits, and observing drug withdrawal periods before slaughter.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • You will be able to run an arrival and quarantine protocol that finds disease in the first two weeks.
  • You will be able to apply a disciplined tick control schedule and recognise the main tick-borne diseases.
  • You will be able to plan parasite and liver fluke treatment and dose it correctly by weight.
  • You will be able to recognise bloat, acidosis and respiratory disease and take the right first action.
  • You will be able to find and manage lameness early and remove the pen conditions that cause it.
  • You will be able to report notifiable disease correctly, handle movement paperwork, and calculate a safe slaughter date from label withdrawal periods.

Lesson 7.115 min

Arrival Health Protocol and Quarantine

In this lesson
  • Prepare a quarantine pen before cattle arrive
  • Run a single processing handling on day two or three
  • Know the signs that require an immediate veterinary call

The riskiest fourteen days of a fattening cycle are the first fourteen. Cattle arriving from an auction, a trader or a distant district have been mixed with strangers, starved, moved and stressed, and they are both more likely to be carrying disease and less able to fight it. What you do in the first week decides how many you lose and how fast the rest start gaining.

Before they arrive

  • Have a quarantine pen ready, physically separate from any cattle you already own, with its own water trough and its own feed bunk. Downwind and downhill from the main pens if you have the choice.
  • Have clean water in it and good quality hay or forage in the bunk. No concentrate on day one.
  • Have your handling facility working: a race, a crush or at least a strong, safe corner where you can restrain an animal without a fight.
  • Have your record book, tags, a thermometer, and your vet's phone number written on the wall.
  • Confirm the movement paperwork required in your country is in order before the animals leave the seller. Ask your local veterinary office what a lawful movement of cattle into your area requires.

Day of arrival

  1. Offload quietly. Do not process, dip, inject or weigh cattle that have just come off a long journey unless something is obviously wrong.
  2. Give clean water and good hay only. A stressed, empty rumen must be refilled with fibre first.
  3. Count them, and walk through slowly to look at every animal: can it walk, is it breathing normally, is anything down, is anything scouring, is anything bleeding or injured from transport.
  4. Leave them alone to rest for at least 12 to 24 hours.

Processing, usually day two or three

Now bring them through the crush once and do everything in one handling.

  • Identify: ear tag or number every animal. You cannot track health or gain on unidentified cattle.
  • Weigh or tape and write the arrival weight down. This is the baseline for the whole cycle.
  • Examine individually: temperature on anything that looks dull, check eyes, nose, feet, navel area, and look for lumps, wounds and existing tick damage.
  • Tick treatment: dip or spray according to the product label and your local tick pressure.
  • Internal parasites: discuss with your vet or animal health technician which dewormer, and whether a flukicide is needed for the area the cattle came from.
  • Vaccinations: which ones are appropriate depends entirely on your country and district. Ask your government veterinary officer what is recommended and what is compulsory locally. Do not copy a protocol from another country.
  • Record every product used: animal, date, product name, batch, dose, and the meat withdrawal period printed on the label.

Quarantine period

Keep new arrivals separate for about 21 to 30 days. During that time:

  • Walk them at least twice daily and take the temperature of anything dull, since tick-borne disease and respiratory disease typically show in the first two to three weeks.
  • Feed and handle the quarantine pen last, after your other cattle, and clean your boots and hands afterwards.
  • Do not move equipment, buckets or manure from quarantine to the main pens.
  • Step concentrate up slowly as taught in the feeding module. Arrival is the worst possible time for a rich ration.

Call the vet immediately if

  • More than one animal is sick with similar signs at the same time.
  • Any animal dies suddenly and unexpectedly. Do not open the carcass. Sudden death with blood from the nose, mouth or anus and a carcass that does not stiffen normally can be anthrax, which is dangerous to people and must be reported.
  • You see blistering or ulcers in the mouth or on the feet with drooling and lameness, which must be reported at once as possible foot and mouth disease.

Lesson 7.215 min

Ticks and Tick-Borne Diseases

In this lesson
  • Recognise the field signs of the four main tick-borne diseases
  • Dip or spray correctly and at the right interval
  • Check whether your tick control is actually working

In most of East and Southern Africa, ticks and the diseases they carry are the largest single disease threat to cattle, and a fattening pen concentrates that risk because you have bought cattle from several places and put them together. Tick control is not an occasional job. It is a schedule you keep whether or not you can see ticks.

The four diseases to know

DiseaseCause and vectorTypical field signs
East Coast feverTheileria parva, carried by the brown ear tickSwollen lymph node in front of the shoulder and under the ear, high fever, difficult breathing, frothing, death often within days in susceptible cattle
Anaplasmosis (gallsickness)Anaplasma, ticks and also needles and biting fliesFever, pale then yellow gums and eyes, weakness, constipation with hard dark dung, no blood in urine
Babesiosis (redwater)Babesia, carried by blue ticksFever, red or brown urine, pale membranes, weakness, sometimes aggression or staggering
HeartwaterEhrlichia ruminantium, carried by bont ticksFever, then nervous signs: high stepping, circling, chewing movements, convulsions with paddling

You cannot reliably tell these apart in the field, and treatment differs. Fever plus dullness in a newly arrived animal in a tick area is a call-the-vet situation, and the earlier the call, the better the outcome. Blood smears taken by a vet or animal health technician give a diagnosis quickly and cheaply.

Dipping and spraying: the discipline

  • Follow the local recommended interval. In high risk East Coast fever areas the long-standing recommendation is weekly treatment during the wet season, lengthening in the dry season. Your district veterinary office knows the local schedule. Follow it rather than a general rule.
  • Never guess the dilution. Mix strictly to the label, measure the water volume, and measure the chemical with a proper measure, not a bottle cap by eye. Under-dosing breeds resistant ticks and leaves animals unprotected. Over-dosing poisons cattle.
  • Top up correctly. A plunge dip loses more chemical than water as cattle carry it out, so replenishment must follow the manufacturer instructions, not simple dilution.
  • Wet the whole animal. With a spray race or knapsack, work in a system: head, ears, neck, brisket, along the back, down both sides, belly, udder or scrotum, under the tail, and between the legs. Ticks attach in exactly the places people miss.
  • Time of day: treat in the cool of the morning, not in the heat, and not on animals that have just travelled.
  • Rotate chemical groups only on veterinary advice. Random switching does not manage resistance.

Checking that it is working

Two days after treatment, restrain a few animals and inspect the predilection sites by hand: inside and around the ears, around the eyes, the dewlap and brisket, the escutcheon and udder or scrotum, under the tail and around the anus, and between the claws. Finding many live engorging ticks after a correct treatment means your dilution, your coverage or your product is failing. Talk to your vet about resistance testing rather than simply doubling the dose.

Safety and the law

  • Wear gloves and eye protection, mix outdoors, and keep concentrate locked away from children and food.
  • Dispose of used dip wash where it cannot reach a river, dam, or borehole. These chemicals are highly toxic to fish.
  • Acaricides have meat withdrawal periods. Read the label and record the date. This matters if slaughter is near.
  • In several countries, dipping at government dip tanks is compulsory and movement of cattle is controlled. Comply, and keep the records you are given.

Do not treat vaccination or immunisation options for East Coast fever as a do-it-yourself matter. Where an immunisation is available it is delivered under veterinary supervision. Ask your veterinary officer what is available and permitted in your district.

Lesson 7.314 min

Worms, Liver Fluke and Deworming

In this lesson
  • Explain why pen cattle usually need only one correct deworming
  • Dose accurately by weight and record it
  • Identify liver fluke risk and choose to ask the right question about products

Internal parasites do not usually kill fattening cattle. They do something quietly more expensive: they reduce feed intake, damage the gut lining and liver, and take a share of the gain you are paying for. Cattle bought from wet grazing areas, river valleys and dambos frequently arrive carrying worms and liver fluke, and you inherit the cost.

What you are dealing with

  • Roundworms of the stomach and intestines. Picked up from grazing. Signs are poor coat, poor condition, scouring, and weak growth despite good feed. Store cattle and young animals are worst affected.
  • Liver fluke. A flatworm with a life cycle that passes through a water snail, so risk is concentrated where cattle graze wet ground: dam margins, marshy vleis, irrigation edges and river banks. Fluke damage shows as poor growth, sometimes bottle jaw (a soft swelling under the jaw) and anaemia, and it causes liver condemnation at the abattoir, which means you lose the value of the liver and may attract abattoir attention.
  • Lungworm and tapeworm matter less in most fattening situations but exist.

The practical rule for a fattener

Because your cattle are in a pen and not re-infecting themselves from pasture, you generally treat once, properly, at arrival, and then you should not need to treat repeatedly during the cycle. That is a real advantage of pen feeding. Treating again and again in a pen wastes money and drives resistance.

  1. At arrival processing, deworm every animal with a product chosen with your vet or animal health technician, based on where the cattle came from and what parasites are common there.
  2. Dose by weight, and dose to the heaviest animal in a weight group rather than the average. Underdosing is the main driver of resistance. Use the girth tape to get real weights.
  3. Check the drenching gun or syringe delivers the volume it claims before you start. Squirt into a measuring cup and confirm.
  4. Give oral drenches over the back of the tongue with the head held level, not high, so the animal swallows rather than inhales.
  5. Record product, batch, dose, date and the withdrawal period from the label for every animal.

Liver fluke needs a specific product

This is the mistake that costs people money. Most ordinary dewormers do not kill liver fluke. A flukicide is a different class of medicine, and some flukicides only kill adult fluke while others also kill immature stages. Which one you need depends on the season and how recently the cattle were exposed. Ask your vet specifically: does this product kill fluke, and does it kill immature fluke. Flukicides commonly carry long meat withdrawal periods, so this is a treatment for arrival, not for the last month.

How to know whether you have a problem

  • Faecal egg counts. Ask your vet or a diagnostic laboratory. Collect fresh dung from the rectum or straight off the ground within minutes, from about ten animals, into separate labelled bags, keep cool and deliver the same day. This is cheap relative to guessing.
  • Abattoir feedback. Ask the abattoir whether livers from your cattle were condemned for fluke. That is free, direct evidence about your source area, and it should change what you do with the next batch.
  • Response to treatment. If dull, poor-doing cattle improve markedly a few weeks after a correct treatment, parasites were part of the problem.

Reducing the risk you buy in

  • Ask sellers where the cattle grazed. Wet ground means assume fluke.
  • Do not let pen cattle graze marshy areas or drink by wading into dam margins during the fattening period.
  • Treat parasite control as part of your purchase cost when you compare two lots of cattle. Cheap cattle from a fluke area are not cheap.

Lesson 7.416 min

Bloat, Acidosis and Respiratory Disease

In this lesson
  • Recognise bloat and take correct emergency action
  • Distinguish acute from subacute acidosis and correct the ration
  • Spot respiratory disease early and get treatment started

Feeding-related emergencies happen fast. Bloat can kill a healthy steer in under an hour, acidosis can wreck a pen's performance in a day, and respiratory disease can turn a productive pen into a treatment ward within a week. All three are more common in the first month, when cattle are adapting to a new ration and recovering from transport.

Bloat

Bloat is gas trapped in the rumen that the animal cannot belch out. The rumen swells, presses on the lungs and heart, and the animal suffocates.

Recognise it: obvious swelling of the upper left flank, sometimes both flanks, so the animal looks lopsided from behind; restlessness, kicking at the belly, stamping; rapid open-mouthed breathing with the neck extended; standing away from the group; in the late stage, staggering and collapse.

What to do:

  1. Get the animal up and walking slowly. Movement alone sometimes releases gas.
  2. Call the vet immediately. Do not wait to see if it improves.
  3. If you or a trained person can pass a stomach tube, do so. Free gas escapes with a rush. Frothy bloat does not, and needs an anti-bloat drench through the tube.
  4. Cutting into the rumen with a trocar is a last-resort life-saving procedure and it belongs to a vet or a properly trained person. Done wrongly it kills the animal.

Prevent it: keep effective long fibre in the ration at all times, avoid finely ground grain, feed at fixed times so cattle never gorge, step concentrate up slowly, and never let cattle onto lush legume forage with an empty rumen.

Acidosis

Acidosis is the result of too much rapidly fermentable starch reaching the rumen too fast. Rumen pH falls, the fibre-digesting microbes die back, and the animal is damaged from the inside.

Acute acidosis: an animal that was fine yesterday is off feed, dull, with a distended splashy-sounding abdomen, watery grey or yellow foul diarrhoea, weakness, staggering, dehydration. This is a veterinary emergency and can be fatal.

Subacute acidosis is the version that quietly costs you money across the pen: variable intake with animals eating well one day and poorly the next, loose bubbly dung with whole grain visible in it, dung consistency varying widely between animals, reduced cud chewing, and lameness appearing two to eight weeks later as laminitis.

What to do now:

  • Pull the concentrate back sharply, to roughly half, and feed good long hay for several days before stepping up again slowly.
  • Make sure clean water is unlimited.
  • Separate and examine the worst animals and involve your vet for individual treatment.
  • Then find the cause: a ration change, a missed feed followed by gorging, poor mixing letting cattle sort grain, a new finer grind, or a bunk management error.

Prevent it: a minimum of effective fibre in the ration, gradual step-ups, consistent feeding times, thorough mixing, and disciplined bunk reading. A buffer such as sodium bicarbonate can help, but it is a support to good management and not a substitute. Discuss inclusion with your vet or a ration adviser.

Respiratory disease

Often called shipping fever, this follows transport, mixing, dust, cold nights and crowding, usually within the first two to four weeks.

Recognise it early: the animal stands apart with drooping ears and a dull eye, does not come to the bunk, breathes faster and deeper than its pen mates, extends its head and neck, has clear then thickened nasal discharge, watery eyes, and coughs, especially when moved. Fever above roughly 39.5 to 40 degrees Celsius is typical.

What to do: take temperatures, isolate affected animals in a clean, sheltered, well ventilated pen with good hay and water, and call your vet the same day. Treatment early works far better than treatment late, and the choice of antibiotic and the withdrawal period must come from a vet. Record every treatment.

Prevent it: rest and water on arrival, avoid mixing groups repeatedly, control dust by damping pens, provide shelter from cold wind and rain, avoid overcrowding, and ask your vet which respiratory vaccinations are appropriate locally.

Lesson 7.514 min

Lameness and Foot Problems

In this lesson
  • Score the pen for lameness weekly
  • Match the common causes to the right first action
  • Remove the pen conditions that cause foot problems

A lame steer stops walking to the bunk, and an animal that does not walk to the bunk does not gain. Lameness is one of the most common and most under-treated problems in fattening pens, and it is largely preventable through the surface the animals stand on.

Find it early

Score the pen as they walk, once a week, on the way to or from the crush or as they move to feed. Look for:

  • A shortened stride, or the head nodding down as the sound foot takes the weight.
  • Standing with one foot rested on the toe, or repeatedly shifting weight.
  • Reluctance to rise, lying more than the pen mates, or arching the back when walking.
  • Any swelling above the hoof, any visible wound, any hoof that looks over-long or has a horizontal ridge or crack.

Mark any lame animal on the day you see it. Lameness treated in the first two days usually resolves. Lameness left for a week can become a bone or joint infection that no treatment will fix, and that animal becomes a loss.

The common causes

ProblemWhat you seeAction
Foot rot (interdigital necrobacillosis)Sudden severe lameness, even swelling of the whole foot above the claws, splitting and a very foul smell between the claws, often feverClean and examine, call the vet. This normally needs an antibiotic prescribed by a vet, and it responds well if treated early
Stone, thorn or wire in the soleSudden lameness in one foot, no swelling above the hoof at firstRestrain safely, lift and clean the foot, find and remove the object, then get veterinary advice on treatment
Laminitis after acidosisSeveral animals, stiff shuffling walk, standing with feet forward, later long misshapen hooves with ridgesTreat this as a ration diagnosis. Fix the feeding as taught in the acidosis lesson
Sole bruising and abrasionGradual lameness in a pen with rough stone or new concreteFix the surface. Cover sharp stone, wear down or grind rough new concrete
Joint or leg injuryNon weight bearing, swelling of a joint, obvious painVeterinary case. Do not simply wait

Handling a lame animal safely

  1. Never examine a foot without proper restraint. A crush with a leg rope, or a purpose-built foot crush, is the minimum. Cattle kick hard and people are seriously injured doing this badly.
  2. Move the animal slowly and on the softest route available.
  3. Wash the foot with clean water so you can see it. Most of the diagnosis is being able to see between the claws.
  4. Do not cut hoof horn unless you have been trained. It is easy to cut into live tissue and make the animal much worse.
  5. Isolate lame animals into a small pen with a soft dry surface, close to feed and water, so they do not have to compete or walk far.

Prevention is mostly the ground

  • Remove stones, wire, nails, broken concrete and sharp objects from pens, races and the crush approach. Walk your handling system barefoot in your mind and imagine standing on it all day.
  • Keep the high traffic areas at the bunk and trough hard, level and drained.
  • Scrape manure regularly. Constant wet manure softens hoof horn and lets bacteria in, which is why foot rot spikes in the rainy season and in muddy pens.
  • Provide a dry lying area at all times.
  • Never run cattle on new rough concrete or over gravel.
  • Ask your vet whether a foot bath or a zinc or mineral supplement is worth using in your situation. Do not set up a chemical foot bath on your own guesswork.

Decision rule: any animal that is not putting weight on a foot at all, any lame animal with a fever, any swelling above the hoof, and any lame animal that is not clearly better within 48 hours of your first action is a veterinary case. Also remember that a severely lame animal may not be fit to transport, and forcing a non-ambulatory animal onto a truck is both cruel and, in most of the region, illegal.

Lesson 7.615 min

Notifiable Disease, Permits, Withdrawal Periods and Your Health Calendar

In this lesson
  • Recognise the signs that legally require immediate reporting
  • Find out what movement paperwork your country requires
  • Calculate a safe slaughter date and build a health calendar

Two things in this lesson are not optional and not negotiable: reporting notifiable disease, and observing drug withdrawal periods before slaughter. Both are legal duties in most countries in the region, and both protect people, not just cattle.

Notifiable diseases and reporting

A notifiable disease is one you are legally required to report to the government veterinary authority when you suspect it. You do not need to be certain. Suspicion is the trigger. The exact list differs by country, so get your own country's list from your district veterinary office and write the office phone number on your pen wall.

Diseases commonly notifiable in the region include foot and mouth disease, anthrax, contagious bovine pleuropneumonia, lumpy skin disease, rabies, and brucellosis. Signs that must make you pick up the phone:

  • Drooling, blisters or raw ulcers in the mouth or on the muzzle, lameness with blisters at the coronary band, several animals affected at once. Suspect foot and mouth disease. Report immediately, stop all movement of cattle on and off the property, and let no visitors into the pens.
  • Sudden death, especially with dark unclotted blood from the nose, mouth or anus, and a carcass that bloats fast and does not stiffen normally. Suspect anthrax. Do not open the carcass. Do not skin it, do not butcher it, do not move it, do not let dogs or people near it. Cover it, keep everyone away and report at once. Anthrax kills people.
  • Any nervous signs such as aggression, staggering, paralysis or excessive salivation. Rabies is a human risk, and heartwater and other causes need diagnosis. Do not handle the mouth.
  • Unusual death rates in your pen or in your neighbours' cattle.

Reporting is not a punishment. Early reporting is how outbreaks are contained, and in most systems it is also how you become eligible for official assistance. Concealing disease is an offence and can cost you your whole herd.

Movement permits

Cattle movement is controlled in most countries here, particularly around foot and mouth disease zones. Before you buy, before you move animals in, and before you send cattle to an abattoir, know the answers to these questions:

  1. What document does my country require to move cattle, and who issues it?
  2. Is my area inside a controlled or buffer zone, and are there standstill periods after arrival?
  3. What identification, branding or tagging is legally required?
  4. Does the abattoir require additional paperwork or a health declaration?

Ask your government veterinary officer directly and get the answer in writing where you can. Cattle stopped at a roadblock without a permit can be seized, and a load turned back at an abattoir gate is a serious loss.

Withdrawal periods

A withdrawal period is the time that must pass between the last dose of a medicine and slaughter, so residues fall to safe levels. It is printed on the label of every product. Withdrawal periods vary enormously between products in the same class, from a few days to many weeks, so never assume and never rely on memory or on what a neighbour says.

  • Read the meat withdrawal period off the label at the moment you treat, and write it in your book with the calculated safe slaughter date.
  • If you cannot read the label, or the product is unlabelled or repackaged, do not use it.
  • Plan treatments backwards from your target slaughter date. Long-withdrawal products such as many flukicides belong at arrival, not in the last month.
  • If an animal must be treated close to slaughter, its slaughter date moves. Meat from an animal inside its withdrawal period is unsafe and can be condemned, and you carry the loss and the reputational damage.

Your herd health calendar

Put one sheet of paper on the wall with the days of your cycle across it, and mark: arrival and processing day, quarantine end, dipping or spraying dates at your local interval, weighing days every 28 to 30 days, ration step-up dates, and the target slaughter date. Then draw a line four weeks before slaughter marked no long-withdrawal treatments after this point without checking the label. Review it every week and tick what you have done.

Knowledge check

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. An animal in your pen dies suddenly overnight with dark blood from the nose and a carcass that bloats quickly. What do you do?

These signs can indicate anthrax, which is dangerous to people. Never open or move the carcass, and report it to the veterinary authority at once.

2. Which statement about liver fluke treatment is correct?

Most general dewormers do not kill fluke. Ask specifically whether the product kills fluke and whether it kills immature stages, and note that flukicides often carry long withdrawal periods.

3. A steer is standing apart with a swollen upper left flank, kicking at its belly and breathing with its mouth open. What is the priority?

This is bloat and it can kill within an hour. Walk the animal and call the vet at once; cutting into the rumen is a last resort for a vet or a trained person.

4. Why must you dose a dewormer to the heaviest animal in a weight group?

Underdosing is a main driver of anthelmintic resistance because it leaves the more resistant parasites alive to breed.

5. What is a meat withdrawal period?

It is printed on the product label, varies widely between products, and meat from an animal slaughtered inside it is unsafe and can be condemned.

Write a one-page health plan for your next batch: quarantine pen and dates, arrival processing list, the local dipping interval confirmed by your veterinary office, your parasite plan with withdrawal periods copied from the labels, your vet's number, and the reporting number for notifiable disease.
