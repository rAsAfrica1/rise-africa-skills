Mango & Citrus Farming

Africa's most widely grown tropical fruits — the 20-year return from grafted trees, flowering management, fruit-fly and HLB defence, hot-water export treatment, and the orchard economics case for the patient investor.

Course Overview

Mango and citrus are Africa's most widely grown tropical fruits and among the highest-earning permanent land uses — once established. The course teaches both the patience and the discipline those returns require. Grafted trees from certified nurseries produce 3–4 years earlier than seedlings, with known variety genetics and rootstock disease tolerance; site selection before planting is the irreversible founding decision (deep well-drained soil, a dry season for mango, frost-free conditions for both). Mango management centres on the flowering cycle: the 2–3 month dry period that triggers it, paclobutrazol as a regulated inducer, nutrition phased by stage (no N at pre-flowering, K and Ca during fill), and the pre-harvest irrigation stop to concentrate sugars. Pest and disease management covers fruit fly (the export disqualifier), mango seed weevil (the quarantine hidden pest), powdery mildew and anthracnose for mango; citrus greening (HLB — the incurable disease), canker, Phytophthora and scale for citrus. Post-harvest discipline determines the channel: hot-water treatment, gentle handling and cold chain for export at 3–5× local price; graded consistent supply for supermarkets at 2–3×; processing for the glut floor. The economics case is built on a 15-year compounding return with inter-crops generating income in years 1–3.

What You Will Learn

  • Choose grafted trees of the right variety for your market channel (local, supermarket, export)
  • Select the site correctly before any planting — drainage, soil depth, frost risk, dry season for mango
  • Establish holes, plant at rain-season start, keep a weed-free circle, form the canopy by year three
  • Manage mango's flowering cycle: dry rest, paclobutrazol option, nutrition by stage, pre-harvest irrigation stop
  • Scout and manage fruit fly, seed weevil, powdery mildew and anthracnose in mango; HLB psyllid, canker and Phytophthora in citrus
  • Harvest at correct maturity stage (float test for mango, Brix ratio for citrus), handle gently, apply HWT for export
  • Intercrop vegetables or beans in years 1–3 to generate income during the establishment patience period
  • Plan the three channels (local, supermarket, export) and build toward the certification that opens each

Lesson 1.1 — Tree crops: the patient investor's cash engine

Mango and citrus are Africa's most widely grown tropical fruits — and among the highest-earning land uses once an orchard is established. The patience premium is real: mango takes 3–7 years to first commercial harvest (grafted trees: 3–4 years; seedling trees: 5–7 years), citrus 3–5 years. But the payoff compounds: a well-managed mango tree yields for 20–40 years, a citrus grove for 15–25 years. The capital is planted once; the labour returns annually. Africa currently exports less than 2% of its potential mango and citrus surplus — the market door is wide open for quality-consistent producers.

  • Mango: Africa's most produced tropical fruit; consumed fresh, dried, juiced and pickled; local markets enormous, export growing rapidly.
  • Citrus (orange, lemon, lime, grapefruit, mandarin): urban juice demand; export for orange and mandarin; lemon and lime in cooking and processing year-round.

Lesson 1.3 — Site selection: the decision before planting

Tree crops are a decades-long commitment: a poorly selected site cannot be fixed after planting. Key requirements: mango tolerates a wide range of soils (well-drained loams to sandy loams; pH 5.5–7.5) but needs a distinct dry season to trigger flowering (2–3 months dry). Citrus needs 1,000–1,500 mm rainfall or irrigation, excellent drainage (waterlogging kills within days), and frost-free temperatures (below -2 °C damages; below -4 °C kills). Both require deep soils without hardpan (roots run 2–4 m in mature trees).

Lesson 2.1 — Propagation: always graft, never seed (for commercial production)

Grafted trees from a certified nursery are the foundation of every commercial orchard: they carry known variety genetics, begin producing 3–4 years earlier than seedlings, and root systems from rootstock can be selected for soil and disease tolerance. Seedling trees are unpredictable (mango is highly heterozygous — seedlings are rarely true to parent). Rules:

  • Source grafted trees from a certified or reputable nursery — ask for rootstock species, graft variety, and any virus or disease free certification.
  • Inspect before purchase: healthy green leaves, no root girdling in the pot, graft union well-healed, no leaf spots or viral symptoms.
  • Plant at the start of the rain season so roots establish in moist soil; if irrigation available, timing is flexible.
  • Hole preparation: 60×60×60 cm minimum; fill base with compost + topsoil mix; P fertiliser in the base (not touching roots).

Lesson 2.3 — Establishing the orchard in years one and two

Young trees (years 1–3) are the most vulnerable: investment so far but no return yet. Priority management: regular watering through the first dry season (a young tree without water in its first dry season may die or be set back 2–3 years in growth); weed control (a 1-metre circle kept clear of weeds around each trunk); formative pruning (removing crossing and inward-facing branches to build an open vase or modified central-leader structure that maximises light penetration and airflow for the 20-year canopy ahead); and training support for young trunks in windy sites.

Month 3: Flowering Management, Nutrition & Irrigation
Locked

Lesson 3.1 — Mango flowering management

Mango flowers on the current season's wood after a dry-cool rest period (2–3 months). In irrigated orchards or areas without a reliable dry season, flowering can be induced by withholding irrigation for 6–8 weeks until slight leaf stress is visible, then restarting. Paclobutrazol (a commercial flowering regulator) is widely used in Southeast Asian and increasing African mangos: applied to the soil or foliage, it halts vegetative growth and triggers floral initiation. It is a real tool but requires training: wrong rates or timing can suppress flowering for 2+ years.

Lesson 3.3 — Irrigation discipline

Mango: withhold irrigation 6–8 weeks before desired flowering; resume at panicle emergence and irrigate through fruit fill and until 4 weeks before harvest (stop to concentrate sugars and improve post-harvest life). Citrus: no dry period needed — maintain consistent soil moisture year-round; drip or micro-jet irrigation at 30–60 L per tree per day in hot dry weather. Both: waterlogging even for 24–48 hours can kill or severely stress a tree in peak growing season — field drainage must work before any irrigation is added.

Lesson 4.2 — Citrus pests and diseases

  • Citrus greening (Huanglongbing / HLB): the most devastating citrus disease globally; transmitted by the Diaphorina citri psyllid; blotchy mottle leaves, lopsided bitter green fruit, tree decline and death over 3–5 years; NO CURE; only removal of affected trees, psyllid control, and certified disease-free nursery stock prevent spread.
  • Citrus canker (Xanthomonas): raised corky lesions on leaves, fruit and stems; bacterial; spreads by rain splash and pruning tools; copper spray, resistant varieties, tool sterilisation.
  • Phytophthora root rot and foot rot: the drainage disease — well-drained sites plus copper-based soil drench where disease occurs.
  • Scale insects and mites: sucking pests causing yellowing and sooty mould; natural enemies (parasitoids) usually control them; spray only at threshold.
  • Fruit fly on citrus: same management as mango.

Month 5: Harvest, Post-Harvest & Value Addition
Locked

Lesson 5.1 — Mango harvest maturity

Mango is climacteric: it ripens after harvest if picked at the right stage. Harvest indicators: shoulders fill above the stem; internal flesh colour (cut test) shows yellow-orange; skin begins to yellow at the nose in some varieties; fruit floats in water (mature) vs sinks (immature). Never pick green unripe mango for home markets — it shrinks and tastes poor. For export, harvest at mature-green under protocol. Post-harvest:

  • Handle with extreme care: mango bruises invisibly (bruises show hours after picking as dark blotches); latex from the stem burn scald skin — cut stems long and away from the fruit; wear gloves.
  • Hot-water treatment (HWT): 48 °C for 60–90 minutes (variety-specific) kills fruit fly and anthracnose — the international export standard.
  • Cooling and cold chain: mango stored at 10–13 °C extends life to 2–4 weeks; ambient temperatures give 5–7 days.

Lesson 5.2 — Citrus harvest

Citrus is non-climacteric: it does not ripen after harvest and must be picked when mature on the tree. Maturity index: internal sugar:acid ratio (the Brix:acid ratio; extension offices have refractometers); external colour is unreliable (Valencia oranges can re-green when temperatures rise — fully ripe but green skinned). Clip rather than pull — stem pull wounds admit post-harvest rots. Post-harvest: wax coating (extends life, improves appearance), fungicide dip (thiabendazole or imazalil), cold storage at 4–8 °C for 4–8 weeks.

Lesson 5.3 — Value addition and processing

  • Dried mango: solar or mechanical drying of ripe slices — premium price in urban and export markets; extension of glut-season surplus.
  • Mango juice / pulp: small-scale press or blender; pasteurised and bottled; longer shelf-life than fresh fruit.
  • Citrus juice, lemon zest and pickled citrus: local processing niches with steady urban demand.

Lesson 6.3 — The orchard growth path

Year 1–3: establishment and zero income — the patience investment; plant inter-crops (vegetables, beans) in the inter-row to generate cash while trees grow. Year 4–6: first harvests, local market, learning post-harvest discipline. Year 7–10: mature yields, quality certified for supermarkets or group export programme. Year 15+: the full return — the compounded annual reward of a well-managed tree crop. The inter-crop in young orchards is the business that keeps the farm alive while the tree investment matures.

📚 Glossary

Grafted tree
A tree produced by joining a desired fruiting variety (scion) to a disease-tolerant rootstock — commercial orchard standard.
Rootstock
The root and lower trunk of a grafted tree, selected for soil adaptation, disease resistance and dwarfing.
Graft union
The healed join between scion and rootstock — inspected at nursery for soundness.
Formative pruning
Pruning in years 1–3 to build the open vase or central-leader canopy structure the mature tree will carry.
Paclobutrazol
A plant growth regulator applied to soil or foliage to halt vegetative growth and trigger mango floral initiation.
Floral initiation
The physiological shift from vegetative to reproductive growth triggered by drought-cool stress in mango.
Panicle
The flower cluster of mango that emerges after the dry-rest period; site of powdery mildew attack.
Hot-water treatment (HWT)
Immersing harvested mango in 48 °C water for 60–90 minutes to kill fruit fly larvae and reduce anthracnose inoculum.
Climacteric fruit
Fruit that continues ripening after harvest (mango, banana, avocado) — can be harvested mature-green.
Non-climacteric fruit
Fruit that must ripen fully on the tree before harvest (citrus) — picking green = permanent underquality.
Fruit fly (Bactrocera / Ceratitis)
Flies whose females sting ripening fruit, laying eggs that hatch into maggots — the export disqualifier.
Mango seed weevil
Invisible quarantine pest inside mango fruit — killed by HWT; export-disqualifying if detected.
Citrus greening (HLB)
Incurable bacterial disease spread by Diaphorina psyllid — the most devastating citrus threat globally.
Phytophthora root rot
Water-mould disease in poorly drained soils — killed by drainage; managed with copper drench.
Brix:acid ratio
Citrus internal maturity index: sugar content (Brix, measured by refractometer) divided by acidity.
Float test
Mango maturity test: mature fruit floats in water (air space in seed cavity); immature sinks.
Phytosanitary certificate
Export document certifying a consignment is free of regulated pests — required by importing countries.
