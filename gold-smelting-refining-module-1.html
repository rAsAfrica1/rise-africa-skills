1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Gold smelting is the process of melting gold-bearing material to separate pure gold from impurities. In Africa, small-scale miners often produce raw gold with mercury or other contaminants. Smelting turns that dirty gold into a clean bar you can sell at a higher price. For example, a gram of impure gold might fetch $40, but after smelting to 99% purity, the same gram can sell for $60 or more. That difference is your profit. This business matters because Africa is rich in gold, yet much of it is exported raw. By smelting locally, you add value, create jobs, and keep more money in your community. You don't need a big factory. A simple charcoal furnace, a crucible, and borax can get you started. Many successful smelters in Ghana, Tanzania, and South Africa began with less than $500 in tools. The key is knowing the right steps and safety rules. This module will teach you the business side: how to set up, what tools to buy, how to smelt safely, and how to avoid common mistakes. You'll also learn how to test your gold and sell it to reputable buyers. Whether you're a miner wanting to upgrade your product or an entrepreneur looking for a new venture, gold smelting offers a practical way to earn income. Remember, gold is heavy and valuable, so even small amounts matter. A single gram of pure gold is worth more than a day's wage for many. With the right skills, you can turn that gram into a reliable business. Let's get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To start smelting gold, you need a few essential tools. First, a furnace. You can build a simple charcoal furnace using a metal bucket, fire bricks, and a blower. A used brake drum works well as a crucible holder. For fuel, charcoal is cheap and available in most African markets. You'll also need a crucible a clay-graphite crucible can handle temperatures up to 1200C and costs about $10. Never use a steel pot; it will melt. Next, a crucible tongs to handle hot crucibles safely. A mold for pouring the molten gold a steel or cast iron ingot mold. For flux, use borax (sodium borate) and soda ash. Borax helps remove impurities and makes the gold flow. You'll also need safety gear: leather gloves, safety goggles, a face shield, and a leather apron. For testing, get a small digital scale accurate to 0.01 grams, and a gold testing kit with acids. A hammer and anvil for shaping. A magnetic stirrer is not needed; a simple graphite rod for stirring. Also, a bucket of water for quenching. For ventilation, set up your furnace outdoors or under a hood. A fire extinguisher is mandatory. In terms of materials, you'll need your raw gold concentrate, borax, soda ash, and possibly a little silver or copper to adjust alloys. Keep a notebook to record weights and temperatures. All these tools can be bought in local markets or online for under $300 total. Start small, and upgrade as you earn.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to smelt gold safely and effectively. Step 1: Prepare your workspace. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Put on all safety gear. Step 2: Weigh your raw gold. For example, you might have 50 grams of gold concentrate. Record the weight. Step 3: Mix flux. For 50 grams of concentrate, use 10 grams of borax and 5 grams of soda ash. Mix well. Step 4: Preheat your furnace. Fill it with charcoal and light it. Use a blower to get the temperature up to 1100C. You can check with a pyrometer or by watching the crucible glow bright orange. Step 5: Place the crucible in the furnace and let it heat for 10 minutes. Step 6: Carefully add your gold and flux mixture into the hot crucible. Close the furnace and let it melt for 20-30 minutes. Stir occasionally with a graphite rod. Step 7: Once the gold is fully molten and shiny, pour it into your preheated mold. Preheat the mold to avoid cracking. Step 8: Let the gold cool for 5 minutes, then quench in water. Step 9: Remove the gold bar and weigh it. You should have about 45 grams of pure gold if your concentrate was 90% pure. Step 10: Clean the bar with a wire brush. Now it's ready to sell. Remember, never pour molten gold into a wet mold it can explode. Always preheat. Also, never leave the furnace unattended. This method works for small batches up to 100 grams. For larger batches, scale up the flux and use a bigger crucible.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many beginners make mistakes that cost them gold and money. Here are the most common and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Using too much flux. Excess borax can eat through your crucible and mix with the gold, lowering purity. Fix: Use the ratio 1 part flux to 5 parts gold. For 50g gold, use 10g flux. Mistake 2: Not preheating the mold. A cold mold causes the gold to splatter and stick. Fix: Heat the mold with a torch until it's hot to the touch (about 200C) before pouring. Mistake 3: Melting in a steel pot. Steel melts at 1400C, close to gold's 1064C, so it can contaminate your gold. Fix: Always use a clay-graphite crucible. Mistake 4: Poor ventilation. Smelting releases toxic fumes like lead and mercury. Fix: Work outdoors or use a fume hood. Wear a respirator. Mistake 5: Not stirring. Unstirred gold can trap impurities. Fix: Stir gently every 10 minutes with a graphite rod. Mistake 6: Overheating. If you get gold too hot, it can vaporize (though only above 2800C, but you can lose silver and copper). Fix: Keep temperature around 1100-1200C. Use a pyrometer. Mistake 7: Skipping the quench. Quenching cools the gold quickly and makes it easier to handle. Fix: Always quench in water after 5 minutes of air cooling. Mistake 8: Not recording weights. You can't calculate your yield or profit. Fix: Weigh before and after. If you started with 50g and got 45g, your recovery is 90%. If it's lower, adjust your flux or temperature. Fix these mistakes and your smelting will be profitable.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional in gold smelting. Molten gold is over 1000C and can cause severe burns. Fumes from impurities like mercury, lead, and arsenic are toxic. Follow these rules to stay safe. First, always wear personal protective equipment (PPE): leather gloves, safety goggles, a face shield, a leather apron, and closed-toe boots. Never wear synthetic clothing it melts. Second, work in a well-ventilated area. Outdoors is best, but if indoors, use an exhaust fan and open windows. A fume hood is ideal. Third, keep a fire extinguisher nearby. A Class D extinguisher for metal fires is best, but a CO2 extinguisher works for small fires. Never use water on a metal fire. Fourth, never smelt alone. Have a buddy who can help in an emergency. Fifth, store chemicals like borax and acids in labeled containers, away from children. Sixth, handle crucibles with tongs only. Never touch a hot crucible. Seventh, preheat molds and tools to avoid steam explosions. Eighth, after smelting, clean your workspace. Gold dust can be swept and recovered. Ninth, dispose of waste properly. Flux waste can contain heavy metals; take it to a hazardous waste facility. Tenth, get trained. If possible, take a short course or apprentice with an experienced smelter. Best practices include keeping a logbook of every smelt: date, weight in, weight out, temperature, and notes. This helps you improve. Also, use a calibrated scale. And always test your gold before selling. A simple acid test can tell you purity. Remember, your health and safety are more valuable than any gold bar. Follow these rules and you'll smelt for years.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. This exercise will guide you through a small smelt using safe methods. You will need: 10 grams of gold concentrate (or clean gold flakes), 2 grams of borax, 1 gram of soda ash, a small clay-graphite crucible, a charcoal furnace, a mold, tongs, and full PPE. If you don't have gold, you can practice with copper or brass to learn the process. Step 1: Set up your furnace outdoors. Light charcoal and use a blower to reach 1100C. Step 2: Weigh your gold and flux. Mix them in a small container. Step 3: Preheat the crucible in the furnace for 5 minutes. Step 4: Carefully add the gold-flux mixture to the crucible. Close the furnace. Step 5: Wait 20 minutes, stirring once after 10 minutes. Step 6: Preheat your mold. Step 7: Pour the molten gold into the mold. Step 8: Let cool for 5 minutes, then quench in water. Step 9: Weigh your gold button. Calculate recovery: (final weight / initial weight) x 100. For 10g input, if you get 9g, recovery is 90%. Step 10: Test purity with an acid test kit. If purity is below 95%, repeat the smelt with fresh flux. Record everything in a notebook. After the exercise, clean your tools and dispose of waste properly. Discuss with your trainer: What went well? What could be improved? How would you scale this up for 100 grams? This exercise costs about $20 in materials if you use copper, or more if using gold. But the experience is invaluable. Do it three times to build confidence. Then you're ready to start your own small smelting business.

<h2>Practical Reference: The Business of Gold Smelting</h2>

<p>
This practical reference is the working toolkit for artisanal and small-scale gold processors, aggregators, and licensed smelters operating in Africa. It consolidates the numbers, tools, techniques, and compliance requirements you need to run a gold smelting operation as a business not just a workshop activity. Whether you are a Ghanaian small-scale miner upgrading from mercury amalgamation, a Tanzanian aggregator setting up a central processing unit, or a Zimbabwean custom smelter serving multiple mines, this lesson gives you the operational benchmarks to plan, cost, execute, and document a smelt.
</p>

<p>
Gold smelting is the controlled melting of gold-bearing concentrate or dor into a homogeneous bar of known purity. In the African context, it is both a technical and a commercial act: the smelt determines the mass and fineness of the bar, which determines the price you receive from a refinery, central bank, or licensed buyer. Getting the smelt right is therefore a direct revenue decision.
</p>

<p style="margin:0;font-family:Arial;font-size:14px;color:#7a0000;">
<strong> SAFETY WARNING MERCURY AND METAL FUMES:</strong> Never smelt mercury-contaminated concentrate or amalgam indoors or without fume extraction. Mercury vapour is odourless, colourless, and toxic at low concentrations; it can cause permanent neurological damage. Smelting must be done in a well-ventilated area, ideally with a fume hood or forced-draft ventilation, and operators must wear appropriate PPE. In many African ASM sites, mercury is still used upstream; if your feed is amalgam-derived, it must be retorted or roasted in a controlled retort <em>before</em> smelting. Molten metal and slag can cause severe burns; always use dry moulds, face shields, and leather aprons. Keep a fire extinguisher and first-aid kit within reach. Follow your national mining and environmental regulations non-compliance can close your business and expose you to prosecution.
</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="font-family:Arial;font-size:14px;line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Licence and permits current:</strong> Confirm your mineral buying/selling licence, environmental permit, and smelting authorisation are valid before accepting feed.</li>
<li><strong>Feed assayed and logged:</strong> Record mass and estimated grade of every batch; never smelt unassayed material without a signed intake sheet.</li>
<li><strong>Flux weighed, not guessed:</strong> Use a calibrated scale for borax, soda ash, and silica; keep a recipe log per ore type.</li>
<li><strong>Furnace at temperature:</strong> Verify 11001200 C with a thermocouple or pyrometer; do not rely on colour alone.</li>
<li><strong>Two-person weigh and pour:</strong> No single person should handle dor alone this is both a security and a safety control.</li>
<li><strong>Sample every bar:</strong> Take a drill or dip sample for fire assay or XRF before sale; keep the assay certificate with the bar.</li>
<li><strong>Chain-of-custody log complete:</strong> Record feed in, dor out, slag mass, and any returns; reconcile daily.</li>
<li><strong>Royalty and tax remitted:</strong> Pay and document government royalties and taxes on every sale; keep receipts for audit.</li>
</ol>
