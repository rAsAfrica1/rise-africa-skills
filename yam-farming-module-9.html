1. Stages of Yam Growth from Planting to Maturity

Yam growth has four clear stages. Knowing them helps you care for the crop correctly.

Stage 1: Sprouting (04 weeks after planting). The sett (seed yam) breaks dormancy and sends out roots and a shoot. Keep the soil moist but not waterlogged. If you planted in ridges, check that the sett is covered with 58 cm of loose soil. Sprouting is faster in warm, moist soil (2530C). In dry conditions, sprouting may take 68 weeks.

Stage 2: Vine Growth (13 months). The vine emerges and climbs. This is when the plant builds leaf area. Provide stakes earlywhen vines are 1520 cm long. Use strong stakes 23 m tall. Train vines gently around the stake in a clockwise direction. Weeds compete heavily now; keep the field clean. Apply first fertilizer (NPK 15-15-15) at 2 months after planting, about 200 kg per hectare, in a ring 1015 cm from the plant base.

Stage 3: Tuber Bulking (36 months). The tuber grows rapidly in size and weight. The vine stops elongating and leaves become darker green. This is the most critical stage for water. In dry spells, irrigate if possibleabout 2030 mm per week. Avoid disturbing the soil around the mound. Earthing up (adding soil to the mound) helps tuber expansion and prevents exposure to sun, which turns tubers green and bitter. Do a second fertilizer application (NPK 15-15-15) at 4 months, 150 kg per hectare.

Stage 4: Maturity (610 months, depending on variety). Leaves yellow and dry, vines die back. The tuber skin hardens and becomes rough. Stop watering 23 weeks before harvest to avoid rot. For most white yam varieties, maturity is 810 months after planting; for yellow yam, 79 months. You can test maturity by digging a small tuberif the skin is tough and the flesh is firm, it's ready. Harvest carefully with a spade or digging stick to avoid bruising. Proper timing gives better storage life.

2. Monitoring Growth and Development

Monitoring means checking your yam field regularly to catch problems early. Walk your field at least once a week. Look at vines, leaves, and soil. Record what you see.

Start with vine length. Measure from the base to the tip of the main vine. Healthy vines grow 510 cm per week during the first 3 months. If growth is slow, check for pests, disease, or lack of water. Look at leaf color. Dark green means good nitrogen. Yellow leaves may mean nitrogen deficiency or waterlogging. Brown, dry leaf edges often mean potassium deficiency. Count the number of leaves per planta healthy plant has 2030 leaves by 3 months.

Check for pests and diseases. Common pests: yam beetle (small holes in tubers), nematodes (stunted growth, galls on roots), and aphids (curled leaves). Diseases: anthracnose (black spots on leaves and vines), yam mosaic virus (mottled leaves), and rot (soft, smelly tubers). If you see more than 5% of plants affected, take action. For anthracnose, remove and burn infected vines. For aphids, spray with neem oil (5 ml per liter of water) every 7 days.

Monitor soil moisture. Dig 10 cm deep with a hand trowel. If soil is dry and crumbly, you need water. If it's soggy, improve drainage. In sandy soil, check every 3 days. In clay soil, every 5 days. Also check mound height. Over time, rain and weeding flatten mounds. Keep mounds at least 30 cm high.

Use a notebook or a simple record sheet. Write the date, what you saw, and what you did. For example: '12 June: 10% of plants have yellow leaves. Applied 100 g NPK per plant.' This helps you learn and improve next season. Take photos with a phone if you have one. Share with extension officers if you need help.

3. Thinning and Gap Filling

Thinning and gap filling ensure you have the right number of healthy plants per hectare. After sprouting, check your field for gaps (missing plants) and doubles (two plants in one mound).

Gap filling: If a sett fails to sprout, replant with a new sett. Do this within 46 weeks after planting, while the soil is still moist. Use a healthy sett of the same variety. Dig a small hole in the same mound, place the sett 58 cm deep, and cover with loose soil. Water lightly if dry. Replanting later than 6 weeks gives weak plants that yield poorly. In a 1-hectare field, you might have 510% gaps. Fill them to maintain plant populationaim for 10,000 to 12,000 plants per hectare (spacing 1 m x 0.8 m).

Thinning: Sometimes two or more sprouts come from one sett. This causes competition. Remove the weaker sprouts, leaving only one strong vine per mound. Do this when sprouts are 1520 cm tall. Use a sharp knife or hand to cut the extra sprouts at soil level. Don't pull, as you may disturb roots. Thinning gives the remaining vine more nutrients and water, leading to bigger tubers.

Also remove any diseased or stunted plants. If a plant is yellow and weak, it may be infected. Pull it out and burn it. Then fill the gap with a new sett if still early in the season.

After thinning and gap filling, apply a light mulch (dry grass or leaves) around the plants to conserve moisture and suppress weeds. This is especially important in dry areas. Keep records: note how many gaps you filled and how many sprouts you removed. This helps you plan for better seed yam selection next time.

4. Earthing Up and Mound Maintenance

Earthing up means adding soil to the base of yam plants to cover the growing tuber and keep the mound in shape. It is one of the most important operations for good tuber development.

Why earthing up? Yam tubers grow upwards and outwards. If they break the soil surface, they turn green (sunburn) and become bitter. Green tubers also rot easily. Earthing up covers the tuber with soil, keeps it dark, and supports the vine. It also improves drainage and reduces weed growth.

When to earth up: Start when vines are about 3040 cm long (about 2 months after planting). Do it again at 4 months and 6 months if needed. In sandy soils, earth up more often because mounds erode faster.

How to earth up: Use a hoe or hand trowel. Pull soil from between the rows to the base of the plant. Build the mound up to 3040 cm high and 5060 cm wide. Be careful not to damage the roots or the tuber. Work when soil is slightly moist, not wet. If the soil is dry, water lightly first to make it easier to work.

Mound maintenance: After heavy rain, check mounds. If they are flattened, rebuild them. Also remove weeds at the same time. Weeds compete for nutrients and can harbor pests. Hand-weed or use a hoe, but don't dig deep near the plant roots. Mulching with dry grass after earthing up helps keep the mound intact and conserves moisture.

In dry areas, you can make sunken beds or use a 'tied ridge' system where you tie the ridges with grass to hold water. This reduces erosion and keeps the mound stable. Always keep the mound free of trash and stones. A well-maintained mound gives you larger, marketable tubers. Do this every season and you will see the difference in your harvest.

5. Managing Environmental Stress (Heat, Drought)

Yam is sensitive to heat and drought. In many parts of Africa, dry spells can reduce yields by 3050% or more. You can reduce the damage with simple practices.

Mulching: Cover the soil around your yam mounds with a 510 cm layer of dry grass, straw, or leaves. Mulch keeps the soil cool, reduces evaporation, and suppresses weeds. Apply mulch after earthing up. Do not use fresh green material, as it may attract termites. In a 1-hectare field, you need about 23 tonnes of dry mulch.

Water management: If you have irrigation, water during dry spells. Give about 2030 mm per week. Early morning or evening is best to reduce evaporation. If you don't have irrigation, dig small trenches between rows to capture rainwater. You can also use 'zai' pits (small planting holes filled with manure) to concentrate water. In very dry areas, plant drought-tolerant varieties like 'TDr 89/02665' or local landraces that mature early.

Shade: Yam vines can be shaded by intercropping with tall crops like maize, sorghum, or pigeon pea. Plant these on the edges or between every 4th row. They provide partial shade and reduce heat stress. But don't overcrowdtoo much shade reduces yam growth.

Timing: Plant early in the rainy season so that the critical tuber bulking stage (36 months) gets enough water. If you plant late, the crop may face drought at bulking. In dry savannah areas, plant with the first consistent rains.

Heat stress: When temperatures exceed 35C, yam leaves may wilt and drop. Mulch and shade help. Avoid applying fertilizer during extreme heatit can burn roots. Instead, apply after a good rain.

If drought is severe, you may need to harvest early. Small tubers are better than no tubers. After harvest, store in a cool, dark place. Record the weather and your actions. This helps you plan for next season. Remember, healthy soil with organic matter holds water better. Add compost or manure every year to improve water retention.

6. Record Keeping and Crop Logs

Keeping records helps you learn from each season and make better decisions. You don't need a computera simple notebook and pen work fine.

What to record: Start with planting date, variety, and source of seed yam. Then record each operation: date of sprouting, date of first fertilizer, date of earthing up, date of pest or disease seen, and date of harvest. Also record weather: rainfall (use a simple rain gauge or note rainy days), temperature (if you have a thermometer), and any extreme events like drought or flood.

For each operation, note the quantity and method. For example: '15 May: Applied 200 kg NPK 15-15-15 per hectare. Broadcast in ring around plants.' Or '20 July: Earthing up. Mounds rebuilt to 35 cm high.' If you see pests, write what you saw and what you did: '5 August: Saw aphids on 10% of plants. Sprayed neem oil (5 ml/L) on affected areas.'

At harvest, record yield. Weigh a sample of 10 tubers from different parts of the field. Calculate average tuber weight. Then estimate total yield: number of plants x average tuber weight. For example, 10,000 plants x 2 kg = 20,000 kg (20 tonnes) per hectare. Also record tuber quality: size, shape, and any damage.

Keep a simple crop log for each field. Use a table with columns: Date, Activity, Details, Cost. Include costs of seed, fertilizer, labour, and tools. This helps you calculate profit. At the end of the season, review your log. What worked? What didn't? For example, if you had many gaps, you might buy better seed yams next time. If drought reduced yield, you might invest in mulch or irrigation.

Share your records with other farmers or extension officers. They can give advice. Over time, your records become a valuable guide for your farm. Even a small notebook can double your yields by helping you avoid past mistakes.

<h2>Practical Reference: Yam Growth Stages and Crop Care</h2>

<p>Yam (<em>Dioscorea</em> spp.) is one of West Africa's most profitable root and tuber crops, with Nigeria alone producing over 60% of global output. For agribusiness operators, the difference between a 12 t/ha harvest and a 25 t/ha harvest is rarely about luck it is about disciplined crop care across six well-defined growth stages. This Practical Reference distils the agronomy into decision-ready guidance you can apply in the field and in your enterprise records. Whether you are running 2 hectares or 20, the same principles govern yield, tuber grade, and market price.</p>

<h3>Why Growth-Stage Discipline Drives Profit</h3>
<p>Yam is a long-duration crop: 812 months from planting to harvest depending on variety and agro-ecology. Because capital (seed yams, labour, stakes) is locked up for most of a year, every management decision compounds. Late weeding, missed earthing up, or unmanaged drought stress can cut marketable tuber weight by 3050%. Conversely, timely thinning, gap filling, and mound maintenance raise the proportion of first-grade tubers that attract premium prices in urban markets and for export.</p>

<h3>Stage-by-Stage Management Priorities</h3>

<p><strong>Stage 1 Planting and Sprouting (Weeks 04).</strong> Seed yams are planted in mounds or ridges at the onset of rains. Ensure seed pieces are 200500 g, disease-free, and treated where necessary. This is the window for <em>gap filling</em> replant any sett that fails to sprout within 34 weeks.</p>

<p><strong>Stage 2 Vine Emergence (Weeks 48).</strong> First vines break the soil surface. Staking must be completed now; delayed staking causes vine tangling and reduces photosynthetic efficiency. Monitor for termites and yam beetles.</p>

<p><strong>Stage 3 Vegetative Growth (Weeks 820).</strong> Rapid leaf and vine expansion. Weed competition is most damaging here. Earthing up begins as tubers start to form and mounds settle.</p>

<p><strong>Stage 4 Tuber Initiation (Weeks 1624).</strong> Tubers begin to differentiate and elongate downward. Avoid root disturbance. Maintain moisture; drought now causes malformed, short tubers.</p>

<p><strong>Stage 5 Tuber Bulking (Weeks 2036).</strong> The yield-determining phase. 7080% of final tuber weight accumulates here. Consistent moisture, adequate potassium, and pest control are critical.</p>

<p><strong>Stage 6 Maturity and Senescence (Weeks 3648).</strong> Vines yellow and die back. Stop irrigation. Harvest 24 weeks after full senescence for maximum starch content and storage life.</p>

<h3>Monitoring Growth and Development</h3>
<p>Scout weekly. Record vine length, leaf colour, pest incidence, and mound condition. Use simple visual indicators: pale yellow leaves signal nitrogen deficiency; purple-tinged leaves may indicate phosphorus shortage; leaf scorch points to potassium deficiency or heat stress. Early detection converts a potential 20% loss into a corrective action costing a fraction of the crop value.</p>

<h3>Thinning and Gap Filling</h3>
<p>Where two or more sprouts emerge from one sett, thin to the single strongest vine at 46 weeks. This concentrates assimilates into one tuber rather than splitting energy across competing shoots. Gap filling should be completed by week 6; later replants will be outcompeted and rarely reach marketable size. Keep a nursery of spare sprouted setts for this purpose.</p>

<h3>Earthing Up and Mound Maintenance</h3>
<p>Earthing up drawing soil around the base of the vine protects developing tubers from sunlight (which causes greening and bitterness), improves drainage, and suppresses weeds. Do it twice: at tuber initiation (around week 1620) and again 46 weeks later. In sandy soils or on slopes, mounds erode quickly; inspect after every heavy rain and rebuild as needed.</p>

<h3>Managing Environmental Stress: Heat and Drought</h3>
<p>Yam is sensitive to moisture stress during tuber initiation and bulking. In the savannah zones of Nigeria, Ghana, and Benin, dry spells of 23 weeks during these stages can reduce yield by up to 40%. Practical mitigations include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Mulching with crop residues to conserve soil moisture and lower soil temperature.</li>
<li>Planting on contour ridges or in moisture-retentive loams where possible.</li>
<li>Supplementary irrigation (even 2030 mm per week) during bulking if water is available.</li>
<li>Shade management: avoid excessive vine removal that exposes soil to direct sun.</li>
</ul>
<p>Heat stress above 35C reduces photosynthesis and can cause flower abortion in flowering varieties. Where feasible, intercropping with maize or legumes during early stages provides partial shade without competing heavily for water.</p>

<h3>Record Keeping and Crop Logs</h3>
<p>For agribusiness, records are not bureaucracy they are the basis for credit access, traceability, and replanting decisions. Maintain a field log per plot with: planting date, variety, sett weight, sprouting percentage, weeding dates, fertiliser applications, pest/disease events, rainfall, and harvest weight by grade. This data lets you calculate cost per hectare, yield per sett, and return on investment the numbers banks and off-takers ask for.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul style="line-height:1.8;">
<li> Plant only certified, disease-free seed yams of 200500 g.</li>
<li> Complete gap filling within 4 weeks of planting.</li>
<li> Stake vines by week 6 to prevent tangling.</li>
<li> Thin multiple sprouts to one vine per sett by week 6.</li>
<li> Earth up mounds at tuber initiation and again 46 weeks later.</li>
<li> Scout weekly for pests, leaf colour, and moisture stress.</li>
<li> Apply mulch or supplementary irrigation during dry spells in bulking phase.</li>
<li> Log all field operations, inputs, and harvest weights per plot.</li>
</ul>
