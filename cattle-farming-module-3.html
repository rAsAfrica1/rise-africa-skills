Module 3

🐄 The Breeding Herd

The breeding herd exists to produce one thing: a calf from every cow, every year. The evidence says most African cows do not manage it, with communal calving rates measured as low as 15 to 23 percent against 61 percent in the commercial sector of the same country. This module works through the four levers a smallholder actually controls - the bull, the breeding season, the heifer, and the culling decision - and shows why raising calving percentage is almost always worth more than adding cows.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Calculate a calving percentage from your own herd numbers
  • State the reference bull-to-cow ratios and where they come from
  • Explain what a breeding soundness examination checks
  • Explain what a defined breeding season makes possible
  • Explain the cost of a late age at first calving
  • Rank the three culling priorities in order of evidence

Lesson 3.1~12 min

The Number That Decides Everything

In this lesson
  • Calculate a calving percentage from your own herd numbers
  • Compare the sourced communal and commercial calving rates honestly
  • Explain why raising calving percentage beats adding cows

There is one number that decides whether your herd is a business or an expensive habit, and most cattle owners have never worked it out for their own animals.

It is called the calving percentage. Count the cows and heifers you exposed to a bull last year. Count the calves born to them. Divide the second by the first and multiply by a hundred. Twenty cows, eight calves, forty percent. That is the whole calculation, and you can do it tonight with a pencil.

Now here is why it matters more than the size of your herd.

A cow that eats grass, drinks water, occupies land, gets dipped and gets watched for a full year, and does not produce a calf, has cost you a full year of everything for nothing. Not a small loss. A complete one. And the evidence says this happens to the majority of cows in many African systems, not to a few unlucky ones.

Here is what was measured:

  • A South African comparison found commercial-sector calving rates of 61 percent against communal-sector rates of 23 percent in the same country. That is commercial herds calving roughly 2.7 times as often, per cow, per year.
  • One communal survey in Venda, in Limpopo, South Africa, found a calving rate as low as 15 percent.
  • A southern Ethiopia dataset averaged 55 percent, ranging from 12 percent to 83 percent across the herds surveyed.
  • Zambian data in the same review ranged from 44 to 80 percent.
  • A twelve-year dataset from Mazvihwa in southern Zimbabwe ranged from 68 to 82 percent.
  • The review summarised the continental picture as roughly six in ten cows in Africa failing to produce a calf in a given year - an approximate 40 percent average across communal cattle-keeping.

Be careful with those numbers in the right way. They were reported in a farming-press review article summarising several underlying studies. The individual primary papers behind the South African, Ethiopian, Zambian and Zimbabwean figures were not independently retrieved and re-checked for this course. So treat each country figure as that review's own citation rather than as something this course verified line by line. What is beyond argument is the direction: a large, persistent, well-documented gap between communal and commercial calving rates across several African countries, consistent in every figure retrieved. Believe the gap. Do not treat any single percentage as a law.

Now the arithmetic that should change what you do next.

Take twenty cows. At a 40 percent calving rate, they wean 8 calves in a year. The same twenty cows at 70 percent - a realistic commercial figure, not a fantasy - wean 14 calves. The land is the same. The labour is the same. The bull, the dip, the water bill are very nearly the same. Six extra calves, from the same twenty cows, for almost no extra cost.

That is the entire economic argument of this course. Raising your calving percentage is usually a far bigger lever on your income than growing your herd. A large herd of unproductive cows is not the same business as a smaller herd of productive ones, and it is often the worse business, because every unproductive animal is still eating.

Before you blame yourself, understand why the rate is low, because it is not only about how hard any one farmer tries. A review of African livestock economics names several structural reasons. Inadequate dry-season grazing and feed is described as probably the single most important constraint on productivity. Disease burden, both epidemic and vector-borne, is named as a second. Cattle serving several purposes at once - draught power, manure, milk, savings, social standing - directly competes with optimising any single output such as calf production, and a cow pulling a plough or feeding a household is not free to be managed purely for calving efficiency. And weak markets, poor price signals and thin breeding-input supply are named as a fourth.

Some of that you cannot fix alone. Land tenure, market access and the disease environment of your district are bigger than one farmer. But bull management, a defined breeding season, dry-season feed planning, a health calendar, records and culling discipline are all inside your control, and they are what the rest of this course is about. Nobody honest will promise you commercial-ranch numbers. Measured, real improvement is a different promise, and the evidence supports it.

The Bull Is Half the Calf Crop

In this lesson
  • State the reference bull-to-cow ratios and where they come from
  • Recognise an overworked bull as a herd-wide calving problem
  • Decide when your herd needs a second bull

Half the genetics of every calf you will ever wean comes from the bull. And in a natural-mating herd, which is nearly every smallholder herd in Africa, one hundred percent of your conceptions depend on him being present, fertile, and physically able to cover your cows inside the mating period.

Artificial insemination is a commercial and research-station tool, needing semen supply, a trained technician, heat detection and cold storage. Where it is genuinely available it is powerful. But it is not the default in most of the settings this course is written for, so this lesson assumes a bull walking with your cows.

A weak, old, injured, too-young or simply outnumbered bull is one of the most common causes of a low calving percentage - and one of the most fixable. That combination is why this lesson comes so early.

Start with the arithmetic of workload. A commonly used United States extension guideline recommends one mature bull per 20 to 30 cows, with fully mature bulls able to work closer to the 1 to 30 end of that range. For a yearling bull - young, not yet fully mature - the same guideline drops to one bull per 20 cows, because a young bull has less mating stamina and should not be worn out in his first breeding season.

Now the caveat, and it is important. That is a temperate, United States commercial-ranch figure. No African-measured or African extension bull-to-cow ratio recommendation was obtained for this course. So use it as a starting point for a conversation with your own extension officer or veterinarian, not as an imported rule. The right ratio for a big rangeland paddock where cows are spread over kilometres of bush is not obviously the same as for a fenced camp, and nobody in the sources read here measured it for African conditions.

The same source adds a useful second figure. Where oestrus is synchronised - a technique that uses a hormonal product and therefore needs veterinary input, and which is governed by veterinary-medicine rules that differ by country - one mature bull can be asked to cover 15 to 30 cows confined to a small area, if most of those cows are expected to come into heat inside a tight window of roughly five days. Notice what that tells you. It is a much heavier instantaneous workload than the same bull carries across a spread-out season, because all the work arrives at once.

And that points at the practical lesson for you even if you never synchronise anything. A bull's limit is not really a number of cows. It is a number of cows coming into heat at the same time. Cows spread over a long, uncontrolled mating period are easier on a bull than the same cows tightly grouped. A tight breeding season, which the next lessons argue hard for, deliberately concentrates that workload - so if you tighten your season, you must check that your bull can carry it.

Here is what happens when a bull is outnumbered. He serves the cows he can reach and misses others, and a cow he fails to settle at her first heat comes back roughly three weeks later. If he misses her again, she comes back again. If the season ends before she is caught, she is open for the whole year. One overworked bull can pull down the calving percentage of an entire herd, and you will not see it happening. You will see it nine months later, as calves that never arrived.

So as your cow numbers grow past the top of whatever ratio your local adviser recommends, a second bull stops being a luxury and becomes a requirement. There are two ways to do it. Keep two bulls, which costs feed, grazing and handling risk all year for two animals. Or rotate bulls in and out of the herd during the season, which spreads the work and lets a tired bull recover, and which some farmers organise by sharing or hiring a bull with a neighbour.

Bull hire or sharing is worth serious thought if you have few cows, because a bull is expensive to own and idle most of the year. Two risks: disease, since a shared bull moving between herds moves infection with him, so ask what he has been exposed to and tested for; and the calendar, since he is only useful if he arrives in the weeks your cows should be served.

When you cost your herd, the bull's cost belongs to the cows he serves. Take his purchase price spread over his working years, or his hire fee, and divide it across the cows he covers. That one line tells you quickly whether a bull standing over five cows is affordable.

Bull Soundness: The Exam Before the Season

In this lesson
  • Explain what a breeding soundness examination checks
  • Use scrotal circumference as a fertility warning sign, with its limits
  • Separate libido from fertility when judging a bull

A bull's fertility is not guaranteed by how he looks, how old he is, or what he did last season. This is the sentence that costs African herds more calves than almost any other single misunderstanding.

Think about how a bull failure actually plays out. You put him in. He walks with the cows. He mounts them. Everything looks normal. Nine months later, the calves do not come. By then you have lost an entire production year across your whole herd, and there is no way to get it back. The cows cannot re-do the season. The grass they ate is gone.

The tool that exists to prevent exactly this is a breeding soundness examination. It is a veterinary check done before the season, not after, and it covers three things: physical soundness, semen quality, and scrotal circumference.

Physical soundness means the parts of a bull that let him do the job. Feet and legs, because a bull with a sore foot will not walk far enough to find cows in a big paddock. Eyes, because he has to see them. Body condition, because mating is hard work. And the reproductive organs themselves, examined properly rather than glanced at.

Semen quality means a sample examined under a microscope. This is the part you cannot do yourself and cannot guess from the outside, and it is the part that catches the bull who looks perfect and is not.

Scrotal circumference is a tape measurement, and it has a real, measured link to fertility. In the sourced data, at a scrotal circumference of 32 centimetres, only 23 percent of the bulls examined had satisfactory semen quality, and the proportion improved as circumference increased toward 38 centimetres.

Read that carefully, because there are two lessons in it. The first is that a visibly small scrotal circumference is a genuine, measurable red flag, not a detail of appearance. If fewer than a quarter of bulls at 32 centimetres had satisfactory semen, then a small measurement is a strong reason to test before you trust.

The second lesson is the caveat, and this course will not hide it. Those figures are United States derived. Their breed and body-size assumptions may not transfer to a smaller-framed indigenous African bull, and no African dataset linking scrotal circumference to fertility was obtained for this course. So do not walk out with a tape and condemn a Nguni or Tuli bull because he does not measure 38 centimetres. Use the principle - scrotal size is worth measuring and a small one is worth investigating - and get the appropriate threshold for your breed and region from a veterinarian who works with those cattle. If you want the number for your own breed, it must come from your national livestock research institute or breed society, because it is not in this course.

Now the finding that catches the most experienced farmers out. Libido and semen quality are independent traits. A bull that mounts cows eagerly, chases them, fights other bulls and looks every inch a working sire can still be subfertile. And a bull with excellent semen quality can be lazy and uninterested. They are two separate things, and one does not predict the other.

This matters because the way most farmers check a bull is by watching him. Watching tells you about libido. It tells you nothing about semen. A keen bull is not proof of a fertile bull, and the eagerness that reassures you may be exactly what stops you testing.

How do you act on this if a veterinarian is far away or expensive? Do what you can, in order. First, look at him properly at least once before every season - feet, legs, eyes, body condition, the sheath and testicles for swelling, injury or asymmetry. An obviously injured or lame bull is a problem you can find yourself for free. Second, measure the scrotum with a tape and write the figure down every year, because the change in one bull over time is information you own even if you do not have a threshold. Third, ask what a breeding soundness examination costs in your district, and set it against the value of one calf. If the exam costs less than a single weaner, and it protects a whole season's calf crop, the arithmetic is not close.

And there is one thing that makes all of this possible, which is the subject of the next lesson. An exam before the season only means something if there is a season - a defined period when the bull is in and a defined date when he comes out. A bull who lives with the cows all year has no before and no after, so there is no moment when testing him would change anything.

Putting the Bull In and Taking Him Out

In this lesson
  • Explain what a defined breeding season makes possible
  • Describe why pregnancy testing depends on a defined season
  • Plan a bull-in and bull-out date for your own herd

Most African smallholder bulls live with the cows every day of the year. It feels natural, it costs nothing to arrange, and it quietly destroys almost every management tool in this course.

A defined breeding season means you decide the date the bull goes in with the cows, and the date he comes out. Nothing more complicated than that. But look at what it unlocks.

First, it creates a calving season. Cows served inside a fixed window calve inside a matching window, roughly nine months later. That means calves of similar age, so you can wean, vaccinate, dose, castrate and market them as one group, in one handling. A herd calving in all twelve months makes every one of those jobs a separate trip to the kraal. Module 4 takes the calving season further.

Second, it makes the bull soundness examination from the last lesson worth doing. There is now a before.

Third, and this is the one most farmers have never thought about, it makes an open cow visible. A cow that never conceives is invisible when the bull runs all year, because she always has a chance still to come. There is no moment when you can say she has failed. Once you have a bull-out date, everything after it is a deadline. A cow that is not pregnant when the bull leaves has failed, definitely and measurably, and now you can do something about it.

That is what makes pregnancy testing useful. It is done by a veterinarian or trained technician, by hand or by ultrasound, some weeks after the bull is removed, and it tells you which cows are carrying a calf. Without a defined season the test says little, because a cow found not pregnant today might have been served last week. With one, it tells you exactly who failed this year, months before the calving that will not happen.

That is worth real money. You can sell an open cow while she still carries condition, instead of feeding her through a dry season for a calf that is not coming. And if many cows test open at once, that is a herd-level signal - it points at the bull, a disease, or a feed collapse, not at bad luck spread across individuals.

Fourth, a defined season lets you match the herd's biggest feed demand to your grass. A cow's demand peaks when she is feeding a calf, and if that peak lands in your worst dry-season month every year, she will lose condition and fail to rebreed, and you will have paid for the privilege.

So how long should the season be, and when should it start? Here this course must stop and be honest with you. No specific recommended calving-season length or calendar timing for any named African region was obtained for this course. It depends on your rainfall pattern, your grass species, and how long your dry season actually runs, and inventing a number would be worse than useless. That figure must come from your national extension service, your district livestock officer, or the nearest well-run commercial ranch, who can answer it for your specific rainfall zone. Ask them two questions: which month should my cows calve in, and how many weeks should my bull be with them.

What you can do without waiting for anyone is start. Here is the honest practical path, because you cannot go from a year-round bull to a tight season in one step.

  1. Pick the date you take the bull out first, not the date you put him in. Removing him is the change that costs nothing and starts the clock.
  2. Have somewhere to put him. This is the real obstacle, and pretending otherwise helps nobody. A bull needs a separate kraal, a fenced camp, a tether, or a neighbour who will hold him. Solve that first.
  3. In the first year, take him out for a short period only. Even eight or ten weeks away creates a genuine calving gap and starts to group your calves.
  4. Each year, lengthen the time he is out and shorten the time he is in, as far as your feed, your fencing and your local advice support.
  5. As soon as you have a bull-out date, arrange pregnancy testing after it, and start recording who was open.

A word of warning about tightening too fast. A short season means every cow must conceive in a narrow window or produce nothing that year. If your cows are thin, your bull untested, or your dry-season feed unplanned, a very tight season can cut your calving percentage instead of raising it. Tighten it in step with the condition of your cows and the soundness of your bull, not ahead of them.

Heifers, Body Condition and the Calving Interval

In this lesson
  • Explain the cost of a late age at first calving
  • Compare the sourced ages at first calving across breeds and systems
  • Describe why body condition after calving decides the next pregnancy

Two things quietly decide how many calves a cow produces in her lifetime. How old she was when she had her first one, and how quickly she comes back for the next. Neither is a mystery, and both come down mostly to feed.

Start with age at first calving. The sourced figures show how much it varies by breed and system.

  • Tuli, measured in Zimbabwe: 36 months
  • Bonsmara, from the breed's own figures: 32 months
  • Jersey, from the African dairy sector comparison: about 30.7 months
  • Holstein-Friesian, same comparison: about 33.8 months

Read that grouping carefully. Beef breeds under extensive rangeland management calve noticeably later than intensively managed dairy cattle. That is not mainly genetics. It is growth rate. A heifer calves when she has grown big enough to conceive and carry a calf, and one growing on unsupplemented veld and crop residue takes longer to get there.

Now the cost, in the plainest arithmetic in this module. If a heifer that could calve at 30 months instead calves at 36 months, she has spent six extra months eating your grass, drinking your water, occupying your carrying capacity and needing your dipping, before her first calf is even born. Six months of full cost and zero output. Multiply that by every heifer you raise, every year, and the size of the leak becomes obvious.

So the biggest lever you have over age at first calving is nutrition and growth rate to breeding weight. For most smallholders that is a grazing and dry-season forage question, not a ration question, because your heifers grow on veld and crop residue, not on a compounded feed. Which grazing they get, whether the growing heifers are pushed to the back of the queue behind the big cows, and whether they lose condition every dry season and have to regain it, all decide how old they are when they first conceive. If you want to build an actual balanced ration for growing heifers, that belongs to the separate Cattle and Steer Feed Formulation course, and this course will not half-teach it.

Now the second half: the calving interval, meaning the time from one calving to the next.

A cow must regain enough body condition after calving to conceive again in the same season if she is to hold a roughly twelve-month interval. If she does not, she slips. And slipping compounds. Look at what the sourced intervals actually are:

  • The Boran breed society reports a 365-day interval as achievable in well-managed pedigree and ranch herds.
  • Tuli, measured: 407 days.
  • Bonsmara, from breed figures: 407 days.

A 407-day interval is about thirteen and a half months. That cow calves roughly six weeks later every year. After four years she has drifted a full season out of position, calving in the wrong month for your grass, and eventually she misses a year entirely.

Why does she slip? Because she is being asked to do two expensive things at once - feed a growing calf, and start another pregnancy - out of the same body reserves. A thin cow that is still nursing a heavy calf is competing with her own next pregnancy, and biology settles that argument in favour of the calf already born.

The strongest sourced evidence for this comes from a South African trial you will meet properly in Module 4. When calves were weaned early at 90 days instead of at 205 days, the dams' calving interval fell from 419 days to 348 days, and the proportion of dams that got back in calf rose from 84 percent to 94 percent. Take the calf off, and the cow rebreeds. That is the mechanism, measured.

One honest gap. No African body-condition-scoring dataset for beef cows, linking a condition score to rebreeding success, was obtained for this course. That is a real gap given how central the principle is. So this course will not give you a target score number and tell you it is African evidence. What you can do is judge condition the way stockmen have always judged it - by hand, over the ribs, the hip bones and the tail head, feeling how much cover is over the bone - and by recording what you feel for each cow at calving and again at the start of the next breeding season. Your own record of which thin cows failed to rebreed is real evidence about your own herd, and it costs nothing but a notebook.

Put the two halves together and you have the shape of a productive cow: she calves as young as your feed allows, and she keeps enough condition after calving to conceive again inside the same season, year after year. The cow that does not is the subject of the final lesson.

Lesson 3.6~12 min

Culling: Removing the Non-Payers

In this lesson
  • Rank the three culling priorities in order of evidence
  • Explain why culling without records becomes guesswork
  • Build a culling list from your own herd this season

Every animal in your herd eats whether or not she produces. She occupies grazing that a productive cow could use, she needs water, she needs dipping and dosing, and she takes labour. A cow that does not wean a calf is not neutral. She is a cost with no matching income, and she is being paid for by the cows that do produce.

Culling is how you stop that. It is the herd-level version of all the arithmetic in this module, put into practice. And it is the hardest lesson in this course to actually follow, because selling a cow feels like losing wealth, and in the store-of-wealth way of keeping cattle it is. This course is not telling you to stop valuing cattle as security. It is telling you that a cow producing nothing is worse security than the same value held in a cow that produces, or in the animal you could buy with what she sells for.

No single sourced African culling-rate figure was obtained for this course, so you will find no percentage here telling you what share of your herd to cull each year. That is a genuine gap. What the evidence does support is an order of priority, and priority is what you actually need.

First priority: the open cow at the end of a defined breeding season. She is the clearest and most measurable case. She has already cost you a full year with nothing to show for it, and you know it for certain rather than suspecting it. Notice that this priority only exists if you did the work in Lesson 4 - a defined season and a pregnancy test. Without those, you cannot identify her, and this best of all culling criteria is simply unavailable to you.

Second priority: the cow that has lost two calves. Whether the loss came from death, poor mothering, or repeated failure to conceive, a documented record of two failures is a much stronger case than one bad year. Be fair about a single failure. One bad year can have innocent causes - a drought, a disease outbreak, or a subfertile bull that failed her rather than the other way round. If your bull was the problem, culling the cows he missed would be exactly the wrong response, and you would repeat it next year with the same bull.

Third priority: old age combined with declining body condition, or unsound teeth, udder or legs. The mouth is the practical check here, and it needs no scale and no veterinarian. A cow whose teeth are worn, broken or missing cannot harvest enough grass to carry a pregnancy through a dry season, however acceptable she looks in the wet. Remember from Module 2 that the Boran breed society reports cows remaining sound-mouthed for 15 years or more - but that describes a well-managed pedigree herd on good management. An ordinary herd on harder forage should expect more variation, which means checking mouths rather than assuming an age.

Now the part that decides whether any of this works: records.

Without a written record of which cow calved in which year, culling collapses into removing the oldest-looking or thinnest animal. That is not the same animal as the one failing to produce. A thin cow may be thin precisely because she is rearing a good calf. A cow in beautiful condition may be fat because she has never carried a pregnancy in her life. Cull by eye and you will systematically remove your producers and keep your passengers.

So the record you need is not complicated. One line per cow, one entry per year, saying whether she calved and whether that calf was weaned. That single page turns a general rule - cows that fail to calve should be culled - into a decision you can defend: cow 14 has now failed twice in a row and goes at the next sale. Module 9 builds the full record system; this is the minimum you need to cull honestly.

Two more practical points.

Timing. A cull cow is a product you sell, not rubbish you throw away, so sell her while she still carries condition. An open cow identified in the weeks after the bull comes out can be sold in reasonable order. The same cow, kept out of sentiment through a dry season, sells thin for less money and eats several months of grazing on her way there.

Replacements. Culling is only half a policy. Every cow removed either shrinks your herd or is replaced by a heifer you kept back. Decide which you are doing before you sell, because a herd that culls hard and keeps no replacements shrinks year on year until there is nothing left to manage.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. How do you calculate your calving percentage?

It measures output against the females that had the chance to produce. Twenty cows exposed and eight calves born is 40 percent, and you can work it out with a pencil tonight.

2. What did the South African comparison find between the commercial and communal sectors?

The same country, the same year, and a gap wide enough that it cannot be explained by luck. One Venda communal survey found a rate as low as 15 percent.

3. Why does this course say to believe the gap but not any single percentage?

Honest sourcing means saying which part of the evidence is firm. The persistent communal-commercial gap appears in every figure; the exact number for any one country is not established here.

4. Twenty cows move from a 40 percent to a 70 percent calving rate. What changes?

The land, labour, bull, dip and water bill are very nearly identical either way. This is why raising calving percentage is usually a bigger lever on income than buying more cows.

5. Which constraint is described as probably the single most important limit on African livestock productivity?

A review of African livestock economics names it first, ahead of disease burden, cattle serving several purposes at once, and weak markets. It is why dry-season feed planning sits underneath every other lever.

6. What ratio does the cited extension guideline give for a mature bull?

The same source drops to one yearling bull per 20 cows, because a young bull has less mating stamina. Both are United States figures, not African measurements.

7. Why does this course refuse to give you an African bull-to-cow ratio?

The retrieved figure is temperate and from United States commercial ranching. Presenting it as an African rule would be inventing authority the evidence does not support.

8. What is the real limit on a bull's workload?

The synchronisation figure shows it clearly - 15 to 30 cows inside a five-day window is a far heavier instantaneous load than the same cows spread over a long season. Tightening your season concentrates the work.

9. How does an outnumbered bull show up in your results?

This is why the problem persists. Nothing looks wrong during the mating season, and by the time the missing calves are obvious it is a full year too late to fix.

10. What are the two main risks of hiring or sharing a bull?

Ask what he has been exposed to and tested for, and fix the dates before you agree. A shared bull who arrives late is a bull you paid for and did not use.

11. At a scrotal circumference of 32 cm, what proportion of examined bulls had satisfactory semen quality?

Fewer than a quarter, with the proportion improving toward 38 cm. That makes a small measurement a real warning sign worth investigating, not a cosmetic detail.

12. Why must you not condemn an indigenous bull for measuring under 38 cm?

No African scrotal-circumference-to-fertility dataset was obtained here. Use the principle, record your own measurements, and get the threshold for your breed from a local veterinarian or research institute.

13. A bull mounts cows eagerly all season. What does that prove about his fertility?

This is the trap that catches experienced farmers. Watching a bull tells you about willingness to mate; only a semen examination tells you whether the mating can produce a calf.

14. Why is a bull failure so expensive compared with most other mistakes?

Nothing looks wrong while it is happening. You discover it nine months later as calves that never arrived, across the whole herd at once.

15. What makes a pre-season soundness exam meaningful in the first place?

A bull living with the cows year-round has no before and no after, so there is no point at which a test result could change any decision you make.

16. What does a defined breeding season mean in practice?

It is that simple to state and that hard to arrange, because the real obstacle is having somewhere to keep the bull when he is not with the cows.

17. Why is an open cow invisible when the bull runs all year?

A bull-out date turns her status into a deadline. Everything after it is measurable, which is what makes pregnancy testing and culling decisions possible.

18. What does this course give as the correct calving-season timing for your region?

Season length and timing depend on your rainfall pattern, grass species and dry-season length. Inventing a continental number would be worse than giving none.

19. Why should you tighten a breeding season gradually rather than all at once?

Tighten in step with the condition of your cows and the soundness of your bull. A short season amplifies whatever else is wrong in your herd.

20. Many cows in your herd test open at the same time. What does that suggest?

A block of failures at one point in time points at something the whole herd shared. You can only see that pattern by testing them all at the same stage of a defined season.

21. What was the sourced age at first calving for Tuli?

Bonsmara was 32 months, and the African dairy comparison gave Jersey about 30.7 and Holstein-Friesian about 33.8. Beef breeds on extensive rangeland calve later, mostly because they grow to breeding weight more slowly.

22. A heifer calves at 36 months instead of 30. What has that cost?

It is the same large-but-unproductive arithmetic as an open cow, applied to one animal. Repeated across every heifer you raise, it is a substantial and invisible leak.

23. What does a 407-day calving interval mean over time?

About thirteen and a half months between calves. Four years of drift moves her a full season out of alignment with your grass, and then she skips.

24. In the South African weaning trial, what happened when calves were weaned at 90 days instead of 205?

Taking the calf off releases the dam's body reserves for her next pregnancy. It is the clearest measured demonstration of why a nursing cow competes with her own next calf.

25. Why does this course give no target body condition score?

The principle is sound and central, but the African data is a genuine gap. Judging by hand over ribs, hips and tail head and recording who rebreeds gives you real evidence about your own herd.

26. Which cow is the clearest culling candidate?

She has cost a full year with certainty rather than suspicion. But the criterion only exists if you have a bull-out date and a pregnancy test to identify her.

27. Why should a single failed year not automatically cull a cow?

A documented record of two failures is a far stronger case. If the bull failed the cows rather than the reverse, removing those cows fixes nothing at all.

28. What goes wrong when you cull by eye instead of by record?

Condition and productivity often run in opposite directions. Without a written record of who calved in which year, the decision is guesswork dressed up as stockmanship.

29. Why sell a cull cow soon after she is identified as open?

A cull cow is a product, not rubbish. Every month you keep her out of sentiment costs you both grazing and sale value at the same time.

30. Why is culling only half a policy?

Decide before you sell whether you are shrinking deliberately or replacing. Heifer retention and culling are one decision, made together, not two separate ones.

Module 3 capstone

Build a Breeding Herd Audit for your own herd, on paper, this season. Step 1: count every cow and heifer that was old enough to be served by a bull last year. Write that number down. Step 2: count every calf actually born to those females. Divide the second number by the first and multiply by 100. That is your calving percentage, and it may be the first time you have ever seen it. Step 3: set your figure beside the sourced range - as low as 15 and 23 percent in communal surveys against 61 percent in the commercial sector of the same country. Write one sentence saying where you sit. Step 4: count your bulls and divide your cow number by them. Compare against the reference guideline of one mature bull to 20 to 30 cows, or one yearling bull to 20, and note that this is a United States extension figure to check with your own extension officer, not an African measurement. Step 5: write down the exact date you would put the bull in and the exact date you would take him out, to create a defined breeding season. Step 6: list every cow that did not calve last year, and every cow that has now failed twice. Step 7: write the name of the veterinarian or extension officer you will ask about a breeding soundness examination, and the date you will call.
