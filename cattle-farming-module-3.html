Lessons

1. Digestive System of Cattle

Cattle are ruminants, meaning they have a four-compartment stomach: rumen, reticulum, omasum, and abomasum. The rumen is the largest compartment and acts as a fermentation vat. It contains billions of microorganisms that break down fibrous plant material that humans cannot digest. When a cow eats, she swallows feed without chewing much, then later regurgitates it as cud to chew again. This process, called rumination, happens 6 to 8 hours a day. A healthy rumen has a pH between 6.0 and 7.0. If pH drops below 5.5, the cow gets acidosis, which can cause lameness and reduced milk. To keep the rumen healthy, feed long fibre like hay or straw daily. Sudden changes in diet disrupt rumen microbes; always transition over 7 to 10 days. Water is critical: a lactating cow drinks 60 to 100 litres per day. Without water, rumen microbes stop working. The reticulum catches heavy objects like nails; magnets can be given to prevent hardware disease. The omasum absorbs water and minerals. The abomasum is the true stomach, secreting acid and enzymes. In young calves, the rumen is not fully developed until about 3 months old, so they need milk or milk replacer. From 2 weeks, offer clean hay and calf starter to encourage rumen development. Understanding this system helps you feed cattle correctly and avoid digestive upsets.

2. Nutrient Requirements for Different Stages

Nutrient needs change with age, weight, and production stage. A dry cow (not lactating, not pregnant) needs about 8% crude protein and 50% TDN (total digestible nutrients) in her diet. A lactating cow giving 10 litres of milk per day needs 14-16% crude protein and 65-70% TDN. For each extra litre of milk, add 0.5 kg of concentrate feed. A pregnant cow in the last 2 months needs 12% protein and 60% TDN to support calf growth. Growing heifers need 14-16% protein and 65% TDN to reach breeding weight (about 300 kg for local breeds) by 18-24 months. A mature bull needs 10-12% protein and 55% TDN when working. Calves from birth to 3 months: milk or milk replacer plus calf starter with 18-20% protein. From 3 to 6 months: 16% protein and 70% TDN. Use local feedstuffs: cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, maize bran, molasses. For example, a lactating cow can get 2 kg of cottonseed cake (36% protein) plus 5 kg of maize bran (8% protein) plus free-choice hay. Minerals: provide salt lick with calcium and phosphorus. In Africa, phosphorus is often deficient; add bone meal or dicalcium phosphate. Water: 60-100 litres for lactating cows, 30-40 litres for dry cows. Weigh feed, don't guess. A simple hanging scale costs little. Adjust rations based on body condition score (BCS 1-5; aim for 3). If cow loses weight, increase energy. If too fat, reduce. Always provide clean water and mineral block.

3. Feedstuffs and Rations

Feedstuffs fall into three groups: roughages, concentrates, and supplements. Roughages are high-fibre feeds like pasture, hay, silage, and crop residues (maize stover, rice straw). They are cheap but low in energy and protein. Concentrates are low-fibre, high-energy feeds like maize, sorghum, and oilseed cakes. Supplements include minerals, vitamins, and non-protein nitrogen like urea. A balanced ration mixes roughage and concentrate. For a 400 kg lactating cow giving 10 litres, a daily ration could be: 5 kg maize bran, 2 kg cottonseed cake, 1 kg molasses, 100 g mineral mix, and free-choice hay or pasture. Mix all together. For a dry cow: 2 kg maize bran, 0.5 kg cottonseed cake, and plenty of roughage. In Africa, common feedstuffs: maize bran (8-10% protein), wheat bran (14-16%), cottonseed cake (32-36%), sunflower cake (28-30%), groundnut cake (40-45%), molasses (energy), and brewers grain. Always check for aflatoxin in maize and cottonseed; mouldy feed poisons cattle. Store feed in dry, raised place. To make silage: chop green maize at dough stage, pack into pit or plastic tube, compress to remove air, cover tightly. Silage ferments and keeps for months. Urea treatment of straw: dissolve 4 kg urea in 100 litres water, sprinkle on 100 kg straw, cover for 2-3 weeks. This raises protein from 3% to 8-10%. Never feed urea alone; mix well. Rations should be cost-effective. Use least-cost formulation: compare prices per kg of protein and energy. For example, if cottonseed cake is cheaper per kg protein than sunflower cake, use more cottonseed. Always introduce new feeds gradually over 7-10 days.

4. Pasture Management and Grazing

Good pasture management means rotating grazing, controlling stocking rate, and resting paddocks. In Africa, common pasture grasses: Napier grass, Rhodes grass, Guinea grass, and natural veld. Legumes like lucerne, desmodium, and stylo add protein. A well-managed pasture can carry 2 to 4 cows per hectare in high-rainfall areas, but only 0.5 to 1 cow per hectare in dry areas. Rotational grazing: divide land into 4 to 8 paddocks. Graze each paddock for 3 to 7 days, then rest it for 21 to 35 days. This allows grass to regrow and prevents overgrazing. Overgrazing kills roots, causes soil erosion, and lowers feed quality. Never let cattle graze below 10 cm height; they eat the best parts and leave stem. For dry season, make hay or silage. Cut grass at flowering stage, dry in sun for 2-3 days, then store in dry shed. Hay loses 20-30% nutrients if rained on. In drylands, use crop residues: maize stover, sorghum stover, groundnut haulms. Supplement with urea blocks or molasses. Fencing: use live fences (thorny bushes) or electric fence. Water points: place water in each paddock or move cattle to water daily. Avoid muddy areas. Stocking rate: calculate based on rainfall. In 600 mm rainfall zone, 1 cow per 4 hectares. In 1000 mm, 1 cow per 1.5 hectares. Improve pasture by oversowing legumes, applying manure, and controlling weeds. Rotational grazing increases production by 20-30% compared to continuous grazing. Keep records: paddock number, date in, date out, grass height. This helps plan. In communal areas, work with neighbours to manage grazing together.

5. Feedlot Nutrition and Finishing

Feedlot finishing means feeding cattle a high-energy diet for 90 to 120 days to rapidly gain weight and improve meat quality. Target: 1.2 to 1.5 kg average daily gain. Start with a transition period of 2 weeks, moving from 60% roughage to 20% roughage. Final diet: 70-80% concentrate (maize, sorghum, molasses) and 20-30% roughage (hay, silage). Example ration for 400 kg steer: 6 kg cracked maize, 2 kg cottonseed cake, 1 kg molasses, 1 kg hay, 100 g mineral mix, and 50 g salt. Mix well. Feed twice daily at 8 am and 4 pm. Provide clean water always. Use feed bunks and water troughs. In Africa, many feedlots use maize bran, brewers grain, and molasses. Add ionophores like monensin (25-30 g per ton) to improve feed efficiency and prevent bloat. Add buffers like sodium bicarbonate (0.5-1% of diet) if acidosis risk. Implant growth promotants? Check local regulations. Vaccinate and deworm before entry. Handle cattle calmly; stress reduces gain. Weigh cattle every 30 days. If gain is below 1 kg/day, increase energy or check health. Market weight: 350-450 kg live weight for local breeds, 450-550 kg for crosses. In Africa, feedlot finishing is growing near cities. Cost of gain: calculate feed cost per kg gain. If feed costs 0.5 USD per kg and gain is 1.2 kg, cost per kg gain is 0.42 USD. Sell when price is good. Keep records: feed intake, weight, health. A simple notebook works. Avoid overfeeding; wasted feed is money lost. Clean bunks daily. Ensure shade and ventilation. In hot climates, feed more at night. This system works if you have reliable feed supply and market.

6. Supplements and Additives

Supplements correct deficiencies in basal diet. Minerals: calcium, phosphorus, salt, and trace minerals (copper, zinc, selenium, iodine). In Africa, phosphorus is often low in dry season. Provide a mineral lick: 50% bone meal, 30% salt, 10% molasses, 10% trace mineral premix. Or buy commercial mineral blocks. Vitamins: A, D, and E are important. Green pasture provides A and E; dry feed loses them. Inject vitamin A if cattle show night blindness or rough coat. Add vitamin premix to feed. Non-protein nitrogen: urea can replace part of protein for ruminants. Use 1-2% of diet, never more. Mix urea with molasses or silage. Too much urea causes ammonia toxicity. Ionophores: monensin and lasalocid improve feed efficiency by 5-10% and prevent coccidiosis. Add 25-30 g per ton of feed. Buffers: sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) at 0.5-1% of concentrate prevents acidosis. Yeast culture: 5-10 g per head per day improves fibre digestion. Probiotics: live yeast or bacteria. Antibiotics: use only for disease treatment, not routine growth promotion (resistance risk). In some countries, antibiotics as growth promoters are banned. Growth implants: ear implants with hormones (trenbolone, estradiol) increase gain by 10-15%. Check legality. In Africa, many smallholders use simple supplements: salt, bone meal, and molasses. For dairy cows, add 100 g of mineral mix per day. For feedlot, add 150 g. Always read labels. Store supplements dry. Don't mix urea with water and let stand. Introduce new supplements gradually. If cattle refuse, mix with molasses. Keep records of supplement use. Consult extension officer for local recommendations. Supplements are not replacements for good roughage; they are additions.

<h2>Practical Reference: Cattle Nutrition and Feeding in African Production Systems</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in the Cattle Nutrition and Feeding module into a single working document. It is written for the African context, where cattle perform multiple functions milk, meat, draught, manure, and savings and where feed is typically the largest single cost in any cattle enterprise. Getting nutrition right is therefore not a technical nicety; it is the difference between a profitable herd and one that consumes capital. Use this reference alongside the detailed lessons and adapt the figures to your own environment, breeds, and market conditions.</p>

<p>In much of sub-Saharan Africa, cattle are kept on mixed croplivestock farms, communal rangelands, or emerging feedlots. Rainfall is often seasonal, pasture quality drops sharply in the dry season, and purchased feeds are expensive relative to milk and liveweight prices. The practical implication is that profitable feeding strategies must be built on (a) maximizing the use of on-farm and communal forage, (b) matching supplementation to the actual limiting nutrient, and (c) monitoring cost per unit of milk or liveweight gain, not just feed intake.</p>

<h3>1. Digestive System of Cattle Why It Shapes Every Feeding Decision</h3>

<p>Cattle are ruminants. They have a four-compartment stomach (rumen, reticulum, omasum, abomasum) and rely on a dense microbial population in the rumen to ferment fibrous plant material. The rumen microbes break down cellulose and hemicellulose into volatile fatty acids (VFAs), synthesize microbial protein, and produce B-vitamins and vitamin K. This is why cattle can convert crop residues, pasture, and agro-industrial by-products into high-value milk and meat a biological advantage that a monogastric animal such as a pig or poultry bird does not have.</p>

<p>The practical consequences are significant. First, cattle need a minimum level of effective fibre to maintain rumen function and chewing activity; sudden removal of roughage causes acidosis. Second, the rumen microbes need time to adapt to any change in diet typically 714 days for a major shift. Third, non-protein nitrogen (NPN) such as urea can be used by rumen microbes to synthesize protein, but only when energy and other nutrients are adequate. Fourth, feed particle size matters: very finely ground roughage reduces chewing and saliva production, lowering rumen pH buffering.</p>

<h3>3. Feedstuffs and Rations Building a Ration That Pays</h3>

<p>A ration is only as good as the cheapest combination of ingredients that meets the animal's requirements. In Africa, the most cost-effective rations typically combine:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Roughage (6080% of DM):</strong> natural pasture, crop residues (maize stover, sorghum stover, rice straw, groundnut haulms), hay, silage, and agro-industrial by-products (bagasse, brewers' grains, pineapple pulp).</li>
<li><strong>Energy concentrates:</strong> maize, sorghum, molasses, cassava chips, wheat bran, rice bran.</li>
<li><strong>Protein concentrates:</strong> cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, groundnut cake, soybean meal, fish meal, poultry litter (processed), and urea (with caution).</li>
<li><strong>Minerals and vitamins:</strong> mineral licks, bone meal, limestone, salt, and commercial premixes.</li>
</ul>

<p>A simple practical guideline is to formulate on a dry matter basis, then check cost per kg DM and cost per kg milk or liveweight gain. For example, a lactating cow producing 10 litres of milk may need about 1214 kg DM per day at 1416% CP. If pasture supplies 8 kg DM at 8% CP, the deficit can be met with 2 kg of a concentrate containing 18% CP plus a mineral lick. The exact mix depends on local prices always calculate before buying.</p>

<h3>4. Pasture Management and Grazing</h3>

<p>Pasture is the cheapest feed in most African systems, but it is also the most variable. Key practices for profitable grazing include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rotational grazing:</strong> divide the farm into 48 paddocks and move animals every 37 days. This allows grass to recover, reduces parasite load, and improves dry matter yield by 2040% compared with continuous grazing.</li>
<li><strong>Stocking rate:</strong> match animal numbers to available forage. A common rule of thumb in semi-arid Africa is 1 tropical livestock unit (TLU = 250 kg) per 24 hectares of natural pasture, but this varies widely with rainfall.</li>
<li><strong>Dry-season planning:</strong> conserve surplus wet-season grass as hay or silage. A well-made maize or sorghum silage can carry cattle through 34 months of scarcity.</li>
<li><strong>Legume integration:</strong> plant forage legumes such as lablab, cowpea, desmodium, or leucaena to fix nitrogen and provide protein-rich fodder.</li>
<li><strong>Water and shade:</strong> ensure clean water within 2 km and shade during heat stress, especially for dairy cows.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Feedlot Nutrition and Finishing</h3>

<p>Feedlot finishing is increasingly common in Southern and East Africa, driven by urban demand for beef. The goal is rapid, efficient liveweight gain (1.01.5 kg/day) over 90120 days. Typical feedlot rations contain 7085% concentrate (maize, molasses, protein cakes) and 1530% roughage, with a gradual adaptation period of 1421 days. Starting cattle on a high-concentrate diet without adaptation causes acidosis, bloat, and sometimes death.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fdecec; padding:14px 16px; margin:18px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING UREA AND ACIDOSIS RISK</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#3a0000;">Urea is a cheap non-protein nitrogen source, but it is highly toxic if misused. Never feed urea to non-ruminants. For cattle, do not exceed 1% of total dry matter intake, introduce it gradually over 23 weeks, and always provide adequate energy (maize, molasses) and water. Symptoms of urea poisoning include salivation, bloat, muscle tremors, and rapid death call a veterinarian immediately. Similarly, sudden introduction of high-grain diets causes rumen acidosis; adapt animals slowly and provide roughage. Store urea and medicated feeds securely, away from children and non-target animals.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Supplements and Additives</h3>

<p>Supplements should correct a specific deficiency, not be used as a substitute for good forage. In African conditions, the most common deficiencies are energy (dry season), protein (low-quality roughage), phosphorus (most soils), and trace minerals (copper, zinc, selenium, iodine). Practical options include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mineral licks:</strong> provide a balanced mineral mix free-choice. A simple home mix can be made from 50% salt, 30% bone meal or limestone, 15% molasses, and 5% trace mineral premix but commercial licks are more reliable.</li>
<li><strong>Protein supplements:</strong> cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, or legume hay for animals on low-protein roughage.</li>
<li><strong>Energy supplements:</strong> molasses, maize bran, or cassava chips during the dry season or for high-producing cows.</li>
<li><strong>Additives:</strong> ionophores (e.g., monensin) can improve feed efficiency by 510% in feedlots; yeast cultures can stabilize rumen pH; buffers (sodium bicarbonate) help during heat stress. Use only approved products and follow withdrawal periods.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Business-First Considerations</h3>

<p>Feed is typically 6075% of the cost of milk or beef production in Africa. A 10% improvement in feed conversion can translate into a 68% improvement in gross margin. Track three numbers every week: (1) feed cost per animal per day, (2) milk yield or liveweight gain per animal, and (3) cost per litre or per kg gain. If feed cost per litre of milk exceeds the farm-gate milk price, the ration or the herd structure must change. Consider buying feed in bulk with neighbours, growing your own forage, and using crop residues instead of purchased roughage.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Weigh or condition-score a sample of animals monthly to track body condition.</li>
<li>Match the ration to the production stage dry cows do not need lactation rations.</li>
<li>Introduce any new feed over 714 days; never change abruptly.</li>
<li>Provide clean water ad libitum a lactating cow drinks 60100 litres/day.</li>
<li>Offer a balanced mineral lick free-choice at all times.</li>
<li>Conserve wet-season forage as hay or silage for the dry season.</li>
<li>Calculate cost per kg DM and cost per litre of milk or kg of gain weekly.</li>
<li>Store urea, medicated feeds, and agrochemicals securely, away from animals and children.</li>
