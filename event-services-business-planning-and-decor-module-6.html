1. Assessing venues: community halls, hotels, gardens, and family compounds

When a client asks you to plan an event, the first step is to assess the venue. In many African communities, you have four main options: community halls, hotels, gardens, and family compounds. Each has strengths and weaknesses. Community halls are affordable, often located in the heart of the neighbourhood, and have basic facilities like toilets and a kitchen. But they may lack air conditioning, have old wiring, or be booked out months in advance. Hotels offer conveniencetables, chairs, power, and catering are usually includedbut they are expensive and may restrict outside decor or food. Gardens are beautiful and flexible, but you must check for uneven ground, lack of shade, and no backup power. Family compounds are personal and free, but space may be tight, and you may need to rent everything from chairs to generators.

Start your assessment with a site visit. Bring a notebook, a tape measure, and your phone for photos. Check access: Can a truck deliver chairs and a tent? Is there a gate wide enough? Are there stairs? Look at power: How many sockets? What is the voltage? In many African countries, it's 220-240V. Ask about water: Is there a tap? Toilets? For gardens, check the soilis it muddy after rain? For compounds, ask about noise restrictions and neighbours.

Talk to the venue manager or owner. Ask about booking fees, deposit, cancellation policy, and any rules about decorations, cooking, or music. Get everything in writing, even if it's a simple handwritten agreement. For hotels, confirm if they charge corkage for outside drinks or if they require their own caterers. For community halls, find out if there is a caretaker who will be present and if you need to pay them separately.

Finally, consider the guest experience. Is the venue easy to find? Is there parking? For elderly guests, are there ramps? For a wedding in a garden, is there a backup plan if it rains? Once you have assessed all options, compare them against the client's budget and needs. A good event planner always has a Plan B venue in mind.

2. Measuring spaces and planning seating arrangements

Measuring a venue accurately saves you from the embarrassment of too few chairs or a cramped dance floor. You need a tape measure (at least 30 metres), a notebook, and a helper. Start by measuring the length and width of the main area. For a rectangular hall, multiply length by width to get square metres. For example, a hall 15m long and 10m wide is 150 square metres. But usable space is less after you subtract for pillars, stage, buffet area, and walkways.

For seating, the rule of thumb: allow 1.2 to 1.5 square metres per person for theatre-style (chairs in rows) and 1.5 to 2 square metres per person for banquet-style (round tables with chairs). So a 150 square metre hall can seat about 100 people at round tables (150 1.5 = 100) or 125 in rows (150 1.2 = 125). But always round down to be safe. For a wedding with a dance floor, reserve at least 3m x 3m. For a buffet, allow 2m x 4m. For a stage, 4m x 3m.

Measure doorways and corridors. A standard chair is 45cm wide; a round table for 8 is 1.5m diameter. Can you fit them through the door? If not, you may need to assemble inside. Also measure ceiling height for decorations like drapes or chandeliers. In many African halls, ceiling height is 3-4 metres; in tents, it's 2.5-3 metres.

Draw a simple floor plan on graph paper. Mark the entrance, stage, dance floor, buffet, cake table, gift table, and seating. Use a scale like 1cm = 1m. Then place your tables and chairs. For round tables, leave 1.2m between tables for chairs and walking. For rows, leave 0.8m between rows for legs. Don't forget a aisle for the bridal party. Share this plan with your client and the venue manager. Adjust as needed. A good plan prevents chaos on the day.

3. Coordinating with venue managers for access and rules

The venue manager is your key partner. Whether it's a community hall caretaker or a hotel events coordinator, you must coordinate access, rules, and timing. Start by scheduling a meeting at least two weeks before the event. Bring your floor plan, a list of equipment, and your timeline. Ask these questions: When can we access the venue for setup? How long before the event? Can we come the day before? What time must we leave after the event? Is there a security deposit? What are the rules about noise, cooking, and decorations?

For access, get the name and phone number of the person who will open the gate. Confirm if there is a loading bay or if you must carry items from the street. In many African cities, parking is tight, so ask if you can reserve space for your delivery truck. If the venue is a hotel, they may require you to use their suppliers for power or AV. Clarify any fees for early access or overtime.

Rules vary. Some halls forbid nails in walls, so you must use tape or stands for banners. Some gardens prohibit open flames, so you need LED candles. Some compounds have curfewsmusic must stop by 10pm. Write down all rules and share them with your team. Also ask about existing furniture: tables, chairs, and kitchen equipment. If they provide chairs, inspect them for damage. If you bring your own, label them with your business name.

On the setup day, arrive early and do a walkthrough with the manager. Confirm power points, water taps, and toilet locations. Ask where you can store empty boxes and rubbish. Establish a contact person for emergencies. After the event, do a joint inspection to avoid deposit disputes. Leave the venue clean and take photos as proof. Good coordination builds trust and leads to repeat bookings.

4. Planning for power, water, and backup generators

Power and water are the lifelines of any event. Without them, you can't run lights, sound, or catering. In many African venues, grid power is unreliable, so you must plan for backup. First, calculate your power needs. List all equipment: sound system (500-2000W), lights (1000-3000W), fridge (200W), fans (100W each), phone chargers (negligible). Add them up. For a medium wedding, you might need 5-8 kilowatts (kW).

Check the venue's power supply. Look at the main breaker box. Is it 220V or 240V? How many circuits? A standard socket in Africa provides 13-15 amps, which is about 3kW. So you may need multiple sockets or a distribution board. If the venue has a generator, ask its capacity and fuel level. If not, rent one. A 10kW diesel generator costs about $100-$200 per day in many African cities, plus fuel. Fuel consumption is roughly 1 litre per hour per 3kW. So a 10kW generator uses about 3 litres per hour. For an 8-hour event, that's 24 litres.

Water: For a garden event, you need water for drinking, cooking, and toilets. Ask if there is a tap and if it's potable. If not, buy bottled water or a water tank. A 1000-litre tank can serve 200 guests for a day. For toilets, rent portable toilets if the venue has none. One toilet per 50 guests is a good rule.

Backup plan: Always have a generator as backup, even if the venue has grid power. Test it before the event. Bring extra fuel, extension cables (at least 30 metres), and power strips. Label cables to avoid tripping. For sound, use a voltage stabiliser to protect equipment. For lighting, use LED bulbs to save power. Assign a team member to monitor power throughout the event. If the generator fails, have a mechanic on call. A well-powered event runs smoothly and keeps guests comfortable.

5. Setting up decor within time limits and with a small team

Decor transforms a plain venue into a memorable space, but you must work fast and smart. With a small team of 2-4 people, you need a plan. Start by creating a setup schedule. For a typical wedding, you might have 4 hours to set up. Divide tasks: one person handles drapes and backdrops, another does table settings, another arranges flowers and centrepieces, and you supervise and handle lighting.

Use the right tools: a step ladder, a staple gun (if allowed), zip ties, fishing line, double-sided tape, and a glue gun. For African fabric decor, use kitenge or ankara cloths. They are colourful and lightweight. Measure the backdrop area and cut fabric in advance. For a 6m x 3m backdrop, you need about 6 metres of fabric (if 1.5m wide, you need 4 panels). Attach with zip ties to a frame or use a curtain rod.

For table settings, work in batches. Set up all tables with tablecloths first, then plates, then centrepieces. Use a trolley or cart to move items. For a small team, avoid heavy liftinguse a dolly for chairs and tables. If you have 20 round tables, each with 8 chairs, that's 160 chairs. Two people can move 20 chairs per trip with a dolly, so 8 trips. It takes about 30 minutes.

Time limits: Always add 30 minutes buffer. If setup starts at 10am and event is at 2pm, aim to finish by 1:30pm. Assign one person to do a final walkthrough at 1pm. Check for missing items, crooked tablecloths, and burnt-out bulbs. Keep a toolkit with spare bulbs, tape, and scissors. After the event, tear down quickly. Pack items in labelled boxes. With a small team, you can set up a 100-guest event in 3-4 hours if you are organised. Practice makes perfect.

6. Handling weather challenges for outdoor events

Outdoor events in Africa face sun, rain, wind, and dust. You must plan for all. First, check the weather forecast a week before and again the day before. In many regions, rainy seasons are predictablee.g., April to June in East Africa. If rain is likely, rent a tent. A 10m x 20m tent can cover 200 guests. Cost: $500-$1000 per day. Ensure the tent has side walls and a floor. For sun, provide shade with umbrellas or a canopy. For wind, secure tents with ropes and stakes. In dusty areas, water the ground before the event.

Have a rain plan. If it starts raining, move the ceremony to the tent or indoors. Assign team members to guide guests. Keep plastic sheets to cover sound equipment and chairs. For mud, lay down wooden pallets or mats as walkways. For heat, provide water stations and fans. In hot climates, schedule events for early morning or late afternoon.

For wind, avoid lightweight decor like paper lanterns. Use weighted bases for centrepieces. Secure tablecloths with clips. For dust, cover food and keep serving dishes closed. If the venue is a garden, ask if you can use their lawnif it's waterlogged, you may need to relocate.

Communicate with guests. Send a message if the event is delayed or moved. Have a backup indoor venue as a last resort. On the day, monitor the sky. If clouds darken, start moving things early. After the event, dry all equipment before storing to prevent mould. Weather is unpredictable, but with a solid plan, you can handle anything. Always have a Plan B and C.

7. Practical Reference

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Venue Selection &amp; Setup Logistics</h2>

<p>In the African event services business, your profit is decided long before the first guest arrives. It is decided the day you sign a venue agreement, measure the space, and calculate how many generators, chairs, and hours of labour you will need. This practical reference gives you the operating numbers, checklists, and field rules used by professional event planners working in community halls, hotels, gardens, and family compounds across the continent.</p>

<p>Read this module as a business document, not an academic one. Every table, diagram, and checklist is designed to help you quote accurately, avoid losses, and deliver a decor setup that looks premium even when your team is small and your budget is tight.</p>

<h2>1. Assessing Venues: The Four Common Types</h2>

<p>African event venues fall into four practical categories. Each has a different cost structure, risk profile, and setup logic.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Community halls:</strong> Low rental cost, but often poor power, no backup generator, limited water, and strict caretaker rules. Best for budget weddings, church events, and community functions.</li>
<li><strong>Hotels:</strong> Higher cost, but reliable power, water, security, and in-house catering. Best for corporate events, conferences, and high-end weddings where the client pays for convenience.</li>
<li><strong>Gardens and outdoor grounds:</strong> Beautiful for photos and ceremonies, but weather-dependent, with no built-in power or water. You must budget for generators, tents, flooring, and portable toilets.</li>
<li><strong>Family compounds:</strong> Culturally important for traditional weddings and funerals. Cheap or free, but you inherit every infrastructure gap power, water, access roads, and space limits.</li>
</ul>

<p>Your first site visit should answer three business questions: What is the maximum safe guest capacity? What infrastructure must I bring in? What rules will slow down my team on setup day? A venue that saves you $200 in rent but costs $600 in generator fuel and extra labour is a loss-making venue.</p>

<h2>3. Coordinating with Venue Managers</h2>

<p>Venue managers control your access, your time, and your reputation. Get everything in writing even a WhatsApp message counts as a written record in most African business disputes. Confirm the following before you sign:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Access times:</strong> When can your team enter? When must you leave? Overtime charges?</li>
<li><strong>Setup and teardown windows:</strong> Many halls allow only 34 hours before an event and 2 hours after.</li>
<li><strong>Power supply:</strong> Is there a dedicated circuit for your equipment? Who pays for fuel if the venue generator runs?</li>
<li><strong>Water access:</strong> Taps, tanks, or must you bring water?</li>
<li><strong>Noise and time limits:</strong> Some compounds restrict music after 10 p.m.; hotels may charge for extended hours.</li>
<li><strong>Damage deposit:</strong> How much, and what conditions trigger deductions?</li>
<li><strong>Exclusivity:</strong> Can the venue book another event the same day in an adjacent space?</li>
</ul>

<p>Assign one person from your team as the venue liaison. That person carries the signed agreement, the contact number of the caretaker, and a printed floor plan at all times.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b;background:#fdecea;padding:16px;margin:20px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<h3 style="color:#c0392b;margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING: Generator Placement and Fuel Handling</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;">Never place a generator inside a tent, hall, or enclosed compound area where guests are seated. Carbon monoxide kills silently and has caused multiple event fatalities across Africa. Place generators at least 5 metres from any occupied space, downwind of guests, on level ground, and away from dry grass or fabric. Store fuel in approved containers, never inside the venue, and never refuel a running generator. Keep a dry-powder fire extinguisher within 3 metres of every generator. Ensure all cables are taped down or covered with cable ramps to prevent tripping and electrocution, especially in wet outdoor conditions.</p>
</div>

<h2>5. Setting Up Decor Within Time Limits and With a Small Team</h2>

<p>Most African event setups are done by teams of 48 people working against a 35 hour window. Efficiency is a business skill, not a talent. Use the following field rules:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Pre-assemble off-site:</strong> Build centrepieces, backdrops, and signage at your workshop, not at the venue. Arrive with finished components.</li>
<li><strong>Work in zones:</strong> Assign one person per zone (stage, tables, entrance, buffet). Do not let the team cluster in one area.</li>
<li><strong>Dress the room from back to front:</strong> Stage first, then tables, then entrance and signage. This prevents walking over finished work.</li>
<li><strong>Use a printed layout sheet:</strong> Every team member should carry a one-page plan showing table numbers, decor style, and power points.</li>
<li><strong>Reserve 30 minutes for inspection:</strong> Walk the room with the client before guests arrive. Fix small issues immediately.</li>
</ul>

<h2>6. Handling Weather Challenges for Outdoor Events</h2>

<p>Weather is the biggest uncontrollable cost in outdoor events. In Africa, the risks vary by region and season: heavy rains in West and Central Africa, dust and wind in the Sahel and North Africa, extreme heat in the Horn of Africa, and sudden coastal storms in East and Southern Africa.</p>

<p>Your contract must include a weather contingency clause. If it rains, who pays for the tent, the flooring, the extra transport, and the rescheduled setup? The client must know before signing.</p>

<p>Practical controls:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Tents and canopies:</strong> Always book a tent with side walls for outdoor events, even in the dry season. A sudden downpour can destroy decor and electronics in minutes.</li>
<li><strong>Flooring:</strong> Use wooden or plastic platforms in gardens and compounds. Muddy ground ruins shoes, chairs, and fabric.</li>
<li><strong>Wind:</strong> Weigh down tent legs with sandbags or water barrels. Secure backdrops with guy ropes. Never use open flames (candles, fire pits) near fabric tents.</li>
<li><strong>Heat:</strong> Provide shade, water stations, and fans. Schedule ceremonies for early morning or late afternoon.</li>
<li><strong>Drainage:</strong> Inspect the site for low-lying areas that flood. Place the stage, buffet, and generator on the highest available ground.</li>
<li><strong>Communication:</strong> Have a WhatsApp group with the client, venue manager, and your team. Send weather updates 48 hours, 24 hours, and 3 hours before setup.</li>
</ul>

<p>For a deeper reference on crowd safety and weather planning, consult your national disaster management or meteorological authority. In Kenya, the <a href="https://meteo.go.ke/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Kenya Meteorological Department</a> provides free seasonal forecasts; in Nigeria, the <a href="https://nimet.gov.ng/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Nigerian Meteorological Agency (NiMet)</a> publishes similar data. Use these free tools in your planning.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Signed venue agreement</strong> with access times, overtime charges, damage deposit, and power/water responsibilities.</li>
<li><strong>Measured floor plan</strong> with table numbers, stage position, buffet, dance floor, and emergency exits marked.</li>
<li><strong>Generator booked and tested</strong> at least 24 hours before the event; fuel calculated and stored safely.</li>
<li><strong>Water plan confirmed</strong> minimum 20 litres per guest, plus handwashing and kitchen supply.</li>
<li><strong>Team briefing done</strong> with zone assignments, printed layout sheets, and a 30-minute buffer before guest arrival.</li>
<li><strong>Weather contingency ready</strong> tents with side walls, flooring, sandbags, and a written rescheduling clause.</li>
<li><strong>Safety equipment on site</strong> fire extinguishers, cable ramps, first aid kit, and generator placed 5 m from guests.</li>
<li><strong>Post-event teardown plan</strong> who stays, who transports, and what time the venue must be cleared.</li>
</ol>
