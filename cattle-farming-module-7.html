1. Newborn Calf Care

When a calf is born, your first job is to make sure it breathes. If the calf is not breathing, clear mucus from its nose and mouth with your fingers or a clean cloth. Rub the calf vigorously with a dry towel or straw to stimulate breathing and circulation. If the calf still doesn't breathe, hold it upside down by its back legs for a few seconds to drain fluid, then rub again. Most calves breathe within 30 seconds. Once breathing, dip the navel in iodine solution (7% tincture) to prevent infection. Use a clean small cup or spray bottle. If you don't have iodine, use a strong salt solution or allow the navel to dry naturally, but iodine is best. Do this immediately after birth.

Next, ensure the calf is warm and dry. In cold or rainy weather, move the calf to a dry, sheltered area with clean bedding. A newborn calf cannot regulate its body temperature well. If the calf is shivering, provide a heat lamp or rub it dry and cover with a blanket. In hot weather, provide shade.

Check the calf's sex and weight. A typical African Zebu calf weighs 15-25 kg at birth, while exotic breeds like Friesian may weigh 30-40 kg. Record the birth weight if you have a scale; if not, estimate. Weighing helps monitor growth.

Ensure the calf stands and nurses within the first 2 hours. If the calf is weak, assist by guiding the teat into its mouth. If the mother rejects the calf, you may need to restrain the cow or feed colostrum by bottle.

Finally, identify the calf. Use ear tags, ear notches, or a simple neck strap with a number. Record the calf's ID, date of birth, and dam's ID. Keep a notebook or use a mobile app. Good records help you track performance and health.

Remember, a healthy newborn calf is the future of your herd. Act quickly and gently. If you see any abnormality, consult a veterinarian or experienced farmer.

2. Colostrum Management

Colostrum is the first milk a cow produces after calving. It is thick, yellowish, and rich in antibodies (immunoglobulins) that protect the calf from diseases. A calf is born with no immunity; it must absorb antibodies from colostrum within the first 6 hours of life. After 12 hours, the gut can no longer absorb these large molecules effectively. So timing is critical.

The rule is: feed 10% of the calf's body weight in colostrum within the first 6 hours. For a 25 kg calf, that's 2.5 liters. If the calf is weak, feed smaller amounts more frequently, e.g., 1 liter every 2 hours. Use a clean bottle or bucket with a teat. If the calf won't suck, use an esophageal feeder (a tube passed gently into the stomach).

Quality matters. Good colostrum comes from a healthy cow that has been vaccinated and well-fed. The first milking is best; later milkings have less antibodies. If the dam's colostrum is not available, use colostrum from another cow that calved at the same time, or frozen colostrum from a previous calving. You can freeze colostrum in clean plastic bottles for up to a year. Thaw in warm water (not hot) to avoid destroying antibodies.

If you have no colostrum, you can use a commercial colostrum replacer, but it is not as good as natural. Never feed cow's milk or milk replacer as a substitute for colostrum in the first 24 hours.

After the first 6 hours, continue feeding colostrum for the first 24 hours. Then transition to regular milk or milk replacer. Monitor the calf for signs of failure of passive transfer: weakness, poor sucking, or diarrhea. If you suspect problems, consult a vet.

In Africa, many smallholders rely on the dam to nurse. But if the calf is not nursing, you must intervene. Watch the calf closely for the first day. A calf that gets enough colostrum will be active, have a shiny coat, and pass meconium (first dark dung) within 24 hours. If not, act quickly. Good colostrum management is the single most important factor for calf survival.

3. Calf Nutrition and Weaning

From day 2 to weaning, a calf needs a steady supply of nutrients. If the calf is with the dam, it will nurse several times a day. But for controlled feeding, give 10% of body weight in milk or milk replacer daily, split into 2-3 feedings. For a 30 kg calf, that's 3 liters per day. Use clean buckets or bottles. Warm the milk to body temperature (37Â°C) â cold milk can cause digestive upsets.

From the first week, offer clean, fresh water at all times. Calves need water to digest solid feed. Also provide a calf starter feed â a concentrated mix of grains and protein. A good starter has 18-20% protein. Offer a handful (about 100g) from day 3, increasing gradually. By 2 months, a calf can eat 1-2 kg of starter per day. Provide chopped hay or fresh green forage from 2 weeks to encourage rumen development.

Weaning should be gradual. Start reducing milk at 6-8 weeks if the calf is eating at least 1 kg of starter per day. Over one week, reduce milk feedings from 2 to 1, then to none. By 3 months, the calf should be fully weaned. If weaning is abrupt, the calf may lose weight or get sick.

For calves on pasture with the dam, weaning can be at 6-8 months naturally. But if you want to milk the cow, early weaning at 3 months is common. Ensure the calf gets enough nutrients from forage and supplements.

In dry seasons, when grass is poor, supplement with hay, silage, or crop residues. Add minerals and salt licks. A mineral block is cheap and prevents deficiencies.

Monitor growth: weigh your calf monthly. A healthy calf gains 0.5-1 kg per day for local breeds, and 0.8-1.2 kg for improved breeds. If growth is slow, check for parasites or inadequate feed. Adjust diet accordingly.

Remember, clean feed and water are essential. Dirty utensils can cause scours (diarrhea). Wash buckets daily with soap and water. Never feed spoiled or moldy feed. Good nutrition sets the foundation for a productive adult cow.

4. Calf Housing and Hygiene

A calf needs a dry, clean, and well-ventilated place to live. In Africa, many farmers keep calves in a simple pen or shed. The key is to protect the calf from rain, wind, and extreme heat, while allowing fresh air.

For a single calf, a pen of 1.5m x 2m is enough. For group housing, allow 2-3 square meters per calf. The floor should be easy to clean and drain. Concrete is ideal, but if not available, use packed earth with a layer of dry bedding like straw, wood shavings, or dry grass. Replace bedding daily if wet or soiled. Damp bedding harbors bacteria and causes pneumonia.

Ventilation is critical. Ammonia from urine can damage calf lungs. Ensure there is an opening for air but no direct draft on the calf. In hot climates, provide shade and possibly a fan. In cold areas, use curtains or sacks to block wind but still allow air exchange.

Hygiene: Clean and disinfect the pen before a new calf arrives. Remove manure daily. Wash feed and water buckets after each use. Use a disinfectant like bleach solution (1 part bleach to 10 parts water) or a commercial product.

If you have multiple calves, keep them in separate pens or groups by age to prevent disease spread. A new calf should be isolated for at least 2 weeks.

For calves on pasture, provide a movable shelter or a simple three-sided shed. Rotate grazing to reduce parasite buildup.

In many African villages, calves are tethered outside. If so, ensure the tether is not too tight, and move the calf to fresh ground regularly. Provide shade.

Remember, a clean environment reduces disease. A calf that is always wet or dirty will get sick. Invest in good bedding and cleaning tools â a broom, shovel, and bucket. It pays off.

If you notice a bad smell in the pen, it needs cleaning. Good hygiene is cheap insurance for calf health.

5. Health Issues in Calves

The most common calf diseases are scours (diarrhea), pneumonia, and navel infections. Early detection and treatment save lives.

Scours: Caused by bacteria, viruses, or parasites. Signs: watery dung, often yellow or green, dehydration, sunken eyes, weakness. Treat immediately: give oral rehydration solution (ORS) â mix 1 liter clean water, 1 teaspoon salt, 6 teaspoons sugar. Feed 2-4 liters per day in small amounts. If severe, a vet may give intravenous fluids. Also, continue feeding milk â do not starve the calf. Antibiotics may be needed for bacterial scours; consult a vet. Prevention: good colostrum, clean bedding, and vaccination of dams.

Pneumonia: Caused by bacteria or viruses, often due to poor ventilation, damp bedding, or drafts. Signs: coughing, rapid breathing, fever, nasal discharge, loss of appetite. Treat with antibiotics prescribed by a vet. Supportive care: keep calf warm and dry, provide fresh air. Prevention: proper housing, avoid overcrowding, vaccinate if available.

Navel infections (omphalitis): Swelling, heat, pain, or pus at the navel. Treat with iodine and antibiotics. Prevention: dip navel in iodine at birth.

Other issues: parasites (worms, coccidia) cause poor growth and diarrhea. Deworm at 2-3 months and repeat as needed. Use a dewormer like albendazole or ivermectin. Coccidiostats in feed can prevent coccidiosis.

External parasites: ticks, lice, fleas. Use acaricides (e.g., spray or dip).

Nutritional deficiencies: e.g., white muscle disease from selenium deficiency. Provide mineral supplements.

In Africa, many vets are scarce. Learn basic first aid. Keep a kit: iodine, ORS, antibiotics, dewormer, thermometer, syringes.

Vaccinate calves according to local schedule: e.g., blackleg, anthrax, foot-and-mouth disease. Consult your local veterinary office.

Isolate sick calves to prevent spread. Always wash hands after handling sick animals.

If you see a sick calf, act fast. A calf can die within 24 hours from scours or pneumonia. Early treatment is key.

6. Record Keeping for Calves

Keeping records helps you make better decisions, spot problems early, and improve your herd. For calves, record the following: identification (ear tag number, name), date of birth, sex, dam ID, sire ID (if known), birth weight, and calving ease (difficulty).

After birth, record daily observations: feeding (how much colostrum, milk, starter), health events (scours, pneumonia, treatments), and weight (monthly). Use a simple notebook or a mobile app like Farm Records or Cattle Manager. Even a chalkboard in the shed works.

Why record? To track growth: if a calf is not gaining weight, you can adjust feed or check for disease. To track health: if a calf gets scours, you know when and how you treated it. To track breeding: you know when a heifer is ready to breed. To track costs: feed, vet, and labor. This helps you know if calf rearing is profitable.

For smallholders, a simple table works: columns for Date, Calf ID, Weight, Feed, Health, Notes. Update weekly. If you have many calves, use a herd management software.

In Africa, many farmers use memory, but that fails as herds grow. Write it down. Share records with your vet or extension officer for advice.

Also record vaccinations, deworming, and any medications. Note the withdrawal period for meat or milk.

At weaning, record weaning weight and date. This helps evaluate dam performance.

Good records help you select the best calves for replacement and cull poor performers. Over time, you build a valuable database.

Start simple: a notebook and pen. Keep it in a dry place. Review records monthly. You will see patterns and improve.

Remember, records are not for the government; they are for you. They turn guesswork into knowledge. A farmer who records is a farmer who progresses.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Calf Management and Rearing

In African cattle enterprises, the calf is not merely a future animal â it is the next production cycle, the next replacement heifer, the next bull for fattening, and the foundation of herd profitability. A calf that survives the first 24 hours, receives adequate colostrum, avoids pneumonia and scours, and reaches weaning at a healthy weight will repay that care many times over. A calf that dies or stunts represents lost milk, lost feed, lost labour, and lost future income.

This practical reference module is designed for smallholder and emerging commercial cattle farmers across Africa. It combines field-ready actions with business discipline: every management step should be measurable, recorded, and linked to cost and return. The lessons covered are Newborn Calf Care, Colostrum Management, Calf Nutrition and Weaning, Calf Housing and Hygiene, Health Issues in Calves, and Record Keeping for Calves.

Why Calf Management Is a Business Decision

Calf mortality in many African smallholder herds ranges from 10% to 30%, and in some pastoral systems it can exceed 40% during drought. Each calf lost represents the cost of the damâs feed, veterinary care, and labour for a full year, plus the lost future value of the animal. In a 20-cow herd with 15 calves born per year, reducing mortality from 20% to 5% saves roughly two to three calves annually â equivalent to several months of household income in many rural economies.

The business case for calf management is therefore straightforward: invest in colostrum, hygiene, and records, and the herdâs replacement and sales capacity improves without increasing the number of cows. This is productivity per cow, not just productivity per herd.

Newborn Calf Care

The first hour after birth is the highest-risk period. The calf must breathe, stand, and suckle. In African conditions where calving may occur in the field or in a shared kraal, the farmer should:

  • Clear mucus from the nose and mouth if the calf is slow to breathe.
  • Dip the navel in iodine or a clean disinfectant to prevent navel ill and joint infections.
  • Ensure the calf is dry and placed on clean, dry bedding away from mud and manure.
  • Identify the calf and record the dam, date, sex, and birth weight where possible.
  • Allow the dam to lick the calf if she is calm; this stimulates circulation and bonding.

Colostrum Management

Colostrum is the first milk and contains antibodies, energy, and growth factors. The calfâs gut can absorb antibodies efficiently only in the first 6 hours, with absorption declining rapidly after 12 hours. The rule is simple: 2â3 litres within the first 6 hours, and another 2 litres within 12 hours for a Holstein-type calf; smaller indigenous calves may need 1.5â2 litres per feed.

Where the dam dies or rejects the calf, pooled colostrum from healthy cows can be used. In many African farms, colostrum is stored frozen in clean bottles for emergencies. Never feed colostrum from a cow with mastitis or Johneâs disease.

Calf Nutrition and Weaning

Whole milk or milk replacer is the main feed for the first 2â4 weeks. From the end of week one, introduce a palatable calf starter â maize, soybean meal, minerals, and molasses â to stimulate rumen development. Clean water must be available at all times; without water, starter intake and rumen development stall.

Weaning should be based on starter intake and weight, not calendar age alone. A common target is 1â1.5 kg of starter per day for three consecutive days, and roughly double the birth weight, before milk is withdrawn. In African dual-purpose systems, weaning at 8â12 weeks is common, but early weaning at 6â8 weeks can reduce milk used for calves and free more milk for sale â if starter intake and health are good.

Calf Housing and Hygiene

Calves need dry, draught-free, well-ventilated housing. In humid and rainy African climates, damp bedding is a major cause of pneumonia and navel infections. Individual pens or calf hutches reduce contact and disease spread. Where group housing is used, keep calves of similar age together and provide at least 2â3 mÂ² per calf.

Clean feeding equipment after every feed. Shared buckets and bottles are common sources of scours. Disinfect pens between calves and remove manure daily.

Health Issues in Calves

The three major calf health problems in African cattle systems are scours (diarrhoea), pneumonia, and navel ill. Scours is often caused by poor colostrum, dirty water, or sudden feed changes. Pneumonia is linked to damp, poorly ventilated housing and draughts. Navel ill results from contamination at birth.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Zoonotic and Drug-Residue Risk

Some calf diseases â including brucellosis, tuberculosis, and cryptosporidiosis â can spread to humans. Always wear gloves when handling sick calves, aborted material, or afterbirth. Wash hands and boots with soap and clean water after contact. Never sell or consume milk from cows treated with antibiotics until the withdrawal period stated on the label has passed. Use only veterinary-approved drugs, and consult an animal health worker or veterinarian before treating calves. Misuse of antibiotics contributes to antimicrobial resistance and leaves harmful residues in meat and milk.

Record Keeping for Calves

Records turn calf rearing from guesswork into management. At minimum, each calf should have an ID, dam ID, birth date, sex, birth weight, colostrum date and amount, vaccination and deworming dates, disease events, treatments, and weaning date and weight. In Africa, simple exercise books, wall charts, or mobile phone apps such as iCow or FarmDrive can be used. The key is consistency, not sophistication.

Quick Reference Checklist

  2. Feed 2â3 litres of clean colostrum within the first 6 hours.
  3. Record calf ID, dam ID, birth date, sex, and birth weight.
  4. Provide dry, draught-free bedding and clean water daily.
