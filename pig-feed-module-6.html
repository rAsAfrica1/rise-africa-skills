Lesson 6.1~12 min

Food Waste and By-Products

In this lesson
  • Measure the dry matter content of a wet by-product and convert its price to a dry matter basis
  • Match a by-product to the class of pig whose fibre ceiling it fits
  • Apply mould and mycotoxin controls to wet and perishable feedstuffs

By-products are the single biggest opportunity to cut feed cost on a small pig unit, and the single easiest way to poison a herd. The difference is measurement and discipline, not luck.

Start with the trap. Wet by-products are sold by weight, and most of that weight is water. Brewers' spent grain, cassava peels, fruit waste, restaurant waste and vegetable trimmings all look cheap per kilogram as delivered. They are only cheap if they are cheap per kilogram of dry matter, because water has no feeding value.

Measure dry matter yourself. You need a kitchen scale and a sunny day.

  1. Weigh exactly 1,000 g of the fresh material.
  2. Spread it thinly on a clean sheet in the sun, or in a low oven if you have one.
  3. Re-weigh it every few hours until the weight stops falling.
  4. Divide the final weight by 1,000 to get dry matter percentage.

Suppose your 1,000 g of wet spent grain dries down to 250 g. That is 25 percent dry matter, which means four kilograms of the wet material contain one kilogram of actual feed. If the brewery sells it at 0.04 USD per kg wet, your dry matter cost is 0.04 x 4 = 0.16 USD per kg. Against maize at 0.35 USD per kg that still looks good, but now add transport. If a 500 kg load costs 15 USD to collect, that is 15 divided by 500 = 0.03 USD per kg wet, or 0.12 per kg of dry matter, taking your true cost to 0.28 USD per kg dry matter. Suddenly the margin is thin, and it will disappear entirely if you have to make the trip for 200 kg instead of 500.

Next, match the by-product to the right pig. Most by-products are fibrous, and the published crude fibre ceilings decide where they fit. Weaner diets tolerate roughly 2 to 4 percent crude fibre, and inclusion above about 5 percent may depress growth in young pigs because their enzyme systems are immature. Grower and finisher diets accept 4 to 6 percent. Lactating sows are held at 3 to 4 percent because trough space must carry energy. Gestating sows accept 6 to 8 percent and actively benefit, because fibre gives a restricted-fed sow satiety. So the order of preference for a fibrous by-product is: gestating sows first, then growers and finishers, then lactating sows, and weaners last or not at all.

Now the safety discipline. Wet materials spoil fast and mould is a mycotoxin factory. Published limits are strict: aflatoxin 20 ppb for breeding animals, pregnant sows and pigs under about 23 kg, rising to 200 ppb for finishers over about 45 kg; deoxynivalenol 5 ppm maximum for all ages, with feed intake already falling at 1 to 3 ppm; zearalenone recommended at 1 ppm or below; fumonisin 10 ppm maximum. Practical rules follow directly: feed wet by-products within a day or two, never store them warm, never feed anything that smells sour, musty or alcoholic to breeding stock, and never mix a fresh load on top of an old one in the same drum.

Animal-derived kitchen and restaurant waste is a separate question. Many countries restrict or ban feeding it to pigs because of disease transmission risk, and where it is permitted it is normally required to be heat treated. Check your national rules before you collect a single bucket, and if in doubt do not use it. The economics of one disease outbreak dwarf the savings.

Worked example. A producer on the edge of Nairobi, Kenya, collects spent grain from a small brewery twice a week. She feeds it only to her twelve gestating sows, at 2 kg wet per sow per day, replacing 0.5 kg of her 2.0 kg gestation concentrate allowance because 2 kg wet at 25 percent dry matter equals 0.5 kg of dry feed. Her saving is 12 sows x 0.5 kg x 0.40 USD per kg = 2.40 USD a day, or 876 USD a year. Her cost is 12 x 2 kg = 24 kg wet a day at 0.04 = 0.96 USD, plus two collection trips a week at 15 USD, which is 30 x 52 = 1,560 USD a year, plus 0.96 x 365 = 350 USD of purchase. That totals 1,910 USD against 876 USD saved: she is losing money. The fix is volume. If she collects enough on the same two trips to also supply a neighbour, or feeds growers as well within the 4 to 6 percent fibre ceiling, the trip cost spreads and the deal turns positive. Transport, not the ingredient, is usually the deciding number.

Lesson 6.2~12 min

Local Ingredients and Substitutions

In this lesson
  • Substitute a local energy source for maize while holding the ration's protein constant
  • Rank protein ingredients by cost per kilogram of protein rather than price per bag
  • Apply published amino acid ratios when checking a substituted ration

The single largest cost in pig production is energy, and in most African markets that means maize, competing directly with human food. Every producer eventually asks the same question: can I use something else? The answer is usually yes, provided you do the substitution arithmetic instead of just swapping bags.

The common local energy alternatives are cassava chips or meal, sweet potato, maize bran, wheat bran, rice bran and molasses. The common local protein alternatives are soybean meal or full-fat roasted soya, sunflower seed cake, cottonseed cake, groundnut cake, palm kernel cake, copra cake and fishmeal or dagaa. Composition figures for these vary widely by processing and batch, so treat any table you are given, including this one, as approximate book values to start from and verify with your supplier or a laboratory. As a rough guide: maize around 9 percent crude protein, cassava meal around 2 to 3 percent, wheat and maize bran in the mid teens, soybean meal around 44 to 46 percent, sunflower cake around 28 to 32 percent, and fishmeal 50 percent and up.

The substitution rule is simple. When you replace maize with a lower-protein energy source, protein leaves the ration and you must put it back. Work it as a running total of protein contributed per 100 kg.

Worked example. A producer near Tamale, Ghana, currently runs a grower ration with 60 kg of maize per 100 kg. Maize at 9 percent contributes 60 x 0.09 = 5.4 kg of protein. Cassava chips are available at 0.22 USD per kg against maize at 0.35, and she wants to replace 20 kg of the maize.

  1. New cereal fraction: 40 kg maize plus 20 kg cassava.
  2. Protein contributed: 40 x 0.09 = 3.6 kg, plus 20 x 0.025 = 0.5 kg, giving 4.1 kg.
  3. Protein lost: 5.4 minus 4.1 = 1.3 kg per 100 kg of ration.
  4. Replace it with soybean meal at 45 percent: 1.3 divided by 0.45 = 2.89 kg, call it 2.9 kg.
  5. That 2.9 kg has to displace something, so take it from the cassava: 40 maize, 17.1 cassava, and 2.9 extra soybean meal.

Now price the change. She saved 20 kg of maize at 0.35 and bought 17.1 kg of cassava at 0.22 and 2.9 kg of soybean meal at 0.60. Saving: 20 x 0.35 = 7.00. Cost: 17.1 x 0.22 = 3.76 plus 2.9 x 0.60 = 1.74, totalling 5.50. Net saving 1.50 USD per 100 kg, or 15 USD per tonne. On the 30 tonnes she mixes a year that is 450 USD.

But protein is not the whole story, and this is where most substitutions fail. The pig needs lysine and the other amino acids, not crude protein. Published grower targets are 1.08 to 1.25 percent SID lysine for 12 to 26 kg pigs and 1.00 to 1.10 percent for 27 to 60 kg, with methionine plus cystine at 56 to 58 percent of lysine, threonine at 62 to 64 percent and tryptophan at 18 to 19 percent. Cassava contributes almost no amino acids at all, so a cassava ration leans harder on the protein source. Sunflower and cottonseed cakes are relatively low in lysine compared with soybean meal, so a ration built on them can hit its crude protein target and still be lysine deficient. Always check lysine, not just protein.

Three practical cautions. Bitter cassava varieties contain cyanogenic compounds and must be properly chipped and sun dried before feeding. Cottonseed cake contains gossypol, which is more of a problem for young pigs and breeding stock than for finishers, so keep inclusion modest and avoid it in gilt and sow rations unless you have advice on your specific batch. And rice bran goes rancid quickly because of its oil content, so buy it fresh and use it fast.

Finally, rank protein sources properly. Divide price per kilogram by protein percentage. Soybean meal at 0.60 and 45 percent is 0.60 divided by 0.45 = 1.33 USD per kg of protein. Sunflower cake at 0.35 and 30 percent is 1.17 USD per kg of protein. Fishmeal at 1.20 and 55 percent is 2.18. On protein alone sunflower wins, but on lysine per dollar soybean meal usually wins back, which is exactly why you check the amino acid, not the bag.

Insect Protein for Pigs

In this lesson
  • Describe where insect meal fits in a pig ration relative to fishmeal
  • Calculate the production cost per kilogram of dried larvae from your own substrate
  • Set quality and consistency requirements before including insect meal in a diet

Black soldier fly larvae have become the most talked-about new protein source in African livestock feeding, and for good reason: they convert organic waste that has no other use into a high-protein meal, on a small footprint, without land or irrigation. They are also the ingredient most often adopted on enthusiasm rather than arithmetic, so this lesson is about the numbers.

Where does insect meal fit? Not as a replacement for soybean meal in a finisher ration, where the economics almost never work. It fits where you are already paying a premium for a concentrated, highly digestible animal protein, which in pig production means the weaner and starter diets. Published starter guidance includes fishmeal at 3 to 5 percent inclusion, valued for 88 to 92 percent protein digestibility, excellent palatability and omega-3 content, alongside spray-dried porcine plasma at 3 to 5 percent inclusion carrying a cost premium of 0.80 to 1.20 USD per kilogram. Those are the slots insect meal competes for. If fishmeal in your market costs 1.20 USD per kilogram and you can produce dried larvae for less, on a like-for-like protein basis, you have a business. If you cannot, you have a hobby.

Composition first. Published pig nutrient standards do not yet carry insect meal tables, so treat every composition figure you are quoted as approximate until you have your own analysis. Dried black soldier fly larvae are typically high in protein and high in fat, and the fat fraction is variable and depends heavily on substrate. Two consequences follow. First, defatted meal and full-fat meal are different ingredients and cannot be substituted for one another in a ration. Second, high fat means the meal can go rancid, so drying, storage and turnover matter as much as they do for rice bran. Insist on a protein and fat analysis for every batch, whether you buy it or grow it, and record it on an ingredient card as described in Module 7.

Now the production arithmetic. The only figure that matters is your own yield ratio: kilograms of dried larvae per 100 kg of wet substrate. Measure it, do not assume it. Take a known weight of substrate, run one full cycle, dry the harvest fully, and weigh. Then work forward.

Worked example. A small unit outside Kisumu, Kenya, runs black soldier fly on market vegetable waste and brewery residue. Over three measured cycles it averages 6 kg of dried larvae per 100 kg of wet substrate. Treat that 6 percent as this unit's own illustrative measured figure, not a universal constant.

  1. Substrate needed for 100 kg of dried larvae: 100 divided by 0.06 = 1,667 kg of wet waste.
  2. Substrate cost: the market waste is free but collection costs 25 USD per tonne, so 1.667 tonnes x 25 = 41.68 USD.
  3. Labour: one person-day per 200 kg of substrate handled, so about 8.3 person-days at 5 USD = 41.50 USD.
  4. Drying fuel and depreciation on crates and screens, estimated locally at 20 USD.
  5. Total for 100 kg dried: 41.68 + 41.50 + 20 = 103.18 USD, or 1.03 USD per kg.

Against fishmeal at 1.20 USD per kg, that is a saving of 0.17 USD per kilogram. At 4 percent inclusion in a starter diet, 100 kg of ration uses 4 kg, so the saving is 4 x 0.17 = 0.68 USD per 100 kg of starter feed. If the unit makes 6 tonnes of starter a year, that is 60 x 0.68 = 40.80 USD saved annually. That is a thin return for a whole production enterprise, which tells you the real business case is usually selling insect meal to others, or valorising a waste stream you are already paying to dispose of, rather than feeding it to your own pigs alone.

Three rules before you include it. Keep to the fishmeal slot, meaning roughly 3 to 5 percent of a starter diet, until you have your own performance data. Never substrate the larvae on material you would not feed to pigs directly; contamination passes through. And check your national feed regulations, because insect protein rules vary and some markets restrict permitted substrates.

Forage and Fiber Supplements

In this lesson
  • Distinguish soluble from insoluble fibre and match each to a stage of pig development
  • Calculate how much forage is needed to lift a gestation diet to its fibre target
  • Set fibre ceilings by pig class and explain the growth penalty for exceeding them

Fibre is the most misunderstood nutrient in small-scale pig feeding. Some producers treat forage as free feed and wonder why their pigs grow slowly; others avoid it entirely and pay for expensive concentrate to feed sows who then chew the pen fittings from hunger. Both are wrong, and the published fibre science is clear enough to settle the argument.

Fibre comes in two functional types and they do opposite things. Insoluble fibre, from cellulose, wheat bran and oat hulls, speeds the passage of digesta through the gut. Faster passage prevents pathogenic bacteria colonising and limits the time available for toxin production, which is why insoluble fibre consistently reduces post-weaning diarrhoea. Its drawback is that in pigs under 10 kg, whose enzyme systems are immature, it reduces nutrient digestibility, and inclusion above about 5 percent in weaner diets can depress growth.

Soluble fibre, from pectins, guar gum and similar sources, does the reverse. It ferments in the hindgut to produce volatile fatty acids, mainly butyrate, propionate and acetate. Those acids feed the epithelial cells directly, promote tight junction development, lower gut pH and act as prebiotics supporting Lactobacillus and Faecalibacterium. The drawback is increased digesta viscosity, which slows passage and can make post-weaning diarrhoea worse in some circumstances.

The published strategy for weaners uses both, in sequence:

  1. Days 0 to 7 after weaning: insoluble fibre at 2 to 3 percent, for faster transit.
  2. Days 7 to 14: a roughly 50/50 mix of soluble and insoluble, letting the microbiota adapt.
  3. After day 14: increase soluble fibre to promote volatile fatty acid production and gut health.

The ceilings by class are the numbers to write on your wall. Weaner diets, 2 to 4 percent crude fibre maximum. Grower and finisher, 4 to 6 percent acceptable. Gestating sows, 6 to 8 percent acceptable and useful. Lactating sows, 3 to 4 percent, because energy density cannot be sacrificed. Digestibility also shifts with age: apparent total tract digestibility of cellulose runs roughly 50 to 70 percent and of soy fibre 60 to 75 percent, but pigs under 15 kg achieve markedly less than mature pigs over 60 kg, whose fibre digestion approaches that of adult cattle.

Common African forages that fit this picture include sweet potato vines, lucerne or alfalfa, Mucuna, desmodium, Leucaena leaf meal, cassava leaf, banana pseudostem and pasture grasses. Composition varies enormously with cutting stage and drying, so treat any crude fibre figure as approximate and, if you can, get one analysis done on the material you actually use.

Worked example. A producer near Musanze, Rwanda, grows sweet potatoes and has abundant vine. Her gestation concentrate tests at about 3 percent crude fibre and she feeds 2.0 kg per sow per day, which is 2,000 x 0.03 = 60 g of crude fibre. To reach the middle of the published 6 to 8 percent target on a 2.0 kg dry intake she would want about 2,000 x 0.07 = 140 g, so she is short 80 g.

If her sun-dried vine meal runs at roughly 25 percent crude fibre, an approximate figure she should verify, then 80 divided by 0.25 = 320 g of dried vine meal per sow per day closes the gap. Fresh vine at about 20 percent dry matter means 320 divided by 0.20 = 1.6 kg of fresh vine daily.

Now the trade-off. That 320 g of dry vine displaces energy. If she keeps total dry intake at 2.0 kg she must remove 320 g of concentrate, and her sow's daily energy falls. At 0.40 USD per kg she saves 0.32 x 0.40 = 0.128 USD per sow per day, which across twelve sows and 114 gestation days is 12 x 114 x 0.128 = 175.10 USD per gestation cycle. The right answer for most producers is to substitute part of it and add back a little, keeping the daily 5.9 Mcal energy target intact while gaining the satiety. Fibre is a gestation tool, not a way to underfeed a pregnant sow.

Antibiotic-Free Feeding Strategies

In this lesson
  • Select feed additives that replace growth-promoting antibiotics using published dose ranges
  • Calculate the cost per tonne of an additive from its inclusion rate and unit price
  • Sequence additives correctly across the nursery phases

This lesson is deliberately narrow. It is about which ingredients go into the mixer and at what rate. The certification, market access, audit and premium pricing questions that come with an antibiotic-free claim are handled fully in Module 10, and there is no point repeating them here. What follows is formulation mechanics.

Start with the honest performance figure. Published reviews put the gap between antibiotic-supported and antibiotic-free production at 0 to 3 percent in growth and feed conversion where the alternatives and the management are good, and 5 to 15 percent where management is poor. That is the whole argument: alternatives work, but only on a farm where hygiene, water quality, weaning age and stocking density are already right. Nothing in a sack fixes a dirty pen.

The additive classes and their published dose ranges are these.

Organic acids. Formic acid at 0.35 to 1.4 percent of the diet, reducing stomach pH to about 3.5 to 4.5 against 4.5 to 5.5 unsupplemented, and bactericidal to E. coli and Salmonella. Lactic acid at 0.5 to 1.0 percent, particularly effective against ETEC F18 with reported 10 to 15 percent reductions in post-weaning diarrhoea in susceptible herds. Propionic acid at 0.5 to 1.0 percent, a weaker acidifier but a useful mould inhibitor in feed. Butyric acid at 0.2 to 0.5 percent, usually as sodium or calcium butyrate for stability, which is not primarily an acidifier at all: it feeds colonocytes, strengthens tight junctions and acts as an anti-inflammatory. Total organic acid inclusion typically runs 0.5 to 1.5 percent.

Essential oils. A carvacrol, thymol and cinnamaldehyde blend at 0.05 to 0.15 percent, meaning 100 to 150 mg per kg of diet. Documented responses include 15 to 25 percent higher average daily gain over days 1 to 14, 10 to 18 percent over days 1 to 42, and 5 to 10 percent better feed conversion, with increased villus height and upregulated tight junction proteins. Individual component levels are carvacrol 5 to 15 mg per kg, thymol 5 to 15, cinnamaldehyde 2 to 5. Microencapsulation costs 1.5 to 2 times more but protects the oils from oxidation and is recommended commercially.

Probiotics. Bacillus subtilis at 1 to 5 x 10^9 CFU per kg, spore-forming and stable through pelleting. Lactobacillus plantarum at 1 to 10 x 10^9 CFU per kg. Saccharomyces cerevisiae yeast at 0.1 to 0.5 percent. Expect 5 to 15 percent gain improvement in challenged conditions and much less in clean ones.

Prebiotics. Mannan oligosaccharides at 0.1 to 0.2 percent, binding pathogens so they cannot adhere. Fructooligosaccharides at 0.5 to 1.0 percent. Expect a modest 3 to 8 percent, best combined with something else.

Minerals used pharmacologically. Zinc oxide at 2,000 to 3,000 ppm in nursery phase 1, tapering to 1,000 to 1,500 in phase 2 and 125 to 250 in phase 3, reducing diarrhoea incidence 40 to 60 percent. Copper at 125 to 250 ppm is bacteriostatic, with chronic toxicity risk above 250 ppm against a nutritional requirement of only 5 to 6 ppm. Note that the EU has banned zinc oxide as a growth promoter since 2022 and permits only nutritional levels of 80 to 150 ppm; check your own national rules and any buyer specification.

Tannic acid at 0.5 to 1.5 g per kg is the emerging post-zinc-oxide option, with research showing efficacy similar to zinc oxide at appropriate doses. Enzymes, including xylanase, protease and phytase, deliver 2 to 5 percent feed conversion improvement depending on ingredients.

Sequence them properly. In phase 1, use an acidifier such as formic or lactic acid for pH plus, where permitted, zinc oxide. In phases 2 and 3, shift the emphasis to butyrate for barrier function and energy, and carry essential oils across the whole nursery.

Worked example. A producer in Ibadan, Nigeria, prices three options for one tonne of nursery feed at local quotes. An encapsulated essential oil blend at 150 mg per kg means 0.15 kg per tonne; at 30 USD per kg that is 4.50 USD per tonne. Sodium butyrate at 0.3 percent means 3 kg per tonne; at 4 USD per kg that is 12.00 USD per tonne. Formic acid at 1.0 percent means 10 kg per tonne; at 1.20 USD per kg that is 12.00 USD per tonne. Her whole programme costs 28.50 USD per tonne, or 0.0285 USD per kg of feed. On a nursery diet costing 0.65 USD per kg that is a 4.4 percent increase in feed cost, set against a documented 5 to 10 percent feed conversion improvement from the essential oils alone. Written down that way, the decision is arithmetic rather than belief.

Lesson 6.6~12 min

Organic Pig Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Reformulate a diet to meet lysine targets without synthetic amino acids
  • Quantify the crude protein penalty that results from removing crystalline lysine
  • Identify which additive classes remain permitted under organic-style rules

Organic production changes the rules of formulation before it changes anything else. This lesson deals only with those mechanics: what you may put in the mixer and how the arithmetic changes. Certification bodies, audit records, market access and pricing the premium are covered in Module 10 and are not repeated here.

The single biggest formulation change is losing crystalline amino acids. In a conventional grower diet you meet the lysine target cheaply by adding a small amount of L-lysine hydrochloride and keeping crude protein modest. Remove that option and every gram of lysine must come from intact protein ingredients, which means adding more oilseed meal, which drags in far more of every other amino acid and a large excess of nitrogen.

Work the numbers. Take a grower target of about 1.10 percent SID lysine, in the middle of the published 1.00 to 1.10 percent band for 27 to 60 kg pigs. Using approximate book values of 0.24 percent lysine and 9 percent crude protein for maize, and 2.8 percent lysine and 45 percent crude protein for soybean meal, both of which you should verify locally:

  1. A conventional-style mix of 65 kg maize and 30 kg soybean meal per 100 kg gives lysine of 65 x 0.0024 = 0.156 kg plus 30 x 0.028 = 0.84 kg, totalling 0.996 kg, so about 1.00 percent total lysine.
  2. Total lysine is higher than SID lysine, so after digestibility that mix sits nearer 0.90 percent SID, short of target. Conventionally you would close the gap with about 2 kg of L-lysine HCl per tonne.
  3. Organically, you must raise the soybean meal instead. Try 59 kg maize and 36 kg soybean meal: 59 x 0.0024 = 0.142 plus 36 x 0.028 = 1.008, totalling 1.15 kg, about 1.15 percent total lysine, which after digestibility lands near the target.
  4. Now check crude protein. The conventional mix: 65 x 0.09 = 5.85 plus 30 x 0.45 = 13.5, giving 19.35 percent. The organic mix: 59 x 0.09 = 5.31 plus 36 x 0.45 = 16.2, giving 21.5 percent.

That is a penalty of about 2.2 percentage points of crude protein to buy the same lysine. The pig excretes the surplus nitrogen, you pay for it, and the extra ammonia load in the house is a genuine welfare and health cost. It also raises feed cost: 6 kg more soybean meal per 100 kg at 0.60 USD is 3.60 USD per 100 kg, or 36 USD per tonne, less the value of the 6 kg of maize saved at 0.35, which is 2.10, so a net 1.50 per 100 kg or 15 USD per tonne. On 40 tonnes a year that is 600 USD.

How to soften the penalty. First, use a protein source with a better amino acid pattern. Fishmeal and full-fat roasted soya carry lysine well; sunflower and cottonseed cakes do not, so they force the crude protein even higher. Second, run a tighter phase structure. The published finisher phases fall from 1.05 percent SID lysine at 27 to 40 kg down to 0.65 percent at 120 to 130 kg, so a producer using six phases rather than one flat diet wastes far less protein. Third, use permitted enzymes: phytase at 500 to 2,500 FTU per kg releases 0.10 to 0.20 percent additional available phosphorus and protease improves protein digestibility, both squeezing more from the same bag.

What remains permitted? Published regulatory summaries list probiotics, phytogenics, organic acids and enzymes as the permitted alternatives in the strictest regime, the EU, which has banned antibiotic growth promoters since 2006. So the additive doses in Lesson 5 remain almost entirely available to you: acids, essential oils, Bacillus subtilis, Lactobacillus plantarum, yeast, mannan and fructooligosaccharides. Selenium is capped, with selenised yeast limited to 0.2 mg selenium per kg of complete feed under EU rules, which sits at the bottom of the 0.30 to 0.50 mg per kg range specified for boars, so organic boar diets need careful attention. Zinc oxide at pharmacological levels is out; only nutritional levels of 80 to 150 ppm apply.

Worked example. A producer in the Eastern Cape, South Africa, mixes 40 tonnes of grower and finisher feed a year. Moving to a synthetic-amino-acid-free formulation costs her the 15 USD per tonne calculated above, or 600 USD a year. She recovers part of it by splitting her single finisher diet into three phases following the published lysine ladder, which alone cuts her average soybean meal inclusion by an estimated two percentage points, worth 2 x 0.60 = 1.20 USD per 100 kg or 12 USD per tonne, giving back 480 USD. Her net cost of the change is about 120 USD a year, a figure she can compare directly against the premium calculations in Module 10.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. 1,000 g of a wet by-product dries down to 250 g. What is its dry matter content?

250 divided by 1,000 is 25 percent dry matter, meaning four kilograms of the wet material supply one kilogram of feed.

2. A by-product at 25% dry matter costs $0.04 per kg wet. What is the cost per kg of dry matter, before transport?

Four kilograms of wet material are needed per kilogram of dry matter, so 0.04 multiplied by 4 gives 0.16 USD.

3. Which class of pig is the best first home for a fibrous by-product?

Gestating sows tolerate 6 to 8 percent crude fibre and benefit from the satiety, while weaners are limited to 2 to 4 percent and lose growth above about 5 percent.

4. Why must wet by-products be fed quickly rather than stored?

Spoilage generates mycotoxins. Aflatoxin is limited to 20 ppb for breeding and young pigs and DON to 5 ppm, with intake already falling at 1 to 3 ppm.

5. In the worked example, what turned an apparently cheap by-product into a loss?

Two collection trips a year cost more than the feed saved. Transport per kilogram of dry matter, not the purchase price, usually decides whether a by-product pays.

6. Replacing 20 kg of maize (9% CP) with 20 kg of cassava meal (2.5% CP) per 100 kg removes how much protein?

Maize contributed 20 x 0.09 = 1.8 kg and cassava contributes 20 x 0.025 = 0.5 kg, a loss of 1.3 kg of protein per 100 kg.

7. How much 45% soybean meal replaces 1.3 kg of lost protein?

1.3 divided by 0.45 gives 2.89 kg, rounded to 2.9 kg of soybean meal.

8. Why can a ration hit its crude protein target and still fail the pig?

Requirements are set as SID lysine with fixed ratios for the other amino acids. Sunflower and cottonseed cakes can meet protein while leaving lysine short.

9. Sunflower cake costs $0.35/kg at 30% protein. What is the cost per kg of protein?

0.35 divided by 0.30 gives 1.17 USD per kilogram of protein.

10. Which caution applies specifically to cottonseed cake?

Gossypol is the concern with cottonseed cake, and young and breeding pigs are more sensitive than finishers.

11. Which ingredient slot does insect meal realistically compete for in pig diets?

Insect meal is a concentrated animal protein. It competes with the 3 to 5 percent fishmeal and plasma inclusions in starter diets, not with cereal energy.

12. At a yield of 6 kg dried larvae per 100 kg of wet substrate, how much substrate is needed for 100 kg of meal?

100 divided by 0.06 gives 1,667 kg of wet substrate.

13. Why can full-fat and defatted insect meal not be substituted for one another?

Fat content varies with substrate and processing, altering energy density and storage life, so they must be treated as separate ingredients with separate analyses.

14. In the worked example, saving $0.17 per kg on 4 kg of insect meal per 100 kg of starter saves how much per 100 kg of feed?

4 kg multiplied by 0.17 USD gives 0.68 USD saved for every 100 kg of starter feed mixed.

15. What is the correct approach to insect meal composition figures?

Standard pig nutrient references do not yet carry insect meal tables, and composition varies with substrate, so batch analysis is essential.

16. Why does insoluble fibre reduce post-weaning diarrhoea?

Faster transit denies pathogens the time to colonise and produce toxins, which is why insoluble fibre performs consistently in the first week after weaning.

17. What is the main published drawback of soluble fibre in weaners?

Soluble fibre raises viscosity and delays passage, which can exacerbate post-weaning diarrhoea even though it produces useful volatile fatty acids.

18. A sow eats 2.0 kg of a 3% crude fibre diet. How much crude fibre is that?

2,000 g multiplied by 0.03 gives 60 g of crude fibre per day.

19. Which class of pig has the lowest crude fibre ceiling alongside weaners?

Lactating sows are held at 3 to 4 percent because every kilogram of intake must carry energy when intake itself is the limiting factor.

20. What do volatile fatty acids produced from soluble fibre do in the hindgut?

Butyrate, propionate and acetate feed the gut lining, support barrier function through tight junctions, and acidify the hindgut.

21. An essential oil blend is included at 150 mg per kg of feed. How much is needed per tonne?

150 mg per kg is 150 g per tonne, which is 0.15 kg.

22. Which additive is described as primarily a colonocyte energy source and barrier strengthener rather than an acidifier?

Butyrate is not mainly about pH. It feeds large intestine epithelial cells, upregulates tight junctions and acts as an anti-inflammatory.

23. What is the published performance gap between antibiotic-supported and antibiotic-free production when alternatives and management are both good?

The gap narrows to 0 to 3 percent with optimal alternatives, but widens to 5 to 15 percent where management is suboptimal.

24. Copper is bacteriostatic at 125-250 ppm. What is the nutritional requirement, and where does toxicity begin?

The baseline requirement is only 5 to 6 ppm, the pharmacological range is 125 to 250 ppm, and chronic toxicity risk begins above 250 ppm.

25. Which additive is named as the emerging alternative to zinc oxide for post-weaning diarrhoea?

Plant tannins, dosed around 0.5 to 1.5 g of tannic acid per kg, show efficacy comparable to zinc oxide in published work.

26. What is the main formulation consequence of losing crystalline amino acids in organic diets?

Every gram of lysine now comes from protein ingredients, so soybean meal rises and crude protein rises with it, all of the surplus being excreted.

27. 59 kg of maize at 0.24% lysine and 36 kg of soybean meal at 2.8% lysine give what total lysine per 100 kg?

0.142 kg from maize plus 1.008 kg from soybean meal gives 1.15 kg, or about 1.15 percent total lysine.

28. Which additive classes are listed as permitted under the strictest antibiotic-growth-promoter ban?

Published regulatory summaries name probiotics, phytogenics, organic acids and enzymes as the permitted alternatives under the EU regime in force since 2006.

29. Why does tighter phase feeding reduce the cost penalty of organic formulation?

Matching the published lysine ladder means less surplus oilseed meal in the later, larger-tonnage phases, where the waste would otherwise be greatest.

30. What selenium constraint applies to organic-style diets under EU rules?

The cap of 0.2 mg selenium per kg sits below the 0.30 to 0.50 mg/kg specified for breeding boars, which is a real constraint on organic boar diets.

Module 6 capstone

Run an Alternative Ingredient Trial and Costing on your own farm. Step 1: walk your local market, mill, brewery, bakery, oil press and restaurant strip and list every by-product or non-traditional feedstuff you could actually obtain weekly, with the quantity available, the asking price per kilogram in USD, and the transport cost to your gate. Step 2: for the three most promising wet or fresh materials, measure dry matter yourself by weighing one kilogram, drying it fully in the sun, and weighing again; record the percentage. Step 3: convert every candidate to a cost per kilogram of dry matter and, where you have a protein figure, a cost per kilogram of protein, and rank them against your current maize and oilseed cake. Step 4: choose one ingredient and one class of pig it genuinely suits, using the crude fibre ceilings in this module, and write a revised ration with inclusion percentages that stays inside those limits. Step 5: feed the revised ration to one pen for four weeks while a matched pen stays on your standard ration, weighing pigs at the start and end of both pens and recording all feed used. Step 6: calculate feed cost per kilogram of gain for both pens and write a one-page verdict, including what you would need the ingredient to cost before you would use it herd-wide.
