1. Proximate analysis: moisture, ash, protein, fat, fiber

Proximate analysis is the basic set of tests that tells you what a feed is made of. It measures six things: moisture, ash, crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, and nitrogen-free extract (NFE). In Africa, you can send samples to labs at universities like Makerere, Nairobi, or Ibadan, or use portable kits. For moisture, weigh a sample, dry it at 105Â°C for 24 hours, and reweigh. If you start with 100 g and end with 88 g, moisture is 12%. High moisture (above 14%) causes mould. Ash is what's left after burning at 550Â°C; it shows mineral content. A feed with 10% ash has a lot of dirt or minerals. Crude protein is measured by the Kjeldahl method: digest with acid, distill, and titrate. If you get 3.2% nitrogen, multiply by 6.25 to get 20% crude protein. That's good for soybean meal but low for fish meal. Crude fat is extracted with ether. A feed with 5% fat gives energy. Crude fiber is the tough part; high fiber (above 18%) means low digestibility for cattle. NFE is calculated by difference: 100% minus moisture, ash, protein, fat, and fiber. For a typical maize grain: 12% moisture, 1.5% ash, 9% protein, 4% fat, 2% fiber, so NFE is 71.5%. This tells you maize is energy-rich. Always test your own ingredients because African feeds vary widely. A farmer in Kenya found his hay had 8% protein, but a lab in Tanzania showed 5% for the same grass species. Proximate analysis is cheap and fast, and it's the first step to balancing a ration.

2. In vitro and in situ digestibility techniques

Digestibility tells you how much of a feed the animal can actually use. In vitro means 'in glass' â you simulate digestion in a lab. The most common method is the Tilley and Terry two-stage technique. You take 0.5 g of dried, ground feed, put it in a tube with rumen fluid from a slaughtered cow (or a fistulated cow), and incubate at 39Â°C for 48 hours. Then you add acid-pepsin and incubate another 24 hours. What's left is undigested. If you started with 0.5 g and 0.35 g disappeared, dry matter digestibility is 70%. That's good for alfalfa but poor for straw. In situ means 'in place' â you put feed samples in nylon bags (porosity 50 microns) and insert them into the rumen of a live cow through a fistula. You remove bags at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours. The weight loss over time gives you a degradation curve. For a farmer, you can estimate how fast a feed breaks down. For example, maize silage might have 60% digestibility at 48 hours, while cottonseed hulls only 30%. In Africa, fistulated cows are available at research stations like ILRI in Nairobi or IITA in Ibadan. If you don't have access, you can use the in vitro method with rumen fluid from a local abattoir. Collect fluid within 30 minutes of slaughter, keep it warm (39Â°C), and filter through cheesecloth. Always run a standard feed (e.g., alfalfa hay) alongside your samples to check the method. These techniques help you decide if a cheap feed like brewers' grains is worth buying. If in vitro digestibility is below 50%, it's poor for cattle. Above 65%, it's excellent. Use these numbers to formulate rations that meet energy needs without overfeeding.

3. Near-infrared reflectance spectroscopy (NIRS) for feed evaluation

NIRS is a fast, non-destructive method that uses light to predict feed composition. You shine near-infrared light (wavelengths 780â2500 nm) onto a ground feed sample. The light absorbed by the sample creates a spectrum, which is like a fingerprint of the feed's chemical bonds. A computer compares this spectrum to a library of known samples and predicts moisture, protein, fat, fiber, and even digestibility. In Africa, NIRS machines are found at big labs like the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organisation (KALRO) or the University of Pretoria. A single scan takes 2 minutes. You need only 50â100 g of ground sample. For example, a sample of maize stover might be predicted to have 4.5% crude protein and 70% neutral detergent fiber. The accuracy depends on the calibration library. If your feed is unusual (e.g., mango seed kernels), the prediction may be off. Always ask for the standard error of prediction (SEP). If SEP for protein is 0.5%, then the true value is within 0.5% of the predicted value. NIRS is cheaper per sample than wet chemistry (about $5 vs $50) and you can test many samples quickly. But you must calibrate with local feeds. A lab in Tanzania that used European calibrations gave wrong protein values for local grasses. So insist that the lab has African feed calibrations. For a farmer, NIRS is useful for screening many ingredients before sending a few for full analysis. You can also use portable NIRS devices like the NeoSpectra, which cost around $5,000 and can be taken to the farm. However, they are less accurate. Use NIRS as a quick check, but confirm with proximate analysis for critical decisions.

4. Energy estimation from chemical composition

Energy is the most important nutrient for cattle, but it's not measured directly in a feed analysis. Instead, you estimate it from chemical composition. The most common measures are total digestible nutrients (TDN), digestible energy (DE), metabolizable energy (ME), and net energy (NE). In Africa, you'll often see TDN on feed tags. To calculate TDN from proximate analysis, use the formula: TDN = digestible protein + digestible fiber + digestible NFE + (digestible fat Ã 2.25). The fat is multiplied by 2.25 because it has more energy. But you need digestibility coefficients. For example, for maize grain, digestibility of protein is 80%, fiber 70%, NFE 90%, fat 90%. If maize has 9% protein, 2% fiber, 71.5% NFE, and 4% fat, then TDN = (0.8Ã9) + (0.7Ã2) + (0.9Ã71.5) + (0.9Ã4Ã2.25) = 7.2 + 1.4 + 64.35 + 8.1 = 81.05%. That's high energy. For a low-quality straw with 4% protein, 35% fiber, 45% NFE, and 1% fat, TDN might be only 45%. In practice, you can use equations from NRC (2001) or AFRC. For tropical feeds, the French system (UFL) is common in West Africa. A UFL is the net energy for lactation of 1 kg of standard barley. Maize grain has about 1.2 UFL per kg dry matter. You can also estimate ME from TDN: ME (Mcal/kg) = TDN% Ã 0.04409. So 81% TDN gives 3.57 Mcal/kg. For a 500 kg cow giving 10 L milk, she needs about 20 Mcal ME per day. If your feed has 2.5 Mcal/kg, she needs 8 kg dry matter from that feed. Always adjust for stage of production. Energy estimation helps you avoid overfeeding expensive grains and use cheap by-products wisely.

5. Anti-nutritional factors and mycotoxin screening

Anti-nutritional factors (ANFs) are natural compounds in feeds that reduce digestion or harm the animal. Common ones in African feeds include tannins in sorghum and acacia leaves, trypsin inhibitors in raw soybeans, gossypol in cottonseed, and phytate in maize bran. Tannins bind proteins and make them unavailable. A feed with more than 5% tannins is poorly digested. You can test tannins with the Folin-Denis method: extract with water, add reagent, and read colour at 725 nm. For trypsin inhibitors, use the casein digestion assay. Raw soybeans have high inhibitors; heating at 120Â°C for 20 minutes destroys them. Gossypol is toxic to cattle; free gossypol should be below 0.02% in the diet. You can test with the aniline method. Mycotoxins are toxins from moulds, especially aflatoxin, fumonisin, and zearalenone. In Africa, aflatoxin is a big problem in groundnuts, maize, and cottonseed. Aflatoxin B1 above 20 ppb in feed is unsafe for dairy cattle. You can screen with ELISA kits (cost about $5 per test) or send to labs like the Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service (KEPHIS). For a quick farm test, use a black light: aflatoxin glows greenish-yellow under UV. But this is not quantitative. Always dry feeds to below 12% moisture and store in dry, ventilated areas. If you suspect mycotoxins, add a binder like bentonite or yeast cell wall to the ration. For ANFs, proper processing (soaking, cooking, roasting) reduces them. For example, soak cottonseed meal in water for 24 hours to reduce gossypol. Never feed mouldy silage. Test every new batch of by-products, especially those from oil extraction.

6. Interpreting feed analysis reports

A feed analysis report can look confusing, but it's just a table of numbers. The first thing to check is the moisture content. If it's above 14%, the feed may spoil. Next, look at crude protein (CP). For a growing steer, you want 12â16% CP. For a dry cow, 8â10% is enough. If the report says 9% CP, that feed is low for milk production. Then check crude fiber (CF). High CF (above 18%) means low energy. For dairy, you want CF below 18% but not too low, or the rumen gets upset. Ash tells you mineral content. If ash is above 10%, there may be dirt or excess minerals. Fat should be 3â5% for energy. If fat is above 8%, it can cause digestive problems. The report may also give neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF). NDF is total fiber; ADF is less digestible fiber. For good intake, NDF should be 30â40% of dry matter. ADF should be 20â30%. If NDF is 50%, the cow will eat less. You also get TDN or ME. For a 400 kg steer gaining 1 kg per day, he needs about 70% TDN. If your feed has 60% TDN, you must supplement with maize. Always compare the report to standard values for that ingredient. For example, cottonseed meal should have 40% CP, 1% fat, 15% fiber. If your report says 30% CP, it's been diluted. Look at the standard error or confidence intervals if given. Also check for anti-nutritional factors like tannins or mycotoxins. If aflatoxin is above 20 ppb, reject the feed. Use the report to calculate how much of each ingredient to mix. If you have maize (9% CP) and soybean meal (44% CP), and you want 14% CP, use Pearson's square: parts maize = 44-14=30, parts soybean = 14-9=5, so 30:5 = 6:1. That means 6 kg maize to 1 kg soybean. Always keep a copy of the report for your records.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Feed Ingredient Evaluation and Analysis for African Cattle Operations

Feed is the single largest recurring cost in any cattle or steer finishing operation â typically 60â75% of total production cost in African feedlots and smallholder fattening systems. Every naira, shilling, cedi, or rand spent on feed that does not convert into liveweight gain is a direct hit to your margin. This lesson gives you a practical, business-first reference for evaluating feed ingredients using laboratory analysis, so you can buy smarter, formulate tighter, and protect animal health.

Whether you are a commercial feedlot in Gauteng, a cooperative fattening scheme in Kenya's Rift Valley, or a peri-urban dairy-beef operation in Nigeria's Ogun State, the principles are the same: know what you are buying, what it is worth, and what could go wrong.

1. Proximate Analysis: The Six Numbers That Define Your Feed

Proximate analysis (Weende method) remains the workhorse of feed evaluation across Africa because it is affordable, widely available (universities, national research institutes, private labs), and standardized. It gives you six values:

  • Moisture â critical for storage and pricing. High moisture means you are paying for water and risking mould. Safe storage for grains and concentrates is typically â¤12â14%.
  • Crude Protein (CP) â the primary driver of price and formulation. Calculated as N Ã 6.25. Does not distinguish true protein from non-protein nitrogen (NPN).
  • Crude Fat (Ether Extract, EE) â energy-dense; high fat (>8%) can cause rancidity and depress fibre digestion in ruminants.
  • Crude Fibre (CF) â inversely related to energy value in concentrates. High CF = low digestibility.
  • Ash â mineral content. High ash in a concentrate often signals soil contamination, bone meal adulteration, or excessive mineral inclusion.
  • Nitrogen-Free Extract (NFE) â calculated by difference; represents soluble carbohydrates (starches, sugars).

Business tip: Always negotiate feed purchases on a dry matter (DM) basis, not as-fed. A 50 kg bag of maize bran at 12% moisture delivers 44 kg DM; at 18% moisture it delivers only 41 kg DM â you are paying for 3 kg of water per bag.

2. In Vitro and In Situ Digestibility Techniques

Chemical composition alone does not tell you how much energy the animal actually extracts. Digestibility testing closes that gap.

In vitro (Tilley & Terry, or DaisyII ANKOM): Feed is incubated with rumen fluid and buffer for 24â48 hours, then with pepsin. Results give in vitro dry matter digestibility (IVDMD) and in vitro organic matter digestibility (IVOMD). This is the most practical option for African labs without cannulated animals.

In situ (nylon bag technique): Feed samples are placed in nylon bags and incubated in the rumen of a cannulated steer. This measures rate and extent of degradation. More expensive and requires surgically prepared animals â usually only available at national research institutes (e.g., ILRI Nairobi, ARC South Africa, NAPRI Zaria).

Business relevance: A maize stover with 5% CP but 55% IVDMD is worth far more than one with 5% CP and 40% IVDMD. Digestibility is what determines your feed conversion ratio (FCR) and therefore your cost per kg gain.

3. Near-Infrared Reflectance Spectroscopy (NIRS)

NIRS shines near-infrared light on a ground sample and measures reflectance at hundreds of wavelengths. A calibration model (built from wet-chemistry reference samples) predicts moisture, CP, fat, fibre, and even digestibility in under two minutes. It is non-destructive, requires no chemicals, and can be operated by semi-skilled staff.

Adoption in Africa: NIRS is now used by commercial feed mills in South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria, and by ILRI and national programs for forage screening. The main barrier is calibration â models built on European temperate forages may not predict African tropical grasses and crop residues accurately. Always ask your lab which calibration set was used.

4. Energy Estimation from Chemical Composition

Energy is the most expensive nutrient you buy. Common equations used in Africa:

  • TDN (Total Digestible Nutrients) â from digestible CP, CF, EE, NFE.
  • ME (Metabolizable Energy, MJ/kg DM) â often estimated from IVDMD or from gas production (in vitro gas technique).
  • NE (Net Energy) â used in beef formulation (NRC, AFRC). Requires more inputs but gives the most accurate prediction of gain.

Rule of thumb: For tropical grasses and crop residues, ME (MJ/kg DM) â 0.16 Ã IVDMD (%) â a rough but useful field approximation.

5. Anti-Nutritional Factors and Mycotoxin Screening

Africa's warm, humid post-harvest conditions make mycotoxins a persistent threat. Common concerns in cattle feeds:

  • Aflatoxin B1 â groundnut cake, maize, cottonseed. Liver damage; milk residues (aflatoxin M1) are a public health and export risk.
  • Fumonisin â maize. Associated with equine leukoencephalomalacia; ruminants are more tolerant but performance drops.
  • Deoxynivalenol (DON) â wheat, maize. Feed refusal, reduced intake.
  • Tannins â sorghum, some browse. Bind protein, reduce digestibility.
  • Trypsin inhibitors, cyanogenic glycosides â raw soybeans, cassava leaf. Manage by heat treatment or limiting inclusion.

â  SAFETY WARNING â MYCOTOXIN CONTAMINATION

Never feed visibly mouldy or musty-smelling grain, silage, or oilseed cake to cattle â especially lactating animals. Aflatoxin B1 above 20 ppb in total diet (or 0.5 ppb aflatoxin M1 in milk) is a food safety violation in most markets and a serious human health risk. Do not attempt to "dilute" contaminated feed by mixing with clean feed without laboratory confirmation. Dispose of contaminated batches according to national regulations (e.g., NAFDAC in Nigeria, KEBS in Kenya, DALRRD in South Africa). Always wear gloves and a dust mask when handling suspect feed, and store feed below 12% moisture in well-ventilated, palletised stacks.

A lab report is only useful if you can act on it. Always check:

  1. Basis â as-fed or DM? Convert everything to DM before comparing.

  3. Reference values â compare against known standards (see Table 1).
  4. Variability â a single sample is a snapshot. For bulk purchases, sample at least 10% of bags.
  5. Cost per unit nutrient â not cost per tonne.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Sample properly â take a representative sample (10% of bags or a composite from the lot), seal it, label it with date, supplier, and batch.
  2. Insist on DM basis â convert every value and every price to dry matter before comparing ingredients or negotiating.
  3. Match analysis to use â proximate analysis for routine buying; NIRS for rapid screening; in vitro digestibility for high-value or unfamiliar ingredients.
  4. Check energy, not just protein â the cheapest CP is worthless if the energy is too low for your target gain.
  5. Screen for mycotoxins â especially aflatoxin in maize, groundnut, and cottonseed products. Reject any batch above regulatory limits.
  6. Verify the lab â use accredited labs (e.g., KEBS, SANAS, NAFDAC-recognised) and ask which method and calibration were used.
  7. Calculate cost per unit nutrient â cost per kg CP, per MJ ME, or per kg TDN. This is your true buying decision tool.
  8. Keep records â build your own local database of ingredient analyses. Over time, this becomes your most valuable formulation asset.
