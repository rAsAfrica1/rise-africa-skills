Lesson 12.1~12 min

Calculating Your True Cost Per Kilogram of Pork

In this lesson
  • List every cost category that belongs in a true cost per kilogram calculation
  • Calculate cost per kilogram of pork sold from a full year of farm records
  • Identify which two or three costs drive the majority of your total and target them

Almost every failing pig business shares one symptom: the owner does not know what a kilogram of their pork costs to produce. They know the feed price and the sale price, and they assume the difference is profit. It is not, and the gap is where the business quietly loses money.

A true cost per kilogram calculation includes eleven categories. Feed by phase, which will be 60 to 75 percent of the total on most units. Breeding stock, meaning the cost of replacement gilts and boars spread over their productive life. Veterinary and medicine including vaccines. Labour, including your own time valued at what you would have to pay someone else, because a business that only works when the owner is unpaid is not a business. Energy for pumping, lighting and milling. Water. Transport, both bringing feed in and taking pigs out. Repairs and maintenance. Depreciation on buildings and equipment, meaning the purchase price divided over the years of useful life. Interest on any borrowed money. And mortality, which is not a separate cash cost but must be accounted for because dead pigs consumed feed and produced no revenue.

Worked example. A producer in Kitwe, Zambia, runs 15 sows and sells 240 finished pigs a year at 85 kg liveweight. His records for the year show:

  1. Feed: 240 pigs x 230 kg x 0.45 USD = 24,840, plus sow and boar feed of 15 sows x 1,200 kg x 0.40 = 7,200, plus boar feed of 2 boars x 900 kg x 0.40 = 720. Feed total 32,760 USD.
  2. Breeding stock: 4 replacement gilts at 250 each = 1,000, plus one boar at 400 amortised over 3 years = 133. Total 1,133.
  3. Veterinary and medicine: 1,450.
  4. Labour: two workers at 90 a month = 2,160, plus his own time valued at 150 a month = 1,800. Total 3,960.
  5. Energy and water: 720.
  6. Transport: 1,100.
  7. Repairs: 600.
  8. Depreciation: buildings and equipment costing 18,000 over 15 years = 1,200.
  9. Interest on a 5,000 loan at 18 percent = 900.

Total annual cost = 32,760 + 1,133 + 1,450 + 3,960 + 720 + 1,100 + 600 + 1,200 + 900 = 43,823 USD.

Output: 240 pigs x 85 kg liveweight = 20,400 kg liveweight. At an approximate 72 percent dressing yield that is 14,688 kg of carcass.

Cost per kg liveweight = 43,823 / 20,400 = 2.15 USD. Cost per kg carcass = 43,823 / 14,688 = 2.98 USD.

Now the decision-making becomes real. If a trader offers him 2.90 USD per kg carcass, he is losing money on every pig even though the cash in his hand feels positive, because he has not paid himself and has not set aside depreciation. If his premium butchery pays 3.60, he makes 0.62 per kg carcass, which is 9.11 USD per pig and 2,186 USD a year. All prices in this example are illustrative and you must substitute your own.

The second use of the calculation is targeting. Feed is 32,760 of 43,823, which is 75 percent. A 5 percent improvement in feed cost or feed conversion saves 1,638 USD. A 5 percent reduction in veterinary spend saves 73 USD. This tells him exactly where to spend his management attention, and it is almost always feed. Within feed, the two levers are the purchase price of ingredients, which Module 12 lesson 5 addresses, and feed conversion, which every previous module has been about.

A warning about the most common error. Producers routinely exclude their own labour and exclude depreciation because neither leaves the bank account this month. Both are real. If you do not value your own time, you cannot tell whether the farm is paying you better than a job would. If you do not set aside depreciation, the day your roof fails or your boar dies you will have no money and will call it bad luck rather than bad accounting.

Do this calculation once a year at minimum, and recalculate whenever feed prices move more than about 10 percent.

The Batch Feed Budget: Forecasting Tonnes and Cash Month by Month

In this lesson
  • Convert a production plan into a monthly feed tonnage requirement by phase
  • Translate that tonnage into a month-by-month cash forecast
  • Identify the cash-flow gap months and plan for them before they arrive

Pig farms rarely fail from lack of profit. They fail from lack of cash at the wrong moment. Feed must be bought weeks before pigs are sold, and a producer who has not forecast that gap will find themselves cutting rations at exactly the point where cutting rations is most expensive.

Build the forecast in three steps: pigs, tonnes, then money.

Step one, the pig plan. Write out a table by month showing how many pigs you expect in each stage: sows in gestation, sows in lactation, piglets in creep, weaners in nursery phases 1 to 3, and finishers in each weight band. If you farrow in batches this is straightforward. If you farrow continuously it is more work but more important.

Step two, tonnes by phase. Use the published phase framework as your structure. Nursery phase 1 covers 5 to 7.5 kg over 2 to 3 weeks, phase 2 covers 7.5 to 11.5 kg over 2 to 3 weeks, and phase 3 covers 11.5 to 23 kg over 2 to 4 weeks. Finisher phases run 27 to 40, 40 to 60, 60 to 80, 80 to 100, 100 to 120 and 120 to 130 kg, each typically 2 to 3 weeks except the last. For each phase, multiply pig numbers by the feed each pig eats in that phase. If you do not know your own intake figures, start by using body weight gain divided by your feed conversion ratio, then correct with real weighbridge or scale data as you collect it.

Step three, money. Multiply tonnes by phase by the price per tonne of that diet. Nursery diets cost far more per tonne than finisher diets because of lactose, specialty proteins and additives, so do not use a single average price. Creep feed runs in the region of 0.50 to 0.80 USD per kg against 0.35 to 0.45 for a plain nursery diet, and finisher diets are cheaper still.

Worked example. A producer in Mbeya, Tanzania, farrows 6 sows a month, weaning an average of 9 piglets each, so 54 weaners a month. Her phase feed intakes, measured on her own farm, are 6 kg per pig in nursery 1, 16 kg in nursery 2, 30 kg in nursery 3, and 175 kg across all finisher phases.

Monthly tonnage:

  • Nursery 1: 54 x 6 = 324 kg
  • Nursery 2: 54 x 16 = 864 kg
  • Nursery 3: 54 x 30 = 1,620 kg
  • Finisher: 54 x 175 = 9,450 kg
  • Sow and boar: 15 sows x 100 kg + 2 boars x 75 kg = 1,650 kg

Total about 13,908 kg, or 13.9 tonnes a month.

Monthly cash at her prices:

  • Nursery 1 at 0.90 USD/kg: 291.60
  • Nursery 2 at 0.65: 561.60
  • Nursery 3 at 0.50: 810.00
  • Finisher at 0.42: 3,969.00
  • Sow and boar at 0.40: 660.00

Total 6,292.20 USD a month, about 75,500 a year. Note that even though nursery feed is only 20 percent of her tonnage, it is a much smaller share of her cash than finisher feed, which is 63 percent of the money. That is where price negotiation matters most.

Now overlay her sales. Pigs sold in a given month were weaned about five months earlier. If she started a new batch cycle in January, her first significant sales fall around June. From January to May she is buying feed for a growing pig population with almost no revenue. Her cumulative cash requirement over those five months, growing from a small figure to the full 6,292 a month, might total in the region of 20,000 USD. That is the number she must have arranged before January, not discovered in March.

Three practical rules follow.

  1. Never start a batch you cannot feed to slaughter. Selling half-grown pigs because you ran out of feed money destroys the margin you were farming for.
  2. Buy the big-tonnage cheap diets, meaning the finisher feeds, as well as you can. A 0.02 USD per kg saving on 9,450 kg a month is 189 USD a month, or 2,268 a year.
  3. Hold a feed reserve of at least two to four weeks. Maize prices in most African markets are seasonal, and a producer with storage buys at harvest instead of at the peak.

Also remember the mycotoxin discipline from Module 9. Buying six months of maize at harvest only works if you can dry it to 15 percent moisture or below and store it clean. Cheap grain that spoils is the most expensive grain there is.

Batch Farrowing and Throughput: More Pigs From the Same Buildings

In this lesson
  • Explain how batch farrowing improves pen utilisation, health and market consistency
  • Calculate pigs produced per sow per year and identify where your losses occur
  • Design a batch schedule that fits your existing farrowing and finishing capacity

The cheapest expansion is the one that uses buildings you already own. Most small pig units are running well below their physical capacity because pigs of every age are mixed through the pens continuously, which wastes space, spreads disease and produces an unpredictable supply for buyers.

Batch farrowing means grouping sows so that a defined number farrow together within a few days, then move through the system as a cohort. The alternative, continuous farrowing, means sows farrow whenever they happen to. Batching delivers four things at once. First, all-in all-out pen use: a pen empties completely, gets washed, dried and rested, and refills with one age group, which is the single most powerful disease control measure available without spending money on drugs. Second, uniform pigs in each pen, which reduces bullying and makes feeding to a phase specification possible. Third, predictable weekly or monthly sale volumes, which is exactly the consistency premium buyers demanded in Module 11. Fourth, concentrated labour, so you handle farrowing, weaning and vaccination in planned blocks instead of every day.

The key productivity metric is pigs sold per sow per year. Calculate it as litters per sow per year multiplied by piglets born alive, less pre-weaning mortality, less post-weaning mortality. Litters per sow per year is 365 divided by the sum of gestation at about 115 days, lactation at your weaning age, and the wean-to-service interval.

Worked example. A producer in Eldoret, Kenya, has 12 sows and sells 168 pigs a year, which is 14 pigs per sow per year. She breaks the number down:

  1. Gestation 115 days + lactation 28 days + wean to service 8 days = 151 days per cycle.
  2. Litters per sow per year = 365 / 151 = 2.42.
  3. Born alive averaged 10.5.
  4. Pre-weaning mortality 14 percent, so weaned = 10.5 x 0.86 = 9.03.
  5. Post-weaning mortality 4 percent, so sold = 9.03 x 0.96 = 8.67.
  6. Pigs sold per sow per year = 2.42 x 8.67 = 20.98 in theory, against 14 actual.

The gap between 21 and 14 is her real opportunity, and it is bigger than anything she would gain from buying more sows. Investigating, she finds her actual wean-to-service interval averages 21 days rather than 8, because thin sows come out of lactation and do not cycle. That pushes the cycle to 164 days and litters to 2.23. She also finds two sows are effectively non-productive. Both problems are nutritional and were covered in Module 10 lesson 4: lactating sows must be fed ad libitum toward roughly 6.4 kg a day, and heat stress above about 29 degrees Celsius cuts intake 15 to 30 percent, which produces exactly the thin, anoestrus sow she is describing.

So the sequence of a real throughput improvement is: measure pigs per sow per year, break it into its components, find the weakest component, and usually discover it is sow nutrition or piglet survival rather than sow numbers.

Designing a batch schedule. Work backwards from farrowing capacity. If you have 4 farrowing pens and sows occupy them for 5 weeks including cleaning, you can farrow 4 sows every 5 weeks, which is about 41 sow-farrowings a year. With 2.3 litters per sow per year that supports roughly 18 sows. If you only have 12 sows, your farrowing house is not the constraint and you can expand the herd without building. If you have 24 sows, the farrowing house is your bottleneck and every dollar spent elsewhere is wasted.

A practical three-week batching system for a small unit: divide the sow herd into groups, serve one group every three weeks, and each group farrows three weeks apart. This gives you a farrowing block, a weaning block and a sales block on a predictable rhythm. It also lets you buy feed in phase-matched quantities rather than guessing.

One caution. Moving from continuous to batch farrowing requires synchronising the sow herd, which usually means holding some sows over a cycle and losing some production during the transition. Plan a transition period of one full reproductive cycle and accept a short-term dip. The permanent gain in health, pen utilisation and market consistency is worth it, but do not attempt it in the same month you take on a new premium contract.

Financing Growth: Loans, Savings Groups, Out-Grower Deals and Retained Earnings

In this lesson
  • Compare the realistic financing options open to a small African pig producer
  • Calculate whether a loan is affordable using the return the investment generates
  • Structure an out-grower or contract arrangement that shares risk fairly

Capital is the binding constraint on most pig businesses that are otherwise well run. Understanding the real options, and the real cost of each, prevents both the timidity that keeps a good farm small and the recklessness that ends one.

Option one, retained earnings. Growing from your own profit is the cheapest capital available and carries no risk of default. It is slow, and it is also the option that most successful small producers actually used. The discipline is to pay yourself a fixed modest amount and treat the remainder as farm capital rather than household income.

Option two, savings and credit groups, known variously as SACCOs, VSLAs, chamas, susu or merry-go-rounds depending on the country. Interest is often lower than a bank, approval depends on social standing rather than collateral, and amounts are modest. These suit incremental investments such as two extra farrowing pens or a feed mixer.

Option three, microfinance institutions and agricultural banks. Larger amounts, formal paperwork, and interest that in many African markets sits somewhere between roughly 15 and 30 percent a year, though this varies enormously by country and by year, so obtain current quotes rather than relying on any figure printed here. Agricultural lenders sometimes offer grace periods matched to production cycles, which matters greatly for pigs.

Option four, input credit from feed suppliers. A feed company supplies feed on credit against a commitment to sell pigs through them or repay after sale. This solves the exact cash-flow gap described in lesson 2. Read these agreements carefully: the effective interest rate is often hidden in the feed price rather than stated as interest.

Option five, out-grower or contract arrangements. A larger integrator or processor supplies weaners, feed and technical support, and you supply housing, labour and management, being paid per pig produced or on a performance grid. This dramatically reduces your capital requirement and your price risk, and it equally reduces your margin and your independence.

Option six, equity, meaning a partner or family member who puts in money for a share of the business. Cheapest in cash terms, most expensive in control, and the option most likely to end in a family dispute if it is not written down.

The affordability test. A loan is affordable when the investment it funds generates enough additional annual margin to cover the repayment with room to spare.

Worked example. A producer in Kampala, Uganda, wants 4,000 USD to build a finishing house that lets her carry 40 more pigs a year. Her margin per pig, after all costs from lesson 1, is 22 USD. So the additional annual margin is 40 x 22 = 880 USD. The loan is offered over 3 years at 22 percent annual interest on a reducing balance. Approximate annual repayment on a straight amortisation is roughly 1,850 USD in year one falling as the balance reduces, averaging around 1,700 USD a year. Her 880 USD of additional margin does not cover it. The loan is not affordable on that expansion alone.

She has three honest responses. Reduce the capital cost by building smaller or with cheaper materials. Increase the margin per pig, for example by capturing the premium market from Module 11, which at 0.60 USD per kg carcass on a 61 kg carcass adds 36.60 per pig and would transform the calculation. Or use the same 4,000 to fix the throughput problem from lesson 3, which may add pigs with no new building at all. Note that the interest rates and repayment figures here are illustrative; get actual quotes.

The general rule: borrow to fix a bottleneck, not to buy more of what you already have too much of. Buying more sows when your farrowing house is full, your feed budget is stretched and your buyers are unreliable makes every existing problem larger.

Four cautions on any agreement. Get everything in writing, including family arrangements. Understand exactly what happens if you cannot pay, and what asset is at risk. Match the repayment schedule to your production cycle, because a monthly repayment starting immediately does not fit a five-month pig cycle. And never borrow in a foreign currency when your income is in local currency, because a devaluation can double your debt without you doing anything wrong.

Mill It or Buy It: The Break-Even Calculation for On-Farm Feed Mixing

In this lesson
  • List the true costs of on-farm feed mixing beyond the ingredient price
  • Calculate the break-even tonnage at which a mixer pays for itself
  • Set the quality controls that make home-mixed feed safe and consistent

Feed is 60 to 75 percent of your cost, so the temptation to mix your own is strong and often correct. But home mixing is not free, and producers who compare only ingredient cost against bagged feed price reach the wrong answer roughly half the time.

The true cost of home mixing includes: ingredients delivered to your farm, not at the market gate; the premix, which you must buy regardless and which carries the vitamins, trace minerals, and often the amino acids and phytase; grinding cost, whether that is your own hammer mill's fuel and maintenance or a toll milling fee; mixing labour; storage, both for ingredients and finished feed; shrinkage and spillage; wastage from poor mixing; the capital cost of the mill and mixer spread over their life; and the risk cost of getting the formulation wrong, which is the biggest and least visible item.

That last one deserves emphasis. A bagged commercial weaner feed carries a formulation done by a nutritionist against specifications like the ones in this course: SID lysine 1.30 to 1.40 percent in nursery phase 1, 1.20 to 1.30 in phase 2, 1.08 to 1.15 in phase 3, with methionine plus cysteine at 56 to 62 percent of lysine, threonine at 62 to 65 percent, tryptophan at 18 to 20 percent, calcium at 0.85 percent and total phosphorus at 0.70 percent for a 5 to 11 kg weaner. Getting that wrong in a home mix costs far more than the mixing saves. The practical resolution most small producers reach is to buy nursery and creep diets ready-made, where volumes are small and precision matters most, and home-mix the grower, finisher and gestation diets, where volumes are large and the specifications are more forgiving.

Break-even calculation. Worked example from Kaduna, Nigeria. A producer buys 12 tonnes of finisher feed a month at 0.42 USD per kg, so 5,040 USD a month. She prices home mixing at 0.34 per kg for ingredients and premix delivered, which is 4,080 a month, an apparent saving of 960.

Now the honest additions. A hammer mill and a 500 kg horizontal mixer, installed, cost 5,500 USD. Over 7 years that is 786 a year, or 65.50 a month. Electricity or diesel for milling 12 tonnes, say 130 a month. One extra worker at 90 a month. Extra storage shed, 1,800 over 10 years, so 15 a month. Shrinkage and spillage at 2 percent of ingredient value, 82 a month. Total added cost 382.50 a month. Net saving 960 minus 382.50 = 577.50 a month, or 6,930 a year. The 5,500 capital pays back in under a year. At this tonnage, mixing is clearly right.

Now run the same numbers at 3 tonnes a month. Ingredient saving becomes 0.08 x 3,000 = 240. Fixed costs barely fall: capital 65.50, worker 90, storage 15, energy about 33, shrinkage 20, total 223.50. Net saving 16.50 a month. At 3 tonnes a month, mixing is not worth the trouble, and the producer would be better served by joining or forming a group that mixes collectively. That is the break-even logic in a single comparison, and all figures here are illustrative. Substitute your own.

Quality control is what separates a home mix that works from one that quietly ruins performance. Five disciplines are non-negotiable.

  1. Weigh every ingredient. Never measure by volume, because bulk density varies with moisture and particle size.
  2. Pre-mix micro-ingredients into a carrier. Anything included below about 10 kg per tonne, which includes premix, phytase, acids, essential oils and any zinc oxide, must be blended into 20 to 50 kg of maize meal first, or it will not distribute.
  3. Mix for the full recommended time and no less. Under-mixing is the most common cause of inconsistent home-mixed feed.
  4. Test incoming grain for mould risk and moisture, applying the Module 9 limits: aflatoxin 20 ppb for breeding stock and young pigs, DON 5 ppm, fumonisin 10 ppm, zearalenone 1 ppm or less.
  5. Label every batch with a date and formula reference, and keep the record, which also serves the traceability that premium buyers require.

One final point on ingredient purchasing. The biggest saving in home mixing is usually not the mixing itself but the ability to buy maize at harvest and store it. That advantage only exists if you have dry, clean, pest-controlled storage at 15 percent moisture or below.

Lesson 12.6~12 min

Writing a Bankable Pig Business Plan and Building a Risk Register

In this lesson
  • Assemble a business plan with the sections a lender or investor actually reads
  • Build sensitivity analysis showing how profit responds to feed and pork price changes
  • Construct a risk register with a named mitigation for every significant risk

A business plan is not a document you write to get money. It is the document that tells you whether the money should be spent at all. Write it for yourself first and the lender second, and it will be better on both counts.

A workable plan has nine sections, in this order.

  1. Summary, one page, written last. What you want, what it costs, what it returns, and the single biggest risk. Many lenders read only this.
  2. The business as it is today. Herd size, buildings, land, staff, current output, current buyers. Facts and numbers only.
  3. Track record. Your last two years of production and financial figures, however imperfect. A modest but honest record beats an impressive forecast with nothing behind it.
  4. The proposal. Exactly what you will build, buy or change, with quotations attached.
  5. The market. Who buys your pigs now, at what price, and who will buy the additional output. Include the buyer interviews from the Module 11 capstone. A named buyer is worth more than a page of market statistics.
  6. Production plan. Batch schedule, pigs per sow per year target, feed budget in tonnes by phase, and the feeding specifications you will work to, referencing life-stage standards such as gestation SID lysine at 0.50 to 0.60 percent, lactation at roughly 60 g SID lysine per day, and finisher phases stepping from 1.05 down to 0.65 percent SID lysine.
  7. Financial projections. Three years, showing revenue, cost by category, margin, cash flow month by month, and the loan repayment schedule if any.
  8. Sensitivity analysis. See below.
  9. Risk register. See below.

Sensitivity analysis is where an experienced lender looks for honesty. Take your projected annual profit and recalculate it under four scenarios: feed price up 20 percent, pork price down 15 percent, mortality doubled, and all three together. If the business survives the first three and merely loses money in the fourth, that is a credible plan. If it fails under a 10 percent feed increase, you are asking someone to fund a business that is already broken.

Worked example. A producer in Dodoma, Tanzania, projects annual revenue of 52,000 USD, feed cost 33,000, other costs 12,000, and profit 7,000. Feed price up 20 percent adds 6,600 of cost, leaving profit of 400. Pork price down 15 percent cuts revenue by 7,800, giving a loss of 800. Both together give a loss of 7,400. This tells her, and her lender, that her margin is thin and her first priority should be raising margin per pig or cutting feed cost per kilogram of gain before borrowing to expand. That is an uncomfortable conclusion and it is exactly what the analysis exists to produce.

The risk register lists each risk, its likelihood, its impact, and what you will actually do about it. For an African pig enterprise the register should include at least: African swine fever or another notifiable disease outbreak, with biosecurity, all-in all-out pen use and a written response plan as mitigation; feed ingredient price spikes, mitigated by harvest-time buying and storage; mycotoxin contamination, mitigated by testing, storage discipline at 15 percent moisture and routing suspect grain to older finishers within the 200 ppb aflatoxin limit rather than to sows at 20 ppb; pork price collapse, mitigated by diversified buyers and premium accounts; drought or crop failure affecting maize supply; loss of your main buyer, mitigated by holding three or four accounts; key person illness, mitigated by written procedures so someone else can run the farm; theft; currency devaluation; and regulatory change, such as tightening rules on zinc oxide or antibiotic use.

Write the mitigation as an action, not a hope. Diversify buyers is a hope. Hold four accounts, none above 40 percent of output, reviewed quarterly is an action.

Finally, keep the plan alive. Update the production and financial figures every quarter, and rewrite the whole document annually. The producers who scale successfully are not the ones with the best plan on day one. They are the ones who compare the plan against reality every three months and correct early, while corrections are still cheap.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Which cost is most often wrongly excluded from a smallholder's cost calculation?

Neither leaves the bank account monthly, so both get ignored. Excluding them hides whether the farm actually pays the owner and leaves no fund for replacing assets.

2. Total annual cost is $43,823 and 14,688 kg of carcass is sold. What is the cost per kilogram of carcass?

43,823 divided by 14,688 gives about 2.98 USD per kilogram of carcass weight.

3. If feed is 75 percent of total cost, a 5 percent feed improvement on a $43,823 total cost saves roughly how much?

Feed is about 32,760 USD, and 5 percent of that is roughly 1,638 USD, far more than the same percentage saved on any other category.

4. Why must mortality be accounted for even though dead pigs generate no invoice?

Every pig that dies has already eaten. That feed cost must be recovered from the pigs that are sold, which raises the true cost per kilogram.

5. How often should the cost per kilogram calculation be redone?

Annual review plus a trigger on significant feed price movement keeps pricing decisions grounded in current reality.

6. Why do pig farms most often fail on cash rather than on profit?

There is a long gap between feed spend and sale revenue. Without forecasting it, producers run out of cash mid-batch and are forced to sell or underfeed.

7. 54 weaners each eat 16 kg in nursery phase 2. How much feed is needed?

54 multiplied by 16 gives 864 kg for that phase in that month.

8. Why should a feed budget never use a single average price per kilogram?

Diet costs differ sharply by phase. Averaging hides both where the cash actually goes and where negotiation would pay off most.

9. What is the most damaging consequence of running out of feed cash mid-batch?

Pigs sold below target weight lose most of the value that the earlier feed investment was building. Never start a batch you cannot feed to slaughter.

10. What condition must be met before buying six months of maize at harvest prices?

Grain stored above 15 percent moisture supports mould growth and mycotoxin formation. Cheap grain that spoils becomes the most expensive grain on the farm.

11. What is the most powerful disease-control benefit of batch farrowing?

Emptying, washing, drying and resting a pen between single-age groups breaks disease cycles more effectively than any additive, and it costs nothing but scheduling.

12. Gestation is 115 days, lactation 28 days and wean-to-service 8 days. How many litters per sow per year is that?

115 + 28 + 8 gives a 151-day cycle. 365 divided by 151 is about 2.42 litters per sow per year.

13. A sow herd shows a 21-day wean-to-service interval instead of 8. What is the most likely nutritional cause?

Sows that lose excessive condition in lactation fail to cycle promptly. Ad libitum lactation feeding and heat stress management are the fixes.

14. You have 4 farrowing pens each occupied 5 weeks per litter. Roughly how many farrowings per year can the house support?

52 weeks divided by 5 gives about 10.4 uses per pen per year, multiplied by 4 pens gives roughly 41 farrowings.

15. What should a producer expect during the transition from continuous to batch farrowing?

Synchronising the herd means holding some sows over, which causes a short-term dip. Plan for it and do not schedule it alongside a new contract.

16. What is the correct affordability test for a farm loan?

The investment must pay for its own repayment out of the extra margin it generates, with a margin of safety for a bad year.

17. In an out-grower contract, what does the producer usually give up?

The integrator supplies weaners, feed and technical support and takes the price risk, so the producer earns a smaller but more predictable return.

18. Where is the interest often hidden in a feed-supplier credit arrangement?

Input credit is frequently priced through a higher feed cost. Compare the credit feed price against the cash feed price to find the true rate.

19. An expansion generates $880 of extra annual margin and the loan repayment averages $1,700 a year. What should the producer do?

The expansion fails the affordability test as designed. Cheaper construction, a premium price, or fixing throughput instead are the honest alternatives.

20. Why should a producer with local-currency income avoid foreign-currency borrowing?

Currency mismatch is one of the fastest ways a sound farm becomes insolvent, because the debt grows in local terms while revenue does not.

21. Which diets do most small producers sensibly buy ready-made rather than home-mix?

Nursery volumes are small and specifications are demanding, so formulation errors are costly. Grower, finisher and gestation diets are larger volume and more forgiving.

22. An ingredient included at 3 kg per tonne must be handled how?

Anything below roughly 10 kg per tonne cannot distribute evenly if added directly. Pre-mixing into a carrier is the only reliable method.

23. Home mixing saves $0.08/kg on 3 tonnes a month, with $223.50 of fixed added cost. What is the net monthly saving?

3,000 kg times 0.08 is 240, minus 223.50 of added fixed cost, leaves 16.50 a month, which is not worth the effort at that scale.

24. Why must ingredients be weighed rather than measured by volume?

A tin of maize meal and a tin of soybean meal weigh very differently, and the same tin of maize varies with moisture. Only weight gives a repeatable formula.

25. What is often the largest real saving from on-farm mixing?

Seasonal grain price differences usually exceed the mixing margin, but the advantage only exists with dry, clean storage at 15 percent moisture or below.

26. Which section of a business plan should be written last but appear first?

The summary distils everything else, so it is written last. Many lenders read only that page, which is why it must state what you want, what it costs, what it returns and the biggest risk.

27. Revenue is $52,000 with $45,000 of costs. If feed cost of $33,000 rises 20 percent, what happens to the $7,000 profit?

A 20 percent rise on 33,000 adds 6,600 of cost, cutting profit from 7,000 to about 400 and revealing a thin margin.

28. Which is a properly written mitigation in a risk register?

A mitigation must be a specific, checkable action with a threshold and a review interval. Everything else is a hope.

29. Where should suspect mycotoxin-contaminated grain be routed?

Finishers over 100 lb have a 200 ppb aflatoxin limit against 20 ppb for breeding stock and young pigs, so routing by class is the standard mitigation.

30. What most distinguishes producers who successfully scale?

Plans are always wrong in detail. Frequent comparison against actual figures allows cheap early corrections rather than expensive late ones.

Module 12 capstone

Produce a complete, bankable Pig Enterprise Plan for your own farm using only your own data. Step 1: build a twelve-month cost record covering feed by phase, breeding stock, veterinary and medicine, labour including your own time valued at a local wage, energy, water, transport, repairs, interest and depreciation. Step 2: record every kilogram of pork or every pig sold in the same twelve months with the price received, and calculate your true cost per kilogram of pork sold and your margin. Step 3: build a batch feed budget for the next twelve months in tonnes by phase, using your own feed conversion figures, and convert it into a month-by-month cash requirement. Step 4: model one specific expansion, such as adding four sows, adding a finishing house, or building a small mixing operation, showing the capital required, the additional annual cost, the additional annual revenue, the payback period and the break-even pig price. Step 5: write a risk register listing at least eight risks including disease, feed price, market price, drought, currency, key-person illness and buyer loss, with a stated mitigation for each. Step 6: assemble everything into a document a lender or investor could read in fifteen minutes, with a one-page summary at the front stating what you want, what it costs, what it returns and what could go wrong.
