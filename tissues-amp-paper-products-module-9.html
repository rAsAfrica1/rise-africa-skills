Lessons

1. Separating Personal and Business Money

When you run a tissue and paper products business, money flows in and out constantly. If you mix your personal money with business money, you will never know if your business is actually making a profit. Start by opening a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for your business. In Kenya, use M-Pesa with a separate SIM card; in Nigeria, use a dedicated bank account or Opay wallet. Even if you operate informally, keep a separate cash box for business cash.

Every time you sell a pack of tissues, that money goes into the business account. When you need to buy raw materials or pay for transport, you take from that account. If you need personal money, pay yourself a fixed salarysay 10,000 shillings per weekand record it as an expense. That way, you can see if the business can afford to pay you.

A simple method: Use two envelopes. One for business cash, one for personal cash. At the end of each day, count both. If you find yourself borrowing from the business envelope to buy personal items, you are stealing from your business. Instead, write down the amount as a loan from the business to you, and repay it.

Separating money also helps when applying for loans or grants. Lenders want to see clean records. If your business account shows regular sales and expenses, you are more likely to get funding. Start today: open that separate account, even if it is just a mobile wallet. Your business will thank you.

2. Using a Simple Cash Book or Mobile App for Record Keeping

You don't need accounting software to keep good records. A simple exercise book can work as a cash book. Draw five columns: Date, Description, Money In, Money Out, and Balance. Every time you receive money, write it in 'Money In'. Every time you spend, write it in 'Money Out'. At the end of each day, calculate the balance: previous balance plus money in minus money out.

For example, on Monday you start with 5,000 KSh. You sell 20 packs of tissues at 100 KSh each, so you write 2,000 in Money In. You buy glue for 500 KSh, so you write 500 in Money Out. Your new balance is 6,500 KSh. Do this every day without fail.

If you prefer a mobile app, try 'M-Pesa Business' or 'Wave' for basic tracking. For a free, simple app, use 'Cash Book' on Android or 'Money Manager Expense & Budget'. These apps let you record transactions on your phone. They automatically calculate balances and even show you charts of where your money goes.

Whichever method you choose, keep it consistent. Record every transaction, no matter how small. At the end of the week, total your Money In and Money Out. This weekly summary is the foundation for tracking profit. Remember, the goal is not to be perfect but to be honest. If you forget to record a sale, your records lie to you. So make it a habit: after every sale or purchase, record it immediately.

3. Tracking Sales, Expenses, and Profit Weekly

To know if your tissue business is making money, you must track sales, expenses, and profit every week. Set aside one hour each Sunday evening to review your cash book or app.

First, total your sales for the week. For example, if you sold 500 packs of tissues at 100 KSh each, your total sales are 50,000 KSh. Next, total your expenses: raw materials (paper, glue, packaging), transport, rent, airtime, and any wages you paid yourself. Suppose expenses are 35,000 KSh. Your profit is 50,000 minus 35,000 = 15,000 KSh.

But don't stop there. Calculate your profit margin: profit divided by sales, times 100. In this case, 15,000 / 50,000 = 30%. A healthy tissue business often has a margin of 20-40%. If your margin is lower, look for ways to reduce costs or increase prices.

Also, track your sales by product. Maybe toilet paper sells faster than facial tissues. If so, focus more on toilet paper. Write these numbers in a notebook or a simple spreadsheet on your phone. Over time, you'll see trends. For instance, sales may drop during school holidays. Knowing this helps you plan.

Finally, compare this week's profit to last week's. If profit is falling, investigate why. Maybe a competitor opened nearby, or your supplier increased prices. By tracking weekly, you catch problems early. This simple habit is what separates thriving businesses from those that close down.

4. Managing Credit Sales to Trusted Customers

Selling on credit can grow your tissue business, but it can also destroy it if you are not careful. Credit means you give goods now and get paid later. Only offer credit to customers you trustthose who have bought from you for at least three months and always pay on time.

Set clear terms: for example, payment within 7 days. Write it down. Use a credit book or a notebook to record: customer name, date, items taken, amount owed, and due date. For each credit sale, get the customer's phone number and, if possible, their national ID number. This reduces the risk of them disappearing.

When payment is due, send a polite reminder via SMS or WhatsApp. For example: 'Hello Mary, your payment of 2,000 KSh for tissues is due today. Please pay via M-Pesa to 0712345678.' If they don't pay, follow up with a call. If they still don't pay after two weeks, stop giving them credit until they clear the debt.

Never let one customer owe you more than 10% of your weekly sales. If your weekly sales are 50,000 KSh, the maximum credit to one customer should be 5,000 KSh. Also, keep a list of bad debtors and share it with other local suppliers if appropriatethis encourages payment.

Remember, a sale is not a sale until the money is in your pocket. Cash is king. Use credit strategically to build loyalty with your best customers, but never let it get out of hand.

5. Saving for Equipment Upgrades and Emergencies

Your tissue business will need new equipmenta sealing machine, a better weighing scale, or a delivery bicycle. And emergencies happen: a machine breaks down, a family member gets sick, or a supplier demands cash upfront. You must save for these.

Open a separate savings account or mobile wallet for your business savings. Every week, after calculating your profit, transfer a fixed percentageat least 10%into that savings. For example, if your weekly profit is 15,000 KSh, save 1,500 KSh. Do this before you spend on anything else.

Set specific goals. If a sealing machine costs 30,000 KSh, you need to save 1,500 KSh per week for 20 weeks. Write the goal on your wall: 'Sealing machine by December.' This keeps you motivated.

For emergencies, aim to save three months of business expenses. If your monthly expenses are 100,000 KSh, your emergency fund target is 300,000 KSh. This may take time, but start small. Even 500 KSh per week adds up.

Use a locked savings account or a SACCO where you cannot easily withdraw. In Kenya, M-Shwari or a chama savings group can help. In Nigeria, use a PiggyVest or a local cooperative.

When you dip into savings for an emergency, repay it as soon as possible. Treat your savings as a loan to yourself. This discipline will keep your business stable and ready to grow.

6. Understanding Basic Taxes and Licenses for Your Business

Many small business owners fear taxes, but understanding them protects you from fines and helps you grow. In most African countries, if your business is small, you may qualify for a simplified tax regime. For example, in Kenya, the Turnover Tax (TOT) is 1% of gross sales for businesses with annual sales below 5 million KSh. In Nigeria, the presumptive tax for small businesses is often a flat fee based on your turnover.

First, register your business. In Kenya, register with the Business Registration Service and get a KRA PIN. In Nigeria, register with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) and get a TIN. This makes you official and allows you to open a bank account and apply for loans.

Next, get a local business permit from your county or municipality. This is usually paid annually and costs between 2,000 and 10,000 KSh depending on your location and size. Keep the receipt.

For taxes, keep accurate records of all sales and expenses. You will need them to file returns. In Kenya, file your TOT returns monthly via iTax. In Nigeria, file with FIRS or your state internal revenue service. If you are unsure, visit your local tax office and ask for helpthey often have officers to assist small businesses.

Also, check if you need specific licenses for tissue products. In some countries, you may need a health certificate or a standards mark from the Bureau of Standards. This ensures your tissues meet quality requirements.

Don't ignore taxes. The penalties can close your business. Instead, budget for them: set aside a small percentage of each sale for tax. When you pay on time, you build a good reputation and avoid stress.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Managing Money and Records for Tissue & Paper Businesses

Whether you convert jumbo reels into toilet rolls, trade in paper towels, or run a small tissue converting unit, money moves fast. Cash comes in over the counter, goes out to suppliers, and often gets mixed with household money. This Practical Reference brings together all six lessons in the module into one working document you can print, pin to the wall, and use daily. It is written for African micro and small enterprises from Kumasi to Kampala, from Lagos to Lusaka where most transactions are still cash or mobile money, and where trust and record keeping decide whether a business survives.

1. Separating Personal and Business Money

The single most common reason small tissue businesses fail is not low sales it is mixing household money with business money. When the same pocket or mobile wallet funds school fees, stock purchases, and airtime, you can never know whether the business is profitable. Open a dedicated bank account or mobile money wallet for the business. Pay yourself a fixed monthly salary (even a small one), and treat all other withdrawals as a loan that must be repaid. This one habit transforms how you see your business.

2. Using a Simple Cash Book or Mobile App

You do not need accounting software. A hardcover exercise book with five columns Date, Description, Money In, Money Out, Balance is enough. Every sale and every expense gets a line. If you prefer digital, apps such as Wave, Kippa, or Moniepoint (Nigeria), M-PESA statements (Kenya/Tanzania), or MTN MoMo / Airtel Money transaction histories work well. The rule is simple: if it is not written down, it did not happen.

3. Tracking Sales, Expenses, and Profit Weekly

Weekly tracking turns guessing into decision-making. Every Sunday evening, total your sales, total your expenses, and calculate profit. If profit is falling, check whether it is a price problem, a cost problem, or a theft/wastage problem. For tissue converters, watch reel prices and glue costs closely they move fast and eat margins.

4. Managing Credit Sales to Trusted Customers

Credit sales grow revenue but destroy businesses when unmanaged. Set a written rule: a customer can only buy on credit after three successful cash purchases. Keep a simple credit ledger with name, phone number, amount owed, date due, and amount paid. Send a polite reminder one day before the due date. Never let one customer's debt exceed 10% of your monthly sales.

5. Saving for Equipment Upgrades and Emergencies

Tissue converting equipment rewinders, log saws, sealing machines is expensive. Save a fixed percentage of weekly profit (1015%) into a separate savings wallet or a chama/sacco/VSLA group. Aim for two funds: an Equipment Fund for growth and an Emergency Fund covering at least one month of expenses. Village Savings and Loan Associations (VSLAs) are the most accessible savings vehicle for African micro-entrepreneurs over 10 million members across sub-Saharan Africa.

6. Understanding Basic Taxes and Licences

Every African country has a simplified tax regime for small businesses. In Kenya, the Turnover Tax applies to businesses with turnover below KES 5 million. In Nigeria, small companies (turnover below 25 million) enjoy a lower Companies Income Tax rate and simplified filing. In Ghana, the GRA operates a presumptive tax for small businesses. In Uganda, URA's Small Business Tax regime applies below UGX 150 million turnover. You must also hold a valid trade licence from your local council or municipality. Register early penalties compound.

SAFETY WARNING Do Not Ignore Tax Notices

Ignoring tax authority letters, SMS reminders, or licence renewal deadlines can lead to business closure, asset seizure, and personal liability. In several African jurisdictions, unpaid turnover tax accrues penalties of 525% plus interest. If you receive a notice you do not understand, visit the nearest tax office or a free business development services (BDS) centre the same week. Never pay a "facilitator" who promises to reduce your tax this is bribery and can lead to prosecution. Use only official channels.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Separate accounts: Business money in its own bank account, mobile wallet, or cash box never mixed with household cash.
  2. Daily entries: Every sale and expense recorded the same day in a cash book or mobile app.
  3. Weekly review: Every Sunday, total sales, expenses, and profit; compare to your target.
  4. Credit rules: Written credit limit per customer; ledger kept; reminders sent one day before due date.
  5. Savings discipline: 15% of weekly profit split between Equipment Fund and Emergency Fund.
  6. Tax calendar: Know your filing dates; keep 10% of profit reserved for tax and licences.
  7. Licence renewal: Mark renewal dates on the wall calendar three months in advance.
  8. Owner's salary: Pay yourself a fixed amount monthly do not draw randomly from the till.
