1. Field Drying: Sun and Wind Techniques

After cutting your grass, the first job is to get it dry as fast as possible. In most of Africa, the sun is strong, but humidity and unexpected rain can ruin your hay. The goal is to reduce moisture from about 75-80% (fresh grass) down to 15-20% for safe storage. Field drying relies on sun and wind. Cut early in the morning after the dew has dried, so the grass has the whole day to dry. Use a sharp sickle or machete, or a simple mower if you have one. Spread the grass in a thin layer, not clumps. A thin layer exposes more surface to sun and wind. In very hot areas, you might need to dry for only 1-2 days; in cooler or humid areas, 3-5 days. Turn the grass once or twice a day with a wooden rake or pitchfork to expose the underside. If you have a tractor, a hay tedder helps, but manual turning works. Watch the weather: if clouds build up, cover the hay with a tarpaulin or move it under shelter. In windy areas, use windrows (long rows) perpendicular to the wind to speed drying. But don't make rows too thick. Test dryness by twisting a handful: if it breaks easily and no moisture squeezes out, it's close. Aim for 18-20% moisture. If you dry too much (below 15%), leaves shatter and you lose nutrients. If too wet, mould grows. So check daily. In humid regions like coastal West Africa, field drying may take longer; consider adding salt (1-2 kg per 100 kg grass) to help preserve, but that's not traditional hay. Better to dry thoroughly. Remember, hay quality depends on quick drying. The longer it stays wet, the more sugars and vitamins are lost. So start early, spread thin, turn often, and bring in before evening dew if not fully dry.

2. Tedding and Raking to Speed Up Drying

Tedding and raking are simple actions that can cut drying time by a day or more. Tedding means fluffing up the grass to expose more surface to air and sun. You can do this with a pitchfork or a wooden rake. After the grass has wilted for a few hours, go through the swath and toss it lightly. This is especially important if the grass is thick or if humidity is high. Do it once in the morning and once in the early afternoon. Raking, on the other hand, is gathering the partially dried grass into windrows (long rows) to dry evenly and to prepare for baling. Use a wooden or bamboo rake, or a simple hay rake with curved teeth. Rake when the grass is about 40-50% moisture (still flexible but not dripping). Rake in the late afternoon when dew is not yet falling. Make windrows that are about 1-1.5 meters wide and 30-50 cm high. This allows air to circulate. If you have a tractor, a side-delivery rake or a tedder can do this faster, but manual methods work for small plots. In very sunny areas, you might ted twice a day. In humid areas, tedding is critical to prevent mould. Be careful not to rake too early when grass is too wet, as it can clump and mould. Also, avoid raking when it's too dry, because leaves shatter and you lose the most nutritious parts. The best time to rake is when the grass is like leather: flexible but not brittle. After raking, let the windrows dry for another half day or full day, then test moisture. If you plan to bale, rake into windrows that match your baler or hand-baling method. For hand baling, smaller windrows are easier to handle. Tedding and raking are labour-intensive but low-cost. They are essential for quality hay in African conditions where humidity can be high.

3. Testing Moisture Content Without a Meter

You don't need an expensive moisture meter to know if your hay is ready to bale or store. Use your senses and simple tests. The most common field test is the twist test: take a handful of hay and twist it tightly. If it feels soft and cool, and no moisture drips out, it's likely around 20-25% moisture. If it feels brittle and breaks when twisted, it's below 15% and may be too dry. For baling, the ideal range is 18-20% moisture. Another test is the salt test: put a pinch of dry salt in a jar with a sample of hay, shake, and if salt sticks to the hay, it's too wet. If it falls off, it's dry enough. You can also use a simple oven method: weigh a small sample (say 100g), dry it in an oven or solar dryer at low heat until constant weight, then reweigh. The weight loss is moisture. For example, if 100g becomes 80g, moisture is 20%. But in the field, use the twist and feel test. Another method: press a handful of hay into a ball. If it springs back and feels dry, it's ready. If it stays in a ball and feels damp, it's too wet. Also, listen: dry hay crackles when you rub it; wet hay is silent. For storing in stacks, you want moisture below 20%. If you store hay at 25% moisture, it will heat up and mould. So test before baling and again before storage. In humid areas, aim for 15-18% moisture. If you're unsure, better to dry an extra day. Remember, over-drying causes leaf loss, so don't go below 15%. Practice these tests on small batches to calibrate your senses. With experience, you'll be able to judge moisture by feel and sound. This skill is crucial for small-scale farmers without meters.

4. Avoiding Rain Damage and Mould Growth

Rain is the biggest enemy of hay. A single rain shower on nearly dry hay can ruin it. When hay gets wet, moulds and bacteria grow, producing toxins that can harm livestock. So you must protect hay from rain. First, watch the weather. In many African regions, rains come in the afternoon. So plan to cut early, dry fast, and bale before rain. If rain threatens, cover the hay with a tarpaulin, plastic sheet, or even banana leaves. But don't cover it so tightly that it sweats; leave some air circulation. If hay gets wet, spread it out to dry again immediately. If it's already in windrows, turn them to expose wet parts. If mould starts to grow (you'll see white, grey, or black patches, and smell a musty odour), do not feed it to animals, especially pregnant ones. Mouldy hay can cause abortions and illness. To prevent mould, ensure hay is properly dried before storage. Also, store in a dry place. In humid areas, consider adding a natural preservative like salt (1-2% by weight) or molasses, but salt can cause rust in metal equipment. Another method is to make haylage (wrapped silage) but that's different. For hay, the key is moisture below 20%. If you must store hay that is slightly wet, add salt at 1 kg per 100 kg hay to inhibit mould. But better to dry it. Also, avoid storing hay on bare ground; use a raised platform or pallets. In the field, if rain is frequent, consider making smaller batches that dry quickly. Or use a solar dryer or barn. Remember, mouldy hay is dangerous. If you see mould, discard it or use as compost. Never feed mouldy hay to young or pregnant animals. Prevention is better than cure: dry thoroughly, protect from rain, and store dry.

5. Baling Hay by Hand or with Simple Presses

Baling makes hay easier to handle, store, and transport. You can bale by hand or with a simple press. For hand baling, you need twine (sisal or plastic) and a frame. A simple frame can be made from wooden poles: two uprights and a crossbar. You place hay in a rectangular box (about 50x40x40 cm) and compress it by standing on it or using a lever. Then tie the bale with twine in a figure-eight pattern. A typical small bale weighs 15-25 kg. To make it, layer hay in the box, compress, add more, and tie when full. For a simple press, you can use a car jack or a lever system to compress hay into a box. The goal is to make dense bales that shed water and take less space. In Africa, many farmers use a manual baler made from a 200-litre drum cut open, with a plunger. You fill the drum with hay, compress with the plunger, and tie. Bale size can vary. For hand baling, wear gloves to avoid cuts. Use twine that is strong; two strings per bale are enough for small bales. If you have a tractor, a round baler is faster, but for small-scale, hand baling is fine. Baling should be done when hay is at 18-20% moisture. If too dry, leaves shatter; if too wet, mould. After baling, leave bales in the field to dry a bit more if needed, but protect from rain. Stack bales in a dry place. Baling reduces storage space by half compared to loose hay. It also makes feeding easier. You can sell bales. So learn to bale by hand; it's a skill that adds value. Start with small bales and practice. With a simple press, you can make 20-30 bales a day with two people.

6. Storing Hay in Stacks and Lofts

Proper storage protects hay from rain, ground moisture, and pests. The two main methods are stacks (outdoor or indoor) and lofts (above animal housing). For stacks, choose a well-drained site. Put a layer of stones, branches, or pallets on the ground to keep hay off the soil. Build the stack with the driest hay in the center and slightly wetter hay on the outside? No, all hay should be dry. Make a stack that is wider at the base and tapers to the top, like a pyramid, so rain runs off. Cover with a thatched roof, tarpaulin, or plastic sheet. In humid areas, indoor stacks are better. A simple hay barn with a roof and open sides allows air circulation. For lofts, store hay above animal pens. The animals' body heat rises and keeps the hay dry. But ensure the loft is well-ventilated and not leaking. Before storing, test moisture again; it should be below 20%. If you store hay at 25% moisture, it will heat up and could even catch fire. So never store wet hay. In the stack, put a few sticks or bamboo poles vertically to allow air movement. Check the stack regularly for heating: put your hand inside; if it's warm, it's fermenting. If hot, dismantle and dry. Also, watch for mould. For long-term storage, keep hay in a dry, dark place. In Africa, many farmers store hay in a corner of the house or in a separate hut. Make sure it's off the ground and covered. If you have no shelter, a well-built stack with a good thatch can last through the dry season. But rain can still penetrate, so check after storms. Hay stored properly can last 6-12 months. So invest in a simple storage structure. It will save your feed and money.

<h2>Practical Reference: Hay Making Drying, Curing, Testing and Storage</h2>

<p>Hay is the cheapest conserved forage an African livestock enterprise can make, but only if it is dried, cured and stored correctly. A poorly cured bale can lose 3050% of its dry matter to mould, heat damage and shattering, which directly cuts milk yield, weight gain and profit. This reference lesson brings together the field skills from the whole module sun and wind drying, tedding, moisture testing, rain protection, baling and storage into one practical, business-first guide for small and medium-scale producers in Africa.</p>

<p><strong>Why this matters commercially:</strong> in much of sub-Saharan Africa, dry-season forage shortages force farmers to buy expensive concentrates or sell animals early. A tonne of well-made hay can carry a dairy cow or a group of goats through 24 months of scarcity. Getting curing right is the difference between a saleable, storeable product and a heap of mouldy waste.</p>

<h3>Field Drying: Sun and Wind Techniques</h3>
<p>Cut forage in the early morning when dew is still on the plant, so the swath has the whole day to dry. In hot, dry African conditions, grass can drop from 7580% moisture to 4050% in a single sunny day. Wind matters as much as sun: a light breeze carries moisture away from the swath, while still, humid air slows drying and encourages mould. Lay material in a thin, even swath rather than a thick heap a swath 510 cm deep dries 23 times faster than a windrow 30 cm deep. In high-rainfall zones (e.g. parts of Uganda, Kenya highlands, Nigeria's middle belt), plan for 23 days of drying; in dry savanna (Sahel, northern Kenya, Botswana), 12 days is often enough.</p>

<h3>Tedding and Raking to Speed Up Drying</h3>
<p>Tedding means turning and fluffing the swath so air reaches the underside. On a small farm this is done with a wooden rake or pitchfork; larger farms use a tractor-drawn tedder. Turn the swath every 46 hours on the first day, then twice on the second day. Raking gathers the dried forage into a windrow for baling and reduces leaf loss if done when the hay is "leathery" leaves still pliable but stems dry. Avoid turning when the hay is brittle (below 20% moisture) because leaves shatter and the most nutritious part is lost. A simple rule: <strong>ted early, rake late, bale fast.</strong></p>

<h3>Testing Moisture Content Without a Meter</h3>
<p>Most smallholders cannot afford a moisture meter, but three field tests work well:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Twist test:</strong> take a handful of hay, twist it firmly. If it breaks with a snap and no free moisture appears, it is near 1820% ready to bale. If it twists like a rope and feels damp, wait.</li>
<li><strong>Salt test:</strong> put a pinch of dry salt in a jar with a sample of hay, shake and leave for 10 minutes. If the salt clumps, the hay is too wet (above 20%). If it stays loose, moisture is safe.</li>
<li><strong>Bottle/heat test:</strong> fill a glass bottle with hay, seal it, and leave in the sun for 2 hours. Condensation on the glass means moisture is still too high.</li>
</ul>
<p>Target <strong>1520% moisture</strong> for small square bales and <strong>1822%</strong> for large round bales that will be ventilated. Above 25%, hay will heat, turn brown and grow mould within days.</p>

<h3>Avoiding Rain Damage and Mould Growth</h3>
<p>Rain is the single biggest cause of hay failure in Africa. A single wetting can leach soluble sugars, double drying time and seed mould spores. Practical defences:</p>
<ul>
<li>Watch the forecast and cut only when 23 dry days are expected.</li>
<li>Keep plastic sheets or tarpaulins at the field edge to cover the swath within minutes.</li>
<li>If rain falls, re-ted immediately after the shower do not leave the swath compacted.</li>
<li>Never bale hay that has been wet and not fully re-dried; it will heat and spoil.</li>
<li>Mouldy hay is dangerous see the safety box below.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:2px solid #c02020;background:#ffe0e0;padding:14px;border-radius:8px;margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#a01010;"> SAFETY WARNING MOULDY HAY IS A HEALTH HAZARD</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7a0a0a;">Mouldy hay can contain <em>Aspergillus</em> and <em>Fusarium</em> moulds that produce mycotoxins and cause "farmer's lung" in people and respiratory disease, abortion and reduced milk yield in livestock. <strong>Never</strong> feed heavily moulded hay to pregnant animals, young stock or dairy cows. Do not enter a closed hay loft without ventilation mould spores and dust can cause severe lung damage. Wear a dust mask when handling suspect hay, and burn or compost mouldy bales away from animal housing.</p>
</div>

<h3>Baling Hay by Hand or with Simple Presses</h3>
<p>For smallholders, hand baling is realistic and profitable. Use a simple wooden or metal box press (often made locally) to compress hay into 1525 kg bales. Hand-tied bales using sisal twine are common in Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda. Key points:</p>
<ul>
<li>Bale when moisture is 1520% test first.</li>
<li>Keep bales tight and uniform so they stack and sell well.</li>
<li>Work in the cool of early morning or late afternoon to reduce leaf shatter.</li>
<li>Weigh a few bales so you can price consistently buyers pay per kg or per bale.</li>
<li>Small square bales (1525 kg) are easier to handle, transport and sell than large round bales on small farms.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Storing Hay in Stacks and Lofts</h3>
<p>Storage decides whether your curing work pays off. Hay must stay dry and ventilated. Options:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Raised stack:</strong> build on a platform of poles, stones or pallets 1520 cm above ground to stop moisture wicking up. Cover with a thatched roof or tarpaulin that overhangs the stack.</li>
<li><strong>Loft storage:</strong> store bales in a ventilated loft above livestock housing the animals' body heat helps keep hay dry, and the hay insulates the roof.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilation:</strong> leave gaps between bales and never stack against a wall. Air must move through the stack.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect monthly:</strong> feel for heat, smell for mustiness, and remove any heating bales immediately.</li>
</ul>
