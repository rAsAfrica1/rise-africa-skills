1. Principles of Farm Biosecurity

Biosecurity is the set of everyday actions that keep diseases out of your pig farm and stop them from spreading if they do get in. Think of it as a shield around your pigs. The three main principles are isolation, traffic control, and sanitation. Isolation means keeping your pigs away from other pigs, wild animals, and sick animals. If you buy new pigs, keep them separate for at least 30 days. Traffic control means limiting who and what comes onto your farm. Only essential people and vehicles should enter, and they must follow your rules. Sanitation means cleaning and disinfecting everything that touches your pigsboots, tools, vehicles, and hands.

In Africa, many pig farms are small and near homes or markets. This increases risk. A neighbours pig with African Swine Fever can spread through shared equipment or even your clothes. So set clear boundaries. Put a fence around your pig area. Have a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance. Change into farm-only boots and overalls before entering. These simple steps cost little but prevent huge losses.

Another key principle is all-in, all-out management. If possible, raise pigs in groups of the same age. When you sell or slaughter a group, clean and disinfect the empty pen thoroughly before bringing in new pigs. This breaks disease cycles. If you cant do all-in all-out, at least keep different age groups in separate pens with separate tools.

Finally, keep records. Write down every visitor, every new pig, and any signs of illness. This helps you trace problems quickly. Remember, biosecurity is not a one-time taskits a daily habit. The cost of prevention is always less than the cost of a disease outbreak. Start with the basics: a fence, a footbath, and a quarantine pen. Your pigs will thank you with better health and higher profits.

2. Cleaning and Disinfection Protocols

Cleaning and disinfection are two different steps. Cleaning removes dirt, manure, and organic matter. Disinfection kills germs. You must clean firstdisinfectants dont work well on dirty surfaces. Start by removing all bedding, manure, and feed from the pen. Scrape and sweep thoroughly. Then wash with water and a detergent. A stiff brush and hot water work best. For small farms, a bucket of soapy water and a scrubbing brush is enough. Rinse with clean water.

After cleaning, apply a disinfectant. Common and affordable options in Africa include bleach (sodium hypochlorite), Virkon, and glutaraldehyde. Always follow the labelmix the correct concentration. For bleach, use 1 part bleach to 10 parts water (about 0.5% solution). Let it sit for at least 10 minutes before rinsing. For Virkon, use 1% solution (10 grams per litre). Disinfectants work better in warm water, but never mix bleach with ammonia or acidsit creates toxic gas.

Pay attention to corners, cracks, and under feeders. These are hiding places for germs. Also disinfect tools, boots, and clothing. Soak boots in a footbath with disinfectant for at least 30 seconds. Change footbath solution daily or when it looks dirty. For vehicles, use a pressure washer if available, then spray with disinfectant, especially tires and undercarriage.

After disinfection, let everything dry completely before bringing pigs back. Sunlight helps kill some germs, so open up the pen to dry. If you have a disease outbreak, you must clean and disinfect twice, and wait at least 3 days before restocking. Always keep a record of what you used and when. Proper cleaning and disinfection can reduce disease by up to 80%. Its hard work, but its the cheapest insurance for your pigs.

3. Visitor and Vehicle Control

People and vehicles can carry diseases onto your farm without showing any signs. A visitor who stepped in mud at a market can bring in African Swine Fever virus on their shoes. A truck that transported pigs from another farm can carry germs on its tires. You must control all traffic. The best rule: if you dont need to enter, dont. Meet visitors at the gate. Use a phone or a bell to communicate. If someone must enter, they must follow your biosecurity steps.

Create a clear entry point. Put a sign that says Biosecurity Area No Entry Without Permission. Have a footbath with fresh disinfectant at the gate. Provide farm boots and overalls for visitors. They must change out of their street clothes and shoes. If you cant provide overalls, at least give disposable plastic boot covers and a clean apron. Visitors should wash their hands with soap and water before entering.

For vehicles, keep them outside the pig area. If a truck must come in to pick up pigs, designate a loading area at the edge of the farm. The truck driver should not enter the pens. After loading, clean and disinfect the loading area. If a vehicle must enter, spray its tires and wheel wells with disinfectant. A simple garden sprayer works well. Use 1% Virkon or 0.5% bleach solution.

Keep a visitor log. Record the date, name, purpose, and any recent contact with other pigs. This helps trace disease if an outbreak occurs. Also, limit visitors to essential onesvets, feed delivery, and buyers. Discourage casual visits from other farmers. If you must visit another farm, change clothes and shower before returning to your own pigs. Remember, you are the biggest risk. Your boots, hands, and clothes can carry germs. So follow your own rules every time.

4. Quarantine Procedures for New Stock

Quarantine means keeping new pigs separate from your main herd for a period to see if they are sick. In Africa, where diseases like African Swine Fever are common, quarantine is critical. Always quarantine new pigs for at least 30 days. If you can, extend to 60 days for higher risk sources. Never mix new pigs with your herd immediately, even if they look healthy. Some diseases have long incubation periods.

Set up a quarantine pen at least 50 metres away from your main pig house. If thats not possible, use a separate building with its own entrance. The pen must have its own feed, water, and tools. Do not share equipment between quarantine and main pens. Assign one person to care for quarantined pigs. That person should care for them last, after tending to the main herd. Wear separate boots and overalls. Wash hands thoroughly after contact.

When new pigs arrive, check them carefully. Look for signs of illness: coughing, diarrhoea, skin spots, or reluctance to move. If any pig looks sick, isolate it further and call a vet. During quarantine, observe daily. Take note of feed intake and behaviour. If a pig dies, do not sell or eat it. Bury it deep or burn it, and report to authorities if you suspect a notifiable disease like African Swine Fever.

After 30 days, if all pigs are healthy, you can introduce them to the main herd. But do it gradually. Mix one or two quarantined pigs with a few from the main herd in a neutral pen for a few days. Watch for fighting or signs of disease. If all goes well, merge them. Always keep records of quarantine dates, health checks, and any treatments. Quarantine is your best defence against bringing a devastating disease into your farm. It costs time, but it saves your whole herd.

5. Rodent and Pest Control

Rodents, birds, flies, and other pests can carry diseases onto your farm. Rats and mice can spread leptospirosis, salmonella, and even African Swine Fever if they have access to infected material. Flies can carry bacteria from manure to feed. Birds can bring in avian influenza, though pigs are not natural hosts, they can carry the virus mechanically. So pest control is a key part of biosecurity.

Start by denying pests food, water, and shelter. Store feed in sealed containersmetal bins or heavy plastic drums with lids. Dont leave spilled feed on the ground. Clean up feed spills immediately. Fix leaking taps and pipes. Remove standing water. Keep the area around your pig pens free of tall grass, bushes, and rubbish. Pests love to hide in clutter. Mow regularly and keep a 1-metre clear border around buildings.

For rodents, use traps and bait stations. Place traps along walls where rats travel. Use snap traps or glue boards. For bait, use rodenticides carefully. Put bait in tamper-resistant boxes so pigs and other animals cant eat it. Follow label instructions. Rotate baits to prevent resistance. If you have a serious rat problem, consider getting a professional pest control service. For flies, use fly traps, sticky ribbons, and insecticide sprays. Keep manure dry and remove it regularly. Compost manure away from pig houses. For birds, use netting or wire mesh on windows and vents. Keep feed covered.

Also, control stray dogs and cats. They can carry diseases and stress your pigs. Fence your farm to keep them out. If you have dogs, keep them vaccinated and away from pig pens. Remember, pests are a sign of poor hygiene. A clean farm is a pest-free farm. Spend 10 minutes each day checking for signs of pestsdroppings, gnaw marks, or fly swarms. Act quickly before they become a big problem.

6. Emergency Disease Response Plans

Even with the best biosecurity, a disease outbreak can happen. You need a plan ready before it strikes. The first sign might be sudden deaths, high fever, or pigs refusing to eat. If you see these signs, act immediately. Isolate sick pigs. Do not move them. Do not sell or slaughter them. Call your vet or local veterinary authority at once. In many African countries, African Swine Fever is a notifiable diseaseyou must report it.

While waiting for the vet, stop all movement on and off the farm. No visitors, no vehicles, no animals in or out. Put up a temporary barrier. Use separate boots and clothing for the sick pen. If you have disposable gloves, wear them. After handling sick pigs, wash hands and change clothes. Do not share equipment with healthy pigs. If a pig dies, do not open it up unless instructed by a vet. Wrap the carcass in plastic and bury it deep (at least 2 metres) or burn it. Never feed dead pigs to other pigs.

If the disease is confirmed, you may have to cull the entire herd. This is painful, but it stops the spread. Follow government instructions for disposal. After culling, clean and disinfect the whole farm. Use 2% bleach or 1% Virkon. Leave the farm empty for at least 40 days before restocking. Restock only with pigs from a known healthy source, and quarantine them.

Keep an emergency contact list: vet, local agriculture office, and neighbouring farmers. Have a basic first-aid kit for pigs: antiseptic, syringes, and gloves. Write down your plan and practise it with your workers. A quick, organised response can save your neighbours farms and prevent a wider outbreak. Remember, hiding a disease only makes it worse. Report early, act fast, and protect your community.

<h2>Practical Reference: Biosecurity and Disease Prevention in African Pig Farming</h2>

<p>Biosecurity is not a cost centre it is the cheapest insurance policy a pig farmer can buy. In sub-Saharan Africa, where African Swine Fever (ASF) has wiped out entire herds from Uganda to Nigeria, and where Classical Swine Fever, foot-and-mouth disease, and porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome (PRRS) remain constant threats, the difference between a profitable pig enterprise and a bankrupt one often comes down to how strictly you control what enters your farm. This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a single operational guide you can print, laminate, and pin to the farm office wall.</p>

<p>Every recommendation below is written for the African smallholder and medium-scale commercial context: limited cold-chain access, mixed crop-livestock systems, shared water sources, free-roaming village pigs, and markets where buyers, traders, and transporters arrive without warning. Where formal laboratory support is scarce, we prioritise practical, low-cost measures that deliver the highest return on investment.</p>

<h3>1. Principles of Farm Biosecurity the business case</h3>
<p>Biosecurity rests on three pillars: <strong>isolation</strong> (keep pathogens out), <strong>traffic control</strong> (control what moves in and out), and <strong>sanitation</strong> (kill what does get in). A single ASF outbreak in a 50-sow unit in Uganda typically costs the farmer US$15,00040,000 in lost stock, lost contracts, and 612 months of downtime versus roughly US$300800 per year to run a credible biosecurity programme. That is a 20:1 to 50:1 return on investment, before you count the premium buyers pay for pigs from farms with a documented health record.</p>

<h3>2. Cleaning and Disinfection Protocols</h3>
<p>The single most common failure on African pig farms is "disinfecting over dirt." Organic matter inactivates almost every disinfectant on the market. The correct sequence is always: <strong>dry clean wash rinse disinfect dry rest</strong>. Allow at least 24 hours of downtime between batches where possible, and 48 hours after a suspected disease event.</p>

<h3>3. Visitor and Vehicle Control</h3>
<p>In most African ASF outbreaks traced by veterinary services, the entry point is a vehicle a trader's pickup, a feed delivery, or a motorbike carrying a buyer. Implement a hard <strong>clean/dirty line</strong> at your gate. No vehicle crosses it unless it has been washed and its tyres and wheel arches disinfected. Keep a visitor log recording name, contact, last pig contact, and purpose. Refuse entry to anyone who has visited another pig farm, abattoir, or live pig market in the previous 48 hours no exceptions, no matter how important the buyer.</p>

<h3>4. Quarantine Procedures for New Stock</h3>
<p>Never mix new pigs with your herd on arrival. Quarantine for a minimum of <strong>30 days</strong> (45 days is safer where ASF is circulating) in a pen at least 50 metres downwind and downhill from your main herd, with separate boots, tools, and feeding equipment. Observe daily for appetite, temperature, skin colour, and gait. Test for ASF and CSF if a laboratory is accessible in Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and Nigeria, national veterinary laboratories offer subsidised testing during outbreak periods. Introduce new genetics only from farms with a documented health history, and never from live markets.</p>

<h3>5. Rodent and Pest Control</h3>
<p>Rats, mice, birds, and stray dogs move pathogens between pens and between farms. Keep a 1-metre cleared perimeter of gravel or short grass around each pig house. Store feed in sealed metal or heavy plastic bins never in open sacks on the floor. Set rodent bait stations (locked, tamper-resistant) every 1015 metres along walls, checked weekly, with bait rotated every 3 months to prevent resistance. Control flies with screens, drainage, and manure removal every 23 days. In tick-endemic areas, treat walls and cracks with approved acaricides.</p>

<h3>6. Emergency Disease Response Plans</h3>
<p>Write your plan before you need it. Every farm should have: (1) the phone number of the nearest government veterinary officer and district veterinary office; (2) a designated isolation pen; (3) a carcass disposal plan (deep burial with lime, or approved incineration never sell or butcher a sick pig); (4) a stop-movement protocol for 30 days; (5) a contact list of buyers to notify. In ASF-endemic regions, report suspected cases within 24 hours early reporting can save neighbouring farms and may qualify you for government compensation schemes where they exist (e.g., Uganda's MAAIF response framework).</p>
