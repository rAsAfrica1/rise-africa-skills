Lessons

1. Introduction and Why It Matters

MIG welding, also called Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW), is the workhorse of modern metal fabrication. In Africa, where industries from auto repair to construction are booming, MIG welding offers speed, versatility, and ease of learning. Unlike stick welding, MIG uses a continuously fed wire electrode and a shielding gas to protect the weld pool. This means you can weld thin sheet metal or thick plates with the same machine by adjusting voltage and wire speed. For a small workshop in Nairobi or Lagos, a 200-amp MIG welder running on 220V single phase can handle most jobs. The key advantage is productivity: you don't stop to change electrodes every few inches. You can weld steel, stainless, and aluminum (with the right setup). In Africa, MIG is increasingly used in fabrication of gates, window frames, truck bodies, and agricultural equipment. It also produces less slag than stick welding, so cleanup is faster. However, MIG requires a shielding gas cylinder, typically 75% Argon/25% CO2 for steel. This adds cost, but the speed and quality pay off. Learning MIG opens doors to jobs in manufacturing, repair shops, and even overseas contracts. This module will take you from setting up the machine to running clean beads on different joints. You'll learn to troubleshoot common defects and work safely. By the end, you'll be able to produce strong, neat welds that meet professional standards. Remember, MIG is a skill best learned by doing. So get your gloves on and let's start.

2. Tools and Materials

To MIG weld effectively, you need the right tools and materials. First, a MIG welder: for most African workshops, a 200-amp machine like the Lincoln Electric Power MIG 210 MP or a Chinese equivalent (e.g., Andeli MIG-200) works well. Ensure it can run on 220V single-phase power, common in many African countries. You'll need a shielding gas cylinder: for mild steel, use 75% Argon/25% CO2 (often called C25). In South Africa, Afrox supplies this; in Kenya, BOC. A flowmeter regulator is essential to set gas flow to 10-15 CFH (cubic feet per hour). Without gas, your weld will be porous. Next, filler wire: for mild steel, use ER70S-6 wire in 0.8mm or 1.0mm diameter. For stainless, ER308L; for aluminum, ER4043. Buy quality wire from welding suppliers, not cheap uncoated wire that rusts. You'll need a wire brush, angle grinder with grinding and cutting discs, and clamps to hold your work. Personal protective equipment: auto-darkening welding helmet (shade 10-13), leather gloves, flame-resistant jacket, and steel-toe boots. For practice, get mild steel plates: 3mm to 6mm thick, cut into 100mm x 150mm pieces. Clean them with a grinder to remove mill scale and rust. A wire cutter and nozzle gel (anti-spatter) help keep the torch clean. Finally, a good workbench and a fire extinguisher. In many African cities, you can find these at industrial areas like Kariobangi in Nairobi or Alaba in Lagos. Don't skimp on gas or wire; poor consumables lead to poor welds.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to make a sound MIG weld. First, set up your machine: connect the gas cylinder to the regulator, set flow to 12 CFH. Install the wire spool, feed it through the drive rolls (tension should be snug but not crushing the wire), and into the torch liner. Use the correct contact tip for your wire size (0.8mm tip for 0.8mm wire). Set voltage and wire speed according to the thickness of your metal. For 3mm steel, start with voltage 18-19V and wire speed 3.5-4.0 m/min (check your machine's chart). Second, prepare your metal: grind the edges to bare metal, clamp it securely. For a butt joint, leave a 1-2mm gap. Third, position yourself: hold the torch at a 10-15 degree push angle (tilted away from the direction of travel). This gives better gas coverage and penetration. Fourth, strike the arc: touch the wire to the metal, pull the trigger, and move. Maintain a consistent travel speedabout 3-5 mm per second for 3mm steel. Keep the contact tip to work distance (CTWD) at 10-12mm. Too close, and you'll clog the tip; too far, and you lose gas coverage. Fifth, watch the weld pool: it should be a smooth, oval shape. If it's too fluid, reduce voltage; if it's too cold and lumpy, increase voltage or wire speed. For a fillet weld, angle the torch at 45 degrees into the joint. Practice on scrap until you can run a straight bead without stopping. Remember to clean the nozzle regularly with pliers to remove spatter. After welding, let it cool, then chip off any slag (though MIG produces little) and inspect. A good weld is uniform, with fine ripples and no undercut or porosity.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced welders make mistakes. Here are common MIG problems and how to fix them. First, porosity (holes in the weld): usually caused by insufficient gas flow, windy conditions, or dirty metal. Check your gas cylinder isn't empty, set flow to 12-15 CFH, and weld indoors or use a wind screen. Clean metal with a grinder before welding. Second, spatter: excessive spatter means voltage too low or wire speed too high. Increase voltage slightly or reduce wire speed. Also, ensure you're using the correct polarity: for MIG, the torch is positive (DCEP). If you accidentally set DCEN, you'll get poor penetration and spatter. Third, burn-through: on thin metal, you may blow holes. Reduce voltage and wire speed, or use a thicker wire (0.9mm) and move faster. For 1.5mm sheet, try 16V and 2.5 m/min. Fourth, lack of penetration: the weld sits on top without fusing. Increase voltage and wire speed, or slow down your travel. Also, ensure your joint has a small gap. Fifth, wire stubbing: the wire pushes back into the torch. This happens when wire speed is too high or voltage too low. Adjust the settings. Sixth, birdnesting: wire tangles at the drive rolls. This is caused by too much tension on the spool or a kinked liner. Check the spool tension and replace the liner if worn. Seventh, undercut: a groove melted into the base metal along the weld toe. This weakens the joint. Reduce travel speed or increase wire speed to fill the groove. In Africa, power fluctuations can affect weld quality. Use a voltage stabilizer if your machine is sensitive. Always practice on scrap before welding a critical part. Keep a notebook of settings that work for different thicknesses.

5. Safety and Best Practice

MIG welding is safe if you follow basic rules. First, protect your eyes and skin: the arc emits intense UV and IR radiation. Always wear an auto-darkening helmet with shade 10-13. Never look at the arc with naked eyes. Wear leather gloves, a flame-resistant jacket (leather or cotton), and closed shoes. Synthetic clothing can melt and stick to skin. Second, prevent fires: welding sparks can travel up to 10 meters. Clear the area of flammable materials like paper, cloth, petrol, and paint thinners. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO2) within reach. In many African workshops, wooden structures are common; use a welding blanket to protect them. Third, ensure proper ventilation: MIG welding produces fumes, especially when welding galvanized or painted metal. Work in a well-ventilated area or use a fume extractor. If you feel dizzy, stop and get fresh air. Fourth, handle gas cylinders safely: secure them upright with a chain or strap. Keep them away from heat and never roll them on their side. When not in use, close the valve and remove the regulator. Fifth, avoid electric shock: check cables for damage, ensure the machine is grounded, and never weld in wet conditions. Wear dry gloves. Sixth, maintain your equipment: inspect the torch liner, contact tip, and nozzle daily. Replace worn parts. Clean the drive rolls. A well-maintained machine gives better welds and is safer. Seventh, practice good posture: weld at a comfortable height to avoid back strain. Use a stool or adjustable table. Finally, never weld alone if possible; have someone nearby in case of emergency. In Africa, where access to medical help may be limited, prevention is critical. Follow these practices and you'll weld for years without injury.

6. Practical Exercise

This exercise will build your MIG skills step by step. You'll need: a MIG welder, 75/25 Ar/CO2 gas, ER70S-6 0.8mm wire, mild steel plates (6 pieces of 3mm x 50mm x 100mm), angle grinder, clamps, and PPE. First, set your machine: voltage 18V, wire speed 3.5 m/min, gas flow 12 CFH. Practice on a scrap plate: run 5 straight beads, each about 75mm long, overlapping slightly. Focus on keeping a consistent travel speed and CTWD. After each bead, inspect for uniformity. Adjust settings if needed. Second, tack weld two plates together in a butt joint with a 1mm gap. Make tacks at both ends and the middle. Then run a continuous bead along the joint. Aim for full penetration; you should see a slight weld bead on the back side. If not, increase voltage to 19V and slow down. Third, do a T-joint: place one plate vertically on another horizontally. Tack weld, then run a fillet weld on both sides. Angle the torch at 45 degrees. Use a slight push angle. Fourth, try a lap joint: overlap two plates by 25mm, clamp, and weld along the edge. Fifth, inspect your welds: look for porosity, undercut, and spatter. Use a wire brush to clean. If you find defects, cut out the weld with a grinder and redo. Repeat until you can produce clean, strong welds. Finally, test your weld strength: clamp one plate in a vise and hit the other with a hammer. The weld should not break; the metal should bend. If it breaks, your penetration was insufficient. Record your settings for each joint type. In Africa, where materials may vary, always test on scrap first. This exercise takes about 2 hours. Practice daily for a week and you'll see rapid improvement.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: MIG Welding In Depth

This practical reference is written for African agribusiness operators, workshop owners, and technical trainers who need MIG (Metal Inert Gas) welding to perform reliably in real commercial conditions. Whether you are fabricating livestock crates in Nakuru, repairing irrigation manifolds in the Nile Delta, or building feed mixer frames in Ogun State, MIG welding offers speed, consistency, and lower operator fatigue than stick welding but only when the machine is set correctly, the consumables match the job, and the operator understands the variables. This module compiles the essential settings, material data, safety controls, and a step-by-step exercise you can run in a training bay or a working workshop.

MIG welding is also known as GMAW (Gas Metal Arc Welding). In agribusiness, it is used for mild steel, stainless steel (with the right gas and wire), and aluminium (with a spool gun or push-pull torch). The business case is straightforward: faster deposition, less slag cleaning, and better-looking welds that reduce rework on gates, trailers, feed augers, and processing equipment.

Core Variables You Must Control

MIG welding success depends on five variables: wire feed speed (WFS), voltage, travel speed, contact-tip-to-work distance (CTWD), and shielding gas flow. In African workshops, the most common failure is not the machine it is inconsistent WFS caused by a worn liner, a kinked torch cable, or a drive roll tensioned incorrectly. Before blaming the power source, inspect the consumables.

For mild steel, the standard gas is 75% argon / 25% CO (C25). For stainless steel, use a tri-mix or 98% argon / 2% CO. For aluminium, use 100% argon. In coastal or humid regions Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Lagos, Durban store wire in a dry cabinet or sealed container with desiccant. Moisture on the wire causes porosity and erratic arc starts.

SAFETY WARNING MIG Welding Hazards

MIG welding produces intense ultraviolet radiation, toxic fumes (especially on galvanised or painted steel), and molten spatter. In African workshops with limited ventilation, this is a serious health risk. Always wear a properly rated auto-darkening helmet (shade 1013), leather gloves, flame-resistant clothing, and a respirator when welding galvanised or coated metals. Never weld in a confined space without forced ventilation. Keep a dry powder or CO fire extinguisher within 3 metres. Check gas cylinders for leaks with soapy water never a flame. In high-temperature environments, ensure the machine has adequate duty-cycle cooling and is not placed on wet ground.

Step-by-Step Practical Exercise: Butt Weld on 3 mm Mild Steel

  1. Prepare the material. Cut two 100 mm 50 mm 3 mm mild steel plates. Grind the edges to a clean, bright surface at least 20 mm back from the joint. Remove mill scale, rust, and oil.
  2. Set the machine. Use 0.8 mm ER70S-6 wire, C25 gas at 1215 L/min, voltage 1820 V, WFS 5.07.0 m/min. Check drive roll tension the wire should deform slightly but not flatten.
  3. Tack the joint. Place the plates with a 1.52.0 mm root gap. Tack at both ends and the centre. Check alignment and flatness.
  4. Set your body position. Stand comfortably, rest your forearms on a bench or support, and hold the torch at 1015 push angle (forehand technique). CTWD should be 1015 mm.
  5. Run the weld. Start at one end, squeeze the trigger, and move steadily. Listen for a consistent crackling sound not popping or hissing. If the arc stutters, increase WFS slightly. If it spatters heavily, reduce voltage or increase CTWD.
  6. Inspect and fix. After cooling, wire-brush the weld. Check for porosity, undercut, and incomplete fusion. If defects appear, adjust one variable at a time and re-run.
  7. Record your settings. Keep a logbook of voltage, WFS, gas flow, and results. This becomes your workshop's own reference more valuable than any generic chart.

Common Mistakes and Business Impact

In agribusiness fabrication, a failed weld is not just a technical problem it is a warranty claim, a downtime cost, and a reputation risk. The most common MIG mistakes are: wrong polarity (MIG is usually DCEP electrode positive), insufficient gas flow, dirty base metal, worn contact tip, and travel speed too fast or too slow. Each of these can be corrected in minutes but can cost thousands in rework if ignored. Train your welders to check consumables at the start of every shift, and keep a spare liner, tip, and nozzle in the workshop store.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Check gas cylinder level and flow rate (1025 L/min depending on material).
  • Inspect contact tip, nozzle, and liner for wear or spatter build-up.
  • Confirm wire diameter and type match the base material.
  • Set voltage and WFS from the chart, then fine-tune by sound and arc stability.
  • Clean base metal to bright steel no rust, paint, oil, or galvanising in the weld zone.
  • Maintain 1015 mm CTWD and a 1015 push angle.
  • Wear PPE: auto-darkening helmet, gloves, respirator, flame-resistant clothing.
  • Record settings and inspection results in the workshop logbook.
