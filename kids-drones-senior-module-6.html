🧠 Brain Opener

A single drone photo shows you a small view. Fifty overlapping photos stitched together become a map you can measure from. This is what farmers, surveyors, and engineers actually pay for.

  • What is photogrammetry?
  • Why do photos need to overlap?
  • What can you measure from a drone map?
🎁 Reveal a good answer

Photogrammetry is the process of turning overlapping photos into a 3D model or a map. Photos must overlap 60 to 80 percent so software can find matching points. From the resulting map you can measure distances, areas, heights, and volumes - like the size of a crop circle or the volume of a soil pile.

📖 Chipo Maps the Community Garden

Chipo was seventeen. Her community in Chitungwiza had a garden plot of about two hectares that fed 40 families. The plot was old and the layout was chaotic. Some rows were overgrown. Some were empty. Nobody knew exactly how much was being used.

The garden committee wanted a proper plan. They asked a local surveyor. He quoted 500 dollars and six weeks.

Chipo offered to try with a drone. She had a small quadcopter with a camera. She had read about photogrammetry online.

She planned the flight. Grid lines 50 metres apart over a rectangle 100 by 200 metres. Altitude 60 metres. Photo every 10 metres with 75 percent overlap.

Total flight time: 8 minutes. The battery was comfortable.

She flew the mission three times over a week - once in the morning, once midday, once late afternoon - to compare shadows.

She uploaded all the photos to a free tool called OpenDroneMap. The software stitched them into one large map. The map showed every path, every row, every tree.

Then she measured. Total garden area: 2.1 hectares. Cultivated: 1.4 hectares. Fallow: 0.5 hectares. Buildings and paths: 0.2 hectares. Trees along the boundary: 37.

She printed the map on two large sheets and pinned them to the committee wall.

The committee had never seen their own garden from above. They discovered two patches that had been abandoned for years and could be reclaimed. They found a path that could be straightened to save 20 metres of walking.

The surveyor charged 500 dollars. Chipo had done it in three hours for free. Both jobs had value. But Chipo now had a skill.

💡 The Big Idea

Overlapping photos become a measurable map

Photogrammetry uses matching points in overlapping photos to reconstruct the 3D shape of the ground. The software finds the same object in multiple photos and triangulates its position. From this, it builds a map you can measure. Every aerial map, from Google Maps to a farm plan, is built this way.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

How photogrammetry works

You take overlapping photos. The software finds features that appear in multiple photos - a rock, a corner of a building, a tree. It matches these features across photos and calculates their 3D positions using the different angles.

The result is a point cloud: thousands of dots in 3D space. Then the software builds a mesh on top of the points and maps the photos onto the mesh. The final output is called an orthomosaic - a flat, measurable image of the whole area.

2

Ground control points

For accurate measurement, you need ground control points. These are visible markers on the ground whose exact GPS position is known. You place them before the flight, photograph them, and tell the software where they are.

With ground control points, accuracy improves from 2 to 5 metres to 2 to 5 centimetres. Professional mapping always uses them. For simple work like a garden plan, they are optional.

3

What you can measure from a drone map

Distances: any two points on the map. Areas: any polygon you draw. Volumes: compare two maps taken months apart and calculate how much soil has been moved or added. Heights: from the point cloud.

🛠 Hands-On - Map a Small Area from Photos

You need

  • A phone or drone with camera
  • An area about 50 by 50 metres
  • Free tool: OpenDroneMap or WebODM
  1. Pick a small area - a garden, a yard, a field.
  2. Fly or walk a grid pattern taking photos every 3 metres.
  3. Make sure each photo overlaps the previous one by about 70 percent.
  4. Upload all photos to OpenDroneMap.
  5. Wait for the software to process. It can take 30 minutes.
  6. Download the orthomosaic (the map).
  7. Measure one known distance to check accuracy.
Why it works: This is the exact workflow used by commercial mapping companies. The scale changes. The method is identical.

🎮 Game - Photo Stitch Challenge

2 to 8 players - 20 min

Each player takes 20 overlapping photos of a small object (a table, a tree, a wall). Upload them to a free stitch app on a phone. Score by how well the stitch comes out. Discuss: what made some stitches fail? Not enough overlap? Too much movement? Bad light?

🤖 AI-Era Skill

AI can help analyse drone maps. But the map itself must be accurate first.

Try this prompt: "I have a drone orthomosaic of a 2-hectare garden. How do I use it to identify which areas are overgrown, which are cultivated, and which have been abandoned? What should I look for in the map?"

Test the analysis against the real garden. What the AI suggests you look for (colour, texture, pattern) will be correct. But the actual identifying must be done by eye.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Rwandan land registry

Rwanda used drones to map the entire country for land registration. Every plot was photographed from above and measured. The mapping cut the cost of land titling by 90 percent and enabled thousands of farmers to prove ownership.

Kenyan coffee cooperative

A Kenyan coffee cooperative mapped 2,000 hectares of member farms in three months. The maps showed which farms had old trees, which had new planting, and which needed irrigation. The cooperative now plans production around the maps.
