GPS and Telemetry - How the Drone Knows Where It Is

~14 min read20 min gameIntermediate
Shared phone

🧠 Brain Opener

Your phone knows where you are. A drone knows where it is. Neither of them can see. They listen to satellites 20,000 kilometres above the earth and do some very clever maths.

  • How does GPS know where you are?
  • What is telemetry?
  • What happens if GPS fails mid-flight?
🎁 Reveal a good answer

GPS uses signals from at least four satellites. The drone measures how long each signal took to arrive, and from those times calculates its distance from each satellite. Four distances give one position. Telemetry is the stream of data the drone sends back to the controller - height, speed, battery, GPS signal. If GPS fails, the drone switches to manual control or attempts a return-to-home using its last known position.

📖 Tapiwa Loses the Signal

Tapiwa was seventeen and had a summer job flying a drone for a maize farm in Mashonaland. He flew the same route every morning - along the fence line, over the irrigation pivot, back to the shed.

One morning, twenty minutes into the flight, the controller screen froze. The GPS icon went red. The drone was 400 metres away, near the far end of the field.

Tapiwa panicked. He pushed the stick forward and the drone did not respond. He tried turning - nothing. The screen was dead.

Then he remembered the training manual. When GPS fails, wait. Do not touch anything. The drone will try to return home using its last known position.

He waited. Twenty seconds passed. The drone turned around on its own, slowly, and started flying back the way it had come. It hovered above the launch point at 30 metres. Then it began to descend.

It landed one metre from where it had taken off.

Tapiwa sat on the grass for a minute, breathing.

His boss, Mr Nyathi, walked over. "Good. You did nothing. That was correct."

"What happened?"

"GPS glitch. There is a tower on the far side of the field. Interference. It happens about twice a year in that spot."

"Why did the drone come back by itself?"

"Because it recorded its takeoff point. When it lost signal, it did the smart thing - it went home."

Tapiwa learned two lessons that day. First, never fly beyond GPS coverage without checking the map first. Second, when the drone does something on its own, do not fight it. It is usually right.

💡 The Big Idea

GPS is four satellites and some very good maths

A GPS satellite broadcasts a signal with the exact time it was sent. The receiver knows the exact time it received it. The difference gives the travel time. Multiply by the speed of light and you have the distance. Three satellites give a rough position. Four give an accurate one. Twenty-four satellites in orbit at all times means at least four are always visible from anywhere on earth.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

How GPS actually works

Every GPS satellite carries an atomic clock accurate to a billionth of a second. It broadcasts a signal every millisecond containing the time. A GPS receiver in a drone receives these signals and calculates how long each took to arrive.

Signal travel time times the speed of light gives distance. Distance to three satellites narrows your position to two possible points. Distance to four satellites chooses between them and confirms altitude. This is called trilateration - not triangulation. The difference matters.

2

What telemetry is

Telemetry is the data the drone sends back in real time. It includes: current position, altitude, speed, heading, battery level, GPS signal strength, motor RPM, and camera status. Every one of these numbers is used by the flight controller and by the pilot.

A modern drone sends telemetry 10 to 50 times per second. This is what allows features like auto-return-home, waypoint navigation, and follow-me mode. Without telemetry, the drone is blind to its own state.

3

What happens when GPS fails

GPS can fail for three reasons: interference from buildings or radio towers, signal loss in canyons or dense forest, or satellite hardware failure (rare). When GPS fails, the drone can do one of four things depending on its programming: switch to manual control, hold position using internal sensors, return to home, or land immediately.

Professional drone pilots plan for GPS failure. They memorise a nearby visual landmark. They know which direction home is. They fly closer to themselves in areas with known interference. GPS is a tool, not a guarantee.

🛠 Hands-On - Map Your Own GPS Glitches

You need

  • A phone with a GPS app
  • A notebook
  • A map of your area
  1. Walk a 200-metre straight line near your home with the GPS app open.
  2. Mark every point where the signal drops or the position jumps.
  3. Walk it again the next day. Do the same points reappear?
  4. Draw the points on your map.
  5. Write down what is near each one - tall building, tower, tree cover, water.
  6. These are the spots where a drone would also lose GPS. Avoid them in your flight plan.
Why it works: GPS interference is local and repeatable. Finding the spots before you fly keeps you out of trouble.

🎮 Game - Satellite Tag

5 to 15 players - 20 min

Three players are satellites. They stand at known positions around the field. A fourth player is the GPS receiver - they can only move by asking each satellite how far away they are. The satellite answers with a number (in metres). The GPS player must work out their position from the three distances. Time how long it takes. Swap roles and try again.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Nairobi GPS interference

In downtown Nairobi, GPS signal is unreliable between tall buildings. Drone pilots plan their routes to fly over open areas, not between towers. This is called urban canyon interference. Every major African city has these zones.

Lake Victoria survey drones

Drones mapping Lake Victoria shoreline have to work around solar flares that disturb GPS. Solar storms happen every 11 years at peak. Scientists plan their missions around solar weather forecasts.
