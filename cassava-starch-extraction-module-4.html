1. Cell Structure and Starch Release

Cassava roots store starch inside tiny cells, like water in a sponge. Each cell has a wall made of cellulose. To get the starch out, you must break these walls open. If you just cut the root, you only get some starch. The rest stays locked inside the cells. That is why grating or crushing is so important.

Think of a cassava root as a long bag of cells. The starch granules are about 5 to 20 micrometres widetoo small to see without a microscope. When you grate the root, you tear the cell walls. The starch granules spill out into the water you use. The smaller you grate, the more cells you break. A fine grater (like a kitchen grater with small holes) breaks more cells than a coarse one. In many African villages, women use a metal sheet with nail holes punched through it. That works, but a motorised grater with a 1-2 mm screen breaks more cells and gives more starch.

After grating, you must separate the starch from the fibre and other stuff. The fibre (cell walls) is light and floats or gets caught in a sieve. The starch is heavier and sinks in water. So you mix the grated pulp with water, stir well, and pour through a sieve. The fibre stays on top; the starch milk passes through. Let it settle, and the starch forms a white layer at the bottom.

One common mistake is not soaking the roots before grating. Soaking softens the cell walls and makes grating easier. In Nigeria, many processors soak peeled roots for 2-4 hours before grating. That simple step can increase starch release by 10-15%. Also, grate the roots as soon as possible after harvest. After 48 hours, the roots start to rot, and the cells break down in a bad way, reducing starch quality.

Finally, remember that not all starch comes out in the first wash. You can re-wash the fibre two or three times to get more starch. Each wash should use clean water. The first wash gives the most starch, but the second and third still have some. A good processor recovers over 90% of the starch by washing three times.

2. Wet Milling vs. Dry Milling

Wet milling and dry milling are two ways to break cassava cells. Wet milling means you add water during grinding. Dry milling means you grind the roots without adding water. Each has advantages and disadvantages. Your choice depends on your scale, your water supply, and what you want to do with the starch.

In dry milling, you grate or pound dried cassava chips or fresh roots without water. The starch comes out as a powder. This method is common in places where water is scarce. But dry milling creates a lot of dust, and the starch can get hot and damaged. Also, dry milling does not separate the starch from fibre well. You get a mix of starch and fibre that is hard to purify. Dry milling is often used for making cassava flour, not pure starch.

Wet milling is the standard for starch extraction. You grate fresh roots with plenty of water. The water helps cool the grater, carries the starch away, and makes separation easier. In a typical wet milling setup, you use about 3 to 5 litres of water per kilogram of fresh roots. The grated pulp is mixed with water to form a slurry. Then you sieve it. The starch passes through the sieve with the water, while the fibre stays behind. The starch milk is then allowed to settle. After settling, you pour off the water and collect the starch. You can repeat the washing to get cleaner starch.

Wet milling gives higher yield and better quality. The starch is whiter and has less fibre. But it uses a lot of water. In dry areas, you may need to recycle water or use a combination: grate dry, then wash with a small amount of water. For most small-scale processors in Africa, wet milling is the way to go. It is simple, effective, and gives a product that sells well.

One practical tip: if you use wet milling, make sure your water is clean. Dirty water introduces bacteria and colours the starch. Also, work quickly. Wet starch can ferment if left too long. Process within 24 hours for best results.

3. Extraction Methods: Traditional and Modern

Traditional cassava starch extraction has been done in Africa for generations. The basic steps are: peel, wash, grate, mix with water, sieve, settle, and dry. In many villages, people use a wooden mortar and pestle to pound the roots, or a hand grater made from a flattened tin can with holes punched in it. The grated pulp is put in a cloth bag and squeezed, or mixed with water in a basin and strained through a woven basket or cloth. The starch settles at the bottom, and the water is poured off. The wet starch is then dried in the sun or over a fire. This method works, but it is slow and yields less starch. A person can process maybe 10-20 kg of roots per day.

Modern methods use machines to do the same steps faster and better. A motorised grater can process 100-500 kg of roots per hour. Instead of a cloth bag, you use a hydraulic press or a screw press to squeeze water out of the pulp. For sieving, you use a vibrating screen or a rotary sieve. For settling, you use large tanks where starch settles faster. For drying, you use a flash dryer or a solar dryer. These machines cost money, but they increase yield and quality. A small modern plant in Nigeria or Ghana can process 1-2 tonnes of roots per day.

But modern does not always mean better for everyone. The machines need electricity or fuel, spare parts, and skilled operators. In rural areas, these can be hard to get. So many processors use a mix: a motorised grater, but manual sieving and sun drying. That hybrid approach is often the most practical.

One key improvement in modern methods is the use of a hydrocyclone or a disc separator to separate starch from fine fibre. These machines use centrifugal force to spin the starch milk, so heavy starch goes to the outside and light fibre goes to the centre. That gives very pure starch. But for a small-scale start, you can skip that and just do multiple settling steps. Each time you re-suspend the starch in clean water and let it settle, you remove more impurities.

Remember: the goal is to break cells, separate starch from fibre, and wash away impurities. Whether you use a mortar or a machine, the principles are the same. Start traditional, then upgrade as you grow.

4. Water Quality and Quantity Requirements

Water is the backbone of starch extraction. You need it to grate, to wash, to separate, and to purify. But not all water is good. The quality of your water directly affects the quality of your starch. If your water is dirty, your starch will be dirty. If your water has iron, your starch may turn grey. If your water has bacteria, your starch will ferment and smell bad.

For good starch, use clean water. The best is potable waterwater you can drink. That means it is free from dirt, harmful bacteria, and excessive minerals. In many African villages, people use well water or borehole water. That is usually fine if the well is covered and not contaminated. River water or open pond water is risky because it can carry soil, bacteria, and parasites. If you must use river water, let it settle first and filter it through a clean cloth. Boiling is not practical for large volumes, but you can add a small amount of chlorine (like 1 teaspoon of bleach per 20 litres) and let it sit for 30 minutes. That kills most bacteria.

Water quantity matters too. For every 1 kg of fresh cassava roots, you need about 3 to 5 litres of water for the whole process. That includes water for grating, washing the pulp, and settling. If you use too little water, the starch milk is too thick, and separation is poor. If you use too much, you waste water and your settling tanks need to be bigger. A good rule: mix the grated pulp with enough water to make a thin slurry, like milk. That is usually 2-3 litres of water per kg of pulp. Then use another 1-2 litres for washing the fibre and for the second and third settling steps.

In dry regions, water is scarce. You can recycle water. After starch settles, the water on top is fairly clean. You can use it again for the next batch of grating or for the first wash. But do not recycle the water from the final wash, because it contains impurities. Also, do not let recycled water sit for more than a day, or it will smell and grow bacteria.

Test your water before you start. Put a sample in a clear bottle. If it looks cloudy or has a colour, treat it. If it smells bad, do not use it. A simple test: add a few drops of bleach to a litre of water. If it turns yellow, there is too much iron. That water will discolour your starch. Find another source or let it settle and filter through sand.

5. pH and Temperature Effects on Extraction

pH and temperature are two hidden factors that can make or break your starch extraction. pH measures how acidic or alkaline your water is. It runs from 0 (very acidic) to 14 (very alkaline), with 7 being neutral. Cassava starch extracts best when the water is slightly acidic to neutral, around pH 5.5 to 7.0. If the water is too alkaline (pH above 8), the starch granules can swell and become harder to separate. If it is too acidic (pH below 5), the starch may break down and you lose yield. In most cases, clean rainwater or well water is fine. But if your water is very alkaline (like some borehole water in limestone areas), you can add a little lime juice or vinegar to bring the pH down. Test with a cheap pH strip from a pharmacy or agro-shop.

Temperature is even more critical. Starch granules are like tiny packages. When you heat them in water, they absorb water and swellthis is called gelatinisation. Gelatinisation starts at about 60C for cassava starch. Once gelatinised, the starch becomes sticky and thick, and you cannot separate it from fibre or water. So never use hot water for extraction. Always use cool or room-temperature water. In hot African climates, water from a tank in the sun can reach 40C, which is still okay, but if it feels hot to the touch, let it cool. The ideal temperature is 25-35C.

During drying, temperature also matters. If you dry starch in the sun, the surface can get very hotup to 50-60C. That is fine because the starch is not in water. But if you dry starch in an oven or a flash dryer, keep the temperature below 60C to avoid gelatinisation. For sun drying, spread the starch in a thin layer (1-2 cm) on a clean mat or tray. Turn it every few hours. It will dry in 1-2 days. In humid weather, it may take longer. You can also use a solar dryer, which keeps the starch out of direct sun and maintains a lower temperature.

One traditional trick: some processors add a little salt (sodium chloride) to the water. Salt raises the boiling point and can help settle fine particles. But too much salt can leave a salty taste. Use no more than 1 teaspoon per 10 litres. Also, avoid using water that has been stored in metal containers that rust, as iron can catalyse oxidation and darken the starch.

6. Yield Calculation and Efficiency

Yield is how much starch you get from a given amount of cassava roots. It is usually expressed as a percentage. For example, if you start with 100 kg of fresh roots and end up with 20 kg of dry starch, your yield is 20%. Good cassava varieties can give 25-30% starch, but in practice, small-scale processors often get only 15-20% because of losses. Knowing your yield helps you see where you are losing starch and how to improve.

To calculate yield, you need to weigh your roots and your final dry starch. Use a simple scale. Weigh the roots after peeling and washing. Then after you have dried the starch, weigh it again. The formula is: Yield (%) = (weight of dry starch / weight of fresh roots) x 100. For example, 50 kg roots give 9 kg starch: 9/50 x 100 = 18%. If you want to compare batches, always use the same moisture content for the starch. Dry it until it is bone dry (no moisture). You can also measure the starch content of the roots using a simple method: grate a known weight of roots, wash the starch, let it settle, and dry it. That gives you the potential yield. If your actual yield is much lower, you are losing starch.

Where do losses happen? First, during grating: if you do not grate finely, many cells remain unbroken. Second, during sieving: if your sieve is too coarse, starch passes through with the fibre, or if too fine, it clogs and you leave starch behind. Third, during settling: if you do not let it settle long enough, fine starch particles remain in the water you pour off. Fourth, during washing: each time you wash, some starch goes with the water. To reduce losses, grate finely, use a two-step sieving (coarse then fine), let settle for at least 4-6 hours, and wash the fibre at least twice.

Efficiency is about getting more starch with less effort and less waste. One way to improve efficiency is to use a settling tank with a conical bottom. The starch collects at the bottom, and you can drain the water from the top without disturbing the starch. Another way is to use a centrifuge or hydrocyclone if you can afford it. But even simple improvements help: use clean water, control pH and temperature, and work quickly. Also, keep records. Write down the weight of roots, the weight of starch, and any problems. After a few batches, you will see patterns. Maybe your yield drops when you use river water. Maybe it goes up when you soak roots longer. That is how you learn and improve.

Finally, remember that yield is not everything. Quality matters too. A slightly lower yield of pure white starch may sell for more than a higher yield of dirty starch. So aim for both: good yield and good quality.

<h2>Practical Reference: Starch Extraction Principles</h2>

<p>For African cassava processors from smallholder cooperatives in Nigeria's Ogun State to semi-industrial mills in Ghana's Ashanti Region and Tanzania's coastal belt the difference between a profitable starch operation and a break-even one usually comes down to a handful of controllable variables: how well the root cells are ruptured, how much water is used, how pH and temperature are managed, and how efficiently starch is recovered from the mash. This practical reference consolidates the extraction principles covered across this module into a working guide you can pin to the wall of your processing shed.</p>

<p>Cassava roots contain roughly 2030% starch on a fresh-weight basis, but only a fraction of that is recoverable with basic equipment. The rest is lost in pulp, wastewater, and fine particles if the process is not managed deliberately. Every point of recovery you gain is money: at a modest 2 tonnes of fresh roots per day, improving extraction efficiency from 65% to 75% recovers an extra ~100 kg of starch daily meaningful revenue for a small enterprise.</p>

<h3>Why Cell Rupture Is the First Profit Lever</h3>
<p>Cassava starch is stored inside parenchyma cells as discrete granules surrounded by a protein matrix and enclosed by a cellulose-pectin cell wall. If the wall is not ruptured, the starch stays locked in and ends up in your pulp waste. In practice, a well-maintained rasping or grating machine should rupture 8595% of cells. Blunt or worn rasps drop this sharply, and every percentage point of intact cells is lost starch. For a business-first operator, the grating stage is where you should inspect equipment daily not weekly.</p>

<h3>Wet Milling vs. Dry Milling A Commercial Choice</h3>
<p>African processors typically choose wet milling (grating roots with water added) because it delivers higher starch recovery and cleaner starch. Dry milling (milling dried chips) is cheaper on water but yields lower-purity starch suitable mostly for feed or fermentation. If your market is food-grade starch, cosmetics, or pharmaceuticals, wet milling is the only commercially viable route.</p>

<h3>Water Quality and Quantity The Silent Cost Driver</h3>
<p>Water is both a processing aid and a cost. In many African processing sites, water is fetched manually or pumped from boreholes, so every litre has a labour or energy price attached. Two rules matter commercially:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Quality:</strong> Use clean water with low iron and low microbial load. Iron contamination darkens starch and reduces its market grade. Turbid or high-iron water can drop your starch from food-grade to industrial-grade pricing.</li>
<li><strong>Quantity:</strong> Counter-current washing where the cleanest water contacts the cleanest starch can cut water use by 4060% without losing recovery. This is one of the highest-ROI changes a small mill can make.</li>
</ul>

<h3>pH and Temperature The Two Variables Most Often Ignored</h3>
<p>Starch extraction is essentially a physical separation, but pH and temperature govern how cleanly that separation happens. Slightly acidic to neutral pH (6.07.0) keeps starch granules stable and helps proteins stay in solution so they can be washed away. If pH drifts above 7.5, proteins co-settle with starch and you lose purity. If pH drops below 5.5, some starch begins to solubilise and you lose yield.</p>
<p>Temperature is the sharper edge. Cassava starch gelatinises at roughly 5870 C depending on variety and moisture. Once gelatinised, granules swell irreversibly, trap water, and cannot be recovered by settling or centrifugation. In hot African climates, this is a real risk if you use heated water for washing or if slurry sits in the sun. Keep process water at ambient temperature (2535 C) and shade your settling tanks.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020;background:#fdecea;padding:14px;margin:18px 0;border-radius:6px;font-family:Arial,sans-serif;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Hydrogen Cyanide (HCN) and Effluent Risks</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#4a0008;">Fresh cassava roots contain cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin and lotaustralin). During grating and pressing, these release <strong>hydrogen cyanide (HCN)</strong>, a toxic gas. Always:</p>
<ul style="margin:6px 0 0 18px;color:#4a0008;">
<li>Process in well-ventilated areas never in enclosed rooms or during still, humid conditions without airflow.</li>
<li>Never taste raw mash or untreated wastewater to "check quality."</li>
<li>Treat and retain wastewater it carries high organic load and residual cyanide. Do not discharge directly into streams or farmland used for food crops.</li>
<li>Wear gloves and eye protection when handling press water and settling tank sludge.</li>
<li>Train all staff on HCN symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, rapid breathing. Seek immediate medical help if symptoms appear.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>Yield Calculation and Efficiency The Numbers That Decide Your Margin</h3>
<p>Yield in cassava starch extraction is normally expressed as kg of dry starch per tonne of fresh roots, or as a percentage of the starch theoretically present in the roots. The two formulas every operator should know:</p>
<p style="background:#f4f4f4;padding:10px;font-family:monospace;font-size:13px;">
Extraction Efficiency (%) = (Dry starch recovered Starch content of roots) 100<br>
Yield (kg dry starch / tonne fresh root) = Dry starch recovered (kg) Fresh roots processed (tonnes)
</p>
<p>For example: if you process 1,000 kg of fresh roots with a 25% starch content (250 kg starch theoretically), and you recover 190 kg of dry starch, your efficiency is 76% and your yield is 190 kg per tonne. Typical small-scale African operations sit at 5570%; well-run semi-industrial mills reach 7585%. Closing that gap is where the profit lives.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="font-family:Arial,sans-serif;font-size:14px;">
<li><strong>Inspect rasps daily.</strong> Replace or sharpen when cell rupture drops below 85%. This is the single biggest yield lever.</li>
<li><strong>Use clean, low-iron water (pH 6.07.5).</strong> Test quarterly if using borehole or surface water.</li>
<li><strong>Keep slurry below 40 C.</strong> Shade tanks; never use heated water for extraction.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain slurry pH at 6.07.0.</strong> Check with pH strips or a meter at least twice per batch.</li>
<li><strong>Adopt counter-current washing</strong> to cut water use 4060% without losing recovery.</li>
<li><strong>Allow 612 hours settling</strong> and decant carefully rushing loses fine starch.</li>
<li><strong>Weigh and record every batch:</strong> fresh root input, dry starch output, water used. Track efficiency weekly.</li>
<li><strong>Manage HCN and effluent</strong> ventilate, protect workers, and treat wastewater before discharge.</li>
</ol>
