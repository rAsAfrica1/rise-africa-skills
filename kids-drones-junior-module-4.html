Safety First - Nobody Gets Hurt

🧠 Brain Opener

A drone propeller spins at 10,000 rotations per minute. That is 167 times per second. A plastic blade moving that fast can cut skin like a knife through paper.

  • What is the first rule of drone flying?
  • How close is too close to another person?
  • What should you check before every single flight?
🎁 Reveal a good answer

Never fly over people. Never fly within 30 metres of someone who has not agreed to be near a drone. Before every flight, do a 30-second pre-flight check: propellers, battery, GPS signal, and the people around you.

📖 Kudzai's Close Call

Kudzai had been flying his drone for two months. He knew the controls well. He had flown it in the field behind his house maybe a hundred times without any problem. So on Saturday morning he did what he always did - took the drone outside, powered it up, and lifted off.

He did not check the propellers. He did not check the battery. He did not look around to see who was nearby. He just flew.

Five minutes in, the drone started wobbling. Kudzai panicked and pushed the stick the wrong way. The drone spun, dipped, and shot sideways toward his little sister Amai, who was playing near the gate.

The drone missed her by one metre. It hit the ground hard, broke two propellers, and cracked the frame.

That night Kudzai's father sat him down. He was not angry - he was quiet, which was worse.

"You did three things wrong today," his father said. "You did not check the props. You did not check the battery. And you did not check who was near you." He held up three fingers. "Which one almost hurt your sister?"

"All three," Kudzai said.

The next weekend he made a small checklist on a card and taped it to the drone box. He still uses it. He has never had another close call.

💡 The Big Idea

The five rules that never change

(1) Never fly over people. (2) Never fly near an airport. (3) Never fly out of sight. (4) Never fly with damaged blades or a swollen battery. (5) Never fly without an adult watching. These rules apply to every drone, everywhere, forever. They do not have exceptions. Not just this once. Not only for a minute. Not ever.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

The three-minute pre-flight check

Before every flight, walk around the drone and do the same four checks every time. Propellers: spin each one gently with your finger, feel for cracks, listen for roughness. Battery: check for swelling, warmth, or a sweet smell. Any of those and the battery is done. GPS signal: wait for the drone to lock onto satellites, usually 30 to 60 seconds; the light will go green. People: look around and count every person within 30 metres. If anyone is too close, wait.

Do these in the same order every time, out loud if you have to. The whole point is to make it a habit - like tying your shoelaces. When the check becomes automatic, you stop skipping it.

2

The danger you cannot see: batteries

Drone batteries are lithium-polymer. They store a lot of energy in a small space. If a battery gets punctured, overheated, or overcharged, it can catch fire - and a lithium fire cannot be put out with water. It burns until it finishes.

Swollen batteries are the warning sign. If a battery looks puffy, or feels squishy, or is warm when it should be cold - stop. Do not use it. Do not charge it. Tell an adult. Take it to a proper disposal point. This is one of the few always rules in drones that people actually die from ignoring.

3

Why the rules exist

Every drone rule in every country comes from a real accident. Airports have rules because a drone got sucked into a jet engine in 2018 and shut down Gatwick Airport for 36 hours. Do not fly over people exists because a child in the UK lost an eye in 2015. Do not fly out of sight exists because search-and-rescue teams could not find crashed drones after losing track.

The rules are not there to spoil your fun. They are there because somebody else already paid the price for learning them. You get to skip that price by following them.

🛠 Hands-On - Hazard Map of Your Flying Space

You need

  • A piece of A4 paper
  • Pencil
  • A red pen or marker
  • A tape measure or string
  1. Walk to the place where you would fly your drone.
  2. Draw a rough map of the space on the paper.
  3. Mark every hazard with a red X: power lines, trees, buildings, busy roads, dogs, water.
  4. Mark every person who lives nearby with a small circle.
  5. Measure or estimate the distance from the launch point to the nearest hazard.
  6. Write the five rules on the back of the map and pin it up at home.
Why it works: Real drone pilots have a mental map of every hazard before every flight. Drawing it on paper is the fastest way to build that habit.

🎮 Game - Safety Check Race

2 to 6 players - 10 min

Two people. One person holds a real drone or a printed checklist card. The other person runs through the four pre-flight checks out loud as fast as possible: propellers, battery, GPS, people. Time each player. Fastest correct run wins.

🤖 AI-Era Skill

AI can help you build safer habits - but only if you ask it clearly. It cannot see your drone, so you must tell it exactly what you have.

Try this prompt: "I have a toy drone with a battery that feels slightly puffy. Should I use it? Give me 3 reasons for your answer and 3 steps to take next."

Check the answer against your drone manual or a parent. Any answer that says it is probably fine is wrong. Swollen batteries are never safe, even slightly.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Gatwick Airport shutdown, 2018

Two drones were reported near the runway at Gatwick Airport in the UK. The airport shut for 36 hours and cancelled over 1,000 flights, affecting 140,000 passengers. Nobody was hurt - but the economic cost was over 50 million pounds.

Kenya drone rules

Kenya requires all commercial drone pilots to be registered with the Kenya Civil Aviation Authority. Rules include no flying above 400 feet, no flying within 50 metres of people or property, and no flying at night. Every country has similar rules.

🏆 Project Challenge

Make Your Own Pre-Flight Checklist Card

Every professional pilot has one. Write yours.

  1. Cut a piece of card the size of a playing card.
  2. Write the four checks in order on one side: props, battery, GPS, people.
  3. On the other side write the five rules.
  4. Decorate it with a red border so you notice it.
  5. Tape it to the lid of your drone box or your flying bag.
  6. Use it - every flight, no exceptions, for one month.
