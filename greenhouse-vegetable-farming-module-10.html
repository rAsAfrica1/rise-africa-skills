1. Greenhouse Site Selection and Construction for African Conditions

Choosing the right site and building a greenhouse that survives African weather is the foundation of profitable vegetable farming. Many growers lose money because they rush into construction without assessing wind, water, sun, and market access. This lesson gives you a practical, business-first approach to site selection and construction that works from Kenya to Ghana to South Africa. Start with the market. Before you look at any plot, identify where you will sell. A greenhouse 30 minutes from a city market or hotel cluster is worth more than a larger one three hours away. Calculate transport cost per crate. If tomatoes sell at 80 shillings per kilo in town and transport adds 15 shillings per kilo, your margin shrinks fast. Choose a site within 20 kilometers of your main buyer if possible. Next, assess water. Vegetables need 4 to 6 liters per plant per day in hot conditions. A 500-square-meter greenhouse with 1,000 tomato plants needs 4,000 to 6,000 liters daily. Confirm you have a reliable source: borehole, river, dam, or municipal supply. Test water quality for salinity and pH. High salt kills seedlings. A simple test kit costs little compared to crop failure. If water is scarce, plan drip irrigation and mulching from day one. Wind is the silent killer of greenhouses. In the Rift Valley, coastal areas, and Sahel regions, gusts above 60 kilometers per hour can tear polythene and bend frames. Visit the site during the windy season. Look for natural windbreaks: trees, hedges, or buildings. If none exist, plant a live fence of Grevillea or Casuarina 10 meters upwind. Orient the greenhouse so the long side faces the prevailing wind, reducing pressure on the structure. Sunlight matters but too much heat is a problem. In lowland tropics, midday temperatures inside a closed greenhouse can exceed 45 degrees Celsius, cooking pollen and reducing fruit set. Choose a site with morning sun and afternoon shade if possible. Deciduous trees on the western side help. In highland areas like Ethiopia or Rwanda, more sun is fine. Always check drainage. Greenhouses need level ground with a 1 to 2 percent slope for runoff. In flood-prone areas, build a raised bed of soil or gravel 30 centimeters high. Never place a greenhouse in a valley bottom where cold air and water collect. Soil testing is cheap insurance. Send samples to a local agricultural office. You want loam or sandy loam with pH 6.0 to 7.0. If your soil is clay, add compost and sand. If it is sandy, add manure and mulch. You can also grow in bags or raised beds with a prepared mix of topsoil, compost, and sand. Now construction. The two main types are wooden and galvanized steel. Wood is cheaper but lasts 3 to 5 years in humid areas. Steel costs more but lasts 10 to 15 years. For a 500-square-meter greenhouse, expect to spend 150,000 to 400,000 Kenyan shillings or equivalent, depending on materials. Use treated poles for wood. For steel, use 2-inch diameter pipes for uprights and 1.5-inch for trusses. The roof should be 3 to 4 meters high at the ridge to allow hot air to rise. Cover with UV-treated polythene of 200 microns. Cheaper plastic degrades in 6 months. Buy from a reputable supplier and check the warranty. Install roll-up sides for ventilation. In hot climates, you need side curtains that open 1.5 meters. Insect netting of 40 to 50 mesh on vents keeps whiteflies and aphids out. The door should be a double-entry system: an outer door and an inner door to prevent pests from flying in when you enter. Floor: use gravel or concrete paths. Weeds harbor pests. A weed-free floor reduces disease. Add a small washing station and a packing shed at one end. This saves time and improves hygiene. Water system: install a tank on a raised platform 2 meters high for gravity flow, or use a pump. Drip lines with 2 liters per hour emitters are standard. Add a fertigation unit: a simple venturi injector costs little and lets you feed plants with every watering. This boosts yields by 20 to 30 percent. Finally, budget for contingency. Construction always costs more than planned. Add 15 percent. And remember: a greenhouse is a tool, not a guarantee. You still need good seeds, proper spacing, and daily care. But a well-sited, well-built greenhouse gives you a 10 to 15 year asset that can produce 3 to 4 harvests per year. That is the business case. Do the math before you dig.

2. Soil Preparation, Planting, and Crop Scheduling for Continuous Harvest

A greenhouse is only as good as what you put in it. This lesson covers soil preparation, planting techniques, and crop scheduling that keep your beds productive every week of the year. The goal is continuous harvest, not one big flush followed by empty beds. Start with soil preparation. If you are growing in the ground, begin 3 weeks before planting. Clear all crop residues. Solarize the soil if you have pest problems: wet the bed, cover with clear plastic for 4 to 6 weeks during hot weather. This kills nematodes and soil-borne fungi. Then add organic matter. For every 10 square meters, mix in 2 to 3 wheelbarrows of well-rotted manure or compost. Do not use fresh manure; it burns roots and carries weed seeds. Add 200 grams of agricultural lime if pH is below 6.0. Add 100 grams of triple superphosphate for root development. Mix into the top 20 centimeters. If you use raised beds, make them 1 meter wide and 20 centimeters high. This improves drainage and makes harvesting easier. For bag culture, use a mix of 60 percent topsoil, 30 percent compost, and 10 percent sand. Add 5 grams of NPK 17-17-17 per bag at planting. Now planting. Use certified seeds or healthy seedlings. In Africa, common greenhouse crops are tomatoes, capsicum, cucumbers, and leafy greens like spinach and kale. For tomatoes, choose determinate varieties for short cycles or indeterminate for long harvests. Hybrids like Anna F1, Padma, or Roma VF work well. Raise seedlings in trays for 3 to 4 weeks. Harden them by reducing water and exposing them to sun for 3 days before transplanting. Transplant in the evening or on a cloudy day. Water the seedlings before removing from trays. Set them at the same depth as in the tray. Spacing matters. Tomatoes: 60 centimeters between plants, 80 centimeters between rows. Capsicum: 45 by 60 centimeters. Cucumbers: 50 by 100 centimeters, trained up strings. Leafy greens: 20 by 20 centimeters. Do not overcrowd. Airflow reduces disease. After planting, water immediately with a starter solution: 10 grams of NPK 17-17-17 per 10 liters of water. Apply 200 milliliters per plant. This reduces transplant shock. Crop scheduling is where profit is made. Do not plant all beds at once. Divide your greenhouse into 4 to 6 blocks. Plant one block every 2 to 3 weeks. This gives you a steady supply for market and avoids price crashes when everyone harvests at once. For example, with 6 blocks of tomatoes, you can harvest from week 10 to week 40 continuously. Keep a simple chart on the wall. Write planting date, expected harvest date, and actual harvest date. After one year, you will know your exact cycle lengths. This is business intelligence. Rotation prevents disease. Never grow tomatoes after tomatoes, or capsicum after tomatoes. They share pests and diseases like bacterial wilt and nematodes. Rotate with leafy greens or legumes. A good sequence: tomatoes, then spinach, then beans, then cucumbers, then back to tomatoes. Legumes fix nitrogen and improve soil. If you grow in bags, remove and replace the growing medium every 2 years. Intercropping can increase income per square meter. Plant lettuce or spring onions between tomato rows during the first 6 weeks before the canopy closes. These quick crops sell fast and use space that would otherwise be empty. But do not intercrop with tall crops that shade the main crop. Mulching is essential. Use dry grass, straw, or black plastic. Mulch conserves water, suppresses weeds, and keeps fruit clean. In hot climates, mulch reduces soil temperature by 5 to 8 degrees Celsius. That means less stress and higher yields. Water management: drip irrigation is best. Run it for 30 to 45 minutes twice a day in hot weather, once a day in cool weather. Check moisture by squeezing soil in your hand. If it crumbles, it is too dry. If it drips, it is too wet. Overwatering causes root rot and leaches nutrients. Underwatering causes blossom end rot in tomatoes. Fertigation: after 3 weeks, feed weekly with a balanced fertilizer. Use 100 grams of NPK 17-17-17 per 100 liters of water. Apply 500 milliliters per plant. When flowering starts, switch to a high-potassium fertilizer like NPK 12-12-36 or 0-0-50. This improves fruit size and sweetness. Organic growers can use compost tea or liquid manure. Always filter it to avoid clogging drip lines. Pest and disease scouting: walk your greenhouse every morning. Look under leaves and at growing tips. Early detection saves crops. Remove and burn diseased plants immediately. Do not compost them. Keep a notebook. Record what you see and what you did. This helps you predict problems. Finally, keep records of everything: inputs, labor, water, electricity, and sales. At the end of each cycle, calculate your cost per kilo and your profit per square meter. If a crop is not profitable, replace it. The greenhouse is a factory. Your job is to maximize output per square meter per month. That is the mindset that turns a small plot into a real business.

3. Integrated Pest and Disease Management in Greenhouse Vegetables

Pests and diseases can wipe out a greenhouse crop in days. But you do not need to spray harsh chemicals every week. This lesson teaches integrated pest and disease management, or IPM, that is practical, affordable, and safe for African growers. The core principle is prevention first, then monitoring, then targeted action. Start with sanitation. A clean greenhouse is your first defense. Remove all weeds inside and within 2 meters outside. Weeds host whiteflies, aphids, and thrips. Keep the floor free of plant debris. After harvest, remove all crop residues and burn or bury them far from the greenhouse. Wash tools with soap and water or a 10 percent bleach solution between uses. Do not smoke or bring tobacco near tomatoes; it spreads tobacco mosaic virus. Use a footbath with disinfectant at the door. Change it weekly. Next, physical barriers. Install 40 to 50 mesh insect netting on all vents and doors. This keeps out whiteflies, aphids, and leafminers. Use a double-door entry. If you cannot afford netting everywhere, at least cover the door and the upwind side. Yellow sticky traps catch whiteflies and aphids. Blue sticky traps catch thrips. Hang 10 to 15 traps per 500 square meters. Replace them every 4 to 6 weeks. These traps also help you monitor pest populations. Check them twice a week. If you catch more than 20 whiteflies per trap per week, take action. Biological control is powerful and cheap. Encourage natural enemies. Ladybirds eat aphids. Lacewing larvae eat thrips and whiteflies. Spiders eat many pests. Do not spray broad-spectrum insecticides that kill these helpers. You can buy predatory mites like Phytoseiulus persimilis for spider mites. In Kenya, Uganda, and South Africa, these are available from commercial suppliers. Release them when you first see pests. For soil-borne diseases like bacterial wilt, use resistant varieties and grafted seedlings. Grafting tomatoes onto resistant rootstocks is common in Asia and now spreading in Africa. It costs more per seedling but saves the crop. If you cannot graft, rotate crops and solarize soil. For fungal diseases like powdery mildew and early blight, improve airflow. Prune lower leaves and side shoots. Water at the base, not on leaves. Use drip irrigation. If you see mildew, spray a solution of 1 tablespoon baking soda and 1 teaspoon liquid soap in 4 liters of water. Apply weekly. For blight, remove affected leaves and spray copper-based fungicide if needed. Always follow label instructions. For bacterial diseases, there is no cure. Remove and destroy infected plants. Disinfect tools. Do not work in wet plants; bacteria spread in water. For viral diseases like mosaic and yellow leaf curl, control the insect vectors. Whiteflies spread yellow leaf curl. Use netting and sticky traps. Remove infected plants immediately. There is no chemical cure for viruses. Chemical pesticides are a last resort. If you must spray, choose the least toxic option. Use neem oil, pyrethrum, or soap sprays first. These are allowed in organic production and have low residue. If you use synthetic pesticides, rotate active ingredients to prevent resistance. Never spray during flowering when bees are active. Spray in the evening. Wear protective clothing: gloves, mask, long sleeves. Keep children and animals away. Observe the pre-harvest interval. Do not harvest before the waiting period. This protects your customers and your reputation. Record every spray: date, product, dose, and reason. This helps you track what works and what does not. It also helps if buyers ask about your practices. Many supermarkets and exporters require records. Use a simple notebook or a phone app. Now, a word on resistant varieties. This is the cheapest IPM tool. Buy seeds that are resistant to common diseases. For tomatoes, look for resistance to Fusarium wilt, Verticillium wilt, and nematodes. For capsicum, look for resistance to bacterial spot. Resistant varieties may cost 20 percent more but reduce losses by 50 percent or more. That is good business. Finally, train your workers. Everyone who enters the greenhouse must understand sanitation, scouting, and safe spraying. Hold a short meeting every Monday. Show them what a pest looks like. Show them what a diseased leaf looks like. Give a small bonus for early detection. This turns your workers into allies. IPM is not about eliminating all pests. It is about keeping them below the level that causes economic damage. A few aphids do not require spraying. A few whiteflies do not require spraying. But a population explosion does. Monitor, act early, and use the softest option first. This saves money, protects your health, and produces clean vegetables that sell for a premium. That is the business case for IPM.

4. Harvesting, Post-Harvest Handling, and Value Addition

You have grown a beautiful crop. Now the money is made or lost in harvesting and post-harvest handling. In Africa, up to 40 percent of vegetables are lost after harvest due to poor handling, heat, and bruising. This lesson shows you how to harvest at the right time, handle produce gently, and add value to increase your income. Start with harvest timing. Each crop has a peak moment. Tomatoes for fresh market: harvest when 50 to 70 percent colored, not fully red. They continue ripening off the vine. For processing, harvest fully red. Capsicum: harvest green for higher volume, or red for higher price. Red capsicum takes 2 to 3 weeks longer but can fetch double. Cucumbers: harvest young, 15 to 20 centimeters long. Do not let them turn yellow. Leafy greens: harvest in the early morning when leaves are crisp. Never harvest in the heat of the day. Use clean, sharp tools. For tomatoes and capsicum, use pruning shears or a sharp knife. Leave a short stem on capsicum to prevent water loss. For cucumbers, twist and pull gently. For leafy greens, cut with a knife and place directly into a clean crate. Do not throw produce. Every bruise is an entry point for rot. Use shallow crates, not deep sacks. Line crates with clean paper or leaves. Do not overfill. Stack crates carefully so the bottom ones are not crushed. Harvest into shade. If you harvest in the sun, produce heats up and respires, losing sugar and water. Move crates to a shaded area immediately. Now post-harvest handling. The goal is to remove field heat quickly. Field heat is the heat stored in the produce from the sun. The faster you cool it, the longer it lasts. In rural Africa, you may not have electricity for a cold room. Use evaporative cooling. Build a simple cooler: a wooden frame with wet sand or charcoal on the sides, covered with a wet cloth. Place crates inside. As water evaporates, it cools the air. This can reduce temperature by 10 to 15 degrees Celsius. It costs almost nothing. Another method: harvest in the evening and store in a ventilated shed overnight. Sell early the next morning. Washing: for leafy greens, wash in clean water with a small amount of food-grade sanitizer if available. For tomatoes and capsicum, wipe with a clean cloth. Do not soak them. Soaking spreads disease. Grading: sort by size, color, and quality. Remove damaged, diseased, or overripe produce. Uniform grades sell faster and for higher prices. Buyers pay more for consistency. Pack in clean, labeled crates or punnets. Use your farm name and contact. This builds trust and repeat business. Value addition is where you can double or triple your income. Instead of selling fresh tomatoes at 50 shillings per kilo, make tomato sauce or paste. A simple process: wash, chop, boil, blend, add salt and preservative (citric acid or vinegar), and bottle in sterilized jars. A kilo of tomatoes makes about 500 milliliters of sauce. Sell for 150 to 200 shillings. That is 3 to 4 times the fresh price. For capsicum, dry and grind into paprika. For leafy greens, blanch and freeze if you have a freezer. Or dry them in a solar dryer and sell as dried vegetables. These have a long shelf life and can be sold in cities or exported to diaspora markets. Another value addition: bundle fresh herbs like coriander, mint, and basil. Restaurants pay premium prices for fresh herbs. Grow them in the greenhouse between main crops. A small bundle sells for 20 to 50 shillings. It adds up. Packaging matters. Use biodegradable punnets or paper bags for premium markets. Plastic is cheap but increasingly banned in some countries. Check local regulations. Label with weight, harvest date, and farm name. For export, you need GlobalGAP or similar certification. That is a bigger investment but opens high-value markets. Start with local hotels, supermarkets, and schools. They pay reliably and often provide feedback. Transport: use ventilated vehicles. Do not transport in closed boots or under tarps in the sun. If you use public transport, pack in small crates and keep them shaded. For long distances, harvest early and travel at night. Record everything: harvest date, quantity, grade, buyer, price. After one season, you will know which crops and which buyers give the best return. Double down on those. Post-harvest loss is not inevitable. With simple, low-cost techniques, you can cut losses from 40 percent to under 10 percent. That difference is pure profit. And value addition turns a perishable crop into a stable product that can be stored and sold when prices are high. This is how greenhouse farming becomes a real business, not just a garden.

5. Marketing, Record Keeping, and Financial Management for Greenhouse Growers

Growing vegetables is only half the business. Selling them profitably and managing money is the other half. This lesson covers marketing, record keeping, and financial management with a practical African focus. Many growers produce well but fail because they do not know their costs or cannot find reliable buyers. Let us fix that. Start with market research. Before you plant, talk to potential buyers. Visit hotels, restaurants, supermarkets, schools, and open markets. Ask what they buy, in what quantity, at what price, and how often. Ask about quality standards. A hotel may want uniform tomatoes of a certain size. A supermarket may want graded and packaged produce. A school may want bulk leafy greens at a low price. Write down what you learn. Then choose crops that match the demand. Do not grow what you like. Grow what sells. Build relationships. A handshake is not enough. Get a simple agreement. It can be a written contract or a verbal understanding with a deposit. For example, a hotel agrees to buy 50 kilograms of tomatoes per week at 80 shillings per kilo for 6 months. You agree to deliver every Tuesday morning. This gives you a predictable income and gives them a reliable supply. Both sides win. Diversify your buyers. Do not rely on one buyer. If they stop, you lose everything. Aim for at least three buyers: one hotel, one supermarket, and one open market trader. This spreads risk. Also consider selling directly to consumers through a farm shop, a roadside stall, or a WhatsApp group. Direct sales give you the highest margin. You can also sell to neighbors and local schools. Pricing is critical. Calculate your cost of production per kilo. Add up seeds, fertilizer, water, electricity, labor, transport, and a portion of the greenhouse depreciation. Divide by total kilos harvested. That is your break-even price. Never sell below that. Add a profit margin of 30 to 50 percent. Check market prices. If your price is too high, you will not sell. If it is too low, you will work for nothing. Adjust based on quality and reliability. Record keeping is not optional. It is the difference between a hobby and a business. Keep four books: a cash book, an inventory book, a labor book, and a sales book. The cash book records every shilling in and out. Date, description, amount. The inventory book records seeds, fertilizer, pesticides, and tools. When you buy and when you use. The labor book records who worked, for how long, and what you paid. The sales book records what you sold, to whom, at what price, and whether you were paid. You can use a notebook and a pen. You do not need a computer. But you must be consistent. Write every day. At the end of each month, add up your income and expenses. Calculate your profit or loss. If you are losing money, find out why. Is it low yield? High input cost? Theft? Poor pricing? Fix the biggest problem first. Financial management: separate business money from personal money. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for the farm. Pay yourself a fixed salary. Do not dip into farm money for personal needs. This discipline is hard but essential. Save 10 percent of every sale for reinvestment. Use it to buy better seeds, repair the greenhouse, or expand. Save another 5 percent for emergencies. Pests, storms, and price crashes happen. An emergency fund keeps you in business. Consider credit. If you need a loan, borrow for assets that increase production, like a drip system or a larger greenhouse. Do not borrow for daily expenses. Before borrowing, calculate the return. If a 50,000 shilling loan for a drip system increases yield by 30 percent and pays back in one season, it is a good loan. If you cannot see the payback, do not borrow. Insurance is available in some countries for greenhouse crops. It covers hail, wind, and sometimes disease. It is worth checking. Finally, plan for the future. Set goals. This season, I will produce 2 tons of tomatoes and earn 160,000 shillings. Next season, I will add cucumbers and earn 250,000 shillings. Write them down. Review them monthly. Share them with your workers. When everyone knows the goal, they work toward it. Marketing and money management are not separate from farming. They are part of farming. The best greenhouse in Africa will fail if you cannot sell the produce or track the money. But a modest greenhouse with good marketing and tight records can support a family, pay school fees, and grow into a real agribusiness. That is the goal. Start today. Write your first record. Talk to your first buyer. Calculate your first cost per kilo. The numbers will guide you better than any guess.

<div style="border:3px solid #c62828;background:#fdecea;padding:14px;margin:18px 0;font-family:Arial,sans-serif;">
<strong style="color:#b71c1c;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING PESTICIDES AND FOOD SAFETY</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7f1d1d;">Pesticides used in greenhouses are more dangerous than in open fields because air exchange is limited and residues persist on foliage and fruit. Never enter a sprayed greenhouse before the label <strong>Re-Entry Interval (REI)</strong> has passed. Never harvest before the <strong>Pre-Harvest Interval (PHI)</strong> has passed doing so exposes consumers to illegal residues and can cost you your buyer contract, certification (GlobalG.A.P., organic), and legal liability. Always wear the PPE stated on the label, never mix concentrates in an enclosed space, never store pesticides near produce, feed, or water, and never reuse pesticide containers for food or drinking water. Keep children and untrained workers away from mixing and storage areas. In case of poisoning, contact your nearest poison centre immediately and bring the product label.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist Weekly Greenhouse Management</h3>

<ol style="font-family:Arial,sans-serif;font-size:14px;line-height:1.55;">
<li><strong>Scout before you spray.</strong> Walk every bed twice a week. Record pest counts. Spray only above threshold this saves money and protects beneficials.</li>
<li><strong>Read the label every time.</strong> Confirm dose, REI, PHI, PPE, and compatibility before mixing. Never mix two concentrates together first.</li>
<li><strong>Wear full PPE.</strong> Coverall, gloves, goggles, boots and respirator when required. Wash PPE separately from family laundry.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilate and exclude workers.</strong> Open vents after spraying, keep all staff out until REI passes, and post a visible warning sign on the door.</li>
<li><strong>Log every input.</strong> Date, plot, product, batch number, dose, operator, weather, and PHI date. Buyers and certifiers want this.</li>
<li><strong>Calibrate irrigation and fertigation.</strong> Check EC and pH weekly. Wrong EC is the most common cause of poor greenhouse yield in Africa.</li>
<li><strong>Harvest only after PHI.</strong> Wash hands, use clean crates, keep produce out of the sun, and cool as fast as possible.</li>
<li><strong>Reconcile cash weekly.</strong> Compare actual costs to Table 2 above and update your gross margin. Adjust next seasons plan.</li>
</ol>
