1. Choosing the Right Broiler House Design for African Conditions

Selecting a broiler house design is one of the most important business decisions you will make because the structure determines your bird density, your ventilation options, your labour needs, and your ability to survive heat stress. In many African production zones, the difference between profit and loss is not feed quality alone but whether the house keeps birds cool in the afternoon and dry during the rains. Start by defining your production target. A smallholder aiming for 200 to 500 birds per cycle can use a simple open-sided deep litter house with a low wall, a wire mesh upper section, and a corrugated iron or aluminium roof. A commercial producer targeting 5,000 birds or more should consider a controlled environment or tunnel-ventilated house, but only if reliable electricity and backup power are available. The first practical rule is to build for the climate you actually have, not the climate you wish you had. In the humid forest zones of West and Central Africa, the priority is maximum cross ventilation and rain protection. In the dry Sahel and savannah zones, the priority is shading, roof insulation, and evaporative cooling. In the highlands of East Africa, the priority is preventing chilling at night and maintaining dry litter during cold rains. Orientation matters. Place the long axis of the house east to west so the sun does not shine directly into the side walls for long periods. In the tropics, a north-south orientation can reduce heat load in some cases, but east-west is generally preferred because the rising and setting sun is blocked by the narrow ends. Keep the roof overhang at least 1 metre on all sides to stop rain from driving in and to shade the walls. Roof material choice is critical. Bare corrugated iron radiates heat downward and can raise inside temperature by 5 to 8 degrees Celsius above outside temperature. Use white or reflective paint on the roof, or install a ceiling of plywood, cardboard, or insulated panels. In low-cost systems, a double roof with a 10 to 15 centimetre air gap works well. Thatch is excellent insulation but harbours rodents and is a fire risk, so many producers now use thatch only as an outer layer over iron sheets. Wall design should allow air movement at bird level. A common design is a solid wall up to 30 to 45 centimetres, then wire mesh up to the eaves, with curtains that can be rolled down during rain or cold nights. The solid lower wall stops drafts on young chicks and prevents predators from reaching under the mesh. The mesh should be 1 to 2 centimetre square openings to keep out wild birds, which carry disease. Curtains should be made from durable tarpaulin or woven plastic and must be easy to open and close from outside the house. Floor design is equally important. The floor should be raised 20 to 30 centimetres above ground level to prevent water ingress during heavy rain. Use a concrete slab if you can afford it, because it is easy to wash and disinfect. A compacted earth floor with a plastic sheet and 5 to 10 centimetres of litter is cheaper but harder to clean. Litter material should be absorbent, locally available, and free of mould. Wood shavings, rice hulls, chopped maize cobs, and groundnut shells are common choices. Avoid sawdust from treated timber and avoid wet or green materials. The house should have a clear internal height of at least 2.5 metres at the eaves and 3.5 to 4 metres at the ridge to allow hot air to rise above the birds. If the roof is low, heat builds up at bird level and mortality rises. For a naturally ventilated house, the total open side area should be at least 40 to 50 percent of the floor area to allow adequate air exchange. In hot climates, aim for 60 percent or more. Ridge vents or chimneys help release hot air. A simple ridge opening of 30 to 45 centimetres covered with a raised cap can reduce inside temperature significantly. If you are building a controlled environment house, you need insulation in the walls and ceiling, a tunnel ventilation system with fans at one end and cooling pads at the other, and a reliable power supply. The investment is high, but stocking density can be 18 to 22 birds per square metre instead of 8 to 12 in open houses. Only consider this if you have consistent market demand, technical support, and a generator or solar backup. Water and feed access must be planned before construction. Place drinkers and feeders so that birds never have to travel more than 3 to 4 metres to reach them. In hot climates, put drinkers on the side of the house that receives shade in the afternoon. Allow space for a feed store, a mortality pit or incinerator, and a footbath at the entrance. Biosecurity starts with the building layout. The house should be at least 10 metres from other poultry houses and away from roads and public paths. A perimeter fence with a gate that stays closed is a basic but powerful disease barrier. Finally, build with expansion in mind. Many African producers start with one house and add more as demand grows. Leave space between houses for ventilation and for movement of equipment. A well-designed house pays for itself through lower mortality, better feed conversion, and fewer health problems. A poorly designed house will cost you every cycle, no matter how good your chicks and feed are. Before you pour a foundation, visit successful farms in your area, talk to extension officers, and calculate the cost per bird housed. The cheapest house per square metre is rarely the cheapest per bird sold. Invest in shade, airflow, and dryness, and your broilers will reward you with faster growth and higher survival.

2. Calculating and Managing Stocking Density for Maximum Profit

Stocking density is the number of birds you place per square metre of floor space, and it is one of the most powerful levers you have over both bird welfare and business profitability. Put too few birds in a house and you waste space, labour, and heating or cooling costs. Put too many birds in and you get poor growth, high mortality, wet litter, respiratory disease, and downgraded carcasses. The correct density depends on bird weight at slaughter, climate, ventilation capacity, litter management, and market requirements. In open-sided naturally ventilated houses in hot African climates, a common target is 8 to 12 birds per square metre for birds grown to 2.0 to 2.5 kilograms. In cooler highland areas or during the cool season, you can push to 12 to 14 birds per square metre if ventilation and litter management are excellent. In controlled environment houses with tunnel ventilation, densities of 16 to 22 birds per square metre are achievable, but only with proper cooling and air speed. Never use controlled environment densities in an open house, because the birds will suffer heat stress and die. The first step in calculating density is to know your target live weight. If you sell at 1.8 kilograms, you can stock more birds per square metre than if you sell at 2.5 kilograms. A simple rule is that total live weight per square metre should not exceed 30 to 35 kilograms in open houses in hot climates. For example, if you plan to sell at 2.2 kilograms and you want 12 birds per square metre, your final biomass is 26.4 kilograms per square metre, which is acceptable in a well-ventilated open house. If you plan to sell at 2.8 kilograms, 12 birds per square metre gives 33.6 kilograms per square metre, which is too high for most open houses in the tropics. Reduce to 10 birds per square metre or less. Floor space is not the only space that matters. Feeder and drinker space must also be calculated. For round tube feeders, allow 5 to 7 centimetres of linear feeding space per bird for birds up to 2 kilograms, and 7 to 8 centimetres for heavier birds. For drinkers, allow 1.5 to 2 centimetres of linear drinking space per bird, or one bell drinker per 80 to 100 birds. If you use nipple drinkers, allow 10 to 12 birds per nipple. These figures must be adjusted upward in hot weather because birds drink more and crowd around water. Inadequate drinker space is a major cause of heat stress mortality. Ventilation capacity is the second pillar of density management. In an open house, the open side area should be at least 40 to 50 percent of the floor area, and more in hot zones. If your open area is less, reduce density. Air speed at bird level should be at least 1.5 to 2.0 metres per second during hot afternoons. You can measure this with a simple anemometer or estimate by watching smoke or dust movement. If air is stagnant, density must come down. Litter condition is the third pillar. Wet litter causes ammonia burns, breast blisters, and footpad dermatitis, all of which reduce sale value. If litter is wet, density is too high or drinkers are leaking. A good manager walks the house and squeezes litter in the hand; if it sticks together, it is too wet. Reduce density or improve ventilation and drinker management. Stocking density also affects feed conversion ratio. Birds that are crowded eat less, grow slower, and convert feed less efficiently because energy is diverted to fighting heat and stress. In trials across Africa, reducing density from 14 to 10 birds per square metre often improves feed conversion by 5 to 10 percent and reduces mortality by 2 to 5 percent. The extra space costs you nothing except a slightly larger house, but the savings in feed and medicine can be substantial. Calculate the economics before you decide. Suppose you have a house of 100 square metres. At 12 birds per square metre, you place 1,200 birds. At 10 birds per square metre, you place 1,000 birds. If the 1,200-bird flock has 8 percent mortality and the 1,000-bird flock has 4 percent mortality, you sell 1,104 birds versus 960 birds. The higher density still sells more birds, but the feed conversion and average weight may be worse. If the high-density birds reach 2.0 kilograms and the low-density birds reach 2.3 kilograms, total meat sold is 2,208 kilograms versus 2,208 kilograms, exactly the same. But the low-density flock used less feed per kilogram of meat and less medication, so profit is higher. This is why density is a business decision, not just a welfare decision. Seasonal adjustment is essential. In the hot dry season, reduce density by 10 to 20 percent. In the cool rainy season, you can increase slightly if litter stays dry. In the harmattan or cold nights, watch for chilling in young chicks and adjust brooding density separately. Brooding density is different from grow-out density. For the first 7 days, use 30 to 40 chicks per square metre under the brooder. At 2 weeks, reduce to 20 to 25 per square metre. At 3 weeks, 15 to 18 per square metre. At 4 weeks and beyond, move to your final grow-out density. If you cannot move birds, start with a lower final density and use partial-house brooding, where you confine chicks to a smaller area with curtains or cardboard brooder guards. Record keeping is how you learn your own correct density. Keep a simple notebook or spreadsheet for each cycle: number of birds placed, floor area, daily mortality, average weight at 7, 14, 21, and 28 days, feed consumed, and litter condition score. After three or four cycles, you will know the density that gives you the best profit in your specific house and climate. Do not copy a neighbour blindly, because their house design, ventilation, and management may be different. Finally, remember that density interacts with bird size, not just bird number. As birds grow, they need more space. Some producers use a step-down approach, starting with more birds and selling some as smaller birds to reduce density for the remaining flock. This can work if you have a market for small birds, but it complicates biosecurity and feed management. For most African broiler producers, the safest and most profitable approach is to set a conservative final density, provide excellent ventilation and drinker space, and focus on getting every bird to market weight in the shortest time. Space is not wasted if it keeps birds healthy and growing. Space is wasted only when it sits empty because your flock died from heat stress or disease.

3. Ventilation and Cooling Systems for Hot Climates

Ventilation is the movement of air through the broiler house to remove heat, moisture, ammonia, and carbon dioxide, and to supply oxygen. In hot African climates, ventilation is not optional; it is the difference between a profitable flock and a catastrophic loss. Broilers begin to suffer heat stress at around 30 degrees Celsius, and mortality rises sharply above 35 degrees Celsius, especially when humidity is high. Because broilers have been selected for fast growth, they produce a lot of metabolic heat and have relatively small lungs and no sweat glands. They cool themselves by panting, which uses energy and reduces feed conversion. Your job is to help them lose heat without panting excessively. There are two main types of ventilation: natural and mechanical. Natural ventilation relies on wind and the buoyancy of hot air. Mechanical ventilation uses fans to move air. Many African producers use a combination of both. The first rule of natural ventilation is to open the house to the prevailing wind. In most of West Africa, the prevailing wind is from the southwest during the rainy season and from the northeast during the harmattan. In East Africa, the monsoon winds change direction seasonally. Observe your site for a full year if possible, and orient the house so that the long axis is perpendicular to the dominant wind. If you cannot observe for a year, ask local farmers and extension officers. The second rule is to make the open side area large enough. For a naturally ventilated house in a hot climate, the total open area on both sides should be at least 40 to 50 percent of the floor area, and up to 60 percent in very hot zones. This means that for a 10 metre by 30 metre house with a floor area of 300 square metres, you need at least 120 to 150 square metres of open side area. If the house is 2.5 metres high at the eaves, each side wall has 75 square metres of potential open area, so both sides together give 150 square metres. That is just enough. If you have solid walls or insect screens that block airflow, you need more. The third rule is to remove obstructions. Trees, tall grass, other buildings, and even stacked feed bags can block wind. Keep the area around the house clear to a distance of at least 10 metres. Inside the house, do not stack feed or equipment against the side walls. Keep the ridge vent open. A ridge vent of 30 to 45 centimetres, covered with a raised cap to keep out rain, allows hot air to escape. Without a ridge vent, hot air traps under the roof and radiates down onto the birds. The fourth rule is to use curtains correctly. Curtains are not just for rain. On hot days, open them fully on the downwind side and partially on the windward side to create a pressure difference that pulls air through. On cold nights, close them to keep chicks warm. On rainy days, close the windward side but leave the downwind side open for moisture removal. The goal is to control airflow, not to seal the house. Mechanical ventilation becomes necessary when natural ventilation cannot keep the house below 30 degrees Celsius, or when you want to increase stocking density. The simplest mechanical system is a set of exhaust fans on one end wall, with air inlets on the opposite end. This is called tunnel ventilation. Air enters through cooling pads or inlets, travels the length of the house at 2.0 to 2.5 metres per second, and exits through the fans. The moving air cools birds by convection and evaporation, even if the air temperature is not lowered. For tunnel ventilation to work, the house must be relatively airtight. If there are large openings on the side walls, the air will short-circuit and not reach the birds. You need to be able to close the side curtains completely. Cooling pads are the most effective way to reduce air temperature in dry climates. They work by passing air through a wet pad, where water evaporates and cools the air. In the Sahel, where relative humidity is low, a well-maintained pad can reduce air temperature by 8 to 12 degrees Celsius. In humid coastal areas, the cooling effect is much smaller, often only 2 to 4 degrees Celsius, because the air is already close to saturation. If you are in a humid zone, do not rely on cooling pads alone. Use high air speed, shade, and low stocking density instead. Foggers and sprinklers are alternative cooling methods. Foggers produce fine droplets that evaporate in the air. Sprinklers produce larger droplets that wet the birds and the floor. Sprinklers can be effective but they wet the litter, which leads to ammonia and disease. If you use sprinklers, use them only in the hottest part of the day, run them for short periods, and increase ventilation afterward to dry the litter. Never use sprinklers on young chicks. Roof cooling is another low-cost option. Painting the roof white reflects solar radiation. Sprinkling water on the roof in the afternoon can reduce inside temperature by several degrees, but it uses a lot of water and can cause rust. A double roof with an air gap is more sustainable. Insulation under the roof, such as foil or mineral wool, reduces radiant heat. Trees planted on the east and west sides of the house provide shade and cool the air through transpiration, but do not plant trees so close that they block wind or drop leaves on the roof. Monitor temperature and humidity daily. Use a simple maximum-minimum thermometer and a hygrometer. Record readings at 6 am, 12 noon, and 4 pm. If the afternoon temperature inside the house is more than 3 degrees Celsius above outside temperature, your ventilation is inadequate. If birds are panting heavily, spreading their wings, or crowding around drinkers, they are heat stressed. Act immediately: open curtains, turn on fans, check water flow, and consider reducing feed during the hottest hours. Feeding management interacts with ventilation. Digestion produces heat, so feeding during the cool parts of the day reduces heat stress. Many African producers now feed at 6 am and 6 pm, and remove or reduce feed between 11 am and 3 pm. This is called restricted feeding or midnight feeding, and it can reduce mortality significantly. Always provide plenty of clean, cool water. Water consumption doubles or triples in hot weather. If water is warm, birds drink less. Bury water lines or shade overhead tanks. Add electrolytes or vitamin C to water during heat waves, following veterinary advice. Finally, have a power backup plan. If you rely on fans and the electricity fails, you can lose a flock in less than an hour during a heat wave. A generator, solar panels with batteries, or a manual backup system is essential. Test your backup monthly. Keep spare fan belts and motors. Train your workers to recognize heat stress and to act without waiting for you. Ventilation is a daily discipline, not a one-time installation. Walk the house, feel the air, watch the birds, and adjust. Your birds will tell you if they are comfortable.

4. Litter Management and House Hygiene for Disease Prevention

Litter is the material on the floor of the broiler house that absorbs moisture, cushions birds, and captures manure. Good litter management is one of the cheapest and most effective disease prevention tools available to African broiler producers. Poor litter management leads to ammonia burns, respiratory disease, coccidiosis, footpad dermatitis, breast blisters, and high mortality. It also increases the cost of production because you have to buy medicine and you lose birds. The goal of litter management is to keep litter dry, loose, and free of mould and ammonia. Start with the right material. Good litter materials are absorbent, non-toxic, and locally available. Wood shavings are excellent if they are dry and free of treated timber. Rice hulls are common in West Africa and work well, but they can be dusty. Chopped maize cobs, groundnut shells, and chopped straw are also used. Avoid sawdust from treated wood, because it can contain chemicals that poison birds. Avoid wet or green materials, because they grow mould. Avoid sand or soil alone, because they do not absorb moisture well and can cause impaction if birds eat them. The ideal litter depth is 5 to 10 centimetres. Too little litter means moisture reaches the floor and creates wet spots. Too much litter makes it hard to manage and can hide wet areas. For brooding, use 5 to 7 centimetres. For grow-out, 7 to 10 centimetres is fine. If you use a concrete floor, you can use less litter because the floor is easy to dry. If you use an earth floor, use more litter and make sure the floor is raised and well drained. Before placing chicks, prepare the house properly. Remove all old litter and manure. This is not optional. Old litter carries coccidia oocysts, bacteria, viruses, and parasites. Scrape the floor, sweep the walls and ceiling, and wash with detergent. Then disinfect with a product effective against the diseases you have had, such as coccidiosis, Newcastle disease, and infectious bronchitis. Use a knapsack sprayer or a pressure washer. Pay attention to corners, cracks, and under the eaves. After disinfection, allow the house to dry completely. Then apply fresh litter. Do not mix old and new litter. During the cycle, manage moisture daily. The main sources of moisture are drinkers, leaking roofs, rain blowing in, and bird manure. Check drinkers every morning and afternoon. A single leaking drinker can wet a large area of litter and create a disease hotspot. Raise drinkers to the correct height so birds do not spill water. The base of the drinker should be at the level of the birds back. As birds grow, raise the drinkers. Use drinker cups or nipples to reduce spillage. If you use open troughs, do not overfill them. Fix roof leaks immediately. If rain blows in, close the curtains on the windward side and open the downwind side after the rain to dry the litter. Stir the litter. A simple garden fork or rake can be used to turn the litter and break up caked areas. Do this at least twice a week, and daily in wet areas. Stirring exposes wet litter to air and helps it dry. It also releases ammonia. If you stir litter, increase ventilation afterward to remove the ammonia. Ammonia is a gas produced when uric acid in manure breaks down. It is a major cause of respiratory disease and eye damage. You can smell ammonia at levels above 20 parts per million, which is already too high for birds. If your eyes water when you enter the house, ammonia is dangerously high. The best way to control ammonia is to keep litter dry and to ventilate well. Some producers use litter amendments such as alum, gypsum, or zeolite to reduce ammonia, but these are not always available or affordable in Africa. The cheapest control is dry litter and fresh air. Caked litter must be removed. If a patch of litter is wet and caked, remove it with a shovel and replace it with fresh dry litter. Do not just cover it with new litter, because the wet material underneath will continue to produce ammonia and bacteria. Remove caked litter at least once a week, and more often around drinkers. After removing caked litter, level the area and add fresh litter. At the end of the cycle, remove all litter and manure. This material is valuable as fertilizer for crops. Compost it properly before use, because fresh poultry manure can burn plants and carry pathogens. Composting also kills coccidia and bacteria through heat. Turn the compost pile every few weeks and keep it moist but not wet. After composting, it is safe to use on vegetables and grains. House hygiene goes beyond litter. Clean and disinfect feeders and drinkers between cycles. Feeders should be emptied, washed, and dried. Drinkers should be scrubbed to remove biofilm, which harbours bacteria. Use a brush and a disinfectant approved for poultry. Rinse well with clean water. Do not use the same equipment for different houses without cleaning, because this spreads disease. Control pests and rodents. Rats and mice eat feed, damage insulation, and carry disease. Keep feed in sealed containers. Remove spilled feed. Use traps or approved rodenticides, but be careful not to poison birds. Wild birds can carry avian influenza and other diseases, so keep them out with wire mesh and by covering feed. Flies breed in wet litter and manure. Control them by keeping litter dry and removing manure regularly. You can use fly traps or approved insecticides, but do not spray insecticides directly on birds or feed. Biosecurity is the set of practices that prevent disease from entering your farm. It includes a footbath at the entrance, clean boots and clothing for workers, no visitors without permission, and a quarantine area for new birds. If you have multiple houses, work from youngest to oldest. Do not move equipment between houses without cleaning and disinfecting. Keep a record of every person and vehicle that enters the farm. If you buy chicks from a hatchery, make sure the hatchery has good biosecurity. If you buy feed, store it in a dry, clean place and use it within the recommended time. Finally, monitor your birds daily. Healthy birds are active, eat well, and have bright eyes and clean feathers. Sick birds are dull, huddle, and may have watery droppings or respiratory sounds. If you see signs of disease, isolate the affected birds and call a veterinarian or extension officer. Do not wait. Early treatment is cheaper and more effective. Litter and hygiene are not glamorous, but they are the foundation of profitable broiler production. A clean, dry house with good litter will reduce your medicine costs, improve feed conversion, and increase your survival rate. In the African context, where veterinary services may be far away and medicine is expensive, prevention is truly better than cure.

5. Space Planning for Feeders, Drinkers, and Bird Movement

Space planning inside the broiler house is about more than floor area. It is about making sure every bird can reach feed and water without competing, and that you and your workers can move through the house to manage the flock. Poor layout leads to uneven growth, wet litter around drinkers, feed wastage, and difficulty in catching birds at market time. A well-planned house saves labour, reduces stress on birds, and increases your profit per square metre. Start with the placement of feeders and drinkers. The general rule is to alternate feeders and drinkers along the length of the house, so that birds do not have to travel far from feed to water. A common layout is a central line of feeders with drinkers on both sides, or two lines of feeders with a line of drinkers between them. The exact layout depends on house width. In a house 10 to 12 metres wide, you can have two or three feeding lines and two or three drinker lines. In a house wider than 12 metres, you need more lines to avoid birds walking too far. The maximum distance a bird should walk to feed or water is 3 to 4 metres. If the house is wider than 8 metres, you need a central access path or additional lines. Leave a clear walkway of at least 60 to 80 centimetres between lines so that you can move a wheelbarrow or a feed trolley. Do not block the walkway with feed bags or equipment. Feeders should be evenly spaced. For tube feeders, allow 5 to 7 centimetres of linear feeding space per bird for birds up to 2 kilograms, and 7 to 8 centimetres for heavier birds. If you use a 15-kilogram tube feeder with a pan diameter of 40 centimetres, the feeding circumference is about 125 centimetres. At 6 centimetres per bird, one feeder serves about 20 birds. So for 1,000 birds, you need about 50 tube feeders. That sounds like a lot, but it is necessary to prevent competition. If you use automatic pan feeders, the number depends on the pan size, but the same linear space rule applies. Drinkers should also be evenly spaced. For bell drinkers, one drinker per 80 to 100 birds is a common recommendation. For nipple drinkers, allow 10 to 12 birds per nipple. For trough drinkers, allow 1.5 to 2 centimetres of linear space per bird. In hot weather, increase the number of drinkers by 20 to 30 percent, because birds drink more and crowd around water. Place drinkers in the shade if possible, and away from direct sunlight. If drinkers are in the sun, the water heats up and birds drink less. Raise drinkers as birds grow. The base of the drinker should be at the level of the birds back. If drinkers are too low, birds spill water and wet the litter. If they are too high, small birds cannot reach them. Check the height every few days. The same applies to feeders. The feed pan should be at the level of the birds back, so they can eat comfortably without scratching feed onto the floor. Feed wastage is a major hidden cost. If feeders are too low or too full, birds scoop feed onto the litter. Never fill a tube feeder more than half full, because birds will bill out the feed. Use a feeder with a lip or grill to reduce spillage. If you see feed on the litter, adjust the feeder height or the feed level. Clean up spilled feed promptly to avoid attracting rodents and wild birds. Bird movement is another important consideration. Broilers are not very active, but they do need to move to feed and water. If the house is too cluttered, birds will not move enough, and their legs and breast muscles will be weak. This leads to more downgrades at processing. Keep the floor as open as possible. Do not place feeders and drinkers in corners where birds can get trapped. Leave space around the perimeter of the house so birds can circle. If you use brooder guards in the first week, remove them gradually so chicks can explore. At market time, you need to catch birds. A good layout makes catching easier. If feeders and drinkers are on lines, you can remove them or move them to one side, and use a catching frame or a corner net. Plan for a catching area near the door. Do not chase birds around the house, because it causes stress and bruising. Use dim lights and catch birds calmly. Some producers use a mobile catching pen that fits over the birds. This reduces labour and injury. Space planning also includes the area outside the house. You need a feed store that is dry, clean, and secure. Feed should be stored on pallets, not on the floor, and away from walls. You need a place to store litter, equipment, and medicine. You need a mortality pit or incinerator, located away from the house and away from water sources. You need a footbath at the entrance, and a handwashing station. If you have a generator, it should be in a separate, ventilated shed. If you have a water tank, it should be shaded and raised so that water flows by gravity or to a pump. A simple layout plan on paper before you build can save you a lot of money and frustration. Draw the house to scale, mark the doors, windows, and curtains, and then draw the feeder and drinker lines. Calculate how many feeders and drinkers you need, and how much space they take. Make sure there is still room for you to walk and for birds to move. If the plan looks crowded, reduce the number of birds or increase the house size. Remember that space planning is not just about the birds. It is about your workers. If workers cannot move easily, they will not clean properly, they will not check drinkers, and they will not notice sick birds. A well-planned house makes good management easier. A poorly planned house makes good management almost impossible. Finally, review your layout after every cycle. What worked? What caused problems? Did you have wet litter in a particular area? Did birds crowd in one corner? Did you waste feed? Adjust the layout for the next cycle. Small changes, such as moving a drinker line or adding a feeder, can make a big difference. In African broiler production, where margins are tight and every bird counts, good space planning is a competitive advantage. It costs little to do it right, but it costs a lot to do it wrong.

<h2>Practical Reference: Broiler House Ventilation and Cooling Systems in African Climates</h2>

<p>Ventilation is the single most important environmental control for broiler production in tropical and subtropical Africa. Get it wrong, and you will see reduced feed intake, poor feed conversion ratios (FCR), increased mortality, and higher energy bills. Get it right, and your birds will eat consistently, gain weight predictably, and deliver the margins your business plan depends on.</p>

<p>This practical reference lesson gives you the numbers, the equipment sizing, and the operating rules you need to ventilate and cool a broiler house under African conditions from the humid coast of Ghana to the semi-arid Sahel and the highveld of Southern Africa.</p>

<h3>Why Ventilation Drives Profitability</h3>

<p>Broilers perform best at an effective temperature of 1824C. Above 28C, birds reduce feed intake to limit metabolic heat production. Above 32C, mortality rises sharply and FCR deteriorates. In most African broiler zones, ambient temperatures regularly exceed 30C for several months of the year, and relative humidity can stay above 70% during the rainy season which makes evaporative cooling less effective and heat stress more dangerous.</p>

<p>The financial impact is direct. A broiler that eats less because of heat stress will not reach target market weight on schedule, which delays sales, increases feed cost per kilogram of gain, and ties up working capital in the house. Ventilation is therefore not a welfare cost it is a revenue protection measure.</p>

<h3>Ventilation System Types and When to Use Them</h3>

<p>Three ventilation approaches are used in African broiler production:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Natural ventilation:</strong> open-sided houses relying on wind and thermal buoyancy. Low capital cost, suitable for small flocks (under 500 birds) and moderate climates. Difficult to control in extreme heat or heavy rain.</li>
<li><strong>Cross ventilation:</strong> fans on one side wall, inlets on the opposite side. Air crosses the house width. Good for houses up to about 12 m wide. Common in West and East Africa.</li>
<li><strong>Tunnel ventilation:</strong> inlets at one end, exhaust fans at the other. Air travels the full house length at high velocity, creating a wind-chill effect on birds. The preferred system for large commercial houses (over 1,000 birds) in hot climates.</li>
</ul>

<p>For most commercial African broiler operations targeting 1,000+ birds per cycle, tunnel ventilation combined with evaporative cooling pads (where water quality and supply allow) delivers the best biological performance per unit of capital invested.</p>

<h3>Evaporative Cooling: What Works in African Humidity</h3>

<p>Evaporative cooling pads (usually cellulose or honeycomb) cool incoming air by 512C depending on relative humidity. The efficiency drops sharply as humidity rises. In coastal West Africa or the Congo Basin, where RH can exceed 80%, evaporative cooling may only deliver 35C of cooling often not enough on its own. In the Sahel, Southern African highveld, and East African highlands, where RH is lower, evaporative cooling is highly effective.</p>

<h3>Practical Sizing Rules for African Conditions</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Fan capacity:</strong> Install at least 1.2 the calculated tunnel ventilation requirement to allow for fan degradation, dust, and belt wear. In dusty African conditions, fans lose 1020% of rated capacity within a year if not maintained.</li>
<li><strong>Inlet area:</strong> Total inlet opening should be at least 1.5 the total fan area. Undersized inlets create negative pressure, reduce airflow, and increase energy cost per unit of air moved.</li>
<li><strong>House length:</strong> Tunnel ventilation works best in houses 60120 m long. Houses longer than 150 m suffer from uneven air distribution and temperature gradients.</li>
<li><strong>Insulation:</strong> A well-insulated roof (R-value equivalent of 2.5 or higher) can reduce heat load by 3040%. In African conditions, reflective roof coatings and ceiling insulation are among the highest-return investments you can make.</li>
<li><strong>Water supply:</strong> Evaporative cooling pads require 48 liters of water per bird per day in peak heat. Confirm your borehole or mains supply can deliver this before committing to the system.</li>
<li><strong>Power backup:</strong> In areas with unreliable grid power, a standby generator sized to run all fans and pumps is not optional it is essential. A single heat stress event during a power outage can kill hundreds of birds in under an hour.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Operational Rules for Hot Weather</h3>

<p>Ventilation equipment only delivers results if operated correctly. The following rules are drawn from Aviagen and university extension guidance adapted for African conditions:</p>

<ul>
<li>Start tunnel ventilation when house temperature exceeds 28C. Do not wait until birds are panting.</li>
<li>Increase air speed gradually as birds age and as temperature rises. Target 1.0 m/s at 3 weeks, 2.53.0 m/s at 56 weeks.</li>
<li>Monitor birds, not just thermometers. Panting, wing spreading, and reduced feed intake are early signs that ventilation is insufficient.</li>
<li>Run fans continuously during the hottest part of the day (11:0016:00). Intermittent operation saves power but risks heat buildup.</li>
<li>Check inlet openings daily. Dust, cobwebs, and vegetation can reduce effective inlet area by 2030%.</li>
<li>Clean fan blades and shutters weekly. Dust buildup on blades reduces airflow and increases power draw.</li>
<li>Record temperature, humidity, and mortality daily. Patterns will tell you whether your ventilation strategy is working before your profit and loss statement does.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:12px 16px; margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Heat Stress and Power Failure</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Heat stress can kill broilers within 3060 minutes when house temperature exceeds 35C and relative humidity is above 70%. If your ventilation system fails through power outage, fan breakdown, or blocked inlets you must act immediately. Open all side curtains and doors, spray water on the roof and floor (not directly on birds in high humidity), and move birds if possible. Never leave a broiler house unattended during hot weather without a working alarm or a person on call. In high-mortality events, dead birds must be disposed of immediately by approved methods (incineration, deep burial, or rendering) to prevent disease spread and comply with local environmental regulations. Do not sell or consume birds that died from heat stress.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li>Calculate tunnel ventilation requirement using the highest expected summer temperature and the heaviest bird weight not average conditions.</li>
<li>Install fans rated at least 1.2 the calculated requirement to allow for dust, wear, and degradation.</li>
<li>Ensure total inlet area is at least 1.5 total fan area; check and clean inlets daily.</li>
<li>Insulate the roof and use reflective coatings this reduces heat load by 3040% at low cost.</li>
<li>Confirm water supply can deliver 48 liters per bird per day if using evaporative cooling pads.</li>
<li>Install a standby generator sized to run all fans and pumps; test it weekly.</li>
<li>Start tunnel ventilation at 28C, not at 32C; increase air speed as birds age.</li>
<li>Record temperature, humidity, and mortality daily; investigate any sudden change immediately.</li>
</ol>
