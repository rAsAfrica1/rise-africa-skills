1. Reproductive Biology of Farmed Fish

To breed fish successfully, you must understand their natural reproductive cycle. Most farmed fish in Africa, like tilapia and catfish, have distinct breeding seasons tied to rainfall and temperature. Tilapia breed year-round in warm water (above 22C), while catfish like Clarias gariepinus spawn at the start of the rainy season when water levels rise. In captivity, you can manipulate these cues by controlling water temperature and photoperiod. For example, keeping broodstock at 26-28C and providing 12-14 hours of light can induce spawning in tilapia. Catfish need a sudden temperature drop and water level increase to trigger spawning. Understanding these triggers is key to planning hatchery operations.

Sexual maturity varies by species. Nile tilapia mature at 3-4 months (females) and 5-6 months (males) in good conditions. African catfish mature at 6-8 months. Broodstock should be at least 1 year old for reliable spawning. Males are typically larger and have distinct genital papillae; females have a swollen, reddish vent when ready to spawn. In catfish, males have a elongated genital papilla, while females have a round, swollen vent. Always check these signs before selecting breeders.

Fish are external fertilizers. Females release eggs into the water, and males release milt (sperm) to fertilize them. Tilapia are mouthbrooders: females carry fertilized eggs in their mouths until they hatch. Catfish are nest builders; they lay eggs on substrates like roots or mats. In hatcheries, you simulate these conditions. For tilapia, you can collect eggs from the female's mouth after spawning. For catfish, you provide spawning mats or tanks where eggs attach. Knowing the mode of spawning helps you decide whether to use natural spawning or induced spawning techniques.

Hormones control reproduction. The brain produces gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), which triggers the pituitary to release gonadotropins, stimulating the gonads to produce eggs and sperm. Environmental cues like temperature and rainfall activate this cascade. In captivity, stress, poor nutrition, or overcrowding can block hormone release, leading to poor spawning. That's why broodstock management is critical. By understanding this biology, you can intervene effectivelyusing hormones for induced spawning or adjusting conditions to encourage natural spawning. This knowledge forms the foundation for all hatchery operations.

2. Selecting Broodstock

Selecting the right broodstock is the first step to a successful hatchery. Broodstock are the mature male and female fish you use for breeding. You need fish that are healthy, fast-growing, and free from deformities. Start by choosing a reputable supplier or from your own best-performing ponds. Never use stunted or sick fish. For tilapia, select fish that have reached at least 150-200 grams for females and 200-250 grams for males. For catfish, females should be 500-1000 grams and males 700-1500 grams. These sizes indicate sexual maturity and good egg/sperm quality.

Sexing fish correctly is crucial. In tilapia, males have two openings (genital papilla and anus) while females have three (genital papilla, anus, and urinogenital opening). The male's genital papilla is pointed and small; the female's is larger and rounded. In catfish, males have a distinct, elongated genital papilla that is pointed, while females have a round, swollen, and reddish vent. When females are ready to spawn, their abdomen is soft and swollen, and the vent is red and protruding. Males release milt when gently pressed on the abdomen. Always handle fish with wet hands to avoid damaging their slime coat.

Condition your broodstock for at least 2-4 weeks before spawning. Feed them a high-quality diet with 35-40% protein, including fishmeal and vitamins. For tilapia, feed 2-3% of body weight daily; for catfish, feed 3-5% daily. Keep them in clean, well-aerated water at 26-28C for tilapia and 25-28C for catfish. Stock at low densities: 2-3 fish per square meter for tilapia, 1-2 fish per square meter for catfish. This reduces stress and allows natural behaviors. Add hiding places like pipes or plants for catfish. Monitor water quality daily; ammonia and nitrite should be near zero.

Record keeping is essential. Tag or mark your broodstock to track their performance. Note spawning dates, egg numbers, hatch rates, and offspring quality. Replace broodstock every 6-12 months to avoid inbreeding depression. Inbreeding leads to slow growth and deformities. Always use unrelated males and females. If you buy from a hatchery, ask for their breeding records. A good practice is to maintain separate lines and rotate males between tanks. By selecting and conditioning broodstock properly, you set the stage for high egg production and healthy fry.

3. Induced Spawning Techniques

Induced spawning is used when fish don't spawn naturally in captivity. It involves injecting hormones to stimulate egg and sperm release. This is common for African catfish (Clarias gariepinus) and sometimes tilapia. The most widely used hormone is Ovaprim, a synthetic salmon gonadotropin-releasing hormone analog. Other options include human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) and pituitary gland extract from carp or catfish. Ovaprim is preferred because it's reliable and easy to use. Store it in a refrigerator at 4C; never freeze it.

For catfish, select females with a swollen, reddish vent and soft abdomen. Males are selected if milt flows when pressed. Inject females with Ovaprim at 0.5 ml per kg body weight. Males receive half that dose. Use a 1 ml syringe with a 0.5-inch needle. Inject intramuscularly, preferably in the dorsal muscle behind the dorsal fin, at a 45-degree angle. After injection, place fish in separate holding tanks with clean, aerated water at 25-28C. Spawning occurs 10-12 hours after injection. For tilapia, induced spawning is less common but can be done with HCG at 1000-2000 IU per kg for females. However, tilapia often spawn naturally if conditioned well.

After injection, monitor fish closely. Females will become restless and may release eggs. For catfish, you must strip the eggs manually. Hold the female gently with a wet towel, wipe the vent dry, and press the abdomen from front to back to release eggs into a dry bowl. Avoid squeezing too hard. Immediately add milt from the male: sacrifice the male, remove testes, macerate them in a small amount of saline, and pour over eggs. Mix gently with a feather or spoon for 1-2 minutes. Then add water to activate sperm. Fertilization occurs within 30 seconds. Rinse eggs and transfer to incubation tanks.

For tilapia, if induced, females may spawn naturally after injection. Provide spawning substrates like flat stones or tiles. After spawning, collect eggs from the female's mouth or from the substrate. Incubate eggs in clean water with gentle aeration. Hatching occurs in 3-5 days for tilapia, 24-36 hours for catfish. Always maintain water quality: temperature 26-28C, dissolved oxygen above 5 mg/L, and pH 6.5-8.0. Disinfect eggs with formalin (1 ml per 10 L) for 10 minutes to prevent fungus. Induced spawning requires precision and hygiene, but it gives you control over timing and genetics.

4. Egg Incubation and Larval Rearing

After fertilization, eggs must be incubated in clean, well-oxygenated water. Incubation systems vary by species. For catfish, use flow-through tanks or troughs with gentle water flow. Eggs are sticky and attach to substrates; you can use mesh screens or spawning mats. For tilapia, eggs are mouthbrooded, so you can incubate them in artificial mouthbrooding jars or upwelling incubators. A simple setup is a 20-liter bucket with aeration and a mesh bottom to hold eggs. Water temperature should be 26-28C for both species. At this temperature, catfish eggs hatch in 24-36 hours, tilapia in 3-5 days.

Water quality is critical. Keep dissolved oxygen above 5 mg/L, ammonia below 0.1 mg/L, and pH between 6.5 and 8.0. Use airstones for gentle aeration; strong currents can damage eggs. Change 20-30% of water daily, but ensure new water is the same temperature to avoid shock. Disinfect eggs with formalin (1 ml per 10 L) for 10 minutes daily to prevent fungal infections. Remove dead eggs (they turn white) with a pipette to prevent fungus spread. Hatching rate should be above 70% with good management.

Newly hatched larvae (called sac fry) live on their yolk sac for 2-3 days. Do not feed them during this time. They are fragile and need gentle water flow. After the yolk sac is absorbed, they become free-swimming fry and must be fed. Start with live food: rotifers, brine shrimp nauplii, or microalgae. For catfish, feed newly hatched Artemia every 4 hours. For tilapia, feed finely ground fish meal or commercial fry feed (40-50% protein) mixed with water to form a paste. Feed 5-10% of body weight daily, split into 4-6 feedings. Overfeeding fouls water; underfeeding stunts growth.

Larval rearing tanks should be indoors or covered to control temperature and prevent predators. Use 100-200 liter tanks at a density of 50-100 larvae per liter. Provide gentle aeration and keep water clean. After 7-10 days, fry can be transferred to nursery ponds. Always acclimatize fry to new water conditions by floating bags for 15-20 minutes. Monitor growth: catfish fry reach 1 gram in 2-3 weeks; tilapia in 3-4 weeks. Record daily mortality and adjust feeding. With proper care, survival from egg to fry can be 50-70%.

5. Nursery Pond Management

A nursery pond is where you raise fry to fingerling stage (2-5 grams). Proper management ensures high survival and healthy growth. Prepare the pond 7-10 days before stocking. Drain and dry the pond for 3-5 days to kill pathogens and predators. If you can't drain, apply lime at 200-500 kg per hectare to disinfect. Fill with clean water through a fine mesh filter (0.5 mm) to keep out wild fish and insects. Fertilize the pond to grow natural food: apply chicken manure at 500-1000 kg per hectare or NPK fertilizer at 100-200 kg per hectare. This promotes plankton, which fry eat. Wait until the water turns green (Secchi disk visibility 30-40 cm) before stocking.

Stock fry at 50-100 per square meter (500,000-1,000,000 per hectare). Higher densities require more feeding and aeration. Acclimatize fry by floating bags for 15-20 minutes, then release gently into the pond. Provide supplemental feed from day one: finely ground fish meal, rice bran, or commercial fry feed (40-45% protein). Feed 10-15% of body weight daily, divided into 3-4 feedings. Scatter feed evenly around the pond. After 2 weeks, reduce to 5-10% as natural food increases. Monitor growth weekly by sampling 20-30 fry with a fine net. If growth is slow, increase feeding or check water quality.

Water quality management is crucial. Test daily for dissolved oxygen (keep above 4 mg/L), pH (6.5-8.5), and temperature (26-30C). If oxygen drops, add fresh water or aerate. Avoid over-fertilization; excess nutrients cause algal blooms that die and consume oxygen. Manage predators: dragonfly larvae, water beetles, and frogs eat fry. Use fine mesh screens on inlets and outlets. Bird predation can be reduced with netting or scarers. Diseases like columnaris and saprolegniasis can occur; treat with salt (3-5 g per liter) or formalin. Always quarantine new fish before adding to nursery ponds.

After 3-4 weeks, fry become fingerlings (2-5 grams). At this stage, they can be harvested and transferred to grow-out ponds. Harvest in the cool morning using a fine seine net. Handle gently to avoid stress. Grade fingerlings by size to reduce cannibalism and competition. Stock grow-out ponds at 3-5 fish per square meter for tilapia, 5-10 for catfish. Record survival rate: aim for 60-80% from fry to fingerling. If survival is low, review pond preparation, feeding, and water quality. A well-managed nursery pond is the backbone of a profitable fish farm.

6. Fry and Fingerling Production

Fry and fingerling production is the business of selling young fish to other farmers. To succeed, you need a steady supply of high-quality fry and fingerlings. Start by planning your production cycle. A single female catfish can produce 10,000-50,000 eggs; tilapia can produce 200-1,000 eggs per spawn. With good management, you can produce 5,000-20,000 fingerlings per month from a small hatchery. Set up multiple tanks to stagger production. Keep records of spawning dates, hatch rates, and sales.

Quality is your selling point. Buyers want fast-growing, disease-free fingerlings. Select broodstock from fast-growing families. Avoid inbreeding by rotating males every 6 months. Condition broodstock on a high-protein diet (40% protein) with added vitamins C and E. For catfish, use Ovaprim for induced spawning; for tilapia, use natural spawning in hapas (fine mesh cages) or tanks. Collect eggs, incubate, and rear larvae as described in earlier lessons. Grade fingerlings by size: small (1-2 g), medium (2-3 g), large (3-5 g). Sell them in oxygen-filled plastic bags (1/3 water, 2/3 oxygen). For short trips, pack 100-200 fingerlings per 10-liter bag.

Marketing your fingerlings requires networking. Contact local fish farmers, extension officers, and NGOs. Attend agricultural shows. Set a competitive price: in many African countries, tilapia fingerlings sell for $0.10-0.30 each, catfish for $0.20-0.50. Offer bulk discounts. Provide after-sales advice on pond preparation and feeding. Build trust by guaranteeing live arrival. If you deliver, use insulated boxes with ice packs in hot weather. Keep bags out of direct sunlight. For long-distance transport, use oxygen cylinders to refill bags.

Biosecurity is essential. Diseased fingerlings can wipe out a buyer's pond and ruin your reputation. Quarantine new broodstock for 2 weeks. Disinfect tanks and nets with chlorine (10 mg/L) between batches. Never mix fish from different tanks without testing. Vaccinate if possible (e.g., against Streptococcus in tilapia). Keep a health record for each batch. If disease occurs, cull and disinfect. Finally, consider value addition: train buyers on best practices, or offer starter packs with feed and instructions. A reliable fingerling producer can earn a steady income and support the growth of fish farming in Africa.

<h2>Practical Reference: Breeding and Hatchery Management for African Fish Farms</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the operational knowledge a farm manager or hatchery technician needs to move from broodstock selection through to saleable fingerlings. It is written for the African context where species choice, water temperature, feed availability, and market demand vary enormously between the Niger Delta, the Lake Victoria basin, the Volta system, and the Zambezi floodplains. The commercial logic is simple: a hatchery that consistently produces healthy fry and fingerlings at the right time captures the highest margin in the value chain, because every grow-out farmer within 200 km is a potential repeat buyer.</p>

<h3>1. Reproductive Biology of Farmed Fish What Determines Your Production Calendar</h3>
<p>Farmed fish in Africa fall into two broad reproductive categories: those that spawn naturally in ponds (tilapias, catfish under some conditions, and some cyprinids) and those that require hormonal induction (African catfish <em>Clarias gariepinus</em>, <em>Heterobranchus</em> species, and most cultured carp). Understanding the reproductive cycle of your chosen species determines when you can produce fry, how many broodstock you must hold, and what conditioning costs you will carry.</p>
<p>Tilapia (<em>Oreochromis niloticus</em>, <em>O. mossambicus</em>) are mouthbrooders. Females incubate eggs and larvae in the buccal cavity for 1828 days depending on temperature. This makes mass fry collection unpredictable unless you use hapa-based or tank-based systems with controlled sex ratios. African catfish are batch spawners with a distinct annual cycle; females reach peak gonadosomatic index (GSI) at the onset of the rains, and males produce milt reliably year-round under good nutrition. The practical implication: catfish hatcheries in West Africa typically run two peak production windows aligned with the rainy season, while tilapia hatcheries with temperature control can produce year-round.</p>
<p>Key biological parameters to record for every broodstock batch: GSI, oocyte diameter (for catfish, >1.0 mm indicates readiness), fecundity (eggs per kg body weight), and latency period after hormone injection. These data, kept over seasons, become the most valuable asset your hatchery owns they allow you to forecast fry output and commit to buyer contracts.</p>

<h3>2. Selecting Broodstock The Highest-Leverage Decision</h3>
<p>Broodstock selection determines growth rate, feed conversion, disease resistance, and market acceptance for the next generation. In Africa, where many farms rely on wild-caught or informally traded broodstock, the risk of inbreeding depression and poor performance is high. A disciplined selection protocol pays back within one production cycle.</p>
<p>Select from known-performance lines where possible. For <em>O. niloticus</em>, the GIFT (Genetically Improved Farmed Tilapia) and related African strains available through national research institutes offer documented growth advantages. For <em>Clarias gariepinus</em>, the Dutch and Nigerian strains are widely available; avoid mixing without records. Target broodstock age: tilapia 612 months, catfish 1018 months. Weight: tilapia 150300 g, catfish 8001,500 g for females, 5001,000 g for males.</p>

<h3>3. Induced Spawning Techniques The Core Hatchery Operation</h3>
<p>For African catfish, induced spawning is the standard method. The protocol below is the one most widely used in African hatcheries and is adapted from the Nigerian Institute for Oceanography and Marine Research (NIOMR) and the FAO manual on catfish seed production.</p>
<p>Female catfish are injected intramuscularly with a single dose of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) or a combination of hCG and pituitary extract. Typical dose: 1,5002,500 IU hCG per kg body weight for <em>C. gariepinus</em>. Latency period: 1014 hours at 2628C. Males are usually not injected; milt is obtained by dissection of the testes or by stripping after a mild dose. Eggs are stripped into a dry plastic bowl, milt is added, and the mixture is gently stirred with a feather or plastic spoon for 6090 seconds. Water is then added to activate the sperm, and the fertilised eggs are spread thinly on incubation substrates.</p>
<p>For tilapia, induced spawning is less common but used in intensive systems. Hormonal induction with hCG or GnRH analogues can synchronise spawning, but the mouthbrooding behaviour complicates egg collection. Most African tilapia hatcheries rely on hapa-in-pond or tank-based natural spawning with controlled sex ratios and egg collection from female mouths at 35 day intervals.</p>

<h3>4. Egg Incubation and Larval Rearing Where Losses Happen</h3>
<p>Catfish eggs hatch in 2448 hours at 2629C. Incubation systems in African hatcheries range from simple flow-through troughs to McDonald jars and Zug jars. The critical variables are water flow (gentle but continuous), dissolved oxygen (>5 mg/L), and temperature stability. Eggs are adhesive in <em>Clarias</em>; they must be spread thinly to prevent fungal infection. Formalin treatment at 100200 ppm for 15 minutes daily is a common prophylactic where Saprolegnia is a problem.</p>
<p>After hatch, larvae absorb the yolk sac for 23 days. First feeding begins when the mouth opens and the gut is fully formed typically 4872 hours post-hatch at 27C. First feed: live <em>Artemia</em> nauplii, rotifers, or finely ground fish meal suspended in water. In African hatcheries where <em>Artemia</em> cysts are expensive, many operators use decapsulated cysts, Moina, or a finely sieved 40% CP starter feed. Larval rearing density: 50100 larvae per litre in flow-through systems, lower in static systems.</p>

Always wear nitrile gloves and eye protection when handling. Never prepare hormone solutions near food or drinking water. Dispose of injection waste and hormone-contaminated water according to national regulations in many African countries, discharge of hormone-laden water into natural water bodies is prohibited. <h3>5. Nursery Pond Management From Fry to Fingerling</h3>
<p>Nursery ponds are the bridge between the hatchery and grow-out. In Africa, most nurseries are earthen ponds of 100500 m, though concrete tanks and hapas are increasingly used. The key management tasks are: pond preparation (drying, liming, filling, fertilising), predator control, water quality monitoring, and graded feeding.</p>
<p>Prepare nursery ponds 710 days before stocking. Apply agricultural lime at 200500 kg/ha if pH is below 6.5. Fill with filtered water (250400 micron mesh) to exclude predatory fish and insects. Fertilise with poultry manure (5001,000 kg/ha) or NPK (20:20:20 at 50100 kg/ha) to stimulate zooplankton. Stock fry at 50100 per m for catfish, 2030 per m for tilapia. Feed with 4045% CP starter crumble, 46 times daily for the first two weeks, reducing to 3 times daily thereafter. Target: 510 g fingerlings in 46 weeks.</p>
<p>Water quality targets in nursery ponds: dissolved oxygen >4 mg/L, pH 6.58.5, ammonia <0.5 mg/L, temperature 2630C. Exchange 1020% of water weekly, or more if algal blooms collapse. Always keep a backup aeration source a power failure in a nursery pond can kill an entire batch within hours.</p>

<h3>6. Fry and Fingerling Production The Business Case</h3>
<p>Fingerling production is the most profitable segment of the African aquaculture value chain when managed well. A well-run catfish hatchery in Nigeria can produce 50,000100,000 fingerlings per month from a modest facility, selling at 3080 each (2023 prices) depending on size and season. Tilapia fingerlings sell for 2050 each. The cost of production per fingerling is typically 4060% of the sale price, giving gross margins of 4060% far higher than grow-out farming in most African markets.</p>
<p>To capture this margin, focus on three things: (1) reliability of supply buyers will pay a premium for consistent weekly availability; (2) size grading uniform fingerlings command higher prices and reduce cannibalism; (3) health certification a simple health record and a reputation for disease-free stock opens institutional buyers (government programmes, NGOs, commercial farms).</p>
<p>Market channels to develop: direct sales to grow-out farms, contracts with government restocking programmes, sales to NGOs and development projects, and online marketplaces (e.g., agro-dealer networks in Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana). The most successful hatcheries in Africa combine hatchery production with a small grow-out demonstration pond it builds buyer confidence and provides a fallback revenue stream.</p>
