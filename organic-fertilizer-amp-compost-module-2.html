1. What is Compost and Why It Matters

Compost is decomposed organic matterâa dark, crumbly, earthy-smelling material that looks like rich soil. It is made from everyday waste: vegetable peels, crop residues, animal manure, dry leaves, and more. When these materials break down, they become 'black gold'âa natural fertilizer that feeds your soil and crops. Compost is not just waste; it is a valuable resource that can save you money and improve your farm.

Why does compost matter? In many African soils, continuous farming without adding organic matter leads to poor fertility, erosion, and low yields. Chemical fertilizers can boost growth quickly but are expensive and can degrade soil over time. Compost, on the other hand, builds soil health slowly but surely. It adds nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, improves soil structure, helps soil hold water, and encourages beneficial microbes. Healthy soil grows healthy plants that resist pests and diseases.

Making compost also reduces waste. Instead of throwing away kitchen scraps or burning crop residues, you turn them into free fertilizer. This saves money and reduces pollution. In cities, organic waste often clogs drains and causes smells; composting turns that problem into a solution. For smallholder farmers, compost can increase yields by 20-50% when used regularly, according to studies in Kenya and Nigeria.

Getting started is simple. You need a mix of 'greens' (nitrogen-rich materials like fresh grass, kitchen scraps, manure) and 'browns' (carbon-rich materials like dry leaves, straw, cardboard). Keep the pile as moist as a wrung-out sponge and turn it regularly. In 4-8 weeks, you'll have compost ready to use. This module will guide you step by step. By the end, you'll be able to turn your waste into black gold and grow healthier crops with less cost.

2. The Four Ingredients: Greens, Browns, Water, Air

Composting is like cooking: you need the right ingredients in the right amounts. The four essential ingredients are greens, browns, water, and air. Greens are nitrogen-rich materials that provide protein for the microbes that break down the compost. Examples include kitchen scraps (fruit and vegetable peels, coffee grounds, tea leaves), fresh grass clippings, animal manure (cow, goat, chicken, but not dog or cat), and green plant trimmings. Browns are carbon-rich materials that provide energy for microbes. Examples include dry leaves, straw, hay, cardboard, newspaper, sawdust, and wood ash (in small amounts).

The ideal ratio is about 2 to 3 parts browns to 1 part greens by volume. If you have too many greens, the pile becomes slimy and smelly. Too many browns, and it breaks down very slowly. A simple way to remember: if it's wet and green, it's a green; if it's dry and brown, it's a brown. For every bucket of kitchen scraps, add two buckets of dry leaves or straw.

Water is the third ingredient. The pile should be as moist as a wrung-out spongeânot dripping wet, not bone dry. In dry climates, you may need to water the pile every few days. In rainy season, you might need to cover it to prevent it from getting too wet. Air is the fourth ingredient. Composting microbes need oxygen to work efficiently. Without air, the pile becomes anaerobic, producing bad smells and slow decomposition. Turning the pile introduces air. You can also add coarse materials like small sticks or maize cobs to create air pockets.

To assemble: start with a layer of browns about 15 cm deep, then add a layer of greens, then a thin layer of soil or old compost (to introduce microbes). Repeat these layers until the pile is about 1 meter high. Water each layer lightly. Your pile will heat up within a few days as microbes get to work. If it doesn't heat up, check your ratio: add more greens if it's too dry, or more browns if it's too wet. With the right balance, you'll have compost in 4-8 weeks.

3. Choosing a Compost Site: Shade, Drainage, and Access

Where you put your compost pile matters. A good site will make composting easier and faster. The three key factors are shade, drainage, and access. First, shade. In hot African climates, direct sunlight can dry out your pile quickly, slowing decomposition. Choose a spot that gets shade for most of the day, like under a tree or on the shady side of a building. If you can't find shade, you can build a simple roof over the pile using palm fronds or corrugated iron. But avoid deep shade where the pile stays too wet and coldâpartial shade is best.

Second, drainage. The site should not flood during heavy rains. If water collects around the pile, it will become waterlogged and smelly. Choose a spot on slightly higher ground, or dig a shallow trench around the pile to divert water. If your soil is clay and drains poorly, put a layer of gravel or small stones at the base before building the pile. Also, avoid placing the pile at the bottom of a slope where water runs off.

Third, access. You need to be able to add materials, turn the pile, and remove finished compost easily. Place the pile close to your kitchen or garden, but not too close to your house to avoid smells or pests. A distance of 5-10 meters from the house is good. Make sure the path is clear so you can carry a bucket of scraps or a wheelbarrow of leaves without difficulty. If you have a large farm, you might want multiple piles in different locations.

Other considerations: Keep the pile away from young trees or buildingsâthe roots of trees may grow into the pile, and the moisture could damage building foundations. Also, avoid areas where pets or livestock can disturb the pile. If you live in a town, check local rules about waste management. A well-chosen site will make your composting experience smooth and productive. Take a few minutes to pick the right spot before you start building.

4. Building a Simple Compost Pile with Local Materials

You don't need expensive bins or tools to make compost. You can build a simple pile using materials you already have. The method below is for a heap that is about 1 meter wide, 1 meter long, and 1 meter highâbig enough to hold heat but small enough to turn. Start by clearing the site of grass and weeds. If the ground is very dry, water it lightly. Then lay down a base layer of coarse browns like maize stalks, small sticks, or palm fronds, about 15 cm deep. This helps air circulate from the bottom.

Next, add a layer of greens, about 10-15 cm deep. This could be kitchen scraps, fresh grass, or manure. Then add a thin layer of soil or old compost, about 2 cm, to introduce microbes. Repeat these layers: browns, greens, soil, until the pile is about 1 meter high. As you build, water each layer lightly so the whole pile is as moist as a wrung-out sponge. If you are using dry leaves, crush them or chop them into smaller pieces to speed up decomposition. For kitchen scraps, bury them in the center of the pile to avoid attracting flies.

To contain the pile, you can use locally available materials. In many parts of Africa, farmers use wooden pallets, woven sticks, or wire mesh to form a bin. You can also use old sacks filled with soil to make a circular ring. If you don't have any of these, a free-standing pile works fine, but it may spread out. A simple bin made from four wooden pallets tied at the corners is easy to build and allows good airflow. Another option is to dig a shallow pit (30 cm deep) and fill itâthis conserves moisture in dry areas.

Once built, cover the pile with a layer of browns (like dry grass or leaves) to insulate it and reduce smells. You can also cover it with a tarp or old sacks to keep rain out, but remove the cover on sunny days to let air in. Within 2-3 days, the pile should heat up. If it doesn't, add more greens or water. Turn the pile every 1-2 weeks to mix materials and add air. After 4-8 weeks, the compost will be dark, crumbly, and ready to use. This simple method turns waste into free fertilizer for your garden or farm.

5. How to Turn and Monitor Your Pile

Turning and monitoring your compost pile are the keys to fast, odor-free decomposition. Turning means mixing the pile to introduce air and redistribute moisture and microbes. Without turning, the pile may become anaerobic (smelly and slow). How often should you turn? In the first two weeks, turn the pile every 2-3 days. After that, once a week is enough. If you want compost faster, turn every 2 days. The best time to turn is in the morning when it's cool. Use a pitchfork or a strong stick. If you don't have a fork, you can use a shovel or even your hands (with gloves).

To turn, start at one side of the pile and pull materials to a new spot next to it. Mix the outside materials into the center and the center to the outside. The center is where decomposition is most active, so mixing it outward spreads microbes. Break up any clumps. If the pile seems dry, add water as you turn. If it's too wet, add dry browns like leaves or straw. After turning, reshape the pile into a dome. A well-turned pile will heat up again within a day.

Monitoring your pile means checking temperature, moisture, and smell. Temperature: stick a metal rod or a long stick into the center of the pile, leave it for 10 minutes, then pull it out. If it's too hot to touch, the pile is active. Ideally, the center should be between 55-65Â°C (130-150Â°F). If it's below 40Â°C (104Â°F), decomposition is slowâadd greens or turn more often. If it's above 70Â°C (158Â°F), the pile may be too dry or have too many greens; turn it and add browns. Moisture: squeeze a handful of compost. It should feel like a wrung-out sponge. If it drips, it's too wet; if it's dusty, it's too dry. Smell: a healthy pile smells earthy, like forest floor. If it smells like rotten eggs or ammonia, it's too wet or has too many greensâturn and add browns.

Keep a simple record: date you built the pile, dates you turned it, and any additions. After 4-8 weeks, the pile will stop heating up and the compost will be ready. It should be dark, crumbly, and smell sweet. If you see large pieces of undecomposed material, sift them out and add them to the next pile. Regular turning and monitoring will give you black gold in less time.

6. Troubleshooting Common Composting Problems

Even with the best efforts, composting can run into problems. Here are common issues and how to fix them. Problem 1: The pile smells bad (rotten, ammonia, or sulfur). Cause: Too wet, too many greens, or not enough air. Solution: Turn the pile to add air. Add dry browns like leaves, straw, or cardboard. If it's very wet, leave the cover off for a day. Problem 2: The pile is not heating up. Cause: Too dry, too many browns, or too small. Solution: Add water until it's as moist as a wrung-out sponge. Add greens like manure or fresh grass. Make the pile biggerâat least 1 cubic meter. Problem 3: The pile is attracting flies or rodents. Cause: Food scraps exposed, or meat/dairy in the pile. Solution: Bury food scraps in the center of the pile. Don't add meat, fish, dairy, or oily foods. Keep the pile covered with browns. Problem 4: The pile is too dry and dusty. Cause: Lack of water or too many dry browns. Solution: Water the pile while turning. Add greens like kitchen scraps or fresh grass. Cover the pile to retain moisture. Problem 5: The pile is too wet and soggy. Cause: Too much rain, too many greens, or poor drainage. Solution: Turn the pile and add dry browns. Cover the pile during heavy rains. Create a trench around the pile to divert water. Problem 6: The compost is ready but has large undecomposed pieces. Cause: Materials too large or not turned enough. Solution: Sift the compost through a mesh or wire screen. Put large pieces back into the next pile. Chop materials smaller next time. Problem 7: The pile has white or gray mold. Cause: This is usually harmless fungi breaking down materials. Solution: No action needed; it's part of decomposition. If it's black and slimy, it's too wetâturn and add browns. Problem 8: The pile is cold and dry in hot weather. Cause: Sun exposure or lack of water. Solution: Move pile to shade or cover with a tarp. Water regularly. Remember, most problems are fixed by adjusting the balance of greens, browns, water, and air. Keep a fork or stick nearby for turning. With practice, you'll learn to read your pile and make quick fixes. Composting is forgivingâeven if you make mistakes, you'll still get compost, just maybe slower.

3. Turning, Monitoring, and Troubleshooting

Turn the pile every 5â7 days for the first three weeks, then every 10â14 days. Turning aerates the pile, mixes outer material into the hot core, and speeds decomposition. If you cannot turn frequently, use passive aeration with perforated pipes â decomposition will be slower but still effective.

Common problems and fixes:

  • Bad smell (rotten eggs): too wet or compacted. Turn and add dry browns.
  • Ammonia smell: too much nitrogen. Add browns and turn.
  • Pile not heating: too dry, too small, or lack of greens. Add water, greens, and increase pile size.
  • Flies and rodents: kitchen scraps exposed. Bury scraps in the centre and cover the pile.
  • Slow decomposition: materials too large. Chop to 5â10 cm and maintain moisture.
  • White mould: normal fungal activity; no action needed.
