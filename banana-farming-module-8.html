Lessons

1. Desuckering and Sucker Management

Desuckering is removing unwanted suckers from the banana mat so the mother plant and one or two followers get all the water and nutrients. Suckers are shoots that grow from the underground corm. If you leave too many, bunches become small and the plantation yields poorly. The best tool is a sharp, clean machete or a desuckering knife. Work when the soil is moist but not waterlogged. Start by identifying the mother plant and the best follower suckerchoose one that is sword-shaped (narrow leaves) and growing on the opposite side of the bunch. Remove all other suckers. For small suckers, cut them at ground level. For larger suckers, cut them at an angle about 5 cm below the soil surface to prevent regrowth. You can also use a spade to dig around the sucker and cut the corm. Do this every 68 weeks. In dry areas, keep two followers per mat; in wet areas, keep one. Never remove the follower that will replace the mother after harvest. After cutting, mound soil around the base of the mother plant to cover wounds and prevent pests. If you want to plant new fields, select healthy sword suckers with a corm about 1015 cm in diameter and plant them immediately. Avoid water suckers (broad leaves) as they produce smaller bunches. Desuckering also reduces hiding places for pests like weevils and nematodes. Always sterilize your tools with a 10% bleach solution between plants to avoid spreading disease. Regular desuckering is one of the cheapest ways to increase bunch weight and plantation life. A well-maintained mat can produce for 510 years.

2. Leaf Pruning and Debris Removal

Leaf pruning means cutting off old, dry, or diseased leaves from the banana plant. This improves air movement, reduces disease, and makes harvesting easier. Use a sharp knife or pruning shears. Cut leaves that are more than 50% yellow or brown, or that hang down and touch the ground. Also remove leaves with black streak disease or cigar end rot. Do not remove more than 34 leaves per plant at one time, and never cut the young, healthy leaves at the top. The best time is during the dry season after harvest. Cut the leaf stalk about 10 cm from the trunk. After cutting, chop the leaves into small pieces and spread them as mulch around the base, or remove them if they are diseased. Diseased leaves should be burned or buried away from the plantation. Debris removal means clearing fallen leaves, pseudostems, and old bunches from the field. This reduces hiding places for rats, snakes, and insects like banana weevils. It also lowers the chance of fire during dry spells. In a mature plantation, walk through every 23 weeks with a machete and collect debris. In Africa, many farmers use the cut leaves as livestock feed or compost. If you use them as mulch, ensure they are free of disease. A clean plantation has better sunlight penetration, which increases photosynthesis and bunch size. Pruning also helps you spot pests early. For example, if you see small holes in the pseudostem, you may have weevils. Remove and destroy infested plants. Always wear gloves and long sleeves to protect yourself from sharp leaves and sap, which can irritate skin. Keep your tools clean to avoid spreading diseases like banana bunchy top virus.

3. Bunch Management: Bagging and Propping

Bunch management means protecting the banana bunch from pests, sunburn, and wind damage. Bagging involves covering the bunch with a special plastic bag. Use blue or clear polyethylene bags that are 0.05 mm thick and about 1.2 m long. Bag the bunch when the last hand has opened and the fingers are still green. First, remove any dried flower parts and small, misshapen fingers. Then slip the bag over the bunch and tie it at the top with a string, leaving the bottom open for air. In Africa, you can buy banana bags from agricultural supply stores or use recycled fertilizer bags if clean. Bagging reduces damage from insects like thrips and scarring beetles, and it speeds up growth by 1015%. It also protects against sunburn, which causes white patches on the peel. Propping means supporting the pseudostem or the bunch with a pole to prevent it from falling over. Use a strong bamboo pole or wooden stake, 23 m long. Place it at a 45-degree angle against the pseudostem, about 1 m from the ground, and tie it with soft rope or banana fiber. Do not tie too tightly. Prop when the bunch is half-grown. In windy areas, prop every plant with a bunch. Check props every week and adjust as the bunch grows. Remove the bag when the fruit starts to ripen (turns light green or yellow). At harvest, cut the bunch with a sharp knife, leaving a 15 cm stalk. Handle bunches carefully to avoid bruising. Bagging and propping can increase marketable yield by 2030%.

4. Weed Management Strategies

Weeds compete with banana plants for water, nutrients, and sunlight. They can reduce yields by 3050% if not controlled. The most common weeds in African banana farms are couch grass, spear grass, black jack, and pigweed. The best strategy is a combination of manual, cultural, and chemical methods. Manual weeding: use a hand hoe or machete to cut weeds at ground level. Do this every 46 weeks during the rainy season. Be careful not to damage banana roots, which are shallow (top 30 cm). Slashing is better than deep digging. Cultural methods: plant cover crops like sweet potato, beans, or groundnuts between banana rows. These smother weeds and provide extra food. Mulching with dry grass, banana leaves, or coffee husks also suppresses weeds. Keep the area around the mat cleanremove weeds within 1 m of the plant. Chemical control: use glyphosate (Roundup) carefully. Spray only when there is no wind, and direct the nozzle at the weeds, not the banana leaves. Avoid spraying young suckers. Always wear protective clothing. In organic farming, use vinegar or salt solutions, but these are less effective. Another method is to plant bananas in double rows with a living mulch of leguminous trees like Gliricidia. This reduces weed growth and adds nitrogen. Rotate weeding tools between fields to avoid spreading weed seeds. After weeding, leave cut weeds on the ground as mulch unless they have seeds. If you use herbicides, keep a record of dates and rates. Never mix different chemicals without advice. A weed-free plantation is easier to harvest and has fewer pests like snails and slugs.

5. Mulching and Soil Conservation

Mulching means covering the soil around banana plants with organic material like dry grass, banana leaves, coffee husks, or rice straw. A 1015 cm thick layer of mulch is ideal. Mulch conserves moisture, suppresses weeds, and adds nutrients as it decomposes. It also keeps soil temperature stable and prevents erosion on slopes. In Africa, use locally available materials. For example, in Uganda, farmers use coffee husks; in Kenya, they use napier grass. Apply mulch at the start of the dry season. Keep mulch 10 cm away from the pseudostem to avoid rot. Reapply every 34 months. Do not use fresh, green material as it may attract pests. Soil conservation means preventing soil loss and maintaining fertility. On slopes, plant bananas along contour lines. Dig contour trenches or basins around each mat to catch rainwater. Add compost or manure (510 kg per mat per year) under the mulch. This improves soil structure and water holding capacity. Avoid burning fields; it destroys organic matter. Instead, incorporate crop residues. If you have livestock, use their manure. A good practice is to plant cover crops like Mucuna or lablab between rows. These fix nitrogen and their residues can be mulched. Terracing is expensive but effective on steep slopes. For smallholders, grass strips (e.g., vetiver grass) along contours reduce erosion. Check soil pH every 2 years; bananas prefer pH 5.57.0. If acidic, add agricultural lime. Mulching and conservation increase yields by 2040% and reduce the need for irrigation. They also protect the shallow banana roots from drying out.

6. Wind Damage Prevention and Recovery

Wind can uproot banana plants, break pseudostems, and shred leaves, causing heavy losses. Prevention starts with choosing a wind-protected site. If your farm is open, plant windbreaks like casuarina, grevillea, or bamboo around the perimeter. Space windbreak trees 510 m apart. They should be at least 10 m from the banana rows to avoid competition. Within the plantation, plant bananas in blocks rather than single rows. Use tall, strong varieties like FHIA-17 or Gros Michel. Avoid planting on exposed hilltops. Propping (as in Lesson 3) is the most important direct protection. Prop every plant with a bunch, especially during windy seasons. Also, maintain good desuckeringtoo many suckers make the mat top-heavy. After a storm, assess damage. If a plant is uprooted but the corm is intact, cut off the pseudostem 30 cm above ground, replant the corm in a new hole, and water well. If the pseudostem is broken but the corm is firm, cut the stem cleanly below the break and allow new suckers to grow. Remove all broken leaves and debris to prevent disease. Apply mulch and water to help recovery. Do not harvest damaged bunches immediately; wait 12 weeks if the fruit is still green. For severely damaged mats, remove all plants and replant with healthy suckers. In windy areas, consider planting on raised beds to improve drainage and root anchorage. After recovery, apply extra potassium fertilizer (e.g., 100 g muriate of potash per plant) to strengthen stems. Wind damage can be reduced by 50% with proper windbreaks and propping. Always keep a few spare suckers for replanting.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Banana Plantation Maintenance

Banana is not a tree. It is a giant herb that produces one harvestable bunch per pseudostem, then dies. Every maintenance decision you make between planting and harvest either protects that single income event or erodes it. In East and Central Africa, where smallholder banana systems dominate and average yields sit between 8 and 15 t/ha against a potential of 4060 t/ha, the gap is almost never genetics. It is maintenance. This reference consolidates the six core maintenance tasks into a business operating standard: what to do, when, at what cost, and what it returns.

Treat the plantation as a factory floor. Desuckering controls your plant population and therefore your cost per unit. Pruning and debris removal manage disease pressure and light interception. Bunch management protects the highest-value asset on the farm. Weed control and mulching defend moisture and nutrients. Wind planning protects capital. Each task has a labour cost, and labour is typically 4060% of total banana production cost in African smallholder systems (FAO, 2020). Maintenance is where you either buy yield or waste money.

1. Desuckering and Sucker Management

A healthy mat produces 1020 suckers per year. If you keep them all, you get a dense thicket of thin pseudostems, small bunches, and heavy competition for water and nutrients. The standard for commercial and semi-commercial plantings is one mother plant, one follower, and one young sucker per mat a three-generation system that keeps continuous production without overcrowding.

Select sword suckers (narrow, pointed leaves) rather than water suckers (broad leaves, low vigour). Retain the follower positioned on the side away from the prevailing wind and toward the inter-row for light. Remove all others by cutting below the corm collar with a sharp, clean cutlass or spade slicing above the collar leaves a stump that regrows. In high-density systems, desucker every 68 weeks; in low-density mats, every 810 weeks.

Business note: Desuckering is a yield-per-unit-area decision. Reducing from 5 stems to 3 stems per mat can raise bunch weight by 1525% with no additional fertiliser input, because the same nutrients serve fewer sinks.

2. Leaf Pruning and Debris Removal

Leaves are the factory: each functional leaf contributes to bunch filling. Do not over-prune. Remove only leaves that are (a) dead and hanging, (b) severely diseased (especially black sigatoka lesions covering more than half the leaf), or (c) rubbing directly on the bunch. Cut 510 cm from the pseudostem, leaving a short petiole stub, and remove the material from the field or chop it finely for mulch.

Debris removal matters because fallen leaves and pseudostem pieces are the primary reservoir for black sigatoka (Pseudocercospora fijiensis), banana weevil, and nematodes. In African highland systems, black sigatoka can cut bunch weight by 3050% if unmanaged (IITA, 2019). Removing infected tissue is the cheapest fungicide you own.

3. Bunch Management: Bagging and Propping

Bagging with blue or clear polythene sleeves protects fruit from bird and insect damage, sunburn, and wind scarring, and can advance maturity by 714 days. Bag when the bunch has emerged and the last hand has formed, and remove the bottom of the bag for ventilation. In areas with high weevil pressure, combine bagging with a collar or insecticide-treated band.

Propping is non-negotiable for any bunch above roughly 15 kg. Use bamboo, timber, or forked sticks placed at a 45 angle under the bunch stalk, padded at the contact point. Without propping, a heavy bunch snaps the pseudostem before harvest a total loss of that plant's entire output.

4. Weed Management Strategies

Weeds compete most aggressively in the first 6 months and during dry spells. The most cost-effective African smallholder strategy is a combination of hand weeding, slashing, and cover cropping with legumes such as Mucuna or Pueraria. Mulching (see below) suppresses weeds while conserving moisture.

Herbicides are an option but carry real risk to banana and to workers. Use only registered products, calibrated equipment, and directed application never broadcast over young suckers.

SAFETY WARNING Herbicide and Tool Handling
  • Never apply herbicide within 30 cm of the banana pseudostem or suckers. Banana roots are shallow and highly sensitive; drift or root uptake causes stunting and pseudostem splitting.
  • Wear gloves, boots, long sleeves, and eye protection when mixing or spraying. Glyphosate and paraquat formulations are toxic; paraquat is banned in many countries and should not be used.
  • Do not spray on windy days or within 6 hours of expected rain.
  • Disinfect cutlasses and spades between mats (10% bleach or flame) to prevent spreading banana weevil and Fusarium wilt.
  • Store agrochemicals locked, away from food and children. Wash contaminated clothing separately.

5. Mulching and Soil Conservation

Banana roots sit in the top 30 cm of soil, making the crop vulnerable to moisture stress and erosion. A mulch layer of 510 cm of dry grass, banana trash, or crop residues conserves moisture, suppresses weeds, moderates soil temperature, and adds organic matter. Keep mulch 20 cm away from the pseudostem to avoid weevil harbourage and stem rot.

On slopes, combine mulch with contour planting, grass strips, and drainage channels. Soil loss of 2050 t/ha/year is documented on unprotected banana slopes in the East African highlands (FAO, 2015) that is your topsoil and your fertiliser budget washing downhill.

6. Wind Damage Prevention and Recovery

Banana pseudostems snap and topple in winds above roughly 40 km/h, especially when bunched or when soils are waterlogged. Prevention beats recovery: plant windbreaks (Grevillea, Casuarina, vetiver) on the windward side, avoid excessive nitrogen which produces tall, weak stems, and maintain propping. After a storm, harvest any marketable bunches immediately, cut broken pseudostems at ground level, and allow the mat's follower to take over.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Walk the plantation weekly. Note sucker density, diseased leaves, bunches ready for bagging, and leaning stems.
  2. Maintain 3 stems per mat mother, follower, young sucker. Remove the rest below the corm collar.
  3. Prune only dead, diseased, or bunch-rubbing leaves. Never strip healthy leaves for "cleanliness."
  4. Bag every bunch once the last hand has formed; prop any bunch above 15 kg.
  5. Keep a 20 cm mulch-free ring around each pseudostem to prevent weevil and rot.
  6. Weed before weeds flower. Combine hand weeding, slashing, and legume cover crops.
  7. Disinfect tools between mats to stop Fusarium and weevil spread.
  8. Inspect windbreaks and props monthly, especially before the rainy/windy season.
