1. Daily Routine and Observation

Start your day with ostriches before sunrise. Walk the perimeter fence firstcheck for holes, loose wires, or predator tracks. Ostriches are curious and will test fences; a single break can mean lost birds. Then move to the water troughs. An adult ostrich drinks 1015 litres daily, more in heat. Clean troughs with a stiff brush and fresh water; dirty water spreads disease fast. Next, observe each bird from a distance. Look for droppings: normal is firm, dark, and well-formed. Runny or bloody droppings mean illness. Count the birdsif one is missing, search immediately. Ostriches hide when sick or stressed.\n\nFeeding comes next. A mature ostrich eats 23 kg of feed per day. In Africa, a good mix is 50% lucerne (alfalfa) hay, 30% maize meal, and 20% commercial ostrich pellet. Add limestone grit for calcium. Feed in troughs, not on the ground, to prevent worm eggs. Watch how they eat: a bird that hangs back or pecks weakly may have a throat injury or infection.\n\nMidday, check shade and dust baths. Ostriches need shade in temperatures above 30C. A simple shade cloth or tree cover works. Dust baths help control lice and ticksprovide a shallow pit with dry sand and wood ash. If birds are panting heavily or holding wings out, they are heat-stressed. Act immediately: sprinkle water on their legs and increase airflow.\n\nAfternoon: inspect legs and feet. Look for swelling, cuts, or scaly leg mites. A lame ostrich cannot breed or gain weight. Treat minor wounds with iodine. Check eyes for dischargea sign of avian influenza or sinusitis.\n\nEvening: secure all gates. Count birds again. Record everything in a notebook: feed eaten, water consumed, any abnormal behaviour, deaths. This daily log is your early warning system. In Africa, where vet services may be far, your eyes are the best medicine.

2. Handling and Restraint Techniques

Ostriches are strong and can kick forward with enough force to break a human leg. Never handle them alone. Always work in pairs, with one person as a distraction and the other as the handler. Wear long sleeves, heavy trousers, and steel-toe boots. A kick from a 120 kg bird can kill.\n\nFor moving a single ostrich, use a 'hood' or a dark cloth over the head. This calms them because they think it is night. A simple burlap sack works. Approach from the side, never from the frontthey kick forward. Place the hood quickly and firmly. Then guide the bird by holding the base of the neck with one hand and the body with the other. Do not pull feathers; that causes pain and stress.\n\nFor restraint, use a 'crush' or a narrow race. A V-shaped trough made of poles works well. Lead the bird in, then close the head gate. For leg examination, two people lift the bird's leg gentlynever twist. For beak trimming, use a small angle grinder with a sanding disc. Trim only the tip; cutting too deep causes bleeding.\n\nFor injections, restrain the bird in the crush. The best site is the breast muscle (pectoral). Use a 1.5-inch needle. For oral medication, use a stomach tubenever force a bottle into the beak; they can choke.\n\nIn Africa, many farmers use a 'trip' method: a rope around the legs to make the bird sit. This is riskyit can break bones. Avoid it. Instead, build a simple crush from local timber. It costs little and saves injuries.\n\nAlways have a escape route for yourself. If the bird panics, let it go. Never corner an ostrich. Handle only when necessary: for vaccinations, moving to new camps, or health checks. The less you handle, the calmer your birds. Record every handling eventdate, reason, and any injuryso you can improve your technique.

3. Stress Reduction and Welfare

Stress kills ostriches silently. A stressed bird stops eating, loses weight, and gets sick. In Africa, common stressors are loud noises, dogs, strangers, and sudden changes in routine. Keep your farm quiet. Do not allow radios or shouting near the camps. Train your dogs to stay away.\n\nProvide plenty of space. An adult ostrich needs at least 250 square metres per bird in a camp. Overcrowding causes fighting and feather pecking. Provide visual barriersstacks of hay or shade clothso birds can escape each other. Separate bulls (males) during breeding season; they fight for dominance.\n\nShade and dust baths are essential. In hot African summers, ostriches need shade from 11am to 4pm. A simple roof of thatch or corrugated iron works. Dust baths help them cool and remove parasites. Make a pit 2m x 2m with dry sand and ash. Refresh monthly.\n\nFeed and water must be consistent. Change feed gradually over 7 days if you switch brands. Sudden changes cause digestive upset. Always provide clean watera thirsty ostrich is a stressed ostrich.\n\nHandle birds calmly. Move slowly and speak softly. Never chase them. Use a flag or stick to guide, not to hit. If a bird is injured, isolate it in a quiet pen with food and water. Minimise visitors.\n\nWelfare also means humane slaughter. If culling, use a captive bolt pistol followed by bleeding. Never cut the throat of a conscious bird.\n\nMonitor stress signs: panting, wing drooping, pacing, and aggression. If you see these, find the cause. Often it is a predator at night, a broken water pipe, or a new person. Fix it fast. Happy ostriches grow faster and lay more eggs. Your profit depends on their comfort.

4. Seasonal Management: Heat and Cold Stress

Ostriches adapt to many climates, but extremes cost you money. In Africa, heat is the bigger killer. When temperatures rise above 35C, ostriches pant, hold wings out, and reduce feed intake. They can die from heat stroke.\n\nHeat management: Provide shadetrees, shade cloth, or a simple roof. Orient shade east-west so it blocks midday sun. Misters or sprinklers on the roof help. Wet the ground in the shade; evaporation cools the air. Feed early morning and late evening when it is cool. Add electrolytes to water (1 teaspoon salt + 1 teaspoon baking soda per 10 litres). Never handle birds in the heat of the day.\n\nCold management: Ostriches can handle cold down to -5C if dry and wind-free. But chicks and sick birds suffer. In cold areas like the Highveld, provide windbreaksbales of hay or mud walls. Bedding of dry straw keeps them off cold ground. Increase feed by 10% in winter; they burn energy to stay warm. Ensure water does not freezecheck twice daily.\n\nRainy season: Mud causes foot rot and slipping. Provide raised dry areas with gravel or wood chips. Move feed troughs to high ground. Wet feathers lose insulation; provide a dry shelter.\n\nDust storms: Common in Sahel. Provide a windbreak of trees or a solid fence. Keep dust baths covered to prevent eye infections.\n\nSeasonal checklist: In hot months, check water 3 times daily. In cold months, check for frostbite on toes. In rains, check for diarrhoea. Adjust feed: more energy in winter, more protein in breeding season. Record daily temperatures and bird behaviour. After one year, you will know your farm's pattern. Act before the stress, not after.

5. Record Keeping for Production and Health

You cannot manage what you do not measure. A simple notebook and pen are enough. Record every day: date, temperature, feed given (kg), water consumed (litres), number of eggs laid, number of birds, and any sick or dead birds.\n\nFor each bird, keep an individual card. Write its tag number, age, sex, and weight. Record vaccinations (date, vaccine name, dose). Record any illness: symptoms, treatment, and outcome. If a bird dies, note the cause if known. This helps you spot patternse.g., three birds died after a cold snap.\n\nProduction records: For breeding, record mating dates, egg collection dates, and which hen laid which egg (if you can identify). Hatch rates tell you if your male is fertile. If less than 60% of eggs hatch, check male-to-female ratio (1:2 is ideal) or nutrition.\n\nFinancial records: Write down all costsfeed, vet, labour, fencingand all incomeegg sales, chick sales, meat, feathers. At the end of the month, subtract costs from income. If you are losing money, look at your records to find why. Maybe feed is too expensive, or you are losing too many chicks.\n\nUse a simple spreadsheet on a phone if you have one. Apps like KoboCollect or even WhatsApp notes work. But paper is fine if kept dry.\n\nAt the end of each season, review your records. Which camp had the most disease? Which feed gave the best weight gain? Which hen laid the most eggs? Use this to make decisions: cull poor layers, change feed, or move camps.\n\nIn Africa, many farmers fail because they do not know their numbers. A record book is your farm's memory. It turns guesswork into profit.

6. Culling and Replacement Decisions

Culling means removing birds that are not profitable. It is not cruelit is good management. Replace them with better birds.\n\nWhen to cull: A hen that lays fewer than 20 eggs per year (good hens lay 4060) should be culled. A male that is infertile (less than 60% hatch rate) should be culled. Birds with chronic illness, lameness, or deformitiescull. Aggressive birds that injure otherscull. Old birds: ostriches can live 40 years, but production drops after 1012 years. Cull at 10 years for meat or leather.\n\nHow to cull: Use a captive bolt pistol to stun, then bleed out. Do it away from other birds to reduce stress. If you cannot do it yourself, hire a trained slaughterman. Never sell a sick bird at marketit spreads disease and gives you a bad name.\n\nReplacement: Keep your best 10% of hens and 5% of males as breeders. Select for: high egg production, good fertility, fast growth, and calm temperament. Buy new bloodlines every 34 years to avoid inbreeding. In Africa, look for local breeds like the South African Black or Kenyan Red. They are adapted to heat and local diseases.\n\nRecord keeping helps: Use your records to identify your best and worst birds. Tag them clearly. When you cull, note the reason. Over time, your flock improves.\n\nDo not keep a bird out of pity. Every mouth costs feed. If it does not pay, it must go. But do it humanely. Plan culling for cool mornings. Have everything readyknives, stun gun, bleeding area. After culling, process the meat quickly or sell to a butcher. Leather is valuablesalt it and sell to a tannery.\n\nReplace immediately so your production does not drop. Buy healthy chicks from a reputable hatchery. Quarantine new birds for 30 days before mixing. Culling is a tool for progress.

<h2>Practical Reference: Adult Ostrich Management</h2>
<p>Adult ostriches are long-lived, high-value livestock. A single breeding hen can produce 4080 chicks per year under good management, and a mature bird's hide, feathers, and meat all carry commercial value. For African producers, adult management is where profitability is won or lost: feed conversion, egg fertility, mortality, and hide quality all hinge on daily discipline. This practical reference consolidates the core routines, handling methods, welfare standards, seasonal tactics, record systems, and culling logic you need to run a productive adult ostrich unit. Treat it as a field manual not a textbook.</p>

<h3>1. Daily Routine and Observation</h3>
<p>Ostriches are creatures of habit. They respond to consistent routines with lower stress and better production. Walk the camp twice daily early morning and late afternoon and observe from a distance before entering. Key indicators of a healthy adult flock include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Feeding activity:</strong> Birds should move to feed eagerly. Off-feed birds are the first sign of trouble.</li>
<li><strong>Droppings:</strong> Normal droppings are firm, well-formed, and dark green-brown. Loose, bloody, or pale droppings indicate disease or parasites.</li>
<li><strong>Posture:</strong> A healthy ostrich stands upright with head held high. Drooping wings, hunched posture, or sitting for long periods signal distress.</li>
<li><strong>Legs and feet:</strong> Check for swelling, wounds, or abnormal gait. Leg problems are a leading cause of adult mortality in African operations.</li>
<li><strong>Behaviour:</strong> Aggression, pecking, or isolation from the group can indicate stress, injury, or social disruption.</li>
</ul>
<p>Record every observation. A daily log that captures feed intake, water consumption, bird count, and any abnormalities becomes your early-warning system. In the African context, where veterinary services may be distant, early detection is often the difference between treating one bird and losing ten.</p>

<h3>2. Handling and Restraint Techniques</h3>
<p>Ostriches are powerful animals. An adult can kick with enough force to break a human leg, and a panicked bird can injure itself and handlers. Safe handling requires:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hooding:</strong> A cloth hood placed over the head calms the bird by blocking vision. This is the single most effective restraint tool.</li>
<li><strong>Wing restraint:</strong> Hold the wings close to the body without twisting. Never grab tail feathers.</li>
<li><strong>Leg restraint:</strong> For examination, two handlers can restrain the legs with soft ropes or straps. Never use wire.</li>
<li><strong>Crush or race:</strong> A V-shaped crush race allows safe examination and treatment without full physical restraint.</li>
</ul>
<p>Always work in pairs. Approach from the side, never from directly behind. Speak calmly and move slowly. In African conditions, where birds may be semi-wild, patience and routine handling from a young age reduce the need for force.</p>

<h3>3. Stress Reduction and Welfare</h3>
<p>Stress costs money. Stressed ostriches grow slower, lay fewer eggs, and are more susceptible to disease. Welfare is not just ethical it is economic. Key stress-reduction practices include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Consistent routines:</strong> Feed, water, and inspect at the same times daily.</li>
<li><strong>Minimal handling:</strong> Handle only when necessary. Use hoods and calm techniques.</li>
<li><strong>Adequate space:</strong> Provide at least 250500 m per adult bird in breeding camps.</li>
<li><strong>Shade and shelter:</strong> Ostriches need shade in hot climates and windbreaks in cold, wet conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Social stability:</strong> Keep groups stable. Mixing unfamiliar birds causes fighting and stress.</li>
</ul>
<p>Welfare indicators to monitor include feather condition, skin lesions, lameness, and behavioural signs such as pacing or feather-pecking. Poor welfare directly reduces hide value the most valuable single product from a slaughtered ostrich.</p>

<h3>4. Seasonal Management: Heat and Cold Stress</h3>
<p>Ostriches are adaptable but not immune to extreme weather. In Africa, producers face both intense heat in the Sahel and Kalahari, and cold, wet winters in the Cape and Highveld.</p>

<h3>5. Record Keeping for Production and Health</h3>
<p>You cannot manage what you do not measure. In ostrich farming, records drive decisions on feeding, breeding, health, and culling. A basic record system should capture:</p>
<ul>
<li>Daily feed and water consumption per camp</li>
<li>Egg production: date, bird ID, egg weight, fertility, hatchability</li>
<li>Health events: date, bird ID, symptoms, treatment, outcome</li>
<li>Mortality: date, bird ID, suspected cause</li>
<li>Handling and movement records</li>
<li>Financial records: feed cost, veterinary cost, sales</li>
</ul>
<p>In African conditions, where labour may be shared and record-keeping inconsistent, use simple paper logs or a smartphone app. The key is consistency, not complexity.</p>

<h3>6. Culling and Replacement Decisions</h3>
<p>Culling is a business decision, not a punishment. Every bird that underperforms consumes feed, space, and labour that could go to a productive bird. Cull or replace adults when:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Reproductive failure:</strong> Hens producing fewer than 20 eggs per year, or males with consistently poor fertility.</li>
<li><strong>Chronic disease:</strong> Birds with recurring lameness, respiratory disease, or poor body condition that does not respond to treatment.</li>
<li><strong>Age:</strong> Breeding ostriches peak between 4 and 12 years. After 1215 years, fertility declines.</li>
<li><strong>Aggression:</strong> Birds that consistently injure others or handlers.</li>
<li><strong>Poor hide quality:</strong> Birds with extensive feather-pecking or skin lesions have reduced hide value.</li>
</ul>
<p>Replacement strategy: retain the best 1020% of chicks each year as future breeders. This allows gradual genetic improvement and avoids the cost of buying in replacement stock. In African operations, where biosecurity is a concern, closed-herd replacement is strongly preferred.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Walk camps twice daily observe feeding, droppings, posture, and behaviour.</li>
<li>Use hoods for all close handling; never work alone.</li>
<li>Provide shade in heat and dry shelter in cold; ensure unlimited clean water.</li>
<li>Record feed intake, egg production, health events, and mortality daily.</li>
<li>Monitor body condition monthly; adjust feed accordingly.</li>
<li>Review breeding records quarterly; cull consistently poor performers.</li>
<li>Maintain a first-aid kit and emergency contact list on site.</li>
<li>Keep groups stable; avoid mixing unfamiliar birds.</li>
</ol>
