<h2>Practical Reference: Pricing, Budgeting, and Financials for Home-Based Catering</h2>

<p>In home-based catering across Africa, the difference between a business that survives and one that thrives is rarely the quality of the food alone. It is the discipline of the numbers. Whether you are cooking for a 30-person wedding in Kumasi, a corporate lunch in Nairobi, or a naming ceremony in Lagos, your ability to calculate costs, price profitably, and track every shilling, naira, or cedi determines whether you build wealth or merely stay busy. This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a working toolkit you can apply immediately.</p>

<h3>1. Calculating Food Costs</h3>

<p>Food cost is the single largest expense in most home-based catering operations, typically representing 3045% of revenue. The mistake most caterers make is estimating costs from memory rather than recording them. You must calculate cost per portion, not cost per pot.</p>

<p><strong>The formula:</strong> Total ingredient cost number of portions = food cost per portion. Then multiply by the number of guests to get total food cost for an event.</p>

<p>Always add a <strong>waste and shrinkage allowance</strong> of 510%. Rice expands, meat trims down, and vegetables spoil. In markets where you buy from open-air vendorscommon across West and East Africaprices fluctuate weekly with season and fuel costs, so update your costing sheet before every quote.</p>

<h3>2. Labor and Overhead Expenses</h3>

<p>Home-based caterers frequently undercharge because they forget to pay themselves. Your labor is a real cost. If you hire one assistant for a 12-hour event day, that is a direct labor cost. Even if you cook alone, assign a notional wage to your own hoursthis protects your business from appearing more profitable than it is.</p>

<p>Overhead includes everything not tied to a single dish: gas or charcoal, electricity, water, transport to market, packaging, disposable plates and cutlery, phone data for client coordination, and equipment depreciation (pots, coolers, gas cylinders). In many African cities, transport and fuel alone can add 1015% to your total cost.</p>

<h3>3. Setting Profitable Prices</h3>

<p>There are three pricing methods. Use all three and compare:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cost-plus pricing:</strong> Total cost per portion (1 + markup). If cost per portion is 800 KES and you want a 40% margin, price at 1,120 KES.</li>
<li><strong>Market-based pricing:</strong> What do comparable caterers charge in your city? Stay within 1015% unless your quality or brand justifies more.</li>
<li><strong>Value-based pricing:</strong> What is the event worth to the client? A wedding client may pay more per head than a funeral client for the same menu.</li>
</ul>

<p>Never price below cost. A common trap is winning a large event at a low price and losing money on volume. If a client's budget is below your cost, reduce the menu scopenot your price.</p>

<h3>4. Creating Quotes and Invoices</h3>

<p>A professional quote wins trust. Include: your business name and contact, client name, event date and venue, number of guests, menu items with per-head price, additional services (serving staff, rentals, delivery), subtotal, deposit required, balance due date, and validity period (typically 714 days).</p>

<p>An invoice is issued after the event or upon deposit. It must reference the quote number, list what was delivered, show payments received, and state the outstanding balance. In many African markets, handwritten invoices are acceptable, but a printed or PDF invoice signals professionalism and helps with record-keeping.</p>

<h3>5. Payment Terms and Deposits</h3>

<p>Cash flow kills more small caterers than bad cooking. Protect yourself with clear terms:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Deposit:</strong> 50% upfront is standard. For large events (100+ guests), request 6070%.</li>
<li><strong>Balance:</strong> Due on delivery or within 7 days of invoice.</li>
<li><strong>Cancellation:</strong> State clearly that deposits are non-refundable within 7 days of the event, since you will have already purchased ingredients.</li>
<li><strong>Accepted methods:</strong> Mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money), bank transfer, or cash with receipt.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:2px solid #c62828;background:#ffebee;padding:14px 18px;border-radius:6px;margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY &amp; FINANCIAL WARNING:</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;color:#b71c1c;">Never begin shopping or cooking for an event without a confirmed deposit in hand. Verbal promises are not payment. In cash-heavy markets, insist on mobile money or bank transfer so you have a written record. Do not accept post-dated cheques from new clients. If a client refuses any deposit, decline the jobthis is the single most common cause of caterer bankruptcy across Africa.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Tracking Income and Expenses</h3>

<p>You cannot grow what you do not measure. Keep two simple records:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Income log:</strong> Date, client, event, amount received, payment method, balance outstanding.</li>
<li><strong>Expense log:</strong> Date, item, category (food, labor, transport, packaging, equipment), amount, receipt number.</li>
</ul>

<p>A notebook works, but a simple spreadsheet (Google Sheets or Excel) on your phone is better because it calculates totals automatically. At month-end, subtract expenses from income to find your profit. Set aside 2030% of profit for equipment replacement and slow seasons.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Update market prices for every ingredient before quotingnever use last month's figures.</li>
<li>Calculate cost per portion, then add 510% for waste and shrinkage.</li>
<li>Include your own labor time as a cost, even if you are not paying yourself cash.</li>
<li>Add fuel, transport, packaging, and equipment depreciation to every quote.</li>
<li>Set a price that gives you at least a 1525% profit margin after all costs.</li>
<li>Issue a written quote with a validity period and require a 50% deposit before shopping.</li>
<li>Record every income and expense the same day it happensno exceptions.</li>
<li>Review your profit monthly and set aside 2030% for equipment and slow seasons.</li>
</ol>
