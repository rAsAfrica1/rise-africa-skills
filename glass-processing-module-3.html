1. Furnace Types: Pot, Day Tank, Continuous Tank

Choosing the right furnace is the first big decision in glass melting. In Africa, many small workshops start with a pot furnace because it is cheap to build and run. A pot furnace uses a clay or silicon carbide pot that holds 50200 kg of glass. You heat it with charcoal, diesel, or waste oil. It takes 46 hours to melt a batch, and you can make different colours by swapping pots. But pots crack often, and you lose heat every time you open the furnace. A day tank is bigger: it holds 5002000 kg and runs for 24 hours. You fill it in the morning, melt all day, and drain it at night. It uses more fuel but gives better quality because the glass stays hot and uniform. For production of bottles, windows, or beads, a continuous tank is best. It runs 24/7 for months. Raw materials go in one end, molten glass comes out the other. You need a team of at least 6 people to keep it going. In Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa, continuous tanks are used by large factories. For a start-up, a pot furnace or a small day tank (200500 kg) is practical. You can build a day tank from local firebricks and a steel casing. Use a diesel burner or a gas burner with a blower. Always insulate with ceramic fibre blanket to save fuel. Remember: the furnace is the heart of your workshop. A well-built pot furnace can pay for itself in 3 months if you sell beads or art glass. A continuous tank needs a big market and steady power. Start small, learn the heat, then scale up.

2. Melting Kinetics and Energy Efficiency

Melting glass is not just about heat; it is about how fast the raw materials turn into liquid. The main ingredients are silica sand (SiO2), soda ash (Na2CO3), and limestone (CaCO3). Soda ash lowers the melting point from 1700C to about 1400C. But even at 1400C, melting takes time. The kinetics depend on particle size, mixing, and temperature. If you use coarse sand (0.51 mm), it melts slower than fine sand (0.10.3 mm). Always sieve your sand to remove stones and lumps. Mix the batch thoroughly: 100 kg sand, 30 kg soda ash, 15 kg limestone, and 5 kg dolomite. Add 12 kg of cullet (broken glass) per 10 kg of batch to speed up melting. Cullet melts faster and saves energy. In Africa, fuel is expensive, so energy efficiency is critical. A well-insulated pot furnace uses 1.52 kg of diesel per kg of glass. A poor one uses 4 kg. To save fuel: preheat the combustion air using a simple metal pipe that runs through the exhaust flue. This can cut fuel use by 20%. Also, keep the furnace door closed. Every time you open it, you lose 510% of the heat. Use a pyrometer or a thermocouple to measure temperature, not just your eyes. The melt should be 14001450C for soda-lime glass. If you see bubbles or unmelted sand, increase temperature by 20C and wait 30 minutes. Stirring helps, but too much stirring introduces bubbles. For small-scale work, melt in batches of 50 kg. It takes 34 hours. If you use charcoal, you need about 20 kg of charcoal per 10 kg of glass. That is expensive. So invest in a diesel or gas burner with a blower. It pays back in 6 months.

3. Refining and Homogenization of Glass Melt

After melting, the glass is full of tiny bubbles and chemical streaks. Refining removes bubbles; homogenization makes the glass uniform. Both happen at 14501500C. The bubbles come from air trapped in the batch, from CO2 released by soda ash and limestone, and from water vapour. To refine, you must raise the temperature to 1500C for 3060 minutes. At this temperature, the glass becomes thinner, and bubbles rise to the surface and pop. You can also add a refining agent: 0.5% sodium sulphate (salt cake) or 0.2% antimony oxide. In Africa, sodium sulphate is cheap and available. But too much causes scum. For homogenization, you need to stir the melt. Mechanical stirrers made from refractory clay or platinum rods work. In small pot furnaces, you can stir with a steel rod coated in clay. Stir slowly for 5 minutes, then let it rest for 15 minutes. The glass should look like honey: clear, no streaks. If you see cords (wavy lines), stir again. Another method is bubbling: inject compressed air or nitrogen through a clay tube at the bottom. Bubbles rise and mix the glass. Use a pressure of 12 bar. Bubbling for 10 minutes is enough for a 100 kg pot. After refining, let the glass cool to 11001200C for working. If you cool too fast, you get cracks. If you cool too slow, you waste fuel. Use a annealing oven or a heated mould. In a day tank, refining happens continuously. You keep the melt at 1480C in a separate chamber. The glass flows to a working end at 1150C. Always test your glass: take a small sample, cool it, and look through it. If it has bubbles or stones, refine longer. Good refining is the difference between cheap beads and high-quality art glass.

4. Bubbling and Stirring Techniques

Bubbling and stirring are the two main tools to mix glass melt and remove bubbles. They are simple but must be done correctly. Bubbling: You need a stainless steel or clay tube (610 mm inner diameter) that reaches the bottom of the pot. Connect it to a compressor or a hand pump. For a 100 kg pot, use an air pressure of 12 bar. Insert the tube through the furnace door or a special port. Turn on the air for 1015 minutes. You will see bubbles rising. This mixes the glass and helps small bubbles join into bigger ones that rise faster. Do not bubble too long, or you will cool the glass and introduce new bubbles. For continuous tanks, bubbling is done continuously in the refining zone. Stirring: Use a refractory rod (clay or platinum) attached to a drill or a hand crank. For small pots, a steel rod coated with clay works. Stir at 3060 rpm for 5 minutes. Then stop and let the glass rest for 15 minutes. Stirring breaks up cords and unmelted sand. But if you stir too fast, you create vortices that trap air. In Africa, many workshops use a simple hand-cranked stirrer made from a bicycle wheel and a clay rod. That is fine. Always preheat the stirrer in the furnace before inserting it into the glass, or it will crack. Also, never use wet tools. Water causes steam explosions. After bubbling and stirring, skim the surface with a metal ladle to remove foam and scum. Then let the glass sit for 30 minutes before gathering. If you are making beads, stir once, then gather. For optical glass, you need longer. Remember: bubbling and stirring are not a replacement for proper temperature. If the glass is too cold, no amount of stirring will help. Keep the melt at 14501480C during these operations. Practice with a small pot first. You will learn the feel and sound of well-refined glass.

5. Temperature Control and Glass Quality

Temperature control is everything in glass melting. If you are 20C off, you get bubbles, stones, or cracks. You need to measure temperature accurately. A simple thermocouple (type K or S) with a digital reader costs about $50. A pyrometer (infrared gun) costs $100. In Africa, many use a ceramic cone (pyrometric cone) that bends at a specific temperature. That is cheap but not precise. For quality glass, invest in a thermocouple. Place it in the melt or in the furnace wall. The ideal melting temperature for soda-lime glass is 14001450C. For refining, 14801500C. For working, 11001200C. If you see bubbles, increase to 1500C for 30 minutes. If you see stones (unmelted sand), increase to 1450C and stir. If you see cords, homogenize longer. Temperature also affects colour. At higher temperatures, some colours (like copper ruby) burn out. So control carefully. Use a damper in the exhaust to control heat loss. Open it to cool, close it to heat. A simple PID controller with a motorised valve can automate this, but manual is fine for small furnaces. Always keep a logbook: record time, temperature, and batch number. After a few weeks, you will see patterns. For example, if your furnace cools too fast at night, you get cracks. So insulate well. Also, preheat the moulds to 500C to avoid thermal shock. In continuous tanks, you have three zones: melting (1450C), refining (1500C), and working (1150C). Each zone has its own burner. For a pot furnace, you have one zone. So you must change temperature manually. Let the glass rest at each stage. Rushing causes defects. Good temperature control gives you clear, strong glass that sells for a higher price. In Kenya, a workshop that switched from visual estimation to a thermocouple reduced rejects from 30% to 5%.

6. Furnace Refractories and Maintenance

Refractories are the heat-resistant bricks and materials that line your furnace. They must withstand 1500C and chemical attack from glass and gases. The most common refractories are fireclay bricks (for up to 1300C), high-alumina bricks (for 15001600C), and silica bricks (for glass contact). In Africa, you can buy fireclay bricks from local brickmakers or import from South Africa or China. For a pot furnace, use high-alumina bricks for the hot face and fireclay for the outer wall. Insulate with ceramic fibre blanket (2550 mm thick). The pot itself is made of clay or silicon carbide. Silicon carbide pots last longer but cost more. Maintenance is key. Every week, check for cracks, especially around the burner port and the door. Patch small cracks with refractory cement. If a brick is loose, replace it. Never let glass drip on the outside; it eats the steel casing. Clean the burner weekly. Soot and carbon build up and reduce efficiency. Use a wire brush. Check the flue for blockages. A blocked flue causes back pressure and poor combustion. Also, check the thermocouple and controller monthly. In continuous tanks, you must replace bricks every 23 years. That is a big job. So keep the furnace clean and avoid thermal shock. Do not heat or cool faster than 50C per hour. For a pot furnace, preheat slowly over 8 hours when new. After a melt, let it cool slowly. If you need to shut down, empty the glass first. Never leave glass in a pot to freeze; it will crack the pot. Keep a spare pot and spare bricks. In Nigeria, a workshop that maintained its furnace weekly got 3 years of service from a pot that normally lasted 1 year. So maintenance saves money. Train one person to be the furnace caretaker. Give them a checklist: daily, weekly, monthly. That is how you keep melting.

<h2>Practical Reference: Melting and Refining in African Glass Operations</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core operating knowledge from the six lessons in this module. It is written for production supervisors, furnace operators, maintenance leads, and owner-managers running glass melting operations in Africa from small pot furnaces serving artisan and laboratory glassware workshops to continuous tanks feeding container, tableware, or flat glass lines. The goal is simple: fewer rejects, longer furnace campaigns, lower energy cost per tonne, and consistent glass quality that satisfies buyers.</p>

<p>In the African context, three constraints dominate furnace economics. First, <strong>energy cost and reliability</strong>: grid power is expensive and unstable in many markets, so heavy fuel oil (HFO), diesel, LPG, or natural gas (where available, e.g., Nigeria, Tanzania, Mozambique, Egypt) often carries the melting load. Second, <strong>batch and cullet supply chains</strong>: soda ash, silica sand, limestone, and dolomite may be imported at high landed cost, making well-sorted cullet a strategic raw material. Third, <strong>refractory and spares lead times</strong>: a cracked crown or worn throat can idle a plant for weeks if replacement bricks must be imported. Every operating decision below should be read through that lens.</p>

<h3>1. Furnace Types: Matching the Furnace to the Business Model</h3>
<p><strong>Pot furnaces</strong> melt discrete batches in clay pots. They suit low-volume, high-mix production: art glass, beads, laboratory ware, and small decorative items. Capital cost is low and colour changes are easy, but fuel per kilogram is high and output is limited. In Africa, pot furnaces are common in artisan clusters (e.g., glass bead and recycled-glass studios in Ghana, Kenya, and South Africa).</p>
<p><strong>Day tanks</strong> melt a batch and are worked out within a day or shift. They are a middle option for small tableware and pressed-glass producers who need more volume than pots but cannot justify a continuous tank. <strong>Continuous tanks</strong> melt continuously with batch charged at one end and conditioned glass drawn at the other. They are the backbone of container, flat, and large tableware production. A continuous tank only pays off when you can keep it fed and keep it full running a tank intermittently destroys both refractories and economics.</p>

<h3>2. Melting Kinetics and Energy Efficiency</h3>
<p>Melting is a sequence: batch heating, solid-state reactions, silicate formation, dissolution of residual sand, and fining. The slowest step usually sand dissolution controls pull rate. Energy efficiency improves when you (a) keep a stable, deep batch blanket to capture heat, (b) use well-graded, dry batch, (c) maximise good cullet, and (d) recover flue-gas heat through regenerators or recuperators. Every 10 C of unnecessary superheat costs roughly 12% extra fuel; every 10% increase in clean cullet typically saves 23% energy and reduces batch cost.</p>

<h3>3. Refining and Homogenisation</h3>
<p>Refining removes bubbles; homogenisation removes cord and composition gradients. Both rely on temperature, time, and controlled mixing. Fining agents sodium sulphate, antimony oxide, arsenic oxide, or tin oxide release gas at high temperature to sweep small bubbles into larger ones that rise faster. Homogenisation is aided by convection currents, bubblers, and stirrers. In practice, the most common African quality complaints seed, blister, and cord trace back to unstable melting temperature, wet or segregated batch, or contaminated cullet.</p>

<h3>4. Bubbling and Stirring Techniques</h3>
<p><strong>Bubbling</strong> injects air or gas through the tank bottom to create controlled upward currents. It accelerates homogenisation, helps remove dissolved gases, and can improve heat transfer. Too much bubbling, however, increases refractory wear and can pull batch into the refining zone. <strong>Stirring</strong> uses mechanical or gas-driven stirrers, mainly in the working end or forehearth, to even out temperature and composition before forming. In small operations, a simple bubbler in the working end often delivers most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost of a full bubbling system.</p>

<h3>5. Temperature Control and Glass Quality</h3>
<p>Temperature stability matters more than any single setpoint. Fluctuations of 10 C in the working end can change gob weight, viscosity, and defect rates. Use calibrated thermocouples and periodic optical checks; log every shift. In markets with unstable power, keep a manual override and a written response plan for burner or power trips.</p>

<h3>6. Furnace Refractories and Maintenance</h3>
<p>Refractories are the single largest maintenance cost in most African glass plants, and imported brick lead times can be 816 weeks. Choose refractories by zone: fused-cast AZS in high-wear melting areas, silica crown where conditions allow, and high-alumina or zircon in throats and working ends. Track hot-spot temperatures, crown expansion, and glass level daily. Weld, patch, or hot-repair where safe; plan cold repairs around market demand, not during peak season.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b"> SAFETY WARNING Molten Glass and Furnace Hazards</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0">Molten glass at 14001550 C causes catastrophic burns. Always wear full PPE: face shield, aluminised coat, heat-resistant gloves, and safety boots. Never stand directly in front of a bubbler port, burner port, or peephole during inspection. Ensure adequate ventilation furnace flue gases and fining agents can release toxic fumes (SO, NO, arsenic/antimony vapours). Keep a dry sand or CO extinguisher at every furnace station; never use water on a molten glass or metal spill. In many African plants, heat stress and dehydration are underrated risks enforce hydration breaks and shaded rest areas.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Batch quality:</strong> Verify sand grading, soda ash moisture, and cullet cleanliness before charging; reject contaminated cullet.</li>
<li><strong>Furnace level:</strong> Keep glass level within 2 mm of setpoint; log every hour.</li>
<li><strong>Temperature:</strong> Check all zones against setpoints each shift; investigate any drift over 10 C.</li>
<li><strong>Bubbling/stirring:</strong> Confirm gas pressure and flow; inspect bubbler ports for blockage weekly.</li>
<li><strong>Refractory watch:</strong> Record hot-spot temperatures and crown expansion daily; flag any rise over 20 C.</li>
<li><strong>Energy log:</strong> Track fuel or kWh per tonne melted; investigate any 5% week-on-week increase.</li>
<li><strong>Defect review:</strong> Sample and classify defects (seed, cord, stones) each shift; trace to furnace or forming cause.</li>
<li><strong>Safety:</strong> Confirm PPE, ventilation, extinguishers, and hydration before every furnace intervention.</li>
</ol>
