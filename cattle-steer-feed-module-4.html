Lesson 4.1~12 min

Finishing Energy Requirements and Targets

In this lesson
  • State the energy density ranges of high, moderate and low concentrate finishing diets
  • Calculate days on feed and total feed requirement for a target finish weight
  • Decide the finishing endpoint on frame size and market rather than on habit

Finishing is the phase where feed costs the most per kilogram and the animal converts it least efficiently, because it is depositing fat rather than lean tissue. That combination makes finishing the phase where careless decisions are punished hardest, and where arithmetic done before the cattle arrive pays best.

Start with requirements. A 1,200 lb steer has a maintenance requirement of roughly 6.0 to 7.0 Mcal NEm per day. Gaining 3 lb a day adds 6.8 to 7.2 Mcal NEg per day on top. Protein requirement is lower than earlier in life: 600 kg finishing cattle gaining 2.0 to 3.5 lb a day need only 8.5 to 9.5 percent crude protein of diet dry matter, because intake is large and the tissue being laid down is fat rather than protein.

The diet must therefore be energy-dense. Published finishing diet energy densities are 1.45 to 1.65 Mcal NEg per lb for high-concentrate diets at 80 to 90 percent concentrate, 1.30 to 1.45 Mcal NEg per lb for moderate-concentrate diets at 60 to 80 percent, and 1.15 to 1.30 Mcal NEg per lb for lower-concentrate diets at 40 to 60 percent. Feed conversion on a high-concentrate feedlot diet runs 5.5:1 to 6.5:1, with daily gain of 3.0 to 4.0 lb per day in the terminal phase, and a typical time to finish of 150 to 180 days taking cattle from 600 lb to 1,200 lb. Compare that with the forage-only alternative at 8:1 to 12:1 conversion, 1.0 to 2.0 lb per day of gain, and 24 to 36 months from birth.

Worked example. You buy steers at 280 kg (617 lb) and want to finish at 500 kg (1,100 lb). That is 220 kg of gain. At a target of 1.4 kg per day, days on feed = 220 / 1.4 = 157 days, which sits inside the published 150 to 180 day window. At a feed conversion of 6.5:1 on a dry matter basis, total dry matter needed is 220 x 6.5 = 1,430 kg per head. If your finishing ration costs 0.26 USD per kg of dry matter, total feed cost is 1,430 x 0.26 = 372 USD per head, or 372 / 220 = 1.69 USD per kg of gain.

Now the break-even. Purchase at 280 kg and 2.10 USD per kg live weight is 588 USD. Add 372 USD of feed and, say, 55 USD of health, labour, transport and marketing. Total cost is 1,015 USD for a 500 kg animal, which is a break-even of 1,015 / 500 = 2.03 USD per kg live weight. If the finished market is paying 2.35 USD per kg, the margin is 0.32 x 500 = 160 USD per head. If it is paying 2.00 USD, you lose 15 USD per head and would have been better off selling the store animal. Run this before you buy the cattle, every time, and re-run it when feed prices move.

Frame size decides the endpoint. Published guidance notes that large-frame cattle may reach an acceptable finish on a lower energy density, saving cost, while small-frame cattle need a higher energy density to avoid finishing over-fat before they reach a saleable weight. In practice, a small-framed local-breed steer put on a long, hard finishing ration lays down external fat that the buyer will not pay for. Matching the plan to the animal in front of you is worth more than copying a ration from a different system.

Economic optimisation is the real skill here. Most feedlots balance energy density against ingredient cost: marginal gain efficiency improves as energy density rises, but whether that pays depends entirely on the ratio between feed price and beef price. When grain is cheap relative to beef, push energy density. When grain is expensive, a moderate-concentrate diet over a slightly longer period often returns more. The full economics of this trade-off are developed in Module 12.

A named African case. Feedlots around Kaduna in northern Nigeria buy in store cattle from pastoral systems and finish them for southern urban markets on rations built around maize, wheat offal, cottonseed cake and cowpea residues. The operations that survive price swings are the ones that recalculate break-even at purchase rather than assuming last season's margin, because the price of maize in Nigeria can move enough within one feeding period to erase a whole pen's profit.

Write the break-even down before the cattle arrive. It is the only number that matters.

Grain-Based Finishing Rations

In this lesson
  • Design a step-up schedule from a forage diet to a high-concentrate finishing diet
  • Hold the fibre floor while raising concentrate to the target level
  • Apply by-product inclusion limits and correct their mineral imbalances

The finishing ration itself is not complicated. Getting the animal onto it without an acidosis wreck is where the skill lies, and where badly run feedlots lose cattle in the first three weeks.

The destination is a diet of 80 to 90 percent concentrate if you are running a high-energy finish, delivering 1.45 to 1.65 Mcal NEg per lb. The starting point is whatever the animal arrived on, usually forage. You cannot jump between them. The governing rules are: change no more than 10 to 15 percent of diet dry matter at a step, allow two to three weeks for the rumen microbial population to adapt to a substantially new feed, and never let forage NDF fall below 15 to 16 percent of total diet dry matter, which is the absolute floor before acidosis and milk fat depression. Note that feedlot practice runs close to the edge here: published guidance describes high-concentrate feedlot diets as needing only 5 to 10 percent forage for rumen health, which is why the fibre floor must be checked at every step rather than assumed.

Worked step-up. A 300 kg steer with a total dry matter intake of about 8 kg a day, arriving on grass hay.

Week 1: 6.5 kg hay, 1.5 kg concentrate. Concentrate share 1.5 / 8 = 19 percent. Week 2: 5.5 kg hay, 2.5 kg concentrate. Concentrate 31 percent. Step size 12 percent - inside the rule. Week 3: 4.5 kg hay, 3.5 kg concentrate. Concentrate 44 percent. Week 4: 3.5 kg hay, 4.5 kg concentrate. Concentrate 56 percent. Week 5: 2.5 kg hay, 5.5 kg concentrate. Concentrate 69 percent. Week 6: 1.6 kg hay, 6.4 kg concentrate. Concentrate 80 percent.

Check the fibre floor at week 6. If the hay is 62 percent NDF, forage NDF is 1.6 x 0.62 = 0.99 kg, which is 0.99 / 8 = 12.4 percent of diet dry matter. That is below the 15 to 16 percent floor. You must either stop at week 5, or raise the forage back to about 2.0 kg (2.0 x 0.62 = 1.24 kg, which is 15.5 percent), or use a higher-NDF fibre source. This is exactly the calculation most operators skip, and exactly where the losses occur.

Also remember the maximum concentrate guideline of roughly 2 to 3 lb per 100 lb of body weight per day. A 300 kg (660 lb) steer at 3 lb per 100 lb is 19.8 lb, about 9 kg as-fed - so 6.4 kg of concentrate dry matter is within that ceiling, but a heavier ration on a lighter animal would not be.

The other quiet effect worth knowing: published digestibility adjustments show starch digestibility falling by about 1.0 percentage unit per unit of DMI as a percent of body weight above 3.5 percent, and NDF digestibility falling 1.1 units on the same basis plus 0.59 units per percentage unit of dietary starch. In short, the harder you push intake and starch, the less of each additional kilogram you capture. There is a point beyond which more grain buys less than it costs.

By-products are usually how an African feedlot makes a grain ration affordable, and each has a ceiling. Distillers dried grains at 50 percent of diet dry matter maximum, with sulphur toxicity above that and a high phosphorus to calcium ratio that must be corrected by adding calcium. Wet corn gluten feed at 25 to 30 percent. Wet brewers grains at 30 to 40 percent, with about 15 percent storage losses and palatability problems above 30 percent. Soybean hulls at 15 to 20 percent, useful precisely because they carry NDF and can help hold the fibre floor. Beet pulp at 15 to 20 percent. Whole cottonseed at 25 percent and no more than 0.5 percent of body weight daily. Overall, by-products should not exceed 50 percent of the concentrate fraction.

Practical management alongside the arithmetic: feed at the same times every day, split into at least two feeds, keep the bunk consistent rather than empty then overfull, keep clean water available at all times, check mixer accuracy weekly, and inspect grain for mould and off odours before it goes in - the aflatoxin feed limit is 20 micrograms per kg and ergot causes clinical signs at around 2 mg/kg of ergotine.

A named African case. Feedlots in Egypt's Nile Delta finish cattle on rations combining maize, wheat bran, rice by-products and berseem clover. Where berseem is available fresh it does double duty as the fibre component and a protein source, and operators who keep a genuine long-fibre component in the bunk through the whole step-up report far fewer digestive upsets than those who chase energy density from week one.

Lesson 4.3~12 min

Implant Interaction with Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Describe what growth implants do and the published effect sizes on gain and efficiency
  • Explain why an implanted animal must be fed differently from a non-implanted one
  • Verify the legal status of implants with your own national regulator before use

Growth implants are small pellets placed under the skin of the ear that release anabolic compounds slowly over the feeding period. They are one of the oldest and most studied feedlot technologies, and they are also among the most tightly regulated, so this lesson has two halves: what the published data say, and what you must check before you act on it.

The published data first. Three classes are described. Estrogens, principally estradiol, are steroid implants reported to improve average daily gain by roughly 10 to 15 percent. Androgens, principally trenbolone acetate, are anabolic steroids described as synergistic with estrogens. Combination implants are used in staged releases. Timing is typically an implant on arrival, with some protocols using re-implantation at the mid-feeding period. Across implant use, published performance is an average daily gain increase of typically 10 to 15 percent with feed efficiency improving 6 to 8 percent. The source material is explicit that specific implant protocols, dosages and approvals vary and require veterinary oversight and current regulatory verification.

Now the nutrition point, which is the reason this lesson sits in a feeding course. An implant does not create nutrients. It redirects the animal's metabolism toward laying down more lean tissue. That only produces extra gain if the nutrients to build that tissue are actually present in the ration. Implant a steer on a low-protein, low-energy dry-season diet and you will see little or no benefit, because the limiting factor was never his hormonal signalling; it was his feed. On a diet already meeting the published requirements - 8.5 to 9.5 percent crude protein of diet dry matter for 600 kg finishing cattle, on an energy-dense ration of 1.45 to 1.65 Mcal NEg per lb at high concentrate - the implant has something to work with.

There is a second consequence. Faster lean deposition raises the demand for protein relative to energy, particularly earlier in the finishing period when the animal is still building muscle rather than fat. If you implant, do not simultaneously economise on the protein supplement; you will spend money on the implant to expose a protein shortage. The practical sequence is: fix the ration first, verify it against requirements on paper, and only then consider whether a growth technology adds anything.

Worked example on the economics. A steer expected to gain 1.30 kg per day over 157 days gains 204 kg. A 12 percent improvement takes daily gain to 1.46 kg and total gain to 229 kg, an extra 25 kg. At 2.35 USD per kg live weight that is 59 USD of additional revenue. A 6 to 8 percent feed efficiency improvement on 1,430 kg of dry matter saves roughly 86 to 114 kg of feed, worth 22 to 30 USD at 0.26 USD per kg. Against an implant cost of a few dollars per head, the published economics are strong - but they are calculated on a ration that was already adequate, and they assume the product is legally available to you.

That is the part you must not skip. Implant regulation differs sharply by country. The published summary states that growth implants are FDA-approved and commonly used in the United States, generally banned in the European Union where consumer preference drives market restriction, and approved with labelling in Canada and Australia. Nothing in that summary tells you the position in your own country, and the source material itself says approvals vary and require current regulatory verification.

So, plainly: before you buy, use or even plan around a growth implant, contact your own national veterinary or agricultural regulator and confirm whether the product is legally registered for beef cattle in your country, under what conditions, and with what withdrawal period. Then confirm with a veterinarian. If you sell into an export market, check the importing market's rules too, because market access can be lost by a residue that was legal where it was administered. This is not a formality. In several African countries the answer will be that the product is not registered, and using it anyway puts both your herd and your market at risk.

A named African case. South Africa has a large and well-established commercial feedlot sector finishing a substantial share of the national beef crop, operating under national veterinary medicines registration. Producers there work within a specific national registration framework, and a producer in a neighbouring country cannot assume the same products are available or lawful for them. Check your own jurisdiction, in writing, before you plan a pen around any of this.

Feed Additives and Performance Enhancers

In this lesson
  • Compare ionophores, beta-agonists and methane inhibitors on published effect size
  • Judge whether an additive pays at your own feed and cattle prices
  • Check the legal status of every additive with your national regulator before use

Feed additives are small inclusions that change how the rumen or the animal handles the ration. Some are well established and cheap; some are heavily restricted; and the marketing around all of them is considerably more confident than the evidence. Work from published effect sizes and from your own arithmetic.

Ionophores are the workhorse. Monensin and lasalocid disrupt ion transport across rumen bacterial membranes, shifting fermentation toward propionate and reducing methane and acetate. Published performance is a 6.4 percent improvement in feed efficiency, a 2.5 percent improvement in average daily gain, and a 1 to 2 percent decrease in dry matter intake. Typical inclusion is in the range of 20 to 30 grams per ton of diet, though specific dosages vary by product formulation. Adoption in United States feedlots is reported at 90 to 97 percent. A secondary benefit is a methane reduction of roughly 3 to 5 percent. Tylosin, an antibiotic used at around 11 g per ton, is listed for liver abscess prevention.

Beta-agonists are the restricted end. Ractopamine hydrochloride redirects nutrients from fat deposition toward muscle through beta-2 adrenergic signalling. Published performance over the terminal 28 to 42 days before slaughter is an average daily gain increase of 15 to 25 percent with no increase in feed intake, plus increased lean meat percentage and reduced fat percentage in the carcass. That is a very large effect size, and it comes with a very large regulatory caveat.

Methane inhibitors are the newest group. 3-nitrooxypropanol inhibits the final step in methanogenesis. Published meta-analysis gives a mean enteric methane reduction of 20 to 40 percent, with 39.0 plus or minus 5.4 percent at an optimised dosage of 123 mg per kg of dry matter, and best results above 100 mg/kg. It works better on high-starch diets, around 40 percent reduction, than on high-NDF forage diets at 20 to 25 percent, and dairy cattle respond more strongly than beef at 20 to 30 percent. No adverse effects on intake, digestibility or production are reported. Its cost-effectiveness currently depends on carbon credit markets rather than on production gains. Asparagopsis seaweed shows 50 to 80 percent reductions in research but has variable palatability and limited commercial availability. Probiotics and enzyme additives are marketed widely but published effect is variable, typically 5 to 15 percent at best on methane, with limited robust peer-reviewed support.

Worked economics for an ionophore. A pen of 50 steers each expected to eat 1,430 kg of dry matter over the finishing period. A 6.4 percent feed efficiency improvement saves 1,430 x 0.064 = 92 kg of dry matter per head, worth 92 x 0.26 = 24 USD. A 2.5 percent gain improvement on 204 kg of gain adds 5.1 kg, worth 12 USD at 2.35 USD per kg. Combined benefit is about 36 USD per head, or 1,800 USD across the pen, against an additive cost that is typically a small fraction of that. On paper this is the best-value technology in the list, which is why adoption is so high where it is approved.

Now the regulatory reality, and it is not optional reading. The published material is explicit that specific dosages, approval status and efficacy claims require verification with current labels and veterinary consultation, and that regulations change and international trade considerations are critical. Ionophores are FDA-approved in the United States. Ractopamine is FDA-approved in the United States for the terminal phase only, and is banned in the European Union and Canada, with some international markets restricting imports carrying ractopamine residues. 3-NOP has FDA approval as a feed ingredient with availability in the EU and North America, and current approvals should be checked.

None of that tells you what is lawful where you farm. Before you buy or feed any of these products, contact your own national veterinary or agricultural regulator, confirm in writing whether the product is registered for beef cattle in your country, and confirm the withdrawal period. Then discuss it with a veterinarian. If you export, or sell to an abattoir that exports, check the destination market's rules as well - a residue that is legal at home can close a market abroad.

A named African case. Namibia and Botswana both hold beef export access to the European Union, and that access depends on a national residue control and traceability system. For producers feeding into those chains, an additive banned in the destination market is not a marginal compliance question; it is the difference between a premium export price and a domestic one. Ask your abattoir what it will and will not accept before you mix anything into a ration.

Carcass Quality and Marbling

In this lesson
  • Explain how diet and time on feed drive marbling and external fat deposition
  • Distinguish the carcass outcomes of grain finishing from grass finishing
  • Match the finishing plan to what your actual buyer pays for

Marbling is intramuscular fat, and it is laid down late. That single fact drives most finishing decisions, because it means the last weeks on feed are the ones that determine grade, and also the ones that deposit the external fat a buyer may not want.

Published descriptions of grain finishing are specific. Intramuscular fat development takes 6 to 8 weeks on a high-concentrate diet to reach USDA Choice or Prime equivalent grading. External fat cover deposits rapidly in the last 30 to 40 days. Lean tissue is a lower percentage of the carcass than in a grass-fed animal, because more of the carcass is internal and external fat. Pasture finishing gives a different result: marbling develops more slowly and may not reach Prime, often grading Select; external fat cover is lower, giving a lean carcass and less trim loss; and lean tissue is a higher percentage of the carcass.

The two systems also differ in fat composition. Grass-fed carcasses carry more omega-3 polyunsaturated fat and conjugated linoleic acid; grain-fed carcasses carry more omega-6 polyunsaturated fat and more marbling, which consumers generally prefer for sensory palatability in steak cuts. Fresh pasture is high in beta-carotene, which can produce a yellow fat colour - cosmetically penalised in some markets and irrelevant in others. Feedlot beef grades more consistently, which is why it dominates the steak trade; grass-fed beef is leaner and needs more careful cooking because low fat means it dries easily.

The practical question for an African producer is not which system is better in the abstract. It is: what does my buyer actually pay for? If you sell live weight into a local market with no grading system, marbling earns you nothing and every extra week of high-concentrate feeding is pure cost. If you sell to a butchery or hotel trade paying a premium for tenderness and consistency, the last 6 to 8 weeks on a high-concentrate diet is where that premium is made. If you sell into an export chain, the specification is written down and you should be feeding to it.

Worked example. A steer at 460 kg is gaining 1.35 kg a day on a finishing ration costing 0.36 USD per kg of dry matter, eating 10 kg of dry matter a day - a feed cost of 3.60 USD per head per day. Over an extra 30 days he gains 40.5 kg, reaching 500 kg, at a feed cost of 108 USD. At an ungraded live weight price of 2.20 USD per kg, that 40.5 kg is worth 89 USD - a loss of 19 USD. Now suppose those same 30 days lift the carcass into a grade paying a 0.30 USD per kg premium on the whole animal. On a 500 kg live weight animal at, say, a 55 percent dressing percentage, the carcass is 275 kg, and the premium is 275 x 0.30 = 82.50 USD. Add that to the 89 USD of extra live weight and the 30 days return 171.50 USD against 108 USD of feed - a gain of 63.50 USD. The same 30 days, the same ration, opposite answers, decided entirely by whether the buyer pays for quality.

Two cautions on the biology. First, overfeeding beyond the point of finish deposits external and internal fat, which is metabolically expensive - feed conversion worsens sharply as an animal fattens - and much of it is trimmed and not paid for. Second, a small-framed animal reaches finish at a lighter weight and will go over-fat if you keep feeding to a large-frame target, which is why published guidance notes that small-frame cattle need higher energy density over a shorter period while large-frame cattle can finish acceptably on less.

A named African case. Botswana's export abattoir system has historically paid on carcass classification for European Union access rather than on live weight alone, which gives producers a direct financial signal about condition and finish. Producers feeding for that specification face a genuinely different optimisation from a producer selling live animals at a rural auction, where a buyer's eye and the day's demand set the price. Find out which market you are actually in before you decide how long to feed.

Pre-Marketing and Finishing Adjustments

In this lesson
  • Build a pre-marketing checklist covering withdrawal periods, records and transport
  • Decide the sale date on marginal cost of gain rather than on a fixed calendar
  • Manage the final weeks to protect both animal condition and carcass value

The last few weeks of finishing decide how much of the value you have built actually reaches your bank account. Three things need managing: the withdrawal and compliance side, the economics of when to stop feeding, and the handling of the animal itself.

Withdrawal comes first because it is not negotiable. Any veterinary product or regulated feed additive carries a withdrawal period before slaughter, and the published material is explicit that specific withdrawal times and residue data require label review with current regulatory guidance - it specifically flags that beta-agonist residue clearance and organ accumulation data are limited in published standards. That means you cannot work it out from a textbook. Get the withdrawal period from the product label, confirm it with your veterinarian and with your national regulator, mark the date on a calendar in the feed store, and stop the product on that date. If you sell into an export chain, confirm the destination market's requirements as well, because a residue that is lawful where it was fed can still block a consignment. Keep written records of every product used, the dates, the animals and the withdrawal dates - many buyers will require them and a good record is also your defence if a consignment is queried.

Second, the sale date. The right time to sell is not when the animal reaches a round number. It is when the marginal cost of another kilogram of gain exceeds what that kilogram is worth. Feed conversion worsens as an animal fattens, so the cost of gain rises week by week while the value per kilogram usually does not.

Worked example. A steer eating 11 kg of dry matter a day on a ration at 0.34 USD per kg costs 3.74 USD a day to feed. Early in finishing he gains 1.4 kg a day, so the cost of gain is 3.74 / 1.4 = 2.67 USD per kg. Late in finishing, at the same intake, he gains 0.9 kg a day, so the cost of gain is 3.74 / 0.9 = 4.16 USD per kg. If live weight sells at 2.35 USD per kg, he was losing money on gain in both cases and should have been marketed earlier or bought cheaper. If a carcass grade premium applies, redo the sum including the premium across the whole carcass, as in the previous lesson. Either way, calculate weekly in the final month rather than deciding by feel.

Third, the handling. Do not change the ration abruptly in the final weeks; the 10 to 15 percent of diet dry matter step rule still applies, and an animal that goes off feed loses condition faster than it gained it. Keep clean water available at all times right up to loading. Published guidance notes that stress from transport and illness raises trace mineral requirements, so this is not the moment to run the mineral programme down. Keep vitamin A supply in mind if the ration is grain and stored hay: beta-carotene degrades 33 to 50 percent per year in storage, and a 1,200 lb animal on stored feed with no green forage is a candidate for deficiency.

Practical pre-marketing checklist:

  1. Confirm and record all withdrawal periods; mark stop dates in the feed store.
  2. Weigh the cattle. Sell on measured weight, not on your estimate of it.
  3. Recalculate the marginal cost of gain weekly through the final month.
  4. Confirm with the buyer exactly how they price and what documentation they need.
  5. Keep the ration steady; no abrupt changes in the last three weeks.
  6. Maintain minerals and clean water through to loading; transport raises requirements rather than lowering them.
  7. Plan loading for cool hours, avoid mixing unfamiliar groups, and minimise time off feed and water before slaughter.
  8. Keep your records - purchase weights and prices, ration costs, weights over time, health treatments - so you can calculate the real margin afterwards and do better next time.

A named African case. Feedlots supplying the Nairobi market through the Athi River corridor in Kenya operate on short holding periods and thin margins, and operators there consistently report that transport and handling in the final 48 hours can undo weeks of feeding through shrink and bruising. The producers who protect margin do the unglamorous things: they weigh before loading, load in the cool of the day, keep groups together, and have the buyer's documentation ready before the truck arrives.

The animal is finished when the arithmetic says so, not when the calendar does.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A high-concentrate finishing diet at 80-90% concentrate has an energy density of:

Published finishing densities are 1.45-1.65 Mcal NEg/lb at 80-90% concentrate.

2. 220 kg of gain at 1.4 kg/day requires how many days on feed?

220 / 1.4 = 157 days, which falls inside the published 150-180 day finishing window.

3. Why is the protein requirement lower in finishing than in growing?

600 kg finishing cattle need only 8.5-9.5% crude protein of diet DM because intake is large and fat deposition dominates.

4. Small-frame cattle on a long high-energy finishing ration tend to:

Published guidance notes small-frame cattle finish over-fat on high energy density, while large-frame cattle can finish on less.

5. At 1,430 kg of dry matter costing 0.26 USD/kg for 220 kg of gain, feed cost per kg of gain is:

1,430 x 0.26 = 372 USD, and 372 / 220 = 1.69 USD per kg of gain.

6. At week 6 a steer eats 1.6 kg of 62% NDF hay in an 8 kg total diet. Forage NDF is:

1.6 x 0.62 = 0.99 kg, and 0.99 / 8 = 12.4% - below the 15-16% floor, so the ration must be corrected.

7. Published guidance describes high-concentrate feedlot diets as needing what forage level for rumen health?

Feedlot diets run with minimal forage, described as 5-10% forage NDF, which is why the 15-16% floor must be checked rather than assumed.

8. Soybean hulls are particularly useful in a finishing ration because they:

Soybean hulls are low in protein but high in NDF, making them an excellent fibre source at 15-20% of diet dry matter.

9. Starch digestibility falls by about how much per unit of DMI/BW above 3.5%?

Published adjustments give a fall of about 1.0 percentage unit per unit DMI/BW above 3.5%, so pushing intake returns progressively less.

10. Distillers dried grains require what mineral correction?

DDG carries a high phosphorus to calcium ratio, so published guidance says to mix it with a calcium source.

11. The published effect of growth implants on average daily gain is approximately:

Published figures put steroid implant gains at typically 10-15% of average daily gain, with feed efficiency improving 6-8%.

12. Why might an implant produce little benefit on a poor dry-season ration?

The limiting factor on a poor ration is nutrient supply, not hormonal signalling, so fixing the ration comes first.

13. Implanted cattle particularly need attention to:

Faster lean deposition raises protein demand relative to energy, so economising on the protein supplement after implanting wastes the implant.

14. The correct first step before using an implant is to:

Approvals vary sharply by country and some markets ban these products entirely; legal status and withdrawal requirements must be confirmed with your own regulator first.

15. Trenbolone acetate is described in the published material as:

The published classification lists trenbolone acetate as an anabolic androgen described as synergistic with estrogen implants.

16. The published feed efficiency improvement from monensin is:

Published figures give monensin a 6.4% feed efficiency improvement, 2.5% better average daily gain and a 1-2% reduction in dry matter intake.

17. Ractopamine is used during which period?

Published use is the terminal phase only, 28-42 days before slaughter, with reported gains of 15-25% in average daily gain.

18. 3-NOP works best on which kind of diet?

Published meta-analysis reports around 40% reduction on high starch diets versus 20-25% on high-NDF forage diets, with high dietary fat reducing efficacy.

19. Before feeding any performance additive, the producer must:

Approval status varies by country and changes over time, and some markets ban specific products outright; verification with your own regulator is required.

20. Why does an export-oriented producer need to check the destination market's additive rules?

Some markets restrict imports carrying residues of products that are approved elsewhere, so market access can be lost by an otherwise lawful additive.

21. How long on a high-concentrate diet does published guidance associate with reaching Choice or Prime grading?

Published descriptions put intramuscular fat development at 6-8 weeks on a high-concentrate diet.

22. External fat cover deposits most rapidly during:

Published material describes rapid external fat deposition in the last 30-40 days of finishing.

23. If you sell live weight into an ungraded local market, extra weeks of high-concentrate feeding are:

Without a grade premium there is nothing to pay for marbling, so only the value of the additional live weight offsets the feed bill.

24. Grass-finished carcasses are typically:

Published comparisons describe pasture-finished carcasses as leaner with lower trim loss, higher lean percentage and more omega-3 and CLA.

25. Feeding a small-framed animal to a large-frame finish target usually produces:

Small-frame cattle finish at a lighter weight; feeding beyond that deposits fat that costs feed and is largely trimmed away.

26. The correct time to market a finishing steer is when:

Feed conversion worsens as an animal fattens, so the decision is economic: stop when the next kilogram costs more than it earns.

27. A steer costs 3.74 USD a day to feed and gains 0.9 kg a day. Cost of gain is:

3.74 / 0.9 = 4.16 USD per kg of gain, well above a live weight price of 2.35 USD/kg.

28. Where should a withdrawal period be obtained from?

Published standards flag that residue and withdrawal data require current label review and veterinary guidance; they are not derivable from general references.

29. Transport and illness affect trace mineral requirements how?

Published guidance notes that stress from transport and illness increases trace mineral requirements, so supplementation should continue through to loading.

30. Why avoid abrupt ration changes in the final three weeks?

The 10-15% of diet dry matter step rule still applies; a digestive upset at this stage destroys value built over months.

Module 4 capstone

Finishing Plan and Break-Even Budget for one pen of cattle. Step 1: describe the cattle you would buy - weight, frame, breed type - and record the purchase price per kg live weight in USD and the total purchase cost. Step 2: set a target finish weight and a target average daily gain, and calculate the days on feed required. Step 3: write the full ration for each phase of a step-up from your starting diet to the finishing diet, showing the concentrate percentage at every step and demonstrating that forage NDF never falls below the published floor. Step 4: cost the ration per head per day in USD at each phase, and calculate total feed cost across the days on feed, allowing for storage losses of 3% dry feed and 15% high-moisture feeds. Step 5: calculate expected feed conversion and feed cost per kg of gain, and add non-feed costs - labour, health, transport, marketing. Step 6: calculate the break-even selling price per kg live weight and compare it against the current market. Step 7: write one paragraph on any regulated technologies you would consider using, naming your national regulatory authority and stating plainly that you would confirm legal status and withdrawal requirements with them and with a veterinarian before use. Present the whole budget as a single page a lender could read.
