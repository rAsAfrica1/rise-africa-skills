1. Water Requirements at Different Growth Stages

Onions need different amounts of water at each stage of growth. Getting this right is the difference between a good harvest and a failed crop. In the first 3 weeks after transplanting or seeding, onion roots are shallow and weak. They need light, frequent watering to keep the top 15 cm of soil moist. In dry African conditions, this means watering every 2 days if there is no rain. A simple test: stick your finger 3 cm into the soil. If it feels dry, water.

From week 4 to week 8, onions grow fast and start forming bulbs. This is when they need the most water. The roots reach about 30 cm deep, so you need to wet the soil to that depth. In sandy soils, water every 2-3 days; in clay soils, every 4-5 days. A good rule: apply 25-30 mm of water per week (about 25-30 litres per square metre). If you use a watering can, that's 2.5 to 3 full 10-litre cans per square metre per week.

When bulbs start to swell (week 9 to week 12), keep the soil evenly moist but not waterlogged. Too much water now can split bulbs or cause rot. Reduce watering to 20-25 mm per week. In hot, dry areas like the Sahel, you may need to water every 2 days, but always check the soil first.

In the final 2-3 weeks before harvest, stop watering completely. This allows the necks to dry and the bulbs to mature. If you keep watering, onions will rot in storage. In most African regions, you can stop watering when about half the tops fall over.

Remember: these are general guidelines. Always watch your crop and the weather. A rain gauge (costing about $5) helps you track rainfall. If you get 10 mm of rain, you can skip one watering. Overwatering wastes money and can leach nutrients. Underwatering stunts growth. The key is to check soil moisture regularly and adjust.

2. Drip Irrigation Systems for Onions

Drip irrigation is the most efficient way to water onions. It saves up to 60% water compared to furrow or sprinkler systems. In Africa, small-scale farmers can use simple drip kits that cost from $50 to $200 for a 100 square metre plot. These kits include a tank, filter, main line, and drip tapes with emitters.

To set up drip for onions, first prepare your beds. Onions grow best on raised beds about 1 metre wide. Lay one or two drip lines per bed, spaced 30 cm apart. The emitters should be spaced 20-30 cm along the line. This gives each onion plant access to water. You need a water source at least 1 metre above the field to create pressure, or a small pump. A 200-litre drum raised on blocks works well.

Drip irrigation works by releasing water slowly, drop by drop, right at the plant roots. This keeps the soil moist but not soaked. It also reduces weeds because only the onion row gets water. You should run the system for 30-60 minutes per day, depending on your soil and weather. In sandy soil, run it twice a day for 20 minutes. In clay soil, once a day for 40 minutes. Always check that water reaches 20 cm deep.

Maintenance is critical. Drip emitters clog easily, especially if your water has sand or dirt. Use a filter and clean it weekly. Flush the lines once a month by opening the end caps. If you see dry spots, check for clogged emitters. In many African countries, you can buy drip tape from agro-dealers or NGOs like KickStart or SolarGen.

Drip irrigation also allows you to apply fertiliser through the system (fertigation). Use water-soluble fertilisers and inject them into the tank. This saves labour and gives nutrients directly to the roots. With drip, you can grow onions even in dry seasons, as long as you have water. It is a smart investment for any serious onion farmer.

3. Sprinkler and Furrow Irrigation Methods

Sprinkler and furrow irrigation are common for onions in Africa, but they need careful management. Sprinklers spray water like rain. They are good for sandy soils and flat land. You can use a small portable sprinkler connected to a hose or a pump. A basic impact sprinkler costs about $10 and covers a 10-metre circle. Run it for 30-45 minutes to apply 10-15 mm of water.

For onions, sprinkler irrigation is best early in the season when plants are small. But as onions grow, sprinklers can cause problems. Wet leaves encourage fungal diseases like downy mildew. Also, sprinklers waste water through evaporation, especially in hot afternoons. So, irrigate early morning or late evening. In windy areas, sprinklers are uneven. You may need to move them around.

Furrow irrigation is cheaper but less efficient. You make small channels (furrows) between onion rows and let water flow. This works well in clay soils and where water is plentiful. But it uses a lot of water and can cause soil erosion. For onions, furrows should be shallow (10-15 cm deep) and water should flow slowly. Never let water touch the onion stems directly, as this causes rot.

To use furrow irrigation, dig furrows 30-40 cm apart. Let water run for 20-30 minutes, then stop. Check that water has soaked 30 cm deep. In sandy soil, water moves fast, so you may need shorter runs. In clay, water moves slowly, so run longer but less often.

Both methods require level land. If your field slopes, water will collect at the bottom and starve the top. You can use basins or borders to control flow. Also, always monitor soil moisture. A simple tensiometer (costing $30) or a shovel can tell you if water reached the roots. Remember, overhead irrigation can wash away seeds or young plants. So, for the first 2 weeks after sowing, use a gentle sprinkle or hand watering.

In many African countries, furrow irrigation is common in river valleys. But with climate change, water is scarce. Combining furrow with mulching can reduce water use by 30%. Mulch (dry grass or plastic) covers the soil and slows evaporation. This is a low-cost way to improve any irrigation method.

4. Monitoring Soil Moisture

Monitoring soil moisture is the most important skill for onion irrigation. You cannot guess. You must check. The simplest tool is your hand. Dig a small hole 15 cm deep near the onion roots. Take a handful of soil and squeeze it. If it forms a ball and leaves a slight moisture mark on your palm, it is perfect. If it crumbles, it is too dry â water now. If it drips water, it is too wet â stop watering.

For more accuracy, use a tensiometer. This is a tube with a gauge that measures how hard roots must work to pull water. For onions, keep the reading between 20 and 30 centibars (cb). If it reads above 40 cb, water immediately. Tensiometers cost about $30-50 and last several seasons. They are available from irrigation suppliers or online.

Another tool is a soil moisture meter. These are cheap ($10-20) and have a probe you push into the soil. They give a reading from 1 (dry) to 10 (wet). For onions, aim for 6-7. But these meters are not very accurate in clay or salty soils. Use them as a guide, not a rule.

You can also use a simple gypsum block or a homemade device: a wooden stick painted with different colours. When the stick is inserted, the colour changes with moisture. But the hand method is free and reliable if you practice.

Monitor at least twice a week, and always before and after irrigation. Keep a notebook. Write down the date, soil moisture, and how much you watered. After a few weeks, you will see patterns. For example, you might learn that in your sandy soil, onions need water every 2 days in hot weather.

Also, watch the plants. Onions show stress when they need water: leaves turn a dull grey-green and the tips start to fold. If you see this, water immediately. But do not wait for stress signs â they reduce yield.

In Africa, where water is often scarce, monitoring helps you avoid overwatering. Overwatering wastes water, leaches nutrients, and causes root diseases. Underwatering stunts bulbs. The goal is to keep soil moisture steady. A simple rain gauge ($5) also helps you track rainfall. If you get 10 mm of rain, you can skip one irrigation.

Finally, remember that soil type matters. Sandy soil dries out fast, so check daily. Clay soil holds water longer, so check every 2-3 days. Loam is in between. Adjust your monitoring to your soil.

5. Water Quality and Salinity Issues

Not all water is good for onions. Onions are sensitive to salt. If you irrigate with salty water, your onions will be small, and bulbs may rot. In many African drylands, well water or river water can be salty. You need to test your water before using it.

A simple electrical conductivity (EC) meter costs about $30. It measures salt level in water. For onions, the EC should be below 1.2 dS/m (deciSiemens per metre). If it is 1.2 to 2.0, onions will grow but yield may drop. Above 2.0, onions will struggle. If you cannot afford a meter, send a water sample to a government agricultural office or a university lab. They often test for free or low cost.

If your water is slightly salty (EC 1.2-2.0), you can still use it with care. Apply extra water to leach salts below the roots. But do not overwater, as this can raise the water table and bring more salt up. Use drip irrigation, which keeps salts away from the seed. Also, add organic matter like compost or manure. It helps the soil hold nutrients and reduces salt damage.

Another issue is water with high sodium. Sodium makes soil sticky and hard. You can add gypsum (calcium sulfate) to the soil. Gypsum costs about $20 per 50 kg bag. Apply 200-500 kg per hectare and mix into the top 15 cm. This replaces sodium with calcium.

Dirty water is also a problem. Water from rivers or ponds may contain weed seeds, bacteria, or silt. Silt clogs drip emitters. Always filter water. Use a simple sand filter or a cloth filter. For sprinklers, dirty water can block nozzles. Clean them regularly.

In coastal areas, seawater intrusion can make wells salty. Never use water with EC above 3.0 dS/m for onions. If you have no other water, consider growing salt-tolerant crops like barley instead.

Finally, test your soil too. If your soil is already salty, you may need to flush it with fresh water before planting. Onions grow best in soil with EC below 1.0 dS/m. You can buy a soil test kit for about $15.

In summary: test your water, use organic matter, and choose the right irrigation method. If salinity is a big problem, talk to your extension officer. They can help you find solutions like gypsum or better water sources.

6. Reducing Water Waste and Conservation

Water is precious in Africa. Every drop counts. Onion farmers can save water and still get a good harvest by using simple conservation methods. The first step is to reduce evaporation. Mulching is the easiest way. Spread a 5 cm layer of dry grass, straw, or plastic mulch around your onions. This keeps the soil cool and cuts water loss by up to 50%. In dry areas, mulch also stops weeds. You can use black plastic mulch, which costs about $30 for a 100-metre roll, or free grass from your farm.

Second, water at the right time. Early morning or late evening is best. Midday watering loses up to 30% to evaporation. Also, water deeply but less often. This encourages deep roots. For onions, apply 25-30 mm per week, but split into two or three applications. Do not sprinkle a little every day â that makes shallow roots that dry out fast.

Third, use drip irrigation. As we learned, drip saves up to 60% water. If you cannot afford drip, use a watering can or hose with a shut-off nozzle. Never flood your field.

Fourth, capture rainwater. In many African regions, rain is seasonal. You can dig small basins or ponds to catch runoff. A 5,000-litre tank can store enough water for a small plot during dry spells. You can also use rooftops to collect rain. A simple gutter system costs about $50.

Fifth, fix leaks. Check your pipes, hoses, and tanks for leaks. A leaking pipe can waste hundreds of litres a day. Also, keep your irrigation system clean. Clogged emitters waste water because you run the system longer.

Sixth, use soil moisture monitoring. As we discussed, do not water if the soil is already moist. A $5 rain gauge helps you skip watering after rain.

Seventh, plant windbreaks. Strong winds dry out soil and plants. A row of tall grasses or trees around your field reduces wind speed and water loss.

Eighth, choose drought-tolerant onion varieties. Some varieties like 'Red Creole' or 'Texas Grano' handle heat better. Ask your local agro-dealer.

Finally, reuse water. Greywater from your kitchen (without soap) can be used on onions if you filter it. But avoid water with chemicals.

By combining these methods, you can cut water use by half. That means you can grow more onions with the same water, or save water for other crops. In the face of climate change, conservation is not just good for the environment â it is good for your pocket.

Do not irrigate by calendar alone. Onion roots extract water mainly from the top 20 cm, so a simple hand-feel test or a cheap tensiometer placed at 15 cm and 30 cm gives better decisions than any fixed schedule. Target thresholds:

5. Reducing Water Waste and Conservation

Every millimetre saved is money in the bank. Practical, low-cost conservation measures for African onion farms:

  • Mulch: straw or plastic mulch cuts evaporation 30â50% and suppresses weeds.
  • Deficit irrigation at maturation: deliberate reduction after bulbing saves 15â20% water with minimal yield loss.
  • Solar-powered pumping: cuts fuel cost and enables precise scheduling; payback 2â4 years.
  • Night/early morning irrigation: reduces evaporation losses by up to 25%.
  • Regular emitter flushing: clogged emitters waste water unevenly and reduce yield.
  • Rainwater harvesting: lined ponds or tanks capture runoff for dry-spell supplementation.
