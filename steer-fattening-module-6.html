Module 6

Daily Management and Tracking Gain

This module turns your fattening pen into a managed system rather than a place where cattle are fed. You will build a fixed daily routine, learn to read the feed bunk and adjust feed with confidence, keep water above suspicion, and measure gain with a heart girth tape so you know at any point whether the cycle is making money. It also covers spotting the animal that has gone off feed, and managing heat, mud and manure.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • You will be able to run a fixed daily routine covering feeding, pen walking, water checks and record keeping.
  • You will be able to feed roughage and concentrate in the correct order and make ration changes without causing digestive upsets.
  • You will be able to score a feed bunk and use that score to decide today's feed amount.
  • You will be able to check, clean and size water troughs so water is never the factor limiting gain.
  • You will be able to estimate liveweight with a heart girth tape and calculate average daily gain over a sensible interval.
  • You will be able to identify an animal that has gone off feed, and recognise and act on heat stress and muddy pen conditions.

Lesson 6.114 min

The Daily Routine and Why Consistency Matters

In this lesson
  • Lay out a fixed daily order of work for the pen
  • Explain why irregular feeding costs gain
  • Set up a simple written daily record

A fattening pen is a factory with one product: kilograms of gain. The machine that makes those kilograms is the rumen, and the rumen is a slow, conservative organ. It builds a population of microbes that suits the feed it gets, and it takes days to rebuild that population after a change. That is the whole reason for a fixed daily routine. Cattle fed the same feed, in the same order, at the same hours, by the same person, eat more and gain more than cattle fed the same total feed at random times.

The shape of a good day

Build your day around two feeding events roughly 10 to 12 hours apart, for example 07:00 and 17:00. Around those, fit the checks that catch problems while they are still cheap to fix.

  1. Before you feed, walk the pen. This is the most valuable ten minutes of your day. Cattle that have been standing all night will rise and move. Watch which one does not rise, which one rises last, which one stretches oddly, which one stands with its head down at the back.
  2. Read the bunk before you put new feed in. Record what is left from yesterday. If you tip fresh feed on top of yesterday's leftovers you have destroyed your only intake record and you are feeding stale feed underneath.
  3. Feed roughage first, then concentrate, or feed a properly mixed ration. Never let hungry cattle meet a pile of grain with an empty rumen.
  4. Check water while they are eating. Cattle drink heavily after feeding. Empty or dirty troughs at that moment cost you intake.
  5. Second walk in the evening feed. Same discipline: watch them come to the bunk, count the animals that come.
  6. Write the day down before you leave. Feed offered, feed refused, water problems, any animal treated, any animal watched.

Why consistency pays

Three things happen when you feed at irregular hours. First, cattle that wait longer than usual eat faster and take a bigger slug of starch at once, which drops rumen pH and pushes them toward acidosis. Second, an animal that is off feed is invisible, because you cannot tell a sick animal from an animal that is simply waiting. Third, your own records become meaningless, so you cannot tell whether a drop in intake is a feeding error or a disease.

The one-person rule

Where possible, the same person feeds and walks the pen every day. Cattle habituate to a person's voice, pace and clothing. More importantly, that person builds a memory of what normal looks like in this pen, with these animals. A stranger sees twenty steers. The regular feeder sees that number 14 is standing further back than usual. That is what finds disease on day one instead of day three.

Move quietly

Rough handling costs money in ways you never see on a receipt. Shouting, dogs, sticks and sudden movement raise stress hormones, cut feed intake for the rest of the day, and in the last week before slaughter can damage meat quality by using up muscle glycogen. Walk at a steady pace, talk quietly and continuously so nothing you do is a surprise, and never run cattle in a pen.

Your written record

Keep one hard-covered exercise book at the pen, not on your phone. One page per day, with columns for: date, roughage offered, concentrate offered, refusals, water checked yes or no, animals treated with what, and a notes line. This book is what tells you, at day 45, whether your ration is working or whether you are quietly losing money. It is also what a buyer, a bank or a vet will ask to see.

Consistency is free. It is the only input in this business that costs you nothing and reliably adds gain.

Lesson 6.215 min

Feeding Times, Feeding Order and Safe Ration Changes

In this lesson
  • Feed roughage and concentrate in the correct order
  • Step cattle up onto a finishing ration safely
  • Handle a missed feed without causing acidosis

Feeding order and timing are not fussiness. They control how fast starch reaches the rumen microbes, and that single factor decides whether your cattle gain steadily or crash with acidosis in week three.

Set the clock and keep it

Pick two feeding times about 10 to 12 hours apart and hold them within roughly half an hour every day, including weekends. If you can only feed once a day, feed at the same hour and accept that intake will usually be a little lower and the acidosis risk a little higher, because the whole day's starch arrives in one meal.

The correct order

  1. Roughage first. Put out hay, stover, silage or standing forage and let cattle take the edge off their hunger. Roughage forms the rumen mat, stimulates chewing, and chewing produces saliva, which is the animal's own natural buffer against acid.
  2. Concentrate second, about 20 to 40 minutes later, once most animals have roughage in them.
  3. Or mix and feed once. A properly mixed total ration, where the animal cannot sort grain out of forage, is better than any feeding order. If you mix by hand or in a drum mixer, mix until you cannot pick a mouthful that is all grain.

The failure to avoid is grain onto an empty rumen after a long gap. That is how healthy steers end up dead or permanently damaged overnight.

Stopping the sorters and the bullies

In every pen, two or three animals eat far more concentrate than their share and two or three eat far less. Both ends lose you money: the bullies get acidosis, the shy ones get no gain. Controls that work:

  • Give enough bunk space that all animals can eat at the same time when you feed a restricted concentrate meal. General feedlot guidance is roughly 45 to 60 cm of bunk per head where feed is limited, and less where feed is always available.
  • Spread concentrate along the whole bunk length, not in three heaps.
  • Damp the mix very lightly with water or molasses solution so fine grain sticks to the forage instead of sifting to the bottom.
  • Sort out a persistent bully into a separate small pen if it is repeatedly keeping others off feed.

Making ration changes safely

Every change to the ration is a change to the microbe population. The rule is: change one thing at a time, and change it slowly.

  • Stepping up concentrate at the start. Bring cattle from a mostly forage diet to a high concentrate finishing diet over about two to three weeks, in three or four steps, holding each step for at least three to five days before the next increase. Never jump.
  • Changing energy source (for example maize to a different grain or to a milling by-product): make the swap over about seven days, replacing roughly a quarter of the old ingredient every second day.
  • Changing forage (for example a new batch of hay or a change from stover to silage): blend the old and new for at least five days.
  • Never change ration and do something else stressful on the same day. No ration step-up on a dipping day, a weighing day or a transport day.

When feeding is interrupted

If cattle miss a concentrate feed for any reason (rain, illness, a supplier failure, you were away), do not make it up by feeding a double meal. Go back to about half to two thirds of the normal concentrate at the next feed and step back up over two or three days. A missed meal costs you a little gain. A double meal can cost you an animal.

Keep a written note of every ration change with the date. When gain slows or animals go loose, the first question is always: what changed, and when.

Lesson 6.314 min

Reading the Feed Bunk and Adjusting Feed

In this lesson
  • Score a bunk on a simple 0 to 3 scale
  • Apply small, safe feed adjustments from the score
  • Diagnose the cause behind a bad bunk score

Bunk reading is the skill that separates a fattener who guesses from one who manages. It means looking at what is left in the trough each morning before you feed, giving it a score, and using that score to decide today's feed amount. It costs nothing and it is the closest thing you have to a daily intake measurement.

Score the bunk before you touch it

Walk the length of the bunk at the same time every morning, before any fresh feed goes in, and give one score for the pen.

ScoreWhat you seeWhat it means
0Bunk completely empty and licked clean, cattle waiting and vocalThey ate everything and probably wanted more
1Scattered crumbs, a thin dusting onlyClose to right. This is the target for most days
2A thin even layer of feed covering the bunk floorSlightly over-fed, or intake is starting to fall
3Clear piles or a thick layer, more than roughly a tenth of what you fedSomething is wrong. Do not add more feed

The adjustment rules

Work with small changes. Feed intake is a slow animal, not a switch.

  • Score 0 two days running: increase the concentrate by roughly 5 percent (about half a kilogram per head on a ten kilogram feed) and hold for two days before deciding again.
  • Score 1: feed the same amount. Do nothing. Most of your days should look like this.
  • Score 2: feed the same amount, or drop by about 5 percent if it is the second day.
  • Score 3: cut the concentrate by roughly 10 to 20 percent, remove and discard the stale feed, and go and find the cause before you increase again.

Never increase two days in a row on a single good bunk score, and never increase after a score of 2 or 3. The most expensive mistake in bunk management is chasing intake upward too fast and then having the pen crash into acidosis, which costs you far more than the extra kilograms you were chasing.

Sanity-check against body weight

Cattle on a good finishing ration commonly eat dry matter equal to about 2 to 3 percent of their body weight per day. For a 350 kg steer that is roughly 7 to 10.5 kg of dry matter. Remember that is dry matter, not fresh weight: green forage and silage may be only a quarter to a half dry matter, so the bucket weight is much higher. If your calculated intake is far outside that range, your feed weights, your dry matter assumption or your weight estimate is wrong.

Reading the causes behind a bad score

A score of 3 is a question, not an answer. Work through this list in order:

  1. Water: is a trough empty, blocked, leaking or foul? Cattle that cannot drink stop eating within hours.
  2. Weather: a very hot day, or the first hot day after cool weather, drops intake sharply.
  3. Feed quality: has a new bag or bale come in? Smell it and feel it. Musty, hot, mouldy or caked feed is refused.
  4. Sorting: is the leftover mostly coarse stalk, meaning they ate the grain and left the fibre? That is a mixing problem and an acidosis risk.
  5. Health: count the animals at the bunk. If two or three are standing away, the pen average is hiding sick individuals.
  6. A ration change or a stressful event in the last 48 hours.

Write it down

One line per day: date, kilograms offered, bunk score, action taken. After three weeks that column of scores tells you more about your ration than any laboratory report, because it shows the trend. A slow drift from mostly 1s to mostly 2s means intake is falling and you should be looking for a reason today, not next month.

Lesson 6.413 min

Water, Troughs and Trough Hygiene

In this lesson
  • Estimate whether your water supply can meet demand
  • Run a five-point daily water check
  • Clean troughs on a schedule and judge water quality

Water is the cheapest input and the fastest way to lose gain. A steer that cannot drink stops eating within a few hours, and one hot day with a dry trough can undo a week of gain across the whole pen. Water gets checked every single day, twice, without exception.

How much they need

Water intake rises with body weight, with dry matter intake and above all with temperature. Cattle on dry finishing rations drink far more than cattle on green forage. A useful working figure from feeding standards is roughly 3 to 5 litres of water per kilogram of dry matter eaten, which for finishing cattle commonly works out somewhere around 25 to 50 litres per head per day in mild weather, and considerably more in hot weather. Do not treat any single number as fixed. Instead, size your supply so that it can never be the limit, and watch the trough.

Trough sizing and placement

  • Provide enough water surface that several animals can drink at once. General guidance is in the order of one drinking space, or roughly half a metre of trough edge, for every 15 to 25 head, with more where the pen is large or hot.
  • The trough must refill fast. A trough that empties in the ten minutes after feeding and takes an hour to refill is functionally too small, whatever its volume.
  • Place troughs where you walk past them, in shade if possible, and away from the feed bunk so drinkers do not block eaters.
  • Set the rim at roughly shoulder height of the smallest animal, and build a hard apron of stone or concrete around it so the approach does not turn into a mud hole.

The daily water check

  1. Look at the water surface. Can you see the bottom of the trough clearly? If not, it needs cleaning.
  2. Put your hand in. Feel for slime on the walls and floor, and for grit or sludge at the bottom.
  3. Smell it. Sour, sulphurous or manure smells mean cattle will drink less than they need even if the trough is full.
  4. Watch the float valve or inlet actually run. A stuck float is the single most common cause of a dry trough.
  5. Look for a wet patch, a soft spot or a running noise that says a pipe is leaking underground.

Cleaning routine

Scrub troughs at least once a week, and more often in hot weather or where birds roost above them. Drain, scrub the walls and floor with a stiff brush to remove the green slime, rinse and refill. Slime is a bacterial film that both puts cattle off and holds pathogens. If you use any disinfectant, rinse thoroughly and refill before letting cattle back, and never use a product not intended for livestock drinking water.

Where troughs are filled by hand from drums or a bowser, clean the drums too, and never use containers that have held agricultural chemicals, fuel or treated seed. That warning is not theoretical: reused chemical drums have killed cattle.

Water source and quality

Borehole water in parts of the region can be high in salts or sulphates, which reduces intake and can cause scouring. If cattle drink poorly from a source that is clean and full, suspect the water itself and ask your local extension or veterinary office whether water testing is available. Surface water from dams and rivers can carry liver fluke snails and bacterial contamination, so where you have a choice, trough water beats letting cattle wade into a dam.

When water has been off

If a supply has failed and cattle have been without water for hours, do not simply open the trough and walk away. Thirsty cattle can drink dangerously fast. Refill and let them drink in a controlled way, watch for animals that will not leave the trough, and expect intake and dung consistency to take a day or two to settle. Then fix the cause the same day.

Lesson 6.515 min

Estimating Weight with a Girth Tape and Calculating ADG

In this lesson
  • Take a repeatable heart girth measurement
  • Convert girth to an estimated liveweight
  • Calculate ADG over a sensible interval and act on it

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Without a weighbridge, the heart girth tape is the standard low-cost tool for estimating liveweight, and it is good enough to run a fattening business on, provided you understand what it is: an estimate, commonly within roughly 5 to 10 percent of true weight, and less reliable at the extremes of very fat or very thin animals and on breeds different from those the tape was built for.

How to take a girth measurement correctly

  1. Weigh at the same time of day every time, ideally in the morning before feeding. A full gut can change the reading meaningfully.
  2. Restrain the animal in a crush or race so it is standing square, head up in a normal position, weight on all four feet. A stretched or bunched animal reads wrong.
  3. Pass the tape around the chest immediately behind the front legs and over the highest point of the shoulder area, in the hollow just behind the elbow.
  4. Pull the tape snug against the hide, firm enough to flatten the hair but not to dent the animal. The same tension every time matters more than the exact tension.
  5. Read the tape at the end of a normal breath out, not while the animal is holding its breath.
  6. Take the measurement twice. If the two differ by more than about 2 cm, take a third and use the middle value.

Turning centimetres into kilograms

A printed weigh band gives you kilograms directly. If you only have a plain tape measure, the long-standing Schaeffer formula is widely used:

Estimated liveweight in kg = (heart girth in cm x heart girth in cm x body length in cm) divided by 10840

Body length is measured from the point of the shoulder back to the pin bone. This formula was developed on European-type cattle and will be biased on Bos indicus and indigenous breeds. That bias does not matter much for your purpose, because you are mostly interested in the change over time, and a consistent bias cancels out when you subtract.

Calculating average daily gain

ADG in kg per day = (weight now minus weight last time) divided by days between weighings.

Worked example: a steer taped at 312 kg on 1 March and 361 kg on 30 March. That is 49 kg over 29 days, so ADG = 49 divided by 29 = 1.69 kg per day, which is high and should make you suspect a measurement error before you celebrate. Re-tape and check.

Weigh often enough, but not too often

Weigh every 28 to 30 days, and never at intervals shorter than about 21 days. The reason is arithmetic: if the tape is out by 10 kg either way and you weigh a week apart, an error of 20 kg spread over 7 days looks like nearly 3 kg per day of phantom gain or loss. Over a month, the same error is diluted to well under half a kilogram per day. Short intervals produce noise that will make you change a ration that was working.

What the numbers should look like

On a well balanced finishing ration, healthy cattle commonly gain in the range of 1.0 to 1.5 kg per day, with poorer rations, poor genetics, parasites or heat giving considerably less. Compare your pen against itself over time rather than against someone else's figures.

Acting on the result

  • Pen ADG below expectation and bunk scores high: an intake problem. Check water, feed quality, heat, parasites.
  • Pen ADG below expectation and bunk clean every day: you are underfeeding, or the ration is short of energy or protein.
  • One or two animals far below the pen: individual problem. Examine them, do not change the pen ration.

Record every animal's tag, girth and estimated weight on a single sheet each weighing day, and keep the sheets. That run of numbers is your evidence of what your system actually delivers.

Lesson 6.615 min

Off-Feed Animals, Heat Stress and Pen Conditions

In this lesson
  • Spot the animal that has gone off feed within a day
  • Recognise heat stress and act in the right order
  • Manage mud and manure so cattle always have a dry place to lie

Most of the money lost in a fattening pen is lost by individual animals that quietly stop eating, and by whole pens that stop eating because they are hot or standing in mud. Both are found by walking, not by reading.

Finding the animal that has gone off feed

Do this at every feeding, from outside the pen, before you disturb them. You are looking for the animal that is different from its neighbours.

  • Does not come to the bunk, or comes and stands without eating. This is the strongest single sign.
  • Head and ears down. A healthy steer holds its ears alert. Drooping ears with a dull eye is an early sign of fever.
  • Stands apart at the back of the pen or in a corner, away from the group.
  • Hollow left flank in front of the hip. A well fed animal is filled out there. A sunken triangle means it has not eaten.
  • Gait and posture: stiff, arched back, reluctant to move, weight shifted off one foot.
  • Dung: too loose and watery, too hard and pelleted, or containing whole undigested grain. Note which animal it came from if you can.
  • Breathing: faster or deeper than the others, nostrils flared, head extended forward.
  • Nose and coat: dry crusted muzzle instead of moist, staring dull coat, discharge from nose or eyes.

What to do when you find one

  1. Mark it now with a stock crayon or a coloured leg band so you can find it again in the crowd.
  2. Move it quietly to a hospital or observation pen with easy access to clean water and good hay.
  3. Take its temperature rectally. Normal adult cattle sit around 38.0 to 39.0 degrees Celsius. Above about 39.5 to 40 degrees, treat it as fever and involve your vet.
  4. Write down: tag, date, what you saw, temperature, what you did.
  5. If it is not eating normally within 24 hours, or if it has fever, laboured breathing, bloat or cannot stand, call the vet. Do not work through a shelf of leftover medicines on your own.

Heat stress

Heat costs gain long before it kills. Watch for: bunching in shade, standing rather than lying, drooling, open-mouthed breathing and obvious panting, and a sharp drop in daytime intake with more eating at night. Rapid shallow breathing well above the normal resting rate of roughly 15 to 30 breaths per minute is your practical field signal.

Actions that work, in order of value:

  • Water first. Add trough capacity before the hot season, not during it. Heat multiplies water demand.
  • Shade. Even simple shade cloth or thatch on poles helps. Feedlot guidance commonly suggests in the order of 2 to 4 square metres of shade per head, oriented so the shadow moves across the pen and the ground under it dries.
  • Shift feeding later. Move the larger meal to late afternoon or evening so the heat of digestion happens at night.
  • Do not handle, dip, transport or weigh cattle in the heat of the day. Do that work before about 09:00.
  • Never crowd hot cattle into a race and leave them standing there.

Mud and manure

Deep mud and packed manure cut gain badly. Cattle standing in mud use energy to move, lie down less, and lying time is when they ruminate. Wet dirty hides also cost you at the abattoir and raise the risk of foot problems and skin disease.

  • Scrape manure out of the pen on a fixed schedule, at least weekly, and always before the rains.
  • Build a mound or raised dry lying area of compacted soil so cattle always have somewhere dry to lie.
  • Slope the pen so water runs off and away, and never let the drainage run into your water source.
  • Harden the high traffic strips at the feed bunk and water trough with stone, rubble or concrete.
  • Stock at a sensible density. Crowded pens go to mud far faster than pens with space.

Knowledge check

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. You have just walked up to the bunk in the morning. What should you do first?

Scoring leftovers before adding feed is your only daily intake measurement, and tipping fresh feed on stale feed destroys both the record and the feed.

2. Finishing cattle commonly eat dry matter equal to about what share of body weight per day?

About 2 to 3 percent of body weight in dry matter is the common working range, and remember it is dry matter, not fresh bucket weight.

3. Why should you avoid weighing with a girth tape every week?

The tape is only accurate to roughly 5 to 10 percent, so over a short interval the measurement error is bigger than the real gain. Weigh every 28 to 30 days.

4. Cattle missed their concentrate feed yesterday because of heavy rain. What do you do at the next feed?

A double meal after a gap is a classic route to acidosis. Feed less, then step back up gradually; the small loss of gain is far cheaper than a sick pen.

5. Which is the correct feeding order when roughage and concentrate are fed separately?

Roughage first fills the rumen, builds the fibre mat and stimulates saliva, which buffers the acid produced when the starch arrives.

Module 6 capstone

Run your pen for seven consecutive days on a written routine: two fixed feeding times, two pen walks, a daily bunk score, a daily water check and a signed record book entry. At the end of the week, tape five animals and file the sheet as your baseline.
