Lessons

1. Vaccination Protocols for Feedlot Steers

Vaccination is your first line of defense against costly disease outbreaks in a feedlot. In Africa, common diseases like blackleg, botulism, and lumpy skin disease can wipe out your profits. Start with a clear protocol: all steers should be vaccinated at arrival, then boostered as needed. For blackleg, use a 7-way clostridial vaccine (e.g., Ultrabac 7) give 2 ml under the skin. For botulism, use a toxoid vaccine 5 ml under the skin. For lumpy skin disease, use a live attenuated vaccine 1 ml under the skin. Always check with a local vet for region-specific diseases. Timing matters: vaccinate healthy animals only. Never vaccinate sick or stressed steers wait until they recover. Handle vaccines carefully: keep them refrigerated at 2-8C, never freeze. Use clean needles change every 10-15 animals to avoid abscesses. Record every vaccination: date, product, batch number, and animal ID. In hot climates, vaccinate in the cool of the morning. A good protocol costs about $2-3 per steer but saves thousands in losses. Work with your vet to design a schedule that fits your farms disease history.

2. Common Diseases: Respiratory and Digestive

Respiratory and digestive diseases are the top killers in feedlots. Bovine Respiratory Disease (BRD) causes pneumonia, often triggered by stress from transport or mixing. Signs: rapid breathing, coughing, nasal discharge, droopy ears, and fever above 39.5C. Treat early with antibiotics like oxytetracycline (20 mg/kg body weight) or tulathromycin (2.5 mg/kg). Digestive diseases include bloat and acidosis. Bloat happens when gas builds up in the rumen left side swells, animal kicks at belly, breathes hard. Treat with a stomach tube or trocar and cannula in emergency. Acidosis from too much grain: symptoms are loose manure, off-feed, and lameness. Prevent by gradually increasing grain over 3 weeks. For both, isolate sick animals immediately. Provide fresh water and good-quality hay. In Africa, plant poisoning (e.g., from Lantana) can mimic these signs check your pastures. Use a thermometer to confirm fever. Keep a treatment log: animal ID, symptoms, drug used, withdrawal period. Withdrawal periods for meat: oxytetracycline 28 days, tulathromycin 18 days. Never sell animals before withdrawal ends. Early detection and treatment reduce mortality by up to 80%.

3. Parasite Control: Internal and External

Parasites steal nutrients and cause disease. Internal parasites (worms) like roundworms and liver flukes are common in Africa. Use dewormers: albendazole (7.5 mg/kg) or ivermectin (0.2 mg/kg) under the skin. Deworm every 3-4 months, or when fecal egg counts are high. Collect fresh manure, mix with salt solution, and count eggs under a microscope your vet can do this. External parasites: ticks, lice, and mange mites. Ticks transmit diseases like heartwater and anaplasmosis. Use tick grease (e.g., amitraz) or spray with cypermethrin every 2 weeks. For mange, inject ivermectin. Rotate chemicals to prevent resistance. Keep pens dry ticks breed in wet, dirty areas. In hot, humid regions, tick control is critical. Also control flies with traps and hygiene. A good parasite program costs about $1-2 per steer per month but improves weight gain by 10-15%. Always follow withdrawal periods: ivermectin 35 days, albendazole 14 days. Record all treatments. Work with your vet to design a strategic deworming schedule based on your farms history and season.

4. Biosecurity Measures

Biosecurity keeps diseases out of your feedlot. Start with a perimeter fence and a single entry point. Use a footbath with disinfectant (e.g., 2% sodium hypochlorite) at the gate. All visitors must wear clean boots and overalls. Quarantine new animals for 30 days keep them 50 meters away from the main herd. Test for diseases like brucellosis and tuberculosis before mixing. Clean and disinfect pens, water troughs, and feed bunks weekly. Use a pressure washer and a disinfectant like Virkon. Control rodents and birds they carry diseases. Store feed in sealed bins. Never share equipment with other farms without disinfecting. When an animal dies, bury it deep (2 meters) or incinerate never leave carcasses around. Isolate sick animals immediately in a hospital pen. Train workers on hygiene: wash hands, change clothes. In Africa, where many farms are small, simple measures like a dedicated pair of boots and a hand-washing station can make a big difference. A biosecurity plan costs little but prevents outbreaks that can kill 20% of your herd. Write down your plan and review it monthly.

5. Recognizing Signs of Illness and Early Intervention

Early detection saves lives. Learn to spot sick steers quickly. Key signs: off-feed (not eating at the trough), droopy ears, sunken eyes, rapid breathing, coughing, nasal discharge, diarrhea, lameness, and isolation from the group. Take the animals temperature normal is 38-39C. Fever above 39.5C means infection. Check gum color: pale gums indicate anemia; yellow gums indicate liver problems. Use a simple body condition score: ribs visible = too thin. Walk the pens twice daily, morning and evening. Look for animals lagging behind. When you find a sick steer, move it to a hospital pen immediately. Provide shade, fresh water, and soft hay. Treat according to your vets protocol dont guess. For BRD, start antibiotics early. For diarrhea, give oral rehydration fluids. Keep a daily log: date, animal ID, signs, treatment, and outcome. In Africa, where vet access may be limited, train one worker as a health monitor. A $10 thermometer and a notebook are your best tools. Early intervention can reduce mortality from 10% to under 2%.

6. Working with Veterinarians and Health Records

A good relationship with a veterinarian is essential. Find a vet who works with cattle and knows your area. Schedule a visit at least once a month, or more if you have outbreaks. Have a written contract: what services, how much, and emergency contact. Your vet can help design vaccination and deworming schedules, diagnose diseases, and prescribe antibiotics. Always follow their prescription dont use human drugs or guess doses. Keep accurate health records: animal ID, date, disease, treatment, drug, dose, withdrawal period, and cost. Use a simple notebook or a smartphone app like Farmbrite. Records help you track patterns if pneumonia keeps happening, you can fix ventilation or reduce stress. They also prove to buyers that your meat is safe. For every animal sold, you must know the withdrawal period has passed. In Africa, where formal vet services may be scarce, join a farmer cooperative that shares a vet. Train a worker to keep records daily. Good records reduce drug costs by 20% and improve herd health. Review records monthly with your vet to adjust your plan.

<h2>Practical Reference: Health Management &amp; Disease Prevention in African Feedlots</h2>

<p>In a commercial feedlot, animal health is not a veterinary cost centre it is a <strong>profit centre</strong>. Every steer that goes off feed, every carcass condemned at slaughter, and every treatment that fails to convert into weight gain is a direct hit to your margin. In African feedlots operating on tight budgets, variable vaccine cold chains, and mixed-source cattle, a disciplined health protocol is the difference between a 1.6 kg average daily gain and a 0.9 kg one.</p>

<p>This practical reference distils the six lessons in this module into actionable, business-first guidance. It assumes you are running or advising a feedlot of 2005,000 head, sourcing cattle from multiple origins (auctions, communal areas, commercial ranches), and working with a consulting veterinarian rather than a resident one.</p>

<h3>Vaccination Protocols: The Non-Negotiable Core</h3>

<p>Vaccination is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy. A full respiratory and clostridial protocol costs roughly USD 35 per head in most African markets. A single BRD treatment costs USD 820, and a death costs you the entire purchase price plus feed. The economics are unambiguous.</p>

<p>Timing matters more than brand. Vaccinate on arrival (Day 0) and booster at Day 21. Modified-live vaccines (MLV) give faster protection but must not be used in pregnant females or stressed, immunosuppressed cattle without veterinary advice. Killed vaccines are safer in highly stressed arrivals but require a booster to achieve protective immunity.</p>

<h3>Common Diseases: Respiratory and Digestive</h3>

<p>BRD and ruminal acidosis account for 7080% of feedlot morbidity and mortality in African operations. BRD is a disease of stress: transport, commingling, dust, and abrupt diet change suppress immunity and allow <em>Mannheimia haemolytica</em> and <em>Mycoplasma bovis</em> to proliferate. Acidosis is a disease of management: too much grain, too fast, too little roughage.</p>

<h3>Parasite Control: Internal and External</h3>

<p>Internal parasites (roundworms, liver fluke, tapeworm) and external parasites (ticks, lice, mange mites, flies) cost African feedlots an estimated 510% of potential weight gain. In wetter zones (e.g. highland Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda), liver fluke and ticks are major constraints. In drier zones (e.g. Botswana, Namibia, northern Kenya), ticks and biting flies dominate.</p>

<p>Treat on arrival with a broad-spectrum anthelmintic (ivermectin or albendazole) and an acaricide (amitraz or cypermethrin dip/spray). Repeat anthelmintic at Day 21 if faecal egg counts remain high. Rotate acaricide classes annually to prevent resistance a growing problem in East and Southern Africa.</p>

<div style="background:#fdecea;border-left:6px solid #c0392b;padding:12px 16px;margin:16px 0;font-family:Arial,sans-serif;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Drug Residues and Withdrawal Periods</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;font-size:13px;">Never slaughter an animal before the mandatory withdrawal period for any drug used has elapsed. Antibiotic and anthelmintic residues in meat cause trade rejections, fines, and loss of export certification. In most African markets, oxytetracycline requires a 28-day meat withdrawal; ivermectin requires 35 days; sulphonamides require 1014 days. Always record the drug, dose, date, and withdrawal date. If you sell to formal processors or export abattoirs, residue testing is routine and a single positive result can cost you the entire contract.</p>
</div>

<h3>Biosecurity Measures</h3>

<p>Biosecurity in an African feedlot is about controlling what walks, flies, or is driven onto your property. Key measures: quarantine new arrivals for 1421 days, maintain a single entry point with a footbath, restrict visitor access to pens, control rodents and birds around feed storage, and disinfect vehicles and equipment between groups. Where possible, source cattle from a limited number of known suppliers rather than open auctions this alone can cut BRD incidence by 3050%.</p>

<h3>Recognising Signs of Illness and Early Intervention</h3>

<p>Pen riders are your first line of defence. Train them to look for the "sick steer" signals: head down, ears drooped, tail head tucked, lagging behind at feed trough, coughing, nasal discharge, and laboured breathing. A steer that does not come to feed is a steer losing money. The rule of thumb: <strong>treat within 12 hours of first signs</strong>. Every 24-hour delay adds roughly 510 kg of lost gain and increases the risk of chronic, non-responding cases.</p>

<p>Use a simple scoring system (14) for BRD severity and record it. This allows you to track treatment success rates and identify pens or suppliers with recurring problems.</p>

<h3>Working with Veterinarians and Health Records</h3>

<p>You do not need a resident vet, but you do need a written veterinary protocol signed off by a licensed veterinarian, reviewed annually. Your vet should train your pen riders, audit your records quarterly, and be reachable for emergency advice. In return, you must keep records that make their job possible: individual animal ID, treatment date, drug, dose, withdrawal date, and outcome.</p>

<p>In African contexts where veterinary services may be stretched, consider forming a feedlot health cooperative with neighbouring operations to share a consulting vet. This reduces cost per head and improves access to professional oversight.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Vaccinate all arrivals on Day 0 and booster on Day 21 (IBR/BVDV/PI3/BRSV, Mannheimia, Clostridial).</li>
<li> Treat all arrivals for internal and external parasites; record drug and withdrawal date.</li>
<li> Quarantine new arrivals for 1421 days before mixing with resident cattle.</li>
<li> Pen ride twice daily; treat sick animals within 12 hours of first signs.</li>
<li> Maintain a written veterinary protocol reviewed annually with your consulting vet.</li>
<li> Record every treatment: animal ID, date, drug, dose, withdrawal date, outcome.</li>
<li> Rotate acaricide classes annually; monitor faecal egg counts every 90 days.</li>
<li> Control access, vehicles, and visitors; disinfect boots and equipment at entry.</li>
</ul>
