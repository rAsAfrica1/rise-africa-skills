Record Keeping & Cash Flow

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Business

1 Daily production log

Every day, record what you made. Simple, but essential.

Daily production log:

  • Date and shift
  • Product made
  • Batch code
  • Quantity (units produced, units rejected)
  • Raw materials used
  • Operator name
  • Any issues (equipment, quality, CCP deviations)

One line per production run. A simple notebook or spreadsheet works. Review weekly to spot patterns.

💡 Production log is your #1 defence in a food safety investigation. Without it, you can't trace problems.

2 Sales and purchase records

Every sale and purchase needs a record. No exceptions.

Sales record:

  • Date
  • Customer name
  • Product and quantity
  • Unit price and total
  • Payment method (cash, card, mobile money)
  • Paid or outstanding

Purchase record:

  • Date
  • Supplier
  • Item and quantity
  • Unit price and total
  • Paid or outstanding

These records feed your cash flow, profit, and tax reporting. Missing records mean missed opportunities and legal problems.

3 Ingredient inventory

Know what you have, what you need, what you've used.

Weekly inventory count:

  • Raw materials (produce, cement for sugar, etc.)
  • Packaging (jars, lids, labels, boxes)
  • Finished products (by product and batch)
  • Cleaning supplies
  • Spare parts

What to do with the count:

  • Compare to last week — any unexplained losses?
  • Flag low-stock items for reordering
  • Flag slow-moving finished products for promotion
  • Update cost calculations with current prices

Investigate any large variances. Theft, spoilage and waste all show up here.

4 Simple bookkeeping

You don't need accounting software to start. A spreadsheet or lined notebook works.

Three core records:

  1. Cash book — daily money in, money out, running balance
  2. Sales ledger — who owes you, how much, when due
  3. Purchase ledger — who you owe, how much, when due

Record every transaction the day it happens. Don't let receipts pile up — you'll lose them and lose track.

As you grow, move to accounting software like QuickBooks, Wave (free) or Zoho Books. Any of these is better than nothing.

5 Monthly profit and loss

Once a month, produce a simple P&L statement. This is where you find out if you're actually making money.

Layout:

  • Sales (revenue) — $10,000
  • Less: Cost of goods sold (materials + labour) — $6,000
  • = Gross profit — $4,000
  • Less: Overheads (rent, power, admin) — $2,500
  • = Net profit — $1,500

Track month over month. Look for trends: growing sales, shrinking margin, rising costs. Take action before small problems grow.

💡 If you can't produce a P&L in 30 minutes, your records are too messy. Simplify.

6 Cash flow planning

Profit is not cash. A profitable business can run out of cash. This kills companies.

Cash flow example:

  • January: sell $10,000, customer pays in 60 days
  • January: pay $6,000 in materials and wages now
  • Net result: $6,000 out, $0 in for 60 days
  • You need cash reserves to cover the gap

Cash flow rules:

  • Collect payment as fast as possible (cash on delivery)
  • Pay suppliers on terms, not early (unless discount given)
  • Keep 3 months of running costs as buffer
  • Plan for seasonal dips — save in peak months

Every month, forecast the next 3 months of cash in and out. Adjust as needed.

7 Tax basics

Tax is unavoidable. Register and pay properly — penalties are worse than the tax.

Typical tax obligations:

  • Income tax / corporate tax — on profits
  • VAT / sales tax — on sales, once you cross the threshold
  • PAYE — withholding from employee wages
  • Business licences — local council fees
  • Customs duty — on imports

Keep receipts for everything. Many expenses are tax-deductible (raw materials, wages, rent, equipment). Track them carefully.

File returns on time. Even if you owe nothing, filing is required in most countries.

8 When to hire an accountant

DIY bookkeeping works up to a point. Know when to hire.

Signs you need an accountant:

  • VAT-registered or above $100K annual turnover
  • More than 3 employees (PAYE complexity)
  • Bank asks for financial statements for a loan
  • You're considering investors or partners
  • You spend more than 5 hours/week on books
  • You've been fined or audited

A good accountant costs $50–$300 per month for a small business. Saves more than they cost — usually in tax savings and penalties avoided.

💰 Records and cash flow are what separate thriving businesses from failed ones. Do this one thing consistently and you'll beat 90% of competitors.
