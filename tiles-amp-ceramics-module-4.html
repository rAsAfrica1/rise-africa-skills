1. Introduction to the Potter's Wheel

A potter's wheel is a simple machine: a heavy disc that spins so you can shape clay with your hands. In Africa, you'll find two main types: the traditional kick wheel (you push a heavy stone or concrete flywheel with your foot) and the electric wheel (a motor turns the head at 0240 volts, common in cities). For a beginner, an electric wheel with a 3040 cm head is easiest. You also need a stool that puts your hips just above the wheel head, a bucket of water, a sponge, a wire clay cutter, and a wooden rib. Clay should be wedged (kneaded) to remove air bubblesif you don't, your pot will explode in the kiln. Use 12 kg of smooth stoneware or earthenware clay for your first attempts. Set your wheel speed to medium (about 100150 rpm for centering). Sit with your elbows braced against your thighs so your hands are steady. Wet the wheel head, slam the clay ball down in the center, and press it firmly to make it stick. Never lean your whole body weight onto the spinning wheeluse your arms and shoulders. In many African workshops, potters share one wheel, so learn to adjust the speed and seat quickly. Always keep a bucket of clean water and a sponge to lubricate the clay; too much water makes it sloppy, too little makes it drag. After throwing, clean the wheel with a sponge and scraper. Store clay in a sealed plastic bag to keep it workable. A well-maintained wheel will last decadesoil the bearings every month if it's electric, and check the belt tension. Practice spinning an empty wheel first to get used to the motion, then try centering a small ball of clay. This is the foundation of all wheel work.

2. Centering Clay on the Wheel

Centering is the hardest skill in wheel throwingif the clay is not perfectly centered, every pot will be lopsided. Start with 1 kg of wedged clay. Slam it onto the center of the wheel head and press down with your palm. Wet your hands, then turn the wheel to medium speed. Place your left hand on the left side of the clay, right hand on the right, elbows locked into your hips. Press inward and downward with the heels of your hands. The clay will wobble; keep pressure steady until it stops moving. This can take 510 minutes for beginners. Use a 'cone' technique: push the clay into a cone shape, then press it back down. Repeat three times. This aligns the particles and removes air pockets. Check centering by holding a finger lightly against the sideif you feel a bump, it's off-center. Fix it by pressing harder on the high side. Water is critical: too dry, the clay tears; too wet, it slips. Dip your fingers in water, not your whole hand. In Africa, many potters use a wooden rib (a flat stick) to help press the clay evenly. Once centered, open the clay with your thumbs, pressing down into the middle, then widen the hole. Keep the wheel speed constant. If the clay starts to spiral, slow down and re-center. Centering 1 kg should take no more than 3 minutes once you're skilled. Practice with 500 g balls firstthey're easier. Never rush; a centered ball is 80% of a good pot. After centering, you can throw a cylinder or bowl. If you fail, wedge the clay again and retry. In a shared workshop, clean the wheel between users so clay doesn't harden. Store your centered clay under plastic if you take a break.

3. Throwing Cylinders and Bowls

Once clay is centered, open it with your thumbs, pressing down to within 1 cm of the wheel head (don't go through). For a cylinder, widen the hole to about 3 cm, then pull the walls up. Use your left hand inside, right hand outside, both wet. Start at the base, squeeze the wall between your fingers, and pull upward slowly. Repeat 34 times, each time thinning the wall. Aim for a wall thickness of 58 mm. Keep the wheel at medium speed. If the wall gets too thin, it will collapse. For a bowl, open wider (810 cm) and pull the walls outward, not straight up. Use a rib to shape the curve. A good bowl has a smooth, even curve from base to rim. Throw a 1 kg cylinder to about 15 cm tall, then a 1 kg bowl to 20 cm wide. Let them dry to leather-hard (firm but still cool) before trimming. In Africa, many potters make nested bowls for serving ugali or stew. You can also throw plates: open a flat disc, then pull the rim up slightly. Always keep the rim evenmeasure with a needle tool or your fingers. After throwing, smooth the rim with a wet sponge. If the pot wobbles, it's off-center; recenter by pressing gently. Remove the pot from the wheel with a wire cutter, sliding it under the base. Lift carefully with two hands. Place on a wooden board to dry. Cover with plastic for slow drying to prevent cracks. Practice daily: throw 10 cylinders in a row, then 10 bowls. Speed comes with muscle memory. In a shared studio, label your pots with your name. Clean your tools and wheel after each session.

4. Trimming and Finishing Wheel-Thrown Pieces

Trimming (also called turning) is done when the pot is leather-hardfirm enough to hold shape but soft enough to cut. If it's too wet, it will deform; too dry, it will chip. Test by pressing a fingernail: it should leave a slight mark. You need a trimming tool (a metal loop on a handle), a chuck (a small clay mound to hold the pot upside down), and a needle tool. Center the pot upside down on the wheel head, pressing gently into the chuck. Spin the wheel slowly. Use the needle tool to mark the base's center. Then hold the trimming tool at a 45 angle and shave off thin layers from the base and sides. Aim for a foot ringa raised ring on the bottom that makes the pot stable. Trim until the wall thickness is even, about 5 mm. For bowls, trim the outside curve to match the inside. Don't over-trim; stop when you see a smooth surface. After trimming, smooth the foot with a damp sponge. For finishing, you can burnish with a smooth stone or spoon to make the surface shiny. In Africa, many potters use a piece of broken gourd or a smooth river stone for burnishing. You can also add handles: roll a coil, attach with slip (clay mixed with water), and blend. Let the pot dry completely (bone dry) before firing. If you want to decorate, carve or paint with engobe (colored slip) at leather-hard stage. Always clean your tools and store them dry. Trimming is where you make a pot look professional. Practice on cylinders first, then bowls. A well-trimmed pot has a clean foot, even walls, and no cracks. If you crack a pot, recycle the clay. In a shared workshop, keep your trimming tools sharp and labeled.

5. Making Large Platters and Tiles on the Wheel

Large platters and tiles are thrown on the wheel using a bata flat disc that attaches to the wheel head. You need a bat pin system or a sticky pad. For a platter, start with 23 kg of clay. Center it, then open a wide, flat disc, leaving a 1 cm thick base. Pull the rim outward with your fingers, keeping the wheel at low speed (5080 rpm). Use a rib to flatten and smooth. Aim for a diameter of 3040 cm. If the clay gets too soft, let it rest for 10 minutes, then continue. For tiles, throw a flat disc 1 cm thick, then cut it into squares or circles with a needle tool while still on the bat. Let the tile dry on the bat until leather-hard, then lift it off. In Africa, large platters are used for serving communal meals; tiles are used for floors and wall decoration. You can also make roof tiles by throwing a curved disc and cutting it. Always support large pieces with your handsdon't let them flop. After throwing, cover with plastic and dry slowly to prevent warping. For a platter, you can add a foot ring by trimming later. If you want a perfectly flat tile, place it between two boards weighted with books while drying. Practice with 1 kg first, then increase. A common mistake is throwing too thinlarge pieces need strength. Use a stiff clay body with grog (sand or crushed fired clay) to reduce cracking. In a shared studio, use a bat that fits your wheel head. Clean the bat after use. Fire platters and tiles slowly to avoid cracks. With practice, you can throw a 50 cm platter in 15 minutes.

6. Troubleshooting Common Wheel Throwing Problems

Even experienced potters face problems. Here are the most common and how to fix them. If the clay wobbles while centering, it's not centeredstop, press harder on the high side, and re-cone. If the pot collapses, the wall is too thin or the wheel is too fast; slow down and use less water. If the rim cracks, it dried too fast; cover with plastic and slow the drying. If the base cracks, you opened too deep or didn't compress the bottom; always press the base with a sponge. If the pot is lopsided, you pulled unevenly; practice pulling with even pressure. If the clay feels sticky, it's too wet; let it dry on a plaster bat for 10 minutes. If it's too dry, dip your fingers in water. If the pot sticks to the wheel head, use a wire cutter and a little water. If you get 'S' cracks in the base, you didn't compress the clay enough after opening. If the pot has a spiral inside, you pulled too fast; slow down. In Africa, humidity affects dryingin coastal areas, pots dry slowly; in dry regions, cover them sooner. Always wedge clay to remove air bubbles; bubbles cause explosions in the kiln. If your pot explodes, you fired too fast or had trapped air. Use a pyrometric cone to monitor temperature. For trimming problems, if the pot won't center on the chuck, use a smaller chuck or dampen the rim. If the trimming tool chatters, hold it firmly and spin slower. If you crack a pot during trimming, it's too dry; spray with water and wait. Keep a notebook of your mistakes and fixes. In a shared workshop, ask others for help. Practice each fix on scrap clay. Remember, every potter makes mistakesthe best ones learn from them.

<h2>Practical Reference: Wheel Throwing and Advanced Shaping</h2>

<p>In African ceramics enterprises, the potter's wheel is not merely a shaping toolit is a production multiplier. Whether you are supplying glazed dinnerware to urban restaurants in Nairobi, producing decorative tiles for hotels in Accra, or crafting high-end platters for export markets, wheel-thrown work commands premium prices when executed consistently. This practical reference consolidates the technical essentials of wheel throwing and advanced shaping into a business-ready format, with specific attention to African clay bodies, market demands, and workshop conditions.</p>

<p>Across the continent, potters work with a remarkable diversity of clays: the red earthenwares of West Africa, the grey stonewares of the Ethiopian highlands, the white-firing ball clays of South Africa's Western Cape, and the alluvial blends of the Nile Delta. Each behaves differently on the wheel. This module does not replace hands-on instruction, but it provides the reference data, troubleshooting logic, and safety protocols that keep a production studio profitable.</p>

<h2>Wheel Speed, Water, and Body Mechanics</h2>

<p>Wheel speed must match the task. Centering demands high speed (200300 rpm on an electric wheel), while opening and pulling walls require moderate speed (120180 rpm), and shaping rims or trimming calls for slow speed (60100 rpm). In manually powered wheelsstill common in rural African workshopsthe kick-wheel rhythm must be steady and consistent; irregular speed causes oval forms and uneven walls.</p>

<p>Water management is equally critical. Too much water weakens the clay and causes S-cracks during drying; too little creates friction and tears the wall. In hot, dry African climates, keep a spray bottle and sponge at hand, and cover work with plastic sheeting between stages. A simple rule: the clay should feel like soft leather, never sloppy mud.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;font-size:1.1em;"> SAFETY WARNING: Silica Dust and Wheel Throwing</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#333;">Dry clay and clay dust contain crystalline silica, a known cause of silicosis and lung cancer. In African workshops where ventilation is often poor and sweeping is done dry, the risk is severe. <strong>Never sweep dry clay dust.</strong> Use wet cleaning methods, wear an N95 or FFP2 respirator when mixing dry clay or sanding fired ware, and install extraction or open windows for cross-ventilation. Keep clay scraps in sealed buckets. If you feel persistent cough or shortness of breath, seek medical attention immediately. Your health is your business's most valuable asset.</p>

<h2>Troubleshooting Common Wheel Throwing Problems</h2>

<p>Even experienced throwers encounter failures. The key is rapid diagnosis and correction to protect production schedules and profit margins.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Clay won't centre:</strong> Insufficient wheel speed, dry clay, or inconsistent pressure. Re-wet hands, increase speed, and use the heel of your hand to push inward, not just fingers.</li>
<li><strong>S-cracks in base:</strong> Compressed clay during opening or uneven drying. Always compress the base with a rib or sponge after opening, and dry slowly under plastic.</li>
<li><strong>Walls collapse:</strong> Too much water, too-fast pulling, or clay too soft. Reduce water, slow the pull, and let clay firm up between pulls.</li>
<li><strong>Uneven rim:</strong> Off-centre clay or inconsistent hand pressure. Re-centre before shaping, and use a needle tool to level the rim while spinning slowly.</li>
<li><strong>Platters crack at rim:</strong> Rapid drying or insufficient compression. Cover rims with plastic and dry upside-down on a bat.</li>
<li><strong>Tiles warp:</strong> Uneven thickness or fast drying. Roll or throw tiles to uniform thickness (810 mm), place between boards, and dry slowly.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Prepare clay:</strong> Wedge thoroughly to remove air pockets; test plasticity by rolling a coil.</li>
<li><strong>Set wheel speed:</strong> High for centering, medium for pulling, low for shaping and trimming.</li>
<li><strong>Centre completely:</strong> No wobble before opening; use both hands and consistent pressure.</li>
<li><strong>Compress the base:</strong> After opening, press with a rib to prevent S-cracks.</li>
<li><strong>Pull walls evenly:</strong> Slow, steady pulls from base to rim; keep walls uniform.</li>
<li><strong>Control water:</strong> Damp, not wet; use a sponge and spray bottle sparingly.</li>
<li><strong>Dry slowly:</strong> Cover with plastic; avoid direct sun or draughts, especially in hot climates.</li>
<li><strong>Trim at leather-hard:</strong> Secure the piece on the wheel, use sharp tools, and check foot ring symmetry.</li>
</ol>
