Learning outcomes

1. The brick hive feeding problem

A wooden hive opens from the top: lift the roof, add a feeder, close it up. A brick hive, built for permanence, often has a fixed roof, a narrow slot entrance and no easy top access. That makes feeding — a seasonal, temporary task — surprisingly difficult.

Plan feeding access before the hive is built, not after.

Three options for access

DesignHow it worksBest forTrade-off
Removable crown boardA hinged or liftable panel set into the roof structure, sealed with a gasketMost brick hivesNeeds careful weatherproofing at the hinge
Front feeder slotA cast-in slot at the front, sized to take a frame or division board feederHives with a fixed roofFeeds only one position; robbing risk at the entrance
External top feeder boxA small masonry or timber box built onto the side or back, opening into the brood cavityPermanent apiariesAdds build complexity; must be bee-tight elsewhere

Never feed through the entrance in a brick apiary

A permanent apiary has many hives close together. Feeding at any entrance — even for a weak colony — triggers a robbing cascade across the whole row within hours. Whatever feeding design you choose, it must keep the syrup inside the hive and invisible from outside.

3. Sugar syrup and quantities

The syrup arithmetic is the same as for any hive — mix by weight, never by volume.

Feed calculation for a permanent apiary

10 brick hives, each needing 12 kg of stores built up before the dearth.
Current average stores: 4 kg per hive.
Missing per hive = 8 kg of honey equivalent.
Bees convert 0.8 kg sugar → 1 kg stored honey.
Sugar per hive = 8 / 0.8 = 10 kg.
Total sugar for 10 hives = 100 kg.

Fed as 2:1 syrup: 100 kg sugar + 50 L water = 150 kg syrup ≈ 105 L.
Delivered over 3 weeks at 3.5 L per hive per week = 35 L per week.

That is a full 25 L drum of syrup every 5 days. Plan the logistics before the dearth starts.

4. Permanent water supply

A fixed apiary needs a fixed water source. Do not rely on rain or on a distant stream.

Design

  • A shallow trough — 20–30 mm deep water, with sloped sides or landing stones.
  • Masonry or concrete — matches the apiary; durable; does not blow away.
  • 1.5–2 m long for 10 hives — enough that 200 bees can drink at once without crowding.
  • Refilled by a permanent line or a hose point, not by hand-carrying buckets.
  • Partial shade — a simple shade structure cuts evaporation by 30–50 %.
  • Drained for cleaning — a plug at the low end so you can empty and scrub it every few weeks.
  • 20–100 m from the hives — close enough for the bees to find; far enough that they do not queue at the entrance.

Water consumption for a permanent apiary

10 hives × 1 L per hive per day (hot weather) = 10 L per day.
Over a 60-day dry season = 600 L.
With 30 % loss to evaporation: plan for 800 L per dry season.

A 2 m × 0.3 m trough holding 30 mm of water = 18 L capacity — refill every 2 days in peak heat, or fit an automatic float valve.

5. Pollen and dry-season support

In a permanent apiary, dry-season pollen shortages are predictable. Plan for them.

  • Maintain a small area of flowering plants near the apiary that blooms during the local dearth — even a strip of hardy shrubs helps.
  • Store pollen patties in a sealed container in a cool place; feed only as needed.
  • Do not feed patties in cool weather — bees will not consume them and small hive beetle will breed in them.
  • Remove uneaten patties after 7–10 days. Sweep away crumbs.

6. Robbing in a permanent apiary

Permanent, closely spaced brick hives are more robbing-prone than a scattered wooden apiary. Robbing spreads along the row within minutes.

7. Cold-season feeding in a brick hive

Brick hives are warmer in cool weather, but the cluster is also less able to move to feed. Emergency feeding in cool weather uses dry feed only.

  • Fondant or candy placed directly over the cluster on the top bars. No liquid — bees will not take it and it will chill the cluster.
  • Dry sugar poured onto wax paper over the top bars, misted with a little water so it forms a crust.
  • Never open a brick hive below 10 °C for feeding. Wait for a warm afternoon.
  • One emergency feed is fine; regular cold feeding means the colony went into the season too light. Review your autumn plan.
