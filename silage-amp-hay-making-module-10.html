1. Feeding Hay to Cattle, Goats, and Sheep

Hay is the backbone of dry-season feeding. For cattle, offer 2-3% of body weight daily. A 400 kg cow needs 8-12 kg of good hay. Goats and sheep eat 3-5% of body weight; a 50 kg goat needs 1.5-2.5 kg. Always feed hay in a rack or trough to reduce waste. A simple wooden rack keeps hay off the ground, preventing contamination and parasites. If you don't have a rack, tie hay in bundles and hang from a fence at head height.

Quality matters. Good hay is green, smells sweet, and is free of mould. Mouldy hay causes abortions and respiratory disease. Test by opening a bale: if it's hot or dusty, don't feed it. For cattle, chop long hay to 5-10 cm to reduce sorting. Goats and sheep prefer finer hay and will waste coarse stems.

Feed hay twice daily: morning and evening. Pregnant and lactating animals need unlimited access. During heavy rain, feed under a shelter. Never feed hay on muddy ground; use a raised platform. In Africa, common hays include Rhodes grass, Napier, and groundnut haulms. Legume hays like lucerne (alfalfa) are higher in protein but more expensive. Mix grass and legume hay for balanced nutrition.

Store hay in a dry, ventilated shed on pallets. Keep bales off the floor and away from walls. A tarpaulin cover prevents rain damage. If hay heats up, spread it out to cool. Feed the oldest hay first.

For goats and sheep, supplement with tree leaves like Leucaena or Gliricidia. These add protein. Always provide clean water and a salt lick. Watch for bloat: if an animal's left side swells, walk it and give vegetable oil. Hay alone cannot sustain high milk production; combine with silage and concentrates.

2. Introducing Silage to Livestock Gradually

Silage is fermented, high-moisture feed. It smells like vinegar or alcohol. Animals need time to accept it. Start with a small amount: for cattle, offer 1 kg per day for the first three days. Mix it with hay or fresh grass. If they refuse, sprinkle a little salt or molasses on top. For goats and sheep, start with 200-300 g per day.

After three days, increase by 1 kg every two days for cattle, and 200 g for goats. By day 10, a cow can eat 10-15 kg of silage daily. A goat can eat 2-3 kg. Always provide hay alongside silage; the fibre in hay stimulates chewing and saliva, preventing acidosis.

Never feed mouldy silage. Good silage is light green to yellowish-brown, smells fruity, and is not slimy. If it smells rancid or is black, discard it. Feed silage immediately after removing from the pit. It spoils within 24 hours in hot weather.

Introduce silage during the day when animals are hungry. Remove leftovers after 2 hours. If animals show diarrhoea, reduce the amount and increase hay. For dairy cows, feed silage after milking to avoid milk taint.

In Africa, silage is often made from maize, sorghum, or Napier grass. Maize silage is high in energy. Mix with legume silage for protein. Always have a transition period of 7-10 days. Sudden change causes off-feed and weight loss.

Store silage in a pit or bunker, covered with plastic and weighted down. Open only what you need. In humid areas, feed within 3 days of opening. For small herds, use polythene tubes.

Remember: silage is not a complete feed. It lacks minerals and vitamins. Provide a mineral block. For pregnant animals, limit silage to 50% of the diet to avoid oversized calves. Introduce gradually and monitor daily.

3. Balancing Rations with Concentrates and Minerals

Silage and hay alone cannot meet all nutrient needs, especially for high-producing animals. Concentrates are energy or protein-rich feeds like maize meal, cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, and molasses. Minerals are essential for bone, milk, and reproduction.

A simple dairy ration for a 400 kg cow giving 10 litres of milk: 6 kg hay, 15 kg silage, and 4 kg concentrate. The concentrate mix: 2 kg maize meal, 1 kg cottonseed cake, 0.5 kg wheat bran, 0.5 kg molasses. Add 100 g of mineral premix and 50 g of salt. For goats, use half these amounts.

Mix concentrates with silage or hay to prevent sorting. Feed concentrates twice daily after milking. Never feed more than 2 kg of concentrate at one time to cattle; excess causes acidosis. For sheep, limit to 0.5 kg per feed.

Minerals: provide a free-choice mineral block. In Africa, soils are often deficient in phosphorus, copper, and selenium. Use a commercial premix or make your own: 50% limestone, 30% bone meal, 10% salt, 10% trace mineral mix.

Protein: legume hay (lucerne, groundnut) provides protein. For pigs and poultry, silage is not suitable.

Energy: molasses is a cheap energy source. Mix 1 kg molasses with 3 kg maize meal. Do not exceed 10% of the diet.

Vitamins: green forage provides vitamins A and E. If animals are dry-fed, add a vitamin supplement.

Water: always provide clean water. A dairy cow drinks 60-100 litres daily.

Monitor body condition. If cows lose weight, increase concentrate. If they get fat, reduce. Use a body condition score of 1-5. Aim for 3.

For goats and sheep, a simple ration: 1 kg hay, 2 kg silage, 0.3 kg concentrate. Add minerals.

Never use urea in silage for young animals. It can be toxic.

Balancing rations saves money and keeps animals healthy.

4. Feeding Dairy Animals for Milk Production

A dairy cow needs a balanced diet to produce milk. For every litre of milk, she needs 0.4 kg of concentrate (16% protein) and 0.5 kg of good hay. A cow giving 15 litres needs 6 kg concentrate and 7.5 kg hay, plus 20 kg silage.

Feed in this order: first hay, then silage, then concentrate. This stimulates chewing and saliva. Feed concentrates after milking to prevent milk taint.

For goats, a dairy doe giving 3 litres needs 1.2 kg concentrate and 1.5 kg hay, plus 4 kg silage.

Water is critical. A cow drinks 3-4 litres per litre of milk. Provide water at all times. In hot weather, add electrolytes.

Minerals: calcium and phosphorus are essential. Use a mineral block or add 100 g of dairy premix per cow daily.

Energy: silage from maize is high in energy. Mix with legume silage for protein.

Protein: cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, or soybean meal. Avoid too much cottonseed; it can cause gossypol poisoning. Limit to 2 kg per cow per day.

Fibre: hay keeps the rumen healthy. Do not feed less than 40% of the diet as forage.

Feeding schedule:
- 6 am: milking, then concentrate
- 8 am: hay
- 12 pm: silage
- 4 pm: milking, then concentrate
- 6 pm: hay

Adjust amounts based on milk yield. If milk drops, increase concentrate. If milk fat drops, increase fibre.

For high yield, use total mixed ration (TMR). Mix all ingredients in a wagon or by hand.

In Africa, use locally available feeds: maize bran, molasses, brewers' grain.

Monitor milk production daily. Keep records. A good cow gives 10-20 litres per day.

Dry off cows 60 days before calving. Feed only hay and minerals.

Clean water and comfortable housing increase milk yield.

Never feed mouldy feed. It reduces milk and causes disease.

5. Feeding During Pregnancy and Lactation

Pregnancy and lactation are the most demanding times. A pregnant cow needs 20% more feed in the last 2 months. A lactating cow needs double maintenance.

For pregnant cows: feed 2 kg concentrate and 10 kg hay daily. Add 1 kg legume hay. Provide minerals. Do not overfeed silage; limit to 10 kg to avoid oversized calves.

For lactating cows: feed 1 kg concentrate per 2.5 litres of milk. For a 20-litre cow, that's 8 kg concentrate. Also 12 kg hay and 25 kg silage.

For pregnant goats: feed 0.5 kg concentrate and 1.5 kg hay. For lactating goats: 1 kg concentrate per 3 litres of milk.

Energy: increase grain in late pregnancy. But avoid excessive grain; it causes ketosis.

Protein: needs rise by 50% in late pregnancy. Use cottonseed cake or lucerne.

Minerals: calcium and phosphorus are critical. A deficiency causes milk fever. Provide a mineral block.

Vitamins: add vitamin A and D.

Water: a lactating cow drinks 80-120 litres daily.

Feeding schedule:
- Pregnant: 2 meals of hay, 1 of silage, 1 of concentrate.
- Lactating: 3 meals of hay, 2 of silage, 2 of concentrate.

Body condition: aim for score 3.5 at calving. Too fat causes difficult birth. Too thin causes weak calves.

After birth: feed warm water with molasses. Offer good hay. Increase concentrate gradually.

For sheep: pregnant ewes need 1 kg hay and 0.3 kg concentrate. Lactating ewes need 1.5 kg hay and 0.6 kg concentrate.

In Africa, use crop residues like maize stover. Treat with urea to improve digestibility.

Never feed mouldy hay or silage to pregnant animals. It causes abortions.

Exercise: give animals 1 hour daily.

Record keeping: note breeding dates and expected calving.

Proper feeding ensures healthy mothers and strong offspring.

6. Avoiding Health Problems from Poor Quality Fodder

Poor quality fodder causes many health problems. Mouldy hay and silage contain mycotoxins. These cause abortions, reduced milk, and liver damage. Never feed mouldy feed. If hay is dusty, soak it in water for 30 minutes.

Silage that smells rancid or is slimy contains harmful bacteria. It causes diarrhoea and acidosis. Discard it.

Nitrate poisoning: from young, fast-growing crops like sorghum and Sudan grass. Symptoms: rapid breathing, blue gums, death. Prevent by not feeding after drought or frost. Test with a nitrate strip.

Bloat: from eating too much lush legume. Symptoms: swollen left side, discomfort. Treat by walking the animal and giving 200 ml vegetable oil. Prevent by feeding hay before legume.

Acidosis: from too much grain or silage. Symptoms: off-feed, diarrhoea, laminitis. Treat with baking soda (50 g in water). Prevent by feeding roughage first.

Urea poisoning: if urea not mixed properly. Symptoms: tremors, salivation, death. Treat with vinegar (500 ml in water). Prevent by mixing urea with molasses and feed.

Milk fever: from calcium deficiency. Symptoms: staggering, cold ears, downer cow. Treat with calcium borogluconate injection. Prevent by providing minerals.

Ketosis: from energy deficiency in early lactation. Symptoms: weight loss, sweet breath, off-feed. Treat with propylene glycol (300 ml daily). Prevent by increasing energy.

Parasites: from feeding on the ground. Use racks. Deworm regularly.

Hardware disease: from wire or nails in hay. Use magnets in feed.

Always inspect feed before offering. Smell, touch, and look. If in doubt, throw it out.

Store feed properly: dry, cool, off the ground.

Keep records of any health issues.

Vaccinate and deworm.

Consult a vet for any sudden deaths.

Practical Reference: Feeding Silage and Hay to Livestock

In African livestock enterprises, feed is the single largest cost â often 60â70% of total production expenses. Whether you run a 5-cow dairy unit near Nakuru, a 200-head goat fattening operation in Kajiado, or a communal sheep flock in the Eastern Cape, the way you present silage and hay determines whether your animals gain weight, produce milk, or simply survive. This practical reference gives you the numbers, the timing, and the danger signs you need to turn stored forage into profit.

Silage and hay are not interchangeable. Hay is dried forage (usually 80â90% dry matter) preserved by removing moisture; silage is fermented wet forage (25â35% dry matter) preserved by acidification. Each has a place in an African feeding calendar shaped by dry seasons, erratic rainfall, and the need to carry animals through the hungry months. The lessons in this module cover cattle, goats, sheep, dairy animals, pregnancy, lactation, and health risks. Here we consolidate the practical feeding rules.

Feeding Hay to Cattle, Goats, and Sheep

Hay is the backbone of dry-season feeding across Africa. But not all hay is equal. The value of hay depends on when it was cut. Grass cut at flowering stage has roughly 8â10% crude protein and 50â55% digestibility. The same grass cut after seed set drops to 4â6% protein and 40â45% digestibility â meaning your animal must eat far more to extract the same energy, and will lose weight anyway.

For cattle, offer hay free-choice in racks rather than on the ground. Ground feeding wastes 20â30% of the hay through trampling and contamination with dung, which spreads parasites. A simple wooden hay rack costs little and pays for itself within one season. For goats, which are browsers rather than grazers, hay must be of higher quality â goats reject dusty, mouldy, or coarse hay and will simply refuse to eat it. Sheep are intermediate; they graze closer to the ground than cattle and will accept slightly lower-quality hay but are highly susceptible to copper toxicity from mineral mixes and to internal parasites from ground feeding.

Introducing Silage to Livestock Gradually

Silage is acidic (pH 3.8â4.5) and highly palatable once animals are accustomed to it â but the rumen microbes that digest it are different from those that digest dry hay. A sudden switch to silage causes rumen acidosis, loose dung, reduced milk fat, and in severe cases, death. The rule is simple: introduce silage over 7â10 days, starting at 2 kg per adult cow per day and increasing by roughly 1 kg daily until the target is reached.

For goats and sheep, start at 0.5 kg per head per day and increase by 0.25 kg daily. Never introduce silage and a new concentrate at the same time â if animals go off feed, you will not know which change caused it. Always provide long fibre (hay or straw) alongside silage during transition, and ensure water is freely available. Silage is 65â75% moisture, so animals on high silage diets drink less but still need constant access.

Balancing Rations with Concentrates and Minerals

Silage and hay alone will maintain animals but rarely produce profit. Milk production, weight gain, and successful reproduction all require energy and protein above maintenance. Concentrates â dairy meal, maize bran, cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, molasses â fill that gap. The mistake most smallholders make is feeding too little concentrate too late. A cow producing 15 litres of milk needs roughly 1 kg of dairy meal for every 2 litres above maintenance, split into at least two feeds.

Feeding Dairy Animals for Milk Production

Milk is 87% water. A cow producing 20 litres of milk loses 17 litres of water through milk alone, plus more through urine and sweat. Restricting water is the fastest way to reduce milk yield. Provide 60â100 litres per day for a lactating cow, and ensure the trough is clean â cows will refuse dirty water and yield will drop within 24 hours.

Feed roughage first, then concentrates. This order stimulates rumination, buffers rumen pH, and improves milk fat percentage. Split concentrates into two or three feeds per day; feeding 6 kg of dairy meal in one sitting will cause sub-acute acidosis, reducing milk fat and eventually causing laminitis. For goats, milk yield responds strongly to browse and legume hay (lucerne, desmodium) plus a small concentrate top-up. Sheep dairying is less common in Africa but follows the same principle: high-quality forage first, concentrate according to yield.

Feeding During Pregnancy and Lactation

The last six weeks of pregnancy (the "steaming-up" period) determine the next lactation. The foetus grows 70% of its birth weight in the final third of gestation, and the cow's rumen capacity shrinks as the uterus expands. She cannot eat enough hay to meet her needs. This is when you must increase concentrate quality and density â not quantity alone. Feed 2â3 kg of a good-quality dairy meal in the final month, and ensure minerals (especially calcium, phosphorus, and selenium) are available. Selenium deficiency causes white muscle disease in newborn calves and retained placentas in cows â common in many African soils.

After calving, energy demand spikes. Do not wait for milk yield to rise before increasing feed â increase concentrate from day one after calving, reaching full lactation ration within 10 days. For goats and sheep, the same principle applies: the last month of pregnancy and first month of lactation are when nutrition determines whether the offspring survive and whether the dam conceives again on time.

Avoiding Health Problems from Poor Quality Fodder

Mouldy hay and spoiled silage are the most common causes of livestock poisoning on African farms. Mouldy hay contains mycotoxins that damage the liver, reduce fertility, and suppress the immune system. Spoiled silage â silage that smells rancid, is slimy, or has turned dark brown or black â contains butyric acid and clostridia bacteria that cause listeriosis and botulism. Never feed spoiled silage, even mixed with good silage. The cost of a sick animal far exceeds the value of a few kilogrammes of saved feed.

Other health risks: bloat from lush legume silage (feed hay first), nitrate poisoning from silage made from heavily fertilised forage (test before feeding), and internal parasites from ground feeding (use racks and rotate grazing).
