Lessons

1. Major Commercial Banana Varieties (Cavendish, Gros Michel, etc.)

When you grow bananas for money, the variety you choose decides your market, your yield, and your problems. The most planted commercial banana in Africa is Cavendish, also called Chinese banana or Williams. It produces large bunches of 1525 kg, tolerates wind better than others, and is the main export type. But Cavendish is highly susceptible to Panama disease (Fusarium wilt) and black sigatoka. Gros Michel, known as 'Big Mike', was the export king before Panama disease wiped it out in the 1950s. It has thicker skin, better taste, and travels well, but it is almost impossible to grow commercially now because of Fusarium. In Africa, Gros Michel survives in backyard gardens and local markets. Other important varieties: Pisang Awak (also called Kayinja or Kiganda) is very hardy, resistant to sigatoka, and used for juice and beer in Uganda. Bluggoe (or Lujugira) is a cooking banana, important in East Africa. Plantain (Musa AAB) is a major staple in West and Central Africa, eaten cooked. Red bananas are sweeter and fetch premium prices in niche markets. For local markets, many farmers grow Sukali Ndizi (sugar banana) or Ndiizi, which is sweet and thin-skinned. For export, you need Cavendish clones like Grand Naine, Williams, and Dwarf Cavendish. Grand Naine is the most popular worldwide because it yields well and is shorter, making harvest easier. In Africa, the choice often depends on altitude: Cavendish does well from 01200 m, while highland cooking bananas (like East African Highland Bananas) thrive above 1200 m. Always match the variety to your local disease pressure, rainfall, and market demand. If you are near a city, sweet bananas and plantains sell fast. If you have a contract with an exporter, plant only certified Cavendish. Never plant a mix without checking what buyers want.

2. Selecting Varieties for Local Markets and Export

Your market decides your variety. For local markets in Africa, buyers want taste, size, and availability. Sweet bananas like Sukali Ndizi and Ndiizi are popular in Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania. They are eaten fresh and sell in small bunches. Plantains (called 'gonja' in Uganda, 'ogede' in Nigeria) are essential for cooking and roasting; they fetch steady prices in West and Central Africa. Cooking bananas like East African Highland Bananas (Matooke) are the backbone of markets in Uganda, Rwanda, and Burundi. For these local markets, you can plant a mix of varieties to spread risk and harvest throughout the year. But for export, the rules are strict. Exporters demand Cavendish clonesmainly Grand Naine, Williams, and Dwarf Cavendish. They want uniform bunches, 3545 mm finger diameter, and no blemishes. You must have a guaranteed volume, usually at least 1 hectare, and follow GlobalG.A.P. standards. Export bananas are harvested green, packed in 1820 kg boxes, and shipped at 13C. If you are far from a port or airport, export is not for you. For local markets, you can sell at farm gate, roadside, or to traders. A good strategy: plant 70% of your land with a proven local variety (e.g., Matooke or plantain) and 30% with Cavendish for nearby urban markets. Always test a small plot of any new variety before planting the whole farm. Ask other farmers about yield, disease resistance, and market price. In Kenya, for example, farmers near Nairobi grow Cavendish for supermarkets and hotels. In Ghana, plantain farmers supply huge urban markets. Know your buyer before you plant.

3. Sources of Healthy Planting Material: Suckers, Tissue Culture

Banana planting material comes from two main sources: suckers and tissue culture plantlets. Suckers are shoots that grow from the mother plant's corm. They are cheap and easy to get, but they often carry diseases like Panama disease, banana bunchy top virus, and nematodes. There are two types of suckers: sword suckers (narrow leaves, good for planting) and water suckers (broad leaves, weak, poor yield). Always choose sword suckers from healthy, high-yielding mother plants. A good sword sucker is 3050 cm tall, has a corm of at least 500 g, and has 35 healthy leaves. Never take suckers from a plant that looks sick or has produced small bunches. Tissue culture (TC) plantlets are grown in a laboratory from tiny pieces of healthy mother plants. They are disease-free, uniform, and produce higher yields. TC plantlets are sold in small bags or trays, usually 515 cm tall. They cost moreabout $0.50 to $2 per plantletbut you need fewer because they survive better. TC plantlets are the best choice if you are starting a commercial farm or if your soil has disease history. In Africa, TC labs exist in Kenya, Uganda, Nigeria, South Africa, and Ghana. You can also get them from certified nurseries. Suckers are still common for smallholders, but you must treat them. Soak suckers in hot water at 5255C for 20 minutes to kill nematodes and weevils. Then dip in a fungicide solution. For TC plantlets, handle gently: keep them in shade, water daily, and plant out after 68 weeks when they have 46 leaves. Never plant a sucker or TC plantlet that looks yellow, stunted, or has root damage.

4. Sucker Selection and Preparation

If you use suckers, you must select and prepare them carefully. Start by choosing mother plants that are healthy, have large bunches, and show no signs of disease. Look for sword suckersthey have narrow, pointed leaves and a strong corm. Avoid water suckers: they have broad, round leaves and a weak connection to the mother. A good sword sucker is 3050 cm tall, with a corm diameter of at least 10 cm and weight of 500 g or more. Use a sharp, clean machete or spade to cut the sucker from the mother corm. Make a clean cut, leaving a small piece of the mother corm attachedthis helps the sucker establish. Do not damage the growing point. After cutting, trim the roots to about 5 cm and remove any damaged or diseased parts. Then treat the sucker to kill pests and diseases. The hot water treatment is simple: heat water to 5255C (use a thermometer), soak the cleaned suckers for 20 minutes, then cool them in clean water. This kills nematodes and banana weevils. Next, dip the corm in a fungicide solution, such as benomyl or thiabendazole, following label instructions. You can also dip in a slurry of wood ash and water to prevent fungal rot. After treatment, plant the suckers immediately in a nursery bed or directly in the field. If you must store them, keep them in a cool, shaded place for no more than 2 days. Plant in holes 40 cm x 40 cm x 40 cm, with the corm 5 cm below the soil surface. Water well. With good preparation, suckers can give you a harvest in 1214 months. Poor suckers give small bunches or die. Always prepare more suckers than you needabout 10% extrato replace failures.

5. Tissue Culture Plantlets: Advantages and Handling

Tissue culture (TC) plantlets are the gold standard for commercial banana farming. They are produced in a laboratory from the growing tip of a healthy mother plant. Because they are grown in sterile conditions, they are free from Panama disease, bunchy top virus, and nematodes. TC plantlets are also uniform: every plant grows at the same rate, so you can harvest all at once. They yield 2030% more than suckers and produce larger bunches. TC plantlets are small when you get themusually 515 cm tall in a small bag or tray. They are delicate, so handle with care. When they arrive, keep them in a shaded nursery for 68 weeks. Water them daily, but do not soak the soil. Use a fine mist spray to avoid damaging leaves. After 2 weeks, start adding a weak liquid fertilizer (e.g., NPK 20-10-10 at 1 g per litre) once a week. By 68 weeks, they should have 46 leaves and be 2030 cm tall. Then they are ready to plant in the field. Plant TC plantlets in the cool of the evening or on a cloudy day. Dig a hole 30 cm x 30 cm x 30 cm, place the plantlet with its root ball intact, and cover with soil. Water immediately. Keep the area weed-free and mulch around the plant. TC plantlets cost more$0.50 to $2 eachbut you need only 1,0001,500 per hectare, compared to 1,2001,800 suckers. The extra cost is recovered in higher yields and lower disease losses. In Africa, TC labs are in Kenya (e.g., KALRO), Uganda (NARO), Nigeria (IITA), and South Africa. Always buy from a certified lab or nursery. Never buy TC plantlets that are yellow, have distorted leaves, or are root-bound. With proper handling, TC plantlets give you a clean, profitable plantation.

6. Certification and Disease-Free Planting Material

Certification is your guarantee that planting material is healthy and true to type. In Africa, many countries have a national certification scheme for banana. For example, in Kenya, the Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service (KEPHIS) certifies tissue culture labs and nurseries. In Uganda, the National Agricultural Research Organisation (NARO) and the Ministry of Agriculture certify planting material. Certified material comes with a label or certificate showing the variety, the lab or nursery, and a batch number. It means the plants have been tested for diseases like Panama disease, bunchy top virus, and banana streak virus. Always ask for the certificate when you buy. If a seller cannot show certification, do not buyespecially for tissue culture plantlets. For suckers, certification is rare, but you can still reduce risk by buying from a known healthy farm. Some cooperatives and NGOs run clean sucker multiplication plots. These are mother plants that have been tested and protected from disease. You can buy suckers from them with confidence. Never take suckers from a neighbour's farm without checking for disease. Panama disease can stay in the soil for decades, and once it enters your farm, you cannot remove it. The same applies to banana bunchy top virus, which is spread by aphids. Certified planting material costs 1020% more, but it saves you from losing your entire plantation. In Africa, the Banana Breeding Program at IITA and the African Banana and Plantain Network promote certification. If you are starting a commercial farm, budget for certified TC plantlets. For smallholders, at least source suckers from a clean plot. Always keep records of where you bought your planting material. If disease appears, you can trace it back. Certification is not just a piece of paperit is your insurance against crop failure.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Banana Varieties and Planting Material

Banana farming in Africa is a business, not just a subsistence activity. Whether you are supplying a local market in Kampala, Nairobi, or Accra, or targeting export buyers in Europe and the Middle East, the variety you plant and the quality of your planting material will determine your yield, your cost of production, and your access to premium markets. This practical reference gives you the decision tools you need: which varieties to plant, where to source clean planting material, how to handle it, and what certification standards you must meet. It is written for commercial growers, nursery operators, and agribusiness managers who need to make profitable, low-risk planting decisions.

1. Major Commercial Banana Varieties

Banana varieties fall into two broad commercial groups: dessert bananas (eaten fresh) and cooking bananas/plantains (cooked or processed). For African markets, the most commercially significant dessert variety is Cavendish (Grand Nain, Williams, Dwarf Cavendish), which dominates export trade because of its high yield, resistance to Panama disease race 1, and long shelf life. Gros Michel, once the global export standard, was wiped out by Panama disease race 1 in the 1950s; it survives in niche markets in Central and West Africa where it commands premium prices for its superior flavour. Apple Banana (Lady Finger) is important in East Africa for local and regional markets. For cooking, East African Highland Bananas (Matooke, Gonja, Mbwazirume) dominate Uganda, Rwanda, and Tanzania, while Plantains (AAB) are the backbone of markets in Nigeria, Ghana, Cameroon, and Cte d'Ivoire.

2. Selecting Varieties for Local Markets and Export

Variety selection is a business decision driven by three factors: market demand, disease pressure, and cash flow timing. For export, Cavendish varieties are non-negotiable because buyers in Europe and the Middle East require uniform size, colour, and shelf life. However, Cavendish is highly susceptible to Tropical Race 4 (TR4) of Fusarium wilt, which has been confirmed in Mozambique and may spread. If you are planting for export in Africa, you must use certified tissue culture plantlets and implement strict biosecurity.

For local and regional markets, the calculus is different. Gros Michel and Apple Banana fetch higher prices per kilogram in Ugandan and Kenyan markets because consumers prefer their taste. East African Highland Bananas (Matooke) are the dominant cooking banana in the Great Lakes region, with steady demand in urban centres. Plantains dominate West African markets. The key is to match your variety to the specific market you can reliably supply, not to chase the highest price without a buyer.

3. Sources of Healthy Planting Material

There are two main sources of banana planting material: conventional suckers and tissue culture plantlets. Each has distinct cost, risk, and business implications.

Suckers are the traditional source. They are cheap or free if you have your own mother plants, but they carry a high risk of pests and diseases, including nematodes, weevils, and Fusarium wilt. Sword suckers (narrow leaves, vigorous) are preferred over water suckers (broad leaves, weak). A good sword sucker weighs 12 kg and has a corm diameter of at least 10 cm.

Tissue culture (TC) plantlets are produced in sterile laboratories from meristem tissue. They are disease-free, genetically uniform, and available in large quantities. They cost more per unit (USD 0.501.50 in East Africa) but deliver higher yields, earlier fruiting, and lower disease risk. TC plantlets are the standard for commercial export production and for any farmer serious about long-term productivity.

4. Sucker Selection and Preparation

If you choose suckers, selection and preparation are critical. Select sword suckers from healthy, high-yielding mother plants that have never shown signs of Fusarium wilt or weevil damage. Avoid suckers from plants with yellowing leaves, split pseudostems, or dark internal corm tissue. After digging, trim the roots and remove all soil. Pare the corm to remove any nematode or weevil damage, then dip the corm in hot water (5255C for 1520 minutes) or a recommended nematicide solution. This treatment reduces pest load but does not guarantee disease-free material. For commercial production, suckers should be a stopgap, not a long-term strategy.

SAFETY WARNING: Fusarium Wilt (TR4) and Planting Material Movement

Tropical Race 4 (TR4) of Fusarium wilt is a lethal soil-borne disease with no cure. It has been confirmed in Mozambique and poses a severe threat to African banana production. Never move suckers or soil from a farm with suspected Fusarium wilt to a clean farm. Do not share planting material between regions. Always use certified tissue culture plantlets for new plantings in high-risk areas. Report any unusual wilting or yellowing to your national plant protection authority immediately. Moving infected material can destroy your farm and your neighbours' farms for decades.

5. Tissue Culture Plantlets: Advantages and Handling

Tissue culture plantlets offer four business advantages: they are disease-free, genetically uniform, available year-round, and produce higher yields with earlier harvests. However, they are delicate. On arrival, plantlets should be removed from their containers and placed in a shaded nursery for 24 weeks to harden off. Water them lightly but regularly. Do not plant them directly into the field without acclimatisation. TC plantlets are more expensive per unit, but the return on investment is higher because of reduced replanting, lower disease losses, and premium market access. For export contracts, buyers increasingly require proof that planting material is TC-derived and certified.

6. Certification and Disease-Free Planting Material

Certification is your insurance policy. In Africa, certified banana planting material is produced by national research organisations (NARO in Uganda, KALRO in Kenya, CSIR in Ghana) and private nurseries that meet standards set by national plant protection authorities. Certification confirms that plantlets are true-to-type, disease-free, and free from pests. When buying TC plantlets, ask for the certification certificate, the variety name, the laboratory source, and the date of production. Avoid uncertified nurseries, no matter how cheap. A single infected plantlet can wipe out your entire plantation.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Match variety to market: Cavendish for export, Gros Michel/Apple Banana for local premium, Matooke/Plantain for cooking markets.
  • Check disease risk: Confirm TR4 status in your region before planting Cavendish; use resistant hybrids where available.
  • Prefer tissue culture for commercial plantings higher yield, lower disease risk, earlier harvest.
  • If using suckers, select sword suckers from healthy mother plants, pare corms, and hot-water treat before planting.
  • Never move planting material from a farm with suspected Fusarium wilt or weevil damage.
  • Demand certification for all TC plantlets: certificate, variety name, lab source, production date.
  • Harden off TC plantlets in a shaded nursery for 24 weeks before field planting.
  • Keep records of variety, source, planting date, and certification for every block essential for export traceability.
