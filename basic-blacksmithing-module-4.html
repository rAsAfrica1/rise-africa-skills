Lessons

1. Drawing Out: Stretching Metal

Drawing out stretches metal to make it longer and thinner. You do this by heating the steel to a bright orange-yellow (around 1,100C) and hammering it on the anvil's face or horn. Start with a 12mm round bar. Heat the first 10cm in your forge until it glows like a setting sun. Place it on the anvil with the hot end hanging over the edge. Strike with the flat face of your hammer, aiming slightly over the edge. Each blow pushes metal outward. Turn the bar 90 degrees after every two or three strikes to keep it square. If you only hit one side, you'll make a flat blade instead of a taper. Work quicklyyou have about 20 seconds before the metal cools to a dull red. When it stops moving, reheat. For a long taper, like a fire poker, you'll need three or four heats. Use the horn of the anvil for curves and the flat face for straight draws. A 2kg hammer is good for heavy drawing; a 1kg hammer for control. In Africa, many smiths use a simple block anvil made from a piece of railway line. That works fine. Keep your hammer face polisheddents in the hammer will transfer to the work. After drawing, you can planish with light blows to smooth the surface. Remember: draw out on the anvil's edge, not the center. The edge acts as a fulcrum. If you hear a ringing sound, your metal is too coldstop or you'll crack it. Quench in water only if you need to cool it fast, but for drawing, let it air-cool or reheat. Practice on scrap rebar until you can make a smooth taper from 12mm to 4mm in three heats.

2. Upsetting: Thickening the End of a Bar

Upsetting is the opposite of drawing out: you make a bar shorter and thicker. This is useful for making bolt heads, decorative finials, or a mushroom shape on a rod. Heat the end of a 12mm bar to a bright yellow (1,200C). Hold the bar vertically with the hot end down. Strike the cold end with a heavy hammer, or better, slam the hot end onto the anvil face. The force travels up the bar and upsets the hot end. Do this quicklythree or four solid hits. You can also place the hot end in a vise and hammer the cold end, but that's slower. For a controlled upset, like a bolt head, heat only the last 2cm. Use a 4kg sledge for the striking blow if you have one. After each blow, the metal thickens and cools. Reheat as needed. If the bar bends, straighten it on the anvil. A common mistake is to hit too lightlyyou need a solid, direct blow. For a decorative ball on a gate, upset a 5cm section to 20mm diameter, then round it with a hammer. In rural Africa, you can use a large stone as an anvil for upsetting, but a steel anvil gives better rebound. Upsetting requires more force than drawing out, so keep your metal hot. If you see the end turning blue, it's too coldreheat immediately. After upsetting, you can forge the thickened end into a square, hex, or round shape. Practice by making a 10cm long, 20mm diameter head on a 12mm bar. That's a classic blacksmith exercise.

3. Bending and Curving Metal

Bending and curving are essential for making hooks, brackets, rings, and decorative scrolls. You can bend cold metal if it's thin (under 6mm) using a vise and hammer, but thicker bars (10mm and up) must be heated to a red-orange (900C). For a sharp 90-degree bend, heat a 5cm section, place it over the anvil's edge, and strike the cold side with a hammer while holding the other end. For a smooth curve, use the anvil's horn. Heat a 10cm section, lay it across the horn, and pull the ends down evenly. For a circle, bend around a pipe or a mandrel. A common method: heat a 30cm bar, clamp one end in a vise, and pull the other end around a 5cm pipe. You can also use a bending forka piece of flat bar with two prongs that fits in the hardy hole. For scrolls, bend a series of small curves in opposite directions. Always bend with the grain of the metal if you can. If you bend cold, the metal may crackwatch for small tears. In Africa, many smiths use a forked tree branch as a bending jig. That works for simple curves. After bending, you can adjust the shape with a hammer on the anvil. For a tight curl, use a pair of scrolling tongs. Remember: metal has a memory. If you bend it too far, it may spring back slightly. Overbend by 5 degrees to compensate. For a decorative S-hook, heat the middle, bend one end 180 degrees, then the other. Practice with 8mm round bar until you can make a perfect 10cm ring.

4. Twisting Metal for Decorative Effects

Twisting gives a decorative spiral to square or round bars, often used for gates, railings, and fire tools. Heat a 20cm section of 10mm square bar to a uniform orange (1,000C). Clamp one end in a vise. Grip the other end with a twisting wrench or a large adjustable spanner. Turn it clockwise or counterclockwisekeep the turns even. For a tight twist, turn 3 full rotations over 10cm. For a loose twist, 1 rotation over 10cm. The metal will shorten as it twists, so allow extra length. If you twist while the metal is too cold, it will crack or shear. Work in one continuous motion. For a double twist, twist one section clockwise, then the next section counterclockwise. This creates a rope-like pattern. You can also twist two bars together: heat both, clamp them side by side in a vise, and twist the free ends together. This is called a cable twist. After twisting, you can flatten the twist slightly with a hammer for a different look. In Africa, twisted bars are popular for window grills and door handles. Use a pipe wrench if you don't have a twisting wrenchit grips better. For a perfect twist, mark the start and end with a chisel before heating. Twist only the marked section. Quench in oil if you want to keep the twist from untwisting, but usually air-cooling is fine. Practice on 30cm of 8mm square bar: make a 10cm twist with 4 rotations. Then try a reverse twist.

5. Cutting Metal with a Chisel or Hardy

Cutting hot metal is done with a hot chisel or a hardy. A hardy is a sharpened tool that fits in the anvil's hardy hole. A hot chisel has a wooden handle and a wedge-shaped blade. To cut a 12mm bar, heat it to a bright orange (1,000C). Place it over the hardy with the cut line aligned. Strike the bar with a hammerthe hardy cuts from below. Turn the bar 180 degrees and strike again. Repeat until the bar is nearly cut through, then break it off. For a cold chisel, you can cut cold metal up to 6mm, but it's hard work. For hot cutting, use a hot chisel struck from above. Place the bar on the anvil face, hold the chisel vertically on the cut line, and hit the chisel with a 2kg hammer. The chisel will sink through the metal. Do not cut all the way throughleave a thin web, then bend the bar to break it. This prevents damage to the chisel edge. Always wear safety glasses: hot metal chips fly. In Africa, many smiths use a machete or a sharpened leaf spring as a hot chisel. That works if you keep it cool by dipping in water. For a clean cut, cut on the hardy for square bars, and use a chisel for round bars. After cutting, dress the end with a file or hammer. Practice cutting a 20mm bar into three pieces. Remember: never cut cold metal with a hot chiselit will chip.

6. Punching and Drifting Holes

Punching makes a hole in hot metal without drilling. You need a punch (a tapered steel rod) and a drift (a larger tapered rod to shape the hole). Heat a 10mm thick flat bar to a bright yellow (1,200C). Place it on the anvil. Hold the punch vertically over the mark and strike it with a 2kg hammer. Drive the punch halfway through, then flip the bar and punch from the other side. This avoids a ragged hole. For a 10mm hole, start with a 6mm punch, then drift it open. A drift is driven through the hole while the metal is hot to enlarge and shape it. For a round hole, use a round drift; for a square hole, use a square drift. After drifting, the metal around the hole thickensthis is good for strength. If the hole needs to be precise, reheat and drift again. In Africa, many smiths punch holes for hinges, handles, and decorative cutouts. Use a punch made from a car axle or a spring steel rod. Keep the punch cool by dipping it in water every few strikes. If the punch sticks, don't twist itstrike it out with a hammer. For a clean hole, punch on a block of wood or a lead block if you have one, but the anvil works. After punching, you can file the hole smooth. Practice by punching a 12mm hole in a 5cm wide, 10mm thick bar. Then drift it to 16mm. Remember: punch while the metal is yellow-hot; if it turns red, reheat. Never punch cold metalyou'll break your punch.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Basic Metalworking Techniques for the African Forge

This Practical Reference lesson is the working heart of the Basic Blacksmithing module. It condenses the six core techniques you have studied drawing out, upsetting, bending and curving, twisting, cutting with a chisel or hardy, and punching and drifting holes into a single bench-side guide you can pin to the wall of your workshop. Whether you are producing agricultural tools for local farmers, decorative ironwork for urban customers, or repair services for your community, these techniques determine the quality, speed, and profitability of every job that leaves your forge.

In the African context, blacksmithing is rarely a hobby. It is a business. A smith in Kumasi, Kano, Hawassa, or Gweru competes on price, durability, and finish. Mastering these six techniques lets you reduce material waste (scrap steel is expensive), cut production time per unit, and command higher prices for finished goods. Every technique below is presented with that commercial reality in mind: what it does, when to use it, what it costs you in fuel and time, and how to avoid the mistakes that destroy stock.

Heat Colours and Working Ranges

Reading heat colour is the single most important skill for efficient forging. Working steel at the wrong temperature wastes fuel, damages tools, and produces weak products. The table below gives the working ranges for mild steel, the most common material in African forges (often reclaimed from vehicle leaf springs, rebar, or scrap plate).

SAFETY WARNING HOT METAL AND FLYING SCALE

Never strike a cold bar with a heavy hammer it can shatter and send fragments into your eyes. Always wear safety glasses or a face shield when cutting with a chisel or hardy, as hot scale and metal chips fly unpredictably. Keep a bucket of clean water within arm's reach of the anvil for burns and for cooling tools. Do not quench your hand in it quench the burn under running water for at least 10 minutes. Ensure your forge has cross-ventilation; charcoal and coal fumes contain carbon monoxide. In shared workshops, mark a 2-metre "hot zone" around the anvil and enforce it. In many African markets, second-hand safety glasses are available cheaply from welding supply stalls buy two pairs and keep one as a spare. A single eye injury can end a smith's career.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Heat to the right colour: Bright orange for drawing out and bending, yellow for upsetting, cutting, and punching. Never work below cherry red.
  2. Draw out on the horn or face: Use the horn for rapid tapering, the flat face for controlled elongation. Rotate the bar 90 between blows to keep it square.
  3. Upset with the cold end down: Strike the cold end against the anvil face to drive metal back into the hot end. Keep the hot section short to avoid buckling.
  4. Bend hot, not cold: For radii tighter than 3 bar thickness, heat to bright orange. Use the horn, vise, or bending forks for consistent curves.
  5. Twist evenly: Clamp one end in the vise, apply a wrench or twisting bar to the other. Count turns and keep heat uniform uneven heat gives uneven twist.
  6. Cut with the hardy for cold, chisel for hot: Place the bar over the hardy and strike. For chisel cuts, cut through, flip, and finish from the other side to avoid a burred edge.
  7. Punch from both sides: Punch halfway, flip the bar, and punch through from the other side. This keeps the hole aligned and prevents a tapered hole.
  8. Drift to size while hot: Drive the drift through the punched hole to enlarge and true it. Reheat as needed never drift cold steel.
  9. Record every job: Note fuel used, time taken, and selling price. This data is the foundation of a profitable forge business.
