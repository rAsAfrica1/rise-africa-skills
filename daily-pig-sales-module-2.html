1. Litters Per Sow Per Year

Litters per sow per year (LPSY) is the number of times a sow gives birth in 12 months. It is the first number you must know because it controls how many pigs you can sell. In Africa, many small farms get only 1.2 to 1.5 litters per sow per year. Good farms get 2.0 to 2.2. The difference is management, not magic.

To calculate LPSY, use this formula: LPSY = 365 Ã· (gestation + lactation + weaning-to-service interval). Gestation is fixed at 114 days (about 3 months, 3 weeks, 3 days). Lactation is how long you leave piglets on the sow. Weaning-to-service is the days from weaning until the sow is bred again. For example, if you wean at 28 days and the sow is bred 7 days later, the cycle is 114 + 28 + 7 = 149 days. 365 Ã· 149 = 2.45 litters per year. But that is theoretical. In reality, some sows return to heat late, some abort, some die. So you must track actual dates.

Use a simple notebook or a wall chart. Write the sow's ID, date farrowed, date weaned, date bred back. At the end of the year, count how many litters each sow gave. Average them. If your average is below 2.0, look at three things: (1) Are you weaning too late? Wean at 21-28 days, not 8 weeks. (2) Are you missing heat? Check for standing heat twice a day, every day, after weaning. (3) Are sows too thin or too fat? Body condition score 3 out of 5 is ideal. Feed a flush of 2.5 kg per day from weaning until breeding.

In Africa, heat stress is a major killer of LPSY. When temperatures rise above 30Â°C, sows stop cycling. Provide shade, wallows, or sprinklers. Breed early morning or late evening. Also, keep a boar near the sowsâhis smell and sound stimulate heat. If you use artificial insemination, order semen from a trusted supplier and breed twice, 12 hours apart.

Improving LPSY from 1.5 to 2.0 means one extra litter per sow every two years. For a 10-sow farm, that is 5 extra litters per year, or about 50 extra pigs. At market age, that is real money. Start tracking today.

2. Pigs Weaned Per Litter

Pigs weaned per litter (PWL) is the number of piglets that survive from birth until weaning. It measures both the sow's mothering ability and your management. In Africa, many farms wean only 5 to 7 pigs per litter. Good farms wean 9 to 11. The difference is often simple: colostrum, warmth, and iron.

To calculate PWL, count the piglets born alive, then count how many are alive at weaning. PWL = piglets weaned Ã· number of litters. For example, if your sow farrows 10 piglets, 2 are stillborn, and 1 dies before weaning, you wean 7. That is your PWL for that litter. Average it over all litters in a year.

Why do piglets die? The first 3 days are the danger zone. Piglets are born with no fat and no immunity. They need colostrum within 6 hours. If a piglet does not suckle, it will die. So watch every birth. Dry the piglets with a clean cloth, clear their noses, and put them on the teat. If a sow has more than 12 piglets, split-suckle: remove the smallest half for 2 hours, then rotate. Or use a foster sow. In Africa, many farmers lose piglets to crushing. Provide a creep areaâa warm, dry corner with a heat lamp or a box with straw. The sow lies on the piglets if she is uncomfortable. Give her a cool floor and plenty of water.

Iron deficiency is common. Piglets are born with low iron and sow's milk has little. Without iron, they become anaemic and die. Inject 200 mg of iron dextran into the neck muscle at 3 days old. Also clip needle teeth and cut tails (if you do that) at the same time. Keep the pen clean and dry. Wet floors cause pneumonia and scours. Scours (diarrhoea) can kill a whole litter in 24 hours. Vaccinate sows against E. coli and rotavirus before farrowing. If scours starts, isolate the litter and give oral rehydration salts.

Weaning age matters. Wean at 21-28 days. Earlier weaning means more litters per year, but piglets need special feed. In Africa, many farmers wean at 6-8 weeks because they lack creep feed. That lowers LPSY. If you can, buy or make a simple creep feed: maize, soya, and fish meal. Start offering it at 7 days old. By weaning, piglets should eat 200-300 g per day.

Track PWL per sow. If a sow consistently weans fewer than 6, cull her. Keep records. A simple notebook works. Your goal: 9+ pigs weaned per litter.

3. Survival Rate to Market

Survival rate to market is the percentage of pigs that reach sale weight (usually 70-90 kg) from those born alive. It is the third core formula. In Africa, many farms lose 20-30% of pigs after weaning. Good farms keep losses under 10%. The difference is feed, water, shelter, and health.

Formula: Survival rate = (pigs sold Ã· pigs born alive) Ã 100. For example, if 100 piglets are born alive and you sell 85, survival is 85%. Track this per batch, not just per year. If you sell daily, keep a tally of pigs that die or are culled. Subtract from the number born alive.

What kills pigs after weaning? The biggest causes are: (1) Feed shortage or poor feed. Pigs need a balanced diet. A simple grower ration: 60% maize, 25% soya or groundnut cake, 10% fish meal, 5% minerals and salt. Mix well. (2) Water. A pig drinks 2-3 times its feed. If water is dirty or scarce, growth stops. Provide clean water always. (3) Parasites. Roundworms, lice, and mange slow growth. Deworm every 3 months with ivermectin or levamisole. (4) Disease. African Swine Fever (ASF) has no vaccine. It kills almost all pigs. Prevent it by strict biosecurity: no visitors into pens, no raw swill, no buying pigs from unknown sources. If ASF hits, cull and burn. Other diseases: pneumonia (treat with antibiotics), diarrhoea (treat with oral rehydration), and erysipelas (vaccinate).

Housing matters. In hot climates, pigs need shade and ventilation. Build pens with open sides, a solid roof, and a concrete floor that slopes to a drain. Overcrowding causes stress and disease. Allow 1.5 square metres per grower pig. In cold areas, provide deep bedding. Predatorsâdogs, snakes, birdsâcan kill small pigs. Cover windows with wire mesh.

Record every death. Write date, pig ID, symptoms, and action taken. After a few months, you will see patterns. If many pigs die at 8-10 weeks, it might be weaning stress. If they die at 4-6 months, it might be parasites or feed quality. Fix the pattern, and survival rises.

Aim for 90% survival. For every 100 piglets born alive, sell 90. That is the target.

4. Pigs Available Per Week

Pigs available per week is the number of pigs you can sell each week from your batch farrowing system. It is the bridge between your breeding herd and your market. If you sell daily, you need a steady supply. This formula tells you if you have enough sows.

Formula: Pigs per week = (Number of sows Ã Litters per sow per year Ã Pigs weaned per litter Ã Survival rate) Ã· 52. For example, 10 sows Ã 2.0 LPSY Ã 9 PWL Ã 0.9 survival = 162 pigs per year Ã· 52 = 3.1 pigs per week. So you can sell about 3 pigs every week.

But batch farrowing changes the flow. In batch farrowing, you breed a group of sows on the same day, so they farrow together, wean together, and finish together. That means you do not get pigs every weekâyou get a batch every few weeks. To sell daily, you need multiple batches staggered. For example, if you have 4 batches, each farrowing 3 weeks apart, you can sell from one batch for 3 weeks, then switch to the next. That gives a steady weekly supply.

To calculate your weekly availability from batches: First, decide your batch interval. Common intervals: weekly, bi-weekly (every 2 weeks), or monthly. If you farrow 10 sows every 5 weeks, and each sow weans 9 pigs, you get 90 pigs per batch. Those pigs will be ready for market at about 5-6 months. So you sell 90 pigs over the next 5 weeksâthat is 18 pigs per week. But you must have another batch farrowing 5 weeks later to keep the flow. So you need a breeding schedule that repeats every 5 weeks.

In Africa, many farmers sell at 5-6 months (70-90 kg). If you want daily sales, you need to plan your batches so that you always have pigs reaching market weight. Use a calendar. Mark farrowing dates, weaning dates, and expected market dates. Then work backwards to breeding dates.

If you have only a few sows, you may not have pigs every week. That is okay. You can sell every 2 weeks or monthly. But if you want daily sales, you need at least 20-30 sows in a batch system. Start small, track your numbers, and expand as you learn.

5. How Many Sows You Need

How many sows you need depends on how many pigs you want to sell per week. This lesson works backwards from your sales goal to your breeding herd. It is the most important planning formula.

Formula: Number of sows = (Pigs needed per week Ã 52) Ã· (Litters per sow per year Ã Pigs weaned per litter Ã Survival rate). For example, you want to sell 10 pigs per week. You expect 2.0 LPSY, 9 PWL, and 90% survival. So sows = (10 Ã 52) Ã· (2.0 Ã 9 Ã 0.9) = 520 Ã· 16.2 = 32.1. You need about 32 sows.

But that is the average. In batch farrowing, you need to round up to the nearest batch size. If you farrow in batches of 8 sows every 3 weeks, then 32 sows means 4 batches. But you also need replacement gilts. About 20-25% of sows are culled each year. So add 20% more: 32 Ã 1.2 = 38.4, so 39 sows. Also, you need boars or AI. One boar can serve 10-15 sows in hand mating, or 20-30 with AI. For 39 sows, use 2-3 boars or AI.

In Africa, many farmers start with 2-5 sows. That is fine. If you have 5 sows, your weekly pigs = (5 Ã 2 Ã 9 Ã 0.9) Ã· 52 = 81 Ã· 52 = 1.6 pigs per week. You cannot sell daily, but you can sell 1-2 pigs per week. If you want more, increase sows. But first, improve LPSY and PWL. A farm with 5 sows and 2.5 LPSY, 10 PWL, 95% survival gets (5 Ã 2.5 Ã 10 Ã 0.95) Ã· 52 = 118.75 Ã· 52 = 2.3 pigs per week. Better management beats more sows.

Also consider housing and feed. Each sow needs 2-2.5 kg of feed per day, plus piglets. A sow with a litter eats 5-6 kg per day. So 10 sows need 50-60 kg of feed daily. Can you afford that? If not, reduce sows. Start with what you can feed well. It is better to have 5 healthy sows than 20 hungry ones.

Use this formula every time you plan to expand. Write your goal, plug in your numbers, and see how many sows you need. Then check your resources. If you cannot feed them, do not buy them.

6. Working the Formula Backwards

Working the formula backwards means starting with your desired sales and calculating what you need to do to achieve it. This is the most powerful planning tool. Instead of guessing, you set a target and work back to sows, feed, and housing.

Let us say you want to earn 100,000 Naira per week from pig sales. If a market pig sells for 25,000 Naira, you need 4 pigs per week. Now use the formula: Sows = (4 Ã 52) Ã· (LPSY Ã PWL Ã Survival). Assume you can achieve 2.0 LPSY, 9 PWL, 0.9 survival. Sows = 208 Ã· 16.2 = 12.8, so 13 sows. Add 20% for replacements: 16 sows. That is your target. Now work back further: 16 sows need 16 Ã 2.5 kg = 40 kg of sow feed per day, plus piglet feed. You need a feed budget. If feed costs 300 Naira per kg, that is 12,000 Naira per day just for sows. Can you afford that? If not, you need a higher sale price or lower feed cost. Maybe you can grow your own maize.

Next, work back to breeding. To get 4 pigs per week, you need 4 Ã 52 = 208 pigs per year. With 16 sows, each must wean 9 pigs and have 2 litters per year. That means each sow must be bred 2 times per year. So you need to breed 16 sows every 6 months, or about 3 sows per month. But batch farrowing means you breed groups. If you want weekly sales, you need 4 batches, each farrowing 4 sows every 13 weeks (quarterly). That is 16 sows total. Each batch farrows 4 sows Ã 9 pigs = 36 pigs. Those 36 pigs will be sold over 13 weeks, which is about 2.8 pigs per week. Close to 4. To get exactly 4, you need 5 sows per batch, so 20 sows total.

Now work back to housing. Each sow needs a farrowing pen (2m x 2m). Each batch of 5 sows needs 5 farrowing pens. You also need grower pens for 36 pigs per batch. At 1.5 sq m per pig, that is 54 sq m per batch. With 4 batches, you need 216 sq m. That is a big barn. You can reduce by selling earlier or using outdoor systems.

Finally, work back to cash flow. You will spend on feed, vaccines, and labour before you sell. You need a reserve. Many farmers fail because they run out of money before the first pigs are sold. Plan for 6 months of expenses. Use this backward method for any goal. Write your target, calculate sows, feed, housing, and money. Then adjust until it is realistic.

The goal is simple: know exactly how many pigs you can sell every week, when they will be ready, and how many sows you need to keep the system full. In African pig production, cash flow is tight and feed is the biggest cost. â ï¸ Safety Warning: Biosecurity and African Swine Fever

African Swine Fever (ASF) is the single greatest threat to pig production in Africa. There is no vaccine and no treatment. If you introduce new pigs without quarantine, share equipment between farms, or feed kitchen waste without proper boiling, you can lose your entire herd in days. Always quarantine new stock for at least 30 days, use dedicated boots and clothing, and never feed uncooked swill. A single ASF outbreak can wipe out 100% of your pigs and destroy your business. Report any sudden deaths to your local veterinary officer immediately.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Know your LPSY. Calculate it every quarter. If it drops below 2.0, investigate sow nutrition and weaning-to-service interval.
  2. Weigh and record piglets at birth and weaning. Low birth weight is the leading cause of pre-weaning mortality in African farms.
  3. Track survival rate monthly. A drop of more than 5% from your baseline means disease, feed, or housing problems.
  4. Use the master formula weekly. Pigs/week = (Sows Ã LPSY Ã Weaned/Litter Ã Survival) Ã· 52. Post it in your office.
  5. Match sows to market orders. Never keep more sows than your market can absorb. Use the backward formula to plan breeding.
  6. Batch farrow in groups of 5â10 sows. This gives you uniform pigs for sale every week and simplifies management.
  7. Keep a breeding calendar. Record service dates, expected farrowing dates, and weaning dates for every sow.
  8. Review feed conversion. Feed is 60â70% of costs. If FCR is above 3.5, your profit margin is disappearing.
