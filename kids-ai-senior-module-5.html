Fine-Tuning Basics - When to Train Your Own Model

~14 min read25 min gameAdvanced
Shared phone

🧠 Brain Opener

Sometimes a general AI is not enough. You need an AI that knows your business, your language, or your niche. That is when you fine-tune. But fine-tuning is not always the answer.

  • What is fine-tuning?
  • When should you fine-tune instead of just prompting?
  • Why is fine-tuning expensive?
🎁 Reveal a good answer

Fine-tuning means training an existing AI model further on your own data so it specialises. You should fine-tune when you have a lot of specific data and the general model cannot learn the pattern from a prompt alone. It is expensive because it requires data, computing power, and careful testing.

📖 Rudo Decides Not to Fine-Tune

Rudo was seventeen and ran a small tutoring service for primary school children. She had 800 past exam questions with model answers. She wanted to build an AI tutor that could answer any child question in the same style as her own teaching.

Her first idea was to fine-tune an AI on her 800 questions. She read about it online. It cost money. It took time. She decided to do it.

She spent two weeks cleaning her data. She found that 100 of her questions were duplicated. Another 50 had spelling mistakes. Another 30 had answers she was no longer happy with. She cleaned, cleaned, and cleaned.

Then she tried to fine-tune. The first attempt failed. The second attempt produced an AI that could answer exam questions but nothing else - not even hello.

She asked her uncle, who worked at a tech company. He listened carefully. Then he asked a question.

"How many examples do you have?"

"800."

"That is not enough for fine-tuning. You need at least 10,000 to do it well. And your data has to be very clean. What you want is a good prompt."

He showed her how to write a detailed prompt: role, task, style, examples, format. She ran it. It worked.

Her AI tutor answered questions in her style. It handled off-topic questions. It refused to answer inappropriate ones. It could be updated by editing the prompt instead of re-training.

She learned an important lesson. Fine-tuning is powerful. But it is not always the right tool. For 800 examples, a good prompt was the answer.

💡 The Big Idea

Prompt first, fine-tune last

Before you fine-tune, ask three questions. Do you have at least 10,000 clean examples? Have you already tried a very good prompt? Do you need this specific model, or would any good model work? If the answers are no, no, and no - do not fine-tune. Fix the prompt instead.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

What fine-tuning is

Fine-tuning takes an existing AI model and trains it further on your own data. The model keeps most of what it learned - language, reasoning, general knowledge - and adds your specific patterns on top.

A fine-tuned model can learn a specific writing style, a specific domain (like medicine or law), a specific language, or a specific task (like grading essays). It is more expensive to build but often more consistent and faster than prompting.

2

The four prerequisites for fine-tuning

Data: at least 10,000 clean, well-labelled examples. Budget: money for computing, or access to a platform that provides it. Time: several weeks of iteration. Expertise: someone who can judge output quality.

If you are missing any of these four, do not fine-tune. Use a prompt. Use retrieval (giving the model facts to use). Use a workflow. Fine-tuning is the last tool you reach for, not the first.

3

Alternatives to fine-tuning

Three alternatives, in order of ease. Better prompting: a five-part production prompt often beats a poorly fine-tuned model. Retrieval: give the model the facts it needs at the moment of answering, from a database or document. Workflow: combine prompts, tools, and logic so the system solves the problem in steps.

Professionals use all three before they consider fine-tuning. Fine-tuning is expensive. The alternatives are cheap. Try cheap first.

🛠 Hands-On - Test the Three Approaches

You need

  • A phone or laptop with AI access
  • A set of 20 real examples of a task
  • A document for notes
  1. Pick a task with a specific style (writing in your own voice, in a local language, in a niche domain).
  2. Approach 1 - write a very good production prompt with role, task, constraints, examples, format.
  3. Test on 20 examples. Score each output for quality.
  4. Approach 2 - write a retrieval prompt. Include the actual facts the AI needs in the prompt itself.
  5. Test on the same 20. Score.
  6. Compare the two scores. If approach 1 and 2 give you 90 percent or more, you do not need fine-tuning.
Why it works: Testing the cheap options first is the professional approach. Fine-tuning is powerful but expensive and unnecessary when a prompt will do.

Try this prompt: "I have 800 exam questions in my own teaching style. I want an AI tutor that answers in my style. Should I fine-tune, or is a prompt enough? Walk me through the decision."

The answer should say: 800 is too few for fine-tuning. Try a detailed prompt with examples first. Test the quality. Only fine-tune if you later have 10,000 or more clean examples.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

African language models, 2023

Several African tech companies are fine-tuning open-source AI models on African languages - Shona, Ndebele, Amharic, Hausa, Yoruba. Each needs tens of thousands of clean text examples. Without this data, the general models perform poorly in these languages. Fine-tuning is the only way.

Zimbabwe medical AI

A Zimbabwean health startup fine-tuned an AI on 50,000 anonymised patient records from local clinics. The model now recognises local disease patterns better than general medical AIs. The work took one year and needed patient consent for every record.

🏆 Project Challenge

Decide: Prompt or Fine-Tune?

Take a real project and decide the right approach.

  1. Pick a project where you want AI to do something specific.
  2. Count how many clean examples you have.
  3. Write a production prompt. Test on 20 examples. Score.
  4. Add retrieval - include the needed facts in the prompt. Test again.
  5. If you are at 90 percent or higher, you do not need fine-tuning.
  6. If you are not, decide what data you would need to collect. Estimate the cost.
  7. Write a one-page decision: what approach, why, and what comes next.
