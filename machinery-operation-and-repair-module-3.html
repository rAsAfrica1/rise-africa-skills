1. Lesson 1: Introduction to Machinery Operation and Repair in Africa

Welcome to Module 3 of the Machinery Operation and Repair course. This lesson sets the foundation for everything that follows. Across Africa, machinery drives agriculture, mining, construction, transport, and manufacturing. From the smallholder farmer using a two-wheel tractor in Kenya to the quarry operator running a jaw crusher in Ghana, machines multiply human effort and create income. But machines also break down. When they do, the cost is not just the repair bill; it is lost production, missed deadlines, unpaid workers, and damaged customer trust. Your ability to operate and repair machinery is therefore a direct business skill. This module is designed for owners, operators, technicians, and supervisors who want to keep equipment running profitably in African conditions. We begin with the operating environment. African conditions are not the same as those in Europe, North America, or Asia. You face dust, heat, humidity, poor roads, unreliable power, long supply chains, and limited access to original spare parts. A machine that performs well in a temperate climate may overheat in the Sahel or rust in the coastal humidity of Mombasa or Lagos. Fuel quality varies. Lubricants may be counterfeit. Operators may be trained informally. These realities shape how you select, operate, maintain, and repair machinery. Good operation starts before the engine turns. You must understand the machine's purpose, capacity, and limitations. Read the manual, even if it is in a language you find difficult. Use diagrams and part numbers. Identify the rated load, speed, temperature range, and fuel type. Know the difference between continuous duty and intermittent duty. A generator rated for standby use will fail quickly if run continuously at full load. A tractor designed for 40 horsepower implements will struggle with 60 horsepower implements. Matching machine to task is the first profit decision. Next, establish a daily operating routine. Before starting any machine, conduct a walk-around inspection. Check fuel, oil, coolant, hydraulic fluid, tyre pressure or track tension, belts, hoses, filters, and loose fittings. Look for leaks, cracks, and abnormal wear. Clean around filler caps before opening them to prevent dirt entering the system. In dusty environments, this single habit prevents many engine failures. Start the machine according to the manual, let it warm up, and listen for unusual noises. Record hours, fuel used, and any observations in a logbook. This logbook becomes your early warning system and your repair history. During operation, monitor gauges and warning lights. Watch for black smoke, blue smoke, white smoke, overheating, low oil pressure, unusual vibration, and loss of power. Stop the machine if a critical warning appears. Many catastrophic failures begin as small, ignored symptoms. Train operators to report problems immediately, not at the end of the shift. Create a culture where stopping the machine is respected, not punished. After operation, cool down, park safely, and perform post-operation checks. Clean the machine, especially around air intakes, radiators, and hydraulic components. Refuel at the end of the day to reduce condensation in the tank. Secure the machine against theft and vandalism. In many African settings, security is a real operational cost. Now consider repair. Repair is not only fixing broken parts; it is restoring function safely and economically. The first step is diagnosis. Ask the operator what happened, when it happened, and what changed. Use your senses: look, listen, smell, and touch carefully. Check the simplest causes first: fuel valve closed, battery disconnected, safety switch engaged, blocked filter, loose wire. Use a systematic approach rather than guessing. For example, if an engine will not start, check fuel supply, air supply, compression, and ignition or injection timing in that order. If a hydraulic system is weak, check fluid level, filter, pump pressure, relief valve, and cylinder seals. Diagnosis saves money because it prevents unnecessary parts replacement. In Africa, parts availability is a major constraint. Build relationships with reliable suppliers locally and abroad. Keep critical spares in stock: filters, belts, hoses, seals, bearings, fuses, relays, injectors, and water pumps. Learn to identify genuine parts versus counterfeit parts. Counterfeit filters, bearings, and lubricants destroy machines. Buy from authorised dealers when possible, and verify part numbers. When genuine parts are unavailable, consider quality alternatives, but never compromise on safety-critical components such as brake parts, steering parts, or lifting gear. Repair skills must be practical. Learn to use basic tools correctly: spanners, sockets, torque wrench, multimeter, pressure gauge, bearing puller, and measuring instruments. Learn to read wiring diagrams and hydraulic schematics. Practice on scrap components before working on live machines. Safety is paramount. Isolate energy sources before repair: turn off engine, disconnect battery, release hydraulic pressure, block raised components, and lock out controls. Use personal protective equipment: gloves, goggles, boots, hearing protection, and dust masks. Never work under a machine supported only by a jack. Use stands. Never repair a machine while it is running unless the procedure explicitly requires it and you are trained. Finally, think like a business. Every machine has a cost per hour of operation. That cost includes fuel, lubricants, maintenance, repairs, operator wages, insurance, and depreciation. Your goal is to minimise downtime and cost per unit of output. Keep records. Track failures. Identify repeat problems. Decide when to repair and when to replace. A machine that costs more to repair than it earns should be sold or scrapped. A well-maintained machine lasts longer, uses less fuel, and fetches a better resale price. In this module, you will learn specific systems: engines, fuel systems, cooling systems, lubrication systems, electrical systems, hydraulic systems, transmissions, brakes, and undercarriage. You will also learn preventive maintenance schedules, troubleshooting methods, workshop safety, and spare parts management. Each lesson builds on the last. By the end, you should be able to operate machinery safely, diagnose common faults, perform routine repairs, and make better business decisions about your equipment. Remember: in Africa, machinery is not a luxury. It is a tool for creating value. Treat it with respect, maintain it diligently, and it will serve you for years.

2. Lesson 2: Engine Systems and Routine Maintenance

The engine is the heart of most machinery. Whether it is a diesel engine in a generator, a petrol engine in a water pump, or a multi-cylinder engine in a tractor or truck, the principles are similar. This lesson covers how engines work, how to maintain them, and how to diagnose common problems in African operating conditions. We focus on practical, business-first knowledge that keeps equipment earning. An internal combustion engine converts fuel into mechanical energy. It draws in air, compresses it, adds fuel, ignites the mixture, and expels exhaust gases. In a four-stroke engine, these events occur in four piston movements: intake, compression, power, and exhaust. In a two-stroke engine, they occur in two movements. Diesel engines compress air until it is hot enough to ignite injected fuel. Petrol engines use a spark plug to ignite a fuel-air mixture. Diesel engines are common in Africa because they are durable, fuel-efficient, and produce high torque at low speed. However, they are sensitive to dirty fuel and poor maintenance. The main engine systems are the fuel system, air intake system, exhaust system, cooling system, lubrication system, and electrical system. Each must work correctly for the engine to run reliably. Let us start with the fuel system. Diesel fuel must be clean, water-free, and of the correct grade. Contaminated fuel is one of the most common causes of engine failure in Africa. Store fuel in clean tanks, allow it to settle, and filter it before use. Use a water separator and a primary fuel filter. Replace fuel filters at the recommended intervals, or more often if fuel quality is poor. Bleed the fuel system after changing filters or running out of fuel. Air in the fuel system causes hard starting, rough running, and power loss. The injectors must spray fuel in a fine mist at the correct pressure and timing. Dirty injectors cause black smoke, misfiring, and high fuel consumption. Have injectors tested and cleaned by a specialist. The injection pump is precision equipment; do not attempt repairs unless you are trained and have the correct tools. The air intake system supplies clean air. Dust is the enemy. In dry, dusty conditions, check and clean the air filter daily. Use the correct filter element. Do not run the engine without an air filter. Replace the filter when it is clogged, and never blow compressed air through a paper filter from the inside unless the manual permits it. Check the intake hoses and clamps for leaks. A leaking intake allows unfiltered air into the engine, causing rapid wear. The exhaust system removes gases. Check for leaks, loose joints, and damaged silencers. A blocked exhaust causes power loss and overheating. The cooling system controls temperature. It includes the radiator, water pump, thermostat, fan, belts, hoses, and coolant. Overheating is a major cause of engine damage. Check coolant level daily when the engine is cold. Use a mixture of clean water and antifreeze or corrosion inhibitor. In hot climates, do not remove the radiator cap while the engine is hot. Check the fan belt tension and condition. Replace cracked or glazed belts. Inspect hoses for softness, cracks, or leaks. Clean the radiator fins with compressed air or water, especially after working in dust or crop residue. The thermostat must open and close correctly. If the engine runs cold, the thermostat may be stuck open; if it overheats, the thermostat may be stuck closed or the radiator may be blocked. The lubrication system reduces friction and carries away heat. It includes the oil pump, oil filter, oil galleries, and sump. Use the correct oil grade for your climate and engine. In Africa, high ambient temperatures may require a heavier oil grade. Check oil level daily. Change oil and filter at the recommended interval, or sooner if the engine works in dusty conditions. Do not mix different oil brands unless necessary, and never use cooking oil or used oil. Dirty oil causes bearing failure, piston ring wear, and turbocharger damage. The electrical system starts the engine and powers instruments and lights. It includes the battery, starter motor, alternator, wiring, fuses, and switches. Keep battery terminals clean and tight. Check electrolyte level in lead-acid batteries. Use distilled water, not tap water. Test the charging system with a multimeter. A weak battery or faulty alternator causes hard starting and unreliable operation. Now consider routine maintenance. A maintenance schedule is a plan that prevents breakdowns. It is based on hours of operation, fuel used, or calendar time. Typical daily checks include fuel level, oil level, coolant level, air filter condition, leaks, tyre pressure, and warning lights. Weekly checks include battery electrolyte, belt tension, radiator fins, and grease points. Monthly checks include fuel filter, oil filter, air filter, hydraulic filter, and bolt torque. Annual checks include injector testing, valve clearance adjustment, cooling system flush, and turbocharger inspection. Keep a logbook for each machine. Record the date, hours, maintenance performed, parts used, and any observations. This record helps you plan repairs, control costs, and prove maintenance history when selling the machine. Troubleshooting engines requires a logical approach. If an engine will not start, check fuel, air, compression, and ignition or injection timing. If it starts but runs rough, check fuel quality, filters, injectors, and air intake. If it overheats, check coolant level, radiator, thermostat, fan belt, and load. If it lacks power, check air filter, fuel filter, exhaust, turbocharger, and injectors. If it uses too much fuel, check injectors, air filter, tyre pressure, and operating technique. If it smokes black, the fuel-air mixture is too rich or the air filter is blocked. If it smokes blue, the engine is burning oil, possibly due to worn rings or valve guides. If it smokes white, there may be water in the fuel or a coolant leak into the cylinder. In Africa, fuel quality and dust are the biggest threats to engine life. Buy fuel from reputable stations. Use a funnel with a fine mesh when refuelling. Keep fuel tanks clean. Service the air filter religiously. Train operators to warm up engines before loading them and to cool them down before shutting off. Avoid idling for long periods; it wastes fuel and causes carbon build-up. Finally, think about the business case for maintenance. A well-maintained engine uses less fuel, lasts longer, and has a higher resale value. A neglected engine fails unexpectedly, causing lost income and expensive repairs. The cost of a daily oil check is almost zero. The cost of a seized engine is enormous. Make maintenance a habit, not an afterthought. Your engine is an investment; protect it.

3. Lesson 3: Hydraulics, Transmissions, and Undercarriage

Machinery often uses hydraulic systems, transmissions, and undercarriage components to convert engine power into useful work. This lesson covers these systems, their maintenance, and common repairs in African conditions. Hydraulics use pressurised fluid to transmit force. They are found in excavators, loaders, tractors, forklifts, tipper trucks, and many other machines. A basic hydraulic system includes a reservoir, pump, filter, control valve, hoses, cylinders, and motor. The pump creates flow. The control valve directs flow. The cylinder converts flow and pressure into linear force. The motor converts flow and pressure into rotary force. The reservoir stores fluid, allows air to escape, and helps cool the system. The filter removes particles that would damage components. Hydraulic fluid must be clean and of the correct type. Contamination is the number one cause of hydraulic failure. Dirt, water, and metal particles cause wear, sticking valves, and pump failure. Always clean around filler caps and connections before opening the system. Use a filter cart when filling. Change hydraulic filters at the recommended interval. Check fluid level daily. Look for leaks, damaged hoses, and loose fittings. Hoses can fail suddenly, causing injury and environmental damage. Inspect hoses for cracks, blisters, and abrasion. Replace hoses that show damage. Use the correct pressure rating. Never repair a hydraulic hose with tape or clamps. Use proper fittings and crimping equipment. Hydraulic oil can be hot. Allow the system to cool before working on it. Release pressure before disconnecting lines. Use a pressure gauge to test pump pressure and relief valve settings. If the system is weak, check fluid level, filter, pump pressure, relief valve, and cylinder seals. If the system is slow, check flow, valve settings, and oil viscosity. If the system overheats, check oil level, cooler, relief valve, and load. Transmissions transfer power from the engine to the wheels or tracks. They include manual gearboxes, automatic transmissions, torque converters, clutches, and final drives. In Africa, manual transmissions are common because they are simpler and cheaper to repair. However, automatic transmissions are increasing in trucks and construction equipment. The clutch connects and disconnects the engine from the transmission. It must engage smoothly and not slip. A slipping clutch causes loss of power and overheating. A dragging clutch causes difficult gear engagement. Check clutch pedal free play and hydraulic fluid level if applicable. Bleed the clutch system if it is hydraulic. Replace worn clutch plates and release bearings. Gearboxes contain gears, shafts, bearings, and synchronisers. They require the correct lubricant. Check oil level regularly. Change oil at the recommended interval. Listen for whining, grinding, or humming noises. These indicate bearing or gear wear. If a gearbox jumps out of gear, the detent springs or synchronisers may be worn. If it is noisy in one gear only, that gear may be damaged. Repairing a gearbox requires cleanliness, precision, and the correct tools. Do not force gears. Do not mix lubricants. Torque converters and automatic transmissions use fluid pressure to shift gears. They are sensitive to dirty fluid and overheating. Check fluid level and condition. Burned fluid smells burnt and looks dark. Change fluid and filter as recommended. If the transmission slips, check fluid level, filter, and pressure. If it shifts harshly, check valve body and controls. Automatic transmission repair is specialised; seek expert help. Final drives and differentials reduce speed and increase torque. They contain gears, bearings, and seals. Check oil level. Look for leaks. Listen for noise. If a final drive is noisy, it may have worn bearings or gears. If it leaks, replace seals. Do not operate a machine with a leaking final drive; it will fail quickly. Undercarriage is found on tracked machines such as bulldozers, excavators, and crawler tractors. It includes tracks, rollers, idlers, sprockets, and track tensioners. Undercarriage is expensive to replace, so maintenance is critical. Check track tension daily. Too tight causes wear and power loss. Too loose causes derailment. Adjust according to the manual. Clean mud and debris from the undercarriage. Inspect rollers and idlers for leaks and wear. Check sprocket teeth for wear. Rotate tracks if recommended. Avoid turning on hard surfaces when possible. Operate smoothly to reduce wear. In Africa, undercarriage wear is accelerated by abrasive soils, rocks, and poor operating techniques. Train operators to avoid spinning tracks and to choose routes carefully. Now consider preventive maintenance for these systems. Daily checks include hydraulic fluid level, leaks, hose condition, transmission oil level, clutch operation, and track tension. Weekly checks include greasing pivot points, checking filter indicators, and inspecting undercarriage components. Monthly checks include hydraulic filter change, transmission oil analysis if available, and clutch adjustment. Annual checks include hydraulic pump testing, transmission overhaul if needed, and undercarriage measurement. Keep records. Track oil consumption, filter changes, and repairs. Use oil analysis if you can access it. Oil analysis detects wear metals and contamination before failure occurs. It is common in mining and large fleets, but smaller operators can also benefit by sending samples to a laboratory. Troubleshooting hydraulics requires a pressure gauge and flow meter. Start with the simplest checks: fluid level, filter, and relief valve. Then test pump pressure. If pressure is low, the pump may be worn or the relief valve may be stuck open. If pressure is high, the relief valve may be stuck closed. If a cylinder drifts, the seals may be leaking. If a valve is stuck, contamination may be the cause. Clean the valve and flush the system. Troubleshooting transmissions requires listening and testing. If the machine does not move, check clutch, linkage, and fluid. If it moves slowly, check clutch slip, transmission pressure, and final drive. If it makes noise, check bearings and gears. If it leaks, check seals and breathers. Safety is critical when working on hydraulics and transmissions. Use stands and blocks. Never work under a raised load. Release pressure. Use gloves and goggles. Hydraulic fluid injected into the skin is a medical emergency; seek immediate help. Do not use your hands to check for leaks; use cardboard or paper. Finally, think about the business. Hydraulic and transmission repairs are expensive. Downtime is costly. Preventive maintenance is cheaper than repair. Keep spare hoses, filters, seals, and oil in stock. Train operators to report problems early. A small leak fixed today prevents a major failure tomorrow. Your machinery is a business asset. Protect it with good maintenance and skilled repair.

4. Lesson 4: Electrical Systems, Diagnostics, and Workshop Safety

Electrical systems are essential for starting, lighting, monitoring, and controlling machinery. In Africa, electrical problems are common due to dust, vibration, moisture, poor wiring repairs, and unstable power supplies. This lesson covers basic electrical principles, components, diagnostics, and workshop safety. Electricity is the flow of electrons through a conductor. It is measured in volts, amperes, and ohms. Voltage is pressure. Amperage is flow. Resistance is opposition to flow. Ohm's law states that voltage equals current multiplied by resistance. This relationship helps you diagnose faults. A circuit must have a power source, a conductor, a load, and a return path. A switch controls the circuit. A fuse or circuit breaker protects it. In machinery, electrical systems are usually 12 or 24 volts direct current. Larger machines may use 24 volts or higher. Batteries store electrical energy. Lead-acid batteries are common. They contain plates, electrolyte, and terminals. Keep terminals clean and tight. Corrosion causes resistance and poor starting. Use a baking soda solution to clean terminals, then rinse with water and apply petroleum jelly. Check electrolyte level. Add distilled water only. Do not overfill. Test battery voltage with a multimeter. A fully charged 12-volt battery should read about 12.6 volts with the engine off. If it reads below 12.0 volts, it needs charging. If it will not hold charge, it may be worn out. The starter motor turns the engine. It draws high current. If the engine cranks slowly, check battery, terminals, cables, and starter. If it clicks but does not crank, check solenoid, battery, and connections. If it does not click, check ignition switch, safety switches, and wiring. The alternator charges the battery while the engine runs. It produces alternating current, which is rectified to direct current. Check charging voltage with a multimeter. It should be about 13.8 to 14.4 volts for a 12-volt system. If it is low, check belt tension, wiring, and alternator. If it is high, the regulator may be faulty. Wiring connects components. Use the correct gauge wire. Use proper connectors and insulation. Avoid twisting wires together and covering with tape. Solder or crimp connections. Use heat shrink tubing. Protect wires from heat, abrasion, and moisture. Use conduit or loom where possible. Label wires and keep diagrams. Poor wiring causes intermittent faults, short circuits, and fires. Fuses and relays protect circuits. Use the correct fuse rating. Never bypass a fuse with wire or foil. If a fuse blows repeatedly, find the fault. Do not install a larger fuse. Relays control high-current circuits with low-current switches. Test relays by swapping with a known good relay or using a multimeter. Sensors and gauges monitor engine and machine conditions. They include oil pressure, coolant temperature, fuel level, and speed sensors. If a gauge does not work, check the sensor, wiring, and gauge. Do not ignore warning lights. They indicate problems that can cause damage. Diagnostics require a systematic approach. Start with the battery. Check voltage and terminals. Then check fuses and relays. Then check switches and wiring. Use a multimeter to test continuity, voltage, and resistance. Use a test light for simple checks. Read wiring diagrams. Trace circuits from power source to load and back. Isolate sections to find the fault. Common electrical faults include dead battery, corroded terminals, blown fuse, broken wire, faulty switch, faulty relay, faulty sensor, and faulty alternator. In Africa, rodents can chew wiring. Heat can damage insulation. Moisture causes corrosion. Dust causes conductive paths. Vibration loosens connections. Protect wiring and inspect regularly. Workshop safety is essential. Electricity can kill. Always disconnect the battery before working on electrical systems. Use insulated tools. Do not work on wet surfaces. Do not wear jewellery. Use rubber gloves for high-voltage systems. If you are not trained, do not work on high-voltage systems such as those in hybrid or electric machines. General workshop safety includes fire prevention, first aid, and safe tool use. Keep a fire extinguisher suitable for electrical and fuel fires. Store fuel and oil safely. Keep the workshop clean and organised. Use the right tool for the job. Inspect tools before use. Replace damaged tools. Use personal protective equipment: safety boots, goggles, gloves, hearing protection, and dust masks. When welding, use a welding helmet, gloves, and apron. Protect others with screens. Remove flammable materials. Ventilate the area. When grinding, use goggles and a face shield. When lifting, use proper lifting equipment. Do not lift beyond your capacity. Use stands, not jacks alone. When working under a machine, chock wheels and use stands. When working on hydraulics, release pressure. When working on engines, allow cooling. When working on fuel systems, avoid sparks. When working on batteries, avoid short circuits. Battery acid burns; flush with water and seek medical help. First aid: keep a kit stocked with bandages, antiseptic, eye wash, and burn gel. Know the location of the nearest clinic. Have a phone available. Train workers in first aid. Report all injuries, even minor ones. Now consider the business case for electrical maintenance. Electrical faults cause downtime. A dead battery can stop a machine for hours. A faulty alternator can drain a battery and leave you stranded. A short circuit can cause a fire and destroy the machine. Preventive maintenance includes checking battery electrolyte, cleaning terminals, inspecting wiring, testing charging voltage, and replacing worn components. Keep spare batteries, fuses, relays, and wiring in stock. Train operators to report electrical problems immediately. Do not ignore flickering lights or slow cranking. These are early warnings. In Africa, power supply can be unreliable. If you rely on the grid for battery charging or workshop tools, consider a solar charger or generator. Protect your equipment from power surges. Use surge protectors. If you use a generator, ensure it is correctly sized and maintained. Finally, think about diagnostics as a skill. A good technician saves money by finding faults quickly and accurately. Practice with a multimeter. Study wiring diagrams. Learn from experienced technicians. Keep a notebook of faults and solutions. Share knowledge with your team. A skilled electrical technician is valuable in any workshop. Your machinery depends on reliable electrical systems. Maintain them well, and they will serve you well.

5. Lesson 5: Preventive Maintenance, Spare Parts, and Business Management

This final lesson brings together operation, repair, and business management. The goal is to help you run machinery profitably in Africa. Preventive maintenance, spare parts management, and business planning are not separate from technical work; they are part of it. A machine that is not maintained cannot earn. A machine without spare parts cannot be repaired. A business without records cannot grow. Let us start with preventive maintenance. Preventive maintenance is planned work that prevents breakdowns. It is based on time, hours, or condition. Time-based maintenance occurs at fixed intervals, such as daily, weekly, monthly, or annually. Hours-based maintenance occurs after a certain number of operating hours, such as 250 hours for oil change. Condition-based maintenance occurs when monitoring shows a need, such as oil analysis or vibration analysis. For most African operations, a combination of time-based and hours-based maintenance is practical. Condition-based maintenance is valuable but requires equipment and training. Create a maintenance schedule for each machine. Use the manual as a guide. Adjust for local conditions. Dusty conditions require more frequent air filter cleaning and oil changes. High temperatures require coolant checks and oil grade selection. Poor fuel quality requires more frequent fuel filter changes. Wet conditions require rust prevention and electrical checks. The schedule should list tasks, intervals, and responsibilities. It should be visible in the workshop. It should be recorded in a logbook. Daily checks include fuel, oil, coolant, air filter, leaks, tyre pressure, lights, and warning indicators. Weekly checks include battery electrolyte, belt tension, radiator fins, grease points, and track tension. Monthly checks include filter changes, bolt torque, hydraulic fluid level, and transmission oil level. Annual checks include injector testing, valve clearance, cooling system flush, and undercarriage measurement. Assign responsibilities. Operators perform daily checks. Technicians perform weekly and monthly tasks. Specialists perform annual overhauls. Train everyone. Use checklists. Sign off completed tasks. Review records monthly. Identify repeat problems. Adjust the schedule if needed. Spare parts management is critical. Without parts, repairs are delayed. Delayed repairs mean lost income. Build a spare parts inventory. Identify critical parts that cause long downtime. Keep them in stock. Critical parts include filters, belts, hoses, seals, bearings, fuses, relays, injectors, water pumps, and brake parts. Non-critical parts can be ordered as needed. But in Africa, ordering can take weeks. Balance stock cost against downtime cost. Use a simple system. Label shelves. Record parts in and out. Set minimum stock levels. Reorder when stock reaches the minimum. Keep parts clean and dry. Protect from dust, moisture, and theft. Buy from reliable suppliers. Verify part numbers. Avoid counterfeit parts. Counterfeit filters, bearings, and lubricants cause failures. If genuine parts are unavailable, consider quality alternatives from reputable brands. Never compromise on safety-critical parts. Build relationships with suppliers. Negotiate prices. Ask for bulk discounts. Consider sharing orders with other operators to reduce costs. Keep a list of alternative suppliers. If one fails, you have another. Now consider business management. Machinery operation and repair is a business. You must know your costs. Costs include fuel, lubricants, filters, repairs, operator wages, insurance, licences, taxes, and depreciation. Depreciation is the loss of value over time. It is a real cost. Calculate cost per hour. Add all costs for a period and divide by hours worked. This tells you the minimum you must charge to break even. Then add profit. Know your market rates. Charge competitively but not below cost. Keep records. Record income and expenses. Record machine hours. Record maintenance and repairs. Record fuel consumption. Use a simple book or spreadsheet. Review monthly. Identify profitable machines and unprofitable ones. Decide whether to repair, replace, or sell. A machine that costs more to repair than it earns should be sold or scrapped. A well-maintained machine lasts longer and has a higher resale value. Plan for replacement. Set aside money for new equipment. Do not wait until a machine fails completely. Sudden failure forces you to buy quickly, often at a higher price or lower quality. Plan major repairs during low season. Use downtime to overhaul. Train your team. A skilled operator uses less fuel and causes less wear. A skilled technician diagnoses faster and repairs correctly. Invest in training. Send staff to courses. Bring in experts. Share knowledge. Create a culture of safety and maintenance. Reward good performance. Track downtime. Analyse causes. Reduce repeat failures. Improve procedures. Finally, think about risk. Machinery operation involves risk: injury, theft, fire, flood, and market changes. Manage risk with insurance, security, fire extinguishers, and safe work practices. Have a contingency plan. If a machine fails, what will you do? If a customer cancels, how will you survive? Diversify. Offer repair services. Rent out machines. Train others. Build a reputation for reliability. In Africa, opportunities are growing. Agriculture, construction, mining, and transport need machinery. But competition is also growing. To succeed, you must be better: better maintenance, better repair, better service, better business. This module has given you the foundation. You have learned about engines, hydraulics, transmissions, undercarriage, electrical systems, diagnostics, safety, preventive maintenance, spare parts, and business management. Now apply it. Start with one machine. Create a maintenance schedule. Keep records. Train your operator. Build your spare parts stock. Monitor costs. Improve step by step. Your machinery is a tool for creating value. Treat it with respect. Maintain it diligently. Repair it correctly. Manage it wisely. It will serve you for years and help you build a successful business. Remember: in Africa, machinery is not just metal and oil. It is opportunity. Use it well.

<p style="margin:0;color:#b00020;font-weight:bold;font-size:1.1em;"> SAFETY WARNING: Fuel, hydraulic fluid, and battery acid</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#333;">Diesel, petrol, hydraulic oil, and battery electrolyte are hazardous. Store fuels and oils in clearly labelled, closed containers away from living quarters, animal feed, and water sources. Never siphon fuel by mouth. Never open a hot radiator cap or hydraulic system under pressure. Battery acid causes severe burns and blindness; wear goggles and gloves when handling batteries and electrolyte. In case of skin or eye contact, flush with clean water for at least 15 minutes and seek medical attention immediately. Keep a fire extinguisher rated for electrical and fuel fires within reach of every workshop and fuel store. Do not weld or grind near fuel, and do not smoke during refuelling. These controls protect both workers and the business: one fire or acid injury can destroy a workshop and its income.</p>

<h3>Practical costing: from workshop hours to profit per hectare</h3>

<p>To make machinery pay, calculate the full cost of ownership and operation, not just fuel. Use this simple formula:</p>

<p><strong>Total cost per hour = (fuel + lubricants + repairs + depreciation + labour + insurance and finance) annual working hours</strong></p>

<p>For example, a 75 kW tractor purchased for USD 45,000, used 800 hours per year, with fuel at USD 1.20 per litre, 12 L/hour fuel use, and annual repair and maintenance of USD 3,500, has a fuel cost of USD 11,520 per year and a repair cost of USD 3,500. If depreciation is spread over 8 years at USD 5,625 per year, and labour, insurance, and finance add USD 4,000 per year, total annual operating cost is about USD 24,645. At 800 hours, that is about USD 30.80 per hour before profit. If the tractor ploughs 1 hectare in 2.5 hours, the cost is about USD 77 per hectare. If custom hire rates in the area are USD 90110 per hectare, the machine can be profitable; if they are USD 60, the business model needs adjustment, such as higher utilisation, lower fuel use, or additional implements.</p>

<p>This kind of calculation is the core of business-first machinery management. It tells you whether to buy, hire, repair, or replace. It also tells you how much downtime actually costs. If a tractor earns USD 200 per day during land preparation, a 10-day spare part delay costs USD 2,000 in lost revenue, plus the risk that customers hire a competitor and do not return.</p>

<h3>Preventive maintenance that protects income</h3>

<p>Preventive maintenance is cheaper than breakdown repair. In African conditions, dust, heat, poor fuel quality, and long supply chains make routine checks even more important. Daily checks should include engine oil level, coolant level, fuel water separator, air filter restriction indicator, tyre pressure, hydraulic leaks, and battery terminals. Weekly checks should include greasing points, belt tension, radiator cleaning, and tightening of nuts and bolts. Seasonal checks should include oil and filter changes, valve clearance, injector testing, brake adjustment, and electrical system inspection.</p>

<p>Keep a simple machine logbook for every tractor, pump, mill, or generator. Record hours, fuel, repairs, parts used, and downtime reasons. After one season, the logbook becomes your most valuable business tool: it shows which machines make money, which operators need training, and which spare parts you must stock before the next peak season.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Match machine to soil and crop:</strong> confirm power, weight, and implement size before purchase or hire.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate cost per hour and per hectare:</strong> include fuel, repairs, depreciation, labour, and finance.</li>
<li><strong>Stock fast-moving parts before peak season:</strong> filters, belts, hoses, bearings, and lubricants.</li>
<li><strong>Record every breakdown and repair:</strong> use a logbook to identify repeat failures and training needs.</li>
<li><strong>Train operators on daily checks:</strong> oil, coolant, air filter, tyre pressure, leaks, and battery.</li>
<li><strong>Plan spare parts lead times:</strong> order imported parts at least 30 days before the season.</li>
<li><strong>Keep safety equipment ready:</strong> fire extinguisher, goggles, gloves, spill kit, and clean water.</li>
<li><strong>Review machine profitability each season:</strong> repair, replace, or sell based on cost per hectare and utilisation.</li>
</ul>
