1. Module 11 Lesson 1: Selecting Greenhouse Crops for African Markets

Welcome to Module 11 of Greenhouse Vegetable Farming. This lesson focuses on one of the most important business decisions you will make: choosing which crops to grow in your greenhouse. In Africa, greenhouse production is not just about agronomy; it is about market demand, cash flow, shelf life, and risk management. A greenhouse is a capital-intensive asset, so every square meter must earn its keep. The crops you select determine your revenue, your labour needs, your input costs, and your ability to compete with open-field growers and imports. We will start with the principle of market-first crop selection. Before you buy a single seed, you must know who will buy your produce, at what price, and in what quantity. In many African cities, the highest-value greenhouse crops are tomatoes, sweet peppers, cucumbers, lettuce, herbs, and strawberries. But high value does not automatically mean high profit. You must match the crop to your local market, your climate, your skills, and your access to inputs. For example, in Kenya, greenhouse tomato production around Nairobi is well established, but the market can be saturated during peak season. In Ghana, sweet peppers and cucumbers often fetch strong prices in Accra and Kumasi, especially during the dry season when open-field supply drops. In Nigeria, lettuce and leafy greens have strong demand from hotels, restaurants, and supermarkets in Lagos and Abuja, but they require reliable cold chain and rapid delivery. In South Africa, strawberries and specialty herbs can be profitable, but competition from established commercial farms is stiff. Your first task is to conduct a simple market survey. Visit local markets, hotels, restaurants, supermarkets, and roadside vendors. Ask what they buy, how much they buy weekly, what price they pay, and what quality problems they face. Talk to fellow farmers and extension officers. Identify gaps: crops that are imported, out of season, or consistently scarce. A crop that is scarce and in demand is a business opportunity. A crop that is abundant and cheap is a trap. Next, consider agronomic suitability. Greenhouse crops fall into three broad groups: fruiting vegetables, leafy vegetables, and specialty crops. Fruiting vegetables such as tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers need pollination, pruning, and support. They have longer cycles and higher input costs but can produce over many months. Leafy vegetables such as lettuce, spinach, and herbs have short cycles, quick returns, and lower labour per cycle, but they are perishable and need fast market access. Specialty crops such as strawberries, cherry tomatoes, and microgreens can earn premium prices but require niche marketing and careful handling. You must also consider your greenhouse structure. A low-cost polytunnel may not support heavy tomato plants or high temperatures. A shade net house may be better for lettuce in hot regions. A insect-proof screen house is essential for crops vulnerable to pests and viruses. Your crop choice must fit your structure, not the other way around. Climate is another critical factor. In hot, humid coastal areas, fungal diseases are a major risk, so you should choose resistant varieties and avoid dense canopies. In cooler highland areas, you can grow a wider range of crops, but you may need heating at night for tropical crops. In arid regions, water efficiency is key, so drip irrigation and mulching are essential. Always choose crops and varieties that are proven in your agro-ecological zone. Do not experiment with your entire greenhouse on an unproven crop. Use a small trial plot first. Labour is often overlooked. Fruiting vegetables require daily attention: pruning, training, pollination, and harvesting. Leafy vegetables require less skilled labour but more frequent planting and harvesting. If you have limited family labour or unreliable hired labour, choose crops that match your capacity. A greenhouse that is not managed properly will fail regardless of market demand. Input availability is another practical consideration. Can you reliably buy quality seeds, fertilizers, pesticides, and packaging? In many African countries, counterfeit inputs are a serious problem. Choose crops for which you can access genuine inputs. For example, hybrid tomato seeds are widely available in Kenya, Tanzania, and Nigeria, but some specialty herb seeds must be imported. If you cannot get inputs, you cannot grow the crop. Water quality and quantity matter too. Greenhouse crops need consistent irrigation. If your water source is saline or unreliable, choose salt-tolerant crops or invest in water storage. Tomatoes and peppers are moderately sensitive to salinity, while some leafy greens are more tolerant. Test your water before planting. Now let us discuss crop rotation and diversification. Growing the same crop continuously in the same soil leads to pest and disease buildup, nutrient depletion, and declining yields. A simple rotation plan might alternate fruiting vegetables with leafy vegetables or herbs. Diversification also spreads market risk. If you grow only one crop and the price crashes, you lose everything. A mix of a main cash crop and a secondary crop can stabilize income. For example, grow tomatoes as your main crop and lettuce as a quick-cycle secondary crop. Finally, think about value addition and grading. A crop that can be graded, packaged, and branded earns more. Lettuce sold in a supermarket in a branded bag earns more than lettuce sold loose at the market. Tomatoes graded by size and ripeness earn more than mixed baskets. Your crop choice should allow for some value addition, even if it is simple washing, grading, and packaging. In summary, crop selection is a business decision. Start with the market, then match the crop to your greenhouse, climate, labour, inputs, and water. Choose crops that are proven, profitable, and practical. Avoid the temptation to grow what everyone else is growing without checking demand. A well-chosen crop is the foundation of a successful greenhouse enterprise. In the next lesson, we will look at soil preparation and growing media for greenhouse vegetables.

2. Module 11 Lesson 2: Soil Preparation and Growing Media in Greenhouses

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 11. In this lesson, we will cover soil preparation and growing media for greenhouse vegetable production. The growing medium is where your plants live, so it must provide anchorage, water, air, and nutrients. In African greenhouses, growers use three main systems: soil-based culture, soilless culture, and hybrid systems. Each has advantages and challenges. Your choice depends on your budget, your location, and the crops you grow. Let us start with soil-based culture. This is the most common and least expensive method in Africa. You grow directly in the ground inside the greenhouse. The soil must be prepared carefully because greenhouse soil is different from open-field soil. It is protected from rain, so salts can accumulate. It is often cropped intensively, so nutrients are depleted quickly. It is also warmer and more humid, which can increase disease pressure. Before planting, you must test your soil. A soil test will tell you the pH, organic matter, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrient levels. It will also reveal salinity and any toxic elements. In many African countries, soil testing services are available through agricultural research institutes, universities, and private labs. The cost is small compared to the cost of a failed crop. Aim for a pH of 6.0 to 6.8 for most vegetables. If the pH is too low, add agricultural lime. If it is too high, add gypsum or elemental sulfur. Organic matter is critical. Greenhouse soils should have at least 3 to 5 percent organic matter. Add well-decomposed manure, compost, or peat moss. In Africa, cattle manure, poultry litter, and goat manure are common. But be careful: fresh manure can burn roots and introduce weed seeds and pathogens. Compost it thoroughly before use. A good compost pile reaches high temperatures and is turned regularly. You can also use vermicompost, which is rich in nutrients and beneficial microbes. Raised beds are highly recommended in greenhouses. They improve drainage, warm up faster, and make management easier. A standard bed is 1 meter wide and 20 to 30 centimeters high. The length depends on your greenhouse. Leave 40 to 50 centimeters between beds for walking and working. If your soil is heavy clay, add sand, compost, and gypsum to improve structure. If it is sandy, add compost and clay to improve water retention. Sterilization is another important step. Greenhouse soils can harbor nematodes, fungi, and bacteria. Solarization is a low-cost method: wet the soil, cover it with clear plastic for 4 to 6 weeks during the hottest part of the year. The heat kills many pests and pathogens. You can also use steam sterilization or chemical fumigants, but these are more expensive and require safety precautions. Now let us discuss soilless culture. This is growing plants without soil, using materials such as coco peat, perlite, vermiculite, rockwool, or hydroponic solutions. Soilless culture offers several advantages: no soil-borne diseases, precise nutrient control, higher yields, and efficient water use. It is ideal for leafy greens, herbs, strawberries, and tomatoes. However, it requires more technical knowledge, reliable electricity, and clean water. The most common soilless medium in Africa is coco peat, also called coir. It is made from coconut husks, which are abundant in coastal regions. Coco peat holds water well, provides good aeration, and is disease-free. It must be buffered with calcium and magnesium before use because it naturally binds these nutrients. Perlite and vermiculite are imported and expensive, so they are used in small quantities as amendments. Rockwool is also imported and not biodegradable, so disposal is an issue. Hydroponics is a form of soilless culture where roots grow in a nutrient solution. The simplest hydroponic system for African greenhouses is the Dutch bucket or the nutrient film technique, known as NFT. NFT is good for lettuce and herbs. Dutch buckets are good for tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers. Hydroponics requires careful monitoring of pH, electrical conductivity, and nutrient levels. You also need a backup power supply for pumps. If the power goes out, plants can die within hours. A hybrid system uses soil but adds amendments and fertigation. Fertigation is the application of fertilizers through the irrigation system. This is common in commercial greenhouses in Kenya, South Africa, and Egypt. It allows precise nutrient delivery and saves labour. You need a fertigation unit, which can be as simple as a venturi injector or as complex as a computerized dosing system. Start simple and upgrade as you gain experience. Water quality is critical for all growing media. Test your water for pH, electrical conductivity, sodium, chloride, and bicarbonates. If your water is high in sodium, you may need to leach the soil or use a soilless system. Rainwater harvesting is highly recommended. Store rainwater in tanks and use it for irrigation. It is low in salts and free of pathogens. Mulching is another best practice. Use plastic mulch or organic mulch to conserve water, suppress weeds, and reduce humidity. Plastic mulch also warms the soil and keeps fruit clean. In Africa, black plastic mulch is widely available. For organic production, use straw or grass mulch. Now let us discuss bed preparation step by step. First, clear the greenhouse of old crop residues and weeds. These can harbor pests and diseases. Second, test the soil and adjust pH and nutrients based on the results. Third, add 20 to 30 kilograms of well-decomposed manure or compost per square meter. Fourth, incorporate the organic matter into the top 20 to 30 centimeters of soil. Fifth, form raised beds. Sixth, install drip irrigation lines. Seventh, cover the beds with plastic mulch if you are using it. Eighth, wet the beds and let them settle for a few days before planting. For soilless culture, prepare your medium by buffering coco peat with calcium nitrate and magnesium sulfate. Fill your containers or troughs. Install the irrigation system. Run plain water through the system to check for leaks and to stabilize the medium. Then plant your seedlings. Remember that growing media management is ongoing. Soil salinity must be monitored, especially in greenhouses where there is no rain to leach salts. Take soil samples every 3 to 6 months. If salinity is high, apply extra water to leach the salts below the root zone. This is called leaching. In soilless systems, replace or sterilize the medium between crops to prevent disease buildup. In summary, soil preparation and growing media are the foundation of greenhouse production. Whether you choose soil, soilless, or hybrid, you must provide a healthy root environment. Test, amend, sterilize, and monitor. Do not cut corners on medium preparation because it will cost you later. In the next lesson, we will cover planting, spacing, and crop management.

3. Module 11 Lesson 3: Planting, Spacing, and Crop Management

Welcome to Lesson 3 of Module 11. In this lesson, we will cover planting, spacing, and crop management in greenhouse vegetable production. These are the day-to-day activities that determine your yield and quality. Good planting and spacing set the stage for healthy plants. Good crop management keeps them productive. Let us start with seedlings. In greenhouse production, you should always use healthy, vigorous seedlings. Do not plant weak or leggy seedlings. They will never catch up. You can raise your own seedlings in a nursery or buy them from a reputable nursery. If you raise your own, use clean trays, sterile media, and quality seeds. Keep the nursery warm, moist, and well-lit. Harden off seedlings before transplanting by reducing water and exposing them to outdoor conditions for a few days. This reduces transplant shock. Transplanting should be done in the cool of the day, either early morning or late afternoon. Water the seedlings before transplanting and water the planting hole after. This helps roots establish quickly. Handle seedlings by the leaves, not the stem, to avoid damage. Spacing is critical. If plants are too close, they compete for light, water, and nutrients, and disease spreads easily. If they are too far apart, you waste space and reduce yield per square meter. Spacing depends on the crop, the variety, the greenhouse structure, and your management system. For tomatoes, a common spacing in African greenhouses is 50 to 60 centimeters between plants in the row and 70 to 80 centimeters between rows. This gives about 2 to 3 plants per square meter. For indeterminate tomatoes, you may use a single stem and tighter spacing, or double stems and wider spacing. For sweet peppers, space plants 40 to 50 centimeters apart in rows 60 to 70 centimeters apart. For cucumbers, space plants 40 to 50 centimeters apart in rows 80 to 100 centimeters apart. Cucumbers are often grown on trellises, so vertical space is used. For lettuce, space plants 20 to 30 centimeters apart in rows 30 to 40 centimeters apart. For herbs like basil, space 20 to 30 centimeters apart. For strawberries, space 25 to 30 centimeters apart in rows 30 to 40 centimeters apart. Always check the seed packet or variety guide for specific recommendations. After planting, you need to manage the crop. This includes irrigation, fertigation, pruning, training, pollination, pest and disease management, and harvesting. Let us start with irrigation. Greenhouse crops need consistent moisture. Drip irrigation is the best method because it delivers water directly to the root zone, saves water, and reduces humidity. Overhead irrigation can promote fungal diseases and is not recommended for most greenhouse vegetables. The amount of water depends on the crop, the growth stage, the weather, and the growing medium. A simple rule is to check the soil or medium moisture daily. If it is dry 2 to 3 centimeters below the surface, it is time to irrigate. In sandy soils, irrigate more frequently with less water. In clay soils, irrigate less frequently with more water. In coco peat, irrigate several times a day with small amounts. Fertigation is the application of nutrients through the irrigation system. It is the most efficient way to feed greenhouse crops. You need a water-soluble fertilizer mix that contains nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients. The exact formula depends on the crop and growth stage. For leafy greens, use a higher nitrogen formula. For fruiting vegetables, use a balanced formula during vegetative growth and a higher potassium formula during fruiting. Monitor electrical conductivity of the nutrient solution. For tomatoes, keep it between 2.0 and 3.5 millisiemens per centimeter. For lettuce, keep it between 1.2 and 1.8. For strawberries, keep it between 1.0 and 1.5. Always check the pH of the solution. Most crops prefer a pH between 5.8 and 6.5 in soilless systems and 6.0 to 6.8 in soil. Pruning and training are essential for fruiting vegetables. Tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers need support. Use trellis twine, stakes, or wires. For indeterminate tomatoes, remove side shoots, also called suckers, weekly. This directs energy to the main stem and fruit. Remove old, yellowing leaves at the bottom of the plant to improve airflow and reduce disease. For cucumbers, train the main stem up the trellis and remove side shoots below the first fruit. For peppers, you may not need heavy pruning, but remove any diseased or damaged leaves. Pollination is a challenge in greenhouses because natural pollinators like bees may not be present. For tomatoes, you can pollinate by gently shaking the flower truss or using an electric toothbrush or vibrating wand. This releases pollen. For peppers and cucumbers, pollination is less critical, but you can still improve fruit set by shaking plants or using bumblebees if available. In Africa, bumblebees are used in some commercial greenhouses in Kenya and South Africa, but they are expensive and require careful management. Pest and disease management is a daily task. Scout your crop regularly. Look for signs of pests such as aphids, whiteflies, thrips, spider mites, and caterpillars. Look for diseases such as powdery mildew, downy mildew, early blight, late blight, and bacterial wilt. Use integrated pest management, known as IPM. This means combining cultural, biological, and chemical methods. Cultural methods include sanitation, crop rotation, resistant varieties, and proper spacing. Biological methods include introducing natural enemies such as ladybirds, predatory mites, and parasitic wasps. Chemical methods should be a last resort. Use approved pesticides and follow safety instructions. Always observe the pre-harvest interval. In Africa, many growers rely on chemical pesticides, but this can lead to resistance and health problems. IPM is more sustainable and often cheaper in the long run. Harvesting is the final step. Harvest at the right maturity for the market. Tomatoes for local markets are often harvested at the breaker stage, when they are starting to turn color. Tomatoes for distant markets are harvested at the mature green stage. Peppers are harvested when they reach full size and color. Cucumbers are harvested when they are young and tender, before seeds harden. Lettuce is harvested when the head is firm. Herbs are harvested before flowering for best flavor. Harvest in the cool of the day and handle produce gently. Use clean crates and avoid stacking too high. Cool produce quickly if possible. In summary, planting, spacing, and crop management are the core of greenhouse production. Use healthy seedlings, correct spacing, consistent irrigation and fertigation, proper pruning and training, regular scouting, and timely harvesting. These practices will maximize your yield and quality. In the next lesson, we will cover climate control and environmental management.

4. Module 11 Lesson 4: Climate Control and Environmental Management

Welcome to Lesson 4 of Module 11. In this lesson, we will cover climate control and environmental management in greenhouses. A greenhouse is a controlled environment, but it is not automatically controlled. You must manage temperature, humidity, light, and carbon dioxide to keep your crops healthy and productive. In Africa, the main challenge is heat, especially in lowland and coastal areas. But cold nights in highland areas can also be a problem. Let us start with temperature. Most greenhouse vegetables grow best between 18 and 28 degrees Celsius during the day and 12 to 18 degrees Celsius at night. Tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers are sensitive to extremes. Above 35 degrees Celsius, pollen becomes sterile and fruit set fails. Below 10 degrees Celsius, growth slows and plants may be damaged. In hot African regions, you must cool the greenhouse. The simplest methods are ventilation and shading. Ventilation can be natural or mechanical. Natural ventilation uses roof vents, side vents, and roll-up sides. Hot air rises and escapes through roof vents, while cooler air enters through side vents. This is the cheapest and most common method in Africa. Mechanical ventilation uses fans to move air. This is more expensive and requires electricity, but it is more effective in hot, humid conditions. Shade nets are also essential. A 30 to 50 percent shade net reduces solar radiation and lowers temperature by 3 to 6 degrees Celsius. Shade nets can be placed on top of the greenhouse or inside. Whitewash or reflective paint on the greenhouse cover can also reduce heat. Evaporative cooling is another option. This uses pads and fans to cool incoming air. It works well in dry climates but not in humid climates. Fogging and misting systems can also cool the air, but they increase humidity. In cool highland areas, you may need heating at night. This is expensive, so most African growers use passive heating methods. These include thermal mass, such as water barrels or stone walls, which absorb heat during the day and release it at night. You can also use double-layer plastic or thermal screens to reduce heat loss. Humidity is the second critical factor. High humidity promotes fungal diseases such as powdery mildew, downy mildew, and botrytis. Low humidity causes water stress and tip burn in lettuce. Most greenhouse vegetables prefer relative humidity between 60 and 80 percent. To reduce humidity, increase ventilation and avoid overhead irrigation. Use drip irrigation instead. You can also use dehumidifiers, but they are expensive and consume electricity. To increase humidity, use misting systems or wet the floor. But be careful not to create disease conditions. Light is the third factor. Greenhouses transmit light, but the cover material reduces light intensity. Dirty plastic, dust, and algae can reduce light by 20 to 50 percent. Clean your greenhouse cover regularly. In Africa, light intensity is usually high, so shading is more important than supplemental lighting. However, in highland areas with cloudy weather, supplemental lighting may be needed for seedlings and high-value crops. Use LED grow lights, which are energy-efficient. Carbon dioxide is the fourth factor. Plants use carbon dioxide for photosynthesis. In a closed greenhouse, carbon dioxide levels can drop below ambient, limiting growth. Ventilation replenishes carbon dioxide. In commercial greenhouses, carbon dioxide enrichment is used to boost yields, but this is not common in Africa due to cost. For most small and medium growers, good ventilation is enough. Now let us discuss environmental management practices. First, monitor your greenhouse environment. Use thermometers, hygrometers, and light meters. Place them at crop level, not near the roof. Record daily maximum and minimum temperatures and humidity. This helps you identify problems and adjust management. Second, manage irrigation to manage humidity. Water in the morning, not in the evening. This allows the greenhouse to dry out before night. Avoid overwatering, which creates humid conditions. Third, manage plant density. Dense canopies trap humidity and reduce airflow. Prune and train plants to maintain good airflow. Fourth, manage the greenhouse cover. Repair tears and holes. Clean the cover regularly. Replace it when it becomes opaque. Fifth, manage the floor. Keep the floor clean and free of weeds. Weeds harbor pests and increase humidity. You can cover the floor with plastic mulch or gravel to reduce evaporation and weeds. Sixth, manage the entrance. Install a double door or a vestibule to prevent pests and hot air from entering. Keep the door closed. Seventh, manage the crop residue. Remove old crops and debris immediately after harvest. This reduces pest and disease carryover. Now let us discuss specific climate challenges in Africa. In the Sahel and other hot, dry regions, the main challenge is heat and low humidity. Use shade nets, evaporative cooling, and mulching. In coastal West Africa, the main challenge is high humidity and fungal diseases. Use ventilation, drip irrigation, and resistant varieties. In the highlands of East and Southern Africa, the main challenge is cold nights and cloudy days. Use thermal mass, double-layer plastic, and supplemental lighting for seedlings. In all regions, climate change is making weather more unpredictable. You must be flexible and adaptive. Keep records and learn from each season. Finally, consider automation. Simple automation can save labour and improve climate control. Thermostats can control fans and vents. Timers can control irrigation. Sensors can monitor temperature and humidity. In Africa, automation is becoming more affordable. Start with simple tools and upgrade as your business grows. In summary, climate control and environmental management are essential for greenhouse success. Manage temperature, humidity, light, and carbon dioxide. Monitor your environment. Use ventilation, shading, and irrigation wisely. Adapt to your local climate. A well-managed greenhouse environment produces healthy, high-yielding crops. In the next lesson, we will cover harvesting, post-harvest handling, and marketing.

5. Module 11 Lesson 5: Harvesting, Post-Harvest Handling, and Marketing

Welcome to Lesson 5 of Module 11. In this lesson, we will cover harvesting, post-harvest handling, and marketing of greenhouse vegetables. This is where your hard work turns into money. Poor harvesting and post-harvest handling can destroy your profits, even if you grew a perfect crop. Good marketing ensures you get the best price. Let us start with harvesting. Harvest at the right time. For tomatoes, harvest at the breaker stage for local markets and mature green for distant markets. For cherry tomatoes, harvest when fully colored. For peppers, harvest when they reach full size and color. Green peppers are harvested before they turn red or yellow. For cucumbers, harvest when they are 15 to 20 centimeters long and still tender. Do not let them grow too large, as they become seedy and bitter. For lettuce, harvest when the head is firm and before it bolts. For herbs, harvest before flowering. Harvest in the cool of the day, early morning or late afternoon. Use clean, sharp tools. For tomatoes and peppers, use scissors or pruning shears to cut the stem, leaving a short peduncle. For cucumbers, cut the stem. For lettuce, cut the head at the base. Handle produce gently. Do not drop or throw produce into crates. Use clean, shallow crates. Do not overload crates, as this causes bruising. Keep harvested produce in the shade immediately. Now let us discuss post-harvest handling. The goal is to maintain quality and extend shelf life. The first step is cleaning. Remove dirt, dust, and plant debris. For leafy greens, wash in clean water and spin dry. For tomatoes and peppers, wipe with a clean cloth. Do not wash tomatoes if you are not going to cool them immediately, as moisture promotes decay. The second step is grading and sorting. Remove damaged, diseased, or undersized produce. Grade by size, color, and quality. Uniform produce fetches a higher price. Use simple grading tables or trays. The third step is packaging. Use clean, appropriate packaging. For tomatoes, use ventilated plastic crates or cartons. For lettuce, use plastic bags or clamshells. For herbs, use bunches or bags. Label your packages with your farm name, product name, and weight. This builds brand recognition. The fourth step is cooling. Cooling removes field heat and slows respiration. This is critical for leafy greens and herbs. If you do not have a cold room, use a shaded, well-ventilated area. You can also use evaporative cooling pads or a simple charcoal cooler. In Africa, cold chain is a major challenge. Invest in a cold room if you can, or partner with a logistics provider. The fifth step is storage. Store produce at the right temperature and humidity. Tomatoes store best at 12 to 15 degrees Celsius. Peppers at 7 to 10 degrees Celsius. Cucumbers at 10 to 12 degrees Celsius. Lettuce and herbs at 0 to 4 degrees Celsius. Do not store tomatoes with ethylene-producing fruits like bananas, as this causes premature ripening. The sixth step is transport. Use clean, covered vehicles. Avoid transporting produce in open trucks under the hot sun. Use refrigerated trucks for long distances if possible. If not, transport at night or early morning. Now let us discuss marketing. Marketing starts before you plant. You must know your market. There are several market channels in Africa. The first is the local market. This is the traditional market where you sell directly to consumers or retailers. Prices are often low and volatile, but you get cash immediately. The second is the wholesale market. This is where you sell in bulk to traders. Prices are lower but volumes are higher. The third is the retail market. This includes supermarkets, hotels, restaurants, and schools. Prices are higher, but you must meet quality and consistency standards. You may need to supply regularly and provide invoices. The fourth is the export market. This is the most demanding. You need certification, cold chain, and consistent quality. It is not for beginners. The fifth is value addition. This includes processing, such as making tomato sauce, dried herbs, or pickles. This can increase your income but requires investment and food safety compliance. To succeed in marketing, you need a marketing plan. Identify your target customers. Determine what they want in terms of quality, quantity, and price. Set a competitive price. Promote your produce through social media, farmer groups, and word of mouth. Build relationships with buyers. Be reliable. Deliver on time. Keep your promises. Consider joining a farmer cooperative or marketing group. This gives you bargaining power and access to larger markets. You can also use digital platforms. In Africa, mobile phone apps and social media are increasingly used to sell produce. WhatsApp groups, Facebook Marketplace, and specialized agri-tech platforms connect farmers to buyers. Use these tools to your advantage. Finally, keep records. Record your harvest, sales, expenses, and profits. This helps you make informed decisions. It also helps you access credit and insurance. In summary, harvesting, post-harvest handling, and marketing are the final and most important steps in greenhouse vegetable farming. Harvest at the right time. Handle produce gently. Grade, package, cool, and store correctly. Know your market and build relationships. A good product with good marketing earns good money. This concludes Module 11. You have learned how to select crops, prepare growing media, plant and manage crops, control the environment, and harvest and market your produce. Apply these lessons to your greenhouse business. Start small, learn, and grow. Thank you for your attention.

<h4 style="color:#c62828; margin-top:0; font-family:Arial, sans-serif;"> SAFETY WARNING: Pesticide and Chemical Handling in Greenhouses</h4>
<p style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; color:#333; margin-bottom:8px;">Greenhouse environments concentrate chemical vapors and reduce ventilation. Improper handling of pesticides, fungicides, or fumigants can cause acute poisoning, respiratory failure, and long-term health damage. <strong>Always follow these rules:</strong></p>
<ul style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; color:#333; margin:0; padding-left:20px;">
<li>Wear full PPE: chemical-resistant gloves, coveralls, boots, goggles, and a respirator rated for organic vapors.</li>
<li>Never enter a treated greenhouse for at least the full restricted-entry interval (REI) stated on the labeloften 2448 hours.</li>
<li>Ventilate thoroughly before re-entry. Use mechanical ventilation if available.</li>
<li>Store chemicals in a locked, ventilated cabinet away from food, feed, and water sources.</li>
<li>Dispose of empty containers according to national regulationsnever reuse for water or food.</li>
<li>In case of exposure, remove contaminated clothing immediately, wash skin with soap and water, and seek medical attention. Call a poison control center if available.</li>
</ul>
<p style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:12px; color:#555; margin-bottom:0;">Source: WHO (2020) <em>Recommended Classification of Pesticides by Hazard</em>; FAO (2021) <em>International Code of Conduct on Pesticide Management</em>.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist: Greenhouse Vegetable Farming Essentials</h3>

<ol style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Select crops with confirmed local demand.</strong> Before planting, secure at least two buyers (retailer, hotel, or wholesaler) and confirm price expectations. Avoid speculative production.</li>
<li><strong>Test your water and media before planting.</strong> Check pH (5.86.5 for most vegetables), electrical conductivity (EC), and nutrient levels. Adjust before transplanting, not after.</li>
<li><strong>Invest in drip irrigation and mulching.</strong> These reduce water use by 4060%, suppress weeds, and lower humidity-related diseasescritical in African greenhouse conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Monitor temperature and humidity daily.</strong> Use a simple max-min thermometer and hygrometer. Ventilate when temperatures exceed 30C or humidity exceeds 80% to prevent fungal outbreaks.</li>
<li><strong>Scout for pests twice weekly.</strong> Early detection of whiteflies, thrips, or aphids allows biological or targeted chemical control, reducing crop loss and input costs.</li>
<li><strong>Harvest at the correct maturity stage.</strong> Tomatoes for distant markets: mature green to breaker stage. Peppers: full color. Leafy greens: early morning, immediately cooled.</li>
<li><strong>Use cold chain or evaporative cooling.</strong> Even a simple charcoal evaporative cooler can extend shelf life by 24 days, reducing post-harvest losses from 3050% to under 15%.</li>
<li><strong>Keep records for every cycle.</strong> Track input costs, yields, and sales. This data is essential for accessing credit, improving efficiency, and negotiating better prices.</li>
</ol>
