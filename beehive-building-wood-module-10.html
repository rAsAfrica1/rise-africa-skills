Module 10 — Feeding, Water and Supplemental Support

When, what and how much to feed — without making weak bees or robbing wars.

Home › Beehive Building — Wood › Module 10

Learning outcomes

  • Know when feeding is necessary and when it is harmful.
  • Mix and use sugar syrup at the correct strength for each season.
  • Choose the right feeder for your hive and climate.
  • Supply pollen substitute and clean water.
  • Calculate how much feed a colony actually needs.
  • Prevent robbing, drowning and fermentation of stored feed.

1. When to feed — and when not to

Feeding is not beekeeping. It is a corrective action. A colony should be able to feed itself from what is in the field and what you left it at harvest. Feed only when the colony cannot.

SituationFeed?Why
New package or swarm, no drawn combYes — 1:1 syrupThey have no stored food and need to build comb
Established colony with 2+ frames of capped honeyNoThey have stores; feeding triggers robbing and congestion
Mid-summer dearth, no nectar for weeksYes — 1:1 syrup, small amountsPrevents brood rearing stopping and absconding
Autumn, building winter storesYes — 2:1 syrupThick syrup is stored, not consumed immediately
Queenless colonyNoFeeding will not fix a queenless colony; it will just fill the box
Weak colony being robbedNo — reduce the entrance insteadFeeding makes robbing worse
Honey supers on the hiveNeverSugar in supers ruins the honey crop and is not legal to sell

Feeding with supers on is not just bad practice — it is illegal in most markets

Honey that contains added sugar cannot be sold as honey. If you must feed during a flow, either remove the supers first or use a feeder the bees cannot store from (a pollen substitute only). Clear any feed from the brood box before putting supers back on.

2. Sugar syrup

Two ratios cover almost every situation. Use white granulated sugar — never brown sugar, never honey from an unknown source, never artificial sweetener.

RatioSugar : Water (by weight)UseNotes
1:1 (light)1 kg sugar : 1 L waterSpring build-up, dearth feeding, new coloniesMimics nectar; stimulates brood rearing and comb building
2:1 (heavy)2 kg sugar : 1 L waterAutumn winter storesBees store it quickly with less evaporation work
1.5:1 (medium)1.5 kg : 1 LTransitional feedingOnly if you want a compromise

Mixing syrup — the arithmetic

1:1 by weight: 5 kg sugar + 5 kg water (5 L).
Total syrup weight = 10 kg. Volume ≈ 7.8 L (sugar adds volume but not linearly).

2:1 by weight: 10 kg sugar + 5 kg water (5 L).
Total = 15 kg. Volume ≈ 10.5 L.

Do not mix by volume alone — a cup of sugar does not weigh the same as a cup of water. Weigh it.

Mixing

  1. Heat the water until hot but not boiling. Boiling caramelises sugar and produces HMF (hydroxymethylfurfural), which is toxic to bees.
  2. Add the sugar and stir until fully dissolved. No crystals left at the bottom.
  3. Let it cool to room temperature before feeding.
  4. Never add honey, molasses, brown sugar or fruit juice. These cause dysentery, robbing and disease spread.
  5. Add a small amount of white vinegar (about 5 mL per 10 L) in hot climates to slow fermentation — optional and climate-dependent.

Do not feed syrup that has fermented

Syrup left standing in warm weather ferments within days. Fermented syrup smells of alcohol and causes dysentery in bees. Mix only what will be consumed in 3–5 days, or refrigerate surplus.

Best for African conditions: top feeder

In warm, robbing-prone conditions, a top feeder over the crown board is safest. It holds plenty, keeps the syrup away from the entrance, and lets you refill without opening the brood nest. Fit it with a mesh float or crumpled plastic so bees do not drown.

4. How much to feed

A colony needs to be able to cover a fist-sized cluster of bees on stored food through the dearth period. Work from the frames, not from a fixed weight.

Feed calculation — autumn build-up

A standard Langstroth frame fully capped with honey holds about 2.0–2.5 kg of honey.
A colony overwintering needs 15–20 kg of stores = 8–10 full frames.

If your hive currently has 3 frames of stores and you want 9:
Missing = 6 frames × 2.2 kg = 13.2 kg of honey to replace.

Bees convert about 0.8 kg of sugar into 1 kg of stored honey (they add enzymes and evaporate water).
Sugar needed = 13.2 / 0.8 = 16.5 kg of sugar.

Fed as 2:1 syrup: 16.5 kg sugar + 8.25 L water = 24.75 kg syrup ≈ 17.5 L.
Feed in 3–4 L batches, every 3–4 days, until stores reach 9 frames.

5. Pollen substitute

Pollen is protein. Without it, brood rearing stops and young bees are short-lived. When natural pollen is scarce — late dearth, early spring, or after a long dry spell — a substitute keeps brood going.

SubstituteCompositionNotes
Soy flour + brewer's yeast3 parts soy flour : 1 part brewer's yeastCheap and effective; add a little sugar syrup to make a patty
Commercial pollen pattyFormulated with real pollen and supplementsReliable but expensive; check the expiry date
Dried egg yolk + soySmall proportion of egg yolk to soyToo much yolk causes dysentery; use sparingly
Real pollen (from a trusted source)Trapped pollen, irradiated or frozenRisk of disease transfer — only from disease-free apiaries

Place patties directly over the brood nest on the top bars, covered with wax paper to slow drying. Replace every 7–10 days. Remove uneaten patties — they attract small hive beetle and mould.

6. Water

A colony uses 0.5–1.5 L of water per day in hot weather. Without a reliable source nearby, foragers will fly to the nearest pool, animal trough, or neighbour's tap — with the problems that brings.

  • Provide a shallow water source: 20–30 mm deep, with landing stones, twigs, or floating cork so bees do not drown.
  • Place it in partial shade — full sun evaporates it, full shade discourages bees.
  • Locate it 20–100 m from the hives: close enough to find, far enough to keep traffic away from entrances.
  • Refresh every 2–3 days. Stagnant water grows algae and mosquito larvae.
  • Add a small amount of salt (1 tsp per 10 L) — bees seek minerals and will otherwise visit urine or manure.

7. Robbing prevention

Robbing is a feeding problem, not a bee problem. Every feeding decision increases or decreases the risk.

ControlAction
Reduce entrances8–10 mm during any dearth or feeding period
Feed inside the hiveTop feeder or frame feeder, never at the entrance
Feed in the eveningBees settle for the night; any spills are cleared before robbers arrive
No spillsWipe up every drop outside the hive
Never open a weak hive in a dearthEach opening is a broadcast to robbers
Close weak colonies in a nucMove them to a nuc box with a small entrance
Robber screenA mesh tunnel at the entrance that lets the resident bees in but confuses robbers

Robbing kills weak colonies

A robbing event can destroy a small colony in an afternoon. If you see bees fighting at the entrance, bees crawling under the landing board, or a sudden burst of activity with frantic flight, treat it as an emergency: reduce the entrance to one bee width, put a wet sheet over the hive, and stop all feeding for 3 days.

8. Storing feed safely

  • Store dry sugar in sealed containers — moisture turns it to brick.
  • Store syrup in the fridge or feed it within 3 days.
  • Never store syrup next to honey for sale — cross-contamination ruins the honey.
  • Do not reuse feed containers for honey collection.
  • Never leave a feeder open near the apiary — it triggers robbing across the whole site.
