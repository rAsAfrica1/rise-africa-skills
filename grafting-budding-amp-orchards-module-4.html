1. Selecting rootstocks for drought tolerance in semi-arid areas

In semi-arid Africa, choosing the right rootstock is the difference between a mango tree that survives and one that dies in the first dry season. Drought-tolerant rootstocks have deep taproots and thick bark that reduces water loss. For mango, local varieties like 'Dodo' or 'Kenya' are known for drought tolerance. For citrus, rough lemon (Citrus jambhiri) and Troyer citrange are good choices. Avocado is less drought-tolerant, but 'Duke 7' and 'Zutano' rootstocks handle dry spells better than most.

Start by testing your soil. Dig a hole 60 cm deep and 30 cm wide. Fill it with water. If it drains within 24 hours, you have good drainage. If not, choose rootstocks that tolerate occasional waterlogging, like rough lemon. In dry areas, plant on raised beds or ridges to capture water. Space trees 8-10 meters apart for mango, 5-7 meters for citrus, and 6-8 meters for avocado.

Collect seeds from local, healthy mother trees that have survived at least 10 years of local droughts. Avoid seeds from irrigated orchardsthey may lack drought-hardiness. For mango, use polyembryonic seeds (they produce multiple seedlings, one of which is a clone). For citrus, extract seeds from ripe fruit, wash them, and plant within 24 hours; they lose viability quickly.

Plant seeds in nursery beds or polythene tubes filled with a mix of 3 parts soil, 2 parts sand, and 1 part compost. Water daily for the first month, then reduce to every 2-3 days. In dry areas, mulch around seedlings with dry grass to keep moisture. After 3-4 months, seedlings are ready for grafting. Remember: a drought-tolerant rootstock does not make the tree immune to droughtyou still need to water young trees during dry spells.

2. Growing rootstocks from local seeds: mango, avocado, citrus

Growing your own rootstocks from local seeds saves money and ensures the rootstock is adapted to your area. Start with fresh, healthy seeds from mature trees. For mango, choose seeds from polyembryonic varieties like 'Dodo' or 'Kenya'these produce multiple seedlings, and you can use the strongest one as rootstock. For avocado, use seeds from local varieties like 'Fuerte' or 'Hass' that have grown well in your region. For citrus, rough lemon or local sweet orange seeds work well.

Extract seeds carefully. For mango, remove the husk and plant the seed immediatelyit loses viability in a few days. For avocado, remove the seed coat and plant pointed end up, about 2 cm deep. For citrus, wash seeds to remove sugar and plant within 24 hours. Plant in nursery beds or black polythene tubes (20 cm x 10 cm) filled with a mix of 3 parts loam soil, 2 parts river sand, and 1 part well-rotted manure. Water daily.

Germination takes 2-4 weeks for mango, 3-6 weeks for avocado, and 2-3 weeks for citrus. Keep seedlings in partial shade for the first month, then gradually expose them to full sun. Thin mango seedlings: keep only the strongest one per pot. For avocado, pinch the tip when the seedling is 30 cm tall to encourage a thick stem. For citrus, remove any thorns or side shoots below 20 cm.

Feed seedlings with a weak solution of NPK 20-10-10 every two weeks. Watch for pests like aphids and whitefliesspray with soapy water (1 tablespoon liquid soap per litre) if needed. After 4-6 months, seedlings should be 40-60 cm tall with a stem diameter of 0.5-0.8 cmready for grafting. Always label your seedlings with the seed source and date.

3. When and how to transplant rootstocks into nursery beds

Transplanting rootstocks into nursery beds gives them more space to grow strong before grafting. The best time is at the start of the rainy season, when soil is moist and temperatures are moderate. In East Africa, this is March-April or October-November. Avoid transplanting in hot, dry monthsseedlings will suffer shock.

Prepare the nursery bed two weeks in advance. Choose a site with full sun and good drainage. Clear all weeds and dig the soil 30 cm deep. Mix in 2 buckets of well-rotted manure per square meter. Make beds 1 meter wide and 10-15 cm high, and any length you need. Space seedlings 20 cm apart in rows 30 cm apart. This gives each rootstock enough room for a strong root system.

Water the seedlings in their tubes or seedbed thoroughly before lifting. Use a hand fork or small spade to lift them with a ball of soil around the roots. Avoid pulling by the stemhold by a leaf. Plant at the same depth as before. Firm the soil around the roots and water immediately. Mulch with dry grass to conserve moisture.

After transplanting, shade the seedlings with a simple shade net or palm fronds for the first week. Water every morning for the first two weeks, then every 2-3 days. Remove any weeds by handdo not use herbicides. After one month, the seedlings should be established. Continue watering and feeding with a weak NPK solution every two weeks. Rootstocks are ready for grafting when they are 6-12 months old, depending on species. Mango: 6-8 months; avocado: 8-12 months; citrus: 6-10 months. Always keep records of transplanting date and seedling source.

4. Preparing rootstock seedlings for grafting: size, age, and health

Grafting success depends on the rootstock being at the right stage. For mango, the rootstock should be 6-8 months old, with a stem diameter of 0.8-1.2 cm (about the thickness of a pencil). For avocado, 8-12 months, stem diameter 0.6-1.0 cm. For citrus, 6-10 months, stem diameter 0.5-0.8 cm. Measure at 10 cm above the soil line. If too thin, wait; if too thick, the graft may not take.

The rootstock must be healthy: no pests, no disease, and actively growing. Check for signs of stress: yellow leaves, wilting, or stunted growth. If you see aphids, whiteflies, or scales, treat with soapy water (1 tablespoon liquid soap per litre) or neem oil (5 ml per litre). Remove any diseased seedlings immediately.

Two weeks before grafting, water the rootstocks well but do not fertilizehigh nitrogen can cause excessive sap flow and reduce graft success. On the day of grafting, the rootstock should be well-watered. Use a clean, sharp grafting knife. Sterilize the blade with alcohol or a flame between cuts. Make a clean cut at 10-15 cm above the soil. The cut should be smooth and flat.

For a wedge graft, cut the rootstock stem at a 45-degree angle, then make a vertical slit 2-3 cm deep. For a side graft, make a slanting cut 3-4 cm long. The scion (the top part) must be from a known good variety, collected from a healthy mother tree, and matched in diameter to the rootstock. Wrap the graft union tightly with grafting tape or polythene strips. Cover with a plastic bag to retain humidity. Keep in shade for 2-3 weeks. After 3 weeks, check for new growthif the scion is green, the graft has taken. Remove the tape after 4-6 weeks.

5. Managing rootstock pests and diseases without expensive chemicals

Rootstock seedlings are vulnerable to pests and diseases, but you don't need expensive chemicals. Start with prevention: keep the nursery clean, remove weeds, and space seedlings well for airflow. Water at the base, not on leaves, to reduce fungal diseases. Rotate nursery beds if possible.

Common pests: aphids, whiteflies, mealybugs, and scales. For aphids and whiteflies, spray with soapy water: mix 1 tablespoon of liquid soap (not detergent) in 1 litre of water. Spray in the evening, covering the undersides of leaves. Repeat every 3 days for two weeks. For mealybugs, use a cotton swab dipped in alcohol to remove them, or spray with neem oil (5 ml per litre of water). For scales, scrape them off with a soft brush, then spray with soapy water.

Common diseases: damping-off (fungal), powdery mildew, and root rot. Damping-off causes seedlings to collapse at the soil line. Prevent by using well-drained soil and not overwatering. If it appears, remove affected seedlings and drench soil with a weak solution of baking soda (1 teaspoon per litre). Powdery mildew shows as white powder on leaves. Spray with a mix of 1 tablespoon baking soda and 1 teaspoon liquid soap in 1 litre of water. Root rot causes yellowing and wilting. Improve drainage and avoid waterlogging. Remove severely affected plants.

For bacterial diseases like citrus canker, there is no cheap cure. Prevent by using clean seed and tools. Sterilize pruning tools with alcohol. Remove and burn infected plants. Always inspect new seedlings before bringing them into the nursery. Keep a log of pest and disease outbreaks, and which treatments worked. Share this with other farmers.

6. Hands-on: Selecting and preparing 10 rootstocks for grafting

In this practical session, you will select and prepare 10 rootstocks for grafting. You will need: 10 healthy rootstock seedlings (mango, avocado, or citrus), a sharp grafting knife, grafting tape, a clean cloth, alcohol for sterilizing, and labels. Work in the morning when it is cool.

Step 1: Select your rootstocks. Choose 10 seedlings that are the correct age and size for your species. For mango, 6-8 months, stem diameter 0.8-1.2 cm. For avocado, 8-12 months, 0.6-1.0 cm. For citrus, 6-10 months, 0.5-0.8 cm. Reject any with pests, disease, or weak growth. Measure stem diameter at 10 cm above soil with a caliper or a string marked against a ruler.

Step 2: Water the seedlings well the day before. On the day, clean the stems with a damp cloth. Sterilize your knife with alcohol. Make a clean cut at 10-15 cm above the soil. For a wedge graft, cut the rootstock stem at a 45-degree angle, then make a vertical slit 2-3 cm deep. For a side graft, make a slanting cut 3-4 cm long. Ensure the cut is smooth and not bruised.

Step 3: Prepare the scion. Collect scion wood from a healthy, high-yielding mother tree of the desired variety. The scion should be the same diameter as the rootstock, with 2-3 buds. Cut the bottom of the scion into a wedge to match the rootstock. Insert the scion into the rootstock so that the cambium layers (the green layer under the bark) meet on at least one side. Wrap tightly with grafting tape. Cover with a plastic bag.

Step 4: Label each grafted plant with the date, rootstock species, and scion variety. Keep in a shaded area for 2-3 weeks. Water lightly. After 3 weeks, check for new growth. Remove the plastic bag and tape after 4-6 weeks. Record your success rate. With practice, you can achieve 80-90% success.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Choosing and Preparing Rootstocks for African Conditions

Rootstock choice is the single most consequential decision in orchard establishment. It determines whether your trees survive the first dry season, whether they tolerate saline irrigation water, how quickly they bear fruit, and how much of your capital is tied up before the first harvest. In semi-arid Africa, where rainfall is unreliable and input costs are high, the rootstock is your insurance policy. This reference lesson covers the practical selection, production, and preparation of rootstocks for mango, avocado, and citrus under African conditions, with a business-first focus on reducing risk and cost.

Selecting Rootstocks for Drought Tolerance in Semi-Arid Areas

Drought tolerance in rootstocks operates through three mechanisms: deep rooting, efficient water uptake per unit of root, and stomatal regulation under stress. For mango in the Sahel and East African drylands, polyembryonic local selections such as 'Dodo' and 'Peach' have demonstrated superior survival under 600800 mm annual rainfall compared to grafted exotic cultivars on poorly adapted rootstocks. For avocado, 'Duke 7' and 'Thomas' are widely used in South Africa and Kenya for their tolerance of cold and drought, while 'Bounty' offers Phytophthora resistance. For citrus, 'Rough Lemon' and 'Volkameriana' remain the workhorses of semi-arid Africa because of their vigorous root systems and tolerance of calcareous soils, though 'Trifoliata' and its hybrids offer better cold tolerance and disease resistance.

The business case is straightforward: a rootstock that survives a drought year preserves the entire investment in the orchard, while a failed rootstock means replanting costs of USD 38 per tree plus three years of lost production. Choose drought-adapted rootstocks even if they are slightly less productive in good years, because the downside protection is worth more than the upside gain.

Growing Rootstocks from Local Seeds: Mango, Avocado, Citrus

Local seed collection is the cheapest way to produce rootstocks, but it requires discipline. For mango, collect seed from polyembryonic varieties (which produce true-to-type seedlings) during the main fruiting season. Remove the husk, sow within 35 days, and use a free-draining medium. For avocado, collect seed from local 'Hass' or 'Fuerte' trees, but be aware that seedlings are variable and may not be true to type; use them only as rootstocks, never as fruiting trees. For citrus, collect seed from healthy, virus-free mother trees, wash thoroughly, and sow immediately citrus seed loses viability rapidly if dried.

Seed treatment matters. In African conditions, fungal damping-off is the biggest killer of young seedlings. Treat seed with a simple hot-water bath (50C for 10 minutes) for citrus, or a 1% bleach solution for mango and avocado, then rinse and sow. This costs almost nothing and can raise germination rates from 40% to 80%.

When and How to Transplant Rootstocks into Nursery Beds

Transplant when seedlings have 24 true leaves and are 812 cm tall usually 48 weeks after sowing depending on species. In African nurseries, the best time is at the onset of the rainy season, when humidity is high and transplant shock is minimal. Prepare nursery beds with a 1:1:1 mix of topsoil, compost, and river sand, sterilised by solarisation (cover with clear plastic for 46 weeks) to kill soil-borne pathogens.

Transplant in the early morning or late afternoon to reduce water stress. Water the seedbed thoroughly before lifting seedlings, then lift with a small fork, keeping as much root as possible. Plant at the same depth as before, firm the soil, and water immediately. Space at 20 30 cm for citrus and avocado, 25 40 cm for mango. Shade with 50% shade net for the first 2 weeks, then gradually remove.

Preparing Rootstock Seedlings for Grafting: Size, Age, and Health

A rootstock is ready for grafting when it reaches pencil thickness (610 mm diameter) at 1520 cm above soil level, has a healthy green stem, and is actively growing. For citrus, this is typically 612 months from sowing; for mango, 814 months; for avocado, 610 months. Do not graft onto stressed, diseased, or waterlogged seedlings the graft will fail and you will waste scion material.

Prepare seedlings by stopping irrigation 35 days before grafting to reduce sap flow, which improves graft union. Remove all side shoots and leaves from the grafting zone. The day before grafting, apply a light fertiliser (NPK 2:3:2 at 20 g per seedling) to boost cambial activity, but avoid high nitrogen, which makes stems soft.

Managing Rootstock Pests and Diseases Without Expensive Chemicals

In African nurseries, the main rootstock threats are damping-off (Pythium, Rhizoctonia), root rot (Phytophthora), termites, and nematodes. Expensive fungicides and nematicides are rarely necessary if you follow cultural controls. Solarise nursery soil before sowing. Use clean containers and tools. Avoid overwatering the single biggest cause of damping-off. Apply wood ash around the collar of seedlings to deter termites. Intercrop with marigold (Tagetes) to reduce nematode populations. For Phytophthora, improve drainage and apply a 1% copper hydroxide drench only when symptoms appear.

SAFETY WARNING: Chemical Handling and Nursery Hygiene

If you use any chemical treatment (bleach, copper hydroxide, or hot water), always wear gloves and eye protection. Never mix chemicals in food containers. Store all treatments locked away from children and animals. Hot water treatment must be monitored with a thermometer water above 52C will kill citrus seed. Copper-based products are toxic to fish and aquatic life; do not allow runoff into streams or ponds. In case of skin contact, rinse with plenty of clean water for 15 minutes. If symptoms persist, seek medical attention and bring the product label.

Hands-On: Selecting and Preparing 10 Rootstocks for Grafting

This practical exercise is designed for a group of 35 farmers or nursery operators. You will need: 10 healthy rootstock seedlings (citrus, mango, or avocado), a sharp grafting knife, pruning shears, a labelling pen, and a record sheet.

  1. Select 10 seedlings that meet the readiness criteria in Table 2. Reject any with yellow leaves, stem lesions, or root damage.
  2. Label each seedling with a unique number, date, and species. Record the stem diameter at the intended graft point.
  3. Prepare each seedling by removing side shoots and leaves from the grafting zone (1525 cm above soil).
  4. Stop irrigation 34 days before the exercise to reduce sap flow.
  5. Clean your knife with 70% alcohol between seedlings to prevent disease spread.
  6. Practice the graft on a spare seedling first, then perform the actual graft on your 10 selected rootstocks.
  7. Record everything date, rootstock number, scion variety, graft type, and operator name.
  8. After-care: keep the grafted plants in 50% shade, water lightly, and inspect daily for 2 weeks. Remove any new shoots below the graft union.

Business tip: a well-prepared rootstock that takes a graft successfully is worth USD 1.503.00 at nursery gate in most African markets. If you prepare 1,000 rootstocks and achieve 80% graft success, that is 800 saleable trees a gross revenue of USD 1,2002,400 from a seed investment of under USD 100. The margin is in the preparation, not the chemicals.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Match rootstock to your rainfall zone <600 mm requires drought-adapted types like Rough Lemon, Dodo mango, Duke 7 avocado.
  • Collect seed from local, healthy, polyembryonic mother trees; sow within 35 days (mango) or immediately (citrus).
  • Treat seed with hot water (citrus, 50C/10 min) or 1% bleach (mango, avocado) before sowing.
  • Transplant seedlings at 24 true leaves into solarised nursery beds at the onset of rains.
  • Graft only when rootstock reaches pencil thickness (610 mm) and is actively growing.
  • Stop irrigation 34 days before grafting and apply low-nitrogen fertiliser to boost cambial activity.
  • Control pests and diseases with solarisation, wood ash, marigold intercrop, and clean tools not expensive chemicals.
  • Label every rootstock and keep records; 80% graft success on 1,000 rootstocks can generate USD 1,2002,400.
