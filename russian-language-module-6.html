2. Describing People: Appearance and Character

To describe appearance in Russian, use these adjectives: (vysokiy) tall, (nizkiy) short, (khudoy) thin, (polnyy) full/plump, (molodoy) young, (staryy) old, (krasivyy) handsome/beautiful, (simpatichnyy) nice-looking. For hair: (chyornyye volosy) black hair, (korotkiye volosy) short hair, (dlinnyye volosy) long hair. For eyes: (kariye glaza) brown eyes, (golubyye glaza) blue eyes. To say He is tall, use (On vysokiy). For She is beautiful, say (Ona krasivaya). 3. Possessive Pronouns and Family

Possessive pronouns show who owns something. In Russian, they must match the gender and number of the noun they modify. The four main sets: (moy) my (masculine), (moya) my (feminine), (moyo) my (neuter), (moi) my (plural). Similarly: (tvoy) your (informal singular), (nash) our, (vash) your (formal or plural), (yego) his, (yeyo) her, (ikh) their. These last three (, , ) never change. For family, practice: (moy brat) my brother, (moya sestra) my sister, (moi roditeli) my parents. (nash papa) our dad, (vasha mama) your mom (formal). To say This is my family, use (Eto moya semya). To say I love my wife, say (Ya lyublyu moyu zhenu). Notice is the accusative case of . For now, just memorize common phrases. In African cultures, family is central. So practice these: (U menya yest moya semya) I have my family. (Moy otets rabotayet) My father works. (Moya mat gotovit) My mother cooks. (Moi deti uchatsya) My children study. To ask Is this your brother? say ? (Eto tvoy brat?). Answer: , (Da, eto moy brat). For her husband, say (yeyo muzh). For their daughter, say (ikh doch). Write a short paragraph introducing your family using at least 6 possessive pronouns. Then exchange with a partner. Remember: never change , , they stay the same for all genders. 7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Russian Language for Family, Friends, and Relationships in African Agribusiness Contexts

This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the vocabulary, grammar, and cultural knowledge from the module Family, Friends, and Relationships into a single working document for African agribusiness professionals. Whether you are negotiating a grain supply contract with a Russian-speaking counterpart, hosting a delegation from the Eurasian Economic Union, or building long-term trust with a partner who values personal connection before business, the language of family and relationships is not small talk it is deal infrastructure. In Russian business culture, as in many African business cultures, trust is built through relationship, and relationship is expressed through family language. This lesson gives you the phrases, tables, and cultural notes you need to move from transactional to relational communication.

Why Family Language Is Business Language

In both African and Russian contexts, asking about someone's family is a sign of respect, not intrusion. A procurement manager in Lagos who asks a Russian supplier about their (children) before discussing cocoa bean moisture content is not wasting time they are investing in the relationship that will carry the contract through a difficult harvest year. Similarly, a Kenyan exporter who can describe their own family in Russian signals cultural competence and long-term commitment. This module's vocabulary is therefore practical commercial vocabulary, not academic decoration.

Possessive Pronouns and Family Structures

Russian possessive pronouns agree with the noun they modify in gender, number, and case. This is different from English and from many African languages, where possession is often marked by a prefix or a separate word that does not change form. The key forms are (my, masculine), (my, feminine), (my, neuter), and (my, plural). For business introductions, the most useful pattern is:

  • . My brother works in agribusiness.
  • . My sister is a cocoa exporter.
  • . My parents live in Kenya.
  • . Our partner in Ghana is reliable.
  • . Their farm produces coffee.

Note that (our) and (your, formal/plural) are essential for business correspondence. When writing to a Russian-speaking partner, use with a capital letter as a sign of respect, similar to the formal .

The Russian word carries more weight than the English "friend." A is someone you trust deeply, often someone you would help without question. In business, calling a counterpart too early can seem insincere. Safer terms are (colleague), (partner), or (acquaintance). SAFETY WARNING: Cultural and Legal Sensitivity When Discussing Family and Marital Status

In Russian-speaking business contexts, questions about family, age, and marital status are common and generally not considered intrusive. However, in some African countries and among some Russian diaspora communities these topics can be sensitive, particularly regarding divorce, widowhood, polygamy, or children outside marriage. Never assume a counterpart's family structure. Avoid asking directly about divorce, deceased relatives, or the number of children. If a counterpart volunteers personal information, acknowledge it briefly and respectfully, then return to business. Additionally, when discussing family-owned agribusinesses, be aware that inheritance and succession laws differ widely between Russia, the Eurasian Economic Union, and African jurisdictions. Do not offer legal or financial advice on family business structures unless you are qualified to do so. When in doubt, redirect to professional topics.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Learn the gender pairs. Russian adjectives and possessive pronouns change form for masculine, feminine, and plural nouns. Practice , , until automatic.
  2. Use formal until invited otherwise. In business, always start with and (capitalized). Switch to only when a counterpart explicitly suggests it.
  3. Ask about family before business. A brief, warm question about family at the start of a meeting builds trust. Keep it to one or two questions, then move to the agenda.
  4. Avoid direct age questions. Ask about experience or career stage instead. If asked your age, answer briefly and reciprocate with a business question.
  5. Know the marital status phrases. (m), (f), , . Answer honestly but briefly.
  6. Use and before . Reserve for genuinely close relationships. In business, is safer and still warm.
  7. Prepare a two-sentence family introduction. Example: . . This is enough for most business dinners.
  8. Respect privacy. If a counterpart deflects a personal question, move on immediately. Never press for details about divorce, death, or family conflict.
