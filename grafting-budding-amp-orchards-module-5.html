Lessons

1. Identifying high-yielding mother trees in your community

Your first job is to find a mother tree that gives you the best possible scion wood. A mother tree is any healthy tree you take cuttings from. It must be high-yielding, disease-free, and true to type. In your community, start by asking farmers which mango, avocado, or citrus tree gives the most fruit every year. Visit that tree during harvest. Look for a tree that has produced heavily for at least three years. Count the fruit on a few branches. A good mango mother tree might carry 200 to 300 fruits in a good season. Check the fruit size and taste. Avoid trees with small, fibrous, or diseased fruit. Also check for pests and diseases. Look for signs of anthracnose (black spots on leaves and fruit), gummosis (sticky sap on the trunk), or viral diseases like tristeza in citrus. Do not take scion wood from a tree with these problems. The tree should be at least five years old and in full production. Young trees may not have stable genetics. Also, avoid trees that have been heavily pruned or stressed by drought. The best mother trees are vigorous, with strong new growth. Look for branches that are exposed to sunlight, as they produce healthier wood. In Africa, many good mother trees are found in home gardens or old orchards. Ask around, and mark the tree with a ribbon or paint so you can return. Never take all your scion wood from one tree; collect from two or three good trees to spread risk. This simple selection process is the foundation of a successful orchard.

2. When and how to cut scion wood for maximum success

Timing is critical. Cut scion wood when the tree is dormant or just before bud break. In most of Africa, the best time is the cool dry season, from June to August in southern Africa, or December to February in West Africa. Avoid cutting during active growth, flowering, or fruiting. The wood should be mature but not old. Choose branches from the current season's growth that are about the thickness of a pencil (6-10 mm diameter). They should have well-developed buds. Use a sharp, clean pair of bypass pruners or a grafting knife. Make a clean cut 30-40 cm long. Cut just above a bud at the top and just below a bud at the bottom. Remove any leaves immediately, but leave a short piece of leaf stalk (petiole) to protect the bud. Do not strip the bark. Cut more wood than you need, as some may dry out or get damaged. For mango, cut 20-30 cm long scions; for citrus, 15-20 cm. After cutting, tie the scions in bundles of 5-10 with the buds pointing in the same direction. Label each bundle with the variety name and date. Wrap the bundles in moist newspaper or banana leaves, then place in a plastic bag. Do not seal the bag completely; leave a small opening for air. Keep them cool and out of direct sun. If you are cutting from a tall tree, use a pole pruner or climb safely. Never cut from the top of the tree where wood may be too soft. Cut from the outer canopy where light is good. The best scions come from branches that are 1-2 years old. Avoid water sprouts (vertical shoots) as they are often too soft. With practice, you will learn to identify the perfect wood.

3. Storing and transporting scion wood without drying out

Scion wood is alive and must not dry out. After cutting, you have about 24-48 hours to use it, but proper storage can extend that to 2-3 weeks. The key is to keep it cool and moist, but not wet. Wrap the bundles in slightly moist (not dripping) newspaper, then place them in a perforated plastic bag. Store in a refrigerator at 4-8C if you have one. If not, find the coolest place in your house, like a clay pot with a lid, or a hole in the ground shaded by a tree. You can also bury the bundles in damp sand or sawdust. For transport, use a cooler box with a little ice in a separate container, or wrap in wet cloth and carry in a shaded bag. Never leave scions in a hot car or in direct sun. If you are traveling long distances, check them every few hours. If the newspaper dries, re-moisten it lightly. Do not soak the wood in water; that causes rot. Also, do not store scions with ripe fruit, as ethylene gas from fruit can damage the buds. Label each bundle clearly with a permanent marker on a plastic tag. Include the variety, date, and location. If you are collecting multiple varieties, keep them separate to avoid mixing. When you are ready to graft, remove the bundles from storage and let them come to room temperature for an hour before grafting. Inspect the wood: it should be green under the bark and the buds should be plump. If the wood is brown and dry, it is dead. With proper storage, you can collect scions from a distant mother tree and still achieve high graft success.

4. Labeling and tracking different varieties

If you are collecting scions from more than one tree, you must label them correctly. Mixing up varieties leads to wasted grafts and confusion in your orchard. Use durable labels. Plastic tags with a hole punched in them work well. Write with a permanent marker or a pencil. Include the variety name (e.g., 'Apple Mango', 'Hass Avocado'), the date collected, and the mother tree's location or a code. If you don't know the variety name, give it a local name like 'Mama Jane's Mango'. Attach one label to each bundle. Also, write the same information on a piece of paper and keep it in your record book. As you graft, keep a map of your orchard. Draw a simple diagram showing which variety is grafted on which rootstock. Number each tree. For example, Tree 1: 'Apple Mango' on local rootstock. This helps you track performance later. If you sell scions or share with neighbors, give them a copy of the label. In Africa, many farmers lose track of varieties and end up with poor quality fruit. Labeling solves that. Use a pencil on plastic because marker can fade in the sun. If you are collecting from wild trees, note the fruit characteristics: size, color, taste, season. That way, even without a name, you can identify the best ones. Keep your labels with the scions during storage and transport. When grafting, double-check the label before making the cut. A simple mistake like swapping labels can ruin a year's work. Also, keep a notebook with dates of grafting, budding, and first harvest. This record becomes valuable for future planning.

5. Avoiding disease transfer when collecting scions

Diseases can spread from the mother tree to your orchard through scion wood. The most common are viruses, bacteria, and fungi. For example, citrus tristeza virus, mango bacterial black spot, and avocado sunblotch viroid can all be carried in scions. To avoid this, only collect from trees that look healthy. Inspect the leaves, branches, and trunk for symptoms: yellowing, mottling, cankers, gumming, or dieback. If you see any signs, do not take wood from that tree. Even if the tree looks healthy, it might be a carrier. The safest approach is to collect from trees that have been certified disease-free, but in most African communities, such certification is rare. So, use your eyes and ask local extension officers. Also, sterilize your tools between trees. Use a 10% bleach solution or rubbing alcohol (70%) to wipe your pruners and knife. Dip the blades for 30 seconds. This prevents moving pathogens from one tree to another. When cutting, avoid touching the cut surface with your hands. Wear clean gloves if possible. After collecting, do not store scions with any diseased plant material. If you see any rot or mold on the scions during storage, discard them immediately. When grafting, make sure your rootstock is also healthy. A weak rootstock can become infected. In some cases, you can hot-water treat scions (50C for 10 minutes) to kill some pathogens, but this is tricky and can damage the buds. Better to start clean. Remember, once a virus is in your orchard, it is almost impossible to remove. So, prevention is everything. Collect from the best, healthiest trees, and keep your tools clean.

6. Hands-on: Collecting and preparing scions from a local mango tree

Now let's put it all into practice. You will need: a sharp grafting knife or bypass pruners, a clean cloth, 70% alcohol, plastic tags, a permanent marker, moist newspaper, a plastic bag, and a cooler box. Choose a healthy mango tree that has produced well for at least three years. Go to the tree in the early morning when it is cool. Look for branches that are about the thickness of a pencil, with plump buds. These are usually in the outer canopy, exposed to sun. Cut 30 cm long scions. Make a clean cut just above a bud at the top, and just below a bud at the bottom. Remove the leaves but leave a short petiole. Cut 10-15 scions from different parts of the tree. As you cut, place them in a bundle with buds pointing up. After cutting, sterilize your knife with alcohol. Then, take a bundle and wrap it in slightly moist newspaper. Write a label: 'Mango - [local name] - [date] - [tree location]'. Attach the label to the bundle. Place the bundle in a plastic bag and tie it loosely. Put the bag in your cooler box with a small ice pack wrapped in a cloth. Do not let the ice touch the scions. If you are grafting the same day, keep them in the shade. If you are storing, put them in a refrigerator at 4-8C. Before grafting, inspect each scion: the bark should be green when scratched, and buds should be firm. Discard any that are dry or brown. Now you are ready to graft. This hands-on practice will teach you the importance of speed, cleanliness, and care. Repeat this process with other fruit trees like avocado or citrus. The more you practice, the better your success rate.

<h2>Practical Reference: Selecting and Collecting Scion Wood</h2>

<p>Scion wood is the single most important input in any grafting or budding programme. A nursery that grafts 5,000 mango seedlings per season will invest more money in polythene tubes, shade netting, water, and labour than in scion wood yet the genetic quality of the final orchard, and therefore the income of the farmer who buys those trees, is decided almost entirely by the wood you tie onto the rootstock. In African fruit-tree nurseries, where a single elite tree can supply thousands of grafts, the difference between a well-selected mother tree and a random one is the difference between a mango orchard that pays school fees and one that does not.</p>

<p>This lesson treats scion collection as a business process: identify elite mother trees, cut at the right physiological stage, keep the wood alive and labelled, and prevent disease from entering your nursery. Each step has a cost, and each step has a return.</p>

<h3>1. Identifying high-yielding mother trees in your community</h3>

<p>Do not collect from the nearest tree. Collect from the best tree you can legally access. In most African communities, elite fruit trees are known locally a mango that fruits heavily in an off year, a tree that bears at 3 years instead of 7, a tree whose fruit fetches a premium at the market. These are your candidate mother trees.</p>

<p>Score candidate trees before you cut anything. Walk the community with a notebook, talk to farmers and extension officers, and record the following for each candidate:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Yield consistency</strong> does the tree crop heavily in most years, or only in alternate years?</li>
<li><strong>Fruit quality</strong> size, colour, fibre content, shelf life, taste, and market preference.</li>
<li><strong>Tree health</strong> no gummosis, no dieback, no severe insect damage, no viral symptoms (leaf curling, mosaic, ring-spot).</li>
<li><strong>Age and vigour</strong> a tree between 6 and 20 years is usually ideal; very old trees may carry latent disease, very young trees may not have proven their yield.</li>
<li><strong>Ownership and access</strong> always obtain the owner's permission in writing or in the presence of a witness. This protects you legally and builds trust for repeat collection.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Business point:</strong> a single elite mother tree, managed well, can supply scion wood for 2,0005,000 grafts per season for many years. That is a repeatable, low-cost asset. Treat mother-tree identification as an investment, not a chore.</p>

<h3>2. When and how to cut scion wood for maximum success</h3>

<p>Timing is everything. Scion wood must be physiologically ready: the shoot should be mature enough to have hardened slightly but still have active cambium. In mango, the best scion wood comes from the last flush that has hardened usually 48 weeks after the flush emerged. Cutting too early gives soft, watery wood that desiccates; cutting too late gives woody, low-vigour wood with poor cambial activity.</p>

<p>In most African fruit-growing zones:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mango:</strong> collect at the start of the dry season, when the last flush has hardened and the tree is entering a slower growth phase. In East Africa, this is typically JuneAugust; in West Africa, NovemberJanuary.</li>
<li><strong>Avocado:</strong> collect in the cool dry season, when shoots are mature but the tree is not flushing.</li>
<li><strong>Citrus:</strong> collect in the cooler months, avoiding the peak of the rainy season when disease pressure is high.</li>
</ul>

<p>Cutting technique:</p>
<ol>
<li>Use a sharp, clean secateur or grafting knife. Disinfect between trees with 70% alcohol or a 10% bleach solution.</li>
<li>Select shoots of pencil thickness (about 610 mm diameter), 2030 cm long, from the outer canopy where light is good.</li>
<li>Cut 610 cm below the terminal bud, leaving a clean angled cut. Remove all leaves immediately, leaving a short petiole stub (23 mm) to protect the bud.</li>
<li>Do not cut from water sprouts or very vigorous vertical shoots they are often too soft and have poor fruit-bearing genetics.</li>
<li>Collect only as much as you can use or store within 2448 hours. Fresh wood is better than stored wood.</li>
</ol>

<h3>3. Storing and transporting scion wood without drying out</h3>

<p>Scion wood dies quickly if it dries. The rule is: <strong>keep it cool, moist, and dark, but never wet.</strong> Excess free water encourages rot; dryness kills the cambium.</p>

<p>Field method for African conditions:</p>
<ul>
<li>Wrap bundles of 2030 sticks in a slightly damp (not wet) cloth or newspaper.</li>
<li>Place inside a polythene bag, but leave the bag slightly open for air exchange.</li>
<li>Keep out of direct sun. A cool box with a damp cloth, or a shaded termite mound, works in the field.</li>
<li>For transport, use a cooler box with a small ice pack wrapped in cloth never place ice directly against the wood.</li>
<li>Store at 410C if refrigeration is available; otherwise use the coolest shaded spot and use within 35 days.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. Labelling and tracking different varieties</h3>

<p>In a commercial nursery, mixing varieties is a business disaster. A farmer who buys 100 "Apple Mango" and receives 100 "Ngowe" will not return. Label every bundle at the moment of cutting, not later.</p>

<p>Use a two-label system:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Inside label:</strong> a waterproof paper or plastic tag written in pencil or permanent marker, placed inside the bundle wrap. Include variety name, mother tree ID, date, and collector's name.</li>
<li><strong>Outside label:</strong> a second tag on the outside of the bundle with the same information, so you can identify it without unwrapping.</li>
</ul>

<p>Keep a simple scion register (a notebook or spreadsheet) with columns for: date, variety, mother tree ID, number of sticks, collector, storage location, and date used. This register becomes your traceability record increasingly important for buyers, certification bodies, and nursery registration in countries such as Kenya, Tanzania, and Ghana.</p>

<h3>5. Avoiding disease transfer when collecting scions</h3>

<p>Scion wood can carry fungal, bacterial, and viral pathogens from the mother tree into your nursery and, eventually, into farmers' orchards. This is a hidden cost: a nursery that sells infected trees may face replacement claims, loss of reputation, and legal liability.</p>

<p>Key practices:</p>
<ul>
<li>Never collect from trees showing symptoms: gummosis, cankers, leaf spots, mosaic, or dieback.</li>
<li>Disinfect tools between trees 70% alcohol, 10% bleach, or a flame.</li>
<li>Do not collect from the same tree if it has a history of anthracnose or bacterial black spot.</li>
<li>Avoid collecting during or immediately after rain, when fungal spores are abundant.</li>
<li>Inspect scion wood before grafting: reject any stick with brown cambium, soft rot, or insect holes.</li>
<li>Where possible, source scions from certified mother blocks or registered nurseries.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Hands-on: Collecting and preparing scions from a local mango tree</h3>

<p><strong>Objective:</strong> By the end of this practical, you will have collected, prepared, labelled, and stored 3050 mango scion sticks suitable for grafting.</p>

<p><strong>Materials:</strong> sharp secateurs, grafting knife, 70% alcohol or 10% bleach, clean cloth, damp newspaper, polythene bags, labels, pencil/permanent marker, cool box, notebook.</p>

<p><strong>Steps:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Select a healthy, high-yielding mango mother tree with the owner's permission. Record its location and ID.</li>
<li>Identify the last hardened flush shoots with green-brown bark, pencil thickness, and visible leaf scars.</li>
<li>Disinfect your secateurs. Cut 2030 cm shoots, 610 cm below the terminal bud.</li>
<li>Immediately remove leaves, leaving a 23 mm petiole stub. Do not damage the buds.</li>
<li>Bundle 2030 sticks, wrap in damp (not wet) newspaper, and label inside and outside.</li>
<li>Place in a cool box or shaded spot. Record in your scion register.</li>
<li>Use within 35 days for best results. Inspect each stick before grafting reject any with brown cambium or soft rot.</li>
</ol>

<p><strong>Assessment:</strong> Each student or trainee should submit 30 labelled sticks, a completed register entry, and a short note on the mother tree's yield and health. This is the same standard expected in a commercial nursery.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Mother tree selected for yield, fruit quality, and health with owner's permission.</li>
<li> Tools disinfected before cutting and between trees.</li>
<li> Scion wood pencil thickness, 2030 cm, from hardened last flush.</li>
<li> Leaves removed, petiole stub left intact.</li>
<li> Bundles wrapped in damp (not wet) cloth or newspaper.</li>
<li> Inside and outside labels with variety, mother tree ID, date, collector.</li>
<li> Stored cool, shaded, and used within 35 days (or refrigerated for longer).</li>
<li> Scion register completed and kept for traceability.</li>
</ul>
