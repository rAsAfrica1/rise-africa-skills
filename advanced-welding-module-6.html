Lessons

1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Structural welding is what holds up buildings, bridges, and machines. In Africa, where infrastructure is growing fast, a bad weld can mean a collapsed roof or a failed truck chassis. This module teaches you to weld structures that are safe and strong, using methods that work in real African workshops. You will learn to read drawings, choose the right electrodes, prepare joints, and inspect your work.

Why does it matter? A structural weld carries loads. If it fails, people can get hurt. In many African countries, welding codes like AWS D1.1 or local standards are used for critical structures. Even if you work in a small shop, you must follow best practices: clean metal, correct amperage, proper technique, and inspection.

This module is practical. You will use tools you likely already have: angle grinder, chipping hammer, wire brush, measuring tape, and a welding machine (stick or MIG). You will learn to make butt joints, T-joints, and corner joints on mild steel from 6 mm to 12 mm thick. You will also learn to avoid common mistakes like undercut, porosity, and lack of penetration.

By the end, you will be able to weld a structural bracket or frame that passes a visual inspection and a simple bend test. You will also know how to stay safe: wear a welding helmet, gloves, leather apron, and boots. Work in a ventilated area. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby.

Remember: a good structural weld is not just about looking pretty. It must be strong. Practice each step until it becomes habit. Your reputation as a welder depends on the quality of your work.

2. Tools and Materials

To do structural welding, you need the right tools and materials. Start with a welding machine. A 200-amp stick welder (SMAW) is common in African workshops. For thicker steel (above 10 mm), you may need 250-300 amps. If you have a MIG welder, use it for speed on thin to medium steel. Always check that your machine is rated for the duty cycle you need.

Electrodes: For mild steel, use E6013 for general work, but for structural welds, use E7018 (low hydrogen) because it gives tougher, stronger welds. Store E7018 in a dry, warm place (rod oven if possible) to prevent moisture pickup. For MIG, use ER70S-6 wire with 75% Argon / 25% CO2 gas.

Metal preparation tools: You need an angle grinder with grinding and cutting discs. A wire brush (hand or drill-mounted) to clean slag and rust. A chipping hammer for slag removal. A measuring tape, square, and soapstone or marker for layout. Clamps (C-clamps, vice grips) to hold parts.

Safety gear: Welding helmet with at least shade 10 for stick, shade 10-13 for higher amps. Leather gloves, apron, sleeves, and boots. Safety glasses for grinding. A fire extinguisher and a bucket of water or sand.

Materials: Mild steel is the most common. You will work with plates, angles, channels, and square tubes. For structural work, use steel that is clean, free of rust, oil, and paint. If you must weld galvanized steel, grind off the zinc coating first and weld in a well-ventilated area to avoid toxic fumes.

Finally, keep a notebook to record your settings and results. This helps you improve. In Africa, where power can be unstable, consider a generator or inverter welder that works on lower voltage.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps for a strong structural weld. First, prepare the joint. Clean the metal with a grinder until it is shiny. Remove all rust, oil, and paint. For butt joints on steel thicker than 6 mm, bevel the edges to 30-37.5 degrees to allow full penetration. Leave a root gap of 2-3 mm.

Second, set up your machine. For E7018 on 10 mm steel, set amperage around 120-140 amps for 3.2 mm electrode. For 4.0 mm, use 140-180 amps. For MIG, set voltage and wire speed according to the thickness chart. Always test on a scrap piece first.

Third, tack weld. Place tacks at both ends and every 150-200 mm along the joint. Check alignment with a square. Tacks should be strong enough to hold but not too large.

Fourth, weld the root pass. For stick, use a slight drag angle (5-15 degrees) and a short arc. Move steadily. For MIG, push or pull depending on preference, but keep a consistent travel speed. After the root, clean slag with chipping hammer and wire brush.

Fifth, fill and cap. For thick steel, use multiple passes. The fill pass fills the bevel. The cap pass covers the joint and should be slightly convex. Overlap each pass by 50% of the previous bead. Keep the arc on the leading edge of the puddle.

Sixth, inspect. Look for undercut, porosity, cracks, and incomplete fusion. Use a magnifying glass if needed. A good weld is smooth, uniform, and free of defects.

Finally, let it cool naturally. Do not quench with water unless specified. For critical structures, perform a bend test or ultrasonic testing if available. In many African workshops, a simple hammer test (striking the weld) can reveal poor fusion.

Practice this method on scrap until you can produce consistent welds. Speed comes with time.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced welders make mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them.

1. Porosity (gas holes in weld). Causes: dirty metal, moisture in electrode, or too long an arc. Fix: Clean metal, use dry electrodes (bake E7018 at 250-300C for 1-2 hours if damp), and shorten arc. For MIG, check gas flow (15-20 CFH) and remove drafts.

2. Undercut (groove melted into base metal along weld toe). Causes: too high amperage, too long arc, or wrong travel angle. Fix: Reduce amps, shorten arc, and use a slight backhand technique.

3. Lack of penetration (weld does not reach root). Causes: too low amps, too fast travel, or no bevel. Fix: Increase amps, slow down, and bevel thick edges. Use a root gap of 2-3 mm.

4. Slag inclusion (slag trapped in weld). Causes: not cleaning between passes, wrong electrode angle. Fix: Chip and brush thoroughly after each pass. Use a drag angle to float slag to the surface.

5. Spatter (small metal balls stuck to surface). Causes: too high amps, long arc, or wrong polarity. Fix: Adjust settings, use anti-spatter spray, or chip off after cooling.

6. Distortion (warping). Causes: too much heat in one area. Fix: Use balanced welding (weld on opposite sides), clamp parts, or use back-step technique.

7. Cracks. Causes: high carbon steel, rapid cooling, or poor fit-up. Fix: Preheat if needed, use low hydrogen electrodes, and allow slow cooling.

In Africa, humidity and dust can worsen these issues. Store electrodes in sealed containers. Keep your work area clean. Always inspect your weld before moving on. If you see a defect, grind it out and re-weld. Never try to cover it up. A small fix now prevents a big failure later.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional in structural welding. You are dealing with high heat, electricity, fumes, and heavy metal. Follow these rules to stay safe and produce quality work.

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE): Wear a welding helmet with the correct shade (10-13 for stick). Use leather gloves, a leather apron, and long sleeves. Wear safety boots with steel toes. Use ear protection when grinding or chipping. Always wear safety glasses under your helmet for grinding.

Ventilation: Welding fumes are toxic. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. If indoors, use a fan or fume extractor. Never weld galvanized steel without a respirator and good ventilation.

Fire safety: Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO2) within reach. Remove flammable materials like paper, wood, and solvents from the area. Have a bucket of sand or water nearby. After welding, stay in the area for at least 30 minutes to watch for smoldering fires.

Electrical safety: Check your welding cables for damage. Do not weld in wet conditions. Use a ground clamp that is securely attached to clean metal. Never change electrodes with bare hands or while the machine is on.

Best practices: Plan your weld. Clean metal, proper fit-up, correct settings, and steady technique. Use jigs and clamps to hold parts. Weld in a sequence that minimizes distortion. Inspect after each pass. Keep your work area organized.

In Africa, power fluctuations can damage machines. Use a voltage stabilizer or surge protector if possible. If using a generator, ensure it is rated for your welder's input.

Finally, take breaks. Welding is physically demanding. Stay hydrated. Do not rush. A safe welder is a productive welder.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. This exercise will test your ability to make a structural T-joint weld on 10 mm mild steel. You will need: two plates 100 mm x 50 mm x 10 mm, a welding machine (stick or MIG), E7018 electrodes (3.2 mm) or ER70S-6 wire, grinder, chipping hammer, wire brush, square, clamps, and PPE.

Step 1: Prepare the plates. Clean all surfaces with a grinder until shiny. Remove mill scale, rust, and oil.

Step 2: Set up the T-joint. Place one plate flat and the other vertically on top, forming a T. Use a square to ensure a 90-degree angle. Clamp securely.

Step 3: Tack weld at both ends of the vertical plate. Check alignment.

Step 4: Weld the first side. For stick, set amps to 120-130 for 3.2 mm E7018. Use a slight drag angle. Weld from left to right (or right to left, whichever is comfortable). Keep a short arc. After welding, chip slag and brush.

Step 5: Weld the second side. Repeat on the other side. To control distortion, alternate sides or use back-step technique.

Step 6: Inspect. Look for undercut, porosity, and incomplete fusion. Measure the leg length of the weld. For a 10 mm plate, the weld leg should be at least 6 mm.

Step 7: Bend test (optional). Cut a 25 mm wide strip across the weld. Bend it in a vice or with a hammer. The weld should not crack or break.

Step 8: Clean and finish. Grind any spatter. Apply a rust-preventive primer if needed.

Repeat this exercise until you can produce consistent, defect-free welds. Then try a butt joint with a bevel. Record your settings and results. In Africa, where jobs are competitive, a welder who can pass a bend test is highly valued. Practice daily.

Remember: quality comes from repetition. Keep your tools sharp, your metal clean, and your mind focused.

<h2>Practical Reference: Structural Welding Best Practice</h2>

<p>In African construction and fabrication markets, structural welding is rarely just a technical task it is a commercial decision. A failed weld on a school roof in Kumasi, a bridge railing in Nairobi, or a warehouse portal frame in Lagos does not only cost repair money. It costs contracts, reputation, insurance access, and in the worst cases, lives. This practical reference distils the standards, data, and field habits that separate welding businesses that win repeat work from those that compete only on price.</p>

<p>Whether you are welding a portal frame for a maize storage shed in Zambia, fabricating staircases in Accra, or repairing a truck chassis in Mombasa, the same principles apply: correct procedure, correct consumables, correct inspection. This module assumes you already know how to strike an arc. It focuses on what makes structural welding commercially reliable.</p>

<h3>Why Structural Welding Standards Matter in Africa</h3>

<p>Across the continent, structural steel demand is rising fast. The African Development Bank estimates that Africa needs between $130 billion and $170 billion annually in infrastructure investment, with a large share going to transport, energy, and industrial structures. Every one of those structures depends on welds that perform under load, fatigue, and weather.</p>

<p>Yet many small and medium fabrication enterprises operate without a qualified welding procedure specification (WPS), without welder qualification records, and without non-destructive testing (NDT). This creates a two-tier market: firms that can bid for formal projects banks, mines, government tenders and firms that cannot. The difference is rarely the welding machine. It is documentation, procedure, and consistency.</p>

<p>International standards such as ISO 3834 (Quality requirements for fusion welding of metallic materials) and AWS D1.1 (Structural Welding Code Steel) are increasingly referenced in African tender documents, particularly for mining, oil and gas, and donor-funded infrastructure. Even where they are not legally required, they are commercially useful because they signal reliability to clients, insurers, and financiers.</p>

<h3>Step-by-Step Field Method for Structural Fillet Welds</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Verify the WPS.</strong> Confirm the welding procedure specification matches the joint, material, and position. If no WPS exists, create one using a qualified welding engineer or reference standard.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare the joint.</strong> Clean to bare metal remove mill scale, rust, oil, paint, and moisture at least 25 mm from the weld zone.</li>
<li><strong>Set fit-up and tack welds.</strong> Maintain root gap and alignment per drawing. Tack welds must be made by a qualified welder and be of production quality they become part of the weld.</li>
<li><strong>Select and store consumables correctly.</strong> Low-hydrogen electrodes (e.g., E7018) must be kept in a heated quiver (100150C) once opened. Moisture causes hydrogen cracking, especially in thick sections.</li>
<li><strong>Apply preheat if required.</strong> For thickness above 25 mm or high-carbon-equivalent steels, preheat to 100150C using a temperature stick or infrared thermometer.</li>
<li><strong>Weld with controlled technique.</strong> Maintain correct travel angle (1015), arc length, and travel speed. Use stringer beads or controlled weave avoid excessive weaving that reduces penetration.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect and record.</strong> Visual inspection for undercut, porosity, and leg size. Use a weld gauge. Record welder ID, date, and consumable batch. For critical joints, arrange ultrasonic or magnetic particle testing.</li>
<li><strong>Document and hand over.</strong> Provide as-built records, WPS references, and welder qualification certificates to the client. This is what wins the next tender.</li>
</ol>

<p style="margin:0; color:#d00000; font-weight:bold; font-size:1.1em;"> SAFETY WARNING HYDROGEN CRACKING AND MOISTURE</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#1a1a1a;">Low-hydrogen electrodes that absorb moisture from humid African climates can cause catastrophic delayed cracking in structural welds sometimes 24 to 48 hours after welding. Always store electrodes in sealed containers, use heated quivers on site, and never leave E7018 electrodes exposed overnight. In coastal or rainy-season conditions, discard electrodes that have been exposed for more than 4 hours unless re-dried per manufacturer instructions. A cracked structural weld can fail without warning under load.</p>
</div>

<h3>Common Mistakes and Commercial Consequences</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Undersized fillet welds.</strong> Often done to save time and consumables. Result: reduced load capacity, failed inspection, rework cost 35 the original weld cost.</li>
<li><strong>Wrong electrode for the steel.</strong> Using E6013 on S355 or high-strength steel. Result: under-matched strength, potential brittle failure.</li>
<li><strong>Poor fit-up.</strong> Excessive gaps force welders to bridge with large volumes of weld metal, increasing distortion and residual stress.</li>
<li><strong>No preheat on thick sections.</strong> Leads to fast cooling, hard brittle zones, and cracking. Common in mining and plant fabrication.</li>
<li><strong>Skipping documentation.</strong> Losing tenders to competitors who can demonstrate ISO 3834 or AWS D1.1 compliance.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring distortion control.</strong> Welding sequence matters. Balanced welding, back-step technique, and restrained fixtures reduce distortion and rework.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="list-style-type:none; padding-left:0;">
<li> WPS available and matched to joint, material, and position</li>
<li> Joint cleaned to bare metal, 25 mm minimum from weld zone</li>
<li> Consumables correct grade, stored dry, heated quiver in use</li>
<li> Preheat applied where thickness &gt; 25 mm or high CE steel</li>
<li> Welder qualification current and recorded</li>
<li> Fillet leg size checked with weld gauge against drawing</li>
<li> Visual inspection done before leaving the joint</li>
<li> Weld records, consumable batch, and welder ID documented</li>
</ul>
