Feed as the First Line of Defence: Gut Barrier and Immune Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Explain how the intestinal lining acts as the bird's main barrier against disease
  • Identify the specific nutrients that support immune function and gut integrity
  • Calculate the cost of a nutrition-driven mortality improvement on your own flock

Most disease losses on small African poultry farms do not start with a virus arriving at the gate. They start in the gut. A chicken's intestine is a thin, single-cell-thick wall separating the feed and its bacteria from the bird's bloodstream. Everything you put in the feed bag either strengthens that wall or weakens it. Vaccines and medicines matter, but a bird with a damaged gut lining will underperform even with a perfect vaccination programme.

The gut wall is covered in finger-like projections called villi. Long villi with shallow crypts mean a large absorptive surface and a bird that gets full value from expensive feed. Short villi with deep crypts mean the bird is spending energy constantly rebuilding damaged tissue instead of growing. Research on feed additives measures exactly this: garlic and thyme supplementation significantly reduced ileal crypt depth compared with untreated birds, which is a positive marker of reduced epithelial turnover.

Several nutrients do specific defensive jobs. Vitamin A maintains the epithelial tissue that lines the gut, the airways and the eyes; Ross 2022 specifies 13,000 IU/kg in starter, falling to 10,000 IU/kg in finisher. Vitamin E and selenium work together in the glutathione peroxidase antioxidant system, and the published guidance is to pair roughly 10-20 IU of vitamin E with each 0.3 mg of selenium. Ross 2022 sets vitamin E at 80 IU/kg starter, 65 grower, 55 finisher, with selenium at 0.30 mg/kg across phases. Zinc at 120 mg/kg supports immune function and feather quality. Note how far above the older NRC 1994 minimums these are - NRC lists vitamin E at just 5-25 IU/kg and selenium at 0.06-0.2 mg/kg. NRC describes the level below which deficiency appears. Ross describes the level at which a fast-growing modern bird performs and defends itself. For a commercial broiler flock, aim at the breeder figures.

Amino acids are also immune nutrients, not just muscle nutrients. Antibodies and immune cells are proteins. When a bird mounts an immune response, a larger share of its amino acid intake goes to maintenance rather than growth. Published work shows maintenance takes about 6% of total daily digestible amino acids in 10-21 day broilers but about 22% in 32-43 day birds, and utilisation efficiency falls from roughly 71% to 62% over the same period. A challenged older bird has very little spare protein.

Work through the money. Suppose you run 500 broilers and lose 8% by day 42, which is 40 birds. Day-old chicks at about $0.80 each and feed already eaten on the birds that died - say an average of 1.5 kg per dead bird at $0.55/kg, roughly $0.83 - means each dead bird costs you around $1.63 in direct inputs, so $65 per cycle, before counting the sale value you never earn. If a corrected vitamin-mineral premix costs $0.02/kg of feed extra, and the flock eats 500 birds x 3.8 kg = 1,900 kg, the extra cost is $38 per cycle. Cutting mortality from 8% to 5% saves 15 birds, about $24 in inputs plus roughly 15 x $4.00 = $60 in lost sales. That is $84 of value for $38 of premix.

A practical Kenyan example: a producer in Kiambu with 300 layers was buying a cheap unbranded premix and seeing pale combs, ragged feathers and 2-3 deaths per week. Switching to a premix from a supplier who prints the vitamin A, D3, E and selenium levels on the label - and storing it out of sunlight in a sealed bucket rather than an open sack - restored feathering within a month. The premix was more expensive per kilogram but represented under 1% of total feed cost.

The rule to carry forward: never cut the premix to save money. It is the smallest line in your feed budget and the largest lever on health.

Coccidiosis Control: Coccidiostats, Immunity and Withdrawal Rules

In this lesson
  • Distinguish ionophore from synthetic coccidiostats by their mode of action
  • Apply the seven-day withdrawal rule correctly before slaughter
  • Compare the cost of in-feed coccidiostat prevention against treating an outbreak

Coccidiosis is caused by Eimeria parasites that invade the cells lining the intestine. It thrives in warm, damp litter, which describes most African broiler houses during the rainy season. Birds show bloody or mucus-streaked droppings, huddling, ruffled feathers and a sudden drop in feed intake. Even mild subclinical infection costs money through poor feed conversion long before you see a dead bird.

The main tool is an in-feed coccidiostat. There are two families and the difference matters.

Ionophores - monensin, lasalocid and salinomycin - are polyether compounds that transport sodium, potassium and hydrogen ions across the parasite's cell membrane. This wrecks the parasite's osmotic balance, causing vacuolisation and cell death. Crucially, ionophores act in the early phase of the parasite life cycle, so a small number of parasites still complete development and the bird builds natural immunity. That is a real advantage in flocks kept beyond six weeks and in layer pullets that must live for eighteen months.

Synthetic coccidiostats work differently. Nicarbazin interferes with the intracellular stages of the Eimeria life cycle, destroying parasites developing inside epithelial cells. Amprolium, in use since the early 1960s, is a thiamine antagonist - it starves the parasite of the thiamine its metabolism depends on rather than lysing its cells directly. Because nicarbazin can worsen heat stress, be careful with it in hot African conditions and discuss alternatives with your supplier.

Withdrawal is a legal and food-safety matter, not a suggestion. Under EU rules coccidiostats may be fed from day one up to seven days before slaughter, and a seven-day minimum withdrawal applies to broilers for monensin, lasalocid, salinomycin, nicarbazin and amprolium alike. Maximum Residue Limits exist for every authorised compound. If you sell to a processor, a hotel chain or an export-linked buyer, residues can lose you the contract. Practically: if you slaughter on day 42, the coccidiostat-free finisher feed must be the only feed the birds see from day 35.

Mind the interactions. Monensin may suppress copper absorption, so a copper adjustment may be needed. High dietary zinc above 150 mg/kg can antagonise copper, iron and manganese uptake - relevant because Ross 2022 already sets zinc at 120 mg/kg and copper at 16 mg/kg, leaving little headroom for careless over-supplementation.

Now the economics. Take 1,000 broilers eating about 3.8 kg each over the cycle, so 3,800 kg of feed. Of that, roughly 3,200 kg is medicated starter and grower and 600 kg is clean withdrawal finisher. A coccidiostat premix costing about $4.00 per kg included at 0.5 kg per tonne means 3,200 kg x 0.0005 = 1.6 kg of product, roughly $6.40 for the whole flock, under $0.007 per bird.

Compare an outbreak. A moderate coccidiosis event at day 25 might kill 6% of the flock (60 birds) and add 0.15 to the feed conversion ratio on the survivors. Sixty birds at $4.00 sale value is $240. The FCR penalty on 940 surviving birds averaging 2.0 kg live weight is 940 x 2.0 x 0.15 = 282 kg of extra feed at $0.55/kg, another $155. Add a water-soluble treatment course at roughly $25. Total near $420 against $6.40 of prevention.

A Nigerian example: a Kaduna producer running deep-litter broilers in the rainy season stopped alternating coccidiostats between cycles and used the same ionophore for two years. Losses climbed each cycle as resistance built. Rotating to a synthetic product for two cycles, and topping up wet litter patches under the drinkers with dry shavings twice a week, brought mortality back down without any change in feed price.

Prevention here is close to free. Losing the discipline is what costs money.

Necrotic Enteritis: Feeding to Keep Clostridium Under Control

In this lesson
  • Describe the feed and management conditions that trigger necrotic enteritis
  • Adjust protein source, fibre and feed form to reduce clostridial risk
  • Build an antibiotic-free gut health programme using organic acids, probiotics and enzymes

Necrotic enteritis is caused by Clostridium perfringens, a bacterium that lives harmlessly in most chicken guts until conditions let it multiply. When it does, it produces toxins that kill the intestinal lining. Birds die suddenly in good condition, often the best-grown ones, usually between days 18 and 30. Opened birds show a thickened, gas-filled small intestine with a rough yellow-brown lining. The subclinical form is more common and more expensive: no deaths, just a flock that eats well and grows badly.

Clostridium needs three things to bloom: undigested protein reaching the lower gut, damaged intestinal lining, and slow gut transit. Every one of these is a feed decision.

  1. Undigested protein. Crude protein that the bird cannot absorb becomes clostridial food. This is why formulating to digestible amino acids rather than crude protein matters. The ideal protein concept holds that nitrogen supplied above amino acid requirement is an economic loss and increases excretion - it is also a health risk. Ross 2022 digestible lysine is 1.32% in starter, 1.18% grower, 1.08% finisher, with methionine plus cysteine at 1.00, 0.92 and 0.86%. Meeting those with a modest crude protein is safer than overshooting crude protein and hoping.
  2. Protein source quality. Poorly rendered meat and bone meal or fish meal is a classic trigger, and both are highly variable - meat and bone meal AMEn ranges 2,200-2,400 kcal/kg with high variability of about plus or minus 200. If you cannot verify the rendering quality, keep animal protein inclusion low.
  3. Gut damage. Coccidiosis is the single biggest predisposing factor, which is why Module 9 Lesson 2 sits ahead of this one. Fumonisins matter too: at 18.6 ppm, below the nominal 20 ppm broiler safe level, fumonisins altered intestinal microflora and predisposed broilers to necrotic enteritis.

Your antibiotic-free toolkit has three parts, and all three are documented.

Organic acids lower gut pH, suppress pathogens, improve mineral availability and strengthen the epithelial barrier. Typical inclusions are formic 0.5-1.0%, propionic 0.5-1.0%, butyric 0.3-0.8% and citric 0.5-1.5%. Published efficacy ranges are a 2-8% FCR improvement, 1-3% better feed intake and 3-5% better weight gain, with the biggest responses under poor sanitation - which is exactly the small African farm situation.

Probiotics have matched or exceeded antibiotic growth promoter flocks for weight gain with no significant feed efficiency compromise, though feed cost per kg of gain rose 0.49-1.80% versus antibiotics. Herbal additives such as oregano, garlic and thyme performed equivalently to antibiotic controls over 0-42 days with no rise in E. coli counts.

Enzymes reduce the viscous, slow-moving gut contents that clostridia love. NSP-degrading enzyme complexes deliver 96-265 kcal/kg of extra energy and 3-7% feed efficiency gains, with the largest response on wheat, barley and oats and modest response on maize.

Costing the acid programme: for 1,000 broilers eating 3,800 kg, an organic acid blend at 0.5% inclusion is 19 kg. At $2.20/kg that is $41.80, or $0.042 per bird. A 3% FCR improvement on a 1.65 FCR flock at 2.0 kg live weight saves 1,000 x 2.0 x 0.05 = 100 kg of feed, worth $55 at $0.55/kg - before counting any mortality saved.

A Ghanaian example: a producer near Kumasi feeding a maize-soya diet with 8% locally rendered fish meal kept losing well-grown birds at day 22. Cutting fish meal to 3%, replacing the protein with soybean meal, adding a butyric acid product and keeping litter dry stopped the deaths within one cycle. The feed cost per kilogram rose slightly; the cost per kilogram of chicken sold fell.

Antioxidant Nutrition and Heat Stress in Hot Climates

In this lesson
  • Calculate dietary electrolyte balance for a given diet
  • Adjust sodium, potassium and chloride sources as ambient temperature rises
  • Design a hot-season feeding routine that protects intake and survival

In most of sub-Saharan Africa, heat is the single largest uncontrolled stressor on poultry. A broiler at 35 degrees Celsius cannot lose heat by sweating; it pants. Panting blows off carbon dioxide, which shifts blood chemistry, and the bird stops eating. Feed intake collapses, growth stalls, and in a bad afternoon you lose the heaviest birds first because they generate the most metabolic heat.

Dietary electrolyte balance, or DEB, is the practical lever. The formula is:

DEB (mEq/kg) = [Na (%) x 435] + [K (%) x 256] - [Cl (%) x 282]

Target ranges are documented by condition. Normal or cool environments call for 200-250 mEq/kg. Mild heat stress at 28-32 degrees needs 250-300. Moderate heat stress at 32-35 degrees needs 300-350. Severe heat above 35 degrees calls for 350-400, achieved by maximising sodium and potassium while minimising chloride.

The mechanism is worth understanding because it tells you which salt to buy. Excess chloride raises blood chloride, pushes the bird toward metabolic acidosis and drops blood pH. An acidotic bird must pant harder to compensate, which reduces growth and raises mortality. Raising DEB by increasing sodium and potassium while cutting chloride keeps the blood more alkaline, so panting works efficiently and the bird dissipates heat with less physiological cost. The practical results are better regulated water consumption, less wet litter and maintained feed intake.

Work an example. Ross 2022 specifies sodium at 0.18-0.23% and chloride at 0.18-0.23% for all broiler phases. Take a maize-soya diet at Na 0.20%, K 0.85%, Cl 0.22%.

DEB = (0.20 x 435) + (0.85 x 256) - (0.22 x 282) DEB = 87.0 + 217.6 - 62.0 = 242.6 mEq/kg

That is fine for a cool morning and inadequate for a 34-degree afternoon. Now replace part of the common salt with sodium bicarbonate. Common salt is roughly 39% sodium and 60% chloride, so it raises both. Sodium bicarbonate supplies sodium with no chloride. If you cut salt so chloride falls to 0.16% and use bicarbonate to hold sodium at 0.22%:

DEB = (0.22 x 435) + (0.85 x 256) - (0.16 x 282) DEB = 95.7 + 217.6 - 45.1 = 268.2 mEq/kg

That moves you into the mild heat stress band. Adding a potassium source to reach K 1.00% would give (0.22 x 435) + (1.00 x 256) - (0.16 x 282) = 95.7 + 256 - 45.1 = 306.6 mEq/kg, inside the moderate heat stress range.

Remember the ceiling: sodium or chloride above 0.5% depresses feed intake and growth, causes wet litter and damages eggshell quality in layers. DEB is a balancing exercise, not a licence to add salt.

Antioxidants matter alongside DEB. Heat stress generates oxidative damage, and vitamin E with selenium supports antioxidant defence, immune response, gut integrity and microbiota stability during heat stress, working better combined than either alone. Keep vitamin E at the Ross starter level of 80 IU/kg through the hot season rather than dropping to 55 IU/kg in finisher.

Management multiplies the nutrition. A Tanzanian producer near Morogoro moved feeding to 5am-9am and 5pm-9pm, leaving feeders empty through the 1pm-4pm peak, ran cool water through the lines at midday, and added a sodium bicarbonate and vitamin premix during heat waves. Digestion generates heat, so a bird digesting at 2pm is fighting itself. Shifting the feeding window costs nothing.

Cost check: sodium bicarbonate at about $0.90/kg included at 2 kg per tonne adds $1.80 per tonne of feed, or $0.0018/kg. On 3,800 kg for 1,000 broilers that is $6.84. Saving even four birds worth $4.00 each pays for it twice over.

Preventing Leg Problems: Calcium, Phosphorus and Vitamin D3

In this lesson
  • Set correct calcium and available phosphorus levels for each broiler phase
  • Check whether your Ca:P ratio falls inside the safe window
  • Use phytase to release bound phosphorus and cut mineral cost

Lame birds are pure loss. They cannot reach the feeder, they get trampled, they are condemned at sale, and each one represents six weeks of feed already spent. On fast-growing broilers the common conditions are tibial dyschondroplasia and angular limb deformities, and both are strongly influenced by what is in the starter bag.

The counterintuitive fact is that too much calcium early causes leg problems. Calcium above 1.0% in the starter phase interferes with phosphorus metabolism and predisposes birds to leg disorders. The critical window is days 0-10. Ross 2022 therefore specifies a deliberately restricted starter calcium of 0.95%, with available phosphorus at 0.50%, giving a 1.9:1 ratio. Grower is 0.75% Ca and 0.42% available P (1.8:1) and finisher is 0.65% Ca and 0.36% available P (1.8:1).

The ratio matters as much as the levels. The optimal Ca to available P ratio for growing poultry is about 2:1. Ratios below 1.5:1 or above 2.5:1 increase skeletal problems and nutrient malabsorption. This is where small farms go wrong most often: someone adds a scoop of limestone to a broiler mix because it is cheap and looks like it should help bones. It does the opposite. Limestone has calcium and no phosphorus, so every extra kilogram pushes the ratio upward and out of the safe window.

Check your own mix. Suppose you mix a tonne of broiler starter with 15 kg of limestone (about 38% Ca) and 12 kg of dicalcium phosphate (about 23% Ca, 18% P). Calcium from limestone is 15 x 0.38 = 5.7 kg. Calcium from DCP is 12 x 0.23 = 2.76 kg. Maize and soya contribute roughly a further 1.5 kg of calcium per tonne. Total is about 9.96 kg per 1,000 kg, or 1.00% calcium - already above the 0.95% target and into the risk zone. Phosphorus from DCP is 12 x 0.18 = 2.16 kg, and plant phosphorus is largely bound as phytate, so available P from maize and soya may add only about 1.2 kg. That gives roughly 3.36 kg or 0.34% available P and a ratio near 2.9:1, well outside the safe window. Dropping limestone to 10 kg brings calcium to about 0.81% and the ratio to about 2.4:1 - much safer and cheaper.

Vitamin D3 is the third leg of the stool. Ross 2022 specifies 5,000 IU/kg starter, 4,500 grower and 4,000 finisher, far above the NRC 1994 range of 200-1,100 IU/kg. If you can source 25-OH vitamin D3, note that it is absorbed more efficiently than D3 - especially in young birds - because it bypasses the liver hydroxylation step, and is roughly 4-5 times more biologically active on a weight basis. Recommended dosage where used as a partial or full D3 replacement is 2,760-5,529 IU/kg, and across twelve trials broilers on 25-OH D3 showed a 0.75% average improvement in breast meat yield. Treat it as a premium option, not a necessity.

Phytase is the money-saver. Plant phosphorus is locked in phytic acid, which also chelates zinc, iron, copper, manganese and calcium. Phytic acid reduces phosphorus bioavailability by 20-50% and zinc by 30-60%. Phytase at 500-1,500 FTU/kg degrades it, releasing bound phosphorus and minerals, and roughly 1,000 FTU/kg is equivalent to about a 0.15-0.25% improvement in available phosphorus. The response is most pronounced in young broilers at days 2-14.

Money: dicalcium phosphate is one of the more expensive feed ingredients. If phytase at 1,000 FTU/kg lets you cut DCP by 5 kg per tonne, and DCP costs about $0.90/kg, you save $4.50 per tonne. Phytase at 100 g per tonne of a 10,000 FTU/g product costing about $12/kg costs $1.20 per tonne. Net saving roughly $3.30 per tonne, plus better zinc and calcium availability.

A Ugandan example: a Mukono broiler producer had 4% of birds lame by week five. Nothing was wrong with the premix. The mixer was adding an extra bucket of limestone to each batch because a neighbour advised it. Removing the extra bucket dropped lameness to under 1% in the next cycle.

Water Quality: The Cheapest Nutrient and the Most Neglected

In this lesson
  • Test drinking water against published poultry standards
  • Identify which water faults harm birds and which damage the water line
  • Cost a water system correction against expected production gains

Birds drink roughly twice as much water as they eat feed, and more in heat. Water is the cheapest input on the farm and the least tested. On many small African farms it comes from a borehole, a shallow well, a river or a roof tank, and nobody has ever measured it. Yet a fault in the water undoes everything you did right in the feed.

The published standards for broiler drinking water give you a checklist:

  1. Total dissolved solids: below 1,500 ppm is recommended, above 3,000 ppm is a concern. High TDS is common in boreholes in arid areas and causes wet droppings.
  2. pH: 6.5 to 7.5 recommended; below 5.5 or above 8.5 is a concern. Alkaline water above 8.5 also inactivates many water-soluble medicines and vaccines, so a vaccination that looks like it failed may actually be a water problem.
  3. Hardness as calcium carbonate: 50-200 ppm recommended; above 400 ppm is a concern. Hard water scales up nipple drinkers and blocks dosing pumps.
  4. Chloride: below 250 ppm; above 1,000 ppm is a concern. Excess chloride compounds the electrolyte problems covered in Lesson 4.
  5. Iron: below 0.3 ppm; above 1.0 ppm is a concern. Iron feeds biofilm, stains everything orange and gives water an off taste that cuts intake.
  6. Sulfates: below 250 ppm; above 1,000 ppm is a concern. High sulfate acts as a laxative and produces wet litter.
  7. Microbial count: below 1,000 CFU/mL; above 10,000 CFU/mL is a concern.

Biofilm deserves special attention. It is the slimy layer inside pipes and drinker lines where bacteria live protected from chlorine. It builds fastest when vitamins, sugars or medicines are dosed through the line and the line is never flushed. If you have ever dosed vitamins through a drinker and seen mortality rise a few days later, biofilm is the most likely explanation. Flush lines with clean water after every medication or vitamin dose, and clean them properly between flocks.

Simple field checks anyone can do without a laboratory. Pour a glass from the drinker at the far end of the line, not the tap at the tank - the far end is where problems show. Look at it against white paper: cloudiness means suspended solids, orange tint means iron. Smell it: rotten egg smell means sulfides. Run a finger inside the drinker cup: slime means biofilm. Buy pH strips from a pharmacy or a swimming pool supplier and test morning and afternoon. Watch the birds: a flock crowding at drinkers but with dry litter suggests inadequate flow, while wet litter with normal intake suggests a water quality or electrolyte fault.

Measure water intake. Fit a cheap inline meter or simply fill a 20-litre bucket for a hand-filled system and count refills. A sudden 20-30% drop in water intake is the earliest disease warning you will get, usually appearing a day before feed intake falls and two days before you see a sick bird. It is a better daily indicator than anything else you can record for free.

Cost example: chlorination for a 1,000-bird flock. Sodium hypochlorite at about $1.50 per litre, dosed to give a low residual chlorine level at the far end of the line, might use 1-2 litres over a 42-day cycle, so $1.50-$3.00. A pool-shop chlorine test kit costs about $12 and lasts years. Against that, a water-borne E. coli challenge that raises mortality by 3% in 1,000 birds costs 30 birds at $4.00 each, or $120.

A Zambian example: a Chongwe layer unit had a stubborn 4% drop in lay and thin shells. Feed analysis was fine. The roof tank had never been cleaned and held a thick sludge and dead lizard; iron staining was visible in every drinker. Draining and scrubbing the tank, fitting a simple filter and flushing the lines returned lay to standard in three weeks at a cost of under $30.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Why do Ross 2022 vitamin E levels far exceed NRC 1994 levels?

NRC 1994 ranges describe the point below which clinical deficiency appears. Breeder specifications like Ross 2022 describe what a modern high-yield bird needs to perform and resist challenge, which is considerably higher.

2. Deep intestinal crypts and short villi indicate what?

Deep crypts mean rapid epithelial turnover - the bird is constantly repairing damage. That energy comes out of growth, and absorptive surface is reduced.

3. What is the recommended pairing ratio of vitamin E to selenium?

Published guidance pairs roughly 10-20 IU of vitamin E with each 0.3 mg of selenium, because both act in the same glutathione peroxidase antioxidant system and work better combined than alone.

4. A 500-bird broiler flock loses 40 birds. At $0.80 per chick plus about $0.83 of feed already consumed per dead bird, what is the approximate direct input loss?

40 birds x ($0.80 chick + $0.83 feed) = 40 x $1.63 = about $65 in direct inputs, before counting the sale revenue never earned.

5. Which nutrient most directly maintains the epithelial lining of the gut and airways?

Vitamin A maintains epithelial tissue, which is the bird's physical barrier in the gut, respiratory tract and eyes. Ross 2022 specifies 13,000 IU/kg in starter feed.

6. What is the mode of action of monensin, lasalocid and salinomycin?

All three are polyether ionophores. They shuttle sodium, potassium and hydrogen ions across the Eimeria cell membrane, destroying its osmotic balance and causing vacuolisation and cell death.

7. Which coccidiostat is a thiamine antagonist?

Amprolium, in use since the early 1960s, blocks thiamine-dependent Eimeria metabolism rather than lysing parasite cells directly.

8. Why is it an advantage that ionophores act early in the parasite life cycle?

Because ionophores act in the initial phase, a small parasite load still develops and stimulates immunity - valuable for birds kept beyond six weeks and essential for layer pullets.

9. You plan to slaughter on day 42. When must the clean, coccidiostat-free feed start?

The seven-day minimum withdrawal counted back from day 42 means the coccidiostat-free feed must be the only feed the birds receive from day 35 onward.

10. 1,000 broilers eat 3,200 kg of medicated feed. A premix at $4.00/kg is included at 0.5 kg per tonne. What is the coccidiostat cost for the flock?

3,200 kg x 0.0005 = 1.6 kg of premix. 1.6 kg x $4.00 = $6.40 for the entire flock, under one cent per bird.

11. What feeds a Clostridium perfringens bloom in the lower gut?

Protein the bird cannot digest and absorb passes into the lower gut where Clostridium uses it as substrate. Formulating to digestible amino acids rather than overshooting crude protein reduces this.

12. Which mycotoxin was shown to predispose broilers to necrotic enteritis even below its nominal safe limit?

At 18.6 ppm - below the 20 ppm broiler threshold - fumonisins altered intestinal microflora and predisposed broilers to necrotic enteritis and digestive upsets.

13. What is the published FCR improvement range for organic acids in broilers?

Published efficacy shows a 2-8% FCR improvement depending on acid type and gut health status, with larger gains where sanitation is poor.

14. 1,000 broilers eat 3,800 kg of feed. An organic acid blend included at 0.5% costs $2.20/kg. What is the total cost?

3,800 kg x 0.5% = 19 kg of acid blend. 19 kg x $2.20 = $41.80 for the flock, about 4.2 US cents per bird.

15. Which enzyme type gives the largest energy response, and on which grains?

NSP-degrading enzyme complexes give 96-265 kcal/kg of extra energy, with the largest responses on wheat (arabinoxylans) and barley or oats (beta-glucans), and only modest gains on maize.

16. A diet contains Na 0.20%, K 0.85%, Cl 0.22%. What is its DEB?

(0.20 x 435) + (0.85 x 256) - (0.22 x 282) = 87.0 + 217.6 - 62.0 = 242.6 mEq/kg, which is adequate for cool conditions but low for a hot afternoon.

17. Why does excess dietary chloride harm birds in heat?

Excess chloride raises blood chloride and drives metabolic acidosis, lowering blood pH. The bird pants excessively to compensate, which reduces growth and raises mortality.

18. Which sodium source raises DEB the most?

Sodium bicarbonate supplies sodium without chloride, so it raises the (Na + K) side of the equation without raising the chloride subtraction. Common salt raises both and barely shifts DEB.

19. What is the recommended DEB range under severe heat stress above 35 degrees?

Severe heat stress above 35 degrees calls for 350-400 mEq/kg, achieved by maximising sodium and potassium while minimising chloride.

20. Why should feeding be shifted to early morning and evening in hot weather?

The heat increment of digestion adds to the bird's heat load. Emptying feeders through the hottest hours and feeding early and late costs nothing and protects intake and survival.

21. Why does Ross 2022 restrict broiler starter calcium to 0.95%?

Early calcium above 1.0% interferes with phosphorus metabolism and predisposes broilers to tibial dyschondroplasia and angular limb deformities. Calcium is raised gradually after the starter phase.

22. Which Ca to available P ratio is outside the safe window?

Ratios below 1.5:1 or above 2.5:1 increase skeletal problems and malabsorption. 2.9:1, typical of over-liming a mix, is well outside the window.

23. By how much does phytic acid reduce phosphorus bioavailability?

Phytic acid chelates minerals and reduces phosphorus bioavailability by 20-50%, and zinc by 30-60%, which is why phytase supplementation pays for itself.

24. Roughly how much more biologically active is 25-OH vitamin D3 than D3 on a weight basis?

25-OH D3 bypasses hepatic 25-hydroxylation, is absorbed more efficiently especially in young birds, and is about 4-5 times more biologically active than D3 on a weight basis.

25. Phytase costs $1.20 per tonne and lets you cut 5 kg of dicalcium phosphate at $0.90/kg. What is the net saving per tonne?

5 kg x $0.90 = $4.50 saved on DCP, minus $1.20 for the phytase, gives a net saving of $3.30 per tonne plus improved mineral availability.

26. What is the recommended upper limit for total dissolved solids in broiler drinking water?

TDS below 1,500 ppm is recommended, with above 3,000 ppm treated as a concern level. High TDS is common in arid-area boreholes and causes wet droppings.

27. Why can water above pH 8.5 make a vaccination appear to fail?

Highly alkaline water inactivates many water-soluble products, so the vaccine or medicine never reaches the bird in an active form even though the dosing was done correctly.

28. Where should you take a water sample to detect real problems?

The far end of the line is where biofilm, sediment and low chlorine residual show up. A clean sample at the tank tells you nothing about what the birds actually drink.

29. A sudden 20-30% drop in water intake usually means what?

Water intake typically falls a day before feed intake and about two days before visible sickness, making it the single best free early-warning indicator available to a small farm.

30. What is biofilm?

Biofilm is the slime layer inside water lines where bacteria live sheltered from disinfectant. It builds fastest when vitamins, sugars or medicines are dosed through lines that are never flushed.

Module 9 capstone

Run a four-week nutrition-health audit on your own flock. Week 1: record daily mortality, cull reasons, droppings appearance and feed intake per 100 birds, and photograph any lame birds or wet litter patches. Week 2: collect your current feed label or mix sheet and compare its crude protein, calcium, available phosphorus, salt and coccidiostat inclusion against the Ross 2022 or NRC 1994 figures in this module, writing down every gap. Week 3: test your drinking water at the farthest drinker - measure pH with strips from a pharmacy or pool shop, check for iron staining and slime, and record water intake per 100 birds on your hottest and coolest days. Week 4: cost out the three highest-value corrections you found (for example adding coarse limestone, correcting salt, cleaning the water line, adding a vitamin-electrolyte pack for hot afternoons) in USD per 1,000 birds, and write a one-page plan showing the expected mortality or feed-conversion improvement needed to pay each one back. Present the plan with your actual mortality figures, not example figures.
