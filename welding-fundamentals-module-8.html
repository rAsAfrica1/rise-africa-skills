1. Basic Blueprint Lines and Views

A blueprint is a drawing that shows how a metal part should be made. In welding, you must read blueprints to know where to weld, what size weld, and what type. The first thing to learn is the lines. A visible line is a thick solid line that shows the outline of the part. A hidden line is a dashed line that shows edges you cannot see from the front. A centerline is a thin line with long and short dashes, used to mark the center of holes or round parts. A dimension line has arrows at both ends and shows the size, like 50 mm. An extension line is a thin line that extends from the part to the dimension line. A break line is a wavy or zigzag line that shows where a part is broken to save space on the drawing.

Blueprints also show different views. The most common is the front view, which shows the part as you look at it from the front. The top view shows the part from above, and the side view shows it from the side. In Africa, many workshops use third-angle projection, where the top view is above the front view and the side view is to the right. But some countries use first-angle projection, where the top view is below the front view. Always check the projection symbol on the drawing. It looks like a small cone or funnel. If the wide end is on the left, it is third-angle. If the wide end is on the right, it is first-angle.

When you look at a blueprint, start by finding the title block. It is usually in the bottom right corner. It tells you the part name, material, scale, and date. The scale might be 1:1, meaning the drawing is the same size as the part. Or 1:2, meaning the drawing is half the size. Use a ruler to measure dimensions if they are not written. But always trust the written dimensions over measuring.

Practice by getting a simple bracket drawing from a local technical college or online. Trace the lines with a pencil and label each type. Then look at the views and imagine the 3D shape. In many African welding shops, blueprints are printed on A3 paper. Keep them clean and dry. If you cannot read blueprints, you will waste metal and time. So spend time learning these basics.

2. Welding Symbol Components

A welding symbol is a drawing note that tells the welder what weld to make. It has several parts. The horizontal line is called the reference line. Everything else attaches to it. At the left end, there is an arrow that points to the joint. The arrow can have a break, called an arrow-side or other-side designation. If the symbol is below the reference line, the weld is on the arrow side. If it is above, the weld is on the other side. If it is on both sides, you weld both sides.

On the reference line, you may see a tail. The tail is where additional information goes, like a welding process (e.g., SMAW for stick welding) or a specification. If there is no tail, it means no special notes. The tail can also have a circle, which means weld all around. A flag at the junction of the arrow and reference line means field weld, meaning weld on site, not in the shop.

The most important part is the weld symbol itself. It sits on the reference line. For a fillet weld, the symbol is a right triangle. For a groove weld, it is a shape like a V, U, or square. The size of the weld is written to the left of the symbol. For a fillet weld, the size is the leg length. For example, a 6 mm fillet weld has a 6 mm leg. For a groove weld, the size is the depth of penetration or the groove size.

Length and pitch are written to the right of the symbol. Length is how long the weld is. Pitch is the distance between centers of welds if you have intermittent welds. For example, 50-100 means a 50 mm long weld every 100 mm. If there is no length, the weld is continuous.

Other components include the angle of countersink, the root opening, and the groove angle. These are written near the symbol. Always read from left to right. In African workshops, symbols are often drawn by hand on a chalkboard or paper. Practice drawing each component. Get a welding symbol chart from a local welding supplier or download one. Memorize the common ones: fillet, V-groove, square groove, and plug weld. When you see a symbol, say out loud what it means. This will help you remember.

3. Types of Welds and Joints

There are many types of welds, but in African welding shops, you will mostly use four: fillet, groove, plug, and spot. A fillet weld is a triangular weld that joins two surfaces at an angle, like a T-joint or a lap joint. It is the most common and cheapest. A groove weld is used to join two pieces edge to edge. It can be a V-groove, U-groove, J-groove, or square groove. Groove welds are stronger but require more preparation. A plug weld is a round weld made in a hole in one plate to join it to another. A spot weld is like a plug weld but smaller and usually made with a resistance welder.

Joints are how the metal pieces fit together. The five basic joints are butt, corner, T, lap, and edge. A butt joint is when two pieces are placed end to end. A corner joint is when they meet at a corner, like an L. A T-joint is when one piece sits on top of another, forming a T. A lap joint is when one piece overlaps another. An edge joint is when two pieces are placed side by side and welded along the edge.

Each joint can have different welds. For example, a T-joint usually uses a fillet weld on one or both sides. A butt joint can use a square groove weld for thin metal or a V-groove weld for thick metal. In Africa, you often work with mild steel plates from 3 mm to 12 mm thick. For 3 mm, a square groove with a 2 mm root gap works well. For 6 mm, a single V-groove with a 60-degree included angle is common. For 12 mm, you might need a double V-groove to reduce distortion.

When you read a blueprint, first identify the joint type. Then look at the weld symbol to see what weld to make. The symbol will tell you the size, length, and type. For example, a fillet weld symbol on a T-joint means you weld along the corner. If the symbol has a tail with 'SMAW', you know to use stick welding. Practice by making sample joints from scrap metal. Measure and mark them, then weld. Compare your weld to the symbol. In many African vocational schools, you will be given a drawing and asked to fabricate the part. Knowing joints and welds is key to passing.

4. Interpreting Size and Length Dimensions

Size and length are the two most important dimensions on a welding symbol. Size tells you how big the weld should be. Length tells you how long it should be. For a fillet weld, the size is the leg length. It is written to the left of the fillet symbol. For example, a symbol with '6' on the left means a 6 mm fillet weld. The leg length is measured on the longest leg. If the weld is on both sides, the size is still the leg length for each weld. If the symbol has a size on both sides of the reference line, you may have different sizes for each side.

For a groove weld, the size is the depth of the groove or the required penetration. For a V-groove, the size is the depth of the V. It is written to the left of the groove symbol. For example, a '6' on a V-groove symbol means a 6 mm deep V-groove. If there is no size, the weld must penetrate the full thickness of the material. That is called a complete joint penetration (CJP) weld. For partial joint penetration (PJP), the size is given.

Length is written to the right of the symbol. If there is no length, the weld is continuous along the whole joint. If a length is given, the weld is intermittent. For example, '50-200' means a 50 mm long weld every 200 mm. The first number is the length, the second is the pitch (center-to-center distance). If there are three numbers, like '50-200-3', the third number is the number of welds. Sometimes you see a length with a dash and another number, like '50-100', which means 50 mm long welds with 100 mm pitch. The pitch includes the weld length and the gap. So if you have a 50 mm weld and 100 mm pitch, the gap between welds is 50 mm.

In African workshops, you often use a tape measure and a soapstone or chalk to mark lengths. For a 6 mm fillet weld, you need to set your welding machine to about 120-140 amps for stick welding with a 3.2 mm electrode. For a 50 mm long weld, you start at one mark and end at the other. If the symbol says 'weld all around', you weld the entire joint. Always check the drawing for any notes about length. If you are unsure, ask your supervisor. Measuring wrong can cause weak joints and wasted material.

5. Supplementary Symbols and Notes

Supplementary symbols give extra information about the weld. They are small marks added to the basic welding symbol. The most common is the all-around symbol, which is a circle at the junction of the arrow and reference line. It means you weld all around the joint. For example, if you are welding a pipe to a plate, the all-around symbol means you weld the entire circumference. Another common symbol is the field weld symbol, which is a flag. It means the weld is made at the site, not in the shop. In Africa, many construction projects use field welds for on-site assembly.

The melt-through symbol is a filled-in circle between the arrow and the reference line. It means you must get complete penetration, often used for root passes. The consumable insert symbol is a rectangle with a line through it, placed on the reference line. It means you use a backing material that becomes part of the weld. The backing symbol is a rectangle on the other side of the reference line, meaning you use a backing strip that does not melt.

The tail of the welding symbol is where you put notes. For example, you might write 'SMAW' for shielded metal arc welding, or 'GMAW' for gas metal arc welding. You might also write a specification number, like AWS D1.1. In Africa, many workshops follow AWS or ISO standards. If you see 'ISO 5817', that is a quality standard for welds. The tail can also have a process number, like '111' for stick welding, '135' for MIG welding, or '141' for TIG welding.

Other notes might be written directly on the drawing, like 'grind flush' or 'remove slag'. These are not symbols but important instructions. Always read all notes on the drawing. Sometimes a note says 'weld size per detail A', so you must find detail A. In many African vocational exams, you will be given a drawing with symbols and notes. You must interpret them correctly. Practice by making a chart of all supplementary symbols and their meanings. Then look at real drawings from local fabricators. Ask them what each symbol means. The more you practice, the faster you will read them.

6. Applying Symbols to Fabrication

Now you have learned the parts of a welding symbol, types of welds and joints, and how to read size and length. The final step is to apply this to real fabrication. In an African welding shop, you might get a blueprint for a gate, a trailer, or a water tank stand. You need to turn that drawing into a finished product. Start by studying the entire drawing. Identify all the parts, their dimensions, and the welding symbols. Make a list of all welds: type, size, length, and process. For example, you might have ten 6 mm fillet welds, 50 mm long, on a T-joint, using stick welding.

Next, gather your materials. Cut the metal to size using a hacksaw, angle grinder, or plasma cutter. Mark the weld locations with a soapstone or center punch. Set up the joint with clamps or tack welds. Check the alignment with a square and tape measure. Then weld according to the symbols. If the symbol says 'weld all around', you weld the entire joint. If it says '50-200', you make 50 mm welds every 200 mm. If it says 'field weld', you leave it for site assembly.

After welding, inspect your work. Use a wire brush to remove slag. Check the weld size with a fillet weld gauge. A fillet weld gauge is a small tool that measures leg length. You can buy one from a welding supplier for about 100 rand or 5000 shillings. If the weld is too small, you must add more metal. If it is too large, you waste material and may cause distortion. Check the length with a tape measure. If the drawing says 'grind flush', use an angle grinder to make the weld smooth.

In many African countries, welding is a key skill for construction and repair. By reading symbols correctly, you can work from any drawing and produce quality work. Practice by taking a simple drawing, like a bracket, and fabricating it. Then compare your work to the drawing. Ask a more experienced welder to check your interpretation. Over time, you will be able to read symbols quickly and weld accurately. Remember, a good welder is not just someone who can run a bead, but someone who can read a drawing and make the part right the first time.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Reading Welding Symbols and Blueprints on the Shop Floor

In a Nairobi fabrication yard, a welder who can read a drawing correctly the first time saves the workshop money. Every misread symbol means rework, wasted electrodes, lost gas, and a delayed delivery to a client. In African metal fabrication from small jobbing shops in Kumasi and Gaborone to structural steel contractors in Lagos and Johannesburg the blueprint is the contract between the designer and the person holding the electrode. This Practical Reference lesson pulls the six earlier lessons together into a single, field-ready guide you can pin above the bench or share with apprentices.

The business case is simple: a welder who interprets a drawing without asking the supervisor three times per job completes more work per shift, produces fewer rejects, and allows the workshop to quote tighter margins on tenders. For a small enterprise bidding for a school roof truss, a water tank stand, or a gate frame, that margin is often the difference between winning and losing the contract.

1. The Blueprint Lines You Meet Every Day

Before you read a weld symbol you must read the drawing. Object lines show the visible edge of the part. Hidden lines (dashed) show edges you cannot see from that view. Centre lines locate holes and symmetry. Dimension lines carry the numbers that matter thickness, length, hole pitch. In African workshops, drawings are often printed on A3 or A4 and photocopied until the lines blur, so learn to confirm scale from a known dimension rather than trusting the print.

2. Views and Projection

Most structural and fabrication drawings use either first-angle (common in former British colonies: Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, South Africa, Zambia) or third-angle (common in North America and some imported equipment). The symbol on the title block tells you which. If you misread projection, you will fabricate a mirror-image bracket a costly mistake when steel is already cut.

3. Welding Symbol Components

Every symbol has four core parts: the arrow (points to the joint), the reference line (horizontal), the tail (process or specification), and the weld symbol itself (fillet triangle, V-groove, square butt, etc.). Information above the reference line applies to the arrow side; below applies to the other side. This is the single most important convention to memorise.

4. Types of Welds and Joints

Common joints: butt, lap, T, corner, edge. Common welds: fillet, square butt, V-groove, U-groove, plug, spot, seam. In African light fabrication, fillet welds dominate gates, trusses, tank stands, trailers. Groove welds appear on pressure vessels and structural columns where full penetration is specified.

5. Size and Length Dimensions

A number to the left of the weld symbol is the size (leg length for fillets, groove depth for groove welds). A number to the right is the length of the weld. A dash and second number (e.g. 50150) means intermittent welds: 50 mm weld, 150 mm pitch. A number in brackets is the pitch. Getting these wrong on a trailer chassis means either a weak joint or a distorted frame.

6. Supplementary Symbols and Notes

Supplementary symbols refine the instruction: all-around (circle at the junction of arrow and reference line), field weld (flag), backing or spacer (rectangle), flush or convex contour (straight or curved line), and melt-through. Notes in the tail or in a general notes block specify electrode classification (e.g. E7018), process (SMAW, GMAW, TIG), preheat, and inspection method. In African workshops supplying to mining or construction clients, these notes often reference SANS, BS EN or AWS codes read them before striking an arc.

SAFETY WARNING Read the drawing, then the safety data.

Never weld from a symbol you have not fully understood. Misread preheat or electrode notes on high-strength or thick steel can cause catastrophic joint failure. Before welding: confirm the drawing revision, check that the material matches the specification, ensure adequate ventilation (especially for galvanised or painted steel common in African salvage yards), wear a welding helmet with correct shade, leather gloves and apron, and keep a fire extinguisher within reach. In confined spaces such as tanks or trailers, use a trained spotter and forced ventilation. Arc flash, fumes and fire are the three biggest risks on any African fabrication floor.

7. Applying Symbols to Fabrication

Practical workflow on the shop floor:

  1. Read the title block: projection angle, scale, revision, material spec.
  2. Identify the joint on the drawing and match it to the physical part.
  3. Decode the symbol: arrow side or other side, weld type, size, length, process.
  4. Mark the part with soapstone or a marker before welding.
  5. Tack, check dimensions against the drawing, then complete the weld.
  6. Inspect against the symbol leg size, length, contour before signing off.

For a small enterprise, this discipline is what allows you to take on bigger clients. A workshop that can demonstrate it welds to drawing will win contracts from NGOs building water tanks, from county governments building footbridges, and from private developers putting up steel-framed warehouses.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Title block checked: projection angle, scale, revision, material.
  • Arrow side vs other side identified before any weld is laid.
  • Weld type confirmed (fillet, groove, plug, spot, seam).
  • Size and length read from correct side of the symbol.
  • Intermittent weld pitch and number of welds verified.
  • Supplementary symbols (all-around, field, backing) noted.
  • Tail notes: process, electrode, preheat, inspection.
  • Safety: ventilation, PPE, fire watch, confined-space plan in place.
