WELDING FUNDAMENTALS · MODULE 8 OF 12

The Joint Types

Five joints do all the work: butt, lap, tee, corner and edge. What each is for, how to fit it up, and which to choose.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Name the five basic joint types and describe each
  • Explain what a butt joint is for and how to prepare it correctly
  • Explain the lap and tee joints and the fusion challenge each presents
  • Describe corner and edge joints and where each is used
  • Set up fit-up and gap correctly for each joint type
  • Choose the right joint for a given job, considering strength and access
Lesson 8.1

The five joints

Every weld you make is one of five basic joints, or a combination of them. Learn the five and you have learned the geometry of the trade.

JointWhat it isTypical use
ButtTwo pieces in the same plane, edges facing each otherJoining plate to make a longer or wider piece; pipe lengths
LapTwo overlapping piecesSheet metal, thin sections, where the back side is not critical
TeeOne piece at right angles to the middle of anotherBrackets, stiffeners, braces onto a plate or beam
CornerTwo pieces meeting at a cornerBox sections, angle-iron frames, tank corners
EdgeTwo pieces joined along their edgesSheet metal, edge build-up, joining thin sheet

Which joint you choose is not arbitrary. Each has advantages and problems, and the choice often determines whether the job is easy or impossible.

Lesson 8.2

Butt joints

The strongest joint, and the one that best uses the material's own strength. Two pieces line up edge to edge, and the weld fuses them along the joint line.

When to use a butt joint

  • Joining plate to make a longer piece
  • Joining plate to make a wider piece
  • Pipe lengths (as in pipeline work)
  • Anywhere the load must pass directly through the joint line

Preparation

Thin plate: square edge, small gap. Weld from one side.

Thick plate: bevel one or both edges so the arc can reach the root. See module 6 for bevel angles and root faces.

Welding a butt joint

  • Fit-up first. The gap must be consistent along the whole joint. A gap that varies from 2mm to 5mm produces an inconsistent bead and unpredictable penetration
  • Tack both ends and the middle, using the same consumable as the main weld. Check the alignment after tacking — the tacks may have pulled the joint
  • Weld from one end. Welding toward the middle from both ends is sometimes used for long joints to control distortion, but a straight run from one end is simpler for beginners
  • Watch for burn-through on thin material. The edges of the plate melt away quickly, and if the gap is too large or the current too high, you will make a hole

The butt joint is unforgiving of poor penetration

Because the load passes straight through the joint, a butt weld with inadequate penetration is a serious weakness. This is where cutting a test piece in half is most important — you cannot see from the surface whether the root fused.

Multi-pass butt welds on thick plate

On plate thicker than about 8mm, a single pass will not fill the joint. The weld is made in layers:

  1. Root pass — the first pass, in the bottom of the bevel. It must fuse both edges
  2. Fill passes — subsequent passes filling the bevel
  3. Cap pass — the final pass, forming the visible face of the weld

Each pass must be cleaned before the next. On stick, this means chipping the slag and wire brushing. Skipping the cleaning traps slag between passes — a defect covered in module 11.

Lesson 8.3

Lap and tee joints

Lap joints

Two pieces overlapped, welded along one or both edges. The weld is a fillet — a triangular cross-section formed in the angle where the two plates meet.

When to use a lap joint

  • Thin sheet metal where a butt joint would be difficult to fuse
  • Where the back of the joint is not visible or not important
  • Where a small amount of adjustment is needed during assembly — a lap allows the pieces to slide against each other
  • Sometimes where the edges of the plate are uneven or damaged, since the overlap hides the edge

Fusion challenge

The lap joint's weak point is the bottom of the joint — the crevice where the two plates touch. The arc must reach into that corner to fuse both plates, and if the electrode is aimed too high, only the top plate fuses to the weld. The bottom plate then holds nothing.

Direct the arc slightly more toward the bottom plate, since it is a heat sink and needs more heat. Watch the corner — the pool should visibly wet out into both plates.

Tee joints

One piece set at right angles to the surface of another. Like the lap, welded with fillet welds, usually on both sides of the upright piece.

When to use a tee joint

  • Brackets
  • Stiffeners and ribs
  • Braces attached to a plate or beam
  • Cross-members on a frame

Fusion challenge

The upright piece is a heat sink that pulls heat away from the joint, while the flat piece underneath can burn through more easily. This imbalance means the electrode angle must be adjusted: tilt slightly toward the upright plate, so more heat goes where it is needed.

The work angle of 45° between the plates is a starting point. If the vertical plate is thick and the base plate is thin, tilt more toward the vertical. If reversed, tilt the other way.

Fillet weld sizes are not in this course

The leg length or throat thickness of a fillet weld depends on the load it carries, the thickness of the plates, and the design of the joint. There is no universal figure.

For non-structural work on a farm — a bracket, a brace, a support — a fillet weld of a size similar to the plate thickness is a reasonable rule of thumb. For anything carrying real load, the size is designed, not guessed.

Lesson 8.4

Corner and edge joints

Corner joints

Two pieces meeting at a corner — most commonly a 90° corner, but sometimes at other angles. Used for box sections, frames, angle-iron assemblies, tank corners.

Three ways to weld a corner

  • Outside corner — the weld is on the outside of the corner, filling the V between the two plates. Easy access, but the inside of the joint remains open unless welded from inside as well
  • Inside corner — the weld is on the inside. Good access, good fusion at the corner, but the outside shows the seam unless finished
  • Both sides — the strongest corner, but requires welding from both sides

The critical detail

A corner welded only on the outside looks finished but is often weak. The load tries to open the corner, and the outside weld alone must resist that. Welding both sides is much stronger, and on load-bearing corners is what should be done.

Edge joints

Two pieces joined along their edges, with the surfaces roughly flush. Used mostly in sheet metal.

When to use an edge joint

  • Joining sheet metal where the surfaces must be flat
  • Building up a worn or damaged edge
  • Small assemblies where other joints will not fit

Fusion challenge

Edge joints are prone to burn-through because the material is thin and the arc is directly on the edge. Low current, fast travel, and often a backing piece (a strip of copper or steel behind the joint that supports the pool) are used.

Some edge joints are welded autogenously (no filler) — the two edges melt and flow together. This is a specialist technique but not difficult on thin sheet.

Lesson 8.5

Fit-up, gap and root face

Module 6 covered the dimensions. This lesson covers how they change per joint.

JointGapRoot face / bevelNotes
Butt, thin plate0–2mmSquare edgeGap helps the arc reach the root; too much and you burn through
Butt, thick plate2–3mmBevel + 2mm root faceBevel lets the arc reach the bottom of the joint
LapNoneNoneOverlap distance usually 3–5× plate thickness
TeeNone (upright sits on base)NoneThe upright must be square to the base
CornerDepends on preparationOften no bevel, but sometimes a small oneOutside corner: gap small, arc reaches the joint easily
Edge0mm (edges touch)NoneBoth edges fuse directly together

Tack placement

  • Butt joint: tack both ends and the middle. On long joints, tacks every 300–400mm
  • Lap joint: tack the free corners first, then along the overlapping edge
  • Tee joint: tack both ends of the upright. If the piece is long, tack the middle as well
  • Corner joint: tack both ends
  • Edge joint: tack both ends, and the middle if the pieces are long enough to move

Alignment check before you weld

Fit the joint, tack, then check alignment before starting the weld. Tacks pull the joint as they cool. A joint that was flush before tacking may be offset afterward.

If alignment has shifted, break the tacks (grind them out), realign, and re-tack. This is faster than welding a misaligned joint.

Lesson 8.6

Choosing the joint

By requirement

If you needUseBecause
Maximum strength in the joint itselfButtLoad passes straight through; weld becomes one piece with the metal
Easy fitting on thin sheetLapOverlap allows adjustment; arc reaches both plates easily
Right-angle connectionTee or cornerDepends on whether the joint is in the middle of a plate (tee) or at the edge (corner)
Flush surfacesButt or edgeNeither has a raised edge from an overlap
Adjustment during fittingLapPieces can slide before tacking

By plate thickness

  • Thin sheet (under ~3mm): lap or edge. Butt joints on thin sheet burn through easily
  • Medium (3–6mm): butt or tee. Square-edge butt with a gap works well
  • Thick (over 6mm): bevelled butt, or fillet welds (lap/tee). Multi-pass is normal

By access

Sometimes the joint choice is dictated by what you can actually reach. A joint on the inside of a closed box section cannot be welded. A joint behind a bracket cannot be seen, let alone welded.

Think about access before committing to a joint type. It is easy to design a joint that cannot be welded in position.

The trap: welding on one side only

A lap or tee joint welded on one side is often adequate. But it is not the same as one welded on both sides, and the difference matters when the joint is loaded in a way that tries to open it.

If you cannot reach both sides, design for the load to be carried by the side you can weld. Or consider a butt joint instead, if the geometry allows.

Practical assignment — Module 8

Do the practical work if you have access to a machine and supervision. Otherwise complete the observation and planning parts only.

  1. Photograph five joints — one of each type — in structures around you: gates, frames, brackets, trailers, machinery. For each, state which joint type it is, what the weld is, and whether it looks sound from outside
  2. Find one joint that has failed or is failing. Photograph it. State what type it is, describe the failure — cracking, separation, corrosion at the joint — and give one possible cause from what you have learned so far
  3. Draw the five joints to scale on paper, showing the weld cross-section for each. Label the joint name and where the weld sits
  4. Practical weld: on three pieces of scrap steel, make one butt joint (thin plate), one tee joint, and one lap joint. Photograph each. Chip any slag before photographing
  5. Cut the butt joint in half and photograph the cross-section. State whether the penetration is adequate. If not, give two changes you would make next time
  6. Design a joint for a specific small assembly you need to make — a bracket, a brace, a support, anything. Draw it. State which joint type you are using and why. Reference the tables in Lesson 8.6

Points 2 and 6 carry the most marks. Point 2 assesses whether you can apply what you have learned to real, existing welds. Point 6 assesses whether you can choose a joint on reasoning, not just on habit.

Module 8 knowledge check

1. Which joint type is generally the strongest, and why?

  1. Lap — the overlap gives extra material
  2. Butt — the load passes directly through the joint, and a proper weld becomes one continuous piece with the parent metal
  3. Tee — the perpendicular geometry resists bending
  4. Corner — the angle adds stiffness
Show answer

b. In a butt joint the load path is uninterrupted when the weld is sound. A well-made butt weld can match the strength of the parent metal. Other joints concentrate load through the weld at an angle or through a fillet that is inherently less efficient.

2. What is the key fusion challenge in a tee joint?

  1. The base plate burns through easily
  2. The upright plate acts as a heat sink, pulling heat away, while the base plate underneath burns through more easily — the arc must be angled to balance the two
  3. The joint requires a bevel
  4. The weld must be autogenous
Show answer

b. The vertical plate pulls heat away from the joint, while the horizontal plate is thinner and can burn through. Tilting the electrode slightly more toward the vertical plate puts heat where it is needed and produces even fusion of both.

3. What is a common mistake in corner joints?

  1. Using too much filler
  2. Welding only the outside, which leaves the joint weak when loaded in the direction that tries to open the corner
  3. Not enough tacks
  4. Using a butt joint instead
Show answer

b. A corner welded only on the outside looks finished but is often weak. The load opens the corner, and only the outer weld resists. For any load-bearing corner, welding both sides is what should be done.

4. When would you typically choose a lap joint over a butt joint?

  1. When maximum strength is needed
  2. On thin sheet where a butt joint would burn through, and where some adjustment is useful during fitting
  3. When the joint must be flush
  4. When welding pipe
Show answer

b. The lap joint is easy to fit — the pieces slide against each other — and the arc reaches both plates easily on thin material. Its weaknesses are that it is not flush and that the bottom of the joint is harder to fuse if the electrode is aimed poorly.

5. Why is access important when choosing a joint?

  1. It affects the weld's appearance
  2. A joint you cannot reach cannot be welded, and a joint welded on one side only is weaker than one welded on both sides
  3. It affects the cost of electricity
  4. It affects the colour of the weld
Show answer

b. Design the joint for the access you actually have, not the access you wish you had. A joint designed for one-sided welding must carry its load through that single weld. Designing a joint that cannot be fully welded is a common and avoidable mistake.
