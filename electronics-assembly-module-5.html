Lessons

1. Wire Stripping and Tinning

Wire stripping is the first step in almost every wiring job. You need to remove the insulation without nicking the copper. For standard stranded wire like 22 AWG, use a wire stripper with gauge markings. Set it to 22, insert the wire, squeeze, and pull. The insulation should slide off cleanly. If you nick the copper, cut that section off and start again. For very thin wire, like 30 AWG used in earphone cables, use a thermal stripper or carefully melt the insulation with a soldering iron. In many African workshops, you'll find manual strippers with adjustable blades; test on a scrap piece first. Strip about 5-7 mm of insulation for most connections. If you're tinning, twist the strands tightly after stripping.

Tinning means coating the stripped wire with a thin layer of solder. This prevents fraying, makes soldering to connectors easier, and improves conductivity. Use a soldering iron set to 350C and rosin-core solder (60/40 tin-lead or lead-free). Clean the tip, then apply a small amount of solder to the tip. Touch the tip to the wire for one second, then feed solder into the wire (not the iron). The solder should flow and coat the strands. Don't overheat; if the insulation melts, you've held it too long. For 22 AWG, tinning takes about 2 seconds. If you're in a humid area, use a damp sponge to keep the tip clean.

In Africa, power fluctuations can affect soldering iron temperature. Use a surge protector or a gas-powered iron for field work. Practice on scrap wire until you can tin 10 wires in a row without melting insulation or leaving lumps. A good tinned wire looks shiny and smooth, with the shape of the strands still visible. If it looks dull or blobby, you used too little or too much heat. Always tin both wires before splicing them together.

2. Crimping Terminals and Connectors

Crimping is a cold-weld process that joins a wire to a terminal without solder. It's faster and more reliable in high-vibration environments like motorcycles or generators. You need a crimping tool, not pliers. For insulated terminals (the red, blue, yellow ones), use a ratcheting crimper with color-coded dies. Strip the wire to the length of the terminal's barrel, usually 5-6 mm. Insert the wire into the barrel so the copper is visible at the end, but not sticking out more than 1 mm. Place the terminal in the correct die and squeeze until the tool releases. You should see a neat indent on the insulation and a solid connection.

For uninsulated terminals, use a crimper with a concave die that rolls the barrel. Common types are ring, spade, and butt connectors. Ring terminals are for screw terminals, spade for quick-disconnect, and butt for splicing two wires. In African markets, you'll find cheap crimpers that flatten rather than crimp. Avoid those; they cut strands and create resistance. A good crimp will hold the wire so tightly that pulling it apart breaks the wire before the crimp fails. Test by pulling firmly after crimping.

If you're crimping a connector like a DB9 or RJ45, use the specific crimper for that connector. For example, RJ45 crimpers have a cycle that crimps the contacts and the strain relief. Strip the cable jacket back 10 mm, untwist the pairs, arrange the wires in the correct order (T568B: orange-white, orange, green-white, blue, blue-white, green, brown-white, brown), trim the ends straight, and insert into the connector. Crimp firmly. Always check continuity after crimping with a multimeter. In dusty environments, keep the crimper clean; dirt can cause a bad crimp.

3. Soldering Wires to Connectors

Soldering wires to connectors gives a permanent, low-resistance joint. It's common for audio jacks, power plugs, and custom circuit boards. Start by tinning both the wire and the connector pin. For the wire, strip 3-4 mm and tin it. For the connector, clean the pin with alcohol, apply flux if needed, and tin the pin with a small amount of solder. Then hold the wire against the pin with tweezers or a helping hand. Touch the soldering iron to both the wire and pin for one second, then feed a tiny bit of solder. Remove the solder first, then the iron. The joint should be shiny and concave. Don't move the wire until the solder cools (about 2 seconds).

For connectors with multiple pins, like a 3.5mm audio jack, solder the ground first, then left, then right. Use heat shrink tubing on each wire before soldering; slide it away from the joint, then after soldering, slide it over and shrink. If you're soldering to a USB connector, be careful: the pins are tiny and close together. Use a fine tip (0.5 mm) and 0.5 mm solder. A common mistake is bridging pins with excess solder. Use solder wick to remove bridges. In Africa, where replacement connectors may be scarce, practice on old connectors before working on a customer's device.

Temperature control is key. For lead-free solder, set your iron to 370C; for leaded, 350C. If the solder doesn't flow, the tip may be oxidized. Clean it with a brass sponge and re-tin. Always work in a ventilated area; solder fumes are harmful. After soldering, inspect the joint with a magnifying glass. A good joint looks like a tiny volcano. A cold joint looks dull and blobby; reheat it. Test with a multimeter for continuity and no shorts between adjacent pins.

4. Wire Harness Assembly

A wire harness is a bundle of wires organized for a specific route, like in a car or a solar panel system. Start with a schematic or a physical layout. Cut each wire to length, adding 10% extra for routing. Use different colors for different signals: red for positive, black for negative, yellow for signal, etc. In Africa, where color-coded wire may be limited, use numbered labels or tape flags. Strip and tin or crimp terminals on each end. Then lay the wires on a board or table in the final shape. Use cable ties or lacing cord to bundle them every 10-15 cm. For high-vibration, use waxed lacing cord instead of plastic ties; it won't cut into insulation.

Branching is common. To branch, you can splice with a butt connector or solder a Y-splice. For a Y-splice, strip 10 mm from the main wire and the branch, tin both, then solder the branch to the middle of the main wire. Wrap with heat shrink. Alternatively, use a crimp splice. After assembly, test each wire for continuity from end to end. Use a multimeter in continuity mode. Also test for shorts between adjacent wires. A short can cause fires. If you find a short, check for stray strands or melted insulation.

Label both ends of each wire with a unique identifier, like "A1" or "Ignition". Use a label maker or write on heat shrink with a permanent marker. In dusty or wet environments, cover the entire harness with a protective sleeve, like split loom or spiral wrap. Secure the harness with clamps every 20-30 cm to prevent chafing. For a motorcycle harness, you may need to remove the tank and seat to route it properly. Always disconnect the battery before working on a vehicle harness. After installation, test all functions: lights, horn, starter. A well-built harness lasts for years.

5. Cable Testing and Continuity Checks

Continuity testing verifies that a wire has a complete path from one end to the other. Set your multimeter to continuity mode (usually a diode symbol or a beep). Touch the probes together; you should hear a beep. If not, replace the battery or check the probes. For a cable, connect one probe to pin 1 on one end and the other probe to pin 1 on the other end. A beep means continuity. No beep means a break. For a 10-meter cable, the resistance should be less than 1 ohm. If it's higher, the wire may be corroded or too thin. Use a known good cable to compare.

For multi-conductor cables, test each conductor individually. Also test for shorts between conductors. Touch one probe to pin 1 and the other to pin 2; you should not hear a beep. If you do, there's a short. Shorts can be caused by damaged insulation, stray solder, or water ingress. In Africa, where cables may be exposed to rain or heat, shorts are common. Use a megohmmeter (insulation tester) for high-resistance shorts. For field work, a simple multimeter is enough for most checks.

If you find a break, you can locate it using a time-domain reflectometer (TDR), but that's expensive. Instead, use the half-split method: cut the cable in the middle, test both halves. The half without continuity has the break. Repeat until you find the exact spot. For a molded connector, the break is often at the strain relief. You can cut off the connector and replace it. Always test after repair. For USB cables, test data lines (D+ and D-) for continuity and also for shorts to power. A good USB cable has less than 0.5 ohms on power lines. For audio cables, test shield continuity. Document your results in a logbook.

6. Heat Shrink Tubing and Insulation

Heat shrink tubing (HST) is a plastic sleeve that shrinks when heated, providing electrical insulation and strain relief. It comes in various diameters and colors. Choose a diameter about 1.5 times the diameter of the wire or splice. For a 22 AWG wire, use 3 mm HST. For a splice, use 6 mm. Cut a piece 2-3 cm long, slide it onto the wire before soldering or crimping. After making the connection, center the HST over the joint and heat it with a heat gun on low setting. Move the gun back and forth until the tubing shrinks tightly and adhesive (if lined) flows out. Don't use a lighter; it leaves soot and can burn the tubing.

In Africa, heat guns may be expensive. You can use a soldering iron held close (but not touching) to shrink small pieces, or a hair dryer on high setting for larger pieces. For field repairs, a butane torch with a diffuser works. Always shrink from the center outward to avoid air bubbles. If the tubing splits, you overheated it. If it doesn't shrink, it's not heat shrink (could be regular tubing). Test on a scrap piece first.

For insulation, you can also use electrical tape, but it's not as durable. Use self-amalgamating tape for wet environments; it fuses to itself. For high-temperature areas, use fiberglass sleeving. When repairing a cable, always replace the outer jacket with a new piece of HST or tape. For a professional look, use clear HST over labels. After shrinking, inspect the joint: the tubing should be smooth and tight, with no exposed wire. Test continuity again. If you're working on a boat or solar installation, use marine-grade HST with adhesive lining to prevent water ingress. Store HST in a cool, dark place; sunlight degrades it over time.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Wire and Cable Assembly for African Electronics Enterprises

Wire and cable assembly is the backbone of every electronics product that leaves a workshop in Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, or Kigali. Whether you are building solar charge controllers, repairing consumer appliances, assembling automotive looms, or producing IoT sensor nodes for agritech, the quality of your wire work determines whether your product survives the field or returns as a warranty claim. This practical reference consolidates the essential techniques, standards, and business considerations for wire stripping, tinning, crimping, soldering, harness assembly, testing, and insulation with an eye on the African market realities of component sourcing, humidity, temperature cycling, and informal-sector competition.

In the African context, wire assembly skills directly translate into revenue. A technician who can reliably crimp and solder can command higher rates, reduce rework, and win contracts from solar installers, telecom tower maintenance firms, and appliance repair chains. The techniques below are drawn from internationally recognised standards (IPC/WHMA-A-620, IPC J-STD-001, NASA-STD-8739.4) and adapted for the tools and materials commonly available across the continent.

1. Wire Stripping and Tinning

Stripping removes insulation without nicking the conductor. For stranded wire, use a strip length matching the terminal or connector specification typically 46 mm for crimp terminals, 35 mm for PCB pads. In humid African climates, avoid stripping more than necessary; exposed copper oxidises within hours. Tinning (coating the stripped end with solder) prevents strand fraying and oxidation, and improves solder joint quality. Use rosin-core solder (Sn60/Pb40 or lead-free SAC305), a 3060 W iron at 350370 C, and apply heat to the wire, then feed solder never the reverse. Keep the iron tip clean and tinned.

2. Crimping Terminals and Connectors

Crimping is the preferred method for terminating stranded wire in production environments because it is fast, repeatable, and gas-tight when done correctly. Use the correct crimp tool and terminal for the wire gauge a mismatch produces either a loose joint (high resistance, heat) or a crushed conductor (mechanical weakness). The insulation crimp must grip the insulation, not the conductor. In African markets, quality crimp tools (e.g., ratchet-type) are available through distributors in Lagos, Nairobi, and Johannesburg; avoid the cheapest plier-style tools, which deform terminals inconsistently.

3. Soldering Wires to Connectors

Soldering is appropriate for low-volume production, repairs, and connectors not designed for crimping (e.g., D-sub, DIN, some JST variants). Pre-tin both the wire and the connector pin, then bring them together with a small amount of fresh solder. The joint should be shiny, concave, and smooth a dull, blobby joint indicates cold solder or contamination. Use flux sparingly and clean residues with isopropyl alcohol. In dusty environments, cover exposed solder joints with heat shrink or conformal coating.

4. Wire Harness Assembly

A harness is a bundle of wires routed and terminated as a single unit. Build harnesses on a jig or layout board to ensure consistent lengths. Use cable ties, lacing cord, or spiral wrap at intervals of 50100 mm. Label both ends with heat-shrink markers or printed labels. For automotive and solar applications in Africa, harnesses must resist vibration, UV, and rodents use braided sleeving or conduit in exposed runs. Document each harness with a wiring diagram and continuity schedule; this is your quality record and your customer's maintenance guide.

5. Cable Testing and Continuity Checks

Every harness must be tested before delivery. Continuity testing confirms each conductor is connected end-to-end and that no shorts exist between conductors. Use a multimeter in continuity mode or a dedicated harness tester. For multi-conductor cables, test all pairs. Record results in a test log. In high-volume production, a simple jig with LEDs or a microcontroller-based tester speeds up the process and reduces human error. Insulation resistance testing (megohmmeter) is recommended for cables operating above 50 V or in wet environments.

6. Heat Shrink Tubing and Insulation

Heat shrink provides strain relief, electrical insulation, and environmental protection. Choose a shrink ratio (2:1 or 3:1) and diameter that fits over the joint and shrinks to grip the wire and connector. Use a heat gun not an open flame and apply heat evenly until the tubing conforms. In African field conditions, adhesive-lined heat shrink is preferred for outdoor and automotive applications because it seals against moisture and dust. Never rely on electrical tape alone for permanent insulation; it degrades quickly in heat and humidity.

SAFETY WARNING Soldering and Heat Shrink
  • Soldering irons reach 350400 C. Always place the iron in its stand when not in use. Never leave a hot iron unattended.
  • Solder fumes contain flux volatiles. Work in a ventilated area or use a fume extractor. In enclosed workshops, open windows and use a fan.
  • Lead-based solder (Sn63/Pb37) is toxic. Wash hands after handling, never eat or drink at the bench, and store solder away from food. Lead-free alternatives (SAC305) are safer but require higher temperatures.
  • Heat guns can ignite flammable materials. Keep away from solvents, paper, and plastic packaging. Allow heat shrink to cool before handling.
  • Use eye protection when cutting wire and trimming leads flying strands are a real hazard.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Strip length correct? Match terminal or connector spec; no nicks or crushed strands.
  2. Strands twisted and tinned? Tight twist before tinning; solder only the stripped area.
  3. Crimp tool matched to terminal? Ratchet-type tool, correct die, insulation crimp grips insulation.
  4. Solder joint shiny and concave? Heat wire and pin, feed solder, clean flux residue.
  5. Harness labelled and documented? Both ends labelled; wiring diagram and continuity schedule filed.
  6. Continuity and short tests passed? All conductors tested; results logged.
  7. Heat shrink applied correctly? Adhesive-lined for outdoor; even shrink, no scorching.
  8. Safety and PPE observed? Ventilation, eye protection, hand washing, iron in stand.
