1. Calculating Your Hourly, Daily, and Per-Unit Rates

To make money with your small Chinese machine, you must know your costs. Start by listing all fixed monthly costs: loan repayment, insurance, storage, and depreciation. For a typical Chinese 8HP diesel engine or 5kW generator, depreciation might be 10% of purchase price per year. If you bought it for $800, that's $80 per year, or about $7 per month. Add fuel, maintenance, and operator wages. Fuel consumption for a small diesel engine is roughly 0.5 liters per hour under load. At $1.20 per liter, that's $0.60 per hour. Oil and filters: change every 100 hours, costing $15, so $0.15 per hour. Operator wage: $5 per day for an 8-hour day = $0.63 per hour. Now add your profit margin. If you want 30% profit, your hourly rate = (total cost per hour) / (1 - 0.30). Suppose total cost per hour is $2.00. Then rate = $2.00 / 0.70 = $2.86 per hour. Round up to $3.00 per hour. For daily rate, multiply by 8 hours: $24. But many clients want a day rate that includes transport. Add transport cost: if you travel 20 km each way at $0.50 per km, that's $20. So daily rate = $24 + $20 = $44. For per-unit rates, like per acre plowing, measure how long it takes. If your machine plows 1 acre in 2 hours, your cost is 2 x $2 = $4. Add profit and transport: $4 / 0.7 = $5.71 + $20 transport = $25.71 per acre. Always add 10% contingency for breakdowns. Keep a notebook and record every job: hours, fuel used, repairs. After one month, recalculate. Your rates must cover all costs and give you a living wage. Never price below cost, even to win a job. In rural Africa, many operators forget to include their own time. Pay yourself a salary. If you don't, you are subsidizing the client. Use a simple spreadsheet or a notebook. Review rates every three months as fuel prices change.

2. Dry Hire vs. Operated Hire

Dry hire means you rent out the machine without an operator. The client provides their own operator and fuel. Operated hire means you provide the machine, operator, and often fuel. Each has different risks and pricing. Dry hire is simpler: you hand over the machine, they return it. But you must protect yourself. Always take a deposit equal to 20% of the machine's value. For a $1,000 machine, deposit is $200. Write a contract that states the client is responsible for damage, fuel, and daily maintenance like checking oil. Charge a daily rate that covers your depreciation and a profit margin. For a small Chinese generator, dry hire might be $15 per day. Operated hire is more common in Africa because many clients don't have trained operators. You charge more because you provide labor and fuel. For a machine that costs $2 per hour to run, you might charge $6 per hour operated. That includes operator wage, fuel, and profit. But you also take on more risk: if the machine breaks, you lose income and must repair it. For operated hire, always inspect the machine before and after each job. Take photos. Keep a logbook of hours. For dry hire, do a demonstration and training session before handing over. Show the client how to start, stop, and check oil. Give them a written checklist. Many disputes happen because the client used wrong fuel or ran the machine without oil. In your contract, state that dry hire clients must use clean diesel and change oil every 50 hours. If they don't, they pay for repairs. Operated hire is better for high-value jobs like construction or agricultural plowing where precision matters. Dry hire works for simple tools like water pumps or small tillers. Choose based on your ability to supervise. If you have multiple machines, dry hire lets you scale without hiring many operators. But you must trust your clients. Start with operated hire to build reputation, then offer dry hire to reliable customers.

3. Pricing for Agricultural Services

Agriculture is the biggest market for small Chinese machines in Africa: tillers, threshers, shellers, and pumps. Pricing must match the value you deliver. Farmers care about results: acres plowed, bags threshed, liters pumped. Price per unit, not per hour. For a 7HP two-wheel tractor with a plow, you can plow 1 acre in about 3 hours in soft soil, 5 hours in hard soil. Your cost per hour: fuel $0.60, oil $0.10, depreciation $0.50, labor $0.63 = $1.83. Add 30% profit: $1.83 / 0.7 = $2.61 per hour. For 4 hours average, cost is $10.44. But farmers expect a per-acre price. In many parts of Africa, custom plowing rates range from $20 to $40 per acre. If you charge $25 per acre, you make $25 - $10.44 = $14.56 profit per acre. That's good. For threshing, a small Chinese maize thresher can do 1 ton per hour. Fuel cost $0.80 per hour, labor $0.63, depreciation $0.30 = $1.73 per hour. Charge per bag: if 1 ton = 10 bags of 100kg, and you charge $3 per bag, you earn $30 per hour. Cost is $1.73, profit $28.27. That's high because threshing is fast. But you must account for setup time. Always add a transport fee: $10 to $20 depending on distance. For water pumps, charge per hour of pumping or per 1000 liters. A 2-inch pump moves about 20,000 liters per hour. Fuel cost $1.20 per hour. Charge $5 per hour operated. Farmers will pay if you guarantee water. Offer packages: plow + harrow for $40 per acre. Give discounts for large farms: 10 acres or more get 10% off. Get paid after harvest? Many farmers have no cash until harvest. You can offer credit but charge 10% more. Or take a portion of the harvest as payment. Always write a simple agreement with the farmer: name, date, acres, rate, total, and payment date. Use a receipt book. In dry season, demand for irrigation pumps is high. In rainy season, plowing and planting. Adjust your rates seasonally. During peak demand, charge 20% more. During off-season, do maintenance and marketing.

4. Pricing for Construction

Construction pays better than agriculture but has more competition and risk. Small Chinese machines used in construction include concrete mixers, block-making machines, vibrators, and water pumps. Pricing is usually per day or per unit (e.g., per block, per cubic meter). For a concrete mixer, a 350-liter Chinese mixer costs about $800. Depreciation over 5 years = $160 per year = $13.33 per month. If you work 20 days per month, that's $0.67 per day. Fuel or electricity: if diesel, 1 liter per day = $1.20. Labor: operator $5 per day. Maintenance: $2 per day. Total cost per day = $0.67 + $1.20 + $5 + $2 = $8.87. Add 40% profit (construction is riskier): $8.87 / 0.6 = $14.78 per day. But you can charge per cubic meter of concrete mixed. A 350L mixer produces about 0.35 m per batch, and you can do 10 batches per day = 3.5 m. If you charge $10 per m, you earn $35 per day. Cost is $8.87, profit $26.13. That's better. For block-making machines, a manual Chinese block machine makes 400 blocks per day. Cost per day: depreciation $1, labor $5, maintenance $2 = $8. Add 40% profit = $13.33 per day. If you charge $0.10 per block, you earn $40 per day. Profit $32. For construction, always charge for transport and setup. A mixer needs to be moved to site: charge $10 to $30. Also charge for water if you supply it. Contracts are essential. Get a written order with quantity, rate, and payment terms. Many contractors pay late. Ask for 50% upfront for jobs over $100. For small jobs, cash on delivery. If you work for a building contractor, they may ask for credit. Only give credit if you know them. Use a simple invoice book. Include your name, date, description, quantity, rate, total, and payment due date. For large projects, you can rent your machine with operator to the main contractor on a monthly basis. A fair monthly rate for a small mixer with operator is $300 to $500. That covers all costs and profit. Always inspect your machine after each job. Construction sites are dusty; clean air filters daily. Keep spare parts: belts, filters, spark plugs. Price your services to cover repairs. Never undercut yourself just to get a job. There is always another job.

5. Contracts and Payment Terms

A contract protects you and your client. It doesn't have to be complicated. For small jobs, a one-page agreement is enough. Include: your name and business, client's name, date, description of work, machine used, rate, total amount, payment terms, and signatures. For example: 'I, John Mwangi, will plow 5 acres for Mr. Peter Ochieng at $25 per acre, total $125. Payment: $50 deposit now, $75 on completion. Date: 1 June 2025.' Both sign and keep a copy. For larger jobs, add: fuel responsibility, operator provided, working hours, breakdown policy, and late payment penalty. Payment terms are critical. Common terms in Africa: cash on delivery, 50% deposit, or net 7 days. For new clients, always ask for a deposit. 30% to 50% is standard. For dry hire, deposit equal to 20% of machine value plus one day's rent. For operated hire, 50% upfront for jobs over $100. If you don't get a deposit, you risk not being paid. For agricultural services, many farmers want to pay after harvest. You can agree, but charge 10% more and get a signed promise to pay with a date. For construction, contractors often pay after they get paid by their client. That can take months. Avoid this by working for small builders who pay weekly. Or charge a higher rate for credit. For example, if your cash price is $100, your 30-day credit price is $115. That covers your risk. Always write a receipt for every payment. Use a duplicate receipt book. Give one copy to client, keep one. For late payments, your contract should state a penalty: 5% per week or $5 per day, whichever is higher. But in practice, you must follow up. Call the client the day before payment is due. If they don't pay, visit them. Be polite but firm. If they still don't pay, you can take them to small claims court or a local elder. But prevention is better: don't work for known bad payers. Ask other operators about a client before you accept a job. Keep records of all jobs: date, client, amount, paid, balance. A simple notebook works. At the end of each month, review who owes you. Follow up immediately. Never let a debt get older than 30 days. The longer it sits, the harder to collect. For repeat clients, you can offer better terms: no deposit, net 14 days. But only after they have paid on time three times. Trust is built slowly. Always have a written contract, even for friends. It avoids misunderstandings.

6. Dealing with Late Payments and Disputes

Late payments are common in small machine hire. You must have a system. First, prevent late payments by taking a deposit and having a written contract with a clear due date and penalty. Second, send a reminder one day before payment is due. A simple SMS: 'Hello Mr. Ochieng, just reminding you that payment of $75 for plowing is due tomorrow. Thank you.' Third, if payment is late, call the client. Be calm and professional. Ask when they will pay. If they give a date, write it down and confirm by SMS. If they don't pay on that date, visit them in person. Go with your contract and receipt book. Explain that you have costs: fuel, loan, family. Ask for partial payment if they can't pay all. Something is better than nothing. If they refuse, you have options. For small amounts under $50, you can involve a local elder or chief. They often mediate and shame the debtor into paying. For larger amounts, you can go to small claims court. In many African countries, small claims courts handle cases up to $500 or $1000 quickly and cheaply. You don't need a lawyer. Bring your contract, receipts, and any SMS messages. The judge will order payment. If the client still doesn't pay, you can get a court order to seize their property. But that is a last resort. Disputes also happen over machine damage or poor work. For example, a client claims you broke their fence while plowing. Always inspect the site before and after. Take photos with your phone. If a dispute arises, stay calm. Listen to the client. If you are wrong, fix it or offer a discount. If you are right, show your photos and contract. If you can't agree, suggest a mediator. Never get into a fight. Your reputation is your biggest asset. In Africa, word travels fast. A good reputation brings more jobs. A bad one kills your business. So treat clients fairly, but don't let them cheat you. Keep a record of every dispute and how you resolved it. Learn from it. For repeat late payers, stop offering credit. Demand cash upfront. It's better to lose a job than to work for free. Finally, build a network of other machine owners. Share information about bad clients. Together you can protect yourselves. If a client owes you, they probably owe others. A united front works. Always be professional, even when frustrated.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Pricing, Hiring, and Service Models for Small Chinese Machines

This lesson is your working reference for turning a small Chinese machine a power tiller, water pump, concrete mixer, block-making machine, maize sheller, or mini excavator into a predictable business. The numbers below are illustrative but built on real African market conditions. Replace them with your own local costs before you quote a client.

Across Sub-Saharan Africa, the economics of small machinery are driven by three realities: high import and spare-part costs, fuel and lubricant prices that move with global markets, and clients who pay in cash but often late. Your pricing must absorb all three. If you price only to cover the machine, you will subsidise the client with your own labour and risk.

1. Calculating Your Hourly, Daily, and Per-Unit Rates

Start with your true cost per hour. Do not guess. Use a simple sheet and review it every quarter, because fuel and spare parts move.

Hourly rate formula: (Fuel + Lubricants + Maintenance reserve + Depreciation + Operator wage + Transport + Overheads) productive hours, then add your profit margin (1025%).

Example: a 12 HP diesel power tiller bought for KES 180,000. Assume a 5-year life and 800 productive hours per year. Depreciation = 180,000 (5 800) = KES 45/hour. Fuel: 1.5 litres/hour at KES 180 = KES 270. Maintenance reserve: 8% of purchase price per year 800 = KES 18/hour. Operator: KES 300/day 6 hours = KES 50/hour. Transport: KES 500 per job 6 hours = KES 83/hour. Overheads: KES 20/hour. Total cost = KES 486/hour. Add 20% profit = KES 583/hour. Round to KES 600/hour.

Daily rate: hourly rate productive hours per day. A 6-hour productive day gives KES 3,600. Do not price a "day" as 10 hours if the machine only works 6 you will undercharge.

Per-unit rate: for shelling, milling, or block-making, divide the daily rate by realistic output. If the maize sheller processes 2 tonnes in a 6-hour day, the per-tonne rate is 3,600 2 = KES 1,800/tonne. Add a handling margin if bags, labour, or transport are included.

2. Dry Hire vs. Operated Hire

Dry hire means you rent the machine only. The client provides the operator, fuel, and daily maintenance. This suits contractors who already have trained staff. You charge a lower rate but carry less risk of operator damage. Typical dry hire is 5065% of the operated rate.

Operated hire means you supply machine, operator, fuel, and basic servicing. You charge more but control the machine. In African markets, operated hire is safer for high-value assets like mini excavators and block-making machines because untrained operators cause most breakdowns.

Use dry hire only with clients you trust and only after a signed condition checklist. Use operated hire for smallholder clients, first-time renters, and any machine with complex settings.

3. Pricing for Agricultural Services

Agriculture is seasonal and price-sensitive. Smallholders compare your rate to manual labour, not to other machines. Price per hectare or per tonne, not per hour, because farmers think in land and crop units.

For tillage, a common approach is KES 8,00012,000 per hectare for a 1220 HP tiller, depending on soil and terrain. For shelling, charge per bag or per tonne. For irrigation pumping, charge per hour of water delivered or per plot per season.

Offer a package: tillage + planting + shelling at a discount. This locks in repeat business and spreads your fixed costs across more hours. Always collect a deposit before the season starts farmers receive money at harvest, not at planting.

4. Pricing for Construction

Construction clients pay more but expect reliability, site safety, and invoicing. Price per day or per project milestone. Mini excavators, mixers, and block machines are hired by contractors who understand machine downtime costs money.

Include mobilisation and demobilisation fees. A machine that travels 40 km to site should charge for that transport. Add a standby rate (5070% of working rate) for days when the machine is on site but idle due to client delays. This protects you from weather and material shortages.

For block-making, price per block (e.g., KES 2540 per block) and include cement handling. For concrete mixing, price per cubic metre or per day. Always inspect the site before quoting rocky ground, steep slopes, and narrow access change the cost.

SAFETY WARNING: Never allow an untrained operator to run a machine you own. Rotating shafts, exposed belts, and high-pressure hydraulics on small Chinese machines cause serious injury and death. Before every hire, check guards, emergency stops, and fuel lines. Insist on closed footwear, eye protection, and no loose clothing. In dry hire, document the machine's condition with photos and a signed checklist this protects you legally and prevents disputes over damage.

5. Contracts and Payment Terms

Put every hire in writing, even for a one-day job. A simple one-page contract should state: machine model and condition, hire period, rate, deposit, fuel responsibility, operator responsibility, breakdown procedure, and late-payment penalty.

Standard terms in African markets: 50% deposit before delivery, balance within 7 days of invoice. For agricultural clients, 30% deposit and balance at harvest. For construction, 40% mobilisation, 40% mid-project, 20% on completion.

Include a late-payment clause: 25% per month or a fixed daily penalty. This is not aggressive it is normal business. Without it, clients treat your invoice as optional.

6. Dealing with Late Payments and Disputes

Prevention beats recovery. Before you release the machine, confirm the client's identity, phone number, and a physical address. For new clients, require a larger deposit or a guarantor.

If payment is late: send a written reminder on day 1 after due date, call on day 3, and visit on day 7. Keep a record of every contact. If the client disputes the amount, refer to the signed contract and the machine log sheet.

For serious disputes, use a neutral mediator a local business association, a chief, or a small-claims court. Avoid seizing property yourself; it can turn a payment dispute into a criminal case. In many African jurisdictions, a written contract with clear terms is enforceable in small-claims court.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Calculate your true hourly cost before quoting include fuel, depreciation, maintenance, operator, transport, and overheads.
  • Add a 1025% profit margin and a contingency for fuel price changes and breakdowns.
  • Price agricultural work per hectare, per tonne, or per bag not per hour.
  • Price construction work per day or per project, and always charge mobilisation fees.
  • Use operated hire for high-value or complex machines; use dry hire only with trusted clients.
  • Take a 3050% deposit before releasing the machine.
  • Use a one-page written contract with late-payment penalties.
  • Keep a machine log sheet and photo condition report for every hire.
