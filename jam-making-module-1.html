JAM MAKING · MODULE 1 OF 12

The Business of Jam

Why jam, who actually buys it, what it costs to make, and how to price a jar so the business pays you instead of the other way round.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Explain why jam is one of the most viable cottage industries in Africa today
  • Identify the six main market channels for jam and what each pays
  • Calculate the true cost of a single jar — including your own labour
  • Price a jar using the retail and wholesale multipliers
  • State the food-safety and licensing requirements before you sell
  • Keep the four records that keep you legal, profitable, and bankable
Lesson 1.1

Why jam, and who actually buys it

Jam is one of the few cottage industries in Africa that can start with almost no capital, produce a finished product in a single afternoon, and sell the same week. The equipment is a pot, a stove, jars, and a wooden spoon. The raw material is fruit that would otherwise rot on the ground.

But the reason jam works as a business is not the making. It is the market. Africa imports billions of dollars of processed fruit products every year — most of it from Europe, China, and South Africa — because local supply does not meet local demand. A clean, well-made jam from a trusted local producer can compete on freshness, on price, and above all on trust.

You are not competing with Smucker's or Robertson's. You are competing with two things: imported supermarket jam that nobody trusts to be local, and the tradition of home-made jam that most African households already know. Your customer is a woman or man who wants local, wants clean, and will pay a small premium for both.

You are not competing with imports or with home cooks. You are filling the gap between them.

Why now is a good time

Three shifts have made small-scale food production viable in Africa in a way it was not ten years ago:

  1. Consumer preference has shifted toward local. Buyers increasingly distrust imports and are willing to pay for locally-made products with a real name and a real face on the label.
  2. Social media sells. A WhatsApp catalog, a Facebook page, an Instagram grid — these are the new storefronts, and they cost nothing.
  3. Payment systems have caught up. EcoCash, M-Pesa, mobile money, and online cards let a jam maker in a small town sell to a customer in the capital without a bank account.

What jam is not

Jam is not a get-rich-quick business. The margins are real but modest — a small jar might net $1.50 to $3.00 of profit if priced correctly. To earn $500 a month you need to sell 200 to 350 jars. That is achievable in a small town, but it is not passive income. It is a real business with real hours.

What this course will not promise you

Anyone who tells you a cottage food business will make you a specific income figure without knowing your local market, your cost of fruit, and your ability to sell, is selling you something. What this course does is give you the methods — costing, pricing, hygiene, packaging, selling — so that whatever you build, you build it on real numbers and real practices, not on optimism.

Lesson 1.2

Find your market before you make your first batch

Most jam businesses fail because they made jam first and looked for buyers second. The product sat on a shelf, spoiled, and the money was lost. Reverse the order. Talk to buyers before you buy fruit.

The market conversation that matters most

Before you make a single jar for sale, walk into three different markets and have this conversation:

"I make jam locally — clean, from real fruit, no artificial anything. What would you pay for a 250ml jar, and how many could you take per month?"

Write down every answer. No editing. What they actually say changes your whole plan.

What different markets pay

In most African towns, the price ranges are roughly:

  • Farmers' market stall: $3.50 to $6.00 per 250ml jar — retail price, you keep it all
  • Corner shop: $2.00 to $3.00 per jar — the shop marks it up to $4 to $5
  • Hotel / restaurant: $2.50 to $4.00 per jar — they use it in service and want consistency, not the cheapest price
  • Online direct: $4.00 to $7.00 per jar plus delivery
  • Export: $4.00 to $8.00 per jar, but you need to be producing hundreds per month

If your local market pays less than $2.00 per jar at retail, the economics are difficult. Look for a higher-value market — usually hotels or online — before committing to jam as a business.

Lesson 1.3

Costing a jar properly

This is the lesson most first-time jam makers skip, and skipping it is the single most common reason the business fails.

The cost of a jar is not just what you paid for the fruit. It includes sugar, pectin, acid, jar, lid, label, and — critically — your own labour time. Most beginners leave out the last one and then wonder why they work for free.

A worked example: 250ml mango jam

Assume you are working in a region where fruit is cheap and labour is fair. Prices are illustrative — use your own local figures.

The $1.05 mistake

Almost every first-time jam maker looks at that table and thinks: "Materials cost $1.05, so I'll sell at $1.50 and make $0.45 profit."

That is wrong, and it is the reason small food businesses fail. You have just paid yourself $0 for 20 minutes of skilled work, ignored every failed batch, ignored the cost of the market stall, and ignored the fact that not every jar sells. At $1.50 you are losing money on every jar and you do not know it yet.

What the true cost must include

  • Ingredients at their real purchase price, not the harvest price, unless you really did grow the fruit yourself
  • Packaging that arrives intact — add 10% for jars that break in transport
  • Your labour at a fair hourly rate. Even if you do not pay yourself, count it. It is the only way to know whether the business is actually earning.
  • A per-jar share of overheads: gas or firewood, water, cleaning materials, delivery fuel
  • A per-jar share of failed batches. If one in twenty jars fails, that failure costs the other nineteen jars.

The single figure you need

At the end of every batch, divide total costs by jars produced. That is your true cost per jar. Write it down. Every pricing decision starts from that number.

Lesson 1.4

Pricing that pays you

Once you know your true cost per jar, pricing is simple — but you have to be disciplined.

The multipliers

ChannelMultiplierWhy
Retail (you sell direct)Cost × 2.5 to × 3Covers unsold jars, market fees, transport, and profit
Wholesale (shops, hotels)Cost × 1.8 to × 2.2Lower because the buyer carries the retail risk — but you sell in bulk
Export / premiumCost × 2.5 to × 4Higher price reflects certification, branding, and shipping overhead

At $2.05 true cost, that means:

  • Retail price: $5.10 to $6.15
  • Wholesale price: $3.70 to $4.50
  • Your profit at retail: $3.05 to $4.10 per jar

The three pricing mistakes that kill a business

1. Under-pricing to win customers

The customer is happy, you lose money, and you have set a price that all future work has to match. It is better to lose a sale at a fair price than to win it at a loss. There are always more customers.

2. Comparing yourself to supermarket jam

Imported jam sells at $2 to $4 because it is made in factories at industrial scale. You cannot beat that on price. You beat it on freshness, on local-ness, on being traceable to a real person. That is worth $1 to $2 extra to the right customer. Find that customer.

3. Dropping price when a buyer haggles

If your price drops the moment someone pushes back, the buyer learns that your first price was never real. Hold the price. If they will not pay it, they are not your customer.

What to do when you genuinely cannot sell at 2.5×

If your market is not paying 2.5×, you have two choices:

  1. Lower your cost — find cheaper fruit, cheaper jars, cheaper labels. Do not lower your labour.
  2. Change your market — move toward wholesale, hotels, or online sales where buyers pay more for local and clean.

What you do not do is lower the multiplier. That is the money you live on.

Lesson 1.5

Food safety, licensing and the law

Any jam sold to the public is a processed food and falls under public health rules. The exact requirements depend on your country, but the categories are the same almost everywhere.

The four categories of permission

PermissionWhat it isWho issues it
Premises registrationHome kitchens typically need a food-handling permit to produce for saleLocal environmental health / public health office
Product registrationSome countries require every food product to be registered with a standards bodyNational standards authority
Labelling complianceIngredients, net weight, best-before date, and a contact address on every jarStandards authority
Health certificateRequired for wholesale, hotels, and any form of exportHealth ministry / food safety authority

The conversation to have before you make anything

Visit your local public health or environmental health office. Ask exactly this:

"I want to sell jam made in my home kitchen. What do I need to do to be legal?"

Write down every answer. In most places this is a 30-minute conversation that saves years of problems. In some places there is no framework for home food production and the officer will tell you what to do instead. Either way, you now know.

What happens if you skip this

Two things, and both are serious. First, an inspector can close you down and condemn your stock at any time. Second — and more dangerous — if someone gets sick from your jam, you have no defence. Even if your jam was fine, without paperwork you cannot prove it. The ten dollars of registration is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.

Hygiene practices you must have from day one

  • Clean hands, clean surfaces, clean equipment. Every batch, every time.
  • Sterilised jars. Boiled for 10 minutes or heated in an oven. This is the single most important step for shelf life.
  • No re-used commercial jars with old labels — the seal is compromised and the label cannot be removed properly.
  • A dedicated space that is not where you also cut raw meat or keep pets.
  • Hair tied back, no jewellery, no eating while working. The basics. But most small failures start here.
Lesson 1.6

The four records

These four records are the difference between a hobby and a business you can grow, borrow against, and sell one day.

1. The recipe record

For every batch: what went in, in what quantities, on what date, using what fruit source. If a customer loves your guava jam from March and asks for more in September, you know exactly what recipe to use and where the fruit came from.

2. The batch number

A short code that identifies each batch. Something like JM-20260315-A — jam, 2026-03-15, batch A. Write it on every jar's label and in your records. If a jar is ever questioned, you can trace the exact batch to its recipe and its fruit source within seconds.

3. The sales record

What sold where, at what price, on what date. Simple. But it tells you which markets are your best, which products sell fastest, and what time of year demand peaks.

4. The cost record

Every purchase — every jar, every lid, every label, every kilo of sugar, every litre of fuel. This is the record that tells you whether your prices are working or whether you have been quietly losing money.

What these four records let you do

A bank, a microfinance institution, or an investor will ask for exactly these four things before lending you money to scale. A shop that wants to stock your jam will ask how long a shelf life you can prove. A customer who complains will want to know the batch number and the process. The four records answer all of those questions.

They also let you see what is actually happening. Every successful food business runs on them.

How to keep them without paperwork agony

You do not need software. A single notebook with four columns works, or a spreadsheet on a phone. What matters is that you write in it every single time, even when tired. Two years of consistent records is worth more than any single great batch of jam.

Practical assignment — Module 1

This is a research assignment. No cooking required. Before you make any jam for sale, complete these four things.

  1. Visit three potential buyers. One shop, one hotel or restaurant, one online or market seller. Ask what they would pay for a 250ml jar of clean, local jam, and how many they could take per month. Write down exactly what each says, without editing.
  2. Cost a single jar using your own local prices. Use the table in Lesson 1.3 as a template. Include your own labour at a fair hourly rate. State the true cost.
  3. Visit your local public health or environmental health office. Ask what is required to sell jam from a home kitchen. Write down what they say, including names and phone numbers.
  4. Set your retail and wholesale prices based on the true cost from point 2, using the multipliers in Lesson 1.4. State both prices and one sentence on why they are right.

Points 1 and 3 carry the most marks. Point 1 changes how you think about the business. Point 3 keeps you legal.

Show answer

c. Retail price is 2.5× to 3× true cost. That multiplier covers failed batches, unsold stock, market fees, transport, and the profit that lets you buy fruit for the next batch.

3. Which is the common hidden cost that most jam makers leave out?

  1. The fruit
  2. The label
  3. Their own labour
  4. The boiling water
Show answer

c. Labour is routinely left out of costing. If you do not count your own time, you will work for free without realising it and set prices that can never pay you.

4. What must appear on a jar label by law in most countries?

  1. Your photo
  2. Ingredients list, net weight, best-before date and contact address
  3. Your WhatsApp number only
  4. A slogan

b. These are the standard minimum labelling requirements in most food-safety frameworks. Check with your local standards authority for the exact wording and format they require.

5. Which four records should you keep for every batch?

a. Recipe, batch number, sales record and cost record. These four let you know your true margin, spot problems early, prove quality if there is a complaint, and give a lender what they will ask for if you ever want to scale.
