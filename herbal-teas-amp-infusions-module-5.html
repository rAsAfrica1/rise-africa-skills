1. Hot Infusion: Water Temperature and Steeping Times

Hot infusion is the simplest way to make herbal tea, but small changes in water temperature and steeping time change the taste and strength. For delicate leaves and flowers like mint, lemongrass, or chamomile, heat water to about 80â85Â°C. If you do not have a thermometer, boil water and let it sit for 2â3 minutes before pouring. For tougher herbs like rosemary, thyme, or dried ginger, use water just off the boil, around 95â100Â°C. Always use fresh, clean water. Bring it to a boil once, then pour.

Use 1 to 2 teaspoons of dried herbs or 2 to 3 teaspoons of fresh herbs per cup (250 ml). Put the herbs in a teapot, French press, or clean jar. Pour the hot water over them and cover immediately to trap volatile oils. Steeping times vary: delicate leaves need 3â5 minutes; tougher herbs need 5â10 minutes. For a stronger brew, add more herbs rather than steeping longer, because long steeping can bring out bitterness. Taste at 3 minutes, then every minute after.

In Africa, many households use a simple kettle and a mug with a saucer as a lid. That works. If you use a tea strainer, make sure it is fine enough to catch small particles. For a large batch, use 20 g of dried herbs per litre of water. Steep for 5 minutes, then strain into a thermos to keep hot. Do not re-boil the tea; reheating kills the flavour.

To serve, strain into cups. You can add honey, lemon, or milk, but offer plain first so customers taste the herb. For cold days, keep the teapot warm by wrapping it in a cloth. For busy markets, pre-make a concentrate: steep 40 g of herbs in 1 litre of hot water for 10 minutes, then dilute with hot water as needed. Always label the brew with the herb name and date. Practice with local herbs like hibiscus, moringa, or baobab leaves to find your perfect temperature and time.

2. Cold Infusion: Making Refreshing Iced Herbal Teas

Cold infusion, also called cold brewing, makes smooth, refreshing teas without bitterness. It is perfect for hot African afternoons. Use 2 to 3 tablespoons of dried herbs or 4 to 5 tablespoons of fresh herbs per litre of cold, clean water. Put the herbs in a clean glass jar or pitcher. Pour cold water over them, cover, and refrigerate for 4 to 8 hours, or overnight. Do not leave it out at room temperature for more than 2 hours, as bacteria can grow.

For best flavour, use herbs that are naturally sweet or fruity: hibiscus, rosehip, mint, lemongrass, or dried fruit pieces like mango or pineapple. Avoid strong bitter herbs like wormwood unless you blend them with something sweet. After steeping, strain through a fine sieve or clean cloth. Squeeze the herbs gently to get all the liquid, but do not press too hard or you will release bitterness. The tea will keep in the fridge for 3 to 5 days. Label it with the date.

To serve, pour over ice cubes made from the same tea so it does not get watery. Add fresh mint leaves, lemon slices, or a little honey syrup. For a fizzy version, top with soda water. In markets, sell cold infusion in clean bottles with a label showing ingredients and date. You can also freeze the tea into ice pops for children.

Cold infusion is safe and easy, but always use clean containers and clean hands. If you use well water, boil it first and cool it before adding herbs. For a stronger flavour, use more herbs, not longer steeping, because after 12 hours the taste can become flat. Try blending hibiscus with ginger and pineapple for a popular West African-style iced tea. Experiment with local fruits and herbs to create your own signature cold brew.

3. Decoction: Brewing Roots, Barks, and Seeds

Decoction is for hard plant parts: roots, barks, seeds, and dried berries. These need boiling to release their medicine and flavour. Use 1 to 2 tablespoons of chopped or crushed material per cup (250 ml) of water. Put the herbs in a stainless steel or enamel pot (not aluminium). Add cold water, bring to a boil, then reduce heat and simmer gently with the lid on. Simmer for 15 to 30 minutes, depending on the hardness. Roots like ginger or turmeric need 20 minutes; barks like cinnamon or African cherry need 30 minutes; seeds like fennel or coriander need 10 minutes.

Keep the lid on to trap volatile oils. If the water level drops too low, add more hot water. After simmering, remove from heat and let it steep for another 10 minutes. Strain through a fine sieve or cloth. For a more concentrated decoction, continue simmering uncovered until the liquid reduces by half. This is called a reduction. Store in a clean bottle in the fridge for up to 3 days.

In Africa, decoctions are common with herbs like moringa root, African potato, and sutherlandia. Always use dried herbs, not fresh, for barks and roots, because fresh ones can be too strong. If you use fresh ginger, no need to peel; just slice thinly. For seeds, crush them lightly with a stone or pestle before boiling. A simple tool is a clay pot or a covered saucepan. Avoid boiling for too long, as some herbs become bitter after 45 minutes.

To serve, dilute the decoction with hot water if it is too strong. You can add honey or milk. For a large batch, use 50 g of roots or barks per litre of water. Simmer for 30 minutes, then strain. Label with the herb name and date. Always test a small amount first if you are unsure about the herb's strength. Practice with local roots and barks to master your timing.

4. Using Teapots, French Presses, and Local Brewing Tools

You do not need expensive equipment to brew great herbal tea. A simple teapot, a French press, or even a clean jar and a sieve will work. A teapot is best for hot infusion because it keeps the heat in. Use a ceramic or glass teapot. Rinse it with hot water first to warm it. Add herbs, pour hot water, cover, and steep. A teapot with a built-in strainer is convenient, but a separate strainer works too. For large batches, use a thermos or a large pot with a lid.

A French press is excellent for both hot and cold infusion. Put herbs in the bottom, add water, and place the plunger on top but do not press down yet. After steeping, press the plunger slowly to separate the herbs. For cold brew, leave it in the fridge overnight, then press. Clean the French press thoroughly after each use to avoid old flavours. A simple metal or cloth strainer over a jug works well for decoctions.

In many African homes, people use a kettle, a mug, and a saucer as a lid. That is fine. For a more traditional feel, use a clay teapot or a calabash gourd for serving. If you use a clay pot, season it first by boiling water in it, then discarding the water. Never use soap on unglazed clay; rinse with hot water only. For market sales, invest in a large insulated urn to keep tea hot for hours. For cold tea, use a clean cooler with a tap.

Always keep your tools clean. Rinse with hot water after each use. For hard water stains, use a little vinegar and water. Do not use harsh chemicals. If you brew different herbs, have separate tools or wash thoroughly to avoid mixing flavours. A simple scale (0â500 g) and a measuring jug help you keep recipes consistent. With practice, you can brew perfect tea with any tool you have.

5. Adjusting Strength and Flavor to Customer Preferences

Customers have different tastes. Some like weak tea, some like it strong. Your job is to adjust quickly and consistently. The main ways to change strength are: amount of herbs, water temperature, and steeping time. For a stronger tea, increase herbs by 50% or steep 2â3 minutes longer. For weaker tea, reduce herbs or steep for less time. Do not add water to a strong brew after steeping; that makes it taste flat. Instead, start with less herb if you want weak tea.

Flavour can be adjusted with natural additions. Honey or sugar adds sweetness. Lemon or lime adds sourness. Ginger adds heat. Mint adds freshness. Milk or cream adds richness. For African customers, popular additions include honey, lemon, and sometimes milk. Always offer plain first, then suggest additions. If a customer wants a sweeter tea, add honey syrup (1 part honey to 1 part hot water) rather than dry sugar, which dissolves slowly. For a spicy tea, add a pinch of cayenne or black pepper.

Temperature also affects flavour. Hot tea releases more aroma; cold tea tastes smoother. For a customer who finds hot tea too bitter, serve it cold. For a customer who wants a comforting drink, serve it hot. You can also blend herbs to balance flavours: hibiscus for tartness, lemongrass for citrus, ginger for warmth, and mint for coolness. A good rule: 70% base herb, 20% flavour herb, 10% accent herb.

Always ask the customer what they like. Keep a notebook of their preferences. For market stalls, offer small samples so people can taste before buying. Adjust one variable at a time. If you change both herbs and steeping time, you will not know what caused the difference. Practice with a few recipes until you can make them consistently. Then you can confidently adjust for any customer.

6. Troubleshooting Common Brewing Problems

Even experienced brewers face problems. Here are common issues and how to fix them. If your tea is too bitter, you likely steeped too long or used too much herb. Next time, reduce steeping time by 2 minutes or use 1 teaspoon less per cup. For decoctions, bitterness can come from boiling too hard; simmer gently instead. If your tea is too weak, increase herbs by 1 teaspoon per cup or steep 2 minutes longer. Do not just add more water.

If your tea tastes flat or dull, the herbs may be old. Check the date. Dried herbs lose flavour after 6â12 months. Store them in airtight jars away from light and heat. Another cause is water temperature: if the water is not hot enough, the flavours will not extract. Use a thermometer or let boiled water sit for only 1â2 minutes. For cold infusion, if it tastes watery, you used too little herb or did not steep long enough. Use 3 tablespoons per litre and steep overnight.

If your tea has a slimy or cloudy texture, it may be from using too much of a mucilaginous herb like marshmallow root or okra. Reduce the amount or blend with a non-slimy herb. Cloudiness can also come from hard water; use filtered water. If your tea smells musty, the herbs are mouldy. Throw them away. Never use mouldy herbs. If your tea gives you stomach upset, you may be allergic or the herb is too strong. Stop and consult a health worker.

If your equipment is stained, clean with baking soda and hot water. If your teapot pours poorly, it may be clogged with fine particles; use a coarser strainer. For cold tea that becomes cloudy in the fridge, that is normal for some herbs like hibiscus; it is safe to drink. Always taste your brew before serving. Keep a log of what went wrong and how you fixed it. That is how you become a master brewer.

3. Decoction: Brewing Roots, Barks, and Seeds

Decoction is the traditional African method for tough plant material â ginger root, turmeric, cinnamon bark, cloves, black pepper, and hard seeds like fenugreek. These materials have thick cell walls that hot infusion cannot break down. Decoction uses sustained simmering to extract medicinal and flavour compounds.

Method: place material in a pot with cold water (1 part herb to 20 parts water for roots), bring to a boil, then reduce to a gentle simmer for 15â45 minutes depending on hardness. Cover the pot to prevent evaporation of volatile oils. Strain while hot through a fine mesh or muslin cloth. Decoctions keep 2â3 days refrigerated; discard if cloudy or off-smelling.

For commercial blends, decoct the hard materials first, then add delicate leaves off the heat for the final 3â5 minutes. This two-stage method protects aromatics while ensuring full extraction â a technique used by Kenyan and Ghanaian herbal processors to standardise quality.

4. Using Teapots, French Presses, and Local Brewing Tools

Your brewing tool affects extraction and customer experience. Choose based on volume, portability, and budget.

  • Ceramic teapot: holds heat well, ideal for table service. Pre-warm with hot water before brewing to prevent temperature drop.
  • French press: excellent for full-leaf and coarse blends; the metal mesh allows oils through. Press slowly and decant immediately to stop extraction.
  • Clay pot (traditional): used across West and North Africa; porous walls slightly cool the brew, producing a smoother, less astringent cup. Season new pots by boiling water twice before use.
  • Calabash / gourd: traditional serving vessel; not suitable for brewing, but excellent for cold infusion service and cultural presentation.
  • Stainless steel urn: best for bulk commercial brewing (50â100 L); use a calibrated thermostat and clean with food-grade descaler weekly.

Whatever tool you use, rinse with hot water before and after every brew. Residual oils from a previous batch will contaminate the next â a common cause of "off" flavour complaints.

6. Troubleshooting Common Brewing Problems

  • Bitter taste: over-steeped or water too hot. Reduce time or temperature; check your thermometer calibration.
  • Weak or watery: too little herb, too short steep, or old stock. Weigh your herb â do not eyeball.
  • Cloudy brew: fine particles passing the strainer, or hard water minerals. Use a finer mesh and filtered water.
  • Off smell or sour taste: bacterial growth from storing at room temperature. Discard and chill immediately after brewing.
  • Inconsistent batches: staff not following the recipe card. Retrain and post the recipe visibly.
  • Colour too pale: herb is stale or poorly stored. Check stock rotation â use FIFO (first in, first out).
