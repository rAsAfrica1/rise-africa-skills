1. Growth Phases and Nutritional Needs

Grow-finish pigs go from about 30 kg to 100 kg. This period is split into two phases: grower (3060 kg) and finisher (60100 kg). Each phase needs different feed. In Africa, many farmers use commercial pig feeds, but you can also mix your own using maize, soybean meal, fishmeal, and additives. For growers, feed should contain 1618% crude protein. For finishers, reduce to 1416% to avoid excess fat and save money. A typical grower diet: 60% maize, 25% soybean meal, 10% fishmeal, 3% wheat bran, 2% minerals and vitamins. Finisher diet: 70% maize, 20% soybean meal, 5% fishmeal, 3% wheat bran, 2% minerals. Always provide clean waterpigs drink 23 times their feed weight. Feed twice daily, at 7am and 4pm. Give each grower 1.52 kg per day, and each finisher 2.53 kg per day. Adjust based on body condition. If pigs are too thin, increase feed; if too fat, reduce. Never feed moldy maizeit causes aflatoxin poisoning. Store feed in dry, raised sacks. You can also supplement with kitchen waste or garden vegetables, but cook them first to kill germs. For faster growth, add lysine and methionine (amino acids) at 0.10.2% of the diet. These are available at agrovet shops. Weigh your pigs every two weeks to track progress. A well-fed pig should gain 600800 grams per day. If gain is below 500 grams, check feed quality and health. Remember, feed is 70% of your cost, so buy ingredients in bulk when prices are low. Use a simple feed mixer or mix on a clean tarpaulin. Always keep records of feed used per pig. This helps you know your cost per kg of meat. With good nutrition, you can finish pigs in 56 months instead of 810 months.

2. Monitoring Growth and Feed Conversion

To make money from pigs, you must know how well they grow and how efficiently they convert feed to meat. This is called feed conversion ratio (FCR). FCR = total feed eaten divided by total weight gained. For example, if a pig eats 200 kg of feed and gains 80 kg, FCR = 200/80 = 2.5. Good grow-finish pigs have FCR between 2.5 and 3.0. If FCR is above 3.5, you are losing money. Weigh your pigs every two weeks using a pig weighing scale or a weighing tape. A weighing tape is cheap and measures heart girth to estimate weight. For accuracy, use a digital scale if you can afford one. Record weights in a notebook or phone app. Also record feed given each day. At the end of two weeks, calculate average daily gain (ADG). ADG = weight gained / number of days. Target ADG is 700800 grams per day. If ADG is low, check for worms, disease, or poor feed quality. Deworm every 3 months with ivermectin or albendazole. Also check feed wastagerats, birds, and spillage waste feed. Use feeders that prevent spillage. Monitor water intake: if pigs suddenly drink less, they may be sick. Keep a close eye on the smallest pig in the group; it may be bullied and need separate feeding. Use a simple chart on the wall to track each pen's performance. Compare pens and learn what works. If FCR is poor, try changing feed brand or mixing your own. But always change feed gradually over 5 days to avoid digestive upset. Good monitoring helps you sell at the right time and avoid losses. It also helps you select the best pigs for breeding. Remember, what gets measured gets managed. So measure, record, and act.

3. Handling and Movement of Growing Pigs

Growing pigs can weigh 30100 kg. They are strong and can hurt you or themselves if handled roughly. Always move pigs calmly. Pigs have poor eyesight but good hearing and smell. They will follow a bucket of feed. Use a plastic sorting board or a piece of plywood to guide them. Never lift a pig by the ears, tail, or legs. To lift a small pig, hold it under the belly with one arm and support the chest with the other. For larger pigs, use a pig snare or a noose around the upper jaw. But only use a snare for short distances. When moving pigs, work in pairs. One person stands behind, the other guides from the front. Move them slowlypigs panic if rushed. Use a race or chute made of metal or wood panels. The chute should be 6070 cm wide so pigs cannot turn around. If a pig stops, don't hit it. Instead, use a rattle (a plastic bottle with stones) to make noise behind it. Pigs will move away from the noise. For loading onto a truck, build a ramp with a gentle slope (no more than 20 degrees). Put bedding on the ramp to prevent slipping. Never overload a truckgive each pig 0.5 square meters of space. In hot weather, move pigs early morning or late evening. Provide water before and after transport. If a pig falls, let it rest, then encourage it to stand. Check for injuries. Isolate any injured pig. Always wash your hands and boots after handling pigs to prevent disease spread. Keep a first aid kit with iodine, bandages, and a thermometer. Handle pigs gentlythey remember bad experiences and become difficult to move. Good handling reduces stress, which means better growth and fewer health problems.

4. Environmental Control in Grow-Finish Barns

Grow-finish pigs need a comfortable environment to grow fast. The ideal temperature is 1822C. In hot African climates, pigs can suffer heat stress. Signs include panting, lying spread out, and reduced eating. To cool pigs, provide shade, open walls for ventilation, and use fans if possible. You can also wet the floor or use sprinklers. But don't make it muddymud can cause skin problems. In cold areas, use curtains or plastic sheets to block drafts. Pigs need 0.81.0 square meters of space each. Overcrowding causes fighting, tail biting, and poor growth. For a pen of 10 finishers, you need at least 810 square meters. Flooring should be concrete for easy cleaning, but provide a dry bedding area with straw or wood shavings. Good ventilation removes ammonia and moisture. Ammonia smell means poor air qualityopen more vents. Humidity should be 5070%. If too humid, use fans and remove wet bedding. Lighting: pigs need 810 hours of light to eat well. Use natural light or low-energy bulbs. Keep noise lowloud noises stress pigs. Clean pens daily: remove wet bedding and manure. Disinfect weekly with a safe disinfectant like Virkon. Keep feed and water away from manure. Check waterers dailya pig needs 610 liters of water per day. In hot weather, they may drink double. Use nipple drinkers or bowls that don't spill. Ensure the water is clean and cool. If you see pigs shivering, add bedding. If they are panting, increase airflow. Monitor temperature with a simple thermometer. Record daily high and low. Adjust curtains and vents accordingly. A comfortable pig eats more and grows faster. This is one of the cheapest ways to improve your profit.

5. Common Health Issues in Grow-Finish

Grow-finish pigs face several health problems. The most common are worms, respiratory diseases, diarrhea, and skin conditions. Worms cause poor growth and pot-belly. Deworm every 3 months with ivermectin injection or albendazole in feed. Respiratory diseases like pneumonia cause coughing, sneezing, and labored breathing. They are spread by poor ventilation and overcrowding. Treat with antibiotics like oxytetracycline or tylosin, but only under veterinary advice. Diarrhea in growers can be caused by E. coli, salmonella, or coccidia. Signs: watery feces, dehydration, and dullness. Isolate sick pigs and give electrolytes. Use antibiotics if bacterial. For coccidia, use amprolium in water. Skin conditions: mange (mites) causes itching and crusty skin. Treat with ivermectin injection or spray. Ringworm causes round, hairless patchesuse antifungal cream. Abscesses from fighting need drainage and iodine. Tail biting is a vice caused by boredom, overcrowding, or lack of salt. Provide toys like chains or tires, and add salt to feed (0.5%). If a pig is bleeding, isolate it. Always check hooves for cracks or injuriestrim if needed. Vaccinate against swine fever (if in your area) and erysipelas. Keep a health record for each pen: date, symptoms, treatment, and outcome. Quarantine new pigs for 3 weeks before mixing. Use footbaths at barn entrances. Control flies and ratsthey spread disease. If a pig dies, bury it deep or burn it. Never sell sick pigs for meat. Call a vet earlywaiting costs money. A healthy pig grows faster and needs less feed. So prevention is cheaper than treatment. Practice good hygiene, provide balanced feed, and reduce stress. That is your best health plan.

6. Preparing Pigs for Market

When your pigs reach 90100 kg, they are ready for market. But preparation starts weeks before. First, check market requirements. Some buyers want 100 kg, others 110 kg. Weigh your pigs and sort by size. Stop feeding antibiotics at least 7 days before slaughterthis is withdrawal period. Also stop any medicated feed. Continue clean water. Reduce feed slightly 12 hours before transport to reduce vomiting, but do not starve them. Wash pigs with clean water and mild soap to remove dirt and manure. This improves presentation. Check for injuries, abscesses, or herniasthese reduce price. If a pig is sick, do not sell it. For transport, use a clean truck with bedding. Load during cool hours. Give each pig 0.5 square meters. Do not tie legs. Transport to the market or abattoir as quickly as possible. If selling live, bring a weighing scale and a clean pen. Buyers pay more for uniform, healthy pigs. Group pigs by weight and body condition. Set a fair price based on current market ratescheck with other farmers or local radio. Do not accept the first offer if it is too low. Negotiate. If you sell to a butcher, agree on price per kg live weight or carcass weight. For carcass, the dressing percentage is 7075%. So a 100 kg live pig gives 7075 kg carcass. Know this to calculate value. After sale, clean and disinfect the truck and pen. Restock only after 1 week. Keep records of sales: weight, price, buyer. This helps you plan next batch. Also, consider selling directly to consumers or restaurants for higher profit. But that requires slaughter facilities and health permits. Start small and build trust. Good preparation gets you better prices and repeat buyers.

<h2>Practical Reference: Grow-Finish Management for African Pig Enterprises</h2>

<p>In African pig production, the grow-finish phase (roughly 30 kg to 90110 kg live weight) is where feed costs, mortality and market timing decide whether a batch makes money or loses it. Feed alone represents 6575% of total production cost in most small and medium commercial units, so every decision in this phase must be judged against one question: <strong>does it increase kilograms of pork sold per kilogram of feed and per day of housing?</strong> This reference lesson consolidates the practical benchmarks, tables and checklists you need to run grow-finish pens profitably, with data drawn from FAO, CGIAR/ILRI and other official sources relevant to African conditions.</p>

<h3>1. Growth Phases and Nutritional Needs</h3>
<p>Grow-finish pigs pass through three practical sub-phases: <strong>grower</strong> (3060 kg), <strong>finisher</strong> (6090 kg) and <strong>heavy finisher/market</strong> (90110 kg). Each has different protein and energy requirements. In Africa, feed is often mixed on-farm using maize, soybean meal, sunflower cake, fishmeal, cottonseed cake, cassava and local by-products. The key is to match crude protein (CP) and lysine to the pig's growth curve, not to feed one ration throughout.</p>

<h3>2. Monitoring Growth and Feed Conversion</h3>
<p>You cannot manage what you do not measure. Weigh a sample of at least 10% of pigs every two weeks, or weigh all pigs at entry, mid-point and exit. Calculate:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Average Daily Gain (ADG)</strong> = (final weight initial weight) number of days</li>
<li><strong>Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR)</strong> = total feed consumed total weight gained</li>
<li><strong>Feed cost per kg gain</strong> = FCR cost per kg of feed</li>
</ul>
<p>Target FCR in well-managed tropical grow-finish units is 2.83.2. FCR above 3.5 signals feed waste, poor health, heat stress or unbalanced rations. In African markets where maize and soybean prices swing sharply, tracking feed cost per kg gain weekly is the single most important business indicator.</p>

<h3>3. Handling and Movement of Growing Pigs</h3>
<p>Pigs are intelligent, social and easily stressed. Rough handling causes bruising, dark firm dry (DFD) meat, reduced growth and increased disease susceptibility. Move pigs in small groups of 510, use boards or panels rather than sticks, and never lift pigs by the ears, tail or legs. Work during the coolest hours (early morning or evening) to reduce heat stress. Provide non-slip flooring in alleys and loading ramps. In African markets where pigs are often transported long distances on poor roads, plan rest stops, ventilation and water access transport stress alone can cost 25 kg of carcass weight per pig.</p>

Always wear boots and gloves when handling sick pigs, disinfect boots between pens, and report sudden deaths to the nearest veterinary officer immediately. Do not sell or move pigs showing fever, skin reddening or abortion.</p>

<h3>4. Environmental Control in Grow-Finish Barns</h3>
<p>Pigs cannot sweat efficiently. The thermoneutral zone for a 6090 kg pig is roughly 1822C. Above 28C, feed intake drops, ADG falls and FCR worsens. In hot African climates, use open-sided barns with ridge ventilation, shade trees, roof insulation (thatch or reflective sheets), wallows or misters, and increased airflow. Ammonia above 20 ppm and hydrogen sulphide above 5 ppm damage lungs and reduce growth. Keep bedding dry, remove wet manure daily, and ensure at least 0.81.0 m per finisher pig. Stocking density above 1.2 pigs/m increases tail biting, respiratory disease and mortality.</p>

<h3>5. Common Health Issues in Grow-Finish</h3>
<p>Watch for: <strong>respiratory disease</strong> (coughing, laboured breathing often <em>Mycoplasma</em> or <em>Pasteurella</em>), <strong>diarrhoea</strong> (E. coli, salmonella, worms), <strong>mange and lice</strong>, <strong>erysipelas</strong> (diamond-shaped skin lesions), <strong>greasy pig disease</strong>, and <strong>lameness</strong> from foot lesions or <em>Mycoplasma hyosynoviae</em>. Deworm every 812 weeks using albendazole or ivermectin. Vaccinate against erysipelas and, where available, <em>Mycoplasma</em>. Isolate new pigs for 2130 days. Keep a simple treatment log with date, pig ID, drug, dose and withdrawal period this protects both pig health and pork safety for market.</p>

<h3>6. Preparing Pigs for Market</h3>
<p>Market readiness is a business decision, not just a weight target. Pigs typically reach optimal carcass value at 90110 kg live weight with 1216 mm backfat. Beyond 110 kg, FCR worsens sharply and feed cost per kg gain rises. Withdraw feed 812 hours before slaughter (water always available) to reduce gut fill and contamination risk. Avoid mixing unfamiliar pigs in the final week fighting causes bruising and DFD meat. Schedule transport for cool hours, load at 0.50.6 m per pig, and deliver directly to the abattoir or market to reduce shrinkage. In many African markets, selling at 95105 kg to a known butcher or cooperative gives the best margin and repeat business.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Weigh a sample of pigs every 14 days and record ADG, FCR and feed cost per kg gain.</li>
<li>Match feed CP and lysine to grower, finisher and heavy finisher phases never one ration throughout.</li>
<li>Keep stocking density at 0.81.0 m per finisher pig and remove wet bedding daily.</li>
<li>Maintain barn temperature below 28C using shade, ventilation and misters in hot months.</li>
<li>Deworm every 812 weeks and follow a written vaccination schedule.</li>
<li>Handle pigs calmly in small groups; never lift by ears, tail or legs.</li>
<li>Quarantine all new pigs for 2130 days and report sudden deaths to a vet immediately.</li>
<li>Withdraw feed 812 hours before slaughter; transport in cool hours to reduce shrinkage.</li>
</ol>
