1. Identifying Your Target Customers: Households, Kiosks, and Restaurants

Before you sell a single jar of tomato paste or sauce, you must know who will buy it. In most African communities, you have three main customer groups: households, kiosks, and restaurants. Each has different needs, buying habits, and price points. Start by walking around your neighbourhood. Count how many households are within a 1-kilometre radius. Then list the small shops (kiosks) that sell food items. Finally, identify restaurants, hotels, and food vendors. For example, in a typical town in Kenya or Nigeria, you might find 200 households, 15 kiosks, and 5 restaurants. Households buy small quantitiesusually 100g to 500g jarsonce or twice a month. They care about taste, hygiene, and price. Kiosks buy in bulkoften 20 to 50 jars at a timeand resell to households. They want a good profit margin, so you must offer wholesale prices. Restaurants buy large volumes, sometimes 5kg tubs, and they need consistent quality and timely delivery. They also care about packaging that keeps the product fresh. To identify your best targets, talk to people. Ask a kiosk owner: What size of tomato paste sells fastest? Ask a restaurant chef: What problems do you have with your current supplier? Keep a notebook. Write down names, phone numbers, and what each customer wants. Once you know your customers, you can tailor your product and your pitch. For example, if most households in your area cook with fresh tomatoes but complain about seasonal price hikes, your paste solves a real problem. If restaurants say they waste money on spoiled tomatoes, your sauce is a solution. Remember: you cannot sell to everyone. Focus on the group that is easiest to reach and most likely to buy regularly. That might be kiosks in your own neighbourhood. Start there, then expand.

2. Selling at Local Markets and Trade Fairs

Local markets and trade fairs are the fastest way to get your tomato paste and sauce in front of many buyers at once. In most African towns, market days happen once or twice a weekfor example, Thursdays and Saturdays. A trade fair might be an annual agricultural show or a small business expo. To succeed, you need to prepare like a professional. First, book your space early. A market stall might cost 500 to 2,000 shillings (or equivalent) per day. A trade fair booth could cost 5,000 to 20,000 shillings for a week. Choose a spot with high foot trafficnear the entrance or next to a popular food seller. Second, set up an attractive display. Use a clean table with a bright cloth. Arrange your jars in rows. Put up a sign with your business name, price, and a simple message like No preservatives. Tastes like fresh tomatoes. Third, offer free tastes. Open one jar and give small spoonfuls on clean toothpicks or small pieces of bread. People buy what they taste. Fourth, have a clear price list. For example: 100g jar = 100 KES, 250g jar = 200 KES, 500g jar = 350 KES. Offer a discount for bulk: buy 10 jars, get one free. Fifth, collect contacts. Keep a notebook and ask every buyer for their phone number. Tell them you can deliver to their home or shop. Sixth, follow up. Two days after the market, send a WhatsApp message: Thank you for buying. How did you like the sauce? I can deliver more tomorrow. Many market sellers fail because they just sit and wait. Be active. Call out to passers-by. Let them smell and taste. Tell a short story: I make this from tomatoes grown in my village. No water added. At a trade fair, bring a small TV or phone to show a video of your production process. People trust what they can see. Finally, keep records. Write down how many jars you sold, what people asked for, and what they complained about. This data helps you improve for the next market day.

3. Using WhatsApp and Facebook to Promote Your Products

WhatsApp and Facebook are powerful, low-cost tools to sell tomato paste and sauce in Africa. Most people have a smartphone or at least access to one. You can start with zero money. First, create a WhatsApp Business account. Its free. Set your profile picture to a clear photo of your product. Write a business description: Fresh tomato paste and sauce. No preservatives. Delivery in [your town]. Add your catalogue with prices and sizes. Second, build a contact list. Save the numbers of everyone who buys from you, plus friends, family, and neighbours. Ask them to share your number with others. Third, post status updates daily. Show photos of your cooking process, your jars, and happy customers. For example, post a short video of you stirring a pot of tomatoes. Add a caption: Todays batch is ready! 100g jar = 100 KES. Order now. Fourth, create a Facebook page for your business. Post the same content. Join local Facebook groupslike Buy and Sell in [Your Town] or Moms in [Your City]. Post your product there, but follow group rules. Dont spam. Instead, offer value: share a recipe using your tomato sauce. Fifth, use WhatsApp to take orders. When someone messages you, reply quickly. Ask: How many jars do you need? Where should I deliver? Confirm the price and delivery time. Send a payment request via M-Pesa, Airtel Money, or bank transfer. Sixth, encourage referrals. After a customer buys, send a message: Thank you! If you refer a friend, you get 10% off your next order. Seventh, run simple ads. On Facebook, you can boost a post for as little as 1,000 shillings. Target people in your town, aged 25-55, interested in cooking and local food. Eighth, measure what works. Check which posts get the most likes and comments. Do more of that. Remember: consistency beats perfection. Post at least 3 times a week. Respond to every message within an hour. Over time, your online presence will become a reliable sales channel.

4. Building Relationships with Retailers and Wholesalers

Retailers (kiosks, small shops, supermarkets) and wholesalers (bulk buyers who resell to retailers) can become your biggest customers. But they only buy from you if they trust you and make a profit. To build strong relationships, follow these steps. First, identify the right retailers. Visit shops that already sell cooking ingredientstomato paste, spices, cooking oil. Introduce yourself and your product. Bring a free sample jar. Say: I make this locally. Try it. If you like it, I can supply you at a wholesale price. Second, offer a clear wholesale price list. For example, if your retail price is 100 KES for a 100g jar, your wholesale price might be 70 KES. Retailers then make 30 KES profit per jar. For wholesalers buying 100 jars or more, offer 60 KES per jar. Always put this in writinga simple invoice or price sheet. Third, ensure consistent quality and supply. Retailers hate running out of stock. Agree on a delivery schedule: every Monday and Thursday, for example. Always deliver on time. If you cannot, call ahead and explain. Fourth, offer credit carefully. Many retailers will ask to pay after selling. You can offer credit to trusted customers, but start small. For example, give 10 jars on credit for one week. If they pay on time, increase to 20 jars. Keep a record of every credit sale. Fifth, support their sales. Give them free posters or stickers with your brand. Offer a small display stand. Teach them how to talk about your product: No preservatives, made locally, great taste. Sixth, visit regularly. Dont just drop off goods. Spend 5 minutes asking: How are sales? What do customers say? Do you need a different size? This shows you care. Seventh, solve problems fast. If a jar leaks or a batch is off, replace it immediately. Never argue. A retailer who trusts you will reorder for years. Finally, build a network. Ask your best retailer to introduce you to other shop owners. Offer a small commission for referrals. Over time, these relationships become your most stable income.

5. Offering Free Samples and Tastings to Attract Buyers

Free samples and tastings are one of the most effective ways to turn a curious person into a paying customer. In Africa, people buy food they have tasted and trust. Heres how to do it right without wasting money. First, choose the right place and time. Go where your target customers gather: outside a busy market, near a church after service, at a football match, or during a community event. Weekends and evenings work best. Second, prepare small, safe samples. Use clean toothpicks, small spoons, or pieces of plain bread or chapati. Put a tiny amount of your tomato paste or sauce on each. Never double-dip. Keep everything covered until the moment you offer it. Third, set up an attractive table. Use a bright cloth. Display your jars with clear price tags. Have a sign: Free taste! No preservatives. Fourth, engage people. Smile and say: Would you like to taste my fresh tomato sauce? Its made from local tomatoes. If they hesitate, dont push. Just say: Its free. Try it. Most will accept. Fifth, have a special offer for tasters. For example: If you buy one jar now, you get a second one at half price. Or Buy 2, get 1 free. This creates urgency. Sixth, collect contact details. After they taste, ask: If you like it, can I save your number to tell you when I have a new batch? Most people agree. Seventh, follow up within 48 hours. Send a WhatsApp message: Thank you for tasting my sauce. I have fresh jars today. Would you like me to deliver one? Eighth, track your results. For every 100 samples, you might sell 20 to 30 jars. Thats a good return. If you sell fewer than 10, change your location or your pitch. Ninth, dont give samples to everyone. Focus on people who look like your ideal customerthose carrying shopping bags, cooking for a family, or running a food stall. Tenth, be consistent. Do tastings at least twice a month. Over time, people will recognise your brand and buy without tasting. Remember: a free sample is not a loss. Its an investment in a loyal customer.

6. Handling Customer Complaints and Building Loyalty

Even with the best tomato paste and sauce, you will get complaints. How you handle them determines whether you keep a customer for life or lose them forever. First, listen without arguing. When a customer says, This jar tastes different or The lid was loose, thank them for telling you. Say: Im sorry you had a problem. Let me fix it. Never blame the customer. Second, solve the problem immediately. If the product is spoiled or leaking, replace it free of charge. If they bought a large quantity, give a full refund or a new batch. For example, if a restaurant complains that a 5kg tub was too salty, replace it and ask for their feedback on the new batch. Third, follow up. After solving the issue, call or message them a day later: How is the new jar? Is everything okay? This shows you care. Fourth, keep a record. Write down every complaint in a notebook: date, customer name, problem, and how you solved it. Look for patterns. If many people say the paste is too thick, adjust your recipe. If lids leak, change your supplier. Fifth, turn complaints into loyalty. Offer a small gifta free 100g jar or a discount on their next order. Say: Thank you for helping me improve. Many customers become more loyal after a problem is fixed well. Sixth, build loyalty proactively. Create a simple loyalty program: buy 10 jars, get 1 free. Use a card or a WhatsApp record. Send a message on their birthday: Happy birthday! Enjoy 20% off your next order. Seventh, ask for feedback regularly. After a sale, send a short survey: On a scale of 1 to 5, how did you like the sauce? Eighth, reward referrals. If a customer brings a new buyer, give them a free jar. Ninth, be consistent. Deliver on time. Keep your prices fair. Answer your phone. Tenth, show appreciation. A simple Thank you for your business goes a long way. In Africa, relationships are everything. A customer who feels heard and valued will not only buy againthey will tell their friends, family, and neighbours. That word-of-mouth is the cheapest and most powerful marketing you can get.

<p>This practical reference gives you the tools, numbers, and scripts to sell tomato paste and sauce profitably in African markets. It is written for small and growing processors who want to move product consistently not just at harvest time, but all year. Use it alongside the six lessons in this module: identifying your target customers, selling at local markets and trade fairs, using WhatsApp and Facebook, building relationships with retailers and wholesalers, offering free samples and tastings, and handling customer complaints.</p>

<h3>1. Know Your Customer Segments Before You Produce</h3>
<p>In most African towns, tomato sauce and paste buyers fall into three groups with very different needs. Households buy small sachets or tins weekly and are price-sensitive. Kiosks and mini-marts buy in cartons and care about shelf life and margin. Restaurants, hotels, and caterers buy in bulk and care about consistency of taste and reliable delivery. Your packaging, price, and sales channel should match each group.</p>

<h3>2. Pricing and Margins</h3>
<p>Your price must cover raw tomatoes, packaging, fuel, labour, transport, and spoilage then leave a margin for you and your retailer. A simple rule used by many small processors: your wholesale price should be about 6070% of the retail price, so the retailer earns 3040%. Never undercut your own retailers by selling cheaper at the market stall next to them; it destroys the relationship.</p>

<h3>4. Selling at Local Markets and Trade Fairs</h3>
<p>Market days are your fastest route to cash and customer feedback. Arrive early, set up a clean, well-labelled stall, and display your product at eye level. Use clear price tags customers distrust stalls without prices. At trade fairs, prepare a one-page flyer listing your product, pack sizes, wholesale price, and phone number. Collect names and numbers of every serious buyer in a notebook or phone contact list.</p>

<h3>5. WhatsApp and Facebook Promotion</h3>
<p>WhatsApp Business is free and powerful in Africa. Set up a business profile with your logo, address, and catalogue. Post short videos of your production process buyers trust what they can see. Use broadcast lists (not groups) to send weekly offers to retailers. On Facebook, join local buy-and-sell groups and post clear photos with price and location. Respond to every message within a few hours; slow replies lose sales.</p>

<h3>6. Building Relationships with Retailers and Wholesalers</h3>
<p>Retailers reorder from people they trust. Offer a simple written agreement: price, delivery day, minimum order, and return policy for damaged goods. Deliver on time, every time. Give a small bonus (one free carton per ten) rather than deep discounts, so the value of your product stays high. Visit your top ten retailers monthly and ask what is selling and what is not.</p>

<h3>7. Free Samples and Tastings</h3>
<p>Tastings convert curiosity into sales. Cook a small pot of rice or ugali and serve it with your sauce. Keep the sample small, hygienic, and clearly linked to the pack you are selling. Record how many people tasted and how many bought this tells you your true conversion rate. A tasting that converts 1 in 5 tasters is strong in most markets.</p>

<h3 style="color:#c0392b;margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING Food Safety and Honest Marketing</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;">Never sell tomato paste or sauce that shows mould, an off smell, leaking seals, or swollen tins. Do not relabel expired stock or dilute paste to stretch volume this is illegal in most African countries and can cause serious illness or death, especially for children and the elderly. Always follow your national food safety authority rules (e.g., NAFDAC in Nigeria, FDA Ghana, KEBS in Kenya) and print batch numbers and expiry dates on every pack. If a customer reports illness after using your product, stop sales of that batch immediately, keep samples, and report to your local health or standards office.</p>
</div>

<h3>8. Handling Complaints and Building Loyalty</h3>
<p>Treat every complaint as free market research. Listen fully, apologise sincerely, and replace or refund faulty product without argument. Record the batch number and the complaint in a simple log. Follow up within 48 hours to confirm the customer is satisfied. Loyal customers who feel heard will bring you new customers word of mouth is the cheapest and strongest marketing in African communities.</p>
