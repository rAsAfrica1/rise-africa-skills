1. Dry-salting and brining techniques for preservation

Salting is the oldest way to keep meat without a fridge. For dry-salting, use coarse saltnot table salt with iodine. For every 1 kg of pork, rub in 50 g of salt. Add 5 g of crushed black pepper and 5 g of brown sugar if you have it. Cut meat into strips no thicker than 5 cm. Rub the salt mix into every surface. Layer meat in a clean plastic bucket or clay pot. Cover and keep in a cool place (below 10C if possible). After 24 hours, liquid will pull out. Pour it off. Re-rub with another 20 g salt per kg. Leave for 3 to 5 days for thin strips, up to 10 days for thick cuts. Meat is ready when it feels firm and dry, not squishy.

For brining, make a solution: 1 litre of clean water, 250 g salt, 50 g sugar. Boil, then cool completely. Submerge meat in the brine in a food-grade container. Weigh it down with a clean stone. Keep in a cool place. Brine time: 1 day per 1 kg of meat. For a 2 kg piece, 2 days. After brining, rinse meat with clean water and pat dry. Then you can air-dry or smoke it.

Important: always use clean hands, clean tools, and clean containers. Salt kills many bacteria but not all. If you see mould (green, black, fuzzy), throw the meat away. If it smells rotten, throw it away. In hot climates, work early morning or evening when its cooler. Salt is cheap; use enough. You can reuse brine once if you boil it again, but better to make fresh. Dry-salting works well for bacon and ham. Brining is good for smaller pieces and for meat you plan to smoke. Start with small batches until you learn the feel.

2. Air-drying meat in a safe, ventilated space

Air-drying removes moisture so bacteria and mould cannot grow. You need a dry, breezy place away from flies and dust. A shaded veranda or a raised platform under a roof works. Do not dry meat in direct sunit cooks the outside and traps moisture inside. Ideal temperature: 1525C with low humidity. In rainy season, use a fan or wait for dry weather.

Cut meat into thin strips, 12 cm thick, 35 cm wide, 1015 cm long. Salt them first (see Lesson 1). Then hang strips on clean wire hooks or cotton string. Space them 5 cm apart so air moves between them. Hang them at least 1 metre above the ground. Cover with a mosquito net or cheesecloth to keep flies off. Flies lay eggs that become maggotsa sign of spoilage.

Drying time depends on weather. In dry heat, 35 days. In humid conditions, 710 days. Meat is dry when it is stiff, dark, and cracks when bent. It should not be sticky or soft. Weigh a piece before and after: it should lose about 5060% of its weight. If it loses less, dry longer.

Turn the meat daily and check for mould. If you see white salt crystals, thats finewipe them off. Green or black spots mean throw it away. After drying, store in paper bags or cloth sacks in a cool, dry place. Do not store in plasticit traps moisture. For longer storage, smoke the dried meat (see Lesson 4). Air-drying is cheap and works well for making biltong or jerky. Start with 2 kg of meat to learn.

3. Building a simple smokehouse using local materials

You can build a smokehouse with mud, sticks, and a metal drum. It needs a firebox, a smoke channel, and a smoking chamber. Dig a trench 30 cm deep, 30 cm wide, and 1.5 m long. This is your firebox and smoke channel. At one end, build a small fire. At the other end, place a metal drum (200-litre) with the top and bottom removed. Or build a box from mud bricks, 1 m x 1 m x 1.5 m tall. Leave a small door on one side for adding wood and hanging meat.

Inside the chamber, hang metal rods or strong sticks 50 cm below the top. Use S-hooks made from wire to hang meat. The smoke must travel from the fire, through the trench, and up into the chamber. Cover the trench with metal sheets or flat stones, leaving small gaps for smoke to escape. This cools the smoke and removes some tar.

Use hardwoods like oak, hickory, or fruit wood (apple, mango). Never use softwoods like pinethey give bad flavour and harmful resin. Do not use treated wood. Start with a small fire of dry wood. Once it burns down to embers, add green wood or sawdust to make smoke. You want thin, blue-grey smoke, not thick white smoke. Thick smoke makes meat bitter.

Maintain temperature: for cold smoking, 2030C. For hot smoking, 6080C. Use a thermometer. If too hot, move fire further away or reduce wood. Smoke for 612 hours for cold smoking, 24 hours for hot smoking. Check meat often. The smokehouse should be in a shaded area, away from houses. Keep children and animals away. Clean the chamber after each use. With care, your smokehouse will last for years.

4. Cold smoking vs. hot smoking for different products

Cold smoking and hot smoking are different. Cold smoking uses low heat (2030C) for a long time (612 hours or more). It adds flavour and helps preserve, but does not cook the meat. The meat stays raw. You must cure it with salt first. Cold-smoked products: bacon, ham, sausages, and dried strips. They need to be cooked before eating. Cold smoking is good for long storage because the meat dries slowly and absorbs smoke. But it requires cool weatherif outside is above 30C, its hard to keep the smokehouse cool. You can use a separate firebox and a long trench to cool the smoke.

Hot smoking uses higher heat (6080C) for a shorter time (24 hours). It cooks the meat while smoking. The meat becomes safe to eat without further cooking, but it does not keep as long as cold-smoked meat. Hot-smoked products: pork chops, ribs, belly, and ready-to-eat snacks. They are juicy and have a smoky flavour. Hot smoking is easier in hot climates because you dont need to keep the temperature low. But you must watch the heatif it goes above 85C, the meat gets tough and dry.

For both, use the same smokehouse but control the fire. For cold smoking, keep the fire small and far from the chamber. For hot smoking, build a bigger fire closer to the chamber. Always use a thermometer. For cold smoking, cure meat first: dry-salt or brine for 13 days. For hot smoking, you can use a simple salt rub for 1 hour. After smoking, cool meat quickly. Store cold-smoked meat in a cool, dry place or fridge. Hot-smoked meat should be eaten within 35 days or frozen. Learn both methodsthey give you more products to sell.

5. Storing preserved meat to prevent spoilage

Even after salting, drying, or smoking, meat can spoil if stored wrong. The enemies are moisture, heat, light, insects, and rodents. Store preserved meat in a cool, dark, dry place. Ideal temperature: below 15C. If you have a fridge, use it for long-term storage. Without a fridge, use a root cellar or a sealed clay pot. Keep meat in cloth bags (cotton or linen) or paper bags. Do not use plastic bagsthey trap moisture and cause mould. If you must use plastic, wrap meat in paper first, then place in a sealed container with a lid.

For dried meat, hang it in a dry, airy room. Or store in a wooden box with a lid. Add a small bag of rice or silica gel to absorb moisture. Check every week for mould or insects. If you see mould, wipe with vinegar and re-dry. If insects (weevils, beetles) appear, freeze the meat for 48 hours or heat in an oven at 60C for 30 minutes. For smoked meat, keep it wrapped in paper or cloth. You can also dip smoked meat in melted fat (lard) to seal itthis is called confit. The fat layer keeps air out. Store in a cool place.

For long-term storage without refrigeration, canning is an option but requires special equipment. Otherwise, keep meat in a sealed container with salt. Salt draws moisture out of the air. For every 1 kg of meat, pack with 200 g of coarse salt. Change salt if it gets damp. Always label with date. Use oldest first. If meat smells sour, feels slimy, or has black spots, throw it away. Do not taste to check. Spoiled meat can make you very sick. Teach your family and workers these rules. Good storage means less waste and more profit.

6. Selling preserved meat to distant markets

Preserved meat can travel far without spoiling. That means you can sell to cities, mining camps, or even other countries. But you need to plan. First, check local laws. Some countries require permits for selling meat products. Talk to your local agriculture office. They may inspect your smokehouse and storage. Get a food handling certificate if possible. It builds trust with buyers.

Package your meat well. Use vacuum-sealed bags if you can afford a machine. Otherwise, wrap in greaseproof paper, then in a cloth bag, then in a cardboard box. Label clearly: product name, weight, date, and your contact. For dried meat, sell in 250 g or 500 g packs. For smoked bacon or ham, sell whole pieces or slices. Set a price that covers your costs: meat, salt, wood, packaging, transport. Add 3050% profit. Check prices at markets in the city to stay competitive.

Find buyers. Restaurants, hotels, and supermarkets are good. Also, sell online via WhatsApp, Facebook, or local e-commerce sites. Take clear photos. Offer free samples to get first orders. For distant markets, use a courier or bus service that delivers. Pack meat in a cool box with ice packs if the journey is more than 6 hours. For international sales, you need export permits and food safety certificatesstart with local markets first.

Build a brand. Give your product a name. Tell customers how you make ittraditional, no chemicals. Use repeat customers: offer discounts for bulk orders. Attend trade fairs. Keep records of sales and expenses. Reinvest profits into better packaging or a bigger smokehouse. With good preservation, you can sell year-round, even when fresh pork is cheap. Thats how you grow from a smallholder to a real business.

<h2>Practical Reference: Preserving Meat Salting, Drying, and Smoking</h2>

<p>Preserved meat is one of the highest-margin products a small pig processor in Africa can sell beyond the village gate. Fresh pork must move within hours; salted, dried, or smoked pork can travel for days, sit on a market stall for weeks, and reach buyers in cities, mining towns, and cross-border markets. This reference brings together the technical steps, the numbers you need for costing, and the market logic for each preservation method. Treat it as your working manual the sequence matters, and shortcuts cost you the whole batch.</p>

<p>The three preservation routes covered here dry-salting/brining, air-drying, and smoking can be combined. A common high-value African product is brine-cured, then hot-smoked, then air-dried briefly: it is shelf-stable, has a deep red colour, and commands a premium in urban markets. But each step has a failure mode. Salt too little and bacteria survive; dry too fast and the outside case-hardens while the inside rots; smoke too hot and you cook rather than preserve. This module gives you the parameters to avoid all three.</p>

<h3>1. Dry-Salting and Brining</h3>

<p>Salt works by lowering water activity (a<sub>w</sub>) in the meat, which stops most spoilage bacteria and slows mould. For pork, the target a<sub>w</sub> for shelf-stable product is below 0.85. Dry-salting is the cheapest method you rub coarse salt (preferably non-iodised, as iodine can cause off-flavours) into the surface and stack the cuts in a clean container. Brining is faster and more even, but requires a food-grade container and clean water.</p>

<p><strong>Dry-salt ratio:</strong> 34% of the meat's weight in salt for a 35 day cure; 2.5% for a longer 7-day cure. Add 0.5% sodium nitrite (cure #2) if you are making a product that will be cold-smoked or air-dried for more than 2 weeks nitrite prevents <em>Clostridium botulinum</em>. In many African markets, nitrite is sold as "pink salt" or "curing salt" and must never be used at more than 0.5% of meat weight.</p>

<p><strong>Brine strength:</strong> A 7080% saturation brine (roughly 1 kg salt per 4 L water) is standard for equilibrium brining. Test with a raw egg: it should float with a coin-sized area above the surface. For a 5 kg pork leg, brine for 57 days at 48 C (or in a cool shaded place if refrigeration is unavailable, but only if you can verify the temperature stays below 10 C).</p>

<h3>2. Air-Drying in a Safe, Ventilated Space</h3>

<p>Air-drying removes moisture until the meat's water activity drops below 0.85. The critical variables are temperature, humidity, and airflow. In most of sub-Saharan Africa, the challenge is not cold it is humidity and insects. A drying room should hold 1218 C, 6575% relative humidity, and move air at 0.51.5 m/s across the meat surface. If you cannot reach those numbers, dry during the coolest, driest months (typically the cool-dry season) and use a solar-powered fan or a chimney effect to pull air through.</p>

<p><strong>Case-hardening</strong> is the most common failure: the surface dries and seals while the interior stays wet and spoils. Prevent it by starting at higher humidity (8085%) for the first 24 hours, then dropping to 7075%. Turn and rotate pieces daily. A 1 kg pork loin dried at 15 C and 70% RH takes 710 days to lose 3540% of its weight; a thin 200 g strip takes 23 days.</p>

<p>Insect control: use insect-proof netting (mosquito net is adequate for small batches), and keep the drying room off the ground on a pallet or concrete slab. In tsetse and screwworm areas, drying must be indoors or under fine mesh flies will blow the meat in minutes.</p>

<h3>3. Building a Simple Smokehouse Using Local Materials</h3>

<p>The diagram above shows the workhorse design for small-scale African processors: a 200 L steel drum, a fire pit at the base, a baffle plate to spread heat, hanging hooks, and a vent. You can substitute a mud-brick or termite-mound structure if steel is expensive. The key dimensions: fire pit 6080 cm below the lowest meat, drum or chamber height 1.21.5 m, vent area roughly 1/10 of the fire pit area.</p>

<p><strong>Fuel:</strong> Use hardwood mango, acacia, eucalyptus (well-dried), or coconut shell. Never use softwood (pine, cypress) or treated timber: the resins and preservatives are toxic. For flavour, add a handful of green leaves (bay, guava, or rosemary) on the embers, not on the fire.</p>

<p><strong>Temperature control:</strong> A simple thermometer through the drum wall is the single best investment (US$35). If you cannot get one, use the hand test: hold your palm at meat level for 3 seconds if you must pull away, it is above 70 C (hot smoking); if you can hold 56 seconds, it is 5060 C (cold smoking).</p>

<h3>4. Cold Smoking vs. Hot Smoking</h3>

<p><strong>Cold smoking (1530 C, 1248 hours)</strong> adds flavour and a light antimicrobial surface but does not cook the meat. It must be applied to meat that has already been cured with salt and nitrite. Products: bacon, ham, dry sausage, lardons. Cold-smoked products still need refrigeration or a dry, cool storage room. In hot African climates, cold smoking is practical only at night or in the cool season.</p>

<p><strong>Hot smoking (6085 C, 28 hours)</strong> cooks the meat and pasteurises the surface. It is the more forgiving method for small processors without refrigeration. Products: hot-smoked ham, smoked ribs, smoked sausage, smoked pork belly. Hot-smoked meat keeps 510 days at ambient if kept dry and wrapped, or 34 weeks refrigerated.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Sodium nitrite (cure #2) is toxic if overdosed. Never exceed 0.5% of meat weight (5 g per 1 kg). Keep it in a locked, labelled container away from children and away from table salt accidental substitution has killed people. Also: never smoke meat above 85 C fat renders, drips onto embers, and creates carcinogenic polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). If the fire flares, remove the meat, cool the embers, and restart. Pregnant women, infants, and immunocompromised people should avoid cold-smoked and air-dried products unless commercially certified.</p>

<h3>5. Storing Preserved Meat to Prevent Spoilage</h3>

<p>Preserved meat fails in storage for three reasons: moisture migration, insects, and mould. The rules are simple. Keep it dry (RH below 70%), keep it cool (below 20 C if possible), keep it dark, and keep it sealed. Wrap in greaseproof paper or clean cotton cloth, then in a breathable sack not plastic, which traps moisture. Hang in a cool, dark, ventilated room. For longer storage, vacuum-seal if you have access to a machine, or coat hams in a layer of clean ash or ground pepper to deter insects.</p>

<p>Check weekly. Any piece with green or black mould, slime, or a sour smell must be discarded do not scrape and sell. A single spoiled piece can contaminate a whole batch and destroy your market reputation.</p>

<h3>6. Selling Preserved Meat to Distant Markets</h3>

<p>This is where the business case lives. Fresh pork in a rural market might sell for US$34/kg. Properly salted, smoked, and packaged pork can sell for US$815/kg in a city supermarket or to a mining catering company, and US$20+/kg as a branded specialty product (e.g., "smoked village ham"). The margin covers the weight loss (3050%), the fuel, the salt, and the transport and still leaves a profit.</p>

<p><strong>Market channels to target:</strong> (1) urban butcheries and delis, (2) mining and construction camp caterers, (3) cross-border traders (e.g., ZambiaDRC, KenyaSouth Sudan), (4) diaspora online sellers, (5) hotels and lodges. Each has different requirements: caterers want consistent weight and food-safety paperwork; delis want vacuum packaging and labels; cross-border traders want durable, low-moisture product.</p>

<p><strong>Pricing rule of thumb:</strong> Price = (raw meat cost + salt + fuel + labour + packaging + transport) 2.5. If that lands below the local fresh-pork price, you are not charging enough for the value you have added raise the price or find a better channel.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Weigh meat and salt never eyeball. 34% salt for dry cure, 0.5% cure #2 maximum.</li>
<li>Keep cure and salt in separate, clearly labelled containers. Lock the nitrite.</li>
<li>Brine at 48 C; if no fridge, cure only in the cool-dry season and verify temperature.</li>
<li>Dry at 1218 C, 6575% RH, with airflow. Start humid, finish dry to avoid case-hardening.</li>
<li>Use only hardwood for smoking no pine, no treated timber, no plastic.</li>
<li>Hot smoke at 6085 C; cold smoke at 1530 C only on cured meat.</li>
<li>Store wrapped in paper or cloth, not plastic. Check weekly for mould, slime, or off-smell.</li>
<li>Price at 2.5 total cost. Sell to caterers, delis, and cross-border traders not just the village gate.</li>
</ul>
