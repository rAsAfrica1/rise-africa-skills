1. Dry-salting and brining techniques for preservation

Salting is the oldest way to keep meat without a fridge. For dry-salting, use coarse saltânot table salt with iodine. For every 1 kg of pork, rub in 50 g of salt. Add 5 g of crushed black pepper and 5 g of brown sugar if you have it. Cut meat into strips no thicker than 5 cm. Rub the salt mix into every surface. Layer meat in a clean plastic bucket or clay pot. Cover and keep in a cool place (below 10Â°C if possible). After 24 hours, liquid will pull out. Pour it off. Re-rub with another 20 g salt per kg. Leave for 3 to 5 days for thin strips, up to 10 days for thick cuts. Meat is ready when it feels firm and dry, not squishy.

For brining, make a solution: 1 litre of clean water, 250 g salt, 50 g sugar. Boil, then cool completely. Submerge meat in the brine in a food-grade container. Weigh it down with a clean stone. Keep in a cool place. Brine time: 1 day per 1 kg of meat. For a 2 kg piece, 2 days. After brining, rinse meat with clean water and pat dry. Then you can air-dry or smoke it.

Important: always use clean hands, clean tools, and clean containers. Salt kills many bacteria but not all. If you see mould (green, black, fuzzy), throw the meat away. If it smells rotten, throw it away. In hot climates, work early morning or evening when itâs cooler. Salt is cheap; use enough. You can reuse brine once if you boil it again, but better to make fresh. Dry-salting works well for bacon and ham. Brining is good for smaller pieces and for meat you plan to smoke. Start with small batches until you learn the feel.

2. Air-drying meat in a safe, ventilated space

Air-drying removes moisture so bacteria and mould cannot grow. You need a dry, breezy place away from flies and dust. A shaded veranda or a raised platform under a roof works. Do not dry meat in direct sunâit cooks the outside and traps moisture inside. Ideal temperature: 15â25Â°C with low humidity. In rainy season, use a fan or wait for dry weather.

Cut meat into thin strips, 1â2 cm thick, 3â5 cm wide, 10â15 cm long. Salt them first (see Lesson 1). Then hang strips on clean wire hooks or cotton string. Space them 5 cm apart so air moves between them. Hang them at least 1 metre above the ground. Cover with a mosquito net or cheesecloth to keep flies off. Flies lay eggs that become maggotsâa sign of spoilage.

Drying time depends on weather. In dry heat, 3â5 days. In humid conditions, 7â10 days. Meat is dry when it is stiff, dark, and cracks when bent. It should not be sticky or soft. Weigh a piece before and after: it should lose about 50â60% of its weight. If it loses less, dry longer.

Turn the meat daily and check for mould. If you see white salt crystals, thatâs fineâwipe them off. Green or black spots mean throw it away. After drying, store in paper bags or cloth sacks in a cool, dry place. Do not store in plasticâit traps moisture. For longer storage, smoke the dried meat (see Lesson 4). Air-drying is cheap and works well for making biltong or jerky. Start with 2 kg of meat to learn.

3. Building a simple smokehouse using local materials

You can build a smokehouse with mud, sticks, and a metal drum. It needs a firebox, a smoke channel, and a smoking chamber. Dig a trench 30 cm deep, 30 cm wide, and 1.5 m long. This is your firebox and smoke channel. At one end, build a small fire. At the other end, place a metal drum (200-litre) with the top and bottom removed. Or build a box from mud bricks, 1 m x 1 m x 1.5 m tall. Leave a small door on one side for adding wood and hanging meat.

Inside the chamber, hang metal rods or strong sticks 50 cm below the top. Use S-hooks made from wire to hang meat. The smoke must travel from the fire, through the trench, and up into the chamber. Cover the trench with metal sheets or flat stones, leaving small gaps for smoke to escape. This cools the smoke and removes some tar.

Use hardwoods like oak, hickory, or fruit wood (apple, mango). Never use softwoods like pineâthey give bad flavour and harmful resin. Do not use treated wood. Start with a small fire of dry wood. Once it burns down to embers, add green wood or sawdust to make smoke. You want thin, blue-grey smoke, not thick white smoke. Thick smoke makes meat bitter.

Maintain temperature: for cold smoking, 20â30Â°C. For hot smoking, 60â80Â°C. Use a thermometer. If too hot, move fire further away or reduce wood. Smoke for 6â12 hours for cold smoking, 2â4 hours for hot smoking. Check meat often. The smokehouse should be in a shaded area, away from houses. Keep children and animals away. Clean the chamber after each use. With care, your smokehouse will last for years.

4. Cold smoking vs. hot smoking for different products

Cold smoking and hot smoking are different. Cold smoking uses low heat (20â30Â°C) for a long time (6â12 hours or more). It adds flavour and helps preserve, but does not cook the meat. The meat stays raw. You must cure it with salt first. Cold-smoked products: bacon, ham, sausages, and dried strips. They need to be cooked before eating. Cold smoking is good for long storage because the meat dries slowly and absorbs smoke. But it requires cool weatherâif outside is above 30Â°C, itâs hard to keep the smokehouse cool. You can use a separate firebox and a long trench to cool the smoke.

Hot smoking uses higher heat (60â80Â°C) for a shorter time (2â4 hours). It cooks the meat while smoking. The meat becomes safe to eat without further cooking, but it does not keep as long as cold-smoked meat. Hot-smoked products: pork chops, ribs, belly, and ready-to-eat snacks. They are juicy and have a smoky flavour. Hot smoking is easier in hot climates because you donât need to keep the temperature low. But you must watch the heatâif it goes above 85Â°C, the meat gets tough and dry.

For both, use the same smokehouse but control the fire. For cold smoking, keep the fire small and far from the chamber. For hot smoking, build a bigger fire closer to the chamber. Always use a thermometer. For cold smoking, cure meat first: dry-salt or brine for 1â3 days. For hot smoking, you can use a simple salt rub for 1 hour. After smoking, cool meat quickly. Store cold-smoked meat in a cool, dry place or fridge. Hot-smoked meat should be eaten within 3â5 days or frozen. Learn both methodsâthey give you more products to sell.

5. Storing preserved meat to prevent spoilage

Even after salting, drying, or smoking, meat can spoil if stored wrong. The enemies are moisture, heat, light, insects, and rodents. Store preserved meat in a cool, dark, dry place. Ideal temperature: below 15Â°C. If you have a fridge, use it for long-term storage. Without a fridge, use a root cellar or a sealed clay pot. Keep meat in cloth bags (cotton or linen) or paper bags. Do not use plastic bagsâthey trap moisture and cause mould. If you must use plastic, wrap meat in paper first, then place in a sealed container with a lid.

For dried meat, hang it in a dry, airy room. Or store in a wooden box with a lid. Add a small bag of rice or silica gel to absorb moisture. Check every week for mould or insects. If you see mould, wipe with vinegar and re-dry. If insects (weevils, beetles) appear, freeze the meat for 48 hours or heat in an oven at 60Â°C for 30 minutes. For smoked meat, keep it wrapped in paper or cloth. You can also dip smoked meat in melted fat (lard) to seal itâthis is called confit. The fat layer keeps air out. Store in a cool place.

For long-term storage without refrigeration, canning is an option but requires special equipment. Otherwise, keep meat in a sealed container with salt. Salt draws moisture out of the air. For every 1 kg of meat, pack with 200 g of coarse salt. Change salt if it gets damp. Always label with date. Use oldest first. If meat smells sour, feels slimy, or has black spots, throw it away. Do not taste to check. Spoiled meat can make you very sick. Teach your family and workers these rules. Good storage means less waste and more profit.

6. Selling preserved meat to distant markets

Preserved meat can travel far without spoiling. That means you can sell to cities, mining camps, or even other countries. But you need to plan. First, check local laws. Some countries require permits for selling meat products. Talk to your local agriculture office. They may inspect your smokehouse and storage. Get a food handling certificate if possible. It builds trust with buyers.

Package your meat well. Use vacuum-sealed bags if you can afford a machine. Otherwise, wrap in greaseproof paper, then in a cloth bag, then in a cardboard box. Label clearly: product name, weight, date, and your contact. For dried meat, sell in 250 g or 500 g packs. For smoked bacon or ham, sell whole pieces or slices. Set a price that covers your costs: meat, salt, wood, packaging, transport. Add 30â50% profit. Check prices at markets in the city to stay competitive.

Find buyers. Restaurants, hotels, and supermarkets are good. Also, sell online via WhatsApp, Facebook, or local e-commerce sites. Take clear photos. Offer free samples to get first orders. For distant markets, use a courier or bus service that delivers. Pack meat in a cool box with ice packs if the journey is more than 6 hours. For international sales, you need export permits and food safety certificatesâstart with local markets first.

Build a brand. Give your product a name. Tell customers how you make itâtraditional, no chemicals. Use repeat customers: offer discounts for bulk orders. Attend trade fairs. Keep records of sales and expenses. Reinvest profits into better packaging or a bigger smokehouse. With good preservation, you can sell year-round, even when fresh pork is cheap. Thatâs how you grow from a smallholder to a real business.

The three preservation routes covered here â dry-salting/brining, air-drying, and smoking â can be combined. 1. Dry-Salting and Brining

Salt works by lowering water activity (aw) in the meat, which stops most spoilage bacteria and slows mould. For pork, the target aw for shelf-stable product is below 0.85. Dry-salting is the cheapest method â you rub coarse salt (preferably non-iodised, as iodine can cause off-flavours) into the surface and stack the cuts in a clean container. Brining is faster and more even, but requires a food-grade container and clean water.

Dry-salt ratio: 3â4% of the meat's weight in salt for a 3â5 day cure; 2.5% for a longer 7-day cure. Add 0.5% sodium nitrite (cure #2) if you are making a product that will be cold-smoked or air-dried for more than 2 weeks â nitrite prevents Clostridium botulinum. In many African markets, nitrite is sold as "pink salt" or "curing salt" and must never be used at more than 0.5% of meat weight.

Brine strength: A 70â80% saturation brine (roughly 1 kg salt per 4 L water) is standard for equilibrium brining. Test with a raw egg: it should float with a coin-sized area above the surface. For a 5 kg pork leg, brine for 5â7 days at 4â8 Â°C (or in a cool shaded place if refrigeration is unavailable, but only if you can verify the temperature stays below 10 Â°C).

2. Air-Drying in a Safe, Ventilated Space

Cold smoking (15â30 Â°C, 12â48 hours) adds flavour and a light antimicrobial surface but does not cook the meat. It must be applied to meat that has already been cured with salt and nitrite.
