1. Creating and Organizing Grocery Lists

A grocery list is your most important tool. Without it, you will forget items, buy duplicates, and waste money. Start by checking what you already have. Open the fridge, freezer, and pantry. Look at expiry dates. If you see you have three bottles of cooking oil, you don't need more. Write down what's missing. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Google Keep or AnyList. For clients with smartphones, share the list so they can add items. For others, keep a paper list on the fridge.

Organize your list by store section. In most African markets and supermarkets, the layout is similar: fresh produce (fruits, vegetables), meat and fish, dairy, dry goods (rice, beans, flour), canned goods, spices, and household items (soap, detergent). Group items accordingly. This saves time and prevents backtracking. For example, list tomatoes, onions, and peppers together under 'vegetables'. Put milk, eggs, and butter under 'dairy'. If you shop at a local market, group by vendor: the tomato seller, the grain seller, etc.

Include quantities and brands. Instead of 'rice', write '5kg bag of long-grain rice, brand: Mama's Choice'. If the client is brand-loyal, note that. For fresh items, specify ripeness: '3 ripe bananas for today, 3 green for later'. Also note any dietary restrictions (see Lesson 2). Add a column for estimated price. In Kenya, a 2kg bag of maize flour costs about KES 150. In Nigeria, a crate of eggs (30) is around NGN 2,500. This helps you stay within budget.

Before you leave, double-check the list against your client's request. Did they ask for something special? Call or text to confirm if unsure. At the store, tick off items as you put them in the cart. If an item is out of stock, note it and suggest a substitute. After shopping, keep the receipt and update the list for next time. A good PA always has a backup list on their phone.

2. Understanding Dietary Restrictions and Preferences

Dietary restrictions are non-negotiable. If you buy the wrong item, it can harm your client's health or offend their beliefs. Always ask upfront: 'Do you have any allergies, religious restrictions, or foods you avoid?' Common restrictions in Africa include halal (no pork, alcohol), kosher (no pork, no mixing meat and dairy), vegetarian (no meat, fish, or poultry), vegan (no animal products at all), and allergies to peanuts, shellfish, or gluten. Some people avoid sugar or salt for medical reasons like diabetes or hypertension.

Write down each restriction clearly. For example: 'No pork, no alcohol, no shellfish. Prefers chicken and fish.' Then, when shopping, read labels carefully. In many African countries, packaged foods may not list all ingredients, so ask the store owner. For fresh items, avoid cross-contamination: use separate bags for meat and vegetables. If your client is halal, buy from a halal butcher. In cities like Nairobi, Lagos, or Accra, you can find halal-certified shops. For vegetarian clients, check that beans or lentils are not cooked with meat broth.

Preferences are different from restrictions. A client might prefer brown bread over white, or organic produce if available. They might dislike onions or want low-sodium soy sauce. Note these preferences and try to honor them, but be flexible if an item is unavailable. Always offer a substitute and get approval. For example, if they want fresh spinach but it's out of season, suggest kale or collard greens. If they want a specific brand of tea, and it's not there, ask if another brand is okay.

When in doubt, ask. A quick text or call can save a trip. Also, keep a 'safe list' of foods your client can eat. For a diabetic client, focus on whole grains, lean proteins, and vegetables. Avoid sugary drinks and white bread. For a client with high blood pressure, choose low-salt options. Remember, you are responsible for their well-being. Never assume you know better than their doctor or religious leader. If you make a mistake, apologize and correct it immediately.

3. Navigating Grocery Stores and Finding Items Quickly

Time is money. A good PA can shop for a week's groceries in under an hour. First, know your stores. In most African cities, you have three main options: open-air markets (cheapest for fresh produce), small neighborhood shops (convenient for staples), and supermarkets (best for packaged goods and brands). Plan your route. For example, in Kampala, you might go to Nakasero Market for fruits and vegetables, then Capital Shoppers for dairy and cleaning supplies. Avoid peak hours: early morning or late evening are less crowded.

Learn the layout of each store. Supermarkets are organized in a standard way: entrance leads to fruits and vegetables, then bakery, then dairy and eggs, then meat and fish, then dry goods, then frozen, then beverages, then household items, and finally checkout. If you only need a few items, go straight to those aisles. Use store maps on apps like Carrefour or Shoprite if available. In local markets, remember which vendor sells the best tomatoes, the cheapest rice, or the freshest fish. Build relationships. A friendly greeting in the local language (e.g., 'Jambo' in Kenya, 'Sannu' in Nigeria) goes a long way.

Make a beeline for items you need. Don't get distracted by promotions. Stick to your list. If you need help, ask a store employee. They can point you to the right aisle or check stock in the back. For items that are hard to find, like gluten-free flour or specific spices, try specialty stores or order online from sites like Jumia or Konga. For fresh produce, inspect carefully: no bruises, no mold, firm texture. For packaged goods, check expiry dates. The longest date is usually at the back of the shelf.

Use a basket or trolley to keep items organized. Put heavy items at the bottom, fragile items on top. Keep your list in hand and tick off as you go. At checkout, bag your own groceries if needed. In some stores, you must pay for bags. Bring your own reusable bags to save money and help the environment. After shopping, head straight to your next errand or home. If you have perishables, don't leave them in a hot car. Plan your route so cold items are last.

4. Handling Returns and Exchanges

Returns and exchanges are part of the job. A client may ask you to return a faulty item, exchange a wrong size, or get a refund. Always keep receipts. Without a receipt, most stores will not accept returns. If your client paid by card, keep the card slip. If they paid cash, the receipt is your proof. In many African countries, consumer protection laws are weak, so store policies vary. Some stores allow returns within 7 days, others 14 or 30. Know the policy before you buy. Ask at checkout: 'What is your return policy?'

When returning an item, go to the customer service desk. Be polite but firm. Explain the problem: 'This blender stopped working after two days.' Show the receipt. If the item is defective, you are entitled to a replacement or refund. If it's a change of mind (e.g., wrong size), you may only get an exchange or store credit. Some stores charge a restocking fee. For online purchases, check the return window. Jumia, for example, allows returns within 7 days for most items, but you must initiate the process on their app. Take photos of the faulty item as evidence.

If the store refuses, don't argue. Ask to speak to the manager. If that fails, contact the manufacturer if the item is under warranty. For small items, it may not be worth the transport cost. In that case, advise your client to cut their losses. But for expensive items like electronics or appliances, pursue it. In Nigeria, you can report to the Consumer Protection Council. In Kenya, the Competition Authority of Kenya handles consumer complaints. Keep a record of all communication: dates, names, and what was said.

After a successful return, update your client. If you got a refund, give them the money and receipt. If you exchanged, show them the new item. If you got store credit, note the amount and where it can be used. Always follow up to ensure the client is satisfied. If you are doing multiple returns, batch them by store to save time. For example, if you have returns at Shoprite and Game, do them on the same trip. Finally, learn from each return: check items more carefully before buying next time.

5. Pharmacy Pickups and Prescription Management

Pharmacy errands require extra care. You are dealing with health, so mistakes can be dangerous. Always get a written prescription from your client or their doctor. It should include the medicine name, dosage, frequency, and duration. If the handwriting is unclear, call the doctor's office to confirm. Never guess. For repeat prescriptions, keep a copy in a folder or on your phone. Note the expiry date and when the next refill is due. Set reminders on your phone. In many African countries, pharmacies are called chemists. Look for a licensed one with a green cross sign.

When you go to the pharmacy, bring the prescription and your client's insurance card if they have one. Some medicines require a doctor's call. If the pharmacist questions the prescription, call the doctor. For over-the-counter (OTC) medicines like paracetamol or antacids, you can buy without a prescription, but still confirm with your client. Check expiry dates on all medicines. Do not accept anything expiring within three months. Also, check the packaging for tampering. If the seal is broken, ask for another.

Pay attention to generic vs. brand names. Generic medicines are usually cheaper and equally effective. For example, paracetamol is the generic for Panadol. If your client insists on a brand, buy it. But if they want to save money, ask the pharmacist for a generic alternative. Also, ask about storage: some medicines need refrigeration (e.g., insulin). If so, bring a cooler bag. For liquid medicines, ask for a measuring spoon or syringe. Never use a kitchen spoon.

After pickup, give the medicine to your client with clear instructions. Write down the dosage on the box if needed. If they have multiple medicines, organize them in a pill organizer. For example, morning, afternoon, evening. Warn them about interactions: some medicines should not be taken with alcohol or certain foods. If you are unsure, ask the pharmacist. Keep a log of all medicines: name, dosage, start date, end date, and who prescribed it. This helps during doctor visits. If your client runs out, remind them to refill before it's too late.

6. Post Office and Shipping Errands

Post office and shipping errands involve sending letters, parcels, and documents. In Africa, you have several options: national post offices (e.g., Kenya Post, Nigeria Post), private couriers (DHL, FedEx, UPS), and local bus services (e.g., Easy Coach in Kenya, ABC Transport in Nigeria). Each has different costs and speeds. For urgent documents, use a courier. For cheap parcels, use the post office or bus. Always ask your client for the destination, weight, and deadline.

Before you go, pack the item securely. Use a box or padded envelope. For fragile items, wrap in bubble wrap or newspaper. Write the address clearly in capital letters. Include the recipient's phone number. For international shipments, you need a customs declaration form. List the contents and value. Do not send prohibited items like cash, liquids, or perishables. At the post office, you will fill out a form. Keep the tracking number. Give it to your client so they can track the package online or by phone.

Compare prices. For example, sending a 1kg parcel from Nairobi to Mombasa might cost KES 300 via post office, KES 500 via bus, and KES 1,200 via courier. For a 5kg parcel to London, DHL might charge KES 8,000, while the post office charges KES 4,000 but takes 2-3 weeks. Always get a quote first. If your client is price-sensitive, suggest the cheapest option. If they need it fast, use a courier. For local deliveries within a city, consider using a boda boda (motorcycle taxi) or a ride-hailing service like Uber or Bolt. They can deliver same-day for a small fee.

After shipping, send your client a photo of the receipt and tracking number. Follow up to ensure delivery. If the package is delayed or lost, file a claim. For lost items, you may need to fill out a claims form and wait weeks or months. Keep copies of everything. For incoming packages, you may need to pick them up from the post office or courier office. Bring your ID and the tracking number. If there are customs fees, pay them and get a receipt. Finally, keep a log of all shipments: date, recipient, tracking number, cost, and status. This helps you stay organized and avoid mistakes.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Grocery Shopping and Household Errands

Errand and personal assistant services are among the fastest-growing informal and semi-formal business opportunities across urban Africa. In Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, Kigali, and Dar es Salaam, busy professionals, dual-income households, and elderly clients increasingly outsource grocery runs, pharmacy pickups, and post office trips. This module gives you the practical systems, checklists, and safety habits that turn a casual favour into a reliable, billable service. Everything here is designed for the African context: open-air markets, neighbourhood dukas, supermarket chains, mobile money, and unpredictable transport.

As a business, your value is not just in carrying bags. It is in saving the client time, avoiding costly mistakes, protecting their money, and keeping a clean paper trail. Clients pay for trust and consistency. Build both deliberately.

1. Creating and Organizing Grocery Lists

A grocery list is a business document, not a scrap of paper. It protects you from disputes, prevents wasted trips, and lets you quote accurately. Always confirm the list with the client in writing (WhatsApp, SMS, or a shared note) before you leave. Group items by section of the store or market: fresh produce, dry goods, dairy and chilled, meat and fish, household cleaning, personal care, and baby items. For open markets, group by vendor type so you are not criss-crossing the whole market.

Record quantities precisely. "Tomatoes" is ambiguous; "2 kg tomatoes" is billable and verifiable. Note brand preferences and acceptable substitutes. If a client says "any cooking oil," clarify whether that includes the cheapest option or only a specific brand. Keep a running template for repeat clients so each weekly order takes minutes, not hours.

2. Understanding Dietary Restrictions and Preferences

Dietary mistakes are the fastest way to lose a client and the most dangerous to their health. Restrictions fall into three groups: medical (diabetes, hypertension, allergies, coeliac disease), religious or cultural (halal, kosher, fasting periods), and personal preference (vegetarian, vegan, low-carb). Never assume. Ask, write it down, and repeat it back.

In African households, common considerations include low-sodium diets for hypertension, reduced sugar for diabetes, and avoidance of specific allergens such as groundnuts or shellfish. Note that many processed foods in local markets carry limited labelling, so you may need to read ingredient lists carefully or ask the vendor. For severe allergies, treat cross-contamination as a real risk and buy from sealed, reputable sources.

3. Navigating Grocery Stores and Finding Items Quickly

Speed is money. Learn the layout of the two or three stores you serve most often, and memorise where staples sit. In supermarkets, the perimeter usually holds fresh produce, meat, dairy, and bakery; the centre aisles hold packaged and processed goods. Shop the perimeter first for fresh items, then move inward. In open markets, build relationships with regular vendors; they will hold stock, give fair prices, and alert you to fresh arrivals.

Use a store app or a saved aisle map if available. Keep a small notebook or phone note listing where unusual items are located. If you serve multiple clients, batch trips by geography and store to cut transport costs. Always carry a reusable bag and a small calculator or phone for price checks.

4. Handling Returns and Exchanges

Returns are a test of your professionalism. Keep every receipt. Most supermarkets in African cities allow returns within 7 to 14 days for non-perishable items with a receipt and original packaging. Perishables are usually non-returnable unless faulty. Know each store's policy before you promise a client anything.

When a client asks you to return an item, confirm the reason, the purchase date, and whether they have the receipt. Photograph the item and receipt before going to the store. If the store refuses, document the refusal in writing and report back promptly. Never refund a client from your own pocket without agreement; instead, follow the store's process and keep the client informed.

5. Pharmacy Pickups and Prescription Management

Pharmacy errands carry legal and health risks. In most African countries, prescription medicines require a valid prescription from a licensed practitioner. You may pick up a prescription on a client's behalf, but you should not advise on dosage, substitutes, or whether a medicine is safe. Confirm the client's name, the prescriber, and the exact medicine and strength.

Check expiry dates and packaging integrity before leaving the counter. Keep medicines in a cool, clean bag, separate from food. Store the receipt and a photo of the label for the client's records. If a medicine is out of stock, do not accept a substitute without the client's or pharmacist's approval. For controlled or sensitive medicines, follow local pharmacy rules strictly.

6. Post Office and Shipping Errands

Post office and courier errands require accuracy. Confirm the recipient's full name, address, and phone number, and the sender's details. Weigh or measure the parcel if the client has not done so. Ask for the correct service level: ordinary mail, registered mail, EMS, or private courier. Always obtain a tracking number and a receipt, and send both to the client immediately.

For international shipments, check customs declarations and prohibited items. Many African postal services restrict or ban certain goods, and incorrect declarations can delay or seize a parcel. Keep a copy of the declaration for your records.

SAFETY WARNING:

Never accept, carry, or deliver a parcel, envelope, or package whose contents you have not seen or that the client refuses to declare. You could be unknowingly transporting illegal goods, which carries serious legal consequences. For medicines, never accept a substitute or change a dosage without the prescribing clinician's or pharmacist's written approval. Always check expiry dates and packaging before leaving the counter. If a client asks you to do anything that feels unsafe or unlawful, refuse politely and document the request.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm the full order in writing before leaving, including quantities, brands, and acceptable substitutes.
  • Note all dietary, medical, religious, and allergy restrictions and repeat them back to the client.
  • Plan your route and budget; batch errands by store and location to save time and transport.
  • Carry a reusable bag, a phone for price checks, and a small notebook or note app.
  • Keep every receipt, and photograph items and receipts before returns or handover.
  • Verify medicine names, strengths, expiry dates, and packaging at the pharmacy counter.
  • Obtain and send tracking numbers and receipts immediately after shipping errands.
  • Record each job, client preference, and cost in a simple log for repeat business and accurate billing.
