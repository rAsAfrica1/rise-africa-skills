1. Cleaning and Dehulling of Oilseeds

Before you press any oilseed, you must clean it. Dirt, stones, sand, and plant trash will damage your expeller and make dark, smelly oil. Start by winnowing: pour seeds from one basket to another in a breeze to blow away chaff and light rubbish. For groundnuts, sorghum, and sunflower, a simple vibrating screen or a hand sieve with 2-3 mm holes removes fines and small stones. For larger impurities like sticks and metal, pass a magnet over the seed or use a destoner. A good target is less than 1% foreign matter.

Dehulling means removing the seed coat. For groundnuts, you can do this by hand rubbing after drying, or use a groundnut decorticator. For sunflower, the hull is tough; a roller mill or impact dehuller works. For sesame, dehulling is often skipped because the hull is thin and oil is high quality. For palm kernel, you crack the shell with a nutcracker or a palm kernel cracker and separate the kernel from shell in a salt water bath (shells float, kernels sink).

After dehulling, separate hulls from kernels. A winnowing fan or a vibrating screen does this. Hulls can be used for animal feed or fuel. If you leave hulls on, they absorb oil and reduce yield by 5-10%. For small-scale operations, a simple aspiration system (a fan pulling air through a cyclone) is enough.

Moisture matters. If seeds are too wet, they stick and clog. If too dry, they shatter and make too many fines. For groundnuts, aim for 6-8% moisture. Test by biting: the kernel should be firm but not brittle. For sunflower, 7-9% is good. Dry seeds in the sun on a clean tarp for 2-3 days, turning every few hours, or use a mechanical dryer at 40-50Â°C.

Finally, store cleaned and dehulled seeds in clean, dry sacks in a cool, dry place. Never store on bare ground. Use pallets. This preparation step is the foundation of good oil yield and quality. Skip it, and you lose money.

2. Size Reduction: Grinding and Flaking

Size reduction breaks oilseeds into smaller pieces so oil can escape easily during pressing. For groundnuts, you want to grind them into a coarse meal, not a fine powder. A hammer mill with a 4-6 mm screen works well. If you grind too fine, the meal turns into paste and clogs the expeller. For sunflower, you flake the seeds: pass them through a roller mill with a gap of 0.2-0.3 mm. The flakes should be thin, like cornflakes, so cells rupture and oil flows.

For copra (dried coconut), you chop it into small pieces then grind in a hammer mill. For palm kernel, you grind to a coarse grit. For sesame, you can grind lightly or skip grinding and press whole. The rule: soft seeds like groundnut need coarse grinding; hard seeds like sunflower and palm kernel need flaking or fine grinding.

A simple hand-cranked grinder can work for small batches, but for 100 kg per hour, you need a motorized mill. A 5 HP hammer mill can handle 200-300 kg per hour. Set the screen size: 4 mm for groundnut, 3 mm for copra, 2 mm for palm kernel. Always check the particle size: if you see whole seeds or large chunks, adjust the mill.

Flaking requires a roller mill with two smooth rollers. The gap should be slightly less than the seed thickness. For sunflower, a gap of 0.25 mm gives good flakes. For soybeans, 0.3 mm. If flakes are too thick, oil extraction is poor. If too thin, you get fines that clog. Run a test: take a handful of flakes and squeeze; they should feel flat and slightly oily.

After grinding or flaking, use the material immediately. Do not let it sit for hours because oil can oxidize and become rancid. If you must store, keep it in a cool, airtight container for no more than 24 hours. For best yield, go straight to conditioning.

3. Conditioning and Tempering of Oilseeds

Conditioning means adjusting the moisture and temperature of the prepared seed before pressing. This step can increase oil yield by 10-20%. The goal is to make the oil less viscous and the cell walls easier to rupture. For most seeds, you heat and add or remove moisture to reach a specific range.

For groundnuts, heat to 60-70Â°C and adjust moisture to 4-6%. You can do this in a steam-jacketed kettle or a simple frying pan over low fire, stirring constantly. If you don't have a kettle, use a double boiler: put the ground meal in a pot, set it over boiling water, and stir for 15-20 minutes. For sunflower, heat to 70-80Â°C and moisture 5-7%. For copra, heat to 80-90Â°C and moisture 3-5%. For palm kernel, heat to 90-100Â°C and moisture 2-3%. For sesame, 50-60Â°C and moisture 4-6%.

How to test moisture without a meter? Take a sample, weigh it, dry it in an oven at 105Â°C for 2 hours, and weigh again. The weight loss is moisture. Or use a cheap moisture meter (cost about $50). If you don't have either, use the hand squeeze test: for groundnut, the meal should clump when squeezed but not drip water. For sunflower, it should feel slightly damp but not sticky.

Tempering is letting the conditioned material rest for a short time (10-30 minutes) in a covered container so moisture spreads evenly. This is important because uneven moisture causes uneven pressing and lower yield. For small-scale, a insulated box or a sack wrapped in blankets works.

Do not overheat. Above 100Â°C, oil quality drops and you get a burnt taste. Use a thermometer. If you don't have one, water droplets on the pan should sizzle and evaporate, not dance. Practice with small batches. Conditioning is the difference between 30% yield and 45% yield.

4. Expeller and Screw Press Preparation

Before you feed conditioned material into an expeller or screw press, you must prepare the press itself. A dirty or worn press will give low yield and dark oil. Start by cleaning all parts: the barrel, screw, cage bars, and nozzle. Remove old cake and oil residue with a wire brush and a cloth soaked in kerosene or hot water. Never use water on the screw if it will rust; dry immediately.

Check the wear on the screw and cage bars. The screw flights should be sharp and not rounded. If the gap between screw and cage is more than 1 mm, oil will leak back and yield drops. Replace worn parts. For a small expeller (like a Komet or a Chinese-made 6YL-80), spare screws and bars are cheap; keep a set.

Set the nozzle or choke. The nozzle controls back pressure. For groundnut, start with a 6-8 mm nozzle. For sunflower, 8-10 mm. For copra, 10-12 mm. If oil comes out dark and cake is powdery, open the nozzle (larger hole). If cake is too hard and oil flow is slow, close it slightly. Adjust in small steps.

Preheat the press. Run it empty for 5-10 minutes to warm the barrel. Cold metal causes oil to solidify and clog. In cold weather, use a heat gun or wrap the barrel with a heating band. The ideal barrel temperature is 60-80Â°C for most seeds.

Feed the material slowly and steadily. Do not dump it in. Use a hopper with a controlled feed. If the press jams, stop immediately, reverse the screw if possible, and clear the blockage. Never put your hand in the press while it is running. For a motorized expeller, 10-15 kg per hour is typical for small models. For manual presses, 2-5 kg per hour.

After pressing, collect oil in a clean container. Let it settle for 24 hours, then filter. Clean the press immediately after use; hardened cake is hard to remove. A well-prepared press gives clear oil and dry cake.

5. Solvent Extraction Feed Preparation

Solvent extraction is used for seeds with low oil content (like soybeans, rice bran, cottonseed) or to recover residual oil from expeller cake. The feed to a solvent plant must be prepared carefully. First, the material must be flaked or ground to a uniform size. For soybeans, flakes should be 0.2-0.3 mm thick. For rice bran, grind to pass a 20-mesh screen. For cottonseed, dehull and flake to 0.25 mm.

Moisture is critical. The feed should be 8-12% moisture for most solvents. Too wet, and the solvent won't penetrate; too dry, and fines are created. Use a dryer or conditioner to adjust. For small-scale, you can sun-dry on a tarp, but for consistent results, use a rotary dryer.

Temperature also matters. The feed should be at 50-60Â°C when entering the extractor. This helps solvent penetrate and oil dissolve. Preheat the feed in a steam-jacketed conveyor or a simple heated trough. Do not exceed 70Â°C or you may degrade oil quality.

Remove fines. Fines clog the extractor and cause channeling. Use a vibrating screen or a cyclone to remove particles smaller than 0.5 mm. You can pelletize fines or mix them back into the main feed at a low ratio (less than 5%).

For solvent extraction, the feed must be free of stones, metal, and other debris. A magnet and a destoner are essential. Also, ensure the feed is not too oily. If oil content is above 20%, pre-press with an expeller to reduce oil to 12-15%. Then the solvent can extract the rest.

Finally, store prepared feed in a sealed bin under nitrogen or with a blanketing gas to prevent oxidation. Use within 24 hours. If you are a small producer, solvent extraction is usually not economical; it is for large-scale plants (above 50 tonnes per day). But if you send your cake to a solvent plant, prepare it as above to get a fair price.

6. Handling and Storage of Prepared Materials

Prepared oilseed materialsâcleaned, dehulled, ground, flaked, conditionedâare perishable. If you store them wrong, they absorb moisture, grow mold, and become rancid. The oil you worked to prepare will be lost. Follow these rules.

First, keep everything dry. Moisture above 10% invites mold and bacteria. For groundnut meal, target 6-8% moisture. For sunflower flakes, 7-9%. For copra, 5-7%. Use airtight containers: plastic drums with lids, metal bins, or heavy-duty polypropylene sacks with liners. Never use open baskets or torn sacks. Store on pallets, not on the floor, to avoid moisture from the ground.

Second, control temperature. Cool, dark conditions slow oxidation. Ideal storage is 15-25Â°C. Avoid direct sunlight and hot rooms. In hot African climates, store in a shaded, ventilated room. If you have a cold room, use it for high-value materials like sesame. Otherwise, use evaporative cooling: a damp cloth over a drum in a breezy place can drop temperature by 5-10Â°C.

Third, keep out pests. Rodents, insects, and birds love oilseeds. Use metal bins with tight lids. For sacks, stack them on shelves or pallets, not against walls. Use traps or cats. Never use rat poison near food materials. For insects, you can use food-grade diatomaceous earth (mix 1 kg per 100 kg of seed) or airtight storage (which suffocates insects).

Fourth, limit storage time. Prepared materials should be pressed within 24-48 hours for best yield and quality. If you must store longer, keep whole seeds instead of ground or flaked material. Whole seeds store for months. Ground meal stores for days. Flakes store for hours. So plan your production: prepare only what you can press in a day.

Fifth, label everything. Write the date, seed type, and moisture level on each container. Use the oldest first. Rotate stock. A simple notebook works. If you see clumping, smell mustiness, or see mold, discard the material. It will ruin your oil. Clean storage areas regularly. A clean, dry, cool store is as important as a good press.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Oilseed and Oilfruit Preparation

In African oil milling, the preparation stage decides your yield, your oil quality, and your margin. A tonne of groundnut, sunflower, sesame, cottonseed, palm kernel, or shea nut will give you between 5% and 20% more recoverable oil if it is cleaned, dehulled, size-reduced, conditioned, and fed to the press correctly. This lesson is a shop-floor reference for operators, supervisors, and small-to-medium processors who want measurable results â not theory.

Treat preparation as a production line, not a series of isolated tasks. Each step changes the physical state of the seed so the next step works. Skip one, and you pay for it twice: once in lost oil, and again in downtime, filter changes, and rejected cake.

1. Cleaning and Dehulling of Oilseeds

Cleaning removes stones, sand, metal, plant stalks, and immature or mouldy seed. In West and East Africa, groundnut and sesame lots often arrive with 2â8% foreign matter. Every 1% of trash you leave in is 1% of press capacity you waste, plus a wear penalty on your expeller barrel and screw.

Basic kit: a vibrating or rotary sieve, a magnetic separator, and a destoner. For sesame and shea, add aspiration to lift light chaff. For cottonseed, delinting (acid or mechanical) must happen before dehulling, otherwise the linters wrap the beater bars.

Dehulling matters most for seeds with high hull content: sunflower, cottonseed, shea, and palm kernel. Hulls absorb oil, lower cake protein, and blunt press parts. Target hull residue below 5% for sunflower and below 3% for cottonseed kernel. Use impact cracking followed by air classification and a gravity separator.

2. Size Reduction: Grinding and Flaking

Small-scale African mills usually grind with a hammer or disc mill to a coarse meal. Industrial lines flake to 0.25â0.35 mm thickness on smooth rolls. Flaking ruptures oil cells and gives the solvent or press a short, uniform path to the oil.

Rule of thumb: the thinner and more uniform the flake, the faster the extraction, but flakes below 0.20 mm crumble and choke percolation beds in solvent plants. For expeller operations, a coarse grind (2â4 mm) is usually enough â over-grinding creates fines that blind the cage bars and raise oil in cake.

3. Conditioning and Tempering of Oilseeds

Conditioning adjusts moisture and temperature so the seed is pliable and oil viscosity drops. Typical targets: groundnut 8â10% moisture at 60â80 Â°C; sunflower 8â9% at 70â90 Â°C; shea kernel 6â8% at 60â70 Â°C. Too dry and the press cake crumbles; too wet and you get slippage, low throughput, and cloudy oil.

Use a vertical stack cooker or a simple jacketed kettle with steam or indirect fire. Hold times of 20â40 minutes are common. Always measure with a calibrated moisture meter and a probe thermometer â don't guess by feel.

4. Expeller and Screw Press Preparation

Before feeding the expeller, confirm: moisture in range, temperature in range, feed rate steady, and cake thickness set. A warm start (pre-heated barrel) reduces torque spikes and improves first-pass yield. Keep spare screws, cages, and collars on site â in many African markets these take weeks to import.

5. Solvent Extraction Feed Preparation

For solvent plants, prepared flakes or collets must be uniform, low in fines, and at the right thickness and moisture (typically 8â10%). Fines reduce percolation and increase solvent hold-up. Expand or extrude where the budget allows â it improves drainage and lowers residual oil in meal.

6. Handling and Storage of Prepared Materials

Prepared material is perishable. Store in clean, dry silos or bins, protect from rain and rodents, and use first-in-first-out. Free fatty acids (FFA) rise fast in hot, humid African conditions â aflatoxin risk in groundnut and cottonseed rises with poor storage. Never store prepared meal for more than a few days before pressing.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Sample every incoming lot. Record moisture, foreign matter, and visual defects before unloading.
  2. Clean before you dehull. Remove stones and metal first â they destroy cracking rolls and press screws.
  3. Set dehulling targets. Hull residue <5% (sunflower) and <3% (cottonseed kernel).
  4. Match grind to process. Coarse (2â4 mm) for expeller; flake 0.25â0.35 mm for solvent extraction.
  5. Condition by measurement, not feel. Use a moisture meter and probe thermometer every batch.
  6. Pre-heat the expeller and check cake thickness, feed rate, and amp draw at start-up.
  7. Control fines in solvent feed. Screen out fines; consider expander/extruder if percolation is poor.
  8. Store prepared material short-term. Dry, clean, rodent-proof, FIFO â press within days, not weeks.
