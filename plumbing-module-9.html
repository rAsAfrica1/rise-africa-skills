1. Module 9 Lesson 1: Setting Up a Profitable Plumbing Business in Africa

This lesson opens Module 9 by helping you move from being a skilled handyman to running a real plumbing business. Across Africa, demand for reliable plumbers is growing fast because of rapid urbanisation, new housing estates, hotels, schools, hospitals and borehole installations. But many skilled plumbers remain poor because they never separate the trade from the business. You will learn how to structure your enterprise, price work correctly, manage cash flow and win repeat customers. Start by deciding your legal identity. In most countries you can register a sole proprietorship cheaply, or a limited company if you want bigger contracts. Register with the revenue authority, obtain a business permit from your local council, and open a separate business bank account or mobile money merchant account. Never mix personal and business money. Your business name should be simple and memorable, for example Kigali Pipe Solutions or Accra Flow Masters. Next, define your services. A general plumber does repairs, installations, leak detection, drain cleaning, water tank connection, pump installation and bathroom fitting. You can specialise later in high-value areas such as solar water heating, borehole pump systems, or commercial kitchen drainage. Write a one-page service list with clear prices. Pricing is where most African plumbers lose money. Do not charge only for time. Charge for the job, the materials, the risk and the travel. Use a simple formula: labour cost plus material cost plus transport plus 30 to 50 percent profit margin. For emergency night calls, double your rate. For contracts, quote per fixture or per metre of pipe. Always put your quote in writing, even if it is a text message, and ask for a deposit of 50 percent before buying materials. This protects you from clients who refuse to pay. Cash flow kills plumbing businesses. You buy pipes, fittings, cement and tanks upfront, then wait for payment. To survive, collect deposits, buy materials only after deposit, keep a small stock of fast-moving items such as elbows, tees, valves, thread tape and glue, and follow up invoices within seven days. Use mobile money to receive payments and keep records. A simple notebook or free phone app can track income and expenses. Separate your tax money immediately. Marketing must be deliberate. Word of mouth is powerful but slow. Create a free business profile on Google Maps and WhatsApp Business. Take before and after photos of every job. Ask satisfied customers for a short testimonial and permission to share it. Print simple business cards and flyers. Visit hardware shops, real estate agents, landlords, hotel managers and borehole drillers. Offer them a referral fee of 5 to 10 percent. Join local plumber associations if they exist. They can connect you to tenders and training. Your reputation is your biggest asset. Always arrive on time, wear clean overalls, protect floors with old sacks, test your work with the client watching, and clean up before leaving. Give a written guarantee of at least three months on workmanship. If something fails, fix it free and fast. That single habit builds a queue of repeat clients. Finally, think about growth. Hire an apprentice when you have more work than you can handle. Pay them fairly and train them in your standards. Buy a second-hand pickup or motorcycle with a box for tools. Keep a tool inventory and lock it up. Insure your vehicle and consider public liability insurance for commercial jobs. Set a monthly revenue target and review it every week. In Africa, the plumber who is trustworthy, reachable and businesslike will never lack work. This lesson gives you the foundation. The next lessons will sharpen your technical and financial systems.

2. Module 9 Lesson 2: Water Supply Systems, Tanks and Pumps for African Conditions

This lesson covers the heart of plumbing in Africa: getting water from the source to the tap reliably, even when municipal supply is intermittent. You will learn how to design and install domestic and small commercial water systems using tanks, pumps, pressure controls and filtration. Most African homes and businesses rely on a combination of mains water, boreholes, rainwater harvesting and tanker deliveries. Your job is to make these sources work together without contamination or waste. Start with storage. A typical family of six needs at least 1000 to 2000 litres of storage to survive two to three days of outage. For a guesthouse with ten rooms, plan 5000 to 10000 litres. Use food-grade polyethylene tanks or lined concrete tanks. Never use open drums or old chemical containers. Site tanks on a strong, level concrete slab, away from toilets, drains and animal pens. Raise them on a stand so the outlet is above the highest tap if you want gravity flow, or install a pump for pressure. Gravity gives about 0.1 bar per metre of height, so a tank three metres above a tap gives weak but usable pressure. For decent showers you need 1 to 2 bar, which means a pump. Next, learn pump types. A centrifugal pump is good for moving large volumes at low pressure. A jet pump can self-prime and is common for shallow wells. A submersible pump sits inside a borehole or tank and pushes water up. A booster pump increases pressure in a building. Choose the pump by flow rate in litres per minute and head in metres. Head is the vertical height plus friction losses in pipes. A rough rule is to add 10 percent head for every 10 metres of horizontal pipe and 20 percent for elbows and fittings. Always install a pressure tank and pressure switch so the pump does not start every time a tap opens. Set the cut-in pressure at about 1.5 bar and cut-out at 3 bar for domestic systems. Protect the pump with a dry-run protector, a surge protector and a non-return valve. Electrical safety is critical. Use a qualified electrician, earth the pump, install an isolator switch and use a circuit breaker. In areas with unstable power, add a surge protector or a small solar pump system. Solar pumps are increasingly affordable and perfect for rural homes, farms and clinics. They need a solar panel array, a controller and a DC or AC pump. Size the panels to run the pump for at least five hours of sunshine. Now consider water quality. Borehole water may contain iron, manganese, fluoride, salt or bacteria. Tank water can grow algae and mosquitoes if left open. Always cover tanks, screen vents and overflow pipes with mosquito mesh, and clean tanks every six months. For drinking water, install a sediment filter, then a carbon filter, then a UV or ceramic filter. Test water at a certified laboratory before recommending treatment. Do not guess. For hard water, use a water softener to protect geysers and pipes. For acidic water, use a neutralising filter. Pipe sizing matters. Use the correct diameter so you do not lose pressure. A 22 mm pipe can serve a few taps. A 28 mm or 35 mm main line is better for a whole house or small block. Use uPVC or HDPE for cold water and PPR or copper for hot water. Support pipes properly, allow for expansion, and install isolation valves on every branch so you can repair without shutting the whole building. Label every valve. Commissioning is your final quality check. Fill the tank, open all taps to flush air, check for leaks, test pressure, test the pump cut-in and cut-out, and record the results. Show the client how to switch off the pump and close the main valve in an emergency. Give them a simple maintenance schedule. In Africa, a well-designed water system means the difference between a happy client and a flooded compound. Master these principles and you will win the most valuable jobs.

3. Module 9 Lesson 3: Drainage, Sewerage and Sanitation Solutions

This lesson tackles drainage and sanitation, the area where poor plumbing causes the most disease, damage and disputes in African communities. You will learn how to design, install and maintain drains, septic systems, soakaways and biogas toilets that work in low-water, high-density and off-grid settings. Start with the golden rule: waste water must flow downhill, and sewer gas must not enter the building. Every drain needs a fall of at least 1 in 60 for small pipes and 1 in 100 for larger ones. If you cannot achieve fall, use a pump or a raised toilet system. Never run a drain uphill. Use the correct pipe sizes. A wash basin needs 32 mm, a sink 40 mm, a toilet 100 mm, and a main sewer line 100 to 150 mm. Use uPVC pipes with rubber ring joints or solvent cement joints. Support pipes every metre and protect them from vehicles with concrete or steel plates. Every fixture must have a trap to hold a water seal. A P-trap or bottle trap stops sewer gas. Every trap must have a vent pipe that goes up through the roof or to an air admittance valve. Without venting, traps gurgle and lose their seal. Install a rodding eye or inspection chamber at every change of direction and every 15 metres of straight run. Inspection chambers should be 600 by 600 mm with a removable cover. Now consider sewage disposal. In cities with a sewer network, connect to the public sewer with the correct gradient and a boundary trap. In areas without sewers, you need an on-site system. A septic tank is a watertight chamber that separates solids, scum and liquid. A three-chamber tank is best. Size it by the number of users: allow 200 litres per person per day and at least two days retention. A tank for ten people should be about 4000 litres. The outlet goes to a soakaway or drain field. A soakaway is a pit filled with stones and covered with soil. It must be at least 30 metres from any well, borehole or river, and at least 15 metres from a building. If the soil is clay or the water table is high, a soakaway will fail. In that case use a raised drain field, a mound system, or a small package treatment plant. For high-density areas, a biogas digester is an excellent option. It treats human waste and produces gas for cooking. A fixed-dome or plastic bag digester can serve a family or a small school. The effluent still needs a soakaway or garden irrigation, but the pathogen load is much lower. Always wear gloves and boots when working with sewage. Wash hands with soap. Do not eat or smoke on site. Ventilate manholes before entering. Never enter a septic tank or manhole without a gas detector and a harness. Hydrogen sulphide kills. For toilets, choose the right pan. A sitting pan is easier for the elderly and disabled. A squatting pan uses less water and is culturally preferred in many communities. A pour-flush pan with a water seal is a good compromise. For dry areas, consider a urine-diverting dry toilet or a composting toilet. These save water and produce fertiliser, but they need user training and careful management. Greywater from sinks, showers and laundry should not go into a septic tank in large quantities because it kills the bacteria. Divert greywater to a separate soakaway, a garden irrigation system, or a grease trap first. A grease trap is essential for restaurants and butcheries. It stops fat from blocking drains. Clean it weekly. Maintenance is where most systems fail. Teach clients to avoid flushing sanitary pads, nappies, wipes, condoms, plastics and cooking oil. These cause blockages. Show them how to use a plunger and a drain snake. Recommend a yearly inspection of septic tanks and a desludging every three to five years. Keep a record of every drain you install, including pipe sizes, depths and inspection chamber locations. That record will save you hours on future repairs and will impress clients. In Africa, proper sanitation is not just plumbing. It is public health, dignity and business. A plumber who understands drainage will always be in demand.

4. Module 9 Lesson 4: Hot Water Systems, Solar Geysers and Energy Efficiency

This lesson focuses on hot water, one of the biggest energy costs and comfort expectations in African homes, guesthouses and clinics. You will learn how to install, size and maintain electric geysers, solar water heaters, heat pumps and instant heaters, with a strong emphasis on safety and energy savings. Start with demand. A person uses about 30 to 50 litres of hot water per day for bathing, washing and cleaning. A family of five needs 150 to 250 litres of hot water storage. A guesthouse room needs 50 to 80 litres. A small clinic needs 200 to 500 litres. Size the geyser slightly larger than demand so recovery is not a problem. Now learn the types. An electric storage geyser is a tank with one or two heating elements and a thermostat. It is simple and common but expensive to run. A solar geyser uses solar collectors to heat water and a tank to store it. It can save 50 to 80 percent of water heating costs. A heat pump works like a refrigerator in reverse, moving heat from the air into the water. It is efficient but needs electricity and space. An instant or tankless heater heats water only when a tap opens. It saves standby losses but needs high electrical power and may not work with low pressure. For most African homes, a solar geyser with an electric backup element is the best choice. There are two main solar collector types. Flat plate collectors are cheaper and good in sunny areas. Evacuated tube collectors are more efficient in cloudy or cold conditions but more fragile and expensive. The tank can be close-coupled on the roof or split with the tank at ground level. Split systems look better and put less weight on the roof, but they need a pump and controller. Always install the collector facing the equator: north in the southern hemisphere, south in the northern hemisphere. Tilt the collector at an angle equal to your latitude plus 10 degrees for year-round performance. Avoid shade from trees and buildings, especially between 9 am and 3 pm. Safety is non-negotiable. Hot water can scald. Set the thermostat to 60 degrees Celsius to kill bacteria, then install a thermostatic mixing valve at the outlet to deliver 50 degrees or less to taps. This prevents burns, especially for children and the elderly. Every geyser must have a temperature and pressure relief valve, a vacuum breaker and an expansion tank or a drip tray with a drain. Never block a relief valve. Never install a geyser without a drip tray and overflow pipe. Electrical work must be done by a licensed electrician. Use the correct cable size, a dedicated circuit breaker, an isolator switch and proper earthing. In areas with power surges, add a surge protector. Bond all metal pipes and the geyser body to earth. For solar systems, protect the controller and pump with a surge protector and a dry-run protector. Pipe insulation is cheap and pays back fast. Insulate all hot water pipes, especially the first three metres from the geyser. Insulate the geyser itself with a blanket if it is old. Fix leaks immediately. A dripping hot tap wastes hundreds of litres and energy every month. Install low-flow shower heads and aerators on taps. These save water and reduce the amount of hot water used. For large buildings, consider a central hot water system with a circulation loop. A circulation pump keeps hot water at the tap, reducing waiting time and water waste. Insulate the loop and set the pump on a timer. Maintenance keeps systems safe and efficient. Flush the geyser every six months to remove sediment. Check the anode rod every two years and replace it if it is more than half consumed. Test the relief valve every six months. Clean solar collector glass and check for leaks. For heat pumps, clean the air filter and check refrigerant pressure. Keep a service log for every client. This builds trust and creates recurring income. Finally, think about the business case. Energy costs are rising across Africa. A solar geyser can pay for itself in two to four years. Offer clients a simple payback calculation. Show them the monthly savings. Offer a maintenance contract for an annual fee. That recurring revenue stabilises your business. A plumber who understands hot water and energy efficiency is not just a repairer. They are a trusted advisor who saves clients money every month.

5. Module 9 Lesson 5: Business Systems, Pricing, Records and Scaling Your Plumbing Enterprise

This final lesson of Module 9 brings everything together into systems that let you grow from a one-person operation to a respected plumbing enterprise with employees, vehicles and contracts. You will learn how to price jobs scientifically, keep records, manage staff, win tenders and protect your business legally. Start with pricing. Many plumbers guess a price and then lose money. Use a cost-plus method. First, calculate direct costs: materials, transport, labour, tool wear and waste. Add 10 percent for unexpected items. Then add overhead: rent, phone, insurance, vehicle, taxes and your own salary. Then add profit of 20 to 40 percent. For example, if a bathroom installation costs 500 in materials, 200 in transport and 300 in labour, direct cost is 1000. Add 10 percent contingency, 100, giving 1100. Add 20 percent overhead, 220, giving 1320. Add 30 percent profit, 396, giving a final price of 1716. This is a fair, defensible price. Write it in a quotation with a clear scope, exclusions, payment terms and validity period. Never start work without a signed or text-confirmed acceptance and a deposit. For maintenance contracts, charge a monthly or annual fee for inspections, priority response and discounts on parts. This gives you predictable income. Records are your memory and your proof. Keep a job file for every client with the quotation, photos, materials list, labour hours, invoices and warranty. Use a simple notebook or a free app. Record every expense, even small ones. Reconcile your mobile money and bank accounts weekly. Track your debtors and follow up politely but firmly. At the end of each month, calculate your revenue, costs and profit. If profit is less than 20 percent, review your prices. If a client is always late, charge a late fee or require full payment upfront. Staff management is the next step. When you hire, use a written contract with job description, pay, hours, leave and notice period. Pay fairly and on time. Train your apprentices in your standards. Give them a uniform, a tool kit and a checklist. Inspect their work. Reward good performance with a bonus or a promotion. Never send an untrained person to a job alone. Your reputation is on the line. For vehicles, keep a logbook for fuel, service and repairs. Insure the vehicle and the tools. Keep a basic first aid kit and safety gear. For tenders, register your business with the relevant authorities and keep your tax clearance, registration certificate and references ready. Read tender documents carefully. Attend site visits. Ask questions in writing. Submit a complete bid with a realistic price. Do not underprice to win. A loss-making contract can destroy your business. Legal protection matters. Have a simple contract template for large jobs. Include scope, price, payment schedule, timeline, variations, warranty, dispute resolution and termination. Consider public liability insurance for commercial work. Keep your tax records. Pay your taxes. It protects you from penalties and makes your business bankable. Marketing should be systematic. Ask every happy client for a review and a referral. Post before and after photos on WhatsApp status, Facebook and Google Business Profile. Offer a seasonal service such as geyser flushing before winter or tank cleaning before the rainy season. Partner with real estate agents, hardware stores, borehole drillers and solar installers. Give them a referral fee. Measure your marketing. Ask every new client how they heard about you. Double down on what works. Finally, plan your growth. Set a one-year goal, a three-year goal and a five-year goal. Maybe you want ten employees, three vehicles and a workshop. Break those goals into monthly actions. Save 10 percent of profit for equipment. Invest in training. Read one business book per quarter. Join a plumber association. Mentor a young plumber. In Africa, the plumbing industry is full of opportunity for those who are skilled, honest, organised and business-minded. This module has given you the technical and business tools. Now go and build something that lasts.

<h3>Compliance and Certification: What African Markets Actually Require</h3>

<p>Plumbing regulation across Africa varies widely, but the direction of travel is consistent: national building codes increasingly reference international standards (ISO, BS EN, SANS) and require licensed practitioners. In Kenya, the National Construction Authority (NCA) registers contractors and the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) certifies pipes. In Nigeria, the Council for the Regulation of Engineering in Nigeria (COREN) and SON (Standards Organisation of Nigeria) play equivalent roles. In South Africa, the South African Bureau of Standards (SANS 10252) governs water supply and drainage installations, and the PIRB (Plumbing Industry Registration Board) licenses plumbers.</p>

<p>For a plumbing business, the practical takeaway is this: <strong>certification is a sales asset</strong>. Clients especially NGOs, clinics, schools and hotels increasingly require proof of compliance before awarding contracts. Budget for registration, keep certificates on file, and quote your compliance status in every tender.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="font-family:Arial; font-size:14px; line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Size pipes to fixture demand</strong> using Table 1, then add 20% headroom never guess.</li>
<li><strong>Match material to application</strong> using Table 2; uPVC for cold and drainage, PPR for hot and cold indoors, copper for premium hygiene jobs.</li>
<li><strong>Install a non-return valve</strong> on every pumped line and an air gap or RPZ on every non-potable connection.</li>
<li><strong>Pressure-test every new line</strong> at 1.5 working pressure for at least 1 hour before concealing or handover.</li>
<li><strong>Disinfect potable lines</strong> with 50 mg/L chlorine for 24 hours, flush, and record residual chlorine.</li>
<li><strong>Keep certificates current</strong> NCA, COREN, PIRB, KEBS or your national equivalent and attach them to tenders.</li>
<li><strong>Document every job</strong> with photos of pipe routes, test results, and material batch numbers for warranty claims.</li>
<li><strong>Price compliance into the quote</strong> testing, disinfection and certification are billable line items, not free extras.</li>
</ul>
