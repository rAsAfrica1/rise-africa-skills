1. Identifying High-Yielding Yam Varieties

High-yielding yam varieties can produce 20 to 30 tonnes per hectare, compared to local varieties that often yield only 8 to 12 tonnes. In West Africa, the most common species are White Yam (Dioscorea rotundata), Yellow Yam (D. cayenensis), Water Yam (D. alata), and Trifoliate Yam (D. dumetorum). White yam is preferred for pounded yam and has varieties like 'TDr 89/02665' (often called 'TDr 89') that mature in 8-9 months and yield up to 25 tonnes/ha. Yellow yam, such as 'Puna' and 'Lokoja', matures in 10-12 months and stores well. Water yam varieties like 'TDa 291' and 'TDa 98/01176' are high-yielding but need more moisture. Trifoliate yam is drought-tolerant and matures in 7-8 months.

To identify a high-yielding variety, look at the leaves, stems, and tubers. White yam has dark green, heart-shaped leaves and a spiny stem; its tubers are cylindrical with white flesh. Yellow yam has yellow flesh and a more branched tuber. Water yam has broad, winged stems and tubers with brown skin and white or purple flesh. Ask local extension officers or visit a research station like IITA in Ibadan, Nigeria, or CSIR-CRI in Ghana to see demonstration plots. Yield is also influenced by soil fertility, spacing, and staking. Plant on ridges or mounds at 1 m by 1 m spacing for large tubers. Use 2.5 to 3 tonnes of seed yams per hectare.

Record your harvest: weigh tubers from a 10-plant sample and multiply by the total plant population. If you plant 10,000 plants and each yields 2.5 kg, you get 25 tonnes/ha. Compare varieties over two seasons before deciding. Avoid mixing varieties in one plot because they have different maturity times. High-yielding varieties are not always the best for your marketsome consumers prefer specific tastes or textures. Test small plots first.

2. Selecting Healthy Seed Yams (Setts) for Planting

Healthy seed yams, also called setts, are the foundation of a good harvest. A good sett weighs 200 to 500 grams, is firm, and has at least two viable buds (eyes). Never plant a sett that is soft, wrinkled, or has holes from yam beetles. Cut setts from the head (proximal end) of the tuber because it has more buds and sprouts faster. Use a sharp, clean knife or machete; dip it in 10% bleach solution between cuts to prevent disease spread. Each sett should be 5 to 10 cm long and 3 to 5 cm thick. For ware yam production, use whole small tubers (200-300 g) or cut larger tubers into setts. For seed yam production, use smaller setts (80-150 g) to multiply seed.

After cutting, treat setts to prevent rot. Dip them in a fungicide solution such as Benlate (benomyl) at 2 g per litre of water for 5 minutes, or use wood ash or a slurry of cow dung. Then allow them to dry in a shaded, well-ventilated area for 24 to 48 hours. This healing process (curing) forms a protective layer. Plant within 3 days. If you must store, keep setts in a cool, dry place with good airflownever in sealed plastic bags. Inspect regularly and remove any rotting setts.

Select setts from healthy mother plants that showed no signs of mosaic virus, anthracnose, or nematode damage. Avoid setts from tubers that were stored for more than 4 months, as they lose vigour. For best results, buy certified seed yams from approved sources. If you save your own seed, select tubers from your best-performing plants, not from the market. A good practice is to plant a seed yam multiplication plot each year, using small setts to produce clean seed for the next season.

3. Seed Yam Treatment and Storage

Proper treatment and storage of seed yams reduce losses from rot, insects, and sprouting. After harvest, cure seed yams in a shaded, well-ventilated shed for 7 to 14 days. Spread them in a single layer on wooden pallets or dry grassnever on bare ground. Curing heals wounds and thickens the skin. Then treat with a fungicide-insecticide mixture: for example, mix 50 g of benomyl (Benlate) and 50 g of actellic (pirimiphos-methyl) in 10 litres of water. Dip tubers for 2 minutes, then dry in shade. This controls storage rot and yam beetles. Alternatively, use neem leaf extract: pound 1 kg of fresh neem leaves, soak in 5 litres of water for 24 hours, strain, and dip tubers for 5 minutes.

Store seed yams in a yam barn, which is a wooden or bamboo structure with shelves, raised off the ground and thatched for shade. Keep temperature between 15C and 20C and relative humidity at 60-70%. In the barn, place tubers in single layers on shelves, not piled. Inspect every two weeks and remove any sprouting, rotting, or insect-damaged tubers. Sprouting can be reduced by storing in darkness and cool conditions. If you have only a few tubers, store them in a ventilated basket in a dark room. Never store seed yams in the same room as onions or potatoesthey release gases that promote sprouting.

For long-term storage (4-6 months), you can use a pit storage method: dig a pit 1 m deep, line with dry grass, place tubers in layers, cover with more grass and soil, leaving a small ventilation hole. Check monthly. Another option is cold storage at 12-15C if available. Always keep records: date of storage, number of tubers, and any treatments. Before planting, re-inspect and discard any soft or mouldy setts. Healthy seed yams should be firm, with no odour, and buds intact.

4. Certified Seed Sources and Quality Assurance

Certified seed yams are produced under strict quality control by national research institutions and registered seed growers. In Nigeria, the National Agricultural Seeds Council (NASC) certifies seed yams through the Seed Certification and Quality Control Service. In Ghana, the Plant Protection and Regulatory Services Directorate (PPRSD) does the same. Certified seed comes with a blue or green label showing the variety name, lot number, germination percentage (for yams, this is sprouting percentage), and date of testing. Always buy from authorised dealers, research institutes (IITA, CSIR-CRI, NCRI), or accredited seed companies. Avoid buying from open markets unless you know the source.

Quality assurance checks include: (1) physical purityno stones, soil, or other varieties; (2) healthfree from visible disease symptoms like mosaic, anthracnose, or nematode galls; (3) vigourfirm tubers with intact buds; and (4) trueness to typetubers match the variety description. For yams, a certified seed lot must have less than 2% disease incidence and less than 5% off-types. Ask for a certificate of analysis from the seller. If buying in bulk, take a random sample of 10 tubers and cut them open to check for internal rot or brown discolouration.

Certified seed yams cost moreoften 2 to 3 times the price of farmer-saved seedbut they give higher yields and reduce disease risk. For example, a 100 kg bag of certified TDr 89 setts may cost 30,000 to 45,000 Naira (about $40-60), while farmer-saved seed might cost 10,000 to 15,000 Naira. However, certified seed can increase yield by 30-50%, making it profitable. To ensure quality, buy from a seller who can show you the seed production plot. Join a seed growers' cooperative to access certified seed at lower prices. Always keep your receipt and the label for traceability.

5. Adapting Varieties to Local Conditions

No single yam variety is best everywhere. You must match the variety to your local climate, soil, and market. Rainfall is key: white yam (D. rotundata) needs 1000-1500 mm of rain and a 7-9 month growing season. Water yam (D. alata) needs more than 1500 mm and can tolerate waterlogging, making it suitable for swampy areas. Yellow yam (D. cayenensis) is more drought-tolerant and does well in forest-savanna transition zones. Trifoliate yam (D. dumetorum) is the most drought-tolerant and can grow in drier savannas with 800-1000 mm rainfall. If you are in a dry area, choose early-maturing varieties like 'TDr 89' (8 months) or trifoliate yam (7 months).

Soil type matters: yams prefer deep, well-drained loamy soils with pH 5.5-6.5. Heavy clay soils cause tuber deformation. If your soil is sandy, add organic matter like poultry manure (5-10 tonnes/ha) to improve water holding. For acidic soils (pH below 5.5), apply lime at 1-2 tonnes/ha before planting. In areas with nematode problems, plant resistant varieties such as 'TDr 95/19177' or rotate with cereals. Also consider market preference: in some regions, consumers want white yam for pounded yam; in others, water yam is preferred for boiling. Visit your local market and ask traders which varieties sell best.

Test new varieties on a small plot (0.1 ha) for one season before expanding. Compare yield, disease resistance, and storage life. Keep records of rainfall, soil type, and inputs. If a variety fails, try another. Participate in farmer field schools or extension demonstrations to see how different varieties perform. Adapting varieties is not just about yieldit's about reducing risk and meeting local demand. For example, in the derived savanna of Nigeria, farmers often plant a mix of white yam and yellow yam to spread harvest and market risks.

6. Cost and Availability of Different Seed Yams

Seed yam costs vary by variety, size, and source. In West Africa, a 100 kg bag of farmer-saved white yam setts (200-300 g each) costs between 10,000 and 20,000 Naira ($13-27) in Nigeria, or 150-300 Ghana Cedis ($13-26) in Ghana. Certified seed yams of improved varieties like TDr 89 or TDa 291 cost 30,000 to 50,000 Naira ($40-67) per 100 kg. Yellow yam seed is often cheaper, 8,000-15,000 Naira per 100 kg, because it is less popular. Water yam seed is intermediate, 15,000-25,000 Naira. Trifoliate yam seed is scarce and can cost 20,000-30,000 Naira per 100 kg. Prices are higher during planting season (February-April) and lower after harvest (November-January).

Availability depends on your location. In Nigeria, major seed yam producing areas include Benue, Taraba, and Nasarawa states. In Ghana, the northern regions (Northern, Upper East, Upper West) produce most seed yams. If you are far from these areas, transport costs add 10-20%. You can also produce your own seed yams by planting small setts (80-150 g) in a dedicated plot. This reduces cost and ensures quality. A 0.1 ha seed yam plot can produce 1,000-1,500 seed yams (about 200-300 kg) in one season, enough to plant 0.5-0.7 ha of ware yam. Start with certified seed once, then multiply.

To reduce costs, join a cooperative or farmer group to buy in bulk. Some research institutes sell seed yams at subsidised prices to farmers. For example, IITA sells breeder seed to registered seed growers, who then multiply and sell to farmers. Always compare prices from at least three sources. Remember that cheap seed from unknown sources often carries disease and gives poor yields. Invest in quality seedit is the cheapest input in the long run. Keep a record of your seed costs per hectare to calculate profit.

7. Practical Reference

<h2>Practical Reference: Yam Varieties and Seed Selection</h2>

<p>Yam (<em>Dioscorea</em> spp.) is one of West Africa's most valuable food and cash crops, with Nigeria alone producing roughly two-thirds of global output. For the commercial grower, however, the difference between profit and loss is decided long before planting: it is decided by <strong>which variety you choose</strong> and <strong>the quality of the seed yams (setts) you put in the ground</strong>. Seed material typically represents 3050% of total yam production costs, making variety and seed selection the single highest-leverage decision in the yam value chain.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons of this module into a working field and business guide. Use it alongside the individual lessons as a decision-support tool.</p>

<h3>1. Varieties: Matching Market to Field</h3>

<p>Yam varieties fall into market-driven categories. <em>Dioscorea rotundata</em> (white yam) dominates West African commercial trade, with popular improved and landrace varieties including TDr 89/02665, TDr 95/19177, TDr 95/18544, and the widely grown landraces like Efuru, Nwaopoko, and Amula. <em>D. alata</em> (water yam) offers higher yields and better storage under some conditions, while <em>D. cayenensis</em> (yellow yam) commands premium prices in certain urban markets due to its colour and taste.</p>

<p>For business planning, the key is not "which variety is best" in the abstract, but which variety delivers the highest <strong>net margin per hectare</strong> for your target market. Pounded yam processors pay a premium for high-dry-matter white yam; fryers and chip processors favour varieties with low sugar and high starch; exporters need uniform tuber shape and consistent sizing.</p>

<h3>2. Seed Yam Selection: The Quality Filters</h3>

<p>Healthy seed yams (setts) determine germination, vigour, and final yield. The following filters should be applied at purchase and again at planting:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Size:</strong> 200400 g setts are optimal for commercial production; smaller setts reduce yield, larger setts waste money.</li>
<li><strong>Shape:</strong> Uniform, cylindrical setts with at least two viable buds (eyes).</li>
<li><strong>Skin:</strong> Free from cracks, rot, nematode galls, and mealybug colonies.</li>
<li><strong>Flesh:</strong> Creamy-white to pale yellow, firm, no brown discolouration (indicating infection).</li>
<li><strong>Age:</strong> 35 months post-harvest; very old setts have low viability, very fresh setts may not have broken dormancy.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. Seed Treatment and Storage</h3>

<p>Treatment reduces losses from fungal rot, nematodes, and insects. Common practices include dipping setts in a fungicide-insecticide slurry (e.g., benomyl + chlorpyrifos) or, for organic growers, coating with wood ash and neem extract. After treatment, setts should be <strong>cured</strong> in a shaded, ventilated area for 714 days to allow wound healing before storage or planting.</p>

<p>Storage must be in a well-ventilated, raised barn or crib, protected from rodents and direct sunlight. Properly stored seed yams can maintain viability for 35 months with losses under 10%. Poor storage can cause 3050% losses a direct hit to your input budget.</p>

<p style="margin:0;color:#c0392b;font-weight:bold;"> SAFETY WARNING: Pesticide Handling</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;color:#333;">Fungicides and insecticides used in sett treatment are <strong>toxic</strong>. Always wear gloves, boots, and a face mask. Follow national pesticide regulations (e.g., NAFDAC in Nigeria, EPA in Ghana).</p>

<h3>4. Certified Seed Sources and Quality Assurance</h3>

<p>Africa's certified seed yam sector is expanding rapidly through the YIIFSWA project and national seed councils. Certified seed carries a label confirming variety purity, phytosanitary status, and germination viability. Buying certified seed costs 1530% more than farm-saved seed but typically delivers 2040% higher yields and reduces disease carry-over. For commercial growers, this is a sound investment.</p>

<p>Key sources include:</p>
<ul>
<li>IITA and partner seed companies (Nigeria, Ghana, Benin)</li>
<li>National Agricultural Seed Councils (NASC Nigeria, CSIR-Ghana)</li>
<li>Community-based seed producers registered with national programmes</li>
<li>AfricaYam project partners</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Adapting Varieties to Local Conditions</h3>

<p>Variety performance is location-specific. A variety that yields 30 t/ha in Ibadan may yield 18 t/ha in a drier or nematode-infested field. Before committing to a variety, run a small on-farm trial (0.10.2 ha) across at least one full season. Record yield, disease incidence, and market acceptance. This "ground-truthing" step protects your investment.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Define your market first</strong> pounded yam, chips, export, or local fresh market then choose the variety that serves it.</li>
<li><strong>Buy certified seed</strong> from NASC/CSIR-registered producers; verify the label for variety, batch, and phytosanitary status.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect every sett</strong> before purchase: 200400 g, 2 buds, no cracks, no rot, firm flesh.</li>
<li><strong>Treat setts</strong> with approved fungicide/insecticide or wood ash + neem; wear full protective gear.</li>
<li><strong>Cure for 714 days</strong> in a shaded, ventilated area before storage or planting.</li>
<li><strong>Store in raised, ventilated barns</strong>; target <10% loss over 3 months; inspect monthly.</li>
<li><strong>Run a small on-farm trial</strong> of any new variety before scaling up to commercial acreage.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate break-even yield</strong> before planting: (total input cost) (farm-gate price per tonne). If expected yield is below this, change variety or seed source.</li>
</ol>
