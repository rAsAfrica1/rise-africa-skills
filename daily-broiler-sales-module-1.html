1. Introduction and Why It Matters

If you sell broilers every day, you know the struggle: some days you have too many birds, some days none. Customers come and go disappointed. You lose money and trust. Batch production solves this. Instead of buying chicks once and selling them all at once, you divide your flock into groups called batches. Each batch is started at a different time, so you always have birds ready for sale. For example, start 50 chicks every week. After 6 weeks, you sell 50 birds per week, every week. This gives you steady income and happy customers. In Africa, where demand for chicken is high in towns and cities, daily sales can double your profit compared to selling only once a month. You need a small space, basic equipment, and a reliable chick supplier. Many farmers in Kenya, Nigeria, and Ghana use this method to supply hotels, restaurants, and local markets. It also reduces risk: if disease hits one batch, you still have others. Start small with 50 chicks per batch. As you learn, increase to 100 or more. The key is consistency. With batch production, you become a reliable supplier, and customers will come to you daily. This module will show you exactly how to set up and run a daily sales operation using batches.

2. Tools and Materials

To run a batch production system for daily broiler sales, you need the right tools. First, housing: a simple deep litter house with wire mesh walls and a leak-proof roof. For 50 birds per batch, you need about 10 square meters. You can use local materials like bamboo or mud bricks, but ensure it's predator-proof. Second, feeders and drinkers: 10 tube feeders and 10 drinkers for 50 birds. Plastic ones are cheap and easy to clean. Third, heat source for brooding: charcoal stove, kerosene lamp, or electric bulb. For 50 chicks, one charcoal stove works. Fourth, bedding: wood shavings or rice husks. Avoid sawdust from treated wood. Fifth, feed: starter, grower, and finisher. Buy from reputable suppliers like Olam or local millers. You'll need about 4 kg of feed per bird total. Sixth, vaccines and medicines: Newcastle, Gumboro, and vitamins. Keep a cool box for vaccines. Seventh, record book and pen: track batch start dates, feed, deaths, and sales. Eighth, weighing scale: to sell by weight if needed. Ninth, transport crate: for delivering to customers. Tenth, water source: clean water daily. All these can be sourced locally. In many African markets, you can buy feeders and drinkers from agro-vet shops. Start with what you have and upgrade as you grow. Remember, cleanliness is key: have a footbath and disinfectant at the entrance.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to set up your batch production system. Step 1: Plan your batches. Decide how many birds you want to sell per week. If you want 50 birds per week, start 50 chicks every week. But start small: begin with 2 batches, then add more. Step 2: Prepare the brooder. Clean and disinfect the brooding area. Spread bedding 5 cm thick. Heat the room to 32-35Â°C for the first week. Step 3: Buy day-old chicks from a hatchery. Choose healthy, active chicks. Place them in the brooder. Give them glucose water for the first 6 hours. Step 4: Feed and water. Use starter feed for 0-4 weeks, grower for 4-6 weeks, finisher for 6-8 weeks. Provide feed and water ad libitum. Step 5: Vaccinate. Day 1: Marek's (at hatchery). Day 7: Newcastle + IB. Day 14: Gumboro. Day 21: Newcastle booster. Follow your vet's advice. Step 6: Manage each batch separately. Keep records of each batch's start date. Step 7: After 6-8 weeks, birds reach 1.5-2 kg. Start selling. But don't sell all at once. Sell 50 birds per week from the oldest batch. Step 8: As you sell, start a new batch. So every week you have a new batch of chicks and a batch ready for sale. Step 9: Keep daily records of sales, feed, and mortality. Step 10: Adjust batch size based on demand. If you sell out quickly, increase chicks per batch. This cycle repeats, giving you daily sales.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many farmers fail at batch production because of avoidable mistakes. Mistake 1: Starting too many batches at once. You get overwhelmed and run out of space or feed. Fix: Start with 2 batches, then add one batch per month. Mistake 2: Mixing batches. If you put different ages together, older birds bully younger ones and disease spreads. Fix: Keep each batch in separate pens. Mistake 3: Poor record keeping. You forget when to vaccinate or sell. Fix: Use a simple notebook or chalkboard. Write batch number, start date, and expected sale date. Mistake 4: Inconsistent feeding. Birds grow slowly and unevenly. Fix: Feed at the same times daily. Use a feeding schedule. Mistake 5: Skipping vaccinations. Disease wipes out entire batches. Fix: Vaccinate on time. Set phone reminders. Mistake 6: Selling all birds from one batch at once. You lose daily sales. Fix: Sell only the oldest batch, and only as needed. Keep a buffer. Mistake 7: Not isolating sick birds. One sick bird infects all. Fix: Have a sick bay. Remove sick birds immediately. Mistake 8: Overcrowding. Birds stress and die. Fix: Allow 0.2 square meters per bird in grow-out. Mistake 9: Poor biosecurity. Visitors bring disease. Fix: Limit visitors, use footbath, and disinfect equipment. Mistake 10: Underestimating costs. You run out of money for feed. Fix: Budget for feed, vaccines, and utilities before starting. Learn from these mistakes and your daily sales will be steady.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety and best practices protect your health, your birds, and your business. First, personal safety: wear boots, gloves, and a mask when cleaning poultry houses. Poultry dust can cause respiratory problems. Wash hands with soap after handling birds. Second, biosecurity: keep visitors out. If they must enter, give them boots and a lab coat. Use a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance. Clean and disinfect feeders and drinkers daily. Third, bird welfare: provide adequate space, fresh water, and feed. Avoid overcrowding. Keep litter dry to prevent ammonia burns. Fourth, vaccine handling: store vaccines in a cool box with ice packs. Never freeze Newcastle or Gumboro vaccines. Use clean water for mixing. Vaccinate in the cool of the day. Fifth, feed safety: store feed in a dry, raised place. Prevent mold. Never feed moldy feed. Sixth, waste management: dispose of dead birds properly by burning or deep burial. Compost litter away from the poultry house. Seventh, fire safety: if using charcoal stoves, keep them away from walls and bedding. Have a bucket of sand or water nearby. Eighth, record keeping: keep sales and expense records. This helps you know your profit and plan. Ninth, market safety: transport birds in clean crates. Do not mix with other birds. Tenth, continuous learning: join a local poultry association. Attend training. Best practices lead to healthy birds, happy customers, and more money. Always prioritize safety.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to put your knowledge into action. This exercise will help you plan your own batch production system. Follow these steps: 1. Determine your target daily sales. How many birds can you sell per day? For example, if you want to sell 10 birds per day, that's 70 birds per week. 2. Calculate batch size. If you sell 70 birds per week and each batch takes 7 weeks to mature, you need 7 batches (one for each week). So each batch should have 70 chicks. But start smaller: if you want 10 birds per day, start with 70 chicks per batch, but only 2 batches initially. 3. Draw a batch calendar. On a piece of paper, write the weeks of the year. Mark when you will start each batch. For example, Batch 1 starts Week 1, Batch 2 starts Week 2, etc. Mark expected sale week: Batch 1 sale Week 8 (if 7 weeks grow-out). 4. List all tools and materials you need. Check what you have and what you must buy. Estimate costs. 5. Create a feeding schedule. For 70 birds, you need about 280 kg of feed total. Break it down: starter (0-4 weeks): 70 birds x 1 kg = 70 kg. Grower (4-6 weeks): 70 x 1.5 kg = 105 kg. Finisher (6-7 weeks): 70 x 1.5 kg = 105 kg. 6. Make a vaccination plan. Write dates for each vaccine. 7. Set up a record book. Create columns for batch number, start date, feed used, deaths, sales, and income. 8. Start your first batch. Buy chicks, set up brooder, and follow the steps. 9. After 7 weeks, sell your first batch. Record everything. 10. Review and adjust. Did you make a profit? If not, find out why. Then start your second batch. This exercise gives you a real plan. Do it now, and you'll be on your way to daily sales.

How to Use These Tools

Place chicks in four equal groups, two weeks apart. Keep a separate pen or a clearly marked section for each batch. Weigh a sample of 10 birds per batch every week â this tells you when the batch is ready for daily sales. Once a batch reaches market weight, sell a fixed number per day (for example, 35â40 birds) to your regular customers: restaurants, hotels, cold rooms, and households. This keeps your market supplied without crashing the price.

Your daily sales sheet is your business brain. It shows which batch is performing, which customer pays on time, and whether feed cost per bird is still below sale price. If feed cost per bird rises above 65â70% of sale value, you are losing money â act immediately by reviewing feed quality, mortality, and price.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Biosecurity and Handling

Never move birds, crates, or personnel between batches without cleaning and disinfecting first. Avian influenza and Newcastle disease spread fast between pens and markets. Wear boots and gloves when handling live birds or manure. Wash hands with soap after every sale. Do not sell sick or suddenly dying birds â this spreads disease to buyers and to your other batches. Keep a footbath at every pen entrance and change the disinfectant daily. If you see sudden high mortality, stop all sales and contact your local veterinary officer immediately.
