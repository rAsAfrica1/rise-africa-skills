1. Growth Rates and Size Classes

Crocodiles grow at different rates depending on species, temperature, diet, and stocking density. In Africa, the Nile crocodile is most common. Hatchlings start at about 30 cm and 50 g. With good management, they can reach 1.2 m in 12 months, 1.8 m in 24 months, and 2.5 m in 36 months. Growth is fastest in the first two years. After that, it slows down. Your goal is to reach market size: 1.52.0 m for meat and skin. To track growth, weigh and measure every month. Use a simple plastic tape measure and a digital scale up to 50 kg. Record data in a notebook. Compare against a standard growth chart. If growth is slow, check temperature, feed, and water quality.

Size classes help you manage feeding and space. Class 1: hatchlings up to 6 months, 3060 cm, 0.050.5 kg. Keep them in small tanks at 3032C. Class 2: juveniles 618 months, 60120 cm, 0.55 kg. Move to bigger pens. Class 3: sub-adults 1830 months, 120180 cm, 525 kg. Class 4: adults 30+ months, over 180 cm, over 25 kg. Separate by size to prevent cannibalism and competition. Feed class 1 five times a week, class 2 four times, class 3 three times, class 4 twice. Adjust as needed.

Temperature control is critical. Crocodiles grow best at 3032C. Below 25C, they stop eating. Above 35C, they get stressed. In cooler regions like South Africa's highveld, use greenhouse tunnels or heaters. In hot areas like northern Kenya, provide shade and cool water. Water quality: change 20% of water daily. Test pH (6.58.0) and ammonia (below 0.5 ppm). Poor water causes disease and slow growth.

Feed conversion ratio (FCR) matters. A good FCR is 1.52.0 kg feed per kg growth. Use cheap protein sources: fish waste, chicken offal, rodents, and commercial crocodile pellets. In Africa, many farmers use tilapia by-catch and slaughterhouse waste. Avoid spoiled meat. Supplement with vitamins and minerals. With proper care, you can harvest 200300 kg of meat per 100 m pond per year. Keep records and cull slow growers early to save money.

2. Grading and Sorting Crocodiles

Grading and sorting is the regular process of separating crocodiles by size and condition. Do it every 46 weeks. Why? Because crocodiles are cannibalistic. A larger croc will eat a smaller one if hungry. Also, size classes need different feed and space. Sorting reduces stress, improves growth, and prevents injuries that damage skins. You will need: a catching net, thick gloves, a holding pen, a measuring board, and a scale. Work in the cool of the morning or evening. Never handle crocodiles when they are overheated.

First, catch crocodiles from the pen. Use a net with a long handle. For small ones, use your hands with gloves. For larger ones, use a noose pole or a trap box. Move them to a holding pen with clean water. Then measure total length (snout to tip of tail) and weight. Use a measuring board with a ruler. For weight, use a digital scale or a spring scale. Record each animal's ID (use a numbered tag or microchip).

Sort into four groups: small, medium, large, and breeder. Small: under 60 cm. Medium: 60120 cm. Large: 120180 cm. Breeders: over 180 cm and healthy. Also remove sick or injured animals. Signs of sickness: cloudy eyes, open mouth, lethargy, skin lesions. Put sick ones in a quarantine pen with clean water and no food for 2 days. Consult a vet if needed.

After sorting, move each group to its own pen. Adjust stocking density. For small: 2030 per m. Medium: 510 per m. Large: 12 per m. Breeders: 1 per 2 m. Provide basking areas and hiding spots. Feed according to size class. Sorting also helps you select future breeders. Choose the fastest growers with clean skins and good body shape. Keep records of each sorting event. This data helps you plan harvests and sales. Good grading means more uniform skins, which fetch higher prices at market.

3. Harvesting Techniques for Meat and Skin

Harvesting is the process of killing and preparing crocodiles for meat and skin. Do it humanely and legally. In most African countries, you need a permit to slaughter. Check with your local wildlife authority. Harvest when crocodiles reach market size: 1.52.0 m for meat and skin. At this size, you get 1525 kg of meat and a skin of 3040 cm belly width. Harvest in the cool morning. Stop feeding 24 hours before to empty the gut. This makes processing cleaner.

There are two main methods: stunning and pithing, or captive bolt. The goal is to render the animal unconscious immediately, then destroy the brain. For small crocodiles (under 1.5 m), a heavy blow to the back of the head with a metal rod works. For larger ones, use a captive bolt gun. After stunning, immediately insert a sharp knife or pithing rod into the brain cavity through the back of the skull. This ensures death. Then bleed the animal by cutting the throat or the base of the tail. Hang it upside down to drain blood for 10 minutes.

Next, skin the crocodile. Make a cut along the belly from throat to tail. Use a sharp skinning knife. Peel the skin away from the meat. Be careful not to cut the skin. The belly skin is most valuable. Remove the skin in one piece if possible. Then remove the meat. Cut along the backbone and separate the fillets. The tail meat is the best. You can also use the legs and body. Wash the meat in clean water. Chill it immediately to 4C.

After skinning, the carcass can be sold fresh or frozen. The skin must be preserved immediately. Salt it or chill it. Do not leave it in the sun. For meat, package in food-grade plastic and freeze at -18C. Clean all tools and the area with disinfectant. Dispose of waste properly. Never release crocodile waste into rivers. Bury or incinerate. Keep records of each harvest: date, size, weight, skin length, meat weight. This helps you track profitability.

4. Slaughter and Processing Procedures

Slaughter and processing must be done in a clean, separate area. This prevents contamination. You need: a slaughter table, a hose with clean water, sharp knives, a stunning tool, a bleeding hook, a skinning knife, a meat saw, and a chiller. Wear protective clothing: apron, gloves, boots, and face mask. The whole process should take less than 30 minutes from stunning to chilling. Work quickly to keep meat safe.

Step 1: Stunning. Use a captive bolt gun or a heavy blow to the back of the head. The crocodile must be unconscious immediately. Check for no eye reflex. Step 2: Pithing. Insert a thin rod into the brain cavity to destroy the brain. Step 3: Bleeding. Cut the throat or the tail base. Hang the crocodile by the tail on a bleeding hook. Let blood drain for 10 minutes. Step 4: Skinning. Lay the crocodile on its back. Cut along the belly from throat to tail. Peel the skin carefully. Use a knife to separate skin from muscle. Avoid cuts. Remove the skin in one piece. Step 5: Evisceration. Cut open the belly and remove internal organs. Keep the liver and heart if you want to sell them. Wash the carcass with clean water. Step 6: Chilling. Put the carcass in a chiller at 4C for 24 hours. This makes meat tender and easier to cut.

Step 7: Cutting. After chilling, cut the carcass into primal cuts: tail, legs, body, and neck. The tail is the most expensive. Cut into steaks or fillets. Remove any fat and connective tissue. Step 8: Packaging. Wrap meat in plastic or vacuum seal. Label with date and weight. Freeze at -18C or sell fresh within 3 days. Step 9: Skin treatment. The skin must be salted or chilled immediately. See the next lesson for details. Step 10: Cleaning. Wash all tools and surfaces with hot water and disinfectant. Clean the floor. Dispose of waste in a pit or incinerator. Never leave waste open to attract flies.

Record everything: date, animal ID, live weight, carcass weight, skin length, meat weight. This helps you calculate yield and profit. A good yield is 50% meat and 10% skin by live weight. For example, a 30 kg crocodile gives 15 kg meat and a 3 kg skin (wet). With practice, you can process 1015 crocodiles per day with two workers. Always follow local health and safety regulations.

5. Skin Preservation and Initial Treatment

Crocodile skin is the most valuable product. A well-preserved skin can fetch $200$500 or more, depending on size and quality. Poor preservation ruins the skin. You must treat the skin within 2 hours of slaughter. The goal is to stop bacterial growth and prepare for tanning. You will need: non-iodized salt (fine or coarse), a clean flat surface, a fleshing knife, and a cool room. Never use iodized salt; it stains the skin.

First, flesh the skin. Lay the skin flat, flesh side up. Use a fleshing knife to remove all meat, fat, and membrane. Be careful not to cut the skin. Work from head to tail. Rinse the skin in clean water. Then salt it. Use 1 kg of salt per 1 kg of skin. Rub salt into the flesh side. Make sure all areas are covered. Then fold the skin flesh-to-flesh and roll it. Place it on a slanted surface so water can drain. Keep it at 1015C for 710 days. This is called wet salting. Check daily. If salt is wet, add more. After 10 days, the skin is preserved. You can then ship it to a tannery.

Alternatively, you can air-dry the skin. Stretch the skin on a wooden frame, flesh side out. Use nails or clips every 10 cm. Dry in the shade with good airflow. Never dry in direct sun; it cracks the skin. Drying takes 35 days. The skin should be stiff and dry. Then salt it lightly and store in a cool, dry place. For short-term storage (up to 1 month), you can chill the skin at 4C in a plastic bag. But salting is better for longer.

Quality matters. Avoid holes, cuts, and scars. These lower the price. Handle the skin with clean hands. Do not let it touch blood or dirt. If you see signs of decay (bad smell, slimy feel), discard it. Keep records of each skin: date, size, and method. Sell to a reputable tannery or exporter. In Africa, countries like South Africa, Zimbabwe, and Kenya have tanneries. You can also export to Asia or Europe, but you need CITES permits. Always check legal requirements. Good skin preservation is the difference between profit and loss.

6. Meat Handling and Storage

Crocodile meat is white, low in fat, and high in protein. It tastes like a mix of chicken and fish. In Africa, it is sold fresh, frozen, or smoked. To get top price, you must handle meat hygienically. After slaughter, chill the carcass quickly to 4C. Do not leave meat at room temperature for more than 2 hours. Bacteria grow fast in warm conditions. Use a chiller or ice boxes. If you don't have electricity, use a cool room or dig a pit with ice. But ice is expensive. Better to invest in a small solar-powered fridge.

After chilling for 24 hours, cut the meat into consumer cuts. Tail meat is best for steaks. Leg meat is good for stews. Body meat can be minced. Remove all fat and connective tissue. Cut into 200500 g portions. Wrap in plastic cling film or vacuum seal. Label with date and weight. For fresh sale, keep at 04C and sell within 3 days. For frozen, store at -18C. Frozen meat lasts 612 months. Never refreeze thawed meat. Thaw in the fridge, not at room temperature.

Smoking is a traditional preservation method in Africa. It adds flavor and extends shelf life. Build a simple smoker with a metal drum. Use hardwood, not softwood. Smoke at 6070C for 46 hours. The meat should reach an internal temperature of 65C. Then cool and pack. Smoked meat lasts 23 weeks in a cool place. You can also make biltong (dried meat). Cut meat into strips, salt, and hang to dry in a fly-proof box. Dry for 35 days. Biltong lasts months.

Hygiene is critical. Wash hands and tools with soap and hot water. Clean surfaces with bleach solution (1 tablespoon per 4 liters). Wear clean gloves and apron. Keep raw meat separate from cooked. Control flies and rodents. Sell only to licensed buyers. In many African countries, you need a health certificate to sell meat. Get one from your local health department. Keep records of all sales. With good handling, you can sell crocodile meat to restaurants, hotels, and supermarkets. It is a premium product. Price: $1020 per kg fresh, $1525 per kg smoked.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Growth Management and Harvesting

This practical reference consolidates the working numbers, benchmarks and procedures that commercial crocodile farmers in Africa need to run a profitable grow-out and harvesting operation. It is written for farm managers, abattoir supervisors and agribusiness owners who must convert feed, labour and capital into sellable meat and skins. The focus is on Nile crocodile (Crocodylus niloticus), the dominant commercial species in sub-Saharan Africa, though the principles apply broadly to other farmed crocodilians. Treat the figures below as planning benchmarks: your own growth curves, feed conversion and dressing percentages will vary with genetics, climate, water temperature, stocking density and feed quality. Record your own data and compare it against these references.

Growth Rates and Size Classes

Growth in crocodiles is temperature-dependent and non-linear. Below roughly 26 C feed intake and growth collapse; between 28 C and 32 C growth is near optimal. In practice, most African farms operate heated or greenhouse-enclosed pens to hold water at 2832 C for at least 1012 months of the year. Hatchlings start at 3060 g and can reach 1.52.5 kg in the first year under good management, then 47 kg by 24 months and 815 kg by 3036 months depending on feeding regime and target market.

Size classes drive everything downstream: pen allocation, feed particle size, grading frequency and harvest scheduling. A common commercial classification is set out below.

Business implication: every extra month an animal stays in the finisher pen consumes feed and space without proportionally increasing skin value. Most African operations target harvest between 24 and 36 months, with skin length and belly width, not body weight alone, as the deciding criteria. Buyers pay for centimetres of belly width and defect-free skin, so plan harvest around skin grade, not just tonnage.

Grading and Sorting Crocodiles

Grading is not optional. Size heterogeneity in a pen causes aggression, bite injuries (which become skin defects), uneven feed access and cannibalism of small animals. Sort at least monthly, and every two weeks during rapid growth phases. Handle animals quickly and at cool times of day to reduce heat stress.

Grade on two axes: body weight (for feed and pen allocation) and belly width (for skin value). Belly width is measured across the widest part of the belly between the front and hind legs. Keep the largest animals together, but avoid mixing animals that differ by more than about 30% in body weight.

⚠ SAFETY WARNING CROCODILE HANDLING AND SLAUGHTER

A crocodile is a dangerous wild animal, not livestock. Never enter a pen alone, and never handle an unrestrained animal without at least two trained adults, a catch pole or noose, and a restraint board. A crocodile can bite through a boot and a hand, and a tail strike can break a leg. During slaughter, use a properly maintained captive-bolt or electrical stun followed immediately by pithing or exsanguination an incorrectly stunned animal is a lethal hazard to workers. Wear cut-resistant gloves, eye protection and steel-toe boots. Keep a written emergency procedure, a first-aid kit and a means of calling for help at the slaughter station at all times. Check national animal welfare and CITES regulations before any harvest.

Harvesting Techniques for Meat and Skin

Harvest planning should start at least 30 days before slaughter. Withdraw feed for 57 days (not water) to empty the gut, reduce faecal contamination risk during dressing and improve meat hygiene. Confirm buyer orders, skin size requirements and CITES permits before scheduling the kill. Batch harvest in groups of 1030 animals to match chilling and processing capacity never harvest more than you can skin and chill within 46 hours.

Stunning and killing must be humane and effective. The accepted sequence is: restrain the animal, apply a captive-bolt stunner or electrical stun to the brain, immediately confirm unconsciousness, then pith or exsanguinate by severing the major vessels at the neck. Bleed over a drained, washable floor. Do not allow the carcass to lie in blood or water.

Slaughter and Processing Procedures

Process on a clean, hard, washable surface with potable water. The sequence: bleed, wash the carcass, remove the head and feet, then open the belly carefully to avoid nicking the intestines. Remove and inspect the viscera; retain the liver and heart if they are to be sold. Remove the belly and back skin in one piece where the buyer requires a whole skin, using a sharp, curved skinning knife and keeping the blade angled away from the skin. Flesh the skin carefully nicks and score marks are permanent defects and reduce grade.

After skinning, rinse the skin in clean water, then chill or salt immediately. The carcass is then broken down into primal cuts: tail fillet, loin, leg and belly meat. Weigh and label every skin and every meat batch with the animal ID, date and weight. Traceability is a commercial requirement for export buyers.

Skin Preservation and Initial Treatment

Skin quality is decided in the first hour after removal. Two accepted methods are used in Africa: wet salting (brining) and dry salting. Wet salting is faster and more even for large volumes; dry salting is simpler where cold storage is limited.

Wet salting: immerse the fleshed skin in a saturated salt solution (approximately 2530% NaCl, density 1.181.20) with a small amount of bactericide, at 1520 C for 2448 hours, then drain and stack flesh-to-flesh with salt between layers. Dry salting: rub fine, clean salt into the flesh side, lay flat, and cover with a 23 cm layer of salt; re-salt and turn every 23 days for 1014 days until the skin is firm and fully cured. Never allow skins to touch fresh water after salting, and never stack a wet skin against a dry one.

After curing, skins are graded, rolled or flat-packed, and stored in a cool, dry, dark room at below 20 C and below 60% relative humidity. Protect against insects, rodents and moisture. Record every skin's grade, weight and destination. For export, skins must be accompanied by CITES documentation and, where required, veterinary health certification.

Meat Handling and Storage

Crocodile meat is a high-value, low-fat white meat with a mild flavour. It is also highly perishable. Chill rapidly: core temperature should reach 4 C within 4 hours of slaughter. Keep meat below 4 C during transport and display, and freeze at 18 C or below for longer storage. Use separate, colour-coded knives and boards for raw meat and never allow contact with the skin-processing area. Package in food-grade vacuum bags where possible; label with species, batch, date and weight. For export, comply with the destination country's veterinary and HACCP requirements.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Grade weekly: sort by weight and belly width; never mix animals differing by more than ~30% in weight.
  • Hold temperature: keep water at 2832 C for optimal growth; below 26 C growth stalls.
  • Plan harvest 30 days ahead: confirm orders, permits and chilling capacity first.
  • Withdraw feed 57 days before slaughter; keep clean water available.
  • Stun, confirm, pith or bleed never handle an improperly stunned animal.
  • Skin within 46 hours of slaughter; avoid nicks and score marks.
  • Salt or brine immediately; never let a fresh skin sit wet.
  • Chill meat to 4 C within 4 hours; freeze at 18 C for storage.
  • Record everything: animal ID, skin grade, weight, date, destination traceability sells.

Bottom line: in crocodile farming, growth is slow, feed is expensive, and skin quality is decided in minutes. The farms that make money in Africa are the ones that grade relentlessly, harvest on schedule against confirmed orders, and treat skin and meat handling as a disciplined, documented production line not an afterthought.
