1. Introduction and Why It Matters

A business plan for daily broiler sales is not a document for banks. It is your daily map. Without it, you will guess how many chicks to buy, how much feed to stock, and when to sell. With it, you know exactly what to do each day to make profit. In Africa, where feed prices change weekly and customers buy in small daily amounts, a plan keeps you from running out of stock or holding unsold birds. Think of Mama Aisha in Kano. She started with 50 chicks, sold at the roadside, and often had 10 birds left at night. She lost money on feed for those unsold birds. After writing a simple plan, she now buys 100 chicks every two weeks, sells 50 birds daily, and never carries stock overnight. Her profit doubled in three months. The plan works because it forces you to answer five key questions: What do I sell? To whom? At what price? With what resources? And how do I grow? These are the five stages. Ignore them, and you will join the many small poultry farmers who quit after two batches. Follow them, and you build a steady income. This module will walk you through each stage using real numbers from African markets. You will learn to calculate costs, set prices, find customers, manage cash, and plan for expansion. By the end, you will have a one-page plan you can use tomorrow. No jargon, no theory. Just practical steps that work in Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, or your own village.

2. Tools and Materials

To create your business plan, you need only simple tools. A notebook and pen cost less than 500 naira or 50 shillings. A basic calculator helps, but your phones calculator works. If you have a smartphone, you can use free apps like Google Sheets or a notebook app. But paper is fine. You also need records from your last batch if you have them: feed receipts, chick purchase price, medicine costs, and sales records. If you are starting fresh, you will estimate using local market prices. Visit your local agro-dealer and ask for current prices: day-old chicks, broiler starter, grower, and finisher feed, and common vaccines. Write them down. Also, talk to three potential customers: a restaurant, a roadside vendor, and a neighbor. Ask what price they pay per bird and how many they need daily. For mapping your customers, a simple hand-drawn map of your area showing markets, restaurants, and roads is useful. You do not need a computer. A cardboard sheet and markers can serve as your planning board. For cash flow, you can use a notebook with columns for date, money in, money out, and balance. If you prefer digital, a free Excel template for small business cash flow works. Finally, a calendar or wall chart helps you track batch cycles: when to order chicks, when to vaccinate, when to start selling. All these tools cost less than 5,000 naira or 500 shillings. Do not wait for a laptop. Start with what you have.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these five stages to build your plan. Stage 1: Define your product and market. Decide if you sell live birds, dressed chicken, or both. For daily sales, dressed birds often fetch higher prices but require plucking and cleaning. Choose based on your skills and customer demand. Identify your customers: households, restaurants, or traders. For example, in Kampala, a restaurant may buy 20 dressed birds daily at 15,000 UGX each. Stage 2: Calculate your costs and set your price. List all costs per bird: chick (2,500 UGX), feed (8,000 UGX for 2.5 kg), vaccines and medicine (500 UGX), labor (1,000 UGX), and miscellaneous (500 UGX). Total cost per bird = 12,500 UGX. To make 30% profit, sell at 16,250 UGX. Round to 16,500 UGX. Stage 3: Plan your production cycle. Broilers take 6 weeks to reach 2.5 kg. If you want to sell 50 birds daily, you need 350 birds per week. With 10% mortality, start 390 chicks weekly. That means ordering every week. Stage 4: Manage cash flow. You need money upfront for chicks and feed. Create a cash flow table: Week 1: spend 975,000 UGX on chicks, 3,120,000 UGX on feed over 6 weeks. Sales start week 6: 50 birds x 16,500 = 825,000 UGX daily. Reinvest part of sales into next batch. Stage 5: Plan for growth. After 3 months, increase to 100 birds daily. Add a second house, hire one helper, and find more customers. Review your plan monthly. Adjust prices if feed costs change. Write everything in your notebook. This is your business plan.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many farmers fail because they skip planning or make simple errors. Mistake 1: Not calculating all costs. You forget labor, transport, or mortality. Fix: Use the cost list from Lesson 3. Add 10% for unexpected expenses. Mistake 2: Setting price too low to beat competition. You sell at 14,000 UGX when cost is 12,500 UGX. After a few losses, you quit. Fix: Know your break-even price. Never sell below it. Compete on quality and reliability, not price. Mistake 3: Overestimating demand. You produce 200 birds daily but only sell 50. Fix: Start small. Sell 20 birds daily for one month. Increase only when you have consistent buyers. Mistake 4: Ignoring cash flow. You spend all sales money on personal needs and cannot buy next batchs feed. Fix: Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for the business. Pay yourself a fixed salary. Mistake 5: No records. You dont know if you made profit. Fix: Record every sale and expense daily. Use a notebook or app. Mistake 6: Not planning for disease. A disease outbreak kills half your flock. Fix: Include vaccination costs and follow a strict biosecurity plan. Mistake 7: Trying to do everything alone. You get tired and make mistakes. Fix: Train one family member or hire a helper for feeding and cleaning. Mistake 8: Not reviewing the plan. Market prices change. Fix: Review your plan every month. Adjust chick orders, feed purchases, and prices. These fixes are simple but powerful. Apply them and your business will survive.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in broiler sales is not just about handling birds. It is about protecting your health, your customers, and your business. Always wash your hands with soap after handling birds or manure. Use gloves and boots when cleaning the pen. Wear a nose mask when mixing feed or cleaning dust. For customers, ensure dressed birds are clean and chilled. Use clean water and a sharp knife. If you sell live birds, keep them in clean crates. Do not sell sick birds. Best practice: Keep a visitor log. Limit visitors to your pen. Vaccinate on schedule: Newcastle (day 7 and 21), Gumboro (day 14 and 21). Use the correct dosage. Store vaccines in a cool box with ice packs. For feed, buy from trusted agro-dealers. Check expiry dates. Store feed in dry, raised pallets to avoid mold. For your business, separate personal and business money. Keep receipts for all purchases. Pay taxes if required. Join a local poultry association for updates and support. Have a plan for emergencies: if bird flu is reported in your area, stop sales immediately and inform authorities. For your own safety, do not lift heavy feed bags alone. Use a wheelbarrow. Take breaks. These practices reduce risk and build trust. Customers return when they know your birds are healthy and your business is honest.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is your turn. Take a notebook and complete this exercise. It will take about one hour. Step 1: Go to your local market and find current prices for: day-old chicks, broiler starter, grower, finisher feed, and vaccines. Write them down. Step 2: Talk to three potential customers. Ask: How many birds do you buy daily? What price do you pay? Would you buy from me if I deliver? Write their answers. Step 3: Calculate your cost per bird. Use this formula: Cost = chick price + (feed consumed x feed price) + vaccine + labor + transport + 10% miscellaneous. For example, if chick = 2,500 UGX, feed = 8,000 UGX, vaccine = 500 UGX, labor = 1,000 UGX, transport = 500 UGX, misc = 1,250 UGX, total = 13,750 UGX. Step 4: Set your selling price. Add 25-30% profit. If cost is 13,750 UGX, sell at 17,500 UGX. Step 5: Plan your batch. If you want to sell 30 birds daily, you need 210 birds per week. With 10% mortality, start 234 chicks weekly. Step 6: Create a simple cash flow for one month. List money you need upfront and money you expect from sales. Step 7: Write your one-page plan. Include: product, customers, price, weekly chick order, monthly sales target, and growth goal for 6 months. Step 8: Review with a friend or mentor. Ask for feedback. Then start. Remember, a plan is useless without action. Take the first step: order your first batch of chicks this week. Track everything. Adjust as you learn. You are now a business planner.

<strong style="color:#c62828; font-size:15px;"> SAFETY WARNING HEALTH, FOOD &amp; CHEMICAL HAZARDS</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#333;">Broiler production involves live animals, veterinary drugs, disinfectants and food products. <strong>Always</strong> observe the following:</p>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 18px; color:#333;">
<li><strong>Withdrawal periods:</strong> Never sell or slaughter birds before the drug withdrawal period stated on the label (typically 57 days for many antibiotics and coccidiostats). Residues harm consumers and can close your market access. (WHO/FAO Codex Alimentarius)</li>
<li><strong>Vaccines:</strong> Store at 28C, use within the stated time after reconstitution, and dispose of vials by incineration never in open pits or water sources.</li>
<li><strong>Disinfectants:</strong> Wear gloves and eye protection; never mix chlorine with ammonia-based products. Keep away from children and feed.</li>
<li><strong>Zoonoses:</strong> Wash hands after handling birds; cook poultry to an internal temperature of 74C. Report unusual mortality to your veterinary officer immediately avian influenza and Newcastle disease are notifiable.</li>
<li><strong>Heat stress:</strong> In temperatures above 32C, mortality can spike within hours. Provide shade, ventilation and cool water; never leave birds without water for more than 2 hours.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Before Day 0:</strong> Clean and disinfect the house, test brooder temperature (3234C at chick level), and pre-warm the brooder 24 hours ahead.</li>
<li><strong>Day 07:</strong> Weigh a sample of 10 chicks on arrival and again on Day 7. If average weight is below 160 g, review brooding temperature and starter feed access.</li>
<li><strong>Daily:</strong> Record feed issued, water consumed, mortality, and any abnormal behaviour. A sudden water drop is your earliest disease warning.</li>
<li><strong>Weekly:</strong> Calculate FCR = total feed consumed total live weight gained. Compare against Table 1; investigate any gap above 0.15.</li>
<li><strong>Vaccination:</strong> Follow the hatchery schedule (typically Newcastle Day 7, Gumboro Day 14). Never vaccinate sick birds.</li>
<li><strong>Day 35:</strong> Calculate your cost per kg live weight. If it exceeds your expected selling price, adjust feed or market timing immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Marketing:</strong> Line up at least two buyers before Day 35. A bird held past Day 45 eats profit feed cost rises faster than weight gain.</li>
<li><strong>After sale:</strong> Clean out, rest the house for 714 days, and record final FCR, mortality and margin for the next batch.</li>
</ol>
