1. Shawarma and Doner: Marination and Cooking

Shawarma and doner are stacked meat cones cooked on a vertical rotisserie. In Africa, you can use beef, chicken, or lamb. Start with 2 kg of boneless chicken thighs or beef sirloin. For the marinade: blend 1 cup plain yogurt, 4 cloves garlic, 2 tbsp lemon juice, 2 tbsp olive oil, 1 tbsp ground cumin, 1 tbsp ground coriander, 1 tsp paprika, 1 tsp turmeric, 1 tsp salt, and 1/2 tsp black pepper. Cut meat into thin strips or leave whole for stacking. Marinate for at least 4 hours, preferably overnight in a fridge.

To cook without a rotisserie, use a heavy skillet or grill. For chicken thighs, cook on medium-high heat for 5-6 minutes per side until internal temperature reaches 75C (165F). For beef, cook to 60C (140F) for medium. Slice thinly against the grain. If you have a rotisserie, stack marinated meat on a skewer, pressing firmly. Cook on high heat for 20-30 minutes, turning, until the outside is crispy. Then slice off the outer layer, and continue cooking the inside.

For doner, you can also make a meatloaf-style block: mix 1 kg ground beef with 1 grated onion, 2 tbsp breadcrumbs, 1 egg, and the same spices. Shape into a log, bake at 200C for 40 minutes, then slice and fry for crispness. This is cheaper and easier for street vending.

Serve in pita bread with tahini sauce, pickles, tomatoes, and onions. In Africa, use local spices like suya pepper or berbere for a twist. Keep meat hot in a cooler box or insulated container. For takeaway, wrap in foil or paper. Always cook to safe temperatures and keep raw meat separate from cooked. Clean your grill after each use to prevent sticking.

2. Falafel and Hummus from Scratch

Falafel and hummus are made from chickpeas (garbanzo beans). For falafel, use dried chickpeas, not canned. Soak 2 cups dried chickpeas in water overnight (at least 12 hours). They will double in size. Drain well. In a food processor, blend chickpeas with 1 onion, 4 garlic cloves, 1 cup fresh parsley, 1 cup fresh cilantro, 2 tsp cumin, 1 tsp coriander, 1 tsp salt, 1/2 tsp baking soda, and 1 tbsp flour. Pulse until a coarse paste formsdo not over-blend, or it becomes mushy. Chill for 30 minutes.

Shape into small balls (about 2 tbsp each). Heat oil to 175C (350F) in a deep pot. Fry in batches for 3-4 minutes until golden brown. Drain on paper towels. For a healthier version, bake at 200C for 20 minutes, turning once. Falafel is best served hot in pita with tahini, pickles, and tomatoes.

For hummus, use canned chickpeas for speed. Drain and rinse 2 cans (400g each). In a blender, combine chickpeas, 1/2 cup tahini, 1/4 cup lemon juice, 2 garlic cloves, 1 tsp salt, and 1/2 tsp cumin. Blend, adding 1/4 cup ice water slowly until smooth and creamy. Adjust lemon and salt. Drizzle with olive oil and paprika. Hummus keeps in the fridge for 4 days.

In Africa, chickpeas are grown in Ethiopia, Sudan, and Kenya. Buy dried chickpeas from local marketsthey are cheaper. For street vending, make falafel fresh daily. Serve hummus as a dip or spread. Use a food processor or heavy mortar and pestle if no electricity. Always taste and adjust seasoning. Fry falafel in clean oil; discard after 3-4 uses.

3. Pita Bread and Flatbreads

Pita bread is a simple flatbread that puffs up in the oven. You need 3 cups all-purpose flour, 1 cup warm water (about 40C), 2 tsp instant yeast, 1 tsp salt, 1 tbsp sugar, and 2 tbsp olive oil. Mix flour, yeast, sugar, and salt. Add water and oil. Knead for 8-10 minutes until smooth and elastic. Cover and let rise for 1 hour, until doubled.

Punch down and divide into 8 balls. Roll each into a circle about 6 inches (15 cm) wide and 1/4 inch thick. Let rest for 15 minutes. Preheat oven to 250C (480F) with a baking stone or inverted baking sheet inside. Place 2-3 pita rounds on the hot surface. Bake for 3-4 minutes until they puff up and are lightly browned. Do not open the oven during baking. Remove and cover with a towel to keep soft.

If you don't have an oven, cook on a dry skillet or tawa on high heat. Cook each side for 1-2 minutes until bubbles form and brown spots appear. The bread will not puff as much but still tastes good. For flatbreads like khubz or taboon, you can add 1/2 cup yogurt to the dough for softness.

In Africa, use local flours like cassava or maize blended with wheat for a twist. For street vending, make pita fresh daily. Store in a plastic bag for 2 days, or freeze for 1 month. Reheat in a dry pan. Serve with shawarma, falafel, or as a wrap. Cut into triangles for dipping in hummus. Always keep dough covered to prevent drying. Adjust water if dough is too stickyadd flour 1 tbsp at a time.

4. Mezze and Salads: Tabbouleh, Baba Ganoush

Mezze are small dishes served together. Tabbouleh is a parsley salad. Use 1 cup fine bulgur (cracked wheat), soaked in 1 cup hot water for 20 minutes. Drain well. Chop 2 bunches parsley (about 4 cups), 1/2 cup mint, 4 tomatoes, 1 cucumber, and 1 small onion. Mix with bulgur. Dressing: 1/4 cup lemon juice, 1/4 cup olive oil, 1 tsp salt, 1/2 tsp black pepper. Toss and chill. Tabbouleh should be green and fresh, not soggy. In Africa, use local parsley and mintthey are cheap and abundant.

Baba ganoush is smoky eggplant dip. Roast 2 large eggplants (about 1 kg) directly over a gas flame or on a grill until skin is charred and flesh is soft, about 15-20 minutes. Alternatively, bake at 200C for 40 minutes, then broil for 5 minutes to char. Cool, peel, and scoop flesh into a bowl. Mash with a fork. Add 1/4 cup tahini, 2 tbsp lemon juice, 2 garlic cloves (minced), 1 tsp salt, and 1/2 tsp cumin. Mix well. Drizzle with olive oil and sprinkle with paprika. Serve with pita.

For takeaway, pack tabbouleh in small containers and baba ganoush in tubs. Keep cold. These dishes are vegetarian and vegan, appealing to many customers. In Africa, eggplants are available in most markets. You can also add roasted red peppers or pomegranate seeds for color. Make small batches daily for freshness. Use clean utensils and gloves. Tabbouleh can be made ahead but add tomatoes just before serving to prevent sogginess. Baba ganoush lasts 3 days in fridge.

5. Wraps and Platters for Take Away

Wraps and platters are the main way to sell Middle Eastern street food. A wrap is a pita or flatbread filled with meat, falafel, vegetables, and sauce. A platter is a plate with the same ingredients but no bread, often with rice or salad. For takeaway, use sturdy containers and wrap tightly.

To assemble a wrap: warm a pita for 10 seconds. Lay it flat. Spread 2 tbsp tahini or hummus. Add 100g sliced shawarma or 3 falafel balls. Top with 2 tbsp chopped tomatoes, 1 tbsp pickles, 1 tbsp onions, and 1 tbsp parsley. Roll tightly, tucking in the sides. Wrap in foil or paper. For platters: use a compartment container. Put 150g meat or falafel, 1/2 cup rice or tabbouleh, 2 tbsp hummus, 2 tbsp salad, and 2 tbsp sauce. Include a piece of pita on the side.

In Africa, use local ingredients: rice, chapati, or ugali instead of pita. Add suya spice or piri piri for heat. For vegetarians, use extra falafel or grilled vegetables. Keep hot foods above 60C and cold foods below 5C. Use insulated bags for delivery. Label containers with contents and date.

For efficient service, set up an assembly line: one person warms bread, one adds fillings, one wraps. Use gloves and clean surfaces. Pre-portion sauces in small cups. For platters, use biodegradable containers if possible. Price wraps affordablythey are a complete meal. Offer combos: wrap + drink + fries. In busy areas, pre-wrap some and keep in a warmer. Always check for allergiestahini contains sesame. Provide napkins and a small bag. This is a high-turnover business; keep it simple and fast.

6. Sauces: Tahini, Amba, and Zhoug

Sauces make Middle Eastern food special. Tahini sauce is creamy and nutty. Mix 1/2 cup tahini (sesame paste) with 1/4 cup lemon juice, 1 garlic clove (minced), 1/2 tsp salt, and 1/4 cup cold water. Whisk until smooth. It will seize first, then become creamy. Add more water for thinner consistency. Tahini sauce is used on falafel, shawarma, and salads. In Africa, you can make tahini by blending roasted sesame seeds with oil until smooth. Store in fridge for 2 weeks.

Amba is a pickled mango sauce, popular in Israel and Middle East. Use 2 ripe mangoes, peeled and chopped. In a pot, combine mangoes, 1/2 cup vinegar, 1/4 cup sugar, 1 tsp salt, 1 tsp turmeric, 1 tsp cumin, 1/2 tsp chili flakes, and 2 cloves garlic. Simmer for 20 minutes until soft. Blend until smooth. Cool and store in jars. Amba is tangy and spicy, great on shawarma or falafel. In Africa, use local mangoesthey are abundant in season. You can also use green mangoes for more tang.

Zhoug is a spicy green sauce from Yemen. Blend 2 cups fresh cilantro, 1 cup parsley, 4 green chilies, 4 garlic cloves, 1 tsp cumin, 1 tsp cardamom, 1/2 tsp salt, and 1/2 cup olive oil. Pulse until combined but not pureed. Store in fridge for 1 week. Zhoug is very hotuse sparingly. It adds a kick to wraps and platters.

For street vending, make sauces in bulk and portion into squeeze bottles or small cups. Label them. Always keep cold. Tahini is vegan and gluten-free. Amba is sweet and spicy. Zhoug is for heat lovers. Offer all three so customers can choose. In Africa, adjust chili to local taste. Use clean spoons to avoid contamination. These sauces increase profit marginsthey cost little but add value.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Middle Eastern Street Eats for African Street Food & Take-Away

Middle Eastern street food is one of the fastest-growing take-away categories across urban Africa. From Nairobi's Eastleigh to Lagos's Falomo, from Accra's Osu to Johannesburg's Fordsburg, shawarma, falafel wraps, hummus platters and mezze bowls are selling at premium margins because they combine cheap staple inputs (chickpeas, wheat flour, onions, tomatoes, sesame) with high perceived value and portable formats. This Practical Reference module gives you the operational numbers, equipment notes, food-safety rules and supplier logic you need to run these items profitably in an African kitchen whether you are a single cart, a cloud kitchen or a 20-outlet chain.

Treat this as your bench-side operating manual. It assumes you have already completed the technique lessons in this module (Marination & Cooking, Falafel & Hummus from Scratch, Pita & Flatbreads, Mezze & Salads, Wraps & Platters, Sauces). Here we focus on yields, costs, holding times, equipment, and the business decisions that turn recipes into revenue.

1. The Middle Eastern Take-Away Kitchen: Layout and Flow

A profitable Middle Eastern take-away unit needs five work zones: (a) a cold prep zone for falafel mix, hummus, tahini and salads; (b) a dough zone with a bench and a flat-top or saj for pita; (c) a hot line with a vertical rotisserie (shawarma/doner), a deep fryer (falafel) and a griddle (wraps); (d) a plating/wrapping station; and (e) a sauce and cold-holding station. In African markets where electricity is unreliable, plan for LPG backup for the rotisserie and fryer, and a chest freezer with a generator or inverter for chickpea and meat storage.

4. Sauces: The Margin Multiplier

Tahini, amba and zhoug cost cents per portion but transform a plain wrap into a signature product. Batch them in the cold prep zone, label with production date, and never refill a dirty squeeze bottle decant into a clean, sanitised bottle each service. Amba, a pickled mango condiment with Iraqi-Indian roots, is increasingly popular in African cities with South Asian and Middle Eastern diaspora communities; source green mangoes in season and pickle in bulk to cut cost.

Tahini must be stirred from the bottom of the bucket before use the paste separates. Thin with cold water and lemon, never hot water, or it will seize. Zhoug is a fresh herb-chilli paste; make small batches daily because it loses colour and heat fast.

SAFETY WARNING RAW CHICKPEAS AND FALAFEL

Falafel made from raw, soaked (not cooked) chickpeas must be fried to a core temperature of at least 74 C and cooked through. Raw chickpeas contain lectins (phytohaemagglutinin) that cause severe vomiting and diarrhoea if undercooked. Never serve falafel that is pale, doughy or cool in the centre. Do not use canned, pre-cooked chickpeas for traditional falafel they will disintegrate in the fryer and create a burn and oil-splash hazard.

Also: never leave soaked chickpeas at ambient temperature for more than 24 hours. In hot African climates (>30 C), soak under refrigeration or in a cool, ventilated area and change the water every 8 hours to prevent fermentation and bacterial growth.

5. Africa-Specific Sourcing and Adaptation

Build your supply chain around local staples to protect margin against imported tahini and olive oil prices. Chickpeas are grown in Ethiopia, Sudan, Kenya and Tanzania; wheat flour is milled locally in most capitals; sesame for tahini is abundant in Ethiopia, Sudan and Nigeria. Buy tahini in 520 kg buckets from regional processors rather than retail jars savings of 4060% are common. Olive oil can be partially substituted with locally refined sunflower or groundnut oil for frying, reserving olive oil for finishing and salads where flavour matters.

Adapt flavours to local palates without losing the Middle Eastern identity: increase chilli in zhoug for West African markets, add a light suya-spice rub option for shawarma in Nigeria, or offer a coconut-tahini variant in coastal Kenya. These "local twist" SKUs often outsell the classic version and justify a 1015% price premium.

6. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Calibrate your probe thermometer daily falafel core 74 C, hot-held meat 63 C, cold items 5 C.
  • Soak chickpeas refrigerated, change water every 8 hours, never exceed 24 hours at ambient temperature.
  • Stir tahini from the bottom before every batch; thin with cold water only.
  • Label every cold container with product name, production date and use-by time.
  • Never refill squeeze bottles decant into clean, sanitised bottles each service.
  • Hold fried falafel no more than 30 minutes; discard if cold, oily or soggy.
  • Separate raw meat and ready-to-eat prep use colour-coded boards and separate tongs.
  • Cost every wrap weekly against market prices; adjust portion or price before margin slips below 60%.
