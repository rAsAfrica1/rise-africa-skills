Lessons

1. Measuring Average Daily Gain and Feed Conversion

Average Daily Gain (ADG) is the simplest way to know if your steers are growing well. To calculate ADG, weigh each steer at the start of the fattening period and again after a set number of days. Subtract the starting weight from the final weight, then divide by the number of days. For example, if a steer starts at 250 kg and reaches 350 kg in 90 days, the ADG is (350-250)/90 = 1.11 kg per day. In Africa, a good ADG for fattening steers is between 0.8 and 1.2 kg per day, depending on breed and feed quality. If your ADG is below 0.5 kg, you are losing money. Weigh your steers every 30 days to track progress. Use a livestock scale if available; if not, use a weigh tape, which measures heart girth and converts it to weight. Keep a notebook or a simple spreadsheet to record weights.

Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) tells you how efficiently your steers turn feed into meat. It is the amount of feed eaten divided by the weight gained. For instance, if a steer eats 8 kg of feed per day and gains 1 kg, the FCR is 8:1. Lower FCR is better. A typical FCR for fattening steers on a balanced diet is between 6:1 and 8:1. If your FCR is above 10:1, your feed is poor or the steers are sick. To improve FCR, ensure your feed is properly balanced with energy, protein, minerals, and vitamins. Use locally available feeds like maize silage, cottonseed cake, and molasses. Weigh the feed you give each day and record it. Then calculate FCR every month. If FCR worsens, check for parasites, adjust the ration, or consult a vet. Remember, feed is your biggest cost, so improving FCR by even 1 point can save you a lot of money.

2. Using Scales and Weighing Schedules

Weighing your steers is the only way to know if they are gaining weight and if your feeding program is working. You need a reliable scale. The best option is a livestock scale, which is a platform with a digital readout. These cost between $500 and $2000, but you can share one with other farmers or hire one from a cooperative. If you cannot afford a scale, use a weigh tape. A weigh tape is a flexible tape that you wrap around the steer's heart girth, just behind the front legs. The tape has markings that convert the measurement to weight. Weigh tapes cost about $5 and are accurate to within 5-10 kg. For small herds, a bathroom scale can be used to weigh calves, but not mature steers.

Set a weighing schedule and stick to it. Weigh each steer at the start of the fattening period, then every 30 days, and at the end before selling. Always weigh at the same time of day, preferably in the morning before feeding, to avoid variations from gut fill. Weigh each animal individually and record the weight in a notebook or a record book. Use ear tags or numbered collars to identify each steer. If you have many steers, weigh them in groups but record individual weights. After weighing, calculate the ADG for each steer and for the herd average. If some steers are not gaining, separate them and check for health problems or adjust their feed. Keep your scale clean and calibrate it regularly by weighing a known object like a 50 kg bag of feed. A good weighing schedule helps you make timely decisions, such as when to sell or when to change feed.

3. Record Keeping Systems and Software

Record keeping is the backbone of a profitable steer fattening business. Without records, you are guessing. Start with a simple notebook or a ledger book. Record every event: date, steer ID, weight, feed given, medication, and costs. Use a separate page for each steer or a table with columns. For example, create a table with columns for Date, Steer ID, Weight (kg), Feed Type, Feed Amount (kg), Medication, and Notes. At the end of each week, transfer the data to a summary sheet. This manual system works well for up to 50 steers. Keep your records in a safe, dry place.

As your herd grows, consider using software. Microsoft Excel or Google Sheets is a good next step. You can create a spreadsheet with formulas to calculate ADG and FCR automatically. For example, in Excel, use the formula =(B2-A2)/C2 for ADG, where B2 is final weight, A2 is initial weight, and C2 is days. There are also specialized livestock management apps like Farmbrite, CattleMax, or Herdwatch. These apps let you record data on your phone, even offline, and generate reports. Some are free for small herds. In Africa, apps like iCow and M-Farm are popular. Choose a system that fits your budget and tech comfort. The key is consistency: record data every day. At the end of the month, review your records to see which steers are performing best and which feeds give the best results. Good records help you get loans from banks or cooperatives because you can prove your profitability.

4. Analyzing Performance Data for Decision Making

Collecting data is useless unless you analyze it to make decisions. Start by calculating key performance indicators (KPIs) for your herd: Average Daily Gain (ADG), Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR), and cost per kg of gain. For example, if your herd's ADG is 0.7 kg and you want 1.0 kg, you know you need to improve feed quality or health. Compare the performance of different groups: steers on feed A versus feed B, or steers in pen 1 versus pen 2. Look for patterns. If one group has lower ADG, check if they are getting less feed, if the feed is moldy, or if they have parasites. Use a simple bar chart in Excel to visualize weights over time. If the line is flat, something is wrong.

Analyze your feed costs. Calculate the cost of feed per kg of gain. If you spend $2 on feed for 1 kg of gain, and the market price is $3 per kg live weight, your margin is $1. If feed cost rises to $2.50, your margin drops. Decide whether to switch to cheaper feed ingredients or sell earlier. Also analyze mortality and morbidity. If more than 2% of your steers die, investigate. Record the cause and adjust management. Use your records to forecast. If your steers gain 1 kg per day and you have 100 steers, you can predict you will have 10,000 kg extra in 100 days. This helps you plan sales and negotiate prices. Finally, set targets and review them monthly. If you miss targets, identify why and take action. Data-driven decisions separate successful farmers from struggling ones.

5. Benchmarking Against Industry Standards

Benchmarking means comparing your performance to what other farmers or industry standards achieve. It helps you know if you are doing well or if you need to improve. In Africa, typical benchmarks for steer fattening are: ADG of 0.8-1.2 kg/day, FCR of 6:1 to 8:1, mortality under 2%, and cost per kg gain under $1.50. These vary by country and breed. For example, in South Africa, feedlot steers achieve ADG of 1.5 kg/day, while in Kenya, smallholders may get 0.8 kg/day. Find local benchmarks from your ministry of agriculture, farmer cooperatives, or extension officers. Visit other farms and ask about their performance. Join a farmer group where you can share data.

Once you have benchmarks, compare your records. If your ADG is 0.6 kg/day, you are below the standard. Analyze why: is your feed low in protein? Are your steers sick? Are they of a poor breed? Then set a goal to reach 0.8 kg/day within three months. If your FCR is 10:1, aim to reduce it to 8:1 by improving feed quality and reducing waste. Benchmarking also applies to costs. If your feed cost per kg gain is $2.00 and the benchmark is $1.50, look for cheaper feed sources or reduce wastage. Use benchmarks to negotiate better prices for inputs or to set selling prices. Remember, benchmarks are guides, not absolute rules. Your context matters: local feed prices, climate, and market demand. But regularly comparing your numbers to others will push you to improve. Keep a benchmark chart on your wall and update it monthly.

6. Financial Records and Profitability Analysis

Financial records track money in and out. You need to know if your steer fattening business is making a profit. Start by recording all costs: purchase of steers, feed, veterinary care, labor, water, electricity, and equipment. Also record all income: sale of steers, manure, and any other by-products. Use a simple cash book or a spreadsheet. For example, in Excel, create columns for Date, Description, Income, Expenses, and Balance. At the end of each month, total your income and expenses. Subtract expenses from income to get profit. If profit is negative, you are losing money and must adjust.

Calculate key financial ratios. The most important is profit margin: (Total Income - Total Expenses) / Total Income x 100. If your margin is 20%, you keep $0.20 of every dollar earned. Another is return on investment (ROI): (Profit / Total Investment) x 100. If you invested $10,000 and made $2,000 profit, ROI is 20%. Also calculate the break-even price: the price per kg you need to cover costs. If your total cost for 100 steers is $50,000 and you expect 30,000 kg live weight, break-even is $1.67/kg. If market price is $2.00/kg, you make a profit. Analyze your records to find ways to increase profit: reduce feed costs by buying in bulk, improve ADG to sell heavier steers, or negotiate better prices. Keep financial records for at least five years for tax and loan purposes. Review your profitability monthly and make adjustments. A profitable farm is one that knows its numbers.

<p style="margin:0; font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:15px; color:#922b21;"><strong> SAFETY WARNING ANIMAL HANDLING AND WEIGHING EQUIPMENT</strong></p>
<p style="margin:10px 0 0 0; font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:13px; color:#641e16;">Cattle handling for weighing is the highest-risk routine activity on a feedlot. Always use a proper crush or race with a head gate; never weigh loose animals on a platform without restraint. Inspect load bars, cables and gates before every session. Never stand between an animal and a solid object, and never work alone with mature bulls or excitable steers. In African field conditions, ensure the weighing crate is level, anchored, and shaded to reduce heat stress. Have a trained handler on the head gate at all times. If an animal goes down, do not attempt to lift it manually use a hip lifter and call for assistance. Record any injury or equipment failure immediately.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Weigh every animal at entry and exit</strong> and at least every 30 days interim. Use a calibrated crush scale, not eye estimation.</li>
<li><strong>Record pen ID, date, weight, and feed delivered</strong> at the same time every day. Inconsistent timing destroys data quality.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate ADG weekly</strong> per pen: (current average weight entry average weight) days on feed.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate FCR per pen</strong>: total feed dry matter consumed total live weight gain. Convert all feed to a dry matter basis.</li>
<li><strong>Track cost of gain</strong> (feed + health + labour + overheads kg gain) this is the number that determines profit, not ADG alone.</li>
<li><strong>Benchmark each pen against Table 1</strong> monthly. Flag any pen below "Average" for immediate investigation.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a backup of all records</strong> whether paper or digital. A duplicate notebook or weekly photo of the record sheet protects you against loss.</li>
<li><strong>Review financial records monthly</strong>: cash flow, feed cost per head per day, and projected break-even sale price.</li>
</ul>
