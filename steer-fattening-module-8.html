Module 8

Records, Costing and Feed Conversion

Fattening is a margin business, not a cattle business. This module teaches the small set of records that tell you whether a batch is making or losing money, how to work out your true cost per kilogram of gain, how to calculate a break-even sale price that is honest about dressing percentage, and how to spot a losing batch in week three instead of on sale day.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • You will be able to keep the six records that actually change decisions, on paper, in under ten minutes a week.
  • You will be able to weigh or estimate weight consistently and calculate average daily gain for a pen and for an individual animal.
  • You will be able to calculate feed conversion in kilograms of dry matter per kilogram of gain and compare it against published ranges.
  • You will be able to work out cost per kilogram of gain, including feed, purchase, health, labour and losses.
  • You will be able to calculate a break-even sale price on a live weight basis and on a carcass weight basis, and explain why the two are not comparable.
  • You will be able to use margin over feed cost to decide each month whether to sell an animal now or feed it on.

Lesson 8.114 min

The Six Records That Change Decisions

In this lesson
  • Identify the small set of records that affect a decision
  • Set up a one page pen card and a batch ledger
  • Separate cash records from physical records

Most fatteners keep either no records or too many. Both fail for the same reason: no number in front of you when you have to decide something. A record is only worth keeping if you can name the decision it changes. Everything else is history.

The six records

  1. Purchase record per animal. Date, seller, price paid, transport cost, ear tag or description, and starting weight. This is the base of every later calculation. If you do not know what an animal cost you and what it weighed on arrival, you can never know what you made on it.
  2. Weights. Arrival weight, then a weight every 28 to 30 days, then a final weight before sale. Weights are what turn feed into a measurable result.
  3. Feed delivered to the pen. Kilograms of each ingredient fed per day, per pen. Not what you planned to feed. What actually went in the trough.
  4. Feed bought and its cost. Date, supplier, quantity, price per bag or per tonne, and moisture condition if it matters. Feed price moves; your costing must use the price you actually paid for the feed you actually fed.
  5. Health and treatments. Date, animal, problem, product used, dose, and the withdrawal period printed on the label. This is a costing record and a food safety record at the same time.
  6. Sales. Date, buyer, weight basis used, weight, price, deductions, transport, and what you were actually paid after everything came off.

Two different kinds of record

Keep cash records and physical records separately, then bring them together. Cash records answer: how much money left my hand. Physical records answer: how many kilograms did I create. Profit lives in the space between them. Farmers who track only cash cannot tell a bad price from bad performance, and those are two completely different problems with completely different fixes.

The pen card

A pen card is one sheet of paper on the pen wall or in a plastic sleeve. Top of the card: pen number, number of animals, date in, total arrival weight. Then a simple grid with one row per day for feed fed, and one row per month for weights. Anyone who feeds can fill it in. If your feeder cannot read easily, use a tally system with a fixed scoop size and count scoops.

The batch ledger

The batch ledger is one page per batch, kept where you keep money records. It carries forward: total purchase cost, total feed cost to date, total health cost to date, other costs, and total kilograms gained to date. You update it once a month, on the same day you weigh. That single monthly hour is the most valuable work you do in the business.

Common failures

  • Recording feed by bags without knowing bag weight or moisture. A bag is not a unit; a kilogram is.
  • Mixing batches in one ledger, so a good batch hides a bad one.
  • Not costing your own home grown feed. If you could have sold that maize or hay, it has a cost, and you must enter the price you could have sold it for.
  • Not costing family labour. It may not be paid in cash, but it is real, and pretending it is free hides a business that cannot afford to hire anyone.

Start simple. Six records, one pen card, one ledger page, one hour a month. Add detail only when a specific decision needs it.

Lesson 8.215 min

Weighing, Weight Estimation and Average Daily Gain

In this lesson
  • Weigh or estimate weight in a consistent, repeatable way
  • Calculate average daily gain for a pen and an animal
  • Understand gut fill and why weighing conditions must be fixed

You cannot manage gain you do not measure. But a weight taken carelessly is worse than no weight, because it makes you confident about a wrong number.

Gut fill: the reason weighing rules exist

A large part of a beef animal's live weight at any moment is feed and water in the gut. That fill changes through the day and with the ration. Two weighings of the same animal hours apart can differ by a noticeable margin without a single gram of real tissue change. The fix is not a better scale. The fix is a fixed routine.

  • Weigh at the same time of day every time, ideally early morning before the first feed.
  • Weigh under the same water conditions every time.
  • Weigh the same animals in the same order, calm, no chasing.
  • Never compare a weight taken off a truck against a weight taken in the pen.

If you have no scale

A heart girth weigh band is the standard field tool. Measure the circumference of the chest just behind the front legs and behind the shoulder blade, with the animal standing square and its head level. The band converts girth to an estimated weight. Estimates from girth are typically within about 5 to 10 percent of scale weight for animals of the type the band was built for, and the error gets larger when breed or body shape differs from the band's reference animals.

The important point is this: even a band that reads a bit high or a bit low is still useful, because you are mostly interested in the change between two measurements taken the same way. A consistent bias largely cancels out when you subtract. An inconsistent method does not.

Calculating average daily gain

Average daily gain, or ADG, is:

ADG = (weight now minus weight at last weighing) divided by number of days between them

Worked example. An animal was 280 kg on day 0 and 318 kg on day 30.

  • Gain = 318 minus 280 = 38 kg
  • Days = 30
  • ADG = 38 divided by 30 = 1.27 kg per day

For a pen, use total pen weight rather than averaging individual ADGs, because animals may be sold or die mid period. Pen ADG = (total weight now minus total weight before) divided by (days times number of animals present).

What good looks like

On a well balanced finishing ration with decent cattle, average daily gain of about 1.0 to 1.5 kg per day is the commonly reported range. Under 0.8 kg per day on a finishing ration means something is wrong: ration, water, parasites, disease, heat, mud, bunk management or the cattle themselves. Above 1.5 kg per day happens, often early in the feeding period when animals that were previously underfed show compensatory gain, but do not build a budget on it.

Weigh the right animals

If handling every animal is not practical, weigh a fixed sample of the same identified animals every time, at least a quarter of the pen, chosen to include your best, your average and your poorest. Never re-select the sample each month, because you will unconsciously pick the ones that look good and your records will lie to you in a very flattering way.

Record it immediately

Write weights on the pen card at the crush, not later from memory. Then transfer to the ledger the same day.

Lesson 8.315 min

Feed Conversion: Dry Matter In, Kilograms Out

In this lesson
  • Convert as fed weights to dry matter
  • Calculate feed conversion ratio for a pen
  • Read what a worsening conversion figure is telling you

Feed conversion ratio, or FCR, is the number of kilograms of feed dry matter it takes to put on one kilogram of live weight gain. It is the single best early warning number in a fattening business, because it moves before your bank balance does.

Dry matter, not wet weight

Feeds carry water in very different amounts. A dry grain or a bag of meal is mostly dry matter. Silage, green forage, brewers grains and wet by products carry a great deal of water. If you compare feeds on as fed weight you will draw wrong conclusions and you will underfeed or overfeed.

Convert like this:

Dry matter kg = as fed kg times (dry matter percent divided by 100)

Worked example. You fed 400 kg as fed of wet brewers grains at 25 percent dry matter, plus 300 kg of maize meal at 88 percent dry matter, plus 200 kg of hay at 90 percent dry matter.

  • Brewers grains: 400 times 0.25 = 100 kg DM
  • Maize meal: 300 times 0.88 = 264 kg DM
  • Hay: 200 times 0.90 = 180 kg DM
  • Total = 544 kg DM

Get dry matter percentages from your feed supplier, a feed analysis, or published feed tables for your region. If you cannot get a figure, say so in your records rather than guessing precisely.

Calculating FCR

FCR = total kg dry matter eaten by the pen divided by total kg of live weight gain in the pen

Worked example. A pen of 10 steers over 30 days. Total dry matter fed: 2,850 kg. Pen weight went from 2,800 kg to 3,190 kg.

  • Gain = 3,190 minus 2,800 = 390 kg
  • FCR = 2,850 divided by 390 = 7.3 kg DM per kg gain

Roughly 6 to 10 kg of dry matter per kg of gain is the commonly reported range for finishing cattle. Below 6 is unusual and should make you check your weighing and feed records before you celebrate. Above 10 means you are buying gain expensively and must find out why.

Reading the number

FCR gets worse as an animal gets heavier and fatter. That is normal biology, not a failure: a bigger animal spends more feed just maintaining itself before any gain happens, and fat tissue costs more energy to lay down than muscle. Expect the figure to drift upward through the feeding period. What you are watching for is a sudden worsening or a level much worse than the range above.

Causes of poor conversion, in the order worth checking

  1. Water. Restricted or dirty water cuts intake before anything else does.
  2. Feed wastage. Feed pushed out of the trough, spoiled, rained on or eaten by other stock is counted as fed but never converted.
  3. Parasites and disease. Internal parasites quietly destroy conversion.
  4. Ration balance. Energy and protein out of balance, or sudden ration changes causing acidosis and off feed days.
  5. Environment. Heat, mud, overcrowding, no shade, constant disturbance.
  6. The cattle. Very old animals and animals already fat convert poorly no matter what you do.

Intake as a cross check

Finishing cattle on a high energy ration commonly eat dry matter equal to roughly 2.0 to 2.5 percent of their live weight per day. If your recorded intake is far outside that, either your feed records or your weight records are wrong.

Lesson 8.416 min

Cost Per Kilogram of Gain

In this lesson
  • List every cost that belongs in cost of gain
  • Calculate feed cost per kg of gain and total cost per kg of gain
  • Use the figure to compare feed options honestly

Cost per kilogram of gain is what you paid to create one extra kilogram of live animal. It is the number that tells you whether feeding is worth doing at all. Note that all money figures below are in CU, a placeholder currency unit. Never use a number from a course as your local price. Get your own.

Feed cost of gain

Feed cost per kg gain = total feed cost for the period divided by total kg gained in the period

Worked example, one pen, 30 days, 390 kg gained (from the previous lesson).

ItemQuantityCost (CU)
Maize meal900 kg2,700
Brewers grains1,200 kg as fed600
Hay600 kg900
Mineral and urea premix30 kg300
Total feed4,500

Feed cost per kg gain = 4,500 divided by 390 = 11.5 CU per kg of gain.

Full cost of gain

Feed is the dominant running cost, commonly the largest share of variable cost in published feedlot budgets, but it is not the only one. Add to the same period:

  • Health: vaccines, dosing, dips, treatment, vet visits
  • Labour: paid wages, and a fair value for your own and family time
  • Water and power: pumping, grinding, mixing
  • Transport: bringing feed in
  • Bedding, repairs, small equipment
  • Mortality: the cost of animals that died, spread over the survivors
  • Interest: if you borrowed, the interest for the days the money was out

Continuing the example, suppose those other costs came to 900 CU for the month. Total cost = 5,400 CU. Full cost per kg gain = 5,400 divided by 390 = 13.8 CU per kg.

Why the purchase price is not in this number

The purchase price of the animal is usually the single biggest cash outlay in the whole batch, but it does not belong in cost of gain, because it did not buy any gain. It bought an animal. Keep it separate. Cost of gain answers should I keep feeding. Purchase price plus cost of gain together answer did the batch make money. Confusing the two is the most common costing mistake in this business.

Using the number

Compare full cost per kg of gain against the price you expect to receive per kg of live weight. If the cost of putting on a kilogram is higher than the value of that kilogram, further feeding destroys money, and the correct decision is to sell, not to try harder.

Also use it to compare feed options. A cheaper feed with worse conversion can easily be the more expensive choice. Work it out:

Feed cost per kg gain = feed price per kg DM times FCR

Worked comparison, in CU per kg of dry matter:

  • Ration A: 3.00 CU per kg DM, FCR 7.0. Cost of gain = 21.0 CU per kg.
  • Ration B: 2.40 CU per kg DM, FCR 9.5. Cost of gain = 22.8 CU per kg.

Ration B is 20 percent cheaper per kilogram and still loses. This is why cheap feed is not the same as low cost gain, and why you cannot buy feed on price alone.

Do it monthly

Calculate cost of gain every time you weigh. One figure, written in the ledger, next to the ADG and the FCR. Three numbers, one line, once a month.

Feed share of variable cost
commonly the largest single share
Feedlot budgets vary widely by country and ration; calculate your own share rather than assuming one
Cost of gain formula
feed price per kg DM times FCR
Lets you compare two rations before you buy either

Lesson 8.516 min

Break-Even Sale Price and the Dressing Percentage Trap

In this lesson
  • Calculate a break-even price on a live weight basis
  • Convert between live weight and carcass weight prices
  • Avoid comparing two prices that are not comparable

Break-even is the price at which a batch returns exactly what it cost you and no more. Knowing it before you talk to a buyer changes the whole conversation, because you know the number below which you are working for nothing.

Live weight break-even

Break-even price per kg live = (purchase cost + all costs to date) divided by expected sale live weight

Worked example in CU. One steer:

  • Purchase price: 9,000 CU for a 280 kg store animal
  • Purchase transport and commission: 600 CU
  • Feed over 100 days: 4,800 CU
  • Health, labour, water, share of mortality: 1,200 CU
  • Total cost: 15,600 CU
  • Expected sale weight: 400 kg live

Break-even = 15,600 divided by 400 = 39.0 CU per kg live weight. Below that price you lose money. At 45 CU per kg you gross 18,000 CU and clear 2,400 CU before selling costs.

The dressing percentage trap

This is where fatteners lose real money, and it is entirely avoidable. Dressing percentage is:

Dressing percent = (cold or hot carcass weight divided by live weight) times 100

For finished cattle it is commonly in the range of about 50 to 58 percent, and it depends on condition, breed, sex, gut fill and whether the carcass is weighed hot or cold. A leaner animal, a bigger gut, or a long green forage diet all pull it down. A well finished animal off a concentrate ration sits higher.

The trap: a price quoted per kilogram of carcass weight is a completely different number from a price per kilogram of live weight, for the same animal, at the same value. Never compare them directly. Convert first.

Live weight equivalent price = carcass price per kg times dressing percent divided by 100

Worked example. A buyer offers 80 CU per kg carcass weight. Another offers 42 CU per kg live weight. Which is better?

  • At 52 percent dressing: 80 times 0.52 = 41.6 CU per kg live. The live weight offer of 42 is slightly better.
  • At 56 percent dressing: 80 times 0.56 = 44.8 CU per kg live. Now the carcass offer is clearly better.

The whole answer hinges on a dressing percentage you do not know in advance. That is the point. When you sell on carcass weight you carry the dressing risk; when you sell on live weight the buyer carries it, and prices the risk into the offer.

Carcass basis break-even

To set a break-even for a carcass sale, work out expected carcass weight first:

  • 400 kg live times 0.53 = 212 kg carcass
  • Break-even = 15,600 divided by 212 = 73.6 CU per kg carcass

Now build a small table across the plausible dressing range, so you know what you need at each outcome. At 50 percent you need 78.0 CU per kg; at 56 percent you need 69.6 CU per kg. That spread is your real risk, and you should quote it to yourself before the buyer quotes anything to you.

Deductions

Break-even must include what comes off after the sale: transport to the abattoir, levies, commission, condemnations, bank charges. Ask any buyer, in advance and plainly, what will be deducted and on what basis. Get it in writing if you can. A price is not a price until you know the deductions.

Confirm locally

Grading and payment systems differ between countries and even between abattoirs. Phone three local buyers, at least one abattoir and one butcher, and ask specifically: do you pay on live weight or carcass weight, hot or cold, what deductions apply, and how do you grade.

Tracking a Batch and Spotting a Losing Batch Early

In this lesson
  • Build a simple monthly batch tracking sheet
  • Use margin over feed cost as a sell or feed on decision rule
  • Recognise the early signs of a batch that will not pay

A losing batch almost always announces itself in the first four to six weeks. The problem is that most fatteners are not looking at the numbers that announce it, so they only find out on sale day, when nothing can be done.

The monthly tracking line

Every 30 days, write one line in the ledger with six figures:

Read that table across. Gain slows, conversion worsens, cost of gain climbs. That is normal as animals finish. But notice what happens at day 90: cost of gain is 19.5 CU per kg while the market pays 44 CU per kg live. Each extra kilogram still earns more than it costs, so feeding on is still adding money, even though the animal is converting worse than it did in month one. The day feeding must stop is the day cost of gain rises above the value of a kilogram of live weight, not the day conversion starts slipping.

Margin over feed cost

Margin over feed is the simplest monthly health check:

Margin over feed = (kg gained times expected sale price per kg) minus feed cost for the period

Worked example. Month with 34 kg gained, expected price 44 CU per kg live, feed cost 1,500 CU.

  • Value of gain = 34 times 44 = 1,496 CU
  • Margin over feed = 1,496 minus 1,500 = minus 4 CU

That month, feed alone ate the entire value of the gain, before any labour, health or interest. One such month is a warning. Two in a row means sell.

Early warning signs, week by week

  • Weeks 1 to 3. Cattle not settling, low intake, scouring, coughing, animals standing away from the bunk. Watch for animals that do not eat within a day or two of arrival. Arrival stress and mishandled adaptation cost weeks of gain you never get back.
  • Week 4 first weighing. ADG under about 0.8 kg per day on a finishing ration. Investigate immediately; do not wait for the second weighing to confirm what you already suspect.
  • Any month. FCR above about 10, or a jump of more than about 1.5 units from the previous month with no ration change.
  • Any month. Feed cost per kg of gain rising while the market price is flat or falling.
  • Any time. Buying animals that were already fat, or very old animals. They convert poorly, and no ration rescues them.

What to do about a losing batch

You have four options and only four. Fix the cause if it is fixable within days, such as water, wastage, parasites or a ration error. Sell early and cut the loss. Change the target market to one that accepts lighter or leaner animals. Or feed on deliberately, but only if you have calculated that the extra kilograms still cost less than they earn.

The wrong option, and the common one, is to keep feeding and hope. Hope is not a decision rule. The table above is.

Knowledge check

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A buyer offers 85 CU per kg carcass weight. You expect a dressing percentage of 54 percent. What is the equivalent live weight price?

Live weight equivalent = carcass price times dressing percent, so 85 times 0.54 = 45.9 CU per kg live. Always convert before comparing offers.

2. A pen ate 2,400 kg of dry matter and gained 300 kg. What is the feed conversion ratio, and how does it compare with the usual range?

2,400 divided by 300 = 8.0 kg DM per kg gain, which sits inside the commonly reported 6 to 10 range for finishing cattle.

3. Why should the purchase price of the animal be kept out of your cost per kilogram of gain?

Purchase price buys an animal, not gain. Cost of gain answers whether to keep feeding; purchase price plus cost of gain answers whether the batch made money.

4. Ration A costs 3.20 CU per kg DM with an FCR of 7. Ration B costs 2.60 CU per kg DM with an FCR of 9.5. Which gives cheaper gain?

Cost of gain equals feed price per kg DM times FCR. A gives 3.20 times 7 = 22.4; B gives 2.60 times 9.5 = 24.7. Cheaper feed is not always cheaper gain.

5. At the day 90 weighing, cost of gain is 19 CU per kg and the live weight market price is 44 CU per kg, but FCR has worsened from 7 to 9.6. What is the correct decision?

Worsening FCR is normal as animals finish. The stop feeding rule is cost of gain rising above the value of a kilogram of live weight, which has not happened here.
