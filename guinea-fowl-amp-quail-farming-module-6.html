1. Brooder Setup: Temperature, Bedding, and Space for Guinea Keets and Quail Chicks

A brooder is a warm, safe box for chicks. For guinea keets and quail chicks, you need a brooder that keeps them at 35C (95F) in the first week. Use a 100-watt bulb for every 30 chicks. Hang the bulb 45 cm above the floor. Raise it by 7 cm each week to lower the temperature by about 3C. By week 4, they need 24C. Watch the chicks: if they huddle under the bulb, they are cold; if they spread to corners, they are too hot. They should be evenly spread.

For bedding, use wood shavings, not sawdust (chicks eat it and get sick). Rice husks or chopped straw also work. Spread 5 cm deep. Never use newspaperit causes leg problems. Clean bedding every 2 days. Wet bedding grows mold and bacteria.

Space is critical. Guinea keets need 0.5 square feet per bird in week 1, increasing to 1 square foot by week 4. Quail chicks are smaller: 0.25 square feet per bird in week 1, up to 0.5 square feet by week 4. Overcrowding leads to pecking and disease. Use a cardboard box or wooden crate. For 50 keets, start with a box 1m x 1m. Cover the top with wire mesh to keep out rats and snakes. Place the brooder in a quiet, dry place away from drafts.

Before chicks arrive, warm the brooder for 2 hours. Dip each chick's beak in water with vitamins (see Lesson 2). Then put them under the bulb. Check them every 2 hours for the first day. Adjust the bulb height as needed.

2. Feeding Chicks: Starter Feed and Water with Vitamins

Chicks need starter feed with 24% protein for guinea keets and 28% for quail chicks. Buy commercial chick starter mash or crumbles. If unavailable, mix your own: 50% maize meal, 20% soybean meal, 15% fish meal, 10% groundnut cake, 5% minerals and vitamins. Grind finelychicks cannot swallow large pieces. Feed them every 3 hours for the first week, then 4 times a day. Use a shallow tray or egg carton as a feeder. Clean leftover feed daily to prevent mold.

Water is more important than feed. Chicks can die from dehydration in 6 hours. Use a shallow dish with clean water. Add vitamins and electrolytes for the first 3 days. You can buy chick vitamin drops at agrovet shops. Mix 1 teaspoon per liter of water. Also add 1 tablespoon of glucose (or honey) per liter for energy. Change water 3 times a day. Never let water sitchicks will poop in it.

For the first 2 days, dip each chick's beak into the water. They learn fast. Use a waterer with a narrow lip to prevent drowning. For quail chicks, put marbles or small stones in the water dish so they don't get soaked.

From week 2, you can add finely chopped green vegetables like kale or amaranth. This gives vitamins and reduces feed costs. But do not exceed 10% of their diet. Always provide grit (small stones) for digestion. A handful of sand in the feed works. By week 4, switch to grower feed with 20% protein. Keep feed and water available at all timeschicks eat little but often.

3. Common Chick Diseases and Simple Treatments

The most common killer is coccidiosis. Signs: bloody droppings, ruffled feathers, chicks standing hunched. Treat with amprolium (e.g., Amprol) in water for 5 days. Prevent by keeping bedding dry and clean. Another killer is Newcastle diseasesigns: gasping, twisted neck, green diarrhea. There is no cure; vaccinate (see Lesson 4).

Pullorum disease (white diarrhea) hits chicks under 3 weeks. Signs: white pasty vent, weakness. Treat with antibiotics like oxytetracycline in water for 5 days. Isolate sick chicks immediately. Always wash hands after handling sick birds.

Fowl pox shows as scabs on head and legs. Treat by applying iodine to scabs. Vaccinate at 6 weeks if common in your area. Aspergillosis (brooder pneumonia) comes from moldy bedding. Signs: gasping, no rales. Remove wet bedding, improve ventilation. No cureprevention is key.

Simple treatments you can do: For diarrhea, give 1 tablespoon of apple cider vinegar per liter of water for 3 days. For respiratory distress, add 1 crushed garlic clove per liter of water. For wounds, clean with warm salt water and apply honey. Keep a sick pen away from healthy chicks. Use a separate waterer and feeder.

Always check with a local vet or extension officer before using any drug. Withdraw antibiotics 5 days before selling eggs or meat. Record all treatments in a notebook. Healthy chicks are active, bright-eyed, and eat well. If you see 3 or more sick chicks, act fastdisease spreads in hours.

4. Vaccination Schedule for Guinea Fowl and Quail in Africa

Vaccines are cheap; dead chicks are expensive. For guinea fowl, start at day 1: Marek's disease vaccine (injected at hatchery). If you hatch at home, skip Marek'sit's not practical. At day 7: Newcastle disease (ND) vaccine via eye drop or drinking water. Repeat at day 21 and day 42. At day 14: Gumboro (IBD) vaccine in drinking water. Repeat at day 28.

For quail, vaccination is less common but recommended in disease-prone areas. At day 7: Newcastle disease vaccine (eye drop). Repeat every 3 months. Quail are more resistant to some diseases, but ND can wipe a flock. Also vaccinate against fowl pox at 6 weeks if you see cases nearby.

How to vaccinate via drinking water: Withdraw water for 2 hours. Mix vaccine in clean water (no chlorine) with 2 grams of skim milk powder per liter to protect the virus. Give enough for 1 hour of drinking. Use plastic drinkers, not metal. For eye drop: put one drop in one eye, hold chick until it blinks. For injection: use a 1 ml syringe under the skin of the neck.

Store vaccines in a flask with ice. Never freeze. Use within 2 hours of mixing. Buy from a reputable agrovet. In Africa, ND is the biggest killer. Many countries have government vaccination programsask your local veterinary office. Keep a vaccination record: date, vaccine name, batch number. If you sell chicks, buyers will pay more for vaccinated birds.

5. Gradual Introduction to Outdoor Pens

Chicks cannot go outside until they are fully feathered and the weather is warm. For guinea keets, wait until 4-5 weeks. For quail, 3-4 weeks. Start with short periods: 30 minutes on day 1, then add 30 minutes each day. Choose a warm, sunny day with no wind. The outdoor temperature should be at least 24C.

Build a simple outdoor pen: a wooden frame covered with wire mesh on all sides, including the top. The bottom should be wire mesh or concrete to stop digging predators. Size: 1 square meter for 20 keets or 40 quail. Put the pen on grass or dirt, but move it daily to fresh ground to avoid disease build-up. Provide shadea piece of corrugated iron or palm fronds. Always provide water and feed inside the pen.

Never leave chicks outside unattended. Hawks, snakes, cats, and even rats will kill them. Stay with them or use a secure covered pen. At night, bring them back to the brooder. After 1 week of daily outdoor time, they can stay outside during the day if the pen is predator-proof. By 6 weeks for keets and 5 weeks for quail, they can live outside permanently if the weather is warm. But provide a shelter at night.

Transition slowly: if chicks huddle or shiver, bring them in. If they pant and spread wings, they are too hotprovide more shade. After 2 weeks of successful outdoor days, you can leave them out overnight in a secure house. This gradual method prevents stress and disease.

6. Sexing Chicks: Telling Males from Females Early

Sexing chicks early saves feed and space. For guinea fowl, you cannot sex day-old keets by looking. But at 4-6 weeks, males start to make a one-syllable call 'check' while females make a two-syllable 'buck-wheat'. At 8 weeks, the helmet (casque) on the male's head is larger and more upright. The male's wattles are larger and hang lower. Also, males have a slightly bigger body and coarser feathers. For accurate sexing, wait until 10-12 weeks and check the vent: males have a small bump (papilla) inside the vent, females do not.

For quail, sexing is easier. At 3-4 weeks, males have a reddish-brown breast with dark spots, while females have a lighter, speckled breast. Males also have a larger, more rounded head and a distinct crow. In some breeds like Japanese quail, males have a cloacal gland (a small bump above the vent) that produces a white foam when pressed. Females lack this. You can also look at the feathers: male quail have longer, pointed feathers on the back and neck.

If you are unsure, separate suspected males into a different pen. Watch for mounting behaviormales mount females. Also, males fight more. For breeding, keep 1 male to 4 females for guinea fowl and 1 male to 3 females for quail. Too many males cause stress and injury.

A simple trick: hold the chick upside down gently. Males often curl their tail up, females keep it down. But this is not 100% reliable. The best method is vent sexing by an experienced person. If you plan to sell breeding stock, learn vent sexing from a vet or extension officer. Practice on older birds first.

<h2>Practical Reference: Brooding and Raising Healthy Chicks</h2>

<p>Brooding is the single most important phase in guinea fowl and quail production. In Africa, where day-old keets and quail chicks can cost 3050% of total production expenses, mortality above 10% in the first three weeks destroys margins. This reference gives you the numbers, schedules, and practical steps to keep brooding losses below 5% and bring birds to market or laying age on time.</p>

<p>Guinea keets and quail chicks differ significantly. Guinea keets are hardy but slow to learn feeding and need warmth for 46 weeks. Quail chicks are tiny, fast, and need higher starting temperatures but feather out quicklybrooding lasts only 23 weeks. Treat them as two separate management systems under one roof.</p>

<h2>Brooder Setup: Temperature, Bedding, and Space</h2>

<p>Temperature is the first control lever. Set the brooder at 35C for guinea keets and 37C for quail chicks at day one. Reduce by 2.53C each week. By week four, keets should be at 2426C; quail at 24C by week two. In hot African climates, ambient temperatures above 30C mean you may only need supplementary heat at nightbut never skip the first 7 days.</p>

<p>Bedding: use dry wood shavings or chopped maize cobs. Never use sawdust (causes crop impaction) or wet straw (aspergillosis). Depth: 57 cm. Space: guinea keets need 0.05 m per bird in week one, rising to 0.15 m by week four. Quail chicks need 0.02 m per bird in week one, 0.05 m by week three. Overcrowding is the leading cause of cannibalism and wet litter.</p>

<h2>Feeding Chicks: Starter Feed and Water with Vitamins</h2>

<p>Guinea keets need 24% crude protein starter crumble for the first 4 weeks; quail chicks need 28% for the first 3 weeks. In many African markets, quail starter is unavailableuse turkey starter or mix your own: 60% maize, 25% soybean meal, 10% fish meal, 4% mineral-vitamin premix, 1% limestone. Always provide clean water with a vitamin-electrolyte solution for the first 5 days (glucose 5% + vitamin C + multivitamins).</p>

<p>Feed intake: keets eat 1015 g/day in week one, rising to 40 g/day by week four. Quail chicks eat 58 g/day in week one, 1520 g/day by week three. Use shallow, red-colored feeders and drinkersred reduces pecking. Add clean pebbles or marbles to drinkers to prevent drowning of quail chicks.</p>

<h2>Common Chick Diseases and Simple Treatments</h2>

<p><strong>Coccidiosis:</strong> bloody droppings, ruffled feathers. Treat with amprolium 1 g/L water for 5 days. Prevent with clean bedding and avoid wet litter.</p>
<p><strong>Newcastle disease:</strong> gasping, twisted neck, green watery droppings. No treatmentvaccinate. Isolate and cull.</p>
<p><strong>Pullorum (salmonellosis):</strong> white pasty vent, weakness. Use enrofloxacin 10 mg/kg for 5 days. Source chicks from certified hatcheries.</p>
<p><strong>Aspergillosis:</strong> gasping without noise, lung nodules. Remove moldy bedding, improve ventilation. No effective treatmentprevent.</p>
<p><strong>Cannibalism:</strong> pecking, bleeding. Reduce light to 15 lux, increase space, add greens. Apply gentian violet to wounds.</p>

<h2>Vaccination Schedule for Guinea Fowl and Quail in Africa</h2>

<p>Vaccination is non-negotiable for commercial production. Newcastle disease (ND) is the biggest killer in Africa. Follow this schedule:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Day 1:</strong> Marek's disease (if from hatchery).</li>
<li><strong>Day 7:</strong> Newcastle disease (ND) eye drop or drinking water.</li>
<li><strong>Day 14:</strong> Gumboro (IBD) drinking water.</li>
<li><strong>Day 21:</strong> ND booster drinking water.</li>
<li><strong>Day 28:</strong> Fowl pox wing web stab (guinea fowl only).</li>
<li><strong>Week 8:</strong> ND + IB combined drinking water.</li>
<li><strong>Week 12:</strong> ND booster injection.</li>
</ul>

<p>Quail are less susceptible to ND but still need vaccination in endemic areas. Use 1/3 of the chicken dose for quail. Always vaccinate in the cool of the morning. Use clean water without chlorine. Never vaccinate sick birds.</p>

<h2>Gradual Introduction to Outdoor Pens</h2>

<p>Do not move chicks outside before they are fully feathered. Guinea keets: week 56. Quail: week 34. Start with 30 minutes per day in a shaded, predator-proof pen. Increase by 30 minutes daily. Ensure outdoor temperature is above 20C. Provide feed and water outside to reduce stress. Guinea fowl are strong flyerscover pens with netting. Quail are ground birdsensure 1.5 m high walls and a roof.</p>

<h2>Sexing Chicks: Telling Males from Females Early</h2>

<p>Guinea fowl: vent sexing at day-old is difficult. At 46 weeks, males have larger, more upright helmets (casque) and louder calls. At 8 weeks, males have longer wattles and a more upright stance. Quail: feather sexing at day 35. Males have a uniform wing feather pattern; females have a staggered pattern. At 3 weeks, males have a reddish-brown breast (Japanese quail) and foam gland in the vent. Sexing accuracy: 8595% when done by trained staff.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li>Preheat brooder to 35C (keets) or 37C (quail) 24 hours before chick arrival.</li>
<li>Use dry wood shavings 57 cm deep. Never sawdust or moldy straw.</li>
<li>Provide vitamin-electrolyte water for first 5 days.</li>
<li>Feed 24% CP starter to keets, 28% CP to quail.</li>
<li>Reduce temperature by 2.53C weekly.</li>
<li>Vaccinate against Newcastle at day 7 and day 21.</li>
<li>Remove wet bedding daily; check for coccidiosis from week 2.</li>
<li>Move outdoors only after full feathering and gradual acclimatization.</li>
</ul>
