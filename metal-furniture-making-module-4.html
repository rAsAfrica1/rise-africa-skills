1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Welcome to Module 4: Cutting and Preparation. In metal furniture making, cutting is not just about making pieces shorter. It is about creating accurate, clean, and square components that will fit together perfectly. If you cut a leg 2mm too short, the table will wobble. If you cut at an angle, the frame will be twisted. Good cutting saves you hours of rework and wasted material.

In Africa, metal furniture is in high demand for homes, schools, offices, and outdoor spaces. Whether you are using mild steel square tubes, round pipes, or angle iron, the quality of your cut determines the strength and appearance of the final product. A poorly cut joint will be weak and ugly, no matter how good your welding is.

Preparation includes measuring, marking, cutting, and cleaning the metal before assembly. You must remove rust, oil, and burrs (sharp edges) so that welds and paint stick well. This module will teach you how to use common tools like the angle grinder, hacksaw, and cut-off saw safely and effectively.

You will learn step-by-step methods, common mistakes and how to fix them, and safety practices that protect your eyes, ears, and hands. By the end, you will be able to cut metal accurately and prepare it for welding or bolting. Remember: measure twice, cut once. Lets get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To cut and prepare metal for furniture, you need the right tools. The most common cutting tool in African workshops is the angle grinder with a 115mm or 230mm cutting disc. A 115mm grinder costs about $30-50 and can cut square tubes up to 50mm. For thicker metal or many cuts, use a cut-off saw (chop saw) with a 355mm abrasive disc. A hacksaw with a 24 TPI blade is good for small pipes and tight spaces.

For measuring and marking, you need a steel tape measure (5m), a try square (or engineers square), a scriber (or permanent marker), and a center punch for marking hole positions. A flat file (10-12 inch) is essential for deburring. You also need a wire brush to clean rust and a bench vise to hold metal securely.

Materials you will cut include mild steel square tubes (20x20mm, 25x25mm, 30x30mm), round pipes (15mm, 20mm, 25mm diameter), flat bars, and angle iron (25x25x3mm). Always check that your metal is straight and free of heavy rust before cutting. For safety, you need safety glasses, ear plugs, leather gloves, and a dust mask. A face shield is better than glasses when using a grinder.

Keep a file and a wire brush near your cutting area. Also have a container of water or a fire extinguisher nearby because grinding sparks can start fires. In many African workshops, a simple 1.5mm or 2mm thick cutting disc is used. Buy good quality discs; cheap ones break easily. Always store discs flat and dry.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Measure and mark. Use your tape measure and try square to mark the exact length on the metal. For square cuts, wrap a piece of paper or use a square to draw a line all around the tube. Use a scriber or marker. For round pipes, use a pipe wrap (a piece of paper wrapped around) to mark a straight line.

Step 2: Secure the metal. Clamp the metal in a bench vise or use clamps to hold it on a workbench. Never hold it with your hand while cutting. The metal should not move.

Step 3: Cut with the right tool. For an angle grinder, put on your safety gear. Start the grinder and let it reach full speed. Hold it at 90 degrees to the metal and move slowly along your line. Do not force it. Let the disc do the work. For a hacksaw, use long, steady strokes. Apply pressure on the forward stroke only.

Step 4: Check the cut. After cutting, check if the cut is square using your try square. If it is not square, you can file it flat. Also check the length. If it is too long, cut again; if too short, you may need to scrap it or use it for a shorter piece.

Step 5: Deburr and clean. Use a flat file to remove sharp burrs from the cut edge. Then use a wire brush to remove rust and dirt. Wipe with a rag. Now your metal is ready for welding or drilling. Always mark and cut all pieces for a project before welding. This saves time and ensures all parts fit.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Cutting without measuring twice. Many beginners mark once and cut, then find the piece is too short. Fix: Always measure twice, mark clearly, and check before cutting. Use a pencil or marker that is easy to see.

Mistake 2: Not using a square. If you cut at an angle, the frame will be twisted. Fix: Always use a try square to mark a line all around the tube. For round pipes, use a pipe wrap. Check with a square after cutting.

Mistake 3: Forcing the grinder. Pushing too hard can break the disc, overheat the metal, or cause kickback. Fix: Let the tool run at full speed and use light pressure. Move steadily. If the disc slows down, you are pushing too hard.

Mistake 4: Not deburring. Sharp burrs can cut your hands and prevent good welds. Fix: Always file the cut edges. A few strokes with a flat file is enough.

Mistake 5: Cutting rusty or dirty metal. Rust and oil can cause poor welds and ruin your cutting disc. Fix: Clean the metal with a wire brush before cutting. If it is very rusty, use a grinding wheel to remove rust first.

Mistake 6: Using a worn or damaged disc. A worn disc can shatter. Fix: Replace discs when they become small (less than 3 inches) or have chips. Always inspect before use.

Mistake 7: Not clamping the work. The metal can move and cause a crooked cut or injury. Fix: Always clamp securely. Use a vise or clamps. Never hold with your hand.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional in metal cutting. Every year, many workshop accidents happen because of flying sparks, broken discs, and sharp edges. Protect yourself.

Wear personal protective equipment (PPE): Safety glasses or a face shield, ear plugs or muffs, leather gloves, and a dust mask. Wear a leather apron and closed shoes. Never wear loose clothing or jewelry that can get caught in the grinder.

Before cutting, check your tools. Make sure the grinder guard is in place. Check the disc for cracks or chips. Do not use a disc that is damaged. Make sure the power cord is not damaged. Use a residual current device (RCD) if possible.

When cutting, keep sparks away from flammable materials. Have a fire extinguisher or bucket of water nearby. Do not cut near paint, petrol, or gas cylinders. Work in a well-ventilated area. Metal dust and fumes can be harmful.

After cutting, unplug the tool before changing discs or making adjustments. Store tools safely. Clean your work area. Dispose of sharp offcuts in a metal bin, not on the floor.

Best practice: Plan your cuts to minimize waste. Cut all pieces for a project at once. Label pieces if needed. Keep your work area organized. A clean shop is a safe shop. Always think ahead: where will the sparks go? What if the disc breaks? Being careful prevents injuries and saves money.

6. Practical Exercise

Now its time to practice. For this exercise, you will cut and prepare metal pieces for a simple stool or small table frame. You need: 4 pieces of 25x25mm square tube, each 400mm long (for legs), and 4 pieces of 20x20mm square tube, each 300mm long (for the seat frame). You also need an angle grinder with a cutting disc, a try square, a tape measure, a file, a wire brush, and safety gear.

Step 1: Measure and mark all 8 pieces. Use your tape and try square. Mark clearly with a scriber or marker. Double-check each measurement.

Step 2: Clamp one piece in a vise. Put on your safety gear. Cut along the line with the angle grinder. Remember to let the tool do the work. After cutting, check the length and squareness. File the burrs.

Step 3: Repeat for all pieces. Keep the pieces organized. You can label them with a marker (e.g., Leg 1, Leg 2).

Step 4: Clean all pieces with a wire brush to remove rust and dirt. Wipe with a rag.

Step 5: Check your work. All legs should be exactly 400mm. All frame pieces should be 300mm. The ends should be square. If any piece is too short, cut a new one. If it is not square, file it flat.

Step 6: Take a photo of your finished pieces and submit to your instructor. Also write down any problems you had and how you fixed them. This exercise will prepare you for the next module, where you will weld the frame. Practice until you can cut accurately and safely every time.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Cutting and Preparation for Metal Furniture Making

In African metal furniture workshops from Kariobangi in Nairobi to Nnewi in Anambra, from Soweto to Kumasi the difference between a product that sells and one that sits unsold often comes down to the first hour of work: cutting and preparation. A welder can be skilled, but if the steel is cut out of square, measured wrong, or prepared with the wrong tools, the finished chair, table, or gate will wobble, reject paint, and fail in the market. This lesson gives you the practical reference numbers, methods, and checklists that working African fabricators use every day. Treat it as your bench-side manual.

This module is business-first. Every technique here is chosen because it reduces waste, saves time, or improves the finish quality that customers pay for. If you are running a small metal furniture enterprise or planning to the numbers below directly affect your profit margin.

RED SAFETY WARNING HIGH RISK ACTIVITY

Cut-off discs and angle grinders cause more workshop amputations and eye injuries in African metal shops than any other tool. Never remove the guard. Never cut without a full-face shield and leather gloves. Never use a disc rated below the grinder's RPM (check the label 355 mm discs must be rated 3,800 RPM). Keep a bucket of clean water and a first-aid kit within 3 metres. If a disc jams, release the trigger and wait for it to stop do not pull the tool out while spinning. Every year, workshops in Nairobi, Lagos, and Johannesburg lose skilled workers to preventable grinder accidents. Protect your hands they are your business.

Quick Reference Checklist Cutting and Preparation

  1. Read the drawing twice. Confirm all dimensions and quantities before touching the steel. Measure twice, cut once.
  2. Calculate kerf. Add 1 mm for hacksaw, 23 mm for grinder or chop saw per cut. Never forget this it is the #1 cause of short pieces.
  3. Mark with scriber or soapstone. Pencil lines disappear under heat and are hard to see on dark steel.
  4. Cut longest pieces first. This maximises usable off-cuts and reduces waste by 1020%.
  5. Clamp firmly. Loose work causes crooked cuts, broken blades, and kickback injuries.
  6. Deburr every cut immediately. A file costs nothing; a tetanus injection and lost work days cost plenty.
  7. Check squareness after every cut. A try square is the cheapest quality-control tool you own.
  8. Label and group pieces. Use chalk or masking tape to mark "front leg," "rail," etc. welding goes faster and mistakes drop.
  9. Store off-cuts 200 mm. They become jigs, brackets, and repair stock real money saved.
  10. Clean and prepare weld zones. Remove mill scale and rust 20 mm from every joint before welding.
