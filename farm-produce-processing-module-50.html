Scaling Up, Export & Long-Term Growth

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Business

1 When to scale (the three signals)

Growing too fast kills businesses. Growing too slow loses opportunities. Watch for these three signals that say "you're ready":

  1. Sold out repeatedly — you regularly run out of stock within a week
  2. Cash reserves — you have 3+ months of running costs in the bank
  3. Systems work without you — quality is consistent even when you're away

If all three are true, you're ready. If any is false, fix it first before scaling.

💡 Scaling before systems are ready creates chaos — quality drops, customers leave, you burn out.

2 Adding new product lines

The easiest way to grow — sell more to existing customers.

Adjacent products: If you make peanut butter, add peanut oil, roasted peanuts, peanut flour. Same equipment, same suppliers, same buyers.

Complementary products: If you make tomato sauce, add tomato paste, ketchup, chilli sauce. Same tomatoes, same bottling line.

How to add successfully:

  1. Test with current customers (samples, pre-orders)
  2. Run a small batch — 100 units
  3. Get feedback before scaling up
  4. Add to price list only after it works

One new product per quarter is aggressive but achievable. Two per year is realistic for most small businesses.

3 Second production facility

Sometimes growth requires a second location. Do it deliberately.

Reasons to open a second facility:

  • Primary location at full capacity
  • Customers in a distant region (reduce delivery costs)
  • Different product line (separate facility makes sense)
  • Cheaper labour or rent in another location

Risks:

  • Splits your attention
  • Doubles overheads immediately
  • Requires a trusted manager at each site
  • Cash flow pressure — new site takes 6–12 months to break even

Before opening a second site, prove the model works at the first — with systems, not just hustle.

4 Export documentation and standards

Exporting is a whole new level. Prepare properly.

Documents required:

  • Commercial invoice
  • Packing list
  • Bill of lading (sea) or airway bill (air)
  • Certificate of origin
  • Phytosanitary certificate (plant products)
  • Health certificate (food products)
  • Insurance certificate
  • Export licence

Standards compliance:

  • HACCP for food safety (required by most buyers)
  • ISO 22000 for international food safety management
  • Organic certification (if applicable)
  • GlobalG.A.P. for fresh produce
  • Fairtrade (for premium pricing)

Get one buyer first, then build the compliance around their requirements.

5 HACCP and ISO certification

Certifications open doors. Two matter most for food export.

HACCP (Hazard Analysis Critical Control Points):

  • Required by EU, US, and most developed markets
  • Costs $1,000–$5,000 to implement (consultant + audit)
  • Renewed annually

ISO 22000:

  • International food safety standard — more comprehensive than HACCP
  • Costs $3,000–$15,000 depending on size
  • Opens doors to global retailers (Tesco, Walmart, Carrefour)

Other certifications to consider:

  • Organic (USDA, EU Organic) — for health food markets
  • Fairtrade — for ethical consumers
  • Halal — for Middle East and Muslim markets
  • Kosher — for Jewish and Israeli markets

Start with HACCP. Add others as your market requires.

6 International logistics and shipping

Getting product from your factory to another country is complex but manageable.

Options:

  • Sea freight — cheapest, slowest (4–8 weeks). Best for bulk dried products and canned goods.
  • Air freight — expensive, fast (2–7 days). Best for premium or perishable items.
  • Courier (DHL, FedEx) — fast, expensive. Best for samples and small orders.

Incoterms (who pays what):

  • EXW (Ex Works) — buyer picks up at your factory
  • FOB (Free On Board) — you deliver to the port
  • CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) — you pay shipping and insurance
  • DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) — you deliver to buyer's door, all costs paid

Start with EXW or FOB. Graduate to CIF and DDP as you gain experience.

7 Distribution partners abroad

Selling directly overseas is hard. Partners make it easier.

Types of partners:

  • Importer — buys in bulk, sells to shops in their country
  • Distributor — exclusive rights in their territory, promotes your brand
  • Agent — takes commission, doesn't buy stock
  • Online marketplace — Amazon, Etsy, Alibaba (direct to consumer)

How to find them:

  • Trade shows (Anuga, SIAL, Gulfood)
  • Trade associations in target countries
  • Embassy trade offices
  • Diaspora business networks

Negotiating with partners:

  • Start small — trial order
  • Agree territory, exclusivity, minimum order
  • Set prices carefully (FOB, CIF, DDP)
  • Payment terms — letters of credit for first orders

Sign a written contract. Review annually.

8 Five-year growth strategy

Write down where you want to be in 5 years. Then work backwards.

Five-year plan template:

  • Year 1 — solid foundation: one product, local market, profitable
  • Year 2 — expand products: add 2–3 items, hire team
  • Year 3 — expand geography: neighbouring regions, maybe another country
  • Year 4 — expand channels: supermarkets, wholesale, first export
  • Year 5 — scale: second facility, automated equipment, dedicated export brand

Review every 6 months:

  • What's working? Double down.
  • What's not? Cut or fix.
  • What's changed in the market? Adjust.

Businesses without a plan drift. Businesses with a plan grow deliberately. Write yours.

💰 Most food businesses that fail did not fail at making food. They failed at scaling well. Plan your growth or it will crush you.
