1. Lesson 1: Introduction to Machinery Operation and Repair in Africa

Welcome to Module 1 of Machinery Operation and Repair. This lesson sets the foundation for everything that follows. In Africa, machinery drives agriculture, mining, construction, transport, and manufacturing. From a small maize mill in a rural trading centre to a large earthmoving fleet on a copper mine, machines create value only when they run reliably. Your ability to operate and repair machinery directly affects productivity, safety, and profit. This module is practical and business first. We will not treat machines as abstract engineering objects. We will treat them as assets that must earn money every day. The African context brings unique challenges: long supply chains for spare parts, dusty and hot environments, variable fuel quality, limited access to grid electricity, and a shortage of certified technicians in remote areas. At the same time, Africa has a growing young workforce, expanding mobile connectivity, and increasing local fabrication capacity. A well trained operator or mechanic can build a strong career or a profitable workshop business. Let us begin with the core idea: machinery is a system. Every machine has a power source, a transmission, a control system, a working attachment, and a support structure. When one part fails, the whole system stops. Your job is to understand how these parts interact so you can operate efficiently and diagnose faults quickly. Consider a common example: a diesel powered maize mill. The engine produces rotational power. A belt or shaft transmits that power to the grinding plates. The operator controls feed rate and plate gap. If the engine overheats, the cause may be a blocked radiator, a failing water pump, or a slipping belt. If the mill produces uneven flour, the cause may be worn plates, incorrect gap, or inconsistent feed. A good operator notices these signs early. A good repair technician traces the symptom to the root cause instead of replacing parts randomly. In Africa, random part replacement is expensive because parts may take weeks to arrive. Diagnosis skills save money and downtime. Business first means you must know the cost of downtime. If a tractor breaks during planting season, every day lost reduces yield and income. If a generator fails at a clinic, vaccines spoil and lives are at risk. If a truck breaks on a long haul route, perishable goods rot. Therefore, maintenance is not a cost centre. It is a profit centre. Preventive maintenance, daily checks, and proper operation extend machine life and reduce emergency repairs. Let us define the three pillars of this module: safe operation, effective maintenance, and competent repair. Safe operation means following start up and shut down procedures, using guards, wearing protective equipment, and never bypassing safety devices. Effective maintenance means daily, weekly, and monthly tasks that keep the machine clean, lubricated, cooled, and adjusted. Competent repair means using the right tools, reading manuals, measuring tolerances, and testing after repair. Throughout this module, we will use case studies from across Africa. In Kenya, a group of youth operate a boda boda repair shed and a small welding shop. They learned to service motorcycle engines and generators, and now they train others. In Nigeria, a cassava processing cooperative installed a motorised grater and press. Their mechanic keeps spare bearings and belts on site. In South Africa, a mining contractor uses condition monitoring on haul trucks to predict failures before they happen. In Ghana, a maize sheller operator uses a daily checklist to prevent breakdowns during harvest. These examples show that success comes from discipline and practical knowledge, not from expensive equipment alone. You will also learn about the business side. Machinery operation and repair is a service industry. You can work as an operator, a mechanic, a maintenance planner, or a workshop owner. You can also train others. To succeed, you need to price your services correctly. Calculate your costs: fuel, lubricants, parts, tools, transport, rent, and your time. Add a profit margin. Keep records of every job. Use a simple notebook or a mobile app. Track which machines fail often and why. This data helps you advise customers and negotiate service contracts. Now let us look at the basic tools and equipment you will need. For operation, you need the machine manual, a daily checklist, fuel and lubricant supplies, and personal protective equipment. For maintenance, you need wrenches, sockets, screwdrivers, pliers, hammers, a torque wrench, a grease gun, an oil drain pan, funnels, cleaning brushes, and rags. For repair, you need measuring tools such as vernier calipers, micrometers, feeler gauges, and a multimeter. You also need lifting equipment, jacks, and safety stands. In many African workshops, tools are shared or improvised. Improvisation can work in an emergency, but it is dangerous for critical repairs. Always use the correct tool for the job. If you cannot afford a tool, rent it, borrow it, or save for it. A stripped bolt or a cracked housing costs more than the right wrench. Safety is non negotiable. Before you start any machine, check the surroundings. Remove loose clothing, tie back long hair, and wear closed shoes, gloves, and eye protection. Never operate a machine under the influence of alcohol or drugs. Never reach into a running machine. Use lockout and tagout procedures when repairing. In Africa, many accidents happen because someone started a machine while another person was working on it. A simple padlock and a warning tag can save a life. Let us also talk about fuel and lubricants. Diesel and petrol quality varies. Contaminated fuel clogs filters and damages injectors. Store fuel in clean, sealed containers. Use a funnel with a fine mesh. Check oil levels daily. Use the correct oil grade for your climate. In hot dusty conditions, change oil and filters more often than the manual recommends. Grease bearings regularly. Keep air filters clean. A dirty air filter wastes fuel and reduces power. Cooling systems are critical in hot African climates. Check radiator coolant level, hoses, and fan belts. Use a 50 50 mix of antifreeze and water unless the manual says otherwise. Antifreeze also raises the boiling point and prevents corrosion. In dusty conditions, clean the radiator fins with compressed air or a soft brush. Never open a hot radiator cap. Electrical systems are another common failure point. Batteries lose water in high heat. Check electrolyte levels and terminals. Keep terminals clean and tight. A weak battery strains the alternator and starter. Use a multimeter to check voltage. A healthy 12 volt battery reads about 12.6 volts at rest and 13.8 to 14.4 volts when the engine runs. If you see less, test the alternator and wiring. Finally, let us discuss documentation. Every machine should have a logbook. Record hours, fuel used, maintenance done, parts replaced, and faults observed. This history helps you plan repairs and proves to customers that you are professional. In the next lesson, we will look at engine systems in detail. For now, review this lesson and complete a simple task: choose one machine you know. Write down its main parts, its daily checks, and three common faults. This exercise will prepare you for the practical work ahead. Remember, machinery operation and repair is a skill that builds Africa. Do it safely, do it well, and do it as a business.

2. Lesson 2: Engine Systems and Daily Operation

In this lesson, we focus on the engine, the heart of most machinery. Whether you operate a generator, a tractor, a truck, or a pump, understanding the engine helps you run it efficiently and spot problems early. We will cover the main engine systems, daily operation procedures, and common faults in African conditions. We will keep it practical and business focused. The engine converts fuel into mechanical energy. Most machinery in Africa uses diesel or petrol internal combustion engines. Diesel engines are common in heavy equipment because they are fuel efficient, durable, and produce high torque at low speed. Petrol engines are common in small generators, motorcycles, and light vehicles. Both types have similar systems: fuel, air, lubrication, cooling, electrical, and exhaust. Let us start with the fuel system. Diesel fuel system includes tank, filters, lift pump, injection pump, injectors, and fuel lines. Petrol fuel system includes tank, filters, fuel pump, carburettor or fuel injection, and lines. The fuel system must deliver clean fuel at the right pressure and timing. In Africa, dirty fuel is a major cause of breakdowns. Always use a funnel with a fine mesh when refuelling. Drain water from the fuel filter daily. Replace fuel filters at the recommended interval, or sooner if fuel is dirty. Bleed the system after filter changes or if the engine runs out of fuel. Air in the fuel lines causes hard starting, rough running, and power loss. For diesel engines, never let the tank run dry. The injection pump relies on fuel for lubrication. Running dry can damage it. Also, do not use petrol in a diesel engine or diesel in a petrol engine. This mistake causes severe damage and is expensive to repair. The air system includes air filter, intake manifold, and turbocharger if fitted. Clean air is essential. Dust from unpaved roads and fields enters the engine if the filter is damaged or poorly sealed. Check the air filter daily in dusty conditions. Clean it by tapping gently or using compressed air from the inside out. Do not use oil on paper filters. Replace the filter when it is clogged or torn. A turbocharger boosts power by forcing more air into the engine. It spins at very high speed and needs clean oil and clean air. Let the engine idle for a few minutes before shutting down after heavy work. This cools the turbo and prevents oil cooking in the bearings. The lubrication system includes oil pan, oil pump, oil filter, and oil galleries. Oil reduces friction, cools parts, and cleans the engine. Check oil level daily with the dipstick. The level should be between the low and high marks. Add the correct grade of oil if low. Do not overfill. Change oil and oil filter at the recommended interval. In hot dusty conditions, change more often. Use a new filter gasket and tighten it by hand. Run the engine, check for leaks, and recheck the level. The cooling system includes radiator, water pump, thermostat, hoses, fan, and coolant. It keeps the engine at the right temperature. Overheating is a common problem in Africa. Check coolant level daily when the engine is cold. Use a 50 50 mix of antifreeze and clean water. Do not use salty or dirty water. Check hoses for cracks and leaks. Check the fan belt tension and condition. A loose belt slips and causes overheating. A tight belt damages bearings. Replace worn belts. Clean the radiator fins regularly. In dusty conditions, they clog and reduce cooling. If the engine overheats, stop safely, let it cool, and find the cause. Do not add cold water to a hot engine. The electrical system includes battery, alternator, starter, wiring, and switches. It starts the engine and powers lights and instruments. Check battery terminals for corrosion. Clean them with a wire brush and apply petroleum jelly. Check electrolyte level in serviceable batteries. Top up with distilled water only. Keep the alternator belt tight. Test the charging system with a multimeter. A healthy system charges at 13.8 to 14.4 volts for 12 volt systems. If the battery is weak, the engine may not start or may run poorly. The exhaust system includes manifold, muffler, and pipes. It removes gases and reduces noise. Check for leaks and loose fittings. A leaking exhaust can cause carbon monoxide poisoning. Never run an engine in a closed room. In Africa, many small generators are used indoors during power cuts. This is deadly. Always place generators outside, away from windows and doors. Now let us discuss daily operation. Before starting any engine, do a walk around inspection. Check fuel, oil, coolant, and hydraulic levels. Look for leaks, loose bolts, and damaged parts. Check the air filter and radiator. Check the battery and wiring. Check the work area for people, animals, and obstacles. Ensure guards are in place. Then start the engine. Follow the correct starting procedure. For diesel engines, turn the key to on, wait for the glow plug light if fitted, then crank. Do not crank for more than 10 seconds at a time. If it does not start, wait 30 seconds and try again. For petrol engines, set the choke if cold, then crank. Once started, let the engine idle for a few minutes to warm up. Check for unusual noises, smoke, or vibrations. Check gauges for oil pressure, temperature, and charging. If something is wrong, shut down and investigate. During operation, monitor the machine constantly. Listen for changes in sound. Watch for smoke. Black smoke means too much fuel or not enough air. White smoke means unburnt fuel or water in the fuel. Blue smoke means burning oil. Each colour points to a different problem. Check gauges regularly. Do not overload the engine. Match the load to the machine capacity. Overloading causes overheating, excessive wear, and breakdowns. After operation, let the engine idle for a few minutes to cool down. Then shut it down properly. Park on level ground. Apply the parking brake. Lower attachments. Turn off fuel if the machine will be stored for a long time. Fill the fuel tank to prevent condensation. Clean the machine. Do a post operation check and record any issues in the logbook. Now let us look at common faults and their causes. Hard starting can be caused by a weak battery, dirty fuel filter, air in fuel lines, wrong fuel, or a faulty starter. Rough running can be caused by dirty injectors, water in fuel, a clogged air filter, or a faulty spark plug in petrol engines. Overheating can be caused by low coolant, a faulty thermostat, a slipping fan belt, a clogged radiator, or a failing water pump. Low oil pressure can be caused by low oil level, a clogged oil filter, a faulty oil pump, or worn bearings. Excessive smoke can be caused by worn injectors, a clogged air filter, or worn piston rings. Unusual noises can be caused by loose bolts, worn bearings, or a faulty timing chain. When you find a fault, diagnose it systematically. Start with the simplest and cheapest checks. Do not replace parts until you are sure they are faulty. Use the manual. If you do not have a manual, search online or ask another technician. In Africa, many machines are old and have been repaired many times. Keep a notebook of what works. Share knowledge with other operators and mechanics. Now let us connect this to business. A well maintained engine uses less fuel. Fuel is a major operating cost. If you save 10 percent on fuel, that is direct profit. A reliable engine means less downtime. Downtime means lost income. If you operate a generator for a shop, every hour of downtime is lost sales. If you operate a tractor, every day of downtime delays planting. So daily checks and proper operation are not optional. They are business decisions. You can also earn money by offering engine maintenance services. Many small businesses do not have a trained mechanic. You can visit them weekly to check oil, filters, and belts. Charge a monthly fee. This creates steady income and prevents breakdowns. You can also sell fuel additives, filters, and oil. But only sell quality products. Your reputation depends on it. Finally, safety. Engines are dangerous. They have hot parts, moving parts, and toxic exhaust. Never touch a hot manifold. Never put your hands near a running fan or belt. Never refuel a hot engine. Never run an engine indoors. Always use the correct tools. Always wear protective equipment. In the next lesson, we will cover transmission and hydraulic systems. For now, practise your daily checks on a real machine. Write down what you find. This habit will make you a better operator and a better mechanic.

3. Lesson 3: Transmission, Hydraulics, and Undercarriage

In this lesson, we move from the engine to the systems that transmit power and control movement. These include transmissions, clutches, drive shafts, differentials, final drives, hydraulic systems, and undercarriage components. Understanding these systems is essential for operating and repairing tractors, trucks, excavators, bulldozers, and other heavy machinery. In Africa, these machines are used in construction, mining, agriculture, and transport. A failure in any of these systems can stop a project and cost a lot of money. Let us start with the transmission. The transmission takes power from the engine and sends it to the wheels or tracks at the right speed and torque. There are several types: manual gearbox, automatic transmission, powershift, and hydrostatic. Manual gearboxes use gears and synchronisers. They are common in trucks and tractors. Automatic transmissions use a torque converter and planetary gears. They are common in cars and some trucks. Powershift transmissions use clutches and gears that shift under load. They are common in heavy equipment. Hydrostatic transmissions use hydraulic pumps and motors. They are common in skid steers and small excavators. Each type has its own operation and maintenance needs. For manual gearboxes, the clutch is a key component. The clutch connects and disconnects the engine from the transmission. It allows smooth starting and gear changes. A worn clutch slips, causing loss of power and overheating. A dragging clutch makes gear changes difficult. Check clutch pedal free play regularly. Adjust it according to the manual. Do not ride the clutch. Do not use the clutch to hold the machine on a hill. Use the brakes. For automatic transmissions, check fluid level and condition. Dirty or burnt fluid causes shifting problems. Change fluid and filter at the recommended interval. Do not tow a heavy load in overdrive. Use the correct towing mode. For powershift transmissions, check oil level and pressure. Use the correct oil. Contaminated oil damages clutches and valves. For hydrostatic transmissions, check oil level and filters. Keep the oil clean. Overheating is a common problem. Check the cooling system and charge pressure. Now let us discuss drive shafts and differentials. The drive shaft connects the transmission to the differential. It has universal joints and a slip joint. Check for loose bolts, worn joints, and vibration. Grease the joints regularly. The differential transfers power to the wheels and allows them to turn at different speeds. It contains gears and bearings. Check oil level and look for leaks. Use the correct oil. A noisy differential may have worn gears or bearings. Repair it before it fails completely. Final drives are the last reduction gears before the wheels or tracks. They are often planetary gears. Check oil level and look for leaks. In dusty conditions, seals can fail and let dirt in. This causes rapid wear. Now let us move to hydraulic systems. Hydraulics use pressurised oil to lift, push, pull, and steer. They are used in excavators, loaders, cranes, tractor implements, and truck tipping trailers. A hydraulic system includes a reservoir, pump, valves, cylinders, motors, hoses, and filters. The pump creates flow. The valves control direction and pressure. The cylinders convert flow into linear force. The motors convert flow into rotational force. Hydraulic systems are powerful but require clean oil and proper maintenance. Check hydraulic oil level daily. Use the correct grade. Check for leaks. Even a small leak can cause a major failure. Check hoses for cracks, abrasion, and bulges. Replace damaged hoses immediately. Do not use tape or clamps as a permanent repair. Check filters and replace them at the recommended interval. Contaminated oil damages pumps and valves. When working on hydraulics, relieve pressure first. Lower attachments to the ground. Turn off the engine. Cycle the controls to release pressure. Wear eye protection. Hydraulic oil under pressure can penetrate the skin and cause serious injury. If injured, seek medical help immediately. Now let us discuss undercarriage. Undercarriage is the tracks, rollers, idlers, sprockets, and track frame on tracked machines. It is a major cost item. In Africa, undercarriage wear is accelerated by abrasive soils, rocky ground, and poor operating practices. Check track tension daily. Too loose, the track can derail. Too tight, it wears out pins and bushings. Adjust according to the manual. Check rollers and idlers for leaks and wear. Check sprockets for worn teeth. Rotate tracks regularly if the machine has a track rotation feature. Avoid turning on rough ground. Avoid spinning tracks. These practices increase wear. For wheeled machines, check tyres, rims, and wheel bearings. Check tyre pressure daily. Correct pressure extends tyre life and improves fuel efficiency. Check for cuts, bulges, and uneven wear. Rotate tyres as recommended. Check wheel bearings for play and noise. Grease them regularly. Now let us discuss operation. Smooth operation extends machine life. Avoid sudden starts, stops, and turns. Warm up the machine before heavy work. Use the correct gear for the load. Do not overload. Do not use the machine for tasks it was not designed for. For example, do not use a tractor to pull a load that is too heavy. Do not use an excavator to lift loads beyond its capacity. Follow the load chart. Keep the machine clean. Dirt and mud hide leaks and cause wear. Wash the machine regularly, especially around seals and joints. Now let us look at common faults and repairs. Transmission problems include slipping, hard shifting, noise, and overheating. Causes include low oil, dirty oil, worn clutches, faulty valves, and broken gears. Hydraulic problems include slow operation, weak power, overheating, noise, and leaks. Causes include low oil, clogged filters, worn pumps, faulty valves, and damaged cylinders. Undercarriage problems include track derailment, uneven wear, and broken components. Causes include incorrect tension, worn parts, and rough operation. When you diagnose a fault, start with the basics. Check oil levels and filters. Check for leaks. Check linkages and cables. Use a pressure gauge to test hydraulic pressure. Use a multimeter to test electrical controls. Do not guess. Use the manual. If you do not have a manual, search online or ask another technician. In Africa, many machines are old and have been repaired many times. Keep a notebook of what works. Share knowledge with other operators and mechanics. Now let us connect this to business. Transmission and hydraulic repairs are expensive. A rebuilt transmission can cost thousands of dollars. A hydraulic pump can cost hundreds. Preventive maintenance is much cheaper. A daily check and a weekly grease job can prevent a major failure. If you operate a machine, keep a maintenance schedule. If you repair machines, offer maintenance contracts. Many customers will pay a monthly fee for regular checks. This creates steady income and prevents emergency breakdowns. You can also specialise in hydraulics. Hydraulic repair requires clean tools, a clean workspace, and training. If you become good at it, you can serve many industries. You can also sell hydraulic hoses and fittings. Keep a stock of common sizes. Make hoses on site. This is a profitable service. Finally, safety. Hydraulics and transmissions are dangerous. They have high pressure, hot oil, and moving parts. Never work under a raised attachment without proper support. Never disconnect a hydraulic line under pressure. Never use your hands to check for leaks. Use a piece of cardboard or paper. Always wear protective equipment. In the next lesson, we will cover electrical systems and electronics. For now, practise your daily checks on a real machine. Write down what you find. This habit will make you a better operator and a better mechanic.

4. Lesson 4: Electrical Systems, Electronics, and Troubleshooting

In this lesson, we focus on electrical systems and electronics. Modern machinery relies heavily on electricity and electronic controls. Even older machines use electrical systems for starting, lighting, and monitoring. In Africa, electrical faults are common due to heat, dust, vibration, and poor wiring repairs. Understanding electrical systems helps you operate machines safely and diagnose problems quickly. We will cover basic electricity, batteries, charging systems, starting systems, wiring, sensors, and electronic control units. We will keep it practical and business focused. Let us start with basic electricity. Electricity is the flow of electrons through a conductor. It is measured in volts, amperes, and ohms. Voltage is the pressure that pushes electrons. Amperage is the rate of flow. Resistance is the opposition to flow. Ohm law states that voltage equals amperage times resistance. This law helps you understand electrical circuits. A circuit is a complete path for electricity. It has a power source, a load, and conductors. A switch controls the circuit. A fuse or circuit breaker protects it. If the circuit is broken, electricity stops flowing. If the circuit is shorted, electricity flows without resistance and can cause a fire. Always use the correct fuse. Never bypass a fuse with wire or foil. Now let us discuss batteries. Batteries store electrical energy. Most machines use lead acid batteries. They have cells with positive and negative plates in electrolyte. A 12 volt battery has six cells. A 24 volt system uses two 12 volt batteries in series. Check battery terminals for corrosion. Clean them with a wire brush and apply petroleum jelly. Check electrolyte level in serviceable batteries. Top up with distilled water only. Do not overfill. Check the battery case for cracks. Check the hold down for tightness. A loose battery can vibrate and break. Test the battery with a multimeter. A healthy 12 volt battery reads about 12.6 volts at rest. If it reads below 12.0 volts, charge it. If it does not hold a charge, replace it. In hot climates, batteries fail faster. Keep them clean and cool if possible. Now let us discuss the charging system. The charging system includes the alternator, voltage regulator, and wiring. It keeps the battery charged and powers electrical loads when the engine runs. Test the charging system with a multimeter. Start the engine and measure voltage at the battery. It should read 13.8 to 14.4 volts for a 12 volt system. If it reads less, the alternator may be faulty. If it reads more, the regulator may be faulty. Check the alternator belt tension and condition. A loose belt slips and reduces charging. Check the wiring for loose or corroded connections. Now let us discuss the starting system. The starting system includes the battery, starter motor, solenoid, ignition switch, and wiring. It cranks the engine. If the engine does not crank, check the battery first. Then check the starter solenoid. You should hear a click when you turn the key. If you hear a click but the engine does not crank, the starter may be faulty. If you hear nothing, check the ignition switch and wiring. Check the ground connections. A bad ground causes many electrical problems. Clean and tighten all grounds. Now let us discuss wiring. Wiring carries electricity to lights, sensors, and controls. It is colour coded. Use the manual to identify wires. Check for broken, chafed, or corroded wires. Repair with proper connectors and heat shrink tubing. Do not use tape alone. Do not twist wires together. Solder them or use crimp connectors. Use the correct gauge wire. Too thin a wire overheats and causes voltage drop. Too thick a wire is wasteful. Route wires away from hot and moving parts. Secure them with clips and ties. Now let us discuss sensors and electronic control units. Modern machines have sensors for temperature, pressure, speed, and position. They send signals to an electronic control unit, or ECU. The ECU controls fuel injection, transmission shifting, hydraulic flow, and other functions. It also stores fault codes. You can read fault codes with a diagnostic tool or a laptop. If you do not have a tool, you can sometimes read codes by flashing lights or counting blinks. Follow the manual. Common sensors include coolant temperature sensor, oil pressure sensor, crankshaft position sensor, and throttle position sensor. A faulty sensor can cause poor performance, hard starting, or shutdown. Test sensors with a multimeter. Check resistance and voltage according to the manual. Replace faulty sensors with the correct part. Do not use cheap substitutes. They may fail quickly and damage the ECU. Now let us discuss troubleshooting. Electrical faults can be frustrating. Use a systematic approach. Start with the symptom. Does the machine not start? Do the lights not work? Does the engine run poorly? Check the simplest things first. Check fuses. Check battery connections. Check grounds. Check switches. Use a multimeter to trace voltage. Start at the power source and work toward the load. If voltage is present at the source but not at the load, the problem is in between. Check wires, connectors, and switches. If voltage is present at the load but the load does not work, the load is faulty. Use a wiring diagram. If you do not have one, draw your own. Label wires as you go. Take photos before disconnecting anything. This helps you reassemble correctly. Now let us connect this to business. Electrical repairs are in high demand. Many workshops avoid electrical work because it seems complicated. If you learn it, you can charge premium rates. You can specialise in auto electrical work for trucks, tractors, and generators. You can also install and repair solar systems. Solar is growing rapidly in Africa. Many homes and businesses use solar panels, batteries, and inverters. The skills are similar. You can also sell and install security systems, alarms, and tracking devices. These are profitable services. To succeed, invest in good tools. You need a quality multimeter, wire strippers, crimping tools, soldering iron, and heat gun. You need a good test light and a power probe. You need a diagnostic scanner for modern machines. These tools pay for themselves quickly. Keep a stock of common fuses, relays, connectors, and wire. Keep a notebook of fault codes and solutions. Share knowledge with other technicians. Finally, safety. Electricity is dangerous. It can cause shocks, burns, and fires. Always disconnect the battery before working on electrical systems. Use insulated tools. Wear protective equipment. Never work on live circuits unless you are trained and authorised. Never short a battery. It can explode. Never use water on an electrical fire. Use a dry chemical extinguisher. In the next lesson, we will cover maintenance planning, record keeping, and business skills. For now, practise your electrical checks on a real machine. Write down what you find. This habit will make you a better operator and a better mechanic.

5. Lesson 5: Maintenance Planning, Record Keeping, and Business Skills

In this final lesson of Module 1, we bring everything together. You have learned about engines, transmissions, hydraulics, undercarriage, and electrical systems. Now we focus on maintenance planning, record keeping, and business skills. These are the tools that turn technical knowledge into a profitable career or business. In Africa, many skilled technicians fail not because they lack technical ability, but because they lack business and organisational skills. This lesson will help you avoid that trap. Let us start with maintenance planning. Maintenance is the work you do to keep machines running. There are two main types: preventive maintenance and corrective maintenance. Preventive maintenance is done before a fault occurs. It includes daily checks, weekly services, and scheduled replacements. Corrective maintenance is done after a fault occurs. It includes repairs and overhauls. Preventive maintenance is cheaper and more profitable. It reduces downtime and extends machine life. Corrective maintenance is expensive and disruptive. Your goal should be to maximise preventive maintenance and minimise corrective maintenance. To do this, you need a maintenance plan. A maintenance plan lists all the tasks required for each machine, the interval for each task, and the person responsible. It is based on the manufacturer manual and your own experience. For example, a daily check might include oil level, coolant level, fuel level, tyre pressure, and leaks. A weekly service might include greasing, cleaning the air filter, and checking battery terminals. A monthly service might include changing oil and filters, checking belts and hoses, and testing the charging system. A yearly service might include a major overhaul. Create a checklist for each machine. Keep it simple and practical. Use pictures if helpful. Laminate the checklist and hang it on the machine or in the workshop. Train operators to use it. Make it part of their daily routine. Now let us discuss record keeping. Records are essential for good maintenance and good business. You need to record what you did, when you did it, and what you found. This helps you track machine history, predict failures, and prove your work to customers. Keep a logbook for each machine. Record the date, hours, fuel used, maintenance done, parts replaced, and faults observed. Record the cost of parts and labour. Record the name of the technician. If you work for a company, use their system. If you work for yourself, use a simple notebook or a mobile app. Many free apps are available for maintenance tracking. Use them if you have a smartphone. If not, use a paper system. The important thing is consistency. Record every job. At the end of each month, review your records. Look for patterns. Which machines fail often? Which parts wear out quickly? Which operators cause more damage? Use this information to improve your maintenance plan and your training. Now let us discuss business skills. Practical Reference: Machinery Selection, Sizing and Costing for African Farms

This practical reference lesson sits alongside Lessons 15 and gives you the numbers, tables and checklists you need when buying, hiring or repairing farm machinery in an African context. It is written business-first: every technical decision is framed around cost per hectare, cost per hour and return on investment. Whether you run a 2-hectare smallholder plot in the Rift Valley, a 40-hectare maize enterprise in the Guinea savannah, or a machinery-hire business serving neighbours, the arithmetic below determines whether your equipment makes money or eats it.

1. Matching Machine Size to Farm Size and Power Source

The single biggest mistake in African mechanisation is buying a tractor that is too large for the farm, or too small for the implement it must pull. Tractor power is measured at the power take-off (PTO). A rule of thumb used by extension services is that you need roughly 0.40.6 kW of PTO power per hectare of tillage per day if you want to complete primary cultivation in a reasonable window. Draft animals remain economically rational below about 34 hectares on many soils; two-wheel tractors (power tillers) fit 15 hectares; four-wheel tractors become competitive above roughly 510 hectares, depending on soil, rainfall window and crop.

This is the "timeliness cost" that business-minded farmers must quantify.

3. Business-First Decision Rules

Rule 1: Calculate cost per hectare before you buy. Add fuel, labour, repairs, depreciation and finance, then divide by the hectares you will actually work each year. If the machine sits idle for eight months, depreciation and finance still run.

Rule 2: Use the machine to earn, not just to save. A tractor that only ploughs your own 5 hectares rarely pays. Hiring out to neighbours at market rates can double annual utilisation from 200 to 500 hours, cutting fixed cost per hour dramatically. This is the single most powerful profitability lever in African mechanisation.

Rule 3: Buy for the implement, not the badge. A 45 kW tractor with the right plough, planter and trailer will out-earn a 75 kW tractor that cannot afford matching implements. Implement matching is a business decision.

Rule 4: Plan for spares and service. Before purchase, confirm that filters, belts, brake parts and hydraulic seals are available within 48 hours locally or regionally. A machine waiting three weeks for a USD 40 part destroys profitability.

Rule 5: Train the operator. Operator skill affects fuel use by 1020% and repair costs by more. A trained operator who checks oil, coolant and tyre pressure daily is cheaper than any warranty.

4. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm the farm size, soil type and available working window before selecting machine size.
  • Calculate cost per hectare including fuel, labour, repairs, depreciation and finance.
  • Check that the tractor has enough PTO power to drive the intended implement at working speed.
  • Verify local availability of filters, belts, seals and tyres within 48 hours.
  • Plan for at least 400500 annual working hours through hire services or diversification.
  • Budget for daily operator checks: engine oil, coolant, tyre pressure, hydraulic oil, air filter.
  • Keep a simple logbook of hours, fuel, repairs and income per machine.
  • Review the buy-versus-hire decision every season using real cost data, not assumptions.
