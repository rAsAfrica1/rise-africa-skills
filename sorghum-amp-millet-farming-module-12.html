1. Expanding land through renting or buying

To grow your sorghum or millet business, you need more land. In most African countries, land is rented or bought through local leaders or family. Start by renting. A hectare of good farmland rents for about $50 to $150 per season in countries like Nigeria, Kenya, or Tanzania. That is cheaper than buying, which can cost $500 to $5,000 per hectare depending on location and water access. Before you rent, test the soil. Dig a hole 30 cm deep. If the soil is sandy and pale, add compost. If it is dark and holds moisture, it is good. Also check water sources: a borehole or river nearby reduces irrigation costs. Always sign a written agreement with the landowner, witnessed by two people. Include the rent amount, duration (at least 2 seasons), and what happens if you improve the land (like building a fence). For buying, save 30% of your profits each year. In 3 years, you can buy 1 hectare. Buy land with clear title or a customary certificate. Avoid disputed land. After acquiring land, plant a cover crop like cowpeas to add nitrogen. Rotate sorghum with legumes every other season to keep the soil healthy. If you rent, negotiate a 5-year lease to justify investing in soil fertility. Remember, expanding too fast without labour or equipment leads to losses. Expand by 1 hectare per year, not 5.

2. Hiring and training farm labour

When your farm grows beyond 2 hectares, you cannot do all the work alone. You need hired labour. In Africa, farm labour is paid daily or per task. A casual worker earns $3 to $8 per day depending on the country and season. For sorghum and millet, you need labour for planting, weeding, bird scaring, harvesting, and threshing. Hire 2 workers per hectare during peak times. For example, weeding 1 hectare takes 4 people 2 days. Pay them fairly and on time to keep them motivated. Train your workers on specific tasks. Show them how to plant seeds 2 cm deep and 30 cm apart. Demonstrate how to identify pests like stem borers. Use a simple checklist: each worker must weed 10 rows per day. Provide tools: hoes, machetes, and gloves. For threshing, train 2 workers to use a mechanical thresher safely. Pay a bonus of $5 per 100 kg of clean grain to encourage quality. Keep records: name, days worked, amount paid. Use a notebook or a free app like FarmLogs. Treat workers with respect. Provide clean drinking water and a shade tree for breaks. Happy workers stay, reducing training costs. Consider hiring a permanent supervisor for $50 to $100 per month. This person manages daily tasks and reports to you. With good labour, your yields can increase by 30% because weeding and harvesting are done on time.

3. Investing in equipment: threshers, mills, and storage

Manual threshing of sorghum and millet is slow and loses grain. A mechanical thresher saves time and money. A small motorized thresher costs $300 to $800 in most African cities. It threshes 500 kg per hour, compared to 20 kg per hour by hand. That means you can process 5 tonnes in 10 hours instead of 250 hours. Rent one first to test. If you grow more than 5 hectares, buy one. For milling, a hammer mill costs $500 to $1,500. It turns grain into flour for local markets. You can charge neighbors $0.10 per kg to mill their grain, earning extra income. Storage is critical. Poor storage leads to weevils and mold. Use hermetic bags like Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS) bags. A 100 kg PICS bag costs $2 to $3 and keeps grain safe for 6 months without chemicals. For larger quantities, build a metal silo. A 1-tonne silo costs $200 to $400. Keep grain moisture below 12% before storage. Test by biting a grain: if it cracks, it is dry enough. Store in a cool, dry place on pallets. Never store on the floor. Check monthly for insects. If you see weevils, sun-dry the grain for 2 days. With proper equipment, you reduce post-harvest losses from 30% to less than 5%. That extra grain is pure profit. Start with a thresher, then a mill, then storage as your volume grows.

4. Contract farming with breweries and food companies

Contract farming means you grow sorghum or millet for a specific buyer at a pre-agreed price. In Africa, breweries like Nigerian Breweries, East African Breweries (Kenya), and SABMiller (now AB InBev) buy white sorghum for beer. Food companies like Nestl and local millers buy millet for porridge and flour. To get a contract, approach the companys procurement office. Bring samples of your grain. They test for moisture (below 12%), foreign matter (below 2%), and variety (white sorghum for brewing). If accepted, you sign a contract. Typical terms: they provide seeds and sometimes fertilizer on credit. You deliver 10 to 50 tonnes per season. Price is fixed at $200 to $350 per tonne, which is 20% higher than local market. You must meet quality and quantity. If you fail, you pay a penalty. To succeed, plant on time. Use recommended varieties like Gadam or Serena. Keep records of inputs. Harvest when grain is hard. Dry on a tarpaulin, not on the ground. Deliver within 2 weeks of harvest. Contract farming reduces market risk. You know your buyer before you plant. But read the fine print. Some contracts require you to sell only to them. Negotiate for a fair price adjustment if fertilizer costs rise. Start with 1 hectare under contract, then expand. Many farmers double their income within 2 years through contract farming.

5. Developing a brand for your sorghum or millet products

A brand makes your sorghum or millet stand out. Start by choosing a name. Use something local and easy to remember, like 'Sahel Gold' or 'Millet Fresh'. Register your business name with the local government. It costs $20 to $50. Next, design a simple label. Include your name, contact, weight, and production date. Use a logo: a grain stalk or a sun. Print labels at a local print shop for $0.05 each. Packaging matters. For flour, use sealed plastic bags or paper bags with a window. For whole grain, use 1 kg, 5 kg, and 25 kg bags. Clean your grain well. Remove stones and broken kernels. Customers pay more for clean grain. Set a price: 10% to 20% above ordinary market price. Sell at local markets, schools, and health food shops. Use WhatsApp to take orders. Post photos of your farm and processing. Tell your story: 'No chemicals, sun-dried, hand-sorted.' That builds trust. Get a food safety certificate if you can. It costs $100 but opens doors to supermarkets. Offer free samples. Ask for feedback. A strong brand allows you to sell directly to consumers, earning 30% more than selling to middlemen. Over time, your brand becomes known for quality. Then you can increase volume and even export to neighboring countries.

6. Creating a 3-year growth plan for your farm business

A 3-year plan turns your farm from a hobby into a business. Write it down. Year 1: Focus on yields. Plant 2 hectares of improved sorghum or millet. Use fertilizer: 50 kg of NPK per hectare at planting. Weed twice. Harvest 2 tonnes per hectare. Sell 1 tonne to a local brewer under contract. Keep 1 tonne for family and seed. Save 30% of profit. Buy a thresher. Year 2: Expand to 4 hectares. Rent 2 more hectares. Hire 2 permanent workers. Buy a hammer mill. Start selling branded flour. Target 3 tonnes per hectare by using compost and rotating with cowpeas. Contract 5 tonnes to a food company. Save 40% of profit. Build a metal silo. Year 3: Reach 6 hectares. Buy 2 hectares. Install a drip irrigation system for dry spells. Train 5 workers on quality control. Launch a second product: millet porridge mix. Sell to supermarkets. Aim for 4 tonnes per hectare. Export 10 tonnes to a neighboring country. Save 50% of profit. Reinvest in a truck for delivery. Track everything: expenses, yields, sales. Use a notebook or free app like Excel. Review your plan every 6 months. Adjust if prices fall or rain fails. The key is steady growth, not rushing. With this plan, you can go from 2 hectares to 6 hectares and triple your income in 3 years.

<h2>Practical Reference: Scaling Up and Building a Sorghum/Millet Business</h2>

<p>Scaling a sorghum or millet enterprise in Africa is rarely about planting more hectares alone. It is about moving from a yield-focused mindset to a <strong>margin-and-market mindset</strong>: controlling land access, labour, processing, contracts, and brand so that each additional tonne sold adds profit rather than risk. This reference module gives you the numbers, structures, and decision rules used by commercial smallholder and medium-scale grain businesses across the Sahel, East Africa, and Southern Africa.</p>

<p>Use this lesson alongside the six core lessons in this module. Everything here is business-first: if a step does not improve cash flow, reduce cost per tonne, or open a higher-value market, it is not scaling it is just spending.</p>

<h3>1. Expanding Land Through Renting or Buying</h3>

<p>Land is your first scaling lever, but buying land ties up capital that should be working in equipment, storage, or working capital. In most African sorghum/millet zones, <strong>renting is the faster and safer route</strong> you can add 210 ha per season without a mortgage, and exit if prices fall.</p>

<p>Key rules for land expansion:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rent near your homestead or store</strong> transport cost kills margin on distant plots.</li>
<li><strong>Get written agreements</strong> witnessed by local leaders or a notary. Verbal land deals are the number-one cause of disputes during harvest.</li>
<li><strong>Check soil and water</strong> before signing. A cheap plot with poor fertility costs more in fertilizer than a fair-priced good plot.</li>
<li><strong>Buy land only when</strong> you have 3 years of consistent profit, a clear title or customary right documented, and cash that is not needed for operations.</li>
</ul>

<h3>2. Hiring and Training Farm Labour</h3>

<p>As you scale past 23 ha, family labour is not enough. You need a <strong>small permanent core plus seasonal casuals</strong>. The permanent core (1 supervisor + 12 skilled workers) protects quality; casuals handle weeding, harvesting, and threshing peaks.</p>

<p>Training is not optional. Untrained labour causes grain loss, contamination, and equipment damage. A 1-day training before each season on planting spacing, pest scouting, and safe thresher operation pays back many times over.</p>

<h3>3. Investing in Equipment: Threshers, Mills, and Storage</h3>

<p>Equipment is where most grain businesses either leap forward or sink. The rule: <strong>buy equipment only when you have confirmed volume to keep it busy</strong>. A thresher used 10 days a year is a liability; one used 60 days a year (yours plus neighbours') is an asset.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Thresher:</strong> reduces labour by 6080% and grain loss by 510%. Cost: $8004,000. Payback: 23 seasons if you also thresh for others.</li>
<li><strong>Mill (hammer or roller):</strong> turns grain into flour, lifting value 50150%. Cost: $1,5008,000. Requires steady power or a diesel engine.</li>
<li><strong>Storage (hermetic bags, metal silo, or warehouse):</strong> lets you sell in the lean season at 2040% higher prices. Cost: $50300 for bags, $8005,000 for a small silo.</li>
</ul>

<p>Always budget for maintenance: 510% of equipment cost per year. Keep spare belts, screens, and bearings on site during harvest.</p>

<h3>4. Contract Farming with Breweries and Food Companies</h3>

<p>Contract farming is the fastest route to predictable income. Breweries (e.g., using white sorghum for lager) and food companies (milling, porridge, snacks) need consistent volume, quality, and food safety. In return they offer fixed prices, inputs on credit, and technical support.</p>

<p>Before signing:</p>
<ul>
<li>Read every clause on quality standards, moisture content, aflatoxin limits, and rejection terms.</li>
<li>Negotiate a <strong>minimum guaranteed volume</strong> and a <strong>price floor</strong>.</li>
<li>Confirm payment terms 7 days, 30 days, or on delivery. Late payment is a hidden cost.</li>
<li>Never borrow against a contract you have not signed.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Developing a Brand for Your Sorghum or Millet Products</h3>

<p>A brand is not a logo it is a promise of consistent quality and supply. Start with one product (e.g., clean white sorghum flour in 1 kg and 5 kg packs), one region, and one clear customer (urban households, health-conscious buyers, or institutional buyers like schools).</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Register your business name and trademark</strong> with your national registry.</li>
<li><strong>Meet food safety standards</strong> labelling, batch codes, and aflatoxin testing.</li>
<li><strong>Package for the buyer</strong> moisture-proof, resealable, with cooking instructions.</li>
<li><strong>Price for margin, not volume alone</strong> branded flour can sell at 23 raw grain price.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Creating a 3-Year Growth Plan</h3>

<p>Write it down. A 3-year plan turns ambition into a bankable document. Use this structure:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Year 1:</strong> Stabilise production on 25 ha, build records, test one contract, buy a thresher.</li>
<li><strong>Year 2:</strong> Add rented land to 815 ha, hire a permanent supervisor, install storage, sign a brewery contract.</li>
<li><strong>Year 3:</strong> Add milling, launch a branded flour, target 3060 t annual sales, and open a second market channel.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b;background:#fdecea;padding:12px;border-radius:6px;margin:16px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING AFLATOXIN AND GRAIN HANDLING</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7b241c;">Sorghum and millet are highly susceptible to <strong>aflatoxin</strong> when dried or stored poorly. Aflatoxin is a silent poison linked to liver cancer and stunting in children, and it will cause breweries and food companies to reject your entire harvest. Always:</p>
<ul style="color:#7b241c;margin:6px 0 0 18px;">
<li>Dry grain to <strong>below 13% moisture</strong> before storage.</li>
<li>Never store grain on bare ground or in damp rooms.</li>
<li>Use hermetic bags or clean, dry silos; inspect monthly for mould and insects.</li>
<li>Discard any grain with visible mould do not sell or feed it to family.</li>
<li>Wear a dust mask when milling or threshing to avoid respiratory harm.</li>
</ul>
</div>
