1. Wool Growth and Fiber Characteristics

Wool grows from follicles in the sheep's skin. Each follicle produces a single fiber. The number of follicles varies by breed: Merino sheep have up to 100 follicles per square millimeter, while indigenous African breeds like the Namaqua Afrikaner may have only 20Ã¢Â€Â“30. More follicles mean denser fleece. Wool grows continuously; a Merino can produce 4Ã¢Â€Â“6 kg of fleece per year, while local breeds yield 1Ã¢Â€Â“2 kg. Growth rate depends on nutrition, genetics, and season. In Africa, sheep on poor grazing may grow only 1 cm per month, while well-fed sheep can grow 2Ã¢Â€Â“3 cm.

Fiber characteristics determine wool quality. Fiber diameter (measured in microns) is the most important: fine wool (under 24 microns) is soft and used for clothing; medium (24Ã¢Â€Â“31 microns) for blankets; coarse (over 31 microns) for carpets. Crimp (natural waviness) gives wool elasticity. Staple length is the length of fibers in one year's growth; longer staples (7Ã¢Â€Â“12 cm) are easier to spin. Clean wool yield is the percentage of wool left after removing grease, dirt, and vegetable matter. African sheep often have high grease content, so yield may be 50Ã¢Â€Â“70%.

Breed choice matters. Merino, Rambouillet, and their crosses produce fine wool but need good management. Indigenous breeds like the Blackhead Persian have carpet-type wool but are hardy. For African smallholders, crossbreeding local ewes with Merino rams can improve wool while keeping hardiness. Wool color is usually white, but some breeds produce black, brown, or spotted fleeces; these are harder to dye and fetch lower prices.

To improve wool growth: provide balanced nutrition, especially protein and sulfur-containing amino acids. Avoid stresses like parasites and extreme heat. Shearing once or twice a year prevents fleece from matting and allows staple length measurement. Keep records of fleece weight and quality to select best animals. Remember: wool is a renewable resourceÃ¢Â€Â”healthy sheep produce good wool year after year.

2. Shearing Techniques and Timing

Shearing is removing the fleece in one piece. It requires sharp hand shears or electric clippers. For small flocks, hand shears are common in Africa; they cost less and need no power. Electric clippers are faster but require electricity or a generator. Before shearing, gather: sharp shears, a clean tarp, a rope or shearing stand, and a first-aid kit. Do not shear wet sheepÃ¢Â€Â”wool must be dry to avoid rot and to get a good price.

Timing is critical. In most African regions, shear once a year, just before the rainy season. This prevents fleece from getting soaked and dirty, and allows sheep to grow a new coat before cold or wet weather. In hot, dry areas, shear twice: early spring and late summer. Avoid shearing in late pregnancy (stress can cause abortion) or during extreme cold. Lambs can be shorn at 6Ã¢Â€Â“8 months for the first time.

Technique: Restrain the sheep by sitting it on its rump, holding it between your legs. Start at the belly and crutch (between hind legs), then the legs, then the sides, and finally the back. Use long, smooth strokes close to the skin, but avoid cutting the skin. Change shears or sharpen every 10Ã¢Â€Â“15 minutes. A skilled shearer can do a sheep in 3Ã¢Â€Â“5 minutes with clippers, or 10Ã¢Â€Â“15 minutes with hand shears. Keep the fleece in one pieceÃ¢Â€Â”it's called a "fleece" and is easier to class and skirt.

After shearing, treat any cuts with antiseptic. Keep the sheep in a clean, dry pen for a few hours. The fleece should be rolled: spread it on a clean tarp, remove dirty edges (skirting), then roll from the back to the belly, tying with paper twine. Store in a dry, ventilated place away from rodents. Never use plastic bagsÃ¢Â€Â”they trap moisture and cause mold. Shearing is also a health check: look for wounds, ticks, and lumps. Record fleece weight and any issues. Good shearing increases wool value and keeps sheep healthy.

3. Wool Handling and Classing

After shearing, wool must be handled carefully to maintain quality. Classing is sorting fleeces into uniform groups based on fiber diameter, staple length, color, and cleanliness. This allows you to sell wool at better prices because buyers want consistent lots. For small flocks, classing can be done on a clean table or tarp.

First, skirt the fleece: remove all dirty, stained, and short fibers from the edges (belly, crutch, and legs). These are called "skirtings" and are sold separately at low prices. Then assess the fleece. Key criteria: fiber diameter (use a micrometer or visual appraisal), staple length (measure with a ruler), crimp (fine wool has more crimp), and color (white is best). Also check for vegetable matter (grass seeds, burrs) and stains (from urine, dung, or branding).

Class into grades. Common grades for African wool: Fine (under 24 microns) Ã¢Â€Â“ for apparel; Medium (24Ã¢Â€Â“31 microns) Ã¢Â€Â“ for blankets and sweaters; Coarse (over 31 microns) Ã¢Â€Â“ for carpets and rugs. In some markets, you may also class by breed or by fleece part (e.g., shoulders are best). Keep each grade separate in clean burlap bags or wool packs. Label each bag with grade, weight, and date. Never mix gradesÃ¢Â€Â”it reduces the value of the entire lot.

For small-scale farmers, classing may be as simple as three piles: good, medium, and poor. But even this improves returns. If you have many sheep, consider a wool classer course or work with a cooperative. Remember: clean wool is worth more. Remove as much dirt as possible before classing. Store bags on pallets in a dry, dark room. Avoid contamination from oil, chemicals, or animal hair. Good classing shows buyers you are professional and can lead to repeat sales and higher prices.

4. Skirting and Packaging

Skirting is the process of removing the edges of the fleece that are dirty, stained, or of low quality. It is done immediately after shearing, before the fleece is rolled. Skirting increases the value of the main fleece by removing contaminants. On a clean tarp, spread the fleece with the cut side down. The belly, crutch, and leg areas are usually the dirtiest. Also remove any short fibers from the neck and head. Use your hands or shears to pull away these parts. The skirtings can be sold separately for low-grade products like felt or insulation.

After skirting, the fleece is rolled. Start from the back (the cleanest part) and roll toward the belly, keeping the fleece compact. Tie the roll with paper twine or a similar biodegradable material. Do not use plastic baler twineÃ¢Â€Â”it contaminates wool during processing. For packaging, use clean burlap bags or woven polypropylene wool packs. These allow air circulation. Never use plastic bags, as they trap moisture and cause mold. Each pack should weigh 100Ã¢Â€Â“150 kg for easy handling, but smallholders can use smaller bags of 20Ã¢Â€Â“50 kg.

Label each pack clearly: your name, farm, date, grade, and weight. If you have multiple grades, use separate packs. Store packs in a dry, well-ventilated shed on wooden pallets, away from walls. Protect from rodents, insects, and moisture. In humid regions, use a dehumidifier or place bags on raised platforms. Check regularly for signs of mold or pests. If you sell to a cooperative or trader, they may have specific packaging requirementsÃ¢Â€Â”ask first.

Proper skirting and packaging prevent downgrading and reduce weight loss. A well-packed fleece can fetch 10Ã¢Â€Â“20% more than a poorly handled one. For African farmers, simple improvements like using clean burlap bags and tying with paper twine make a big difference. Remember: buyers judge wool by its presentation. Clean, well-labeled packs show you are serious and can lead to better prices and long-term relationships.

5. Wool Marketing

Wool marketing involves selling your wool for the best possible price. In Africa, options include selling to local traders, cooperatives, or through auctions. Each has pros and cons. Local traders offer immediate cash but often pay low prices. Cooperatives pool wool from many farmers to get better prices and access to larger buyers. Auctions, like those in South Africa (e.g., Port Elizabeth), require grading and volume but can fetch premium prices. For smallholders, cooperatives are often the best route.

Before selling, know your wool's value. Factors: fiber diameter (finer is better), staple length (longer is better), cleanliness (low vegetable matter), and color (white is best). Get a sample tested if possibleÃ¢Â€Â”many agricultural extension offices offer this. Current market prices vary: in 2023, fine Merino wool in South Africa sold for around R150Ã¢Â€Â“R200 per kg, while coarse wool sold for R50Ã¢Â€Â“R80 per kg. In East Africa, prices are lower, but demand for carpet wool is growing.

To market effectively: join or form a cooperative. Pool your wool with neighbors to meet minimum volumes (often 500 kg or more). Grade and class your wool properlyÃ¢Â€Â”buyers pay more for uniform lots. Build relationships with buyers: attend agricultural shows, contact wool brokers, and ask for feedback. Consider value addition: some farmers sell washed or carded wool for hand-spinning, which can double the price. But this requires extra labor and equipment.

Timing matters. Wool prices fluctuate with global demand. Avoid selling immediately after shearing when markets are flooded. Store wool properly and sell when prices are higher. Keep records of sales, prices, and buyer contacts. Use the money to reinvest in your flockÃ¢Â€Â”better rams, feed, or shearing equipment. Remember: marketing is not just selling; it's understanding what buyers want and delivering it consistently. With good quality and reliable supply, you can build a reputation and get better prices year after year.

6. Record Keeping for Wool Production

Record keeping is essential for improving wool production and profitability. Without records, you cannot know which sheep produce the best wool, how much you harvest, or where you can improve. Start simple: a notebook or a spreadsheet on a phone. Record each shearing event, fleece weight, grade, and any health issues. Also record feed, treatments, and breeding dates.

For each sheep, keep an individual record: ear tag number, breed, date of birth, and fleece weight at each shearing. Note fiber diameter if tested, staple length, and any defects (e.g., tender fleece, stains). This helps you select replacement ewes and rams. Cull sheep that consistently produce poor wool. Over time, you can increase average fleece weight and quality.

At the flock level, record total wool produced per year, average fleece weight, and income from sales. Calculate cost of production: feed, labor, shearing, packaging, and transport. This tells you if wool is profitable. For example, if you produce 100 kg of wool and sell at R100 per kg, income is R10,000. If costs are R6,000, profit is R4,000. Compare across years to see trends.

Use records for decision-making. If a certain breed or cross performs better, expand it. If a particular season gives lower fleece weight, adjust nutrition. If a buyer pays more for a certain grade, focus on producing that grade. Records also help when applying for loans or grantsÃ¢Â€Â”they show you are a serious farmer. In Africa, mobile apps like FarmBook or simple Excel sheets can help. Share records with your cooperative or extension officer for advice. Remember: what gets measured gets managed. Good records lead to better wool and more money.

Practical Reference: Wool Quality, Shearing Shed Management and Enterprise Records

This practical reference lesson consolidates the technical and commercial skills covered in Module 8. It is written for African sheep producers who want to move beyond subsistence wool production and into consistent, market-ready fleece supply. Whether you are running Merino crosses in the Eastern Cape, indigenous fat-tailed sheep in the Sahel, or dual-purpose flocks in the Ethiopian highlands, the principles below determine whether your wool earns a premium or a discount at the buyer's gate.

Wool is a biological product. Its value is set before shearing, influenced by nutrition, genetics, health and handling. The practical decisions you make in the six weeks before shearing, on shearing day, and in the shed afterwards can change the price you receive by 20â40%. This lesson gives you the reference tables, diagrams and checklists to make those decisions consistently, season after season.

Why Wool Quality Starts in the Paddock

By the time you catch a sheep for shearing, 80% of its fleece value is already determined. Staple strength, fibre diameter, colour and yield are all influenced by what the animal ate, how healthy it was, and how much stress it experienced in the previous 12 months. A sheep that has been sick, underfed, or kept in muddy conditions will produce wool that is tender, dusty, and discounted at sale.

In African production systems, the most common quality killers are:

  • Nutritional stress: Seasonal feed gaps cause breaks in the wool staple (a "tender" fleece) that reduce processing yield.
  • Ectoparasites: Ticks, lice and keds cause fleece damage, skin lesions and stained wool.
  • Vegetable matter (VM): Grass seeds, burrs and thorns contaminate fleece and require expensive carbonising by the buyer.
  • Staining: urine, dung and bacterial discolouration (canary yellow) lower colour grade.
  • Poor shearing: Second cuts, skin pieces and double-fleece handling reduce classing options.

Each of these is manageable. The tables below give you the decision thresholds and the record-keeping format that turns observation into action.

Wool production involves handling animals and, in many systems, treating them with ectoparasiticides (dipping, pour-on, injectable). Always observe the meat and wool withholding periods stated on the product label. Shearing sheep before the wool withholding period expires can leave chemical residues that fail buyer testing and result in rejection of the entire bale. Wear gloves, eye protection and a mask when handling concentrated chemicals. Store all products in a locked, ventilated area away from feed and water. In case of accidental exposure, follow the label's first-aid instructions and contact a veterinarian or poison centre immediately. For a full list of registered products and withdrawal periods in your country, consult your national veterinary authority or the MSD Animal Health veterinary manual.

Quick Reference Checklist: Shearing Day and Shed Management

  1. Withhold feed overnight (not water) before shearing to reduce rumen fill and make handling safer and easier.
  2. Inspect the shed floor â it must be clean, dry, swept and free of oil, dirt or previous fleece residue before the first sheep enters.
  3. Set up three zones: shearing board, fleece table (skirting/classing), and baling/packing area. Keep them physically separated to prevent contamination.
  4. Check shearing equipment â combs, cutters, handpiece tension, and power supply. Dull or misaligned gear causes second cuts and animal injury.
  5. Record every fleece â tag or mark the fleece with the animal ID, then record weight, class and any defect in your wool record book or mobile app.
  6. Skirt immediately â remove belly wool, stained pieces, and short crutchings before the fleece cools and the fibres set.
  7. Class into lines â keep micron, colour and VM categories separate. Never mix superfine with strong wool; it destroys the value of both.
  8. Store bales on pallets in a dry, dark, ventilated area. Moisture and sunlight degrade wool colour and strength within weeks.
