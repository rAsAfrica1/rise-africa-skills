1. Introduction and Why It Matters

If you want to sell broilers every day, you cannot rely on one big batch. You need a steady supply. That means setting up weekly batches. Instead of buying 200 chicks at once and selling them all in one week, you buy 50 chicks every week. After a few weeks, you have birds ready for sale every single day. This is the batch production system.

Why does this matter? Because customers want chicken every day. If you only have chicken once a month, you lose sales. Restaurants, hotels, and local buyers need a reliable supplier. With weekly batches, you always have birds at different ages. Some are one week old, some are three weeks, some are five weeks, and some are ready for market. You sell the oldest batch and replace it with new chicks. This gives you daily sales and daily income.

In Africa, this system works well because you can start small. You don't need a huge house. You can use simple deep litter on the floor with wood shavings. You can buy feed in small quantities each week. Your risk is also spread out. If disease hits one batch, you don't lose everything. You still have other batches to sell.

Let's look at real numbers. Suppose you start with 50 chicks per week. Broilers reach market weight in 6 weeks. After 6 weeks, you will have 6 batches of 50 birds each, at different ages. Each week, you sell the oldest batch of 50 birds. If each bird sells for 5,000 shillings (or equivalent), you earn 250,000 per week. That's over 1 million per month. Your costs include chicks, feed, and health products. With good management, you can make a profit.

The key is consistency. You must buy chicks every week without fail. You must keep records. You must follow a feeding and vaccination schedule. This module will show you exactly how to set up weekly batches step by step. You will learn the tools you need, the method, common mistakes, safety practices, and you will do a practical exercise. By the end, you will be ready to start your own daily broiler sales system.

2. Tools and Materials

To set up weekly batches, you need the right tools and materials. You don't need expensive equipment. Most items are available in local agro-vet shops or markets. Here is a list of what you need.

First, housing. You need a brooder house for young chicks and a grower house for older birds. You can use a simple structure with wooden poles, mud walls, and iron sheets. The floor should be concrete or packed earth with deep litter. For 50 chicks per batch, you need about 1 square meter per 10 birds. So a house of 5 meters by 5 meters can hold 250 birds at different stages if partitioned. Use wire mesh for ventilation and to keep out predators.

Second, brooding equipment. For each new batch of 50 chicks, you need a brooder. You can use a charcoal stove, a kerosene lamp, or an electric bulb. A simple charcoal brooder made from a metal drum works well. You also need feeders and drinkers. For 50 chicks, provide 4 tube feeders and 4 drinkers. As birds grow, increase to 8 feeders and 8 drinkers per 50 birds.

Third, feed. Broilers need starter feed (0-4 weeks), grower feed (4-6 weeks), and finisher feed (6 weeks to market). Buy feed weekly based on the number of birds. For 50 birds, you need about 10 kg starter per week, 15 kg grower, and 20 kg finisher. Store feed in a dry, raised place to avoid mould.

Fourth, health products. You need vaccines for Newcastle and Gumboro, vitamins, and antibiotics. Keep a small fridge or cool box for vaccines. Also have disinfectant for cleaning the house.

Fifth, record-keeping tools. A notebook and pen are enough. Record the date you received chicks, number of deaths, feed consumed, vaccinations, and sales. You can also use a simple chalkboard on the wall.

Sixth, other tools: a weighing scale to check market weight, a wheelbarrow for transporting feed and litter, a rake and shovel for cleaning, and a water hose or buckets for cleaning drinkers. You also need a source of clean water.

All these items can be bought gradually. Start with the basics and add as you grow. The total cost for 50 chicks per week setup is modest. Many African farmers start with less than $500. The key is to have a separate space for each batch to avoid mixing ages.

3. Step by Step Method

Setting up weekly batches follows a clear step-by-step method. Follow these steps carefully and you will have a smooth operation.

Step 1: Plan your batches. Decide how many chicks you will start with each week. For beginners, 50 chicks per week is good. You will have 6 batches at any time (since broilers take 6 weeks to mature). So your total flock will be 300 birds. Make sure your house can hold that many.

Step 2: Prepare the brooder. One week before chicks arrive, clean and disinfect the brooder area. Spread dry wood shavings on the floor. Set up the heat source. For charcoal brooder, light it and check the temperature. It should be 32-35C for day-old chicks. Use a thermometer or watch chick behaviour: if they huddle, it's too cold; if they spread out and pant, it's too hot.

Step 3: Buy chicks every week. Order 50 day-old chicks from a reputable hatchery. Choose a fixed day each week, say Monday. This keeps your schedule consistent. On arrival, give the chicks glucose water for the first 6 hours. Then start them on starter feed.

Step 4: Follow a feeding schedule. Week 1-4: starter feed, feed ad libitum (always available). Week 4-6: grower feed. Week 6 to market: finisher feed. Provide clean water always. For 50 birds, you will use about 10 kg starter in week 1, increasing to 20 kg by week 4. Adjust based on consumption.

Step 5: Vaccination and health. Day 1: Marek's (often done at hatchery). Week 1: Newcastle (NCD) via eye drop. Week 2: Gumboro via drinking water. Week 3: NCD booster. Week 4: Gumboro booster. Always give vitamins before and after vaccination. Keep records.

Step 6: Move birds to grower house. At 4 weeks, move the batch from brooder to grower house. This frees the brooder for the next batch. Ensure the grower house is clean and has enough space. Continue feeding and watering.

Step 7: Sell the oldest batch. At 6-7 weeks, when birds reach 1.8-2.2 kg, sell them. You can sell live birds or dressed. Weigh a sample to check average weight. Set your price based on market rates. After selling, clean and disinfect the house, then rest it for a few days before the next batch arrives.

Step 8: Replace with new chicks. The same week you sell, you should receive a new batch of 50 chicks. This keeps the cycle going. Always maintain the weekly rhythm. If you skip a week, you break the daily sales chain.

Step 9: Keep records. Record all income and expenses for each batch. This helps you know your profit and make improvements. With practice, you can increase to 100 chicks per week.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many farmers fail at weekly batch production because of avoidable mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them.

Mistake 1: Starting too many chicks at once. Some farmers buy 200 chicks in the first week. They get overwhelmed by feed costs and disease. Fix: Start small. Begin with 50 chicks per week. Increase gradually as you gain experience and profit.

Mistake 2: Not separating batches by age. If you mix young and old birds, the older ones bully the young, eat their feed, and spread disease. Fix: Use separate pens or partitions for each batch. Always keep batches of the same age together. Never mix a 1-week-old batch with a 4-week-old batch.

Mistake 3: Poor brooder management. Chicks die from cold or heat stress. Fix: Check brooder temperature daily. Use a thermometer. Adjust heat source. Ensure proper ventilation but no draughts. Provide enough space: 50 chicks need about 1 square meter in the brooder.

Mistake 4: Skipping vaccinations. Newcastle and Gumboro can wipe out your flock. Fix: Follow a strict vaccination schedule. Buy vaccines from reliable agro-vets. Store them in a cool box with ice packs. Vaccinate on time. If you miss a vaccination, consult a vet for advice.

Mistake 5: Inconsistent feeding. Some farmers feed whenever they remember. This causes stunted growth. Fix: Feed at fixed times, at least twice a day. Provide feed and water ad libitum for broilers. Use feeders that prevent spillage. Keep feed dry.

Mistake 6: Poor record keeping. Without records, you don't know if you are making a profit. Fix: Keep a simple notebook. Record daily mortality, feed used, vaccinations, and sales. Review weekly. This helps you spot problems early.

Mistake 7: Not cleaning and disinfecting between batches. Disease builds up in dirty houses. Fix: After selling a batch, remove all litter, scrub the floor and walls with disinfectant, and let the house dry. Rest it for at least 3 days before bringing new chicks.

Mistake 8: Overcrowding. Too many birds in a small space cause stress, disease, and poor growth. Fix: Provide 1 square meter for every 10-12 birds in grower house. For 50 birds, give at least 4-5 square meters.

Mistake 9: Ignoring market weight. Selling underweight birds reduces profit. Fix: Weigh a few birds weekly. Broilers should reach 1.8-2.2 kg by 6-7 weeks. If they are underweight, check feed quality and health.

Mistake 10: Lack of water. Water is essential. Fix: Provide clean water always. In hot weather, birds drink more. Ensure drinkers are clean and filled at least twice a day.

By avoiding these mistakes, your weekly batch system will run smoothly and profitably.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety and best practice are crucial for the health of your birds and your own well-being. Follow these guidelines to run a safe and professional operation.

First, biosecurity. Keep your poultry house secure from visitors and wild birds. Wild birds can carry diseases like avian flu. Use wire mesh on windows and doors. Limit visitors. If you must allow someone in, provide boots and a lab coat. Disinfect boots in a footbath before entering. Never bring live birds from other farms into your compound.

Second, personal hygiene. Always wash your hands with soap after handling birds or cleaning the house. Wear protective clothing: overalls, boots, and gloves. When handling vaccines, use sterile equipment. Dispose of dead birds properly by burning or deep burial. Never sell dead birds for consumption.

Third, safe use of equipment. Brooders using charcoal or kerosene produce carbon monoxide. Ensure proper ventilation to avoid suffocation. Keep the brooder away from flammable materials. Use electric brooders with caution; check for faulty wiring. Keep a fire extinguisher or sand bucket nearby.

Fourth, feed and water safety. Store feed in a cool, dry place away from chemicals. Never feed mouldy feed. Clean drinkers daily to prevent algae and bacteria. Provide clean, fresh water. In hot weather, add electrolytes or vitamins to water to reduce stress.

Fifth, vaccination best practices. Follow the cold chain. Vaccines must be kept between 2-8C. Use a cool box with ice packs when transporting. Reconstitute vaccines only when ready to use. Use the entire vial within 2 hours. Burn or disinfect empty vials. Never reuse needles.

Sixth, litter management. Keep litter dry and loose. Wet litter causes ammonia build-up and respiratory diseases. Remove wet spots daily. Add fresh wood shavings as needed. Replace litter completely between batches.

Seventh, rodent and insect control. Rats and flies spread disease. Keep the compound clean. Store feed in rodent-proof containers. Use traps or approved rodenticides carefully, away from birds.

Eighth, emergency preparedness. Have a plan for disease outbreaks. Isolate sick birds immediately. Consult a vet. Keep a basic first-aid kit for birds with antibiotics and vitamins. Know the signs of common diseases: Newcastle (twisted neck, gasping), Gumboro (whitish droppings, depression), coccidiosis (bloody droppings).

Ninth, market safety. When selling, handle birds humanely. Use clean crates. If dressing, use clean water and a hygienic surface. Wash hands and knives. Sell fresh meat quickly or refrigerate.

Tenth, record keeping for safety. Record all vaccinations, medications, and mortality. This helps trace problems and ensures you follow withdrawal periods for drugs before selling.

By following these safety and best practices, you protect your investment, your health, and your customers.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to put what you have learned into practice. This exercise will guide you through setting up your own weekly batch system. You will need a notebook, a pen, and access to your planned poultry house. If you don't have a house yet, you can do this exercise on paper as a plan.

Exercise 1: Calculate your batch size. Decide how many chicks you will start with each week. For beginners, 50 is recommended. Write down your number. Then calculate the total number of birds you will have at any time. Since broilers take 6 weeks to mature, multiply your weekly batch size by 6. For 50 chicks, that's 300 birds. Check if your house can hold that many. You need 1 square meter for every 10-12 birds. So 300 birds need 25-30 square meters. If your house is smaller, reduce your weekly batch size.

Exercise 2: Draw a batch calendar. On a piece of paper, draw a table with 7 columns: Week, Batch 1, Batch 2, Batch 3, Batch 4, Batch 5, Batch 6. Rows for weeks 1 to 8. In week 1, write '50 chicks' under Batch 1. In week 2, write '50 chicks' under Batch 2, and for Batch 1 write '1 week old'. Continue this for 8 weeks. By week 6, Batch 1 will be 6 weeks old and ready for sale. In week 7, you sell Batch 1 and start Batch 7 (which replaces Batch 1). This calendar shows you when to expect sales.

Exercise 3: List your tools and materials. Make a shopping list of everything you need for your first 6 weeks. Include: 50 chicks per week (for 6 weeks = 300 chicks total, but you buy weekly), brooder, feeders, drinkers, feed (starter, grower, finisher), vaccines, disinfectant, wood shavings, notebook, weighing scale. Estimate costs. Visit local agro-vet shops to get prices. Write down the total startup cost.

Exercise 4: Create a vaccination schedule. For a batch of 50 chicks, write down the vaccines and ages: Day 1: Marek's (at hatchery). Week 1: Newcastle (eye drop). Week 2: Gumboro (drinking water). Week 3: Newcastle booster. Week 4: Gumboro booster. Note that you will have multiple batches, so you will vaccinate different batches each week. Make a weekly planner showing which batch gets which vaccine.

Exercise 5: Plan your first sale. Assume your birds reach 2 kg at 6 weeks. If the market price is 5,000 shillings per kg live weight, each bird sells for 10,000 shillings. For 50 birds, that's 500,000 shillings. Calculate your costs: chicks (50 x 1,000 = 50,000), feed (approx. 200,000), vaccines and health (20,000), labour and miscellaneous (30,000). Total costs = 300,000. Profit = 200,000 per batch. Write this down.

Exercise 6: Set up a record book. Create a simple table with columns: Date, Batch Number, Number of Birds, Feed Used (kg), Mortality, Vaccination, Sales, Expenses. Fill it in daily. This will help you track performance.

After completing these exercises, review your plan with a local poultry extension officer or experienced farmer. Ask for feedback. Then start your first batch. Remember, consistency is key. Good luck!

<h2>Why the Numbers Matter</h2>
<p>Setting up weekly batches is not only about biology it is a cash-flow decision. With four batches running, you tie up roughly four times the working capital of a single batch, but you also receive money every week instead of once every six weeks. In most African markets, consumers buy live birds daily, so a weekly batch system matches demand and reduces the risk of a price crash from flooding the market on one day.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist (Pin This Up)</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Confirm your market first.</strong> Before placing chicks, know how many birds you can sell daily and at what price. Do not start batches you cannot sell.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare the brooder 24 hours ahead.</strong> Heat it to 3234C, spread clean dry litter, and set water and feed in chick feeders before chicks arrive.</li>
<li><strong>Place one batch per week, same day.</strong> Keep the interval fixed (e.g. every Monday) so records and cash flow stay predictable.</li>
<li><strong>Use separate pens or partitions.</strong> Never mix batches of different ages older birds carry disease and bully younger ones.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a batch register.</strong> Record date placed, number of chicks, feed used, deaths, vaccinations, and date sold for each batch.</li>
<li><strong>Follow the vaccination schedule.</strong> Newcastle (day 7 and 21), Gumboro (day 14 and 21), and any local requirements. Cold chain must be maintained.</li>
<li><strong>Clean and rest pens between batches.</strong> Remove litter, wash, disinfect, and rest the pen for at least 37 days before the next batch.</li>
<li><strong>Review weekly.</strong> Compare actual feed conversion and mortality against the plan and adjust the next batch size.</li>
</ol>

<p style="margin:0;color:#b71c1c;font-weight:bold;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING Disease and Biosecurity</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#333;">Running multiple batches at once increases the risk of disease spreading between pens. Newcastle disease and avian influenza can wipe out an entire unit in days. You <strong>must</strong> practise strict biosecurity: footbaths at every pen entrance, no visitors into the brooder house, separate boots and clothing per age group, and immediate isolation of sick birds. Do not sell or move birds showing respiratory or nervous signs. Report unusual sudden deaths to your local veterinary officer immediately.</p>
