4. Making Agar Cultures

Agar is where every spawn batch begins. This module teaches you how to make your own agar, pour sterile plates, transfer mycelium cleanly, and identify healthy growth versus contamination. This is the most technique-heavy part of the course — take your time.

Module 4 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 4.1 — Agar types — PDA, MEA, and homemade alternatives

Agar is a jelly-like substance derived from seaweed. It provides a solid surface for mycelium to grow on while allowing you to see and inspect it. The mycelium eats the nutrients you mix into the agar.

TypeIngredientsBest forCost
PDA (Potato Dextrose Agar)Potato infusion, dextrose, agarGeneral purpose, oysterLow
MEA (Malt Extract Agar)Malt extract, agarShiitake, wood-loving speciesLow
SabouraudPeptone, glucose, agarYeasts, generalMedium
Homemade PDABoiled potato water, sugar, agarEverything, if you cannot buy pre-mixedVery low

For most African producers, homemade PDA is the practical choice. You literally take 200 g of potato, boil it in 1 litre of water for 30 minutes, strain, add 20 g of sugar and 15–20 g of agar, and you have a working medium. It works beautifully and costs cents.

Commercial pre-mixed PDA or MEA is convenient but not necessary. Buy it if you can afford it, but do not delay starting because you cannot find it.

Recipe — Homemade PDA for 1 litre: 200 g potato (peeled, sliced, boiled in 1 L water, strained) + 20 g white sugar + 15 g agar powder. Stir, heat to dissolve agar, pour into jars or bottles, sterilise in pressure cooker at 15 PSI for 20 minutes. Makes about 40 standard petri dishes.
Practical exercise 4.1: Make your first batch of homemade PDA this week. Use a digital scale if possible — agar quantity matters. Aim for 15 g per litre for firm plates.

Lesson 4.2 — Mixing, sterilising, and pouring plates

Step-by-step PDA plate preparation:

  1. Prepare the potato infusion. Peel and slice 200 g of potato. Boil in 1 litre of water for 30 minutes. Strain through a clean cloth. Discard solids.
  2. Add sugar and agar. Weigh 20 g sugar and 15 g agar. Add to the infusion.
  3. Dissolve. Heat gently while stirring until the agar dissolves completely. It should be clear with no lumps.
  4. Dispense into containers. Pour into glass jars or bottles. Fill halfway at most — you need space for shaking.
  5. Sterilise. Pressure cooker at 15 PSI for 20 minutes.
  6. Cool slightly. Let the jars cool to about 50–60°C (warm to the touch but not scalding).
  7. Pour plates in the still-air box. Open a sterile Petri dish, pour 15–20 ml of liquid agar, close the lid. Swirl gently to spread. Repeat.
  8. Let set. Within 20 minutes the agar solidifies. Plates are ready to use or store.

Plate storage: keep plates upside down in a sealed bag or box in the fridge for 3–4 weeks. Upside-down keeps condensation on the lid, not on the agar surface.

Pour plates while the agar is still hot enough to be liquid but cool enough not to burn. Too hot and you will get heavy condensation and kill any antibiotics in the mix. Too cold and the agar sets before it spreads evenly. 50–60°C is the sweet spot.
Practical exercise 4.2: Pour 20 plates in one session. Leave 2 as blank controls — do not open them. If mould grows on the blank plates after 5 days, your technique has a problem.

Lesson 4.3 — Aseptic transfer — the technique that makes or breaks you

Transferring a piece of mycelium from one plate to another, or from a mushroom to a plate, is where contamination happens. This is the single technique most worth practising.

Step-by-step tissue culture transfer:

  1. Set up the still-air box. Wipe with 70% alcohol. Place the alcohol lamp inside and light it.
  2. Gather materials: fresh mushroom, sterile scalpel, fresh agar plate, alcohol wipe, marker.
  3. Sterilise the scalpel by flaming until red-hot, then let it cool inside the box (do not touch anything while cooling).
  4. Break open the mushroom. Using your hands (gloved), split it in half. The inside is sterile — no bacteria or spores have touched it.
  5. Cut a small piece. Using the cool scalpel, cut a piece about the size of a grain of rice from the interior tissue.
  6. Open the agar plate just enough to place the tissue in the centre. Close immediately.
  7. Label with the species, date, and source.
  8. Incubate at 22–26°C for 5–14 days.

Technique rules:

  • Never let the lid of the plate stay open for more than 2 seconds at a time.
  • Never pass your hands over an open plate.
  • Never set down a sterile tool on a non-sterile surface.
  • If in doubt, restart the transfer — one contaminant ruins a whole plate.
The 2-second rule: A plate should never be open for more than 2 seconds. Open, place, close. If you fumble, throw the plate away and start over. The agar is cheap; the wasted weeks are not.
Practical exercise 4.3: Do 5 transfers in one session. Expect 1–2 to contaminate. That is normal for beginners. Practise the movements slowly. Within 10 plates, you should be at 90% clean.

Lesson 4.4 — Incubating dishes — temperature, time, humidity

Once you have a mycelium piece on agar, it must grow. Ideal conditions for most species:

FactorIdeal rangeNotes
Temperature22–26°CWarmer than 28°C risks bacterial contamination; colder than 20°C slows growth
Time to cover plate5–14 daysFast strains cover in 5–7 days; slow ones take 14
Humidity50–70%A sealed box maintains this naturally
LightNone or dimDirect light inhibits some species' growth
OrientationUpside-downPrevents condensation dripping onto mycelium

An incubation box is simple: a clean, sealable plastic box or drawer in the warmest part of your house. In most of Africa, room temperature is already close to ideal. In cooler climates, a heat mat or insulated box will help.

Check plates daily. You are looking for:

  • White, thread-like growth spreading from the tissue piece — healthy mycelium
  • Coloured patches (green, blue, black, orange, pink) — contamination
  • Grey fuzzy growth — mould contamination
  • Wet, slimy patches — bacterial contamination
Practical exercise 4.4: Set up your incubation box. Place 3 plates inside. Check daily and log the day when mycelium first appears and when it covers the whole plate.

Lesson 4.5 — Identifying healthy mycelium vs contaminants

This is the single most important skill in the entire course. If you cannot tell healthy mycelium from contamination, you will sell bad spawn and destroy your reputation.

What you seeWhat it isAction
Bright white, thread-like, radiating growth from a single pointHealthy mushroom myceliumContinue — this is good
Green patches (any shade of green)Trichoderma mould — the most common contaminantDiscard plate. Do not open inside the lab. Dispose outside.
Black or dark grey fluffy patchesVarious moulds (Aspergillus, Rhizopus)Discard
Orange, pink, or red slimy patchesBacteria (Serratia, Rhodotorula)Discard
Wet, glossy streaks with no structureBacteriaDiscard
Yeast-like cream-coloured massesYeast contaminationDiscard
White but flat and yeast-textured, no threadsYeast or bacterial — not myceliumDiscard

Distinguishing subtle cases:

  • Mycelium grows radially from the tissue piece, with visible thread structure at the edges. Contaminants often appear randomly across the plate, not radiating from one point.
  • Mycelium is tough — if you try to scrape it with a sterile loop, it resists. Contaminants wipe off easily.
  • Mycelium smells like mushrooms. Contaminants smell sour, yeasty, or like nail polish remover (acetone from bacteria).
Never open a contaminated plate inside your lab. Green Trichoderma releases billions of spores the moment you open it. Take it outside, open it there, and dispose of the agar. Wipe the outside of the plate with bleach before bringing the container back in.
Practical exercise 4.5: Every plate you incubate, examine at 5, 7, and 10 days. Take a photo of a healthy plate and a contaminated plate for your own reference. This visual record is worth more than any book.

Lesson 4.6 — Storing and expanding cultures

Once you have a healthy plate of pure mycelium, you have two jobs: preserve it, and expand it.

Preserve: Take the healthy plate and make 3–5 slant tubes (agar in slanted test tubes, sterilised). Using sterile technique, transfer a small piece of mycelium to each slant. Incubate 5–7 days. Then store in the fridge at 4–8°C. These are your backups.

Expand: From the same original plate, take 5–10 additional transfers to new plates. Each new plate can grow a full colony in 5–10 days. Then from each of those plates, you can transfer to grain spawn (module 5). This is called "scaling up" — one plate becomes 10 plates, which become 20 jars of grain spawn, which become 40 kg of finished spawn.

StageOutputTime
1 agar plate1 pure culture7–14 days
Transfer to 10 plates10 pure cultures+7 days
Transfer to 10 grain jars (per plate)100 grain jars+14 days
Finished spawn (500 g per jar)50 kg spawn+3 weeks total

This is the multiplication math. From one mushroom, you can produce enough spawn to sell for months — as long as you maintain sterile technique throughout.

The expansion rule: Never transfer from a plate older than 4 weeks. Older mycelium loses vigour and accumulates mutations. Always use fresh plates for expansion. Keep the original as backup only.
Practical exercise 4.6: Take your best plate and expand it to 10 new plates. Label each with a date. This is your production line. In module 5 you will transfer these to grain.
