1. Metals and Their Properties

When you weld, you are not just joining metalâyou are changing its internal structure. To control that change, you first need to know what metals you are working with. In Africa, the most common metals in welding shops are mild steel, stainless steel, aluminium, and cast iron. Mild steel (also called low-carbon steel) is the easiest to weld. It contains less than 0.3% carbon, which makes it soft, strong, and forgiving. You can weld it with any common processâstick, MIG, or TIGâand it rarely cracks. Stainless steel has chromium (at least 10.5%) to resist rust. It is great for food equipment, dairy tanks, and outdoor railings, but it warps more and needs more careful heat control. Aluminium is light and resists corrosion, but it conducts heat away fast, so you need higher heat and a different welding technique. Cast iron is brittle and cracks easily when heated; welding it requires preheating and slow cooling.

How do you tell them apart in the shop? Use a magnet. Mild steel and stainless steel are magnetic (though some stainless grades are weakly magnetic). Aluminium and cast iron are not magnetic (cast iron is magnetic, actuallyâwait, cast iron is magnetic because it contains iron. Correction: cast iron is magnetic. Aluminium is not). A grinder spark test helps too: mild steel gives long yellow sparks, stainless gives short red sparks, aluminium gives no sparks, and cast iron gives short red sparks that burst. Always clean metal before weldingâremove rust, paint, oil, and grease with a grinder or wire brush. Dirty metal causes porosity and weak welds.

Properties that matter most for welding are thermal conductivity, thermal expansion, and hardness. Copper and aluminium conduct heat away quickly, so you need more amps or preheat. Stainless steel expands more than mild steel when heated, leading to more distortion. Hardness increases when you cool metal quickly, making it brittle. In Africa, where many workshops use recycled metal from vehicles or buildings, you often donât know the exact grade. Start with low amps, test on a scrap piece, and adjust. A good rule: if the metal is magnetic and rusts, itâs likely mild steelâweld it with confidence. If itâs shiny and non-magnetic, itâs probably aluminiumâuse a spool gun or TIG with AC. If itâs rusty and magnetic but hard to cut, it might be cast ironâpreheat to 200â300Â°C before welding.

2. Heat Input and Cooling Rates

Heat input is the amount of energy you put into the metal per unit length of weld. It is measured in kilojoules per millimetre (kJ/mm). Too little heat, and you get lack of fusion. Too much heat, and you burn through thin metal or create a wide, weak heat-affected zone. The formula is: Heat Input (kJ/mm) = (Voltage Ã Current Ã 60) / (Travel Speed in mm/min Ã 1000). For example, if you weld at 25 volts, 150 amps, and travel at 200 mm/min, heat input = (25 Ã 150 Ã 60) / (200 Ã 1000) = 1.125 kJ/mm. Thatâs a typical value for stick welding mild steel. For TIG welding thin stainless, you might use 0.5 kJ/mm or less.

Cooling rate is how fast the weld and surrounding metal lose heat after welding. Fast cooling makes the metal hard and brittle, especially in steels with higher carbon or alloy content. Slow cooling makes it softer and more ductile. In Africaâs hot climate, cooling can be slower than in cold countries, but wind and thin sections can still cause rapid cooling. To control cooling, you can preheat the metal (see Lesson 5) or cover the weld with a heat blanket or dry sand immediately after welding. For mild steel, cooling rate is rarely a problem. For stainless steel, fast cooling can cause cracking; for cast iron, it almost always causes cracking unless you cool very slowly.

How do you know if your heat input is right? Watch the weld pool. It should be calm, with a distinct rim, and the slag should peel off easily. If the pool is too fluid and the edges are washed out, you are too hot. If the pool is sluggish and the edges are not melting, you are too cold. Adjust your amps or travel speed. A good rule for stick welding mild steel: use 1 amp per 0.025 mm of thickness (e.g., 100 amps for 2.5 mm plate). For MIG, use 1 amp per 0.025 mm plus 10%. For TIG, use 1 amp per 0.025 mm minus 10%. Always test on scrap. In many African workshops, power supply is unstable, so voltage can drop. Use a voltage meter or adjust your technique if the arc sounds different. Keep a notebook of settings that work for your common jobs.

3. Heat-Affected Zone (HAZ) Basics

The heat-affected zone (HAZ) is the area of base metal next to the weld that has been heated but not melted. Even though it didnât melt, the HAZ can become harder, weaker, or more brittle than the original metal. The size and properties of the HAZ depend on the metal type, heat input, and cooling rate. In mild steel, the HAZ is usually narrow and not a big problem. In high-carbon steel or alloy steel, the HAZ can become very hard and crack. In aluminium, the HAZ loses strength because the heat destroys the work-hardening or heat treatment.

How do you recognise a HAZ? After welding, clean the weld and look at the metal beside it. You will see a discoloured bandâblue, purple, or greyâwhere the metal was heated. Thatâs the HAZ. The wider the band, the more heat you put in. For critical jobs, you can test hardness with a file: if the file skates over the HAZ instead of cutting, itâs too hard. In Africa, where many welders work on truck frames, water tanks, and gates, a hard HAZ can lead to cracks that cause failures. To reduce HAZ problems, use the lowest heat input that still gives good fusion, and avoid weaving too wide. Stringer beads (straight, narrow beads) create a smaller HAZ than wide weaves.

If you must weld high-carbon steel (like old railway track or plough blades), preheat the whole area to 200â300Â°C before welding and cool slowly. This slows the cooling rate in the HAZ, making it softer and less likely to crack. For stainless steel, the HAZ can become sensitisedâchromium carbide precipitates at grain boundaries, reducing corrosion resistance. Use low heat input and keep interpass temperature below 150Â°C. For aluminium, the HAZ strength loss is permanent unless you re-heat-treat the whole part, which is rarely practical. So design joints with extra thickness or use rivets or bolts instead of welding in critical areas. Always remember: the HAZ is often the weakest point of a weldment. Inspect it for cracks after cooling.

4. Distortion and Shrinkage

When metal heats, it expands. When it cools, it shrinks. But the shrinkage is not uniform because the weld and HAZ cool faster than the rest of the part. This causes distortionâbending, twisting, or buckling. In Africa, where many welders fabricate gates, window frames, and trailers, distortion is a daily battle. The three main types are longitudinal shrinkage (along the weld), transverse shrinkage (across the weld), and angular distortion (the parts bend like a hinge). Thin sheets are worstâthey can buckle like a potato chip.

How do you control distortion? First, use the least amount of heat possible. Weld with the smallest electrode or wire that does the job, and use stringer beads instead of wide weaves. Second, balance your welds. If you weld on one side of a T-joint, the part will bend toward that side. So weld on both sides alternately, or use back-step welding (weld a short length, then start the next bead behind the first). Third, use clamps, jigs, or tack welds to hold parts in place. Tack welds should be strong enough to resist movement but not so large that they cause distortion themselves. Fourth, pre-set the parts in the opposite direction of the expected distortionâthis is called presetting or counter-setting. For example, if a beam will bend up when you weld on top, set it slightly down before welding.

Shrinkage can also cause cracks if the metal is restrained. If you weld a patch in the middle of a rigid plate, the weld shrinks and pulls the plate, creating high stress. To avoid this, preheat the area or use a soft, ductile weld metal. In many African workshops, you can use a hammer and dolly to straighten distorted parts after welding, but this can damage the weld if done cold. Better to prevent distortion in the first place. For long welds, use a sequence: weld a short section, skip a length, weld another section, then fill in the gaps. This spreads the heat and reduces distortion. Always check your work with a straight edge or square as you go.

5. Preheating and Post-Weld Heat Treatment

Preheating means heating the base metal before welding. It is one of the most effective ways to prevent cracking in hard-to-weld metals like cast iron, high-carbon steel, and thick sections. Preheating slows the cooling rate, which reduces hardness in the HAZ and allows hydrogen to escape. For mild steel up to 25 mm thick, preheat is usually not needed. For thicker sections or high-carbon steel, preheat to 100â200Â°C. For cast iron, preheat to 300â400Â°C. How do you preheat in a typical African workshop? You can use a gas torch (propane or oxy-acetylene), a charcoal brazier, or even a wood fireâbut you must control the temperature. Use temperature-indicating crayons or a simple infrared thermometer. If you donât have those, spit on the metal: if it sizzles and dances, itâs around 200Â°C. If it just evaporates slowly, itâs hotter.

Post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) means heating the welded part after welding to improve its properties. The most common PWHT is stress relieving: heat the whole part to 550â650Â°C for one hour per 25 mm of thickness, then cool slowly in still air or inside insulation. This reduces residual stresses and softens the HAZ. For cast iron, PWHT can be done by burying the part in dry sand or ash immediately after welding and letting it cool overnight. For stainless steel, PWHT is usually not stress relieving but solution annealing at 1040â1120Â°C, which requires a furnaceârare in small shops. In Africa, many welders skip PWHT because of lack of equipment, but you can improvise: use a charcoal fire with a blower, or a gas forge. Always monitor temperature with a thermocouple if possible.

When should you preheat or PWHT? Preheat for any steel with carbon equivalent (CE) above 0.4%. CE = %C + %Mn/6 + (%Cr + %Mo + %V)/5 + (%Ni + %Cu)/15. If you donât know the composition, preheat if the metal is thick, rusty, or hard to drill. PWHT is needed for pressure vessels, structural welds in bridges, and any part that will be machined after welding. For everyday jobs like gates and trailers, preheat is often enough. Remember: preheating and PWHT are not magicâthey just control cooling. Always test your procedure on a scrap piece first.

6. Weldability of Common Metals

Weldability is how easily a metal can be welded to produce a sound joint without cracks or defects. Some metals are easy, some are difficult, and some cannot be welded at all. Here is a practical guide for the African workshop.

Mild steel (low-carbon steel): Excellent weldability. Weld with stick (E6013 or E7018), MIG (ER70S-6), or TIG. No preheat needed for thickness under 25 mm. Clean the surface. Use 1 amp per 0.025 mm thickness. Watch for porosity if the metal is dirty. This is the metal you will weld most often.

Stainless steel: Good weldability but tricky. Use TIG or MIG with matching filler (308L for 304, 316L for 316). Use low heat input and keep interpass temperature below 150Â°C. Back-purge with argon if welding pipe. Clean with a stainless wire brush onlyânever use a carbon steel brush. Stainless warps more than mild steel, so use lots of tack welds and alternate sides.

Aluminium: Difficult but doable. Use TIG with AC or MIG with a spool gun. Clean with a stainless brush and acetone just before welding. Use high heat and fast travel. Aluminium conducts heat away quickly, so preheat thick sections to 100â150Â°C. The HAZ loses strength, so design joints with extra material. Do not weld aluminium near flammable materialsâit can spatter.

Cast iron: Very difficult. It cracks easily. Preheat to 300â400Â°C, weld with nickel-based electrodes (like ENi-Cl) or bronze, and cool very slowly (bury in sand). Use short beads and peen each bead while hot. If you cannot preheat, braze instead. Many African welders avoid cast iron unless necessary.

Copper and brass: Difficult. Copper conducts heat away fastâpreheat to 200â300Â°C. Use DCEN (electrode negative) for TIG. Brass contains zinc, which fumes; use good ventilation and a respirator. Bronze filler is often used for repair.

Galvanised steel: Do not weld without removing zinc first. Zinc fumes are toxic. Grind off the coating in the weld area, weld with good ventilation, and re-coat with zinc-rich paint after. If you must weld galvanised, use a respirator and weld outside.

In Africa, you often work with unknown metals. Start with a test weld on scrap. If it cracks, try preheating. If it still cracks, consider bolting or riveting instead. Always keep a notebook of what works.

Distortion and Shrinkage â Business Cost Control

Distortion is the single largest cause of rework in African fabrication shops. It is not a welding defect in the traditional sense â it is a planning defect. Shrinkage happens because metal contracts as it cools; the weld pulls the surrounding plate toward the joint. Control measures that cost almost nothing:

  • Balanced welding sequence: alternate sides of the joint; back-step technique.
  • Tack welds: short, evenly spaced tacks hold alignment before full welding.
  • Fixtures and clamps: rigid restraint reduces movement but can increase residual stress.
  • Pre-setting (preset): offset the parts slightly opposite to expected shrinkage.
  • Minimum passes: don't over-weld; each extra pass adds heat and shrinkage.

For a shop quoting a 10-tonne structural job, a 2% distortion-related rework rate can wipe out the entire profit margin. Training welders to sequence and tack correctly is cheaper than buying a straightening press.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Identify the metal before welding. Mild steel, stainless, aluminium, and galvanised steel all demand different procedures â never assume.
  2. Clean the joint. Remove rust, oil, paint, and zinc coating at least 50 mm from the weld line.
  3. Check electrode/filler condition. Damp low-hydrogen electrodes cause porosity and cracking â store them in a heated quiver or sealed container.
  4. Set heat input deliberately. Know your amps, volts, and travel speed; don't guess. Too much heat = wide HAZ; too little = lack of fusion.
  5. Preheat when thickness or steel grade demands it. Use a contact thermometer or temperature crayon â not your hand.
  6. Control distortion with sequence and tacks. Weld balanced, back-step where possible, and clamp or fixture the work.
  7. Ventilate and wear respiratory protection. Especially for galvanised, painted, or stainless steel â hexavalent chromium and zinc fume are real hazards.
  8. Record what you did. Heat input, preheat temperature, and consumables used â this record wins you the next contract and protects you if a weld is questioned.
