WELDING FUNDAMENTALS · MODULE 9 OF 12

Out of Position

Flat, horizontal, vertical, overhead. Why the same joint welded vertically is a different job, and what changes.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Name the four welding positions and their standard codes
  • Explain why gravity changes the challenge of each position
  • Adjust current, electrode type and technique for vertical and overhead work
  • Recognise the specific defects each position promotes
  • Know what this course will and will not teach you about position work
  • Build a practice plan for out-of-position welding
Lesson 9.1

Why position matters

The same joint welded flat and welded overhead are different jobs. Not because the physics changes, but because gravity acts on the molten pool, and the pool is molten metal that wants to fall.

The four positions

PositionGroove codeFillet codeWhat it is
Flat1G1FWelding from above, work horizontal. The pool sits on the work and gravity helps
Horizontal2G2FWelding along a horizontal joint line, on a vertical face. The pool is on its side
Vertical3G3FWelding up or down a vertical joint. The pool is on a vertical face and gravity pulls it down
Overhead4G4FWelding from below, work above you. Gravity pulls the pool away from the work

These codes are standard in many countries and appear on welding procedures. A "6G" position is a pipe welding position used for qualification testing and combines all positions — it is a professional-level test, not a beginner's concern.

What changes with position

  • Gravity. In flat position, gravity holds the pool in place. In vertical and overhead, gravity pulls it out of the joint. You must control the pool against gravity
  • Heat flow. In flat position, heat rises away from you. Overhead, heat comes down onto you, and the work is heated unevenly
  • Access. Your body is in the way in out-of-position work. You contort yourself to reach the joint, and control becomes harder
  • Visibility. Overhead work in particular is awkward to see — you are looking up at the pool and must often weld by sound and feel
  • Current. Out-of-position work usually requires lower current, so the pool freezes quickly and does not run

This module describes, it does not train

Out-of-position welding is where the real skill of the trade lives. It is learned over hundreds of hours with supervision, on scrap, on real joints, watching the pool and correcting your own mistakes.

This module tells you what changes with position and what to look for. It does not turn you into an out-of-position welder. That requires workshop practice.

Lesson 9.2

Flat position (1G / 1F)

The easiest position. The pool sits on top of the work under gravity, and the arc is aimed straight down or slightly angled. Everything in modules 6 and 7 was written for this position.

Why it is easiest

  • Gravity holds the pool in the joint
  • You can see everything — the arc, the pool, the edges of the joint
  • Weld spatter falls away from you, not onto you
  • You can rest your hand on the work for stability
  • Higher currents can be used without the pool running, giving deeper penetration and faster deposition

Technique summary

  • Set current high. This is where you use the top of the electrode's recommended range
  • Travel angle: drag slightly, or perpendicular — either works
  • Arc length: standard, roughly one core wire diameter
  • Brace your hand on the work. This is a habit to build early

Practise flat until it is boring

Before you move to out-of-position work, you should be able to produce a consistent bead in the flat position with no thought. That consistency is the foundation for everything else — out-of-position work multiplies the difficulty, and if flat position is still uncertain, out-of-position is chaos.

Lesson 9.3

Horizontal position (2G / 2F)

Welding along a joint line that runs horizontally, on a vertical surface. The pool is on its side, held against the plate by surface tension and by the shape of the joint.

What changes

  • Gravity pulls the pool down. The upper edge of the joint is more vulnerable — the pool tends to sag away from the top plate and pile up on the bottom plate
  • The upper plate is a heat sink and pulls heat away. On a fillet joint, the vertical plate needs more heat than the horizontal
  • Undercut is common at the top edge — the arc burns into the upper plate, and the pool cannot fill the groove it leaves
  • Overlap is common at the bottom edge — the pool sags onto the bottom plate rather than fusing into the joint

Technique summary

  • Slightly lower current than flat — the pool must freeze more quickly
  • Angle the electrode slightly more toward the vertical plate — it is the heat sink
  • Watch the top edge specifically. If you see undercut forming, reduce current or speed up slightly
  • Slight upward travel angle helps push the pool toward the upper plate
Lesson 9.4

Vertical position (3G / 3F)

Welding up or down a vertical joint. The pool is on a vertical surface and gravity pulls it down continuously.

Vertical up versus vertical down

Vertical upVertical down
DirectionWelding from bottom to topWelding from top to bottom
PenetrationDeeper — the arc has longer to heat each pointShallower — the arc moves with the pool
Bead shapeWider and more convexNarrower and flatter
TechniqueMore difficult to control the pool against gravityEasier to control but limited penetration
Typical useStructural work, thick plate, root passesThin sheet, sheet metal, high-speed work where penetration is less critical
ElectrodeBasic or rutileCellulosic, sometimes rutile

The vertical up challenge

In vertical up, the pool is above the arc, held in place by surface tension and by the rapid freeze of the weld metal. Your job is to keep the pool small enough that it does not run, while still feeding the arc into the joint so that it fuses properly.

Techniques used:

  • Whip or step motion — move the electrode up, then back down a short distance, then up again. This lets the pool freeze slightly between passes
  • Weave — a controlled side-to-side motion, allowing the pool to freeze at the edges of the weave before you return to the centre
  • Smaller electrodes — 2.5mm instead of 3.2mm, so the pool is smaller
  • Lower current — the pool must not be too hot or too fluid

Common defects in vertical work

  • Undercut at the edges — the arc burns in, and the freezing pool cannot fill the groove
  • Slag traps — the pool freezes before the slag has risen to the surface, and the slag is trapped in the weld. This is the specific defect vertical welding is prone to
  • Lack of fusion — if you move up too fast, the pool freezes without fusing into the joint edges

Vertical down is not easier, it is different

Vertical down looks easier because the pool does not run — it follows the arc. But the penetration is shallow, and vertical down on thick plate or in a joint that needs deep fusion will leave you with a weld that fails. For structural work, vertical up is the correct direction.

Lesson 9.5

Overhead position (4G / 4F)

Welding from below, with the work above you. Gravity pulls the pool straight down and away from the joint. The most difficult position.

What makes it hard

  • Gravity pulls the pool away from the joint. The pool falls before it can freeze, causing sagging, overlap and burn-through
  • Spatter falls on you. Protective clothing is critical. Sleeves buttoned, collar up, no pockets, no turn-ups
  • Vision is difficult. You are looking up past the electrode at the pool
  • Access is awkward. You often cannot brace your hand on the work
  • Heat comes down on you. Radiated heat from the work and the arc is uncomfortable and dangerous

Technique summary

  • Lower current — the pool must freeze fast
  • Smaller electrodes — 2.5mm typically
  • Shorter arc — the pool is smaller and more controllable
  • Faster travel — the pool must not have time to fall
  • Slight backhand angle — the electrode leans back so the arc pushes the pool upward into the joint
  • Controlled weaving — a tight side-to-side motion helps the pool freeze at the edges
  • Full PPE — leather apron, long sleeves buttoned, no exposed skin, no pockets

Overhead welding at height is genuinely dangerous

Welding overhead from a ladder is a serious hazard. Falling, dropping hot metal, spatter in the eye, and losing balance when the arc startles you are all real risks.

Never weld overhead from a ladder. Use scaffolding, a proper platform, or wait until the work can be repositioned. If you must work at height, use a proper work platform with guardrails and a person below watching.

Lesson 9.6

Choosing position and technique

The simplest approach

Whenever possible, reposition the work to the flat position. Flat position is:

  • Faster
  • Easier
  • Produces better-quality welds with less risk of defects
  • Less tiring
  • Less dangerous

The welder who moves the work to the flat position whenever possible is the welder who produces good work efficiently.

When flat is not possible

  • Large structures that cannot be turned — building frames, trailers, tanks, machinery
  • Field repairs where the item cannot be moved
  • Repairs on assembled equipment
  • Pipe welding, where the pipe is fixed and the welder moves around it

Position codes and what they mean

Standard position codes appear in welding procedures and qualification tests. As a beginner, you do not need to know them in detail, but you should recognise them:

  • 1G / 1F — flat
  • 2G / 2F — horizontal
  • 3G / 3F — vertical
  • 4G / 4F — overhead

Codes with a "G" refer to groove welds (butt joints). Codes with an "F" refer to fillet welds (tee, lap, corner). The "6G" position for pipe combines all positions and is the standard qualification test for pipeline welders — a job for trained professionals, not beginners.

What this course does not certify you for

Position welding in structural applications — especially 4G and 6G — is tested and coded work. If you are welding on a trailer, a lifting point, a tank, or anything else where the position matters and the load matters, that work must be done by a welder coded to the applicable standard.

This module tells you what is involved. It does not make you a coded out-of-position welder.

Practical assignment — Module 9

Do the practical work if you have access to a machine and supervision. Otherwise complete the observation parts only.

  1. Photograph five real welds in the out-of-position categories. At least one each of horizontal, vertical up, vertical down, and overhead. State which position each weld was made in and why you think so
  2. Find one out-of-position weld with a visible defect. Describe the defect and give one likely cause from the position-specific defect lists
  3. If you have machine access: weld the same joint in all four positions. Photograph each. State which was hardest and why
  4. Your electrode and current adjustments. For each position, state what changes you would make to current and electrode diameter compared to flat position, and why
  5. Your safety plan for overhead work. State the specific PPE you would add, and the platform or scaffolding arrangement you would use
  6. Your practice plan. State which position you intend to practise first, and why

Points 2, 4 and 5 carry the most marks. Point 2 assesses whether you can apply the position-specific defect knowledge. Point 4 tests the technical adjustments. Point 5 tests whether you know overhead work needs different arrangements, not just different technique.

Module 9 knowledge check

1. What is the fundamental challenge common to all out-of-position welding?

  1. Difficulty seeing the arc
  2. Gravity acting on the molten pool, which wants to fall out of the joint
  3. Lack of electrode access
  4. Higher electricity costs
Show answer

b. The physics does not change, but gravity does — in flat position it holds the pool in place, in vertical and overhead it pulls the pool out of the joint. Managing the pool against gravity is the core skill of out-of-position welding.

2. Why is vertical up generally preferred over vertical down for structural work?

  1. It looks better
  2. Vertical up gives deeper penetration, because the arc has longer to heat each point as the pool moves against gravity
  3. It uses less electricity
  4. It is faster
Show answer

b. Vertical up produces deeper penetration and a stronger weld. Vertical down is faster and easier to control but has shallow penetration and is unsuitable for work that carries load through the joint.

3. What defect is horizontal position work particularly prone to?

  1. Crater cracking
  2. Undercut at the top edge and overlap at the bottom edge, because gravity pulls the pool down and away from the upper plate
  3. Porosity
  4. Burn-through
Show answer

b. The pool sags down, piling up on the lower plate (overlap) while the upper plate is left with a groove where the arc burned in but the pool did not fill (undercut). Watching the top edge specifically and adjusting current and angle prevents both.

4. Why is overhead welding the most difficult position?

  1. It uses the most electricity
  2. Gravity pulls the pool straight down and away from the joint, spatter falls on the welder, vision is restricted, and access is awkward
  3. Overhead joints are always thicker
  4. Overhead welding requires a different machine
Show answer

b. Overhead combines multiple difficulties at once. Low current, small electrodes, short arc, fast travel and full PPE are the standard adjustments. Overhead welding at height also requires a proper platform — never a ladder.

5. What should you do whenever possible before welding a joint?

  1. Use the highest current available
  2. Reposition the work to the flat position
  3. Weld in the position you find it in
  4. Weld vertical down
Show answer

b. Flat position is faster, easier, produces better welds with fewer defects, is less tiring and is safer. A welder who positions the work for flat welding produces better work in less time than one who welds everything in whatever position they find it.
