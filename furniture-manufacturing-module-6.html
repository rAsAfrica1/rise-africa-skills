1. Sanding Techniques: Grits, Tools, and Sequences

Sanding is the foundation of a professional finish. Start with coarse grits to remove mill marks and flatten surfaces, then progress to finer grits. In Africa, common abrasives are aluminum oxide and silicon carbide. Grit numbers indicate particles per square inch: lower numbers are coarser. For bare wood, begin with 80-grit if the surface is rough, then 120, 150, and stop at 180 or 220 for most furniture. Going finer than 220 can burnish the wood, preventing stain absorption. For metal, use 120, 240, 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1500, 2000 for a mirror polish. For plastic, start with 400 and go up to 2000, using wet sanding to prevent clogging.

Tools: Sandpaper sheets, sanding blocks (cork or rubber), random orbital sanders (ROS), belt sanders, and detail sanders. In Africa, a common ROS is the Makita BO5030 or a local copy. Always sand with the grain on wood, except when using a ROS which can leave swirl marks; finish by hand with the grain. For curved surfaces, use flexible foam pads. For metal, use files and then wet/dry paper with water or oil as lubricant.

Sequence: For wood, start with 80 if needed, then 120, 150, 180. Between grits, remove all dust with a tack cloth or compressed air. For a high-gloss finish, go to 220. For an oil finish, stop at 150 or 180 to leave pores open. For metal, after filing, use 120, 240, 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1500, 2000, polishing with a cloth wheel and compound. For plastic, wet sand with 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1500, 2000, then polish with plastic polish.

Safety: Wear a dust mask (N95 or better) and safety glasses. Sand in a well-ventilated area. In Africa, many workshops are open-air, which helps, but still use protection. Clean dust regularly to avoid fire risk from sparks. Store sandpaper in a dry place; humidity in coastal regions can ruin it. Use a sanding block for flat surfaces to avoid rounding edges. Replace paper when it loads up; clogged paper causes scratches. For efficient work, use a ROS for large areas, but always hand-sand with the grain after to remove swirls. Test on scrap before working on the final piece.

2. Surface Preparation for Wood, Metal, and Plastic

Surface preparation is 80% of a quality finish. For wood, start by removing old finishes with a chemical stripper or sanding. In Africa, common strippers contain methylene chloride; use outdoors with gloves and goggles. After stripping, sand progressively: 80, 120, 150, 180. Remove all dust with a tack cloth. For open-grain woods like mahogany or oak, use a grain filler to get a smooth surface. Mix filler with a solvent (naphtha or mineral spirits) to a paste, apply with a squeegee across the grain, then wipe off excess. Let dry, then sand lightly with 220. For oily woods like teak or iroko, wipe with acetone or lacquer thinner before finishing to remove oils. Repair defects: fill cracks with wood filler (sawdust mixed with glue), let dry, sand flush.

For metal, remove rust with a wire brush or sandpaper (80 to 120), then treat with a rust converter (phosphoric acid). Degrease with acetone or lacquer thinner. For aluminum, etch with a mixture of water and white vinegar (1:1) or use a commercial etching solution. Sand progressively: 120, 240, 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1500, 2000. For a brushed finish, use a Scotch-Brite pad in one direction. For a polished finish, use a buffing wheel with rouge or aluminum polish. Always wear gloves to avoid oils from skin.

For plastic, clean with soap and water, then wipe with isopropyl alcohol. Sand with 400 wet, then 600, 800, 1000, 1500, 2000. For scratches, start with 240 if deep. Use a sanding block for flat areas. After sanding, polish with a plastic polish or a flame treatment (carefully pass a torch quickly over the surface to melt minor scratches). For painting plastic, use a primer designed for plastic (e.g., Rust-Oleum plastic primer).

General tips: Work in a clean, dust-free area. In Africa, avoid working in direct sunlight as it dries finishes too fast. Use a tack cloth before each coat. Wear nitrile gloves when handling solvents. For wood, raise the grain by wiping with a damp cloth, let dry, then sand with 220. This prevents fuzz after the first coat. For metal, after sanding, wipe with a tack cloth to remove metal dust. For plastic, avoid over-sanding which can generate heat and melt the surface. Always test on a scrap piece.

3. Staining and Dyeing Wood: Application and Color Matching

Staining adds color while allowing grain to show; dyeing penetrates deeper for vibrant, even color. In Africa, common stains are oil-based (e.g., Minwax) and water-based (e.g., General Finishes). Dyes are often aniline dyes dissolved in water or alcohol. For a natural look, use a gel stain or a wiping stain. For a deep, rich color, use a dye first, then a stain. Always test on scrap wood of the same species.

Application: For oil-based stain, apply with a brush or rag, let sit 5-15 minutes, then wipe off excess with a clean rag. Work in small sections. For water-based stain, apply quickly and wipe off before it dries. For dye, mix powder with hot water (1 oz per quart) or alcohol. Apply with a sponge or spray gun. Dye can be layered for deeper color. For even color, pre-raise the grain: wipe with water, dry, sand with 220. For end grain, which absorbs more, apply a sanding sealer or a coat of clear finish before staining to prevent blotching.

Color matching: Use a color wheel. To match an existing piece, test combinations on scrap. Common African woods: mahogany (reddish-brown), teak (golden-brown), iroko (yellow-brown), ebony (black). To make mahogany darker, add a little black dye. To make teak more golden, add yellow dye. For a weathered look, use a gray dye. For a cherry tone, mix red and brown. Always mix enough for the whole project; measure precisely. Use a scale for powders. For touch-ups, use a artist's brush and blend.

Troubleshooting: Blotching on pine or maple? Use a pre-stain conditioner. Streaky? Apply faster and wipe evenly. Too light? Apply another coat. Too dark? Wipe with mineral spirits (for oil) or water (for water-based) immediately. Dye can be lightened with bleach (oxalic acid) if needed. For a two-tone effect, use a gel stain over a dye. Seal with a coat of shellac (1 lb cut) before topcoat to prevent bleeding. In humid African climates, allow longer drying times: oil stain 24 hours, water-based 2 hours. Always dispose of rags safely; oil-soaked rags can self-ignite. Store in a sealed metal container with water.

4. Topcoats: Lacquer, Polyurethane, Varnish, and Oil Finishes

Topcoats protect the wood and determine sheen. Choose based on durability, ease of application, and environment. In Africa, high humidity and heat affect drying. Lacquer (nitrocellulose) dries fast (15-30 minutes), is easy to spray, and can be rubbed out to a high gloss. It is less durable than polyurethane but easy to repair. Use with a spray gun and thinner (lacquer thinner). Apply 3-4 coats, sanding with 400 between coats. In Africa, lacquer is common in workshops with spray booths.

Polyurethane (oil-based or water-based) is durable and resistant to water and alcohol. Oil-based polyurethane (e.g., Minwax) dries in 6-8 hours, needs 3 coats, and yellows over time. Water-based polyurethane (e.g., General Finishes) dries in 2-4 hours, is clear, and low odor. Apply with a brush or foam pad. Sand with 320 between coats. For a smooth finish, thin the first coat 10% with mineral spirits (oil) or water (water-based).

Varnish (e.g., spar varnish) is tough and flexible, good for outdoor furniture. It dries slowly (24 hours) and needs 3-4 coats. It has a amber tone. Apply with a brush, tip off with a dry brush to remove bubbles. Sand with 320 between coats.

Oil finishes (tung oil, linseed oil, Danish oil) penetrate and leave a natural look. They are easy to apply with a rag, but require frequent reapplication. Tung oil dries in 24 hours, needs 3-5 coats. Linseed oil (boiled) dries in 12-24 hours, but can darken. Danish oil is a mix of oil and varnish, dries in 8-12 hours. Apply wet, let sit 30 minutes, wipe off excess. For a satin sheen, use 0000 steel wool between coats. In Africa, oil finishes are popular for their natural feel and ease of repair.

Application tips: Work in a dust-free area. For brushing, use a high-quality brush (Purdy or similar). For spraying, use a HVLP gun with a 1.3-1.5 mm nozzle. Thin according to manufacturer. For all finishes, apply thin coats. Sand between coats with 320-400. For the final coat, do not sand if you want a gloss; for satin, sand lightly. Allow proper drying: in humid conditions, add 50% more time. Test on scrap. Always wear a respirator when spraying.

5. Spray Finishing Equipment and Techniques

Spray finishing gives a smooth, professional finish faster than brushing. In Africa, common spray equipment includes HVLP (high volume low pressure) guns, conventional guns, and airless sprayers. For small workshops, a HVLP gun with a 1.3-1.5 mm nozzle is ideal. You need a compressor with at least 2 HP and a 20-gallon tank to keep up. For water-based finishes, use a gun with stainless steel parts to avoid rust. For lacquer, use a conventional gun with a 1.5 mm nozzle.

Setup: Use a spray booth or a well-ventilated area. In Africa, many use a makeshift booth with plastic sheeting and a fan. Wear a respirator (NIOSH-approved) and goggles. Filter the finish with a paint strainer. Adjust the gun: fluid knob for amount, air knob for pattern, and air pressure (HVLP: 20-30 psi at the gun; conventional: 40-50 psi). Test on cardboard.

Technique: Hold the gun 6-8 inches from the surface, perpendicular. Use a 50% overlap. Start the stroke before the surface and release after. Move at a consistent speed. For a full coat, spray horizontal then vertical passes. For corners, spray first, then flats. For edges, spray at a 45-degree angle. For a smooth finish, apply thin coats; 2-3 coats are better than one thick coat. Allow flash-off time between coats (5-10 minutes for lacquer, 30 minutes for water-based).

Troubleshooting: Orange peel (rough texture) means too little air or too thick finish; thin and increase pressure. Runs mean too much finish; apply thinner coats and keep gun moving. Dry spray (sandy texture) means too much air or gun too far; move closer and reduce air. Clogging means finish too thick or dirty; strain and thin. In humid climates, add retarder to slow drying and prevent blushing (white haze). Clean gun immediately after use with the appropriate solvent (lacquer thinner for lacquer, water for water-based). Disassemble and clean the nozzle. Store gun with a few drops of oil. For large projects, consider a pressure pot or airless sprayer. Practice on scrap until you get a perfect finish.

6. Rubbing Out and Polishing for Professional Results

Rubbing out removes imperfections and creates a uniform sheen, from satin to high gloss. After the final topcoat has fully cured (lacquer: 24 hours; polyurethane: 72 hours; varnish: 1 week), start with fine abrasives. For a satin finish, use 0000 steel wool or a gray Scotch-Brite pad with a lubricant like mineral oil or soapy water. Rub with the grain, using light pressure. Wipe clean. For a semi-gloss, use 600-grit wet/dry paper with water. For high gloss, use progressively finer grits: 800, 1000, 1500, 2000, then polish with a compound.

Tools: Sanding blocks (felt or rubber), a random orbital sander with a foam interface pad, or hand rubbing. For large flat surfaces, a ROS on low speed (1-2) with a fine grit can save time, but finish by hand with the grain. For curved surfaces, use flexible pads. For polishing, use a buffing wheel with white rouge or a commercial polish like 3M Finesse-It. In Africa, automotive polishes are often used.

Technique: Lubricate the surface to prevent clogging. Use a spray bottle with water and a drop of dish soap. For steel wool, dip in mineral oil. Rub in straight lines with the grain, not circles. For edges, be careful not to rub through the finish. Check your progress under good light. For a mirror gloss, after 2000-grit, use a polishing compound on a soft cloth, then a swirl remover. For a satin finish, stop after 0000 steel wool and apply a paste wax (e.g., Johnson's) for protection.

Common mistakes: Rubbing too hard or too long can cut through the finish. Always test on a scrap piece. Use a block for flat surfaces to avoid rounding edges. Clean between grits to avoid scratches. For oil finishes, rubbing out is not needed; just apply more oil. For water-based finishes, wait 48 hours before rubbing. In humid climates, allow extra curing time. After polishing, apply a coat of wax for added protection. For a professional look, ensure the sheen is even across the entire piece. Use a tack cloth to remove all dust before waxing. Store abrasives in a dry place.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Surface Preparation and Finishing for Furniture Manufacturing

In African furniture manufacturing, the finishing stage is where value is created or destroyed. A well-prepared and finished piece can sell for three to five times the price of raw timber, while a poor finish will trap a workshop in low-margin contract work. This practical reference consolidates the grits, tools, sequences, and business decisions that matter for workshops operating in humid coastal cities, dry Sahelian zones, and high-altitude East African markets.

Whether you are finishing mahogany in Accra, mukwa in Lusaka, or eucalyptus in Nairobi, the principles are the same: remove defects, seal the surface, build film thickness, and polish to the required sheen. What changes is the product selection, drying time, and cost per square metre.

Sanding Techniques: Grits, Tools, and Sequences

Sanding is not a single step it is a sequence that removes previous scratches and prepares the surface for the next grit. In African workshops, the most common mistake is jumping from 80 grit straight to 220, which leaves visible pigtails under a glossy topcoat.

  • 80100 grit: Remove machine marks, planer chatter, and old finish.
  • 120150 grit: Remove 80-grit scratches; first "smooth" feel.
  • 180 grit: Standard pre-stain grit for most hardwoods.
  • 220 grit: Between coats of sealer and topcoat.
  • 320400 grit: Final sand before stain on dense woods like ebony or iroko.
  • 6001500 grit: Wet sanding between topcoats for gloss finishes.

Always sand with the grain on the final passes. Random orbital sanders are efficient for flattening, but finish with a hand block or orbital in the grain direction. For turned legs and carved details, use abrasive cord or flexible foam pads.

Surface Preparation for Wood, Metal, and Plastic

Modern African furniture increasingly combines materials: wooden frames with metal legs, plastic edge trim, and composite panels. Each substrate needs its own preparation.

  • Wood: Sand to final grit, raise grain with a damp cloth, re-sand lightly, and remove dust with a tack cloth or compressed air.
  • Metal: Degrease with solvent, abrade with 120180 grit, apply self-etching primer or epoxy primer within 30 minutes of abrading. In coastal regions, use zinc-rich primer.
  • Plastic: Wash with warm soapy water, scuff with 400600 grit, and use a plastic adhesion promoter before painting. ABS and PVC are common in African furniture trim.

Moisture content is the single biggest variable. In humid Mombasa or Lagos, timber must be kiln-dried to 810% MC and finished quickly. In dry Kano or Windhoek, allow timber to acclimatise to workshop conditions before finishing to avoid checks.

Staining and Dyeing Wood: Application and Color Matching

Stains are pigment-based (sit on the surface, highlight grain) while dyes are dissolved (penetrate, give deeper colour). For African export markets, pigment stains are more colour-stable; dyes give richer tones on mahogany and mukwa.

Application methods: brush, wipe-on rag, spray, or foam pad. Always test on an offcut from the same board. Colour matching across a batch requires consistent stain ratios, temperature, and dwell time. Keep a "master sample" sealed in plastic for each product line.

Topcoats: Lacquer, Polyurethane, Varnish, and Oil Finishes

Topcoat choice determines durability, sheen, and repairability all of which affect warranty and repeat business.

  • Nitrocellulose lacquer: Fast-drying, easy to repair, but low heat and solvent resistance. Common in South African and Kenyan workshops.
  • Catalysed lacquer (pre-cat / post-cat): Better chemical resistance; requires accurate mixing and PPE.
  • Polyurethane (oil-based or water-based): Excellent durability; water-based is preferred for indoor air quality and fast recoat.
  • Varnish (spar, alkyd): Traditional, high-build; slow drying in humid climates.
  • Oil finishes (tung, linseed, Danish oil): Penetrating, easy to repair, low sheen; popular for artisan and export "natural" lines.

Build 35 coats for lacquer and polyurethane, 23 for varnish, and 34 for oil. Sand lightly between coats with 320400 grit. In humid conditions, add retarder or use fast-cure catalysts to avoid blushing (white haze).

SAFETY WARNING Finishing Chemicals

Finishing solvents and catalysts are hazardous. Lacquer thinners, catalysed lacquer hardeners, polyurethane isocyanates, and solvent-based stains can cause serious lung damage, skin burns, and are highly flammable.

  • Always wear a NIOSH/EN-approved respirator with organic vapour cartridges a dust mask is not sufficient.
  • Work in a spray booth with extraction, or outdoors with wind at your back and no ignition sources within 15 m.
  • Store solvents in a locked, ventilated metal cabinet away from direct sun. In hot African climates, drums can pressurise and rupture.
  • Never mix solvents with waste oil or pour down drains. Arrange licensed disposal.
  • Keep a dry powder or CO fire extinguisher within 5 m of the finishing area.

Spray Finishing Equipment and Techniques

Spraying is 510 times faster than brushing and gives a more even film. For African workshops, the practical options are:

  • HVLP (high-volume low-pressure): 8090% transfer efficiency, less overspray, works with small compressors (23 HP). Best value for small workshops.
  • LVLP (low-volume low-pressure): Even less overspray; good for detail work.
  • Conventional air spray: Cheapest gun, but 3050% transfer efficiency and heavy overspray.
  • Airless / air-assisted airless: High production, but expensive and less suited to small batches.

Technique: keep the gun 1520 cm from the surface, perpendicular, with 50% overlap. Trigger before the stroke and release after. Two thin coats beat one thick coat. Clean guns immediately after use dried lacquer destroys nozzles.

Rubbing Out and Polishing for Professional Results

Rubbing out converts a good finish into a premium one. After the final topcoat has cured (2472 hours depending on product and humidity), level the surface with 8001500 grit wet paper using a lubricant (water with a drop of soap, or mineral oil). Then polish with a fine abrasive compound and a foam pad or wool bonnet.

Sheen levels: matte (no rub), satin (grey pad), semi-gloss (white pad), gloss (wool bonnet + compound). Match sheen across all components of a piece a satin table with a gloss leg will not sell.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm timber moisture content is 812% before any finishing step.
  2. Sand through the full grit sequence never skip more than one step.
  3. Raise grain with a damp cloth, re-sand, and remove all dust before staining.
  4. Test stain and topcoat on an offcut from the same batch of timber.
  5. Record product batch numbers, mix ratios, and drying times for every job.
  6. Spray in a dedicated booth with extraction and wear a cartridge respirator.
  7. Sand lightly between every coat; clean dust before the next coat.
  8. Allow full cure before rubbing out; match sheen across the entire piece.

Business takeaway: Finishing is the highest-margin stage in furniture manufacturing. A workshop that masters surface preparation and finishing can move from selling raw furniture to selling branded, export-ready products. Invest in a spray booth, a moisture meter, and training before you invest in more machines.
