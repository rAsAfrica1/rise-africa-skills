1. Building a Simple Pot Still from a Pressure Cooker and Copper Pipe

To build a pot still, you need a pressure cooker (6-8 litres), copper pipe (1/2 inch diameter, about 1.5 metres), a compression fitting to connect the pipe to the cooker lid, a bucket for cooling water, and a heat source (gas stove or charcoal). First, drill a hole in the pressure cooker lid that fits the compression fitting. Attach a short copper pipe (30 cm) vertically from the lidthis is the column. Then connect a longer pipe (1 metre) at a 45-degree angle downward to act as the lyne arm. Finally, coil the remaining copper pipe into a spiral (the condenser) and place it inside a bucket. Connect the lyne arm to the top of the coil. Seal all joints with flour paste (mix flour and water to a thick paste) to prevent vapour leaks. Never use plastic or rubberhot alcohol vapour dissolves them. Test your still by boiling water: if steam comes out only at the end of the coil, it's sealed. For cooling, fill the bucket with cold water and run a slow trickle of water in and out to keep it cool. This simple still can produce 2-4 litres of spirit per run. Safety: always distil outdoors or in a well-ventilated area, and never seal the output endexplosion risk. Clean the copper with vinegar and salt before first use.

2. Fermenting a Sugar Wash with Local Yeast

A sugar wash is the cheapest and easiest ferment for gin. You need: 5 kg white sugar, 20 litres water, 100 g yeast (dry active yeast from a bakery or local market), 1 tablespoon lemon juice or 2 crushed vitamin C tablets, and 1 teaspoon yeast nutrient (optional but helps). Dissolve sugar in 5 litres of hot water, then top up to 20 litres with cold water. The temperature should be below 30C before adding yeastif it's too hot, it kills the yeast. Add lemon juice to make the wash slightly acidic (yeast likes pH 4-5). Sprinkle yeast on top and stir gently. Cover the bucket with a clean cloth and secure with a rubber bandthis keeps flies out but lets gas escape. Keep it in a warm place (25-30C) for 5-7 days. It will bubble vigorously for the first 3 days, then slow down. When bubbling stops and the wash tastes dry (not sweet), it's ready. In hot African climates, fermentation can finish in 3-4 days. If it smells like rotten eggs, add a teaspoon of yeast nutrient. Do not use bread yeast if you can get distiller's yeastit produces less off-flavours. Strain the wash through a cloth before distilling to remove dead yeast. You should get about 20 litres of wash at 10-12% alcohol. This yields roughly 2 litres of pure alcohol after distillation.

3. First Distillation: Stripping Run and Collecting Low Wines

The stripping run is a fast, rough distillation to separate alcohol from water and solids. Pour your fermented wash into the pressure cooker, filling no more than two-thirds full to leave room for foaming. Add a teaspoon of cooking oil or a few pieces of clean copper to reduce foaming. Heat slowly at firstif it boils too hard, foam will carry over into the condenser and contaminate your spirit. Once it starts dripping, increase heat slightly. Collect everything that comes out until the spirit coming off smells weak and watery (like wet cardboard). This is called 'low wines' and will be about 25-30% alcohol. Do not make cuts in this runjust collect it all in one container. A 20-litre wash will take 2-3 hours to strip. Stop when the total volume collected is about 4-5 litres or when the temperature of vapour reaches 98C. Discard the first 50 ml (foreshots) as it contains methanol and other toxinsit smells like nail polish. After the run, clean your still with water. You can store low wines in a glass or stainless steel container for a few days before the spirit run. If you have a lot of wash, do multiple stripping runs and combine the low wines. This first run removes most water and solids, making the second distillation easier and cleaner.

4. Second Distillation: Making Cuts and Blending Hearts

The spirit run is where you make gin. Dilute your low wines to 40% alcohol with clean water (use a hydrometer to check). Pour into the still, filling no more than two-thirds. Heat very slowlythis run takes 4-6 hours. Collect the distillate in small jars (200-300 ml each). You will make three cuts: foreshots, heads, hearts, and tails. Foreshots: discard the first 50-100 ml (poisonous). Heads: collect the next 15-20% of the total alcoholthese have sharp, solvent-like smells. Hearts: the middle portion, which smells clean and sweet. Tails: the last 20-30%, which smell oily and cardboard-like. To identify cuts, use your senses: dip a finger in each jar, rub, and smell. Hearts will smell pleasant. Collect hearts separately. You can blend a little heads and tails back into hearts for complexity, but only after diluting and tasting. A good rule: for 4 litres of low wines at 40%, you might get 1.5 litres of hearts at 60-70% alcohol. Keep records of temperatures and volumes. After blending, let the spirit rest for a day before flavouring. This second distillation concentrates alcohol and removes bad flavours. If you want a cleaner gin, do a third distillation.

5. Infusing Botanicals: Maceration vs. Vapor Infusion

Gin gets its flavour from juniper berries and other botanicals. Two main methods: maceration and vapour infusion. Maceration: soak botanicals directly in the spirit (or in the still during distillation). Use 20-30 g juniper berries, 5 g coriander seeds, 2 g angelica root, 1 g orris root, and optional citrus peel, cardamom, or lavender per litre of 40% spirit. Crush the botanicals lightly with a mortar and pestle. Soak for 24 hours, then distill. This gives a deep, earthy flavour. Vapour infusion: place botanicals in a mesh bag or basket in the vapour path above the boiling liquid. The hot alcohol vapour passes through them, extracting lighter, brighter flavours. This is how many premium gins are made. For a simple setup, tie a muslin bag of botanicals to the inside of the still's column. Use 15-20 g juniper per litre for vapour infusion. You can combine both: macerate half the botanicals and vapour-infuse the other half. In Africa, local botanicals like rooibos, hibiscus, baobab, or grains of paradise can replace or complement traditional ones. Start with small batches (1 litre) to test ratios. After infusion, you may need to redistill to avoid cloudiness. If you macerate and don't distill, the gin will be coloured and may go cloudy when chilled. For clear gin, always redistill after maceration.

6. Diluting, Filtering, and Bottling Gin at 40% ABV

After distillation, your gin is likely 60-70% alcohol. To bring it to 40% ABV (the standard for gin), use the formula: Volume of water to add = (Current ABV / Target ABV - 1) x Current Volume. For example, 1 litre at 65% needs (65/40 - 1) x 1 = 0.625 litres of water. Always add distilled or filtered water slowly while stirring. Use a hydrometer to check. If you overshoot, add a little more spirit. After dilution, the gin may become cloudy (called 'louche') due to oils from botanicals. Chill the gin to 0-4C for 24 hours, then filter through coffee filters or a clean cotton cloth. This removes fatty oils and gives a clear spirit. For small batches, chill in a freezer then filter. Bottle in clean glass bottles with airtight caps. Rinse bottles with boiling water before filling. Fill to the neck, leaving 2 cm headspace. Label with date, ABV, and batch number. Store in a cool, dark place. Gin does not improve with age like wine, so drink within 1-2 years. For a professional look, use shrink caps and custom labels. If you sell, check local regulationsmany countries require a licence for spirit production. Always test your final product: 40% ABV should burn slightly when rubbed on skin, and it should smell clean and juniper-forward.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Gin Distillation from Ferment to Bottle

Audience: African micro-distillers, agribusiness entrepreneurs, and artisan beverage producers.
Goal: Build a small-batch gin production line using locally available materials, compliant with safety and quality standards.

1. Building a Simple Pot Still from a Pressure Cooker and Copper Pipe

For many African entrepreneurs, imported stainless steel stills are prohibitively expensive. A pressure cooker converted into a pot still is a proven, low-cost entry point. Use a 610 litre aluminium or stainless steel pressure cooker (avoid non-stick coatings). Replace the weight with a copper pipe (15 mm diameter) fitted with a compression fitting. The pipe should rise vertically 3045 cm, then angle downward through a condensera copper coil submerged in a bucket of cold water. Seal all joints with food-grade silicone or PTFE tape. Never use lead solder or galvanised fittings.

This still can process 58 litres of wash per run. For higher output, scale to a 2030 litre stainless pot with a copper dome and liebig condenser. Local metal fabricators in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa can build these for under $150.

2. Fermenting a Sugar Wash with Local Yeast

Commercial distiller's yeast is ideal but costly. In many African markets, you can use palm wine yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae from tapped palm sap), sorghum beer yeast, or dried baker's yeast as a backup. For a 20-litre wash:

  • 4 kg white sugar (or 5 kg brown sugar for more flavour)
  • 20 L clean water (chlorine-free; let tap water sit 24 hours)
  • 1 tablespoon yeast nutrient (or 1 crushed multivitamin tablet)
  • 50 g fresh palm wine yeast or 10 g dried baker's yeast
  • 1 teaspoon citric acid or juice of 2 lemons (to prevent bacterial infection)

Dissolve sugar in warm water, cool to 30C, add yeast and nutrient. Ferment in a food-grade plastic bucket with an airlock or loose lid for 510 days at 2030C. The wash is ready when bubbling stops and it tastes dry (specific gravity ~1.000). In hot climates, ferment in the shade and monitor temperatureabove 35C produces off-flavours.

3. First Distillation: Stripping Run and Collecting Low Wines

The stripping run separates alcohol from water and solids. Fill the pressure cooker no more than two-thirds full. Heat slowlynever use high heat, as foaming can block the pipe. Discard the first 50 ml per 20 L wash (foreshots, containing methanol and acetone). Collect the rest until the distillate drops below 20% ABV (test with an alcoholmeter or by tasteit becomes watery). This collected liquid is "low wines" at 2540% ABV. Stop before the wash smells burnt or the temperature reaches 98C.

SAFETY WARNING: Methanol is highly toxic. Never drink foreshots or heads. Always discard the first 50 ml per 20 L wash. Ensure your still is never sealedpressure build-up can cause an explosion. Distill outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. Never leave a running still unattended. In many African countries, home distillation without a licence is illegalcheck your national alcohol control laws before production.

4. Second Distillation: Making Cuts and Blending Hearts

Dilute low wines to 2025% ABV with clean water. Run the still again, this time collecting in small jars (200300 ml). You will make three cuts:

  • Heads: First 1520% of distillate. Sharp, solvent-like smell. Discard or save for cleaning.
  • Hearts: Middle 6070%. Clean, sweet, fruity aroma. This is your gin base.
  • Tails: Last 1520%. Oily, cardboard smell. Discard or add to next stripping run.

Blend hearts only. Use a spoon to mix small samples and taste diluted (1 part spirit to 3 parts water). The final spirit should be 6070% ABV before botanicals.

5. Infusing Botanicals: Maceration vs. Vapor Infusion

Maceration: Soak botanicals in your 6070% ABV spirit for 1224 hours, then redistill. This gives deep, complex flavour. Use 2030 g botanicals per litre of spirit. Classic African botanicals: hibiscus (zobo), grains of paradise (alligator pepper), baobab fruit, rooibos, lemongrass, ginger, black pepper, and citrus peel (orange, lemon, grapefruit).

Vapor infusion: Place botanicals in a mesh bag inside the still's vapor path (between pot and condenser). This gives lighter, brighter flavours. Best for delicate botanicals like rose petals or chamomile.

For a London Dry style, macerate juniper (10 g/L), coriander (4 g/L), angelica root (1 g/L), and citrus peel (3 g/L). For a local twist, add 2 g/L hibiscus and 1 g/L grains of paradise.

6. Diluting, Filtering, and Bottling Gin at 40% ABV

After redistillation with botanicals, you will have gin at 6070% ABV. Dilute to 40% ABV using the formula:

Volume of water to add = (Current ABV / Target ABV Current Volume) Current Volume

Example: 1 L at 65% ABV to 40% ABV: (65/40 1000) 1000 = 625 ml water.

Use distilled or filtered water. Chill the diluted gin to 4C for 24 hours to allow oils to precipitate. Filter through coffee filters or a 1-micron filter. Bottle in clean glass bottles with tight caps. Label with ABV, volume, and production date. Store in a cool, dark place.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Use food-grade materials onlyno lead solder, no galvanised metal.
  • Ferment at 2028C; use local yeast (palm wine, sorghum beer, or baker's).
  • Discard foreshots (first 50 ml per 20 L) and headsnever drink.
  • Collect hearts at 6070% ABV for botanical infusion.
  • Macerate 1224 hours or vapor-infuse for lighter flavour.
  • Dilute to 40% ABV with clean water; chill and filter before bottling.
  • Label bottles with ABV, volume, and date; store cool and dark.
  • Check local licensing laws before selling any distilled spirits.
