Lessons

1. L1

Daily maintenance starts before you start the engine. Check the engine oil with the dipstick. The level must be between the two marks. If it is low, add 15W-40 diesel oil until it reaches the top mark. Check the coolant level in the radiator when the engine is cold. If low, add clean water or antifreeze mix. Check the fuel level and top up with clean diesel. Drain any water from the fuel filter using the drain plug at the bottom. Check the air filter. If it is dusty, tap it gently to remove dust, or replace it if very dirty. Check all belts for tension and cracks. A loose belt will slip and overheat. Check the pump for leaks. Look under the pump for drips. Check the suction and discharge hoses for cracks or soft spots. Check the impeller by turning the pulley by hand; it should turn freely without grinding. Grease the pump bearings every 50 hours of operation with a grease gun. Use 2-3 pumps of grease. Do not over-grease. Keep the radiator fins clean. Blow compressed air or use a soft brush to remove dust and chaff. In dusty conditions, clean the air filter daily. Record the hours on a log sheet. This helps you follow the maintenance schedule. Daily checks take 10-15 minutes and prevent 80% of breakdowns.

2. L2

Weekly maintenance covers items that need attention every 50-100 hours. Change the engine oil and oil filter every 100 hours or once a month, whichever comes first. Use a filter wrench to remove the old filter. Smear clean oil on the new filter gasket before installing. Tighten by hand until snug, then a quarter turn more. Fill with 15W-40 oil to the correct level. Replace the fuel filter every 200 hours. Bleed the fuel system after replacement: loosen the bleed screw on the filter housing and pump the hand primer until bubble-free fuel comes out. Tighten the screw. Check the battery electrolyte level. If low, add distilled water to cover the plates. Clean battery terminals with a wire brush and apply petroleum jelly to prevent corrosion. Check the alternator belt tension. It should deflect 10-15 mm when pressed with your thumb. Adjust if needed. Inspect all hoses for cracks, swelling, or hardness. Replace any hose that shows these signs. Check the pump impeller clearance. For a centrifugal pump, the clearance between impeller and casing should be 0.5-1.0 mm. If greater, replace the wear rings or impeller. Lubricate the pump shaft bearing with grease. Check the mechanical seal for leaks. A few drops per minute is normal; a steady stream means replace the seal. Clean the radiator exterior with a garden hose. Check the fan blades for cracks. Tighten all bolts on the pump and engine mounts. Loose bolts cause vibration and damage. Record all maintenance in a logbook.

3. L3

Monthly maintenance includes tasks that keep your system reliable over the long term. Change the air filter if you have not done so in the last 200 hours. In dusty African conditions, replace it every 100 hours. Clean the crankcase breather. Remove the breather cap and wash it in diesel. Dry it and reinstall. Check the valve clearance. For most diesel engines, intake valve clearance is 0.25 mm and exhaust is 0.35 mm when the engine is cold. Use a feeler gauge to check and adjust. This requires removing the valve cover. If you are not confident, hire a mechanic. Check the injection timing. Incorrect timing wastes fuel and reduces power. This is a specialist job; have it done every 500 hours. Inspect the radiator cap. It must hold pressure. Test with a radiator pressure tester. Replace if it fails. Flush the cooling system every 6 months. Drain the old coolant, flush with clean water, and refill with a 50/50 mix of antifreeze and water. Check the pump coupling alignment. Misalignment causes vibration and bearing failure. Use a straight edge or dial indicator to check. Adjust shims as needed. Check the suction pipe for air leaks. A loose connection will cause the pump to lose prime. Tighten all clamps and use thread sealant on threaded joints. Inspect the foot valve at the bottom of the suction pipe. Clean it and replace if worn. Check the discharge pressure gauge for accuracy. Replace if faulty. Test the safety shut-off system. If your engine has a low-oil-pressure shutdown, test it by grounding the sensor wire; the engine should stop. If not, repair the circuit. Record all findings.

4. L4

Troubleshooting starts with symptoms. If the engine will not start, check fuel first. Is there fuel in the tank? Is the fuel valve open? Is the fuel filter clogged? Bleed the fuel system. Check the battery voltage. It should be 12.6 volts for a 12V battery. If lower, charge or replace. Check the starter motor. If it clicks but does not turn, the battery may be low or the starter solenoid faulty. Check the air filter. A clogged filter will prevent starting. If the engine cranks but does not fire, check the fuel shut-off solenoid. It should click when the ignition is on. If not, check the wiring. If the engine starts but runs rough, check for air in the fuel lines. Bleed again. Check the injectors. A faulty injector will cause misfire. Test by cracking each injector line one at a time; if the engine note does not change, that injector is bad. If the engine overheats, check the coolant level. Check the radiator for blockages. Check the thermostat. Remove it and test in hot water; it should open at 82C. Check the water pump belt. If loose, tighten. Check the fan. If the engine loses power, check the air filter, fuel filter, and fuel quality. Check for a restricted exhaust. If the pump does not deliver water, check the suction pipe for air leaks. Check the impeller for damage. Check the foot valve. Check the discharge valve. If the pump vibrates, check alignment, bearings, and impeller balance. Always diagnose before replacing parts.

5. L5

Common problems and fixes: 1) Engine overheats: Clean radiator, replace thermostat, tighten fan belt, check coolant. 2) Engine knocks: Check oil level, replace oil, check for loose bearings or worn pistons. 3) White smoke: Indicates unburned fuel or water in fuel. Drain fuel filter, check injectors. 4) Black smoke: Too much fuel or not enough air. Clean air filter, check injection timing. 5) Blue smoke: Burning oil. Check oil level, worn rings or valve guides. 6) Engine surges: Air in fuel, clogged filter, faulty governor. 7) Pump loses prime: Air leak in suction, foot valve stuck, suction lift too high. 8) Low water output: Worn impeller, clogged suction, wrong speed. 9) Excessive vibration: Misalignment, worn bearings, unbalanced impeller. 10) Bearing overheating: Lack of grease, over-greasing, misalignment. 11) Seal leaks: Worn seal, replace. 12) Belt squeals: Loose or worn belt, tighten or replace. 13) Battery not charging: Loose belt, faulty alternator, bad connections. 14) Starter does not turn: Low battery, faulty solenoid, bad ignition switch. 15) Fuel in oil: Leaking injector, faulty fuel pump. For each problem, have a spare part ready. Keep a toolkit with wrenches, screwdrivers, pliers, a hammer, a feeler gauge, a multimeter, and a grease gun. Keep spare filters, belts, hoses, and oil. In remote areas, carry a spare pump seal and impeller. Learn to listen to your engine. A change in sound means a problem is developing. Fix small issues before they become big. Keep a logbook of all repairs. This helps you spot recurring problems. Train a local mechanic to assist you. Share knowledge with other farmers.

6. L6

Preventive maintenance saves money and downtime. Follow a schedule: Daily: check oil, coolant, fuel, air filter, belts, leaks. Weekly: clean radiator, check battery, grease bearings. Monthly: change oil and filter, check valve clearance, inspect hoses. Every 6 months: flush cooling system, replace fuel filter, check injection timing. Annually: overhaul pump, replace seals and bearings. Use a logbook to record hours and maintenance. Keep a stock of critical spares: oil filters, fuel filters, air filters, belts, hoses, clamps, grease, oil, coolant, pump seals, impellers, and gaskets. Store them in a clean, dry place. In dusty conditions, service air filter every 50 hours. In wet conditions, check for rust and water in fuel. Use clean fuel; filter it through a cloth before filling. Keep the engine clean. Dirt hides leaks and causes overheating. Wash the radiator fins with water and compressed air. Check the exhaust system for leaks. Tighten all bolts regularly. Vibration loosens bolts. Use lock washers or thread lock. Check the alignment of the pump and engine every 3 months. Misalignment causes bearing and seal failure. Train yourself and your workers on daily checks. Make a checklist and post it near the engine. Do not skip maintenance because you are busy. A breakdown during irrigation can kill your crops. Keep a spare pump or a backup engine if possible. Network with other farmers to share tools and spares. Attend training sessions. Read the engine manual. If you cannot find a manual, search online for your engine model. Many manuals are free. When you do maintenance, do it safely: turn off the engine, let it cool, and disconnect the battery. Use the right tools. Do not overtighten bolts; use a torque wrench. If you are unsure, ask for help. A well-maintained engine can run for 10,000 hours or more.

<h2>Practical Reference: Maintenance &amp; Troubleshooting for Converted Engine Water Pumps</h2>

<p>This lesson is the working reference you keep in the pump shed. It assumes you have already completed the conversion of a small petrol or diesel car engine (typically 1.01.8 L, e.g. Toyota 4K/5K, Nissan A-series, Peugeot XU, or a Kubota/Listr single-cylinder) into a stationary irrigation pump. Your goal is not mechanical perfection it is <strong>uptime during the growing season</strong>. Every hour the pump is down during a dry spell is money lost on a crop you have already paid to plant. Treat this module as a business tool: a maintained pump is a revenue asset; a neglected pump is a liability that fails exactly when demand (and price) for your produce is highest.</p>

<h3>1. The Eight Checkpoints (Map to the Diagram)</h3>
<p><strong>1. Air filter</strong> In dusty African conditions (Sahel, East African rangelands, southern African dry season), the air filter clogs in days, not weeks. A clogged filter richens the mixture on petrol engines and starves diesel engines of air, causing black smoke and power loss. Clean every 25 hours; replace every 200 hours or when torn.</p>
<p><strong>2. Engine oil</strong> Check daily before first start. Oil degrades faster on a stationary engine running at constant load than in a car. Change every 100150 hours or 3 months, whichever comes first.</p>
<p><strong>3. Coolant / radiator</strong> If your engine retains a radiator, keep it clean of chaff and dust. In hot climates, overheating is the number one killer of converted engines. Consider a larger radiator or a thermosiphon tank.</p>
<p><strong>4. Belt coupling</strong> The belt or direct coupling transmits torque to the pump. Check tension weekly; a slipping belt means reduced flow and burnt belts. Keep a spare belt on site.</p>
<p><strong>5. Pump bearing / gland seal</strong> The pump end is where water meets metal. A dripping gland is normal (a few drops per minute lubricates the packing); a stream means tighten or repack. Bearings should run cool.</p>
<p><strong>6. Impeller / volute</strong> Sand and silt from rivers and dams erode impellers. Inspect every 500 hours. A worn impeller loses head and flow your irrigation coverage drops.</p>
<p><strong>7. Suction strainer</strong> The most neglected item. A blocked strainer causes cavitation, which sounds like gravel in the pump and destroys impellers. Clean it every time you see flow drop.</p>
<p><strong>8. Discharge valve and pressure gauge</strong> Use the valve to throttle flow to match your irrigation set (e.g. drip lines need lower pressure than sprinklers). A gauge tells you when something is wrong before the crop does.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #c0392b;background:#fdecea;padding:14px 18px;margin:22px 0;border-radius:6px">
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;color:#c0392b;font-weight:bold;font-size:1.05em"> SAFETY WARNING CARBON MONOXIDE &amp; ROTATING PARTS</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0">Never run a converted car engine in an enclosed pump house, grain store, or shed. Petrol and diesel engines produce <strong>carbon monoxide (CO)</strong>, an invisible, odourless gas that kills. In many African farm settings, pumps are housed in small block structures to deter theft this is a lethal combination. <strong>Always vent exhaust outdoors, away from doors and windows, and never sleep or work in a closed pump room while the engine runs.</strong></p>
<p style="margin:0">Also: never adjust the belt, coupling, or gland while the engine is running. Isolate the drive, stop the engine, and let it cool before any hands-on work. Keep loose clothing, kangas, and headscarves away from rotating shafts.</p>
</div>

<h3>4. Spares and Consumables to Keep on Site</h3>
<p>Downtime is a business cost. Keep a locked spares box with: engine oil (2 changes), oil filter, air filter, fuel filter, spark plug or injector nozzle, drive belt (2), gland packing rope, suction strainer mesh, and basic tools (spanners, screwdrivers, feeler gauge, multimeter). In remote areas, a spare impeller and a spare starter battery are worth their weight in maize. Order parts <em>before</em> the dry season, not during it.</p>

<h3>5. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Before every start:</strong> oil level, coolant, fuel, air filter dust cup, suction strainer, belt tension, no leaks.</li>
<li><strong>Every 25 hours:</strong> clean air filter, check gland drip, inspect belt.</li>
<li><strong>Every 100 hours:</strong> change engine oil and filter, grease pump bearings, check coupling alignment.</li>
<li><strong>Every 200 hours:</strong> replace air filter, check plug/injector, clean radiator fins.</li>
<li><strong>Every 500 hours:</strong> inspect impeller and volute, repack gland, check discharge valve.</li>
<li><strong>Weekly:</strong> record running hours, flow rate, and fuel used this is your cost-per-cubic-metre figure for pricing irrigation services.</li>
<li><strong>Before each season:</strong> full strip-down, rust treatment, and restock of spares.</li>
<li><strong>Always:</strong> run engine outdoors or with forced ventilation; never in a closed pump house.</li>
</ul>
