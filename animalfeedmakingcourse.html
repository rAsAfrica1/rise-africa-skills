Animal Feed Making

A complete 10-module program covering the science of feed formulation and then going animal by animal — cattle, goats & sheep, pigs, broilers, layers, horses, dogs & cats, and fish — plus milling, mixing, quality control, storage and the business of selling feed to a market that buys every single day.

Course Overview

Why feed making, in depth? Feed is 60–75% of the total cost of producing any farmed animal. But "balanced feed" means something completely different for a lactating dairy cow, a growing broiler, a gestating sow, a working horse, a house cat, and a pond of tilapia — each has a different gut, a different growth curve, and a different set of nutrients it can and cannot digest. A feed maker who understands these differences can formulate correctly for every customer on the farm and every customer in the village, instead of selling one generic "animal feed" that under-serves most of them.

Structure: 10 modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen at the feed table, the mixer and the scale.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • A ration is only as good as its weakest ingredient. Mouldy maize, rancid fish meal or aflatoxin-contaminated groundnut cake will sicken or kill animals no matter how correct the formula looks on paper. Inspect every bag before it goes into the mix.
  • Weigh, don't guess — and never assume one species' feed suits another. Cattle, pigs, poultry, dogs, cats and fish all have different digestive systems and different nutrient tolerances. A ration correct for one species can injure or kill another (see the species notes in each module).
  • Some ingredients are outright dangerous to specific species. Chocolate, onion, grapes/raisins and xylitol are toxic to dogs; raw soybean is toxic to pigs and poultry unless heat-treated; ruminants fed excess urea or poultry litter can die of ammonia toxicity; cats fed a diet without taurine and without pre-formed vitamin A will develop severe, sometimes fatal, deficiency disease. These species-specific dangers are covered in full in their own modules below.
MODULE 1 — Nutrition Science Foundations ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Why formulate your own feed

  • Commercial feed carries the cost of the miller's ingredients, milling, packaging, transport and profit margin — often 20–40% above the raw ingredient cost of an equivalent home-mixed ration.
  • Home formulation lets you substitute whatever local ingredient is cheapest that week while keeping nutrient targets the same, and lets you formulate correctly for species commercial feed shops in your area may not stock at all (dog/cat, fish, horse).
  • A feed-making business that can correctly serve multiple species — not just poultry — reaches every kind of livestock keeper in a community, not just one segment of it.

Lesson 1.2 — The five nutrients every ration must balance

NutrientFunctionCommon sources
EnergyGrowth, movement, body heat, milk/egg productionMaize, cassava, broken rice, millers' bran, molasses, fats/oils
ProteinMuscle, feathers, milk, eggs — the most expensive nutrient, and the one that differs most sharply between speciesSoybean meal, fish meal, groundnut cake, sunflower cake, cotton seed cake, blood meal, meat & bone meal

Lesson 1.3 — Gut types: why one ration cannot fit every species

  • Ruminants (cattle, goats, sheep) have a four-chamber stomach with a rumen that ferments fibre using microbes, letting them extract energy from grass and crop residue that no other farmed species can use efficiently.
  • Monogastric omnivores (pigs, poultry) have a single simple stomach — they need denser, lower-fibre diets with all essential amino acids supplied directly, since they cannot ferment fibre for meaningful energy.
  • Hindgut fermenters (horses) ferment fibre in an enlarged caecum/colon after the stomach — they need continuous access to forage and are highly sensitive to sudden concentrate overload (a major cause of colic).
  • Obligate/strict carnivores (cats especially, and to a lesser extent dogs) have short digestive tracts built around meat-based nutrients and specific amino acids/vitamins that only come from animal tissue in usable form.
  • Fish are cold-blooded and use feed energy far more efficiently than land animals (no body heat to generate), but need floating or slow-sinking feed matched to feeding behaviour and much higher dietary protein than most land animals.

Lesson 1.4 — The Pearson Square and least-cost formulation, recapped

  • The Pearson Square cross-multiplies two ingredients (one energy-rich, one protein-rich) to hit a target CP% — write the target in the centre of an X, subtract diagonally, and the results give the parts of each ingredient needed.
  • Least-cost formulation means finding the cheapest combination of ingredients that still meets every nutrient target for that specific species and stage — compare cost per unit of protein delivered (price ÷ CP%), not just price per kg.
  • Every species module below gives you the target nutrient levels; the Pearson Square and least-cost method from this lesson is the tool you apply to hit them with whatever local ingredients you have.

MODULE 2 — Cattle: Dairy, Beef & Fattening Rations ▾

Lesson 2.1 — The rumen and forage-first feeding

  • Forage (pasture, hay, silage, crop residue) should always form the base of a cattle diet — the rumen needs adequate fibre length and bulk to function correctly; a concentrate-heavy diet without enough forage causes acidosis (rumen acid build-up), a serious and sometimes fatal condition.
  • Introduce any concentrate ration gradually over 7–10 days, never suddenly — the rumen's microbial population needs time to adapt to a change in diet composition.
  • Clean water access (a lactating dairy cow can drink 60–100+ litres/day) is as important as any formulated ingredient — water shortage drops both milk yield and feed intake fast.

Lesson 2.2 — Dairy cattle rations by lactation stage

StageTarget CP%Concentrate guide
Dry cow (pre-calving)12–14%Minimal concentrate; forage-based, avoid over-conditioning
Early lactation (peak yield)16–18%Highest concentrate feeding of the cycle — roughly 0.3–0.4 kg concentrate per litre above maintenance
Mid–late lactation14–16%Concentrate tapers as yield naturally declines
Calf starter (weaning)18–20%Introduced from about 3 weeks alongside milk, to develop rumen function early

Lesson 2.3 — Beef cattle: growing and finishing/fattening rations

  • Growing beef cattle (weaner to yearling) need a moderate-protein, moderate-energy ration (roughly 12–14% CP) built mainly on good pasture or hay plus modest concentrate to support steady frame growth without excess fat.
  • The finishing/fattening phase (final 60–120 days before slaughter) shifts to a higher-energy ration (increased maize/grain, reduced fibre proportion) to deposit fat and marbling that the market pays for — CP can actually drop slightly (10–13%) as energy density rises.
  • Fattening rations must still be introduced gradually from the growing ration — a sudden jump to high-grain feeding is a leading cause of acidosis and bloat in feedlot cattle.

Lesson 2.4 — Total Mixed Ration (TMR) and silage basics

  • A Total Mixed Ration combines all forage, concentrate, minerals and additives into one uniform mix fed together — this prevents cattle from sorting out and over-eating the tastiest concentrate portion while leaving fibre behind.
  • Silage (fermented, high-moisture forage stored airtight) extends forage availability through the dry season and, correctly made, retains more nutritional value than hay — but poorly sealed or spoiled silage can be toxic and must never be fed if mouldy or foul-smelling.
  • Mineral supplementation (calcium, phosphorus, salt, trace minerals) is essential year-round for cattle — deficiency symptoms (poor fertility, weak bones, reduced appetite) often appear only after weeks of shortage, making prevention far cheaper than correction.

MODULE 3 — Goats & Sheep: Small Ruminant Feeding & Fattening ▾

Lesson 3.1 — How goats and sheep differ from cattle

  • Both are ruminants with the same basic four-chamber digestive system as cattle, but at much smaller body size — rations are formulated per kg of body weight and total daily intake is far smaller, so small formulation errors have proportionally bigger effects.
  • Goats are natural browsers (preferring leaves, shrubs, twigs over ground-level grass) while sheep are natural grazers (preferring grass) — this affects pasture management and supplementary feeding strategy even though nutrient targets are similar.
  • Sheep are particularly sensitive to copper toxicity — mineral premix and mineral licks formulated for cattle or goats can contain copper levels that are dangerous for sheep; always use a sheep-appropriate or sheep-safe mineral product.

Lesson 3.2 — Maintenance and growth rations

ClassTarget CP%Notes
Maintenance (dry, non-productive adult)8–10%Good pasture/browse alone often sufficient
Growing kid/lamb14–16%Concentrate supplementation supports frame growth
Late pregnancy12–14%Nutrient demand rises sharply in the final 6 weeks (most fetal growth happens here)
Lactating doe/ewe14–16%Highest demand period; twin/multiple births raise requirement further

Lesson 3.3 — Fattening goats and sheep for market

  • The fattening/finishing period (typically the final 6–10 weeks before sale) shifts from a maintenance/growth ration to a higher-energy concentrate-supplemented ration to add market-desired weight and finish quickly.
  • Introduce fattening concentrate gradually, exactly as with cattle — small ruminants are equally vulnerable to acidosis and bloat from sudden high-grain feeding, arguably more so given their smaller rumen volume.
  • Flushing (increasing nutrition in the 2–3 weeks before mating) improves conception rates and litter size in does/ewes — a low-cost, high-return practice often skipped by smallholders.

Lesson 3.4 — Creep feeding and weaning

  • Creep feeding offers young kids/lambs access to a separate, high-quality starter ration (16–18% CP) that the mother cannot reach — this reduces weaning stress and improves post-weaning growth rates.
  • Wean gradually where possible rather than abruptly, and ensure the creep ration is already familiar to the young animal before full separation from the mother.
  • Provide clean water and free-choice mineral/salt lick at all life stages — small ruminants are highly susceptible to internal parasites, and good nutrition supports natural resistance alongside a proper deworming programme.

MODULE 4 — Pigs: Starter to Finisher, Gestation to Lactation ▾

Lesson 4.1 — The pig's digestive profile

  • Pigs are monogastric omnivores with a digestive system closer to a human's than to a ruminant's — they need dense, well-balanced diets and cannot extract meaningful energy from high-fibre feed the way cattle can.
  • Amino acid balance (particularly lysine, the first-limiting amino acid in most maize-based pig diets) matters as much as total CP% — a ration can hit the right CP% on paper and still underperform if lysine is short.
  • Raw soybean must never be fed to pigs — it contains a trypsin inhibitor that blocks protein digestion; soybean must be heat-treated (roasted or extruded) before inclusion.

Lesson 4.2 — Growth-stage rations

StageTarget CP%Notes
Creep/starter (weaning to ~15 kg)20–22%Highly digestible ingredients; young pigs have limited digestive capacity
Grower (15–60 kg)16–18%Lysine level is the key formulation checkpoint at this stage
Finisher (60 kg to market)14–16%Energy increases as protein need eases, building muscle and finishing fat

Lesson 4.3 — Gestating and lactating sows, and the boar

  • Gestating sows need a moderate ration (roughly 13–14% CP) with controlled energy — overfeeding during gestation causes excess fat that harms farrowing ease and subsequent milk production.
  • Lactating sows have the highest nutrient demand of any pig class — roughly 16–18% CP with high energy density, since milk production for a large litter draws heavily on the sow's own body reserves if the ration falls short.
  • Boars (breeding males) need a moderate-protein maintenance ration (roughly 14% CP) with attention to avoiding both under- and over-conditioning, both of which reduce fertility.

Lesson 4.4 — Common pig feeding mistakes

  • Overfeeding cotton seed cake — gossypol toxicity is a real risk for pigs at high inclusion levels; keep cotton seed cake to a small proportion of the ration or avoid it for young/breeding stock.
  • Feeding spoiled or mouldy feed — pigs are notably susceptible to mycotoxin-related reproductive problems (reduced litter size, early farrowing) from contaminated grain, making ingredient inspection especially important for breeding stock.
  • Skipping the transition between growth stages — an abrupt ration change (especially at weaning) causes digestive upset; blend the old and new ration gradually over several days at every stage transition.

MODULE 5 — Poultry: Broilers, Layers & Growers ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Why broilers and layers need completely different feed

  • A broiler is bred and fed for maximum growth rate over a short 5–8 week life — its ration is high-energy, high-protein throughout, phased to match its rapid growth curve.
  • A layer is fed for a long productive life (12+ months of laying) — its ration prioritises steady body maintenance and calcium supply for eggshell over maximum growth, since a layer that grows too fast or too fat lays fewer eggs.
  • Feeding a broiler ration to layers causes excess fat and reduced laying; feeding a layer ration to broilers stunts growth and wastes the genetic potential paid for in the chicks.

Lesson 5.2 — Broiler phase feeding

PhaseAgeTarget CP%
Starter0–2 weeks22–23%
Grower2–4 weeks20–21%
Finisher4 weeks to slaughter18–19%

Energy density rises through the phases as protein need eases slightly — finisher rations are built for rapid weight gain in the final weeks, when feed conversion efficiency matters most to the farmer's margin.

Lesson 5.3 — Layer and pullet-grower feeding

PhaseAge/stageTarget CP%Notes
Chick starter0–6 weeks20–22%Similar to broiler starter
Pullet grower6–18 weeks15–16%Deliberately lower energy — controls growth rate and body fat ahead of lay
Pre-lay16–18 weeks (approaching point of lay)17–18%Calcium begins rising ahead of first egg
Layer mashPoint of lay onward16–18%Calcium raised to 3.5–4% for eggshell strength — this is the single biggest formulation difference from any other poultry ration

Lesson 5.4 — Common poultry feeding mistakes

  • Underfeeding calcium to layers — produces thin-shelled or shell-less eggs, and over time draws calcium from the hen's own skeleton, weakening bones.
  • Overfeeding calcium to growing broilers or pullets before point of lay — excess calcium in young growing birds can damage developing kidneys; calcium levels must be matched to the correct phase, never applied as a flat rate across all poultry.
  • Switching feed phases too early or too late — following age/weight benchmarks rather than "the bag is empty so we'll buy whatever's available" keeps growth and laying performance on target.

MODULE 6 — Horses: Forage-First Feeding ▾

Lesson 6.1 — The horse as a hindgut fermenter

  • Horses ferment fibre in an enlarged caecum and colon after a relatively small stomach — they are built to eat small amounts of forage almost continuously, not two or three large meals a day.
  • Forage (pasture or hay) should make up at least 1.5–2% of a horse's body weight per day as a baseline, with concentrate added only to meet energy needs forage alone can't supply for the animal's workload.
  • Sudden access to a large amount of grain/concentrate, or to lush spring pasture, is a leading cause of colic and laminitis — both serious, sometimes fatal, conditions in horses that a careful feeding routine largely prevents.

Lesson 6.2 — Matching ration to workload

Work levelTarget CP%Notes
Maintenance/idle8–10%Good forage alone is often sufficient
Light work10–12%Small concentrate addition
Moderate–heavy work12–14%Concentrate rises with energy demand, not just protein
Broodmare (late pregnancy/lactation)12–14%Energy and calcium demand rise sharply in late pregnancy and through lactation
Growing foal14–16%Carefully controlled growth rate — overfeeding young horses causes developmental bone problems

Lesson 6.3 — Feeding practices that prevent problems

  • Feed concentrate in small, multiple portions rather than one large meal — a horse's stomach is relatively small for its body size, and large single meals overwhelm digestion.
  • Introduce any new feed (new hay batch, new concentrate, new pasture access) gradually over 7–10 days — the hindgut microbial population needs time to adjust, exactly as with a ruminant's rumen.
  • Never feed mouldy hay or grain — horses are highly sensitive to certain moulds and mycotoxins, with respiratory and digestive consequences that can be severe.

Lesson 6.4 — Water, salt and minerals

  • Clean water must be available at all times — a horse can drink 20–40+ litres per day, more with hard work or hot weather, and water restriction directly increases colic risk.
  • Provide a salt lick or loose salt year-round — horses in work sweat out significant sodium and won't necessarily compensate through diet alone.
  • Balance calcium and phosphorus carefully (roughly 1.5–2:1 calcium to phosphorus is a common target) — an inverted ratio over time causes serious bone problems, particularly in growing and working horses.

MODULE 7 — Dogs & Cats: Companion Animal Nutrition ▾

Lesson 7.1 — Dogs: omnivores with real protein needs

  • Dogs are omnivores that thrive on a mixed diet, but still need a meaningfully higher protein level than most people assume — roughly 18–25% CP for an adult, higher (25–30%) for puppies, pregnant/lactating females, and working dogs.
  • Good quality protein sources (meat meal, fish meal, eggs) matter more than raw protein percentage alone — poor-quality protein sources may hit a CP% target on paper while still leaving the dog short of essential amino acids.
  • Fat provides concentrated energy and supports coat/skin health — roughly 8–15% fat is typical for adult maintenance, higher for working or lactating dogs.

Lesson 7.2 — Cats: obligate carnivores with unique requirements

  • Cats are obligate (strict) carnivores — unlike dogs, they cannot thrive on a plant-based or low-meat diet and have specific nutrient needs that only reliably come from animal tissue.
  • Taurine (an amino acid found almost exclusively in animal tissue) is essential for cats — deficiency causes serious heart disease and blindness over time; a cat fed a dog-food-style or plant-heavy diet will become taurine deficient.
  • Cats also need pre-formed vitamin A (they cannot convert plant beta-carotene to vitamin A the way dogs and many other species can) and arachidonic acid (an essential fatty acid found in animal fat) — both must come from animal-source ingredients in the diet.
  • Target roughly 26–30% CP for adult cat maintenance, higher for kittens — cat rations should never be formulated using a dog or generic livestock formula as the base.

Senior/less active16–20%26–28%

Lesson 7.4 — Foods and ingredients to avoid entirely

  • Toxic to dogs and cats: chocolate/cocoa, onions and garlic (in meaningful quantity), grapes and raisins, xylitol (an artificial sweetener), and alcohol — these should never appear in any homemade or commercial ration for either species.
  • Cooked bones (especially poultry) can splinter and cause serious internal injury — raw bones are generally safer than cooked, but bone feeding in general should follow careful, size-appropriate guidance rather than assumption.
  • Excess raw fat trimmings and spoiled/rancid meat scraps commonly cause digestive upset or pancreatitis — kitchen scraps intended for pet feed must be assessed with the same food-safety standard as feed for any other species, not treated as "waste that's fine for the dog."

MODULE 8 — Fish & Aquaculture Feeding ▾

Lesson 8.1 — Why fish feed differs from land-animal feed

  • Fish are cold-blooded (ectothermic) and don't spend energy maintaining body temperature — this makes them among the most feed-efficient farmed animals, converting feed to body weight better than most land livestock.
  • Fish need much higher dietary protein than land animals at equivalent life stage — a tilapia grower ration commonly targets 28–32% CP, and carnivorous species (catfish, especially at fry stage) need even more.
  • Feed must be formulated to float or sink at the correct rate for the target species' feeding behaviour — tilapia generally feed at or near the surface, while catfish often feed lower in the water column; a mismatched feed form is wasted feed that pollutes the water instead of being eaten.

Lesson 8.2 — Life-stage feeding for tilapia and catfish

StageTarget CP%Notes
Fry (first weeks)35–40%Highest protein requirement of any stage; small, easily ingested particle size essential
Fingerling30–35%Transition toward grower ration as fish size increases
Grower28–32%Bulk of the growth period
Broodstock30–35%Higher protein/energy supports reproduction and egg quality

Lesson 8.3 — Feeding rate and water quality

  • Feed is typically dosed as a percentage of estimated total pond/tank body weight per day (commonly 3–5% for growing fish, tapering as fish approach market size) rather than a fixed daily amount — this requires periodically estimating fish biomass.
  • Overfeeding is a major and common mistake in fish farming — uneaten feed decomposes in the water, consuming oxygen and degrading water quality, which can cause a mass mortality event far more damaging than any single feed cost saved by underfeeding would be worth avoiding.
  • Feed multiple small portions per day rather than one large feeding where practical — this improves feed conversion and reduces the amount of any single feeding that goes uneaten.

Lesson 8.4 — Local ingredients and pellet-making basics

  • Fish meal, blood meal, and locally available protein sources (silverfish/small dried fish, insect meal where accessible, soybean meal) supply the high protein fish feed demands — fish meal is often the single largest cost item in a fish ration and the one most worth price-shopping.
  • A simple pellet mill or extruder is needed to produce a stable, water-durable pellet — loose mash disintegrates in water before fish can eat it, wasting most of the nutritional value and fouling the water.
  • Dry and store finished fish feed carefully — fish feed with its high protein and often some oil content is particularly prone to spoilage and rancidity if stored damp or too long.

MODULE 9 — Milling, Mixing & Quality Control ▾

Lesson 9.1 — Milling ingredients

  • Whole grains must be milled before mixing — animals cannot digest whole maize efficiently, and uneven particle size causes uneven nutrient intake within the same batch.
  • Target particle size varies by species: coarser for pigs and cattle, finer for poultry mash, fine crumble for young quail/fish fry, and pelleted for fish and some pet food to prevent water disintegration or ensure complete nutrition per bite.
  • A shared community hammer mill is often the most practical entry point — full mill ownership only pays for itself at real volume across multiple species/customers.

Lesson 9.2 — Mixing correctly

  • Mixing order matters: largest-volume ingredient first, progressively smaller ones after, ending with the smallest (premix, medication) — this "dilution" approach avoids clumping and uneven distribution.
  • Premix must first be pre-mixed into a small batch of the base ingredient (a "carrier") before being added to the full batch — sprinkling a tiny quantity directly into a large mix gives some animals none and others too much.
  • Mix for the full recommended time (a drum or paddle mixer typically 8–15 minutes) — under-mixing leaves pockets of unbalanced feed, which matters even more for species-specific batches where cross-contamination between species runs (e.g. cattle mineral into a cat batch) must also be avoided.

Lesson 9.3 — Species cross-contamination and dedicated equipment

  • Running multiple species' feed through the same equipment risks cross-contamination — cattle mineral/medicated feed residue in a pet or fish batch, or ruminant-derived meat and bone meal residue in cattle feed (a known disease transmission risk in some countries), are real hazards, not theoretical ones.
  • Where possible, dedicate a cleaning step (full sweep-out and wash) between different species' batches, and sequence production species-appropriately — clean, low-risk batches (e.g. plain grain-based rations) before medicated or animal-protein-heavy batches where cross-contamination risk is highest.
  • Label and store finished batches by species clearly and separately — a mix-up at the storage stage is as costly as a mix-up during mixing itself.

Lesson 9.4 — Quality control checks

  • Smell every batch of protein ingredients before use — rancid or musty smell means spoilage or fungal growth; discard rather than risk aflatoxin poisoning, which affects every species covered in this course.
  • Check moisture: ingredients should feel dry, not damp — mix moisture above about 12–13% risks mould growth in storage.
  • Keep a simple batch record per species: date, ingredients, quantities, and buyer — this protects you if an animal health problem is ever traced back and builds trust with repeat customers across every species you serve.

MODULE 10 — Storage, Costing & the Multi-Species Feed Business ▾

Lesson 10.1 — Storing finished feed by species

  • Store bagged feed off the ground on pallets, away from walls, in a dry, ventilated, rodent-proof store — damp floors and walls wick moisture into bags regardless of which species the feed is for.
  • High-fat feeds (fish feed, some pet food) go rancid faster than plain grain-based livestock feed — aim to sell or use within 2–4 weeks of mixing, especially in hot climates, and communicate this shelf life clearly to customers.
  • Label every bag with species, life stage, and mix date — feed bought without a clear species/stage label is a common source of costly misuse and customer disputes.

Lesson 10.2 — Costing across species

  • Cost = sum of (ingredient weight × price/kg) for every ingredient, plus milling cost, plus your labour and a margin — factor in the extra care (cleaning, dedicated batches) that multi-species production requires, since it's real cost even without an extra ingredient.
  • Compare true cost per kg against local commercial feed price for each species you serve — pet food and fish feed often command a bigger margin over ingredient cost than commodity livestock feed, since fewer local competitors formulate them correctly.
  • Track cost per kg per species over time as ingredient prices move — protein sources (soybean meal, fish meal) tend to be the most volatile cost line across every species ration in this course.

Lesson 10.3 — Building a multi-species feed business

  • Start with the species you know best or have the readiest market for, build confidence and a track record, then expand into adjacent species — a customer buying poultry feed from you is a natural prospect for pig, cattle or pet feed if you can formulate it correctly.
  • Offer a few standard formulations per species (e.g. broiler starter, layer mash, pig grower, adult dog) rather than infinite custom recipes — standard products are faster to mix, cost, and market, while still covering the range this course teaches.
  • Differentiation from generic feed sellers is exactly the depth this course provides — most informal feed sellers in many markets only really understand poultry feed; correctly serving cattle, small ruminants, pigs, horses, pets and fish as well is a genuine competitive advantage.

Lesson 10.4 — Final project: your bankable multi-species business plan

  • Assemble: your local ingredient price list, costed standard formulations for at least 4 different species/classes covered in this course (with CP%, cost/kg, target stage), your milling/mixing equipment and cross-contamination prevention plan, storage plan by species, quality-control checklist, target customer list by species with expected volumes, and a 3-month growth path.
  • Submit with your batch records and photos for your rAs certificate — multi-species feed businesses that document formulation accuracy and contamination prevention across species are exactly what serious commercial buyers, veterinary services and lenders look for.

📖 Glossary

  • CP% (crude protein) — the standard label measure of protein content, estimated from nitrogen.
  • Pearson Square — a diagonal-subtraction method for mixing two ingredients to hit a target CP%.
  • Ruminant — an animal (cattle, goat, sheep) with a multi-chamber stomach that ferments fibre via a rumen.
  • Monogastric — an animal (pig, poultry) with a single simple stomach, unable to ferment fibre for meaningful energy.
  • Hindgut fermenter — an animal (horse) that ferments fibre after the stomach, in an enlarged caecum/colon.
  • Obligate carnivore — an animal (cat) that cannot thrive without animal-source nutrients such as taurine.
  • Taurine — an amino acid essential for cats, found almost exclusively in animal tissue.
  • Lysine — the first-limiting amino acid in most maize-based pig and poultry diets.
  • TMR (Total Mixed Ration) — all forage, concentrate and additives combined into one uniform cattle feed.
  • Flushing — increasing nutrition before mating to improve conception rate and litter size.
  • Creep feeding — offering young animals access to starter feed the mother cannot reach.
  • Acidosis — dangerous rumen/hindgut acid build-up from too much concentrate too fast.
  • Aflatoxin — a mould toxin on grains/oilseed cake, dangerous across every species in this course.
