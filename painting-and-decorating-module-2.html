1. Surface Preparation for African Climates

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on the single most important step in any painting or decorating job: surface preparation. In Africa, this step is more critical than almost anywhere else because our surfaces face intense sun, heavy seasonal rains, dust, and in many areas high humidity. A paint job that fails in Europe after ten years can fail in Africa within one rainy season if the surface was not prepared correctly. This lesson gives you a practical, business-first approach to preparing walls, wood, and metal so your work lasts and your reputation grows. First, understand your substrate. In most African towns and villages you will meet four main surfaces: cement and plaster, mud or adobe, timber, and metal. Each behaves differently. Cement and plaster are porous and alkaline. Mud walls are soft, dusty, and sensitive to water. Timber expands and contracts with heat and moisture. Metal rusts fast in coastal and humid regions. Your first job on any site is to identify the substrate and test it. Carry a small spray bottle of water. Spray a wall. If water soaks in quickly, the surface is porous and will need a sealer or primer. If it beads up, there may be old oil paint or a glossy coating that needs sanding. Scratch a small hidden area with a blade to see how many old coats exist. Check for damp patches, salt deposits, and mould. In coastal cities like Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, or Lagos, salt in the air attacks metal and paint films. Inland, the harmattan dust in West Africa and the dry winds in Southern Africa dry out timber and crack plaster. Your pricing must reflect these realities. Never quote a job before you inspect the surface. Many decorators lose money because they assumed the wall was sound and then spent three extra days filling cracks. Build a surface inspection into your quotation process. Charge for preparation as a separate line item. Clients respect transparency and you protect your margin. Now let us go through the steps. Step one is cleaning. Remove dust, grease, mould, and loose particles. In dusty areas, sweep and then wipe with a damp cloth. For greasy kitchen walls, use warm water with a little dish soap or a degreaser. For mould, use a diluted bleach solution, one part bleach to three parts water, and let it dry completely. Always wear gloves and ventilate the room. Step two is repairing. Fill cracks and holes with filler or a cement-based patching compound. For deep cracks in plaster, widen the crack slightly, dust it out, dampen it, and fill in layers. Do not fill a deep crack in one go or it will shrink and crack again. For mud walls, use a mixture of clay, sand, and a little cement or cow dung in traditional settings, and let it cure slowly. Step three is sanding. Sand rough patches, old paint edges, and glossy surfaces. In Africa, sandpaper can be expensive, so teach your team to use it efficiently. Use coarse paper for shaping filler and fine paper for finishing. Step four is priming. Primer is not optional. It seals porous surfaces, improves adhesion, and reduces the amount of topcoat you need. On alkaline cement, use an alkali-resistant primer. On timber, use an oil-based or shellac primer to block knots. On metal, use a rust-inhibiting primer. Step five is protecting the work area. Mask edges, cover floors, and remove or cover furniture. In many African homes, families live in the space while you work, so plan your day to minimise disruption. Now let us talk about tools and materials available locally. You do not need imported products to do excellent work. Local filler, local primer, and locally manufactured paint can perform well if used correctly. Build relationships with hardware suppliers in your area. Ask them what contractors are buying and why. Keep a simple inventory: scrapers, wire brushes, sanding blocks, filler knives, masking tape, drop cloths, buckets, and clean rags. In hot climates, work early in the morning or late afternoon. Paint dries too fast in direct midday sun, leaving brush marks and poor adhesion. On exterior work, follow the shade around the building. Never paint in the rain or when rain is expected within a few hours. Check the forecast and plan around the rainy season. For your business, preparation is where you can differentiate yourself. Many competitors skip steps. You should document your preparation with before-and-after photos. Show clients the cracks you filled and the mould you treated. This justifies your price and builds trust. Offer a written guarantee that covers preparation-related failures, such as peeling or blistering, for a defined period, for example twelve months. Keep records of the products you used and the date of application. If a problem appears, you can diagnose it quickly and fix it without arguing. Finally, train your team. Hold a short toolbox talk every morning. Demonstrate the correct way to fill a crack or treat mould. Give each worker a specific responsibility. Quality comes from consistency, not from luck. In summary, surface preparation in Africa requires you to respect the climate, inspect carefully, clean thoroughly, repair properly, prime always, and protect your work area. Do this and your paint will last, your clients will refer you, and your business will grow. In the next lesson we will look at choosing the right paint systems for different African conditions.

2. Choosing Paints and Coatings for African Conditions

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 2. Now that you understand surface preparation, we turn to the products themselves. Choosing the right paint is a business decision as much as a technical one. The wrong choice leads to callbacks, refunds, and damaged reputation. The right choice leads to repeat clients and profitable referrals. In Africa, you must balance performance, price, availability, and local climate. This lesson gives you a practical framework for selecting paints and coatings for walls, wood, and metal in African conditions. Start with the binder. Paint is made of binder, pigment, solvent, and additives. The binder determines how the paint performs. In water-based paints, the binder is usually acrylic or vinyl. In solvent-based paints, it is alkyd or polyurethane. Acrylic paints are excellent for exterior walls in Africa because they resist ultraviolet light and do not chalk as quickly as cheaper binders. Vinyl and emulsion paints are cheaper and fine for interior walls in dry areas, but they can fail in humid coastal regions. Alkyd paints are tough and washable, good for doors, windows, and trim, but they yellow and become brittle under strong sun. Polyurethane and epoxy coatings are more expensive but give superior protection on metal and floors. Understand the difference between interior and exterior paints. Exterior paints contain more binder, more UV stabilisers, and fungicides. Never use interior paint outside. It will fail within months. In many African markets, sellers may not label products clearly. Ask for the technical data sheet. If none exists, ask other contractors about the product performance. Build a shortlist of trusted brands available in your area. Now consider the climate zones. Coastal areas such as Accra, Mombasa, and Durban have high humidity and salt. Here you need paints with good mould resistance and metal coatings with strong rust inhibition. Use acrylic exterior paint with fungicide, and for metal use a zinc-rich primer followed by a polyurethane topcoat. Inland savannah areas such as Abuja, Harare, and Gaborone have intense sun and large day-night temperature swings. Paints must be flexible and UV resistant. Acrylic and silicone-modified paints perform well. In desert and semi-arid areas such as northern Kenya, Niger, and Namibia, dust and heat dominate. Choose paints that resist chalking and can be cleaned easily. In tropical rainforest zones such as the Congo Basin and coastal Cameroon, rain and humidity are constant. Fungal growth is a major problem. Use paints with added fungicide and ensure good ventilation during application. Next, understand sheen levels. Matt or flat paint hides surface imperfections and is popular for interior walls. It is less washable. Silk or satin paint is more washable and is good for kitchens, corridors, and children rooms. Gloss paint is durable and easy to clean but shows every bump. Use gloss on wood and metal trim, and on doors. In many African homes, gloss is used on walls because it is easy to wipe, but it can look cheap if the surface is not perfectly smooth. Advise clients honestly. Now let us talk about specific products. For interior walls, a quality acrylic emulsion is a safe choice. For exterior walls, use a 100 percent acrylic exterior paint or a textured coating where cracking is a risk. For wood, use an alkyd or water-based wood primer followed by an undercoat and a gloss or satin topcoat. For metal, use a rust-inhibiting primer, then an undercoat, then a gloss or polyurethane topcoat. For roofs, use specialised roof paints that reflect heat and resist expansion. For floors, use epoxy or polyurethane floor coatings. Always check compatibility. You cannot put a solvent-based paint over a water-based paint without testing. You cannot put a hard paint over a soft paint. If in doubt, test a small area and wait a week. Now consider cost. Cheap paint is expensive in the long run. It covers poorly, fades fast, and needs more coats. Calculate the cost per square metre per year of service, not just the price per litre. A paint that costs twice as much but lasts three times longer is better value. Explain this to clients using simple numbers. For example, if a cheap paint costs 5,000 shillings per bucket and lasts one year, and a quality paint costs 9,000 shillings and lasts three years, the quality paint costs 3,000 shillings per year versus 5,000. Clients understand this. Availability matters. In many African towns, the best paint may not be in stock. Build relationships with suppliers and keep a small stock of trusted products. If you must use an unfamiliar brand, test it first. Buy one bucket, paint a sample board, and leave it outside for a month. Check for fading, cracking, and adhesion. Now let us talk about colour. Colour choice is emotional and cultural. In many African markets, bright colours are popular for exteriors, while interiors often use warm neutrals. Offer a colour consultation as a paid service. Use colour fans and sample pots. Paint large samples on the actual wall, not on paper, because light changes colour. In hot climates, light colours reflect heat and keep buildings cooler. Dark colours absorb heat and can cause paint to fail faster. Advise clients accordingly. For your business, create a simple product guide. List the paints you recommend for each substrate and climate. Include the brand, the sheen, the number of coats, and the drying time. Train your team to follow it. This reduces mistakes and speeds up quoting. Also track your paint usage. Record how many litres you use per square metre for each product. This helps you quote accurately and avoid waste. Finally, think about health and safety. Many solvent-based paints contain harmful fumes. In enclosed spaces, ensure ventilation and use respirators. Water-based paints are safer but can still cause skin irritation. Provide gloves and eye protection. Dispose of paint waste responsibly. Do not pour paint or solvents into drains or onto the ground. In many African communities, water sources are shared, so pollution affects everyone. In summary, choosing the right paint means matching the product to the substrate, the climate, the sheen requirement, and the client budget. Use acrylics for exteriors, alkyds for trim, and specialised coatings for metal and floors. Test unfamiliar products. Calculate cost per year, not per litre. Train your team and keep records. Do this and your paint jobs will last, your clients will be satisfied, and your business will be profitable. In the next lesson we will cover application techniques for professional results.

3. Professional Application Techniques

Welcome to Lesson 3 of Module 2. You have prepared the surface and selected the right paint. Now you must apply it correctly. Application is where skill shows. A well-applied coat looks smooth, covers fully, and lasts. A poorly applied coat shows brush marks, drips, and uneven colour. In Africa, application is also about working smart in heat, dust, and sometimes difficult access. This lesson covers brushes, rollers, sprayers, and the techniques that give professional results. Let us start with tools. Brushes come in different shapes and bristle types. For water-based paints, use synthetic bristles. For solvent-based paints, use natural bristles. A good brush holds paint well and leaves a smooth finish. Cheap brushes shed bristles and leave streaks. Buy the best brushes you can afford and clean them after every use. Rollers are faster than brushes for large areas. Use a short-pile roller for smooth walls and a medium-pile roller for textured walls. A long-pile roller holds more paint but can leave a stippled finish. For gloss paints, use a foam roller or a short-pile roller for a smooth finish. Sprayers are fast and give a fine finish, but they require masking and ventilation. In many African settings, sprayers are used for large exterior jobs and for furniture. If you use a sprayer, thin the paint according to the manufacturer instructions and keep the nozzle clean. Now let us go through the basic techniques. First, loading the brush. Dip only the bottom third of the bristles into the paint. Tap the brush against the side of the container to remove excess. Do not wipe it on the rim, as this wastes paint and creates mess. Second, cutting in. Use a brush to paint a strip about 5 to 8 centimetres wide along edges, corners, and ceilings. This is called cutting in. Do this before rolling the main area. Keep a steady hand and use a guide if needed. Third, rolling. Pour paint into a roller tray. Roll the roller into the paint and then roll it back and forth on the tray ramp to distribute the paint evenly. Apply the paint in a zigzag pattern, then roll over it in straight lines to smooth it out. Work in sections about one metre square. Do not let the edges dry before you blend them. Fourth, laying off. After rolling, use a dry brush or roller to lightly go over the surface in one direction. This removes roller marks and gives a uniform finish. Fifth, two coats. Almost all paints need two coats. The first coat may look patchy. That is normal. Wait for it to dry, then apply the second coat. In hot climates, the first coat may dry in thirty minutes. In humid climates, it may take several hours. Check the paint label and test with your finger. If it is tacky, wait. Now let us talk about specific surfaces. For ceilings, use a roller with an extension pole. Work in small sections and avoid overloading the roller, which causes drips. For walls, start at the top and work down. For doors, remove them if possible and paint them flat. Paint the edges first, then the panels, then the stiles and rails. For windows, mask the glass or use a paint shield. Do not paint over the moving parts. For metal, apply thin coats to avoid runs. For wood, sand lightly between coats to remove raised grain. Now let us talk about working in African conditions. Heat makes paint dry fast. Work in the early morning or late afternoon. On exterior walls, follow the shade. If you are painting a large building, start on the shady side and move as the sun moves. Do not paint in direct sun if you can avoid it. Wind blows dust and insects onto wet paint. If it is windy, work on the sheltered side or wait. In dusty areas, dampen the ground around the building to settle dust. Rain is a major risk. Check the forecast. If rain is expected within four hours, do not start an exterior coat. If rain catches you, stop and let the surface dry completely before continuing. Humidity affects drying. In coastal areas, paint may take twice as long to dry. Be patient. Do not apply a second coat too soon. Now let us talk about quality control. Check your work as you go. Look for runs, drips, missed spots, and uneven coverage. Fix them immediately while the paint is wet. Use a lamp or torch to check surfaces at an angle. This reveals imperfections that you cannot see in normal light. Keep a rag and a small brush for touch-ups. At the end of each day, clean your tools. Water-based paint cleans with warm soapy water. Solvent-based paint needs white spirit or turpentine. Clean brushes thoroughly and reshape the bristles. Store rollers upright to avoid flat spots. Now let us talk about productivity. Time is money. Organise your team so that one person cuts in while another rolls. Use the right size tool for the job. A small brush on a large wall wastes time. A large roller in a small room wastes paint. Plan your sequence: ceilings first, then walls, then trim, then doors. This avoids redoing work. Protect floors and furniture before you start. Masking tape saves time in the long run. Now let us talk about client communication. Before you start, confirm the colour, the sheen, and the number of coats. Show the client a sample on the wall. Explain that the first coat may look different from the final finish. Keep the client informed about drying times and when they can use the room. In many African homes, families need access to rooms. Plan your work to minimise disruption. For example, paint one room at a time and let it dry before moving to the next. Finally, let us talk about safety. Paint fumes can be harmful. Ventilate rooms. Use a respirator when spraying or using solvent-based paints. Wear gloves and eye protection. Keep paint away from children and animals. Store paint in a cool, dry place away from food. Dispose of empty containers responsibly. Do not burn them, as the fumes are toxic. In summary, professional application requires the right tools, the right technique, and respect for the climate. Cut in, roll, lay off, and apply two coats. Work in the cool hours, protect your work, and check quality as you go. Train your team, plan your sequence, and communicate with clients. Do this and your work will look professional, last longer, and earn you more referrals. In the next lesson we will cover special finishes and decorative techniques that can increase your income.

4. Special Finishes and Decorative Techniques

Welcome to Lesson 4 of Module 2. Basic painting pays the bills, but special finishes and decorative techniques can increase your income significantly. In Africa, clients increasingly want more than a plain wall. They want texture, colour, and character. Decorative finishes allow you to charge more per square metre and differentiate yourself from cheap competitors. This lesson covers practical decorative techniques that work in African conditions and can be done with locally available materials. First, let us talk about textured finishes. Texture hides imperfections and adds depth. Common textures include stipple, roller texture, and trowel texture. Stipple is created by dabbing a brush or sponge onto wet paint. Roller texture is created with a textured roller. Trowel texture is created with a trowel and a joint compound or specialised texture product. In many African markets, textured walls are popular for exteriors because they hide cracks and reduce glare. To create a simple texture, apply a base coat and let it dry. Then apply a second coat and, while it is wet, use your tool to create the pattern. Work in small sections and keep the pattern consistent. Practice on a board first. Second, let us talk about sponging. Sponging creates a mottled effect. You need two or three colours that blend well. Apply a base coat and let it dry. Dampen a natural sponge, dip it in the second colour, and dab it onto the wall. Use a light touch and vary the pressure. Build up the colour gradually. Sponging is popular for interior feature walls. Third, let us talk about ragging. Ragging is similar to sponging but uses a cloth. Roll the cloth into a loose ball, dip it in paint, and dab it onto the wall. The result is softer than sponging. Use ragging for a subtle, elegant finish. Fourth, let us talk about colour washing. Colour washing creates a soft, cloudy effect. Mix a glaze with your chosen colour. Apply the glaze with a brush or roller, then use a dry brush or rag to soften the edges. Work in small sections and blend while wet. Colour washing is popular for bedrooms and living rooms. Fifth, let us talk about stencilling. Stencils are reusable patterns. You can buy stencils or make your own from plastic sheets. Tape the stencil to the wall, apply paint with a stencil brush or a small roller, and remove the stencil carefully. Stencilling is popular for borders, feature walls, and children rooms. In many African markets, stencils with traditional patterns are in demand. Sixth, let us talk about faux finishes. Faux finishes imitate materials such as marble, wood, and stone. They require skill and practice. Start with a base coat, then apply layers of glaze and use tools such as feathers, sponges, and brushes to create the pattern. Faux marble is popular for columns and feature walls. Faux wood is popular for doors and furniture. Seventh, let us talk about metallic finishes. Metallic paints contain metal particles and create a shiny, reflective surface. They are popular for feature walls, furniture, and trim. Apply a base coat of a similar colour, then apply the metallic paint in thin coats. Use a soft brush to avoid brush marks. Metallic finishes are popular in hotels, restaurants, and upmarket homes. Eighth, let us talk about lime wash and clay paint. These traditional finishes are gaining popularity because they are natural and breathable. Lime wash is made from lime, water, and pigment. It is applied in thin coats and creates a soft, matte finish. Clay paint is made from clay, water, and pigment. It is popular for eco-friendly buildings. In Africa, these finishes are suitable for mud walls and natural buildings. Ninth, let us talk about murals. Murals are large-scale paintings that tell a story or decorate a space. They are popular for schools, hospitals, restaurants, and community centres. Murals require artistic skill and careful planning. Charge by the square metre or by the project. Use exterior acrylic paints for outdoor murals and interior acrylics for indoor murals. Protect the mural with a clear coat if it is in a high-traffic area. Tenth, let us talk about polished plaster. Polished plaster, also known as Venetian plaster, is a decorative finish that looks like marble. It is applied in thin layers with a trowel and polished to a shine. It is expensive but highly profitable. It is popular in upmarket hotels and homes. It requires training and practice. Now let us talk about pricing. Decorative finishes take more time and skill than plain painting. Charge accordingly. A plain wall might earn you a certain amount per square metre, but a textured or faux finish can earn two to five times more. Calculate your material cost, your labour time, and your skill premium. Offer a sample board so the client can see the finish before you start. Get written approval on the sample. This avoids disputes later. Now let us talk about marketing. Take photos of your decorative work. Post them on social media, WhatsApp, and local business groups. Create a portfolio. Offer a free consultation where you show samples and discuss options. Partner with interior designers, architects, and furniture makers. They can refer clients to you. Offer a small commission for referrals. Now let us talk about training. Decorative finishes require practice. Set aside time each week to practice on boards. Watch videos, read books, and attend workshops when possible. Teach your team the basic techniques. Assign one person to be your decorative specialist. This person can handle the more complex jobs while you focus on business development. Finally, let us talk about quality and durability. Decorative finishes must last. Use quality products and follow the manufacturer instructions. Test the finish in a small area before applying it to the whole wall. Check for adhesion, fading, and cracking after a few weeks. If a finish fails, fix it quickly and learn from the mistake. In summary, special finishes and decorative techniques can transform your business. Textures, sponging, ragging, colour washing, stencilling, faux finishes, metallic finishes, lime wash, murals, and polished plaster are all viable in African markets. Practice, price correctly, market your work, and train your team. Do this and you will attract higher-paying clients and grow your reputation as a skilled decorator. In the next lesson we will cover business management, pricing, and customer care for painting and decorating contractors.

5. Business Management, Pricing, and Customer Care

Welcome to Lesson 5 of Module 2. You now have the technical skills to prepare surfaces, choose paints, apply them professionally, and create decorative finishes. But technical skill alone does not build a successful business. You must manage money, price correctly, find clients, and keep them happy. This lesson covers the business side of painting and decorating in Africa. It is practical, direct, and designed to help you earn more and stress less. Let us start with pricing. Many contractors underprice their work and then struggle to pay for materials and labour. To price correctly, you must know your costs. List your direct costs: paint, primer, filler, sandpaper, masking tape, brushes, rollers, fuel, and transport. Add your labour cost. Decide how much you pay yourself and your team per day. Add overheads: rent, phone, insurance, tools replacement, and marketing. Add a profit margin. A common mistake is to forget overheads and profit. If you only charge for materials and labour, you are not running a business, you are volunteering. Now let us talk about quoting. Always inspect the site before quoting. Measure the area. Check the surface condition. Ask about access, water, and electricity. Note any special requirements such as high ceilings, furniture moving, or working at night. Write a clear quotation that lists the scope of work, the products to be used, the number of coats, the timeline, the payment terms, and the warranty. Include a line for surface preparation. Include a line for decorative finishes if applicable. State that the price is valid for a certain period, for example thirty days. Ask for a deposit before starting. A deposit of fifty percent is common. This covers materials and protects you if the client cancels. Never start a job without a written agreement. Even a simple one-page contract signed by both parties is better than a verbal agreement. Now let us talk about cash flow. Cash flow is the lifeblood of a contracting business. You need money to buy materials, pay workers, and cover transport. If clients pay late, you suffer. To manage cash flow, ask for a deposit, invoice promptly, and follow up on payments. Keep a simple record of money in and money out. Use a notebook or a spreadsheet. Separate your business money from your personal money. Open a bank account or mobile money account for the business. Pay yourself a fixed salary. Reinvest a portion of your profit into tools and marketing. Now let us talk about finding clients. In Africa, word of mouth is powerful. Do excellent work and ask satisfied clients for referrals. Give them a small gift or a discount on their next job for every referral that becomes a paying customer. Use social media. WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram are widely used. Post photos of your work. Ask clients for testimonials. Join local business groups and community associations. Partner with real estate agents, landlords, hotels, schools, and churches. They often need painting and decorating services. Offer a professional flyer or business card. Keep your phone number and email address on all your marketing materials. Now let us talk about customer care. Good customer care turns one job into many. Communicate clearly. Answer calls and messages promptly. Arrive on time. Keep the site clean. Protect the client furniture and floors. Respect the client home and culture. Ask before moving personal items. Do not play loud music. Do not smoke on site. Dress neatly. Speak politely. If a problem arises, listen to the client and solve it quickly. Do not argue. A satisfied client tells others. An unhappy client tells more. Now let us talk about managing workers. If you employ painters, treat them fairly. Pay them on time. Provide safety equipment. Train them. Give them clear instructions. Set quality standards and check their work. Reward good performance with bonuses or extra training. Deal with poor performance firmly but fairly. Keep records of hours worked and wages paid. Comply with local labour laws. A motivated team produces better work and stays with you longer. Now let us talk about tools and equipment. Buy quality tools when you can afford them. They last longer and produce better results. Keep a tool inventory. Store tools safely. Clean and maintain them after every job. Replace worn brushes and rollers. A well-maintained toolkit saves time and money. Now let us talk about health and safety. Painting and decorating involves chemicals, dust, and heights. Provide gloves, masks, and eye protection. Use ladders and scaffolding safely. Do not overload ladders. Check electrical cords for damage. Keep a first aid kit on site. Know the emergency numbers. Train your team on safe work practices. A safe site is a productive site. Now let us talk about legal and financial matters. Register your business if required by local law. Pay taxes. Keep receipts for all purchases. Keep copies of quotations, contracts, and invoices. If you employ workers, comply with tax and social security requirements. Consider insurance for your business, tools, and public liability. Insurance protects you if something goes wrong. Now let us talk about growth. As your business grows, you can offer more services: interior design, wallpaper installation, floor coating, and furniture restoration. You can hire more workers and take on larger projects. You can open a shop selling paint and supplies. You can train other painters for a fee. Set goals for each year. Review your progress. Adjust your strategy. Now let us talk about reputation. Your reputation is your most valuable asset. Protect it. Do what you promise. If you make a mistake, fix it. Be honest about timelines and costs. Do not cut corners. Use quality materials. Treat clients and workers with respect. A good reputation brings clients without advertising. A bad reputation destroys your business quickly. Finally, let us talk about balance. Running a business is hard work. Take care of your health. Rest when you can. Spend time with family. Manage your stress. A healthy business owner makes better decisions. In summary, business management, pricing, and customer care are essential for success in painting and decorating. Know your costs, quote clearly, manage cash flow, find clients, care for customers, manage workers, maintain tools, stay safe, comply with the law, and protect your reputation. Do this and your business will grow, your income will increase, and you will build a lasting career in the painting and decorating industry. This concludes Module 2. You now have the technical and business knowledge to deliver professional work and run a profitable business. Apply what you have learned, keep learning, and take pride in your craft.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Surface Preparation, Paint Estimation, and Site Safety for African Climates

This Practical Reference lesson supports Module 2 of the RISE AFRICA Painting and Decorating course. It is designed as a working document for painters, contractors, and small decorating businesses operating in African conditions. The focus is business-first: how to prepare surfaces correctly, estimate paint quantities accurately, price jobs profitably, and protect workers on site. Poor surface preparation is the single largest cause of premature paint failure in hot, humid, dusty, and high-UV African environments, and it is also the largest source of unbudgeted rework that erodes contractor profit. Getting preparation and estimation right is therefore both a technical and a commercial discipline.

Why Surface Preparation Drives Profit in African Climates

Across much of sub-Saharan Africa, surfaces face conditions that accelerate coating failure: intense ultraviolet radiation, seasonal heavy rain, high humidity, harmattan dust, and in coastal zones, airborne chloride from sea spray. A coating applied over dust, grease, efflorescence, or loose old paint will blister, peel, or chalk within months rather than years. For a decorating business, this means callbacks, reputational damage, and lost margin. The practical rule is simple: preparation should consume 6070% of total labour time on a repaint, and the paint specification should be matched to the substrate and exposure rather than to the cheapest available tin.

Preparation also determines how much paint is actually needed. A porous, unpainted cement render will absorb far more primer and first-coat paint than a previously sealed surface. Contractors who estimate paint volume by floor area alone routinely under-order by 1530% on new work, then absorb the extra cost or return to the client for a variation. Accurate estimation protects both cash flow and customer trust.

SAFETY WARNING Chemicals, Dust, and Lead

Paint and preparation work involves hazardous chemicals and dust. The following precautions are mandatory:

  • Wear nitrile gloves, eye protection, and a P2/P3 respirator when sanding, mixing, or spraying.
  • Ensure adequate ventilation when using solvent-based paints, thinners, and fungicidal washes.
  • Never eat, drink, or smoke in the work area. Wash hands and face before breaks.
  • Keep solvents, acids, and fungicides in original labelled containers, away from children and food.
  • Do not burn painted timber or sand lead-based paint dry this releases toxic lead dust. Test old paint before disturbing it.
  • Dispose of paint residues, solvents, and contaminated rags through approved waste routes, never into drains or open ground.
  • Have a first-aid kit and clean water on site. In case of eye or skin contact, rinse for at least 15 minutes and seek medical help.

Reference: WHO guidance on safe handling of chemicals and lead in paint; national occupational health regulations.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Inspect before you quote. Check moisture, cracks, efflorescence, rust, rot, and previous coatings. Photograph defects and confirm them with the client in writing.
  2. Test the substrate. Use a moisture meter on render and concrete; confirm cure time. Test old paint for lead before sanding.
  3. Clean thoroughly. Remove dust, grease, salt, mould, and loose paint. In coastal and humid zones, use a fungicidal wash and rinse.
  4. Repair and prime. Fill cracks, sand flush, treat timber and metal, and apply the correct primer for the substrate.
  5. Estimate paint accurately. Calculate area, divide by coverage, multiply by coats, then add 1015% waste and touch-up allowance.
  6. Price preparation properly. Budget 6070% of labour time for preparation and include it as a visible line item in the quotation.
  7. Respect the weather window. Apply between 10C and 35C, below 85% relative humidity, and with no rain forecast for 24 hours.
  8. Protect people and property. Use PPE, ventilate, mask floors and fittings, and dispose of chemical waste responsibly.
