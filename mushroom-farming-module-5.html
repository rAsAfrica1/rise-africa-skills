1. What is Mushroom Spawn?

Mushroom spawn is the equivalent of seeds for a mushroom farmer. It is a carrier materialâusually grain like sorghum, millet, or wheatâthat has been fully colonized by mushroom mycelium. When you buy spawn, you are buying living fungal tissue that will grow into your substrate and produce mushrooms. In Africa, spawn is often sold in bags containing 1 to 2 kilograms. The mycelium appears as white, cottony threads covering the grain. Good spawn smells like fresh mushrooms, not sour or rotten.

Spawn comes in different types: grain spawn, sawdust spawn, and plug spawn. Grain spawn is most common for small-scale farmers because it is easy to handle and gives fast colonization. Sawdust spawn is used for log cultivation, while plug spawn is for drilling into logs. For growing oyster or button mushrooms on pasteurized straw or compost, grain spawn is your best choice. You can buy spawn from reputable suppliers or make your own, which we cover in Lesson 3.

The quality of spawn directly affects your yield. Poor spawn can be contaminated with mold or bacteria, or it may be too old and weak. Always check the date on the package. Spawn should be used within 2 to 4 weeks of arrival if stored at room temperature, or up to 3 months if kept in a refrigerator at 2-4Â°C. Never freeze spawn; freezing kills the mycelium.

In Africa, many farmers produce their own spawn to save money, but this requires a clean workspace. If you are just starting, buy certified spawn from a trusted supplier. A 2 kg bag of grain spawn can inoculate about 20 kg of wet substrate, roughly a 10% spawn rate. This ratio gives you a good head start against contaminants. Remember, spawn is aliveâtreat it gently, keep it cool and dark, and use it promptly for the best results.

2. Choosing Quality Spawn

Choosing quality spawn is critical because contaminated or weak spawn will ruin your entire crop. Start by inspecting the bag. The grain should be uniformly covered with white mycelium. You should not see any green, black, or pink patchesâthese are molds. A slight yellowing is normal as the mycelium ages, but avoid bags with large yellow or brown areas. The spawn should smell pleasant, like mushrooms. A sour, fermented, or alcohol smell indicates bacterial contamination.

Check the age of the spawn. Ask the supplier for the production date. Fresh spawn is best; use it within 2 weeks if stored at room temperature (20-25Â°C). If you must store it longer, keep it in a refrigerator at 2-4Â°C for up to 3 months. Never buy spawn that has been exposed to high heat or direct sunlight. In hot African climates, transport spawn in a cooler box with ice packs if possible.

The strain of mushroom also matters. Different strains are suited to different temperatures. For example, oyster mushrooms have tropical strains that fruit well at 25-30Â°C, while temperate strains prefer 15-20Â°C. Choose a strain that matches your local climate. If you are in a hot region like the Sahel, select heat-tolerant strains. Ask your supplier for advice.

Finally, consider the supplier's reputation. Buy from known producers who follow sterile techniques. Cheap spawn from unreliable sources often contains contaminants. It is better to pay a little more for certified spawn than to lose your whole crop. If you can, visit the supplier's lab or farm to see their setup. A clean, professional operation is a good sign. Once you have good spawn, use it promptly and handle it aseptically during inoculation.

3. Making Grain Spawn

Making your own grain spawn saves money and gives you control, but it requires strict cleanliness. You will need: whole grains (sorghum, millet, or wheat), a large pot for boiling, glass jars or heat-resistant plastic bags, a pressure cooker or a large pot with a lid, and a still air box or clean room. Start by boiling the grain for 15-20 minutes until it is soft but not mushy. Drain well and spread on a clean surface to dry until the surface is dry to touch.

Next, fill your jars or bags about two-thirds full with the grain. Add a breathable filter (like a piece of cotton cloth or synthetic filter disc) over the opening and secure with a lid or rubber band. You need to sterilize the grain to kill all contaminants. If you have a pressure cooker, process at 15 psi for 90 minutes. If not, use a large pot with a steamer basket and steam the jars for 2 hours on two consecutive days (tyndallization). This method kills most contaminants.

After sterilization, let the jars cool to room temperature. Now you need to inoculate with mushroom culture. You can use a liquid culture syringe or a piece of colonized agar. Work in a still air box or a clean room with no drafts. Flame-sterilize your needle or scalpel until red hot, then cool it. Inject 1-2 ml of liquid culture per jar through the filter or a self-healing injection port. If using agar, cut a small wedge and place it in the jar.

Shake the jar to distribute the culture. Incubate at 25-28Â°C in a dark place. Within 3-7 days, you will see white mycelium growing. Shake the jar every few days to speed colonization. It takes 2-3 weeks for the grain to be fully colonized. Once fully white, the spawn is ready to use. Use it within 2 weeks or refrigerate for up to 3 months. Always check for contamination before use; any green or black spots mean discard the jar.

4. Aseptic Inoculation Techniques

Aseptic inoculation means introducing spawn into your substrate without introducing contaminants. This is the most critical step in mushroom farming. Work in a clean, still-air environment. A still air box (SAB) is a simple plastic box with two arm holes, which you can make from a large storage container. Alternatively, work in a small room with doors and windows closed, and turn off fans and air conditioning. Wear clean clothes, a face mask, and gloves. Wipe all surfaces with 70% alcohol.

Before inoculation, ensure your substrate is properly pasteurized or sterilized and cooled to below 30Â°C. Hot substrate will kill the spawn. For grain spawn, break up the spawn bag by shaking it, then open it carefully. Flame-sterilize your toolsâspoon, scoop, or handsâwith alcohol or a flame. If using a flame, let the tool cool before touching spawn. Work quickly but carefully. Open the substrate bag, add spawn at a rate of 5-10% of the wet substrate weight. For example, for 10 kg of wet straw, use 0.5 to 1 kg of spawn.

Mix the spawn thoroughly into the substrate. You can layer it or mix it evenly. Mixing gives faster colonization. After mixing, close the bag with a filter or loose tie to allow gas exchange. Label the bag with date and strain. Clean up any spilled substrate immediately. If you are using multiple bags, sterilize your tools between bags to avoid cross-contamination.

After inoculation, place bags in a clean incubation area. Do not open bags for 7-10 days to avoid contamination. If you see green mold, remove the bag immediately. Aseptic technique takes practice, but it is essential. In Africa, many farmers use a simple still air box made from a plastic drum cut in half. With careful practice, you can achieve high success rates even in basic conditions.

5. Incubation Conditions

Incubation is the period when mycelium grows through the substrate. The right conditions are crucial for fast, contaminant-free colonization. Temperature: most mushroom mycelium grows best at 24-28Â°C. For tropical oyster mushrooms, 25-30Â°C is ideal. Do not exceed 30Â°C, as this encourages contaminants. If your area is hotter, incubate in a cool, shaded room or use evaporative cooling. In cold regions, use a small heater or incubate in a insulated box.

Humidity: during incubation, you do not need high humidity. Relative humidity of 60-70% is fine. The substrate itself provides moisture. If the substrate dries out, colonization slows. You can cover bags with a plastic sheet to retain moisture, but ensure some gas exchange. Darkness: mycelium prefers dark conditions. Light can trigger early fruiting, which reduces yield. Keep bags in a dark room or cover them with a dark cloth. Some light is okay, but avoid direct sunlight.

Air exchange: mycelium produces carbon dioxide (CO2). High CO2 levels can slow growth. Bags with filters allow gas exchange. If using sealed bags, you may need to open them briefly after 7 days to release CO2. But be careful not to introduce contaminants. A simple method is to use bags with a microporous filter patch. Shaking: for grain spawn, shaking every 3-4 days speeds colonization. For bulk substrate, you can break up the mycelium after it is 30% colonized to distribute it, but this increases contamination risk. Only do this in a clean environment.

Monitor daily. Healthy mycelium is white and grows radially. If you see green, black, or pink patches, or a sour smell, remove the bag immediately. Incubation typically takes 2-4 weeks for bulk substrate, depending on spawn rate and temperature. Once the substrate is fully white, it is ready for fruiting. Move bags to the fruiting chamber with proper humidity and fresh air.

6. Troubleshooting Spawn Run

Even with good technique, problems can occur during spawn run. Here are common issues and solutions. Green mold (Trichoderma): appears as green patches. Cause: contaminated spawn, poor sterilization, or dirty environment. Solution: remove and discard the affected bag immediately. Increase sterilization time, use fresh spawn, and improve cleanliness. In Africa, Trichoderma is common in humid conditions. Ensure your substrate is properly pasteurized and cooled.

Sour rot (bacteria): caused by too much moisture in substrate or insufficient air exchange. The bag smells sour and mycelium does not grow. Solution: reduce substrate moisture (squeeze test: a handful should release a few drops, not a stream). Increase gas exchange by using filters or opening bags briefly. Discard severely affected bags. Black mold (Aspergillus): black powdery patches. Cause: dirty environment or wet substrate. Solution: improve hygiene, reduce moisture, and increase air circulation.

Slow colonization: mycelium grows very slowly or stops. Causes: temperature too low or too high, spawn too old, or substrate too dry. Solution: adjust temperature to 24-28Â°C. Use fresh spawn. If substrate is dry, mist with sterile water (boiled and cooled). Do not overwater. Yellowing mycelium: indicates stress, often from lack of oxygen or too high CO2. Solution: increase air exchange by opening bags slightly or moving to a ventilated area. Yellowing can also mean the mycelium is old; use it soon.

No growth at all: check if spawn is alive. Break open a bag; if it smells like mushrooms and is white, it's alive. If not, get new spawn. Also check temperature; if below 20Â°C, growth is very slow. In cold African highlands, use a heater or incubate in a warm room. Always keep records: date, strain, temperature, and observations. This helps you identify patterns and improve. If contamination is frequent, review your aseptic technique and sterilization process. Remember, patience and cleanliness are key.
