1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Every day you sell milk, money leaves your pocket before you see any profit. These are your expenses. If you don't track them, you might think you're making money when you're actually losing it. For example, a cow eats 10 kg of feed per day at 50 KES per kg, that's 500 KES. She gives 15 litres of milk sold at 60 KES per litre, earning 900 KES. But you also pay for labour, transport, and vet care. Without tracking, you might spend 800 KES on expenses, leaving only 100 KES profit. That's why every shilling counts.

In Africa, dairy farmers often face hidden costs: water for cleaning, electricity for cooling, and repairs for milking machines. A smallholder with 5 cows might spend 2,500 KES daily on feed alone. Add 500 KES for labour, 200 KES for transport, and 100 KES for miscellaneous, total expenses 3,300 KES. If milk sales bring 4,500 KES, profit is 1,200 KES. But if you forget the 300 KES for veterinary drugs, your profit drops to 900 KES. Over a month, that's 9,000 KES lost.

Tracking expenses helps you set the right price for your milk. If your cost per litre is 40 KES and you sell at 50 KES, you make 10 KES per litre. But if costs rise to 45 KES, you need to raise your price or reduce costs. It also helps you plan for big expenses like buying a chaff cutter or a cooling tank. You can save small amounts daily.

This module will show you how to record every expense, from cow feed to transport, and how to use that information to make better decisions. You will learn simple tools like a notebook and pen, and methods like the daily expense sheet. By the end, you will know exactly where your money goes and how to keep more of it.

2. Tools and Materials

To track expenses, you don't need expensive software. Start with a simple notebook and a pen. A 100-page exercise book costs about 50 KES and lasts months. Use one page per day. Draw columns for date, item, quantity, unit cost, total cost, and notes. For example: 5/10/2023, dairy meal, 10 kg, 50 KES, 500 KES, morning feed.

A calculator helps avoid mistakes. A basic solar calculator costs 200 KES. If you have a smartphone, use a free app like 'Expense Manager' or 'Mint'. But be careful: phones can be lost or stolen. Keep a backup paper record. A small cash box with a lock (500 KES) keeps receipts safe. Always ask for receipts when buying feed or drugs. If the seller doesn't have receipts, write the details yourself and ask them to sign.

For larger farms, use a ledger book (200 KES) with separate pages for feed, labour, transport, veterinary, and utilities. A wall calendar (100 KES) helps mark when bills are due, like electricity or loan repayments. A folder for receipts (150 KES) keeps everything organised. If you sell milk to a cooperative, they give you a payment slip. Keep those slips to compare with your own records.

You also need a weighing scale for feed. A 50 kg scale costs 1,500 KES. Without it, you might overfeed or underfeed, wasting money. For transport, keep a logbook for your bicycle or motorcycle: record fuel, repairs, and daily distance. All these tools together cost less than 3,000 KES, a small investment to save thousands.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: At the start of each day, write the date at the top of a new page. List all expected expenses: feed, water, labour, transport, vet, and miscellaneous. For example, if you have 5 cows, you know you need 50 kg of dairy meal (2,500 KES), 2 labourers at 300 KES each (600 KES), and 200 KES for transport. Total expected: 3,300 KES.

Step 2: As you spend, record immediately. Don't wait until evening. If you buy 10 kg of feed for 500 KES, write it down. If you pay a vet 1,000 KES, note it. Use a small notebook in your pocket. At the end of the day, transfer to your main ledger. For each item, write the quantity, unit cost, and total. For example: 'Feed, 50 kg, 50 KES, 2,500 KES'.

Step 3: At the end of the day, total all expenses. Subtract from your milk sales. If you sold 75 litres at 60 KES, income is 4,500 KES. Expenses 3,300 KES, profit 1,200 KES. Write this profit in a separate column. If profit is negative, you know you overspent.

Step 4: At the end of the week, review your records. Add up daily totals. Look for patterns: did you spend more on feed on certain days? Did transport costs increase? Compare with last week. If feed costs rose, check if you changed brands or if prices went up. Adjust your buying.

Step 5: At the end of the month, calculate total expenses per category. For example, feed might be 60% of expenses, labour 20%, transport 10%, vet 5%, miscellaneous 5%. Use this to set next month's budget. If feed is too high, consider growing your own fodder or buying in bulk. If transport is high, find a closer buyer. Always keep a reserve for emergencies like cow sickness.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not recording small expenses. A 20 KES cup of tea for a worker, a 50 KES boda ride to the vet, or 100 KES for rope. These add up. Over a month, 200 KES daily becomes 6,000 KES. Fix: Carry a small notebook and write every coin you spend. At the end of the day, add them up. You'll be surprised.

Mistake 2: Mixing personal and farm money. If you take 500 KES from milk sales to buy shoes for your child, you might forget it. Then your records show more profit than you actually have. Fix: Pay yourself a fixed salary from the farm. For example, 1,000 KES per day. Record it as an expense. Any other personal spending comes from that salary, not directly from farm cash.

Mistake 3: Forgetting depreciation. Your bicycle, milking machine, or chaff cutter wears out. If you don't save for replacement, you'll be stuck. Fix: Estimate the life of each tool. A bicycle costing 15,000 KES lasts 5 years. That's 3,000 KES per year, or about 8 KES per day. Add that to daily expenses.

Mistake 4: Not accounting for your own labour. Many farmers don't pay themselves. If you work 8 hours a day, that's a cost. Fix: Decide a fair daily wage, say 500 KES, and record it as an expense. If the farm can't pay it, you're not profitable.

Mistake 5: Ignoring seasonal price changes. Feed prices rise during dry seasons. If you use last month's prices, your budget will be wrong. Fix: Check market prices weekly. Buy in bulk when prices are low. Store properly to avoid spoilage. Also, milk prices may drop when supply is high. Adjust your expenses accordingly.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety first: When handling money, keep it in a secure place. A locked cash box prevents theft. Don't carry large sums to the market. Use mobile money like M-Pesa where possible. It's safer and gives you a record. If you must carry cash, split it between pockets. Never show money in public.

Best practice: Keep receipts for all purchases. If you buy feed from a shop, ask for a receipt. If the seller doesn't have one, write the details and get a signature. This protects you if there's a dispute. Also, record payments immediately. Memory can fail after a long day.

Separate farm and personal accounts. Open a bank account for the farm. Deposit milk payments there. Pay expenses from that account. This makes tracking easy and builds credibility if you need a loan. Many African banks offer small business accounts with low fees.

Review your records weekly with your family or workers. Transparency prevents suspicion and helps everyone see where money goes. If you have employees, show them how their work affects profit. Motivate them to reduce waste.

Plan for emergencies. Set aside 10% of daily profit into a savings box or mobile savings account. After a month, you'll have a cushion for vet bills or feed price hikes. Also, insure your cows if possible. Livestock insurance costs about 5% of the cow's value per year. For a 50,000 KES cow, that's 2,500 KES per year, or about 7 KES per day. It's worth it.

Finally, stay organised. At the end of each month, file your records in a folder. Keep them for at least 5 years. You never know when you'll need them for tax or loan applications.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. For the next 7 days, you will track every expense on your dairy farm. Use a notebook and pen. If you don't have a farm, imagine you have 3 cows and use realistic prices from your area.

Day 1: Set up your expense sheet. Draw columns: Date, Item, Quantity, Unit Cost (KES), Total Cost (KES), Notes. Write today's date. Record all expenses: feed, water, labour, transport, vet, and any other. For example: 'Dairy meal, 30 kg, 50 KES, 1,500 KES'. At the end of the day, total your expenses. Also record milk sales: litres sold and price per litre. Calculate profit = sales - expenses.

Day 2-6: Repeat the process. Be diligent. Don't skip small items. At the end of each day, total expenses and profit. Notice any patterns.

Day 7: Review your week. Add up total expenses for each category. For example, feed might total 10,500 KES, labour 2,100 KES, transport 700 KES, vet 0 KES, miscellaneous 350 KES. Total expenses = 13,650 KES. Total milk sales: if you sold 105 litres at 60 KES, that's 6,300 KES. Wait, that's less than expenses? That means you're running at a loss. Check your numbers. Maybe you sold more milk or expenses are too high. This exercise will reveal if your farm is profitable.

After 7 days, write a short report: What was your biggest expense? What surprised you? How can you reduce costs? For example, if feed is 70% of expenses, consider planting napier grass or buying feed in bulk. If transport is high, find a buyer closer to your farm. Set a goal for next week: reduce one expense by 10%.

Submit your report to your trainer or share with your group. Discuss what you learned. This practical exercise will change how you view money on your farm.

<h2>Practical Reference: Expenses at Every Stage Daily Milk Sales in an African Dairy Herd System</h2>

<p>This Practical Reference is the working document for the module <strong>Expenses at Every Stage</strong> in the course <em>Daily Milk Sales: Dairy Herd System</em>. It is written for smallholder and emerging commercial dairy farmers across Africa who sell milk daily and need to know, at any point in the day, where money is leaking and where it is working. The reference pulls together cost categories, benchmark numbers, a step-by-step cost-tracking method, and a practical exercise you can run with your own herd this week.</p>

<p>Dairy is a cash-flow business. Unlike a maize crop that pays once a season, a milking herd pays every day and costs every day. Feed, labour, water, animal health, transport, and spoilage all interact. In much of East and Southern Africa, feed alone accounts for 6075% of total milk production cost, and a single missed cost line can quietly turn a profitable litre into a loss-making one. This reference gives you the tools to see that clearly.</p>

<h2>Why Cost Visibility Drives Profit, Not Just Records</h2>

<p>Many African dairy farmers know their milk price and their daily litres, but not their cost per litre. That gap is where profit disappears. A farmer selling 40 litres at KES 50 (about USD 0.39) earns KES 2,000 per day, but if feed, labour, transport, and health costs total KES 2,100, the farm is losing money while appearing busy and productive. The purpose of expense tracking is not paperwork it is pricing, culling, feeding, and negotiation decisions.</p>

<p>Three business questions every dairy farmer should be able to answer from records:</p>
<ul>
<li>What is my cost per litre of milk produced today?</li>
<li>Which cost category changed most this month, and why?</li>
<li>At what milk price do I break even, and how much buffer do I have?</li>
</ul>

<h2>Step-by-Step Method: Tracking Expenses at Every Stage</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Set up a simple daily log.</strong> Use a notebook or a free mobile app (e.g., Farm Records, ODK-based tools). Columns: date, litres sold, price per litre, feed cost, labour, health, transport, other.</li>
<li><strong>Record every cash and in-kind expense.</strong> In-kind costs (family labour, home-grown fodder, milk consumed at home) must be valued at market price otherwise your cost per litre is understated.</li>
<li><strong>Assign costs to stages.</strong> Stages: (a) feed and water, (b) animal health and breeding, (c) milking and hygiene, (d) storage and transport, (e) marketing and sales.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate cost per litre weekly.</strong> Total weekly expenses total weekly litres. Compare with your selling price.</li>
<li><strong>Review monthly.</strong> Identify the top two cost categories and set one concrete reduction target for next month.</li>
<li><strong>Adjust pricing and culling decisions.</strong> If cost per litre exceeds price, either raise price, cut feed waste, improve yield per cow, or cull low performers.</li>
</ol>

<div style="border:2px solid #b71c1c;background:#ffebee;padding:14px;margin:20px 0;font-family:Arial,sans-serif;">
<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7f0000;">Never use expired veterinary drugs, unregistered acaricides, or agrochemical containers to store milk. In many African markets, counterfeit or expired animal health products are common and can leave drug residues in milk, causing rejection at collection centres and potential harm to consumers. Always buy from licensed agrovets, observe withdrawal periods, and keep records of every treatment. Milk containing residues is a food safety and legal risk, not just a financial one.</p>
</div>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist (68 Points)</h2>
<ul>
<li> Record every expense daily cash and in-kind.</li>
<li> Value home-grown fodder and family labour at market price.</li>
<li> Calculate cost per litre weekly, not monthly.</li>
<li> Separate feed, health, labour, and transport costs into distinct lines.</li>
<li> Compare your cost per litre with the benchmark table above.</li>
<li> Set one cost-reduction target each month (e.g., 5% feed waste reduction).</li>
<li> Keep all veterinary and input receipts for 12 months.</li>
<li> Review break-even price before every milk price negotiation.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Practical Exercise</h2>
<p><strong>Exercise: Build Your Own Cost Per Litre for 7 Days</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>For the next 7 days, record every litre of milk sold and every expense (cash or in-kind).</li>
<li>At the end of the week, total expenses and total litres.</li>
<li>Calculate cost per litre = total expenses total litres.</li>
<li>Compare with your selling price. Write down the margin (or loss) per litre.</li>
<li>Identify the single largest cost category and propose one realistic reduction action for the following week.</li>
<li>Repeat for 4 weeks and plot a simple line graph of cost per litre versus price per litre.</li>
</ol>
<p>Bring your 4-week cost per litre figure to the next module session. This becomes your baseline for all future herd and feed decisions.</p>
