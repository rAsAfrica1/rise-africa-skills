1. Measuring and Marking Out Metal

Before you cut, drill, or bend any metal, you must measure and mark it accurately. In tool making, a mistake of 2 mm can mean a wasted piece of steel. Start with a steel rule (300 mm or 600 mm) and a scribera sharp, hardened steel pin with a point. For agricultural tools like hoe blades or mower parts, you often work from a drawing or a sample. Place your metal on a flat surface, like a steel plate or a heavy bench. Use a try square to check that edges are straight and at 90 degrees. If the edge is rough, file it first.\n\nTo mark a line, apply layout dye (or chalk, or even a permanent marker) to the metal. This makes scribed lines visible. Hold the steel rule firmly against the edge, and scribe a line by pulling the scriber oncedo not go back and forth. For circles, use a pair of dividers or a trammel. Set the distance between the points to the radius you need. For example, to mark a 10 mm hole, set dividers to 5 mm. Place the fixed point at the center punch mark, then swing the dividers to scratch the circle.\n\nCenter punching is critical. A center punch is a hardened steel rod with a pointed tip. Place the punch on your intersection mark, hold it vertical, and hit it once with a hammer (1 kg to 2 kg). This creates a small dent that guides your drill bit later. For accurate work, use an automatic center punchit has a spring inside and you just press down.\n\nAlways double-check your measurements before cutting. Use a caliper or micrometer for precise work. A vernier caliper can measure to 0.02 mm. For most agricultural tools, 0.5 mm is acceptable. Remember: measure twice, cut once. In rural workshops, where replacing metal is costly, this rule saves time and money.\n\nFinally, keep your marking tools clean and dry. Rust on a scriber or rule ruins accuracy. Store them in a wooden box or cloth roll. Practice on scrap metal until you can scribe a straight line and punch a center mark without slipping.

2. Cutting Metal with Hacksaws and Shears

Cutting metal is a core skill for making tools. You will use a hacksaw for thick bars and pipes, and tin snips or shears for thin sheet metal. Start with a hacksaw: it has a frame and a blade with 14, 18, or 24 teeth per inch (TPI). For general work on mild steel, use 18 TPI. For thin-walled pipe or sheet, use 24 TPI to avoid snagging. For thick solid bar, use 14 TPI.\n\nMount your work in a bench vise. Mark your cut line with a scriber and center punch. Start the cut by drawing the saw backward a few times to make a groove. Then use full strokes, pushing forward and pulling back. Apply light pressure on the forward stroke only. Let the saw do the workpushing too hard breaks teeth. Keep the blade lubricated with a few drops of engine oil or water. A good cut should take 30 to 60 seconds for a 25 mm steel bar.\n\nIf the blade wanders, don't twist it. Stop, reposition the saw in the groove, and continue. For long cuts, rotate the blade 90 degrees in the frame to cut deeper. When you are almost through, slow down and support the offcut to prevent it from falling and bending the blade.\n\nFor sheet metal up to 1.5 mm thick, use tin snips. There are straight, left-cutting, and right-cutting snips. For straight cuts, use straight snips. For curves, use left or right snips depending on the direction. Mark your line, then clamp the sheet to a bench. Start at an edge and cut with short, firm strokes. Do not close the snips completelythis creates a jagged edge. For thicker sheet (up to 2 mm), use a bench shear or a nibbler.\n\nAlways wear gloves and safety glasses. Cut edges are razor-sharp. After cutting, file the edges to remove burrs. In African workshops, a hacksaw and a pair of good snips are often the only cutting tools available, so master them. Practice on scrap metal until your cuts are straight and square.

3. Filing and Shaping Metal Edges

After cutting, metal edges are rough and sharp. Filing removes burrs, squares edges, and shapes curves. You need a set of files: a flat file for straight edges, a half-round for curves, and a round file for holes. For mild steel, use a single-cut file for finishing and a double-cut file for rapid removal. File lengths range from 100 mm to 300 mm. Start with a 200 mm bastard cut file for general work.\n\nSecure your work in a vise at about elbow height. Hold the file with one hand on the handle and the other on the tip. For flat filing, push the file forward and slightly down, using the full length of the file. Apply pressure on the forward stroke only; lift the file on the return. Do not rock the filekeep it flat. For draw filing (finishing), hold the file at 90 degrees to the edge and push it sideways. This produces a smooth surface.\n\nTo square an edge, file until the edge is flat and at 90 degrees to the face. Check with a try square. For curved shapes, use a half-round file. Clamp the work so the curve is accessible. File with a rocking motion, following the curve. For holes, use a round file. Turn the file slightly as you push.\n\nFile cards (wire brushes) clean the file teeth. Clogged files scratch instead of cut. Clean your file every few minutes. Chalk on the file reduces clogging when filing soft metals like aluminum.\n\nFor agricultural tools, filing is used to sharpen hoe blades, machetes, and axes. A sharp edge is a filed edge. But for tool making, filing is for shaping and fitting. After filing, deburr all edges. A smooth edge prevents cuts and improves fit. In rural Africa, a good file can last years if kept dry and clean. Store files separately to avoid dulling. Practice on scrap steel until you can file a flat edge and a smooth curve.

4. Drilling Holes in Metal

Drilling holes is essential for bolting, riveting, and fitting parts. You need a drilleither a hand drill or an electric drill. For accuracy, use a drill press. But in many African workshops, a portable electric drill (500 W to 800 W) is common. Use high-speed steel (HSS) drill bits. For mild steel, a bit with a 118-degree point angle works well. Common sizes: 3 mm, 5 mm, 6.5 mm, 8.5 mm, 10 mm.\n\nFirst, mark the hole center with a center punch. This prevents the bit from wandering. Apply a few drops of cutting oil (or engine oil) to the punch mark. Start with a small pilot bit (3 mm) to create a guide hole. Then switch to the final size. Run the drill at a moderate speed: for a 6 mm bit in steel, about 600 to 800 RPM. Too fast burns the bit; too slow chips the edge.\n\nHold the drill perpendicular to the surface. If using a hand drill, use both hands and keep steady pressure. Let the bit cutdo not force it. Clear chips by withdrawing the bit occasionally. For deep holes, withdraw the bit every few millimeters to clear swarf. Use a brush, not your fingers, to remove chips.\n\nFor large holes (over 12 mm), drill a pilot hole first, then use a step drill or a hole saw. For precision, clamp the work to a drill press table. If the bit squeals, reduce speed and add oil. If it smokes, stop and let it cool.\n\nAfter drilling, deburr the hole with a larger drill bit or a countersink. This removes sharp edges. For tapping (next lesson), the hole size must match the tap. For a 6 mm bolt, drill a 5 mm hole for tapping. Always wear safety glasses. In agriculture, you will drill holes for plow bolts, mower blades, and tool handles. Practice on scrap metal until your holes are straight and clean.

5. Threading and Tapping

Threading creates screw threads on a rod (external) or inside a hole (internal). For tool making, you will mostly tap holes to accept bolts. You need a tap set (taper, plug, and bottoming taps) and a tap wrench. For external threads, you need a die and die stock. Common thread sizes: M6, M8, M10, M12. In Africa, both metric and imperial (Whitworth) threads are used. Know which one you need.\n\nTo tap a hole, first drill the correct tap drill size. For M6, use a 5.0 mm drill; for M8, 6.8 mm; for M10, 8.5 mm; for M12, 10.2 mm. Clamp the work securely. Start with a taper tap (first tap). Insert it into the hole, keeping it straight. Turn it clockwise with light pressure until it bites. Then turn one full turn forward, then half a turn back to break chips. Apply cutting oil generously.\n\nAfter the taper tap goes through, use the plug tap (second tap) to cut threads closer to the bottom. For blind holes, use a bottoming tap. Never force a tapif it binds, back it out, clear chips, and add oil. A broken tap is very difficult to remove.\n\nFor external threads, use a die. Clamp the rod in a vise. Chamfer the end with a file. Start the die straight, turn clockwise, and reverse every half turn. Use oil. For a 10 mm rod, you need a 10 mm die. The rod diameter should be slightly less than the die size (e.g., 9.8 mm for M10).\n\nIn agriculture, threading is used for bolts, clamps, and adjustable linkages. Practice on scrap metal. Always clean threads with a wire brush. A well-tapped hole holds a bolt securely. Remember: the tap drill size is critical. Too small, and the tap breaks; too large, and the threads strip.

6. Basic Joining Techniques: Riveting and Bolting

Joining metal parts is how you assemble tools. Two common methods are riveting and bolting. Riveting is permanent; bolting is removable. For agricultural tools, both are used. You need rivets (solid or blind), a rivet gun or hammer, a bucking bar, and a drill. For bolting, you need bolts, nuts, washers, and wrenches.\n\nFor solid riveting, drill a hole slightly larger than the rivet shank (e.g., 4.1 mm for a 4 mm rivet). Insert the rivet through the parts. Place the head on an anvil or bucking bar. Use a ball-peen hammer to flatten the protruding shank into a second head. Start with light taps around the edge, then work toward the center. For a 4 mm rivet, the shank should protrude about 1.5 times the diameter (6 mm).\n\nFor blind rivets (pop rivets), use a rivet gun. Drill a hole matching the rivet size. Insert the rivet into the gun, place it in the hole, and squeeze the handle until the mandrel snaps. Blind rivets are fast and work from one side. They are good for sheet metal.\n\nBolting is stronger and can be disassembled. Use graded bolts (8.8 or 10.9) for high stress. For a 10 mm bolt, drill an 11 mm hole. Place washers under the nut and bolt head. Tighten with a spanner or socket. For vibration, use a lock washer or thread-locking compound. In Africa, many farmers use bolts to attach plow shares, mower blades, and tool handles.\n\nAlways match rivet or bolt material to the base metal to avoid galvanic corrosion. Stainless steel rivets in aluminum can cause corrosion. For outdoor tools, use galvanized or stainless bolts. After joining, check that the parts are tight and aligned. Practice on scrap metal until your joints are strong and neat. Remember: a good joint is as strong as the metal around it.

<h2>Practical Reference: Basic Metalworking Skills for African Agribusiness</h2>

<p>Metalworking is the backbone of agricultural tool production across Africa. From the jua kali workshops of Kenya to the metal fabrication clusters of Nnewi, Nigeria, and the artisan sheds of Kumasi, Ghana, small-scale fabricators turn raw steel into hoes, planters, maize shellers, water pumps, and animal-drawn implements. This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the six core skills in this module into a single job-floor guide you can pin to a workshop wall or load on a phone. The focus is business-first: every technique here is framed around producing a sellable, repairable, quality-controlled product that farmers will pay for and recommend.</p>

<p>According to the FAO, smallholder farmers supply up to 80% of food in sub-Saharan Africa, yet access to affordable, locally made, and locally repairable tools remains a major constraint. This gap is your market. A fabricator who can measure accurately, cut cleanly, file precisely, drill squarely, tap threads reliably, and join components securely can produce tools that outperform imported alternatives on price, fit, and after-sales support.</p>

<h3>Skill 1: Measuring and Marking Out Metal</h3>
<p>Accurate marking is the difference between a tool that sells and scrap steel. Use a steel rule, try square, scriber, and dividers. For African workshop conditions where humidity and dust are constant, keep marking tools oiled and stored in a wooden box. Always mark from a datum edge a known straight, square face. Use engineer's blue or a permanent marker for visibility on bright steel. For production runs (e.g., 50 hoe blades), make a plywood or sheet-metal template to cut marking time by 6070%.</p>

<h3>Skill 2: Cutting Metal with Hacksaws and Shears</h3>
<p>For straight cuts in bar stock and pipe, the hacksaw is king. Use 1824 TPI blades for thick sections and 32 TPI for thin sheet and tubing. Mount the blade with teeth pointing forward and tension it so it "rings" when plucked. For sheet metal up to 1.5 mm, manual or treadle shears give a cleaner edge than a hacksaw and are faster for production. In many African workshops, a treadle guillotine made from local materials can cut 1.2 mm mild steel at 3040 strokes per minute.</p>

<h3>Skill 3: Filing and Shaping Metal Edges</h3>
<p>Filing removes saw marks, squares edges, and creates the chamfers that make a tool safe and comfortable. Use a bastard cut for rough work and a smooth cut for finishing. Draw-filing (moving the file sideways along the edge) produces a flat, polished surface. For agricultural blades, a 45 chamfer on the cutting edge improves penetration and reduces drag in soil. Keep files clean with a file card; a clogged file scratches rather than cuts.</p>

<h3>Skill 4: Drilling Holes in Metal</h3>
<p>Hole placement determines whether a bolt-on implement fits. Centre-punch every hole before drilling. Use cutting fluid in African workshops, used engine oil or palm oil works where commercial fluid is unavailable to cool the bit and flush swarf. Drill at low speed for large bits (300500 rpm) and higher speed for small bits (10001500 rpm). Clamp the workpiece; never hold it by hand.</p>

<h3>Skill 5: Threading and Tapping</h3>
<p>Tapping creates internal threads for bolts; threading (dieing) creates external threads on rods. The rule of thumb: for a bolt of diameter D, drill a tapping hole of D minus the pitch. For M10 1.5, drill 8.5 mm. Use a tap wrench and reverse a quarter-turn every full turn to break the chip. In African agribusiness, standardising on M8, M10, and M12 threads across your product range simplifies repairs and spares supply.</p>

<span style="color:#641e16;">Metal cutting, drilling, and grinding produce sharp swarf, sparks, and airborne particles. Never wear loose clothing or open sandals near a bench grinder or drill press. In workshops without fume extraction, avoid galvanised steel grinding zinc fumes cause metal fume fever. Keep a first-aid kit with eye wash and burn gel within 10 metres of every metalworking station.</span>

<h3>Skill 6: Basic Joining Techniques Riveting and Bolting</h3>
<p>Bolted joints allow field repair with a spanner; riveted joints are permanent, vibration-resistant, and cheaper for high-volume production. For agricultural implements that farmers will repair themselves, bolting is usually the better business choice it reduces warranty claims and builds customer confidence. Use grade 8.8 bolts for load-bearing joints and apply anti-seize compound in coastal or humid regions to prevent galvanic corrosion.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Mark from a datum edge; use a template for production runs over 10 units.</li>
<li> Select blade TPI by material thickness (1824 TPI thick, 32 TPI thin).</li>
<li> Clamp work in a vise or clamp; never hand-hold for cutting or drilling.</li>
<li> Centre-punch every hole; apply cutting fluid and clear swarf.</li>
<li> For tapping, drill = bolt diameter thread pitch (e.g., M10 1.5 8.5 mm).</li>
<li> Deburr all edges with a file before assembly sharp edges injure customers.</li>
<li> Choose bolted joints for farmer-repairable tools; rivets for permanent frames.</li>
<li> Inspect finished tools against a written quality checklist before sale.</li>
</ul>
