1. Common Timber Pests in Africa: Termites, Borers, and Fungi

In Africa, timber structures face three main enemies: termites, borers, and fungi. Termites are the most destructive. Subterranean termites build mud tubes from soil to wood, eating from the inside out. Drywood termites leave small holes and frass (sawdust-like pellets). In West and Central Africa, Macrotermes species can destroy a door frame in months. Borers are beetle larvae that tunnel into wood, leaving 2-3mm holes and fine dust. The African powder-post beetle attacks dry seasoned wood, while the longhorn beetle prefers moist timber. Fungi cause rot, appearing as white or brown patches. Brown rot makes wood crumbly; white rot makes it spongy. Fungi need moisture above 20% and temperatures between 20-30Ccommon in humid regions. To identify pests: look for mud tubes (termites), exit holes (borers), or soft, discolored wood (fungi). Tap timber with a hammera dull thud means internal damage. Probe with a screwdriver; if it sinks easily, rot or termites are present. Prevention starts with keeping timber dry. Use treated wood, elevate posts off the ground, and ensure ventilation. In storage, stack timber with spacers to allow airflow. For active infestations, remove and burn damaged wood. For termites, apply termiticide like imidacloprid or fipronil to the soil and wood. For borers, inject insecticide into holes or fumigate. For fungi, cut out rot and treat with fungicide. Always wear gloves and a mask when handling chemicals. Local solutions include neem oil, which repels termites, and cashew nut shell liquid, a natural preservative. Remember: pests are easier to prevent than to eliminate. Inspect your timber at least twice a year, especially after the rainy season.

2. Applying Natural and Chemical Preservatives

Preservatives protect timber from pests and rot. You can use natural or chemical methods. Natural preservatives are cheap and locally available. Neem oil: crush neem seeds, soak in water for 24 hours, strain, and brush onto wood. It repels termites and borers. Cashew nut shell liquid (CNSL): a dark oil from cashew processing, mix with diesel (1:1) and paint on timber. It waterproofs and kills insects. Tannins from acacia bark: boil bark in water, reduce, and apply as a stain. For chemical preservatives, three types are common: water-based (e.g., copper chrome arsenate, CCA), oil-based (creosote, pentachlorophenol), and solvent-based (permethrin). CCA is effective but toxichandle with gloves and mask. Creosote smells strong but protects against moisture and insects; use only outdoors. Permethrin is safer for indoor use. Application methods: brushing is simplest for small areas; dip for 5-10 minutes for posts; pressure treatment forces preservative deep into woodbest for structural timber. For a door frame, brush two coats of preservative, allowing 24 hours drying between coats. Mix your own borate solution: 1 kg borax, 1 kg boric acid, 10 liters water. Soak timber for 24 hours. This is low-toxicity and effective against fungi and borers. Always treat cut ends and jointsthey absorb water. After treatment, let timber dry completely before painting or installing. Safety: wear gloves, goggles, and a dust mask. Do not eat or smoke while handling chemicals. Store preservatives away from children and animals. In Africa, many artisans use spent engine oil as a preservativeit works but is toxic and pollutes soil. Avoid it. Instead, use neem or borate for family homes. For heavy structural timber, invest in CCA or creosote. Reapply preservative every 3-5 years, especially in coastal or humid areas. Test effectiveness by checking for new pest holes after 6 months.

3. Repairing Rotting or Broken Timber Beams

When a timber beam rots or breaks, you can repair it instead of replacing the whole piece. First, assess the damage. Probe with an awl or screwdriver. If rot is soft and spongy, it must be removed. For minor rot (less than 20% of cross-section), cut out the rotten wood with a chisel and mallet. Use a wire brush to clean the area. Treat with fungicide and let dry. Then fill with epoxy wood filler or a mixture of sawdust and wood glue. For structural beams, you need a stronger repair. If a beam is broken or has major rot, use a 'sister' beam: cut a new piece of timber the same size, treat it, and bolt it alongside the damaged section with 12mm galvanized bolts every 300mm. Use washers under bolt heads. For a split beam, wrap it with a metal plate and bolt through. Another method is 'scarf joint': cut matching angled ends on both pieces, apply waterproof glue, and bolt together. For a post that has rotted at the base, cut off the bottom 300mm, treat the cut, and set the post on a concrete plinth or metal shoe. Never bury untreated timber in soil. If the beam is load-bearing, support the structure with temporary props before starting. Use acrow props or wooden posts. Work from the top down. After repair, apply preservative to all new cuts. Check the repair monthly for the first year. In rural areas, you can use local hardwoods like mvule or iroko for repairsthey are naturally durable. Avoid softwoods like pine unless treated. For a quick fix, wrap the beam with bitumen felt and wirebut this is temporary. Proper repair saves money and preserves the building's character. Always wear gloves and eye protection when cutting or drilling. If unsure, consult a carpenter or engineer.

4. Jointing Techniques for Structural Timber

Strong joints are critical for structural timber. Weak joints cause sagging and collapse. Here are five practical joints for African conditions. 1) Mortise and tenon: cut a rectangular hole (mortise) in one timber and a matching projection (tenon) on the other. Glue and pin with a wooden peg or bolt. Use for beams and posts. 2) Lap joint: cut half the thickness from each timber where they overlap, then bolt or screw together. Good for splicing beams. 3) Scarf joint: for joining two beams end-to-end. Cut angled steps (1:6 slope) on each end, glue, and bolt. This resists tension. 4) Dovetail joint: interlocking wedge shapes, used for corner joints in frames. Strong against pulling apart. 5) Bolted joint: simplestoverlap timbers and drill 12-16mm holes, insert galvanized bolts with washers, and tighten. Use at least two bolts per joint. For all joints, apply waterproof wood glue (e.g., polyurethane) before assembling. Clamp until dry (24 hours). Pre-drill holes to avoid splitting. Use galvanized or stainless steel fixingsplain steel rusts in humidity. In termite-prone areas, wrap joints with bitumen tape or apply preservative after assembly. For heavy loads, add metal plates (gang nails or T-straps) on both sides. Check joints annually for looseness. Tighten bolts and replace rusty ones. In traditional African construction, joints are often lashed with rope or rawhidethese work for light loads but not for structural beams. For modern buildings, use mechanical fasteners. Always ensure joints are not in contact with soil or concrete. If timber is green (unseasoned), allow for shrinkageuse washers and re-tighten after 6 months. Practice on scrap wood before working on a building. A good joint is tight, flush, and treated.

5. Protecting Timber from Moisture and Sun

Moisture and sun are timber's silent enemies. Moisture causes rot, fungi, and termites. Sun causes cracking, warping, and fading. To protect timber, control water and shade. First, design to shed water. Use roof overhangs of at least 600mm. Slope surfaces so water runs off. For posts, set them on concrete plinths or metal basesnever directly in soil. If in soil, use a concrete collar and treat the post with creosote. For beams, ensure no water traps. Drill weep holes in horizontal timbers. Second, apply finishes. Paint or varnish creates a barrier. Use exterior-grade paint with fungicide. For natural look, apply linseed oil or tung oil every year. For decks, use a water-repellent sealer. Third, ventilate. In roofs, leave a 50mm gap between insulation and roofing. In walls, use breathable membranes. Fourth, control ground moisture. Install drainage around foundations. Use gutters and downpipes to direct water away. Fifth, protect from sun. In hot climates, use light-colored paint to reflect heat. Plant trees for shade but keep branches 2m from timber to allow airflow. For timber in direct sun, apply UV-resistant varnish. Sixth, inspect after rains. Look for damp patches, mold, or soft wood. Dry wet timber within 24 hours. Use a fan or sunlight. Seventh, use naturally durable species: teak, iroko, mahogany, or mvule. These resist moisture and insects. For less durable woods, treat with preservative. In coastal areas, salt air accelerates corrosionuse stainless steel fixings. In desert areas, sun is the main threatuse thick sections and shade. In rainforests, moisture is constantuse elevated structures and good drainage. Remember: water is the enemy. Keep timber dry and it will last decades.

6. Workshop: Treating a Timber Door Frame

In this workshop, you will treat a timber door frame. You need: a door frame (or a 2m x 1m frame), sandpaper (80 and 120 grit), brush, gloves, mask, preservative (borate solution or commercial wood preservative), wood filler, caulk, and paint. Step 1: Inspect. Check for rot, termite damage, or cracks. Probe with a screwdriver. If soft, cut out and replace that section. Step 2: Clean. Remove old paint with a scraper or sandpaper. Sand the frame smooth. Wipe with a damp cloth. Step 3: Treat. Mix borate solution: 1 kg borax, 1 kg boric acid, 10 liters water. Apply liberally with a brush, especially on cut ends and joints. Let dry for 24 hours. For chemical preservative, follow label instructions. Step 4: Repair. Fill cracks and holes with epoxy wood filler. Let dry, then sand smooth. Step 5: Seal. Apply a coat of primer, then two coats of exterior paint. Pay attention to the bottom of the framewater collects there. Step 6: Install. If installing, ensure the frame is plumb and level. Use galvanized screws. Seal gaps with caulk. Step 7: Maintain. Check every 6 months. Reapply preservative every 2-3 years. For a door frame in a humid area, use a raised threshold and a drip cap above the door. If termites are a problem, apply a termiticide barrier around the base. Safety: wear gloves, mask, and goggles. Work in a ventilated area. Do not eat or smoke. Clean up spills. Dispose of waste properly. This workshop takes about 4 hours plus drying time. By treating your door frame, you extend its life by 10-15 years. Practice on a small piece first. Take photos before and after to show your work.

<h2>Practical Reference: Timber Treatment and Repair</h2>

<p>In African construction, timber remains one of the most versatile and cost-effective building materialsused in roof trusses, door frames, window casements, flooring, and decorative elements. Yet timber is also under constant attack from termites, borers, fungi, and the relentless tropical sun. For the African agribusiness entrepreneur, mastering timber treatment and repair is not just a technical skillit is a business survival strategy. A well-treated timber door frame can last 20 years; an untreated one may fail in 18 months. That difference translates directly into repeat customers, reduced warranty claims, and a reputation for quality that commands premium pricing.</p>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the essential knowledge from Method 4, providing field-ready data, safety protocols, and a quick-reference checklist you can use on site or in your workshop. Whether you are treating timber for a client's home, a farm storage shed, or a commercial building, these guidelines will help you deliver durable, profitable results.</p>

<h3 style="color:#c62828; margin-top:0; font-family:Arial, sans-serif;"> SAFETY WARNING: Chemical Preservative Handling</h3>
<p style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:13px; color:#333; margin-bottom:0;">
<strong>Chemical timber preservatives are toxic.</strong> CCA (Copper Chrome Arsenate), creosote, and copper azole can cause skin burns, respiratory damage, and long-term health effects. Always wear <strong>chemical-resistant gloves, goggles, and a NIOSH-approved respirator</strong> when handling or applying these substances. Never eat, drink, or smoke in the treatment area. Store preservatives in locked, labeled containers away from children, livestock, and water sources. Dispose of treated timber offcuts as hazardous waste<strong>never burn CCA-treated wood</strong>, as it releases toxic arsenic fumes. In case of skin contact, wash immediately with soap and water. If inhaled, move to fresh air and seek medical attention. Follow national regulations (e.g., Kenya's NEMA, Nigeria's NESREA, South Africa's SANS 10234) for chemical handling and disposal.
</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist: Timber Treatment and Repair</h2>

<ol style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:13px; line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Identify the pest first.</strong> Termites = hollow galleries + mud tubes. Borers = small exit holes + frass. Fungi = soft, cracked, or stringy wood. Correct identification determines the right treatment.</li>
<li><strong>Check moisture content before treatment.</strong> Timber should be below 20% moisture content for effective preservative absorption. Use a moisture meternever guess.</li>
<li><strong>Select the right preservative for the exposure.</strong> Interior door frames: borate or boron+polymer. Ground contact: CCA or creosote. Eco-sensitive areas: natural oils with frequent reapplication.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare the surface.</strong> Remove paint, dirt, and old finish. Drill 6-8mm holes at 30-45 angle along the grain for deep penetration in structural members.</li>
<li><strong>Apply preservative correctly.</strong> Brush-on: 2-3 coats, allowing drying between coats. Soaking: 24-48 hours for cut ends. Pressure treatment: use a certified facility for structural timber.</li>
<li><strong>Repair rotten beams by splicing.</strong> Cut back to sound wood, treat the exposed end, and use a structural splice with galvanized plates or a scarf joint. Never fill rot with puttyit will fail.</li>
<li><strong>Protect from moisture and sun.</strong> Install drip caps on door frames, ensure roof overhangs, apply UV-resistant varnish or paint on exposed surfaces, and maintain a 150mm clearance between timber and soil.</li>
<li><strong>Document and guarantee your work.</strong> Record treatment method, product used, and date. Offer a 5-year guarantee on treated timberthis builds customer trust and justifies premium pricing.</li>
</ol>
