1. How to Measure Windows and Doors Accurately

To measure a window or door opening for aluminum fabrication, you need the width and height of the rough opening (the hole in the wall where the frame will sit). Use a steel tape measure (5m or 8m) and always measure in millimeters. For width, measure at three points: top, middle, and bottom of the opening. Write down the smallest number. For height, measure left, center, and right. Again, take the smallest. For example, if top width is 1200mm, middle is 1195mm, bottom is 1198mm, the width is 1195mm. This ensures the frame fits even if the wall is not perfectly straight.

For doors, also measure the diagonal from top-left to bottom-right and top-right to bottom-left. If the two diagonals differ by more than 6mm, the opening is out of square and you must note that. You will need to either adjust the frame or plan for packing. Always measure from the inside face of the wall to the inside face of the opposite wall. Do not measure from the outside because plaster or cladding can change the thickness.

If the opening has a sill or threshold, measure from the top of the sill to the top of the opening. For windows, measure from the bottom of the opening (where the frame will rest) to the top. Never assume the opening is a perfect rectangle; old buildings in Africa often have uneven walls. Use a spirit level to check if the sill is level. If not, measure at the highest point of the sill and note the difference.

Record all measurements in a notebook or on your phone. Take a photo of the opening with the tape measure visible for reference. Double-check every number before leaving the site. A 5mm mistake can mean a window that does not fit, costing you time and money. Remember, aluminum frames cannot be easily cut and re-welded on site, so accuracy is critical.

2. Using a Tape Measure and Laser Measure

A steel tape measure is your basic tool. Choose one with a blade width of at least 16mm and a length of 5m or 8m. The hook at the end should be loose so it can slide for inside and outside measurements. When measuring, hook the end on one side of the opening and extend the tape to the other side. Keep the tape straight and taut. Read the measurement at the point where the tape meets the opposite edge. For accuracy, look directly at the mark, not at an angle. Most tapes show millimeters and centimeters; always use millimeters to avoid confusion.

A laser measure is faster and more accurate for long distances. Tools like Bosch GLM 50 or Leica DISTO are common. Turn it on, place the base against one side of the opening, and press the button. The laser dot hits the opposite side, and the distance appears on the screen. For width, measure from left to right. For height, measure from bottom to top. Laser measures can also calculate area and volume, but for windows and doors you only need length. They work well in bright sunlight if you use a target plate. Always check the battery before going to site.

When using a tape, avoid the common mistake of reading the wrong side of the tape. Some tapes have imperial on one side and metric on the other. Stick to metric. If you use a laser, make sure the device is set to millimeters, not inches. Also, laser measures can be fooled by reflective surfaces like glass or polished tiles. In those cases, place a piece of white paper at the target point.

For both tools, measure each dimension at least twice. If the two readings differ by more than 2mm, take a third reading and use the average. Record the numbers immediately. In Africa, dust and heat can affect tape measures; keep them clean and dry. A laser measure costs more but saves time on large sites. For a beginner, start with a good tape measure and upgrade later.

3. Checking for Square, Level, and Plumb

Square means the opening has 90-degree corners. Level means horizontal surfaces are flat, not sloping. Plumb means vertical surfaces are straight up and down. These three checks are essential before you order an aluminum frame. If the opening is out of square, the frame will not fit properly, and you will have gaps or difficulty opening the window.

To check square, measure the two diagonals of the opening. For a window 1200mm wide and 1500mm high, the diagonal should be the same both ways. Use a tape measure from the top-left corner to the bottom-right corner, then from top-right to bottom-left. If the difference is more than 6mm, the opening is out of square. Note the difference and decide if you need to pack the frame or adjust the wall.

To check level, use a spirit level (600mm or 1200mm long). Place it on the sill or floor where the frame will sit. If the bubble is not centered, the surface is not level. Mark the high point. For a door, check the floor threshold. If it slopes, water may run in. You may need to use packers under the frame to make it level.

To check plumb, hold the spirit level vertically against the left and right sides of the opening. The bubble should be centered. If not, the wall is leaning. This is common in old buildings. You can either fix the wall or make the frame slightly smaller and pack it. Always check plumb at the top, middle, and bottom of each side.

Use a plumb bob (a weight on a string) for tall openings. Hang it from the top corner and see if the string touches the bottom corner. If it does not, the wall is out of plumb. Record all deviations. In your fabrication order, specify that the frame must be made to the smallest dimensions and that packers will be used. Never assume the opening is perfect. In Africa, many walls are built by hand, so deviations are normal. Your job is to measure and document them.

4. Assessing Wall Types: Block, Brick, and Concrete

The wall type determines how you fix the aluminum frame and what tools you need. In Africa, you will encounter three main types: concrete block (also called cement block or cinder block), clay brick, and reinforced concrete. Each has different hardness and drilling requirements.

Concrete blocks are large, hollow, and relatively soft. They are common in residential buildings. To fix a frame, you drill with a masonry bit (6mm or 8mm) and use plastic wall plugs and screws. The blocks can crumble if you drill too fast. Use a hammer drill on low speed. For a window, you typically fix through the frame into the wall every 600mm. If the block is hollow, use expanding anchors or chemical anchors for better grip.

Clay bricks are smaller, solid, and harder than blocks. They are often used in older buildings. Drilling requires a hammer drill and a carbide-tipped bit. Bricks can crack if you drill too close to the edge. Keep at least 50mm from the edge. Use screws and plugs, or for heavy doors, use anchor bolts. Brick walls are usually straighter than block walls, but they can still be out of plumb.

Reinforced concrete is the hardest. It contains steel rebar. You cannot drill through rebar with a standard masonry bit. You need a hammer drill with a concrete bit, and you must avoid rebar. Use a rebar detector or drill slowly and feel for resistance. If you hit rebar, move the hole 50mm to the side. For fixing, use heavy-duty anchors or cast-in inserts. Concrete walls are often found in high-rise buildings and are very strong but difficult to modify.

Before drilling, test the wall with a small hammer. A dull sound means hollow block; a sharp sound means solid brick or concrete. Also check for plaster thickness. Plaster can be 10-20mm thick and may hide the true wall type. Always wear safety glasses and a dust mask when drilling. In Africa, dust from concrete and brick is a health hazard. Use a vacuum or wet the area to reduce dust.

5. Documenting Measurements for Fabrication

Documentation is the bridge between the site and the factory. If you write measurements on a scrap of paper and lose it, you waste time and money. Use a standard form or a notebook with pre-printed columns. For each window or door, record: location (e.g., 'Master bedroom, north wall'), opening width (smallest), opening height (smallest), diagonal difference, wall type, and any notes (e.g., 'sill not level, 10mm slope').

Take clear photos. Use your phone. Take one photo of the whole opening, one close-up of the tape measure showing the width, and one showing the height. Include a reference object like your hand or a pencil for scale. Name the photos with the location and date. For example, 'MasterBedroom_Window_2025-03-15.jpg'. Store them in a folder on your phone or cloud. If you have a site plan, mark each opening with a number and use that number in your notes.

Create a simple sketch for each opening. Draw the shape (rectangle, arch, etc.) and write the dimensions on the lines. Note which way the window or door opens (inward, outward, sliding). For doors, note the hinge side (left or right) and whether it is a single or double door. For windows, note if it is fixed, casement, sliding, or awning. This information is critical for fabrication.

After measuring, review your notes with a colleague or your supervisor. Read the numbers aloud. Check that the smallest width and height are clearly marked. If you have a laser measure, download the data to your phone or write it down immediately. Never rely on memory. In Africa, network coverage can be poor, so keep a paper backup.

Finally, give a copy of your documentation to the fabricator. Include your contact number in case they have questions. A well-documented job reduces errors and speeds up installation. Remember, the fabricator is not on site; they only know what you tell them. So be precise and complete.

6. Common Measuring Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Mistake 1: Measuring only once. Always measure at least twice. If the numbers differ, measure a third time. For example, if you get 1200mm and 1203mm, take the smaller (1200mm) and note the difference. A single measurement can be off by 5mm if the tape slips.

Mistake 2: Using the wrong units. Mixing inches and millimeters causes major errors. In Africa, building codes use metric. Always use millimeters. If you have an imperial tape, ignore the inch side. Write 'mm' after every number.

Mistake 3: Not checking for square. Many installers assume the opening is square. If you don't check diagonals, the frame may not fit. Always measure both diagonals. If they differ by more than 6mm, note it and plan to pack the frame.

Mistake 4: Measuring from the wrong surface. For a window, measure from the inside face of the wall, not from the plaster or tiles. Plaster can be uneven. If you measure from the plaster, the frame may be too small. Remove any loose plaster or trim before measuring.

Mistake 5: Forgetting to account for tolerances. Aluminum frames need a small gap (5-10mm) around them for expansion and packing. So if the opening is 1200mm, the frame should be 1190-1195mm. Do not order the frame exactly the same size as the opening. Always subtract 5-10mm from the smallest width and height.

Mistake 6: Not recording the location. If you measure ten windows and don't label them, you will confuse which measurement belongs to which window. Use a numbering system and stick to it.

Mistake 7: Ignoring the wall type. If you use the wrong fixings, the frame can come loose. Always note the wall type and test with a hammer.

Mistake 8: Not taking photos. A photo can save you a return trip. Take photos of every opening with the tape measure visible.

Mistake 9: Rushing. Measuring is not a race. Take your time. A 10-minute measurement can save hours of rework. In Africa, sites can be hot and noisy, but focus on the numbers.

Mistake 10: Not communicating. If you find a problem (e.g., out of square), tell your supervisor immediately. Do not hide it. The fabricator can adjust the frame if they know in advance.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Measuring and Site Assessment for Aluminum Windows & Doors

In African aluminum fabrication, the difference between a profitable job and a costly rework often comes down to the site assessment. A window that is measured 10 mm too narrow cannot be "stretched" on installation day. In markets where a single fabricator may serve a 200 km radius â from Nairobi's Kikuyu trading centre to Kisumu's lakeside estates, or from Accra's Spintex Road to Kumasi's Adum â a return trip to re-measure or re-make a unit can wipe out the entire margin on a project. This Practical Reference consolidates the measurement and site-assessment rules that keep fabrication shops profitable: how to measure, what tools to trust, how to verify geometry, how to read the wall, how to document for the factory, and the mistakes that quietly destroy margins.

Aluminum systems are unforgiving. Unlike timber, which can be planed on site, or uPVC, which can be shimmed with larger trims, aluminum profiles are cut to fixed lengths and assembled with fixed corner cleats. The opening must therefore be correct before fabrication begins. This lesson treats measurement as a commercial control point, not a technical formality.

1. The Measurement Workflow

Measure in a fixed sequence so that no dimension is forgotten and every number is traceable to a location. The standard sequence used by most East and West African fabricators is:

  1. Identify the unit â room, elevation, and a unique reference (e.g., "Block B, Unit 4, Bedroom 2, East").
  2. Measure the structural opening â not the old frame, not the plaster line. The structural opening is the masonry or concrete reveal that the new frame must sit within.
  3. Record width at three heights â top, middle, bottom â and take the smallest as the governing width.
  4. Record height at three positions â left, centre, right â and take the smallest as the governing height.
  5. Measure the reveal depth (wall thickness) at four points to confirm the frame will sit correctly and to select the right sub-frame or fixing method.
  6. Check diagonals to confirm the opening is square.
  7. Note obstructions â conduits, columns, beams, tiling returns, air-conditioner pipes.
  8. Photograph and sketch every opening, with the tape or laser reading visible.

The governing dimension rule is the single most important commercial discipline in the trade. If the opening is 1,200 mm at the top and 1,190 mm at the bottom, the frame must be made to fit 1,190 mm (minus the specified clearance). Fabricating to the larger figure guarantees that the frame will not enter the opening without destructive grinding â a cost that is never recovered from the client.

2. Tape Measure vs. Laser Measure

Both tools belong in every assessment kit, but they serve different purposes. A steel tape is the reference instrument for short, hard dimensions and for verifying that a laser is reading correctly. A laser measure is faster for long spans, high openings, and repeated dimensions, and it reduces the human error of a sagging tape.

3. Working with Tape and Laser in the Field

On a typical African site â often a self-build or a small contractor project with no site office â the assessor may be measuring in strong sunlight, on uneven ground, or from a ladder. Practical habits that protect accuracy:

  • Use a Class II tape with a positive lock and check the hook for bending; a bent hook adds 1â2 mm of error on every reading.
  • Zero the laser on the same face each time (either the front or back of the device). Mixing reference faces is a common source of 20â30 mm errors.
  • Take laser readings in shade where possible; strong direct sunlight can overwhelm the receiver on lower-cost units.
  • Verify every laser span over 2 m with a tape at least once per opening â this catches device drift and target-surface errors.
  • Record the tool and the reading in the site sheet so that the fabricator can trust the number.

For high openings â common in double-storey townhouses in Lagos, Nairobi, and Johannesburg â a laser with a minimum range of 40 m and an integrated spirit level is the professional choice. For narrow reveals and internal corners, a tape remains superior because the laser requires a flat target surface.

4. Checking Square, Level, and Plumb

Three geometric checks determine whether a standard rectangular frame will fit or whether a made-to-shape unit is required.

Square: Measure both diagonals of the opening. If they differ by more than the tolerance allowed by the system (commonly 3â5 mm for a 1.5 m opening), the opening is out of square. A frame forced into an out-of-square opening will bind on the sash and leak.

Level: Check the head and sill with a spirit level. A sill out of level by more than 5 mm across a 1.2 m opening will cause water to pool against the gasket and eventually enter the building â a serious risk in high-rainfall markets such as the Kenyan coast, the Ghanaian forest belt, and coastal Tanzania.

Plumb: Check both jambs. A jamb out of plumb by more than 5 mm will cause the sash to swing open or closed on its own, and will misalign the locking hardware.

Where the opening fails any of these checks, the correct commercial response is to price the remedial work â rendering, packing, or a made-to-shape frame â into the quotation before fabrication. Absorbing remedial costs after installation is the fastest way to turn a profitable job into a loss.

6. Documenting for Fabrication

The site sheet is the commercial contract between the assessor and the factory. A well-run African fabricator will not release a cutting list without a signed or photographed site sheet. The sheet must carry:

  • Project name, client, date, and assessor name.
  • Unit reference and elevation drawing or sketch.
  • Governing width and height, clearly marked as minimum.
  • Reveal depth and wall type.
  • Square, level, and plumb results with tolerances.
  • Photographs of every opening with the tape or laser reading visible.
  • Special notes â obstructions, tiling returns, security bars, air-conditioner sleeves.
  • Client signature or WhatsApp confirmation for the recorded dimensions.

In markets where the client is often the homeowner and the fabricator is a small workshop, a WhatsApp-confirmed site sheet is a legitimate and legally useful record. It prevents the classic dispute: "I told you the window was 1,200 mm" versus "the opening was 1,190 mm".

â  SAFETY WARNING â Working at height and on active sites

Measuring openings above ground floor, on scaffolding, or on a site with active construction carries serious risk of falls, falling objects, and contact with live services. In African markets where site safety enforcement varies, the assessor must not rely on the contractor. Always: (1) use a ladder rated for the load and set it on firm, level ground â never on blocks or rubble; (2) wear a hard hat and safety boots on any site with overhead work; (3) confirm that electrical conduits and water pipes are isolated before probing or drilling near reveals; (4) use a two-person team for any opening above 2.5 m; and (5) never measure from an unguarded edge or a partially built slab. Falls remain a leading cause of serious injury on construction sites across Africa, and a single incident will cost far more than any contract margin.

7. Common Measuring Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

  • Measuring the old frame instead of the structural opening. Always remove or clearly identify the structural reveal before measuring.
  • Using the largest dimension. Always use the smallest width and height as the governing dimensions.
  • Forgetting the reveal depth. A frame that is too deep will not sit flush; too shallow and it will not cover the reveal.
  • Ignoring obstructions. Columns, beams, conduits, and tiling returns can reduce the usable opening by 50 mm or more.
  • Mixing laser reference faces. Zero on one face and stay on it for the whole opening.
  • Not photographing the reading. A number without a photo is a dispute waiting to happen.
  • Skipping the diagonal check. Squareness failures are the most expensive to fix after fabrication.
  • No client confirmation. A signed or WhatsApp-confirmed site sheet is the cheapest insurance in the trade.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Identify the unit by room, elevation, and unique reference before measuring.
  2. Measure the structural opening, not the old frame or plaster line.
  3. Record width at three heights and height at three positions; use the smallest values.
  4. Measure reveal depth at four points and note the wall type.
  5. Check both diagonals, level of sill and head, and plumb of both jambs.
  6. Photograph every opening with the tape or laser reading visible.
  7. Note all obstructions, services, and finishing returns.
  8. Complete a site sheet and obtain client confirmation before releasing the cutting list.
