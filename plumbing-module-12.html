1. Water Supply Systems in African Buildings

Welcome to Module 12, Lesson 1. This lesson covers the practical design, installation, and maintenance of water supply systems in buildings across Africa. Whether you are working in Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, or a rural district, the principles remain the same but the context changes everything. You must understand how water gets from the source to the tap reliably, safely, and affordably. In most African cities, the municipal supply is intermittent. That means you cannot rely on constant pressure. Your design must include storage. The typical system starts with a connection from the main line, then a water meter, then a storage tank. From the tank, you either use gravity or a booster pump to distribute water to fixtures. Gravity systems work well where you have a tall building or an elevated tank stand. A tank placed 10 meters above the highest tap gives you roughly 1 bar of pressure. That is enough for most domestic use. But for multi-storey buildings, you need a pump. In many parts of Africa, power outages are common. So you must design for backup. That could be a manual pump, a solar pump, or a generator. Solar pumps are increasingly popular because they reduce running costs and work well in sunny climates. When sizing a tank, calculate daily demand. A typical person uses 50 to 100 liters per day for domestic purposes. For a family of six, that is 300 to 600 liters. Add 20 percent for cleaning and losses. So a 1000-liter tank is a good starting point for a small home. For commercial buildings, you need a bigger tank. A restaurant might use 2000 liters per day. A small hotel might use 5000 liters. Always round up. Never undersize a tank. The cost of a larger tank is small compared to the cost of running out of water. Pipes must be sized correctly. If pipes are too small, you get low pressure and noise. If too large, you waste money. Use the fixture unit method. Each fixture has a demand in fixture units. A sink might be 1 unit, a toilet 3 units, a shower 2 units. Add them up and use a pipe sizing chart. In Africa, we often use PVC or PPR pipes for cold water. For hot water, use PPR or copper. Galvanized steel is common but corrodes faster in humid coastal areas. Always use pressure-rated pipes. For underground lines, use heavy-duty PVC or HDPE. HDPE is flexible and resists ground movement. It is ideal for areas with shifting soils. When installing, bury pipes below the frost line if you have frost, but in most of Africa, you bury them at least 60 cm deep to avoid damage from traffic and farming. Use sand bedding around pipes to protect them from rocks. Test all pipes before covering them. Pressure test at 1.5 times the working pressure for at least one hour. Fix any leaks immediately. Leaks waste water and money. In many African cities, water is expensive. A leaking pipe can double your bill. So teach your clients to check for leaks monthly. Look for damp patches, running meters when no water is used, and unexplained high bills. Maintenance is key. Clean tanks every six months. Check pumps and valves. Replace worn washers. Keep a log of all maintenance. This helps you predict failures and schedule repairs. For business, offer maintenance contracts. Charge a monthly fee to inspect and service systems. This gives you steady income and builds trust. Many plumbers in Africa only do emergency repairs. That is a mistake. Recurring revenue is the backbone of a successful plumbing business. Also, consider water treatment. In many areas, water is hard or contaminated. Install filters and softeners. A simple sediment filter removes dirt. A carbon filter removes chlorine and odors. A water softener removes calcium and magnesium. This protects pipes and appliances. For drinking water, recommend reverse osmosis or UV treatment. These systems need maintenance too. So you can sell service plans. When you design a system, think about future expansion. Leave space for an extra tank. Use larger pipes than you need now. It costs a little more but saves a lot later. Document everything. Take photos of before and after. Keep a folder for each client. This helps when you need to troubleshoot. It also shows professionalism. In rural areas, you might work with boreholes. A borehole pump fills a tank. Then gravity feeds the house. You need to size the pump based on borehole depth and tank height. Use a pump with a pressure switch and a bladder tank. This gives consistent pressure. Solar pumps are excellent for rural areas. They have no fuel cost and low maintenance. But you need a large tank because you can only pump during sunny hours. A 5000-liter tank is common for a rural home. For communities, you might design a small water network. That requires careful planning. You need a source, a tank, and a distribution line. You also need a committee to manage it. As a plumber, you can train local operators. That creates goodwill and future work. Always follow local plumbing codes. In many African countries, codes are based on British or French standards. But they are adapted. Know the code for your area. If there is no code, use good practice. Never cut corners. Your reputation is your business. A single bad job can ruin you. So do it right the first time. In summary, water supply systems in Africa must be robust, simple, and maintainable. Design for intermittent supply. Use storage. Size pipes and tanks correctly. Test everything. Offer maintenance. Think business. That is how you succeed. Next lesson, we will cover drainage and sanitation.

2. Drainage and Sanitation Systems for African Conditions

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 12. This lesson focuses on drainage and sanitation. In Africa, poor drainage causes disease, property damage, and costly repairs. Your job is to design and install systems that work despite challenges like heavy rains, poor soil, and limited infrastructure. Start with the basics. There are two types of drainage: stormwater and sanitary. Stormwater comes from rain. Sanitary drainage carries human waste and greywater. Never mix them. In many African cities, combined sewers exist, but they overflow during rains. So separate systems are better. For stormwater, you need gutters, downpipes, and surface channels. Gutters must be sized for the roof area and rainfall intensity. In tropical Africa, rainfall can be 50 mm per hour or more. Use a rule of thumb: 1 square meter of roof needs 1 square centimeter of gutter cross-section. So a 100 square meter roof needs a 100 square centimeter gutter. That is about 10 cm wide by 10 cm deep. But always check local rainfall data. Downpipes should be at least 75 mm diameter for small roofs, 110 mm for larger ones. Space them every 10 to 15 meters. Use leaf guards to prevent blockages. For surface drainage, use concrete channels or French drains. French drains are trenches filled with gravel and a perforated pipe. They work well in areas with sandy soil. In clay soil, they clog. So use concrete channels instead. Slope all channels away from buildings. A minimum slope of 1 percent is good. That is 1 cm drop per meter. For sanitary drainage, you need soil pipes, waste pipes, and vents. Soil pipes carry toilet waste. Waste pipes carry sinks, showers, and baths. Vents allow air into the system so water flows smoothly. Without vents, you get gurgling and slow drains. Every fixture needs a trap. A trap holds water to block sewer gases. The water seal should be at least 50 mm deep. P-traps are common. S-traps are banned in many codes because they can siphon. Use P-traps or bottle traps. The main soil pipe should be at least 100 mm diameter. Branch pipes can be 50 mm for sinks and 75 mm for toilets. Slope horizontal pipes at 2 percent for 50 mm pipes and 1 percent for 100 mm pipes. That means 2 cm drop per meter for small pipes. If slope is too steep, water runs away and leaves solids behind. If too shallow, water flows too slowly. So get it right. Vent pipes should be the same size as the soil pipe or one size smaller. They must extend above the roof. In Africa, many buildings have flat roofs. So run the vent up a wall and use an air admittance valve if you cannot go through the roof. Air admittance valves are cheaper and easier but they can fail. So use them only where allowed. For septic tanks, they are common in areas without sewers. A septic tank separates solids and liquids. Solids settle and are digested by bacteria. Liquids flow to a drain field. Size the tank based on daily flow. A house with 6 people produces about 600 liters per day. A septic tank should hold at least 2 days of flow. So 1200 liters. But add 50 percent for sludge storage. So 1800 liters. A 2000-liter tank is good. The drain field must be sized based on soil percolation. Do a percolation test. Dig a hole, fill with water, and see how fast it drains. If it drains too fast, effluent can pollute groundwater. If too slow, it backs up. In sandy soil, use a larger drain field. In clay, you need a raised bed or a mound system. Never discharge septic effluent into a storm drain or a river. That causes disease. In many African cities, septic tanks are emptied manually. That is dangerous. Promote safe emptying by licensed operators. As a plumber, you can offer septic tank cleaning as a service. Buy a vacuum truck or partner with someone who has one. That is a good business. For greywater, you can reuse it for irrigation. But use only from baths and sinks, not from kitchens or toilets. Kitchen greywater has fats and food particles. It can clog pipes. So use a grease trap. A grease trap is a box where fats float and are removed. Clean it weekly. For toilets, consider waterless options. In areas with water scarcity, composting toilets or pour-flush toilets with twin pits work well. They save water and produce fertilizer. But they need training. So teach users how to manage them. In public buildings, install urinals with waterless technology. They save thousands of liters per year. For maintenance, inspect drainage systems monthly. Look for blockages, cracks, and root intrusion. Roots are a big problem in Africa. Trees like ficus and mango send roots into pipes. Use root killer or replace pipes with PVC. Never use metal pipes underground. They rust. For blockages, use a plunger or a drain snake. Avoid chemicals. They damage pipes and the environment. If you must use chemicals, use enzymes. They are safer. For business, offer drain cleaning as a service. Buy a high-pressure jetter. It costs money but pays for itself quickly. Many hotels and restaurants need regular drain cleaning. Charge per visit or per meter. Also, sell drain guards to prevent blockages. They are cheap and easy to install. For new construction, work with the builder early. Ensure the drainage plan is correct. Check the slope of the ground. Ensure water flows away from the building. If the ground slopes toward the building, you need a French drain or a swale. A swale is a shallow ditch that carries water away. Plant grass in it to prevent erosion. For sanitation, promote handwashing. Install handwash stations near toilets. Use a tippy-tap or a simple bucket with a tap. That reduces disease. As a plumber, you can build and sell these stations. They are cheap and save lives. In summary, drainage and sanitation are critical. Do not cut corners. Use the right materials. Size correctly. Slope correctly. Vent properly. Maintain regularly. And always think about the business side. You are not just a plumber. You are a problem solver. People will pay for solutions. So learn to sell those solutions. 3. Plumbing Tools and Materials for African Markets

Welcome to Lesson 3. This lesson is about tools and materials. In Africa, you need tools that are durable, affordable, and easy to repair. You also need to know which materials work best in local conditions. Let us start with tools. Every plumber needs a basic hand tool kit. That includes pipe wrenches, adjustable wrenches, pliers, screwdrivers, a hammer, a chisel, a hacksaw, and a tape measure. Buy good quality. Cheap tools break and cause injuries. In many African markets, you can find tools from China, India, and Europe. Chinese tools are affordable but may wear out faster. European tools are expensive but last longer. For a beginner, start with mid-range tools. Replace them as you grow. You also need specialty tools. A pipe cutter is essential for copper and PPR. A deburring tool smooths cut ends. A flaring tool makes flares for copper pipes. A press tool joins PPR and copper without soldering. Press tools are expensive but save time. If you cannot afford one, use compression fittings. They work well for small jobs. For threading galvanized steel, you need a pipe threader. Manual threaders are cheap but slow. Electric threaders are fast but costly. For most African jobs, manual is fine. You also need a drain snake. A manual snake is good for small blockages. A drill-powered snake is better. For serious blockages, use a high-pressure jetter. That is a big investment. But you can rent one or hire a service. For leak detection, use a listening device or a thermal camera. These are expensive. Start with a simple stethoscope. It works surprisingly well. For testing, you need a pressure gauge and a test pump. You can make a test pump from a bicycle pump and a gauge. But buy a proper one when you can. For safety, wear gloves, goggles, and boots. In Africa, many plumbers work without safety gear. That is a mistake. Injuries cost you time and money. So protect yourself. Now materials. Pipes are the most important material. In Africa, common pipes are PVC, PPR, HDPE, galvanized steel, and copper. PVC is cheap and easy to install. Use it for cold water and drainage. It cannot handle hot water. PPR handles hot and cold water. It is joined by heat fusion. That requires a fusion tool. PPR is popular in East Africa. HDPE is flexible and comes in coils. Use it for underground water lines. It is tough and resists chemicals. Galvanized steel is strong but corrodes. Use it only where required by code. Copper is excellent but expensive. Use it for hot water and where high pressure is needed. For fittings, use the same material as the pipe. Do not mix metals without a dielectric union. That causes corrosion. For valves, use brass or stainless steel. Plastic valves crack under pressure. For tanks, use polyethylene or fiberglass. Poly tanks are common and affordable. They come in sizes from 100 liters to 10000 liters. Fiberglass tanks are stronger but more expensive. For pumps, use centrifugal pumps for boosting pressure. Use submersible pumps for boreholes. Use jet pumps for shallow wells. Choose pumps with stainless steel impellers. They last longer. For solar pumps, use a DC pump with a controller. They are efficient and reliable. For water treatment, use sediment filters, carbon filters, and softeners. For drinking water, use reverse osmosis or UV. UV is cheaper but needs clear water. RO removes everything but wastes water. In Africa, water waste is a problem. So use RO only for drinking water, not for whole-house. For fixtures, choose low-flow toilets and showerheads. They save water and money. In many African cities, water is metered. So low-flow fixtures pay for themselves. For taps, use ceramic cartridge taps. They last longer than rubber washers. For toilets, use dual-flush. They save up to 60 percent water. For urinals, use waterless. They save 100 percent. For pipes, always use the correct size. Do not reduce size to save money. That causes pressure loss. For joints, use solvent cement for PVC. Use Teflon tape for threaded joints. Use hemp and paste for large threads. For PPR, use fusion welding. For HDPE, use compression or butt fusion. For copper, use soldering or press fittings. For galvanized, use threaded joints. Always follow the manufacturer instructions. Do not guess. For tools, keep them clean and dry. Oil moving parts. Replace worn parts. Store tools in a toolbox. Do not leave them in the sun. UV damages plastic and rubber. For materials, buy from reputable suppliers. Fake materials are common in Africa. Fake PVC pipes crack easily. Fake pumps burn out. So check for certification marks. Ask for references. Buy in bulk to save money. But do not overbuy. Storage is a problem. Keep materials off the ground. Use pallets. Cover them with tarps. For business, you can sell materials to clients. That adds profit. But do not overcharge. Be fair. Your reputation is worth more than a quick profit. Also, consider renting tools. Many plumbers cannot afford a jetter or a press tool. So rent them for big jobs. That keeps your costs low. For apprentices, teach them to use tools safely. Show them how to maintain tools. That builds a skilled workforce. In summary, use the right tools and materials for the job. Buy quality when you can. Maintain everything. Think about the local market. What works in Europe may not work in Africa. So adapt. Be practical. Be business-minded. 4. Plumbing Codes and Standards in Africa

Welcome to Lesson 4. This lesson covers plumbing codes and standards. In Africa, codes vary by country. Some countries follow British Standards, some follow French Standards, and some have their own. But the goal is the same: safe, reliable, and sanitary plumbing. You must know the code for your area. If you do not, you risk failed inspections, fines, and dangerous installations. Start with water supply codes. Most codes require a minimum pressure at fixtures. For example, a shower needs at least 0.5 bar. A toilet needs 0.3 bar. If pressure is too low, you need a booster pump. Codes also require backflow prevention. A backflow preventer stops dirty water from entering the clean water supply. This is critical in hospitals, laboratories, and industries. In homes, use a simple check valve. In commercial buildings, use a reduced pressure zone valve. Test it annually. Codes also require insulation on hot water pipes. In Africa, many plumbers skip insulation. That wastes energy. Insulate all hot water pipes. Use foam or fiberglass. For cold water pipes, insulate where condensation occurs. That prevents mold. For drainage codes, slope is critical. Codes specify minimum slopes. For 50 mm pipes, slope 2 percent. For 100 mm pipes, slope 1 percent. For 150 mm pipes, slope 0.5 percent. Never exceed 4 percent. Too steep causes solids to separate. Codes also require vents. Every building must have a vent pipe. It must extend above the roof. The vent must be at least 75 mm for a small house. For larger buildings, use a vent stack. Codes also require cleanouts. A cleanout is a capped opening for cleaning blockages. Install cleanouts at the base of stacks, at changes in direction, and every 15 meters on horizontal runs. Cleanouts must be accessible. Do not bury them. For septic tanks, codes specify setbacks. A septic tank must be at least 15 meters from a well. It must be at least 3 meters from a property line. It must be at least 1 meter from a building. The drain field must be at least 30 meters from a water source. These distances prevent contamination. In dense African neighborhoods, these setbacks are often impossible. So use alternative systems. A bio-digester or a composting toilet may be allowed. Check with local authorities. For materials, codes specify standards. PVC pipes must meet ASTM or ISO standards. PPR pipes must meet DIN standards. Copper pipes must meet ASTM B88. Fittings must match. Do not mix standards. For example, do not use a British standard fitting on an American standard pipe. It will leak. For installation, codes require permits. In many African cities, you need a permit for new plumbing or major renovations. Do not work without a permit. It can lead to fines and demolition. As a plumber, help your clients get permits. That builds trust. For inspections, codes require certain tests. A pressure test for water lines. A water test for drainage lines. A smoke test for vents. A flow test for fixtures. Always perform these tests. Document the results. Give a copy to the client. For safety, codes require protection from scalding. Hot water must not exceed 50 degrees Celsius at the tap. Use a thermostatic mixing valve. Codes also require protection from electrical hazards. Bond metal pipes to the electrical ground. That prevents shock. For fire protection, codes require fire sprinklers in large buildings. If you are not trained in fire sprinklers, do not install them. Partner with a specialist. For backflow, codes require annual testing. Offer this as a service. It is a recurring revenue stream. For grease traps, codes require them in restaurants. Size them based on flow. Clean them regularly. For oil and sand interceptors, codes require them in garages and car washes. They prevent pollution. For hospitals, codes are stricter. They require medical gas piping. That is a specialty. Do not attempt it without training. For laboratories, codes require acid-neutralizing tanks. They prevent corrosion of pipes. For schools, codes require drinking fountains. They must be accessible. For public buildings, codes require accessible toilets. They must have grab bars and wide doors. As a plumber, know these requirements. They create business opportunities. For example, you can retrofit old buildings to meet accessibility codes. That is a growing market in Africa. For codes, get a copy. Read it. Keep it on your phone. Many codes are available online. If you cannot find it, ask the local building authority. They will help. For business, offer code compliance inspections. Many building owners do not know if their plumbing meets code. You can inspect and report. Then fix any issues. That is a valuable service. Also, teach your clients about codes. They will appreciate your professionalism. In summary, codes are not optional. They protect health and safety. They also protect your business. A code-compliant job is a job you can be proud of. So learn the codes. Follow them. And use them to grow your business. 5. Building a Profitable Plumbing Business in Africa

Welcome to Lesson 5. This lesson is about business. You can be the best plumber in your city, but if you cannot run a business, you will struggle. In Africa, plumbing is a huge market. Every building needs plumbing. Every building needs repairs. So there is plenty of work. But competition is also high. To succeed, you must be professional, reliable, and business-minded. Start with pricing. Many plumbers undercharge. They think low prices will get more work. But low prices attract bad clients. They also leave you with no profit. So calculate your costs. Include fuel, tools, materials, labor, and overhead. Add a profit margin of at least 20 percent. For emergency calls, charge more. For after-hours work, charge double. Do not be afraid to charge what you are worth. Clients who value quality will pay. Clients who only want cheap will go elsewhere. Let them. Next, get a license. In many African countries, plumbing is regulated. You need a license to work legally. Get one. It shows credibility. It also allows you to get permits. If you cannot get a license, join an association. They can help. Next, build a brand. Choose a name. Make a logo. Print business cards. Create a simple website or a WhatsApp business profile. Use social media. Post photos of your work. Ask happy clients for reviews. In Africa, word of mouth is powerful. But social media amplifies it. So use both. Next, manage your time. Plan your day. Group jobs by location. That saves fuel. Keep a calendar. Use a notebook or a phone app. Arrive on time. Call if you are late. Clients hate waiting. Next, manage your money. Open a business bank account. Keep personal and business money separate. Save for taxes. Save for tools. Save for emergencies. Do not spend everything you earn. In Africa, many plumbers live job to job. That is stressful. So budget. Track income and expenses. Use a simple spreadsheet. Next, hire help. You cannot do everything alone. Hire an apprentice. Train them well. Pay them fairly. Give them responsibility. That frees you to do more skilled work. It also grows your capacity. Next, offer maintenance contracts. This is the key to steady income. A maintenance contract means you visit a client every month or every quarter. You check their plumbing. You fix small issues before they become big. You charge a monthly fee. This gives you predictable income. It also builds loyalty. Target hotels, restaurants, schools, and offices. They need regular maintenance. Next, diversify. Do not only do repairs. Offer installations. Offer drain cleaning. Offer water treatment. Offer solar pump installation. Offer septic tank cleaning. The more services you offer, the more you earn. Next, invest in tools. Buy the best tools you can afford. A high-pressure jetter pays for itself. A press tool saves time. A thermal camera finds leaks fast. These tools let you charge more and work faster. Next, build relationships. With suppliers. With builders. With real estate agents. With facility managers. These people can send you work. Treat them well. Give them a small commission for referrals. That is normal in Africa. Next, handle complaints. Every business gets complaints. How you handle them matters. Listen. Apologize. Fix the problem. Do not argue. A happy client tells others. An unhappy client tells everyone. So protect your reputation. Next, keep learning. Plumbing technology changes. New pipes. New pumps. New codes. Attend workshops. Read manuals. Watch videos. Join online groups. The more you know, the more you earn. Next, think about scale. Can you serve more clients? Can you open a shop? Can you sell materials? Can you train other plumbers? Many successful plumbers in Africa have moved from doing the work to managing the work. They run teams. They run shops. They run training centers. That is the path to wealth. Next, give back. Train young people. Help your community. Install a free tap at a school. Fix a leak at a clinic. That builds goodwill. It also builds your brand. Next, stay ethical. Do not cheat clients. Do not use fake materials. Do not overcharge. Do not cut corners. Your integrity is your most valuable asset. In Africa, trust is everything. If people trust you, they will call you. They will recommend you. They will pay you. So be trustworthy. In summary, plumbing is not just a trade. It is a business. Treat it like one. Price correctly. Manage money. Hire help. Offer contracts. Diversify. Invest. Build relationships. Handle complaints. Keep learning. Scale up. Give back. Stay ethical. If you do these things, you will not just survive. You will thrive. This concludes Module 12. You now have the knowledge to design, install, maintain, and profit from plumbing systems in Africa. Go out there and build something great. Remember, every drop counts. Every job counts. Every client counts. So do your best. And never stop learning.

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020;background:#fdecec;padding:14px;margin:20px 0;font-family:Arial;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING WATER QUALITY AND CHEMICALS</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7a0015;">Plumbing work frequently involves contact with sewage, chlorinated water, and disinfection chemicals such as calcium hypochlorite and sodium hypochlorite. These are hazardous. Always wear nitrile gloves, eye protection and a dust mask when handling chlorine powders. Never mix chlorine with acids or ammonia toxic gas is released. When working on drainage or septic systems, assume the contents are infectious: use gloves, cover cuts, and wash thoroughly with soap after work. If you or a worker develops diarrhoea, fever or skin infection after sewage contact, seek medical attention. Store all chemicals in original labelled containers, away from food and children, and never decant into drinking water bottles. Refer to WHO (2017) <em>Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality</em> and the WHO <em>Water Safety Plan</em> manual for safe handling and disinfection procedures.</p>
</div>

<h3>3. Water Quality Testing: What to Test and When</h3>

<p>In Africa, water quality is the single largest determinant of whether a plumbing installation is judged a success. Boreholes and shallow wells are frequently contaminated with <em>E. coli</em>, nitrates and iron. Your role as a plumber is not to certify potability that is the job of a laboratory but to sample correctly, advise on treatment, and install the right point-of-use protection.</p>

<p><strong>Minimum field test suite for any new installation:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Microbiological:</strong> total coliforms and <em>E. coli</em> (WHO recommends zero <em>E. coli</em> per 100 mL).</li>
<li><strong>Physical:</strong> turbidity (WHO recommends &lt;5 NTU, ideally &lt;1 NTU), colour, odour, pH (6.58.5).</li>
<li><strong>Chemical:</strong> nitrate (&lt;50 mg/L as NO), iron (&lt;0.3 mg/L aesthetic), fluoride (&lt;1.5 mg/L), arsenic (&lt;0.01 mg/L).</li>
<li><strong>Disinfection residual:</strong> free chlorine 0.20.5 mg/L at point of use where chlorination is practised.</li>
</ul>

<p>Sample using a sterile bottle, flame the tap or borehole outlet, run for two minutes, and deliver to the laboratory within six hours on ice. In the absence of a laboratory, use field test kits (HS vial test for faecal contamination, and portable colorimeters for chlorine and pH). Record results in a simple logbook and give the client a copy this is a business differentiator and a defence against liability.</p>

<h3>4. Costing Your Work: A Simple African Market Model</h3>

<p>Price plumbing work in four parts: materials, labour, equipment and overhead/profit. In most African markets, materials represent 5570% of the quotation, labour 2030%, and overhead and profit 1520%. Never quote a lump sum without a written bill of quantities. A simple model for a three-bedroom house with borehole connection, 1,000 L tank, pump, and full internal plumbing might break down as follows: materials 60%, labour 22%, transport and consumables 8%, overhead and profit 10%. Adjust to your market, but keep the structure it allows you to explain and defend your price when a client negotiates.</p>

<p><strong>Payment terms that protect you:</strong> 4050% mobilisation deposit, 30% on rough-in completion, and the balance on testing and handover. Never start work without a signed quotation and deposit. In many African markets, cash flow, not technical skill, is the reason plumbing businesses fail.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li>Confirm daily demand in litres using the Table 1 figures, and size the storage tank at 1.52.0 times daily demand.</li>
<li>Size rising mains and distribution pipes using Table 2, keeping velocity below 1.5 m/s to avoid water hammer and pump damage.</li>
<li>Specify material, pressure class and standard (ISO 1452 for uPVC, ISO 15874 for PPR) in every written quotation.</li>
<li>Test water for <em>E. coli</em>, turbidity, pH, nitrate, iron and fluoride before handover, and give the client a written result.</li>
<li>Install a first-flush diversion, sediment filter and, where chlorination is used, a residual chlorine test point.</li>
<li>Pressure-test all new pipework at 1.5 times working pressure for at least one hour before covering or boxing in.</li>
<li>Vent every soil stack to roof level and slope all drainage at 1:60 or steeper to the septic tank or sewer.</li>
<li>Use the four-part costing model (materials, labour, equipment, overhead/profit) and secure a 4050% mobilisation deposit before starting.</li>
</ol>
