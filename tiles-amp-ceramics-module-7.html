Lessons

1. Designing Tile Shapes and Sizes

Start by deciding what your tile will be used for. Floor tiles in a busy market need to be at least 30 cm x 30 cm and 10 mm thick. Wall tiles can be smaller, like 20 cm x 20 cm and 6 mm thick. Roof tiles are usually 40 cm long and 25 cm wide, with a curve or interlocking edge. Draw your design on paper first, using a ruler and pencil. Make a wooden or metal template so every tile is the same. In Africa, common shapes are squares, rectangles, and hexagons. For interlocking tiles, you need a tongue on one side and a groove on the other. Measure twice, cut once. Use a jigsaw or angle grinder to cut your template from 12 mm plywood or 3 mm steel. Sand the edges smooth so clay doesn't stick. Test your template by pressing it into soft clay. If the clay sticks, dust it with fine sand or talcum powder. Remember that clay shrinks about 10% when drying and firing. So if you want a finished tile of 30 cm, make your mold 33 cm. For mass production, you'll need multiple molds. Keep them identical by stacking templates and cutting together. Also think about drainage and grip. Floor tiles should have a slightly rough surface to prevent slipping. Roof tiles need a channel for water to run off. Draw your design with these practical points in mind. Finally, make a sample tile, dry it, and fire it. Check if the size and shape are what you wanted. Adjust your template if needed. This step saves you from wasting hundreds of tiles later.

2. Mass Production of Tiles Using Press Molds

To make many tiles quickly, you need a press mold. A simple hand press is a steel frame with a top and bottom plate. You fill the bottom plate with clay, then pull a lever to press the top plate down. This compacts the clay and gives a smooth surface. For a small workshop, you can make a press from a car jack and a strong steel frame. But for real mass production, use a hydraulic press that can apply 20 to 50 tons of pressure. That sounds expensive, but you can hire one or build a manual press with a 2-ton car jack. The key is consistent pressure. Too little pressure and the tile is weak; too much and it cracks. Start with 10 tons for a 30 cm x 30 cm tile. The clay must be prepared well. Mix dry clay powder with 15% water by weight. Let it sit for 24 hours to even out the moisture. Then wedge it to remove air bubbles. Air bubbles cause tiles to explode in the kiln. For each tile, weigh out the same amount of clay. A 30 cm x 30 cm tile that is 10 mm thick needs about 2.5 kg of clay. Place the clay in the mold, press down, and release. Remove the tile and place it on a drying board. You can make 100 tiles per hour with a two-person team: one fills and presses, one removes and stacks. Dry the tiles slowly in the shade for 3 to 5 days. Then fire them. With good molds and a steady rhythm, you can produce 500 tiles per day. That's enough to roof a small house or floor a shop. Remember to keep your molds clean and oiled with used engine oil so clay doesn't stick. Replace worn molds every 1000 tiles.

3. Creating Interlocking Tiles for Roofs and Floors

Interlocking tiles save time and mortar because they fit together like puzzle pieces. For roofs, the most common design in Africa is the S-shaped tile or the pantile. Each tile has a raised ridge on one side and a groove on the other. When you lay them, the ridge of one tile fits into the groove of the next. This stops rain from blowing in. To make them, you need a mold with a curved profile. You can carve a wooden mold from hardwood like mahogany or iroko. Or weld a steel mold. The mold should be 40 cm long, 25 cm wide, and have a 5 cm curve. For floors, interlocking tiles have tabs and slots on all four sides. They click together without adhesive. This is perfect for temporary floors or areas that get wet. To make floor interlocking tiles, your mold needs precise tabs. A 1 cm tab on two sides and a 1 cm slot on the other two sides works well. Use a CNC machine or a steady hand with a router to cut the mold. When pressing, fill the mold evenly. Press at 15 tons for floor tiles because they need to be dense. After pressing, trim the edges with a wire cutter while the clay is still soft. Dry the tiles on a flat rack so they don't warp. Fire them to 1000C. After firing, test the interlock by fitting two tiles together. They should hold tight but still be easy to separate. For roofs, overlap each tile by 10 cm. For floors, lay them on a bed of sand and tap them down with a rubber mallet. Interlocking tiles are strong and quick to install. A team of two can lay 20 square meters of floor tiles in a day. That's faster than cement tiles. So invest in good molds and you'll save money on labour and glue.

4. Decorating Tiles with African Motifs

African motifs make your tiles unique and sell for a higher price. You can decorate before or after firing. Before firing, the clay is soft and you can press patterns into it. Use wooden stamps carved with Adinkra symbols, Ndebele geometric shapes, or Kente cloth patterns. To make a stamp, draw your design on a 5 cm x 5 cm block of hardwood. Carve away the background so the design stands out 3 mm. Press the stamp into the tile when the clay is leather-hard (not too wet, not too dry). Use a mallet to tap it evenly. For repeating patterns, mark the tile with a grid first. Another method is sgraffito: paint the tile with a coloured slip (clay mixed with water and pigment), let it dry, then scratch through the slip with a nail or knife to reveal the clay body underneath. This gives a two-tone effect. You can also use a slip trailer to draw lines and dots. After firing, you can paint with underglaze colours. Mix ceramic pigments with a glaze base. Common colours are iron oxide for red, copper oxide for green, and cobalt oxide for blue. Paint your motif with a brush. Then dip the tile in a clear glaze and fire again to 1050C. The glaze makes the colours permanent and food-safe. For outdoor tiles, use a matte glaze that resists weathering. Remember that African motifs are often symmetrical. Use a ruler and compass to plan your design. Practice on paper first. Then make a set of tiles with the same motif. Sell them as a set for a kitchen backsplash or a tabletop. A set of 12 decorated tiles can sell for three times the price of plain tiles. So take time to learn one motif well, then repeat it perfectly.

5. Firing and Glazing Tiles for Durability

Firing turns soft clay into hard ceramic. You need a kiln. For small-scale production, build a kiln from firebricks and a metal drum. Or buy a second-hand electric kiln. The firing schedule is critical. First, bisque fire your tiles to 950C. This drives off water and carbon. Heat slowly: 100C per hour until 600C, then 150C per hour to 950C. Hold at 950C for 30 minutes. Then let it cool slowly overnight. Do not open the kiln hot or tiles will crack. After bisque firing, the tiles are porous and ready for glazing. Glaze is a glass coating. You can buy commercial glaze powder or make your own. A simple recipe: 40% feldspar, 30% silica, 20% whiting, 10% kaolin. Add water to make a creamy liquid. Dip each tile for 3 seconds. Wipe the bottom clean so it doesn't stick to the kiln shelf. For a glossy finish, fire to 1050C. For a matte finish, fire to 1100C. Hold for 20 minutes. The glaze melts and forms a waterproof surface. For roof tiles, you don't need glaze if you fire to 1100C because the clay becomes vitrified. But glaze does add colour and makes cleaning easier. Test your glaze on a small tile first. If it bubbles or crawls, adjust the recipe. Keep a log of every firing: temperature, time, and results. After firing, check each tile for cracks, warping, or glaze defects. Only sell perfect tiles. A well-fired tile can last 50 years. That's why firing and glazing are the most important steps for durability.

6. Quality Control for Tiles

Quality control starts before the clay is dug. Test your clay for shrinkage, strength, and water absorption. Make 10 test tiles from each batch. Dry them and fire them. Measure before and after. Shrinkage should be between 8% and 12%. If it's more, add sand or grog. Water absorption should be less than 6% for floor tiles. To test, weigh a dry tile, boil it in water for 2 hours, then weigh again. The difference is the water absorbed. If it's too high, fire to a higher temperature. During production, check every tile for cracks, warping, and thickness. Use a caliper to measure thickness. Floor tiles must be 10 mm 1 mm. Wall tiles 6 mm 0.5 mm. Check the edges: they should be straight and square. Use a try square. For interlocking tiles, test the fit with a sample. They should not wobble. After firing, sort tiles into grades. Grade A: perfect, no defects. Grade B: minor chips or colour variation. Grade C: cracks or warping. Sell Grade A at full price. Grade B at 30% discount. Grade C as seconds or break them for mosaic. Also test strength. Drop a 1 kg weight from 1 meter onto a tile. It should not break. For roof tiles, test by standing on one tile (if you weigh under 80 kg). It should hold. Keep records of every batch. If a customer complains, you can trace the problem. Finally, train your workers to check each tile as they handle it. A simple checklist: size, thickness, edges, surface, and colour. Reject any tile that fails. Quality control saves your reputation. In Africa, word of mouth travels fast. Good tiles bring repeat customers.

<h2>Practical Reference: Tile Making from Clay to Finished Product</h2>

<p>Tile manufacturing is one of the most bankable value-addition plays in African construction supply chains. Sub-Saharan Africa imports billions of dollars of ceramic tiles annually, yet many countries sit on suitable clay deposits, have low-cost labour, and face rising demand from housing, roofing, and flooring projects. This practical reference compresses the technical essentials of tile making into a business-first guide: what to test, what to buy, what to watch, and how to turn raw clay into a sellable, standards-compliant product.</p>

<p>Use this module alongside the six companion lessons Designing Tile Shapes and Sizes, Mass Production of Tiles Using Press Molds, Creating Interlocking Tiles for Roofs and Floors, Decorating Tiles with African Motifs, Firing and Glazing Tiles for Durability, and Quality Control for Tiles. The reference below is the operator's bench manual: numbers you can act on, checks you can run today, and links to free official guidance for deeper study.</p>

<h3>4. Business-First Production Notes</h3>

<p><strong>Start with the clay, not the kiln.</strong> Many African tile ventures fail because the founder buys a press and kiln before testing the local clay. Commission a full clay characterisation plasticity, shrinkage, iron content, firing curve before you spend on equipment. A clay that fires red at 950C is perfect for earthenware wall tiles and roofing tiles; it is not suitable for porcelain. Match the product to the clay, not the other way round.</p>

<p><strong>Drying is where money is lost silently.</strong> In humid tropical and coastal African climates, uncontrolled drying causes warping, cracking, and kiln explosions. Budget for a controlled drying room or tunnel with temperature and humidity control. The cost is recovered within months through reduced rejects.</p>

<p><strong>Firing fuel is your largest variable cost.</strong> Whether you fire with wood, LPG, natural gas, or heavy fuel oil, the kiln's thermal efficiency determines your margin. Insulate the kiln, recover waste heat for drying, and monitor the firing curve with a pyrometer and, ideally, a data logger. A 10% fuel saving on a 20,000-tile monthly run is a direct profit line.</p>

<p><strong>Decoration is your differentiation.</strong> African motifs Adinkra, Ndebele geometric patterns, Kente-inspired colourways, Berber symbols, Maasai beadwork palettes command premium prices in hospitality, retail, and export markets. Invest in screen printing, pad printing, or digital decoration capability once your base production is stable. A plain tile competes on price; a decorated tile competes on identity.</p>

<p><strong>Quality control is your sales licence.</strong> Formal contractors, government housing programmes, and export buyers require test certificates. Build a simple in-house QC lab: a water absorption test rig, a flexural strength jig, a vernier caliper for dimensional checks, and a warpage gauge. Log results per batch. This is not overhead it is the document that unlocks bigger contracts.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #c0392b;background:#fdecea;padding:16px;margin:24px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#c0392b;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING FIRING, GLAZING AND DUST HAZARDS</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;font-size:14px;">Tile making involves three serious health and safety risks that must be controlled before production begins:</p>
<ul style="margin:0;padding-left:20px;font-size:14px;">
<li><strong>Respirable crystalline silica dust</strong> from clay handling, pressing, and dry glazing causes silicosis and lung cancer. Use local exhaust ventilation, water suppression, and certified N95 or P2 respirators. Never dry-sweep clay dust.</li>
<li><strong>Kiln burns and carbon monoxide</strong> from firing. Kilns must be in a well-ventilated, separate structure with CO detectors, fire extinguishers, and no combustible materials within 3 metres. Never enter a kiln until it has cooled below 50C and been gas-tested.</li>
<li><strong>Lead and heavy-metal glazes</strong> are still used in some informal operations. Lead glazes are banned for food-contact and are restricted in many markets. Use lead-free fritted glazes, provide gloves and eye protection, and never eat or smoke in the glazing area.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;font-size:13px;color:#7b241c;">Comply with your national occupational safety and health authority (e.g. NIOH South Africa, DOSHS Kenya, OSHA Nigeria) and the ILO <em>Safety and Health in the Non-Ferrous Metals Industries</em> guidance for ceramic workplaces.</p>
</div>

<h3>5. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol style="font-size:15px;line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Clay tested and characterised</strong> plasticity, shrinkage, iron content, firing curve documented before equipment purchase.</li>
<li><strong>Body formulation locked</strong> clay, sand, flux, and water ratios recorded and repeatable batch to batch.</li>
<li><strong>Moisture controlled</strong> press body at 58% moisture; dried tiles below 2% residual moisture before kiln entry.</li>
<li><strong>Press and molds maintained</strong> daily check of mold wear, pressure gauge calibration, and release agent application.</li>
<li><strong>Drying schedule followed</strong> temperature and humidity ramp logged; no tiles rushed to the kiln.</li>
<li><strong>Firing curve verified</strong> peak temperature, soak time, and cooling rate recorded per batch with pyrometer or data logger.</li>
<li><strong>Glaze fit and thickness checked</strong> 0.20.4 mm, no crazing or peeling after firing; lead-free glaze only.</li>
<li><strong>QC tests run and logged</strong> water absorption, flexural strength, dimensions, warpage, and visual defects per batch; certificates issued for buyers.</li>
</ol>
