2. Mushroom Biology and Strain Selection

You cannot produce spawn without understanding what you are producing. This module teaches the biology of mushroom growth, the species that matter in Africa, how to isolate clean cultures, and how to store genetic stock so you never need to buy spawn or cultures from anyone else.

Module 2 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 2.1 — Oyster, shiitake, button, milky — the common species

Africa's mushroom market is dominated by a handful of species, each with different growth habits and different economics.

SpeciesBest forSubstrateDifficultySpawn price
Oyster (Pleurotus spp.)Beginner growers, fast turnoverStraw, sawdust, cotton wasteEasy$8–$12/kg
Shiitake (Lentinula edodes)Premium fresh and dried marketHardwood sawdust blocksMedium$12–$18/kg
Button (Agaricus bisporus)Supermarket supply, canningComposted manure, casingHard$15–$25/kg
Milky (Calocybe indica)Hot climates, tropical AfricaStraw, cotton wasteMedium$8–$14/kg
Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum)Medicinal market, extractsHardwood sawdust blocksMedium-hard$20–$40/kg
Lion's mane (Hericium erinaceus)Premium restaurants, wellnessHardwood sawdustMedium$20–$35/kg

For most start-up producers, oyster mushrooms are the entry point. They grow fast, tolerate heat, accept many substrates, and have the widest buyer base. Once you have mastered oyster spawn, add shiitake and milky as premium lines. Button mushrooms require entirely different substrates and are best left to specialists.

The choice matters because each species requires a slightly different grain for spawn. Oyster spawn works well on sorghum, millet, or wheat. Shiitake spawn is traditionally on hardwood sawdust or rye grain. Growing the wrong species on the wrong substrate wastes weeks and produces weak spawn.

Practical exercise 2.1: Pick the two species you want to produce. Write down why for each — market demand, climate, substrate availability, and difficulty. Do not try to produce more than two in your first year.

Lesson 2.2 — The mushroom life cycle — spore, mycelium, fruiting body

Mushrooms have three main stages, and spawn production works entirely in the second one.

Stage 1 — Spores. Microscopic, wind-borne cells produced by the gills or pores of a mature mushroom. Spores are the sexual stage — millions produced, but each requires specific conditions to germinate, and they are unreliable for commercial production. You will not work with spores directly.

Stage 2 — Mycelium. The white, thread-like network that grows through substrate. This is the mushroom's "body" — analogous to roots, stems, and leaves combined. Mycelium is what spawn contains. It is what you multiply. When you see a bag of grain spawn covered in white fuzz, that is millions of kilometres of mycelium threads.

Stage 3 — Fruiting body. What we call "the mushroom". It is the reproductive organ of the mycelium. When conditions change (humidity, temperature, oxygen), the mycelium decides to fruit and pushes up mushrooms to release spores and start the cycle again.

Your business operates entirely in Stage 2. You take a small amount of mycelium from a pure culture, grow it on sterile grain, and sell the colonised grain as spawn. The grower plants that grain into their substrate, and their own mycelium spreads to Stage 2 again. After weeks, they trigger Stage 3 and harvest mushrooms.

The life-cycle insight: If you understand that spawn = mycelium, and mushrooms = fruit, you understand why spawn quality determines everything. Weak mycelium produces weak mushrooms. Strong mycelium produces strong mushrooms. Your job is to produce strong mycelium.
Practical exercise 2.2: Sketch the three stages of the mushroom life cycle. Label what you sell at each stage and who buys it. You will never be confused about what you are producing again.

Lesson 2.3 — Isolating pure cultures from wild or cultivated mushrooms

A "pure culture" is a lab-grown sample of a single mushroom's mycelium, with no bacteria, no moulds, no other mushroom strains mixed in. Every spawn business starts with pure cultures. Here is how to get them.

Option 1 — Buy from a culture bank. Culture banks (like ATCC, or local universities) sell pure cultures on agar plates for $30–$100. This is the safest, fastest way to start. It requires a business email and payment; some allow small-batch purchases.

Option 2 — Tissue culture from a fresh mushroom. Take a fresh, healthy mushroom. Break it open in a sterile environment. Using a sterile scalpel, cut a small piece from the inside (where no bacteria or spores exist). Place it onto a sterile agar plate. Incubate. If clean, the piece will grow into a pure culture over 7–14 days.

Option 3 — Spore print to culture. Make a spore print on sterile paper, then streak the spores onto agar. This produces genetic variation (spores are sexual), so results are less predictable. Used mainly for breeding new strains. Not recommended for beginners.

For 95% of start-up businesses, Option 2 (tissue culture) is the method of choice. It is free — you only need one healthy mushroom — and produces identical offspring to the parent (cloning, essentially). If you can find a good quality mushroom at a market or from a grower, you can start a culture from it.

Sterile technique is everything here. The single biggest reason new producers fail at this stage is contamination. The still-air box, the alcohol flame, the sterilised tools, the slow deliberate movements — all of it is mandatory. If you rush, you will lose every plate.
Practical exercise 2.3: Source a fresh, healthy mushroom this week (from a market, grower, or nature). Do not yet do the tissue culture — first, set up your still-air box (module 3). The mushroom will keep in the fridge for 3–5 days while you prepare.

Lesson 2.4 — Selecting for vigour, yield, and disease resistance

Not every culture is equally good. Some produce fast, heavy yields. Others fruit slowly, resist contamination, or tolerate heat better than others. As a producer, you should be selecting the best, not just multiplying whatever you have.

What to look for when choosing a culture:

  • Vigour — mycelium that spreads across the agar plate in 5–7 days, not 14. Fast colonisers = fast spawn = happy customers.
  • Density — thick, white, cottony mycelium. Thin, wispy growth is a sign of weakness.
  • Uniformity — even growth without sectors or colour changes. Irregular growth often indicates contamination or mutation.
  • Yield potential — documented by the original isolate or your own fruiting trials. Test the culture through a full cycle before mass-producing spawn from it.
  • Climate adaptation — a strain that fruits well at 25–30°C is worth 10× more in Africa than one that needs 15°C.

Once you find a strong strain, protect it. Do not risk losing it to contamination. Keep at least 3 backup copies on agar slants in the refrigerator, and one on a long-term medium (see lesson 2.5).

The breeder's discipline: Test every new culture through a full fruiting cycle before you sell spawn from it. A culture that looks great on agar might produce poorly. Only cultures that have produced mushrooms successfully should become commercial spawn lines.
Practical exercise 2.4: When you isolate your first culture, note the date and check daily. Time how many days it takes to cover the plate. Save the fastest, densest plate as your "mother culture". This becomes your production line.

Lesson 2.5 — Strain storage — slant tubes, liquid nitrogen, glycerol

Once you have a great culture, you must preserve it — otherwise it slowly loses vigour or gets contaminated. There are three main storage methods, from cheapest to most advanced.

MethodStorage lifeCostEquipment
Agar slants in refrigerator6–12 monthsVery lowRefrigerator
Sterile water or oil overlay2–5 yearsVery lowRefrigerator
Glycerol stocks at -20°C or -80°C5–10+ yearsMediumFreezer (-20 or -80)
Liquid nitrogen (-196°C)20+ yearsHighLN2 dewar

For a start-up, agar slants in a refrigerator are the practical method. Grow the culture on agar in a slanted test tube; once well grown, seal and store upside down in a clean fridge at 4–8°C. These slants keep for 6–12 months, and you can revive them by transferring a piece to a fresh plate.

The trick is regular sub-culturing — every 6–8 months, transfer a piece from each stored slant to a fresh one. This keeps the strain alive and healthy without changing its genetics. If you forget for a year, the culture may die or mutate.

Never store cultures in the same fridge as food. Mould spores and bacteria from food will contaminate your cultures. If you have only one fridge, use a sealed plastic box inside it, and never store unpackaged food nearby.
Practical exercise 2.5: Once you have a good culture, make 5 slant tubes as backups. Label with species, strain name, and date. Store in the fridge. Set a calendar reminder for 6 months to sub-culture them.

Lesson 2.6 — Common strains for African climates

Europe and North America dominate mushroom genetics, but their strains fail in African conditions. Here is what actually works.

SpeciesRecommended strains for AfricaIdeal fruiting temp
Oyster (Pleurotus)P. ostreatus (warm-adapted), P. pulmonarius, P. eryngii (king oyster)20–28°C
Milky (Calocybe indica)Local isolates from India/South Asia25–35°C
Shiitake (Lentinula edodes)Warm-weather strains (e.g. 901, 939)18–25°C
Reishi (Ganoderma)G. lucidum, G. lingzhi22–30°C

The oyster species P. pulmonarius and P. eryngii are particularly strong in Africa — they tolerate heat and humidity better than P. ostreatus. If you can source these from another African producer, do. If not, the standard P. ostreatus sold as "oyster" works acceptably in cooler regions or with environmental control.

For high-value medicinal species (reishi, lion's mane), local isolates from Africa are being developed in countries like Kenya, Ghana, and South Africa. Joining the African Mushroom Growers network will help you find them.

Practical exercise 2.6: Identify 2 African mushroom producers or grower networks in your country. Contact them about strain availability. Many will trade or sell slants to established producers — this builds your genetic library for free.
