1. Edge Retention Testing: Cutting Tests

Edge retention testing tells you how long your blade will keep cutting before it needs sharpening. You don't need fancy equipment. A simple test is to cut sisal rope or cardboard. Get a 1-meter length of 10mm sisal rope, tie it to a post, and make clean slices. Count how many cuts you get before the knife stops shaving hair or slicing paper easily. For a good carbon steel knife (like 1080 or 5160) hardened to 58-60 HRC, you should get 50-100 cuts on rope. If you get less than 20, your edge geometry is too thick or the heat treat is off. Another test: cut into a soft pine board 10 times, then check if it still slices newspaper cleanly. In Africa, many makers use old leaf springs (5160) or files (W1). Test with local materials: cut through a maize stalk or a thick banana trunk. Record results in a notebook. Compare blades. If edge rolls or chips, note it. For a working knife in rural Africa, edge retention matters more than extreme sharpness. A blade that holds a working edge for a day of farm work is better than one that shaves but dulls in 10 minutes. Always test on the same material for fair comparison. After testing, sharpen and repeat. This builds a baseline for your forging and heat treating.

2. Toughness Testing: Chopping and Flexing

Toughness is a blade's ability to absorb impact without cracking or breaking. A hard blade can be brittle; a tough blade bends before it breaks. To test chopping toughness, secure a dry hardwood log (like acacia or mahogany) and chop into it 20 times with moderate force. A good chopper (e.g., a machete or camp knife) should not chip, roll, or crack. If the edge chips, your steel is too hard or has a coarse grain. If it rolls, it's too soft. For flex testing, clamp the blade horizontally in a vise with the edge up, leaving 10 cm of the tip exposed. Pull the tip sideways with a pipe until it bends 20-30 degrees. It should spring back straight. If it stays bent, the steel is too soft; if it snaps, too hard. In Africa, many blades are made from car springs. A spring steel blade at 55-57 HRC should flex without breaking. Test small blades by pushing the tip into a softwood board and prying sideways. Never test a blade you intend to sell without safety glasses and gloves. A broken blade can fly. Record how much force you used. For a bush knife, you want a balance: hard enough to hold an edge, tough enough to survive hitting a bone or a knot. If a blade fails, adjust your tempering temperature. Tempering at 200Â°C gives more toughness than 150Â°C.

3. Hardness Testing: Files and Rockwell

Hardness testing tells you if your blade will hold an edge. The simplest test is the file test. Take a new, sharp mill file (like a Nicholson). Draw it across the blade's edge bevel at a 30-degree angle. If the file bites and cuts metal, the blade is too soft (below 50 HRC). If it skates without cutting, the blade is harder than the file (around 60 HRC). If it scratches slightly, it's around 55 HRC. This is a rough test but works in a rural workshop. For more precision, use a Rockwell hardness tester. Many city workshops or technical colleges have one. A typical knife should be 56-60 HRC. A machete or chopper: 52-56 HRC. A fine slicing knife: 60-62 HRC. Test at least three points along the edge: near the handle, middle, and tip. Variations of more than 2 HRC mean uneven heat treatment. In Africa, you can make a simple hardness tester using a hardened ball bearing and a hammer, but it's not accurate. Better to send a sample to a lab. If no lab, use the file test and compare to a known good knife. Always test after tempering, not before. If too soft, re-harden and temper at a lower temperature. If too hard, temper again at a higher temperature (e.g., 220Â°C for 1 hour). Record results. A consistent hardness across the blade means good forge control.

4. Inspecting for Cracks and Flaws

Cracks and flaws can cause a blade to fail catastrophically. Inspect your blade after forging, after hardening, and after tempering. Start with a visual inspection under good light. Look for fine lines, especially near the edge, spine, and tang. Use a magnifying glass (10x) if available. A common flaw is a hardening crack, which appears as a jagged line running perpendicular to the edge. Another is a cold shut, a fold in the steel from forging that didn't weld. To check for internal cracks, do a bend test: clamp the blade in a vise and bend it 10 degrees. If it snaps, examine the break for dark spots or grain boundaries. A bright, silky gray break is good; a dark, rough break means poor grain structure. Also check for delamination if you used layered steel (e.g., cable damascus). Tap the blade with a small hammer; a dull thud may indicate a crack. In Africa, many blades are made from recycled steel with unknown defects. Always normalize (heat to critical and cool in air) three times before hardening to refine grain and reduce stress. After quenching, temper immediately. If you find a crack, do not try to sell the blade. Rework it into a smaller knife or scrap it. A cracked blade can injure the user. Keep a log of flaws and their causes. Most cracks come from quenching too cold, too fast, or from steel with high sulfur. Use clean water or oil at the right temperature (oil at 50-60Â°C for most carbon steels).

5. Correcting Common Defects

Even experienced smiths get defects. Here's how to fix the most common ones. Warping: after quenching, a blade may bend. While still hot (around 200Â°C), clamp it in a vise and bend it straight. If it's already cold, reheat to 200Â°C and straighten. Do not hammer cold steel. Cracks: if a crack is small and near the spine, you can grind it out and re-profile. If it's near the edge, the blade is scrap. Soft edge: if the file test shows softness, re-harden. Normalize, heat to non-magnetic (around 800Â°C for carbon steel), quench in oil, then temper. Hard/brittle edge: if it chips easily, temper again at a higher temperature (e.g., 230Â°C for 1 hour). Chipping can also be from a too-thin edge; sharpen at a wider angle (20-25 degrees per side). Rolled edge: if the edge rolls over, it's too soft. Re-harden and temper at a lower temperature. Pitting: from forge scale or rust. Grind it out. If deep, it may become a design feature. Decarburization: a soft skin on the blade after forging. Grind off 1mm from all sides before hardening. In Africa, where electricity is unreliable, use a charcoal forge and a bucket of oil. Always have a fire extinguisher or sand bucket. After correcting, retest. Never sell a blade that has been re-hardened more than twice; the steel may be compromised. Keep notes on what worked.

6. Final Sharpening and Maintenance

Final sharpening is the last step before your blade is ready. Start with a coarse stone (200-400 grit) to set the bevel. Use a consistent angle: 15-20 degrees per side for a slicing knife, 20-25 for a chopper. Push the edge into the stone like you're slicing a thin layer. Count 20 strokes per side. Then move to a medium stone (800-1000 grit). Repeat. Then a fine stone (3000-6000 grit). Finally, strop on leather with polishing compound (like chromium oxide). Test by slicing newspaper or shaving arm hair. In Africa, you can use natural stones like novaculite or water stones from a river. Always keep the stone wet with water or oil. After sharpening, clean the blade with alcohol and apply a thin coat of oil (mineral oil or coconut oil) to prevent rust. For maintenance, tell your customer: hand wash and dry immediately, never put in a dishwasher, and oil every few months. Store in a leather sheath (not plastic, which traps moisture). For a working knife, touch up with a honing rod or a fine stone every week. If the edge rolls, hone it. If it chips, sharpen on a coarse stone. A well-maintained blade can last a lifetime. In rural Africa, a knife is a tool for farming, cooking, and protection. Teach users to respect it. Always cut away from your body. Never use a knife as a pry bar or screwdriver. A sharp knife is safer than a dull one because it requires less force.
