1. Seasoning and Curing the Oven

Before you fire your oven for pizza, you must season and cure it. This process removes moisture from the refractory materials (brick, clay, or mortar) and prevents cracking. If you skip this, your oven will crack and fall apart. Curing takes 5-7 days, with small fires increasing in size each day.

Day 1: Build a small fire using 2-3 handfuls of dry kindling (small sticks) and one small log. Keep the fire near the oven door. Let it burn for 2 hours. The oven should feel warm, not hot. Day 2: Use 4-5 handfuls of kindling and two small logs. Burn for 3 hours. Day 3: Use 6-7 handfuls and three medium logs. Burn for 4 hours. Day 4: Use a full fire with 4-5 medium logs. Burn for 5 hours. Day 5: Build a normal fire with 6-7 logs. Burn for 6 hours. Day 6: Build a hot fire with 8-10 logs. Burn for 8 hours. Day 7: Build a very hot fire with 10-12 logs. Burn for 10 hours. The oven should reach 750F (400C) on the last day.

During curing, keep the fire small and controlled. If you see steam coming from the oven, that's moisture escapinggood. If you hear cracking sounds, reduce the fire immediately. After curing, let the oven cool completely before using it for pizza. The oven is now ready for high-heat baking. Remember, curing is not a one-time thing; if the oven gets wet (rain, high humidity), you must re-cure it with small fires before baking. In Africa, where humidity can be high, always cover your oven when not in use. Use a metal or clay lid, or a tarp. This prevents moisture from entering the bricks. Curing also hardens the mortar and makes the oven durable for years. Take your timerushing curing ruins the oven.

2. Types of Wood for Firing

Not all wood is good for firing a pizza oven. You need dry, hard wood that burns hot and clean. Soft woods like pine, eucalyptus, and bamboo burn fast and produce lots of smoke and creosote, which coats the oven and gives pizza a bad taste. Hard woods like oak, acacia, mango, and olive burn long and hot with little smoke. In Africa, good options include acacia (common in East and Southern Africa), mango (West and Central Africa), olive (North Africa), and eucalyptus (but only if well-seasoned). Avoid treated wood, painted wood, or wood from construction sitesthese release toxic chemicals.

Seasoning is key. Wood must be dried for at least 6-12 months. Freshly cut wood has 50% moisture; seasoned wood has less than 20%. To test, knock two pieces togetherdry wood makes a sharp crack, wet wood makes a dull thud. Or use a moisture meter (available at hardware stores for about $20). If you don't have one, weigh a piece, dry it in the sun for a week, and weigh againif it loses more than 15% weight, it's not ready. Store wood off the ground, under cover, with good airflow. Stack it loosely so wind can pass through.

Cut wood into pieces about 30-40 cm long and 5-10 cm thick. For a 12-inch pizza oven, you need about 4-6 kg of wood per firing session. Split larger logs into smaller pieces for faster ignition. Keep a mix: small kindling (finger-thick), medium logs (wrist-thick), and large logs (arm-thick). Never use charcoal briquettesthey contain additives and don't produce the same heat. Natural lump charcoal can be used as a backup, but wood is best. In Africa, many pizzaiolos use acacia or mango because they are abundant and cheap. Buy in bulk during dry season when prices are low. Store enough for 3-6 months. Remember: dry wood = hot fire = great pizza.

3. Building and Maintaining a Fire

Building a fire in a wood-fired oven is different from a campfire. You need a hot, clean-burning fire that heats the oven floor and dome. Start by clearing old ash from the oven. Then place two medium logs parallel to the oven door, about 20 cm apart. Put crumpled newspaper or dry palm fronds between them. Add a handful of kindling (small dry sticks) on top. Light the paper. As the kindling catches, add more kindling, then medium logs. Keep the fire in the center of the oven, not against the walls. Once the fire is established, push it to one side (usually the left or right) to make room for pizza. But for initial heating, keep it central.

Maintain the fire by adding one log every 10-15 minutes. Use a metal poker or long-handled tongs to rearrange logs. You want a bright orange flame, not a smoldering fire. If the fire smokes heavily, the wood is too wet or not enough air. Open the oven door slightly or use a bellows to blow air into the fire. The oven will take 45-60 minutes to reach 750F (400C). Use an infrared thermometer to check the floor temperature. Once hot, you'll need to add a log every 15-20 minutes to maintain heat. For a 12-inch pizza, you want the floor at 750-800F and the dome at 900F. If the fire dies down, add kindling and blow air.

Safety: Never leave the fire unattended. Keep a bucket of sand or water nearby. Wear leather gloves and use long tools. In Africa, where ovens are often outdoors, be aware of windit can blow sparks. Build a windbreak if needed. After baking, let the fire burn out naturally. Do not pour water on a hot fireit can crack the oven. Close the oven door to retain heat for next time. A well-maintained fire is the heart of your pizza oven.

4. Reading Oven Temperature with an Infrared Thermometer

An infrared thermometer (also called a laser thermometer) is the most important tool for consistent pizza. It measures surface temperature without touching. You can buy one online or at hardware stores for $15-$30. Look for a range up to 1000F (538C) and a distance-to-spot ratio of 12:1 or better. To use it, point the laser at the oven floor where you will bake. Hold it 30-50 cm away. Press the trigger and read the number. For a 12-inch pizza, you want the floor at 750-800F (400-427C). The dome should be 850-900F (454-482C). Measure in three spots: left, center, right. If one spot is cooler, rotate the pizza or move the fire.

Before baking, check the floor temperature. If it's below 700F, add more wood and wait 10 minutes. If it's above 850F, let it cool for 5 minutes or move the fire to the side. The infrared thermometer also helps you check the dome. Point it at the curved ceiling. If the dome is below 800F, your pizza won't cook on top. Add a log and wait. For baking, you want the floor hot enough to cook the base in 60-90 seconds. If it takes longer, the floor is too cool. If it burns in 30 seconds, it's too hot.

Calibrate your thermometer occasionally. Use a pot of boiling water (212F at sea level) or a known temperature source. If it reads off by more than 20F, replace it. In Africa, dust and heat can affect the sensorkeep it clean and store it in a case. Also, remember that the thermometer reads surface temperature, not air temperature. The air inside the oven can be hotter. Use the floor reading as your main guide. With practice, you'll learn to read the oven by sight (glowing coals, white dome) but the thermometer removes guesswork. Always keep a spare battery.

5. Managing Heat for 12-Inch Pizzas (750-900F)

A 12-inch pizza needs high heat: 750-900F (400-482C). At this temperature, the pizza cooks in 60-90 seconds. The floor must be 750-800F to crisp the base without burning. The dome must be 850-900F to melt cheese and char the crust. To manage this heat, you need a good fire and a properly heated oven. Start firing 1 hour before baking. Use 4-6 kg of dry hardwood. Build a hot fire in the center. After 45 minutes, check the floor with an infrared thermometer. If it's 750F, push the fire to the left side. Wait 5 minutes for the floor to even out. Then you're ready.

Maintain the heat by adding one medium log every 15 minutes. Keep the fire on one side. The other side is your baking zone. If the floor cools below 700F, add two logs and wait 10 minutes. If it gets above 850F, move the fire further to the side or reduce airflow by closing the door slightly. For multiple pizzas, you need to rotate. Bake one pizza at a time. After each pizza, check the floor temperature. If it drops, add a log. If it rises, wait. A 12-inch pizza takes about 90 seconds: 30 seconds on one side, then rotate 180 degrees, then 30 seconds, then check. Use a metal peel to turn.

The dome heat is critical. If the dome is too cool, the cheese won't melt. If too hot, the crust burns before the cheese melts. Aim for 850-900F. You can check with the infrared thermometer. If the dome is below 800F, add a log and let it flame up. If above 950F, let the fire die down a bit. In Africa, where ambient temperatures are high, your oven may heat faster. Adjust by using less wood. Also, wind can cool the ovenuse a windbreak. Remember, every oven is different. Practice with a few test pizzas. Keep a log of temperatures and times. Soon you'll manage heat by feel.

6. Clearing Coals and Preparing the Floor for Baking

Before baking pizza, you must clear the coals and ash from the oven floor. Leaving coals on the floor will burn the bottom of your pizza. Use a metal rake or a long-handled brush to push all coals and ash to one side (the side where your fire is). Then use a damp (not wet) cloth mop to wipe the floor clean. The mop should be made of cotton or microfiber, attached to a long handle. Dip it in water, wring it out so it's damp, then quickly wipe the floor in one sweep. The water creates steam, which cleans the floor and slightly cools it. Do not use a soaking wet mopit can crack the oven. After wiping, check the floor temperature with an infrared thermometer. It should be 750-800F. If it dropped below 700F, wait 2 minutes for it to recover.

Clearing coals also prevents ash from getting on the pizza. Ash is bitter and gritty. Push all ash into a metal bucket. Never leave ash in the ovenit will accumulate and reduce heat. For a 12-inch pizza, you need a clean, hot floor. Once cleared, you can start baking. Work quickly: stretch your dough, add toppings, and slide the pizza onto the floor with a peel. The pizza will cook in 60-90 seconds. After baking, use the rake to push coals back over the floor if you are done, or leave them on the side if you are baking more. If you are finished, spread the coals evenly and close the door to keep the oven warm for next time.

In Africa, where wood ash is common, you can save it for gardening (it's good for soil). But never leave it in the oven. Also, check the floor for cracks or uneven spots. If the floor is uneven, your pizza will cook unevenly. Use a stone or sand to level it if needed. A clean floor is the foundation of a perfect pizza. Practice clearing and wiping quicklyit should take less than 30 seconds. The faster you work, the less heat you lose. Keep your tools nearby: rake, brush, mop, peel, and thermometer. With a clean floor and proper heat, your 12-inch pizza will have a crispy base, melted cheese, and a perfect char.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Firing and Temperature Management for a 12-Inch Wood-Fired Pizza Oven

In African agribusiness, a wood-fired pizza oven is not a novelty it is a revenue engine. From the peri-urban food hubs of Nairobi and Accra to the tourist lodges of Arusha and the weekend markets of Lusaka, operators who understand firing and temperature management produce consistently good 12-inch pizzas at volume, control their fuel costs, and protect their equipment. This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a single working document you can print, laminate, and hang beside your oven. It is written for the operator who measures success in pizzas per hour, cost per pizza, and repeat customers not in culinary romance.

Everything below assumes a 12-inch (30 cm) internal diameter wood-fired oven with a brick or refractory dome, a 6070% dome-to-floor height ratio, and a chimney or flue. Adjust fuel quantities and timings proportionally for larger ovens, but keep the temperature targets for 12-inch pizzas: 750900F (400480C) on the floor, with a slightly hotter dome.

1. Seasoning and Curing the Oven

A new oven carries moisture in its mortar and refractory mass. Firing it hard on day one causes steam expansion, cracking, and permanent structural weakness. Curing is a controlled, multi-day drying schedule that drives moisture out slowly. In humid African climates coastal Mombasa, the Niger Delta, the Great Lakes region allow an extra day or two of low-temperature drying.

2. Types of Wood for Firing

Wood is your primary fuel cost and your primary flavour variable. The best firing wood is dry, seasoned hardwood with a moisture content below 20%. Wet wood wastes energy boiling water, creates smoke, and deposits creosote in the chimney a fire hazard. In Africa, excellent options include Acacia (especially Acacia mearnsii, black wattle), Eucalyptus (well-seasoned), Combretum (bushwillow), and fruit woods such as mango, guava, and citrus. Avoid resinous softwoods (pine, cypress) and treated or painted timber they taint food and release toxic compounds.

3. Building and Maintaining a Fire

Build the fire in the same position every day: slightly off-centre, toward the mouth and to one side. This leaves a clear baking zone and lets you control heat by moving coals. Start with kindling and paper or natural firelighters never paraffin or petrol. Add progressively larger sticks in a loose criss-cross so air can move. Once a bed of glowing coals forms (2040 minutes), stop adding large wood and let the dome heat soak. For a 12-inch oven, a typical firing uses 35 kg of dry wood to reach baking temperature.

Maintain the fire by pushing coals together with a long-handled rake or metal peel. Keep the flame small and the coal bed even. A roaring fire wastes fuel and heats the dome unevenly; a lazy, smoking fire fails to reach 750F. The target is a bright, clean-burning fire with visible flames and a deep orange coal bed.

4. Reading Oven Temperature with an Infrared Thermometer

An infrared (IR) thermometer is the single most important tool for consistent pizza. Point it at the floor where the pizza will bake, not at the fire or the dome. Take readings at three points: left, centre, and right of the baking zone. The floor should read 750900F. The dome, measured at its inner curve, should read 850950F. If the floor is below 700F, add small wood and wait; if above 950F, let it cool with the door open for 510 minutes.

IR thermometers are inexpensive, widely available in African cities, and battery-powered. Keep a spare battery and a backup thermometer. Calibrate by comparing readings against a known boiling-water test (212F / 100C) or a calibrated probe. In dusty environments, keep the lens clean a dirty lens reads low and leads to undercooked pizza.

SAFETY WARNING FIRE AND BURN HAZARD

Never leave a firing oven unattended. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO) and a bucket of sand within arm's reach of the oven. Never use water on a hot oven floor thermal shock cracks refractory brick. Wear leather gloves, long sleeves, and closed shoes when firing or raking coals. Keep children and customers at least 2 metres from the oven mouth. Ensure the chimney draws properly before firing indoors or under a roof; carbon monoxide is odourless and lethal. In shared market or food-court settings, confirm your landlord's fire safety rules before installation.

5. Managing Heat for 12-Inch Pizzas (750900F)

Twelve-inch pizzas bake in 6090 seconds at 800F. That speed is the business case: high throughput, low labour per pizza, and a blistered, leopard-spotted crust customers pay premium prices for. To manage heat:

  • Soak the dome first. Let the fire burn 3045 minutes before the first pizza so the refractory mass stores heat. A cold dome drops temperature the moment you open the door.
  • Work in batches. Bake 24 pizzas at a time, rotating every 1520 seconds. This keeps the door open less and maintains floor temperature.
  • Recharge between batches. Add one small stick every 23 batches, or push coals toward the baking zone, to hold 800F.
  • Watch the floor, not the clock. An IR reading every 15 minutes tells you more than any timer.
  • Adjust for altitude. At 1,500 m and above (Nairobi, Addis Ababa, Johannesburg), flames burn cooler and dough hydrates differently; expect to fire slightly longer and use a marginally wetter dough.

6. Clearing Coals and Preparing the Floor for Baking

Before each baking session, clear the floor completely. Use a long-handled metal rake or a stiff-bristled oven brush to push all coals and ash to the side or into a metal ash bucket. A clean floor gives even contact heat and prevents ash on the pizza base. Leave a small coal bank at the side or rear to maintain ambient heat, but never under the baking zone. Wipe the floor with a dry, heat-resistant cloth on a long handle never a wet cloth. Once the floor reads 750900F at all three points, you are ready to bake.

After service, let the oven cool naturally with the door partially open. Never douse it with water. Empty the ash bucket into a metal container and store it away from combustible materials for 48 hours before disposal.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Cure before you cook: follow the 45 day curing schedule; never fire a new oven to full heat on day one.
  2. Buy dry hardwood: moisture below 20%; avoid pine, treated, or painted wood.
  3. Build the fire off-centre: leave a clear baking zone; use kindling, never accelerants.
  4. Soak the dome: burn 3045 minutes before the first pizza.
  5. Read the floor with an IR thermometer: target 750900F at left, centre, and right.
  6. Clear coals and ash before baking: clean floor, small coal bank to the side.
  7. Bake in batches, rotate every 1520 seconds: recharge heat between batches.
  8. Keep safety equipment ready: extinguisher, sand bucket, gloves, and a clear 2 m zone.
