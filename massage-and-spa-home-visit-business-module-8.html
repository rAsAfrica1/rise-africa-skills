Lessons

1. Scheduling and Route Planning for Home Visits

Start by grouping clients by area. In Lagos, if you have clients in Ikeja, Surulere, and Lekki, don't schedule them on the same day. Instead, assign one day to Ikeja, another to Surulere, and so on. Use a paper diary or a free app like Google Calendar. For each client, note their address, preferred time, and session length. A typical massage session lasts 60 minutes, but add 15 minutes for setup and 15 for pack-up. So block 90 minutes per client. If you have three clients in one area, schedule them at 9am, 11am, and 1pm. That gives you 30 minutes between sessions for travel. Always add a 30-minute buffer at the end of the day for delays. Confirm appointments the day before via WhatsApp or SMS. If a client cancels, you can offer the slot to someone on your waiting list. For route planning, use Google Maps or a local app like Waze to check traffic. In many African cities, traffic peaks from 7-9am and 4-7pm. Avoid scheduling across town during those times. If you don't have a smartphone, ask a friend or family member to help you plan routes, or use a paper map and ask other therapists for advice. Keep a record of how long it takes to reach each client so you can improve your estimates. Always have a backup plan: if a client is not home, call them, wait 10 minutes, then move to the next client if possible. Never overbook; it's better to have fewer clients and arrive on time than to rush and provide poor service.

2. Managing Travel Time and Transportation

Your travel time is part of your workday, so treat it seriously. In Nairobi, a 10km trip can take 30 minutes off-peak but over an hour during rush hour. Always add 50% more time than Google Maps suggests. If you use public transport like matatus or danfos, you can't control delays. So leave 30 minutes earlier than you think you need. For example, if a session is at 10am and the trip normally takes 40 minutes, leave at 8:50am. That gives you a 30-minute buffer. If you use a boda boda or motorbike taxi, negotiate a fixed price before you get on. In Kampala, a 5km boda ride might cost 5,000 UGX; agree on that before you start. If you drive your own car, keep it serviced and always have a spare tyre and jerrycan of fuel. In many African cities, fuel stations can be far apart, so fill up when you reach half a tank. For long distances, consider hiring a taxi for the day; it might cost 50,000 NGN in Lagos, but you can carry all your equipment and work comfortably. If you use public transport, invest in a good backpack or trolley bag to carry your oils, towels, and table. A foldable massage table is heavy, so if you don't have a car, you might need to hire a taxi for those clients. Track your travel expenses: fuel, fares, and parking. Add these to your service price or charge a travel fee. For example, if a client is more than 10km away, charge an extra 2,000 NGN. Always carry a power bank for your phone, and keep emergency numbers saved. If you're running late, call the client immediately and apologise. Most clients understand if you communicate.

3. Inventory Management for Mobile Supplies

Your mobile supplies are your business lifeline. You need massage oil, towels, sheets, hand sanitiser, tissues, and a first-aid kit. For a typical 60-minute massage, you use about 30ml of oil. If you see 4 clients a day, that's 120ml. A 1-litre bottle of oil costs around 3,000 NGN and lasts about 8 days. So buy in bulk to save money. Keep a minimum stock level: when you have 2 bottles left, order more. Store your supplies in a dedicated bag or box. Use clear plastic containers for oils to prevent leaks. Label everything. For towels, have at least 6 clean towels per day: 2 for the table, 2 for the client, and 2 spare. Wash and dry them after each use. In humid climates, dry them in the sun to prevent mildew. For sheets, use disposable couch covers if you can't wash daily; they cost about 500 NGN each. Keep a checklist on your phone or paper: before leaving, tick off each item. After each session, restock your bag immediately so you don't forget. For hygiene, carry alcohol wipes and hand sanitiser. Replace your first-aid kit every 6 months. Track your inventory weekly: count what you have, what you used, and what you need to buy. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Google Keep. If you run out of oil during a session, you can't finish properly. So always carry a spare small bottle in your bag. Also, consider the weight: a full bag can be 10-15kg. Use a trolley bag with wheels to save your back. Buy supplies from local markets or wholesale shops to get better prices. For example, in Accra, Makola Market has cheap oils and towels. Never compromise on quality; cheap oil can irritate skin and ruin your reputation.

4. Setting Up and Breaking Down Equipment Efficiently

Efficiency in setup and breakdown saves you time and energy. Aim to set up in 10 minutes and break down in 10 minutes. First, choose a suitable space: a quiet room with enough space for your table. Ask the client to clear the area before you arrive. When you enter, greet the client, then immediately start. Unfold your massage table first. A standard table is 180cm long and 60cm wide. Place it in the centre of the room, leaving space to walk around. Lock the legs. Then put on the fitted sheet. Next, arrange your oils, towels, and bolster within arm's reach. Use a small tray or stool to hold items. Put a towel on the table for the client to lie on. Have a small bin for used tissues. For lighting, use a portable lamp if the room is dim. For music, use a small Bluetooth speaker. Keep everything in a consistent order so you don't waste time searching. After the session, strip the sheets and put them in a laundry bag. Wipe the table with disinfectant. Fold the table and put it in its carry case. Pack oils and towels in your bag. Do a final check: look around for anything you might have left. If you have multiple clients, you can save time by not fully packing between clients if you're in the same area. But always clean and reset. Practice your setup at home until you can do it blindfolded. Time yourself. The faster you are, the more clients you can see. Also, keep your equipment in good condition: oil the hinges, check for tears, and replace worn parts. A broken table mid-session is a disaster. So carry a repair kit with duct tape and spare screws.

5. Handling Multiple Clients in One Day

Seeing multiple clients in one day can boost your income, but it requires planning. Start with a maximum of 4 clients per day. Each session takes about 90 minutes including setup and breakdown. So 4 clients = 6 hours of work. Add travel time: if clients are in different areas, you might spend 2 hours travelling. That's an 8-hour day. Don't overbook. Your energy and quality will drop. Schedule your first client at 9am, second at 11:30am, third at 2pm, and fourth at 4:30pm. That gives you 30-minute buffers. If you have clients in the same neighbourhood, you can shorten buffers to 15 minutes. Always eat a good meal before you start and carry snacks like nuts or bananas. Drink water throughout the day. Between clients, wash your hands and use sanitiser. Change your shirt if you sweat a lot. For your equipment, you might need two sets of sheets and towels so you don't have to wash between clients. Keep a cooler with cold water and a small towel to refresh yourself. If you feel tired, take a 10-minute power nap in your car or a quiet corner. But never rush a massage; clients will notice. If a client wants to chat, keep it brief and polite. Stick to your schedule. If a session runs over, politely say you have another appointment. For payment, collect after each session to avoid confusion. Use a mobile money app like M-Pesa or MoMo for safety. At the end of the day, record your earnings and expenses. Rest and stretch. If you do this daily, you'll burn out. So mix days with fewer clients. For example, Monday: 2 clients, Tuesday: 4, Wednesday: 2, etc. Listen to your body.

6. Emergency Preparedness and Contingency Plans

Emergencies happen. A client might feel dizzy, you might get a flat tyre, or a sudden storm might flood the roads. You need a plan. First, carry a first-aid kit with plasters, antiseptic, bandages, and painkillers. If a client feels unwell during a massage, stop immediately. Ask if they have a medical condition. If they faint, lay them on their side, check breathing, and call for help. Save emergency numbers: local ambulance, a nearby clinic, and a friend who can assist. In many African cities, ambulance services are unreliable, so know the nearest hospital. For your own safety, share your schedule with a friend or family member. If you're going to a new client, send the address and expected time to someone. Carry a whistle or personal alarm. If you feel threatened, leave immediately. For transport emergencies, keep a spare tyre, jack, and jump cables if you drive. If you use public transport, have a backup plan: save the number of a trusted taxi driver. If you get stuck, call the client to reschedule. For weather, check forecasts. In rainy season, avoid areas prone to flooding. Carry a raincoat and waterproof bag for your equipment. If you can't reach a client, call them. If they cancel last minute, you can offer a discount for rebooking. Always have a contingency fund: save 10% of your income for emergencies like equipment repair or medical costs. For equipment failure, carry spares: extra oil, a spare sheet, and a repair kit. If your table breaks, you can offer a floor massage with a mat, but only if the client agrees. Never compromise safety. After any emergency, write down what happened and how you handled it. Learn from it. Update your plan. Being prepared gives you confidence and protects your business.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Logistics & Operations for Massage and Spa Home Visits in Africa

Running a massage or spa home-visit business in Africa is a logistics business as much as a wellness business. Your clients pay for relaxation, but your profit is determined by how well you plan routes, manage travel time, control inventory, set up equipment, handle multiple bookings, and respond to the unexpected. This module gives you practical, field-tested systems for operating efficiently across African cities from Nairobi and Lagos to Accra, Kigali, and Dar es Salaam.

The core principle is simple: every minute spent stuck in traffic or searching for supplies is a minute you are not earning. Your goal is to maximize billable hours while delivering a consistent, professional experience. This lesson covers the operational backbone of a mobile spa business.

1. Scheduling and Route Planning for Home Visits

Route planning is your single biggest profit lever. A poorly planned day can waste 34 hours in traffic. A well-planned day can fit 45 clients instead of 3.

Practical system:

  • Cluster by geography, not by time. Group clients in the same suburb or district. If you serve Nairobi's Kilimani and Westlands, do them on the same day. Do not zigzag across the city.
  • Use a booking window of 90 minutes. Standard massage is 60 minutes; add 15 minutes setup and 15 minutes breakdown.
  • Leave 3045 minutes travel buffer between clients. In Lagos, Nairobi, or Accra, this is not optional.
  • Confirm 24 hours and 2 hours before. A WhatsApp confirmation reduces no-shows by up to 40% (industry practice).

2. Managing Travel Time and Transportation

Transport is your second-largest cost after labour. In many African cities, private car ownership is expensive, and ride-hailing (Uber, Bolt, Little, Yango) is common but can eat 2030% of revenue if unmanaged.

Cost-control strategies:

  • Negotiate a flat daily rate with a trusted driver. In Nairobi, a full-day driver (8 hours) costs roughly KES 3,0005,000 often cheaper than 4 Bolt trips.
  • Use boda boda or motorbike taxis for short hops in cities like Kampala or Kigali where traffic is dense and parking is scarce.
  • Charge a travel fee beyond a set radius. Standard practice: free within 5 km, then a per-km surcharge.
  • Track your fuel/transport cost per client. If transport exceeds 15% of the service price, your pricing is wrong.

3. Inventory Management for Mobile Supplies

Running out of massage oil mid-session is unprofessional and costly. Your mobile kit must be standardized, restocked daily, and tracked.

Standard mobile kit (per therapist):

  • 2 bottles massage oil (250ml each) one in use, one backup
  • 4 clean towels per client + 2 spare
  • Disposable sheets or washable covers (2 per client)
  • Sanitizer, wet wipes, tissue
  • Portable massage table + carry bag
  • Bolster, eye pillow, hot stones (if offered)
  • Payment device / mobile money QR code
  • Client intake forms and consent sheets

Restock rule: Every evening, check stock against tomorrow's bookings. If you have 3 clients and only 2 bottles of oil, restock tonight.

4. Setting Up and Breaking Down Equipment Efficiently

Setup and breakdown should take no more than 15 minutes each. Speed comes from repetition and a fixed sequence.

Setup sequence (15 min):

  1. Confirm space and client preferences (2 min)
  2. Unfold table, lock legs, check stability (3 min)
  3. Layer sheets, arrange towels, bolster, eye pillow (4 min)
  4. Set oils, stones, music, lighting (3 min)
  5. Wash hands, final client check-in (3 min)

Breakdown sequence (15 min):

  1. Fold linens into laundry bag (3 min)
  2. Wipe table, fold, bag (5 min)
  3. Pack oils and tools (3 min)
  4. Client payment and rebooking (3 min)
  5. Final room check (1 min)
SAFETY WARNING Fire, Electricity & Heat Safety

If you use hot stones, candles, or heating equipment during home visits, always confirm the client has a working fire extinguisher and that electrical outlets are not overloaded. In many African homes, power surges and unstable voltage are common. Never leave heated stones or candles unattended. Use a surge protector for any electrical equipment. If the client's home has exposed wiring or no ventilation, decline the service or modify it. Your safety and the client's safety come before the booking.

5. Handling Multiple Clients in One Day

Three to four clients per day is the sustainable maximum for a solo therapist. Beyond that, quality drops and burnout rises.

Rules for multi-client days:

  • Cap at 4 clients. 5+ leads to rushed service and errors.
  • Alternate heavy and light services. Deep tissue followed by a relaxing Swedish massage.
  • Take a real lunch break. 30 minutes minimum. Eat protein, not just carbs.
  • Hydrate constantly. You are doing physical work in hot climates.
  • Keep a "day bag" separate from your "client bag" snacks, water, change of shirt, phone charger.

6. Emergency Preparedness and Contingency Plans

Things go wrong: the client cancels, the car breaks down, a client has an allergic reaction, or a sudden storm floods the route. You need a plan for each.

Contingency checklist:

  • Client cancellation: Have a waitlist of 23 clients per day who can take a slot on short notice.
  • Transport failure: Keep a backup driver contact and a ride-hailing app pre-loaded with payment.
  • Allergic reaction: Carry antihistamine (with client consent), know nearest clinic, and have emergency contact on file.
  • Weather: In rainy season, build extra 30 minutes into every route.
  • Power outage: Carry a portable rechargeable lamp and battery-powered speaker.
  • Personal emergency: Share your daily route with a colleague or family member.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm all clients 24 hours and 2 hours before
  • Cluster bookings by geography, not by time
  • Load vehicle with full inventory + 1 backup of each consumable
  • Carry mobile money / card payment option
  • Keep 3045 min travel buffer between clients
  • Cap at 4 clients per day maximum
  • Carry emergency kit: antihistamine, first aid, torch, charger
  • Share your route with a colleague or family member
