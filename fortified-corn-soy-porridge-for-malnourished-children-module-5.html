1. Washing hands and utensils before preparing porridge

Before you touch any food, you must wash your hands with soap and clean water. Use any soap availablebar soap, liquid soap, or ash if soap is not available. Wet your hands, rub soap all over, including between fingers and under nails, for at least 20 seconds. Rinse with clean running water. If you don't have running water, pour water from a clean cup or bucket. Dry your hands with a clean cloth or let them air dry. Do this every time you start cooking, after using the toilet, after handling raw ingredients, and after touching animals.

Utensils must also be washed. Use a separate basin for washing. Scrub pots, spoons, bowls, and grinding stones with soap and clean water. Use a brush or sponge dedicated to cleaning. Pay attention to cracks and corners where food can hide. Rinse well with clean water to remove all soap. Then, if possible, sanitize by pouring boiling water over them or soaking in a mild bleach solution (1 teaspoon of bleach per 1 litre of water) for 2 minutes. Rinse again with clean water.

After washing, let utensils air dry on a clean rack or cloth. Do not dry with a dirty towel. Store them in a clean, covered place. If you have workers, show them this method step by step. Make it a rule: no one touches food or utensils with dirty hands. Keep a nail brush and soap near the washing area. In many African homes, a simple tippy-tap made from a jerrycan can provide running water for handwashing. This is cheap and effective.

Remember, germs are too small to see. Even if your hands look clean, they can carry bacteria that cause diarrhoea and other illnesses. Malnourished children are especially vulnerable. By washing hands and utensils properly, you protect the child and your whole family. Make it a habit, not a choice.

2. Keeping raw and cooked ingredients separate

Raw ingredients like uncooked corn, soybeans, eggs, and meat can carry harmful germs. Cooked porridge is safe to eat, but if it touches raw ingredients or their juices, it becomes contaminated again. This is called cross-contamination. To prevent it, always keep raw and cooked foods separate.

Use different utensils for raw and cooked foods. For example, have one knife and cutting board for raw ingredients and another for cooked porridge. If you only have one, wash it thoroughly with soap and hot water after using it on raw food before using it on cooked food. Never put cooked porridge in a container that held raw ingredients without washing it first. Also, store raw ingredients in covered containers on the bottom shelf of your fridge or in a separate area from cooked food. If you don't have a fridge, keep raw ingredients in a cool, dry place away from where you store cooked porridge.

When preparing the porridge, grind or mill the corn and soybeans separately from any cooked food. If you use eggs or milk, keep them refrigerated until you are ready to cook. After cooking, serve the porridge immediately or store it in a clean, covered container. Do not let cooked porridge sit out for more than 2 hours. If you have leftovers, cool them quickly and refrigerate within 2 hours. Reheat thoroughly before serving.

In many African kitchens, space is limited. You can use colour-coded cutting boards or mark containers with tape to remind you. Teach your workers: raw on one side, cooked on the other. Always wash hands after touching raw ingredients. This simple separation can prevent serious illness.

3. Using clean water: boiling or filtering

Water can look clear but still contain germs that cause diarrhoea, cholera, and typhoid. For making porridge for malnourished children, you must use clean water. The safest water is boiled water. Bring water to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute. If you are at high altitude, boil for 3 minutes. Let it cool covered before using. Boiling kills all germs. If you cannot boil, use a filter. Ceramic filters, biosand filters, or commercial filters can remove germs. Follow the manufacturer's instructions. If you have no filter, you can use chlorine bleach: add 2 drops of unscented bleach (like Clorox) per 1 litre of water, stir, and let it stand for 30 minutes. Or use purification tablets. Always store clean water in a clean, covered container. Use a ladle or cup to take water outnever dip your hands in.

For washing utensils and hands, you also need clean water. If water is scarce, use a tippy-tap for handwashing and reuse water for rinsing utensils only after it has been boiled or treated. Do not use untreated water to rinse utensils that will touch the porridge.

In rural areas, collect rainwater or use protected wells. If you use river or stream water, always treat it. Boiling is the cheapest and most reliable method. Make sure your workers know how to boil water and store it safely. Keep a covered pot of boiled water in the kitchen at all times. Change it daily. Never mix clean water with dirty water. Teach children to drink only clean water. By using clean water, you protect the child from infections that worsen malnutrition.

4. Cleaning your workspace and equipment daily

A clean workspace is essential for safe food preparation. Every day, before and after making porridge, clean your kitchen or cooking area. Start by removing all food scraps and wiping surfaces with a clean cloth and soapy water. Use a disinfectant if available, or a bleach solution (1 tablespoon of bleach per 1 litre of water). Scrub tables, counters, and shelves. Don't forget corners and under equipment. Sweep and mop the floor. Empty rubbish bins and wash them. Keep animals out of the kitchen.

Equipment like pots, spoons, grinding mills, and storage containers must be cleaned after each use. Wash with hot soapy water, rinse, and sanitize. For grinding mills, disassemble if possible and brush out all flour residue. Dry equipment completely before storing. Store clean equipment in a clean, dry place, covered if possible. If you use a mortar and pestle, wash and dry it after every use. Never leave dirty utensils in the sink overnight.

Create a daily cleaning schedule. For example: Morning: sweep floor, wipe surfaces. After cooking: wash all utensils, clean stove, wipe surfaces. Evening: sweep again, take out rubbish. Assign tasks to workers if you have them. Check their work. Keep cleaning supplies like soap, bleach, brushes, and clean cloths in a dedicated area. Replace dirty cloths daily and wash them with hot water.

A clean workspace reduces flies, rodents, and germs. It also makes your porridge safer and more appealing. In many African communities, a simple routine like this can prevent disease. Make it a habit. Remember, a clean kitchen is a healthy kitchen.

5. Storing finished porridge to avoid spoilage

After cooking, porridge can spoil quickly, especially in hot climates. To keep it safe, you must store it properly. First, cool the porridge quickly. Do not leave it in a large pot at room temperature for more than 2 hours. Divide it into smaller shallow containers to cool faster. Once cooled, cover tightly and store in a refrigerator if you have one. Set fridge to 4C or below. If you don't have a fridge, use a cool, dark place like a clay pot or a evaporative cooler (zeer pot). You can also keep it in a clean, covered container and place it in a basin of cool water, changing the water often.

Porridge should be eaten within 24 hours if refrigerated. If kept without refrigeration, eat within 2 hours. Never leave porridge at room temperature for more than 2 hours. If you must store it longer, reheat it to boiling every 6 hours to kill germs, but this affects taste and nutrients. Better to make fresh porridge daily. If you see any mould, smell sour, or see bubbles, throw it away. Do not taste it to checkit could make you sick.

When reheating, bring porridge to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute, stirring constantly. Serve immediately. Do not reheat more than once. For feeding malnourished children, always serve fresh porridge. If you have leftovers, cool and refrigerate within 2 hours. Label with date and time. Use within 24 hours. Teach your workers these rules. Use clean, food-grade containers with tight lids. Never store porridge in containers that held raw meat or chemicals. Keep storage area clean and free of pests. By storing properly, you prevent spoilage and keep the child safe.

6. Teaching hygiene rules to your workers

If you have workers helping you make porridge, you must teach them hygiene rules. Start by explaining why hygiene matters: malnourished children can die from infections. Then, show them the correct methods. Demonstrate handwashing with soap for 20 seconds. Show them how to wash utensils with soap and hot water. Explain that they must wash hands after using the toilet, after handling raw ingredients, and after touching animals. Make it clear: no dirty hands touch the porridge.

Create simple rules and post them in the kitchen. Use pictures if workers cannot read. For example: a drawing of hands with soap, a drawing of a clean pot. Have daily reminders. Check their work. Praise good hygiene. Correct mistakes gently. Provide the tools they need: soap, clean water, brushes, clean cloths, and a handwashing station. If possible, use a tippy-tap. Assign one person to be the hygiene leader each day. That person checks hands and utensils before cooking starts.

Train workers on all aspects: keeping raw and cooked separate, using clean water, cleaning workspace daily, and storing porridge safely. Use a checklist. For example: Did you wash hands? Are utensils clean? Is water boiled? Is workspace clean? Is porridge stored properly? Go through the checklist together at the end of each day. Give feedback. If a worker is sick, especially with diarrhoea or vomiting, they must not handle food. They should rest and see a health worker.

Hold a short meeting once a week to review hygiene. Ask workers for ideas to improve. Reward good practices with small incentives. Remember, you are responsible for the safety of the porridge. By teaching your workers, you multiply your efforts. They will also teach others. In this way, you build a culture of hygiene that protects all the children you feed.

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Safe Food Handling and Hygiene for Fortified Corn-Soy Porridge</h2>

<p>In the production of fortified corn-soy porridge for malnourished children, food safety is not just a regulatory requirement it is a business-critical control point. A single contamination event can sicken vulnerable children, trigger product recalls, destroy customer trust, and close your processing unit. In the African context, where cold chains are limited, water quality varies, and many processors operate semi-formally, hygiene discipline is the difference between a thriving enterprise and a liability. This reference module gives you practical, low-cost, verifiable hygiene controls you can implement today whether you run a small household-based production unit or a growing registered food business.</p>

<h3>1. Washing Hands and Utensils Before Preparing Porridge</h3>
<p>Hands and utensils are the most common vehicles for transferring pathogens such as <em>Salmonella</em>, <em>E. coli</em>, and <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em> into porridge. Because fortified porridge is often consumed by children under five, even a low infectious dose can cause severe diarrhoea and worsen malnutrition. Establish a handwashing station at the entrance of your production area with running water (a tap with a bucket or a tippy-tap), soap, and a clean drying cloth or air dryer. Wash hands for at least 20 seconds, scrubbing palms, backs of hands, between fingers, and under nails. Utensils mixing bowls, spoons, ladles, measuring cups, and sieves must be washed with hot water and detergent, rinsed in clean water, and sanitised by immersion in boiling water for at least 30 seconds or by using food-grade sanitiser. Air-dry utensils on a clean rack; do not wipe with a shared cloth, which is a common source of recontamination in African food businesses.</p>

<h3>2. Keeping Raw and Cooked Ingredients Separate</h3>
<p>Cross-contamination between raw agricultural commodities (maize, soy, groundnuts) and finished or cooked porridge is a major risk. Raw grains and legumes can carry mould spores, aflatoxins, and bacteria. Store raw ingredients in sealed, labelled containers on lower shelves, and finished porridge or cooked ingredients on upper shelves or in a separate area. Use colour-coded utensils: for example, red for raw ingredients, green for cooked. Never use the same bowl or spoon for raw and cooked porridge without washing and sanitising in between. In many African processing units, the same mill or blender is used for raw and finished products if this is unavoidable, establish a documented clean-down procedure between batches and verify it with a supervisor's signature.</p>

<h3>3. Using Clean Water: Boiling or Filtering</h3>
<p>Water is used for cooking, cleaning, and handwashing. If your water source is a borehole, well, river, or unprotected spring, assume it is contaminated. Bring water to a rolling boil for at least one minute (or three minutes at high altitude) before using it for porridge preparation or for rinsing utensils that will contact food. Alternatively, use a certified filter (ceramic, membrane, or biosand) followed by boiling if the source is highly turbid. Store treated water in a clean, covered container with a tap or ladle never dip cups into stored water. Test your water source quarterly for total coliforms and <em>E. coli</em> using a local laboratory or a field test kit. Document results and corrective actions. This record is also useful for buyers and regulators who audit your food safety system.</p>

<h3>4. Cleaning Your Workspace and Equipment Daily</h3>
<p>Daily cleaning is a non-negotiable routine. At the end of every production day, sweep or vacuum floors, wash work surfaces with detergent and clean water, and sanitise with a food-safe sanitiser (e.g., diluted bleach at 100200 ppm available chlorine, or 70% alcohol for small surfaces). Clean equipment such as mixers, mills, sealers, and drying trays. Remove all food debris from corners, under equipment, and in drains. Keep cleaning chemicals in a locked cabinet away from food. Maintain a cleaning schedule that specifies what is cleaned, how, how often, and by whom. In shared market spaces or community processing centres, assign a cleaning roster and post it visibly. A clean workspace also improves worker morale and reduces pest problems, which are costly to control.</p>

<h3>5. Storing Finished Porridge to Avoid Spoilage</h3>
<p>Fortified corn-soy porridge is a low-moisture product if properly dried, but if it is stored with residual moisture or exposed to humid air, mould and bacteria can grow. Cool the porridge completely before packaging. Use food-grade, airtight containers or sealed sachets. Store in a cool, dry, dark place, off the floor on pallets, away from walls, and away from chemicals or fuel. Label each batch with production date, batch number, and expiry date. For wet or semi-moist porridge, refrigerate below 5C and use within 48 hours, or freeze for longer storage. In areas with unreliable electricity, consider solar drying to below 10% moisture and use oxygen absorbers or vacuum sealing. Conduct a daily check for pests, moisture, and off-odours; remove any compromised product immediately and record the loss.</p>

<h3>6. Teaching Hygiene Rules to Your Workers</h3>
<p>Your workers are your first line of defence. Train every new employee before they handle food, and refresh training quarterly. Cover handwashing, clean clothing and hair covering, no jewellery or nail polish, exclusion from work when sick (especially diarrhoea, vomiting, or open wounds), and proper use of gloves when handling ready-to-eat porridge. Use simple visual aids in local languages posters, pictograms, and demonstrations. Keep a training log with names, dates, and topics. Empower a hygiene supervisor to conduct daily checks and correct unsafe behaviour immediately. Consider incentives for teams that maintain high hygiene scores; this turns compliance into a shared business goal. For small businesses, a one-page hygiene policy signed by each worker is a low-cost but powerful accountability tool.</p>

<h3 style="color:#b71c1c;margin-top:0;"> RED SAFETY WARNING: Aflatoxin and Moisture Control</h3>
<p style="color:#b71c1c;font-weight:bold;">Never use maize, soy, or groundnuts that show visible mould, discolouration, or a musty smell. Aflatoxins produced by <em>Aspergillus flavus</em> and <em>A. parasiticus</em> are heat-stable and cannot be destroyed by cooking or boiling. They cause liver cancer, immune suppression, and stunted growth in children. In Africa, aflatoxin contamination is a leading cause of foodborne illness and export rejections. Test suspect batches with a rapid aflatoxin test kit (available from local agricultural extension offices) before processing. Store all raw ingredients below 13% moisture and in dry, ventilated conditions. If a batch tests above 10 ppb total aflatoxins for children's food, reject it entirely do not blend it with clean stock. Document every rejection and inform your supplier in writing. This protects children's lives and your business licence.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul style="font-size:15px;line-height:1.8;">
<li> Wash hands for 20 seconds with soap and running water before handling porridge, after toilet use, and after handling raw ingredients.</li>
<li> Wash and sanitise all utensils and work surfaces before and after each production batch.</li>
<li> Keep raw ingredients and cooked/finished porridge in separate containers, shelves, and utensils.</li>
<li> Boil or filter all water used for cooking, rinsing, and handwashing; store treated water in covered containers.</li>
<li> Clean floors, walls, equipment, and drains daily; keep cleaning chemicals locked away from food.</li>
<li> Cool porridge completely before packaging; use airtight, food-grade packaging and label with batch number and date.</li>
<li> Store finished product in a cool, dry, pest-free area off the floor; check daily for moisture, pests, and off-odours.</li>
<li> Train every worker on hygiene rules before they handle food; keep a signed training log and refresh quarterly.</li>
</ul>
