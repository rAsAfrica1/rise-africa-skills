1. Module 1 Lesson 1: Foundations of Business Translation in Chinese, Arabic and Russian for African Markets

Welcome to Module 1 of Business Chinese, Arabic and Russian Translation. This lesson establishes the foundation for translating business documents across three major trade languages: Mandarin Chinese, Modern Standard Arabic and Russian. Africa is not a single market. It is fifty-four countries with distinct legal systems, currencies, trade blocs and business cultures. A translator working between these languages and African contexts must understand that a contract signed in Lagos, a tender submitted in Nairobi, an invoice issued in Cairo and a shipping document processed in Durban all carry different expectations. Your role is not word replacement. Your role is commercial risk reduction. Begin with the concept of register. Business Chinese uses four-character phrases known as chengyu in formal correspondence, but modern trade emails often mix these with direct English loanwords. Arabic business writing favors elaborate openings and religious formulas such as bismillah in some regions, yet Gulf and North African styles differ sharply. Russian business prose prefers passive constructions, nominalizations and precise numeric formatting. When you translate a quotation from a Chinese supplier into Arabic for a buyer in Senegal, you must preserve the payment terms, Incoterms and validity period exactly. A single misplaced decimal or a confused date format can void a deal. Dates are a classic trap. Chinese uses year-month-day. Arabic and Russian both commonly use day-month-year, but Russian numeric dates may appear as day.month.year with dots. African partners may use British or French conventions depending on colonial history. Always convert dates into an unambiguous format such as 15 March 2025 and add the day of the week when the deadline is critical. Currency is another trap. The CFA franc, the Nigerian naira, the South African rand, the Kenyan shilling and the Egyptian pound all behave differently. Never translate a currency name without checking whether the source means the West African CFA franc or the Central African CFA franc. They share a name but are not interchangeable. Numbers also require care. Chinese uses ten thousand and one hundred million as grouping units, not thousands and millions. A Chinese contract stating 5000 wan means fifty million, not five thousand. Russian uses a space as a thousands separator and a comma as a decimal separator. Arabic numerals may be Eastern Arabic or Western Arabic depending on the country. For pan-African business, standardize on Western Arabic numerals unless the client requests otherwise. Legal terminology demands consistency. Create a glossary before you translate. Include terms such as force majeure, letter of credit, bill of lading, proforma invoice, certificate of origin, customs declaration and arbitration clause. Map each term to one approved equivalent in Chinese, Arabic and Russian. Do not improvise synonyms in a legal text. In Chinese, force majeure is bu ke kang li. In Arabic, it is al-quwwa al-qahira. In Russian, it is nepreodolimaya sila. Use these consistently. Cultural context shapes tone. A Chinese business letter may open with pleasantries about the weather or the recipient health. An Arabic letter may open with blessings and a reference to the relationship between the parties. A Russian letter may open with a direct statement of purpose. When translating into an African business context, decide whether to preserve the source tone or adapt to local expectations. For a government tender in Ethiopia, a direct Russian-style opening may be acceptable. For a family-owned import business in Morocco, an Arabic-style relational opening builds trust. Your job is to advise the client, not to decide unilaterally. Practical workflow for this lesson. First, receive the source document and identify its type: contract, tender, invoice, packing list, certificate or correspondence. Second, identify the target audience: government agency, private buyer, customs broker or bank. Third, build a mini glossary of ten to twenty key terms. Fourth, translate the document in full, preserving all numbers, dates, names and references. Fifth, run a back-translation check on critical clauses. Sixth, format the target document to match local conventions. Seventh, deliver with a translator note listing assumptions and unresolved ambiguities. Common errors to avoid. Do not translate company names unless an official registered translation exists. Do not translate addresses into local script unless the client requests it. Do not change units of measure without explicit instruction. Do not omit seals, stamps or signature blocks. Do not assume that a Chinese guanxi relationship term has a direct Arabic or Russian equivalent. Do not assume that Russian gastarbaiter slang belongs in a formal business text. Do not use machine translation output without full human review. Africa-focused examples. A Kenyan tea exporter receives a Chinese purchase order. The translator must convert Chinese metric tons and RMB amounts into Kenyan shillings and confirm whether the price is FOB Mombasa or CIF Shanghai. An Egyptian cotton trader sends an Arabic proforma invoice to a Russian textile importer. The translator must convert Arabic dates and Egyptian pound amounts into Russian conventions and confirm the bank details. A South African mining equipment supplier receives a Russian technical specification. The translator must handle Cyrillic abbreviations, GOST standards and metric units while preparing an English or French version for an African buyer. Each of these scenarios requires more than language skill. It requires commercial awareness. Homework for this lesson. Select one real business document in Chinese, Arabic or Russian. Identify its type, audience and five critical terms. Create a glossary with English, Chinese, Arabic and Russian columns. Translate the first page. Write a 200-word translator note explaining your choices. Submit both the translation and the note. 2. Module 1 Lesson 2: Chinese Business Correspondence and Contract Translation for African Trade

This lesson focuses on Chinese business correspondence and contract translation for African trade. China is Africa largest trading partner. Translation between Chinese and African languages, or between Chinese and the English, French, Portuguese and Arabic languages used in African business, is a daily necessity. You will learn to handle emails, letters, contracts, purchase orders and invoices. Start with the structure of a Chinese business email. A typical email begins with a salutation such as zun jing de plus the recipient title. It then states the purpose, provides details, requests action and closes with a polite formula. Unlike many African business emails, Chinese emails often place the most important request in the middle rather than the first line. When translating into English or French for an African reader, move the request to the opening paragraph. When translating from English or French into Chinese, restore the indirect structure unless the client prefers directness. Chinese business letters use honorifics. Nin is the polite form of you. Gui gong si means your honorable company. Ben gong si means our company. These terms have no exact Arabic or Russian equivalents. In Arabic, you may use hadratukum or al-sharika al-muhtaramah. In Russian, you may use Vasha kompaniya or uvazhaemaya kompaniya. Choose one style per client and stay consistent. Contracts require special care. A Chinese contract typically includes a preamble, definitions, scope of work, price and payment, delivery, quality assurance, intellectual property, confidentiality, force majeure, dispute resolution and signatures. Each section has standard phrases. For example, the parties agree as follows is jia yi shuang fang da cheng xie yi ru xia. This contract shall come into effect upon signature is ben he tong zi qian shu zhi ri qi sheng xiao. Payment terms may state that the buyer shall pay thirty percent deposit within seven days of contract signing. Translate this as a clear obligation, not as a suggestion. Chinese contracts often use the term ying dang for shall and ke yi for may. Do not confuse them. Incoterms are critical. FOB, CIF, EXW, DDP and CFR appear in Chinese contracts as English abbreviations or as Chinese translations. Always keep the Incoterm in its official three-letter form and add a local-language explanation in parentheses on first use. For example, FOB Shanghai means free on board at Shanghai port. An African buyer in Ghana must understand that FOB does not include freight or insurance. A CIF Tema price includes cost, insurance and freight to Tema. A DDP Accra price includes all duties and delivery. Mistranslating an Incoterm can shift thousands of dollars of cost. Payment methods also require precision. Chinese exporters commonly request T/T, which means telegraphic transfer, or L/C, which means letter of credit. A letter of credit may be at sight or usance. Usance means the buyer pays after a specified period, such as ninety days after bill of lading date. Translate usance clearly as deferred payment and state the number of days. Do not use vague terms such as later or after some time. Quality and inspection clauses are frequent sources of dispute. Chinese contracts may refer to CIQ, which is China Inspection and Quarantine, or to SGS inspection. African buyers may require inspection by a local agency such as Cotecna or Bureau Veritas. Translate the inspection standard, the inspection location and the cost bearer. If the contract says the seller shall bear the inspection cost, do not translate it as the buyer may pay. Legal names and addresses must be exact. Chinese company names include the city, the trade name, the industry and the legal form. For example, Shanghai Xinyuan Trading Co., Ltd. becomes Shanghai Xinyuan Maoyi Youxian Gongsi. Do not translate the trade name. Transliterate it using pinyin or the official English registration. African company names may include Ltd, PLC, SARL, GmbH or Pty. Keep the legal form in the original language and add a note if the target reader may not understand it. Seals and signatures matter. Chinese contracts often use a red company seal called a gongzhang. A signature alone may not be sufficient. When translating, note where the seal appears and instruct the client to apply a corresponding seal or stamp on the translated version. Arabic and Russian contracts may require notarization or consular legalization for use in Africa. Advise the client to check with the relevant embassy. Correspondence about delays requires tact. A Chinese supplier may write that due to raw material shortages, delivery will be postponed. Translate this as a factual statement and add a request for a revised delivery schedule. Do not soften it so much that the African buyer misses the delay. Do not harden it so much that the relationship is damaged. Russian and Arabic translations of the same message should preserve the same level of formality. Practical exercise. Take a sample Chinese purchase order for 5000 metric tons of urea fertilizer, FOB Qingdao, at 320 USD per metric ton, payment by irrevocable L/C at sight, shipment within 45 days. Translate it into English for a Nigerian importer. Then translate the English version into Arabic for a Moroccan importer and into Russian for a Russian-speaking African intermediary. Compare the three versions. Check that the quantity, price, Incoterm, payment method and shipment period are identical in all three. Common errors in Chinese contract translation. First, confusing wan with thousand. Second, translating ying dang as should instead of shall. Third, omitting the dispute resolution clause. Fourth, changing the arbitration venue. Fifth, translating a company name instead of transliterating it. Sixth, ignoring the Chinese seal requirement. Seventh, using machine translation for legal terms. Eighth, failing to convert Chinese date format. Ninth, missing the difference between gross weight and net weight. Tenth, translating RMB as yuan when the contract uses CNY. Homework. Obtain a real or sample Chinese contract or purchase order. Identify ten key terms. Create a bilingual glossary in Chinese and English. Translate the payment and delivery clauses into Arabic and Russian. Write a 250-word memo to an African client explaining the three highest risks in the document. 3. Module 1 Lesson 3: Arabic Business Correspondence and Contract Translation for African Markets

This lesson covers Arabic business correspondence and contract translation for African markets. Arabic is an official language in more than twenty countries and a working language in many African trade corridors, especially North Africa, the Sahel and the Horn of Africa. It is also the language of Islamic finance, which is growing across Africa. You will learn to handle emails, letters, contracts, invoices and Islamic finance documents. Begin with the direction of writing. Arabic is written right to left. Numbers may be written left to right within Arabic text. When you translate a table from English or French into Arabic, reverse the column order so that the first column appears on the right. Do not reverse the numbers themselves. For example, a price column should still read from lowest to highest or as the client arranged. Dates in Arabic business documents may use the Gregorian calendar or the Hijri calendar. Always ask which calendar the client requires. If both appear, label them clearly. For example, 15 March 2025 corresponds to 15 Ramadan 1446. A contract that mixes calendars without labels can create a deadline dispute. Arabic business correspondence often opens with a religious formula. Bismillah al-rahman al-rahim means in the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful. Some African clients, especially in secular or Christian-majority contexts, may prefer a neutral opening such as al-salamu alaykum or a simple greeting. Ask the client. Do not assume. The body of an Arabic business letter often uses long, flowing sentences with multiple clauses. When translating into English or French for an African reader, break long sentences into shorter ones. When translating from English or French into Arabic, combine related short sentences into a more formal flow. Honorifics matter. Sayyid or sayyidah may precede a name. Hadratukum means your presence or your honor. Al-muhtaram means the respected. Use these appropriately. Do not overuse them in internal emails. Contracts in Arabic require careful attention to gender and number. Arabic verbs and pronouns change based on whether the subject is masculine or feminine, singular, dual or plural. A company is grammatically feminine in Arabic, even if the company name is masculine. So the company shall pay becomes tadafa al-sharika, with a feminine verb. If you use a masculine verb, the sentence is grammatically wrong and may be considered unprofessional. Islamic finance terms are essential for African markets such as Sudan, Somalia, Nigeria, Kenya, Senegal and South Africa. Murabaha means cost-plus financing. Musharakah means joint venture or partnership. Mudarabah means profit-sharing. Ijarah means leasing. Sukuk means Islamic bonds. Takaful means Islamic insurance. Riba means interest, which is prohibited. Gharar means excessive uncertainty, which is also prohibited. When translating a conventional loan agreement into Arabic for an Islamic finance client, you must not simply translate interest as faida, which means benefit. Use riba when referring to the prohibited concept, and use profit rate or return when referring to a Sharia-compliant profit. This distinction is legally and religiously significant. Invoices in Arabic must include the seller name, address, tax registration number, buyer name, invoice date, invoice number, description of goods, quantity, unit price, total amount, currency, payment terms and bank details. Some African countries require the invoice to be stamped and signed. Some require a legalized translation. Check the destination country rules. For example, Egypt requires commercial invoices to be certified by the Egyptian embassy in the exporting country. Saudi Arabia and the UAE have similar requirements. A translation that ignores these formalities may be rejected at customs. Shipping documents in Arabic include the bill of lading, packing list, certificate of origin and insurance certificate. The bill of lading is a document of title. Do not translate the carrier name or the port name unless an official Arabic name exists. For example, Jebel Ali remains Jebel Ali. Port Said remains Bur Said. Keep the original spelling in Latin letters in parentheses on first use. Certificates of origin often require the exporter to declare that the goods are of a certain origin. In Arabic, this declaration may include a religious oath. Translate the oath accurately and advise the client that it has legal weight. Do not omit it. Correspondence about payment delays requires cultural sensitivity. In many Arabic-speaking African contexts, direct confrontation is avoided. A polite reminder may be phrased as we kindly request your esteemed attention to the outstanding amount. When translating into English or French, you may need to make the request more explicit for a bank or legal team. When translating from English or French into Arabic, you may need to soften the demand while preserving the legal claim. Practical exercise. Take a sample Arabic murabaha contract for the sale of agricultural machinery to a Sudanese buyer. Identify the following: the parties, the asset, the cost price, the profit amount, the selling price, the payment schedule, the late payment clause and the Sharia supervisory board approval. Translate the payment schedule into English and then into Russian. Check that the profit amount is not translated as interest. Common errors in Arabic contract translation. First, using a masculine verb for a feminine company. Second, translating riba as interest without explanation. Third, reversing numbers in tables. Fourth, omitting the Hijri date when the client requires it. Fifth, translating a port name into a non-standard form. Sixth, ignoring the embassy legalization requirement. Seventh, using a neutral greeting when the client expects an Islamic greeting. Eighth, using an Islamic greeting when the client expects a neutral one. Ninth, confusing murabaha with musharakah. Tenth, translating takaful as insurance without noting the Sharia-compliant structure. Homework. Obtain a sample Arabic invoice or contract. Identify ten key terms. Create a bilingual glossary in Arabic and English. Translate the payment and delivery clauses into Chinese and Russian. Write a 250-word memo to an African client explaining the three highest risks in the document. 4. Module 1 Lesson 4: Russian Business Correspondence and Contract Translation for African Markets

This lesson covers Russian business correspondence and contract translation for African markets. Russia has expanded trade with Africa in agriculture, energy, mining, defense and education. Russian is also used in some African countries as a legacy language, though English, French, Portuguese and Arabic dominate. Your work will often involve translating Russian contracts, tenders, technical specifications and invoices into English or French for African clients, and translating African business documents into Russian for Russian partners. Begin with the Russian alphabet. It has thirty-three letters. Do not confuse Cyrillic letters with Latin lookalikes. The Cyrillic letter R looks like a Latin P. The Cyrillic letter S looks like a Latin C. The Cyrillic letter N looks like a Latin H. When transliterating names, use the official transliteration system. For example, the Russian name Gorbachev is not Gorbachov. The city Moskva is Moscow in English. The city Sankt-Peterburg is Saint Petersburg. For African names, use the spelling preferred by the person or the official document. Do not Russify an African name without permission. Russian business correspondence is often more formal and direct than Chinese or Arabic correspondence. A typical letter begins with a salutation such as uvazhaemyi or uvazhaemaya, meaning respected. It then states the purpose, provides details and closes with a formal signature block. Unlike Arabic, Russian does not usually open with religious formulas. Unlike Chinese, Russian does not usually open with weather pleasantries. Directness is acceptable. However, Russian business culture values hierarchy. Address the recipient by their full name and patronymic if known. For example, Ivan Petrovich. If you do not know the patronymic, use the first name and surname with the appropriate title. Contracts in Russian follow a standard structure. The preamble identifies the parties and the legal basis. The subject section defines the goods or services. The price and payment section states the amount, currency and method. The delivery section states the Incoterm, the place and the date. The quality section states the standards, such as GOST or TU. The liability section states penalties and damages. The force majeure section states exceptions. The dispute resolution section states the court or arbitration venue. The signature section includes the legal addresses and bank details. Russian contracts often use the term postavshchik for supplier and pokupatel for buyer. The term tovar means goods. The term usluga means service. The term oplata means payment. The term srok means deadline or term. Do not confuse srok with data, which means date. A delivery srok is a period. A delivery data is a specific day. Translate accordingly. Numbers and units require precision. Russian uses a comma as a decimal separator and a space as a thousands separator. For example, 1 000 000,50 means one million and fifty kopecks or cents. Russian contracts may use the metric system exclusively. Convert to local units only if the client requests it. For example, one metric ton equals one thousand kilograms. One hectare equals ten thousand square meters. One kilometer equals one thousand meters. Do not convert currency without stating the exchange rate and the date. Russian banks may require a currency control form called a passport of a transaction. This is a Russian regulatory document. It is not a passport in the ordinary sense. Translate it as transaction passport or currency control form and explain its purpose. African clients may be unfamiliar with it. Russian tenders often use the term zakupka for procurement and tender for tender. The term lot means a specific package within a tender. The term zayavka means application or bid. The term obespechenie zayavki means bid security. The term garantia means guarantee or warranty. The term shtraf means fine or penalty. The term neustoika means penalty for breach. Translate these terms consistently. Technical specifications may include GOST standards. GOST is the Russian state standard system. For example, GOST 12.1.005-88 is a standard for air quality in working zones. Do not translate the GOST number. Keep it as is and add a note if the African client needs an equivalent standard such as ISO or EN. Russian invoices include the seller name, address, tax identification number called INN, the buyer name, the invoice number, the date, the goods description, the quantity, the unit price, the total amount, the currency, the bank details and the signature. Some Russian invoices require a round seal called pechat. Some require a stamp for foreign trade. Advise the client to check the Russian customs requirements. Correspondence about payment delays should be firm and factual. Russian business culture accepts direct reminders. A polite reminder may be phrased as we request payment of the outstanding amount within ten banking days. When translating into English or French for an African client, preserve the deadline and the amount. When translating from English or French into Russian, use the formal vy form for you, not the informal ty form. The informal form is unacceptable in business correspondence. Practical exercise. Take a sample Russian contract for the supply of wheat to an Egyptian buyer. Identify the parties, the goods, the quantity, the price, the Incoterm, the delivery period, the payment method, the penalty clause and the arbitration venue. Translate the payment and penalty clauses into English and then into Arabic. Check that the penalty amount and the currency are identical in all versions. Common errors in Russian contract translation. First, confusing Cyrillic and Latin letters. Second, using the informal ty form. Third, omitting the patronymic when it is known. Fourth, translating GOST numbers. Fifth, converting currency without stating the rate and date. Sixth, confusing srok and data. Seventh, translating neustoika as interest. Eighth, ignoring the transaction passport requirement. Ninth, using a comma as a thousands separator in the target language when the target uses a space. Tenth, translating a Russian company name instead of using the official registered form. Homework. Obtain a sample Russian invoice or contract. Identify ten key terms. Create a bilingual glossary in Russian and English. Translate the payment and delivery clauses into Chinese and Arabic. Write a 250-word memo to an African client explaining the three highest risks in the document. 5. Module 1 Lesson 5: Quality Assurance, Certification and Localization for African Business Translation

This lesson covers quality assurance, certification and localization for African business translation. You have learned the foundations of Chinese, Arabic and Russian business translation. You have practiced correspondence and contracts. Now you will learn how to ensure quality, meet certification requirements and localize for African markets. Quality assurance begins with a translation brief. A brief is a written instruction from the client. It should include the source language, the target language, the document type, the target audience, the purpose, the deadline, the format, the reference materials and the review process. If the client does not provide a brief, create one and ask the client to confirm it. A translation without a brief is a guess. A guess is a risk. The brief should state whether the translation is for information, for signature, for customs, for a court or for publication. Each purpose requires a different level of accuracy and formatting. A translation for information may tolerate minor stylistic differences. A translation for signature must be exact. A translation for customs must include all required fields and stamps. A translation for a court may require a sworn translator and an affidavit. A translation for publication may require copywriting and localization. Terminology management is the second pillar of quality. Build a glossary for each client. The glossary should include the source term, the target term, the part of speech, the context, the source of the term and the date. Use a spreadsheet or a terminology tool. Do not rely on memory. For Chinese, include the characters, the pinyin and the English. For Arabic, include the Arabic script, the transliteration and the English. For Russian, include the Cyrillic, the transliteration and the English. Update the glossary after every project. The third pillar is revision. A translation should be revised by a second translator who is a native speaker of the target language and who understands the subject matter. The reviser checks accuracy, completeness, terminology, grammar, spelling, punctuation and formatting. The reviser should not rewrite the translation to match their personal style. The reviser should correct errors and note any unresolved issues. The fourth pillar is proofreading. Proofreading checks the final formatted document for typos, missing text, broken tables, incorrect fonts and incorrect page numbers. Proofreading is not the same as revision. A document can be accurately translated but poorly proofread. The fifth pillar is certification. Some African countries require certified translations for legal, immigration, customs and tender documents. Certification may be a stamp from a sworn translator, a notary, a ministry of justice, a chamber of commerce or an embassy. The requirements vary by country. For example, Egypt requires many commercial documents to be legalized by the Egyptian embassy. Nigeria may require a notarized translation for some court documents. Kenya may require a certified translation for immigration. South Africa may require a sworn translation for court use. Always check the destination country requirements before you begin. Do not promise certification unless you can deliver it. Localization is the sixth pillar. Localization means adapting the translation to the target market. It goes beyond language. It includes date formats, number formats, currency formats, units of measure, address formats, name formats, payment methods, legal references and cultural references. For example, a Chinese contract may refer to the PRC Contract Law. An African client may need a reference to the local contract law or to the CISG, the United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods. A Russian contract may refer to the Russian Civil Code. An African client may need a reference to the OHADA uniform acts if the country is a member of OHADA. OHADA is the Organization for the Harmonization of Business Law in Africa. It covers seventeen countries, mostly in West and Central Africa. If your client is in Senegal, Cote d Ivoire, Cameroon or Gabon, OHADA references may be more relevant than Russian or Chinese legal references. Localization also affects names. An African name may include a family name, a given name, a middle name and a traditional name. The order may differ from the order in Chinese, Arabic or Russian. Ask the client how they want their name to appear. Do not rearrange names without permission. Localization also affects addresses. An African address may include a street, a area, a landmark, a post office box and a city. Do not force it into a Chinese, Arabic or Russian address format. Localization also affects payment methods. Mobile money is common in Kenya, Tanzania, Ghana and other African countries. A Chinese or Russian contract that assumes a bank transfer may need a note about mobile money options. Do not change the contract terms without client approval. Instead, add a translator note. Quality assurance also requires confidentiality. Business documents contain trade secrets, prices, customer lists and bank details. Use secure file transfer. Do not share documents on public platforms. Do not use free machine translation tools that store your data. If you use machine translation, use a secure enterprise tool and review the output fully. Practical exercise. Take one of the translations you produced in the previous lessons. Create a translation brief. Build a glossary of twenty terms. Revise the translation with a partner. Proofread the formatted document. Write a certification checklist for the destination country. Write a localization checklist for the target market. Submit all six items. Common errors in quality assurance. First, translating without a brief. Second, using inconsistent terminology. Third, skipping revision. Fourth, confusing revision with proofreading. Fifth, promising certification without checking the requirements. Sixth, ignoring OHADA or local legal references. Seventh, rearranging African names. Eighth, forcing African addresses into foreign formats. Ninth, using insecure machine translation. Tenth, delivering a file that cannot be opened by the client. Homework. Select a real African business scenario involving Chinese, Arabic or Russian. Prepare a complete translation package: brief, glossary, translation, revision note, proofreading note, certification checklist and localization checklist. Present your package in a 500-word report. This concludes Module 1. You now have the foundation to translate business documents across Chinese, Arabic and Russian for African markets. 6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Terminology, Units and Quality Benchmarks for Translating African Agribusiness Documents

Translators working between Chinese, Arabic, Russian and the major African trade languages (English, French, Portuguese, Swahili, Amharic) quickly discover that agribusiness translation is not a literary exercise. It is a commercial function. A single mistranslated unit, pesticide concentration, or veterinary certificate clause can stop a container at the port, void an import permit, or expose a buyer to a product recall. This practical reference lesson gives you the working tools standard terminology, real benchmark figures, verification habits and authoritative sources that you will apply across Lessons 1 to 5 of this module. Treat it as your desk reference whenever you translate a contract, a phytosanitary certificate, a feed specification, or a cold-chain label destined for an African market.

Why Units and Terminology Are Commercial Risks

African agribusiness imports and exports move through a dense regulatory layer. A Chinese feed additive specification written as "500 g/ton" must not become "500 kg/ton" in a Swahili or French label. An Arabic veterinary certificate stating a withdrawal period of "7 days" must not be rendered as "7 weeks." Russian fertiliser contracts frequently express nutrient content as mass percentage of N, PO and KO; if the translator converts these into elemental phosphorus or potassium without saying so, the buyer may reject the batch as non-compliant. SAFETY WARNING Pesticides, Veterinary Drugs and Food Additives

Never translate active ingredient names, concentrations, pre-harvest intervals, withdrawal periods or maximum residue limits (MRLs) by analogy or machine translation alone. A single digit error in a pesticide label can cause crop loss, human poisoning or a permanent export ban. Always verify against the importing country's registered product list for example NAFDAC (Nigeria), KEPHIS (Kenya), DALRRD (South Africa), NFSA (Egypt) or the Ghana EPA. Where the source document is in Chinese, Arabic or Russian, obtain the manufacturer's official English or French safety data sheet (SDS) and cross-check every number. If the numbers do not match, stop and escalate to the client before delivery.

Practical Workflow for Module 1 Lessons

When you receive a Chinese sales contract, an Arabic veterinary certificate or a Russian phytosanitary document, work through five fixed steps. First, identify the document type and the destination authority. Second, build or open a term base that already contains the FAO and ILRI equivalents for the product category. Third, convert and annotate every unit hectares, tonnes, ppm, g/kg, C showing both the source and target expression in a translator's note. Fourth, verify all regulated values (MRLs, withdrawal periods, moisture, protein) against the tables above and the importing authority's published list. Fifth, deliver with a signed translator's certificate if the client requires it, and log any rejected clause into your feedback file. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Identify the destination authority first. Know whether the document goes to NAFDAC, KEPHIS, DALRRD, NFSA, Ghana FDA or a regional body before translating a single term.
  • Lock the term base before drafting. Use FAO, ILRI and Codex glossaries; never invent a term for a regulated substance.
  • Show every unit twice. Source unit and target unit, with conversion factor, in a translator's note or bracket.
  • Distinguish oxide from elemental. For fertilisers, state N, PO, KO explicitly; for feeds, state as-fed or dry matter.
  • Verify every number in a safety or health clause. Concentrations, MRLs, withdrawal periods and storage temperatures must be cross-checked against an official source.
  • Preserve certificate wording. Phytosanitary and veterinary certificates follow IPPC and WOAH models; do not paraphrase.
  • Keep a rejection log. Every port rejection or buyer complaint becomes a term-base entry and a checklist update.
  • Sign and stamp where required. Many African authorities require a certified translator's declaration with name, registration number and date.
