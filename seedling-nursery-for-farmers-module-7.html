1. Natural Light Requirements for Seedlings

Most vegetable seedlings need 12 to 16 hours of bright, indirect light each day to grow strong and stocky. In many parts of Africa, natural sunlight is abundant, but direct midday sun can be too intense and scorch tender leaves. The goal is to give seedlings enough light without overheating them. If you are growing in an open nursery, choose a spot that receives morning sun and afternoon shade, especially in hot lowland areas. In cooler highland regions, full morning sun is fine, but still protect from harsh early afternoon rays.

A simple way to measure light is to observe the shadows. If a seedling casts a sharp, dark shadow, the light is strong. If the shadow is soft and blurry, light is moderate. Seedlings that are not getting enough light become tall, thin, and palethis is called etiolation. They stretch toward the light source and have weak stems. If you see this, move the seedlings to a brighter spot or increase the number of hours they receive light.

In Africa, many farmers use natural shade from trees or buildings. But be careful: trees can also drop leaves or harbour pests. A better option is to use a simple shade structure made from local materials like bamboo or wooden poles, covered with shade netting. For most seedlings, 50% shade netting is ideal in hot climates. In cooler areas or during cloudy weather, you may remove the netting to let in more light.

If you are growing seedlings indoors or in a greenhouse, place them near a window that faces the sun. Rotate the trays every few days so all sides get light. Without rotation, seedlings will lean toward the window and grow unevenly. Remember that light intensity drops quickly with distance from a windoweven 30 cm away can be too dark. So keep trays close to the glass, but not touching it to avoid overheating.

Finally, consider the season. During the rainy season, days are shorter and cloudier, so you may need to reduce shade or use supplemental lighting. In the dry season, light is intense, so increase shade. Always watch your seedlings: their colour, stem thickness, and leaf size tell you if they are getting the right amount of light. Adjust as needed.

2. Using Shade Nets and Row Covers

Shade nets and row covers are simple, affordable tools that protect seedlings from too much sun, heavy rain, and pests. Shade nets come in different percentages: 30%, 50%, 70%, and 90%. The percentage tells you how much sunlight is blocked. For most vegetable seedlings in Africa, 50% shade netting works well in hot, sunny conditions. In very hot areas like the Sahel, you might need 70% during the hottest part of the day. In cooler highlands, 30% is often enough.

To set up a shade net, build a simple frame using wooden poles or bamboo. The frame should be about 1.5 to 2 meters high so you can walk under it. Stretch the net over the top and tie it down securely. Make sure the net is at least 30 cm above the seedlings to allow air movement. Good airflow prevents fungal diseases like damping off. You can also use shade nets on the sides of a nursery if the sun comes in low in the morning or evening.

Row covers are lightweight fabrics that you drape directly over seedlings or over low hoops. They are excellent for protecting against insects like aphids and whiteflies, and they also moderate temperature. A floating row cover allows light, air, and water to pass through but keeps bugs out. Use hoops made from wire or flexible sticks to keep the cover off the leaves. Bury the edges with soil to secure it. Row covers are especially useful for seedlings that are prone to pest attacks, such as tomatoes and brassicas.

In Africa, you can buy shade nets and row covers from agricultural supply shops or online. They come in different sizes; a common size is 3 meters wide by 10 meters long. Expect to pay between $20 and $50 for a good quality shade net, depending on the percentage and size. Row covers are cheaper, around $10 to $20 for a 10-meter roll. They last for several seasons if you store them out of the sun when not in use.

When using shade nets, remember that they also reduce air movement, which can increase humidity and disease. So on cool or cloudy days, remove or roll up the nets to let in more light and air. During heavy rain, shade nets alone may not protect seedlings from being battered. You can add a plastic sheet on top of the net during storms, but remove it afterwards to avoid overheating. Always check seedlings daily for signs of stress: yellowing leaves, wilting, or stretched stems. Adjust your shade and covers accordingly.

3. Supplemental Lighting Options

In some situations, natural light is not enough for healthy seedling growth. This happens during the rainy season when days are short and cloudy, or if you grow seedlings indoors in a dark room. Supplemental lighting can help, but it must be used correctly to avoid wasting money and electricity. The goal is to provide light that mimics the sun's spectrum, especially the blue and red wavelengths that plants use for photosynthesis.

For small-scale seedling production, fluorescent tubes (T5 or T8) are a good option. They are cheap to buy and run, and they produce little heat. Place them 15 to 30 cm above the seedlings. A single 1.2-meter tube can cover a tray of about 30 cm by 60 cm. You can use a mix of cool white and warm white tubes to get a broader spectrum. LED grow lights are more expensive but last longer and use less electricity. They come in panels or strips. A 50-watt LED panel can cover about 1 square meter and should be hung 30 to 45 cm above the plants.

Light duration matters as much as intensity. Most seedlings need 14 to 16 hours of light per day. If you rely on natural light for part of the day, you only need to supplement to reach that total. For example, if seedlings get 8 hours of natural light, add 6 to 8 hours of artificial light. Use a timer to turn lights on and off consistently. Running lights 24 hours a day is not good; seedlings need a dark period to respire and grow properly.

In Africa, where electricity can be unreliable or expensive, consider solar-powered LED grow lights. They are available in many agricultural supply stores and online. A small solar panel charges a battery during the day, and the lights run at night. This is ideal for off-grid locations. Another low-cost option is to use a generator or inverter if you already have one. But always calculate the cost: running a 100-watt light for 8 hours a day uses 0.8 kWh. If electricity costs $0.15 per kWh, that's about $0.12 per day per light. For many seedlings, that is affordable.

When using supplemental lights, keep them clean and replace old tubes or bulbs as they dim over time. Also, monitor temperature: some lights produce heat, which can dry out seedlings. Place a thermometer near the plants and adjust ventilation if needed. Remember that light is only one factor; seedlings also need proper water, nutrients, and temperature. Supplemental lighting works best when combined with good overall management.

4. Temperature Control in Nurseries

Temperature is critical for seedling growth. Most vegetable seedlings grow best between 18C and 24C during the day and slightly cooler at night (15C to 18C). If temperatures are too high, seedlings become leggy, wilt, or dry out. If too low, growth slows and leaves may turn purple or yellow. In Africa, temperature extremes are common: hot days in the lowlands and cold nights in the highlands. So you must manage temperature actively.

In hot climates, the main goal is to cool the nursery. Shade nets reduce direct sun and can lower temperatures by 5C to 10C. Ventilation is also key: open sides of a greenhouse or shade house allow hot air to escape. You can also use evaporative cooling. A simple method is to hang wet burlap sacks or cloth on the windward side of the nursery. As air passes through, it cools. Misting seedlings with water in the morning can also help, but avoid wetting leaves in the evening to prevent disease. If you have a fan, use it to circulate air, but don't blow directly on seedlings.

In cold climates, especially in highland areas where nights can drop below 10C, you need to keep seedlings warm. A greenhouse or cold frame traps heat. You can also use row covers or plastic tunnels over seedlings at night. Another option is to place black plastic or stones around the nursery; they absorb heat during the day and release it at night. In very cold areas, you might use a small heater, but this is expensive. A cheaper method is to compost manure under the seedling benches; the composting process generates heat. Just make sure it doesn't get too hot or attract pests.

Monitoring temperature is essential. Hang a simple max-min thermometer in the nursery. Check it every morning and evening. Record the readings so you can see patterns. If you notice temperatures regularly exceed 30C or drop below 10C, take action. For example, if days are too hot, add more shade or increase ventilation. If nights are too cold, add row covers or move seedlings to a more sheltered spot.

Remember that temperature affects water needs. In hot weather, seedlings transpire more and need more frequent watering. In cold weather, they need less. Always check soil moisture before watering. Also, avoid sudden temperature changes: hardening off (which we cover in another lesson) helps seedlings adapt to outdoor conditions. By controlling temperature, you give your seedlings the best chance to grow strong and healthy.

5. Protecting Seedlings from Extreme Weather

Extreme weather can destroy a nursery in minutes. In Africa, common threats include heavy rain, strong winds, hail, and heatwaves. You need to prepare for these events to protect your investment. The first step is to choose a good site. Avoid low-lying areas that flood. If possible, place the nursery on a slight slope so water drains away. Also, avoid spots exposed to strong winds, like hilltops or open fields. If you must use an exposed site, build a windbreak using trees, tall crops like maize, or a fence made of woven sticks.

For heavy rain, a simple roof over the nursery is essential. Use clear plastic sheeting or corrugated iron sheets. Make sure the roof slopes so water runs off. If you use shade nets, they alone won't stop rain. You can add a plastic sheet on top during the rainy season, but remove it when the sun comes out to prevent overheating. Also, ensure that seedling trays are not sitting directly on the ground; place them on benches or raised beds. This prevents waterlogging and reduces disease.

Strong winds can knock over trays and break stems. Secure trays to benches with wire or string. If you use row covers, anchor them well with soil or rocks. For shade nets, use sturdy poles and tight ropes. During a windstorm, you can also move seedlings into a shed or garage if possible. If not, create a temporary barrier using palm fronds or tarpaulins on the windward side.

Hail is rare but devastating. If hail is forecast, cover seedlings with a thick blanket or cardboard. Remove it immediately after the storm to let light in. Heatwaves can be managed with shade, misting, and increased watering. You can also whitewash greenhouse walls to reflect heat. In extreme heat, avoid transplanting; wait for cooler weather.

Finally, have a plan. Listen to weather forecasts on radio or phone apps. If you know a storm is coming, take action early. Keep emergency supplies like plastic sheets, ropes, and extra shade nets. After any extreme weather, inspect seedlings for damage. Remove broken leaves and stems, and treat any wounds with fungicide if necessary. Healthy seedlings recover faster, so maintain good overall care. By being prepared, you can save your nursery from disaster.

6. Hardening Off Seedlings

Hardening off is the process of gradually exposing seedlings to outdoor conditions before transplanting. Seedlings grown in a protected nurseryunder shade nets, in greenhouses, or indoorsare soft and tender. If you transplant them directly into the field, they often suffer transplant shock: wilting, sunscald, or even death. Hardening off toughens them up over 7 to 14 days. It is a simple but crucial step for success.

The process starts about two weeks before you plan to transplant. On the first day, move seedlings outdoors for just 2 to 3 hours in a sheltered spot with morning sun and no wind. Bring them back inside or under shade afterwards. On day two, extend the time to 4 hours. Increase by 1 to 2 hours each day. By the end of the first week, they should be outside for 6 to 8 hours. During the second week, leave them out overnight if temperatures are mild (above 10C). If nights are cold, bring them in but reduce watering to toughen them.

Reduce watering during hardening off. Let the soil dry slightly between waterings; this encourages roots to grow deeper and makes leaves tougher. But don't let them wilt completely. Also, reduce shade gradually. If they were under 50% shade, move them to 30% shade, then to full sun by the end of the period. If you use a greenhouse, open vents and doors more each day. If you use row covers, remove them for longer periods.

Watch for signs of stress. If seedlings wilt, give them a little water and move them back to shade for a day. If leaves turn white or brown (sunscald), reduce sun exposure. Hardening off is not about punishing plants; it's about gradual adaptation. Different crops need different times: tomatoes and peppers take 7 to 10 days; cabbage and broccoli take 10 to 14 days; lettuce and onions take about 7 days.

In Africa, where conditions can be harsh, hardening off is especially important. For example, in the hot lowlands, seedlings must be gradually exposed to intense sun. In the highlands, they must adapt to cool nights. If you skip hardening off, you may lose many seedlings. So plan ahead: mark your calendar, and follow a schedule. After transplanting, continue to protect seedlings for a few days with shade or row covers if possible. With proper hardening, your seedlings will establish quickly and grow into strong, productive plants.

<h3>Light Requirements at a Glance</h3>

<p>Most tropical vegetable and tree seedlings germinate in darkness or low light but require strong, indirect light immediately after emergence. Direct, unfiltered midday sun in Africa often exceeding 100,000 lux will scorch young leaves, while deep shade below 3,000 lux produces weak, elongated (etiolated) stems that fail at transplant. The target band for most vegetable seedlings is 15,00030,000 lux, or roughly 3050% of full sun under a shade net.</p>

<h3>Temperature Windows</h3>

<p>Seedling growth is governed by the temperature of the growing medium as much as air temperature. In most African lowland nurseries, midday air temperatures inside a closed structure can reach 4045C, which causes heat stress, flower abortion in advanced seedlings, and damping-off. In highland nurseries (e.g., Kenya's Rift Valley, Ethiopia's highlands), night temperatures below 10C slow germination and can cause chilling injury in tomato and pepper.</p>

<h3>Managing the Daily Cycle</h3>

<p>In most of sub-Saharan Africa, the practical nursery day runs from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. Light peaks between 11 a.m. and 3 p.m., which is exactly when heat stress is highest. A simple operating rhythm works well:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Early morning (68 a.m.):</strong> Open vents, remove overnight row covers, check minimum night temperature.</li>
<li><strong>Mid-morning (811 a.m.):</strong> Irrigate; ensure shade nets are in place before the sun intensifies.</li>
<li><strong>Midday (11 a.m.3 p.m.):</strong> Monitor temperature; add temporary shade or mist if medium exceeds 30C.</li>
<li><strong>Late afternoon (35 p.m.):</strong> Reduce shade to allow late light; check for pests.</li>
<li><strong>Evening (56 p.m.):</strong> Close vents to trap warmth if nights are cool; replace row covers in highland areas.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Hardening Off: The Commercial Payoff</h3>

<p>Hardening off is not optional if you sell seedlings. Buyers reject soft, leggy transplants that wilt within hours of delivery. Over 710 days, gradually reduce shade, reduce watering frequency (not volume per event), and expose seedlings to full sun for increasing periods. A hardened tomato seedling can survive transplant shock with minimal loss, while an unhardened one may lose 3050% of its value in the field. For a nursery producing 10,000 seedlings per cycle, that difference can be worth several hundred dollars per cycle.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li>Measure light at seedling canopy height with a lux meter or smartphone app at 12 p.m. target 15,00030,000 lux for most vegetables.</li>
<li>Install shade nets before the hot season; use 3050% shade depending on crop and region.</li>
<li>Record minimum and maximum temperatures daily in a nursery logbook; act on readings above 32C or below 12C.</li>
<li>Open vents by 8 a.m. and close them in late afternoon when nights are cool.</li>
<li>Use row covers at night in highland areas; remove them by mid-morning.</li>
<li>Irrigate early morning or late afternoon never at midday in full sun.</li>
<li>Begin hardening off 710 days before sale or transplant; reduce shade and watering gradually.</li>
<li>Keep a simple weather log; use it to plan sowing dates and shade adjustments for the next cycle.</li>
</ol>
