1. Newborn Piglet Processing and Care

When a sow farrows, each piglet needs immediate attention. First, clear mucus from the mouth and nose with a clean cloth. Rub the piglet dry with a soft towel or straw to stimulate breathing and circulation. If a piglet isn't breathing, hold it upside down and gently swing it, or rub its chest. Next, tie the umbilical cord with clean thread about 2 cm from the belly, cut it with sterilized scissors, and dip the stump in iodine. This prevents infection. Keep the farrowing pen at 30-32C for the first week using a heat lamp or a wooden box with a lid and a light bulb. Piglets are born with no fat, so they chill easily. Provide a creep area with dry bedding like wood shavings or rice husks. Within 24 hours, clip needle teeth using clean side-cutters to prevent facial injuries to the sow and other piglets. Also, clip the tips of the eight needle teeth, not the whole tooth. If you plan to castrate males, do it at 3-7 days old with a sharp scalpel and iodine. Ear notch or tag for identification. Weigh each piglet at birth and record. A healthy piglet weighs 1.2-1.8 kg. Any piglet under 1 kg needs extra careput it on the sow's best teat or supplement with a bottle. Always handle piglets gently and quickly to avoid stress. Wash your hands before and after handling. Keep the pen clean and dry. These steps reduce mortality from 30% to under 10%.

2. Colostrum Management and Early Nutrition

Colostrum is the first milk a sow produces, rich in antibodies and energy. Piglets are born with no immunity; they must absorb antibodies from colostrum within 6 hours of birth. After 24 hours, their gut closes and antibodies cannot pass. So ensure every piglet suckles within 30 minutes of birth. If a sow has more piglets than teats (common with 12-14 piglets), split-suckle: put half the litter on the sow for 1 hour, then the other half. Or rotate every 2 hours. Weak piglets should be helped onto a teat. The best teats are the front onesthey give more milk. Attach small piglets to front teats and larger ones to back teats. If a sow dies or rejects a piglet, use a foster sow. Rub the piglet with the foster sow's afterbirth or bedding to mask scent. If no foster sow, feed colostrum from another sow or a commercial colostrum replacer using a bottle or syringe (without needle). Give 20-30 ml per piglet every 2 hours for the first 6 hours. After 24 hours, piglets need regular milk. Sow's milk is best. If the sow is not producing enough, supplement with a milk replacer (e.g., 1 part milk replacer to 4 parts warm water) fed at 28-30C. From day 3, offer a creep feeda highly digestible pellet with 20-22% protein, 1-2% fibre, and added vitamins. Put a small amount in a shallow tray in the creep area. Change it daily. Piglets will nibble and learn to eat. By 2 weeks, they should be eating 50-100 g per day. Always provide fresh, clean water. Dehydration kills piglets fast. Use shallow bowls so they can drink easily.

3. Weaning Strategies and Transition Diets

Weaning is the most stressful time for piglets. In Africa, many farmers wean at 4-6 weeks, but early weaning (3-4 weeks) requires excellent hygiene and feed. The goal is to move piglets from milk to solid feed without growth check. Prepare piglets by offering creep feed from day 7. By 3 weeks, they should eat 150-200 g per day. Wean gradually: remove the sow for 2-3 days, then return her for 1 day, then remove permanently. Or use fence-line weaning: keep piglets in the same pen but separate them from the sow with a fence so they can see and smell her. This reduces stress. After weaning, keep piglets in the same pen for 3-5 days to avoid moving stress. Then move to a clean, warm nursery pen. Feed a transition diet for 7-10 days: the same creep feed they were eating, then gradually mix in a nursery diet (18-20% protein). Change feed over 5 days: day 1: 75% creep, 25% nursery; day 3: 50-50; day 5: 25% creep, 75% nursery; day 7: 100% nursery. Feed small amounts 4-6 times a day. Piglets have small stomachs; they cannot eat much at once. Overfeeding causes diarrhoea. Use feeders that allow easy access. Provide clean water always. Add electrolytes to water for the first 3 days after weaning. Keep the nursery at 28-30C for the first week, then reduce by 1C per week until 22C. Good ventilation but no draughts. Weigh piglets weekly and record. If a piglet loses weight, isolate and feed separately. Weaning weight should be 6-8 kg at 4 weeks. With good management, you can wean 10-12 piglets per sow.

4. Nursery Environment and Comfort

The nursery must be warm, dry, clean, and well-ventilated. Piglets cannot regulate body temperature well. For the first week after weaning, keep the pen at 28-30C. Use a heat lamp (250-watt infrared bulb) hanging 45-60 cm above the floor. Or use a wooden box with a lid and a 100-watt bulb. Provide dry bedding: wood shavings, rice husks, or chopped straw. Never use wet beddingit grows bacteria. Clean and replace bedding daily. The floor should be non-slip. Concrete is good but must be rough. Smooth floors cause leg injuries. If using slatted floors, gaps should be 10-12 mm wide so piglets don't get stuck. Space: each piglet needs 0.3-0.4 square metres. For 10 piglets, a pen of 3-4 square metres is enough. Ventilation: ammonia from urine burns lungs. You should not smell ammonia when you enter. Open windows or use a ridge vent. Avoid draughtspiglets catch cold easily. Use a curtain or wall to block wind. Lighting: 12-16 hours of light helps piglets find feed and water. Use natural light or a bulb. Keep the pen quiet. Loud noises stress piglets. Limit visitors. Always have a hospital pen for sick or small piglets. That pen should be warmer (30-32C) and have soft bedding. Feeders: use a shallow trough or a creep feeder with 5-6 cm of feeding space per piglet. Water: use nipple drinkers or shallow bowls. Check water flow daily. Clean and disinfect the nursery between batches. Use 2% caustic soda or a commercial disinfectant. Let it dry for 2 days before new piglets arrive. This breaks disease cycles.

5. Managing Piglet Health and Mortality

The first 3 weeks are when most piglets die. Common causes: chilling, starvation, diarrhoea, and crushing by the sow. To prevent crushing, install farrowing railsmetal or wooden bars 20-25 cm from the wall and 20 cm above the floor. This gives piglets a safe zone when the sow lies down. Also, keep the sow calm. Feed her well before farrowing. For diarrhoea (scours), act fast. A piglet with watery yellow or white stool can die in 12 hours. Isolate it. Give oral rehydration solution: 1 litre clean water, 1 teaspoon salt, 6 teaspoons sugar. Feed 20-30 ml every 2 hours. For bacterial scours, use antibiotics like amoxicillin (follow vet advice). For coccidiosis, use toltrazuril. Keep the pen dry and clean. Disinfect with 1% bleach. For pneumonia, look for coughing and rapid breathing. Improve ventilation and reduce dust. Use antibiotics if needed. For anaemia (pale skin), inject iron dextran at 3 days old (200 mg per piglet). Also, give vitamin E and selenium injection at 3 days if your soil is deficient. Deworm at 2 weeks with fenbendazole or ivermectin. Vaccinate according to local diseases: usually at 3 weeks for mycoplasma, at 4 weeks for circovirus. Keep records of all treatments. Mortality target: under 10% from birth to weaning. If you lose more, check your management. Weigh piglets weekly. A piglet that doesn't gain weight is sick. Isolate and treat early. Always wash hands and boots before entering the nursery. Use footbaths with disinfectant. Quarantine new pigs for 2 weeks. These steps save lives.

6. Record Keeping for Piglet Performance

Good records help you spot problems and improve. You need a notebook or a simple record sheet. For each litter, record: sow ID, farrowing date, number born alive, number stillborn, number mummified, birth weights of each piglet (or average), and any deformities. Then record daily: deaths (with date and cause), treatments given, and feed intake. Weekly: weigh a sample of piglets (at least 5) to track growth. At weaning, record weaning date, number weaned, average weaning weight, and any health issues. Also record feed used per pen. Use a simple code: e.g., 'D' for diarrhoea, 'R' for respiratory, 'C' for crushing. At the end of each batch, calculate: pre-weaning mortality rate = (number born alive - number weaned) / number born alive x 100. Target under 10%. Average daily gain = (weaning weight - birth weight) / days. For a 4-week weaning, target 200-250 g per day. Feed conversion ratio = total feed eaten / total weight gain. Target 1.5-2.0 kg feed per kg gain. Keep records for at least 2 years. Compare batches. If mortality rises, check environment, nutrition, and disease. If growth is slow, check feed quality and health. Use records to decide which sows to keep. A sow that consistently weans few or small piglets should be culled. Share records with your vet or extension officer. They can help you interpret. Even a simple exercise book works. Write with a pen, not pencil. Keep it in a dry place. At the end of the year, you will see patterns and make better decisions.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Piglet Care & Nursery Management for African Pig Enterprises

Piglet survival from birth to 10 weeks is the single strongest lever on profitability in an African pig enterprise. On a typical smallholder or semi-commercial unit in Uganda, Kenya, Nigeria, or South Africa, pre-weaning mortality of 1530% is common, compared with 812% on well-run commercial farms. Every piglet lost before weaning represents lost feed conversion, lost labour invested, and lost revenue of roughly US$60120 at market weight, depending on local prices. This module gives you the operational numbers, benchmarks, and routines to move your farm from survival-driven to margin-driven piglet management.

1. Newborn Piglet Processing and Care

Processing must happen within the first 24 hours. Piglets are born with no brown fat reserves worth mentioning, a wet coat, and immature thermoregulation. In tropical highland areas such as Kiambu (Kenya) or Mbale (Uganda), night temperatures can drop to 1215C lethal for a newborn piglet whose thermal comfort zone is 3034C in week one.

Standard processing sequence (first 24 hours):

  • Clear mucus from mouth and nostrils; dry the piglet with clean, dry jute or paper towels.
  • Dip the navel cord in iodine (27%) or chlorhexidine; cut 34 cm from the body if needed. Never cut flush with the abdomen.
  • Weigh and record birth weight immediately this is your earliest performance data point.
  • Clip needle teeth (8 teeth) with disinfected side-cutters, avoiding the gum line.
  • Iron dextran injection (100200 mg IM) at day 13 to prevent anaemia.
  • Tail docking and, where practised, castration at day 37 with strict hygiene.
  • Ear notching or tagging for identification, aligned to your record system.

2. Colostrum Management and Early Nutrition

Colostrum is the piglet's only source of passive immunity. The gut can absorb intact immunoglobulins for only the first 6 hours after birth, with closure occurring by 24 hours. A piglet needs at least 200 g of colostrum in the first 24 hours roughly 50 mL/kg birth weight. In large litters (12+), the last-born piglets frequently miss out. Cross-fostering within 2448 hours, split-suckling, or bottle-feeding surplus colostrum from a sow that farrowed within the last 6 hours is standard commercial practice and should be adopted on any African farm with a litter size above 10.

From day 7, offer a highly palatable creep feed (1820% crude protein, 1.41.5% lysine) containing cooked cereals, soybean meal, and a milk by-product. In many African markets, commercial creep feed is expensive; a well-formulated on-farm mix using locally milled maize, soybean, and fishmeal can reduce cost by 2035% while maintaining performance. Introduce feed in small handfuls 46 times daily to stimulate intake.

3. Weaning Strategies and Transition Diets

Weaning age in African smallholder systems typically ranges from 21 to 42 days. Earlier weaning (2128 days) improves sow productivity but demands higher-quality starter diets and better nursery environments. Later weaning (3542 days) suits low-input systems where creep feed quality is limited.

Key transition rules:

  • Wean by weight, not just age target 6.58.0 kg minimum.
  • Move piglets, not the sow, to reduce stress where infrastructure allows.
  • Keep the first 4 days post-weaning feed identical to pre-weaning creep feed.
  • Feed little and often (45 times daily) for the first 10 days.
  • Ensure ad-libitum clean water a weaned piglet drinks 1.52.5 L/day.

SAFETY WARNING Iron Dextran and Injections

Iron dextran must be injected intramuscularly into the neck, using a new sterile needle for every 1015 piglets. Reusing needles causes abscesses and spreads Streptococcus suis and Mycoplasma in African herds. Never inject iron into the ham carcass condemnation at slaughter will cost you the full value of the pig. Store iron dextran away from sunlight; a brown, cloudy solution is expired and must be discarded. Dispose of needles in a sharps container not in the manure pit.

4. Nursery Environment and Comfort

The nursery must deliver three things: warmth, dryness, and low draught. In tropical Africa, the challenge is often not cold but humidity and heat stress in tin-roofed pens. Ideal nursery conditions: 2628C in week 1 post-weaning, dropping 12C per week to 22C by week 6. Relative humidity 5070%. Floor space 0.150.25 m per piglet at weaning, rising to 0.35 m by week 8. Provide a solid, dry lying area and a separate dunging area piglets naturally defecate away from where they sleep if the pen allows it.

5. Managing Piglet Health and Mortality

The main killers in African nurseries are: colibacillosis (E. coli scours), coccidiosis, Streptococcus suis, greasy pig disease, and iron deficiency anaemia. Vaccination of the sow pre-farrowing (E. coli, rotavirus, where available) is the cheapest defence. Piglet-level interventions: iron at day 13, coccidiostat (toltrazuril) at day 35, and strict all-in/all-out between batches. Record every death with age, weight, and probable cause patterns emerge within 3 batches.

6. Record Keeping for Piglet Performance

You cannot manage what you do not measure. At minimum, keep a farrowing card per litter and a nursery batch sheet. Record: sow ID, farrowing date, born alive, born dead, birth weights, cross-fostering moves, weaning date, weaning weight, mortality by cause. A simple notebook or a free app such as PigCHAMP Mobile or a Google Sheet template works. Calculate three KPIs monthly: pre-weaning mortality %, average weaning weight, and feed cost per kg weaned piglet. These three numbers drive your margin.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Dry and weigh every piglet within 30 minutes of birth; record on farrowing card.
  • Dip navel in iodine; clip needle teeth with disinfected cutters.
  • Iron dextran injection day 13, new needle per 1015 piglets.
  • Ensure 200+ g colostrum per piglet in first 24 h; cross-foster if litter >10.
  • Offer creep feed from day 7; keep water clean and ad-libitum.
  • Wean at 6.58.0 kg; keep feed identical for first 4 days post-weaning.
  • Maintain nursery at 2628C in week 1, dropping 12C weekly.
  • Record mortality daily with cause; review KPIs every 30 days.
