1. Designing Ostrich Pens and Shelters

Ostriches need space to run, but also shelter from sun, wind, and rain. A good pen design prevents injury and stress. For a breeding pair, provide at least 250 square meters (about 2,700 sq ft) of outdoor run. For growing chicks, allow 5 square meters per bird in the first month, increasing to 20 square meters by six months. Pens should be rectangular, not square, so birds can run in straight lines without hitting fences. Use a 2-meter high fence with small mesh at the bottom to stop chicks from slipping through. The shelter should be at least 3 meters by 4 meters per adult bird, with a solid roof and open sides for airflow. In hot African climates, orient the shelter east-west so the roof shades the midday sun. Flooring inside the shelter can be packed earth or concrete; concrete is easier to clean but needs bedding like wood shavings or sand for comfort. Provide a dust bath area: a shallow pit with dry sand or ash helps ostriches clean their feathers and control parasites. Always include a catch pen or small holding area attached to the main pen for easy handling. Avoid using wire mesh with sharp edges; ostriches have long legs and can easily cut themselves. Use smooth, rounded posts and rails. In areas with predators like jackals or lions, reinforce the fence with strong posts and buried wire. Remember, ostriches are curious and will peck at shiny objects; keep feeders and waterers simple and sturdy. A well-designed pen reduces stress, injuries, and escapes, leading to healthier birds and better production.

2. Fencing Requirements and Materials

Fencing is the most important part of ostrich housing because ostriches are strong and can run fast. A fence must be at least 1.8 to 2 meters high. Use woven wire mesh with small openings (5 cm by 10 cm) at the bottom to prevent chicks from escaping and predators from digging in. The mesh should be at least 1.5 meters high, with two or three strands of smooth wire above it to reach 2 meters. Wooden posts (10 cm diameter) or steel posts should be placed every 3 meters. Corner posts need extra bracing. In Africa, treated eucalyptus or pine posts last longer against termites. Do not use barbed wire; ostriches can tear their skin on it. For internal partitions, use the same mesh but lighter gauge. Gates should be 1.5 meters wide and swing inward or slide, made of metal or strong wood. In predator-prone areas, bury the bottom of the fence 30 cm deep and add an apron of wire on the ground. Electric fencing can be used as an additional deterrent, but keep it at least 30 cm away from the mesh so birds don't get shocked. Check fences weekly for holes, loose wires, or rust. Repair immediately. A good fence keeps birds in, predators out, and reduces stress. Remember, ostriches can jump up to 1.5 meters, so never leave gaps at the top. Also, ensure gates are self-closing or have strong latches that ostriches cannot nudge open. Proper fencing is an investment that pays off in fewer losses and healthier birds.

3. Handling Facilities: Chutes, Crates, and Loading Ramps

Handling ostriches can be dangerous because they kick and can run up to 70 km/h. A well-designed handling facility makes the job safer and easier. Start with a race or chute: a narrow alley 60 cm wide and 2 meters high, made of solid panels so birds cannot see out. The chute should lead to a crush or head gate where you can examine or treat the bird. Use non-slip flooring like rubber mats or grooved concrete. For loading, build a ramp with a gentle slope (no more than 20 degrees) and solid sides. The ramp should be 1 meter wide and have cleats every 30 cm for grip. Crates for transport must be sturdy, with a floor that won't break, and ventilation holes. A crate for an adult ostrich should be at least 2.5 meters long, 1.2 meters wide, and 1.8 meters high. For chicks, smaller crates with soft bedding. Always move birds in the cool of the day to avoid heat stress. Use a flag or stick to guide them, never hit them. Two people should handle an adult ostrich: one at the head with a hood or blindfold, one at the body. A hood calms the bird. For loading, lead the bird up the ramp with a hood on, then secure the crate door. Practice moving birds calmly and regularly so they get used to the routine. Well-designed facilities reduce injuries to both birds and handlers.

4. Climate Control and Ventilation

Ostriches can tolerate a wide range of temperatures, but extremes cause stress and lower production. In hot African climates, the main challenge is heat. Provide shade: trees, shade cloth, or roofed shelters. Shelters should have open sides to allow breeze. A good rule: the shelter should have at least 1 square meter of open vent per adult bird. In very hot areas (above 35C), use sprinklers or misters on the roof to cool the air. Ensure plenty of cool drinking water; ostriches drink 10-15 liters per day in heat. In cold climates, ostriches need dry bedding and windbreaks. They can survive near freezing if dry and out of wind. Never house ostriches in fully enclosed buildings without ventilation; ammonia from droppings builds up and causes respiratory disease. Use ridge vents or open eaves. For chicks, provide a brooder area with a heat lamp (infrared) for the first 4-6 weeks, starting at 32C and reducing by 3C each week. Use a thermometer at bird level. Ventilation removes moisture and ammonia. A simple test: if you smell ammonia, ventilation is inadequate. In humid areas, use fans to move air. Always monitor birds for panting or huddling; they will tell you if they are too hot or cold. Adjust shelters seasonally: open them in summer, add windbreaks in winter. Good climate control keeps birds eating, growing, and laying.

5. Biosecurity Measures in Housing

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out of your ostrich farm. Start with a perimeter fence and a gate that stays locked. Only allow essential visitors; they must wear clean boots and coveralls. Keep a footbath with disinfectant (e.g., 2% sodium hypochlorite) at the entrance to each pen. Change it daily. Never mix birds of different ages; chicks are vulnerable to diseases carried by adults. Use an all-in, all-out system if possible: clean and disinfect a pen before new birds arrive. Quarantine new birds for 30 days in a separate area, away from the main flock. Watch for signs of illness: diarrhea, lethargy, dropped wings, or sudden death. Isolate sick birds immediately. Control rodents and wild birds; they carry diseases. Store feed in sealed containers, not on the ground. Keep water clean; scrub waterers daily. Dead birds should be buried deep (1.5 meters) or incinerated. Disinfect equipment like shovels and wheelbarrows between pens. In Africa, diseases like avian influenza and Newcastle disease are threats. Vaccinate if recommended by your vet. Keep records of all treatments and movements. Train workers to follow biosecurity rules. A simple routine: boots, disinfectant, and quarantine can save your whole flock. Remember, prevention is cheaper than treatment.

6. Maintenance and Cleaning Schedules

Regular maintenance and cleaning keep ostriches healthy and facilities lasting. Daily tasks: remove leftover feed and dirty water, scrub waterers with a brush and disinfectant, and check fences for damage. Pick up any sharp objects or trash. Weekly: replace bedding in shelters (sand or wood shavings) and dispose of soiled bedding in a compost pile away from pens. Clean feed troughs with a mild detergent and rinse well. Check gates and latches for smooth operation. Monthly: inspect the entire fence line for holes, loose posts, or rust. Repair immediately. Check roof sheets for leaks. Oil hinges and moving parts. Quarterly: deep clean shelters: remove all bedding, scrub floors and walls with disinfectant (e.g., 1% Virkon), let dry, and add fresh bedding. Check the chute and loading ramp for worn flooring or loose boards. Annually: repaint or treat wooden posts and rails. Replace any broken mesh. Service water pumps and lines. Keep a logbook of all maintenance and cleaning. In Africa's rainy season, check drainage around pens to prevent mud; add gravel if needed. In dry season, control dust by lightly sprinkling water on roads. Always use gloves when cleaning. A clean farm reduces flies and disease. Set a schedule and stick to it; don't wait for problems. A well-maintained facility means fewer escapes, injuries, and vet bills.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Housing and Facilities for Ostrich Farming in Africa

Ostrich farming is one of the fastest routes into high-value livestock agribusiness in Africa. Birds reach slaughter weight in 1014 months, and every part of the carcass leather, feathers, meat, and oil has a market. But the single largest cause of failure for new ostrich operations is not feed or genetics. It is housing. Poor pens, weak fencing, and badly designed handling facilities drive mortality, injury, and stress, and those translate directly into lost revenue.

This Practical Reference consolidates the seven lessons in this module into one working document: pen and shelter design, fencing, handling infrastructure, climate control, biosecurity, and cleaning schedules. Treat it as your site-planning checklist before you spend money on birds. The figures below are drawn from South African and broader African commercial practice, where the industry is most mature.

1. Designing Ostrich Pens and Shelters

Ostriches are large, fast, and easily panicked. An adult can run at 6070 km/h and will run into fences rather than stop. Housing design must therefore prioritise space, sightlines, and non-injurious materials over elaborate construction.

Commercial breeders in South Africa typically use paddocks of 0.51 hectare for a breeding trio (one male, two females), with a minimum of 250 m per adult bird in intensive systems. Chicks need far less space but far more shelter. A practical African layout separates the site into four zones: chick rearing, grow-out, breeding, and quarantine/hospital. Keep these physically separate, with a footbath and vehicle dip at the entrance to each zone.

Shelters should be open-sided, with the long axis running eastwest so birds get shade through the hottest part of the day and can move out of prevailing wind and rain. Roof overhang of at least 1.5 m prevents driving rain from wetting bedding. Flooring inside shelters should be dry sand or wood shavings, never concrete for chicks hard surfaces cause leg splay and foot damage.

2. Fencing Requirements and Materials

Fencing is the highest-risk line item in the whole facility. Ostriches do not see thin wire well and will hit it at speed. The industry standard in South Africa is 1.82.4 m high woven wire mesh with a mesh size no larger than 50 mm at the bottom, on timber or steel posts set at 34 m spacing. Barbed wire must never be used on the inside face of pens it causes deep lacerations and feather damage that devalues the skin.

For perimeter security against predators (jackal, caracal, wild dogs, and in some regions lions), add a 1 m apron of wire mesh buried at the base, angled outward. Electric fencing is useful on the outside of the perimeter but is not reliable as the primary barrier for ostriches because their feather coat insulates them.

3. Handling Facilities: Chutes, Crates, and Loading Ramps

Handling is where most injuries occur to birds and to staff. Ostrich legs are long and fragile; a bird that kicks or struggles in a poorly designed chute can break a leg and be culled. Build a curved raceway rather than a straight chute: ostriches follow the bird in front and a curve blocks the view of the exit, reducing balking.

Specifications that work in practice:

  • Raceway width: 0.80.9 m for adults, 0.5 m for growers. Wider and birds turn around; narrower and they panic.
  • Walls: solid to 1.2 m, then open above, so birds cannot see out but air flows.
  • Head gate: padded, with a quick-release catch. Never use a neck crush on ostriches.
  • Loading ramp: maximum 20 incline, with cleats every 300 mm. A 20 ramp is the practical limit before birds refuse.
  • Crates: for transport, use 2.4 m 1.2 m 2.0 m crates for adults, with a roof and ventilation gaps. Load no more than one adult per crate.

4. Climate Control and Ventilation

Ostriches tolerate heat far better than cold, wet conditions. In the Sahel and East African lowlands, the priority is shade and airflow. In the Highveld and Mediterranean-climate zones of South Africa and North Africa, winter wind and rain are the greater risk, especially for chicks under three months, which chill easily.

Design rules that hold across African climates:

  • Orient shelters eastwest; provide 23 m of shade per adult bird.
  • Keep ridge ventilation open. In closed rearing houses, aim for 0.1 m of inlet per 10 m of floor area.
  • Never house chicks on damp floors. Sand or deep litter, turned weekly.
  • In hot zones, add misting or sprinklers to cool sand, but keep bedding dry.
  • In cold zones, use removable windbreak panels on the prevailing wind side, not fully enclosed walls.

5. Biosecurity Measures in Housing

African ostrich operations face avian influenza, Newcastle disease, and fowl typhoid, plus parasites carried by wild birds. Biosecurity is not optional one outbreak can shut down your market access for months.

  • Single entry point per zone, with a footbath (fresh disinfectant daily) and a vehicle spray dip.
  • All-in / all-out per pen where possible; never mix age groups.
  • Quarantine every incoming bird for 30 days, 50 m from the main flock.
  • Control wild birds and rodents: net roofs, store feed in sealed bins.
  • Restrict visitors; log every entry; provide farm boots and overalls.
  • Dead birds removed within 12 hours and incinerated or buried 1.5 m deep, away from water.
SAFETY WARNING Handling and fencing hazards

Ostriches can deliver a kick powerful enough to break a human femur or rupture internal organs. Never enter a breeding camp on foot during the breeding season without a shield or barrier. Never use barbed wire inside pens it causes deep lacerations and skin damage that devalues hides. Loading ramps steeper than 20 cause falls and leg fractures; inspect all ramps and chute gates before every use. Ensure all electrical fencing is properly earthed and labelled, and that staff receive documented handling training before working with adult birds.

6. Maintenance and Cleaning Schedules

Consistency beats intensity. A simple weekly and monthly routine keeps mortality low and skin quality high.

  • Daily: remove wet bedding, check water, inspect fences for damage, count birds.
  • Weekly: turn litter, scrub troughs, check gate hinges and latches, top up footbaths.
  • Monthly: full pen inspection, replace broken posts/wire, service water lines, review mortality log.
  • Quarterly: deep clean and disinfect shelters, rest and reseed paddocks, inspect handling equipment.
  • Annually: structural review of all shelters, ramps, and crates; retrain staff on handling.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Site zoned into chick, grower, breeder, and quarantine areas with separate entry points.
  2. Shelters oriented eastwest, open-sided, with 1.5 m roof overhang and dry sand floor.
  3. Perimeter fence 2.4 m woven wire, no barbed wire inside, buried predator apron fitted.
  4. Curved raceway 0.80.9 m wide, solid walls to 1.2 m, padded quick-release head gate.
  5. Loading ramp 20, cleats every 300 mm, crates sized 2.4 1.2 2.0 m for adults.
  6. Shade at 23 m per adult; ventilation inlets sized to floor area in closed houses.
  7. Footbath and vehicle dip at every zone entry; 30-day quarantine for incoming birds.
  8. Written daily, weekly, monthly, and quarterly cleaning and maintenance schedule posted on site.
