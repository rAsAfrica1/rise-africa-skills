1. Design and Operation of Flow-Through Systems

A flow-through system is the simplest way to grow fingerlings in tanks or raceways. Water enters at one end, flows through the tank, and exits at the other, carrying waste away. You need a continuous supply of clean water, usually from a river, borehole, or reservoir. The flow rate must be enough to renew the water and keep oxygen high. For tilapia or catfish fingerlings, a common rule is to exchange 1020% of the tank volume per hour. For example, a 1,000-litre tank needs 100200 litres per hour. In raceways, which are long rectangular channels, water flows from the head to the tail. A typical raceway might be 10 metres long, 1 metre wide, and 0.5 metres deep, holding 5,000 litres. Water enters through a pipe or weir at the head and exits through a standpipe or screen at the tail. The slope should be 12% so water flows smoothly. You must screen the inlet to keep out wild fish and debris, and screen the outlet to prevent fingerlings from escaping. A simple gravel filter or drum filter can remove large particles. Flow-through systems are cheap to build using locally available materials like concrete, plastic liners, or even wooden frames with UV-resistant plastic. They work well where water is abundant and cheap. But they use a lot of water and can spread diseases if the source is contaminated. In Africa, many small-scale farmers use flow-through tanks made from old shipping containers or ferrocement. You must monitor dissolved oxygen daily; it should stay above 5 mg/L. If fish gasp at the surface, increase flow. Clean the tank bottom weekly to remove settled waste. This system is ideal for remote areas with reliable water but no electricity for pumps.

2. Recirculating Aquaculture Systems (RAS) for Fingerlings

A recirculating aquaculture system (RAS) reuses water by filtering and recycling it. Instead of letting water flow away, you pump it through filters and back to the fish tank. This saves water and allows you to control temperature, oxygen, and waste. A basic RAS for fingerlings has four parts: a fish tank, a mechanical filter (to remove solid waste), a biofilter (to convert toxic ammonia into less harmful nitrate), and a pump. You also need an aerator or oxygen cone to keep oxygen high. For a small-scale RAS in Africa, you can use a 1,000-litre tank for fish, a 200-litre drum as a mechanical filter filled with filter pads or sponge, and another 200-litre drum as a biofilter filled with bio-balls or plastic media. A small submersible pump (e.g., 2,000 litres per hour) moves water. The biofilter must be cycled for 46 weeks before adding fish, by adding a small amount of ammonia or fish waste to grow nitrifying bacteria. Once cycled, you can stock 100200 fingerlings per 1,000 litres, depending on species and size. RAS gives you full control: you can heat water in cold areas or cool it in hot areas. But it needs electricity, and a power cut can kill fish within hours. Always have a backup battery or generator. Test water daily for ammonia (should be 0 mg/L), nitrite (0 mg/L), and nitrate (below 50 mg/L). Do partial water changes of 10% weekly to remove nitrate. RAS is more expensive to set up but uses only 510% of the water of a flow-through system. It is ideal for urban farms or areas with limited water. Many African entrepreneurs use RAS in greenhouses to grow tilapia and catfish fingerlings year-round.

3. Stocking and Density Management in Tanks

How many fingerlings you put in a tank decides whether they grow fast or die. Overstocking causes oxygen drops, ammonia spikes, and disease. Understocking wastes space and money. The right density depends on fish species, size, water flow, and aeration. For Nile tilapia fingerlings of 15 grams, a common density in a flow-through tank is 510 fish per litre if you exchange water 1020% per hour. In a RAS with good filtration, you can stock 1020 fish per litre. For African catfish (Clarias), which breathe air, you can stock 2030 fish per litre in well-aerated tanks. But these are maximums; start lower. A safe starting point for a beginner with a 1,000-litre tank and moderate aeration is 2,0003,000 tilapia fingerlings (23 per litre). As fish grow, you must reduce density. Split the stock or move some to another tank. A good rule is to never exceed 30 kg of fish per 1,000 litres in a flow-through system, or 60 kg per 1,000 litres in a RAS. Weigh a sample of 20 fish every two weeks to track growth. If average weight doubles, you need to double water flow or split the tank. Always acclimatise fingerlings before stocking: float the transport bag in the tank for 1520 minutes, then slowly add tank water to the bag over 10 minutes. Stock in the early morning or evening when temperatures are cool. Count fish accurately by weighing a small sample and counting, then extrapolating. Never guess. Record stocking date, number, and average weight in a notebook. Poor density management is the number one cause of fingerling mortality in African tank systems.

4. Feeding and Nutrition in Intensive Systems

In tanks and raceways, fingerlings cannot find natural food, so you must feed them complete diets. A good fingerling feed has 3545% protein, 510% fat, and essential vitamins and minerals. For tilapia, use a 40% protein crumble (0.51.5 mm) for fish up to 5 grams, then 35% protein pellets (2 mm) for larger fingerlings. For catfish, use 4550% protein. You can buy commercial feed from companies like Aller Aqua, Skretting, or local millers. If you want to make feed, a simple recipe for 10 kg: 4 kg fishmeal, 3 kg soybean meal, 2 kg maize meal, 0.5 kg vitamin-mineral premix, 0.5 kg fish oil. Mix and pelletise. Feed 46 times daily for small fingerlings, 3 times daily for larger ones. Give only what they eat in 1015 minutes. Overfeeding wastes money and fouls water. A good daily ration is 510% of body weight for fish under 1 gram, 35% for 15 grams, and 23% for 520 grams. Adjust based on water temperature: at 2530C, feed full ration; below 20C, reduce by half. Always remove uneaten feed after 30 minutes using a siphon or net. Store feed in a cool, dry place, away from sunlight. Never use mouldy feedit contains aflatoxins that kill fish. In Africa, many farmers use locally available ingredients like cassava, rice bran, and brewery waste, but these are low in protein. Supplement with fishmeal or soybean. For best growth, use sinking pellets for catfish and floating pellets for tilapia so you can see if they are eating. Keep a feeding record: date, amount, and fish behaviour. Healthy fingerlings swim actively and rush to feed.

5. Waste Management and Biofiltration

Fish produce waste: solid faeces and dissolved ammonia from gills and urine. In tanks and raceways, waste builds up fast and can poison fish. You must remove solids and convert ammonia. Solid waste settles at the bottom or floats. Remove it daily by siphoning the tank bottom or using a swirl separator. A simple swirl separator is a cone-shaped tank where water enters tangentially, causing solids to settle at the bottom while clean water exits the top. For a small farm, you can use a 200-litre drum with a pipe entering at an angle. Drain the sludge daily. Dissolved ammonia is more dangerous. Even 0.5 mg/L can stress fingerlings; 2 mg/L can kill them. Ammonia is converted to nitrite and then nitrate by nitrifying bacteria in a biofilter. A biofilter is any surface with lots of area for bacteria to grow. Common media: bio-balls, plastic rings, lava rock, or even nylon scrubbers. The biofilter must be aerated and kept dark. For a 1,000-litre tank, you need at least 100 litres of biofilter media. Cycle the biofilter before adding fish: add a source of ammonia (e.g., 5 ml of household ammonia per 100 litres) and test daily until ammonia and nitrite read zero and nitrate rises. This takes 46 weeks. Once running, never clean the biofilter with chlorinated waterit kills bacteria. Rinse gently with tank water. Test ammonia and nitrite every 23 days. If ammonia rises above 0.5 mg/L, do a 30% water change and reduce feeding. Nitrate should be kept below 50 mg/L by weekly partial water changes (1020%). In Africa, many farmers use water hyacinth or lettuce in a gravel bed to absorb nitratethis is a simple aquaponic filter. Always have a backup biofilter or sponge filter in case of failure.

6. Monitoring and Maintaining Optimal Water Parameters

Fingerlings are fragile. Water quality must stay within tight ranges. You need to test daily or every two days. The key parameters: temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate. For tilapia, temperature should be 2630C. Catfish prefer 2528C. Use a simple glass thermometer (cost: $2). Dissolved oxygen must be above 5 mg/L for tilapia, above 4 mg/L for catfish. Use a digital DO meter ($100200) or a chemical test kit ($20). If DO is low, increase aeration or water flow. pH should be 6.58.5. Test with a pH pen ($30) or liquid kit. Ammonia (NH3) must be 0 mg/L. Even 0.1 mg/L is dangerous. Use a test kit ($15 for 50 tests). Nitrite must be 0 mg/L. Nitrate should be below 50 mg/L. Test ammonia and nitrite every 2 days for the first month, then weekly. Keep a logbook: date, time, temperature, DO, pH, ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and any fish behaviour. If ammonia or nitrite rises, do a 3050% water change immediately and stop feeding for 24 hours. Check your biofilterit may be clogged or uncycled. For pH crashes (below 6.0), add agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) at 1020 g per 100 litres. For high pH (above 9.0), add vinegar at 1 ml per 10 litres, but slowly. Always aerate water before adding to the tank to remove chlorine if using tap water. In Africa, many boreholes have high iron or low pH. Test your source water before use. Clean tank walls weekly to remove algae that can harbour pathogens. Remove dead fish immediatelythey spike ammonia. Use a simple dip net. Record everything. Good records help you spot problems before fish die. A $50 test kit and a notebook are your best insurance.

<h2>Practical Reference: Fingerling Grow-Out in Tanks and Raceways</h2>

<p>Fingerling grow-out is the bridge between the hatchery and the food-fish pond or cage. In Africa, where tilapia and catfish dominate production, the difference between a profitable cycle and a loss-making one is often decided in the first 48 weeks after fry are transferred to tanks or raceways. Well-designed flow-through and recirculating systems let you compress grow-out time, control mortality, and hit the market with uniform, healthy fingerlings when demand peaks typically at the start of the rains or before the festive season. This reference pulls together design, stocking, feeding, biofiltration, and monitoring into a single operational guide for African conditions.</p>

<p>Treat this module as an operating manual for your farm. Every recommendation here is framed around cost per fingerling, survival rate, and cash-flow timing the three numbers that decide whether a tank system pays for itself.</p>

<h3>1. System Design at a Glance</h3>

<p>Two system families dominate: <strong>flow-through</strong> (single-pass, water used once) and <strong>recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS)</strong> (water treated and reused). Flow-through is cheaper to build and simpler to run, but it needs a reliable, gravity-fed or pumped water source and it discharges nutrient-rich effluent. RAS costs more upfront and consumes electricity, but it uses 9099% less water and can be sited near urban markets where land and water are expensive.</p>

<h3>2. Stocking and Density Management</h3>

<p>Overstocking is the single most common cause of stunted fingerlings and disease outbreaks on African farms. Stocking density is governed by biomass, not fish number. As fish grow, either split tanks or increase water exchange. A safe planning rule for tilapia fingerlings: <strong>1.53.0 kg of biomass per 100 L</strong> in a well-aerated RAS, and <strong>0.81.5 kg per 100 L</strong> in a simple flow-through tank. For <em>Clarias gariepinus</em> (African catfish), densities can be higher because the species tolerates low oxygen but only if you can remove solids quickly.</p>

<h3>3. Feeding and Nutrition in Intensive Systems</h3>

<p>Feed is 5070% of fingerling production cost. In intensive tanks, natural food is negligible, so the feed must be complete. Use a crumble or 1.52.0 mm sinking pellet with <strong>3540% crude protein</strong> for tilapia and <strong>4245%</strong> for catfish. Feed 46 times daily at 58% body weight for fry, dropping to 34% for 2050 g fingerlings. Convert to a feeding chart based on sampled average weight, not guesswork.</p>

<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING Electrical and Water Hazards in RAS</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Recirculating systems combine pumps, aerators, and heaters with wet floors. In many African farms, power supply is unstable and earthing is poor. <strong>Never</strong> run a pump or aerator from an unearthed socket, and never stand in standing water while handling electrical equipment. Install a residual current device (RCD/earth-leakage breaker) on every circuit serving the tank room. Keep a battery-backed air pump as backup a 2-hour oxygen failure in a stocked RAS can kill an entire tank. Store and handle formalin, potassium permanganate, and chlorine-based disinfectants away from feed, with gloves and eye protection, and never mix chlorine with acid.</p>
</div>

<h3>4. Waste Management and Biofiltration</h3>

<p>In RAS, fish excrete ammonia (toxic), which nitrifying bacteria convert first to nitrite (also toxic) and then to nitrate (much less toxic). The biofilter is the heart of the system. Common media include moving-bed K1 media, trickling filters, and static submerged beds. Size the biofilter for the <em>peak</em> feed load, not the average. As a rule of thumb, allow <strong>0.51.0 m of biofilter surface area per kg of feed fed per day</strong> for moving-bed media. Flush solids daily; a swirl separator or settling cone plus a drum or screen filter removes 6080% of suspended solids before they reach the biofilter.</p>

<p>Flow-through systems avoid biofilter complexity but produce effluent. Capture it in a settling pond and reuse it to irrigate vegetables, maize, or fodder grass. This turns a waste-disposal cost into a second income stream a practice increasingly promoted in integrated aquaculture-agriculture (IAA) schemes across East and West Africa.</p>

<h3>5. Monitoring and Maintaining Optimal Water Parameters</h3>

<p>Fingerlings are far less tolerant than adult fish. Test at least twice daily in RAS and once daily in flow-through. Keep a written log; trends matter more than single readings.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Dissolved oxygen:</strong> &gt;5 mg/L (tilapia), &gt;4 mg/L (catfish). Below 3 mg/L, stop feeding and increase aeration.</li>
<li><strong>Temperature:</strong> 2630 C for tilapia; 2632 C for catfish. Insulate tanks or use shade nets in hot lowland areas.</li>
<li><strong>pH:</strong> 6.58.5. Add agricultural lime or sodium bicarbonate if pH drops below 6.5.</li>
<li><strong>Ammonia (NH-N):</strong> &lt;0.05 mg/L un-ionised. <strong>Nitrite (NO-N):</strong> &lt;0.5 mg/L. <strong>Nitrate:</strong> &lt;150 mg/L.</li>
<li><strong>Alkalinity:</strong> 50150 mg/L as CaCO buffers against pH crashes.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Business Notes for African Operators</h3>

<p>Fingerling production is a <em>high-margin, high-risk</em> enterprise. A well-run RAS producing 50,000 mixed-sex tilapia fingerlings per month at USD 0.080.15 each can gross USD 4,0007,500 monthly, with feed and electricity the largest costs. Key commercial levers:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Timing:</strong> Align harvest with the start of the rains, when pond stocking demand spikes.</li>
<li><strong>Uniformity:</strong> Graded, uniform fingerlings command a 2030% price premium over mixed sizes.</li>
<li><strong>Certification:</strong> Buyers increasingly ask for disease-free, certified stock. Work with national fisheries authorities for health certification.</li>
<li><strong>Value chain:</strong> Sell directly to pond farmers, not through middlemen, to capture margin and build repeat orders.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Confirm water source flow rate and quality <em>before</em> stocking test pH, ammonia, and temperature.</li>
<li>Calculate maximum biomass per tank; split or harvest before density exceeds 3 kg/100 L (RAS) or 1.5 kg/100 L (flow-through).</li>
<li>Grade fish every 1014 days to reduce size variation and cannibalism.</li>
<li>Feed by chart, not by eye; weigh a sample of 30 fish weekly to adjust ration.</li>
<li>Flush solids daily; backwash mechanical filters and check biofilter flow.</li>
<li>Record DO, temperature, pH, and ammonia twice daily in a logbook.</li>
<li>Maintain a battery or generator backup for aerators and pumps.</li>
<li>Reuse nutrient-rich effluent for irrigation or fodder crops to offset costs.</li>
</ol>
