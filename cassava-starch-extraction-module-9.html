1. Grinding Dried Starch Cakes

After drying, cassava starch forms hard cakes or lumps. You need to grind them into powder. The most common tool in African villages is the hammer mill, powered by a diesel engine. A good hammer mill for starch has a screen with holes around 1-2 mm. You feed the dried cakes into the hopper. Do not feed too fast or the mill will clog. Feed slowly and steadily. The machine will break the cakes into fine powder. If you don't have a hammer mill, you can use a motorized grater or even a manual mortar and pestle for very small batches, but that is labour-intensive. For commercial production, a hammer mill is essential. In Nigeria, a hammer mill with a 10-15 horsepower engine can process about 500 kg per hour. In Ghana, many processors use a 'cassava grater' that has been modified with a different screen. The key is to adjust the mill so the powder is fine enough. After grinding, let the powder cool before sieving. The heat from grinding can cause moisture to condense. If the powder is too hot, it will clog the sieve. Also, check the mill's blades regularly. Dull blades produce uneven particles and overheat the starch. Sharpen or replace blades every 100 hours of operation. Always wear a dust mask and goggles when grinding. Starch dust can irritate your lungs and eyes. After grinding, you should have a fine powder that feels like flour. If you squeeze a handful, it should not stick together. If it does, the starch is still too moist and needs more drying before grinding. Store the ground starch in clean, dry sacks. Never use sacks that previously held fertiliser or chemicals. Proper grinding is the first step to getting high-quality starch powder that will fetch a better price at the market.

2. Particle Size Reduction Equipment

To turn dried starch cakes into powder, you need the right equipment. The most common machine in Africa is the hammer mill. It has a rotor with swinging hammers that beat the starch against a screen. The screen size determines the final particle size. A 1 mm screen gives fine powder, while a 3 mm screen gives coarser powder. Hammer mills are powered by diesel or electric motors. A typical small-scale mill in Kenya costs around 80,000 to 150,000 Kenyan shillings. Another option is the disc mill, also called a plate mill. It has two discs that grind the starch between them. Disc mills are good for making very fine powder, but they can overheat the starch if not adjusted properly. In Tanzania, some processors use a roller mill, which crushes the starch between rollers. Roller mills are more expensive but produce uniform particles. For a beginner, a hammer mill is the best choice because it is rugged and easy to repair. You can also use a pin mill, which has rotating pins that break the starch. Pin mills are good for small batches. If you are just starting, you might rent a mill service from a local miller. Many African towns have a mill that will grind your dried cassava for a fee. For example, in Uganda, a miller might charge 500 UGX per kilogram. But if you want to control quality, you need your own mill. When choosing equipment, consider the power source. Electric motors are cheaper to run but need reliable electricity. Diesel engines are more expensive but work anywhere. Also, look at the capacity. A mill that processes 100 kg per hour is good for a small business. You also need a cyclone or dust collector to capture the fine dust. Without it, you lose product and pollute the air. Finally, always buy from a reputable dealer who can provide spare parts. In many African countries, local welders can fabricate a simple hammer mill if you cannot afford an imported one.

3. Sieving and Classification

After grinding, the starch powder contains particles of different sizes. Sieving separates them into uniform grades. You need a set of sieves with different mesh sizes. The most common sieve in Africa is a woven wire mesh sieve, often mounted on a wooden frame. You can buy sieves from agricultural supply stores or have them made by a local artisan. For manual sieving, you shake the sieve back and forth. For larger operations, you use a mechanical sifter, which can be a vibrating screen or a rotary sifter. A vibrating screen is powered by a small motor and shakes the sieve rapidly. A rotary sifter has a rotating drum with mesh. In Nigeria, many processors use a 'gyratory sifter' that moves in a circular motion. The process is simple: pour the ground starch onto the top sieve. The fine particles fall through, while coarse particles stay on top. You can stack sieves with different mesh sizes. For example, a 60-mesh sieve on top, then 80-mesh, then 100-mesh. The powder that passes through all sieves is the finest grade. The powder that stays on the 60-mesh is the coarsest. You can then sell different grades at different prices. Fine starch is used in pharmaceuticals and cosmetics, while coarser starch is used in food. After sieving, you must clean the sieves regularly. Starch can clog the mesh, especially if it is slightly moist. Use a soft brush to clean the mesh. Never use water, as it will cause rust. Also, check for tears in the mesh. A torn sieve will let coarse particles through, ruining your classification. For quality control, take a sample from each grade and feel it between your fingers. It should be smooth and free of lumps. If you find lumps, you need to grind again or sieve again. Store each grade separately in labelled sacks. Proper classification adds value to your product and allows you to target different markets.

4. Mesh Size Selection for Desired Texture

The mesh size of your sieve determines the texture of your final starch powder. Mesh size is measured in numbers: a higher number means smaller holes. For example, a 100-mesh sieve has 100 openings per inch, so the particles are very fine. A 40-mesh sieve has larger holes, so the particles are coarser. In Africa, different markets want different textures. For food use, like making fufu or starch for soups, a medium texture is often preferred. That is around 60 to 80 mesh. For baking, you might need finer starch, around 100 mesh. For industrial uses, like making adhesives, a coarser starch of 40 to 60 mesh is acceptable. So how do you choose? First, decide what your customer wants. If you are selling to local markets, ask the traders what they prefer. In many West African countries, customers like starch that is fine but not powdery, like talcum powder. That is usually 80 to 100 mesh. If you are selling to a pharmaceutical company, they may require 200 mesh or finer. You can achieve different mesh sizes by using different sieves. You can also blend different grades to get a custom texture. For example, mix 50% of 60-mesh starch with 50% of 100-mesh starch to get a medium-fine product. When you buy sieves, make sure they are standard. In Kenya, you can buy sieves with mesh sizes from 20 to 200. The price ranges from 2,000 to 10,000 Kenyan shillings depending on the size and quality. Always test your sieve before buying. Take a sample of starch and sieve it. Check if the particle size matches your expectation. Also, consider the capacity. A larger sieve will process more per hour. But if you are small-scale, a 30 cm diameter sieve is enough. Remember, finer mesh sizes slow down the sieving process because the holes are smaller. So you may need to shake longer or use a mechanical shaker. Finally, document your mesh sizes and the resulting texture. This helps you repeat the same quality every time.

5. Quality Control for Particle Size

Quality control for particle size ensures your starch powder is consistent and meets customer expectations. You don't need a laboratory to do basic QC. Start with a simple test: take a handful of starch and rub it between your fingers. It should feel smooth, not gritty. If it feels gritty, there are coarse particles. You can also use a simple sieve test. Take a known weight of starch, say 100 grams, and sieve it through a 100-mesh sieve for 2 minutes. Weigh what passes through and what stays on top. Calculate the percentage. For fine starch, at least 90% should pass through. If less, you need to grind more or sieve longer. Another test is the sedimentation test. Put 10 grams of starch in a glass of water, stir, and let it settle. The particles will settle at different rates. The finest particles settle last. After 1 hour, measure the height of the sediment. A higher sediment means finer particles. You can also use a simple microscope or a magnifying glass to look at the particles. They should be uniform in size. If you see large lumps, your sieving is not working. For a more precise measurement, you can use a set of standard sieves and a mechanical shaker. This is called sieve analysis. You record the weight retained on each sieve and plot a graph. This is common in larger factories. But for small-scale, the feel test and the sieve test are enough. You should also check for moisture. Moist starch will clump and clog sieves. Use a moisture meter or the salt test: mix a pinch of starch with dry salt. If the salt sticks, the starch is too moist. Keep records of your tests. Write down the date, batch number, and results. This helps you trace problems. If a customer complains, you can check your records. Also, train your workers to do these tests. Consistency is key. If you produce the same quality every time, you will build a reputation and get repeat buyers. Remember, particle size affects how the starch behaves in cooking and industrial use. So never skip QC.

6. Dust Control and Safety

Starch dust is a serious health and safety hazard. When you grind and sieve starch, fine dust fills the air. Breathing this dust can cause respiratory problems like asthma and bronchitis. It can also irritate your eyes and skin. In severe cases, starch dust can explode if it is concentrated and there is a spark. So you must control dust. The best way is to use a dust extraction system. This can be as simple as a cyclone attached to your mill. A cyclone separates dust from the air using centrifugal force. The dust falls into a collection bin, and clean air exits. You can also use a bag filter. For small-scale operations, you can wear a dust mask. A simple N95 mask is good. But masks are not enough; you need to reduce dust at the source. Enclose your mill and sieves with covers. Use flexible ducting to carry dust away. In many African workshops, you can build a simple dust collector using a 200-litre drum and a small fan. The fan pulls dusty air into the drum, where water sprays trap the dust. This is called a wet scrubber. It is cheap and effective. Also, keep your work area clean. Sweep or vacuum spilled starch immediately. Do not use compressed air to blow dust, as it will just spread it. Wear protective clothing: long sleeves, goggles, and gloves. Avoid wearing loose clothing that can get caught in moving parts. Ensure your electrical equipment is properly grounded to prevent sparks. Never smoke or use open flames near starch dust. Store starch in sealed containers. If you feel dizzy or have trouble breathing, stop work and get fresh air. Regular health checks for workers are important. In Nigeria, the Factory Act requires employers to provide a safe workplace. Even if you are a small business, you should follow these guidelines. Train your workers on dust hazards and safe practices. Post signs reminding them to wear masks. A safe workplace is a productive workplace.

<h2>Practical Reference: Milling and Sieving of Dried Starch</h2>

<p>In cassava starch processing, the milling and sieving stage transforms brittle dried starch cakes into a free-flowing powder with a consistent particle size. For African processors whether operating a village-level enterprise in Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania, or Uganda this stage directly determines product grade, market price, and customer satisfaction. Food-grade starch buyers, pharmaceutical companies, and textile manufacturers all specify mesh size and particle distribution; failing to control these means selling at commodity prices rather than premium rates.</p>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the operational knowledge needed to grind dried starch cakes, select equipment, sieve and classify the powder, choose mesh sizes for target textures, run quality control, and manage dust safely. Each section is written for the realities of African processing: variable humidity, intermittent power, locally available equipment, and buyers who inspect product before payment.</p>

<h3>1. Grinding Dried Starch Cakes</h3>

<p>Dried starch cakes from sun-drying or flash-drying typically contain 1014% moisture. At this moisture, starch is brittle and fractures cleanly under impact or attrition. Above 15% moisture, the material smears, heats up, and clogs screens a common cause of downtime in small mills. Below 8% moisture, excessive fines and dust are generated, reducing yield of the target grade.</p>

<p>Grinding is usually a two-stage operation: a coarse crusher (hammer mill or knife mill) breaks cakes into 25 mm granules, then a fine mill (pin mill, disc mill, or turbo mill) reduces them to the target particle size. For African small and medium enterprises (SMEs), a locally fabricated hammer mill with a 23 mm screen followed by a pin mill is a cost-effective configuration. Throughput of 150500 kg/hour is achievable with a 7.515 kW motor, matching the output of a typical 12 tonne/day drying line.</p>

<h3>3. Sieving and Classification</h3>

<p>Sieving separates milled starch into uniform fractions. A vibratory or rotary sifter with interchangeable screens is standard. For small operations, a manual or motorised vibratory sieve (0.52 m screen area) handles 100500 kg/h. Classification may include air classification for very fine grades (pharmaceutical, &lt;75 m).</p>

<p>Oversize particles return to the mill; undersize fines may be blended into lower-grade products or sold to adhesive manufacturers. This two-way separation is critical: it prevents both customer rejection (oversize) and yield loss (excess fines).</p>

<h3>4. Mesh Size Selection for Desired Texture</h3>

<p>Mesh size is the single most important specification for buyers. African starch buyers typically request 80100 mesh for food use, 100200 mesh for pharmaceutical and cosmetic use, and 4060 mesh for industrial adhesives and textiles.</p>

<h3>5. Quality Control for Particle Size</h3>

<p>QC at this stage is non-negotiable for premium pricing. Minimum tests:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Moisture content:</strong> 1013% (oven method, 105C, 4 hours).</li>
<li><strong>Particle size distribution:</strong> Ro-tap sieve analysis with stacked sieves (e.g., 60, 80, 100, 200 mesh) for 10 minutes.</li>
<li><strong>Bulk density:</strong> 0.50.7 g/cm for food-grade; measure with a graduated cylinder.</li>
<li><strong>Colour:</strong> White to off-white; compare against standard sample.</li>
<li><strong>Foreign matter:</strong> Visual and sieve-based; reject if sand or fibre exceeds 0.1%.</li>
</ul>

<p>Maintain a batch log recording mill settings, screen mesh, sieve results, and moisture. Buyers especially exporters and pharmaceutical firms increasingly require this traceability.</p>

<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING STARCH DUST EXPLOSION AND RESPIRATORY HAZARD</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;color:#4e342e;">Starch dust is combustible. Concentrations above 40 g/m in enclosed spaces can ignite from a spark, static discharge, or hot surface, causing a dust explosion. Additionally, prolonged inhalation of fine starch dust causes respiratory irritation and, in susceptible workers, occupational asthma. Mandatory controls: (1) install dust extraction and filtration at mill and sieve discharge points; (2) use anti-static and grounded equipment; (3) prohibit smoking, open flames, and non-ATEX electricals in the milling room; (4) provide N95 or P2 respirators, goggles, and hearing protection; (5) enforce a no-dry-sweeping rule use wet cleaning or industrial vacuums; (6) train workers annually on dust explosion risks. Reference: OSHA Combustible Dust National Emphasis Program (CPL 03-00-008) and ILO Encyclopaedia of Occupational Health Flour and Starch Dust.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Dust Control and Safety</h3>

<p>Dust control is both a safety and a product-loss issue. In a typical African SME mill, 38% of starch is lost as airborne dust. Capturing it via cyclone or bag filter recovers saleable product and protects workers. Simple, low-cost measures include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Enclosing mill and sieve discharge points with flexible connectors.</li>
<li>Installing a cyclone separator on the mill exhaust, discharging into a collection bag.</li>
<li>Using local exhaust ventilation (LEV) with a 1.53 kW fan for a 500 kg/h line.</li>
<li>Grounding all metal equipment to prevent static sparks.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Confirm dried starch cake moisture is 1014% before milling.</li>
<li>Inspect and tighten mill screens; replace worn or torn screens immediately.</li>
<li>Set coarse mill to 23 mm screen; fine mill to target mesh.</li>
<li>Verify sieve stack (e.g., 60/80/100/200) is clean and undamaged.</li>
<li>Run Ro-tap sieve analysis on each batch; record distribution.</li>
<li>Check bulk density and moisture against buyer specification.</li>
<li>Confirm dust extraction is running and respirators are worn.</li>
<li>Label and store finished starch in moisture-barrier bags; log batch number.</li>
</ol>
