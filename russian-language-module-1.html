<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="font-size:14px;line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Scan for false friends first.</strong> Before reading any Cyrillic document, circle , , , , , these are the letters most likely to mislead a Latin-script reader.</li>
<li><strong>Find the stress.</strong> In an unfamiliar word, say it aloud with the stress on different syllables until it matches a word you know. Stress controls vowel quality and meaning.</li>
<li><strong>Apply akanye mentally.</strong> Unstressed sounds like . "" is "Moskva," not "Moskvo."</li>
<li><strong>Check hard vs soft pairs.</strong> A soft sign () or a following , , , , changes the consonant this affects names and place names on paperwork.</li>
<li><strong>Verify numbers in words, not just digits.</strong> Russian invoices often spell out sums in words; misreading "" (100) vs "" (6) is a real risk.</li>
<li><strong>Use transliteration tables for official documents.</strong> The ALA-LC and ISO 9 systems give consistent Latin equivalents for passports, contracts, and customs forms.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm names by voice.</strong> On a call, ask your counterpart to spell their surname letter by letter in Cyrillic this catches errors that email cannot.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a personal glossary.</strong> Build a one-page list of the 50 Cyrillic words most common in your sector (cargo, payment, contract, customs, delivery) and review it before every Russia/EAEU meeting.</li>
</ol>
