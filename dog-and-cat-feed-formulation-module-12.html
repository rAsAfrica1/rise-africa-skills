1. Project Planning and Ingredient Selection

Start your capstone by choosing a target animal: a 15 kg adult dog or a 4 kg adult cat. You will formulate a complete dry food for that animal. First, set a production goal: 20 kg of finished kibble. Next, list ingredients available locally and affordably. In many African markets, good options include maize, sorghum, cassava flour, soybean meal, fishmeal (omena/dagaa), meat and bone meal, poultry by-product meal, sunflower cake, and vegetable oil. For cats, you must include animal protein sources like fish or chicken because cats are obligate carnivores. For dogs, you can use more plant protein but still need at least 18% animal protein for palatability and amino acid balance.

Use a simple spreadsheet (Google Sheets or Excel) to record each ingredient's cost per kg, dry matter protein, fat, fibre, calcium, and phosphorus. You can get these values from feed tables like the FAO African Feed Resources or local university extension leaflets. For example, maize meal has about 9% protein, 4% fat; soybean meal has 44% protein, 20% fat; fishmeal (omena) has 60% protein, 8% fat. Note any anti-nutritional factors: raw soybeans need heating; cassava flour must be dried to remove cyanide.

Plan your processing method early. For dry kibble, you will need a grinder, mixer, extruder (or a simple pellet mill), and dryer. If you cannot access an extruder, you can make a baked or sun-dried biscuit-type food, but extrusion is best for digestibility. Decide on batch size: 20 kg is manageable for a small mixer. Calculate ingredient quantities using a Pearson square or simple algebra to hit your target protein and fat. For a dog food targeting 22% protein and 10% fat, you might use 40% maize, 25% soybean meal, 15% fishmeal, 10% meat and bone meal, 5% oil, 3% vitamin-mineral premix, 2% salt and calcium sources. For cats, target 30% protein and 15% fat: 30% fishmeal, 20% chicken meal, 20% maize, 15% soybean meal, 10% oil, 3% premix, 2% salt.

Write down your final ingredient list with exact weights for a 20 kg batch. Check that all ingredients are available in your area and within your budget. If an ingredient is too expensive, substitute with a similar nutrient profile. For instance, replace fishmeal with poultry by-product meal plus a little oil. Keep a notebook of your decisions; you will need it for cost analysis and presentation.

2. Nutrient Profile Calculation and Balancing

Now calculate the nutrient profile of your 20 kg batch. Use the guaranteed analysis of each ingredient: protein, fat, fibre, calcium, phosphorus, and metabolizable energy (ME). For each ingredient, multiply its percentage in the mix by its nutrient percentage. For example, if you use 8 kg of maize (40% of batch) with 9% protein, that contributes 0.4 Ã 9 = 3.6% protein to the final mix. Do this for all ingredients and sum the contributions. Your target for adult dogs: 18-25% protein, 8-15% fat, 3-5% fibre, 1.0-1.5% calcium, 0.8-1.2% phosphorus, and 3,200-3,600 kcal ME/kg. For adult cats: 26-35% protein, 9-20% fat, 2-4% fibre, 1.0-1.5% calcium, 0.8-1.2% phosphorus, and 3,400-3,800 kcal ME/kg.

If your protein is low, add more soybean meal or fishmeal. If fat is low, add vegetable oil or animal fat. If calcium is low, add limestone or bone meal. If phosphorus is low, add dicalcium phosphate. Always keep the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio between 1.2:1 and 1.4:1. For example, if you have 1.0% calcium and 0.8% phosphorus, ratio is 1.25:1, which is good. If you add too much calcium, it can block absorption of other minerals.

Use a free online tool like the Feed Formulation Spreadsheet from the University of Missouri or a simple Excel sheet you build yourself. You can also use the Pearson square for two-ingredient mixes, but for multiple ingredients, use trial and error with a spreadsheet. Remember to account for moisture: if you use fresh meat, it contains 70% water, so you need more of it to match dry matter protein. For dry kibble, final moisture should be below 12%, so most ingredients should be dry.

Check amino acids: dogs need methionine and lysine; cats need taurine and arginine. Fishmeal and meat meals are good sources. If using mostly plant proteins, add synthetic methionine (for dogs) or taurine (for cats) at 0.1% of the mix. Also add a vitamin-mineral premix at 1-2% to cover micronutrients. Salt (sodium chloride) at 0.5% is enough. After balancing, recalculate your final nutrient profile to confirm it meets the targets. Write down the final percentages for your presentation.

3. Processing Method Selection

Choose a processing method that matches your equipment and market. The most common for complete dry pet food is extrusion. Extrusion involves grinding ingredients to a fine powder (less than 1 mm), mixing with water and steam, then forcing the mix through a die under high pressure and temperature (100-150Â°C) for 10-20 seconds. This cooks the starch, kills bacteria, and expands the kibble. You then dry the kibble at 120Â°C for 10-15 minutes until moisture is below 12%. Extrusion requires an extruder, which can cost from $500 for a small single-screw model to $5,000 for a larger one. In many African cities, you can rent time on an extruder at a local feed mill.

If you cannot access an extruder, you can use a pellet mill. Pelleting grinds and mixes ingredients, then presses them through a die with steam. The temperature is lower (80-90Â°C), so starch is less cooked, but it still makes a decent dry food. Pellet mills are cheaper ($200-$1,000) and easier to find. Another option is baking: mix ingredients with water to form a dough, roll it flat, cut into biscuits, and bake at 180Â°C for 20-30 minutes. Baking is simple and works for small batches, but the food may be less digestible and more crumbly. Sun-drying is not recommended because it does not kill pathogens and takes too long.

For cats, you must ensure the food is palatable. Cats prefer smaller kibble (3-5 mm diameter) and often reject food with too much plant protein. Extrusion with a meat-based mix works best. For dogs, larger kibble (8-12 mm) is fine. After processing, cool the kibble to room temperature before packing. Store in airtight bags or containers away from moisture and sunlight. If you are selling, include a label with ingredient list, guaranteed analysis, and feeding guidelines.

Consider your local context: if electricity is unreliable, a manual pellet press or baking may be more practical. If you have access to a hammer mill and mixer, you can do the grinding and mixing yourself, then take the mash to a pellet mill. Always clean equipment between batches to avoid cross-contamination. For a 20 kg batch, a small extruder can process it in 10-15 minutes. Record your processing steps and any problems you encounter for your presentation.

4. Palatability and Safety Assessment

Palatability is how much an animal likes the food. You can test it with a simple two-bowl test. Offer your formulated food and a commercial food (or a previous batch) side by side to 5-10 dogs or cats for 15 minutes. Record which bowl they eat first and how much they consume. A good result is when your food is eaten first at least 70% of the time and total intake is similar to the commercial food. If animals reject your food, try adding 1-2% animal fat, 0.5% salt, or a palatant like hydrolysed liver or fish oil. Cats are especially sensitive to texture and smell; warm the food slightly to release aromas.

Safety assessment ensures the food does not harm the animal. First, check for anti-nutritional factors: raw soybeans contain trypsin inhibitors that reduce protein digestion; heat them at 100Â°C for 30 minutes. Cassava contains cyanide; dry it or soak it for 24 hours. Rancid oils can cause vitamin E deficiency; smell your oil before use. Second, microbial safety: after processing, test for Salmonella and E. coli. You can send samples to a local veterinary lab or use a simple petri film test. If you cannot test, ensure processing temperatures reach at least 80Â°C for 10 minutes. Third, check for foreign objects: metal, stones, or plastic from equipment. Use a magnet and sieve.

Also assess nutritional safety: too much calcium can cause bone problems in growing puppies; too much vitamin A can be toxic. Stick to your premix recommendations. For cats, ensure taurine is added because deficiency causes blindness and heart disease. For dogs, avoid excess salt (above 1% can cause dehydration). After feeding your test batch to animals for 2 weeks, watch for vomiting, diarrhoea, or dull coat. If any sign appears, stop and reformulate.

Document all observations: date, animal ID, amount eaten, any health issues. Take photos of the food and the animals eating. This data will be part of your presentation. If you cannot access live animals, do a physical test: smell, texture, and colour. A good kibble is uniform, not too hard, and has a pleasant meaty smell. Remember, safety first: never feed a batch you suspect is contaminated.

5. Cost Analysis and Production Feasibility

Calculate the cost of your 20 kg batch. List each ingredient and its cost per kg in your local currency. For example, in Kenya, maize meal might be KES 50/kg, soybean meal KES 80/kg, fishmeal KES 200/kg, meat and bone meal KES 120/kg, vegetable oil KES 150/kg, premix KES 500/kg. Multiply each by the weight used in your formula. Sum all ingredient costs to get total ingredient cost. Then add processing costs: electricity or fuel for grinding, mixing, extrusion, and drying. For a small extruder, electricity might be KES 100 per batch. Labour: if you do it yourself, estimate your time at a fair wage, say KES 200 per hour. Packaging: a 1 kg bag costs KES 20. For 20 kg, you need 20 bags, so KES 400. Add overheads like rent and equipment depreciation: KES 200 per batch.

Total cost example: ingredients KES 2,500 + electricity KES 100 + labour KES 400 + packaging KES 400 + overhead KES 200 = KES 3,600 for 20 kg, or KES 180 per kg. Now check market price: commercial dog food in Nairobi sells for KES 250-400 per kg. Your cost is lower, so you can sell at KES 250 per kg and make KES 70 per kg profit, or KES 1,400 per batch. For cats, commercial food is KES 400-600 per kg, so you have more room. But if your ingredient costs are higher, you may need to adjust formula or find cheaper sources.

Feasibility also depends on scale. If you can only make 20 kg per week, your income is small. To be viable, aim for at least 100 kg per week. That requires a larger mixer and extruder, and more labour. You can start small and reinvest profits. Consider selling directly to pet owners at markets, vet clinics, or online. Offer free samples to build trust. Keep records of all costs and sales. If your cost per kg is higher than commercial food, you need to either reduce costs (buy in bulk, use alternative ingredients) or target a premium niche (organic, grain-free).

Finally, calculate break-even point: fixed costs (equipment, rent) divided by profit per kg. If equipment costs KES 50,000 and profit is KES 70/kg, you need to sell 714 kg to break even. That is 36 batches of 20 kg. Write this analysis in your report. Be honest about challenges: inconsistent ingredient quality, power outages, and competition. But with good planning, you can succeed.

6. Presentation and Peer Review of Formulations

Your final task is to present your complete pet food formulation to peers and an instructor. Prepare a 10-minute presentation covering: target animal, ingredient list with percentages, nutrient profile, processing method, palatability and safety results, cost analysis, and production plan. Use visuals: a photo of your final product, a table of ingredients, a graph of nutrient levels vs. targets, and a cost breakdown pie chart. If you made a physical batch, bring a sample for peers to see and smell. If not, show a video of your process.

Structure your presentation: start with the problem (e.g., expensive commercial pet food in your area) and your solution (a locally made, affordable complete food). Then explain your formulation step by step. For example: 'I targeted 22% protein and 10% fat for dogs. I used 40% maize, 25% soybean meal, 15% fishmeal, 10% meat and bone meal, 5% oil, 3% premix, 2% salt. My calculated protein was 22.5%, fat 10.2%, calcium 1.2%, phosphorus 0.9%. Processing was extrusion at 120Â°C. Palatability test showed 8 out of 10 dogs ate my food first. Cost is KES 180/kg, selling at KES 250/kg.' Be ready to answer questions: Why did you choose fishmeal? What if fishmeal is unavailable? How do you ensure taurine for cats?

Peer review: after each presentation, peers fill a simple feedback form. Rate clarity (1-5), scientific accuracy (1-5), practicality (1-5), and give one suggestion for improvement. The instructor also gives feedback. Use this feedback to write a one-page reflection: what you learned, what you would change, and how you would scale up. For example, a peer might point out that your calcium is too high, so you would reduce limestone. Or they might suggest using a cheaper protein source like sunflower cake.

Finally, submit your full project report: 5-10 pages including all calculations, test results, cost analysis, and reflection. This capstone proves you can formulate a complete pet food from scratch. You can use this report to apply for a small grant or to pitch to a local feed store. Remember, the goal is not perfection but a safe, nutritious, and affordable product that meets AAFCO or FEDIAF standards as closely as possible with local resources. Congratulations on completing the course!

Practical Reference: Dog and Cat Feed Formulation Capstone

This capstone module brings together everything you have learned about dog and cat feed formulation and asks you to produce a complete, defensible pet food formulation for the African market. The goal is not simply to balance nutrients on paper â it is to design a product that a real business can manufacture, sell at a profit, and stand behind. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the technical benchmarks, cost logic, and safety checks you need to complete your capstone project with confidence.

Across Africa, the pet food market is growing rapidly, driven by urbanisation, rising middle-class incomes, and changing attitudes toward companion animals. Yet most commercially available pet food is imported, expensive, and formulated for temperate-climate ingredients. A locally formulated product using African ingredients â poultry by-products, fish meal, maize, sorghum, cassava, insect protein, and local oilseeds â can compete on price and freshness if the formulation is technically sound and the processing is hygienic. Your capstone project is a business plan disguised as a formulation exercise. Treat it that way.

1. Project Planning and Ingredient Selection

Start with the animal and the market, not the ingredients. Define whether you are formulating for dogs or cats, life stage (growth, adult maintenance, gestation/lactation), size breed, and the price tier you are targeting. A maintenance diet for adult dogs in Nairobi is a very different product from a growth diet for kittens in Lagos.

Ingredient selection in Africa must balance four constraints: nutrient contribution, cost per kilogram, local availability, and safety. Prioritise ingredients that are consistently available within 200 km of your production site. Imported vitamin-mineral premixes are usually necessary and should be factored into both cost and lead time.

2. Nutrient Profile Calculation and Balancing

Once ingredients are selected, you must calculate the nutrient profile of the mix and compare it against the target standard. For dogs and cats, the two most widely used references are AAFCO Nutrient Profiles and FEDIAF Nutritional Guidelines. In Africa, many formulators also reference the NRC (2006) Nutrient Requirements of Dogs and Cats for scientific rigour.

Work on a dry matter (DM) basis first to avoid moisture confusion, then convert to as-fed for labelling and production. Cats are obligate carnivores â they require preformed taurine, arachidonic acid, and vitamin A (not beta-carotene). Dogs are omnivores with more flexibility but still require careful amino acid balancing.

Use the Pearson Square method for simple two-ingredient blends or linear programming software (e.g., free tools like WinFeed or Excel Solver) for multi-ingredient formulations. Always check the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio (target 1.2:1 to 1.4:1 for dogs; 1.1:1 to 1.3:1 for cats) and the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio.

3. Processing Method Selection

Your formulation must be manufacturable with the equipment you can afford. In Africa, the three most realistic processing routes are:

  • Extrusion (dry kibble): Requires a extruder, dryer, and coating system. High capital cost (USD 15,000â80,000) but produces shelf-stable, marketable kibble. Best for medium-to-large operations.
  • Baking / oven-drying: Lower capital, suitable for small batches and soft-moist products. Shorter shelf life unless moisture is controlled below 12%.
  • Wet / canned or retort pouch: High moisture (75â85%), requires retort sterilisation. Expensive packaging and logistics; best for premium urban markets.

Whatever route you choose, the critical control points are: grinding particle size (target 500â800 microns for extrusion), preconditioning temperature (85â95Â°C), extrusion temperature (110â150Â°C), drying to <12% moisture, and fat coating after drying. For wet foods, achieve F0 â¥ 3.0 in retort.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Aflatoxin and Mycotoxin Control

Maize, groundnuts, and sorghum in sub-Saharan Africa are frequently contaminated with aflatoxins, especially in humid storage conditions. Aflatoxin B1 is a potent hepatocarcinogen and can cause acute toxicity and death in dogs and cats. Never use visibly mouldy grain. Test every incoming batch with a rapid aflatoxin test kit (target <20 ppb total aflatoxin for pet food). Use a mould inhibitor (e.g., hydrated sodium calcium aluminosilicate) at 0.5â1.0% in the final mix. Store ingredients below 12% moisture and below 25Â°C. Document every test result for traceability and regulatory compliance.

4. Palatability and Safety Assessment

A formulation that animals refuse to eat is worthless. Palatability in dogs and cats is driven by aroma, fat content, protein source, texture, and moisture. In African markets, dogs often show strong preference for fish and poultry aromas; cats prefer fish, liver, and high-fat animal proteins.

Conduct a simple two-bowl preference test with at least 20 animals per species, over 3â5 days, rotating bowl positions to eliminate side bias. Record first-choice, intake ratio, and total consumption. A product should achieve at least 60% intake ratio against the control to be considered commercially palatable.

Safety assessment includes microbial testing (Salmonella, E. coli, total plate count), mycotoxin screening, heavy metals (lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury), and verification that no prohibited ingredients (e.g., ruminant material in ruminant feeds, though not relevant for pets) are present. For pet food, ensure compliance with the destination market's regulations â in Kenya, KEBS; in Nigeria, NAFDAC; in South Africa, the Fertilizers, Farm Feeds, Agricultural Remedies and Stock Remedies Act.

5. Cost Analysis and Production Feasibility

Your capstone must include a full cost breakdown: ingredient cost per kg, processing cost, packaging, labour, utilities, overhead, and distribution. Calculate the cost per kg of finished product and compare it to the prevailing retail price of imported and local competitors.

A practical African formulation should target an ingredient cost of 40â60% of the selling price. If your ingredient cost exceeds 70% of the likely selling price, reformulate with cheaper local ingredients or reduce protein inclusion. Remember that protein is usually the most expensive nutrient â every percentage point of crude protein adds cost.

Feasibility also depends on volume. A small extruder may produce 100â200 kg/hour; to be viable, you need consistent demand for at least 5â10 tonnes per month. Consider contract manufacturing or shared facilities in the early stage to reduce capital risk.

6. Presentation and Peer Review of Formulations

Your final submission should include: the formulation sheet (ingredients and inclusion rates), the calculated nutrient profile versus target, the processing flow diagram, palatability test results, safety test results, cost analysis, and a one-page business summary. Present it as if you were pitching to an investor or a distributor.

Peer review is part of the assessment. Reviewers should check for: nutrient adequacy, ingredient safety, cost realism, processing feasibility, and clarity of presentation. Use the checklist below as your self-assessment and peer-review tool.
