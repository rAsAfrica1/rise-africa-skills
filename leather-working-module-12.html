1. Project Planning and Design

Start your capstone by choosing a product you can realistically make with the leather you can produce. Good beginner items include a simple tote bag, a belt, a pair of sandals, or a notebook cover. Decide on the size, shape, and number of pieces. For example, a tote bag might need two side panels (30 cm x 40 cm), one bottom panel (30 cm x 15 cm), and two handles (60 cm x 5 cm). Draw your pattern on cardboard or old newspaper, cut it out, and test it with cheap fabric or paper before cutting leather.

Next, plan your tanning batch. If you are using goat or sheep skins, you will need 3â5 skins for a small bag. For cowhide, one half-hide is enough for several items. Estimate the chemicals: for vegetable tanning, you need 500 g of tannin powder (e.g., mimosa or quebracho) per 5 kg of hide, plus 200 g of salt and 50 g of soda ash for soaking. For chrome tanning, you need 100 g of chrome tanning agent, 50 g of salt, and 20 g of formic acid per 5 kg. Always wear gloves and goggles.

Create a timeline. Tanning takes 2â4 weeks for vegetable tanning and 1â2 days for chrome tanning. Add 2â3 days for drying and finishing. Then allow 3â5 days for stitching and assembly. Write down each step with dates. For example: Week 1 â soak and dehair; Week 2 â tan; Week 3 â dry and soften; Week 4 â cut and sew. If you are in a humid area like coastal Kenya, drying may take longer; use a drying rack with good airflow.

Finally, set a budget. List all materials and tools: hides (free if you source from local butchers), chemicals (about 2,000â5,000 KES), needles, thread (polyester or linen), awl, knife, cutting board, and a wooden mallet. You can make a simple stitching pony from scrap wood. Keep a notebook to record every expense. Share your plan with a mentor or experienced tanner for feedback before you start.

2. Sourcing Materials and Tools

For your capstone, you need raw hides, tanning agents, and basic tools. In many African countries, you can get fresh goat or sheep skins from local butchers for free or a small fee (50â200 KES per skin). Ask them to remove the skin carefullyâno cuts. If you use cowhide, visit an abattoir; a full hide may cost 500â1,500 KES. Always inspect for holes, scars, or rot. Fresh skins should be salted within 2 hours to prevent spoilage. Use 1 kg of coarse salt per 5 kg of hide.

For vegetable tanning, you can use local materials: acacia pods (contain tannins), tea leaves, or buy mimosa extract from leather supply shops. In Kenya, try Kamukunji or online suppliers. For chrome tanning, you need basic chromium sulfate, which may require a permit; check with local tanneries. Alternatively, use alum (potassium aluminium sulfate) from pharmacies or chemical shops. A simple alum tanning recipe: for 5 kg of hide, dissolve 1 kg of alum and 500 g of salt in 20 litres of water.

Tools: you need a fleshing knife (or a blunt machete), a beam (wooden plank or PVC pipe), a soaking drum (plastic bucket), a stirring stick, a knife, a cutting board, a stitching awl, needles (size 1â3), waxed polyester thread, a wooden mallet, and a stitching pony. You can make a stitching pony from two wooden boards and a bolt. For finishing, get sandpaper (120, 220, 400 grit), beeswax, and a piece of smooth bone or glass for burnishing.

Source locally: visit a hardware store for sandpaper and beeswax; a tailor for thread and needles; a carpenter for scrap wood. If you cannot find a fleshing knife, use a dull axe head or a strong spoon. Always prioritise safety: thick gloves, apron, and eye protection. Borrow or share tools with classmates to reduce costs. Keep a list of where you got each item and the price.

3. Executing a Tanning Batch

Follow your plan step by step. First, soak dried skins in clean water for 6â12 hours to rehydrate. Use 20 litres of water per 5 kg of hide. Add 50 g of soda ash to help remove dirt and blood. Stir every 2 hours. If using fresh skins, skip soaking and go straight to fleshing. Fleshing means removing fat and meat with a fleshing knife on a beam. Push away from you, keeping the blade at a slight angle. Be careful not to cut the skin.

Next, dehair. For vegetable tanning, you can use a lime bath: mix 1 kg of hydrated lime in 20 litres of water, soak the skin for 3â7 days, stirring daily. The hair will slip. Scrape it off with a dull knife. For chrome tanning, you may skip dehair if you want hair-on leather, but for most items, dehair. Rinse the skin in clean water for 2 hours to remove lime.

Now tanning. For vegetable tanning, make a tanning liquor: dissolve 500 g of mimosa powder in 20 litres of warm water. Add the skin and stir daily. Change the liquor every 3 days, increasing concentration: first 500 g, then 750 g, then 1 kg. Total time: 2â4 weeks. The skin is tanned when it is uniform brown and no white core remains when cut. For chrome tanning: mix 100 g of chrome tanning agent, 50 g of salt, and 20 g of formic acid in 10 litres of water. Soak the skin for 24 hours, stirring occasionally. Then add 50 g of sodium bicarbonate to raise pH, and soak another 12 hours. The skin should be blue-grey.

After tanning, rinse and dry. Hang the skin in shade for 2â3 days. When partly dry, apply a thin layer of oil (neatsfoot or coconut) and work the skin by stretching and rubbing. This softening is crucial. For vegetable-tanned leather, you can also drum it in a rotating barrel with wooden balls for 2 hours. Store in a cool, dry place. Record the exact times, temperatures, and amounts used.

4. Producing a Finished Leather Item

Now turn your tanned leather into a product. Start by selecting the best parts of your hide. Avoid areas with scars or thin spots. For a tote bag, lay your pattern pieces on the leather and trace with a silver pen or awl. Cut with a sharp knife or leather shears. Cut on a cutting board. For straps, cut long strips 5 cm wide using a strap cutter or a straight edge and knife. Bevel the edges with a edge beveler or sandpaper.

If you want a smooth edge, burnish it: dampen the edge with water, then rub with a piece of canvas or wood. Apply beeswax and rub again with a bone folder or glass bottle. This seals the edge. For stitching, mark stitch holes with a stitching chisel or an awl. Use a spacing of 4â5 mm. Place the leather in a stitching pony. Use two needles and waxed thread (about 1.5 times the length of the seam). Stitch in a saddle stitch: pass the right needle through the hole, then the left needle through the same hole from the opposite side. Pull tight. Continue.

For a belt, cut a strip 3â4 cm wide and 100â120 cm long. Punch holes at one end for the buckle and at the other for adjustment. Use a hole punch. Attach a buckle with rivets or stitching. For sandals, cut a sole from thick leather (4â5 mm), and straps from thinner leather. Glue and stitch the straps. Use contact cement for temporary hold.

Finishing: apply a leather conditioner (e.g., a mix of beeswax and coconut oil) with a cloth. Buff with a brush. If you want a darker colour, use a dye (e.g., Fiebingâs) before conditioning. Let dry overnight. Check all stitches and edges. Trim any loose threads. Your item should be strong and neat. Take photos at each stage.

5. Documenting Processes and Results

Documentation is part of your capstone. It shows what you did and helps others learn. Start a notebook or a digital file. For each day, write the date, weather (temperature and humidity), and steps taken. For example: '15 March 2025, 28Â°C, 60% humidity. Soaked 2 goat skins in 20 L water with 50 g soda ash for 8 hours.' Record exact amounts of chemicals, water, and time. If something goes wrongâlike a skin spoiledâwrite what happened and how you fixed it.

Take photos or short videos. Use a phone. Photograph: raw skins, fleshing, tanning bath, drying, softening, cutting, stitching, and the final product. Include a ruler or coin for scale. Label each photo with a caption. If you cannot take photos, draw sketches. Keep all receipts for materials. Note the source of each hide and chemical.

At the end, write a summary of results. How many skins did you start with? How many finished? What was the yield? For example, 'Started with 3 goat skins, finished 2.5 (one had a tear).' Describe the quality: softness, colour, thickness. Measure thickness with a ruler or caliper. Note any defects. Compare your product to the plan. Did you meet the timeline? If not, why?

Also document costs. Total spent: chemicals, tools, transport. Calculate cost per item. For example, if you spent 3,000 KES and made 2 bags, each bag cost 1,500 KES in materials. This helps you price for sale. Finally, write a reflection: what went well, what you would change. This documentation can be used for a portfolio or to teach others. Share a copy with your trainer.

6. Presentation and Evaluation

Prepare a 10â15 minute presentation of your capstone project. Show your finished leather item, your notebook, and photos. Explain each step: sourcing, tanning, and production. Use simple language. For example, 'I used mimosa extract because it is local and cheap.' Show your budget and timeline. Mention challenges: 'The skin dried too fast because of wind, so I softened it with oil.' Be honest about mistakes.

Set up a display table. Place your item on a clean cloth. Include raw materials (a small piece of hide, a handful of tannin powder) and tools. If you made multiple items, show them all. Label everything with small cards. Invite classmates, trainers, and local artisans. Ask for feedback: What do they like? What would they improve? Write down their comments.

Evaluation criteria: Did you follow safety rules? Was your tanning successful (leather is soft, no raw spots)? Is your product well-finished (straight stitches, neat edges)? Did you document thoroughly? Did you stay within budget? Your trainer will use a rubric. You can also self-evaluate. For example, rate yourself 1â5 on each step.

After the presentation, collect your feedback and write a final reflection. What will you do differently next time? If you plan to sell, calculate a fair price: materials + labour (e.g., 2,000 KES for 10 hours at 200 KES/hour) + 20% profit. For example, a bag costing 1,500 KES in materials and 2,000 KES labour should sell for at least 4,200 KES. Consider packaging and marketing. Take a final photo of you with your product. Celebrate your achievementâyou are now a leather maker.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Leather Making and Tanning â Capstone Project and Workshop Practice



This practical reference is written for African agribusiness entrepreneurs, vocational trainers and students who are preparing to run a complete tanning batch and produce a market-ready leather item. It assumes you already understand basic hide handling and now need to plan, source, execute, document and present your capstone work as a business proposition, not just an academic exercise. In Africa, leather value addition is one of the fastest routes to job creation because raw hides are abundant but finished leather is often imported. A well-run capstone project can become the seed of a small tannery, a leather goods brand or a training centre.



Use this module as a workshop companion. Each section gives practical steps, decision points and references. The goal is to move from raw material to a finished product that can be sold, costed and replicated.



1. Project Planning and Design


Start with the market, not the hide. Ask: who will buy this leather or leather item, and at what price? In many African markets, demand is strongest for durable bags, sandals, belts, upholstery and motorcycle seats. Design your project around a specific product and a specific customer. Write a one-page project brief covering: product, target customer, quantity, quality grade, budget, timeline and risk. Choose a tanning method that fits your resources â vegetable tanning is slower but uses locally available tannins from acacia or mangrove; chrome tanning is faster but requires controlled chemicals and waste treatment. For a capstone, vegetable tanning is often safer and more accessible, but chrome tanning may be required for soft garment leather.



Set measurable objectives: e.g. "produce 5 sq ft of vegetable-tanned goat leather at 1.2 mm thickness, then make 3 belts and sell 2 within 30 days." This makes evaluation objective and business-focused.



2. Sourcing Materials and Tools


Source hides from abattoirs, butchers or tanneries that preserve them with salt or chilling. In many African cities, hides are wasted because they are not preserved within 4â6 hours. Build a relationship with your supplier and agree on a preservation method. For vegetable tanning, source tannins from Acacia mearnsii (black wattle), Acacia nilotica or mangrove bark; these are available in East and Southern Africa. For chrome tanning, source basic chromium sulphate from a licensed chemical supplier and never substitute unknown powders.



Tools: fleshing knife, beam, drums or pits, pH meter, thermometer, measuring scale, gloves, goggles, boots, and a drying rack. For finishing: slicker, glass or stone, beeswax, neatsfoot oil, dyes and a stitching awl or sewing machine. Buy locally where possible to reduce cost and build supply chains. Keep a simple inventory sheet with supplier, price, quantity and date.





3. Executing a Tanning Batch


Work in batches of 5â10 hides so you can control quality. Record every step: weight of hide, volume of water, chemical dose, pH, temperature and time. Soaking removes salt and dirt; use clean water and change it until the hide is soft. Liming uses lime and sodium sulphide to swell and remove hair; this step is hazardous and must be done in a ventilated area with gloves and goggles. Fleshing removes flesh and fat; a blunt fleshing knife on a beam is safer for beginners than a machine. Deliming and bating prepare the hide for tanning. Pickling lowers pH to 2.5â3.0 using salt and acid. Tanning with vegetable tannins takes 2â7 days depending on thickness; start with a weak liquor and strengthen gradually. Chrome tanning takes hours but requires careful pH control and proper effluent treatment.



After tanning, sammy (remove excess water), split to uniform thickness, then dry slowly in shade to avoid case hardening. Finish by oiling, slicking and buffing. For a capstone, keep a batch log and take photos at each stage. This documentation becomes your evidence for evaluation and your standard operating procedure for future production.




SAFETY WARNING â Corrosive and toxic chemicals

Lime, sodium sulphide, acids and chromium salts can cause severe burns, blindness and respiratory damage. Always wear chemical goggles, a face shield, nitrile gloves, rubber boots and an apron. Work outdoors or in a forced-ventilation area. Never add water to acid â add acid to water. Never mix sodium sulphide with acid, as it releases toxic hydrogen sulphide gas. Keep a clean water eyewash station and first-aid kit within 10 metres. Pregnant women and children must stay away from the tanning yard. Dispose of spent liquors only after neutralisation and according to local environmental regulations.




4. Producing a Finished Leather Item


Choose a product that matches your leather's thickness and softness. Vegetable-tanned goat or cowhide at 1.2â2.0 mm is ideal for belts, bags, sandals, wallets and tool sheaths. Cut patterns using a paper template, then transfer to leather with a scratch awl. Skive edges, punch holes, and stitch with waxed thread using a saddle stitch for strength. Burnish edges with gum arabic or beeswax and a wooden slicker. Add hardware such as buckles and rivets sourced locally. For market readiness, standardise sizes and colours, and label each item with your workshop name and leather type.



Cost the item fully: hide, chemicals, water, labour, tools, packaging and transport. A simple belt may cost 30â50% of its selling price in materials and labour, leaving room for profit. Price for your market, not for a European market, unless you are exporting. In many African cities, a handmade leather belt sells for $8â$25, while a tote bag can sell for $40â$120.

5. Documenting Processes and Results


Documentation is not paperwork for its own sake â it is your business memory. Keep a batch log with date, hide ID, weight, chemicals, pH, temperature, time and observations. Take before-and-after photos of each hide and each finished product. Record yield: square feet of finished leather per hide and per batch. Record defects: scars, holes, uneven colour, shrinkage. Calculate cost per square foot and selling price per item. This data lets you quote customers accurately and improve your process.



Use a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on a phone. In low-connectivity areas, paper is fine; photograph pages for backup. At the end of the capstone, produce a one-page summary: batch number, inputs, outputs, cost, revenue, profit and lessons learned. This summary is what a bank, investor or training institution will ask for.

6. Presentation and Evaluation


Present your capstone as a business pitch, not just a craft show. Structure: problem, product, process, proof, price, profit, plan. Show your leather and finished items, your batch log, your cost table and your customer feedback. Explain what you would do differently next time. Evaluators should score you on: quality of leather, quality of product, safety compliance, documentation, cost accuracy, market understanding and presentation. If you are in a training institution, invite a local leather buyer or artisan to give feedback. If you are already in business, use the evaluation to set your next production target.



Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Confirm market demand and target price before buying hides.

  2. Preserve raw hides within 4â6 hours using salt or chilling.

  3. Wear full PPE for liming, pickling and tanning; keep eyewash nearby.

  4. Record every batch: weight, chemicals, pH, time, temperature, yield.

  5. Neutralise and dispose of wastewater responsibly.

  6. Cost every item fully â materials, labour, water, tools, transport.

  7. Standardise product sizes and quality for repeat sales.

  8. Document results with photos and a one-page profit summary.

  9. Present your work as a business case with evidence.
