Module 12

🧰 The Workshop and the Business

Everything you have learned so far happens inside a building, uses water, produces waste, and has to pay for itself. This final module covers workshop layout and equipment, water use, effluent and the discharge consent you must obtain before you build, solid waste, quality control and grading, the costing method, records and markets. It is deliberate about two things it will not do: it will not give you a discharge limit as though it were your law, and it will not give you a price to plan on.

Lesson 12.1~12 min

Workshop Layout: Designing the Hazard Out

In this lesson
  • Lay out a workshop so alkaline and acid processes cannot meet by accident
  • List the capital items each process stage requires
  • Explain why layout is a safety control that works even when people are tired

This is the most important workshop-design lesson in the course, and it is a physical-layout issue as much as a procedural one.

Go back to the rule you learned in the beamhouse module. Sulphide-bearing liquor from liming and unhairing, and acid-bearing liquor from deliming, bating, pickling and tanning, must never meet. When they do, the sulphide releases hydrogen sulphide gas, a gas that kills quickly and destroys your ability to smell it before it kills you. Toxicity documented in the technical literature runs from about 10 ppm producing irritation and headache, through 100 ppm capable of causing unconsciousness and possible death, to 300 ppm and above causing rapid unconsciousness and death.

Now apply that to a floor plan rather than a procedure.

The design rule is this: alkaline and sulphide processes, that is soaking, liming and unhairing, and acid processes, that is deliming, bating, pickling and chrome tanning, should be physically separated wherever the workshop's size allows. Separate pits or drums. Separate drains. Separate chemical storage. So that a spill, an overflow, or one moment of inattention cannot bring the two liquors into contact.

Here is why layout beats procedure. A procedure depends on a person doing the right thing on a particular day. Layout works on the day someone is tired, new, in a hurry, or working in poor light. A cramped workshop where the same drum, the same channel, or the same low point in the floor serves both processes has a standing, structural version of that hazard built into it, independent of how careful any individual worker is on any individual day.

So when you draw your workshop, draw the drains first. Ask where water goes when it is spilled. Ask where the floor slopes. Ask what happens if a drum overflows at night. If your answer to any of those is that the two streams would end up in the same place, you have not finished designing.

A vegetable tannery has a real advantage here that is worth stating plainly. Vegetable tanning removes almost the entire chemical-hazard profile from a small workshop. It is slower, its colour range is limited, and its wet-heat resistance is lower, but it is generally the safer and more forgiving starting point for a small, undercapitalised rural operation, and this layout problem is one of the main reasons.

Now the equipment, organised by process stage so you can see what each stage actually needs.

  • Curing area: frames, a salting floor, drainage.
  • Beamhouse: pits or drums for soaking, liming and unhairing; a fleshing beam and knife, or a machine.
  • Pickling and tanning vessels, kept physically separate from the alkaline vessels.
  • Post-tanning drums for retanning, dyeing and fatliquoring.
  • Drying frames or a drying area, and staking tools.
  • Finishing tools, including buffing equipment.
  • Protective equipment: gloves, boots, aprons, eye protection, and given the hazard above, a means of ventilation and a written plan for what to do if hydrogen sulphide is suspected.
  • Measuring tools: a thickness gauge and an area-measuring tool.
  • Leathercraft tools if you go on to finished products: cutting knives, stitching tools, hardware-setting tools.

That list is complete as a structure. What it deliberately does not carry is a price or a specification, because no costed, quantified equipment list or workshop layout specification for a small African tannery was retrieved for this course. Every workshop-scale figure in the material behind this course was extrapolated down from industrial sources and flagged as such. Lesson 5 teaches you how to turn this list into real numbers from your own market.

Two more layout points that cost nothing at the drawing stage and are expensive later.

Ventilation. Liming and unhairing pits release some hydrogen sulphide even under correct alkaline operation, especially when agitated or when a cover is lifted after standing. Work in the open air or with genuine cross-ventilation. Never in an enclosed pit room with the door shut. Design that in.

And storage clearance, which you met in the curing module: drying and storage racks and stacked hides should be at least 15 cm above the floor to protect against white ants and termites, and pallets should give at least 6 inches, about 15 cm, of clearance above the ground. That is a layout decision, not a housekeeping one, and it belongs on the drawing.

One last principle. Design for the workshop you will have in three years, not only the one you can afford this month, and leave the separation in place from day one even when your volumes are small. Retrofitting separation into a working tannery is far harder and far more expensive than drawing it in before the first block is laid.

Effluent: Find Your Authority Before You Build

In this lesson
  • State what tannery wastewater actually contains and where each component comes from
  • Identify the kind of national authority that grants discharge consent and how to approach it
  • Explain why no number in this course is your legal discharge limit

Read this lesson before you spend money, not after. The order matters more than the content.

First, know what you would be discharging. Tannery wastewater carries four things, and each traces to a step you have already learned.

  • High biological oxygen demand, a heavy organic load, from soaking, liming and fleshing.
  • High salt and chloride content, from curing and pickling.
  • Sulphide, from unhairing.
  • Chromium, if you chrome tan. Conventional low-exhaustion chrome tanning discharges 3 to 7 kg of chromium per tonne of wet-salted hide processed, while high-exhaustion technology cuts this to about 0.4 kg per tonne.

That last pair is worth reading twice: it is roughly a 90 percent reduction, and it is the size of the gap between ordinary and improved practice. Chrome exhaustion, how much chromium the hide actually takes up, runs at only 60 to 80 percent conventionally, so 20 to 40 percent of the chromium you paid for leaves in the spent float. High-exhaustion technology raises uptake to about 98 percent, and chrome recovery from the float can reach 90 and up to 95 to 98 percent with good practice. Each of those is a cost number as well as an environmental one: chromium that leaves in the drain was bought and not used.

Second, and this is the part of the lesson that matters most, understand who decides what you may discharge.

Every country that regulates tannery effluent does so through a national environmental regulatory authority, typically the same body that issues broader industrial-discharge permits and environmental-impact approvals. Kenya's is the National Environment Management Authority, NEMA, which requires a valid effluent discharge licence before any industrial effluent, including tannery effluent, may be discharged into the environment, with specific numeric standards set out in regulatory schedules that differ depending on whether discharge goes to a sewer or directly to the aquatic environment.

Kenya is named here only as a worked example of the kind of authority you must find. It is not your rule unless you are in Kenya, and even then the numbers must come from NEMA itself and not from this course. Other African countries have their own equivalent body, an Environmental Protection Authority, a Ministry of Environment, or something similarly named. You must identify and contact your own country's version. Never assume Kenya's standard, or any other country's, applies to you.

And this course gives you no discharge limit at all, because it has none to give honestly. No current African national tannery effluent-discharge standard was retrieved for the material behind this course. Any number printed here as though it were your limit would be a fabrication with legal and environmental consequences attached.

What can be shown, purely as an example of how such a rule is written in some countries, is a foreign table: France sets BOD5 at 25 mg/l, COD at 90 mg/l and chromium at 2 mg/l; Italy 20, 80 and 2; India 30, 250 and 2. Notice what to take from this and what not to. Take the shape: a discharge standard is normally written as a small set of numeric limits on specific parameters, differing by country. Do not take the numbers. All three examples happen to land on 2 mg/l for chromium, and that convergence is not evidence that 2 mg/l is your rule. Your country may set a different figure, a different test method, or may not yet have one at all. That is precisely why you must ask.

So here is the sequence, and it is the whole lesson.

  1. Before you design the workshop, find the authority. Name it. Find its office.
  2. Ask them what consent an operation of your size and type needs, and what standards attach to it.
  3. Write down what they tell you, with the date and the name of the person.
  4. Then design the workshop around what you will be allowed to discharge, at the scale you will actually be allowed to operate.

Doing it in that order is the difference between a business and a business that gets shut down after it has spent its capital.

Finally, three things you can do on site regardless of where the regulatory conversation lands.

Separate the streams at source. Keeping chrome-bearing, sulphide-bearing and general wash water apart from the start is far cheaper than separating them after they mix, and it is the same rule that keeps hydrogen sulphide out of your workshop, so it earns its keep twice.

Settle the solids. Simple settling before discharge is low-cost and worthwhile, but be realistic: primary treatment alone gives BOD5 removal of 25 to 50 percent, suspended-solids removal of 50 to 70 percent, and oil and grease removal of about 65 percent. Not a substitute for full treatment.

And recover chrome if you chrome tan and the volumes justify it, turning a disposal cost into a partly reusable input.

Water, Solid Waste and the Things That Leave Your Workshop

In this lesson
  • Compare water-use figures correctly by understanding what each one measures
  • Plan for the solid waste streams a tannery produces from day one
  • Treat chrome-bearing solid waste as a hazardous-waste question, not just rubbish

Tanning is a water trade. Every wet process from soaking through washing after tanning uses it, and where you get it and where it goes will shape your workshop more than any machine you buy.

Here are the sourced water figures, and the first skill is reading them correctly, because they look contradictory and are not.

  • A general industrial cleaner-technology reference gives an example conventional process using about 45 cubic metres of water per tonne of hide processed, against below 30 cubic metres per tonne as the mark of a well-managed tannery.
  • A global waste-management study gives traditional-process water use at about 50 cubic metres per tonne, falling to about 20 with water-saving methods, and separately quotes 35 cubic metres of wastewater produced per tonne of raw hide.
  • A chrome-tanning-specific example gives about 50 cubic metres per tonne for wet-salted hides at the pickling and tanning stage alone.
  • A water-footprint analysis, citing a South African Water Research Commission tannery report and a global water-footprint study, gives about 170 to 550 litres per hide, midpoint about 360 litres, for the tanning and finishing stages specifically.

Why do they differ? Because they measure different scopes and different technology tiers. Whole tannery per tonne is not the same as one process stage per tonne, and neither is the same as per hide. When someone quotes you a water figure, your first question is always: per what, and covering which steps.

The consistent, safely teachable lesson across all of them is this. Water use per hide or per tonne varies by a factor of at least two to three between poorly managed and well-managed practice at the same basic technology level. That gap is available to any workshop willing to invest in reuse and better process control. It is not reserved for large industrial tanneries.

Be honest about the limits of these numbers, though. Every one of them is industrial-scale or global and generic. No current small-scale-tannery-specific water-use figure for Africa was retrieved for this course. So measure your own. Put a meter on your supply if you can, or count buckets and drum fills per batch if you cannot. Write it against the batch number. Within a few months you will know your own water use per hide, and you can then work at halving it, which is the achievable range the figures above describe.

Now solid waste, which small workshops consistently forget to plan for.

At industrial scale, per tonne of raw hide processed, the sourced figures are: trimmings roughly 32 to 120 kg depending on processing stage, fleshings 70 to 230 kg, wet-blue splits around 115 kg, and total solid waste of roughly 450 to 600 kg per tonne of raw hide. Read that total again. A large majority of the raw hide's original weight leaves the process as waste or by-product rather than as finished leather.

That is an industrial figure and your profile at small scale will be different. But the streams exist in miniature in every workshop, and two of them, fleshings and hair, putrefy and smell if they are not managed. A workshop that has not decided in advance where its fleshings go will decide it badly, in a hurry, in the heat, in front of its neighbours.

So plan for waste and by-product handling from day one. Decide now: where do fleshings go, where does hair go, where do trimmings and shavings go, how often are they removed, and who removes them.

One stream needs particular care. About half of chrome-tannery solid waste of this kind carries roughly 3 percent chromium on a dry-matter basis. That means chrome-bearing trimmings, shavings and splits are themselves a hazardous-waste management question, not just liquid effluent. Do not burn them, do not let children play near the pile, and ask your environmental authority what disposal route they require, at the same time as you ask about discharge consent.

Finally, energy, briefly. The same industrial reference gives about 439 kWh of electricity and about 108 cubic metres of gas per tonne of raw hide processed. That is an order-of-magnitude figure for an industrial operation and not a budget for you. Its only purpose here is to make one point: tanning heats water, runs drums and runs machinery. It is not a purely manual craft, and your energy cost is a real line in the costing you build in lesson 5, not an afterthought.

One closing thought that ties the module together. Water in, chemicals in, hide in; leather out, effluent out, solid waste out. A workshop that measures only the leather is measuring about a third of what it handles.

Quality Control and Grading

In this lesson
  • Run a checkpoint at every stage rather than one inspection at the end
  • Apply a threshold-based grading system to your own hides
  • Recognise the jump between grades that actually destroys value

Quality control in this trade is not a single inspection at the end. It is a series of checkpoints, and each one traces back to a stage you have already studied. Miss a checkpoint and the defect travels forward, invisibly, until it appears somewhere it cannot be fixed.

Here is the full checkpoint list. Put it on the wall.

At intake: is the cause of the animal's death known and safe? Is the hide free of flay cuts? Is it correctly cured, judged by salt content and ash indicators? Is it free from any smell of putrefaction? The first of those is a life-safety control, not a quality one. Never accept a hide from an animal that died suddenly, was found dead, was not inspected, or whose cause of death is unknown.

Before the beamhouse: is rehydration complete after soaking, and is there any residual salt caking?

Before tanning: is the liming and unhairing even, with no residual hair and no over-limed looseness? Is the pickle pH correct?

After tanning: is the tan even all the way through? Cut a corner and check the colour and penetration to the centre. An untanned raw centre is a serious and unfixable defect. Does the leather behave as its tannage should for wet-heat resistance?

After post-tanning: is the dye colour even? Is the softness and fatliquor feel right for the end use?

After finishing: is the grain classification correct and honestly declared, full-grain, top-grain, corrected or split? Is the thickness right for the intended product?

Six checkpoints. Each takes minutes. Each catches a defect at the stage where it is still cheap.

Now grading, which is how you turn quality into a price.

The worked example in this course is the four-grade system from the ALLPI trainer's manual, and it is used because it is one of the few genuinely African-sourced grading frameworks available in full. Read it as one real example of how a grading system is built, not as the standard every country or buyer uses. Grading criteria are commonly set or adopted at national or trade-association level, so confirm what applies in your own market.

The example thresholds are these.

  • First grade: up to 5 blind warbles in the butt or neck region; few score marks; at most 1 hole in the belly.
  • Second grade: up to 10 open warbles, or up to 20 blind warbles; dung or urine staining up to 30 cm by 30 cm per hind shank; a brand mark located entirely within 18 cm of the hide's perimeter.
  • Third grade: defects covering up to 30 percent of the hide area; a brand mark with any portion falling more than 18 cm from the perimeter.
  • Fourth grade: defects covering up to 50 percent of the hide area.

Reading that table correctly is itself a skill, so read it again and look for the jump.

Between first and second grade you are counting things: warbles, score marks, holes, the size of a stain, where a brand sits. These are small, localised, countable blemishes.

Between second and third grade something different happens. The criterion changes from counting blemishes to a percentage of the hide area lost. Thirty percent, then fifty percent. That is not more defects in the abstract, it is wholesale loss of usable area.

Learn to see that jump on a real hide, not just to recite the percentages. A hide with a scatter of small warbles is a hide you can still cut good panels from. A hide with a third of its area unusable is a different commercial object entirely, and your cutting plan for it must change completely.

Notice also what the brand-mark rule teaches. It is not simply a fixed penalty for a brand. It is weighted by where the brand falls, because a brand within 18 cm of the perimeter costs you edge material, while a brand in the centre destroys the largest and most usable panel of the hide. That is a direct link between an animal-husbandry decision, where the herder places the brand, and the cash value of the hide. If you buy hides regularly, that is a conversation worth having with the people who supply you.

One closing point about who grades. If you grade your own hides at intake and record the grade, you will start to see which suppliers consistently bring you second-grade material and which bring you fourth. That knowledge changes what you pay them and who you buy from, and it is available to you for the price of a notebook and five minutes per hide.

Costing: The Method, and Why You Get No Price

In this lesson
  • Build a cost per unit from raw material, chemicals, labour, capital and yield
  • Gather three dated written quotations for every input from your own market
  • Act on a costing that shows the plan does not pay

This lesson gives you no price. Not one. Let me say why before I say anything else, because the reason is the lesson.

No current African price for raw hides, wet-blue, finished leather, tanning chemicals, small-tannery equipment or leathercraft products was retrieved for the material behind this course. Every price figure that exists in that material is from before 2010 and is used only to show the direction and scale of value gaps, never as a current number. That is the largest single gap in the reference behind this course, and it is stated openly rather than filled with a guess.

A price you cannot verify is worse than no price, because you would plan on it. So instead you get the method, and a way to get real numbers.

The formula is this.

Cost per unit equals raw material cost, plus all chemical and process cost, plus labour, plus a fair share of capital and overhead cost, all divided by units of usable output.

Now work through it line by line.

One. Cost the raw hide or skin correctly, including its curing cost. Salt is a real, budgetable input, not free. Wet salting uses 30 to 40 percent salt on green hide weight, a reduced short-term regime about 25 percent, and dry salting roughly 10 percent less salt than wet salting.

Here is the arithmetic method, and note carefully that the hide weight is an illustrative round number, not a sourced average, because no sourced average African cattle hide green weight was retrieved. If a green hide weighs 25 kg and you cure it at 35 percent salt on green weight, then 25 times 0.35 equals 8.75 kg of salt for that one hide. Now take 8.75 kg to your own salt supplier, get the price, and you have a real curing cost per hide for your own market. The method is transferable. The 25 kg is not; weigh your own hides.

Two. Cost the process chemicals you actually use: lime, sulphide, acid, salt for pickling, the tanning agent whether chrome powder or vegetable extract, and retanning, dyeing and fatliquoring chemicals. The course has given you the structural list of what is used at each step. You must price your own local suppliers' quantities.

Three. Cost labour honestly, including your own time at a real rate. A business plan that forgets to cost the owner's own labour is not a business plan. Every hour of cutting, stitching, edge burnishing, staking and hardware setting from the last module is an hour that must appear here.

Four. Spread capital cost, drums, pits, tools, protective equipment, over its realistic working life to get an annual or per-unit ownership cost. No sourced working-life figure for small-tannery equipment under African conditions was retrieved for this course, so estimate it locally and conservatively. If you think a drum lasts ten years, cost it over seven.

Five. Account for yield loss at every stage. Usable output is always less than raw input, and it must be measured, not assumed. You measured your cutting yield in the last module. Measure the tannery yield too.

Six. Divide total cost by actual usable output, in square feet of finished leather or in finished units of product. Then compare that against what your market will actually pay.

Now the discipline that turns this from arithmetic into a business tool: three dated written quotations for every single input.

Three, because one price tells you nothing about whether it is a good price and two leaves you guessing. Written, because a remembered price changes when you are not in the room. Dated, because in six months you must know whether the figure you planned on is still real. Keep them in a file with the date and supplier on each, and re-quote every input at least once a year and before any large order.

Do the same on the selling side. Walk your market, note what similar products sell for this month, and date what you write down.

And here is the part most people skip. If the costing shows your plan does not cover its costs, that is the most valuable single output of the exercise, not a discouraging result to be smoothed over. Discovering it on paper costs you an afternoon. Discovering it after you have bought the drums costs you the business.

When a costing comes out negative you have four honest moves, and only four. Reduce input cost, by better buying or better yield. Reduce process cost, by using less water, less chemical, less time. Raise price, if the market will bear it and your quality justifies it. Or change the product, to something the same hide and the same hours produce more value from. Doing none of those and starting anyway is the fifth option, and it is the one that empties bank accounts.

Records, Markets and What You Do Next

In this lesson
  • Keep the five record sets a small leather operation actually needs
  • Identify the market facts you must find locally because no document holds them
  • Turn the whole course into a written plan with dated next steps

This is the last lesson of the course, so it does two jobs: it gives you the record system that holds everything together, and it tells you honestly what this course could not tell you and where to go for it.

Start with records. Five sets, all small.

Intake records. Date, source, animal type, confirmation of slaughter or cause of death, curing method and date. That third item is a safety control, not paperwork: it is the record that proves you refused hides from animals that died unexplained, the single control that removes almost the entire anthrax risk from your intake.

Process records. Which tannage was used, chrome or vegetable, and the batch's key parameters: chrome offer or tannin source, pickle pH if measured. A chrome batch and a vegetable batch cannot be finished, dyed or sold the same way, and six weeks later nobody remembers which drum held which.

Grading records. The grade assigned at intake and the grade and type of finished leather produced. Over a year this connects input quality to output value with your own hides. It is the most useful internal dataset a small operation can build, precisely because no outside document can supply it.

Effluent and waste disposal records. What was discharged, when, and where a permit exists, confirmation of compliance. A workshop with no records has no defence if a regulator ever asks.

Cost and sales records, feeding straight into the costing method from the last lesson.

Five books, or five columns in one book. An hour a week. That is the whole system.

Now the markets, and the honest gap.

No sourced current African leather or leathercraft market-price data, demand analysis or export-requirement checklist was retrieved for the material behind this course. So here is what you must go and find, and finding it is your first job after this course ends.

The current farm-gate price for raw and cured hides, the current wet-blue or crust price, and the current finished-leather and craft product price, in your own district, this month. These prices move by margins large enough to justify real investment in quality: a projected 1.75 million US dollars a year in Kenya from a 5-percentage-point wet-blue grading improvement, and up to a 20 percent premium at Sagana Tannery for top-grade wet-blue. Both are old figures showing direction only, never current prices. You need your own numbers to act on that lesson.

Whether a national hide, skin or leather grading and certification scheme exists in your country, and whether the four-grade example in this course lines up with it or differs.

Your regulatory requirements: business licensing, the environmental discharge consent from lesson 2, and if you sell internationally, the importing country's chemical-content requirements. Some export buyers require private certification standards even where no domestic law does. The Leather Working Group's audit protocol, for example, requires finished leather to test below 3 ppm hexavalent chromium to the ISO 17075 method at an accredited laboratory, and defines chrome-free leather as under 1,000 mg per kg total chromium on dry weight to BS EN 15987. Those are a private certification's thresholds adopted voluntarily by brands, not a law anywhere, and your own country's legal limit, if one exists, must come from your national standards body.

And whether a premium market exists locally or for export for vegetable-tanned, chrome-free or clean-tannery leather. Vegetable tanning's real costs, the time and the colour limits, are partly offset by a genuine safety and environmental advantage. Whether that commands a price premium in any African market was not sourced for this course, and if the answer in your market is yes, it is the strongest position a small vegetable tannery can hold.

Now, the course itself, in one page.

Africa holds roughly 523 million head of cattle, sheep and goats, about 16.7 percent of the world total, yet produces only 113 million hides and skins against a world total of 1,059 million, a 10.7 percent share. That gap, between the share of the animals and the share of the hides, was the first lesson of this course and it is the last one too. It is not a shortage of animals. It is hides lost to poor flaying, poor curing, disease and weak collection before value can be added, and value given away by selling at the bottom of the ladder.

Every module has been one rung of that ladder. Live animal. Green hide. Cured hide. Pelt. Tanned crust. Finished leather. Finished product. You now know what happens at each rung, what is lost there, and what it is worth to hold on one rung longer.

What this course could not give you is your own numbers. Your hide area. Your yield. Your water use per batch. Your hours per product. Your local prices. Every one of those has been named as something you must measure, and every one of them is measurable with a notebook, a gauge, a scale and a watch.

Go and measure them. That is the work.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Why is physical separation of alkaline and acid processes better than a written procedure alone?

A procedure depends on a person doing the right thing on a particular day. A shared drum, channel or low point in the floor is a standing structural hazard regardless of how careful anyone is.

2. What should you draw first when planning a tannery layout?

If both liquor streams could end up in the same place through a spill, an overflow or a floor slope, the design is not finished, regardless of how the vessels are arranged.

3. Why does this course give no equipment prices?

It is a flagged gap. The stage-by-stage item list is real structure you can use, but the numbers must come from three dated quotations in your own market.

4. Why is ventilation a design decision rather than a housekeeping one?

The hazard exists in normal running, not only during accidents, so the answer is open air or genuine cross-ventilation designed in, never an enclosed pit room with the door shut.

5. What advantage does vegetable tanning give a small workshop at the layout stage?

Vegetable tanning is slower, more limited in colour and lower in wet-heat resistance, but it takes the sulphide-acid and chromium hazards largely off the floor plan. Its effluent is still a high-BOD organic load that must be managed.

6. What are the four main components of tannery wastewater?

Each traces to a step: organic load from soaking, liming and fleshing; salt from curing and pickling; sulphide from unhairing; chromium from chrome tanning.

7. What is the correct status of Kenya's NEMA in this course?

Every country has its own equivalent body, and its numeric standards differ. Assuming another country's standard applies to you is exactly the error this lesson exists to prevent.

8. France, Italy and India all set a chromium discharge limit of 2 mg/l. What does that tell you about your own limit?

Those are foreign national figures quoted only as an example of the format of such a regulation. Your country may set a different figure, a different test method, or none yet, which is why you must ask.

9. When should a learner contact the environmental authority?

Doing it in that order is the difference between a business and a business that gets shut down after spending its capital. Write down what they say, with the date and the name of the person.

10. Why is separating effluent streams at source worth doing twice over?

One decision serves both worker safety and effluent management, which is why stream separation is the first on-site action worth taking regardless of where the regulatory conversation lands.

11. Two sources give tannery water use as 45 m3 per tonne and 360 litres per hide. Why do they differ?

Whole-tannery per tonne, one process stage per tonne, and per hide are three different measurements. The figures are not contradictory once you know what each is counting.

12. What is the consistent lesson across all the water figures?

Reuse and better process control close that gap, and they are not reserved for industrial operations. Measuring your own use per batch is the first step to halving it.

13. Why does a workshop need to decide where fleshings go before it opens?

The solid waste streams exist in miniature in every workshop even at a fraction of industrial scale, and two of them rot fast. Planning them is a day-one decision, not an afterthought.

14. Why are chrome-tannery trimmings and shavings a special case?

Chrome waste is not only a liquid effluent problem. Do not burn it, keep it away from people, and ask your environmental authority what disposal route it requires.

15. What is the purpose of the 439 kWh and 108 m3 gas per tonne energy figures?

They are industrial order-of-magnitude figures. Their only job is to stop a learner treating tanning as a purely manual craft with no energy cost in the costing.

16. What separates second grade from third grade in the worked example?

First and second grade count warbles, scores, holes and stain size. Third grade allows defects covering up to 30 percent of the area, which is a wholesale loss of usable panel, not just more blemishes.

17. Why does the example grading system weight a brand mark by its location?

It is a direct link between an animal-husbandry decision and the hide's cash value, and it is worth discussing with the people who supply your hides.

18. What is the correct check after tanning?

A raw centre means the tanning agent never reached the middle of the fibre weave. No amount of finishing corrects it, and it will rot from the inside.

19. Which intake check is a life-safety control rather than a quality one?

A hide from an animal that died suddenly or uninspected can carry anthrax, which can kill a person who never went near the animal. Refusing such a hide costs nothing but the refusal.

20. What does grading your own intake hides and recording the grade give you?

It costs a notebook and five minutes a hide, and it is information about your own supply chain that no outside document can give you.

21. Why does this course give you no price to plan on?

Every price in the source material predates 2010 and is used only to show the direction and scale of value gaps. The honest replacement is the costing method plus three dated quotations from your own market.

22. A green hide weighs 25 kg and is cured at 35 percent salt on green weight. How much salt?

25 times 0.35 equals 8.75 kg. The arithmetic method transfers; the 25 kg does not, because no sourced average African hide green weight exists in this material, so weigh your own.

23. Why three quotations, written and dated?

Re-quote every input at least once a year and before any large order, and do the same on the selling side by dating what your market actually pays.

24. Why must the owner's own labour be costed?

Unpaid owner time makes a losing plan look like a winning one, and the shortfall comes silently out of the owner's own income month after month.

25. Your costing shows the plan does not cover its costs. What are the honest options?

Discovering the shortfall on paper costs an afternoon; discovering it after buying the drums costs the business. Only those four moves change the arithmetic honestly.

26. Why is the intake record of an animal's cause of death a safety control rather than paperwork?

Anthrax spores persist for years on untreated hides and a documented case shows transmission from an African goat hide to a person who never went near the animal. Refusing suspect hides costs only the refusal.

27. What is the correct status of the Leather Working Group's 3 ppm hexavalent chromium threshold?

It is tested to ISO 17075 at an accredited laboratory as part of a voluntary audit protocol. Any legal limit in your own country must come from your national standards body.

28. What does the gap between Africa's 16.7 percent share of livestock and 10.7 percent share of hides represent?

523 million head against 113 million pieces of a world total of 1,059 million. It is a preventable loss, which is precisely why hide improvement, curing and collection were taught before any chemistry.

29. Which record set connects input quality to output value over time?

Over a year it becomes the most useful internal dataset a small operation owns, precisely because it is built from your own hides in your own market and no outside document can supply it.

30. What did this course deliberately not supply, and what must the learner do about it?

Every one of those was named through the course as something to measure rather than to look up, because no document written elsewhere can hold the numbers of your own workshop and your own market.

Module 12 capstone

Write the plan for your own workshop, using the whole course, and take it to two people who can tell you it is wrong. Step 1: draw your workshop on one page, marking where alkaline and sulphide processes happen, where acid processes happen, where they drain to, and the physical separation between them. If you cannot draw the separation, you cannot build the workshop. Step 2: identify your national environmental authority by name, find out what consent an operation like yours needs before it discharges anything, and write down the name, the office and what they told you. Do this before you spend money on equipment. Step 3: list every capital item and every recurring input, traced to the process stage that needs it, and get three dated written quotations for each from your own market. Step 4: measure your own numbers, hide area, cutting yield, hours per product, water used per batch, because no course can supply them. Step 5: build your cost per unit using the method in lesson 5, including your own labour at a real rate and capital spread over a realistic working life. Step 6: compare that cost against what your market actually pays, which you found by walking it. Step 7: if it does not cover its costs, do not smooth it over. Change the plan. Discovering that on paper is the most valuable thing this course can do for you. Step 8: set up the record book from lesson 6 and enter your first batch on the day you open.
