1. Module 7 Lesson 1: Reading the African Forge Fire and Fuel Choices

Welcome to Module 7 of your Knife Blacksmith training. By now you have learned to shape steel, to grind bevels, and to fit handles. In this module we move into the heart of the craft: the fire itself. A blacksmith without fire is just a person holding a hammer. In Africa, the forge has always been a living thing, fed by charcoal, shaped by breath, and read by eye. This first lesson teaches you to understand your fuel, your fire, and the signals it gives you before you ever strike hot metal. In many African traditions, from the Nubian forges of the Nile to the Maasai smiths of East Africa and the Yoruba ironworkers of West Africa, the smith is also a keeper of the fire. The fire is not merely a tool. It is a partner. Your first task is to choose your fuel wisely. Charcoal remains the most common fuel across the continent because it burns hot, clean, and predictable. Hardwood charcoal made from acacia, mukwa, or ebony produces intense heat with little smoke once fully lit. Softwood charcoal burns faster and cooler, better for gentle heating or annealing. In some regions, smiths use dried cow dung or maize cobs as starter fuel, but these create ash and uneven heat, so they are best used only to begin a fire, not to forge a blade. Coal is rare in many parts of Africa, but where it is available, it burns hotter and longer than charcoal. However, coal contains sulfur and other impurities that can damage steel if the fire is not managed carefully. If you use coal, keep your steel above the oxidizing zone and never let it touch the burning coal directly. Gas forges are becoming more common in cities, and they offer precise control, but they lack the soul and the subtle feedback of a solid fuel fire. For traditional work, charcoal is your teacher. Building the fire is a ritual. Start with dry tinder, add small kindling, then gradually add charcoal. Do not dump a large pile of charcoal on a small flame. Feed the fire like you feed a child: little by little, with patience. Arrange your charcoal in a mound or a trench, depending on the shape of your forge. A trench fire concentrates heat in a line, good for heating long blades. A mound fire gives a wider hot spot, good for general forging. Your air source matters as much as your fuel. In rural Africa, many smiths use a bag bellows made from goat skin, or a pot bellows with clay nozzles. These are efficient and repairable. A hand crank blower or an electric fan can also work, but you must learn to control the blast. Too much air cools the fire by blowing heat away and burns fuel too fast. Too little air starves the fire and creates smoke. The sweet spot is a steady, gentle blast that makes the coals glow bright orange without sparks flying everywhere. Learn to read the fire by color. A dark red fire is around 600 degrees Celsius, too cold for forging most steels. A cherry red is around 800 degrees, good for annealing. An orange fire is around 900 to 1000 degrees, perfect for forging. A yellow fire is around 1100 to 1200 degrees, good for welding but risky for burning thin steel. A white fire is above 1300 degrees and will destroy your blade in seconds. Your goal is to keep your steel in the orange range for most forging tasks. Watch the steel, not just the fire. As you heat a blade, look for the shadow line that moves along the metal. That shadow tells you the steel is heating through. When the steel reaches a uniform orange, it is ready to hammer. If you see sparks shooting from the steel, you are burning it. If the steel turns bright yellow or white, pull it out immediately and let it cool slowly in ash. Never quench a burnt blade in water, as it may crack. In Africa, smiths often work in open-air forges under a tree or in a simple shelter. Wind can change your fire in an instant. Build a windbreak from mud, stone, or corrugated iron. Keep your water bucket and your quench tank nearby, but not so close that steam or splashing water hits the fire. Keep your anvil close to the forge so you lose less heat when moving the steel. Every second out of the fire is a second of lost heat. Practice this lesson by building three different fires: one with charcoal only, one with charcoal and a little coal, and one with a gas forge if available. Heat a piece of scrap steel in each and note how the fire behaves. Write down the colors you see and the time it takes to reach orange. This journal will become your personal fire map. In the next lesson, we will learn how to control the fire for different stages of blade work, from forging to normalizing to hardening. But for now, master the basics. Feed your fire with respect. Read its colors. Listen to its breath. The fire will teach you everything else. Remember, in the African forge, the smith does not command the fire. The smith converses with it. Your hammer is only as good as your fire. So tend it well, and it will reward you with blades that sing.

2. Module 7 Lesson 2: Fire Control for Forging, Normalizing, and Annealing

In Lesson 1 you learned to build and read your fire. Now you must learn to control it. A blacksmith who cannot control the fire is like a drummer who cannot keep a beat. The fire must be your instrument, and you must play it with precision. In this lesson we will cover three critical stages of heat treatment that happen at the forge: forging, normalizing, and annealing. Each requires a different fire condition, and each will make or break your blade. Let us begin with forging. Forging is the shaping of steel with hammer and anvil while the metal is hot. The ideal forging temperature for most carbon steels is between 850 and 1050 degrees Celsius. That is a bright orange to a light yellow color in the fire. At this temperature, the steel is plastic and moves under the hammer without cracking. If you forge too cold, the steel becomes brittle and may develop micro-cracks that will later become breaks. If you forge too hot, you burn the steel, which destroys the grain structure and makes the blade weak. To achieve the right forging heat, you need a fire that is hot but not wild. Build a mound of charcoal and use a gentle, steady blast of air. Place your steel in the hottest part of the fire, just above the air inlet, but not touching the burning coal. Rotate the steel every few seconds so it heats evenly. Watch for the shadow line to disappear. When the steel is a uniform orange, pull it out and hammer it. Work quickly. You have maybe thirty to sixty seconds before the steel cools below forging temperature. If you need more time, reheating is fine, but do not hammer cold steel. After forging, you must normalize the blade. Normalizing is a heat treatment that refines the grain structure and relieves internal stresses. It involves heating the steel to a temperature above its critical point, usually around 800 to 850 degrees Celsius, and then letting it cool in still air. In the fire, this means bringing the steel to a cherry red to orange color, then removing it from the fire and setting it aside on a dry, non-flammable surface. Do not quench it. Do not blow air on it. Let it cool naturally. For many African smiths, normalizing is done three times. Heat to orange, cool to black, repeat. This cycle makes the steel tougher and more predictable. The fire for normalizing should be slightly cooler than for forging. You want a steady, even heat, not a roaring blast. A trench fire works well because it gives a long, even hot zone. If you are normalizing a long blade, you may need to move the blade back and forth through the fire to heat the entire length evenly. Do not let one part get hotter than another. Uneven heating causes warping and stress. Annealing is the softest heat treatment. It makes the steel as soft as possible so you can file, grind, and drill it easily. Annealing requires heating the steel to its critical temperature, then cooling it very slowly. In a modern shop, you might use a kiln or vermiculite. In a traditional African forge, you can anneal by burying the hot steel in dry wood ash, sand, or lime. The ash insulates the steel and allows it to cool over several hours, sometimes overnight. The fire for annealing is the same as for normalizing: cherry red to orange. But the key is the cooling, not the heating. Do not rush it. If you pull the steel out of the ash too soon, it will cool too fast and become harder than you want. Patience is the smith's greatest tool. Now let us talk about fire control techniques. The first technique is air control. Your bellows or blower should have a way to adjust the blast. A simple leather flap or a wooden valve can work. Practice making the fire go from a gentle glow to a bright orange without creating a shower of sparks. The second technique is fuel placement. Do not pile charcoal high in the center. Instead, build a ring or a trench so the air can flow evenly. The third technique is the use of water. A small spray of water on the coals can temporarily cool the fire, but too much water will kill it. Use water sparingly. The fourth technique is the use of a damper. If your forge has a chimney or a hood, a damper can control how much air is drawn through the fire. This is useful for maintaining a steady temperature. In Africa, many smiths work with a simple ground forge and a bag bellows. They control the fire by the rhythm of their pumping. Slow, steady pumps give a gentle heat. Fast, hard pumps give a fierce heat. Learn to feel the fire through your bellows. The resistance of the leather, the sound of the air, the glow of the coals, all tell you what the fire is doing. Practice this lesson by taking a piece of scrap steel and putting it through all three stages. Forge it into a simple shape, then normalize it three times, then anneal it in ash. Test the hardness with a file after each stage. You will feel the difference. The forged steel is hard and springy. The normalized steel is tougher. The annealed steel is soft and easy to cut. This is the power of fire control. In the next lesson, we will focus on hardening and tempering, the two most critical steps for making a knife that holds an edge. But without the skills you learn here, hardening will fail. So take your time. Control your fire. And let the steel tell you what it needs.

3. Module 7 Lesson 3: Hardening and Tempering African Blades

You have forged your blade, normalized it, and annealed it. Now comes the moment of truth: hardening and tempering. These two steps transform a soft piece of steel into a knife that can cut, chop, and hold an edge. In Africa, hardening and tempering have been practiced for centuries, often with simple tools and deep knowledge. The smiths of the Congo, the Zulu, the Tuareg, and the Ethiopian highlands all developed methods to harden steel using fire, water, oil, and sometimes urine or plant juices. In this lesson, you will learn the principles behind these methods and how to apply them safely and effectively. Hardening, also called quenching, is the process of heating steel to its critical temperature and then cooling it rapidly. The rapid cooling traps the carbon in a hard, brittle structure called martensite. The critical temperature for most carbon steels is around 750 to 850 degrees Celsius, which is a cherry red to bright orange color. You must heat the entire blade evenly to this temperature. If one part is hotter than another, the blade will warp or crack during quenching. Use a trench fire or a wide mound fire to heat long blades evenly. Move the blade back and forth through the fire. Watch the color. When the blade is a uniform cherry red, with no dark spots, it is ready. Now you must quench. The quenching medium can be water, oil, brine, or even air, depending on the steel. Water is the fastest quench and produces the hardest steel, but it also causes the most stress and can crack thin blades. Oil is slower and safer, producing a slightly softer but tougher blade. Brine, which is salt water, is faster than water and can produce a very hard edge, but it is also very risky. In Africa, many smiths use water from a river or a well, sometimes with a little ash or clay mixed in to slow the quench slightly. Others use palm oil or groundnut oil. The choice depends on the steel and the desired result. For a typical carbon steel blade, I recommend warm oil. Heat the oil to about 50 to 60 degrees Celsius. It should be warm to the touch but not smoking. Hold the blade by the tang with tongs. plunge the edge into the oil first, then the spine. Move the blade in a slight slicing motion to break the vapor jacket that forms around the steel. Keep the blade in the oil until it is completely cool, about ten to fifteen seconds. Do not pull it out early. If you do, the steel will not harden fully. After quenching, test the hardness with a file. A hard blade will skate across the file. A soft blade will bite. If the blade is not hard enough, you can reheat and re-quench, but you must first normalize it again to relieve stress. Now comes tempering. Hardened steel is too brittle to use. It will chip or break under stress. Tempering is the process of reheating the hardened blade to a lower temperature, usually between 150 and 250 degrees Celsius, to reduce brittleness while keeping most of the hardness. In a traditional African forge, tempering is often done by placing the blade on a hot piece of iron or in a bed of hot sand. You can also use an oven or a kiln. The color of the steel tells you the temperature. As you heat the blade, watch for the oxide colors. A pale straw color appears around 200 degrees Celsius. A brown color appears around 220 degrees. A purple color appears around 250 degrees. A blue color appears around 300 degrees. For a knife, you want a straw to brown color for the edge, and a blue color for the spine. This gives you a hard edge and a tough back. To achieve this, you can use a technique called differential tempering. Heat the spine of the blade with a torch or a hot iron while keeping the edge cool. The colors will run from the spine to the edge. Stop when the edge reaches a straw color. Then quench the whole blade in water to stop the tempering process. This is a skill that takes practice. In Africa, some smiths temper by sticking the blade into a yam or a potato and heating the spine. The moisture in the vegetable keeps the edge cool. Others use a wet cloth wrapped around the edge. The principle is the same: keep the edge hard, make the spine tough. After tempering, let the blade cool slowly in air. Do not quench it again. Then clean the blade with sandpaper or a stone to remove the oxide colors. You will see the bright steel underneath. Test the edge by cutting a piece of soft wood or rope. A well tempered blade will cut cleanly and hold its edge. If the edge rolls or chips, you may need to adjust your tempering temperature. Practice this lesson with several blades. Make notes on the steel type, the quenching medium, the temperature, and the results. Every steel is different. Every fire is different. But with patience and observation, you will learn to harden and temper with confidence. Remember, the African smith does not rely on fancy equipment. The smith relies on knowledge, experience, and a deep connection to the fire and the steel. In the next and final lesson of this module, we will discuss finishing, sharpening, and maintaining your blade, as well as the ethics and safety of the forge. But for now, focus on the quench and the temper. These are the steps that turn a piece of metal into a knife.

4. Module 7 Lesson 4: Finishing, Sharpening, Safety, and the Smith's Code

You have forged, normalized, annealed, hardened, and tempered your blade. Now it is time to finish it. Finishing is not just about making the knife look good. It is about making it work well, last long, and serve its owner with pride. In Africa, a knife is more than a tool. It is a companion, a status symbol, and sometimes a spiritual object. The smith who finishes a blade with care honors the user and the craft. In this final lesson of Module 7, we will cover grinding and polishing, sharpening, handle finishing, safety in the forge, and the ethical code of the African blacksmith. Let us start with grinding and polishing. After tempering, your blade will have a rough surface, oxide colors, and maybe some scratches from forging. You need to remove these to create a clean, smooth blade. Use a file or a belt grinder to refine the bevels. Work from coarse to fine. Start with a coarse file or a 120 grit belt, then move to 220, 400, 800, and finally 1200 or higher for a polished finish. Always grind with the edge facing away from you, or use a jig to hold the blade safely. Keep the blade cool by dipping it in water frequently. If the steel turns blue or purple, you are overheating it and ruining the temper. For a traditional African finish, you might leave some forge marks on the spine and flats, while polishing the bevels to a mirror shine. This contrast is beautiful and shows the hand of the smith. You can also etch the blade with acid to bring out the hamon, the visible line between the hard edge and the soft spine. In Africa, some smiths use lemon juice or vinegar to etch. Others use a paste made from mud and plant ash. Experiment with local materials. Next, sharpening. A sharp knife is a safe knife. A dull knife requires more force and is more likely to slip. To sharpen, start with a coarse stone, around 400 grit. Hold the blade at a consistent angle, usually 15 to 20 degrees per side. Push the edge across the stone as if you are slicing a thin layer off the top. Do this ten to twenty times per side. Then move to a finer stone, around 1000 grit, and repeat. Finally, use a leather strop with a polishing compound to remove the burr and refine the edge. Test the edge by slicing paper or shaving arm hair. If it catches and cuts cleanly, it is sharp. If it slides, keep working. In Africa, many smiths use a simple water stone found in riverbeds. These stones are often sandstone or slate. They work well if you keep them flat and wet. Now, handle finishing. Your handle should be comfortable, secure, and beautiful. Sand it smooth with progressively finer grits. Then apply a finish. Traditional African finishes include palm oil, beeswax, shea butter, and animal fat. These penetrate the wood and protect it from moisture. For a more durable finish, you can use a mixture of beeswax and linseed oil. Apply it warm and buff it with a cloth. If you use horn or bone for the handle, polish it with fine sandpaper and a cloth wheel. Make sure the handle is securely attached to the tang. Use pins, rivets, or epoxy. Test it by tapping the handle on a hard surface. It should not move or rattle. Safety in the forge is not optional. It is a duty. You must protect your eyes, your lungs, your ears, and your skin. Always wear safety glasses or a face shield when grinding, chipping, or quenching. Wear a leather apron, leather gloves, and closed toe shoes. Tie back long hair and avoid loose clothing. Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand nearby. Never quench hot steel in a plastic bucket, as it can melt and spill. Use a metal bucket or a clay pot. Keep your work area clean and free of clutter. Good ventilation is essential. Charcoal fires produce carbon monoxide, a deadly gas. Work outdoors or in a well ventilated shelter. Never use a charcoal forge inside a closed room. Listen to your body. If you are tired, take a break. If you are angry, put down the hammer. Fatigue and frustration cause accidents. Finally, let us talk about the smith's code. In many African cultures, the blacksmith is a respected figure, sometimes feared, sometimes revered. The smith is a keeper of secrets, a shaper of metal, and a servant of the community. Your code should be simple. Make tools that serve. Do not make weapons for harm. Teach others what you know. Respect your materials. Waste not. A good smith is humble. A good smith is patient. A good smith never stops learning. When you sell a knife, stand behind it. If it breaks, fix it. If it dulls, sharpen it. Your reputation is your greatest asset. In this module, you have learned to read the fire, control the fire, harden and temper, and finish a blade. You have learned the African way: simple tools, deep knowledge, and a connection to the earth and the ancestors. Now go to your forge. Build a fire. Heat a piece of steel. And make something that will last. The fire is waiting. The anvil is waiting. Your hands are ready. This is the way of the knife blacksmith. This is your path. Walk it with pride.

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-weight:bold; color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING FORGE FUMES AND BURNS</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;">Charcoal and wood fires produce carbon monoxide, a colourless, odourless gas that kills without warning. Never forge inside a closed room, a tin-roofed shed without cross-ventilation, or a dwelling. Work outdoors or under an open-sided shelter with airflow across the fire. Carbon monoxide poisoning causes headache, dizziness, and confusion before collapse if you feel these symptoms, move to fresh air immediately and seek medical help.</p>
<p style="margin:0;">Also: wear leather gloves and eye protection when hammering hot steel; keep a bucket of water or sand within arm's reach; and never quench a blade in an open container of oil near a flame. Quench oil can flash. Use a metal lid to smother an oil fire never water on burning oil.</p>

<p><strong>Business takeaway:</strong> Consistency is what separates a smith who sells one knife to a tourist from one who supplies a lodge, a butcher, or an agricultural cooperative with twenty knives a month. Write the actual temperature or colour you used on every blade. When a customer orders a repeat, you can deliver the same edge.</p>

<h3>Shop records: the ledger that pays for itself</h3>

<p>Module 7 Lesson 4 introduced the Smith's Code. Records are how you keep it. A simple exercise book costs almost nothing and turns your craft into a business. Record at minimum:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Blade log:</strong> date, steel source, forging colours used, quench temperature, temper cycles, final hardness test (file or brass rod), and a sketch or photo.</li>
<li><strong>Fuel log:</strong> supplier, weight per sack, price, and number of blades produced per sack. This is your true cost per blade.</li>
<li><strong>Labour log:</strong> hours per blade, including fire lighting, forging, grinding, and finishing. Price your work at a living wage, not at what a competitor charges.</li>
<li><strong>Customer log:</strong> name, contact, knife type, price, and feedback. Repeat customers are your cheapest sales channel.</li>
<li><strong>Failure log:</strong> every cracked blade, failed quench, or returned knife, with the cause. This is the most valuable page in the book.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Business takeaway:</strong> With three months of records you can quote a price with confidence, identify your most profitable knife model, and drop the models that lose money. Without records you are guessing, and guessing is how workshops close.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li>Fire lit outdoors or in a cross-ventilated shelter never in a closed room.</li>
<li>Fuel weighed and price recorded before the first blade of the day.</li>
<li>Blade steel identified or tested on a scrap piece before forging.</li>
<li>Forging, normalizing, hardening, and tempering temperatures checked against Table 2 and your own colour chart.</li>
<li>Quench oil covered with a metal lid nearby; water bucket and sand within arm's reach.</li>
<li>Gloves, eye protection, and leather apron worn for all hot work.</li>
<li>Every blade logged with steel, heat treatment, and result before it leaves the anvil.</li>
<li>Weekly review of fuel cost per blade and hours per blade adjust prices before margins erode.</li>
</ol>
