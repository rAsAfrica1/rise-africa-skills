1. Building a copper pot still from locally available materials

To build a copper pot still, you need a copper pot (or stainless steel if copper is unavailable), a copper pipe for the lyne arm, and a condenser. In many African markets, you can find copper sheets at scrap metal dealers or plumbing supply shops. A 20-liter pot is a good size for beginners. First, ensure the pot has a tight-fitting lid. Cut a hole in the lid to fit a copper pipe (typically 1/2 inch diameter). Solder or weld the pipe into the lid, ensuring an airtight seal. The pipe should extend about 30 cm above the lid, then angle downward at about 45 degrees. This is your lyne arm. For the condenser, you can use a copper coil (worm) inside a bucket of cold water, or a shotgun condenser made from a larger pipe with inner tubes. Connect the lyne arm to the condenser. Use food-grade silicone or flour paste to seal joints. Never use lead solder. Test for leaks by filling the pot with water and running a small amount of air through; look for bubbles. If leaks, reseal. Finally, clean the still by running a vinegar-water solution (1:1) and discarding the first run. This removes flux and impurities. With basic tools like a hacksaw, drill, and soldering iron, you can build a functional pot still for under $100 using locally sourced materials. Remember, safety first: always work in a well-ventilated area and never leave a still unattended.

2. Using a column still for higher proof spirits

A column still, also called a reflux still, allows you to produce higher proof spirits (up to 95% ABV) in a single run. Unlike a pot still, it has a vertical column packed with material (like copper mesh or marbles) that increases surface area for repeated vaporization and condensation. To build a simple column still, attach a tall copper pipe (at least 60 cm) to your pot. Inside, pack it loosely with copper scrubbers or glass marbles. At the top, add a condenser. The key is reflux: as vapor rises, it cools and falls back down, reboiling and concentrating alcohol. You control reflux by adjusting heat and cooling water flow. For higher proof, run slowly and maintain a steady temperature around 78-80Â°C. Collect the distillate in small jars. You'll notice the first run may still have off-flavors; a second run through the column will yield a cleaner, stronger spirit. In African settings, you can use a modified pressure cooker or a stainless steel keg as the boiler. Connect a copper column with a shotgun condenser. Use a gas burner for precise heat control. Monitor vapor temperature with a thermometer at the top of the column. Aim for 78Â°C for hearts. If temperature rises above 85Â°C, you're into tails. With practice, you can achieve 85-90% ABV. Always dilute to drinking strength (40%) before consumption. Column stills are efficient but require more attention to cuts. They are ideal for making vodka, gin, or neutral spirits for liqueurs.

3. Heating sources: firewood, gas, and electric elements for African settings

Choosing the right heat source depends on availability, cost, and control. Firewood is the most accessible in rural Africa. Build a simple three-stone fire or a metal stove. Use dry, hard wood for steady heat. However, firewood gives uneven heat and is hard to control; you'll need to adjust the fire by adding or removing wood. For better control, use a gas burner (LPG). A single-burner camping stove works well for small stills. Gas provides instant heat adjustment, crucial for making cuts. In urban areas, gas is often available. Electric elements are cleanest but require reliable electricity. A 2-3 kW immersion heater or hot plate can be used. If electricity is unstable, a gas backup is wise. For a 20-liter still, you need about 1.5-2 kW of heat. With firewood, you'll use about 5-10 kg of wood per run. With gas, a 5 kg cylinder lasts for 3-4 runs. Electric elements are efficient but may cause scorching if not stirred. Always use a heat diffuser (a metal plate) between the flame and the pot to prevent scorching. For safety, never use a open fire indoors; carbon monoxide kills. In all cases, have a fire extinguisher or sand bucket nearby. Insulate your still with a blanket or clay to retain heat. The goal is a steady, gentle boil, not a rolling boil. Adjust heat to maintain a slow drip from the condenser.

4. Condenser design: worm coils vs. shotgun condensers

The condenser turns vapor back into liquid. Two common designs are the worm coil and the shotgun condenser. A worm coil is a long copper tube (3-5 meters) coiled inside a bucket of cold water. Vapor travels through the coil and condenses. It's simple and cheap to build. Use 1/4-inch or 3/8-inch copper tubing. Coil it around a bottle or pipe to make a spiral. Place in a bucket with cold water and ice. The water must be changed or circulated to stay cold. Worm coils work well for pot stills but can cause smearing (mixing of cuts) if too long. A shotgun condenser is a larger pipe (2-3 inches diameter) with multiple smaller tubes inside. Vapor flows through the small tubes while cold water flows around them in the shell. This design is more efficient and gives sharper cuts. You can build one with a copper pipe, end caps, and several smaller tubes soldered in place. Water flows in at the bottom and out at the top. Shotgun condensers are compact and require less water. For African settings, a worm coil in a bucket is easier to make with local materials. However, if you can weld, a shotgun condenser is superior. In both, ensure the condenser is mounted so that distillate flows out and is collected. Use food-grade materials. Clean the condenser after each run by flushing with hot water and vinegar. A dirty condenser can impart off-flavors. Always have a steady flow of cold water; if water is scarce, recirculate from a large drum with ice.

5. Making cuts: foreshots, heads, hearts, and tails

Making cuts means separating the distillate into fractions based on when they come off the still. The first fraction is foreshots: the first 50-100 ml from a 20-liter run. These contain methanol and other toxic compounds. Discard them or use as fire starter. Never drink. Next come heads: the next 10-15% of the run. Heads contain acetone and other volatile compounds that cause headaches and harsh flavors. They smell like nail polish remover. Collect heads separately; you can redistill them later. The hearts are the middle fraction, about 60-70% of the total alcohol. This is the clean, smooth spirit you want. Hearts start when the distillate tastes and smells clean, and the temperature stabilizes around 78Â°C. Collect hearts in small jars, numbered, so you can blend. Tails come last, after the temperature rises above 85Â°C. Tails contain fusel oils, which are oily and smell like wet cardboard. They can be redistilled. To make cuts, use your senses: smell, taste (dilute with water), and feel. Also use a alcoholmeter to measure proof. Hearts are usually between 60-80% ABV for pot stills. Collect in 200-500 ml jars. After the run, blend jars to taste. Keep hearts separate. In Africa, many traditional distillers make cuts by experience. Practice with small runs. Always discard foreshots. A good rule: if it smells bad, don't drink it. With practice, you'll learn to identify the hearts by the sweet, clean aroma.

6. Cleaning and maintaining your still between runs

Cleaning your still after each run prevents buildup that can cause off-flavors and health hazards. First, let the still cool completely. Disassemble all parts: pot, column, condenser, and any tubing. Rinse with warm water to remove residue. For copper parts, use a solution of vinegar and salt (1 cup vinegar, 1 tablespoon salt per liter of water) to remove tarnish and verdigris. Scrub with a soft brush. Rinse thoroughly with clean water. For stubborn stains, use a paste of lemon juice and baking soda. Never use harsh chemicals like bleach, as they can leave toxic residues. For the condenser, flush with hot water and vinegar to remove any buildup. If you have a worm coil, run a mixture of vinegar and water through it, then rinse. Dry all parts with a clean cloth and let them air dry completely before reassembling. Store in a dry place. Before the next run, do a cleaning run: distill a mixture of water and vinegar (1:1) and discard the distillate. This removes any remaining impurities. Also check for leaks and replace any worn gaskets or seals. Use food-grade lubricant on joints if needed. In humid African climates, copper can corrode quickly, so dry thoroughly. A clean still produces better spirits and lasts longer. Set a routine: after every run, clean; before every run, do a cleaning run. This takes 30 minutes but saves hours of frustration and ensures safety.

4. Condenser Design: Worm Coils vs. Shotgun Condensers

The condenser converts vapour back to liquid. Two designs dominate small-scale African production.

Worm coil: a coiled copper pipe immersed in a trough of cold water. Cheap, easy to fabricate, and effective for pot stills up to ~100 L. Requires large water volume and manual water changes.

Shotgun (shell-and-tube) condenser: multiple parallel tubes inside a water jacket. Far more efficient, compact, and consistent â but requires precision fabrication and a reliable water flow. Best for column stills and higher-throughput operations.

5. Making Cuts: Foreshots, Heads, Hearts, Tails

Cutting is where product quality and profitability are won or lost. During a run, the distillate is collected in fractions:

  • Foreshots (first 1â3%): methanol, acetone, aldehydes. Discard or use as solvent/fire starter. Never sell.
  • Heads (next 15â20%): sharp, solventy, hangover-causing. Recycle into the next run.
  • Hearts (middle 55â65%): the sellable spirit. Collect at 60â75% ABV for pot stills.
  • Tails (final 15â20%): oily, cardboardy. Recycle into next stripping run for extra yield.

Business impact: a poorly cut 50 L run can lose 20â30% of sellable hearts or produce a product that damages brand reputation. Use a parrot's beak with an alcoholmeter, track temperature, and taste-dilute every 200 ml sample.

6. Cleaning and Maintaining Your Still Between Runs

Consistency is a commercial asset. A dirty still produces off-flavours and shortens equipment life. After each run:

  1. Rinse the pot, swan neck and condenser with warm water immediately â before residue hardens.
  2. Use a citric acid solution (2 tbsp per litre) to remove copper oxide (verdigris) inside the vapour path.
  3. Never use steel wool on copper â it scratches and embeds iron, causing off-flavours.
  4. Flush the worm coil or shotgun tubes with clean water and blow through with air to prevent mould.
  5. Inspect gaskets, clamps and solder joints monthly for leaks â vapour leaks are both a fire and yield risk.
  6. Log every run: date, wash volume, ABV, cuts, yield. This data drives pricing and process improvement.
