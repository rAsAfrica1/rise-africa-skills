1. Inspecting and Cleaning the Frame

Start by placing the frame on a clean, flat surface like a wooden pallet or a sturdy table. You need good lightwork outdoors or use a bright LED lamp. Put on safety glasses and gloves. First, check for any bends, cracks, or deep rust. Look closely at the steering head tube, the swingarm pivot area, and the rear shock mounts. A cracked frame is dangerous; if you find one, set it aside and get a replacement. For minor rust, use a wire brush or sandpaper (80-grit then 120-grit) to remove it. In humid coastal areas like Mombasa or Lagos, rust spreads fast, so treat any bare metal immediately with a rust converter like phosphoric acid. Next, clean the frame with a degreaser. Mix dish soap with warm water if you don't have a dedicated degreaser. Use a stiff nylon brush to scrub away dirt, oil, and grease. Pay attention to welds and tight corners where grime hides. Rinse with clean water and dry with a cloth. Then wipe down with acetone or methylated spirits to remove any residue. This step is critical because paint and threadlocker won't stick to oily surfaces. After cleaning, inspect all threaded holes. Run a tap (M6, M8, M10) through them to clear paint or rust. If a thread is stripped, you can install a Helicoil insert. Finally, check the frame's alignment by placing a straight edge along the main tube. Any gap greater than 2 mm means the frame is bent. For African workshops, a simple plumb bob and string can check vertical alignment. Once clean and straight, apply a thin coat of primer if you plan to paint, or leave bare for assembly. Always store the frame on a padded surface to avoid scratches.

2. Mounting the Frame on Assembly Jig

An assembly jig holds the frame steady while you install heavy components. You can build a simple jig using square steel tubes (40x40 mm) welded to a base plate. The jig must be level; use a spirit level to check both directions. If you don't have a welder, bolt together wooden beams (100x100 mm) with M12 bolts. Place the frame onto the jig so the steering head and swingarm pivot are supported. The frame should not rock. Use C-clamps or U-bolts to secure it. For a typical EV motorbike frame, the steering head tube should be about 800-900 mm from the ground, and the swingarm pivot about 300-350 mm. Measure these heights with a tape measure. If your jig is adjustable, set these dimensions. Next, check that the frame is horizontal side-to-side. Place a spirit level across the top tubes. If it's off, shim the low side with metal plates. Once level, tighten all clamps. Now you can test fit the swingarm and rear shock. The jig should allow you to remove and install parts without the frame moving. In many African workshops, a jig made from scrap metal and a wooden pallet works fine. Just ensure it's stable. After mounting, mark the jig with a paint pen for future frames. This saves time on the next build. Remember, a wobbly frame leads to misaligned wheels and poor handling. Take 10 minutes to get it right.

3. Installing Steering Head Bearings

The steering head bearings let the front fork turn smoothly. Most EV motorbikes use tapered roller bearings. You need a bearing driver, a hammer, and grease. First, clean the steering head tube inside. Use a rag with solvent. Then apply a thin layer of high-temperature grease to the inside of the tube. Take the lower bearing cone (the inner race) and press it onto the steering stem using a pipe or bearing driver. Tap evenly until it seats fully. You'll hear a change in sound. Next, pack the bearing with grease. Work grease into the rollers by hand. For the upper bearing, place it into the top of the head tube. Then insert the steering stem through the head tube. Install the upper cone and dust seal. Thread on the adjusting nut. Tighten it by hand until snug, then use a spanner to turn it another 1/8 to 1/4 turn. You want zero play but no binding. Check by turning the fork side to side; it should move smoothly with slight resistance. If it feels gritty, the bearings are not seated or are damaged. In dusty African conditions, use sealed bearings if available. Otherwise, pack extra grease. After adjustment, install the lock nut and tighten it while holding the adjusting nut. Torque the lock nut to 25-30 Nm. Recheck play after tightening. A common mistake is overtightening, which causes flat spots and dangerous handling. Test ride at low speed to confirm.

4. Attaching Swingarm and Rear Suspension

The swingarm connects the rear wheel to the frame. Start by greasing the swingarm pivot bolt and the bearings inside the swingarm. Use a waterproof grease like lithium complex. Slide the swingarm into the frame's pivot brackets. Insert the pivot bolt from the left side. Apply threadlocker (blue Loctite) to the bolt threads. Thread on the nut and tighten to 60-70 Nm for a typical M14 bolt. Do not overtighten; you'll crush the bearings. Check that the swingarm moves up and down freely without side play. If it's tight, the bearings are misaligned. Next, install the rear shock absorbers. Most EV bikes have twin shocks. Mount the top eye to the frame and the bottom eye to the swingarm. Use new mounting bolts and nuts. Torque the shock bolts to 40-45 Nm. For the shock preload, set it to the middle position for a rider of 70-80 kg. In Africa, roads can be rough, so you might increase preload by 2 clicks. Check that the shock springs don't rub on the swingarm or frame. Finally, install the rear wheel temporarily to check alignment. The wheel should be centered between the swingarm legs. Use a string line from the front wheel to the rear wheel to check tracking. Adjust the chain adjusters if needed. After everything is tight, bounce the rear suspension a few times. Listen for clunks. If you hear any, something is loose. Recheck all bolts.

5. Front Fork Installation and Alignment

Front forks hold the wheel and absorb bumps. Begin by sliding the fork tubes into the triple clamps. The lower triple clamp holds the tubes; the upper clamp secures them. Leave the upper clamp bolts loose for now. Push the tubes up until they protrude about 10-15 mm above the upper clamp. This height affects steering. For most EV motorbikes, 10 mm is a good starting point. Tighten the lower clamp bolts to 20-25 Nm. Then tighten the upper clamp bolts to 20-25 Nm. Do not overtighten; you can crack the aluminum clamp. Next, install the front wheel. Slide the axle through the fork dropouts and wheel hub. Tighten the axle nut to 50-60 Nm. Check that the wheel spins freely. Now check alignment. Stand in front of the bike and look at the fork tubes. They should be parallel. If one tube is forward or backward, loosen the clamps and adjust. Use a straight edge against the fork tubes to check. Also, ensure the handlebar is straight. With the wheel pointing forward, the handlebar should be perpendicular to the frame. Adjust the handlebar clamps if needed. Finally, check the fork oil. Most forks need 150-200 ml of 10W fork oil per leg. If you don't know the history, drain and refill. In dusty conditions, change fork oil every 5000 km. After installation, compress the forks a few times to settle the springs. Then recheck all bolts.

6. Torque Specifications for Chassis Fasteners

Torque is the tightness of a bolt, measured in Newton-meters (Nm). Too loose, parts fall off. Too tight, bolts break or threads strip. You need a torque wrench. The click-type is best for beginners. Always use the correct size socket. For chassis fasteners on an EV motorbike, here are common specs: Steering stem lock nut: 25-30 Nm. Triple clamp bolts (M8): 20-25 Nm. Swingarm pivot bolt (M14): 60-70 Nm. Rear shock bolts (M10): 40-45 Nm. Front axle nut (M12): 50-60 Nm. Rear axle nut (M14): 80-90 Nm. Motor mount bolts (M10): 45-50 Nm. Battery box bolts (M8): 15-20 Nm. Always clean threads and apply threadlocker (blue) unless specified dry. Tighten in a crisscross pattern for multiple bolts, like on a wheel. After torquing, mark the bolt and nut with a paint pen so you can see if it loosens. Recheck torque after the first 50 km and then every 1000 km. In African heat, metal expands, so torque can change. Keep a torque chart in your workshop. If you don't have a torque wrench, tighten until snug plus a quarter turn for small bolts, but this is risky. Save up for a good wrench; it's cheaper than replacing a stripped frame. Always use a calibrated wrench. Store it at its lowest setting.

<h2>Practical Reference: Frame and Chassis Preparation for EV Motorbike Assembly</h2>

<p>In the African e-mobility market, the frame and chassis are the backbone of every electric motorbike. Whether you are assembling boda-bodas in Kampala, delivery bikes in Nairobi, or commuter EVs in Lagos, the quality of your chassis preparation directly determines vehicle safety, battery longevity, and customer trust. A poorly prepared frame leads to vibration-induced electrical faults, premature bearing failure, and costly warranty claims. This practical reference gives workshop technicians and assembly line supervisors the exact procedures, torque values, and inspection criteria needed to prepare frames to professional standards using tools and methods appropriate for African workshop conditions.</p>

<h3>1. Inspecting and Cleaning the Frame</h3>
<p>Before any component is mounted, the frame must undergo a three-stage inspection: visual weld check, dimensional check, and surface preparation. In African workshops, frames often arrive after long overland transport, coated in dust, rust-preventive oil, or occasionally with transit damage. Use a bright LED torch to inspect all welds at the steering head, swingarm pivot, and rear shock mounts. Any crack, porosity, or deep scratch on a load-bearing weld is grounds for rejection never attempt to weld-repair a frame without manufacturer approval.</p>
<p>Clean the frame with a degreaser (isopropyl alcohol or a water-based alkaline cleaner) and a lint-free cloth. Pay special attention to threaded holes and bearing seats. After cleaning, apply a thin anti-corrosion film to unpainted surfaces if the bike will not be assembled within 48 hours. In humid coastal cities like Mombasa or Dar es Salaam, this step is critical.</p>

<h3>2. Mounting the Frame on Assembly Jig</h3>
<p>The assembly jig holds the frame in a known reference position so that steering geometry, swingarm alignment, and rear wheel tracking can be set accurately. Clamp the frame at the swingarm pivot and the lower frame rails using rubber-padded clamps to avoid scratching the paint. Verify that the jig is level using a spirit level or digital inclinometer a 1 error at the jig translates to measurable misalignment at the wheels.</p>
<p>Once clamped, re-check the steering head angle against the manufacturer's specification. For most EV motorbikes, the steering head angle is between 24 and 28 from vertical. Record the reading in your assembly log.</p>

<h3>3. Installing Steering Head Bearings</h3>
<p>Steering head bearings are the most safety-critical bearings on the motorcycle. Install the lower bearing race first using a bearing driver or a brass drift, ensuring it seats fully and squarely. Pack the bearings with high-quality lithium-based grease in dusty African conditions, use a sealed bearing where the manufacturer permits. Torque the steering stem nut to the specified preload, then check for smooth rotation with no binding or play. The handlebar should fall freely to either side under its own weight when tapped.</p>

<h3>4. Attaching Swingarm and Rear Suspension</h3>
<p>The swingarm pivot bolt must be torqued with the suspension at normal ride height (wheels on the ground or jig set to ride height). Tightening at full droop can preload the bearings and cause stiction. Apply thread-locking compound (medium strength, e.g. Loctite 243) to the pivot bolt threads. Check that the swingarm moves freely through its full travel without contacting the frame or battery box a common issue on converted EV frames where the battery enclosure reduces clearance.</p>
<p>Rear shock absorbers should be mounted with the correct bushings and torqued to specification. In African conditions, consider upgrading to shocks with dust seals if the bike will operate on unpaved roads.</p>

<h3>5. Front Fork Installation and Alignment</h3>
<p>Slide the fork tubes into the triple clamps with the correct fork protrusion above the upper clamp (typically 515 mm, per manufacturer). Tighten the lower clamp bolts first to 2025 Nm, then the upper clamp bolts. Check fork alignment by installing the front axle and spinning the wheel the wheel should spin freely without lateral runout. Misaligned forks cause uneven tyre wear and poor braking, a major issue on African roads where potholes are common.</p>
<p>Set fork oil level and spring preload according to the rider weight and intended use (delivery bikes often need stiffer springs). Bleed the forks by pumping them several times before final tightening.</p>

<h3>6. Torque Specifications for Chassis Fasteners</h3>
<p>Use the torque table above as a workshop reference, but always prioritise the specific OEM service manual for your frame model. Torque values vary with thread pitch, lubrication, and fastener material. In African workshops, the most common error is over-tightening small bolts (stripping threads) and under-tightening large structural bolts (causing fatigue failure). Mark each torqued fastener with a paint pen after final tightening this creates a visual audit trail and prevents missed bolts during quality control.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Frame inspection:</strong> Check all welds with LED torch reject any cracked or porous weld at steering head, swingarm pivot, or shock mounts.</li>
<li><strong>Cleaning:</strong> Degrease frame with isopropyl alcohol; apply anti-corrosion film if assembly is delayed &gt;48 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Jig mounting:</strong> Clamp at swingarm pivot and lower rails; verify jig level within 0.5 using spirit level.</li>
<li><strong>Steering bearings:</strong> Seat races fully, pack with NLGI 2 lithium grease, preload stem nut to 1525 Nm, check for zero play.</li>
<li><strong>Swingarm:</strong> Torque pivot bolt to 6070 Nm at ride height with thread lock; verify full travel without frame contact.</li>
<li><strong>Front forks:</strong> Set correct protrusion, tighten lower clamp first (2025 Nm), then upper; spin wheel to check alignment.</li>
<li><strong>Torque audit:</strong> Use calibrated torque wrench on all chassis fasteners; mark each with paint pen after tightening.</li>
<li><strong>Final check:</strong> Re-inspect all fasteners after first 50 km of customer use retorque if any show movement.</li>
</ol>
