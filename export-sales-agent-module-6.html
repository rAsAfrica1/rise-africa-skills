1. Understanding Export Sales in the African Context

Welcome to Module 6 of the Export Sales Agent training program. This module focuses on the practical realities of selling goods and services across African borders, with an emphasis on the unique opportunities and challenges that define the continent's trade landscape. As an export sales agent, your primary role is to connect producers, manufacturers, and service providers with buyers in foreign markets. In Africa, this role carries particular weight because intra-African trade remains relatively low compared to other regions, yet the potential is enormous. The African Continental Free Trade Area, or AfCFTA, has created a single market of over 1.3 billion people with a combined GDP exceeding 3 trillion US dollars. This agreement reduces tariffs on the vast majority of goods and aims to eliminate non-tariff barriers over time. For you as an export sales agent, this means that markets which were once difficult to access due to customs duties and complex regulations are becoming more open. However, the AfCFTA is still being implemented, and the reality on the ground varies from country to country. Your job is to navigate these variations and find the best opportunities for your clients. Let us begin by understanding what export sales actually involves. Export sales is not simply finding a buyer and shipping goods. It is a comprehensive process that includes market research, buyer identification, negotiation, financing, logistics, compliance, and after-sales support. In the African context, each of these steps has specific characteristics. For example, market research in Africa requires understanding local preferences, purchasing power, distribution channels, and informal networks. In many African countries, a significant portion of trade happens through informal channels, and formal contracts may be less common than in Europe or North America. This does not mean that you should avoid formal agreements, but it does mean that relationship-building is critical. Trust is the currency of African trade. Before you can sell to a buyer in another country, you often need to invest time in getting to know them, their business, and their community. This can involve multiple visits, sharing meals, and demonstrating a genuine commitment to the relationship. Another key aspect is understanding the regulatory environment. Each African country has its own import regulations, standards, and documentation requirements. For instance, exporting to Nigeria requires a Form M and a Certificate of Capital Importation, while exporting to Kenya may require a Certificate of Conformity from the Kenya Bureau of Standards. As an export sales agent, you must be able to guide your clients through these requirements or connect them with customs brokers and freight forwarders who can handle the paperwork. You also need to be aware of regional economic communities such as ECOWAS in West Africa, SADC in Southern Africa, EAC in East Africa, and COMESA in Eastern and Southern Africa. These communities have their own trade protocols and preferential tariff arrangements. Knowing which community a country belongs to can help you identify opportunities for duty-free or reduced-duty access. For example, a product manufactured in a SADC member state may enter another SADC member state with lower tariffs than a product from outside the region. This is a powerful selling point that you can use when approaching buyers. Now, let us consider the types of products and services that are in demand across Africa. Agricultural products such as cocoa, coffee, cashew nuts, sesame seeds, and shea butter are traditional exports from Africa to the rest of the world. But intra-African trade in agricultural products is also growing. For example, Kenya exports tea and flowers to other African countries, while South Africa exports maize, wine, and processed foods to its neighbors. Manufactured goods such as textiles, clothing, footwear, and household items are also traded within Africa. Services such as logistics, construction, education, and healthcare are increasingly exported as well. As an export sales agent, you might work with a client who produces organic honey in Tanzania and wants to sell it in Rwanda, or a client who provides software development services in Ghana and wants to serve clients in Nigeria. The key is to identify where your client's product or service has a competitive advantage and where there is unmet demand. One of the most important skills you will develop is the ability to assess market potential. This involves looking at factors such as population size, income levels, urbanization, and consumer trends. For example, a country with a growing middle class and a young population may be a good market for affordable consumer goods. A country with a large agricultural sector may need farm machinery, irrigation equipment, and fertilizers. A country with a booming construction industry may need building materials, plumbing supplies, and electrical components. You can gather this information from sources such as the World Bank, the African Development Bank, national statistics offices, and trade promotion organizations. You should also talk to people on the ground, including business associations, chambers of commerce, and other exporters who have experience in the market. Another critical element of export sales in Africa is logistics. The continent's infrastructure varies widely. Some countries have excellent ports, roads, and railways, while others have poor infrastructure that can cause delays and increase costs. For example, landlocked countries such as Uganda, Rwanda, and Burkina Faso rely on neighboring countries' ports for their imports and exports. This means that goods must travel through multiple borders, each with its own customs procedures. As an export sales agent, you need to understand these logistics challenges and plan for them. You may need to advise your client on the best routes, modes of transport, and packaging to ensure that goods arrive in good condition and on time. You should also consider the cost of freight, insurance, and handling, and factor these into the final price. In some cases, it may be more cost-effective to export to a nearby country by road than to a distant country by sea. In other cases, air freight may be necessary for perishable or high-value goods. Financing is another area where export sales agents add value. Many African buyers may not have the cash flow to pay upfront, and many sellers may not have the working capital to offer credit. You can help bridge this gap by introducing your client to trade finance instruments such as letters of credit, documentary collections, and export credit insurance. You can also explore opportunities through development finance institutions such as the African Export-Import Bank, which provides financing and guarantees for intra-African trade. Understanding these options can make the difference between a deal that closes and one that falls apart. Finally, let us discuss the mindset you need to succeed as an export sales agent in Africa. You must be patient, persistent, and adaptable. Deals may take longer to close than you expect. Regulations may change. Political situations may shift. But if you are committed to building long-term relationships and providing real value to your clients and their buyers, you will find that Africa is a continent of immense opportunity. In the lessons that follow, we will dive deeper into specific aspects of export sales, including market research, buyer negotiation, documentation, logistics, and financing. Each lesson will provide practical tools and examples that you can apply immediately in your work. By the end of this module, you will have a solid foundation for success in African export sales.

2. Conducting Market Research for African Export Markets

In this lesson, we focus on the critical first step of any successful export sale: market research. Without a clear understanding of your target market, you risk wasting time, money, and effort on deals that will never materialize. In the African context, market research requires a blend of desk research, field visits, and networking. Let us start with desk research. This involves gathering information from published sources such as government reports, industry publications, trade statistics, and online databases. Key sources include the International Trade Centre's Trade Map, which provides detailed export and import data by country and product. The World Bank's Doing Business reports, now replaced by the Business Ready reports, offer insights into the regulatory environment. The African Development Bank publishes country profiles and sector studies. National statistics offices and central banks provide data on population, GDP, inflation, and trade. Trade promotion organizations such as the International Trade Centre and national export promotion agencies can also provide market guides and buyer lists. When conducting desk research, you should aim to answer several key questions. What is the size of the market? What is the demand for your client's product or service? Who are the competitors? What are the prices? What are the import regulations and tariffs? What are the distribution channels? What are the cultural and consumer preferences? For example, if your client produces packaged fruit juice, you need to know how much fruit juice is consumed in the target country, who the main suppliers are, whether local production exists, what the retail price is, and whether there are any import restrictions or standards. You also need to understand the retail landscape. In many African countries, modern retail such as supermarkets is growing but still accounts for a minority of sales. Traditional markets, kiosks, and informal vendors dominate. This means that your client's product may need to be priced and packaged differently for different channels. A premium product in a supermarket may not sell in a traditional market where price sensitivity is higher. Field research is equally important. Whenever possible, you should visit the target market to see things for yourself. Talk to potential buyers, visit retail outlets, attend trade fairs, and meet with industry associations. Trade fairs are particularly valuable because they bring together buyers, sellers, and service providers in one place. Major African trade fairs include the Intra-African Trade Fair, the Lagos International Trade Fair, the Nairobi International Trade Fair, and the SAITEX exhibition in South Africa. At these events, you can meet buyers face-to-face, showcase your client's products, and learn about market trends. When you visit a market, pay attention to details. What products are on the shelves? What are their prices? How are they packaged? What languages are on the labels? What are the payment methods? Are there any products from your client's country already? If so, how are they performing? If not, why not? These observations can provide valuable insights that you cannot get from reports. Networking is another essential component of market research. In Africa, personal connections often open doors that formal channels cannot. You should build relationships with business associations, chambers of commerce, diaspora communities, and other exporters. These contacts can provide introductions to buyers, share their experiences, and warn you about potential pitfalls. For example, a fellow exporter who has sold to a particular buyer can tell you whether that buyer pays on time, whether they are difficult to work with, and what their preferences are. You should also consider using local consultants or market research firms. They can conduct surveys, mystery shopping, and focus groups on your behalf. However, be careful to choose reputable firms and to verify their findings. In some cases, local consultants may have their own biases or may not have the expertise you need. Always cross-check their information with other sources. Once you have gathered data, you need to analyze it and draw conclusions. A useful tool is the SWOT analysis, which examines strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats. For your client's product, what are the strengths? For example, unique quality, competitive price, or reliable supply. What are the weaknesses? For example, high shipping costs, lack of brand recognition, or limited production capacity. What are the opportunities? For example, growing demand, new trade agreements, or gaps in the market. What are the threats? For example, strong competitors, changing regulations, or political instability. You should also assess the competitive landscape. Who are the main competitors? What are their prices, quality, and distribution strategies? How does your client's product compare? If your client's product is not competitive, you need to either find a niche where it can compete or advise your client to adjust the product, price, or packaging. Another important analysis is the cost structure. You need to calculate the landed cost of your client's product in the target market. This includes the ex-works price, packaging, inland transport, export documentation, customs clearance, freight, insurance, import duties, port charges, inland transport in the destination country, and any applicable taxes. Once you know the landed cost, you can determine a competitive selling price. If the landed cost is too high, you may need to find ways to reduce it, such as consolidating shipments, negotiating better freight rates, or sourcing inputs more cheaply. You should also consider the payment terms and financing costs. If you offer credit to the buyer, you need to factor in the cost of that credit. If you use a letter of credit, you need to account for bank charges. These costs can eat into your client's margins, so it is important to be transparent about them from the beginning. Finally, market research is not a one-time activity. Markets change, and you need to keep learning. You should regularly review your assumptions, update your data, and adjust your strategy. For example, a new competitor may enter the market, a new trade agreement may reduce tariffs, or a change in government policy may affect demand. By staying informed, you can help your client seize opportunities and avoid risks. In the next lesson, we will discuss how to identify and approach potential buyers based on your market research.

3. Identifying and Approaching Buyers in African Markets

Once you have completed your market research, the next step is to identify and approach potential buyers. This is where the rubber meets the road. In Africa, buyer identification requires a mix of formal and informal methods. Let us start with formal methods. Trade directories and databases are useful starting points. The International Trade Centre's Trade Map and Market Access Map can help you identify importing companies. National importers' associations, chambers of commerce, and industry federations often publish member directories. Online platforms such as Alibaba, TradeIndia, and Africa Trade Network list buyers and sellers. However, you should verify the legitimacy of any buyer you find online. Scams are common, and you do not want to waste your client's time or money on a fraudulent deal. Trade fairs and exhibitions are excellent venues for meeting buyers. As mentioned in the previous lesson, events such as the Intra-African Trade Fair, the Lagos International Trade Fair, and the Nairobi International Trade Fair attract thousands of buyers and sellers. At these events, you can meet buyers face-to-face, show samples, and discuss terms. You should prepare in advance by studying the list of exhibitors and attendees, scheduling meetings, and bringing plenty of business cards and product samples. Another formal method is to work with trade promotion organizations. Many African countries have export promotion agencies that can provide lists of potential buyers, organize trade missions, and offer financial support for market entry. For example, the Nigerian Export Promotion Council, the Kenya Export Promotion and Branding Agency, and the South African Department of Trade, Industry and Competition offer such services. You should also consider using commercial officers at embassies and consulates. They can provide market intelligence, make introductions, and advocate on your behalf. Now let us discuss informal methods. In Africa, personal networks are often the most effective way to find buyers. You should tap into your own network, as well as your client's network. Ask for referrals from business associates, friends, family, and diaspora communities. Diaspora communities are particularly valuable because they have connections in both their home country and their country of residence. For example, a Nigerian diaspora member in South Africa may know importers in both countries. You can also attend industry events, seminars, and workshops where you can meet people who work in your target sector. Sometimes, the best introductions come from unexpected places. A taxi driver, a hotel receptionist, or a fellow traveler may know someone who is looking for your client's product. Always be open to conversations and always carry business cards. When you identify a potential buyer, you need to approach them professionally. The first contact is usually by email or phone. Your email should be concise, personalized, and focused on the value you can offer. Do not send a generic mass email. Instead, research the buyer's business and mention something specific about their company. For example, you might say that you noticed they import a certain product and that your client can offer a competitive alternative with better quality or price. You should also attach a company profile, product catalogue, and price list. Keep the attachments small and in common formats such as PDF. If you are calling, be prepared to introduce yourself and your client in less than a minute. Be polite, clear, and confident. If the buyer is not interested, ask if they know anyone else who might be. If they are interested, ask for a meeting or a video call. In many African cultures, it is important to establish a personal connection before discussing business. You may need to spend some time asking about the buyer's family, health, or general well-being. This is not just small talk; it is a way of building trust. You should also be respectful of hierarchy and titles. Use appropriate titles such as Mr, Mrs, Dr, or Chief, and address the buyer formally until they invite you to use their first name. When you meet a buyer in person, dress professionally, arrive on time, and bring samples if possible. Be prepared to answer questions about your client's product, production capacity, quality control, packaging, pricing, delivery times, and payment terms. You should also be ready to negotiate. In African markets, negotiation is often expected, and the first price you quote may be met with a counteroffer. Do not be offended by this; it is part of the process. However, you should know your bottom line and not go below it. You should also be clear about what you can and cannot offer. For example, if your client cannot offer credit, say so upfront. It is better to lose a deal than to promise something you cannot deliver. Another important aspect of approaching buyers is understanding their needs and pain points. What problems are they trying to solve? Are they looking for a cheaper supplier, a more reliable supplier, a higher quality product, or a unique product? By understanding their needs, you can tailor your pitch to address them. For example, if a buyer is frustrated with late deliveries from their current supplier, you can emphasize your client's track record of on-time delivery. If a buyer is looking for a product that meets a specific standard, you can provide documentation showing that your client's product meets that standard. You should also be aware of the competition. The buyer may already have suppliers, and you need to give them a reason to switch. This could be a lower price, better quality, faster delivery, more flexible payment terms, or additional services such as marketing support or after-sales service. You should also consider offering a trial order or a sample so that the buyer can test the product before committing to a large order. When you are approaching buyers in multiple countries, you need to prioritize. Not all buyers are equal. Some may be more serious, more creditworthy, or more strategic than others. You should focus your efforts on the buyers who offer the best potential for long-term business. You can use a simple scoring system to rank buyers based on factors such as order size, payment history, growth potential, and strategic fit. Finally, remember that building a relationship with a buyer takes time. You may need to follow up multiple times before you get a response. Do not give up after one or two attempts. However, do not be a nuisance. Space out your follow-ups and always provide value in each communication. For example, you might send a market update, a new product specification, or a success story from another market. By being persistent and professional, you will eventually win the buyer's trust and business. In the next lesson, we will cover the documentation and logistics involved in executing an export sale.

4. Export Documentation and Logistics in Africa

In this lesson, we turn to the operational side of export sales: documentation and logistics. These are the areas where deals can fall apart if not handled properly. In Africa, documentation requirements vary by country and by product, but there are some common documents that you will encounter frequently. Let us start with the commercial invoice. This is a document that describes the goods, their quantity, unit price, total value, and the terms of sale. It is used by customs authorities to assess duties and taxes. The commercial invoice should include the names and addresses of the seller and buyer, the date, the invoice number, a detailed description of the goods, the country of origin, the port of loading and discharge, the terms of delivery such as FOB, CIF, or EXW, and the payment terms. You should ensure that the information on the invoice matches the information on other documents, such as the packing list and bill of lading. The packing list is another essential document. It lists the contents of each package, including the number of items, weight, and dimensions. This helps customs officials and freight handlers to identify and handle the goods. The bill of lading is a contract between the shipper and the carrier. It serves as a receipt for the goods, a document of title, and a contract of carriage. There are different types of bills of lading, including ocean bills of lading, air waybills, and road waybills. The type you use depends on the mode of transport. For ocean freight, you will need an ocean bill of lading. For air freight, you will need an air waybill. For road freight, you will need a road waybill or a CMR document. The certificate of origin is a document that certifies the country where the goods were produced. It is often required to claim preferential tariff treatment under trade agreements such as the AfCFTA or regional economic communities. For example, if you are exporting from a SADC country to another SADC country, you may need a SADC certificate of origin to benefit from reduced tariffs. The certificate of origin must be issued by an authorized body, such as a chamber of commerce or a customs authority. Other documents that may be required include phytosanitary certificates for agricultural products, health certificates for food and pharmaceuticals, certificates of conformity for electrical goods and toys, and import licenses or permits. You should always check the specific requirements of the destination country before shipping. You can find this information from the destination country's customs authority, embassy, or trade promotion organization. You can also use online tools such as the International Trade Centre's Market Access Map, which provides information on import regulations and required documents. Now let us discuss logistics. Logistics involves the planning, execution, and control of the movement and storage of goods, services, and information from the point of origin to the point of consumption. In the African context, logistics can be challenging due to poor infrastructure, border delays, and informal practices. However, with proper planning, you can overcome these challenges. The first decision you need to make is the mode of transport. The main options are sea, air, road, and rail. Sea freight is the most common for bulk cargo and is generally the cheapest, but it is also the slowest. Air freight is the fastest but the most expensive. Road freight is flexible and can reach landlocked countries, but it can be affected by poor road conditions and border delays. Rail freight is efficient for certain routes, such as the TAZARA railway between Tanzania and Zambia, but it is not available everywhere. When choosing a mode of transport, you should consider the nature of the goods, the distance, the cost, the speed, and the reliability. For perishable goods such as fresh produce, air freight may be necessary. For non-perishable goods such as textiles, sea freight may be sufficient. For urgent shipments, air freight may be worth the extra cost. You should also consider the total landed cost, not just the freight cost. Sometimes, a cheaper freight option may end up costing more due to delays, damage, or additional handling. Once you have chosen the mode of transport, you need to select a freight forwarder. A freight forwarder is a company that arranges the transportation of goods on behalf of the shipper. They can handle booking, documentation, customs clearance, and insurance. A good freight forwarder can save you time and money, while a bad one can cause delays and losses. You should choose a forwarder with experience in your target market and a good reputation. Ask for references and check their track record. You should also get multiple quotes and compare not just the price but also the service level. In some cases, you may need to use a customs broker. A customs broker is a licensed professional who handles customs clearance on behalf of the importer or exporter. In many African countries, customs procedures are complex and require local knowledge. A customs broker can help you navigate these procedures, avoid delays, and ensure compliance. You should include the cost of customs brokerage in your landed cost calculation. Another important aspect of logistics is packaging. Goods must be packaged in a way that protects them during transport and handling. For sea freight, goods may be exposed to moisture, heat, and rough handling. For air freight, goods may be exposed to pressure and temperature changes. You should use appropriate packaging materials, such as pallets, crates, cartons, and shrink wrap. You should also label packages clearly with the buyer's name, address, and any handling instructions. In some cases, you may need to comply with specific packaging regulations, such as those for hazardous materials. Insurance is also crucial. Goods can be lost, damaged, or stolen during transit. You should advise your client to take out cargo insurance to protect against these risks. The cost of insurance is usually a small percentage of the value of the goods, but it can save your client from a major loss. You should also understand the terms of delivery, such as Incoterms. Incoterms are standard trade terms that define the responsibilities of the seller and buyer for the delivery of goods. For example, under EXW, the seller makes the goods available at their premises, and the buyer is responsible for all costs and risks from that point. Under CIF, the seller is responsible for the cost of goods, insurance, and freight to the destination port. Under DDP, the seller is responsible for delivering the goods to the buyer's premises and paying all duties and taxes. You should choose the Incoterm that best suits your client's capabilities and the buyer's preferences. For new exporters, it is often safer to use terms such as FOB or CIF, where the seller's responsibilities end at the port of loading or destination. Finally, you should track your shipments and communicate regularly with the buyer and the freight forwarder. Delays can happen, and it is important to keep the buyer informed. If there is a problem, such as a customs hold or a missed connection, you should work with the forwarder to resolve it as quickly as possible. Good communication can turn a potential crisis into a demonstration of your reliability. In the next lesson, we will discuss financing and payment methods for export sales in Africa.

5. Financing and Payment Methods for African Export Sales

In this final lesson of Module 6, we address one of the most critical aspects of export sales: financing and payment. Without proper financing and payment mechanisms, even the best deal can turn into a loss. In Africa, where access to credit can be limited and currency volatility can be high, it is essential to understand the available options and how to use them effectively. Let us start with payment methods. The most common methods are cash in advance, letters of credit, documentary collections, open account, and consignment. Cash in advance is the safest method for the seller because the buyer pays before the goods are shipped. However, many buyers, especially in Africa, may be unwilling or unable to pay in advance. It ties up their working capital and exposes them to the risk that the seller may not deliver. Therefore, cash in advance is usually only used for small orders or for first-time transactions with unknown buyers. Letters of credit, or LCs, are a widely used method in international trade. An LC is a bank's promise to pay the seller on behalf of the buyer, provided that the seller presents the required documents within a specified time frame. The documents typically include the commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin, and inspection certificate. LCs reduce the risk for both parties: the seller is assured of payment if they comply with the terms, and the buyer is assured that payment will only be made if the documents are in order. However, LCs can be expensive and complex. Banks charge fees for issuing, advising, and confirming LCs, and the documentation requirements can be strict. A single discrepancy can delay payment or cause the LC to be rejected. Therefore, you should carefully review the LC terms before shipment and ensure that all documents are accurate and complete. Documentary collections are another option. In this method, the seller ships the goods and sends the documents to their bank, which forwards them to the buyer's bank. The buyer's bank releases the documents to the buyer only after the buyer pays or accepts a bill of exchange. There are two types: documents against payment, where the buyer must pay immediately, and documents against acceptance, where the buyer agrees to pay at a future date. Documentary collections are cheaper than LCs but riskier for the seller because the buyer may refuse to pay or accept the documents. If that happens, the seller may have to arrange for the goods to be returned or sold to another buyer, which can be costly. Open account is a method where the seller ships the goods and invoices the buyer, who pays at a later date, typically 30, 60, or 90 days. This is the most convenient method for the buyer but the riskiest for the seller. It is usually only used with trusted buyers with a good payment history. To mitigate the risk, the seller can use export credit insurance, which covers the risk of non-payment due to commercial or political reasons. Consignment is a method where the seller ships goods to the buyer, but the buyer only pays after the goods are sold. This is common in some African markets for consumer goods. It is very risky for the seller because they retain ownership of the goods until they are sold, and they may not be paid if the goods do not sell. It should only be used with highly trusted partners and with a clear agreement on how the goods will be stored, marketed, and accounted for. In addition to these traditional methods, there are newer digital payment platforms that are gaining traction in Africa. Mobile money services such as M-Pesa, MTN Mobile Money, and Airtel Money are widely used for domestic transactions, and some are being adapted for cross-border payments. Fintech companies such as Flutterwave, Paystack, and Chipper Cash offer APIs that enable businesses to send and receive payments across African countries. These platforms can be faster and cheaper than traditional banking channels, but they may have limits on transaction size and may not be suitable for large B2B transactions. You should research the options available in your target market and consider whether they can be integrated into your payment process. Now let us discuss financing. As an export sales agent, you may need to help your client access financing to produce goods for export, cover the costs of shipping and insurance, or offer credit to buyers. There are several sources of export financing in Africa. Commercial banks are the most common source. They offer pre-shipment finance, which provides working capital to produce or purchase goods for export, and post-shipment finance, which provides funds after shipment while waiting for payment. They also offer export credit guarantees and letters of credit. However, commercial banks in Africa may be reluctant to lend to small and medium-sized exporters due to perceived risk and lack of collateral. You may need to help your client prepare a strong business plan and financial statements to secure financing. Development finance institutions such as the African Export-Import Bank, the African Development Bank, and the International Finance Corporation provide financing and guarantees for export trade. Afreximbank, for example, has a trade finance program that supports intra-African trade and has a special focus on small and medium-sized enterprises. You can explore these options and connect your client with the relevant institutions. Export credit agencies from other countries, such as the Export-Import Bank of the United States, UK Export Finance, and Euler Hermes, also provide financing and insurance for exports to Africa. These agencies can help mitigate the risk of non-payment and provide access to credit. You should be aware of their requirements and how to apply. Another option is supply chain finance, where a financier pays the seller based on the buyer's creditworthiness. This is often arranged through the buyer's bank and can provide immediate cash to the seller at a discount. It is particularly useful for open account transactions. You should also consider the impact of currency fluctuations. Many African currencies are volatile, and exchange rate movements can erode profits. You can advise your client to invoice in a stable currency such as US dollars or euros, or to use hedging instruments such as forward contracts. However, some buyers may prefer to pay in local currency, so you need to negotiate. You should also be aware of currency controls in some countries, which may restrict the ability to convert or transfer foreign currency. You should check the regulations in both the seller's and buyer's countries before agreeing on a payment currency. Finally, you should always conduct due diligence on buyers before extending credit. Check their credit history, ask for references, and use credit reporting agencies if available. You can also start with small orders and gradually increase the credit limit as trust is built. By understanding the financing and payment options available, you can help your client close more deals and manage risk effectively. This concludes Module 6. You now have a solid foundation in export sales in the African context, from market research to buyer identification, documentation, logistics, and financing. Remember that success in this field requires patience, persistence, and a genuine commitment to building relationships. As you apply these lessons in your work, you will not only help your clients grow their businesses but also contribute to the growth of intra-African trade and economic development across the continent.

<div style="border:3px solid #b71c1c;background:#ffebee;padding:16px;margin:20px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#b71c1c;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING Food, Feed and Chemical Products</p>
<p style="margin:0;color:#7f0000;">If your export product is food, animal feed, or any agricultural chemical, you are legally responsible for its safety in the destination market. Key risks:</p>
<ul style="color:#7f0000;margin:8px 0 0 0;">
<li><strong>Aflatoxin and mycotoxin limits</strong> vary by destination. The EU maximum for aflatoxin B1 in groundnuts is 2 g/kg; many African markets allow 1020 g/kg. Exceeding the destination limit means rejected consignments, destruction, and blacklisting.</li>
<li><strong>Pesticide residue limits (MRLs)</strong> differ between the EU, the Gulf, and African RECs. A product cleared in one market may be rejected in another.</li>
<li><strong>Veterinary and phytosanitary certificates</strong> must match the actual consignment. Falsified or mismatched certificates are a criminal offence in most jurisdictions.</li>
<li><strong>Never ship without a valid certificate of analysis</strong> from an accredited laboratory for the destination market's specific parameters.</li>
<li><strong>Chemical exports</strong> (fertilisers, veterinary drugs, pesticides) require registration in the destination country. Unregistered products are illegal to sell, regardless of origin.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist: Export Sales Toolkit</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Market screen done?</strong> Confirm the destination's tariff line (HS code), import permit requirements, and any quota or ban before you quote.</li>
<li><strong>Buyer qualified?</strong> Score the buyer on: years in business, trade references, payment history, and physical presence. Do not proceed on email alone.</li>
<li><strong>Landed cost calculated?</strong> Include FOB price, freight, insurance, import duty, port charges, and inland transport. Quote in the currency the buyer pays in.</li>
<li><strong>Payment term agreed in writing?</strong> Match the term to the buyer type using Table 2. Get it into the proforma invoice and the sales contract.</li>
<li><strong>Documentation pack assembled?</strong> Commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or road manifest, certificate of origin, phytosanitary or veterinary certificate, certificate of analysis, and insurance certificate.</li>
<li><strong>Quality and safety verified?</strong> Certificate of analysis from an accredited lab, matching the destination market's limits. No shipment without it.</li>
<li><strong>Logistics booked and tracked?</strong> Confirm the corridor, border post, and estimated clearance time. Build buffer days into the delivery promise.</li>
<li><strong>Follow-up scheduled?</strong> Diary the arrival date, the buyer's receipt confirmation, and a 30-day post-delivery check-in for the repeat order.</li>
</ol>
