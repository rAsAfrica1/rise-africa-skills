Lessons

1. Choosing the Right Spot: Size, Shade, and Water Access

Choosing the right spot for your compost heap is the first step to success. In Africa, where sun can be intense, you need a place that gets some shade during the hottest part of the day. Partial shade from a tree or building is ideal. Full sun will dry out your heap too fast, and full shade might keep it too cool and slow decomposition. Aim for a spot that gets morning sun and afternoon shade. The size of your heap matters: a heap that is too small (less than 1 cubic meter) won't heat up enough, while one that is too large (over 2 cubic meters) is hard to turn. A good size for a household is about 1 meter wide by 1 meter high by 1 meter deep. That's roughly 1 cubic meter. You also need easy access to water. Compost needs moisture, so choose a spot within 10-15 meters of a water source, like a tap, well, or rainwater tank. If you have to carry water far, you'll be less likely to keep the heap properly moist. Also, make sure the spot is level and well-drained. If water pools around the heap, it can become soggy and smelly. Avoid low spots where rain collects. Finally, consider convenience: you want to be able to add materials regularly without a long walk. A spot near your kitchen garden or where you process crops is ideal. Clear the area of weeds and grass, and loosen the soil underneath to allow worms and microbes to enter. If you have problems with termites, place a layer of gravel or broken bricks at the base. With the right spot, you're ready to build.

2. Layering Browns and Greens: The Ideal Ratio

The secret to a hot, fast-working compost heap is the right mix of 'browns' and 'greens'. Browns are dry, carbon-rich materials like dry leaves, straw, hay, maize stalks, sawdust, shredded paper, and cardboard. Greens are wet, nitrogen-rich materials like fresh grass clippings, vegetable scraps, fruit peels, coffee grounds, tea leaves, and animal manure (from cows, goats, chickens, but not dogs or cats). The ideal ratio is about 2 to 3 parts browns to 1 part greens by volume. That means for every bucket of greens, add 2-3 buckets of browns. If you have too many greens, the heap becomes slimy, smells like ammonia, and attracts flies. Too many browns, and it decomposes very slowly. To build the heap, start with a 15-20 cm layer of coarse browns like maize stalks or small twigs at the bottom for drainage and airflow. Then add a layer of greens, about 10-15 cm thick. Sprinkle a little soil or old compost on topthis adds microorganisms. Then add a layer of browns, 15-20 cm thick. Repeat these layers: greens, soil, browns, until the heap is about 1 meter high. Each time you add a layer, water it lightly to keep it moist like a wrung-out sponge. If you have manure, mix it with the greens. Chop large pieces into smaller bitsthis speeds up decomposition. Turn the heap after a week to mix the layers. If you notice a bad smell, add more browns. If it's too dry, add water and more greens. With the right ratio, your heap will heat up within 2-3 days, reaching temperatures of 55-65C, which kills weed seeds and pathogens.

3. Adding Water: How to Test Moisture by Hand

Water is the lifeblood of your compost heap. Without enough moisture, the microbes that break down organic matter slow down and stop. With too much, the heap becomes waterlogged, anaerobic (without oxygen), and smells like rotten eggs. The goal is to keep the heap as moist as a wrung-out sponge. To test moisture by hand, stick your hand into the middle of the heap and pull out a handful of material. Squeeze it firmly in your fist. If it drips water, it's too wetadd more browns and turn it. If it feels dry and crumbles apart, it's too dryadd water and turn it. If it feels like a damp sponge and only a few drops come out, it's perfect. You should check moisture every 2-3 days, especially in hot, dry weather. In Africa, where temperatures can soar, you may need to water every day. Use a watering can or hose with a gentle spray to avoid compacting the heap. Add water slowly, letting it soak in. If you live in a rainy area, you may need to cover the heap with a tarp or banana leaves to prevent it from getting too wet. During the rainy season, check moisture less often. If the heap is too wet, turn it to add air and mix in dry browns. A simple tool: a stick or machete inserted into the heap can help you feel the moisture at different depths. Remember, the center of the heap should be as moist as the edges. If the edges are dry, push them toward the center when you turn. Consistent moisture is key to fast composting.

4. Building a Heap with a Pallet or Wire Bin

You can build a compost heap with simple materials found around your home or farm. Two popular methods are using wooden pallets or a wire bin. For a pallet bin, collect four wooden pallets of the same size. Stand three pallets on edge to form a U-shape, and tie them together at the corners with wire or rope. Drive wooden stakes or metal posts into the ground at the corners for support. The fourth pallet can be used as a removable front, so you can easily turn and harvest the compost. If you don't have pallets, use a wire bin: buy a roll of wire mesh (chicken wire or welded wire) at least 1 meter high. Form it into a circle about 1 meter in diameter, and secure the ends with wire. You can also use wooden stakes to hold it in place. The advantage of a wire bin is good airflow, but it can dry out faster, so monitor moisture. For both methods, place the bin on level ground, preferably on soil, not concrete, so worms and microbes can enter. If you have termites, place a layer of gravel at the base. Start your heap by adding a 15 cm layer of coarse browns at the bottom. Then alternate greens and browns as described in Lesson 2. With a pallet bin, you can remove the front pallet to turn the heap easily. With a wire bin, you can simply lift the wire and move it to the side, then fork the compost back into the bin. Turn the heap every 1-2 weeks. In hot, dry areas, you may need to water more often. These bins are cheap, easy to build, and can be made from recycled materials. They keep your heap neat and make turning and harvesting much easier.

5. Turning Schedule: When and How Often

Turning your compost heap is like stirring a pot of stewit mixes everything, adds oxygen, and speeds up decomposition. The general rule is to turn the heap every 1-2 weeks for the first month, then every 2-3 weeks until it's finished. But the schedule depends on your goals. If you want compost fast (in 1-2 months), turn every 3-4 days. If you're not in a hurry, turn every 2-4 weeks. How do you know when to turn? Stick a metal rod or long stick into the center of the heap. Leave it for 10 minutes, then pull it out. If it's hot to the touch, the heap is active and doesn't need turning yet. If it's warm or cool, it's time to turn. Also, if the heap starts to smell bad or looks matted, turn it. To turn, use a pitchfork or shovel. Start by pulling materials from the outside to the center, and from the top to the bottom. Mix thoroughly. If the heap is too dry, add water as you turn. If too wet, add dry browns. If you have a pallet bin with a removable front, simply remove the front, fork the material into a new pile beside it, then put it back. With a wire bin, lift the wire off, set it aside, and fork the compost back into the wire. After turning, reshape the heap into a dome. In hot African climates, turning may dry out the heap, so water after turning. If you turn too often, you might disrupt the heating process. If you turn too little, it becomes anaerobic. Aim for a balance. A well-turned heap will heat up again within a day after turning. Keep a simple calendar or mark on a stick to remind you when to turn.

6. Harvesting Finished Compost: Signs and Timing

After weeks of layering, watering, and turning, your compost is ready to harvest when it looks and smells like rich, dark soil. The signs are clear: the heap no longer heats up, even after turning. The original materials are mostly unrecognizableyou can't tell a banana peel from a leaf. It has a sweet, earthy smell, like forest floor after rain. It's crumbly and cool to the touch. The volume is about half to one-third of what you started with. Depending on your climate and how often you turned, this can take 1-3 months in hot weather, or 4-6 months in cooler conditions. To harvest, stop adding new materials to the heap about 2-3 weeks before you expect it to finish. This lets the last additions decompose. If you have a pallet or wire bin, remove the bin and let the pile sit for a few days. Then, use a fork or shovel to move the compost to a screen or sieve. A simple screen can be made with wire mesh over a wooden frame. Sift the compost to remove any large, unfinished piecesthese can go back into the next heap. The finished compost is ready to use. Store it in sacks or a covered pile in a dry place. Use it in your garden by mixing it into the soil at planting, or as a top dressing. You can also make compost tea by soaking a sack of compost in water for a day. Never use compost that still has recognizable food scraps or smells sourit's not ready. With practice, you'll learn to judge the perfect timing.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Building a Compost Heap in African Farm and Urban Settings

For African agribusinesses, compost is not just a soil amendment it is a cost-control tool, a waste-to-value stream, and a marketable product. A well-built heap can cut fertilizer bills by 3060% on vegetable and maize plots, while surplus compost can be bagged and sold to nurseries, landscapers, and urban gardeners. This Practical Reference consolidates the seven lessons in this module into a single field-ready guide. It is written for the smallholder, the peri-urban farmer, and the agro-entrepreneur who needs consistent, repeatable results rather than theory.

Across Africa, the raw materials are rarely the problem. Crop residues, market waste, animal manure, poultry litter, kitchen scraps, and sawdust are abundant. The problem is process discipline: the right spot, the right ratio, the right moisture, and the right turning schedule. Get these four right and you will harvest finished compost in 610 weeks instead of 6 months.

1. Choosing the Right Spot: Size, Shade, and Water Access

Site selection determines how much labour your heap will demand. A heap in full sun in the Sahel will dry out within 48 hours; a heap in a waterlogged depression in the Congo Basin will go anaerobic and smell. Aim for:

  • Size: Minimum 1 m 1 m 1 m (roughly 1 cubic metre) to hold heat. For commercial volumes, build three bays of 1.5 m each one for building, one for turning, one for maturing.
  • Shade: Partial shade from a tree, a north-facing wall, or a shade net. In hot lowland areas, dappled shade is ideal; in cooler highlands, morning sun helps the heap reach 5565C.
  • Water access: Within 10 metres of a tap, well, or water drum. You will need 2050 litres per turn in dry season.
  • Drainage: On a slight slope or raised bed. Never in a flood path.
  • Access: A wheelbarrow path wide enough for loading and harvesting.

For urban agribusinesses in Nairobi, Lagos, or Accra, a pallet bin on a concrete slab behind a restaurant or school is ideal it captures food waste, avoids pests, and produces saleable compost within a term.

2. Layering Browns and Greens: The Ideal Ratio

The single most common failure is too many greens (nitrogen) and too few browns (carbon). The target is 2530 parts carbon to 1 part nitrogen by weight, which in practice means roughly 23 volumes of browns to 1 volume of greens. Do not overthink it: if it smells like ammonia, add browns; if it is dry and nothing is happening, add greens and water.

3. Adding Water: How to Test Moisture by Hand

Compost needs the moisture of a wrung-out sponge roughly 5060% by weight. The hand test is the only tool you need:

  1. Take a handful from 30 cm inside the heap.
  2. Squeeze firmly.
  3. Correct: 12 drops of water appear between your fingers; the ball holds shape.
  4. Too dry: No drops, ball crumbles. Add water while turning.
  5. Too wet: Water streams out. Add browns (sawdust, dry grass, maize cobs) and turn.

In dry-season Africa, cover the heap with a tarpaulin, banana leaves, or a jute sack to reduce evaporation. In the rainy season, cover to prevent leaching of nutrients.

4. Building a Heap with a Pallet or Wire Bin

Pallet bins are the standard for African urban and peri-urban compost enterprises because they are cheap, modular, and stackable. A four-pallet bin (1 m 1 m 1 m) costs almost nothing where pallets are discarded.

  • Site: Level ground, partial shade, near water.
  • Base: A layer of coarse branches or maize cobs 10 cm deep for drainage and airflow.
  • Layers: Alternate browns (15 cm) and greens (8 cm). Add a handful of soil or old compost between layers to inoculate.
  • Height: Stop at 11.2 m. Taller heaps compact and go anaerobic.
  • Cover: Top with a brown cap and a tarpaulin.

Wire bins (1 m diameter, 1 m high, 5 cm mesh) work equally well and allow better airflow, but dry out faster in hot climates.

6. Harvesting Finished Compost: Signs and Timing

Finished compost is dark, crumbly, earthy-smelling, and no longer heats up when turned. The original materials should be unrecognisable except for woody stems. Test by:

  • Temperature: Below 40C for 3 consecutive days.
  • Smell: Sweet, earthy never sour or ammonia-like.
  • Appearance: Uniform dark brown to black.
  • Seed test: Plant cress or bean seeds in a sample; germination above 80% means it is ready.

Harvest by sieving through a 10 mm mesh. Oversize material goes back into the next heap. Store finished compost in sacks in a dry, shaded place; it keeps for 612 months.

SAFETY WARNING Read before building any heap

  • Never compost meat, bones, dairy, or oily food in open heaps they attract rodents, dogs, and snakes, and cause foul odours.
  • Do not add human or pig manure to compost intended for vegetables eaten raw. If used, apply at least 120 days before harvest and only on non-leafy crops.
  • Wear gloves and closed shoes when turning. Sharp maize stover and wire bins cause cuts.
  • Keep heaps away from wells, boreholes, and surface water at least 30 metres to prevent leachate contamination.
  • Pesticide-treated crop residues should be avoided unless you are certain of the pre-harvest interval.
  • In hot weather, heaps can reach 70C. Do not leave children unattended near the heap.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Site: Partial shade, 1 m minimum, within 10 m of water, on well-drained ground.
  2. Base: 10 cm of coarse branches or maize cobs for airflow.
  3. Layers: 15 cm browns 8 cm greens sprinkle soil water. Repeat.
  4. Moisture: Squeeze test 12 drops. Cover in dry or rainy season.
  5. Temperature: Aim for 5565C in the core within 3 days.
  6. Turning: Weekly for farm compost; every 23 days for commercial speed.
  7. Harvest: Dark, crumbly, earthy, below 40C, seed test above 80%.
  8. Store: Sieve, bag, keep dry and shaded. Sell or apply within 12 months.

Business Notes for African Agribusinesses

Compost is a margin business, not a hobby. A 1 m heap yields roughly 400600 kg of finished compost. At a farm-gate value of US$0.100.25 per kg in East and West Africa, a three-bay system can generate US$5001,500 per cycle from waste that costs nothing. The key costs are labour (turning) and packaging. Standardise your recipe, train one worker per site, and keep a simple log of temperature, moisture, and turn dates. Buyers nurseries, tea estates, vegetable exporters, and urban landscapers pay a premium for consistent, weed-seed-free, pathogen-safe compost. That consistency is built through the discipline in this module.
