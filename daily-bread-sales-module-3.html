1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Bread is a daily staple in many African households, and small bakeries can thrive by selling fresh loaves at affordable prices. But without a consistent recipe and proper scaling, your bread will vary in taste, size, and qualitycustomers will notice and may not return. This module teaches you how to use a standard bread recipe and scale it up or down accurately, so you can produce the same great bread every day, whether you bake 10 loaves or 100.

Why does scaling matter? If you double a recipe by simply doubling all ingredients, you might run into problems: dough that's too stiff or too soft, bread that doesn't rise properly, or inconsistent baking times. Professional bakers use baker's percentages, where flour is always 100%, and other ingredients are a percentage of the flour weight. This makes scaling easy and precise. For example, if your recipe calls for 1000g flour, 600g water (60%), 20g salt (2%), and 10g yeast (1%), you can scale to any flour weight by multiplying.

In Africa, ingredients like flour, yeast, and salt are available in most markets, but prices fluctuate. Accurate scaling reduces waste and controls costs. If you know exactly how much dough each loaf requires, you can calculate your cost per loaf and set a profitable price. Also, consistent size and quality build trustcustomers will come back for your reliable bread.

This module will guide you through tools, step-by-step methods, common mistakes, safety, and a practical exercise. By the end, you'll be able to write your own scalable bread recipe and produce uniform loaves that sell. Remember, baking is a skill that improves with practice, and mastering scaling is the foundation of a successful bakery business.

2. Tools and Materials

To bake bread consistently and scale recipes, you need the right tools and materials. Most are affordable and available in local markets or online. First, a digital kitchen scale is essential. It should measure in grams, up to at least 5kg. A scale ensures accuracyvolume measures like cups are not precise enough for scaling. You can find digital scales in supermarkets or electronics shops for around $10-$20.

Next, you need large mixing bowls. Plastic or stainless steel bowls work well. For scaling up, you might need a very large bowl or a clean plastic basin. A sturdy wooden spoon or a dough scraper helps mix and handle dough. If you plan to bake many loaves, consider a dough mixer, but for small-scale, hand mixing is fine. You'll also need measuring spoons for small amounts like yeast and salt, though a scale is better.

For shaping and proofing, you need loaf pans. Standard loaf pans are about 9x5 inches. If you don't have pans, you can shape round loaves and bake on a tray. Parchment paper or greased trays prevent sticking. A clean cloth or plastic wrap is needed to cover dough while it rises. A thermometer is useful to check water temperatureyeast works best at 25-30C. If you don't have a thermometer, use lukewarm water (comfortable to touch).

An oven is the biggest investment. If you don't have a commercial oven, a home oven or even a charcoal oven can work. You'll need a baking stone or a heavy tray for even heat. A spray bottle for water helps create steam for a crispy crust. Finally, keep a notebook to record your recipes and scaling calculations. This helps you replicate successful batches. In Africa, you can source these tools locally: markets sell bowls and pans, and hardware stores have scales. Start with basics and upgrade as your business grows.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to scale and bake bread consistently. Start with a basic recipe: 1000g bread flour, 600g water (60%), 20g salt (2%), 10g instant yeast (1%). This makes about 2 large loaves (each around 800g dough). To scale, decide how many loaves you want. For 10 loaves, multiply all ingredients by 5: 5000g flour, 3000g water, 100g salt, 50g yeast.

Step 1: Weigh all ingredients accurately using your scale. Use warm water (25-30C). Step 2: In a large bowl, mix flour and salt. In another bowl, dissolve yeast in water. Pour yeast mixture into flour and mix with a spoon until a shaggy dough forms. Step 3: Turn dough onto a lightly floured surface and knead for 10-15 minutes until smooth and elastic. If scaling up, knead in batches or use a mixer. Step 4: Place dough in a greased bowl, cover, and let rise until doubled in size, about 1-2 hours depending on room temperature. In hot African climates, rising may be faster, so check after 45 minutes.

Step 5: Punch down dough and divide into equal pieces. For 800g loaves, weigh each piece. Shape into balls, then flatten and roll into loaf shapes. Place in greased loaf pans. Step 6: Cover and proof again until dough rises just above the pan, about 30-45 minutes. Step 7: Preheat oven to 220C (425F). If using a home oven, preheat with a baking stone or tray. Step 8: Bake for 30-35 minutes until golden brown and hollow-sounding when tapped. For steam, place a pan of water in the oven or spray loaves with water before baking. Step 9: Remove from pans and cool on a wire rack. Cooling is important for texture. Now you can repeat this process with any scale, ensuring consistent results.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced bakers make mistakes. Here are common issues when scaling bread recipes and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Not using a scale. Volume measurements like cups vary, leading to inconsistent dough. Fix: Always weigh ingredients in grams. If you don't have a scale, use a known container to calibrate, but invest in a scale soon.

Mistake 2: Incorrect water temperature. Too hot water kills yeast; too cold slows fermentation. Fix: Use lukewarm water (25-30C). If you don't have a thermometer, test on your wristit should feel neither hot nor cold. Mistake 3: Over-scaling without adjusting mixing time. When you double or triple a recipe, mixing and kneading take longer. Fix: Knead until dough is smooth and elastic, regardless of time. If using a mixer, watch for dough climbing the hook.

Mistake 4: Not adjusting rising time for climate. In hot African regions, dough rises faster. Fix: Check dough after 30 minutes; if doubled, proceed. In cold weather, rising may take longerfind a warm spot. Mistake 5: Inaccurate division. Uneven loaves bake unevenly. Fix: Weigh each piece of dough. Use a scale to ensure equal weight. Mistake 6: Skipping steam. Steam gives a crispy crust and better rise. Fix: Place a metal pan with water in the oven, or spray loaves with water before baking.

Mistake 7: Underbaking. Bread may look done but be raw inside. Fix: Use a thermometerinternal temperature should be 90-95C. Or tap the bottom; it should sound hollow. Mistake 8: Not cooling properly. Cutting hot bread leads to gummy texture. Fix: Cool completely on a wire rack before slicing. Mistake 9: Stale ingredients. Old yeast or flour gives poor results. Fix: Store ingredients in airtight containers and check expiration dates. By avoiding these mistakes, your scaled bread will be consistent and sellable.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Baking involves heat, sharp tools, and electrical equipment, so safety is crucial. Always wash your hands before handling food. Keep your work area clean and sanitized. When using an oven, use oven mitts to avoid burns. Never leave hot pans unattended. If using a gas oven, check for leaks regularly. For charcoal ovens, ensure proper ventilation to avoid carbon monoxide buildup.

Handle knives and dough scrapers carefully. Cut away from your body. Store knives safely when not in use. If using a dough mixer, keep hands away from moving parts. Unplug before cleaning. Also, be mindful of electrical safety: don't use wet hands to plug or unplug appliances. Keep cords away from water.

Best practices for scaling: Always write down your scaled recipe and keep records. This helps you replicate success and adjust for cost. Use baker's percentages to easily scale up or down. For example, if you want to use 2kg of flour, calculate water as 60% of 2000g = 1200g, salt 2% = 40g, yeast 1% = 20g. This method works for any amount.

Maintain a clean workspace. Clean as you go to prevent cross-contamination. Store ingredients properly: flour in a cool, dry place; yeast in the fridge or freezer for longer shelf life. Label containers with dates. When baking for sale, follow local health regulations. Wear clean aprons and hairnets if required. Practice good personal hygiene. Finally, don't rush. Baking is a science; accurate measurements and patience yield the best results. By following these safety and best practices, you'll produce safe, high-quality bread that customers trust.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice scaling and baking. For this exercise, you will scale a basic bread recipe to make 6 loaves of about 800g each. Start with the standard recipe: 1000g flour, 600g water, 20g salt, 10g yeast. Calculate the total dough weight: 1000+600+20+10 = 1630g. This makes about 2 loaves of 800g (with a little extra). To make 6 loaves, you need 6 x 800g = 4800g dough. Scale factor = 4800 / 1630 2.94. Round to 3 for simplicity. So multiply all ingredients by 3: 3000g flour, 1800g water, 60g salt, 30g yeast. Total dough = 3000+1800+60+30 = 4890g, which makes 6 loaves of about 815g each.

Now, gather your tools: scale, large bowl, spoon, loaf pans, oven. Weigh ingredients accurately. Mix and knead as described in Lesson 3. Let dough rise until doubled. Divide into 6 equal pieces (weigh each to be precise). Shape into loaves and place in pans. Proof until risen. Bake at 220C for 30-35 minutes. Cool on racks.

After baking, evaluate your loaves: Are they uniform in size? Did they rise well? Taste a slice. Record your observations. Calculate cost: if flour costs $1 per kg, water negligible, salt and yeast minimal, your total cost for 6 loaves is about $3.50. If you sell each loaf for $1.50, your profit is $9 - $3.50 = $5.50. Adjust pricing based on local market.

Repeat the exercise with a different scale, say for 12 loaves, to practice. Try using baker's percentages: for 5000g flour, water = 3000g, salt = 100g, yeast = 50g. This makes about 10 loaves. Practice until you can scale confidently. This skill will save you time and money, and ensure consistent quality for your customers.

<h3>Why Baker's Percentage Beats Cup Measuring</h3>
<p>Volume measuring (cups, spoons) is unreliable because flour compacts differently depending on humidity, storage, and how it was scooped. In many African markets, flour is sold in 50 kg bags whose moisture content varies with the rainy or dry season. Baker's percentage removes this guesswork. Once you fix flour at 100%, every other ingredient is a percentage of that weight. Scaling then becomes a single multiplication the "scale factor" applied to every ingredient.</p>

<p><strong>Business-first insight:</strong> the same formula lets you calculate cost per loaf, forecast flour purchases, and price your bread without guessing. A bakery that cannot scale accurately cannot quote a contract for 500 loaves to a school, hotel, or supermarket.</p>

<strong style="color:#b52b27"> SAFETY WARNING Scaling and Dough Handling</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#222">Never scale dough or ingredients on the same surface used for raw meat, poultry, or eggs. Flour dust is a respiratory hazard wear a dust mask when handling 50 kg bags, and tie bags securely to prevent weevil and rodent contamination. Use food-grade, calibrated scales only; a broken scale can mean under-salted dough (poor shelf life) or over-yeasted dough (collapsed loaves). When scaling hot water, keep it below 60C to protect yeast and avoid burns. Store yeast away from strong odours and moisture in African humid climates, opened yeast must be resealed and refrigerated within 24 hours.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Calibrate your scale</strong> before every batch use a known 1 kg weight or a sealed bag of salt.</li>
<li><strong>Convert the recipe to baker's percentage</strong> with flour always at 100%.</li>
<li><strong>Decide your flour weight first</strong> this is your anchor, not the number of loaves.</li>
<li><strong>Multiply every ingredient by the same scale factor</strong> never scale one ingredient alone.</li>
<li><strong>Check water temperature</strong> against flour temperature to hit a final dough temperature of 2426C.</li>
<li><strong>Record actual weights and yields</strong> in a batch log for costing and consistency.</li>
<li><strong>Adjust water by 12%</strong> if the flour is new or the weather has changed.</li>
<li><strong>Weigh dough pieces individually</strong> equal pieces bake evenly and sell fairly.</li>
</ol>
