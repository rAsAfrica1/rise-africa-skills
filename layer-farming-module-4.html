1. Nutrient Requirements for Layers at Different Stages

Layers need different nutrients as they grow. From day-old to 6 weeks (brooding), feed them chick starter mash with 20% protein. This builds strong bones and organs. From 7 to 18 weeks (growing), switch to grower mash with 16% protein. This stage prepares them for laying without making them too fat. At 18 weeks, move to layer mash with 16-18% protein and 3.5-4.5% calcium. Calcium is critical for eggshells. A hen laying 5 eggs per week needs about 4 grams of calcium daily. If you see thin or soft shells, increase calcium. Energy comes from maize, millet, or sorghum. A layer needs about 2800-3000 kcal per kg of feed. Too little energy means fewer eggs. Too much makes them fat and stops laying. Protein is for egg white and body repair. Use soybean meal, fishmeal, or groundnut cake. Vitamins and minerals are needed in small amounts. A lack of vitamin A causes weak chicks; vitamin D3 helps calcium absorption. Always provide clean water. A hen drinks 250-500 ml per day. Without water, she stops eating. In hot weather, add electrolytes. For 100 hens, you need about 12-15 kg of feed per day. Adjust based on how much they eat. Weigh a sample of eggs monthly. If egg size drops, check protein and energy. Keep records of feed intake and egg production. This helps you spot problems early.

2. Types of Feed: Mash, Pellets, and Crumbs

Feeds come in three forms: mash, pellets, and crumbs. Mash is ground feed, like coarse flour. It is the cheapest and easiest to make on-farm. You can mix your own ingredients. But hens waste some mash by billing it out. To reduce waste, feed in troughs and do not fill more than half full. Pellets are mash pressed into small sticks. Hens eat pellets faster and waste less. Pellets also prevent selective eatinghens cannot pick out only maize. This means they get a balanced diet. Pellets cost more because of processing. Crumbs are broken pellets, used for chicks. They are easier for small beaks. For layers, you can use mash or pellets. In Africa, many farmers use mash because it is affordable. If you make your own mash, grind ingredients to medium texturenot too fine, or it becomes dusty. A hammer mill with a 3-5 mm screen works well. Store feed in dry, ventilated bags. Never use mouldy feed. Moulds produce aflatoxins that kill hens and lower egg production. If you buy commercial feed, check the label for protein, fat, fibre, and calcium. Good layer mash has 16-18% protein, 3.5-4.5% calcium, and 0.5% phosphorus. Pellets should be 3-4 mm diameter. Crumbs are 1-2 mm. Use pellets in rainy season when mash gets sticky. Always provide grit (small stones) with mash to help digestion. Grit stays in the gizzard and grinds food. Without grit, mash passes undigested. You can buy granite grit or use clean river sand. Offer 1 kg of grit per 100 hens per week.

3. Formulating Balanced Rations with Local Ingredients

You can mix your own layer feed using local ingredients. A balanced ration has energy, protein, minerals, and vitamins. For 100 kg of layer mash, use: 55 kg maize (energy), 20 kg soybean meal (protein), 10 kg fishmeal (protein and minerals), 8 kg wheat bran or rice bran (fibre and energy), 5 kg limestone or oyster shell (calcium), 1 kg bone meal (phosphorus), 0.5 kg salt, and 0.5 kg vitamin-mineral premix. This gives about 17% protein and 4% calcium. If soybean meal is expensive, use 15 kg soybean meal and 5 kg groundnut cake. But groundnut cake can have aflatoxintest it or buy from trusted sellers. Fishmeal should be 60-65% protein. Local fishmeal from small dried fish works if ground well. Maize can be replaced with millet, sorghum, or cassava chips. If using cassava, add 0.2% methionine because cassava lacks this amino acid. Mix all ingredients thoroughly on a clean concrete floor. Use a shovel to turn the pile 3 times. Store in clean bags. Make only what you will use in 2 weeks to avoid mould. For 100 hens, you need about 12 kg per day. So 100 kg lasts about 8 days. Weigh ingredients with a scaledo not guess. A 50 kg scale is enough. Keep a notebook of your formula and how hens respond. If egg production drops, adjust protein or energy. Always provide clean water and grit. This ration works for layers from 18 weeks onwards. For chicks and growers, adjust protein and calcium: chicks need 20% protein and 1% calcium; growers need 16% protein and 1% calcium.

4. Feeding Schedules and Feed Management

Layers eat best when fed at the same times daily. Give them two main meals: one in the morning (7-8 am) and one in the afternoon (3-4 pm). This matches their natural eating pattern. In hot climates, feed early and late to avoid the heat of the day. Hens eat less when it is hot. If you feed only once, they may overeat and waste. For 100 hens, give about 6 kg in the morning and 6 kg in the afternoon. Adjust based on how much they leave. If feed is left after 30 minutes, reduce the next meal. Always provide clean water. A hen drinks up to 500 ml daily. In hot weather, she may drink double. Water should be cool and clean. Use drinkers that are easy to clean. Feed in troughs that allow all hens to eat at once. Allow 10-12 cm of trough space per hen. For 100 hens, you need 10-12 meters of trough. If troughs are too short, strong hens eat more and weak ones starve. Clean troughs daily to remove old feed. Old feed can grow mould. Store feed in a dry, dark room on pallets. Keep bags off the floor and away from walls. Use oldest feed first. Never mix new feed with old. Weigh a sample of hens monthly to check body condition. A layer should weigh 1.8-2.2 kg. If they are too thin, increase feed. If too fat, reduce energy. Keep records of feed used and eggs collected. This helps you calculate feed conversion ratio (FCR). Good FCR for layers is 2.0-2.5 kg feed per kg eggs. If FCR is high, check for disease or feed waste.

5. Supplements: Vitamins, Minerals, and Grit

Even with a good ration, layers may need supplements. Vitamins are needed for health and egg production. Vitamin A for eyes and immunity, vitamin D3 for calcium absorption, vitamin E for fertility and hatchability. A lack of vitamin D3 causes soft-shelled eggs. You can buy commercial vitamin premixes. Add 0.5 kg per 100 kg of feed. If hens are on deep litter and get sunlight, they make vitamin D themselves. But in cloudy weather, supplement. Minerals: calcium and phosphorus are the most important. Calcium for eggshells, phosphorus for bones. The ratio should be 2:1 calcium to phosphorus. If you use limestone and bone meal, you are covered. But if you see thin shells, add oyster shell free-choice. Offer it in a separate feeder. Hens will eat what they need. Grit is small stones that help grind feed in the gizzard. Without grit, feed passes undigested. Use granite grit or clean river sand. Give 1 kg per 100 hens per week. Sprinkle on feed or offer separately. Salt is needed but in small amounts. 0.5 kg per 100 kg feed. Too much salt causes water belly and death. Do not add extra salt if your premix already has some. Other supplements: probiotics for gut health, toxins binders if feed may have mould. In Africa, many farmers use local herbs like aloe vera or garlic as supplements. These can help but are not a substitute for balanced feed. Always follow label instructions. Overdosing vitamins can be toxic. Store supplements in cool, dry place. Mix only what you will use in one week. Keep records of what you add and when.

6. Avoiding Feed Waste and Contamination

Feed is your biggest cost. Waste and contamination cut your profit. To avoid waste: use proper feeders. Troughs should be deep enough so hens cannot bill feed out. Fill only half full. If feeders are too full, hens scoop feed onto the floor. For 100 hens, use 10-12 meters of trough. Feed at fixed times. Do not leave feed in troughs all day. Remove leftover feed after 30 minutes. Store feed in clean, dry bags on pallets. Keep bags off the floor and away from walls. Use oldest feed first. Never mix new feed with old. Protect feed from rodents and birds. They eat feed and spread disease. Use metal bins with tight lids. For contamination: never use mouldy feed. Moulds produce aflatoxins that damage the liver and lower egg production. If feed smells musty or has green spots, throw it away. Do not feed it to hens. Keep water clean. Dirty water can spread disease. Clean drinkers daily. Do not let hens walk in feed. Use feeders that prevent them from standing in feed. If you make your own feed, use clean equipment. Do not mix on dirty floors. Store ingredients separately. Check for insects. If you see weevils, sun-dry the grain or use food-grade diatomaceous earth. Never use pesticides on feed. Keep a record of feed bought and used. This helps you spot waste. A good layer farm loses less than 5% of feed. If you lose more, check feeders and storage. Also, watch for feed theft by workers or animals. Lock feed store. Weigh feed before and after. If you see sudden drop in feed use but eggs are same, check for waste. Healthy hens, clean feed, clean waterthese are the keys to profit.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Nutrition and Feeding for Layer Farming in Africa

Feed represents 6075% of the total cost of producing a tray of eggs. For a commercial layer enterprise in Africawhether 500 birds in Kenya or 50,000 in Nigeriafeed conversion efficiency is the single largest determinant of profitability. This practical reference consolidates the nutrient requirements, feed forms, ration formulation, feeding schedules, supplementation, and waste-control practices covered in this module. It is written for farm managers and agripreneurs who need actionable numbers, not theory.

The guiding business principle is simple: every gram of feed must convert into eggs, body maintenance, or manurenever into waste. A flock laying at 85% with a feed conversion ratio (FCR) of 2.0 kg feed per kg egg mass is far more profitable than one laying at 90% with an FCR of 2.4. Precision in nutrition beats volume.

1. Nutrient Requirements at a Glance

Layers have distinct nutritional needs across four production phases. The critical transition is from grower to layer feed at 1819 weeks, when calcium jumps from ~1.0% to ~4.0% to support eggshell formation. In Africa, where limestone and oyster shell may be inconsistent in quality, calcium particle size matters: a mix of fine and coarse (24 mm) limestone improves shell strength more than fine alone.

2. Feed Forms: Mash, Pellets, and Crumbs

The form in which feed is presented affects intake, digestibility, and waste. In many African markets, mash is the default because it is cheapest to produce locally. However, pellets and crumbs reduce selective feeding (birds picking out maize and leaving protein-rich meals) and improve FCR by 38%.

Business decision: If you are mixing on-farm, start with mash but invest in a hammer mill with a 34 mm screen to achieve uniform particle size. Uniformity reduces sorting. If you buy commercial feed, compare the cost per kg of egg produced, not the cost per bagpellets often win despite a higher sticker price.

3. Formulating Balanced Rations with Local Ingredients

A balanced ration supplies energy, protein, minerals, and vitamins in the right proportions. In Africa, the most available energy sources are maize, sorghum, millet, and cassava chips. Protein sources include soybean meal, sunflower cake, groundnut cake, fish meal, and blood meal. Limestone, oyster shell, and bone meal supply calcium and phosphorus.

A practical layer mash for peak production (1945 weeks) might include:

  • Maize: 5560%
  • Soybean meal: 1822%
  • Sunflower or groundnut cake: 58%
  • Fish meal: 35%
  • Limestone: 78%
  • Wheat bran or rice bran: 58%
  • Premix (vitamins + minerals): 0.250.5%
  • Salt: 0.25%
  • DL-Methionine: 0.050.1%

Always test local ingredients for aflatoxin, especially maize and groundnut cake. Aflatoxin B1 above 20 ppb reduces laying rate and egg quality. In high-humidity regions, buy from suppliers who provide a certificate of analysis or use a rapid test kit.

4. Feeding Schedules and Feed Management

Layers perform best on a consistent schedule. Feed twice daily: 60% in the morning (78 AM) and 40% in the afternoon (34 PM). The afternoon feeding is critical because shell formation occurs at night; giving calcium-rich feed late improves shell quality.

  • Starter: Ad libitum, refresh troughs 34 times daily.
  • Grower: Restrict to 6070 g/bird/day to control body weight.
  • Layer: 110120 g/bird/day, adjusted for body weight and egg mass.
  • Late lay: 105115 g/bird/day; monitor body weight weekly.

Weigh feed weekly and record intake. A sudden drop of more than 5 g/bird/day signals disease, heat stress, or feed quality problems. In hot climates (above 30C), shift the main feeding to early morning and late evening to reduce heat stress-related intake depression.

5. Supplements: Vitamins, Minerals, and Grit

Commercial premixes supply vitamins A, D3, E, K, B-complex, and trace minerals (zinc, manganese, iron, copper, iodine, selenium). In Africa, where premix quality varies, buy from reputable suppliers and check expiry dates. Store premix away from sunlight and moisture.

Grit (insoluble granite or limestone chips, 24 mm) aids gizzard function and improves nutrient absorption. Provide grit free-choice once weekly, or mix 0.51% into the feed. Soluble grit (oyster shell) should be available free-choice for layer flocks, especially in the afternoon.

Calcium tip: In many African farms, shell quality problems trace back to insufficient coarse calcium. A 50:50 mix of fine and coarse limestone is more effective than fine limestone alone.

SAFETY WARNING: Aflatoxin and Feed Contamination

Never feed mouldy or discoloured maize, groundnut cake, or other ingredients. Aflatoxin contamination is common in tropical Africa and can cause liver damage, reduced egg production, and residue in eggs that is harmful to consumers. Store feed in a dry, ventilated place on pallets, never directly on the floor. Discard any feed with a musty smell, visible mould, or caking. If you suspect contamination, stop feeding immediately and consult an extension officer or veterinarian.

6. Avoiding Feed Waste and Contamination

Feed waste of 10% on a 1,000-bird farm is equivalent to 100 birds eating for free. Control measures:

  • Use troughs with lips or anti-waste grills; fill to one-third capacity.
  • Adjust trough height so the feed level is at the birds' back level.
  • Repair leaking roofs and store feed on pallets, 10 cm off the floor.
  • Use feed within 46 weeks of mixing; buy premix in small quantities.
  • Keep rodents and wild birds out of the feed store.
  • Clean troughs weekly to prevent mould and bacterial growth.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Match feed formula to the correct production stage (starter, grower, layer, late lay).
  2. Weigh feed daily and record intake; investigate drops above 5 g/bird/day.
  3. Provide 3.84.2% calcium in layer feed, with 50% as coarse particles.
  4. Feed twice daily; give the larger portion in the afternoon for shell quality.
  5. Test maize and groundnut cake for aflatoxin before mixing.
  6. Provide grit and oyster shell free-choice, especially in the afternoon.
  7. Store feed on pallets in a dry, ventilated store; use within 6 weeks.
  8. Check troughs daily for waste, mould, and rodent contamination.
