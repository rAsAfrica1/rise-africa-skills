Lessons

1. Cleaning and Conditioning of Grains

Before you mill any grain into breakfast cereal, you must clean it. Dirty grain ruins flavor, wears out your mill, and can make people sick. Start with a simple winnowing basket or a mechanical aspirator to blow away chaff, dust, and light seeds. For maize, sorghum, or millet, use a vibrating screen with 2 mm holes to remove sand and stones. A destoner works on density: a slanted vibrating deck with air blowing from below makes heavier stones travel uphill while grain moves downhill. For small-scale operations, a 500 kg/hour destoner costs about $1,200. After cleaning, condition the grain. Conditioning means adjusting moisture so the bran and germ separate easily during milling. For maize, add clean water to raise moisture from 12% to 16%. Mix for 10 minutes in a horizontal mixer, then rest the grain in a bin for 4 to 12 hours. This tempering softens the bran while keeping the endosperm hard. For sorghum, temper to 15% moisture for 6 hours. Millet needs less: 14% moisture for 2 hours. Never skip conditioning if you want clean grits and low fiber in your final cereal. In Africa, where humidity varies, test moisture with a cheap digital meter (around $150) or the traditional salt-and-bottle method: mix 10 g of ground grain with 20 g of dry salt in a sealed jar, shake for 3 minutes, and if salt sticks to the sides, moisture is above 15%. After conditioning, degerminate maize using a degerminator (like a Beall degerminator) or a roller mill set with a 3 mm gap. This removes the germ, which contains oil that turns rancid. For sorghum and millet, the germ is smaller and can stay. Finally, store conditioned grain in clean jute bags in a dry room for no more than 24 hours before milling. Clean and conditioned grain gives you consistent, safe breakfast cereal.

2. Milling and Grinding Techniques

Milling breaks cleaned, conditioned grain into flour, grits, or meal. Your choice of mill depends on the final cereal texture. For flaked cereals, you need cracked grits, not fine flour. A roller mill with two steel rolls spinning at different speeds (e.g., 500 rpm and 800 rpm) shears the grain. Set the gap to 1.5 mm for coarse grits. For porridge or instant cereals, you need fine flour under 500 microns. A hammer mill with a 1 mm screen does this well. In Africa, many small processors use a plate mill (disc mill) made in Kenya or India. A 10 HP plate mill grinds 200 kg of maize per hour. Set the plates 0.5 mm apart for fine flour, 1.5 mm for meal. Always check the gap with a feeler gauge. Grinding generates heat. If flour temperature exceeds 50C, starch damages and flavor changes. Feed grain slowly and take breaks every 30 minutes. For sorghum and millet, use a stone mill or a pin mill to avoid a burnt taste. After milling, sift the flour. A vibrating sifter with 250-micron and 500-micron screens separates fine flour, middlings, and coarse bran. You can sell the bran as animal feed. For flaking, you need grits between 1 and 2 mm. Pass them through a roller mill again with a 0.8 mm gap, then steam them for 20 minutes at 100C to gelatinize starch. Roll the hot grits between smooth rolls at 10 rpm to flatten into flakes. Dry the flakes at 150C for 10 minutes. In Nigeria, a common setup is a hammer mill for flour and a roller mill for flakes. In South Africa, small plants use a degerminator followed by a roller mill. Always keep spare screens and rolls. Dull rolls crush instead of cut, making uneven particles. Sharpen or replace rolls every 500 hours of use. Proper milling gives you the right particle size for your product, whether you sell fine maize meal or crunchy flakes.

3. Mixing and Blending of Ingredients

Mixing blends milled grain with other ingredients to make a consistent cereal. For a basic maize porridge, you might mix 80% maize meal, 15% soy flour, and 5% sugar and salt. For a fortified cereal, add vitamins and minerals. Always weigh ingredients on a scale accurate to 1 gram. Never guess. Use a horizontal ribbon blender or a planetary mixer. A 100 kg capacity ribbon blender costs about $3,000 and mixes in 10 minutes. For small batches, a drum mixer works: a 200-liter drum turned at 30 rpm for 15 minutes. Add dry ingredients first, then liquids like oil or water. If you add oil to reduce dust, use 2% vegetable oil and mix for 5 minutes. For extrusion cooking, you need a uniform blend with 12-18% moisture. Add water slowly while mixing. A moisture meter helps. Without one, squeeze a handful: it should hold shape but not drip. For coating, you mix a slurry of sugar, salt, and flavor in water. Use a high-shear mixer to dissolve solids. A common mistake is adding ingredients in the wrong order. Always add minor ingredients (vitamins, colors, flavors) last and mix for at least 5 minutes after. For African cereals like ogi or koko, you ferment maize for 2-3 days before mixing. Fermentation lowers pH to 4.0 and improves flavor. After fermentation, wet-mill the grain, then blend with other flours. If you make instant cereal, blend pre-gelatinized flour with sugar and milk powder. Keep mixing times consistent. Overmixing can heat the blend and damage vitamins. Undermixing gives uneven nutrient levels. Test uniformity by taking 5 samples from different parts of the blender and checking color or salt content. A simple salt test: dissolve 1 g of sample in 10 ml water, add 2 drops of silver nitrate, and compare turbidity. If samples differ, mix longer. Clean your mixer after every batch to avoid allergens and spoilage.

4. Extrusion Cooking: Principles and Equipment

Extrusion cooking pushes a moist flour blend through a barrel under high heat and pressure, then forces it out a small die. The sudden pressure drop puffs the cereal into shapes like curls, balls, or stars. This is how many breakfast cereals are made. You need an extruder. A single-screw extruder with a 50 kg/hour capacity costs about $8,000. A twin-screw extruder handles sticky blends better and costs $20,000. For African startups, a small single-screw extruder from China or India is common. The key parameters are moisture, temperature, screw speed, and die size. Start with a blend of 70% maize flour, 20% soy flour, and 10% sugar. Add water to reach 15% moisture. Set barrel temperatures: zone 1 at 60C, zone 2 at 120C, zone 3 at 160C. Screw speed at 300 rpm. The die has 3 mm holes. As the blend moves, friction and steam heat it to 150C. Starch gelatinizes and protein denatures. When it exits the die, water flashes off and the product expands. Collect the extrudates on a conveyor and dry them at 120C for 15 minutes to reduce moisture to 3-5%. Without proper drying, extrudates become soggy. For colored cereals, add natural colors like beetroot powder or turmeric at 0.5% in the blend. For flavored cereals, coat after extrusion. Always preheat the extruder for 10 minutes. Start with a low feed rate, then increase. If the extruder jams, stop and clean the die. Never put your hands near the die when running. In Kenya and Nigeria, extruded snacks and cereals are popular. You can make puffed maize balls, soy-fortified curls, or millet-based rings. Test expansion by measuring the diameter of 10 extrudates. Good expansion is 2-3 times the die diameter. If expansion is low, increase moisture or temperature. If product is too dark, lower temperature. Keep records of settings for each product. Extrusion is fast, continuous, and gives you many shapes from the same blend.

5. Drying and Toasting Methods

After extrusion, flaking, or mixing, cereal needs drying or toasting to reach 3-5% moisture for crunch and shelf life. Drying removes water; toasting adds flavor and color. For drying, use a tray dryer, fluid bed dryer, or solar dryer. A solar dryer with a black mesh tray and a glass cover can dry 20 kg of cereal in 6 hours on a sunny day. For larger scale, a fluid bed dryer at 80C for 10 minutes dries 100 kg per batch. A tray dryer with forced air at 70C takes 30 minutes. Always spread cereal in a thin layer, no more than 3 cm deep. Stir every 10 minutes for even drying. Test moisture by weighing 10 g of sample, drying in an oven at 105C for 3 hours, and weighing again. The weight loss is moisture. For toasting, use a rotary toaster or a belt toaster. A rotary toaster at 200C for 2 minutes gives a golden color and nutty flavor. For flaked cereals, toast at 180C for 3 minutes. For puffed cereals, toast at 220C for 1 minute. In Africa, you can use a modified drum roaster for coffee. Set it to 180C and turn at 30 rpm. Watch for smoke; if it smokes, lower heat. Toasting too long makes bitter cereal. Toasting also kills microbes. After toasting, cool the cereal to room temperature on a clean tray. Never pack hot cereal; condensation makes it soggy. For sugar-coated cereals, dry the coating before toasting. For honey-coated cereals, toast at lower temperature (150C) to avoid burning honey. Store dried cereal in airtight containers or foil bags with a desiccant. A 500 g bag of cereal should have less than 5% moisture. If you live in a humid area, use a dehumidifier in the packing room. Always check moisture before packing. A cheap moisture meter costs $150. Without it, bite a piece: it should snap, not bend. Proper drying and toasting give you a safe, crunchy product that lasts 6 months.

6. Coating and Enrobing Technologies

Coating adds sugar, flavor, color, and nutrients to the outside of cereal. Enrobing covers the cereal completely with a thin layer. For sugar coating, make a syrup with 60% sugar, 30% water, and 10% honey or glucose. Heat to 110C. Use a rotating coating drum (like a cement mixer but food-grade). Put 10 kg of dried cereal in the drum. Spray the syrup at 5 ml per second while the drum turns at 20 rpm. After spraying, add 1% flavor powder (e.g., vanilla, cinnamon) and tumble for 2 minutes. Then dry the coated cereal at 80C for 10 minutes to set the coating. For chocolate coating, melt 1 kg of cocoa butter with 1 kg of cocoa powder and 2 kg of sugar. Temper the chocolate to 32C. Pour over 5 kg of cereal in a drum and tumble until coated. Cool at 15C for 10 minutes. For yogurt coating, mix 1 kg of yogurt powder with 500 g of sugar and 200 ml of water. Spray and dry. For savory coatings, use 2% salt, 1% onion powder, and 1% garlic powder in a starch slurry. Spray and toast at 150C for 5 minutes. For enrobing with a continuous curtain, use an enrober machine. A small enrober costs $5,000. It pumps coating over a wire mesh belt carrying cereal. Excess coating falls through and is recycled. For small-scale, hand-dipping works: hold a handful of cereal in a sieve, dip into coating, and shake off excess. Always coat after drying and toasting. Never coat wet cereal. Coatings add 10-30% weight. Keep coating room at 20C and 50% humidity. Too humid, coating sticks. Too dry, coating cracks. In Africa, popular coatings are honey, sugar, chocolate, and moringa. Moringa coating adds nutrition. Use 5% moringa leaf powder in the syrup. Test coating adhesion by dropping 10 pieces from 30 cm onto a hard surface. If coating flakes off, add more syrup or dry longer. Store coated cereal in foil bags to prevent moisture. Coating makes cereal attractive and tasty, but it also adds cost. Calculate your coating cost per kg before selling.

<h3>Stage 1 &amp; 2: Cleaning and Conditioning Protecting Yield and Equipment</h3>

<p>In African processing, grain arrives with stones, sand, chaff, metal fragments, and sometimes aflatoxin-contaminated kernels. Cleaning is not optional it protects your mill, your customer, and your brand. A typical cleaning train for maize, sorghum, or millet includes aspiration (removes light chaff), sieving (removes oversized and undersized material), destoning (removes stones by density), and magnetic separation (removes ferrous metal). For small and medium enterprises (SMEs), a combined destoner-aspirator unit is often the most cost-effective first investment because stone damage to mill plates is a leading cause of unplanned downtime.</p>

<p>Conditioning (tempering) adjusts grain moisture to optimise milling. For maize dry milling, target moisture is typically 1416%; for sorghum and millet, 1214% is common. Tempering time ranges from 15 minutes to several hours depending on grain hardness and desired particle size. In humid African climates, conditioning is often about reducing moisture rather than adding it, especially before storage. Under-conditioned grain produces excessive fines and broken grits; over-conditioned grain gums up mills and reduces throughput.</p>

<h3>Stage 3 &amp; 4: Milling and Grinding The Yield-Critical Step</h3>

<p>Milling is where your raw material becomes saleable product, and where most SMEs lose money. Hammer mills are common across Africa for their low capital cost and versatility, but they produce a wider particle size distribution than roller mills. For breakfast cereals, consistency matters: uneven particle size leads to uneven cooking, uneven drying, and rejected batches. If you are producing extruded flakes or puffed cereals, invest in a roller mill or a pin mill with a classifier, or at minimum add a sifter to recycle oversize material.</p>

<p>Energy consumption is a major cost driver. In many African countries, electricity tariffs for agro-processing range from USD 0.100.25 per kWh, and milling can account for 3050% of total processing energy. Regular maintenance of mill plates or hammers sharpening or replacing every 200400 tonnes of throughput can reduce energy use by 1020% and improve particle size consistency.</p>

<strong style="color:#c62828;"> SAFETY WARNING Dust Explosion and Aflatoxin Risk</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; font-size:13px;">Cereal dust (especially from maize, wheat, and rice) is combustible. A dust cloud in a confined space a mill room, a bucket elevator, or a bin can explode if ignited by a spark, hot surface, or static discharge. In African processing facilities, inadequate ventilation and open electrical fittings are common causes of dust explosions. <strong>Always:</strong> install explosion venting on bins and dust collectors; use grounded, dust-tight electrical equipment; enforce no-smoking and hot-work permits; and clean settled dust daily. Separately, maize and groundnuts contaminated with <em>Aspergillus flavus</em> can carry aflatoxins, which are carcinogenic and regulated in export markets. Reject mouldy grain at intake and test finished flour for aflatoxin if supplying formal retail or export buyers.</p>
</div>

<h3>Stage 5 &amp; 6: Mixing, Blending, and Extrusion Cooking</h3>

<p>Mixing and blending determine nutritional consistency and product uniformity. For fortified porridge blends a major growth category in Africa vitamin and mineral premixes must be distributed evenly. A ribbon blender or paddle mixer with a 1015 minute cycle is adequate for most SME volumes. Over-mixing can cause segregation of dense premix particles; under-mixing leaves hot spots that fail fortification compliance tests.</p>

<p>Extrusion cooking is the technology behind many puffed, flaked, and shaped breakfast cereals. A single-screw extruder is the entry point for most African SMEs; twin-screw extruders offer better control for high-fibre or high-protein blends. Key parameters are barrel temperature (120180C), screw speed, feed moisture (1422%), and die design. Extrusion gelatinises starch, which improves digestibility and texture. In Africa, extrusion is increasingly used for instant maize-soy blends, fortified porridges, and snack pellets.</p>

<h3>Stage 7 &amp; 8: Drying and Toasting Texture, Colour, and Shelf Life</h3>

<p>After extrusion or flaking, products must be dried to 35% moisture for shelf stability. Fluidised bed dryers are efficient but capital-intensive; tray and tunnel dryers are common in African SMEs but slower and less uniform. Toasting follows drying and develops colour and flavour through Maillard reactions. Over-toasting creates bitterness and acrylamide; under-toasting leaves a pale, under-flavoured product. Target moisture after drying is typically 34% for flaked cereals and 46% for extruded puffs.</p>

<h3>Stage 9 &amp; 10: Coating, Enrobing, and Packaging</h3>

<p>Coating adds sugar, honey, vitamins, or flavour. A rotating drum coater with a spray system is standard. For vitamin coating, apply after drying to avoid heat degradation. Packaging must protect against moisture and insects the two biggest causes of product rejection in African retail. Multi-layer laminated film with a moisture barrier is recommended; simple polyethylene bags are inadequate for long shelf life in humid markets.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Grain intake:</strong> Test moisture and aflatoxin; reject mouldy or stone-heavy lots before unloading.</li>
<li><strong>Cleaning:</strong> Verify destoner and magnet function daily; a single stone can destroy mill plates.</li>
<li><strong>Conditioning:</strong> Record moisture and tempering time per batch; do not guess.</li>
<li><strong>Milling:</strong> Check particle size weekly with a sieve stack; replace hammers/plates on schedule.</li>
<li><strong>Blending:</strong> Run a 1015 minute cycle and verify premix uniformity with a spot test.</li>
<li><strong>Extrusion:</strong> Log barrel temperature, feed moisture, and screw speed for every batch.</li>
<li><strong>Drying:</strong> Target 35% moisture; calibrate your moisture meter monthly.</li>
<li><strong>Packaging:</strong> Use moisture-barrier film and seal integrity checks; label with batch and date codes.</li>
</ul>
