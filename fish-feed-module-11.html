Lesson 11.1~11 min

Matching Species to Your Water: Tilapia, African Catfish or Carp

In this lesson
  • Compare the temperature, oxygen and water quality tolerance of the four main farmed species
  • Select a species based on your measured site conditions rather than on market fashion
  • Estimate the feed cost and FCR consequences of each species choice

Choosing a species is choosing a set of constraints you will live with for years. Do it against measured water temperature and dry-season water supply, not against whichever fish is fetching a good price this season.

Nile tilapia. Optimal 25-30 C, tolerates 15-35 C, and grows fast above 25 C, reaching 500 g in six to eight months. Extremely tolerant of salinity, so it works in brackish and inland saline water. FCR 1.2-1.6 overall, with grow-out at 1.6-2.0 in the 27-30 C range. It needs dissolved oxygen above 5 mg/L for good growth and ammonia below 0.05 mg/L. It breeds readily in ponds, which is both an advantage for seed supply and a problem for grow-out because uncontrolled recruitment crowds the pond. Grow-out protein 25-32 percent. Global production is about 6.5 million tonnes a year, so the market is deep.

African catfish (Clarias gariepinus). Optimal 20-28 C, tolerates a remarkable 8-42 C. It breathes air, so it survives down to about 1.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen, though it prefers above 4 mg/L. This is the species for farmers with poor water exchange, warm shallow ponds or unreliable aeration. It has the best feed conversion of the tropical species at 1.2-1.4 for fingerlings and 1.4-1.7 at grow-out. Grow-out protein is higher at 28-35 percent, and the fry are hardy. Its cost-optimised diet is the cheapest of the group at USD 0.30-0.50/kg do-it-yourself, partly because fish by-products can carry more of the protein load.

Channel catfish. Optimal 18-32 C, and it grows slowly below 15 C. Its FCR is the weakest of the catfishes at 1.8-2.2 at grow-out. It dominates in the United States and is less relevant in most of Africa, where African catfish fills the same role better.

Common carp. Optimal 18-28 C, tolerates 0-38 C. Herbivorous to omnivorous, so it uses cheaper vegetable-based feeds and tolerates 30-40 percent soluble carbohydrate with 85-95 percent digestibility of gelatinised starch. But it takes two to three years to market size and its grow-out FCR is 2.0-2.5. It suits farmers with plentiful cheap plant material, low cash and patience.

Worked comparison. Suppose you can produce 1,000 kg of fish. At a feed price of USD 0.70/kg:

  • African catfish at FCR 1.5: 1,500 kg of feed, USD 1,050
  • Nile tilapia at FCR 1.8: 1,800 kg of feed, USD 1,260
  • Common carp at FCR 2.2: 2,200 kg of feed, USD 1,540

The carp costs USD 490 more in feed for the same tonne, and takes far longer to get there. That gap only closes if the carp is eating material you would otherwise throw away.

Worked example, Ethiopia. A farmer in the Rift Valley measures pond water at 22 C at dawn and 27 C in the afternoon during the cool season. Tilapia at 22 C is below its optimal band and its FCR will suffer; the reference data shows tilapia FCR worsening to 3.0-5.0 below 20 C. African catfish, optimal at 20-28 C, sits comfortably in his range. Unless his market pays a strong premium for tilapia, catfish is the better technical fit for that site.

One more consideration. Polyculture, meaning tilapia with a small number of African catfish, is widely practised because the catfish eat unplanned tilapia fry and control recruitment while adding their own saleable weight. If your tilapia ponds are full of stunted small fish, this is the field-practical answer.

Finally, check the market before the biology. Ask three local buyers what species and what size they want, what they pay, and how often they buy. A technically perfect species with no buyer is a loss.

Broodstock Conditioning Diets That Raise Fecundity

In this lesson
  • Specify the protein, lipid, vitamin and fatty acid levels a broodstock diet needs above grow-out
  • Quantify the fecundity and larval survival gains from proper conditioning
  • Cost a broodstock diet against the value of the extra fry it produces

Broodstock feed is the highest-return feed on a fish farm, and most small farmers feed their breeders exactly the same pellet as their grow-out fish. That single change is worth more than almost anything else in this course.

What a broodstock diet must be, compared with grow-out:

  • Crude protein 35-40 percent instead of 28-32 percent. This supports vitellogenesis, the process of building egg yolk. Result: 15-30 percent more eggs per spawn than at 28 percent protein.
  • Total lipid 12-18 percent instead of 6-8 percent. Result: 20-40 percent more viable larvae and 10-20 percent higher egg survival.
  • EPA plus DHA at 1.5-2.5 percent instead of 0.3-0.8 percent. These long-chain fats drive prostaglandin synthesis, which triggers spawning, and larval neurological development. Result: 10-20 percent higher spawn frequency and better swim-up success.
  • Vitamin C at 200-300 mg/kg instead of 100 mg/kg. It is transferred into the eggs and primes the larva's immunity. Result: egg mortality down 20-30 percent.
  • Vitamin E at 200-400 mg/kg instead of 100-150. It protects the high level of unsaturated fat in the eggs from oxidation. Result: 10-15 percent higher fecundity and fewer abnormal eggs.
  • Phospholipids or lecithin at 3-5 percent instead of the incidental 0.5-1 percent. These build egg yolk structure and larval nerve tissue. Result: better larval pigmentation and 10-15 percent higher survival.

Add those up. Compared with a maintenance diet, a properly formulated broodstock diet gives 20-40 percent more eggs per female, 10-20 percent better hatch rate, 15-25 percent better larval survival from yolk-sac to free feeding, 10-15 percent faster early larval growth, and reduces deformity rates from 5-10 percent down to 1-3 percent.

Now the economics. A high-performance broodstock diet costs USD 1.50-3.00/kg against USD 0.50-1.20/kg for grow-out feed. That looks expensive per kilogram until you divide it across the fry. Feed cost works out at roughly USD 0.001-0.005 per larva for 100,000 fry produced. Those fry are worth USD 0.03-0.10 each at retail, so USD 3,000-10,000 per 100,000 batch. The reference data puts the return at 300-1,000 percent, and calls it clearly justified for seed production and much less relevant for a grow-out farm that buys its fry in.

Worked example, Nigeria. A hatchery near Abeokuta holds 40 African catfish broodfish averaging 1.2 kg, so 48 kg of broodstock. Over an eight-week conditioning period at an average 100 g of feed per kg of body weight per day, that is 48 x 0.1 = 4.8 kg of feed per day, or 4.8 x 56 days = 269 kg over the period. At USD 2.00/kg for a good broodstock diet that is USD 538, against USD 188 if she fed grow-out pellet at USD 0.70/kg. The extra cost is USD 350. If the improved conditioning raises her output from 200,000 to 250,000 viable fry, that is 50,000 extra fry. Even at the bottom of the value range, USD 0.03 each, that is USD 1,500 against USD 350 spent.

What to do if you cannot buy a broodstock diet. Build one from grow-out feed by adding, per 10 kg of pellet: a higher-protein ingredient such as good fish meal or black soldier fly larvae meal to lift crude protein toward 35-40 percent, oil to raise lipid, and a vitamin premix at the broodstock rate. Fish oil is the one place in this whole course where the reference data recommends paying for it rather than substituting vegetable oil, precisely because EPA and DHA drive spawning. Keep the mixed feed fresh; a high-lipid feed goes rancid fast and rancid feed will undo everything you paid for.

Lesson 11.3~11 min

The Conditioning Calendar From Recovery to Spawning

In this lesson
  • Lay out the five conditioning phases with their durations, diets and feeding rates
  • Calculate daily feed quantities for a broodstock unit at each phase
  • Recognise the physical signs that a female is ready to spawn

Conditioning is a calendar, not a decision. Fish need weeks of the right diet before they will produce good eggs, and a farmer who starts feeding a broodstock diet three days before he wants to spawn has wasted the money.

The five phases:

  1. Pre-conditioning, 4-8 weeks. Diet 28-30 percent crude protein and 6-8 percent lipid. Feed at maintenance, 60-80 g per kg of body weight per day. The aim is baseline health and recovery from the previous spawn.
  2. Conditioning phase 1, 4-6 weeks. Diet 35 percent protein, 12 percent lipid, plus phytoplankton or algae if available. Feed at 80-100 g per kg of body weight per day. Gonads develop, and females begin to show visible swelling.
  3. Conditioning phase 2, 2-4 weeks. Diet 38-40 percent protein, 15-18 percent lipid, with live food if you can get it. Feed at 100-120 g per kg of body weight per day. Egg yolk accumulates and females become opaque-bellied.
  4. Trigger, 3-7 days. Same high-lipid diet, and add live food such as Artemia or chironomid larvae if available. Feed ad libitum, one or two feeds a day. Spawning is then induced by an environmental change: a water temperature drop, a photoperiod change, or relief of crowding.
  5. Recovery after spawning, 2-4 weeks. Diet 30-32 percent protein, 8-10 percent lipid, at maintenance feeding. Tissue repairs and lipid reserves rebuild for the next cycle.

That is roughly twelve to twenty weeks from a rested fish to a spawned one, depending on how long the pre-conditioning takes.

Worked example, Kenya. A hatchery at Sagana holds 30 Nile tilapia females averaging 400 g, so 12 kg of female broodstock, plus 10 males averaging 500 g, so 5 kg. Total 17 kg.

  • Pre-conditioning at 70 g/kg/day: 17 x 0.07 = 1.19 kg feed per day. Over 6 weeks (42 days) that is 50 kg.
  • Conditioning phase 1 at 90 g/kg/day: 17 x 0.09 = 1.53 kg per day. Over 5 weeks (35 days) that is 53.6 kg.
  • Conditioning phase 2 at 110 g/kg/day: 17 x 0.11 = 1.87 kg per day. Over 3 weeks (21 days) that is 39.3 kg.

Total feed for the cycle before the trigger phase is 50 + 53.6 + 39.3 = 142.9 kg. If the pre-conditioning feed is a 30 percent grow-out pellet at USD 0.70/kg (50 x 0.70 = USD 35) and the conditioning feed is a broodstock formulation at USD 2.00/kg (92.9 x 2.00 = USD 185.80), the total is USD 220.80 to bring 30 females into full condition. Judge that against the fry those females will produce and the price fingerlings fetch locally.

What to look for physically. In tilapia and African catfish females, a swollen, soft, rounded abdomen and a reddened, protruding genital papilla indicate ripeness. The belly should feel full but not hard. In males, look for good condition and, in catfish, well-developed body condition rather than any external swelling. Handle broodstock as little as possible and always with wet hands or a wet net; scale and mucus damage from dry handling opens the door to infection at exactly the moment the fish is most stressed.

Three practical cautions. First, do not overfeed during the trigger phase in a pond with poor oxygen, because the extra digestion demand comes at the worst time. Second, keep the broodstock feed fresh, because 15-18 percent lipid goes rancid quickly and rancid feed destroys the vitamin E you are paying for. Third, keep a written record of every spawn: date, female weight, eggs or fry counted, and what she was fed. After three cycles that record will tell you more about your own fish than any manual.

Lesson 11.4~11 min

Feeding Fry and Larvae Through the First Eight Weeks

In this lesson
  • Match pellet or particle size to mouth width at each early life stage
  • Set the correct protein level for fry, early fingerlings and fingerlings
  • Plan a nursery feeding schedule that minimises sorting waste and mortality

The first eight weeks decide how many fingerlings you sell. Fry are small, they cannot digest what adults digest, and they starve inside a day if the particle is the wrong size.

Protein first. Requirements fall steeply as the fish grows:

  • Fry and larvae: Nile tilapia 50-56 percent crude protein; African catfish 45-50 percent; channel catfish 45-50 percent; carp 45-50 percent
  • Fingerlings: tilapia 40-45 percent; African catfish 40-45 percent; channel catfish 35-40 percent; carp 35-42 percent
  • Juveniles: tilapia 30-40 percent; African catfish 35-40 percent
  • Grow-out: tilapia 25-32 percent; African catfish 28-35 percent

The reason is amino acid density, not total protein mass. Fry need a high concentration of the ten indispensable amino acids in a very small volume of food. Tilapia fry need lysine at 1.8-2.0 percent of the diet and methionine plus cystine at 0.9-1.0 percent. Those are hard numbers to hit with a home mix, which is why buying a good commercial starter crumble for the first weeks is usually money well spent even on a low-budget farm.

Particle size. The rule of thumb is that optimal pellet diameter is about 60 percent of the fish's mouth width. Undersized particles cause sorting waste; oversized particles reduce intake and stress the fish.

  1. Fry, 0-2 weeks: mouth width under 1 mm, particle 0.8-1.5 mm, often a fine powder microdiet
  2. Early fingerling, 2-8 weeks: mouth 1-2 mm, particle 1.5-3 mm, crumble texture
  3. Fingerling, 8-12 weeks: mouth 2-3 mm, particle 2-4 mm, often a 50:50 crumble and pellet mix
  4. Juvenile, 12-24 weeks: mouth 3-5 mm, particle 3-6 mm, standard pellet with under 5 percent sorting waste
  5. Grow-out, over 24 weeks: mouth 5-8 mm, particle 4-8 mm, with 6-8 mm preferred for tilapia and 5-6 mm for catfish

Energy and lipid. Tilapia fry need 3,300-3,500 kcal digestible energy per kg with 8-12 percent lipid and 0.8-1.5 percent EPA plus DHA. Channel catfish fry need 10-12 percent lipid. Fry diets are simply richer in every direction, which is also why they spoil faster and must be bought in small, fresh quantities.

Carbohydrate limits matter here. Catfish fry are effectively carnivorous and digest gelatinised starch at only 50-70 percent and raw starch at 20-40 percent, so keep crude fibre under 10 percent and soluble carbohydrate under 15 percent in a fry diet. Tilapia fingerlings tolerate more, up to 10-15 percent fibre and 20-30 percent soluble carbohydrate.

Worked example, Uganda. A hatchery at Mukono raises 60,000 African catfish fry. Over the first four weeks they average about 1 g and are fed roughly 10 percent of body weight per day. Standing biomass rises from almost nothing to 60,000 x 1 g = 60 kg. Taking an average biomass across the period of about 25 kg, feed use is 25 x 0.10 x 28 days = 70 kg of starter feed. At an assumed starter crumble price of USD 1.50/kg, which is higher than grow-out feed because of its protein and vitamin density, that is USD 105. Spread over 60,000 fry it is USD 0.00175 per fry. Against a fingerling value of USD 0.03-0.10, the feed cost of the nursery phase is small, and skimping on it is the false economy that costs a hatchery its reputation.

Management points that matter more than the feed brand: feed little and often, four to eight times a day for fry; keep the nursery shaded and stable in temperature; grade the fry at three to four weeks because catfish fry in particular will cannibalise smaller siblings; and keep dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L, which is the optimum for both tilapia and channel catfish fry.

Fingerling Grading, Transport and Nursery Management

In this lesson
  • Grade fingerlings by size and explain the growth and survival benefit
  • Prepare fish correctly for transport to reduce mortality and ammonia build-up
  • Set nursery stocking and feeding to deliver a uniform, saleable fingerling

A hatchery is judged on two things: how many fingerlings survive the journey, and how uniform they are when they arrive. Both are management, not luck.

Grading. Fish populations spread in size, and the spread widens because bigger fish feed better and dominate the feeding point. Left alone, a nursery pond gives you a few large fish, a mass of mediums and a tail of stunted fish that will never be saleable. Grade at three to four weeks and again before sale, using a simple grader box or a graded net. The benefits are concrete: you can feed each grade the correct particle size instead of a compromise size, competition drops so FCR improves, and in catfish you dramatically reduce cannibalism. Recall that high stocking density alone costs 10-15 percent of FCR through stress and competition, and uneven sizes make that worse.

Transport. This is where most fingerling mortality happens. The principles:

  1. Stop feeding 24 hours before transport. A fish with a full gut excretes ammonia into the transport water and consumes more oxygen. This one free step probably saves more fingerlings than anything else.
  2. Move in the cool of the day, early morning or evening, and keep the containers shaded.
  3. Use oxygenated bags or aerated tanks. Fingerlings are small but they are packed densely, so oxygen is the binding constraint.
  4. Add salt at 1-2 g per litre of transport water. It reduces nitrite toxicity by around half and reduces osmotic stress from handling.
  5. Acclimatise on arrival. Float the bag to equalise temperature, then mix pond water in gradually over 15-30 minutes before release. A sudden temperature or chemistry change after a long journey is what kills fish that survived the trip.
  6. Handle with wet hands and wet nets only. Dry handling strips mucus, which is the fish's first defence.

Worked example, Tanzania. A hatchery at Mwanza sells 20,000 tilapia fingerlings to a farmer four hours away. At an assumed price of USD 0.05 per fingerling the consignment is worth USD 1,000. A careless transport losing 20 percent costs USD 200. A disciplined transport losing 5 percent costs USD 50. The difference, USD 150, is recovered by measures that cost almost nothing: a day of not feeding, an early start, and about 60 kg of salt across the load if the transport volume is large, or a few kilograms for typical bagged consignments. Even at an assumed USD 0.15/kg for coarse salt, the salt for a modest transport volume is a few dollars.

Nursery management to produce a good fingerling:

  • Keep dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L, which is the fry optimum for tilapia and channel catfish.
  • Feed four to eight times a day in small amounts, so that small fish always find food and nothing sits uneaten.
  • Keep the water warm and stable. Tilapia below 20 C essentially stop growing efficiently, with FCR falling to 3.0-5.0.
  • Watch ammonia closely. Nursery ponds are stocked densely relative to their volume, and fry are more sensitive than adults.
  • Do not hold fingerlings past their size window. A fingerling that outgrows the size your buyers want becomes an expensive fish to feed and a harder one to sell.

Record keeping is what turns this into a business. For each batch, record the spawn date, number of fry stocked into the nursery, feed used, mortalities, grading dates, number sold and the price. Your survival percentage from spawn to sale is the single number that tells you whether the hatchery is working.

Costing and Pricing a Fingerling Enterprise

In this lesson
  • Build a cost-per-fingerling calculation including broodstock feed, nursery feed and mortality
  • Compare hatchery returns with grow-out returns on the same farm
  • Decide whether to produce, buy or do both based on your own numbers

Fingerling production is often the most profitable square metre on a small fish farm, but only if you count properly. Most farmers who claim their hatchery is profitable have never allocated the broodstock feed.

The cost sheet has five lines.

  1. Broodstock feed. Take your broodstock biomass, the feeding rates from the conditioning calendar, and the days in each phase.
  2. Nursery and fry feed. Starter crumble is expensive per kilogram but small in quantity.
  3. Mortality. Losses from spawn to saleable fingerling. Everything you spent on the fish that died is carried by the fish that lived.
  4. Labour and water. Even if you do not pay yourself, put a figure on it, because a hatchery is daily work.
  5. Fixed costs. Hapas, nets, tanks, grading equipment, spread over the batches they will serve.

Worked example, Kenya, one cycle. A farm at Sagana conditions 30 tilapia females and 10 males, 17 kg of broodstock in total.

  • Broodstock feed from the conditioning calendar: 142.9 kg, costing USD 220.80 as calculated earlier
  • Fry and nursery feed: assume the batch yields 40,000 fry stocked into nursery and uses 45 kg of starter crumble at an assumed USD 1.50/kg = USD 67.50
  • Survival from stocked fry to saleable fingerling: assume 70 percent, so 28,000 saleable fingerlings
  • Labour: assume 30 days at an assumed USD 3.00/day = USD 90
  • Equipment allocated to this batch: assume USD 40

Total cost = 220.80 + 67.50 + 90 + 40 = USD 418.30 Cost per saleable fingerling = 418.30 / 28,000 = USD 0.0149, about 1.5 US cents

Against a fingerling value of USD 0.03-0.10 each, at even the bottom of that range the batch is worth 28,000 x 0.03 = USD 840, giving a margin of USD 421.70 on one cycle. At USD 0.05 it is USD 1,400 revenue and USD 981.70 margin.

Now test the assumptions, because this is where the honesty lives.

  • If survival is 40 percent instead of 70 percent, saleable fingerlings fall to 16,000 and cost per fingerling rises to USD 0.026. Still viable at USD 0.03, but the margin has almost vanished.
  • If you cannot sell 28,000 fingerlings and only move 10,000, cost per sold fingerling is USD 0.042 and at USD 0.03 you lose money.
  • If you feed grow-out pellet instead of a broodstock diet, you save USD 150 on feed but the reference data indicates you would produce 20-40 percent fewer eggs and 15-25 percent fewer surviving larvae. On this batch a 25 percent reduction is 7,000 fewer fingerlings, worth USD 210 at USD 0.03. The saving costs you more than it saves.

The two variables that decide everything are survival rate and market offtake. That is why the capstone for this module asks you to check with three real buyers before you build a hatchery.

Compare with grow-out on the same farm. Producing 1,000 kg of tilapia at FCR 1.8 uses 1,800 kg of feed costing USD 1,260 at USD 0.70/kg. At an assumed farm-gate price of USD 2.00/kg that is USD 2,000 of revenue and USD 740 gross of feed, before fingerling purchase, labour and everything else. The hatchery in this example produced a comparable gross margin from a fraction of the water and feed, but it demands daily skilled attention and a reliable buyer network. Many farms run both, using their own fingerlings and selling the surplus.

Pricing. Set your price against your real cost per fingerling, your local competition and the value you offer: uniform size, known species, mono-sex if you can produce it, and a survival guarantee on delivery. Uniformity and reliable supply are what buyers pay a premium for, and both come from the grading and transport discipline in the previous lesson.

Do this today: write down your own four numbers on one line: how many fry you stocked last batch, how many fingerlings you sold, what you spent on broodstock and fry feed, and what price you got. Divide cost by fingerlings sold. That figure is your real cost per fingerling.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Which species can survive at around 1.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen?

African catfish is an air-breather, which lets it survive near-anoxic water, although it grows best above 4 mg/L.

2. Nile tilapia grows fastest at:

Its optimal band is 25-30 C, reaching 500 g in six to eight months in that range.

3. At FCR 2.2 versus 1.5, producing 1,000 kg of fish with feed at USD 0.70/kg costs extra:

2,200 kg minus 1,500 kg is 700 kg of feed, and 700 x 0.70 = USD 490.

4. Stocking a few African catfish into a tilapia pond is commonly done to:

The catfish predate on unwanted fry, preventing the crowding and stunting caused by uncontrolled breeding.

5. A site measuring 22 C at dawn in the cool season is best suited to:

African catfish is optimal at 20-28 C, whereas 22 C is below tilapia's 25-30 C optimum.

6. Broodstock crude protein should be raised to about:

35-40 percent supports vitellogenesis and yields 15-30 percent more eggs per spawn than a 28 percent diet.

7. Raising broodstock dietary lipid to 12-18 percent mainly delivers:

Lipid drives HUFA deposition in eggs, improving larval viability and egg survival.

8. EPA and DHA matter for broodstock because they:

At 1.5-2.5 percent of the diet they increase spawn frequency 10-20 percent and improve swim-up success.

9. Feed cost per larva for a well-conditioned broodstock unit is roughly:

Spread across 100,000 fry, even a USD 2-3/kg diet costs only fractions of a cent per larva.

10. The one place fish oil is clearly worth buying rather than substituting is:

Grow-out can use vegetable oil blends, but broodstock need preformed EPA and DHA for spawning and larval quality.

11. The pre-conditioning phase is fed at approximately:

Pre-conditioning is maintenance feeding at 60-80 g/kg/day on a 28-30 percent protein diet.

12. Conditioning phase 2 targets a dietary lipid level of:

Phase 2 uses 38-40 percent protein and 15-18 percent lipid to drive egg yolk accumulation.

13. For 17 kg of broodstock fed at 110 g/kg/day, the daily ration is:

17 x 0.11 = 1.87 kg per day.

14. Spawning is typically triggered by:

After conditioning, spawning is induced by an environmental cue over 3-7 days alongside high-lipid feeding.

15. Why must a 15-18 percent lipid broodstock feed be kept fresh?

High-lipid feeds oxidise fast, and oxidised fat depletes vitamin E and reduces feed intake.

16. Nile tilapia fry require crude protein of about:

Fry need 50-56 percent because they need very high amino acid density in a small volume of food.

17. Optimal pellet diameter is about what fraction of mouth width?

About 60 percent of mouth width balances intake against sorting waste.

18. Catfish fry diets should keep soluble carbohydrate below:

Carnivorous fry digest starch poorly, at 50-70 percent even when gelatinised, so carbohydrate is limited to under 15 percent.

19. Grading catfish fry at three to four weeks is important mainly because:

Size spread leads to cannibalism in catfish, so grading protects survival and evens out growth.

20. For 60,000 fry, a nursery feed cost of USD 105 works out at about:

105 divided by 60,000 is USD 0.00175, tiny against a fingerling value of USD 0.03-0.10.

21. Fingerlings should be fasted before transport for about:

A 24-hour fast empties the gut, cutting ammonia excretion and oxygen demand during the journey.

22. Salt in transport water at 1-2 g/L primarily:

Chloride competes with nitrite at the gill, halving toxicity, and it eases osmotic stress.

23. Grading fingerlings improves results mainly because:

Uniform sizes allow correct pellet sizing, reduce competition and, in catfish, reduce cannibalism.

24. On arrival, fingerlings should be:

Gradual temperature and chemistry equalisation prevents shock in fish already stressed by travel.

25. Reducing transport mortality from 20 percent to 5 percent on a 20,000 fingerling load worth USD 0.05 each saves:

20 percent of 20,000 is 4,000 fish (USD 200) versus 5 percent, 1,000 fish (USD 50); the difference is USD 150.

26. The single largest driver of cost per saleable fingerling is usually:

Everything spent on fish that die is carried by the survivors, so survival dominates unit cost.

27. A batch costing USD 418.30 that yields 28,000 saleable fingerlings has a unit cost of about:

418.30 divided by 28,000 is about USD 0.0149, roughly one and a half cents.

28. Feeding broodstock on grow-out pellet to save money typically results in:

Conditioning diets raise fecundity and larval survival substantially, so the feed saving costs more than it saves.

29. Before investing in a hatchery, the most important thing to verify is:

Market offtake and survival rate decide the economics; unsold fingerlings turn a profitable batch into a loss.

30. What do fingerling buyers usually pay a premium for?

Uniformity and reliability come from grading and transport discipline and are the hatchery's main commercial advantage.

Module 11 capstone

Design and cost a fingerling enterprise for your own farm. Start by matching species to your site: record your water temperature at dawn and mid-afternoon for two weeks, note your dry-season water availability, and check three local buyers for what species and size they want and what they pay. Then select one species and write a full production plan covering broodstock numbers, the conditioning diet you will feed with local ingredients priced in USD, the twelve-week conditioning calendar with dates, your nursery arrangement and the pellet sizes you will need for the first eight weeks. Build a costing sheet showing broodstock feed cost, fry feed cost, labour, mortality assumptions and cost per fingerling produced, then compare that with the price fingerlings actually sell for locally. Finish with a go or no-go decision and state clearly which assumption, if it is wrong, would change the answer.
