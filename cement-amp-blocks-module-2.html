Lessons

1. Types of cement available in local markets (OPC, PPC, etc.)

When you go to buy cement for making blocks, you will find different types. The most common are OPC (Ordinary Portland Cement) and PPC (Portland Pozzolana Cement). OPC is pure cement and gains strength quickly. It is good for high-strength blocks and for use in areas with a lot of water, like foundations. PPC is mixed with pozzolana (like fly ash or volcanic ash) and is cheaper. It gains strength slower but is more resistant to chemicals and is better for plastering and general block making. In many African markets, you will also see 'masonry cement' or 'rapid hardening cement'. Rapid hardening is more expensive and not needed for blocks. For block making, PPC is often the best choice because it is affordable and works well. But check the label: it should say 'Portland Pozzolana Cement' and have a strength grade like 32.5 or 42.5. The number means the compressive strength in megapascals (MPa) after 28 days. For load-bearing blocks, use 42.5. For non-load-bearing, 32.5 is fine. Also, beware of fake cement. Buy from reputable dealers and look for the manufacturer's logo and a production date. In some countries, cement is sold in 50 kg bags. A bag of OPC typically costs more than PPC. If you are making blocks for a small house, PPC 32.5 is usually sufficient. If you are making blocks for a commercial building, use OPC 42.5. Remember, the type of cement affects how much water you need and how fast the blocks cure. Always follow the manufacturer's recommendations.

2. How to check cement quality before buying: lumps, date, and packaging

Before you pay for cement, you must check its quality. Poor cement will make weak blocks that crumble. First, look at the packaging. The bag should be strong, not torn, and printed with the manufacturer's name, cement type, strength grade, and production date. If the date is older than 3 months, the cement may have absorbed moisture and lost strength. Ideally, buy cement that is less than 1 month old. Second, feel the bag. It should be soft and loose, not hard or lumpy. If you squeeze the bag and it feels like a rock, the cement has set. Walk away. Third, open a bag if the seller allows. Good cement should be a fine, smooth powder, like flour. If you see lumps, even small ones, it means moisture got in. You can also do a simple test: take a handful of cement and squeeze it in your fist. When you open your hand, it should fall apart easily. If it stays in a lump, it's bad. Another test: rub a little cement between your fingers. It should feel smooth, not gritty. Gritty cement has sand or dirt mixed in. Also, check for a strong smell. Fresh cement has a slightly earthy smell. If it smells musty or like chemicals, it's old. Finally, buy from a shop that stores cement on pallets, not directly on the floor. The floor can transfer moisture. If the bags are stacked against a wall, the bottom bags may be damp. Ask the seller how long the cement has been in the shop. If it's been there for months, find another supplier. Remember, good cement is the backbone of strong blocks. Don't gamble with bad cement.

3. Sourcing sand: river sand vs. pit sand and which is best for blocks

Sand is a key ingredient in blocks. It gives them strength and shape. There are two main types: river sand and pit sand. River sand is found in riverbeds. It has been washed by water, so it is usually clean and free of clay. The grains are round and smooth. This makes it easy to mix with cement and water. River sand is excellent for making blocks because it produces a smooth finish and good workability. However, river sand can be expensive, especially if you are far from a river. Pit sand is dug from the ground. It has sharp, angular grains. This can make stronger blocks because the grains interlock. But pit sand often contains clay and silt, which weaken the block if not washed. Pit sand is usually cheaper and more available. For block making, river sand is generally preferred, but if you use pit sand, you must wash it or test it for clay. In many parts of Africa, river sand is scarce, so pit sand is common. You can also find 'plaster sand' which is fine, and 'concrete sand' which is coarser. For blocks, use concrete sand or a mix of river and pit sand. The best sand for blocks has a mix of particle sizes: some fine, some coarse. This fills voids and makes a dense block. Avoid sand with too much clay. You can test by putting sand in a bottle with water, shaking it, and letting it settle. Clay will form a layer on top. If the clay layer is more than 10% of the total, the sand is not good for blocks unless you wash it. Also, avoid sand with salt (from sea sand) because salt attracts moisture and weakens cement. So, choose river sand if available, but test any sand before using it.

4. Testing sand for clay and silt content using a simple bottle test

Clay and silt in sand weaken your blocks. They prevent cement from binding properly. You can test your sand with a simple bottle test. You need a clear glass bottle or jar, water, salt, and a sample of sand. Fill the bottle about one-third with sand. Add water until the bottle is about two-thirds full. Add a teaspoon of salt. Salt helps clay particles settle faster. Shake the bottle vigorously for one minute. Then set it down and wait for 24 hours. The sand will settle at the bottom, silt in the middle, and clay on top. After 24 hours, measure the layers. If the clay layer is more than 10% of the total height, the sand has too much clay. For example, if the total settled material is 10 cm and the clay layer is 1.5 cm, that's 15% clayâtoo much. You can also use a ruler. If the clay is more than 10%, you must wash the sand. To wash sand, put it in a bucket, add water, and stir. Let it settle for a few seconds, then pour off the muddy water. Repeat until the water is clear. Another quick test: put some sand in a jar, add water, and shake. If the water becomes very cloudy and stays cloudy for hours, there is a lot of clay. If it clears quickly, the sand is clean. Also, rub wet sand between your fingers. If it feels slippery and sticky, it has clay. If it feels gritty, it's good. Remember, clay acts like a sponge and sucks up water, so your blocks will need more water and will be weaker. Always test your sand before starting a block-making project. It takes 24 hours but saves you from building with bad materials.

5. Choosing the right stone or gravel size for strong blocks

For strong blocks, you need the right size of stone or gravel. In block making, we often use coarse aggregate, which is crushed stone or gravel. The size matters. For solid blocks, the aggregate should be no larger than 20 mm (about the size of a small marble). For hollow blocks, you can use up to 25 mm. If the stones are too big, they create weak spots and the block may crack. If they are too small, you use more cement and the block may be less strong. The best aggregate for blocks is a mix of sizes: from fine dust to 20 mm. This is called 'well-graded' aggregate. It fills voids and makes a dense block. In many African markets, you can buy 'chippings' or 'crushed stone' in different sizes. For blocks, ask for 3/4 inch (19 mm) or 1/2 inch (12.5 mm) chippings. You can also use river gravel, but it should be clean and free of clay. Test the aggregate by washing a sample. If the water is dirty, wash the gravel before use. Also, avoid stones that are flat or elongated; they weaken the block. Round stones are better. If you are making blocks by hand, you can use a mix of 50% 20 mm stones and 50% 10 mm stones. For machine-made blocks, you can use up to 25 mm. Remember, the aggregate should be hard and durable. Do not use soft stones like sandstone or shale. Also, avoid stones with dust or dirt. If you can, sieve the aggregate to remove fines. A good ratio for block making is 1 part cement, 3 parts sand, and 3 parts aggregate by volume. But this can vary. Always test your mix by making a few blocks and checking their strength after 7 and 28 days. If they break easily, adjust the aggregate size or ratio.

6. Storing cement and aggregates to prevent moisture damage

Cement and aggregates must be stored properly to keep them dry and clean. Moisture is the enemy of cement. If cement gets wet, it starts to set and becomes useless. So, store cement in a dry, well-ventilated place. Keep it off the floor. Use wooden pallets or a raised platform. Stack bags at least 10 cm away from walls. Do not stack more than 10 bags high, or the bottom bags will compress and harden. Use the oldest cement first. If you buy cement in bags, keep them sealed. Once you open a bag, use it within a few days. If you must store opened cement, put it in an airtight container. For aggregates like sand and gravel, store them on a clean, hard surface, like a concrete slab. Do not store them on bare ground, because dirt and clay can mix in. Cover them with a tarpaulin or plastic sheet to keep off rain. But allow some air circulation to prevent mold. If you store sand in a pile, keep it covered but not sealed tight. Also, separate different sizes of aggregate. Do not mix sand and gravel. If you have space, build a simple storage shed with a roof and open sides. This protects from rain and sun. In humid areas, you can put a moisture absorber like quicklime in the storage area. But keep it away from cement. Check your cement regularly. If you see lumps, use it immediately for non-critical work, like filling, or throw it away. Never use lumpy cement for blocks. For aggregates, check for dirt and clay. If they get dirty, wash them before use. Good storage saves money and ensures strong blocks.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Understanding Cement and Aggregates

In African blockyards, most failures blamed on "bad cement" actually start with poor material selection, contamination, or moisture damage before a single block is cast. This lesson gives you a field-ready reference for choosing cement, checking quality on delivery, sourcing and testing sand, selecting aggregate, and storing materials so your investment does not turn into rubble. The goal is simple: buy right, store right, and protect your margin.

Types of cement available in local markets

Across Africa, the most common cements are OPC (Ordinary Portland Cement), PPC (Portland Pozzolana Cement), and blended cements with limestone or slag. OPC gives faster early strength and is widely used for structural blocks. PPC uses pozzolana (volcanic ash or fly ash) and offers better long-term durability and sulfate resistance, often at lower cost. In East and Southern Africa, PPC is common; in West Africa, OPC 42.5 and 32.5 are widely sold. For block making, CEM I 42.5 or CEM II/A-V 42.5 are reliable choices. Avoid unknown "bulk" cement without a grade or manufacturer mark.

How to check cement quality before buying

Do not accept cement on trust. Check the bag date, look for lumps, and confirm packaging integrity. Cement older than 3 months loses strength; older than 6 months is high risk. Bags should be printed with grade and standard mark. If the bag is torn, damp, or caked, reject it. A quick hand test: squeeze a handful â good cement should flow like flour, not clump. In humid markets, always buy from a supplier with high turnover.

SAFETY WARNING:

Cement dust is hazardous. Wear a dust mask, goggles, and gloves when handling. Avoid skin contact â wet cement causes severe burns. Keep bags away from children and animals. Never use cement that has been wet or contains hard lumps, as it can cause structural failure and injury.

Sourcing sand: river sand vs pit sand

River sand is usually cleaner, with rounded grains and less clay, making it the preferred choice for strong blocks. Pit sand (excavated from land) often contains clay, silt, and organic matter, which weakens the bond. However, well-graded pit sand can work if washed and tested. In many African cities, river sand is scarce and expensive; pit sand is cheaper but must be tested. For load-bearing blocks, use coarse, clean sand with less than 5% silt. Avoid beach sand with high salt content unless washed.

Testing sand for clay and silt: the bottle test

Use a clear bottle or jar. Fill one-third with sand, add water to two-thirds, add a pinch of salt, and shake vigorously for one minute. Let it settle for 1 hour. Sand settles first, silt next, clay last. If the clay/silt layer is more than 5â8% of total height, the sand is too dirty for structural blocks. Wash it or source better sand. Repeat the test for every new delivery. This simple test prevents costly block failures.

Choosing the right stone or gravel size

For solid blocks, use coarse aggregate of 10â20 mm. For hollow blocks, 10â15 mm is ideal. Larger stones (25 mm+) create voids and weak points. Aggregate must be clean, hard, and free of clay, silt, and organic matter. Crushed stone gives better strength than rounded gravel. In many African markets, 3/4-inch (19 mm) crushed stone is the standard. Always check for dust coating â wash if necessary.

Storing cement and aggregates to prevent moisture damage

Cement is hygroscopic â it absorbs moisture from the air. Store bags on wooden pallets, at least 150 mm off the ground, and 300 mm away from walls. Keep the store dry, ventilated, and covered. Stack bags no more than 10 high to avoid compaction. Use oldest stock first (FIFO). Aggregates should be stored on a hard, clean surface, covered with tarpaulin to prevent rain and contamination. Separate sand and stone to avoid mixing. In coastal or humid areas, use within 4â6 weeks.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Check cement date: reject bags older than 3 months or with hard lumps.
  • Confirm grade and standard mark on every bag (e.g., CEM I 42.5).
  • Do the bottle test on every sand delivery; silt must be under 5%.
  • Choose river sand for structural blocks; wash pit sand if needed.
  • Use 10â20 mm clean crushed stone; avoid clay-coated aggregate.
  • Store cement on pallets, off ground, dry, and covered.
  • Use FIFO: oldest cement first, never mix old and new bags.
  • Cover aggregates with tarpaulin; keep sand and stone separate.
