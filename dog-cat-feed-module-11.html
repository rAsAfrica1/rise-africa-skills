Lesson 11.1~12 min

Pre-Breeding Condition and Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Assess breeding condition before mating instead of correcting it afterwards
  • Switch a bitch or queen onto a growth and reproduction profile at the right time
  • Cost an eight-week pre-breeding feeding period at real local prices

The cheapest reproductive nutrition you will ever sell is the food an animal eats before she is mated. Once a bitch is pregnant, her body is committed; once she is lactating, her intake is already at its physical limit. Condition put on or taken off in the eight weeks before mating is the part of the cycle you can still influence cheaply and safely.

Start with the profile, not the packaging. AAFCO publishes two nutrient profiles for each species. The adult maintenance profile keeps a healthy adult ticking over. The growth and reproduction profile is the one a breeding female must be on, and the differences are real. For dogs, crude protein rises from 18.0 to 22.5 percent of dry matter and crude fat from 5.5 to 8.5 percent. Calcium rises from 0.5 to 1.2 percent and phosphorus from 0.4 to 1.0 percent, with the calcium to phosphorus ratio held between 1:1 and 2:1. Lysine rises from 0.63 to 0.90 percent. For cats, protein rises from 26.0 to 30.0 percent and calcium from 0.6 to 1.0 percent, while crude fat stays at 9.0 percent because the cat minimum never drops for adults in the first place.

Cats carry four extra non-negotiables into breeding, and they are the same four that make cats obligate carnivores. Taurine at 0.10 percent of dry matter in a dry extruded food or 0.20 percent in a canned food, because taurine deficiency in cats causes reproductive failure as well as retinal degeneration and dilated cardiomyopathy. Preformed vitamin A at 6668 IU per kilogram for growth and reproduction, double the adult figure of 3332, because cats cannot cleave beta-carotene into retinol. Arachidonic acid at 0.02 percent, which is absent from vegetable oils. Arginine at 1.24 percent, well above the dog figure. A plant-heavy ration will not deliver these. For a breeding queen this is not a preference argument, it is a formulation limit.

Now the arithmetic. Take a 20 kg bitch you intend to mate in two months. RER is 70 x 20^0.75. 20^0.75 is 9.46, so RER is 70 x 9.46, which is 662 kcal per day. An intact adult dog sits at 1.8 x RER, so 662 x 1.8 = 1192 kcal per day. On a dry food at the mid-range density of 3800 kcal per kg, that is 1192 divided by 3800 = 0.314 kg, or 314 grams per day. At a local price of USD 1.30 per kg, her feed costs 0.314 x 1.30 = USD 0.41 per day. Across a 56-day pre-breeding period she eats about 17.6 kg costing USD 22.90. Compare that with the cost of a failed mating, a resorbed litter or a thin dam going into lactation, and the pre-breeding spend is the best value in the whole cycle.

Condition is assessed with your hands, not with a formula. Most veterinary practice uses a nine-point body condition score, and you should learn it from the vet who will be attending the litter. Our verified nutrient reference does not contain a body condition table, so do not quote body-fat percentages you cannot source. What you can do is weigh the animal on the same scale each week, feel the ribs and the waist, and write the numbers down. A female who is visibly overweight at mating should be brought down before mating, using the weight-loss factor of 1.0 x RER, not during pregnancy.

A kennel outside Nakuru, Kenya running six breeding bitches switched all of them onto a growth and reproduction formulation eight weeks before their planned matings instead of at confirmation of pregnancy. The extra feed cost across six dogs for eight weeks was about USD 137. They stopped buying a separate 'conditioning supplement' that cost more than that and had no stated analysis.

One firm boundary before you go further. A pre-breeding veterinary check, including vaccination status, parasite control and any decision about supplements, belongs to a veterinarian. In particular, do not add calcium to a breeding female's ration on your own judgement at any point in the cycle. The reasons for that are in the next two lessons, and they are serious.

Lesson 11.2~12 min

Pregnancy Nutrition and Fetal Development

In this lesson
  • Split gestation into its two energy phases and calculate feed for each
  • Explain why calcium supplementation during pregnancy is a veterinary decision
  • Cost a full gestation ration for a bitch and for a queen

Pregnancy in the dog and the cat follows two different energy shapes, and getting them the wrong way round is one of the most common feeding errors in small-scale breeding.

In the bitch, energy demand barely moves for most of the pregnancy and then jumps. The published factors are 1.8 x RER for the first 42 days and 3.0 x RER for the last 21 days. Fetal growth is back-loaded, so overfeeding early simply makes a fat dam who then struggles at whelping. In the queen, demand rises steadily instead: the factor at breeding is 1.6 x RER and at parturition it is 2.0 x RER, with the increase spread across the whole pregnancy. A queen gains weight from mating onward. A bitch should not.

Work it through for a 20 kg bitch. RER is 70 x 20^0.75 = 70 x 9.46 = 662 kcal per day. First 42 days: 662 x 1.8 = 1192 kcal, which on a 3800 kcal per kg dry food is 1192 divided by 3800 = 314 grams a day. Last 21 days: 662 x 3.0 = 1986 kcal, which is 1986 divided by 3800 = 523 grams a day. That is a 67 percent increase in food volume in the final three weeks, arriving exactly when the uterus is pressing on the stomach. This is why late-gestation feeding is split into three or four small meals a day, and why energy density matters. A dry food at the top of the published range, 4800 kcal per kg, delivers the same 1986 kcal in 414 grams instead of 523.

Now the queen. A 4 kg queen has an RER of 70 x 4^0.75 = 70 x 2.83 = 198 kcal per day. At breeding, 198 x 1.6 = 317 kcal. At parturition, 198 x 2.0 = 396 kcal. On a 3800 kcal per kg dry food that moves from 83 grams to 104 grams a day. On a wet food at about 1000 kcal per kg it moves from 317 grams to 396 grams, which is nearly five 85 gram pouches a day, and that is worth knowing before you promise a wet feeding plan.

The cost picture over a full gestation of roughly 63 days. Bitch: 42 days at 314 g is 13.2 kg, plus 21 days at 523 g is 11.0 kg, giving 24.2 kg. At USD 1.30 per kg that is USD 31.50 for the whole pregnancy. Queen: about 63 days averaging 93 g is 5.9 kg of dry food, which at USD 2.10 per kg for a cat formulation is USD 12.30. Those are small numbers. Breeders who try to save money here are saving cents and risking a litter.

The diet itself must meet the growth and reproduction profile throughout: dog 22.5 percent protein, 8.5 percent fat, 1.2 percent calcium, 1.0 percent phosphorus, ratio between 1:1 and 2:1; cat 30.0 percent protein, 9.0 percent fat, 1.0 percent calcium, 0.8 percent phosphorus, plus the taurine, arachidonic acid, preformed vitamin A and arginine minima covered in the previous lesson. Watch the ceilings too. The AAFCO dog vitamin D maximum is 3000 IU per kilogram of dry matter, cut from 5000 in 2016 after Great Dane puppies fed 4000 IU per kilogram showed disrupted endochondral ossification. The EU legal ceiling is lower still at 2270 IU per kilogram of dry matter, and for cats in reproduction the EU vitamin A ceiling is 33,333 IU per 100 g DM. Vitamin D poisoning in the real world comes from premix and formulation error, not from raw ingredients.

Now the boundary that matters most in this lesson. Do not add calcium to a pregnant bitch or queen. Feeding a correctly formulated growth and reproduction diet supplies calcium at 1.2 percent for dogs and 1.0 percent for cats, and that is the level the profile is built around. Calcium supplementation during pregnancy is associated with eclampsia, a life-threatening drop in blood calcium around whelping, and the decision to give or withhold calcium at any stage of the cycle is a veterinary one, made for a named animal with clinical information you do not have. If a breeder asks you for a calcium product for a pregnant animal, your answer is to send them to their veterinarian. Say it plainly and do not soften it.

A breeder in Kumasi, Ghana lost two bitches in successive years to seizures shortly after whelping while feeding a home ration topped with crushed eggshell. The shells were free. The dogs were not. The clinic that eventually saw the third bitch put her on a commercial growth and reproduction food with no top-dressing at all, and asked to see every ingredient in writing before the next mating.

Lesson 11.3~12 min

Lactation Demands and Milk Production

In this lesson
  • Calculate peak lactation energy for a bitch and for a queen with a real litter size
  • Design a feeding and water routine that a dam can physically eat
  • Recognise eclampsia as a veterinary emergency and act accordingly

Lactation is the largest nutritional demand either species will ever face, larger than heavy work, larger than growth. The published dog factor runs from 3.0 x RER to 6.0 x RER or more, varying with litter size. For cats the figure is expressed differently: RER plus 30 to 90 percent per kitten during weeks one to six. Both are wide bands because litter size, milk yield and the dam's own reserves vary enormously, so the number you calculate is a starting point that you then correct by weighing the dam.

Take the 20 kg bitch again, RER 662 kcal. A modest litter at 3.0 x RER needs 1986 kcal a day. A large litter at 6.0 x RER needs 662 x 6 = 3972 kcal a day. On a 3800 kcal per kg dry food that is 523 grams at the bottom of the band and 3972 divided by 3800 = 1045 grams at the top. One kilogram of dry food a day for a 20 kg dog. Most bitches cannot eat that in two meals, which is why lactating dams are usually fed free choice or four times a day, and why the food must be at the dense end of the published range, 2800 to 4800 kcal per kg, rather than the cheap end.

Now the queen. A 4 kg queen has an RER of 198 kcal. With five kittens at the middle of the band, 60 percent per kitten, the calculation is 198 x (1 + 5 x 0.60) = 198 x 4.0 = 792 kcal a day. On a 3800 kcal per kg dry food that is 208 grams. Her maintenance intake before breeding was about 63 grams. She now has to eat more than three times that, and cats are grazers, so free access to food day and night is the practical answer rather than fixed meals.

Cost it, because breeders always underestimate this. The bitch at the top of the band eats 1.045 kg a day. Across a six-week lactation of 42 days that is 43.9 kg, and at USD 1.30 per kg the lactation feed bill alone is USD 57. The queen at 208 g a day for 42 days eats 8.7 kg, USD 18.30 at USD 2.10 per kg. Add the puppies or kittens starting to eat the dam's food from about three to four weeks, and the real bill is higher again. When you quote a breeding customer, quote the whole cycle, not the bag.

The diet stays on the growth and reproduction profile: dog 22.5 percent protein and 8.5 percent fat, cat 30.0 percent protein and 9.0 percent fat, with the full calcium, phosphorus, taurine, vitamin A and arachidonic acid minima. Merck restates the dog growth and reproduction protein minimum on an energy basis as 56.3 g protein per 1000 kcal ME, and the cat figure as 75 g per 1000 kcal. That energy-basis form is the more useful one in lactation, because when intake doubles, a diet that is merely adequate per kilogram is still adequate per calorie, but a diet you dilute with cheap filler to hold the price is not.

Water is the nutrient people forget. Milk is mostly water. A dam producing milk at peak needs continuous clean water within reach of the whelping or queening box, not across the yard.

Now the emergency. Eclampsia, also called puerperal tetany or milk fever, is a sudden fall in blood calcium in a lactating dam. Signs commonly reported include restlessness, panting, stiffness, tremors, and progression to seizures and collapse. It is a veterinary emergency requiring intravenous calcium given by a veterinarian. It is not something you treat with a feed change, an oral supplement or advice over the phone, and the risk is not reduced by giving calcium during pregnancy. Your job as a feed producer is to make sure every breeding customer knows the signs, knows which clinic is open at night, and knows to travel immediately. Write the clinic's number on the bag if you have to.

A breeder in Lusaka, Zambia keeping four bitches moved her lactating dams from a 2900 kcal per kg general-purpose food to a 4100 kcal per kg reproduction formulation. Intake at peak fell from about 1.37 kg a day to 0.97 kg, the dams stopped losing condition by week four, and the higher price per kilogram was almost cancelled out by the smaller quantity. She now buys the reproduction food only for the eight weeks that matter.

Lesson 11.4~12 min

Post-Breeding Dam and Queen Recovery

In this lesson
  • Step energy down safely from peak lactation to adult maintenance
  • Judge recovery on measured weight and condition rather than on the calendar
  • Identify which recovery questions must go to a veterinarian

Weaning ends the dam's biggest demand almost overnight, and the feeding plan has to follow it down. A dam left on a lactation intake after the litter has gone will gain fat quickly, because the difference is very large. Take the 20 kg bitch. At peak she was on 3972 kcal a day. As a neutered adult she needs 662 x 1.6 = 1059 kcal, and as an intact adult 662 x 1.8 = 1192 kcal. The step down from 3972 to 1192 is a 70 percent cut. In grams on a 3800 kcal per kg food, that is 1045 grams a day falling to 314 grams a day.

The step is not made in one day. In practice the drop is spread across the weaning period as the litter takes more solid food and takes less milk, so the dam's demand is already falling before the litter leaves. A workable pattern is to reduce toward maintenance over about a week, monitoring intake and weight, and to keep her on the growth and reproduction profile until her weight has stabilised rather than dropping her to an adult food on the day of weaning. Nothing in our verified reference sets a published recovery schedule, so treat that pattern as practical guidance, not a standard, and adjust it on the scale.

The queen follows the same logic with different numbers. Our 4 kg queen was on 792 kcal a day with five kittens. Neutered adult is 198 x 1.2 to 198 x 1.4, which is 238 to 277 kcal. Intact adult is 198 x 1.4 to 198 x 1.6, which is 277 to 317 kcal. So her intake falls by roughly two thirds. On a 3800 kcal per kg dry food she goes from 208 grams a day to about 63 to 83 grams.

The cost side is worth showing to a breeder, because it is the one part of reproduction that saves money. The bitch dropping from 1045 g to 314 g a day saves 731 g a day. At USD 1.30 per kg that is 0.731 x 1.30 = USD 0.95 a day, or USD 28.50 a month. Failing to make that cut is not only a welfare problem, it is a real cash leak on a small kennel with several dams.

Recovery is judged on the animal, not the calendar. Weigh her weekly on the same scale, at the same time of day, and record it. A dam who has lost heavy condition through lactation should be brought back up gradually using the weight-gain band of 1.2 to 1.8 x RER, and a dam who has gained fat should be brought down using the weight-loss factor of 1.0 x RER, which is RER itself. Both are slow processes measured in weeks. If she is not regaining condition on a correctly calculated intake, that is a clinical question, not a feeding question.

Several recovery decisions belong squarely with a veterinarian, and you should hand them over without hesitating. How soon a female should be bred again, or whether she should be bred again at all. Any discharge, fever, poor appetite or mastitis after whelping or queening. Persistent weight loss on an adequate ration. Any decision about supplements. Our verified reference contains no inter-litter interval figures, no post-partum nutrient targets and no supplement recommendations, and you should not fill those gaps with numbers you found on a forum.

A cattery in Mbeya, Tanzania keeping eight breeding queens started weighing every queen on the day of weaning and again four weeks later, writing both weights on a card in the queen's file. Two queens who had failed to regain weight after two litters were examined by a vet and retired from breeding. The owner's comment was that the scale told her something the eye had not, and a kitchen scale plus a notebook had cost her about USD 15.

One last commercial point. If you sell feed to breeders, the recovery phase is where you should be offering a smaller bag of the reproduction diet rather than pushing a full bag they will overfeed. A customer who trusts you not to sell them more than they need is a customer for every litter after this one.

Lesson 11.5~12 min

Sperm Quality and Male Reproduction

In this lesson
  • Feed a working stud dog or tom to the correct energy requirement and condition
  • State honestly what the verified nutrient standards do and do not say about male fertility
  • Refuse to sell fertility claims you cannot substantiate

This lesson is as much about restraint as about nutrition, because the market for male fertility products is full of claims nobody can support with a published requirement.

Start with what the standards actually contain. AAFCO publishes two profiles per species: adult maintenance, and growth and reproduction. There is no separate profile for breeding males. The growth and reproduction profile is the one written for reproduction, and it is what a stud dog or a working tom should be fed on: dog 22.5 percent crude protein, 8.5 percent crude fat, 1.2 percent calcium, 1.0 percent phosphorus, zinc 100 mg per kg, selenium 0.35 mg per kg, vitamin E 50 IU per kg; cat 30.0 percent protein, 9.0 percent fat, 1.0 percent calcium, 0.8 percent phosphorus, zinc 75 mg per kg, selenium 0.3 mg per kg, vitamin E 40 IU per kg, plus the feline essentials of taurine at 0.10 or 0.20 percent, arachidonic acid at 0.02 percent, preformed vitamin A at 6668 IU per kg and arginine at 1.24 percent.

Now the honest part. Our verified reference set contains no requirement figures for semen quality, no sperm-specific nutrient targets, and no dose for any nutrient given to improve fertility in males. Zinc, selenium and vitamin E are widely discussed in relation to male reproduction, and they are genuinely essential nutrients with published minima, but the reference gives no evidence that feeding above those minima improves sperm quality in dogs or cats. Note also that AAFCO sets a selenium maximum of 2 mg per kg for dogs. Selenium is a nutrient with a narrow window, and more is not better. If you cannot source a number, do not print one. A bag that says 'boosts fertility' with a percentage on it is a claim you will not be able to defend to a regulator, a veterinarian or a customer whose stud dog failed.

What you can act on is condition and energy, and it matters more than any supplement. Take a 30 kg stud dog. RER is 70 x 30^0.75. 30^0.75 is 12.82, so RER is 897 kcal per day. Intact adult at 1.8 x RER is 897 x 1.8 = 1615 kcal a day, which on a 3800 kcal per kg food is 425 grams. If he is genuinely working, covering distance or being campaigned, the light work factor of 2.0 gives 1794 kcal and 472 grams. If he is overweight and living in a run, the obesity-prone band of 1.2 to 1.4 gives 1076 to 1256 kcal, and a deliberate weight-loss programme uses 1.0 x RER, which is 897 kcal and 236 grams a day. That is a difference of nearly 240 grams a day between the fat stud and the fit one, USD 0.31 a day at USD 1.30 per kg, or USD 113 a year.

For a 5 kg tom, RER is 70 x 5^0.75 = 70 x 3.34 = 234 kcal. Intact adult at 1.4 to 1.6 x RER is 328 to 374 kcal a day, which is 86 to 98 grams of a 3800 kcal per kg dry food. Obesity-prone is 1.0 x RER, 234 kcal, 62 grams.

Heat matters too, and this is a genuine African-context issue rather than a nutritional one. Testicular function is temperature sensitive, which is why shade, ventilation and water in the kennel or cattery are part of a male's reproductive management. That is a housing recommendation, not a feeding claim, and it costs almost nothing.

A breeder in Ibadan, Nigeria was spending about USD 22 a month on an imported 'stud conditioner' powder with no guaranteed analysis on the tub. His two stud dogs were both carrying obvious excess weight. Switching them to a correctly measured growth and reproduction ration at 425 grams a day, dropping the powder, and roofing the run cost him nothing extra in feed and saved the USD 22. Any change in fertility after that was for his veterinarian to assess, not for him or for his feed supplier to claim.

The veterinary boundary here is clear. Breeding soundness examination, semen collection and evaluation, investigation of infertility, and any decision about a supplement or medication for a male that is not producing belong to a veterinarian. If a stud is failing, the honest answer from a feed producer is: feed him correctly to condition, and get him examined.

Lesson 11.6~12 min

Neonate Quality and Colostrum

In this lesson
  • Explain how the dam's diet, not a neonatal product, determines early litter quality
  • Weigh and record neonates as the primary early warning system
  • Route colostrum failure, fading neonates and milk replacer decisions to a veterinarian

Newborn puppies and kittens are the part of this module where a feed producer must be most careful about what they claim, because the verified nutrient standards say almost nothing about them. AAFCO publishes profiles for growth and reproduction and for adult maintenance. It publishes no colostrum composition, no neonatal nutrient requirement and no milk replacer specification, and neither does the rest of our verified reference set. Anyone quoting you a precise colostrum figure should be asked for the source.

What is well established is where neonatal nutrition comes from: the dam. For the first weeks the litter eats nothing but milk, and milk is manufactured out of the dam's diet and her body reserves. That is why the whole of this module has been about her. In cats the link is direct and documented: taurine deficiency causes reproductive failure, alongside central retinal degeneration and dilated cardiomyopathy, which is one reason the feline taurine minimum of 0.10 percent of dry matter in extruded food and 0.20 percent in canned food is not negotiable in a breeding cattery. Taurine is absent from plants, so a plant-heavy queen ration is a litter problem waiting to happen.

Colostrum, the first milk, carries maternal antibody to the newborn. The window for absorbing it is short and it does not reopen. A neonate that does not nurse in the first hours is a veterinary case, not a feeding case. So is a litter whose dam has no milk, a puppy or kitten that is losing weight, one that is cold, limp or crying continuously, and any decision about whether to use a milk replacer and which one. Say that to breeders plainly. Home-made milk mixtures made from cow's milk and eggs are common and are a frequent route to dead neonates; if a replacer is needed, the veterinarian chooses it.

The single most useful thing a breeder can do, and the thing you should teach with every bag you sell into a kennel, is to weigh every neonate daily on a kitchen scale that reads in grams, and write the weights in a ruled notebook with one line per animal. A neonate that is not gaining is in trouble long before it looks like it. A scale that reads to 1 gram costs about USD 12 to USD 20 in most African cities, which is less than one veterinary consultation and less than the value of a single puppy.

Weaning is where your product returns. Puppies and kittens start taking solid food from around three to four weeks, and what they take is normally the dam's food, softened. That has two consequences. First, the dam's food must already meet the growth and reproduction profile, because it is now the litter's food too. Second, the large-breed calcium rule applies from the first mouthful. AAFCO limits calcium to 1.8 percent of dry matter in any formula that may be fed to large size puppies, defined as those weighing 70 pounds or more as mature lean adults, against 2.5 percent for other life stages. Large-breed puppies have limited ability to downregulate intestinal calcium absorption, so excess intake becomes systemic excess and disturbs endochondral ossification. Merck notes that supplementation above 3 percent of dry matter causes severe osteochondrosis. Never top-dress a growth diet with calcium, and never sell a calcium product into a kennel with large-breed litters.

Now the arithmetic for the weaning stage, which is where you can be genuinely useful. Puppies from weaning to four months are fed at 3.0 x RER, and from four months to adult at 2.0 x RER. Kittens from weaning to adult are fed at 2.5 x RER. Take a 2 kg kitten: RER is 70 x 2^0.75 = 70 x 1.68 = 118 kcal, and 118 x 2.5 = 294 kcal a day, which on a 3800 kcal per kg food is 77 grams. Take a 5 kg puppy under four months: RER is 70 x 5^0.75 = 234 kcal, and 234 x 3.0 = 703 kcal, which is 185 grams a day. Both figures are starting points to be corrected against weekly weights.

A cattery in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania weaning about forty kittens a year introduced daily weighing at a cost of one USD 15 scale and one notebook. In the first season they picked up three kittens failing to gain in the first 72 hours and got them to a vet on the same day. The owner's summary was blunt: the scale did not save the kittens, the vet did, but the scale was what told her to go.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Which AAFCO profile should a bitch be on before mating?

The growth and reproduction profile raises dog protein to 22.5 percent DM, fat to 8.5 percent, calcium to 1.2 percent and phosphorus to 1.0 percent. Adult maintenance minima are lower and are not intended for a breeding female.

2. What is the daily energy requirement of a 20 kg intact bitch before breeding?

RER is 70 x 20^0.75 = 70 x 9.46 = 662 kcal, and the intact adult factor is 1.8, so 662 x 1.8 = 1192 kcal per day. 662 is her RER alone and 1986 is the late-gestation figure.

3. Why can a plant-only ration not support a breeding queen?

Taurine is absent from plants, cats cannot cleave beta-carotene to retinol so they need preformed vitamin A at 6668 IU per kg for reproduction, and arachidonic acid is absent from vegetable oils.

4. An overweight bitch is due to be mated in two months. What is the correct approach?

Condition should be corrected before mating. Restricting a pregnant female risks the litter, and calcium supplementation is a veterinary decision that is not appropriate here.

5. What is the correct calcium to phosphorus ratio range for a dog growth and reproduction diet?

AAFCO sets the dog ratio at a minimum of 1:1 and a maximum of 2:1, with growth and reproduction minima of 1.2 percent calcium and 1.0 percent phosphorus on a dry matter basis.

6. When does a bitch's energy requirement rise sharply during pregnancy?

The published factors are 1.8 x RER for the first 42 days and 3.0 x RER for the last 21 days. Fetal growth is back-loaded, so early overfeeding only makes a fat dam.

7. How many grams a day does a 20 kg bitch need in late gestation on a 3800 kcal per kg food?

662 kcal RER x 3.0 = 1986 kcal, and 1986 divided by 3800 = 0.523 kg, so 523 grams a day. 1986 is the kcal figure, not grams.

8. A breeder asks you to sell a calcium supplement for a pregnant bitch. What should you do?

Calcium supplementation in pregnancy is associated with eclampsia around whelping and is a veterinary decision for a named animal. A correctly formulated growth and reproduction diet already supplies 1.2 percent calcium for dogs.

9. How does the queen's pattern differ from the bitch's?

The feline factors rise steadily across gestation, from 1.6 at breeding to 2.0 at parturition, unlike the late jump seen in dogs.

10. Why does energy density matter in late gestation?

At 4800 kcal per kg the 1986 kcal requirement is met by 414 g instead of 523 g at 3800 kcal per kg, which matters when the uterus is pressing on the stomach.

11. What is peak lactation energy for a 20 kg bitch at 6.0 x RER?

RER is 662 kcal, and 662 x 6.0 = 3972 kcal a day, which is 1045 g of a 3800 kcal per kg dry food.

12. How is feline lactation energy expressed?

The feline figure scales with litter size directly. A 4 kg queen with five kittens at 60 percent per kitten needs 198 x 4.0 = 792 kcal a day.

13. A lactating bitch shows tremors, stiffness and panting. What is the correct action?

Eclampsia is a sudden fall in blood calcium requiring intravenous calcium given by a veterinarian. It is not managed with feed changes or oral supplements.

14. Why does energy density matter more in lactation than at any other life stage?

At 2900 kcal per kg a 20 kg bitch at peak would need 1.37 kg a day, against 0.97 kg on a 4100 kcal per kg food. Density is what makes the requirement physically eatable.

15. Why is the energy-basis protein minimum useful in lactation?

Merck restates the dog growth and reproduction minimum as 56.3 g protein per 1000 kcal ME and the cat as 75 g per 1000 kcal, which exposes dilution that a per-kilogram figure can hide.

16. By how much does a 20 kg bitch's energy requirement fall from peak lactation to intact adult maintenance?

Peak lactation at 6.0 x RER is 3972 kcal and intact adult maintenance at 1.8 x RER is 1192 kcal, a fall of roughly 70 percent.

17. How should a dam who lost condition during lactation be brought back up?

Weight gain is a slow process guided by the scale. Failure to regain on a correctly calculated intake is a clinical question for a veterinarian.

18. Which of these should be referred to a veterinarian?

Discharge, fever, poor appetite, mastitis and persistent weight loss are clinical signs. Inter-litter interval decisions are also veterinary, and our verified reference contains no interval figures.

19. Why is a smaller bag often the right sale to a breeder at weaning?

Intake falls by two thirds to three quarters at weaning. Selling only what is needed protects the dam and builds a customer who returns for every litter.

20. What does a 4 kg queen need after weaning if she is intact?

Her RER is 198 kcal and the intact adult cat band is 1.4 to 1.6 x RER, giving 277 to 317 kcal a day.

21. Which AAFCO profile applies to a breeding male dog?

AAFCO publishes only adult maintenance and growth and reproduction profiles. There is no separate male breeding profile, so growth and reproduction is the standard used.

22. What does the verified reference say about nutrient doses that improve sperm quality?

The reference contains no sperm-specific targets or fertility doses. Zinc, selenium and vitamin E have published minima only, and dog selenium also has a 2 mg per kg maximum.

23. What is the daily requirement of a 30 kg intact stud dog at 1.8 x RER?

30^0.75 is 12.82, so RER is 897 kcal, and 897 x 1.8 = 1615 kcal a day, about 425 g of a 3800 kcal per kg food.

24. A customer asks for a product to fix his infertile stud dog. What is the honest response?

Infertility investigation, semen evaluation and any medication decision are veterinary. Overfeeding selenium is actively risky given the 2 mg per kg dog maximum.

25. Which management change is a legitimate, low-cost recommendation for a male in a hot climate?

Testicular function is temperature sensitive, so shade and ventilation are a housing recommendation. It is not a feeding claim and should not be sold as one.

26. What does the verified reference give for colostrum composition?

AAFCO publishes growth and reproduction and adult maintenance profiles only. There is no sourced colostrum composition, neonatal requirement or milk replacer standard.

27. A newborn puppy has not nursed in the first hours after birth. What is this?

Colostrum carries maternal antibody and the absorption window closes quickly. Failure to nurse, fading neonates and milk replacer choice are all veterinary decisions.

28. Why does the dam's diet have to meet the growth and reproduction profile at weaning?

The litter's first solid food is normally the dam's food, softened. It must therefore already meet growth and reproduction minima for the species.

29. What is the calcium ceiling for a formula that may be fed to large size puppies?

AAFCO sets 1.8 percent DM for formulas that may be fed to puppies of 70 pounds or more as mature lean adults, because they cannot downregulate intestinal calcium absorption, and 2.5 percent for other life stages.

30. What does a 2 kg kitten need per day at the weaning-to-adult factor?

RER is 70 x 2^0.75 = 118 kcal and the kitten factor is 2.5, so 118 x 2.5 = 294 kcal a day, which is about 77 g of a 3800 kcal per kg food.

Module 11 capstone

Build a costed breeding-season feeding plan for one real breeding animal in your own town, using prices you collect yourself. Step 1: find a breeder, kennel, cattery or veterinary clinic within reach and get permission to work with one bitch or one queen. Record species, breed type, age, body weight on a scale, and the date of her last season or litter. Step 2: write down exactly what she eats now, brand or home ration, grams per day, and the guaranteed analysis from the label if there is one. Step 3: calculate her RER as 70 x kg^0.75, then produce a four-stage energy table: pre-breeding at the adult intact factor (dog 1.8, cat 1.4 to 1.6), early gestation (dog 1.8 for the first 42 days, cat 1.6 at breeding), late gestation (dog 3.0 for the last 21 days, cat 2.0 at parturition), and peak lactation (dog 3.0 to 6.0 depending on litter size, cat RER plus 30 to 90 percent per kitten). Step 4: convert every stage to grams per day at the energy density of the food she eats, or at 3800 kcal per kg if the label does not state one. Step 5: collect real local prices per kilogram and calculate the feed cost of each stage and the total cost from mating to weaning. Step 6: check the food she eats against the AAFCO growth and reproduction minima for her species and write down every value you cannot verify without a laboratory. Step 7: write half a page listing which decisions in your plan belong to a veterinarian, naming calcium supplementation, eclampsia and whelping or queening difficulty specifically, and how the owner would reach a vet at night.
