1. Introduction to Agricultural Irrigation in Africa

Welcome to Module 1 of your Agricultural Irrigation Technician training. This first lesson sets the foundation for everything that follows. Across Africa, agriculture employs over 60 percent of the population and contributes roughly 15 to 20 percent of GDP in most countries. Yet less than 6 percent of cultivated land is irrigated. That gap is your opportunity. As an irrigation technician, you will help farmers move from rain-fed uncertainty to reliable, profitable production. Rain-fed farming in Africa is a gamble. Droughts, erratic rainfall, and dry spells during critical growth stages can wipe out an entire season. Irrigation removes that gamble. It allows farmers to grow high-value crops year-round, sell into markets when prices are high, and plan their finances with confidence. Your role is not just to install pipes and pumps. You are a business enabler. Every system you design, install, and maintain directly affects a farmer's income, food security, and ability to pay school fees, medical bills, and reinvest in their farm. Think of yourself as a service provider in a growing industry. The demand for skilled irrigation technicians is rising because governments, NGOs, and private investors are all pushing for expanded irrigation. In Kenya, the Galana Kulalu project aims to irrigate over one million acres. In Nigeria, the National Irrigation Plan targets hundreds of thousands of hectares. In Ethiopia, small-scale irrigation is a national priority. These projects need trained hands. But large projects are only part of the story. The real growth is in smallholder farms, one to ten hectares, where a single farmer or a cooperative needs a technician they can trust. That is where you come in. Let us define irrigation. Irrigation is the artificial application of water to soil to supply moisture essential for plant growth. It supplements rainfall when it is insufficient or poorly distributed. There are several types. Surface irrigation includes furrow, basin, and flood methods. It is cheap but often inefficient, losing 40 to 60 percent of water to evaporation, runoff, and deep percolation. Sprinkler irrigation sprays water through the air like rain. It is more efficient, 60 to 75 percent, and works well on uneven terrain. Drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root zone through emitters. It is the most efficient, 85 to 95 percent, and is ideal for high-value crops, water-scarce areas, and sandy soils. Sub-surface drip buries the lines below the soil surface, reducing evaporation further and protecting the system from damage and theft. Each type has a place. Your job is to match the right system to the farmer's soil, water source, crop, budget, and labour availability. Now, why does Africa need irrigation technicians specifically? Because generic advice from temperate countries does not work here. African soils are often sandy, lateritic, or shallow. Rainfall is highly variable. Water sources range from rivers and boreholes to dams, pans, and even greywater. Electricity is unreliable or absent, so solar-powered systems are becoming the norm. Farmers have limited capital and need low-cost, repairable solutions. You must understand local materials, local skills, and local markets. A technician who can design a solar-powered drip system for a one-hectare tomato plot in northern Ghana, using locally available pipes and a pump that a village mechanic can repair, is worth more than a textbook engineer who specifies imported equipment that breaks and cannot be fixed. Your training will cover water sources, pump selection, pipe sizing, filtration, emitters, scheduling, maintenance, and business skills. But before we go into technical details, let us talk about the mindset. You are running a business. You are not a charity worker. Farmers pay for results. If your system increases yields and reduces labour, farmers will pay you well and recommend you to neighbours. If your system breaks down or wastes water, your reputation suffers. So treat every installation as a reference project. Keep records. Take photos. Follow up after one week, one month, and one season. Ask the farmer what is working and what is not. That feedback loop will make you better than any certificate. Let us also address the African context of water rights and community management. In many countries, water is a communal resource. You cannot simply drill a borehole or divert a stream without consulting local authorities, water user associations, or traditional leaders. In Kenya, the Water Resources Authority regulates water permits. In Tanzania, basin water boards manage allocations. In South Africa, the Department of Water and Sanitation issues licenses. As a technician, you must advise farmers to secure the right permits. Illegal abstraction can lead to fines, conflict, and even imprisonment. You also need to understand the difference between water rights for domestic use, livestock, and irrigation. Often, irrigation is the lowest priority in times of scarcity. So design systems that are water-efficient and can be scaled back during droughts. Another key concept is the water-energy-food nexus. Irrigation uses water and energy to produce food. In Africa, energy is often the bottleneck. Diesel pumps are expensive to run. Solar pumps have high upfront costs but low running costs. Grid electricity is cheap per unit but unreliable. Your job is to calculate the life-cycle cost of each option and present it clearly to the farmer. A simple table showing upfront cost, annual fuel or electricity cost, maintenance cost, and expected lifespan will help the farmer make an informed decision. Do not just sell the most expensive system. Sell the system that gives the best return on investment. Let us end this lesson with a practical exercise. Visit a nearby farm or imagine one you know. Identify the water source, the crop, the area, and the current method of irrigation, if any. Write down three problems the farmer faces: for example, water shortage in the dry season, high labour for watering by hand, or crop failure due to drought. Then write down three possible irrigation solutions. Do not worry about technical details yet. Just think about the problem and the possible fix. This exercise will train your mind to see irrigation as a solution to a business problem, not just a set of pipes. In the next lesson, we will look at water sources and how to assess them. You will learn how to measure flow rate, test water quality, and calculate how much water a source can reliably supply. That knowledge is the foundation of every good irrigation design. Remember, a system that runs out of water is worse than no system at all, because the farmer has invested money and lost confidence. So let us build your skills step by step, always with the African farmer's reality in mind. Welcome to the profession. Let us get to work.

2. Water Sources and Assessment for Irrigation

In this lesson, we focus on the most critical part of any irrigation system: the water source. Without a reliable source, the best pump, pipes, and emitters are useless. Your first job on any farm is to assess the water source. You must answer three questions. How much water is available? How good is the water quality? And how reliable is the source throughout the year? Let us start with types of water sources common in Africa. Surface water includes rivers, streams, lakes, dams, ponds, and pans. It is often the cheapest and most accessible source. But it is also the most variable. A river that flows strongly in the rainy season may dry up to a trickle in the dry season. A dam may silt up after a few years. A pan may be shared by livestock and wildlife, leading to contamination. Groundwater includes boreholes, wells, and springs. It is generally more reliable than surface water, but it can be depleted if abstraction exceeds recharge. In many parts of the Sahel and East Africa, groundwater levels are falling due to over-extraction. You must advise farmers to monitor water levels and avoid drilling too many boreholes in one area. Rainwater harvesting includes roof catchments, rock catchments, and small dams. It is excellent for supplementary irrigation, especially for kitchen gardens and tree nurseries. But storage is expensive, and evaporation losses can be high. Treated wastewater, also called greywater or recycled water, is increasingly used for irrigation in peri-urban areas. It provides nutrients and reduces pollution, but it must be treated to avoid health risks. Now, how do you measure the flow rate of a source? For a river or stream, you can use the float method. Choose a straight section of about 10 metres. Measure the width and average depth. Drop a float, like a stick or orange peel, and time how long it takes to travel 10 metres. Repeat three times and average. The velocity is distance divided by time. The flow rate is velocity multiplied by cross-sectional area, which is width times average depth. For example, if the stream is 2 metres wide, 0.3 metres deep, and the float takes 20 seconds to travel 10 metres, velocity is 0.5 metres per second. Area is 0.6 square metres. Flow rate is 0.3 cubic metres per second, or 300 litres per second. That is a lot of water. But remember, you cannot take all of it. You must leave enough for downstream users and the environment. A safe rule is to take no more than 10 to 20 percent of the dry-season flow. For a borehole, you need a pump test. This is best done by a qualified hydrogeologist or drilling company. They pump water at a known rate and measure the drawdown, which is how much the water level drops. The safe yield is the maximum pumping rate that does not cause the water level to drop below the pump intake or cause the borehole to dry up. If you do not have a pump test, you can estimate by pumping for an hour and measuring the recovery rate. But this is not as accurate. For a dam or pond, you can estimate volume by measuring surface area and average depth. Then divide by the expected daily consumption to see how many days of irrigation you have. For example, a dam with 1,000 cubic metres of usable water and a crop that needs 20 cubic metres per day will last 50 days. That may be enough for a short-season crop but not for a year-round operation. Water quality is equally important. You must test for salinity, sodicity, pH, and specific ions like chloride, sodium, and boron. Salinity is measured as electrical conductivity, EC, in decisiemens per metre, dS/m. Most crops can tolerate EC up to 1.5 dS/m without yield loss. Above 3 dS/m, only salt-tolerant crops like barley, date palm, and cotton will survive. Sodicity is measured as sodium adsorption ratio, SAR. High SAR causes soil structure to break down, reducing infiltration. The combination of high EC and high SAR is especially dangerous. pH should be between 6.5 and 8.4. Outside this range, nutrients become unavailable, and pipes and pumps may corrode. Chloride above 140 parts per million can damage citrus and avocado. Boron above 0.75 parts per million can damage many crops. You can buy simple test kits from agricultural suppliers. For a full analysis, send samples to a laboratory. In Africa, many universities and research institutes offer water testing services for a small fee. Always test before designing a system. A farmer who invests in a drip system only to find that the water is too salty has wasted money and time. Reliability is the third factor. You need to know how the source behaves across seasons. Ask the farmer: Does the river dry up? For how many months? Does the borehole ever run dry? How did the source perform in the last drought? Talk to neighbours. Check historical rainfall data from the meteorological department. If the source is unreliable, you must design storage. A storage tank or reservoir allows you to pump when water is available and irrigate when it is not. For solar pumps, storage is essential because you can only pump during sunny hours. A typical design might store one to three days of water demand. This buffers against cloudy days, pump breakdowns, and peak demand. Let us talk about water lifting and conveyance. If the source is below the field, you need a pump. If it is above, you can use gravity. Gravity systems are cheaper and more reliable because they have no moving parts. Always check if gravity is possible. Even a small elevation difference, like 5 metres, can provide enough pressure for drip irrigation if the pipes are sized correctly. For pumps, you need to calculate total dynamic head, TDH. TDH is the sum of static lift, which is the vertical distance from water surface to the highest point of discharge, plus friction losses in pipes and fittings, plus pressure required at the emitters. For drip, you need about 10 metres of head, or 1 bar, at the emitters. For sprinklers, you need 20 to 30 metres. Friction losses depend on pipe diameter, length, and flow rate. Smaller pipes and higher flows cause more friction. You will learn to use friction loss tables or simple formulas in a later lesson. For now, remember that undersized pipes waste energy and money. Now, let us consider legal and social aspects. In most African countries, water is owned by the state or the community. You cannot just take it. You need a permit or at least a written agreement with the water user association. In Kenya, the Water Resources Authority issues permits for abstraction above 5 cubic metres per day. In Tanzania, you need a water right from the basin water board. In South Africa, a water use license is required for irrigation. Advise farmers to start the process early because it can take months. Also, consider downstream users. If you take too much water, you may cause conflict with other farmers, livestock keepers, or domestic users. A good technician designs systems that are efficient and fair. Finally, think about climate change. Rainfall is becoming more erratic. Droughts are longer. Floods are more intense. Your designs must be resilient. That means using drought-tolerant crops, efficient irrigation methods like drip, and storage that can capture excess water during floods. It also means training farmers to monitor their water source and adjust irrigation schedules based on actual conditions, not just a calendar. In the next lesson, we will look at pumps and power sources, including solar, diesel, and grid electricity. You will learn how to select the right pump for the job and how to size a solar array. But before that, practice assessing a water source. Visit a local stream or borehole. Measure the flow rate if possible. Test the water with a simple kit. Ask the caretaker about reliability. Write a short report. This hands-on experience is what will make you a confident technician. Remember, water is the lifeblood of irrigation. Get the source right, and everything else follows.

3. Pumps, Power Sources, and System Design

Now that you understand water sources, let us move to the heart of the irrigation system: the pump and its power source. In Africa, the choice of pump and power is often the difference between a profitable farm and a failed investment. You must be able to select, install, and maintain pumps that match the water source, the field layout, and the farmer's budget and technical capacity. Let us start with pump types. The most common are centrifugal pumps, submersible pumps, and positive displacement pumps. Centrifugal pumps are used for surface water like rivers, lakes, and shallow wells. They work by spinning an impeller that throws water outward, creating suction. They are good for high flow and low to medium head. They are not self-priming, so you need a foot valve and a priming port. They lose suction if the water level drops more than 7 to 8 metres below the pump. Submersible pumps are placed inside a borehole or deep well. They are multistage centrifugal pumps, meaning they have multiple impellers stacked to achieve high head. They are efficient and reliable but more expensive. They require a control box and a drop pipe. Positive displacement pumps include piston, diaphragm, and helical rotor pumps. They are good for low flow and high head. They are often used for solar pumping because they can start under load and do not need priming. Helical rotor pumps are popular in Africa for boreholes because they handle sand and silt better than centrifugal pumps. When selecting a pump, you need to know the flow rate required and the total dynamic head, TDH. Flow rate depends on the crop, area, and irrigation method. For drip irrigation, a typical design might apply 5 millimetres per day. On one hectare, that is 50 cubic metres per day, or about 2 cubic metres per hour if you irrigate for 25 hours per week. But you usually irrigate for 4 to 6 hours per day, so you need 8 to 12 cubic metres per hour. For sprinklers, you might need 20 to 30 cubic metres per hour. TDH is the sum of static lift, friction losses, and pressure required at the emitters. Static lift is the vertical distance from the water surface to the highest point of discharge. If you pump from a borehole with a water level 20 metres below ground and discharge into a tank 5 metres above ground, static lift is 25 metres. Friction losses depend on pipe diameter, length, and flow. As a rule of thumb, keep friction losses below 20 percent of static lift. If friction is too high, use a larger pipe. Pressure required at emitters is 10 metres for drip and 20 to 30 metres for sprinklers. Add all three to get TDH. For example, static lift 25 metres, friction 5 metres, emitter pressure 10 metres, total TDH is 40 metres. Now you need a pump that delivers the required flow at 40 metres head. Pump curves show the relationship between flow and head. You must select a pump whose curve passes through your duty point. If you choose a pump that is too small, it will not deliver enough water. If too large, it will waste energy and may cause pipe bursts. Always ask the supplier for the pump curve and verify the duty point. Power sources are the next decision. Diesel and petrol pumps are common because they are portable and can be used anywhere. But fuel is expensive and often unavailable in remote areas. Maintenance is high: oil changes, filters, spark plugs, and injectors. They are noisy and produce fumes. They are best for temporary or emergency use. Grid electricity is cheaper per unit but unreliable in many African countries. Load shedding, voltage fluctuations, and power surges can damage pumps. You need surge protection and a backup generator or solar system. Solar pumping is transforming irrigation in Africa. Solar panels have become cheaper and more efficient. A solar pump system consists of panels, a controller or inverter, a pump, and often a storage tank. Solar pumps have no fuel cost, low maintenance, and a lifespan of 10 to 20 years. The main disadvantage is high upfront cost and variable output depending on sunlight. You must size the solar array to meet the daily water requirement even on cloudy days. A rule of thumb is to design for the worst month, usually December or January in the southern hemisphere and July or August in the northern hemisphere. You also need a tank to store water for cloudy periods and for irrigation when the sun is not shining. Sizing a solar array: first calculate daily water requirement in cubic metres. Then calculate the hydraulic energy needed: water volume times TDH times 9.81, divided by 3,600, gives kilowatt-hours. For example, 50 cubic metres per day at 40 metres head: 50 times 40 times 9.81 divided by 3,600 equals 5.45 kilowatt-hours. Then divide by the pump efficiency, say 0.5, to get 10.9 kilowatt-hours. Then divide by the peak sun hours, say 5, to get 2.18 kilowatts of panels. Add 20 percent for losses and dust, so about 2.6 kilowatts. That is roughly six 450-watt panels. You also need a pump that can run on the available voltage and current. Solar pump controllers often accept DC or AC. DC pumps are more efficient but more expensive. AC pumps require an inverter, which adds cost and losses. For smallholder farms, DC submersible pumps with helical rotors are popular because they are efficient and can run directly from panels without batteries. Batteries are expensive and need replacement every 3 to 5 years, so avoid them if possible. Use a tank instead. Now let us talk about system design. A good design starts with a field map. Draw the field to scale. Mark the water source, the pump location, the tank, the mainline, sub-mains, and laterals. Decide on the irrigation method: drip, sprinkler, or surface. For drip, you need filters, pressure regulators, and emitters. For sprinklers, you need sprinkler heads, risers, and couplers. Size the pipes so that velocity does not exceed 1.5 metres per second. Higher velocities cause friction and water hammer. Use the smallest pipe that meets this criterion to save money. But do not go too small, or friction losses will be high. A common mistake is to use 25-millimetre pipe for a mainline that carries 10 cubic metres per hour. That is too small. You need at least 50 millimetres. Use friction loss charts or online calculators. If you do not have internet, use the Hazen-Williams formula or simple tables from textbooks. Always include a filter. Sand filters for surface water, screen or disc filters for boreholes. Filters must be sized for the flow rate and cleaned regularly. A clogged filter reduces pressure and can damage the pump. Include a pressure gauge before and after the filter to monitor clogging. Include a valve to flush the filter. Include a check valve to prevent backflow. Include a pressure relief valve to protect the system from surges. Include a tank with a float switch to turn the pump off when the tank is full. This prevents overflow and saves water and energy. For drip systems, include a venturi or fertilizer injector if the farmer wants to fertigate. Fertigation saves labour and improves nutrient use efficiency. But it requires clean water and proper filters. Now, consider the farmer's capacity. A complex system with many valves and gauges may be too difficult for a farmer to operate. Simplify where possible. Use colour-coded pipes. Label valves. Provide a simple written schedule in the local language. Train the farmer and at least one family member. Leave a spare parts kit: extra emitters, filters, and a pump seal. Show them how to replace parts. A system that cannot be maintained will fail. Finally, calculate the cost. Include pumps, panels, pipes, fittings, filters, tank, labour, and transport. Add 10 percent for contingencies. Present the cost to the farmer along with the expected increase in yield and income. If the payback period is more than three years, the farmer may not accept it. Look for ways to reduce cost: use gravity instead of a pump, use a smaller tank, use local materials, or phase the installation. In the next lesson, we will focus on drip irrigation, the most efficient and profitable method for many African farmers. You will learn how to design and install a drip system from scratch. But before that, practice sizing a pump and solar array for a hypothetical farm. Use the formulas and rules of thumb from this lesson. Check your work with a colleague or mentor. The more you practice, the more confident you will become.

4. Drip Irrigation Design and Installation

Drip irrigation is the most efficient irrigation method available. It applies water directly to the root zone of each plant, drop by drop. Efficiency is 85 to 95 percent, compared to 60 to 75 percent for sprinklers and 40 to 60 percent for surface irrigation. In Africa, where water is scarce and expensive to lift, drip irrigation is a game-changer. It reduces water use by 30 to 50 percent, reduces labour for weeding and watering, and increases yields by 20 to 50 percent. It also allows farmers to grow high-value crops like tomatoes, peppers, onions, watermelon, and strawberries, even in dry areas. But drip irrigation requires careful design, installation, and maintenance. A poorly designed drip system will clog, leak, and fail. In this lesson, you will learn how to design and install a drip system step by step. Step one: gather information. You need the field size, shape, and slope. You need the soil type: sandy, loamy, or clay. You need the crop, plant spacing, and row spacing. You need the water source flow rate and pressure. You need the farmer's budget and labour availability. You need the climate: rainfall, temperature, and wind. Step two: calculate water requirement. The crop water requirement depends on the crop, growth stage, and climate. You can use the FAO Penman-Monteith method or simpler pan evaporation data. For a practical approach, use the following daily water requirements for common crops in Africa: tomatoes 5 to 7 millimetres per day, peppers 4 to 6, onions 4 to 5, watermelon 6 to 8, maize 5 to 7, and leafy vegetables 3 to 5. These are peak requirements. Multiply by the area to get cubic metres per day. For example, one hectare of tomatoes at 6 millimetres per day needs 60 cubic metres per day. If you irrigate for 4 hours per day, you need 15 cubic metres per hour. Step three: choose emitters. Emitters are the devices that release water. They come in two main types: inline drippers and online drippers. Inline drippers are pre-installed inside the lateral pipe at fixed spacing, usually 30, 40, or 50 centimetres. They are cheaper and easier to install. Online drippers are punched into the lateral pipe at the desired spacing. They are more flexible but more labour-intensive. Emitter flow rates are typically 1, 2, 4, or 8 litres per hour. For sandy soil, use higher flow rates to ensure water spreads laterally. For clay soil, use lower flow rates to avoid runoff. For most vegetables, 2 litres per hour is a good choice. Step four: design the lateral layout. Laterals are the small pipes that carry water to the emitters. They are usually 16 millimetres in diameter. The length of a lateral depends on the emitter flow rate, spacing, and inlet pressure. If the lateral is too long, the pressure drops and the last emitters discharge less water. This is called non-uniformity. A good rule is to keep pressure variation below 20 percent. For 2 litre per hour emitters spaced 30 centimetres apart, a 16-millimetre lateral can be up to 80 metres long on flat ground. On sloping ground, go downhill, not uphill. If you need longer laterals, use a larger diameter or a pressure-compensating emitter. Pressure-compensating emitters discharge the same flow regardless of pressure, from 0.5 to 4 bar. They are more expensive but allow longer laterals and uneven terrain. They are ideal for African farms with variable slopes. Step five: design the sub-main and mainline. The sub-main carries water from the mainline to the laterals. The mainline carries water from the pump or tank to the sub-mains. Size these pipes to keep velocity below 1.5 metres per second. For a flow of 15 cubic metres per hour, a 50-millimetre mainline gives a velocity of about 2.1 metres per second, which is too high. Use a 63-millimetre pipe, which gives about 1.3 metres per second. Use friction loss tables to check pressure loss. If the pressure loss is more than 20 percent of the total head, increase the pipe size. Step six: design the filtration system. Filtration is the most important part of a drip system. Without proper filtration, emitters clog and the system fails. Use a sand filter for surface water like rivers and dams. Sand filters remove organic matter and silt. Use a screen or disc filter for boreholes. Screen filters are cheap but clog quickly. Disc filters are more robust and easier to clean. Use a combination: a sand filter followed by a screen filter. The filter must be sized for the flow rate. A typical sand filter for 15 cubic metres per hour is about 60 centimetres in diameter. Install a pressure gauge before and after the filter. When the pressure difference exceeds 0.5 bar, clean the filter. Install a flush valve at the bottom of the sand filter and at the end of the mainline. Step seven: design the fertigation system. Fertigation is the injection of soluble fertilizers into the irrigation water. It saves labour and improves nutrient use efficiency. The cheapest method is a venturi injector, which uses a pressure difference to suck fertilizer into the water. A venturi requires a pressure difference of about 1 bar. Install it parallel to a valve or pump. Alternatively, use a fertilizer tank that connects to the mainline. Always inject fertilizers after the filter to avoid clogging the filter with precipitates. Use only fully soluble fertilizers. Flush the system with clean water after fertigation. Step eight: install the system. Start at the water source. Install the pump, check valve, and pressure relief valve. Install the mainline, then the sub-mains, then the laterals. Use compression fittings for pipes. Do not use glue on polyethylene pipes; it does not work. Use barbed connectors and clamps. Punch holes for online drippers with a hole punch, not a nail. Install the drippers with the correct orientation. Flush the laterals before installing the drippers to remove debris. Install the end caps and flush valves. Test the system for leaks and pressure. Adjust the pressure regulator to the recommended setting. Step nine: train the farmer. Show the farmer how to operate the system. Explain the irrigation schedule: how long to run the pump, how often to irrigate. For drip, you usually irrigate every day or every two days, applying the crop water requirement. Do not over-irrigate. Over-irrigation wastes water, leaches nutrients, and causes root diseases. Use a simple soil moisture meter or the feel method: squeeze a handful of soil. If it holds together, it is moist enough. If it crumbles, it is dry. Show the farmer how to clean the filters, flush the laterals, and replace emitters. Leave a spare parts kit. Step ten: maintenance. Drip systems require regular maintenance. Daily: check the pressure gauges and clean the filters if needed. Weekly: flush the laterals and mainline. Monthly: check for leaks and clogged emitters. Annually: clean the tank, replace damaged parts, and test the water quality. Use chlorine or hydrogen peroxide to control algae and bacteria. Use acid to remove mineral deposits. But be careful: too much chlorine can damage plants. Follow the instructions. Now, let us talk about costs. A typical drip system for one hectare in Africa costs between 1,500 and 3,000 US dollars, depending on the quality and the source. This includes the pump, filters, pipes, emitters, and tank. Labour for installation is extra. The farmer can recover this cost in one or two seasons with high-value crops. For example, one hectare of tomatoes can yield 30 to 50 tonnes. At 0.50 US dollars per kilogram, that is 15,000 to 25,000 US dollars. Even with input costs, the profit is substantial. Drip irrigation is not just a technology; it is a business investment. Your job is to make the business case clear. Show the farmer the numbers. Compare the cost of the drip system with the increased income. Calculate the payback period. If it is less than two years, the farmer will be interested. If it is more, look for ways to reduce cost or increase income. Finally, consider the challenges. Drip systems can be damaged by rodents, livestock, and theft. Bury the mainline and sub-mains. Use thick-walled laterals. Fence the field if possible. Train the farmer to check the system daily. In some areas, emitters clog due to high iron or calcium. Test the water and use appropriate filters and treatments. In the next lesson, we will cover sprinkler irrigation and maintenance of all systems. But before that, practice designing a drip system for a small farm. Use the steps in this lesson. Draw the layout. Calculate the water requirement. Choose the emitters and pipes. Estimate the cost. Present your design to a colleague or mentor. The more you practice, the better you will become.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Irrigation System Troubleshooting, Water Quality, and Cost Control for African Farms

This practical reference lesson is designed for working irrigation technicians and farm managers who need fast, field-ready answers. In African agribusiness, irrigation is rarely just an agronomic decision it is a financial one. A pump that runs inefficiently, a clogged drip line, or poor water quality can quietly erode profit margins long before crops show visible stress. This module gives you the numbers, checks, and references to diagnose problems quickly, protect investments, and keep irrigation profitable across smallholder and commercial operations.

Use this lesson alongside your toolbox. Every table, checklist, and diagram is built for real conditions: variable water sources, off-grid power, mixed drip and sprinkler systems, and limited access to spare parts. Where possible, figures are drawn from FAO, ILRI, and university extension sources relevant to African contexts.

SAFETY WARNING WATER QUALITY AND CHEMICAL HANDLING

Never assume irrigation water is safe for human contact or for crops eaten raw. Untreated river, dam, or wastewater can carry bacteria, viruses, and parasites that cause cholera, typhoid, and diarrhoea. Always wear gloves and boots when handling filters, flushing lines, or applying chemicals. If you use chlorine or acids for water treatment or line cleaning, work outdoors or in a ventilated area, never mix chemicals, and keep them away from children and animals. Wash hands and tools after work. If you or a worker develops fever, diarrhoea, or skin infection after working with irrigation water, seek medical attention promptly. Follow WHO (2006) and national public health guidance.

Quick Reference Checklist: Field Troubleshooting and Cost Control

  1. Check pressure at the pump and at the furthest zone. A pressure drop greater than 20% between pump and last emitter or sprinkler usually indicates clogging, leaks, or undersized pipe. Fix before adjusting irrigation times.
  2. Compare filter pressure differential. If the difference before and after the filter exceeds 0.5 bar (7 psi), clean or backwash. A dirty filter wastes energy and starves the system.
  3. Measure flow at least weekly. Use a simple bucket and stopwatch at a known emitter or sprinkler. A 10% flow reduction signals clogging or wear. Act early replacing emitters is cheaper than losing a crop.
  4. Flush drip lines and sub-mains every 24 weeks. Open end caps and let water run until clear. This removes sediment and biological slime, especially with surface water sources.
  5. Test water quality at the start of each season and after heavy rain. Record EC, pH, and visual clarity. If you use wastewater, test for faecal coliforms and follow WHO guidelines.
  6. Log pump running hours and fuel or energy use. Track cost per cubic metre of water pumped. If cost rises without increased flow, service the pump, check suction leaks, and clean filters.
  7. Inspect for leaks and evaporation losses. Fix visible leaks immediately. Mulch beds and repair damaged pipes. In many African farms, 2030% of water can be lost before it reaches the crop.
  8. Train workers on safety and simple record-keeping. A notebook or phone app recording pressure, flow, and repairs helps you spot trends and justify maintenance spending. Business-first irrigation means knowing your numbers.
