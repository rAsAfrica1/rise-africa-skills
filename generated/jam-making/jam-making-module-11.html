JAM MAKING · MODULE 11 OF 12

Marketing, Sales and Growing the Business

How to find customers, keep them, and grow a jam business that pays you. The unglamorous work that decides whether the business succeeds.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Find and prioritise your best customers
  • Sell through farmers' markets, direct, wholesale, and online
  • Build a pricing and promotion strategy that grows revenue without discounting
  • Photograph and present your product professionally
  • Use WhatsApp, Instagram and Facebook to sell
  • Write a simple 12-month growth plan
Lesson 11.1

Finding your customers

Jam sells to six categories of customers. Each buys for different reasons and pays different prices. Knowing which is your customer decides everything else.

The six customer types

CustomerWhat they buyWhat they pay
Local families and householdsOne jar at a time, for personal useRetail price, small volume
Gift buyersSets of 3–5 jars, especially at holidaysRetail or slight premium
Local groceries and shops20–50 jars at a timeWholesale price, regular orders
Hotels, lodges, restaurants50–200 jars a monthWholesale price, consistent
Online buyersDirect, often in cities away from your areaRetail + delivery
Diaspora and exportBulk, sometimes through agentsPremium, but with certification

Which customer to focus on first

You cannot serve all six at once. Pick two to focus on for your first year. The best pair depends on your location and your capacity:

  • If you are in a city: local groceries and online direct sales. High volume, good margin, easier logistics.
  • If you are in a tourist area: hotels and lodges plus gift buyers. High-value customers, seasonal.
  • If you are in a farming area: local families through farmers' markets, plus one or two shops. Steady, small volume, low logistics.
  • If you are near an airport or border: diaspora export through agents. Highest margin, requires certification.

Start with one channel. Master it. Then add a second. Businesses that try to sell through every channel at once do none of them well.

The 10-customer rule

Before you scale production, have 10 customers who reorder within 3 months. Ten repeat customers is a viable business. One hundred one-time customers is not.

Repeat customers are worth 5–10× a new customer. They cost nothing to acquire the second time, they refer friends, and they buy more over time. Focus every marketing effort on getting customers to come back.

Lesson 11.2

Direct sales — farmers' markets and events

Selling face-to-face is the fastest way to learn what customers want, at the highest margin, with the lowest upfront investment. Every successful food business started here.

Setting up a market stall

  • Table with a clean cloth — the cloth is not optional. It signals hygiene and professionalism.
  • A clear sign with your brand name, visible from 10 metres away
  • Your products arranged by fruit type, with the most eye-catching in the centre
  • Open sample jars for tasting — critical, tasting converts 3–5× better than seeing
  • Small tasting spoons and paper napkins — never re-use a spoon
  • Price signs, clearly visible — customers do not like to ask
  • A card or flyer with your WhatsApp number — this is how you build repeat customers
  • A simple cash box — never take payments from a pocket or an open wallet

What sells at a market

  1. Samples. "Would you like to try it?" converts 5–10× better than "Would you like to buy it?"
  2. The story. "I make it in my kitchen in [town]. The mangoes are from [farmer name]." Customers buy stories.
  3. The fruit. Lead with what fruit is in season. Seasonal fruit is fresh and it is what customers want right now.
  4. Clear pricing. $7 per jar. Not "$6.50–$7 depending on..." Confusion kills sales.
  5. The bundle. "Buy 3 for $18" works. It moves 2.5× more jars per customer.

Small-scale food producers talk about what actually works on a market stall.

Market day economics

A typical market day:

  • Cost: stall fee ($5–20), transport ($5–15), samples and packaging ($5–10)
  • Sales: 20–60 jars at retail $6.50 = $130–$390
  • Profit after costs: $90–$340

Two markets a month is a real business. But the value is not just today's sales — it is the WhatsApp list you build from every customer who buys. That list is what pays next month.

The 30-second pitch that works

When someone stops at your stall, say this:

"Hi — I make jam from local fruit. Would you like to try some? This one is mango, made this week with mangoes from [farmer]. It's $7 a jar. If you buy 3, it's $18."

Four pieces of information: who you are, what the product is, the price, and the bundle offer. That is all a market customer needs. Do not tell your life story. Do not oversell. Let the taste do the selling.

Lesson 11.3

Wholesale — shops, hotels and restaurants

Wholesale is the path to volume. Lower margin per jar, but far less work per sale and predictable monthly revenue.

The wholesale pitch

When approaching a shop, hotel, or restaurant:

  1. Bring a sample jar. Not a business card. A jar.
  2. Ask for the person who decides what to stock. Not the cashier. The owner, the buyer, or the F&B manager.
  3. Lead with what you offer: "Local jam, made this week, three flavours, wholesale price $X per jar, minimum order 20 jars, delivered."
  4. Offer a small first order at full wholesale. "Start with 20 jars. If they sell, order 40 next time."
  5. Leave a sample jar and a card. Give them time to taste it.
  6. Follow up in 5 days. Not a week. Five days. Persistence is not rude — it is professional.

Wholesale terms that work for both sides

TermWhat to offer
Minimum order20–30 jars (below that, delivery cost eats the margin)
Wholesale priceCost × 1.8–2.2 (typically 60–65% of retail)
Payment termsCash on delivery for first order, then 14–30 days for regular customers
DeliveryFree for orders above a threshold; small fee below
Shelf life guaranteeMinimum 6 months remaining at delivery
ReturnsReplace any jar that fails to sell within 30 days — trade, not cash

The wholesale trap

Wholesale customers demand consistency. If your strawberry jam varies from batch to batch in colour, texture, or taste, a shop will drop you. This is why the batch records and recipe discipline from earlier modules matter so much — they are what allows you to make the same product every time.

Do not take a wholesale order you cannot repeat. A $200 order that damages your reputation is worth less than no order at all.

The three buyers to approach first

  1. A small independent grocery in your neighbourhood. Low volume, easy to manage, good for testing.
  2. A local hotel or guesthouse where the owner is likely the decision-maker. Direct contact, no corporate approval chain.
  3. A cafe or deli that already stocks local products and is proud of that fact.

Avoid supermarkets as your first wholesale attempt. Their terms, paperwork, and demands are much heavier, and they are unforgiving of small producers who cannot scale.

Lesson 11.4

Online selling — WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook

Online selling in Africa today happens primarily on WhatsApp and secondarily on Instagram and Facebook. A website helps but is not the primary channel.

WhatsApp — your main sales channel

  1. Set up a WhatsApp Business account. Free. Adds a catalog feature, greeting message, and business hours.
  2. Create a product catalog. Each flavour with a good photo, a short description, and the price.
  3. Set your greeting message: "Hi, thanks for messaging [brand]. We make local jam. Delivery $X within [area]. Order 3 jars for $18. How can I help?"
  4. Save every customer's number with a tag: "Jam customer — bought strawberry". This is your database.
  5. Post to status once a week. Photos of new batches, market days, customer feedback. Keeps you visible in their feed.
  6. Send broadcast messages sparingly. No more than once a month, and only about a real new product or a real offer. Over-messaging gets you blocked.

Instagram and Facebook

  • One account per platform, same name and same visual identity as your brand
  • Post 3–5 times a week — not more. Consistency matters more than frequency.
  • What to post: production photos, finished product on a nice surface, customer photos (with permission), market day photos, short videos of the process
  • Use your bio to state your WhatsApp number and where you sell
  • Reply to every comment and DM within a few hours. Speed of reply is what turns interest into a sale.

The one thing that sells online

Good photos. Not a photo of a jar on a kitchen counter with yesterday's dishes behind it. A clean photo of a jar on a plain surface, natural light from a window, no clutter, product label visible.

Six good photos of six products will sell more than any amount of clever text. Take photos in the morning near a window facing north (in the southern hemisphere) or south (in the northern hemisphere). That light is soft, even, and free.

Delivery logistics

  • Local delivery — within your town, deliver by motorbike or on foot for $1–3 per order, or free above $20
  • Courier — for out-of-town, use a courier service. Pack jars properly (bubble wrap, sturdy box) because jam jars are heavy and break easily
  • Pickup — offer a pickup point in your neighbourhood at a set time. Cheapest option, but limits customers.

Whichever method, communicate timing clearly. "Delivery within 2 days" is a promise. If you cannot keep it, do not make it.

Lesson 11.5

Pricing strategy and promotions

Price is the single most important business decision. Here is how to use it well.

The three pricing rules

  1. Never discount the base price. If you drop to $5 when a customer haggles, you have taught them your real price is $5 and your first price was theatre.
  2. Use bundles instead of discounts. "3 for $18" (works out to $6 per jar) is better than "all jars $6" — you move 3× the volume per customer with the same per-jar revenue.
  3. Raise prices deliberately, not accidentally. If your costs go up 20%, raise prices 20% — do not quietly absorb it and lose your margin.

Bundle pricing that works

BundlePricePer jarDiscount vs single
Single jar$7.00$7.00—
Bundle of 3$18.00$6.0014% off
Bundle of 6 (gift box)$33.00$5.5021% off
Subscription (4 jars/month)$24.00$6.0014% off + delivery included

The subscription is the most valuable. Predictable monthly revenue, delivered once a month, with almost no ongoing marketing cost.

Seasonal promotions

  • Valentine's Day — gift bundles, romantic packaging
  • Easter — family bundles, egg-shaped labels
  • Mother's Day — gift sets, personalised labels
  • Christmas — the biggest gift season, order in advance for gift boxes
  • Harvest season — "Made with this week's [fruit]" campaign
  • Back to school — family jam bundles

Plan your production calendar around these. The two weeks before Valentine's Day will outsell all of January combined if you market properly.

Do not compete on price

If a big jam company undercuts you, you cannot beat them on price and you should not try. What you can offer that they cannot:

  • Freshness — your jam was made within the last month
  • Locality — the fruit came from a farmer the customer could meet
  • Traceability — the jar has a batch number and a story behind it
  • The person — a real name and face behind the product
  • Customisation — wedding favours, corporate gifts, personalised labels

These are worth a 30–50% premium over the cheapest jam on the shelf. Sell on those, not on price.

Lesson 11.6

Your 12-month growth plan

Bring the whole course together into a single document that tells you what to do and when.

The plan structure

  1. Where you are now. Current products, volumes, prices, and monthly revenue.
  2. Where you want to be in 12 months. Specific monthly revenue target. Not vague.
  3. The products. What flavours you will sell, in what sizes.
  4. The customers. Which two channels you will focus on in the first 6 months, and how you will add the third in months 7–12.
  5. The pricing. Retail price, wholesale price, bundle pricing.
  6. The production. Monthly volume target, and how you will produce it (equipment, time, staff).
  7. The marketing calendar. What you will do each month — market days, social media, promotions, outreach.
  8. The financial projection. Monthly revenue, monthly costs, monthly profit, cash reserve.
  9. The risks. The 3–5 things that could stop you, and how you will manage each.

A simple 12-month example

MonthFocusTarget jars/monthTarget revenue
1–2Set up: branding, first three products, one market stall50$325
3–4Add WhatsApp catalog, second market100$650
5–6First wholesale customer (shop or guesthouse)200$1,200
7–9Add online, second wholesale, gift season prep350$2,100
10–12Christmas gift season, third wholesale, consider hire500–600$3,200–$3,900

This is achievable in a first year for a serious producer. It requires steady, consistent work — not heroics. Most months you will feel like you are not doing anything special. Then you look back and realise you have built a real business.

The four numbers you must know every month

  1. Revenue: total money in
  2. Cost: total money out
  3. Profit: revenue minus cost
  4. Repeat customers: how many bought twice or more

Everything else is a detail. If those four numbers are trending the right way — up, up, up, up — the business is working. If not, those are the four numbers to focus on.

Practical assignment — Module 11

Write your 12-month growth plan.

  1. Current state: your products, prices, monthly volume, monthly revenue. If you have not started selling yet, use your planned launch numbers.
  2. 12-month target: specific monthly revenue figure. State it in numbers.
  3. Two channels for months 1–6. Name the two sales channels you will focus on first.
  4. Third channel for months 7–12. Name the sales channel you will add in the second half of the year.
  5. Three products. List the three flavours you will sell in your launch range. Give one reason for each.
  6. Pricing table. Retail price, wholesale price, three bundles.
  7. Marketing calendar. One row per month for the next 12 months. What is your focus that month?
  8. Financial projection. Monthly revenue, cost, and profit for each of the 12 months.
  9. Top three risks. What could go wrong, and how you will manage each.
  10. One thing you will measure every month that tells you whether you are on track. Recommend: repeat customers.

Points 2, 3 and 10 carry the most marks. Point 2 tests whether you can commit to a specific number. Point 3 tests whether you can focus rather than doing everything at once. Point 10 tests whether you have chosen the right metric to track.

Module 11 knowledge check

1. Which customers are worth the most to your business long-term?

  1. One-time buyers at markets
  2. Repeat customers — they cost nothing to re-acquire and they buy more over time
  3. Social media followers
  4. Wholesale buyers only
Show answer

b. A repeat customer is worth 5–10× a new one. They cost nothing to acquire the second time and they refer friends. Focus every marketing effort on turning first-time buyers into repeat buyers.

2. What is the single most effective thing to do at a market stall?

  1. Talk loudly
  2. Offer samples — tasting converts 5–10× better than seeing
  3. Discount your prices
  4. Hand out business cards
Show answer

b. Offering a taste converts 5–10× better than asking a stranger to buy. "Would you like to try it?" opens the sale. "Would you like to buy it?" closes it before it starts.

3. Why should you use bundles instead of discounts?

  1. Customers prefer bundles
  2. Bundles move 3× the volume per customer at the same per-jar revenue, while discounts only lower your price without growing the sale
  3. Discounts are illegal
  4. Bundles are easier to make
Show answer

b. A discount gives the same customer the same product for less money. A bundle gives them more product for a slightly better price per jar — you earn more, they feel they got a deal, and your price stays credible.

4. What is the most important thing to do when pitching a wholesale buyer?

  1. Bring a business card
  2. Bring a sample jar, speak to the decision-maker, lead with the wholesale offer, and follow up in 5 days
  3. Offer the lowest price
  4. Send an email
Show answer

b. Bring a jar, not a card. Speak to the owner or buyer — not the cashier. Lead with a specific offer (price, minimum order, delivery). Follow up in 5 days. Persistence is professional, not rude.

5. Which four numbers should you track every month?

  1. Followers, likes, shares, comments
  2. Revenue, cost, profit, repeat customers
  3. Jars made, jars sold, jars in stock, jars broken
  4. Prices, costs, volumes, margins
Show answer

b. Revenue, cost, profit, and repeat customers. Everything else is detail. If those four numbers are trending up, the business is working. If not, those are the four to focus on.
