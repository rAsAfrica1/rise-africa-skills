Lesson 12.1

What a launch plan contains

A jam business launch plan is not a formal business plan. It does not need 40 pages of market analysis or five-year financial projections. It needs to answer ten specific questions, in writing, with real numbers.

The ten sections

  1. Purpose. What you are making, for whom, and why. One paragraph.
  2. Products. Your three launch flavours, in one jar size.
  3. Customers. Who buys, in what channel, at what price.
  4. Costing. True cost per jar, including fruit, sugar, pectin, jar, lid, label, labour, overheads.
  5. Pricing. Retail, wholesale, and bundle prices derived from the cost.
  6. Production plan. How many jars you make per week, on which days, using what equipment.
  7. Compliance position. What permissions apply, and where you are in obtaining them.
  8. Brand and packaging. Brand name, label design, jar choice, packaging cost.
  9. Marketing plan. Where and how you will sell, starting from next Monday.
  10. Economic case. Revenue, cost, profit, and payback.

That is the whole document. Some sections will be one paragraph. Some will be a table. What matters is that every number is real and every claim is verifiable.

The one section people skip

Section 10 — the economic case. Most beginners skip it because they assume the business will work. Then they discover six months in that it does not, and they cannot work out why. Do the numbers before you make the first jar. If the numbers do not work, change the plan, not the reality.

Test your pricing before you commit

Before you print a price list, take one of your jars to a shop, a hotel, and a customer. Ask what they would pay. Compare to the price you calculated. If your calculation says $7 but the market says $5, you have three options:

  1. Lower your cost — find cheaper jars, cheaper fruit, more efficient production
  2. Change your market — move to buyers who pay the price you need (hotels, online)
  3. Change the product — a smaller jar costs less per unit and can sell at $5 with the same margin

Never lower the multiplier. That is the money you live on.

Lesson 12.3

Production planning

How many jars, when, and using what. This is where the business either works smoothly or becomes chaos.

Work backward from demand

  1. Monthly target — how many jars will you sell in a month? (Start small: 100 jars is a reasonable launch target.)
  2. Weekly target — monthly ÷ 4.3. A 100-jar month is 23 jars a week.
  3. Batches per week — how many cooking sessions? One batch of 30 jars, cooked twice a week, is a good rhythm.
  4. Fruit needed — 30 jars × 500g usable fruit = 15kg. With yield adjustment, 24kg whole fruit per week.
  5. Jars needed — 30 jars per batch × 2 = 60 jars per week, plus 5% breakage = 63.
  6. Labels needed — 60 plus 5% misfires = 63.
  7. Hours — cooking (2 hours per batch), filling (30 min), labelling (30 min), cleanup (30 min). Total 3.5 hours per batch. Two batches a week is 7 hours.

Lesson 12.4

Compliance and labelling position

Regulations vary by country. The categories are almost universal, and the conversation with your local authority is what tells you the specifics.

The labelling checklist

Every label must include (verify exact wording with your local authority):

  • Product name — "Strawberry Jam"
  • Ingredients list — in descending order by weight
  • Net weight — 250g
  • Best-before date — typically 12–24 months from production
  • Batch number — for traceability
  • Producer name and address
  • Country of origin
  • Storage instructions — "Store in a cool dark place. Refrigerate after opening."

The conversation to have before printing 500 labels

Take a draft label to your local public health office. Show it to them. Ask:

"I want to sell jam from a home kitchen. Does this label meet the requirements? What do I need to add or change?"

Fifteen minutes of conversation, one draft label, and one small revision. Compare that with the cost of printing 500 non-compliant labels that have to be thrown away.

Lesson 12.5

Marketing plan — starting Monday

The marketing plan is not aspirational. It is a specific list of actions you will take, starting the first week after launch.

Week 1

  • Set up WhatsApp Business account with a product catalog and greeting message
  • Photograph all 3 products properly — natural light, plain background, 6 images per product
  • Create Instagram and Facebook accounts in the same name
  • Post the first 3 product images on both platforms

Week 2

  • Message 20 people you know personally. Not a mass broadcast — individual messages. "Hi [name], I've started making jam. Would you like to try a jar? First one's on me."
  • Book one farmers' market stall for the following weekend
  • Print 50 flyers with your WhatsApp number and product range

Week 3

  • Attend the market. Offer samples. Take WhatsApp numbers.
  • Follow up every market customer within 3 days: "Thank you for buying. How did you find the jam?"
  • Post market day photos on social media

Week 4

  • Visit 3 local shops with a sample jar and a wholesale offer
  • Visit 1 hotel or guesthouse with the same approach
  • Message every WhatsApp customer with a bundle offer: "Buy 3 jars for $18"

That is one month of consistent marketing. It is not glamorous. It is not viral. It is what actually grows a small food business — one customer, one conversation, one reorder at a time.

The first 30 days matter more than the next 300

Everything that will determine whether your jam business succeeds over the next year is set in the first 30 days. Not because those days are magic, but because the habits you establish — consistent posting, following up, delivering on time, writing things down — either become your business or never do.

Start as you intend to continue. Monday, not someday.

Lesson 12.6

The economic case

The final section. The one that decides whether the business is real.

The three questions

  1. Does the margin pay you? Revenue per jar minus true cost per jar must leave enough profit to cover your time and reinvestment.
  2. Does the volume cover your costs? At your achievable monthly volume, does the profit cover the fixed costs of the business and leave you with income?
  3. Is the payback period shorter than the business's useful life? Your initial investment should be repaid from profit in a reasonable time — typically under 12 months for a small food business.

When the numbers do not work

Walk away, or change the plan

If your numbers show:

  • Monthly profit under your local minimum wage for the hours you would work — the business is not viable as designed
  • Payback period over 24 months — you are financing a hobby, not starting a business
  • You cannot cover fixed costs at realistic volume — the model does not work; change the products, the pricing, or the market

Better to know this now than six months and $3,000 later. A jam-making skill is still valuable — you can use it for personal use, for gifts, or as a side product to a bigger business. What you should not do is pour money into a model that does not work.

The pessimistic test

Your numbers are based on estimates. Before you launch, test them at three levels:

  1. Realistic: your honest projections
  2. Pessimistic on revenue: you sell 60% of what you expect
  3. Pessimistic on cost: your costs come in 30% higher than expected

If the business works in the realistic case but fails in both pessimistic cases, you are relying on everything going right. On a farm, in a season, in a small business, it will not. Scale back the plan until it survives the pessimistic case, or find a different model.

What this course has taught you — and where it refused to answer

You have covered the business of jam, fruit sourcing, the science of setting, the cooking process, sterilisation and filling, water bath canning, troubleshooting, packaging and branding, scaling, low-sugar jam, and marketing. Just as important is the list of things this course deliberately would not tell you, because the answer depends on your market, your country, or your fruit:

  1. Jam shelf life on the label — depends on your local food safety rules; ask the authority, not a book
  2. Exact labelling requirements — vary by country; your national standards authority has the answers
  3. Wholesale and retail prices — depend entirely on your local market; ask buyers, not this course
  4. Whether to invest in commercial equipment — depends on your margin and demand; do the numbers before you buy
  5. Your pectin content in local fruit — varies by variety and season; test your own fruit
  6. Whether low-sugar jam is right for your market — depends on your customers, not on theory
  7. The specific altitude adjustment for your location — get your elevation from a topographic map or your meteorological office
  8. Your own labour rate — value your time as you would pay an employee; that is a personal and local decision

Knowing where your competence ends is a skill, not a gap. A jam maker who says "I need to ask my local health authority about that" is more professional than one who guesses, and considerably cheaper than one who guesses wrong.

Capstone assignment — Jam Making

Produce a complete launch plan for your own jam business, using all ten sections from Lesson 12.1.

This is the assignment the whole course has been building toward. It should be a single document you would be willing to show a lender, a partner, or a shop.

Requirements:

  1. All ten sections present, in order, each headed. Sections can be short — one paragraph or one table each.
  2. Real figures throughout — real prices from your own local suppliers, real dimensions from your own kitchen, real contacts from your compliance conversations. No placeholders.
  3. Your pricing table derived from a real cost calculation. Show your working. State the multiplier you chose and why.
  4. Your compliance position stated explicitly. For every permission, either write "obtained on [date] from [name]" or "not yet obtained — conversation with [name] pending on [date]". An honest "not yet" is acceptable; a blank section is not.
  5. The economic case tested three ways — realistic, pessimistic on revenue, pessimistic on cost. Conclude in one sentence: launch now, launch reduced, wait, or do not launch.
  6. A recommendation. State clearly whether the plan is viable, and what the next specific action is.
  7. Photographs of: your three test jars, your label mockup applied to a jar, and your market stall setup or proposed setup.

How this is assessed: the marks are in the reasoning, not the presentation. A student who produces a rigorous plan and concludes "on these numbers the payback is 18 months, which is longer than I can fund, so I am launching with one product instead of three and adding the second product when cash flow allows" has demonstrated exactly what this course set out to teach. A student who produces an optimistic plan for a business they cannot fund has not.

Module 12 knowledge check

1. Why must you do the economic case before making the first jar?

  1. It is required by law
  2. It tells you whether the business model works — before you spend money and time finding out the hard way
  3. It helps you get a loan
  4. It is good for record keeping
Show answer

b. If the numbers do not work on paper, they will not work in reality. Knowing this before you buy jars and fruit saves you months and thousands of dollars. A jam-making skill is still valuable if the business does not work — what you should not do is pour money into a model that cannot pay.

2. Which section of the launch plan do most beginners skip?

  1. The product list
  2. The economic case (section 10)
  3. Compliance
  4. Marketing
Show answer

b. Beginners skip the economic case because they assume the business will work. Then they discover six months in that it does not, and cannot work out why. Do the numbers first.

3. What is the correct response if your pricing calculation says $7 but the market says $5?

  1. Lower your price to $5 and accept a smaller margin
  2. Lower your cost, change your market to one that pays $7, or change the product — but never lower the multiplier
  3. Wait until the market is ready to pay $7
  4. Sell at $5 now and hope to raise prices later
Show answer

b. The multiplier (2.5–3× for retail) is the money you live on. If the market will not pay it, change one of the three variables: cost, market, or product. Never lower the multiplier — you will work for free without realising it.

4. What is the pessimistic test for a launch plan?

  1. Assuming everything goes wrong
  2. Running the numbers with revenue 40% below target and costs 30% above target — if it still works, the plan is robust
  3. Assuming no customers show up
  4. Assuming the worst-case scenario only
Show answer

b. A plan that only works in the optimistic case is a plan that depends on everything going right. On a small farm or in a small business, it will not. Scale back the plan until it survives the pessimistic case.

5. What should a good capstone launch plan conclude?

  1. Only positive outcomes
  2. An honest recommendation — launch now, launch reduced, wait, or do not launch — based on the numbers and the reasoning
  3. Whatever will impress a lender
  4. Nothing — the plan stands on its own
Show answer

b. Any of the four recommendations can score full marks if the reasoning supports it. A student who honestly says "on these figures this does not pay back within its useful life, so I am deferring it and building the cheaper model instead" has demonstrated exactly what this course set out to teach.
