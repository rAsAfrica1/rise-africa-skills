JAM MAKING · MODULE 3 OF 12

Sugar, Pectin and the Science of Setting

Why jam sets, why it sometimes doesn't, and how to fix it. The four things that have to be right for a jar to gel.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Explain what sugar does in a jar of jam, beyond sweetness
  • State the four conditions required for jam to set
  • Identify natural pectin sources and use them to replace commercial pectin
  • Understand why acid matters and how to adjust it
  • Recognise the warning signs that a batch will not set
  • Fix a jam that has not set — and know when it cannot be fixed
Lesson 3.1

What sugar actually does

Most beginners think of sugar as the sweetener in jam. It is not. Sugar is the preservative, the thickener, the setting agent, and the carrier of flavour. It is the single most important ingredient after the fruit.

The three jobs sugar does

  1. Preserves. Sugar binds with the water in the fruit, making that water unavailable to moulds, yeasts and bacteria. A jam with the correct sugar concentration does not need refrigeration.
  2. Sets. Sugar, acid, and pectin work together to form the gel network that gives jam its texture. Without enough sugar, no set. With too much, sugar crystals form.
  3. Preserves colour and flavour. Sugar protects fruit colour and aroma molecules from being destroyed by heat. A jam with the right sugar content tastes more like fruit than one with too little.

How much sugar?

The traditional ratio is 1:1 fruit to sugar by weight for most fruit. Some fruit needs less (very sweet fruit like figs), some needs more (very tart fruit like blackcurrant). But 1:1 is the correct starting point.

Fruit typeSugar ratioExample
Very acidic fruit1:1 or slightly more sugarBlackcurrant, cranberry, lemon
Standard fruit1:1Strawberry, plum, mango, guava
Sweet fruit0.8:1 (less sugar)Very ripe figs, sweet cherries, over-ripe banana
Very low-pectin fruit1:1 with added pectinStrawberry, melon, pear

Reducing sugar is not as simple as it sounds

You cannot just cut the sugar in a jam recipe by half. The set depends on it. The preservation depends on it. A jam made with 30% sugar instead of 60% is not a low-sugar version of the same product — it is a different product that must be refrigerated and eaten within a few days. This is why "no-sugar jam" recipes use different setting agents (like modified pectin or agar) and different storage rules.

Lesson 3.2

The four conditions for set

Jam sets when all four of these are true at the same time:

  1. Enough sugar — usually 60–65% sugar concentration in the finished jam
  2. Enough acid — pH around 3.0 to 3.4
  3. Enough pectin — natural from the fruit, or added
  4. The right temperature and time — cooked to the setting point

If any one is missing, the jam will not set. If two are missing, it will become syrup. If three are missing, you have made fruit soup.

Pectin, acid and sugar must all be balanced at the same time. Cooking to the setting point is the fourth condition.

Lesson 3.3

Pectin — natural and commercial

Pectin is a natural carbohydrate found in the cell walls of fruit. When heated with sugar and acid, it forms a gel network that traps the fruit juices. Without pectin, no set.

The two forms of pectin

Natural pectin in the fruit

Every fruit contains pectin, more in the skin, seeds and core than in the flesh. The level peaks as fruit approaches ripeness and declines as it becomes over-ripe. This is why you use just-ripe fruit for jam (Module 2).

You can boost natural pectin by:

  • Adding the cores, skins, and seeds of apples, citrus, and other high-pectin fruit, tied in a muslin bag and cooked with the jam, then removed. This is the traditional method.
  • Mixing in 10–15% under-ripe fruit. The natural pectin in under-ripe fruit is higher than in fully ripe fruit.
  • Adding apple or citrus as a base. A spoonful of apple pulp or citrus zest thickens a low-pectin jam naturally.

Commercial pectin

Bought as a powder or a liquid. Comes in two types:

  • HM pectin (high methoxyl) — the standard type. Requires sugar and acid to set. What most recipes use.
  • LM pectin (low methoxyl) — sets with calcium rather than sugar. Used for low-sugar and no-sugar jams.

Do you need commercial pectin?

Only if your fruit is naturally low in pectin, or if you are working with over-ripe fruit, or if you want a very consistent set every time. Many traditional African jams use no added pectin — the fruits are naturally high enough.

When starting out, try the jam without commercial pectin first. If it sets, you have saved money and used a purer product. If it does not set, learn to test for set (Lesson 3.5) and add pectin next time.

How much commercial pectin to use

Follow the packet instructions. Do not improvise. Commercial pectin is standardised by the manufacturer and adding more than the packet says will make the jam rubbery, not stronger. Adding less will not set it.

If you are converting a recipe that was written without pectin, add pectin at the ratio the packet suggests for that quantity of fruit and sugar. Around 1% of the total weight is typical for HM pectin.

Lesson 3.4

Acid — why lemon juice is not optional

Acid is the ingredient beginners forget. It does three jobs in jam:

  1. It helps pectin set. Pectin needs an acidic environment to form a gel. Without enough acid, even high-pectin fruit will not set.
  2. It balances flavour. Jam that is only sweet tastes flat. A little acid makes the fruit taste more like fruit.
  3. It aids preservation. Acid slows the growth of spoilage organisms.

Which acid to use

AcidTypical amountNotes
Lemon juice (fresh)1 tbsp per kg of fruitThe standard. Its flavour is neutral in jam.
Lemon juice (bottled)Same as freshWorks, but check it is pure juice, not lemon squash
Citric acid (powder)1/4 tsp per kg of fruitConcentrated. Use less. Available at pharmacies and food suppliers.
Lime juiceSame as lemonWorks, but adds a lime flavour
Vinegar (white)Not recommended for most jamFlavour too strong. Use only for savoury preserves.

Fruits that need extra acid

  • Sweet ripe mango — always add lemon juice
  • Papaya — naturally very low acid, needs a lot
  • Banana — no natural acid at all
  • Fig — low acid
  • Sweet cherry — needs a little extra
  • Pear — low acid and low pectin — needs both lemon and pectin

Taste before you commit

Take a small spoonful of the jam just before you stop cooking. Taste it. If it tastes flat — sweet but not fruit-forward — add a bit more lemon juice and cook for another minute. If it tastes sharp and citrusy, it has too much acid. This is where a jam becomes good, not just sweet.

Lesson 3.5

Testing for set — the three tests

You cannot tell whether jam will set by looking at it in the pot. You have to test. There are three methods, and the best jam makers use two.

The wrinkle test, the temperature test, and the plate test — demonstrated side by side.

1. The wrinkle test (traditional)

  1. Put a small plate in the freezer for 15 minutes before you start cooking.
  2. When you think the jam is close to set, put a teaspoon of jam on the frozen plate.
  3. Wait 1 minute. It should cool and form a skin.
  4. Push it with your finger. If it wrinkles and holds its shape, it is set. If it runs, keep cooking.

Best for: traditional jam making, low-tech kitchens. Works with any fruit.

2. The temperature test

  1. Use a jam thermometer or a digital probe thermometer.
  2. Insert into the jam but not touching the pot bottom.
  3. Bring to 104–105°C (220°F) at sea level.
  4. At this temperature, the sugar concentration is correct and the jam will set.

Altitude adjustment

Water boils at a lower temperature at altitude. At 1,000m above sea level, jam sets at about 102°C. At 2,000m, around 100°C. If you are in Harare, Johannesburg, Nairobi or another high-altitude city, you must subtract 1°C for every 300m of elevation. Get this wrong and every batch will fail.

Best for: consistency, larger batches, high-altitude kitchens (with adjustment).

3. The spoon or sheet test

  1. Dip a wooden spoon into the jam.
  2. Lift it out and let a few drops fall back into the pot.
  3. The last drops should fall as a single sheet, not as separate drops.
  4. If the drops join together into a "flap" as they fall, the jam is set.

Best for: quick judgement in the middle of cooking. Not as reliable as the other two.

Use two tests every time

The temperature test is fast and reliable. The wrinkle test confirms it. Using both means you know for certain. Using only one means you are guessing half the time.

If you are making jam for sale, invest in a jam thermometer. It costs a few dollars and pays for itself the first time it saves a batch.

Lesson 3.6

Troubleshooting setting problems

Your jam will sometimes not set. That is normal. It happens to everyone. What matters is knowing why and how to fix it.

Diagnosis: why jam does not set

SymptomCauseFix
Runny, tastes correctUnder-cooked — did not reach setting temperatureReturn to pot, cook to 104°C, retest
Runny, tastes flatNot enough acidAdd lemon juice, cook 5 more minutes
Runny, but very sweet and thickNot enough pectinAdd commercial pectin dissolved in water, boil 2 minutes
Rubbery or toughToo much pectin, or over-cookedCannot be fixed — next time use less pectin
Grainy or crunchyUndissolved sugar crystalsCannot be fixed in this batch. Next time stir sugar fully before boiling hard, or add a knob of butter during cooking
Mould growth after a few weeksNot enough sugar, or unsterile jars, or bad lid sealDiscard. Check jar sterilisation and sugar level next time
Fruit floats to topFruit was not cooked enough before sugar addedNext time, cook fruit gently before adding sugar and let it sit overnight

Emergency fix for unset jam

  1. Return the jam to the pot. You need at least 1L of jam for this to work — small quantities evaporate too fast.
  2. Add 1 tbsp lemon juice per 1kg of fruit. This addresses the most common cause (low acid).
  3. Bring to a rolling boil and cook for 5 more minutes. Check the temperature.
  4. Retest. If still runny, add commercial pectin per packet instructions.
  5. If still runny after both attempts, accept that this batch is a "jam sauce" and sell it as such — over pancakes or yogurt. Do not throw it away. It is still good food.

What you cannot fix

Once a batch is over-cooked (rubbery), over-sugared (grainy), or has mould on top, it cannot be rescued. Do not try to "fix" it by adding more water, more fruit, or more sugar. It will only make it worse. Accept the loss, note what went wrong, and adjust the recipe for the next batch.

Adjusting a recipe for next time

Every batch that does not set is information. Note in your recipe record:

  • Fruit variety and ripeness
  • Sugar quantity
  • Lemon juice quantity
  • Whether you added pectin
  • The final temperature reached
  • Whether it set or not

After two or three batches, you have your own recipe — tuned to your fruit, your water, your pot, and your altitude. That is worth more than any book recipe.

Practical assignment — Module 3

This is your first cooking assignment. Do it with 1kg of fruit — a small, low-risk batch.

  1. Choose a fruit. Pick one from the medium-pectin list in Lesson 3.3. It should be a fruit you can buy cheaply right now.
  2. Make a small batch: 1kg of prepared fruit, 1kg sugar, 1 tbsp lemon juice. No commercial pectin for this first test.
  3. Test for set using two methods. Use the wrinkle test (with a frozen plate) and either the temperature test or the spoon test. Note the temperature at which you stopped cooking.
  4. Cool, jar, and label. Write the date, fruit variety, and whether it set on the jar label.
  5. Photograph the result. One photo of the jar, one photo of the jam on a spoon showing the set.
  6. Write a batch record using the fields in Lesson 3.6. This is your template for the future.
  7. Two days later, open the jar and re-check the set. Some jams firm up over 24–48 hours. Note whether it firmed.

Points 3 and 6 carry the most marks. Point 3 tests whether you can actually judge set. Point 6 establishes the habit that makes every future batch better.

Module 3 knowledge check

1. What are the three main jobs sugar does in jam?

  1. Sweetens, colours, thickens
  2. Preserves, sets with pectin and acid, protects colour and flavour
  3. Ferments, crystallises, dissolves
  4. Adds weight, adds cost, adds calories
Show answer

b. Sugar is not just sweetener — it preserves by binding water, it is essential to the gel network formed with pectin and acid, and it protects the fruit's colour and aroma during cooking.

2. What are the four conditions required for jam to set?

  1. Sugar, acid, pectin, and the right temperature and time
  2. Fruit, water, heat, and time
  3. Boiling, stirring, cooling, jarring
  4. Sweetness, colour, texture, aroma
Show answer

a. All four must be in balance at the same time. If any one is missing, the jam will not set or will set poorly. This is why a jam can fail even when the fruit is perfect and the recipe was followed.

3. Why is lemon juice not optional in most jam recipes?

  1. It adds flavour
  2. It helps pectin set, balances flavour, and aids preservation
  3. It stops the fruit from browning
  4. It replaces sugar
Show answer

b. Pectin needs an acidic environment to gel. Lemon juice also brightens flavour (so the jam tastes of fruit, not just sugar) and slows spoilage. It is one of the most underrated ingredients in jam making.

4. At what temperature does jam set at sea level, and how does altitude affect this?

  1. 100°C — altitude does not matter
  2. 104–105°C at sea level — subtract about 1°C for every 300m of elevation
  3. 80°C regardless of location
  4. 120°C
Show answer

b. Water boils at a lower temperature at higher elevation, so the jam reaches setting concentration at a lower temperature. In Harare, Johannesburg, Nairobi or other high-altitude cities, use the adjusted figure or your jam will not set.

5. If your jam has not set, what is the emergency fix?

  1. Add more sugar and reboil
  2. Return to the pot, add lemon juice, cook 5 more minutes, retest; if still runny, add commercial pectin
  3. Throw it away
  4. Add flour to thicken it
Show answer

b. The most common cause is not enough acid or not enough cooking. Adding lemon and cooking longer fixes most cases. Pectin is the second-line fix. Never add flour or cornflour — it changes the texture and can make the jam unsafe for long storage.
