7. Quality Control and Food Safety

Up to this point you have learned what to make, where to source it, and how to make it. This module is about making sure every single batch — batch after batch, month after month — is safe and consistent. It is the module that separates a hobby from a food business.

Module 7 of 12 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · printable checklists · off-grid QC tests · video demonstrations · quiz at the end
🌍 No Laboratory Required

Everything in this module can be done with no electricity, no lab, and no expensive equipment.

You will use your senses (sight, smell, touch, taste), everyday objects (a glass of water, a clean spoon, your hand), and paper records (a notebook and pen).

These methods are the same ones used by food inspectors and quality managers in commercial factories — reduced to their essentials.

Lesson 7.1 — The five most important food-safety rules for a home or workshop kitchen

Food safety is not complicated. It is a small number of rules, followed every single time. These five rules prevent 90% of food poisoning cases worldwide.

Rule 1 — Clean hands, clean surfaces, clean utensils

Your hands are the most common vehicle for bacteria entering food. Every time you handle ingredients:

  • Wash hands with soap and clean water for at least 20 seconds
  • Dry on a clean cloth or air-dry (wet hands transfer bacteria better than dry)
  • Clean surfaces with soap and water before use
  • Wash utensils after every batch — spoons, bowls, mixers, containers
  • Never use the same cloth for wiping hands and wiping surfaces

If you have running water, use it. If you don't, use a "tippy-tap" or a jerry can with a tap — pouring water is cleaner than dipping into a shared basin.

Rule 2 — Separate raw and cooked

In your kitchen or workshop, keep raw ingredients and finished products apart:

  • Separate containers, spoons and surfaces for raw soybeans, raw maize, raw milk powder
  • Never put finished porridge mix on a surface that had raw soybean on it without washing first
  • Never store finished flour in a container that previously held raw ingredients without proper washing and drying
  • Keep raw meat, fish and eggs out of the porridge production area entirely — the same kitchen can host both, but not at the same time, and never with shared utensils

Rule 3 — Cook to correct temperature and time

The correct temperatures and times for this course:

ProcessTargetHow to know
Boiling soybeansRolling boil for 20–30 minutesBubbles break the surface continuously; steam rises steadily
Roasting soybeansGolden brown colour, nutty smell, beans crackDo the water-drop test
Cooking porridgeRolling simmer for 5–7 minutesPorridge thickens and becomes glossy
Boiling drinking waterRolling boil for 1 minuteSteady bubbles, then cool

Do not shorten any of these times to save fuel. The heat is what destroys pathogens. Under-processed food is the leading cause of diarrhoea in vulnerable children.

Rule 4 — Keep food out of the danger zone

Bacteria multiply fastest between 5°C and 60°C — this is called the "danger zone." Without a fridge, you must work around it:

  • Above 60°C = hot, safe for a limited time
  • Below 5°C = cold, safe (you probably don't have this)
  • Between 5°C and 60°C = dangerous — bacteria double every 20 minutes at the warm end

Practical implication: cook porridge fresh, serve hot, discard after 2 hours. Keep dry ingredients dry — dry food is outside the danger zone entirely.

Rule 5 — Use clean water

All water that touches food — for cooking, for washing utensils, for handwashing — must be safe. If you would not drink it, do not cook with it.

If your water source is uncertain:

  • Boil for 1 minute, then cool
  • Or use a household filter (ceramic pot, biosand filter)
  • Or add a small amount of chlorine (following local health guidance)
The five rules in one sentence: Clean hands and tools, keep raw and cooked apart, cook to the right time and temperature, keep food out of the danger zone, and use clean water.

Lesson 7.2 — Aflatoxin: what it is, how to spot contaminated maize, and how to avoid it

Aflatoxin is the single greatest food-safety risk in this course. It is invisible, tasteless, and it accumulates in the body over time. It causes liver cancer, stunting, immune suppression and — in acute doses — death. Africa has the highest aflatoxin exposure in the world.

You cannot smell it, taste it, or see it. But you can prevent it. This lesson shows you how.

What aflatoxin actually is

Aflatoxin is a poison produced by two moulds: Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus. These moulds grow on crops when:

  • Humidity is high (above 14% moisture in the grain)
  • Temperature is warm (25–35°C — tropical conditions)
  • Grain is damaged by insects or poor handling
  • Storage is wet or poorly ventilated

They produce aflatoxin as a byproduct of growth — invisible to the eye, but present throughout the entire batch, not just on mouldy patches.

Which crops are highest risk?

CropRisk levelNotes
Groundnuts (peanuts)🔴 Very highMost common source of aflatoxin poisoning in Africa
Maize🔴 HighSecond most common — especially in humid storage
Soybean🟡 MediumLower risk, but not zero
Sorghum, millet🟡 MediumUsually lower than maize
Rice🟢 LowerLess prone, but not immune
Cassava (dried)🟡 MediumRisk during drying if wet

Because your porridge contains 65% maize and 25% soy, you must be careful with both. The maize is the bigger risk.

How to spot contaminated maize or soy

Aflatoxin itself is invisible. But the mould that produces it leaves visible signs:

  • Discoloured kernels — greyish, greenish, or blackish patches on the maize
  • Mouldy smell — musty, earthy, or "mouldy cellar" odour
  • Shrivelled or broken kernels — damaged grain is more susceptible
  • Clumping or caking in storage — sign of moisture
  • Insect damage — weevils and borers create entry points for mould
  • Visible white or yellow-green fuzz on the surface of the grain
If you see visible mould, throw away the entire batch. Not just the mouldy parts. Aflatoxin spreads through the whole quantity — cutting off the mouldy section and using the rest is dangerous. A 50 kg bag with mould at the bottom has aflatoxin throughout.

Prevention is the only real protection

Since aflatoxin cannot be destroyed by cooking (it survives boiling, roasting and frying at temperatures that would destroy any food), prevention is the only reliable strategy. Here are the six rules:

  1. Buy from trusted sources. Farmers who dry and store their grain properly produce safer grain. Build a relationship with a specific supplier you can trust.
  2. Buy fresh. New season's grain is safer than old stock. Ask when it was harvested — if more than 12 months ago, be suspicious.
  3. Test at purchase. Reject grain that smells musty, looks discoloured, or has visible mould. This is non-negotiable.
  4. Dry thoroughly. If you buy damp grain, dry it in the sun or a solar dryer until it cracks when bitten. Never store grain above 13% moisture.
  5. Store dry, cool, sealed. Airtight containers, elevated off the ground, in the coolest room. No exceptions.
  6. Use within a safe window. Freshly stored grain is safest within 6–9 months. Beyond that, the risk of aflatoxin rises even with good storage.

The maize "float test" — a useful screen but not a guarantee

Put a handful of maize kernels in a glass of water:

  • Good kernels sink
  • Mouldy or insect-damaged kernels float

Skim off the floaters and discard them. This reduces but does not eliminate aflatoxin. Use it as a first-line check, but always combine it with the visual, smell and storage rules.

What to do if you suspect aflatoxin

  • Do not use the batch. Not even "just this once."
  • Do not feed it to animals and then eat the animals — aflatoxin passes into milk and meat.
  • Compost it or return it to the supplier if you have that relationship.
  • Report to your local extension officer if a specific supplier is repeatedly selling contaminated grain.

Lesson 7.3 — The water activity test: a simple way to check your dry mix will store safely

Water activity is the amount of "free water" in a food — water that is not bound to the food molecules and is available to microorganisms. It is the single most important indicator of whether a dry food will store safely.

The good news: you can test for it without any instruments.

What is "water activity"?

Water in food comes in two forms:

  • Bound water — chemically attached to proteins, starches and sugars. Microorganisms cannot use it.
  • Free water — the "extra" water that is available. Microorganisms use this to grow.

Water activity (aW) is measured on a scale of 0 to 1:

aW rangeWhat growsTypical foods
0.95–1.00Bacteria (including dangerous ones)Fresh meat, milk, cooked porridge
0.90–0.95Most bacteria, some yeastsJams, soft cheeses
0.85–0.90Some bacteria, most yeastsDried fruits, honey
0.70–0.85Moulds, some yeastsFlour, dried grains
Below 0.70Nothing growsDry spices, milk powder, biscuits

Your target: keep your dry mix below aW 0.70. Below that level, no microorganisms can grow. Your flour will store safely for months.

The home test — "does it crack or squash?"

You cannot measure aW precisely without equipment, but you can get close with these tests:

  1. The bite test (for grains): Bite a grain. If it shatters/cracks between your teeth, it is dry enough. If it dents or squashes, it is still wet.
  2. The pinch test (for flour): Squeeze a handful of flour. It should flow through your fingers and leave almost no residue. If it holds its shape or feels clammy, moisture is present.
  3. The glass test (for flour): Put a spoonful of flour in a clear glass. Add a spoonful of cooking oil and stir. If bubbles rise, moisture is present. If the flour just darkens with no bubbles, it is dry enough.
  4. The salt test (traditional): Put a pinch of dry salt in a sealed jar with a spoonful of flour. Shake well. Leave 24 hours. If the salt stays free-flowing, the flour is dry. If the salt clumps, moisture has migrated from the flour.

These tests are not as precise as lab equipment, but they will catch 90% of the moisture problems that cause spoilage.

The "clump test" — the quickest daily check

Squeeze a handful of your dry mix in your closed fist for 5 seconds. Open your hand:

  • Flour falls freely, leaves almost no residue = dry enough ✅
  • Flour clumps or holds the shape of your fist = too much moisture ❌

Do this test every single day of production. It takes 10 seconds and catches moisture problems before they ruin the whole batch.

What to do if the flour is too wet

  1. Do not package it. Packaging wet flour guarantees spoilage within weeks.
  2. Dry it further. Spread thin on trays and dry in sun or a solar dryer. Turn regularly.
  3. Re-test after drying. Do the clump test and salt test again.
  4. Then package. Only when both tests pass.
Rule of thumb: If you have to ask "is this dry enough?", the answer is "dry it more." You lose nothing by extra drying, but you lose the whole batch by packaging wet flour.

Lesson 7.4 — Sensory checks: colour, smell, taste, texture — what a bad batch looks like

Your senses are your best quality-control instruments. Learn what a good batch looks, smells, feels and tastes like — and you will spot a bad batch instantly.

The four sensory checks

1. Colour

Fresh fortified corn-soy porridge mix should be:

  • Pale golden cream (if using white maize) or warm gold (if yellow maize)
  • Uniform throughout — no lighter or darker patches
  • Slightly speckled — fine dots from the premix and milk powder are normal

Bad colour signs:

  • Grey or dull patches = mould or age
  • Dark brown or black spots = burnt ingredients
  • Uneven colour = poor mixing (premix concentrated in some areas)
  • Greenish tinge = mould growth (discard)

2. Smell

Fresh mix should smell:

  • Nutty and toasty (from the roasted soy)
  • Sweet and cereal-like (from the maize and milk powder)
  • Clean and dry — no mustiness

Bad smell signs:

  • Paint-like or crayon-like smell = rancid oils in the soy flour (discard)
  • Musty, earthy, or cellar-like smell = mould or aflatoxin (discard)
  • Sour or fermented smell = bacterial growth (discard)
  • Chemical or "off" smell = contamination

3. Taste

Taste a tiny pinch of dry mix (spit it out after):

  • Mild, slightly sweet, nutty, cereal-like = good
  • Bitter or beany = soy not roasted or boiled enough (do not use until reprocessed)
  • Soapy or chemical = contamination
  • Rancid or "old oil" = oxidised oils (discard)
  • Unpleasantly sour = bacterial activity (discard)

4. Texture

Rub a pinch of dry mix between your fingers:

  • Smooth, fine powder = good
  • Clumpy = moisture problem
  • Gritty or sandy = coarse grinding (adjust mill)
  • Sticky = high fat content or moisture

The sensory check checklist

✅ Do this for every batch before packaging

If every box is checked, the batch is ready to package. If ANY box fails, do not package. Investigate and fix.

The "second opinion" rule

You will go nose-blind to your own product. After a while, everything smells the same to you. This is a real phenomenon and it has caused food businesses to ship bad batches.

So: get one other person to smell and taste every batch. Someone who is not in the kitchen every day. Their nose is fresher than yours. Their reaction — "hmm, this smells a bit off" — is information you should not ignore.

Lesson 7.5 — Batch labelling: date, weight, ingredients, and a batch number for traceability

Every package you sell must tell the buyer exactly what is in it, when it was made, and how to reach you. This is both a legal requirement in most countries and a fundamental business practice.

The seven things every label needs

  1. Product name — "Fortified Corn-Soy Porridge for Children" or similar
  2. Weight — net weight in grams or kilograms (not "one scoop")
  3. Ingredients list — in descending order of weight: maize flour, soy flour, milk powder, sugar, vegetable oil, vitamin & mineral premix
  4. Batch number — unique code that identifies the specific production run
  5. Made-on date — the day the batch was produced
  6. Best-before date — typically 4–6 months after production for this product
  7. Producer contact — your name, phone number, and location

Optional but recommended

  • Usage instructions — "Mix 1 part flour with 5 parts water. Cook 5 minutes."
  • Feeding guide — age-appropriate amounts (see Module 5)
  • Storage instructions — "Store in a cool, dry place. Reseal after opening."
  • Warning text — "Not a medicine. Supplementary food for children over 6 months."

The batch number system

A batch number is a code that identifies when and how a batch was made. The purpose is traceability — if a customer reports a problem, you can find out exactly which ingredients were in that batch.

Format: FCS-YYYYMMDD-X

PartMeaning
FCSFortified Corn-Soy (product code)
YYYYMMDDDate of production (2026 September 12 = 20260912)
XBatch letter of the day (A = first, B = second, etc.)

Example: FCS-20260912-A = Fortified Corn-Soy, made on 12 September 2026, first batch of the day.

The batch log book

For every batch, write in a paper log:

📓 Batch Log Entry

Keep the log for at least 2 years. If you ever have a food safety incident, the log tells you which batches to recall and which customers to alert.

How to label without a printer

You do not need a fancy printing press. Cheap options:

  • Rubber stamp + ink pad — get a custom stamp made for your product name and address. ~$15 one-time. Use a pen for the variable data (batch number, dates, weight).
  • Sticker labels — buy blank adhesive label sheets in bulk. Write with permanent marker.
  • Pre-printed labels with blank fields — order from a local printer. More professional look, still cheap.
  • Direct writing on the bag — with a permanent marker, on a smooth paper or plastic bag. Least professional but functional.

For institutional buyers (clinics, NGOs), pre-printed labels are worth the small cost. For home sales to neighbours, handwritten labels are fine.

What NOT to put on your label: No health claims ("cures malnutrition"). No medical language ("treatment"). No false credentials ("approved by WHO"). Just describe the product and its ingredients. Claims can trigger regulatory action and undermine trust.

Lesson 7.6 — When to throw a batch away: the three rules you must never break

Every food producer eventually faces the question: "Is this batch still good, or should I throw it away?" For a small producer, throwing away food feels like throwing away money. But there are three situations where throwing away is the only correct answer.

Rule 1 — Visible mould = discard the entire batch

If you see mould anywhere in the batch — a patch on the surface, fuzzy growth in a corner, discoloured spots — the entire batch is unsafe.

Why? Because aflatoxin and other mould toxins spread invisibly through the whole quantity. The mould you can see is just the visible surface. The poison is everywhere.

Cutting off the mouldy part does not work. It never works. This is the single most common mistake small food producers make. A mouldy patch on one side of a 10 kg bag means the entire 10 kg is contaminated.

Rule 2 — Bad smell = discard the entire batch

If the batch smells rancid (paint-like, crayon-like), sour, or musty, it is unsafe. Rancid oils produce free radicals that cause digestive distress and long-term health damage. Sourness indicates bacterial growth. Mustiness indicates mould.

Do not try to "mask" the smell by adding more sugar or flavour. The underlying problem is still there. Discard.

Rule 3 — You are unsure = discard

This is the hardest rule but the most important. If you are not sure whether a batch is safe, it is not safe.

Why? Because your uncertainty is a signal. Your senses have picked up something your conscious mind has not yet named. Trust that signal. When in doubt, throw it out.

The cost of discarding one batch: $20–$100 depending on size. The cost of feeding a contaminated batch to 20 children: their health, your reputation, potential legal liability, and the moral weight of having caused harm.

The math is not close.

What to do with a discarded batch

  1. Do not feed it to animals. Aflatoxin passes into milk, meat and eggs. Feeding contaminated porridge to chickens means contaminated eggs tomorrow.
  2. Do not sell it as animal feed. Same reason — the poison persists through the food chain.
  3. Do not use it as compost on crops you will eat. Aflatoxin can be taken up by some plants. Compost it far from food crops, or better: dispose in a sealed bag to landfill.
  4. Do not return it to the supplier as "unsold stock" — someone else might buy it. If you suspect the supplier sold you contaminated ingredients, tell them exactly why and stop buying from them.

Preventing the need to discard

Prevention is cheaper than discard:

  • Source carefully. Buy from trusted suppliers (Module 3).
  • Store dry. Airtight containers, cool, dark, elevated (Module 6).
  • Rotate stock. First in, first out. Use oldest first.
  • Produce in small batches. 10 kg that gets sold in a week is better than 100 kg that sits for 6 months.
  • Track every batch. Logs tell you how long each batch has been stored.
  • Do sensory checks weekly. Catch problems early, when you can still use the ingredients for something else.

The financial reality of discard

Budget for it. Every food business — from Nestlé down to a home kitchen — loses 2–5% of production to quality failures. Plan for it. A 50 kg batch that fails costs you ~$150 in raw materials. Over a year of monthly production, that is $1,800 in potential losses.

But if you source, store and process correctly, you will rarely lose a batch. In the first year, aim for zero discards. In the second, aim for zero. If you are losing more than 2% of production, something in your process needs fixing.

Your reputation is worth more than any single batch. One contaminated batch sold to one clinic can end your business. No batch, no matter how large, is worth that risk.
