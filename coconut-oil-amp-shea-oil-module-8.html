1. Calculating Cost of Production per Unit

To know if you are making a profit, you must first know exactly how much it costs to produce one unit of oil. For example, one 500ml bottle of coconut oil or shea butter. Start by listing all your direct costs: raw materials (coconuts or shea nuts), packaging (bottles, labels, caps), and labour (yours and any helpers). If you bought a machine, like a press or grinder, divide its cost by the number of units it will produce over its lifetime. For instance, a $500 press that lasts 5 years and produces 1000 bottles per year gives $0.10 per bottle. Add indirect costs: rent, electricity, transport, and any fees. Suppose you make 100 bottles of coconut oil in a month. Raw coconuts cost $50, bottles and labels $30, labour $40, rent $20, electricity $10, transport $15. Total cost = $165. Cost per unit = $165 / 100 = $1.65. That means each bottle costs you $1.65 to make. You must sell above this to profit. Keep a notebook or use a simple Excel sheet to track every expense. Even small costs like a matchbox or phone call add up. Review your costs monthly because prices of raw materials change. If you buy in bulk, your cost per unit drops. For shea oil, if you process 20kg of shea nuts to get 5 litres of oil, and nuts cost $10, labour $15, packaging $10, total $35, cost per litre = $7. Always include a small buffer for spoilage or spillage. This number is your foundation for pricing. Without it, you are guessing. So calculate carefully, write it down, and update it regularly.

2. Setting a Profitable Price

Once you know your cost per unit, you can set a price that gives you a profit. A simple method is cost-plus pricing: add a markup to your cost. For example, if your cost per bottle is $1.65, you might add 50% markup, giving a price of $2.48. But you must also consider what customers are willing to pay. If the market price for similar oil is $3, you can price at $2.80 to be competitive and still make profit. Another method is target profit pricing: decide how much profit you want per unit. If you want $1 profit per bottle, price at $2.65. But be realistic. In many African markets, customers are price-sensitive. So test your price with a few customers. Start with a price that covers cost and gives a small profit, then adjust as you grow. Also consider value-based pricing: if your oil is organic, cold-pressed, or in fancy packaging, you can charge more. For export, you might use a higher markup because of extra costs like export documentation and shipping. Always calculate your break-even point: how many units you must sell to cover all costs. If your total fixed costs are $200 per month and you make $1 profit per bottle, you need to sell 200 bottles to break even. Anything above that is profit. Don't forget to include a margin for discounts and returns. A good rule: your selling price should be at least 30-50% above your cost. But in competitive markets, you might need to lower costs instead of raising prices. So focus on reducing production costs through bulk buying or efficient methods. Finally, review your prices regularly. What works today may not work next year.

3. Understanding Local Market Prices

Before you set your price, you must know what others are charging. Visit local markets, shops, and supermarkets that sell coconut oil or shea butter. Note the prices for different sizes: 250ml, 500ml, 1 litre. For example, in a Ghanaian market, a 500ml bottle of coconut oil might sell for $2.50 to $4.00, while shea butter might be $3.00 to $5.00. Prices vary by region and quality. Talk to vendors. Ask them what sells best and why. Some customers prefer cheaper oil even if quality is lower; others will pay more for pure, unrefined oil. Check online platforms like Jumia or Kilimall to see prices in other areas. Also consider the season: during harvest, prices may drop; during scarcity, they rise. If you are in a rural area, prices might be lower than in cities. But your costs might also be lower. So compare your cost per unit with local prices. If your cost is $1.65 and local price is $2.00, your profit margin is small. You might need to find ways to reduce costs or differentiate your product. For example, offer a unique scent or use recycled bottles to lower packaging costs. Also, look at imported oils. They are often cheaper due to large-scale production, but customers may prefer local, natural products. Use that to your advantage. Attend local trade fairs and farmer markets to gauge demand. Ask potential customers what they would pay. You can also do a simple survey: show them your product and ask if they would buy at $2.50, $3.00, etc. This gives you real data. Remember, pricing is not just about covering costs; it's about what the market will bear. So do your homework before you set a price.

4. Pricing for Export Markets

Exporting your coconut or shea oil can bring higher prices, but it also comes with extra costs. You need to calculate these carefully. First, research the target market. For example, in Europe or the US, organic shea butter sells for $10 to $20 per 500g. But you must meet their standards: organic certification, fair trade, quality testing. These add costs. You also need export documentation: certificates of origin, phytosanitary certificates, customs clearance. These can cost $200 to $500 per shipment. Shipping is a big cost. A 20-foot container from West Africa to Europe might cost $2000 to $4000. If you ship 1000 bottles, that's $2 to $4 per bottle just for freight. Then there are import duties and taxes in the destination country. Also, you need to package according to their regulations: labels in English or French, nutritional information, barcodes. You might need to hire a freight forwarder. All these costs must be added to your production cost. Suppose your cost per bottle is $1.65. Add $0.50 for certification, $0.30 for documentation, $2.50 for shipping, $0.50 for import duty, total extra = $3.80. So your total cost per bottle becomes $5.45. To make a profit, you might sell at $8 to $10. But you also need a distributor or agent in the destination country; they take a commission, often 20-30%. So your final price to the distributor might be $8, and they sell at $12. You must negotiate well. Start with small trial shipments to test the market. Use platforms like Alibaba or export portals. Also, consider fair trade pricing: many buyers are willing to pay a premium if you can prove fair labour and environmental practices. But be prepared for paperwork and delays. Exporting is not for everyone, but if you do it right, it can be very profitable.

5. Offering Discounts and Bulk Pricing

Discounts and bulk pricing can help you sell more and attract loyal customers, but you must do it without losing money. First, know your break-even point. If your cost per bottle is $1.65, you cannot sell below that. A common strategy is to offer a discount for larger quantities. For example, single bottle at $3.00, but if a customer buys 10 bottles, sell at $2.50 each. That still gives you $0.85 profit per bottle, and you move more stock. But you must calculate: if you sell 10 at $2.50, total revenue is $25, cost is $16.50, profit $8.50. If you sold 10 at $3.00, profit would be $13.50. So you are giving up $5 to encourage a bigger sale. That can be worth it if it brings repeat business. Another tactic: offer a free bottle for every 10 bought. That's effectively a 9% discount. Or give a discount for early payment. For shea butter, you might sell in bulk to soap makers or cosmetics companies. They buy in 5kg or 20kg containers. You can offer a lower price per kg because your packaging and handling costs are lower. For example, retail 500g at $5, but bulk 5kg at $40 ($4 per 500g). That's a 20% discount. But ensure your bulk price still covers costs. Also, consider seasonal discounts: during harvest, you might have more supply and can afford to lower prices to clear stock. But don't devalue your product. Always explain why the discount is offered: for bulk, for loyalty, for off-season. Use a simple price list: 1-5 units: $3.00; 6-20 units: $2.80; 21+ units: $2.50. This encourages larger orders. Track your discounts: if you give too many, you might erode profits. Set a limit: no more than 20% off retail. And never discount below cost. Finally, train your sales people to upsell: when a customer buys one, ask if they want to buy three at a discount. This increases average sale.

6. Tracking Expenses and Profit

To stay profitable, you must track every expense and every sale. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Wave or Excel. Record daily: money spent on raw materials, packaging, transport, labour, rent, electricity, and any other costs. Also record money received from sales. At the end of each week or month, calculate total expenses and total revenue. Profit = revenue minus expenses. For example, if you sold 200 bottles at $3 each, revenue = $600. Your expenses: raw materials $100, packaging $60, labour $80, rent $40, transport $30, other $20, total = $330. Profit = $600 - $330 = $270. That's your gross profit. But you also need to set aside money for reinvestment and emergencies. A good practice is to pay yourself a salary as an expense. If you don't, you might think you're making profit when you're just working for free. Decide on a monthly salary, say $100, and include it in expenses. Then your profit is $170. Also, track your inventory: how many bottles you have at the start and end of the month. If you started with 50 bottles, made 200, sold 200, you should have 50 left. If not, investigate theft or spillage. Use a simple cash book: date, description, money in, money out, balance. At the end of the month, review your numbers. Are your costs going up? Are your sales increasing? If profit is low, find ways to cut costs or increase prices. If profit is high, consider expanding. Also, separate business and personal money. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for the business. This makes tracking easier. And always keep receipts. Even for small purchases, ask for a receipt or write it down. This discipline will help you grow your business and secure loans or grants in the future.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Costing and Pricing Your Coconut & Shea Oil Products

This reference lesson is the working document you keep at the production table. It pulls together the seven lessons in this module cost of production per unit, profitable pricing, local market prices, export pricing, discounts and bulk pricing, and expense/profit tracking into one practical toolkit you can use with a calculator, a notebook, and your phone. The numbers below are illustrative but built from realistic African small-and-medium oil processing figures. Replace them with your own costs as soon as you have one full production cycle of data.

Two rules govern everything that follows. First, you cannot price what you have not costed. Second, your selling price is set by the market, but your profit is set by your costs. You control costs; you negotiate price. That is the entire game.

1. Calculating Cost of Production per Unit

Cost of production per unit means every cedi, naira, shilling, or rand that goes into producing one litre of oil or one kilogram of shea butter not just the raw material. For small-scale coconut and shea processors in Africa, the biggest hidden costs are labour (often family labour that is never counted), fuel or firewood for boiling and roasting, and losses from spoilage or low extraction yield.

Use this formula:

Cost per unit = (Direct materials + Direct labour + Direct overheads + Processing losses) Usable output units

Worked example 20 litres of cold-pressed coconut oil per batch:

  • Copra or fresh coconuts: GHS 180
  • Labour (crushing, pressing, filtering, bottling): GHS 90
  • Fuel/firewood and electricity: GHS 40
  • Bottles, labels, caps: GHS 60
  • Equipment depreciation (press, grinder, over 200 batches): GHS 25
  • Spoilage/loss allowance (5%): GHS 20
  • Total batch cost: GHS 415 GHS 20.75 per litre

If you had not counted your own labour, you would think the cost was GHS 15.25 per litre. That GHS 5.50 gap is where many African processors unknowingly sell at a loss.

2. Setting a Profitable Price

Once you know your cost per unit, pricing is arithmetic plus positioning. The three standard methods:

  • Cost-plus pricing: Cost per unit (1 + target margin). Simple, safe, but ignores what the market will pay.
  • Competitor-based pricing: Match or undercut local sellers. Risky if their costs are lower or they are dumping.
  • Value-based pricing: Price on what the buyer saves or gains organic certification, cold-pressed quality, fair-trade story, consistent supply. This is where African shea and coconut brands earn two to three times commodity prices.

Set a floor (break-even), a target (full margin), and a ceiling (what the premium buyer will pay). Never sell below the floor, even to clear stock it destroys your price reference in the market.

3. Understanding Local Market Prices

Walk your market weekly. Record prices for comparable products refined vs. unrefined, bottled vs. bulk, branded vs. unbranded. In most African cities, unrefined shea butter retails between the equivalent of USD 2.50 and USD 6.00 per kg depending on packaging and season, while cold-pressed coconut oil ranges from USD 3.00 to USD 8.00 per litre. Local price intelligence is your first defence against underpricing.

4. Pricing for Export Markets

Export pricing is not local price plus shipping. It is a completely different cost stack: certification (organic, fair trade, cosmetic grade), export documentation, freight, duties, importer margin, and currency risk. Work backwards from the landed price the buyer will pay.

Export price formula: FOB price = (Landed buyer price freight insurance import duty importer margin) (1 + your margin)

SAFETY & COMPLIANCE WARNING

Exporting shea butter or coconut oil to the EU, US, or UK without the correct phytosanitary certificate, cosmetic safety assessment (CPSR), and REACH/registration compliance can result in your entire shipment being rejected, destroyed, or your company blacklisted. Never quote an export price or accept a purchase order before confirming certification and customs requirements with your national export promotion agency (e.g., GEPA in Ghana, NEPC in Nigeria, EPZA in Kenya). Also verify that any preservative or additive you use is permitted in the destination market this is a common and costly mistake for small African cosmetic oil exporters.

5. Offering Discounts and Bulk Pricing

Discounts are a tool, not a habit. Structure them so larger orders still protect your margin:

  • Volume tiers: 19 units at full price; 1049 units at 10% off; 50199 at 15%; 200+ at 20% (never below your floor).
  • Payment terms as discount: 2% off for payment within 7 days; net 30 for established buyers.
  • Off-season promotions: Move slow stock without permanently resetting the price.
  • Barter and co-op deals: Trade oil for packaging or transport with other SMEs common and effective in African value chains.

Always calculate the discounted price against your break-even floor before agreeing.

6. Tracking Expenses and Profit

Use a single notebook or a free spreadsheet (Google Sheets, or apps like Wave, Zoho Books free tier). Record every expense same day. At month end, calculate:

  • Gross profit = Revenue Direct costs
  • Net profit = Gross profit Overheads (rent, transport, marketing, fees)
  • Gross margin % = Gross profit Revenue 100
  • Net margin % = Net profit Revenue 100

Healthy small-processor targets in Africa: gross margin 3550%, net margin 1020%. If your net margin is below 8% for three consecutive months, revisit your cost of production not your price first.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Costed every batch this month, including family labour and spoilage.
  2. Know my cost per litre/kg to the nearest 10 cents.
  3. Know my break-even floor and never sell below it.
  4. Checked local market prices for competing products this week.
  5. Confirmed certification and customs requirements before quoting export prices.
  6. Discounts are tiered and never breach the floor price.
  7. Every sale and expense recorded the same day.
  8. Reviewed gross and net margin at month end.
