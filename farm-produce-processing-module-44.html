Food Safety & Hygiene (HACCP)

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Business

1 Why food safety matters

Food safety is not optional. A single contaminated batch can:

  • Kill or hospitalise customers
  • End your business with lawsuits and fines
  • Destroy your brand reputation permanently
  • Trigger a national recall costing millions

Food poisoning is usually invisible — the customer eats, feels fine, then falls ill hours later. By the time symptoms appear, dozens of jars may have been sold. Prevention is everything.

Three main hazards to control: biological (bacteria, viruses, moulds), chemical (cleaning agents, pesticide residue), and physical (glass, metal, stones).

⚠ Never skip a step to save time. One shortcut can cost you your business.

2 Personal hygiene rules

Humans are the biggest source of food contamination. Every worker must follow these rules:

  • Wash hands with soap for 20 seconds before handling food, after toilet, after touching face
  • Wear clean uniform, hairnet, apron
  • No jewellery — rings, watches, earrings trap bacteria and can fall into food
  • Nails short and clean — no polish
  • Cover all cuts with waterproof blue plaster (blue shows up in food if it falls off)
  • No eating, drinking, smoking in production areas
  • Stay home if sick — especially with vomiting, diarrhoea, jaundice, or infected wounds

Enforce these rules with signs, training, and daily checks. Every worker signs a food handler's agreement.

3 Cleaning and sanitising

Cleaning and sanitising are two different steps. Both are needed.

Cleaning: Removes visible dirt, grease, food residue. Uses detergent and hot water. You can see it working.

Sanitising: Kills invisible bacteria and viruses. Uses heat (hot water 82°C+) or chemicals (chlorine 100 ppm, quaternary ammonium). You cannot see it working — trust the process.

Order of operations:

  1. Scrape and rinse off loose food
  2. Wash with hot soapy water
  3. Rinse with clean water
  4. Sanitise (chemical or heat)
  5. Air dry (never use a dirty cloth)

Clean-as-you-go: wipe spills immediately. Deep clean at end of each day. Weekly deep clean of equipment.

4 Pest control

Rats, mice, cockroaches, flies and ants carry disease. Pest control is non-negotiable.

Prevention:

  • Seal all cracks, holes, gaps
  • Screen windows and doors
  • Keep food in sealed containers
  • Clean up crumbs and spills immediately
  • Store waste in sealed bins away from production
  • Trim vegetation around the building

Control:

  • Bait stations (protected, not open poison — food safety risk)
  • Fly traps and UV zappers
  • Regular professional pest control (monthly)
  • Log every pest sighting — track patterns
⚠ Never use poison sprays in food production areas. Fly sprays contaminate food.

5 HACCP — the 7 principles

HACCP (Hazard Analysis Critical Control Points) is the global standard for food safety.

  1. Identify hazards — list every biological, chemical and physical risk at each step
  2. Determine CCPs — Critical Control Points where you can prevent or eliminate the hazard
  3. Set critical limits — e.g. pasteurisation at 90°C for 30 seconds
  4. Monitor CCPs — check and record at every production run
  5. Corrective actions — what to do when a CCP fails (reject batch, re-process)
  6. Verification — periodic testing (lab samples, audits)
  7. Record keeping — write everything down, keep for years

HACCP is required by law in EU, US and increasingly in Africa. Certification opens doors to supermarkets and export.

6 Critical control points in processing

Every food process has 3–6 critical control points. Examples:

Peanut butter: CCP1 roasting temperature, CCP2 grinding temperature, CCP3 metal detection, CCP4 jar seal integrity.

Juice: CCP1 washing water chlorine level, CCP2 pasteurisation time/temp, CCP3 bottle sanitising, CCP4 cap seal.

Dried fruit: CCP1 pre-treatment (sulphite/citric), CCP2 drying temperature, CCP3 moisture check, CCP4 packaging seal.

For each CCP, write: what you monitor, how often, the critical limit, what you do if it fails.

7 Monitoring and record keeping

HACCP lives or dies on records. If it isn't written down, it didn't happen.

Daily records:

  • Chlorine levels in wash water
  • Pasteuriser temperature charts
  • Fridge/freezer temperatures (twice daily)
  • Cleaning schedule sign-off
  • Pest sighting log
  • Batch production records (what, when, who, CCPs)

Keep records for at least 2 years (longer for exports — up to 5 years). Store in a locked cabinet or cloud backup. Review weekly.

💡 When a regulator or supermarket buyer visits, the first thing they ask for is records. Have them ready.

8 Recall procedures

If a problem is detected — customer complaint, lab test failure, or your own check — you must recall the affected batch.

Recall steps:

  1. Identify the batch code affected
  2. Stop all sales of that batch immediately
  3. Contact everyone you sold to (distributors, shops, direct customers)
  4. Get the product back and quarantine
  5. Inform the regulator (may be legally required)
  6. Investigate root cause
  7. Fix the process, document changes
  8. Communicate publicly if customer safety is at risk

A fast, well-managed recall saves your reputation. A slow or hidden one destroys it.

💰 HACCP certification is required for export and major supermarkets. Get it before you need it — retrofitting is harder and more expensive.
