1. Preparing Jars: Washing, Sterilizing, and Drying

Start by washing jars in hot soapy water. Use a bottle brush to scrub inside, especially if jars held oily foods. Rinse well. You need clean jars with no cracks or chips, and two-piece lids (flat disc and screw band) that are not rusty. For sterilizing, you have three practical options in an African kitchen. First, boiling: place jars and lids in a large pot, cover with water, boil for 10 minutes. Remove with clean tongs and place upside down on a clean cloth to dry. Second, oven: preheat oven to 120Â°C, place washed jars (without lids) on a baking tray, heat for 20 minutes. Lids can be boiled separately for 5 minutes. Third, steam: use a pot with a steamer insert, steam jars for 10 minutes. Whichever method, do not dry with a clothâair dry or use the ovenâs heat. Cloths can recontaminate. Jars must be hot when you fill them with hot jam. If they cool, reheat in oven for 5 minutes. Always fill jars to within 1 cm of the rim. Wipe rim with a clean damp cloth, then place lid and screw band finger-tight. Do not overtighten. After filling, process in a hot water bath (see Lesson 5) for a proper seal. If you skip sterilizing, mold and bacteria will spoil your jam within weeks. In hot African climates, sterilization is not optional. Work quickly and keep everything clean. Use tongs, not fingers. Have a clean towel under jars to catch drips. Prepare jars just before jam is ready, not hours ahead. For a small batch, 6 to 8 jars of 250 ml each is typical. Always make extra sterilized jars in case you have more jam than expected.

2. Mashing, Chopping, and Preparing Fruit

The texture of your jam depends on how you prepare the fruit. For a smooth jam, mash fruit thoroughly with a potato masher or wooden spoon. For a chunky jam, chop fruit into 1 cm cubes. Some fruits like mango, pawpaw, and pineapple are best chopped. Berries and soft fruits like guava can be mashed. Always remove skins, seeds, and cores unless the recipe says otherwise. For example, mango skin and seed must be removed. Pineapple core is toughâremove it. Citrus fruits need peeling and seeding. Weigh fruit after preparation. A standard ratio is 1 kg prepared fruit to 750 g sugar. If fruit is very sweet, reduce sugar to 600 g. If tart, use up to 1 kg. Add lemon juice: 2 tablespoons per kg of fruit. Lemon juice adds acid, which helps set and preserves color. For low-acid fruits like mango and pawpaw, acid is critical. Let fruit and sugar sit for 1 hour. This draws out juice, so you need less added water. Then mash or chop as desired. If you want seedless jam, put seeds and skins in a muslin cloth and boil with fruit, then remove. For a smoother jam, after cooking, press through a sieve or blend carefully. But blending hot jam is dangerousâcool slightly first. In Africa, fruits vary in water content. Mangoes in dry season are firmer; in rainy season, juicier. Adjust by adding 1-2 tablespoons of lemon juice or a small amount of pectin if available. Never use overripe or rotten fruit. One bad spot can spoil the whole batch. Wash all fruit well. For bananas, use ripe but firm ones; mash with a fork. For tomatoes (yes, tomato jam is common), peel, seed, and chop. Always taste fruit before starting. If it lacks flavor, your jam will too. Add a pinch of salt to enhance sweetness. Prepare fruit in batches no larger than 2 kg to ensure even cooking.

3. Cooking Methods: Open Kettle vs. Water Bath

There are two main cooking methods for jam: open kettle and water bath. Open kettle means you cook jam in a wide pot on the stove, stirring often, until it reaches set point. This is the traditional method in many African homes. Use a heavy-bottomed pot to prevent burning. Stainless steel or enamel is best; avoid aluminium as it reacts with acid. Cook over medium heat, stirring constantly with a long wooden spoon. Skim off foam with a metal spoon. The jam is ready when it sheets off the spoon (see Lesson 4). This method is fast and gives a rich flavor, but you must watch closelyâjam can burn in seconds. Water bath method means you cook jam first in a pot, then process filled jars in a boiling water bath. The cooking step is the same as open kettle, but after filling jars, you submerge them in boiling water for 10-15 minutes. This creates a vacuum seal and kills any mold spores. Water bath canning is safer for long-term storage, especially in hot humid climates. For both methods, use a thermometer if possible. Jam sets at 104-105Â°C (219-221Â°F) at sea level. At higher altitudes, subtract 1Â°C per 300 m. In Africa, many areas are above 1000 m, so adjust: at 1500 m, set point is about 100-101Â°C. If you donât have a thermometer, use the sheet or drop test. Open kettle jam must be stored in the fridge and eaten within 3 weeks. Water bath processed jam lasts 1 year in a cool dark place. For beginners, start with open kettle for small batches, then move to water bath for larger batches. Always stir from the bottom to prevent scorching. If jam starts to stick, remove from heat immediately and stir vigorously. Never leave jam unattended. Use a timer. A batch of 2 kg fruit takes 20-40 minutes to cook. Test frequently near the end.

4. Testing for Set: Sheet, Drop, and Thermometer

Knowing when jam is set is the most critical skill. If you undercook, jam is runny. If you overcook, it becomes hard and caramelized. Use three tests. First, the sheet test: dip a cold metal spoon into the jam. Lift it out and let it cool for 5 seconds. Tilt the spoon. If the jam falls in a single sheet or flake rather than dripping, it is set. If it drips like water, cook longer. Second, the drop test: place a small spoonful of jam on a cold plate (put plate in freezer for 5 minutes). Let it cool for 1 minute. Push it with your finger. If it wrinkles and holds its shape, it is set. If it runs, cook longer. Third, thermometer: use a candy or jam thermometer. Clip it to the pot, ensuring the bulb is in the jam but not touching the bottom. Bring jam to 104-105Â°C (219-221Â°F) at sea level. At 1000 m, set point is 103Â°C. At 2000 m, 101Â°C. If you donât have a thermometer, you can calibrate: boil water, note the temperature (e.g., 98Â°C at 1000 m). Then set point is 98 + 4 = 102Â°C. Always test at the end of cooking, not the beginning. Stir jam before testing to ensure even heat. Remove from heat while testing to prevent overcooking. If jam passes the test, skim foam, then fill jars immediately. If it fails, return to heat and boil for 2 more minutes, then test again. Repeat until set. For low-pectin fruits like strawberries, add 1 tablespoon lemon juice per kg to help set. For high-pectin fruits like apples and guavas, set is easier. Never rely on cooking time aloneâfruit moisture varies. A rainy season mango has more water and takes longer. Always use at least two tests. The sheet test is quick; the drop test is more reliable. The thermometer is most accurate. Practice with a small batch of cheap fruit like pawpaw. Over time, you will learn the visual cues: jam thickens, bubbles become slower and larger, and the color deepens.

5. Hot Water Bath Canning Step-by-Step

Hot water bath canning gives your jam a long shelf life. Follow these steps exactly. 1. Sterilize jars and lids (Lesson 1). Keep jars hot. 2. Fill a large deep pot with water and bring to a rolling boil. The pot must be deep enough to cover jars by 2.5 cm (1 inch). Place a rack or folded cloth at the bottom to prevent jars from touching the pot. 3. Fill hot jars with hot jam, leaving 1 cm headspace. Wipe rims. Place lids and screw bands finger-tight. 4. Use jar tongs to lower jars into boiling water. Do not let jars touch each other. 5. Cover pot and return water to a full boil. Start timing when water boils. Process for 10 minutes for altitudes up to 1000 m. For 1000-3000 m, process 15 minutes. For above 3000 m, 20 minutes. 6. After processing, turn off heat. Wait 5 minutes, then remove jars with tongs. Place on a clean cloth, upright, 5 cm apart. Do not tighten bands. 7. Let cool for 12-24 hours. You may hear a pop as lids seal. After cooling, press the center of each lid. If it does not move, it is sealed. If it pops up and down, it is not sealed. 8. Remove screw bands. Wipe jars. Label with date and fruit. Store in a cool, dark place. Sealed jam lasts 1 year. If any jar did not seal, store in fridge and eat within 3 weeks. Never use a jar with a cracked lid or rust. Never process in a pot that is too small. Always use a timer. For a small batch of 6 jars, use a pot that holds at least 8 liters. In Africa, many homes use a large sufuria or stock pot. That works. Do not use a pressure cooker unless you know the correct settingsâpressure canning is for low-acid foods, not jam. Jam is high-acid, so water bath is safe. If you live at high altitude, increase processing time. Always check seals before storing. If you see mold on the surface after opening, discard the whole jar. Do not scrape it off.

6. Troubleshooting Common Jam Problems

Even experienced cooks face jam problems. Here is how to fix them. Problem 1: Jam is too runny. Cause: undercooked or insufficient pectin. Fix: Return to pot, add 1 tablespoon lemon juice per kg, boil 5-10 minutes, retest. If still runny, add 1 tablespoon powdered pectin (if available) dissolved in a little water. Or cook longer. Problem 2: Jam is too hard. Cause: overcooked or too much sugar. Fix: Once hard, you cannot fully reverse it. Use it as a filling for cakes or melt with a little water for a syrup. Next time, reduce cooking time and test earlier. Problem 3: Jam has mold. Cause: unsterilized jars, dirty lids, or not processed. Fix: Discard the entire jar. Do not eat. Mold produces toxins. Problem 4: Jam has crystals (sugar crystals). Cause: undissolved sugar or too much sugar. Fix: Next time, stir sugar until fully dissolved before boiling. Add 1 teaspoon lemon juice to help prevent. Problem 5: Jam is too sweet. Cause: too much sugar. Fix: Add more lemon juice or a little citric acid. Or mix with tart fruit like tamarind. Problem 6: Jam is too tart. Cause: too little sugar or overripe fruit. Fix: Add a little sugar and reboil. Problem 7: Jam has a burnt taste. Cause: scorching. Fix: Do not stir burnt jam into the rest. Discard burnt batch. Use a heavy-bottomed pot and stir constantly. Problem 8: Jam separates (liquid on top). Cause: insufficient cooking or too much water. Fix: Stir well and reboil for 5 minutes. Problem 9: Lids do not seal. Cause: dirty rim, old lids, or not enough headspace. Fix: Re-process with new lids within 24 hours. Problem 10: Jam ferments (smells like alcohol). Cause: wild yeast from dirty fruit or jars. Fix: Discard. Always wash fruit and sterilize jars. In Africa, high humidity can cause mold faster. Store jam in a cool, dark place. If you are unsure, keep in fridge. Use clean spoons when serving. Never double-dip. A little care prevents most problems.

Practical Reference: Jam Making in the African SmallâScale Kitchen

This practical reference is written for the African agripreneur who wants to turn surplus fruit into a reliable, sellable product. Whether you are working in a home kitchen in Nairobi, a shared community kitchen in Accra, or a small processing unit in Kigali, the principles are the same: consistent quality, food safety, and a product that can compete in local markets. Jam making is a lowâbarrier valueâaddition business, but it demands discipline in technique and equipment. This lesson consolidates the core techniques from the module into a single, practical guide you can pin to your kitchen wall.

Before you cook a single batch, understand that jam is a preserved fruit product made by boiling fruit, sugar, and acid to a point where the natural pectin sets the mixture into a gel. The science is simple, but the execution is unforgiving. A jam that is too runny will not sell; a jam that is too stiff or crystallised will damage your brand. The techniques below are designed to help you hit the set point consistently, using equipment that is available and affordable across most African markets.

Preparing Jars: Washing, Sterilising, and Drying

Jars are your packaging and your preservation vessel. In many African markets, you can source glass jars from usedâbottle collectors, but you must inspect them for cracks and chips. Wash jars and lids in hot, soapy water, then rinse thoroughly. Sterilise by boiling jars and lids in a large pot of water for 10 minutes, or by placing them in an oven at 120Â°C for 15 minutes. If you use an oven, ensure jars are completely dry before filling. Wet jars dilute the jam surface and encourage mould. Always handle sterilised jars with clean tongs or clean hands; never touch the inside of the jar or the sealing surface of the lid.

For a business, invest in a jar rack or a dedicated pot for sterilising. This reduces breakage and speeds up your workflow. If you are producing more than 20 jars per day, consider a small commercial sterilising cabinet or a dedicated water bath canner. The cost is recovered quickly through reduced spoilage and fewer customer complaints.

Mashing, Chopping, and Preparing Fruit

Fruit preparation determines texture and yield. For a smooth jam, mash or crush fruit with a potato masher or a wooden spoon. For a chunky jam, chop fruit into uniform pieces of 1â2 cm. Uniformity is critical: uneven pieces cook at different rates, leading to some fruit turning to mush while other pieces remain hard. Remove stones, cores, and any bruised or mouldy parts. Bruised fruit carries spoilage organisms and offâflavours.

In Africa, popular jam fruits include mango, pineapple, guava, papaya, banana, and mixed tropical fruit. Each fruit has different pectin and acid levels. Mango and banana are low in acid and pectin, so they need added lemon juice and sometimes commercial pectin. Guava and pineapple are higher in pectin and acid, so they set more easily. Know your fruit. If you are buying fruit from smallholder farmers, agree on ripeness: slightly underâripe fruit has more pectin and acid, which helps setting. Overâripe fruit has more sugar and less pectin, requiring longer cooking and possibly added pectin.

Cooking Methods: Open Kettle vs. Water Bath

There are two main cooking approaches for jam. The open kettle method involves boiling fruit, sugar, and acid in a wide, heavyâbottomed pot until the mixture reaches the set point. This is the traditional method used across Africa. It requires constant stirring to prevent burning, especially in the final stages. The advantage is that you can make large batches with simple equipment. The disadvantage is that you lose water and volatile flavours, and the colour can darken.

The water bath method, also called the âwater bath canningâ method, is used after the jam is cooked and poured into jars. The filled, sealed jars are submerged in boiling water for a specified time to create a vacuum seal and kill any microorganisms on the surface. This method is not a cooking method for the jam itself; it is a preservation step. Do not confuse the two. Some recipes use a âwater bathâ to cook the fruit gently, but for jam, the open kettle is the primary cooking method. The water bath is the final safety step.

For a business, the open kettle method is more practical for smallâscale production. Use a heavyâbottomed stainless steel pot, ideally with a capacity of at least 10 litres. Avoid aluminium pots, as acid in fruit reacts with aluminium and gives a metallic taste. A wide pot increases evaporation and speeds up setting. A longâhandled wooden spoon or heatâresistant spatula is essential. If you are producing more than 50 jars per day, consider a steamâjacketed kettle, but this is a significant capital investment.

Hot water bath canning is the final step that gives your jam a long shelf life. It is not optional if you want to sell your jam in shops or export it. Follow these steps:

  1. Fill a large, deep pot with water and bring it to a rolling boil. The pot must be deep enough to cover the jars by at least 2.5 cm.
  2. Place a rack or a folded cloth at the bottom of the pot to prevent jars from touching the bottom and cracking.
  3. Fill sterilised jars with hot jam, leaving 0.5 cm of headspace. Wipe the rim with a clean, damp cloth.
  4. Place the lid on the jar and tighten it fingerâtight. Do not overtighten, as air needs to escape during processing.
  5. Using jar tongs, lower the jars into the boiling water. Ensure they are not touching each other.
  6. Process for 10 minutes for jars up to 500 ml, and 15 minutes for larger jars. Start timing when the water returns to a full boil.
  7. Turn off the heat and carefully remove the jars with tongs. Place them on a clean cloth or wooden board, away from drafts.
  8. Let the jars cool undisturbed for 12â24 hours. You should hear a âpopâ as the lids seal. Check the seal by pressing the centre of the lid; it should be firm and not move.

If a jar does not seal, refrigerate it and use it within two weeks. Do not sell unsealed jars. For a business, label each batch with the production date and a bestâbefore date (usually 12 months for properly sealed jam).
