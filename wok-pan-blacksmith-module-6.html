1. Module 6 Lesson 1: Sourcing and Preparing Local Scrap Steel in African Markets

Welcome to Module 6 of the Wok and Pan Blacksmith training. In this first lesson we focus on the raw material that will make or break your business: steel. Across Africa, the blacksmith who can reliably source, identify, and prepare quality scrap steel will always have work. The blacksmith who depends on one supplier or buys blindly will struggle. Your goal is to build a supply chain that is cheap, steady, and close to your workshop. Start by mapping every source of steel within a 50 kilometre radius of your forge. Visit mechanics, welders, construction sites, farms, mines, transport companies, and municipal dumps. Talk to the people who handle metal every day. Ask them what they throw away. In many towns, old vehicle leaf springs, coil springs, axles, and chassis rails are the best blacksmith steel available. Leaf springs from trucks and buses are thick, tough, and already shaped in long flat bars. Coil springs from cars and pickups are excellent for punches, chisels, and small tools. Axles and drive shafts give you round bar for tongs, hinges, and decorative work. Do not ignore smaller items. Broken hammer heads, old files, masonry drill bits, and bearing races are high carbon steel and can become excellent cutting tools. Rebar is common but usually low carbon and soft. It works for simple hooks, grills, and reinforcement but not for knives or chisels. Angle iron, flat bar, and square tube from bed frames and gates are mild steel and good for stands, frames, and general fabrication. Build relationships, not just transactions. Pay a fair price, arrive when you say you will, and bring a small gift or cold drink. In many African markets, trust is the real currency. A mechanic who trusts you will save the best springs for you instead of selling them to the first buyer. Set up a simple grading system at your workshop. Use a paint marker or punch to label steel as high carbon, medium carbon, or mild steel. Test unknown steel with a grinder spark test. High carbon steel throws short, bright, branching sparks. Mild steel throws long, straight, orange sparks. Medium carbon sits between. A file test also helps. A sharp file bites into mild steel but skates over high carbon steel. Keep a small notebook or phone note recording where each piece came from and what it tested as. Preparation is as important as sourcing. Before steel enters your forge, clean it. Remove dirt, paint, grease, and rust with a wire brush, sandpaper, or a weak acid bath. Paint and galvanising release toxic fumes when heated, so never burn them off inside a closed workshop. Work outdoors or use strong ventilation. Cut long springs and bars to manageable lengths with a hacksaw, angle grinder, or chisel. Anneal hard steel before working it. Annealing means heating the steel until it glows red or orange, then burying it in ash, sand, or vermiculite and letting it cool slowly overnight. This softens the steel so you can cut, drill, and file it without cracking. Store your steel off the ground on wooden pallets or racks. Keep it dry and oiled lightly to prevent rust. Organise by type and size so you do not waste time searching. A well-organised scrap pile is a bank account. Treat every piece as money. Price your raw material into every job. If a leaf spring costs you 5000 shillings and makes three knives, each knife carries a raw material cost. Know that number. Many blacksmiths fail because they charge for labour only and ignore material, transport, and fuel. Build a simple cost sheet for every product: steel, charcoal or gas, grinding discs, sandpaper, handle material, transport, and your time. Add 30 percent profit. Review prices every three months because scrap prices and fuel prices move. Finally, think about sustainability and community. As African cities grow, scrap steel will become more valuable. Consider training young people to collect and sort steel for you. Pay them per kilogram. This creates income, reduces waste, and secures your supply. Keep a backup supplier in a nearby town in case your main source dries up. In summary, your steel supply is a business system. Map sources, build trust, test and grade, clean and anneal, store well, and cost every piece. Do this and your forge will never run dry.

2. Module 6 Lesson 2: Forge Design, Fuel Efficiency, and Workshop Layout for African Conditions

In this second lesson we look at the heart of your business: the forge itself and the workshop around it. A good forge is not the biggest or the most expensive. It is the one that uses the least fuel, produces the right heat, and fits your products and your climate. Across Africa, blacksmiths use charcoal, coal, gas, and sometimes waste oil. Each fuel has strengths and weaknesses. Charcoal is widely available, lights easily, and gives a clean fire, but it burns fast and can be expensive in cities. Coal burns hotter and longer but produces smoke and sulphur, and good coal is not available everywhere. Gas is clean and controllable but requires cylinders, regulators, and a steady supply chain. Waste oil forges are cheap to run but smoky and need careful design to burn cleanly. Choose your fuel based on what is reliably available within one day of travel. Do not build a coal forge if coal arrives once a month. Build a simple side-blast or bottom-blast forge from local materials. A car wheel rim, a metal drum, or a welded steel box works well. Line it with clay, sand, and ash mixed with a little water. This refractory lining protects the metal and holds heat. Shape the fire pot like a shallow bowl with a hole at the bottom or side for the air pipe. The air pipe, called the tuyere, should be made from thick pipe that can survive heat. Connect it to a blower. You can use a hand-cranked blower, a vacuum cleaner motor, a car heater blower, or a bellows made from leather and wood. For most small workshops, a variable-speed electric blower with a gate valve is best because you can control the air. Too much air wastes fuel and burns steel. Too little air gives a weak fire. Learn to read the fire. A bright yellow-white fire is too hot for mild steel and will burn it. A cherry red to orange fire is right for most forging. A dull red fire is too cold. Control heat with air, fuel size, and distance from the tuyere. Build a hood and chimney to carry smoke away from your lungs. This is not optional. Smoke from charcoal, coal, and oil causes serious lung disease. Use local materials: mud brick, sheet metal, and clay. A simple hood over the forge and a vertical chimney above head height will save your health and make the workshop more comfortable. Design your workshop in zones. Zone one is the forge and anvil area. Keep the anvil close to the forge, about one or two steps away, so you do not lose heat walking. Mount the anvil on a solid stump or concrete block. The anvil should sit at knuckle height when you stand relaxed. Zone two is the cutting, grinding, and finishing area. Keep grinders, files, and sandpaper here, away from the fire. Zone three is the assembly and handle area. Zone four is storage for steel, fuel, and finished goods. Zone five is a small sales or display area near the entrance. Good layout reduces movement and increases output. A blacksmith who walks ten steps for every hammer blow will produce half as much as one who has everything within reach. Lighting matters. Work near a door or window for natural light. Add a solar panel and LED lights if grid power is unreliable. A small solar setup can run lights, a grinder, and a phone charger. This is a good investment. Ventilation matters too. Open eaves, high windows, and a chimney create airflow. In hot climates, a shaded work area with a breeze keeps you productive. Build a simple roof from iron sheets, thatch, or shade cloth. Protect your tools from rain and theft. Security is a real concern in many African towns. Lockable steel boxes, a strong door, and a watchman or neighbour agreement can protect your investment. Water is needed for quenching and cleaning. Keep a drum of clean water near the forge. Add a second drum for washing hands and tools. Do not quench in dirty water because it affects the hardness of your steel. Change water regularly. Think about fuel efficiency every day. Bank your fire when not in use by covering it with ash and damp coal or charcoal. This saves fuel for the next morning. Use the right size fire for the job. A small fire for small work, a large fire for large work. Do not heat a large fire to forge a small hook. Reuse heat. If you are making several items, heat several pieces at once and work them in sequence. This is called batch production and it saves fuel and time. Keep a fuel log. Record how much charcoal, coal, or gas you use each day and how many items you produce. After one month you will know your true fuel cost per item. This number lets you price accurately and spot waste. Finally, maintain your forge. Replace clay lining when it cracks. Clean the tuyere of clinker and ash. Check the blower and pipe for leaks. A well-maintained forge uses less fuel and gives better heat. In summary, build a forge that fits your fuel, your climate, and your products. Lay out your workshop in zones. Protect your health with a hood and chimney. Track fuel and cost. A smart forge is a profitable forge.

3. Module 6 Lesson 3: Advanced Tool Making and Heat Treatment for African Markets

Welcome to lesson three. Here we move from basic forging to advanced tool making and heat treatment. This is where your reputation is built. In African markets, a farmer, builder, or butcher will pay a premium for a tool that holds an edge, does not break, and lasts. Your goal is to make tools that customers bring back to show their friends, not to complain about. Start with tool steel selection. High carbon steel is your friend. Leaf springs, coil springs, old files, bearing races, and masonry drill bits are good sources. Test every piece with a spark test and a file test before you invest time. Remember, high carbon steel throws short, branching sparks and resists a file. Mild steel throws long, straight sparks and cuts easily. Do not waste fuel trying to harden mild steel. It will not harden. It will only frustrate you. Forge at the right temperature. High carbon steel should be forged at a bright orange to yellow heat, around 900 to 1000 degrees Celsius. Do not forge it at a dull red heat because it will crack. Do not overheat it to a white sparkle because it will burn and lose carbon. Work efficiently with quick, firm hammer blows. Reheat as soon as the colour drops to cherry red. Do not hammer cold steel. Normalise your tools before hardening. Normalising means heating the steel to its forging temperature, then letting it cool in still air. This relieves stress and refines the grain. For a knife or chisel, normalise two or three times. This simple step prevents cracks and warping during hardening. Now hardening. Heat the cutting edge or the whole tool to the correct temperature. For most high carbon steels, this is when the steel becomes non-magnetic. Use a magnet to test. When the magnet no longer sticks, the steel is at the right temperature, usually around 780 to 820 degrees Celsius. Hold it there for a short time so the heat soaks through, then quench. Quenching is the moment of truth. Use the right quench medium. Water is fast and can crack thin or complex tools. Oil is slower and safer for most knives and chisels. Use clean motor oil, vegetable oil, or commercial quenching oil. Warm the oil to about 50 to 60 degrees Celsius for best results. Do not use dirty oil or water with salt unless you know what you are doing. Quench by plunging the tool into the oil edge first, moving it slightly, and holding it until the red colour is gone. Do not pull it out early. After quenching, the tool is hard but brittle. It will snap like glass. You must temper it. Tempering means reheating the tool to a lower temperature to reduce brittleness while keeping hardness. Clean the steel to bright metal so you can see the oxide colours. Heat slowly and watch the colours run. Straw yellow, around 200 degrees Celsius, is good for knives and razors. Bronze or brown, around 250 degrees, is good for chisels and punches. Blue, around 300 degrees, is good for springs and axes. For most African farm tools, a bronze to blue temper gives a good balance of edge holding and toughness. You can temper in an oven, over a charcoal fire, or with a torch. If using a fire, bury the tool in hot sand or ash and watch the colours. Quench again in water or oil to stop the temper when the right colour appears. This is called colour tempering and it works well in a simple workshop. Test your tools. A good knife should shave hair or cut paper cleanly. A good chisel should cut mild steel without rolling its edge. A good axe should chop hardwood without chipping. If a tool fails, adjust your process. Cracks usually mean too fast a quench or too high a hardening temperature. Soft edges usually mean too low a hardening temperature or too high a temper. Brittle edges usually mean not enough tempering. Keep a log of every tool you make: steel source, hardening temperature, quench medium, temper colour, and test result. After twenty tools you will have your own recipe. Now think about product design for African markets. Farmers need hoes, machetes, axes, sickles, and pruning knives. Builders need chisels, punches, crowbars, and trowels. Butchers need cleavers, boning knives, and sharpening steels. Households need kitchen knives, spoons, and cooking tools. Each product has a price point. A farmer may pay 15000 to 30000 shillings for a good hoe. A butcher may pay 40000 to 80000 for a cleaver. Know your market and build to the price. Do not overbuild a cheap product or underbuild an expensive one. Add value with handles. A well-fitted wooden handle from local hardwood, secured with a metal ferrule and epoxy or rivets, turns a good blade into a great tool. Shape the handle for comfort. Sand it smooth. Oil it with linseed or palm oil. A comfortable tool sells faster and commands a higher price. Brand your work. Punch or stamp your initials or a simple mark on every tool. Wrap it in paper or cloth with a small card showing your name and phone number. This builds trust and brings repeat customers. Finally, teach others. Take on one apprentice at a time. Teach safety, steel selection, forging, and heat treatment. A skilled apprentice increases your output and spreads your reputation. In summary, advanced tool making is about control. Control your steel, your heat, your quench, and your temper. Test everything. Log everything. Build tools that work and your business will grow.

4. Module 6 Lesson 4: Pricing, Costing, and Selling Blacksmith Products in African Markets

In lesson four we turn to the money side of blacksmithing. You can be the best smith in your region, but if you cannot price and sell your products, the business will fail. This lesson gives you practical tools to cost, price, and sell in African markets. Start with the true cost of a product. Many blacksmiths only count steel and fuel. That is a mistake. Your cost has seven parts. First, raw material: steel, wood for handles, rivets, epoxy, and finishing oil. Second, fuel: charcoal, coal, gas, or oil used to make the item. Third, consumables: grinding discs, sandpaper, drill bits, welding rods, and quenching oil. Fourth, transport: the cost of moving steel and finished goods. Fifth, labour: your time and your workers time. Sixth, overhead: rent, electricity, water, tool wear, and security. Seventh, waste and failure: broken tools, failed heat treatments, and unsold stock. Add all seven. Then add profit. A simple formula is: selling price equals total cost plus 30 to 50 percent profit. For custom work, add a design fee. For urgent work, add a rush fee. For wholesale, reduce profit to 15 to 20 percent but sell in bulk. Know your break-even point. Break-even is the number of items you must sell each month to cover all costs. If your monthly costs are 500000 shillings and your average profit per item is 10000 shillings, you must sell 50 items per month to break even. Everything above 50 is true profit. Write this number on your workshop wall. It keeps you focused. Price by value, not just by cost. A farmer who uses your hoe to grow food for a year will earn far more than the price of the hoe. A butcher who uses your cleaver every day will earn its price in a week. Explain this to customers. Show them how the tool saves time, reduces injury, and lasts longer than a cheap imported tool. Compare your tool to the cheap alternative. A cheap machete may cost 8000 shillings and last three months. Your machete may cost 25000 shillings and last three years. Over three years, yours is cheaper. This is called total cost of ownership and it is a powerful sales argument. Sell in multiple channels. First, your workshop. Keep a clean display area with your best work. Second, local markets. Rent a stall one or two days a week. Third, hardware shops and agro-dealers. Sell wholesale to them at a discount. Fourth, institutions. Schools, hospitals, farms, and construction companies buy in bulk. Write a simple letter or visit them with samples. Fifth, online. Use WhatsApp, Facebook Marketplace, and local buy-and-sell groups. Post clear photos and prices. Sixth, export. As your quality improves, you can sell to neighbouring countries or to diaspora customers through agents. Build a simple brand. Choose a name that is easy to remember and pronounce. Make a sign for your workshop. Print simple business cards or flyers with your name, phone number, and products. Use a consistent mark on every tool. A brand is a promise of quality. Keep that promise. Handle customer complaints well. If a tool fails, replace it or repair it free within a reasonable time. This builds trust and word-of-mouth marketing. In African markets, reputation travels fast. One happy customer brings five more. One angry customer turns away ten. Train your customers. Show them how to sharpen, store, and use your tools. Give a small sharpening stone with every knife. This small gift creates loyalty and reduces complaints. Offer sharpening and repair services. This brings customers back to your workshop and creates steady income between big orders. Many blacksmiths earn more from repairs and sharpening than from new tools. Keep records. Use a simple notebook or a phone app. Record every sale, every expense, and every customer. At the end of each month, review your numbers. Which products sell best? Which customers pay on time? Which costs are rising? Adjust your prices and production accordingly. Manage cash flow carefully. Do not spend all your money on steel and fuel. Keep a reserve for slow months, tool repairs, and emergencies. Save at least 10 percent of every sale. Open a mobile money account or a bank account. Separate business money from personal money. This simple habit prevents business failure. Finally, think about growth. As demand grows, you can hire apprentices, buy better tools, and open a second workshop. You can specialise in high-value products like knives, axes, or agricultural tools. You can supply wholesalers and institutions. You can train other blacksmiths for a fee. Each step increases income and reduces risk. In summary, costing and pricing are not guesswork. They are systems. Know your costs, price for profit, sell in many channels, build a brand, and keep records. Do this and your blacksmith business will not just survive. It will thrive.

5. Module 6 Lesson 5: Scaling, Apprenticeships, and Building a Blacksmith Enterprise in Africa

Welcome to the final lesson of Module 6. Here we look at scaling your blacksmith business from a one-person workshop into a real enterprise. Scaling does not mean getting bigger for its own sake. It means increasing income, reducing risk, and creating opportunities for others. In Africa, blacksmithing can be a strong small business that supports a family, employs young people, and supplies essential tools to the community. Start with your own role. As the owner, you cannot do everything forever. You must move from doing all the work to leading the work. Your time should go to the highest-value activities: designing new products, finding customers, managing money, and training workers. The routine forging can be done by apprentices and skilled workers. This shift is difficult but necessary. Write down every task in your workshop. Mark each task as owner-only, skilled worker, or apprentice. Owner-only tasks include pricing, customer negotiations, quality control, and buying steel. Skilled worker tasks include forging, heat treatment, and finishing. Apprentice tasks include cleaning, cutting steel, tending the fire, and simple forging. This simple list tells you who to hire next. Hire slowly and train well. A bad worker costs more than an empty position. Look for young people who are reliable, honest, and willing to learn. Do not hire family only. Hire for skill and attitude. Start apprentices on a trial period of one to three months. Pay a fair wage and provide food or transport if possible. Teach safety first. A workshop with a bad accident will lose money, reputation, and workers. Create a simple training plan. Week one: safety, workshop layout, and tool names. Week two: fire management and steel identification. Week three: basic forging, drawing out, bending, and cutting. Week four: simple products like hooks, spoons, and nails. Month two: heat treatment and tool making. Month three: finishing, handles, and quality control. Month four: customer service and pricing. Month five: independent work on simple products. Month six: specialisation based on talent. This plan can be adapted to your products and your pace. Pay for skill, not just time. A worker who produces 20 quality items a day is worth more than one who produces 5. Set production targets and pay a bonus when targets are met. This motivates workers and increases your output. But do not sacrifice quality for speed. A broken tool costs you a customer. Quality control is your job. Check every item before it leaves the workshop. Use a simple checklist: correct size, correct hardness, smooth finish, secure handle, and clean packaging. Reject or repair anything that fails. Keep your best workers. Offer them a share of profits, a path to becoming a supervisor, or help starting their own workshop. In many African towns, a skilled blacksmith can open a second workshop and supply you with products. This is not competition. It is a network. You can focus on high-value items and let others make simple items under your brand. Diversify your income. Do not rely on one product or one customer. Add sharpening services, repair services, and custom orders. Add training workshops for other blacksmiths and for youth groups. Add sales of raw materials like steel, charcoal, and tools. Add a small retail shop selling your products and related goods like files, hammers, and safety gear. Each stream reduces risk and increases income. Build partnerships. Work with agricultural extension officers, vocational schools, NGOs, and government programs that support small businesses. Many African countries have grants, loans, and training programs for artisans. Ask at your local government office, chamber of commerce, or business development centre. Prepare a simple business plan. It does not need to be long. One page is enough: what you sell, who buys it, what it costs, what you charge, and how you will grow. A clear plan helps you get loans and support. Manage risk. Keep records of income and expenses. Save for slow seasons. Insure your workshop if possible. Train your workers in first aid and fire safety. Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand near the forge. Store fuel safely away from the fire. Protect your tools from theft. Build a strong reputation. In African markets, trust is everything. Deliver on time. Charge a fair price. Stand behind your work. Treat customers and workers with respect. A good name is worth more than any advertisement. Think about the future. As Africa grows, demand for quality tools will grow. Farmers, builders, and households will need more and better products. A well-run blacksmith enterprise can supply that demand. You can also export to neighbouring countries and to the diaspora. You can use social media to reach customers far away. You can train the next generation of blacksmiths. In summary, scaling is a journey. Move from doing to leading. Hire and train well. Diversify income. Build partnerships. Manage risk. Protect your reputation. Do these things and your blacksmith enterprise will grow from a small workshop into a lasting business that supports your family and your community. Thank you for completing Module 6. Go and build something strong.
