6. Logistics and Distribution

Seedlings are living products with a short shelf life and high fragility. Getting them from your nursery to the farmer in good condition is the difference between a happy customer and a dead shipment. This module covers packing, transport, timing, and loss reduction.

Module 6 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 6.1 — Packing seedlings for transport

Seedlings need protection from shock, drying, heat, and physical damage during transit.

MethodBest forProtection
Trays stacked on racksShort distance, same dayVery good
Cardboard boxes with dividersMedium distanceGood
Plastic crates (with holes)Medium distance, reusableVery good
Wooden cratesLong distance, heavy loadsExcellent
Bare root with damp clothTree seedlings, professionalGood if done right
Individual poly bagsFruit trees, high-valueExcellent

Packing rules:

  • Water seedlings 2 hours before packing. Not soaking — just damp.
  • Do not stack more than 3 trays high. Weight crushes lower seedlings.
  • Keep trays level. Tilting causes media to shift, damaging roots.
  • Cover with shade cloth for transport during hot hours.
  • Label each tray or box with customer name, crop, quantity, date.
  • Never leave packed seedlings in direct sun while waiting for transport.

For tree seedlings, bare-root transport is common for long distances:

  1. Remove seedling from tube or bag.
  2. Shake off excess soil gently.
  3. Wrap roots in damp (not wet) cloth or newspaper.
  4. Bundle 20–50 seedlings together.
  5. Keep cool and shaded.
  6. Plant within 48 hours — bare-root seedlings dry out fast.
The 2-hour rule: Water seedlings 2 hours before packing. Any longer and they become too wet and heavy. Any shorter and they may dry out during transport.
Practical exercise 6.1: Pack 5 trays for a hypothetical delivery. Time the process. Note any damage to seedlings. Improve your technique before the real delivery.

Lesson 6.2 — Transport options

ModeCapacityCostBest for
Own car boot20–50 traysFuel onlySmall local deliveries

Hired pickup100–300 trays$30–$100 per tripOccasional large deliveries
Hired truck500+ trays$100–$400 per tripVery large orders
Motorbike with trailer10–30 traysLowShort trips, small orders
Bus / shared transport5–30 traysVery lowSmall orders, distant

Transport rules:

  • Deliver early morning or late afternoon. Not during the hottest hours.
  • Drive slowly. Every bump damages seedlings.
  • Cover with shade cloth. Always.
  • Do not stack too high. Weight damages lower layers.
  • Plan routes. Deliver to nearest customers first, or the ones whose seedlings are most sensitive.
  • Have a backup plan. Vehicles break down. Have a phone number for a hired truck.
Practical exercise 6.2: Get a quote from 3 transport providers. Note capacity, cost, and reliability. Test one on a small delivery. Build a relationship before you need them urgently.

Lesson 6.3 — Timing the delivery window

Seedlings have a short window of being "ready". Sell them too early and they fail. Too late and they are overgrown and stressed.

CropBest age for saleWindow
Tomato4–6 weeks2 weeks before or after
Cabbage4–5 weeks2 weeks before or after
Onion6–8 weeks3 weeks before or after
Pepper6–8 weeks2 weeks before or after
Kale4–5 weeks2 weeks before or after
Fruit trees6–12 monthsMonths (flexible)

How to time delivery:

  • Coordinate with the farmer's planting calendar. They need seedlings on a specific day.
  • Confirm 3 days before. Call to confirm quantity, time, and location.
  • Deliver at the right time of day. Morning or late afternoon.
  • Have backup stock. If the farmer wants more than expected, you want to be able to supply.
  • Do not let seedlings sit too long. Once ready, they need to be delivered — every extra week reduces quality.
The window rule: Every crop has a peak sale window of about 2 weeks. Miss it and your seedlings lose value fast. Plan production around delivery dates, not the other way around.
Practical exercise 6.3: Map your customers' planting calendars. When do they need seedlings? Plan your sowing schedule to hit those dates 4–6 weeks in advance.

Lesson 6.4 — Delivery day logistics

Delivery day is where everything comes together — or falls apart. Here is the checklist:

Day before:

  • Confirm delivery with the customer (phone or WhatsApp).
  • Water seedlings.
  • Prepare packing materials and labels.
  • Confirm transport.
  • Check seedlings for quality (disease, damage, uniformity).

Morning of delivery:

  • Pack early, before the heat.
  • Load carefully, level trays, cover with shade cloth.
  • Bring a printed invoice and receipt book.
  • Bring extra cash for change if customer pays cash.
  • Have a phone with the customer's number and your transport provider's number.

On arrival:

  • Unload carefully.
  • Present the invoice.
  • Let customer inspect — walk with them.
  • Give a brief on handling and planting.
  • Collect payment (or confirm deposit balance).
  • Thank them and ask for feedback.

After delivery:

  • Record the sale in your log.
  • Take a photo (if customer agrees) for marketing.
  • Follow up in 5 days to ask how seedlings performed.
Practical exercise 6.4: Write out a delivery day checklist. Print it. Follow it for every delivery for the next 3 months.

Lesson 6.5 — Reducing losses in transit

Even with careful handling, some seedlings are lost. Target less than 5% loss. Common causes:

CausePrevention
Wilting from heatCover with shade cloth, deliver early morning
Media spillingPack trays level, secure against tilting
Crushed seedlingsDo not stack more than 3 trays high
Rough roadsSlow down, strap trays in place
Long transportWater lightly before load, cover with damp cloth
No airflowDo not fully seal boxes — seedlings need air
Customer's delayCall 30 minutes before arrival so they are ready

If loss occurs:

  • Assess the damage. What percentage is affected?
  • Compensate the customer. Replace the lost seedlings on the next delivery — even if the loss was not your fault. Goodwill is more valuable than the small cost.
  • Log the incident. What caused it? How do you prevent it next time?
The 5% rule: Expect to lose 5% of seedlings in transit even with perfect handling. Budget for this. If losses exceed 5%, investigate — something is wrong with your handling.
Practical exercise 6.5: Track transport losses for your first 10 deliveries. Log every damaged seedling. Find the pattern. Fix it.

Lesson 6.6 — Building customer relationships through delivery

Delivery is not just logistics — it is customer service. How you deliver determines whether the farmer buys from you again.

What makes a customer come back:

  • On-time. You promised a day. You deliver that day. No excuses.
  • Correct quantity. If they ordered 5,000, they get 5,000. Not 4,800.
  • Clean, healthy seedlings. No disease, no damage, uniform size.
  • Helpful handover. A brief on handling. Answer questions.
  • Follow-up. A call 5 days later to check how seedlings are doing.
  • Simple invoice. Clear, correct, professional.

Building the relationship:

  • Learn their names. Mr. Moyo, Mrs. Chirwa. Not "the tomato farmer."
  • Ask about their season. How did the crop yield? What went well?
  • Remember their preferences. Some farmers want bigger seedlings, some smaller. Track it.
  • Do not push extra sales. If they ordered 5,000, do not try to sell them 6,000 on the spot. Trust first.
The repeat rule: The second order is worth 3× the first. A farmer who buys once and is happy tells 3 others. A farmer who buys once and is disappointed tells 10 others — and never returns.
Practical exercise 6.6: After every delivery, write one sentence about the customer — name, crop, quantity, anything personal you learned. Build this record. It becomes your marketing gold.
