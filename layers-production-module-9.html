1. Why Biosecurity Is Your Cheapest Insurance in Layer Farming

Every layer farmer in Africa knows the pain of a disease outbreak. One morning your birds look fine. By evening you see ruffled feathers, greenish droppings, and a sudden drop in egg production. Within a week you could lose half your flock or be forced to cull everything. The financial damage goes far beyond dead birds. You lose future egg income, you spend money on drugs and vet fees, you may lose customers who switch to another supplier, and you may need months to rebuild your flock. Biosecurity is the set of everyday practices that stop disease from entering your farm and spreading inside it. Think of it as insurance you pay for with discipline rather than cash. A good biosecurity program costs very little compared to the cost of a single outbreak of Newcastle disease, avian influenza, or infectious bronchitis. In Africa, several factors make biosecurity both harder and more important. Many farms are small and located close to neighbours, markets, and live bird traders. Wild birds, rodents, and insects move freely between farms. Veterinary services may be far away, and vaccines may not always be available or properly stored. Power cuts make refrigeration unreliable. Despite these challenges, the basic principles of biosecurity work everywhere. They are physical separation, cleaning and disinfection, traffic control, pest control, vaccination, and monitoring. Physical separation means keeping your birds away from other birds, people, and equipment that could carry disease. If you can put a solid fence around your poultry house and keep a footbath at the entrance, you have already blocked many common disease pathways. Cleaning and disinfection means removing organic matter before applying disinfectant, because dirt stops disinfectants from working. Traffic control means limiting who enters your farm and making sure vehicles, crates, and boots are cleaned before they come in. Pest control means keeping feed in closed containers, clearing bushes around houses, and controlling rats and wild birds. Vaccination protects birds against specific diseases, but it must be done correctly with good quality vaccines and proper handling. Monitoring means watching your birds daily for signs of illness and acting fast when something looks wrong. A practical African example: a farmer in Uganda with 500 layers noticed that traders who came to buy eggs were walking directly into the layer house. After a disease outbreak that killed 200 birds, she built a small sales area at the gate, provided boots for workers, and stopped visitors from entering the house. Her next flock had no disease outbreak, and she saved money on antibiotics. That is biosecurity in action. Another example from Nigeria: a farmer lost an entire flock to Newcastle disease after buying live birds from a market and mixing them with his layers without quarantine. After that, he built a simple isolation pen 30 metres from the main house and kept new birds there for three weeks before mixing them. He also started vaccinating on a strict schedule. His mortality dropped from 30 percent to under 5 percent. Biosecurity is not about building a laboratory. It is about consistent daily habits. The most common mistakes are letting visitors into the poultry house, sharing equipment with neighbours, buying birds from unknown sources, using dirty water, and skipping vaccination because the vaccine is expensive or hard to get. Each of these mistakes can wipe out your profit for the year. As a business, your goal is to produce eggs reliably and sell them at a profit. Disease destroys reliability. A flock that gets sick produces fewer eggs, smaller eggs, and eggs that may be unsafe to eat. Customers remember when you fail to deliver. Biosecurity protects your brand as much as your birds. Start today by writing a simple biosecurity plan for your farm. List the biggest risks: visitors, new birds, feed delivery, water source, rodents, wild birds, and shared equipment. For each risk, write one action you will take. Then train your workers on those actions and check them every week. You do not need expensive technology. You need a gate that stays closed, boots that stay clean, and a routine that does not break. Remember that biosecurity is cheaper than treatment. A bag of disinfectant costs far less than a course of antibiotics or a lost flock. Vaccines cost a fraction of the income you lose when disease hits. The time you spend cleaning and controlling traffic is paid back in steady egg production. In the next lessons, we will go deeper into specific practices: cleaning and disinfection, vaccination schedules, quarantine and isolation, and dealing with disease outbreaks when they happen. For now, understand this: biosecurity is not an optional extra. It is the foundation of profitable layer farming in Africa.

2. Cleaning, Disinfection, and Farm Hygiene That Actually Works

Cleaning and disinfection are the backbone of biosecurity. Many farmers think that spraying disinfectant is enough. It is not. Disinfectants do not work well when dirt, manure, or organic matter is present. The first step is always cleaning: removing all physical dirt, manure, feed, and dust. Only then does disinfection kill the remaining germs. In layer houses, you need to clean and disinfect between flocks, and you need to maintain hygiene daily while birds are producing. Start with the between-flock cleanout. Remove all birds, then remove all feed and manure. Scrape floors, walls, and equipment. Wash with water and detergent. Pay attention to corners, cracks, fans, feeders, drinkers, and egg belts. After washing, let surfaces dry. Then apply a disinfectant according to the label. Common disinfectants include glutaraldehyde, quaternary ammonium compounds, and bleach-based products. In Africa, bleach is widely available and cheap, but it must be used correctly. Bleach loses strength over time and is inactivated by organic matter. Use it on clean surfaces and mix it fresh. If you use a knapsack sprayer, make sure it is clean and only used for disinfectants, not for herbicides or pesticides. After disinfection, allow the house to dry completely before bringing in new birds. If possible, leave the house empty for at least one week. This resting period helps break disease cycles. For daily hygiene, focus on water and feed. Water can carry bacteria, viruses, and parasites. If you use borehole or well water, test it at least once a year. If you use surface water, treat it. You can add chlorine at a rate that leaves a free chlorine level of 1 to 3 parts per million. Do not guess. Use a simple test kit. Clean drinkers every day with a brush and disinfect them weekly. Feed should be stored in closed containers off the floor. Spilled feed attracts rodents and wild birds. Clean up spills immediately. Footbaths are a simple but powerful tool. Place a footbath at the entrance of every poultry house. Use a disinfectant that works in the presence of organic matter, and change the solution daily or when it looks dirty. A footbath only works if everyone uses it, including you, your workers, and any visitor who must enter. Many farmers put a footbath at the gate but then walk around it. That defeats the purpose. Make it easy to use by placing it directly in the path. Provide boots for workers and visitors. If you cannot provide boots for everyone, at least provide disposable plastic shoe covers or a boot wash with a brush and disinfectant. A boot wash is a shallow tray with disinfectant and a stiff brush. You scrub your boots before stepping in. This is cheaper than boots and works well. Pest control is part of hygiene. Rats and mice carry Salmonella and other diseases. They also eat feed and damage buildings. Keep grass and bushes cleared around houses. Store feed in metal or heavy plastic bins with tight lids. Fix holes in walls and roofs. Use traps or approved rodenticides carefully so birds and children cannot access them. Wild birds can carry avian influenza and Newcastle disease. Prevent them from entering houses by screening windows and vents with wire mesh. Do not let wild birds nest in the roof. If you keep ducks or other poultry, keep them separate from layers. Ducks can carry influenza viruses without showing signs. They should have separate housing, separate water, and separate equipment. Never share equipment between species without cleaning and disinfecting. Water quality is often overlooked. A clean water source protects your birds. If your water is dirty, birds will get sick even if the house is clean. Use a filter if possible. If not, let water settle in a tank and use the clearer water. Add chlorine or another approved water sanitizer. Test the water after treatment to make sure the level is right. Too little chlorine does not kill germs. Too much can harm birds. Record keeping helps you stay consistent. Keep a simple notebook or use a phone app to record when you clean, when you disinfect, when you change footbath solution, and when you see pests. This record helps you spot problems and shows buyers or inspectors that you are serious about hygiene. A practical example from Kenya: a farmer with 1,000 layers had a recurring problem with watery droppings and reduced egg production. She cleaned the house but did not disinfect drinkers properly. After training, she started scrubbing drinkers daily with a brush and disinfecting them weekly. She also tested her water and found it had high bacterial load. She started treating water with chlorine and cleaning the storage tank. Within three weeks, droppings returned to normal and egg production improved by 8 percent. The cost of chlorine and a brush was tiny compared to the extra eggs she sold. Another example from Ghana: a farmer used the same boots to visit his neighbour's farm and then walk into his own layer house. After a disease outbreak, he bought two pairs of boots. One pair stayed at the neighbour's farm, and one pair stayed at his own farm. He also stopped borrowing crates from traders without disinfecting them. These simple changes prevented another outbreak. Remember that cleaning and disinfection are not one-time events. They are habits. The most effective farms are not the ones with the most expensive equipment. They are the ones where workers understand why hygiene matters and do it every day. Train your workers. Show them how to mix disinfectant correctly. Show them how to scrub drinkers. Explain that dirty boots can kill a flock. Give them the tools they need: brushes, buckets, soap, disinfectant, and gloves. Then check their work. A quick daily inspection of drinkers, feeders, and footbaths will tell you whether hygiene is happening. If you see manure build-up or dirty water, fix it immediately. Do not wait for a disease outbreak. Hygiene is a business investment. Clean farms produce more eggs, have lower mortality, and can sell to buyers who care about food safety. In many African markets, buyers are increasingly asking about hygiene and disease control. A farm with good hygiene can command a better price or secure contracts with hotels, schools, and supermarkets. Start with the basics: clean, disinfect, control pests, and manage water. Do them consistently, and you will see the difference in your flock and your profit.

3. Vaccination Schedules and Vaccine Handling for African Layer Farms

Vaccination is one of the most effective tools for preventing disease in layers. But a vaccine is only as good as its handling and administration. In Africa, challenges such as power cuts, long transport distances, and limited cold chain can reduce vaccine effectiveness. This lesson explains how to build a practical vaccination schedule and how to handle vaccines correctly so your birds get real protection. First, understand what vaccines do. They expose birds to a weakened or killed form of a disease organism. The bird's immune system responds and builds protection. If the bird later meets the real disease, its immune system is ready. Vaccines do not cure sick birds. They prevent disease in healthy birds. Therefore, timing matters. You must vaccinate before birds are exposed. For layers, common diseases to vaccinate against include Newcastle disease, infectious bronchitis, infectious bursal disease (Gumboro), fowl pox, Marek's disease, and avian influenza where vaccines are available and approved. The exact schedule depends on your country, local disease pressure, and the vaccines available. Always consult a veterinarian or extension officer for a schedule suited to your area. A typical layer vaccination schedule in many African countries looks like this. Day 1: Marek's disease vaccine at the hatchery. Day 7: Newcastle disease plus infectious bronchitis, often given by eye drop or nasal drop. Day 14: infectious bursal disease, given in drinking water. Day 21: second Newcastle plus infectious bronchitis. Day 28: second infectious bursal disease. Week 6: fowl pox, given by wing web stab. Week 8: Newcastle disease, often killed vaccine by injection. Week 10: infectious bronchitis booster if needed. Week 12: Newcastle disease booster. Week 16 to 18: Newcastle disease plus infectious bronchitis plus infectious bursal disease boosters before laying. Then every 8 to 12 weeks during laying, give Newcastle disease booster according to local advice. This is a general guide. Your local schedule may differ. The key is to follow a written plan and record every vaccination. Vaccine handling is critical. Most vaccines must be kept cold, between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius. Some are stored frozen, but not all. Read the label. If the cold chain breaks, the vaccine may lose potency. In areas with power cuts, use a vaccine carrier with ice packs. Do not freeze ice packs directly against the vaccine vials unless the label says the vaccine can be frozen. Wrap ice packs in paper or cloth to prevent freezing. When transporting, use a good cooler. Do not leave vaccines in a hot car. Do not store vaccines in a fridge door, because temperature changes when the door opens. Keep them in the main compartment. Use a thermometer to monitor fridge temperature daily. If you do not have a fridge, buy only the amount of vaccine you can use immediately. Plan vaccination days so you collect vaccines and use them the same day. For reconstituted vaccines, use them within two hours. Do not save mixed vaccine for later. Do not mix more than you need. Use clean, cool water for drinking water vaccines. Do not use chlorinated water because chlorine kills the vaccine virus. If your water contains chlorine, add skim milk powder or use a vaccine stabilizer according to instructions. Remove drinkers and let birds get thirsty before giving the vaccine water. Make sure every bird drinks. For eye drop or nasal drop vaccines, use a clean dropper and give one drop per bird. For injections, use a new needle for every 100 to 200 birds or when the needle becomes blunt. Do not inject sick birds. Handle birds gently to avoid stress. Stress reduces vaccine response. Vaccinate in the cool part of the day, early morning or late evening. Do not vaccinate during extreme heat or when birds are already sick. If birds are sick, consult a vet. Sometimes you must treat first and vaccinate later. Record keeping is part of vaccination. Write down the date, vaccine name, batch number, expiry date, method, and number of birds vaccinated. This record helps you know when the next booster is due and helps trace problems if a disease occurs. If you sell eggs or birds, buyers may ask for vaccination records. Good records build trust. A practical example from Tanzania: a farmer received a Newcastle vaccine from a supplier but left it in his car for three hours while he attended a meeting. The vaccine became warm. He used it anyway. Two weeks later, his birds showed Newcastle symptoms and 40 percent died. The cost of the vaccine was small, but the loss was huge. After that, he bought a cooler and ice packs and always collected vaccines last, going straight home. Another example from Zambia: a farmer used chlorinated tap water to mix a drinking water vaccine. The chlorine killed the vaccine. The birds were not protected. A vet advised him to use borehole water or add skim milk powder. He repeated the vaccination correctly, and his next flock had no disease. Vaccine quality matters. Buy from reputable suppliers. Check expiry dates. Check that the cold chain was maintained. If a vaccine arrives warm or the ice packs are melted, do not use it. Report the problem to the supplier. Do not buy vaccines from open markets or unknown sellers. In some areas, counterfeit vaccines exist. They cost money and give false security. Work with a vet or a trusted agro-vet shop. Combine vaccination with biosecurity. Vaccines do not replace cleaning, traffic control, and pest control. A vaccinated flock can still get sick if exposed to a heavy disease challenge or a different strain. Use vaccines as one layer of protection, not the only one. Finally, train your workers on vaccine handling. Show them how to read a thermometer, how to mix vaccine, and how to give eye drops. Explain why cold chain matters. If workers understand the reason, they are more likely to follow the rules. Keep a simple vaccination calendar on the wall. Mark the dates. Set phone reminders. Plan ahead so you are not caught without vaccine or ice packs. Vaccination is an investment in your flock's health and your farm's profit. Done correctly, it prevents disease and keeps egg production steady. Done poorly, it wastes money and gives false confidence. Be disciplined, and your birds will reward you.

4. Quarantine, Isolation, and Managing New Birds Safely

Bringing new birds onto your farm is one of the highest-risk activities in layer production. New birds can carry diseases without looking sick. They can introduce Newcastle disease, infectious bronchitis, Salmonella, mites, and worms. Quarantine and isolation are the practices that reduce this risk. Quarantine means keeping new birds separate from your existing flock for a period of time. Isolation means keeping sick birds away from healthy birds. Both are essential for biosecurity. In Africa, many farmers buy point-of-lay pullets or day-old chicks from other farms or markets. Sometimes they buy from neighbours or traders. Each purchase is a potential disease event. A good rule is to buy from a reputable hatchery or breeder with a known health record. Ask for vaccination records. Ask about the health status of the source flock. If possible, visit the source farm. Look for clean housing, healthy birds, and good biosecurity. If the source farm looks dirty or the birds look sick, do not buy. When you bring new birds home, put them in a quarantine house or pen. This pen should be at least 30 metres from your main flock if possible. If 30 metres is not possible, use a solid wall or a separate building. The quarantine area should have its own feeders, drinkers, boots, and tools. Do not share equipment between quarantine and main flock without cleaning and disinfecting. Ideally, assign one worker to care for quarantine birds and a different worker for the main flock. If that is not possible, care for the main flock first, then the quarantine birds. Never go from quarantine to main flock without changing clothes and boots. The quarantine period should be at least 21 days, and preferably 30 days. During this time, watch the birds daily. Look for signs of illness: ruffled feathers, drooping wings, watery or green droppings, coughing, sneezing, swollen eyes, reduced feed intake, or sudden deaths. If you see any signs, contact a vet immediately. Do not move birds out of quarantine until you are sure they are healthy. If a bird dies during quarantine, send it to a vet or laboratory for diagnosis if possible. Do not throw dead birds into the bush or feed them to dogs. Burn or bury them deeply. After handling quarantine birds, wash your hands and change clothes. If you buy birds from a market, quarantine is even more important. Market birds are mixed with many other birds and are exposed to many diseases. Many farmers make the mistake of buying market birds and putting them directly with their flock. This is one of the fastest ways to introduce disease. If you must buy from a market, keep those birds separate for at least 30 days and monitor closely. Better still, avoid market birds for your layer flock. Buy from a hatchery or a trusted breeder. Isolation is for sick birds. If you notice a bird or a few birds showing signs of illness, move them to a separate isolation area immediately. This prevents them from spreading disease to healthy birds. The isolation area should be far from the main house, with its own equipment. Wear gloves and boots when handling sick birds. Wash your hands afterwards. Do not carry sick birds through the main house. Do not use the same bucket or feeder for sick and healthy birds. If many birds are sick, do not try to isolate each one. Instead, isolate the whole group or house. Stop moving birds between houses. Stop visitors. Increase cleaning and disinfection. Call a vet. A practical example from Uganda: a farmer bought 200 point-of-lay pullets from a trader and put them directly into his layer house with 800 existing birds. Within ten days, birds started dying. The diagnosis was Newcastle disease. He lost 600 birds. If he had quarantined the new birds for 30 days, he would have seen the disease before mixing them. Another example from Nigeria: a farmer noticed three birds with swollen eyes. He moved them to a small pen behind the house. He wore different boots when caring for them. He called a vet, who diagnosed a respiratory infection. The farmer treated the sick birds and did not lose any others. Isolation saved his flock. Quarantine and isolation require planning. Before you buy new birds, prepare the quarantine pen. Clean and disinfect it. Set up separate feeders, drinkers, and boots. Make sure you have a way to dispose of manure from the quarantine area without contaminating the main flock. If you cannot provide a separate pen, do not buy new birds until you can. It is better to wait than to risk your whole flock. Record keeping helps. Write down the date you received new birds, the source, the number, and the quarantine end date. Record any signs of illness. This information helps you make decisions and trace problems. Train your workers on quarantine rules. They must understand that quarantine birds are not to be mixed with main flock birds. They must use separate equipment and clothes. They must report any signs of illness immediately. If workers do not follow the rules, quarantine fails. In Africa, where farms are often close together and birds move through markets, quarantine is one of the most important biosecurity tools. It is not expensive. It requires space, discipline, and time. The cost of a separate pen and a few extra feeders is tiny compared to the cost of losing your flock. Think of quarantine as a waiting period that protects your business. It gives you time to observe new birds and catch problems before they spread. It also gives you time to vaccinate new birds if needed, according to your vet's advice. Do not rush. Do not skip it. Your flock's health depends on it. Finally, remember that quarantine is not just for new birds. It is also for birds returning from shows, markets, or other farms. If a bird leaves your farm and comes back, quarantine it. If you borrow equipment, clean and disinfect it before use. If a visitor has birds at home, do not let them enter your houses. Quarantine and isolation are habits that protect your investment. Build them into your routine, and you will sleep better at night.

5. Responding to Disease Outbreaks and Building a Resilient Farm

Even with good biosecurity, disease outbreaks can happen. The difference between a small problem and a disaster is how fast you respond. This lesson covers how to recognize disease early, what to do when you suspect an outbreak, and how to build a farm that recovers quickly. Early recognition saves birds. Learn the normal behaviour of your flock. Healthy layers are active, eat well, drink well, and have bright eyes and smooth feathers. They produce eggs consistently. Signs of disease include a drop in feed or water intake, a drop in egg production, soft-shelled or misshapen eggs, ruffled feathers, drooping wings, pale combs, watery or green droppings, coughing, sneezing, gasping, swollen eyes, paralysis, and sudden deaths. Any of these signs should trigger action. Walk through your flock at least twice a day. Look at the birds, the droppings, the feed, and the water. Listen for coughing or sneezing. Check the litter for wet spots. Count dead birds daily. If you see something unusual, do not wait. Act immediately. The first step is to isolate the problem. If only a few birds are sick, move them to an isolation pen. If many birds are sick, isolate the whole house. Stop all movement of birds, people, and equipment between houses. Stop visitors. Do not sell or give away birds or eggs from the affected house until you know what is happening. Call a veterinarian or extension officer. Describe the signs, the number of sick birds, the number of deaths, and when it started. If possible, send a dead or sick bird to a laboratory for diagnosis. In many African countries, government veterinary services or regional laboratories can help. Do not guess and do not self-medicate without advice. Wrong treatment wastes money and can make things worse. While waiting for diagnosis, increase biosecurity. Clean and disinfect boots, equipment, and vehicles. Change footbath solution. Wear gloves when handling sick birds. Wash hands frequently. Dispose of dead birds properly by burning or deep burial. Do not sell sick birds or eggs from sick birds. This is illegal in many countries and can spread disease to people and other farms. If the diagnosis is a viral disease such as Newcastle or avian influenza, there may be no treatment. You may need to cull affected birds to save the rest. This is painful, but it is often the fastest way to stop the outbreak. Follow veterinary advice. If the disease is bacterial, such as Salmonella or E. coli, antibiotics may be prescribed. Use them exactly as directed. Do not use antibiotics preventively without veterinary advice. Overuse creates resistance and leaves residues in eggs. After the outbreak, clean and disinfect thoroughly. Remove all litter and manure. Wash and disinfect the house, feeders, drinkers, and equipment. Leave the house empty for at least two weeks. If possible, test the environment before bringing in new birds. Review what happened. How did the disease enter? Was it new birds, visitors, wild birds, feed, water, or equipment? Write down what you find. Fix the weak point. If you did not quarantine new birds, start doing it. If visitors entered the house, build a barrier. If water was dirty, treat it. If vaccination was missed, update your schedule. A practical example from Kenya: a farmer noticed a drop in egg production and a few deaths. He called a vet the same day. The vet diagnosed infectious bronchitis. The farmer isolated the affected house, stopped selling eggs from it, and increased disinfection. He lost some birds but saved the rest. Because he acted fast, his neighbour's farm, which waited two weeks, lost three times as many birds. Another example from Ghana: a farmer had an outbreak of fowl typhoid. He treated with antibiotics prescribed by a vet, culled the worst-affected birds, and disinfected the house. He also improved his water sanitation and started vaccinating against the disease where a vaccine was available. His next flock was healthy. Building a resilient farm means preparing before an outbreak. Keep a disease emergency kit: gloves, disinfectant, footbath supplies, a thermometer, and a notebook. Save the phone number of your vet and the nearest laboratory. Know where to buy vaccines and medicines. Have a plan for culling and disposal. Train workers on what to do if they see sick birds. Make sure everyone knows who to call. Financial resilience also matters. An outbreak costs money. Keep some savings or access to credit for emergencies. Consider insurance if available in your area. Diversify income so that one flock loss does not destroy your business. For example, you might sell manure, grow crops, or keep a small number of other livestock. Record keeping helps you recover. Keep records of flock performance, vaccination, feed, and sales. If you need to claim insurance or prove losses, records help. They also help you see patterns. If disease happens every year at the same time, you can plan ahead. Learn from other farmers. Join a farmer group or cooperative. Share information about disease outbreaks in your area. If you hear of a disease nearby, increase your biosecurity immediately. Do not wait for it to reach your farm. In Africa, where veterinary services may be stretched, farmer networks are a vital source of early warning. Finally, remember that disease outbreaks are not a sign of failure. They are a sign that biology is unpredictable. What matters is how you respond. Fast, informed action limits losses. Good biosecurity reduces the chance of outbreaks. Good records and planning help you recover. Your goal is not a farm that never has disease. Your goal is a farm that survives disease and keeps producing. With the right habits, you can protect your birds, your income, and your future in layer farming.

All disinfectants must be stored in original labelled containers, locked away from children, feed, and eggs. Used vaccine vials, needles, and sharps must be disposed of in a designated sharps container and never reused.

Quick Reference Checklist: Daily and Weekly Biosecurity Actions

  1. Footbath check (daily): Confirm footbath at Zone 2 entrance contains fresh disinfectant at correct dilution. Replace at least every 48 hours or when visibly dirty.
  2. Visitor log (every entry): Record name, farm visited in last 72 hours, purpose, and time in/out. No visitor who has been on another poultry farm within 72 hours enters Zone 3.
  3. Cold chain audit (daily): Check vaccine fridge temperature twice daily. Log readings. Any reading above 8C triggers immediate review of vaccine viability.
  4. Mortality check (twice daily): Remove dead birds immediately. A sudden rise above 0.5% per day in layers is an outbreak signal call your vet the same day.
  5. Feed and water hygiene (daily): Clean drinkers and feeders. Never allow feed to sit mouldy. Aflatoxin in African maize is a silent production killer.
  6. Rodent and wild bird control (weekly): Inspect bait stations, netting, and roof gaps. Wild birds carry Newcastle Disease and Avian Influenza.
  7. Egg collection and storage (daily): Collect eggs at least twice daily. Store at 1518C. Dirty eggs must never enter the sale crate they spread Salmonella to your customers.
  8. Record review (weekly): Compare feed intake, egg production, mortality, and water consumption against the previous week. A 5% drop in any metric warrants investigation.
