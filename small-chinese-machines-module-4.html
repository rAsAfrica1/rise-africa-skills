1. HS Codes and Import Duties for Machinery

Every machine you import has an HS codea six- to ten-digit number that tells customs what it is and how much duty you pay. For small Chinese machines like brick makers, maize grinders, or sewing machines, codes usually start with 84 (mechanical appliances) or 85 (electrical). For example, a diesel engine falls under HS 8408.90, while a sewing machine is HS 8452.10. Get the code wrong and you risk overpaying, underpaying (with penalties), or having your goods seized.

To find your code, use the World Customs Organization's HS database or your country's customs tariff book. In Nigeria, use the Nigeria Customs Service Trade Portal; in Kenya, the Kenya Revenue Authority iTax system. Ask your Chinese supplier for the HS code they usebut verify it yourself. Check the duty rate: many African countries charge 520% import duty on machinery, plus 035% excise duty on some items, and 7.518% VAT. For example, Ghana charges 5% duty on most industrial machines, plus 12.5% VAT and 0.5% ECOWAS levy.

Duty is calculated on CIF value: Cost of goods + Insurance + Freight. If your machine costs $2,000, insurance $50, and freight $300, CIF = $2,350. At 10% duty, you pay $235. Always ask for the correct HS code before shipping. If unsure, hire a customs broker to classify your goodscosts $50$200 but saves thousands in fines.

Keep a record of the HS code on your commercial invoice, packing list, and bill of lading. Mismatched codes cause delays. Use online tools like TariffData or DutyCalculator to estimate total landed cost. Remember: duty rates change, so check annually. For small machines, many countries offer reduced rates under the AfCFTA if you meet rules of originbut that requires proof the machine is made in Africa, which Chinese imports do not qualify for.

2. Pre-Shipment Verification of Conformity (PVoC) and SONCAP

Before your machine leaves China, it must pass a conformity check called PVoC (Pre-Shipment Verification of Conformity) or SONCAP (Standards Organisation of Nigeria Conformity Assessment Programme). Without it, your goods will be rejected at the port. PVoC is required in Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, and others; SONCAP is Nigeria's version. The goal is to stop unsafe or substandard products.

How it works: You hire an accredited inspection company like SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek, or Cotecna. They inspect the machine at the factory or warehouse in China, test it against local standards, and issue a Certificate of Conformity (CoC). For SONCAP, you need a Product Certificate first, then a SONCAP Certificate per shipment. Costs: $300$800 per shipment, depending on machine value. The process takes 510 days. You must provide the supplier's test reports, ISO certificates, and a proforma invoice.

If you skip PVoC, your goods arrive without a CoC. Customs will flag them, and you'll pay a penalty (15% of CIF) plus re-inspection fees. In Nigeria, without SONCAP, goods are seized and you may need to re-export at your own cost. In Kenya, PVoC is mandatory; non-compliant goods are destroyed or returned.

To avoid trouble: Tell your supplier you need PVoC/SONCAP before shipping. Book inspection early. Ensure the machine has a nameplate with model, voltage, and power. If the machine is second-hand, many countries ban it outrightcheck first. For small Chinese machines, buy from suppliers who already have experience with African PVoC. Ask for a sample CoC from a previous shipment. Keep digital copies of all certificates. Your customs broker will need them to clear goods.

3. Sea Freight vs. Air Freight: Cost and Time

Sea freight is cheap but slow; air freight is fast but expensive. For small Chinese machines, sea freight is usually the only practical option because machines are heavy and bulky. A 20-foot container from Shanghai to Mombasa costs $1,500$3,000 and takes 2540 days. Air freight from Guangzhou to Lagos costs $4$8 per kg and takes 37 days. A 100 kg machine by air would cost $400$800; by sea, maybe $150$300 but wait a month.

Sea freight options: FCL (Full Container Load) means you rent the whole container. LCL (Less than Container Load) means you share with otherscheaper for small volumes but slower (add 714 days for consolidation) and higher risk of damage. For a single small machine, LCL is fine. For 5+ machines, compare FCL. Always get quotes from freight forwarders like DHL Global Forwarding, Kuehne+Nagel, or local African forwarders. Ask for all-in cost: ocean freight, terminal handling, documentation, and customs brokerage.

Air freight is best for urgent spare parts or very high-value, low-weight items. But for a 200 kg brick maker, air freight could cost more than the machine. Also, air freight has weight and size limitscheck with the airline. Some forwarders offer air-sea combination (sea to Dubai, then air to Africa) but it's rarely worth it.

Key tip: Never choose the cheapest sea freight without checking transit time and reliability. Some cheap carriers transship multiple times, causing delays. Use a forwarder with a proven track record on your route. For landlocked countries like Uganda or Rwanda, add trucking from Mombasa or Dar es Salaamanother $1,000$2,500 and 510 days. Always insure your shipment; sea freight insurance costs 0.51% of CIF value.

4. Customs Clearance: Documentation and Agents

Customs clearance is where most importers lose money and time. You need a complete document set: commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading (B/L), certificate of origin, PVoC/SONCAP certificate, and import declaration (IDF in Kenya, Form M in Nigeria). Missing one document can delay your goods for weeks.

The commercial invoice must show seller and buyer details, HS code, description, quantity, unit price, total value, and Incoterms (FOB, CIF, etc.). The packing list shows how goods are packed, weights, and dimensions. The bill of lading is your title to the goodswithout it, you cannot collect cargo. Always request a telex release or original B/L from your supplier. For sea freight, the B/L arrives after the ship; for air freight, use an air waybill (AWB).

Hire a licensed customs agent (broker). In Nigeria, use a licensed agent from the Nigeria Customs Service; in Kenya, a clearing agent registered with KRA. A good agent knows the local system, can classify your goods correctly, and fast-tracks clearance. Expect to pay $200$500 per shipment for brokerage, plus government fees. Ask for a breakdown: customs duty, VAT, levies, terminal charges, and agent fee. Never pay cash without a receipt.

Use the customs portal to track your declaration. In Kenya, use the Kenya TradeNet System; in Nigeria, the NICIS II. If you import regularly, consider getting a customs bond or a deferment account to pay duties monthly. For small machines, many countries have a simplified clearance for low-value shipments (under $1,000), but you still need documents. Always keep copies of everything for 5 yearscustoms can audit later.

5. Understanding VAT and Other Taxes

VAT (Value Added Tax) is a consumption tax you pay on imports. In most African countries, VAT is 1518% of CIF value plus duty. For example, if CIF is $2,350 and duty is $235, VAT is calculated on $2,585. At 16% (Kenya), VAT = $413.60. In Nigeria, VAT is 7.5%, so $193.88. Some countries charge VAT on the duty-inclusive value, others on CIF onlycheck your local rules.

Other taxes and levies: ECOWAS levy (0.5% of CIF) in West Africa, East African Community levy (0.5% of CIF) in EAC, and various port levies. In Ghana, there's an African Union levy (0.2%) and a COVID-19 levy (1%). In Kenya, there's a Railway Development Levy (1.5% of CIF) and an Import Declaration Fee (2% of CIF, minimum KES 5,000). These add up. Always ask your broker for a full tax computation before you commit.

Can you avoid VAT? Only if you are VAT-registered and can claim input credit. For a small business, you may not be registered. But if you sell machines, you should registerthen you can offset import VAT against VAT you charge customers. Keep all import documents; they are proof for input credit. Some countries exempt certain machinery from VAT if used for agriculture or manufacturing. For example, Kenya exempts VAT on solar equipment and some agricultural machinery. Check the VAT Act or ask customs.

Never undervalue your goods to reduce VAT. Customs uses valuation databases and can penalize you 25 times the duty. If you think the value is wrong, appeal with evidence (invoices, bank transfers). Pay all taxes through the official portalnever cash to an agent. Get an electronic receipt. For small machines, total taxes can add 2040% to your landed cost, so budget for it.

6. Avoiding Demurrage and Port Delays

Demurrage is the fee you pay when your container sits at the port beyond free time. Free time is typically 37 days for sea freight. After that, charges start at $50$150 per day per container, rising daily. In Lagos or Mombasa, demurrage can quickly exceed the value of your machine. Port delays happen due to missing documents, customs queries, unpaid duties, or slow inspection.

To avoid demurrage: Start clearance before the ship arrives. Send all documents to your agent 7 days before ETA. Pre-pay duties and taxes so customs can release immediately. Ensure your PVoC/SONCAP certificate is ready. If customs asks for inspection, be present or have your agent push for same-day inspection. Use a reputable agent who knows the port officials. In Nigeria, demurrage at Apapa Port can be $100/day after 5 free days. In Kenya, Mombasa charges $80/day after 7 days.

Other port delays: Terminal handling charges (THC) must be paid before cargo release. In some ports, you need a gate pass and a delivery order. For landlocked countries, your container may sit at the port of entry waiting for transit documents. Always clear transit bonds in advance. If you have multiple containers, clear them one by onedon't wait for all.

If demurrage starts, negotiate. Some shipping lines waive part if you have a good reason (e.g., customs strike). But don't rely on it. The best strategy is speed: have your agent on standby, pay all fees online, and collect your goods the same day they are released. Consider using a bonded warehouse if you cannot clear immediatelycosts $10$20 per day but cheaper than demurrage. For small machines, always budget an extra $200$500 for port delays. Never leave your container at the port; it's the fastest way to lose money.

<h3>Pre-Shipment Verification of Conformity (PVoC) and SONCAP</h3>
<p>Many African countries require a Pre-Shipment Verification of Conformity (PVoC) or a similar conformity assessment before goods leave China. In Nigeria, this is SONCAP (Standards Organisation of Nigeria Conformity Assessment Programme). In Kenya, it is PVoC under KEBS. In Tanzania, it is PVoC under TBS.</p>

<p>Without a valid Certificate of Conformity (CoC) or SONCAP Certificate (SC), your goods will be rejected at the port, and you will pay for storage, re-shipment, or destruction. The process is:</p>
<ol>
<li>Supplier applies to an accredited inspection agency (e.g., SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek, Cotecna) in China.</li>
<li>Product is tested and inspected against the destination country's standards.</li>
<li>Certificate is issued and must accompany the shipping documents.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Customs Clearance: Documentation and Agents</h3>
<p>Customs clearance is where most importers lose money and time. The documents you need are:</p>
<ul>
<li>Bill of Lading (B/L) or Air Waybill (AWB)</li>
<li>Commercial Invoice and Packing List</li>
<li>Certificate of Origin</li>
<li>PVoC / SONCAP Certificate</li>
<li>Import Declaration Form (IDF) or equivalent</li>
<li>Tax Identification Number (TIN) and business registration</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Choosing a customs agent:</strong> Use a licensed, reputable clearing agent with experience in machinery. Ask for their license number, recent references, and a written fee schedule. Avoid agents who promise to "under-declare" value this leads to penalties, seizure, and blacklisting. A good agent will pre-clear your documents before the vessel arrives, so clearance takes hours, not weeks.</p>

<h3>Understanding VAT and Other Taxes</h3>
<p>Beyond import duty, you will pay Value Added Tax (VAT) on the CIF value (Cost, Insurance, Freight) plus duty. In most African countries, VAT on machinery is 1518%. Some countries exempt agricultural machinery from VAT check your national revenue authority.</p>

<p>Other taxes and levies may include:</p>
<ul>
<li>ECOWAS Trade Liberalisation Scheme (ETLS) levy (0.5%)</li>
<li>African Union import levy (0.2%)</li>
<li>Environmental or excise levy on engines</li>
<li>Port development levy and terminal charges</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Business tip:</strong> Build a landed-cost spreadsheet before you order. Include FOB price, freight, insurance, duty, VAT, port charges, agent fees, and inland transport. Many importers are surprised by the final landed cost sometimes 4060% above the FOB price.</p>

<h3>Avoiding Demurrage and Port Delays</h3>
<p>Demurrage is the charge you pay when your container stays at the port beyond the free period (usually 37 days). In African ports, demurrage can be USD 50150 per container per day. To avoid it:</p>
<ul>
<li>Ensure all documents are ready before the vessel arrives.</li>
<li>Pre-pay duty and taxes where possible.</li>
<li>Use an agent who can clear within the free period.</li>
<li>Arrange inland transport in advance.</li>
<li>Monitor vessel arrival and port congestion.</li>
</ul>

<ol>
<li>Confirm the correct 810 digit HS code with a licensed agent before ordering.</li>
<li>Verify PVoC / SONCAP requirements for your destination country before shipment.</li>

<li>Choose sea freight (FCL preferred) for machines; air freight only for urgent spare parts.</li>
