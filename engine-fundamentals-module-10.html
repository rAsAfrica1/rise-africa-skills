Lessons

1. L1

In this first lesson, you will learn how to wire a car alternator to charge a 12-volt battery and power your irrigation pump's electrical system. Start by identifying the alternator's terminals: B+ (positive output), D+ (indicator light), and W (tachometer). Use a 10-gauge wire to connect B+ to the battery positive terminal through a 60-amp fuse. Connect the alternator case to a clean metal ground on the engine block. The D+ terminal goes to a 12-volt warning light (2-watt bulb) then to the ignition switch. When the engine runs, the alternator should output 13.8 to 14.4 volts. Check with a multimeter set to DC volts. If voltage is below 13.5, the alternator is weak. For a typical 3-kilowatt pump, you need at least 50 amps from the alternator. In Africa, many used alternators from Toyota or Nissan vehicles work well. Clean all connections with sandpaper and tighten firmly. Use heat shrink tubing on exposed wires to prevent corrosion from dust and humidity. Mount the battery in a wooden box with a lid to protect it from sun and rain. Add a 100-amp circuit breaker between the battery and the pump motor for safety. Test the system by running the engine at 1500 RPM and measuring voltage at the battery. It should rise to 14 volts. If not, check the alternator belt tensionit should have about 1 cm of play. This basic charging system will keep your battery topped up for long irrigation days.

2. L2

Now that you have a charging system, you need a battery to store energy for when the engine is off or at low RPM. A deep-cycle lead-acid battery is best for irrigation because it can handle slow discharge. A 12-volt, 100 amp-hour (Ah) battery will run a 1-horsepower (746-watt) pump for about 1.5 hours. For a 2-horsepower pump, use two batteries in parallel (positive to positive, negative to negative) to double capacity. Always use thick cablesat least 25 square millimeters for battery connections. Install a battery isolator switch so you can disconnect the battery when the pump is not in use. Check battery water level every month; add distilled water only, never tap water. In hot African climates, battery water evaporates faster, so check weekly. Keep terminals clean with a mixture of baking soda and water. Apply petroleum jelly to prevent corrosion. Charge the battery fully before first useuse a 10-amp charger for 10 hours. Never discharge below 11.8 volts; that damages the battery. Install a voltmeter on your control panel to monitor. For a 24-volt system, connect two 12-volt batteries in series (positive of one to negative of the other). This gives more power for larger pumps. Always wear safety glasses when working with batteriesthey contain sulfuric acid. If acid touches skin, rinse with plenty of water. Store batteries upright and in a ventilated area. A well-maintained battery lasts 3-5 years.

3. L3

In this lesson, you will build a simple control panel to start and stop your pump safely. You need a metal or plastic box, a 30-amp toggle switch, a 40-amp relay, a 15-amp fuse, and a push-button start switch. The relay is the keyit allows a small current from the ignition switch to control the large current to the pump motor. Wire the relay's coil terminals (85 and 86) to the ignition switch and ground. Connect the relay's common terminal (30) to the battery positive through a 40-amp fuse. The normally open terminal (87) goes to the pump motor positive. Ground the pump motor case to the engine block. Use a 12-volt test light to check connections. For automatic control, add a float switch in your water tank. When the tank is full, the float switch opens the relay circuit, stopping the pump. When water level drops, the pump restarts. Float switches are available in most hardware stores in Africa for about $10. Mount the control panel on a wall away from water spray. Label all switches clearly: 'Pump On/Off', 'Battery Voltmeter', 'Fuse'. Use cable glands to seal wire entries against dust and insects. Test the panel by pressing startyou should hear the relay click and the pump hum. If not, check the fuse and relay. Always disconnect the battery before working on the panel. A neat, well-labeled panel prevents accidents and makes troubleshooting easy.

4. L4

Your pump motor needs protection from overload, low voltage, and phase loss (for three-phase motors). Start with a thermal overload relay. Set it to 110% of the motor's full load amps (FLA). For a 2.2 kW (3 HP) single-phase motor, FLA is about 13 amps, so set the overload to 14.3 amps. If the motor draws more, the relay trips and cuts power. Next, install a low-voltage disconnect. Use a 12-volt DC voltage monitor relay set to 11.5 volts. When battery voltage drops below 11.5, the relay opens and stops the pump, preventing deep discharge. For three-phase motors, add a phase failure relay to protect against lost phases. In rural Africa, voltage fluctuations are common, so a voltage stabilizer (automatic voltage regulator) is wise. It keeps voltage steady at 230 volts 5%. Also install a 30-amp circuit breaker on the AC side if you use an inverter. For DC pumps, use a 50-amp DC breaker. Mount all protection devices in a dust-proof enclosure. Test each device monthly: press the test button on the overload relay; it should trip. Simulate low voltage by disconnecting the battery and checking that the pump stops. Keep a spare fuse and relay on hand. These protections may cost $50-100 but save a $500 motor. In dusty environments, clean relay contacts with contact cleaner every 3 months. Never bypass a protection deviceit's there for a reason.

5. L5

To run an AC pump from your 12-volt battery, you need an inverter. Choose a pure sine wave inverter, not modified sine wave, because motors run cooler and last longer. For a 1.5 kW (2 HP) pump, use a 3000-watt inverter to handle starting surge. Connect the inverter directly to the battery with 35 square millimeter cables, keeping the cable length under 1.5 meters. Use a 300-amp fuse on the positive cable. Mount the inverter in a cool, dry placenot in direct sun. In Africa, ambient temperatures can reach 40C, so ensure good ventilation. If the inverter overheats, it will shut down. For three-phase pumps, you need a three-phase inverter, which is more expensive. Alternatively, use a single-phase motor with a capacitor start. When wiring the inverter, connect the pump's live wire to the inverter's live output, neutral to neutral, and ground to ground. Use a 20-amp AC circuit breaker between inverter and pump. Check inverter output voltage with a multimeterit should be 220-240 volts AC. If you see less than 200 volts, the battery may be low or cables too thin. Never run the inverter without a ground connection. Test the system by running the pump for 10 minutes and feeling the inverter caseit should be warm, not hot. If hot, add a small 12-volt fan to blow air over it. Keep the inverter manual for troubleshooting codes. A good inverter costs $200-400 but is essential for AC pumps.

6. L6

In this final lesson, you will learn to diagnose and fix common electrical problems. Start with a multimetera must-have tool. Set it to DC volts to check battery and charging. A fully charged battery reads 12.6 volts; 12.0 volts means 50% charged. If your pump won't start, check these in order: battery voltage, fuse, relay, switch, and motor connections. Use a test light to trace power from battery to pump. If the relay clicks but pump doesn't run, the relay contacts may be burntreplace it. If the pump hums but doesn't turn, the capacitor may be bad (for AC motors) or the motor is seized. For DC motors, check brushes. A common problem in Africa is corroded connections due to humidity. Clean all terminals with a wire brush and apply dielectric grease. If the alternator isn't charging, check the belt, then the voltage regulator. A quick test: run engine at 2000 RPM and measure battery voltageshould be 13.8-14.4V. If not, replace the alternator. For intermittent faults, wiggle wires while the pump runs to find loose connections. Keep a logbook of all electrical issues and fixes. Carry spare fuses, relays, and a multimeter in your toolbox. If you smell burning, disconnect battery immediatelyit's a short circuit. Never work on live circuits. With practice, you'll diagnose most problems in minutes. Remember: 90% of electrical issues are bad connections or dead batteries. Start there.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Electrical and Control Systems for a Car-Engine Irrigation Pump



This practical reference supports Lessons L1L6 of the Electrical and Control Systems module in the course Convert a Car Engine to an Irrigation Water Pump. It is written for African agribusiness operators who want a reliable, low-cost, and commercially viable pumping set built from a salvaged petrol or diesel car engine. The focus is on the electrical and control side: starting, charging, protection, and automation. Every decision here should be judged by one business question: does this keep water flowing at the lowest cost per hectare irrigated?



In most African smallholder and commercial contexts, the engine is the cheapest part of the system because salvaged units are abundant. The expensive failures are electrical: flat batteries, burnt starters, corroded wiring, and uncontrolled dry-running that destroys pumps. A disciplined electrical and control design turns a rough conversion into a bankable asset that can be rented out, sold as a service, or used to irrigate high-value crops such as vegetables, orchards, or fodder.





1. Starting and Charging System


A car engine needs a battery, a starter, and an alternator. In an irrigation conversion, the battery must do more than crank the engine: it must also power control relays, sensors, and, in many cases, the pump contactor. A 12 V, 70100 Ah lead-acid or AGM battery is the practical baseline for engines up to about 2.0 litres. For diesel engines, especially indirect-injection units common in African workshops, use a larger battery (100120 Ah) and heavier cables (2535 mm) to avoid voltage drop on cold starts.



The alternator should be matched to the battery and to the duty cycle. A 4070 A alternator is usually sufficient for a pump running 48 hours per day, but if you add lighting, a water-level controller, or a mobile-phone charging point for a farm shop, upgrade to 7090 A. Always fit a charge warning lamp or a voltmeter; a failed alternator is the most common cause of a "dead" irrigation set in the field.

2. Protection and Control


Control is where the business case is protected. An unprotected engine can be destroyed in minutes by low oil pressure or by running dry. Fit three layers of protection:



  • Low-oil cut-out: a pressure switch that breaks the ignition circuit if oil pressure falls below about 0.5 bar.

  • Low-water cut-out: a float switch or electrode probe in the sump, tank, or borehole that stops the engine or closes the pump contactor when water is absent.

  • Overload / overcurrent: a thermal-magnetic breaker or a contactor with a thermal overload relay sized to the pump motor.


Use a 4-pin or 5-pin automotive relay to isolate the starter circuit from the control circuit. This prevents the starter from being engaged while the engine is running, a common failure when operators are untrained. Label every wire with a number and a function; in the field, a labelled loom saves hours of fault-finding and protects your service margin.

SAFETY WARNING Electrical and Fuel Hazards


Petrol engines produce explosive vapours and high-voltage ignition. Never work on the electrical system with the engine running or the battery connected. Disconnect the negative terminal first and reconnect it last. Use insulated tools, remove jewellery, and keep a dry-powder or CO extinguisher within reach. In Africa, where workshop power may be unstable, never bypass fuses with wire or foil; a bypassed fuse has started more fires than any other single fault. Ensure all metal enclosures and the engine block are bonded to a common earth. If you are not a qualified auto-electrician, engage one for the starter and alternator circuits.




3. Automation for Business Reliability


Automation is not a luxury; it is a labour-saving and asset-protecting investment. A simple timer, a float switch, and a contactor can turn an irrigation set into a semi-automatic system that runs without an operator present. This allows a single operator to manage multiple plots, or to irrigate at night when evaporation losses are lowest and electricity or fuel demand is lower.


For off-grid sites, consider a 12 V DC pump for small plots and a 230 V AC pump driven by an inverter or a generator for larger plots. In both cases, the control logic is the same: sense water, sense oil pressure, allow start, run, and stop on fault. Document the logic on a laminated card in the pump house; this is a selling point when you rent the set to neighbouring farmers.



Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Battery terminals clean, tight, and greased; voltage above 12.4 V at rest.

  2. Main fuse within 300 mm of the battery positive terminal and correctly rated.

  3. Starter and alternator cables sized to Table 1 and free of chafing.

  4. Low-oil cut-out tested and set to 0.30.7 bar.

  5. Low-water cut-out tested by lifting the float or shorting the probe.

  6. Pump contactor and thermal overload set to motor full-load current.

  7. All wires labelled, loomed, and bonded to a common earth.

  8. Emergency stop and fire extinguisher accessible and checked monthly.

Business takeaway: the electrical and control system is the difference between a converted engine that runs for a season and one that runs for a decade. Invest in correct cable sizing, real protection devices, and clear labelling. That investment pays back through fewer breakdowns, lower repair costs, and the ability to rent your pumping service to other farmers a direct path to higher income per hectare in African agribusiness.
