1. Farrowing Supervision

Farrowing supervision means being present when the sow gives birth. Most piglets die during the first 3 days, so your presence can save many lives. Start by preparing a clean, dry farrowing pen with a heat lamp or heat box for the piglets. The sow needs a quiet, dark area with no drafts. Write down the expected farrowing date (114 days after mating) and check the sow every 4 hours starting 2 days before. When she starts nesting, restless, and her udder swells, she will farrow within 12-24 hours. Stay nearby. As each piglet is born, tear the sac if it covers the face, wipe mucus from the nose and mouth with a clean cloth, and dry the piglet with a towel or dry straw. Tie the umbilical cord with a clean string about 2 cm from the belly and cut it with disinfected scissors. Dip the stump in iodine. Put the piglet under the heat lamp or in a warm box. Make sure it gets colostrum within 30 minutes. If a piglet is born without breathing, rub its chest vigorously and hold it upside down for a few seconds. If the sow is in distress or no piglet comes after 30 minutes of straining, call a vet. Count the placentas; there should be one per piglet. Keep a record of births, deaths, and any problems. Supervised farrowing can reduce stillbirths and crushing. In Africa, where many farmers cannot afford constant vet care, simple supervision by the farmer is the cheapest way to save piglets. Stay calm, keep the area clean, and act quickly.

2. Colostrum in the First 6 Hours

Colostrum is the first milk a sow produces. It is thick, yellowish, and rich in antibodies that protect piglets from disease. Piglets are born with no immunity, so they must drink colostrum within the first 6 hours of life. After 6 hours, their gut can no longer absorb the antibodies. The first 30 minutes are best. Make sure every piglet gets colostrum. If a piglet is weak, hold it to a teat and squeeze a few drops into its mouth. The best teats are the front ones, which give more milk. If a sow has more piglets than teats, rotate the smaller piglets to the front and the stronger ones to the back. If a sow dies or has no milk, use colostrum from another sow that farrowed within 24 hours, or use a commercial colostrum replacer. In Africa, you can also use cow colostrum as a last resort, but pig colostrum is best. Never give water or glucose before colostrum; it can damage the gut. Keep the piglets warm while they suckle. A cold piglet will not suckle. If a piglet has not suckled within 2 hours, assist it. Check that the sow has enough teats; if not, consider cross-fostering to another sow that farrowed at the same time. Cross-fostering works best within 24 hours. Always wash your hands before handling piglets. After the first 6 hours, piglets can drink regular milk. But colostrum is the foundation of their health. Without it, they will likely die from diarrhea or pneumonia. So be there at farrowing and make sure every piglet gets its share.

3. Keeping Piglets Warm

Newborn piglets cannot control their body temperature. They need a warm, dry place away from the sow. The ideal temperature for a newborn piglet is 32-35Â°C. For a 3-week-old piglet, 24-28Â°C is fine. If piglets are cold, they huddle, shiver, and refuse to suckle. They can die within hours. In Africa, many farmers use a heat lamp (infrared bulb) hung 45-60 cm above the floor. A 100-watt bulb is enough for a small pen. If you have no electricity, use a heat box: a wooden box with a lid and a small entrance, filled with dry straw. The piglets' body heat warms the box. You can also use a charcoal stove, but keep it outside the pen to avoid fumes. Another option is a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel. Place the heat source in a corner, away from the sow. The piglets will learn to go there. Bedding must be dry and clean. Wet bedding draws heat away. Use dry straw, wood shavings, or shredded paper. Change it daily. Avoid drafts. Fix holes in walls or use curtains. In hot climates, piglets may need cooling during the day, but nights can be cold. Monitor piglets: if they are spread out and panting, it's too hot; if they are piled up and shivering, it's too cold. Adjust the heat lamp height or add more bedding. A simple thermometer helps. Keep the farrowing pen at 20Â°C for the sow, but provide a warmer microclimate for piglets. This reduces crushing because piglets will not seek warmth under the sow. Warm piglets are active, suckle well, and grow faster. So invest in a heat source; it pays back in saved piglets.

4. Iron Injection and Iron Deficiency

Piglets are born with very little iron. Sow's milk has almost no iron. Without extra iron, piglets become anemic: their skin looks pale, they breathe fast, and they grow slowly. Anemia can kill them. In nature, piglets get iron from soil, but in confinement, you must inject iron. Give 200 mg of iron dextran injection to each piglet at 3 days of age. Use a 1 ml syringe with a 20-gauge needle. Inject into the neck muscle, behind the ear, or into the ham. Clean the skin with alcohol first. Do not inject into the same spot twice; rotate sides. If you cannot get iron dextran, some farmers use iron paste or iron-rich soil, but injection is best. In Africa, iron dextran is available from agrovets. Store it in a cool, dark place. Do not use expired product. If piglets are very pale at birth, give iron at 1 day old. Repeat if needed at 2 weeks. Signs of iron deficiency include pale gums, rough hair, and poor weight gain. If you see these, inject iron immediately. Also, ensure piglets have access to clean soil or a mineral block after 1 week. Some farmers give iron via the sow's feed, but that does not pass enough to piglets. So injection is necessary. Keep records of dates and doses. A simple notebook works. Iron injection is quick and cheap. One bottle of iron dextran treats many piglets. It prevents anemia and helps piglets survive to weaning. So do not skip it.

5. Castration and Tail Docking

Castration and tail docking are done to reduce boar taint and tail biting. Castrate male piglets at 3-7 days old. At this age, they are small and heal fast. Use a sharp, clean scalpel or a castration knife. Wash your hands and the piglet's scrotum with soap and water. Hold the piglet upside down by the hind legs. Make a small cut in the scrotum, push out the testicle, and pull it out with a gentle twist. Cut the cord. Repeat on the other side. Apply iodine to the wounds. Do not use a knife that is rusty. Do not castrate sick or weak piglets. If you are not confident, get a vet or experienced farmer to show you. Tail docking: cut the tail 2-3 cm from the base with clean scissors or a hot iron. This prevents tail biting later. Do it at the same time as castration. Use a hot iron to cauterize and reduce bleeding. After docking, apply iodine. Keep piglets in a clean, dry pen. Watch for bleeding or infection. If a piglet bleeds a lot, press a clean cloth on the wound. In Africa, some farmers use rubber rings for castration, but they are not recommended because they cause pain and tetanus risk. Always use sterile tools. Do not castrate after 2 weeks; it is more stressful. If you sell piglets for meat, castration is important because uncastrated males have a strong smell. Tail docking reduces injuries from biting. Both are quick if done correctly. So learn the technique and do it early.

6. Reducing Crushing Deaths

Crushing is the biggest killer of piglets before weaning. The sow accidentally lies on them. To reduce crushing, you need to change the pen and the sow's behavior. First, provide a safe creep area for piglets: a corner with a heat lamp and bedding, blocked off by a low wall so the sow cannot enter. Piglets will learn to sleep there. Second, keep the pen warm for piglets but cool for the sow. If the sow is too hot, she will lie spread out and may crush piglets. Use a heat lamp only over the creep area, not the whole pen. Third, handle the sow gently. A stressed sow is more likely to lie down suddenly. Fourth, install anti-crushing rails: a horizontal bar 20-25 cm from the floor along the walls. When the sow lies down, the rail gives piglets a gap to escape. Fifth, supervise farrowing and the first 3 days. When the sow is about to lie down, watch her. If a piglet is under her, move it. Sixth, keep the floor dry and non-slip. Wet floors cause sows to slip and fall on piglets. Seventh, feed the sow in a trough away from the creep area. Eighth, if a sow is known to crush piglets, consider using a farrowing crate, but crates are expensive and can stress the sow. In Africa, simple pens with a creep area and rails work well. Also, ensure piglets are not too cold; cold piglets huddle near the sow and get crushed. So warmth is key. Check pens every few hours, especially at night. Remove dead piglets immediately. With these steps, you can cut crushing deaths by half.

Farrowing Supervision: The Non-Negotiable Routine

Farrowing supervision is not optional in a batch system. A sow left unattended during farrowing will lose piglets to stillbirth, crushing, and hypothermia. Your standard operating procedure must include:

  • Check every 30 minutes during the farrowing window (typically 3â5 hours from first piglet).
  • Clear membranes from the piglet's nose and mouth immediately after birth.
  • Dry the piglet with clean, dry cloth or paper â this stimulates breathing and prevents chilling.
  • Place under heat source or on the sow's teat within 2 minutes.
  • Record birth time, weight, and any difficulty â this data drives your breeding and nutrition decisions.

For a 20-sow batch, budget one dedicated staff member for 3â4 days of peak farrowing. The labour cost (US$15â25 in most African contexts) is trivial compared to the US$600â1,200 in piglets you will save.

Colostrum: The 6-Hour Window That Determines Everything

Piglets are born with no immunity and almost no energy reserves. Colostrum â the first milk â delivers antibodies (immunoglobulins) and energy. The piglet's gut can only absorb antibodies for the first 6 hours after birth; after 24 hours, the window is effectively closed. Every piglet must receive at least 200g of colostrum within 6 hours.

Practical steps for African smallholder and commercial farms:

  • Split-suckling: If the litter is large (12+), divide into two groups and alternate access to the sow every 45â60 minutes for the first 12 hours.
  • Hand-milking: If a piglet is weak, milk the sow manually and feed via syringe or bottle.
  • Foster within 24 hours: Move surplus piglets to a sow that farrowed within the last 48 hours.
  • Colostrum substitute: In emergencies, use a commercial colostrum replacer or fresh cow colostrum (less ideal but better than nothing).

Iron Injection and Iron Deficiency

Piglets are born with limited iron reserves, and sow milk is iron-poor. Without supplementation, piglets develop anaemia by day 7â10, leading to poor growth, increased disease susceptibility, and higher mortality. Inject 200mg of iron dextran intramuscularly into the neck at day 3â5. In high-risk herds, a second injection at day 14â17 may be warranted.

Business impact: Anaemic piglets weigh 1â2kg less at weaning. Across 200 piglets, that's 200â400kg of lost live weight â worth US$300â600 at market. The cost of iron injections for 200 piglets is approximately US$20â30. The return on investment is 10â20x.

Castration and Tail Docking

Castration is essential for male pigs destined for meat â it prevents boar taint and reduces aggression. Perform at day 3â7 when piglets are small and recovery is fastest. Use a clean, sharp scalpel, apply antiseptic, and ensure the piglet is restrained properly. Tail docking is optional but reduces tail-biting in confined systems; if done, use a sharp side-cutter and leave at least 2cm of tail.

In African markets where intact males may fetch lower prices or be rejected, castration is a direct market-access requirement. Budget US$0.10â0.20 per piglet for consumables.

Reducing Crushing Deaths

Crushing is the single largest cause of pre-weaning mortality. The sow is not being "bad" â she is simply large, clumsy, and unaware of piglets under her. Prevention is structural and behavioural:

  • Farrowing rails: Install horizontal bars 20â25cm from the floor and 20cm from the wall. Cost: US$5â10 per pen using local timber or metal pipe.
  • Creep area: A warm, draught-free corner that attracts piglets away from the sow's body.
  • Supervision during feeding: Sows are most restless at feeding time â ensure piglets are in the creep area.
  • Quiet handling: Minimise noise and sudden movements around farrowing pens.
