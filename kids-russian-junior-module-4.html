1. Module 4 Lesson 1: Translating African Trade Contracts into Chinese, Arabic and Russian

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 1 of Business Chinese, Arabic and Russian Translation. This lesson focuses on the high-stakes work of translating African trade contracts into Chinese, Arabic and Russian. Across Africa, contracts are the backbone of cross-border commerce. A cocoa supply agreement between a Ghanaian exporter and a Chinese importer, a construction subcontract between a Kenyan firm and a Russian engineering company, or a logistics deal between a Moroccan freight forwarder and a Gulf investor all depend on precise translation. Your role is not to produce beautiful prose. Your role is to protect your client from financial loss, legal ambiguity and reputational damage. Start with the structure of a standard African trade contract. Most contain parties, recitals, definitions, scope of work, price and payment terms, delivery and Incoterms, inspection, force majeure, dispute resolution, governing law and signatures. Each section carries risk. In Chinese, pay special attention to "- and -, which mean Party A and Party B. These are not always equivalent to buyer and seller. If you translate "- as buyer when the contract defines "- as the seller, you create a fundamental error. In Arabic, watch for and , meaning first party and second party. In Russian, look for 1 and 2. Always map the defined terms before translating the body. A practical technique is to build a termbase before you translate a single sentence. For an African coffee export contract to China, your termbase might include FOB Mombasa, phytosanitary certificate, certificate of origin, letter of credit, bill of lading, and quality arbitration. Do not invent terms. Use the accepted equivalents found in customs documentation and banking practice. Payment terms are the most dangerous area. In Arabic, you may see ...... 30 ... and 70 ... ... ... . In Russian, you may see 30 and 70 . A single misplaced percentage or a missing word like copy can change the obligation. Always verify numbers in words and figures. In many African jurisdictions, courts require that amounts be written in words as well as figures. Remember, in African trade, trust is built on accuracy. A mistranslated payment term can delay a shipment in Mombasa, freeze funds in Guangzhou, block a letter of credit in Dubai or trigger a penalty in Moscow. Your precision is your client's protection.

2. Module 4 Lesson 2: Business Correspondence and Email Translation for African Markets

3. Module 4 Lesson 3: Translating Business Presentations and Negotiation Documents for African Delegations

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 3. This lesson covers business presentations and negotiation documents for African delegations working with Chinese, Arabic and Russian counterparts. Presentations are used in trade missions, investment summits, mining conferences, agricultural expos, infrastructure roadshows and banking forums. Negotiation documents include term sheets, memoranda of understanding, letters of intent, pricing schedules and concession agreements. Your translation must be clear, persuasive and culturally appropriate. Start with the presentation deck. African delegations often present investment opportunities in sectors such as energy, mining, agriculture, logistics, fintech, tourism and manufacturing. A typical slide contains a title, bullet points, a chart, a map and a call to action. Your job is to translate the text, not redesign the slide. But you must adapt for length. Chinese is usually shorter than English. Arabic is often longer. Russian is variable. If the English bullet is three words, the Chinese may be two characters, the Arabic may be five words and the Russian may be four words. Plan for font size and line breaks. 3. Language-specific pitfalls in African trade translation



Chinese. Chinese business documents often use compact noun phrases and omit subjects. Translators must expand these into full English or French sentences without adding meaning. HS codes must be checked against the Chinese customs tariff, because the same product may fall under different codes depending on processing. Chinese importers frequently require a Chinese-language label with the product name, ingredients, net content, origin, and importer contact. The Chinese name of an African product should be verified with the buyer before printing.



Arabic. Arabic is written right to left, but numbers, Latin brand names and Incoterms are usually written left to right. This creates layout problems in invoices and certificates. Halal wording is commercially critical for meat, poultry, dairy and processed foods. The Arabic term for "slaughter" and "certification body" must match the recognised authority in the destination country. Translators should avoid literal translations of English legal phrases and use established Arabic commercial equivalents.



Russian. Russian trade documentation follows EAEU technical regulations and GOST standards. Product names, additive codes and measurement units must comply with these norms. Russian uses Cyrillic script, so Latin brand names are usually transliterated and the original may appear in brackets. Dates are written day-month-year, which can cause confusion if not converted carefully. Weights in metric units are standard, but the translator should still state the unit explicitly.



4. Safety and compliance warning




RED SAFETY WARNING FOOD, HEALTH AND CHEMICAL CONTENT


Agricultural and food documents often contain health, pesticide, veterinary or chemical information. A translation error in this content can cause illness, death or a public health incident. Never guess.



  • Do not translate maximum residue limits (MRLs), withdrawal periods, dosage instructions or allergen statements without verifying the official source.

  • Pesticide names must be translated using the ISO common name and the approved national registration name, not a brand name alone.

  • Veterinary drug and vaccine instructions must be checked against the manufacturer's approved label and the destination authority's requirements.

  • If a term is unclear, mark it for review by a qualified agronomist, veterinarian or food safety officer. Do not release the document until confirmed.

  • Keep a signed record of who verified the health-related content and on what date.




5. Quick Reference Checklist




  1. Confirm the document set. Check that invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin, phytosanitary certificate and, where required, Halal or health certificate are all present before translating.

  2. Lock the terminology. Use one controlled glossary per target language and apply it consistently across all documents in the shipment.

  3. Verify names and numbers. Check botanical and zoological names, HS codes, weights, volumes, currency amounts and dates against the source document and official registers.

  4. Respect script direction. For Arabic, keep numbers and Latin terms in the correct reading direction; for Chinese and Russian, check font and character rendering in the final PDF.

  5. Flag health and chemical content. Treat MRLs, dosage, allergens and veterinary instructions as high-risk; require a qualified reviewer's sign-off.

  6. Match the destination requirement. Confirm whether the buyer or customs authority requires a certified translation, a notarised copy, or a translator's declaration.

  7. Check Incoterms and payment terms. Ensure FOB, CIF, CFR and letter of credit conditions are translated with their standard equivalents, not paraphrased.

  8. Record the review. Keep the source file, the translation, the glossary version and the reviewer's name and date for audit and dispute purposes.
