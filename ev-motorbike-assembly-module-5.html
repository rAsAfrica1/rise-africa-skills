Lessons

1. Installing Motor Sprocket

Start by gathering your tools: a torque wrench, a set of metric hex keys, a sprocket puller if needed, and a clean rag. The motor sprocket is the small gear that goes on the motor shaft. Before installing, clean the motor shaft with a rag and check for any burrs or rust. If the shaft is dirty, the sprocket won't sit flat, causing vibration and rapid wear.

Apply a thin layer of high-temperature grease to the shaft. This helps prevent corrosion and makes future removal easier. Slide the sprocket onto the shaft. It should fit snugly but not require hammering. If it's too tight, do not force ituse a fine file to gently clean the shaft. If it's too loose, you may need a new sprocket or a shim.

Most EV motorbike motor sprockets are secured with a keyway and a set screw or a retaining ring. Align the keyway on the sprocket with the key on the shaft. Insert the key (a small rectangular metal piece) into the keyway. Then tighten the set screw to the manufacturer's torquetypically 8 to 12 Nm for an M6 screw. Over-tightening can strip the threads; under-tightening will let the sprocket slip.

After tightening, check that the sprocket spins true. Rotate the motor by hand and watch the sprocket's edge. If it wobbles more than 1 mm side-to-side, remove and reinstall. A wobbly sprocket will wear out the chain quickly and can derail. Finally, apply a thread-locking compound like Loctite 243 to the set screw to prevent it from vibrating loose on rough African roads. Let it cure for 24 hours before riding.

2. Mounting Rear Wheel Sprocket

Mounting the rear wheel sprocket is a critical step because it transfers power from the chain to the wheel. You'll need a socket set, a torque wrench, a rubber mallet, and a clean workbench. Start by removing the rear wheel from the motorbike. Place it on a flat surface with the sprocket side facing up.

Clean the mounting surface on the wheel hub. Any dirt or old gasket material will cause the sprocket to sit unevenly. If your sprocket uses bolts, insert them through the sprocket and into the hub. Apply a small amount of medium-strength thread locker to each bolt. Tighten the bolts in a star pattern, like you would a car wheel, to ensure even pressure. Torque them to 20-25 Nm for M8 bolts. Do not guessuse a torque wrench.

For sprockets that mount with a circlip or snap ring, compress the ring with circlip pliers and seat it fully into the groove. You should hear a click. Test by pulling the sprocket outwardit should not move. If it does, the ring is not seated.

After mounting, check that the sprocket is centered. Spin the wheel and watch the sprocket's teeth. If it wobbles more than 1.5 mm, the mounting surface may be dirty or the sprocket may be bent. Remove and inspect. A bent sprocket must be replaced; do not try to straighten it.

Finally, reinstall the wheel on the motorbike, but leave the axle nut slightly loose until you tension the chain. In many African workshops, a common mistake is to overtighten the sprocket bolts, which strips the threads in the aluminum hub. Always use a torque wrench and never an impact gun on small bolts.

3. Chain or Belt Tensioning

Correct chain or belt tension is essential for performance and safety. Too tight, and you'll damage bearings and the motor; too loose, and the chain can jump off or break. You'll need a tape measure, a ruler, and a 10mm or 12mm spanner for the axle nuts.

First, locate the axle adjusters at the rear of the swingarm. Loosen the axle nut slightlyjust enough so the wheel can slide. Most EV motorbikes have a chain with about 20-30 mm of free play when you push it up and down at the midpoint between the sprockets. For belts, the deflection should be 10-15 mm. Use a ruler to measure. Push the chain up with your finger and measure how far it moves. If it moves more than 30 mm, tighten it. If less than 20 mm, loosen it.

To adjust, turn the adjuster bolts on both sides of the swingarm. Turn them clockwise to tighten the chain (pull the wheel back) or counterclockwise to loosen. Turn each side the same number of turns to keep the wheel aligned. After adjusting, recheck the tension. Then tighten the axle nut to 50-60 Nm. Spin the wheel to ensure it rotates freely without binding.

A common mistake in African workshops is to overtighten the chain to prevent it from coming off. This causes the motor to work harder, drains the battery faster, and can snap the chain. Always follow the manufacturer's spec. If you don't have a manual, a good rule is: with the rider seated, the chain should have about 15-20 mm of play. Test ride slowly and recheck after 10 km.

4. Aligning Drivetrain Components

Misalignment is the number one cause of premature chain and sprocket wear. If the front motor sprocket and rear wheel sprocket are not in a straight line, the chain will rub against the sprocket sides, make noise, and wear out in a few hundred kilometers. You need a straight edge, a string, or a laser alignment tool. A simple method is to use a long steel ruler or a piece of string.

First, ensure the rear wheel is properly tensioned. Then, place a straight edge against the side of the rear sprocket. It should also touch the side of the motor sprocket. If there is a gap, the rear wheel is angled. Adjust the axle adjusters: turn the left adjuster out (counterclockwise) and the right adjuster in (clockwise) to pivot the wheel. Make small adjustmentsa quarter turn at a time. Recheck with the straight edge.

Another method is the string method: tie a string to the rear axle, pull it forward past the motor sprocket, and sight down the string. The string should be parallel to the chain. If it crosses the chain, misalignment exists.

After aligning, tighten the axle nut to 50-60 Nm. Spin the wheel and listen for rubbing or clicking. A well-aligned drivetrain runs quietly. Also check that the motor sprocket and rear sprocket are parallel verticallyif one is higher, the chain will run at an angle. Use a spirit level if needed. In many African workshops, alignment is ignored, leading to rapid wear. Take 10 minutes to do it right.

5. Lubricating Chain/Belt

Lubrication reduces friction, prevents rust, and makes your chain or belt last longer. For chains, use a dedicated chain lubenot engine oil or grease, which attract dirt. For belts, use a silicone-based belt dressing or simply keep them clean and dry; most EV belts are maintenance-free but can squeak if dirty.

Start by cleaning the chain with a stiff brush and kerosene or a dedicated chain cleaner. Never use petrol or dieselthey damage the O-rings in modern chains. Scrub both sides of the chain while rotating the wheel backward. Then wipe with a dry rag. For belts, wipe with a damp cloth and mild soap.

Apply lubricant to the inside of the chain, where it meets the sprocket. Spray or drip lube while slowly rotating the wheel. Do not spray on the tire or brake disc. A common mistake is to over-lubricate; excess lube flies off and makes a mess. A thin, even coat is enough. Let it soak for 10 minutes, then wipe off the excess.

Lubricate every 500 km, or more often in dusty conditions. In Africa, dust and sand are everywhere, so check your chain weekly. If you ride on dirt roads, clean and lube after every ride. For belts, check for cracks, missing teeth, or glazing. A glazed belt (shiny) will slip; replace it. Keep a small can of chain lube in your toolkit. It's cheap insurance against breakdowns.

6. Checking Drivetrain Free Play

Free play is the amount of movement in the drivetrain before the motor engages. Too much free play means the chain is too loose and can derail; too little means the chain is too tight and will damage bearings. You'll need a ruler and a helper to sit on the bike.

First, put the motorbike on a stand so the rear wheel is off the ground. Rotate the wheel to find the tightest spot on the chainchains wear unevenly. Measure the total up-and-down movement at the midpoint between sprockets. For most EV motorbikes, the spec is 20-30 mm. If you don't have a manual, aim for 25 mm.

Next, check for tight spots. Rotate the wheel slowly and feel for binding. If the chain tightens and loosens as you rotate, it has a tight spot, often caused by a stiff link. Lubricate and work the link free. If it remains stiff, replace the chain.

Also check the sprocket teeth for wear. Worn teeth look like shark finscurved and pointed. If you see that, replace both sprockets and the chain as a set. Mixing new and worn parts causes rapid wear.

Finally, check the free play with the rider seated. The suspension compresses, making the chain tighter. If it's too tight with the rider on, adjust again. A correctly adjusted drivetrain will have about 15-20 mm of play when loaded. Test ride and recheck after 50 km. In African conditions, check free play every week or after any rough ride.

<h2>Practical Reference: Drivetrain and Transmission Assembly for EV Motorbikes</h2>

<p>In the African e-mobility market, the drivetrain is where torque meets tarmac and where warranty claims are won or lost. A 3 kW hub or mid-drive electric motorbike used for boda-boda passenger work in Kampala, Nairobi, or Kigali will cover 120180 km per day on mixed tarmac and murram. That means the chain or belt, sprockets, and rear wheel interface are under constant load. Unlike a petrol engine, an electric motor delivers full torque instantly at zero rpm. There is no clutch to absorb shock. Every hard acceleration goes straight into the chain pins, rollers, and sprocket teeth. Assembly tolerances that would be "acceptable" on a 125cc petrol bike will destroy an EV drivetrain in weeks.</p>

<p>This lesson is written for assembly technicians, workshop owners, and fleet operators who need to build drivetrains that survive African operating conditions: dust, heat, overloaded panniers, and riders who accelerate hard from standstill. The procedures below follow manufacturer torque specifications and field-proven practices from EV assemblers in Kenya, Rwanda, and Nigeria.</p>

<h2>1. Installing the Motor Sprocket</h2>
<p>The motor sprocket is the smallest and most highly loaded component in the drivetrain. On a mid-drive EV, it turns at 2,5004,500 rpm. On a hub-drive conversion, it may be eliminated entirely but for chain-driven mid-drive systems, correct installation is critical.</p>

<p><strong>Step-by-step:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Clean the motor output shaft with contact cleaner. Any grit will cause the sprocket to seat off-axis.</li>
<li>Inspect the keyway and key. Replace the key if it shows any deformation a sheared key will leave the rider stranded.</li>
<li>Slide the sprocket onto the shaft. It should slide on with hand pressure only. If it requires hammering, the bore or shaft is damaged.</li>
<li>Apply medium-strength threadlocker (Loctite 243 or equivalent) to the retaining bolt.</li>
<li>Torque the retaining bolt to the manufacturer specification. Typical values: 2025 Nm for M8, 3545 Nm for M10. Over-torquing can crack the sprocket hub; under-torquing allows the sprocket to walk and destroy the shaft.</li>
<li>Check radial runout with a dial gauge. Maximum allowable runout: 0.15 mm. Anything more will cause vibration and premature chain wear.</li>
</ol>

<h2>2. Mounting the Rear Wheel Sprocket</h2>
<p>The rear sprocket carries the highest torque load. On a 3 kW EV with a 4:1 reduction, the rear sprocket sees four times the motor torque. In African conditions, where bikes are frequently overloaded with passengers and cargo, this component must be mounted perfectly flat and concentric.</p>

<p>Mount the sprocket to the wheel hub using the specified bolts and washers. Tighten in a star pattern in two stages: first to 50% torque, then to full torque. This prevents warping. Check sprocket flatness with a straight-edge across the face maximum deviation 0.2 mm. A warped sprocket will cause the chain to ride up on the teeth and jump under load.</p>

<p><strong>Business note:</strong> Cheap sprockets made from mild steel will wear out in 3,0005,000 km under African load conditions. Hardened steel (45 HRC or higher) sprockets last 15,00020,000 km. The cost difference is typically 3050%, but the labour savings and customer satisfaction justify the upgrade. Fleet operators should standardise on hardened sprockets across their entire fleet.</p>

<h2>3. Chain or Belt Tensioning</h2>
<p>Tension is the single most important factor in drivetrain life. Too tight, and you destroy motor shaft bearings and rear wheel bearings. Too loose, and the chain can derail or jump teeth under acceleration a dangerous failure mode for a rider in traffic.</p>

<p><strong>Chain tension:</strong> With the bike on level ground and the rider off, measure vertical movement at the midpoint of the bottom chain run. The correct slack for most EV motorbikes is 1020 mm. Adjust using the axle adjusters on both sides equally. After adjustment, re-check alignment (see Section 4).</p>

<p><strong>Belt tension:</strong> Belt drives are increasingly common on premium EV motorbikes because they are cleaner and quieter. Belt tension is measured in Newtons using a sonic tension gauge or a spring scale. Typical specification: 250450 N for a 20 mm belt. Belt tension is far more critical than chain tension an over-tensioned belt can fail catastrophically.</p>

<p style="margin:0; font-weight:bold; color:#c0392b; font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING CRUSH AND AMPUTATION HAZARD</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#1a1a1a;">Never adjust chain or belt tension while the motor is energised or the battery is connected. An EV motor can produce full torque instantly if the throttle is accidentally actuated. Always disconnect the battery isolator, remove the key, and wait 60 seconds for capacitor discharge before working on the drivetrain. Failure to follow this procedure has caused serious hand injuries in workshops in Nairobi and Lagos. Use a chain tension gauge, not your fingers, to check slack while rotating the wheel.</p>
</div>

<h2>4. Aligning Drivetrain Components</h2>
<p>Misalignment is the silent killer of drivetrains. A chain that is misaligned by just 1 mm over a 500 mm span will wear out three times faster than a perfectly aligned chain. In African workshops, the most common cause of premature chain failure is incorrect rear wheel alignment after a puncture repair.</p>

<p><strong>Alignment procedure:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Place the bike on a centre stand so the rear wheel spins freely.</li>
<li>Use a long straight-edge (at least 600 mm) placed against the rear sprocket face. It should touch the motor sprocket face evenly. If there is a gap at either end, the sprockets are not coplanar.</li>
<li>Alternatively, use a laser alignment tool clamped to the rear sprocket. This is more accurate and faster for production assembly.</li>
<li>Adjust the axle adjusters in equal increments. Most bikes have alignment marks on the swingarm but these are often inaccurate from the factory. Trust the straight-edge or laser, not the marks.</li>
<li>After alignment, torque the axle nut to specification (typically 6080 Nm for M14, 80100 Nm for M16).</li>
<li>Spin the wheel and check for tight spots. A correctly aligned and tensioned chain will have uniform slack throughout the rotation.</li>
</ol>

<h2>5. Lubricating Chain/Belt</h2>
<p>In dusty African conditions, lubrication is a balance: enough to protect the chain, but not so much that it attracts grit. The recommended practice is to clean and lubricate every 300500 km, or more frequently in the rainy season.</p>

<p><strong>Chain:</strong> Use a dedicated motorcycle chain lubricant, not engine oil or grease. Apply to the inside of the chain while rotating the wheel slowly. Wipe off excess with a clean rag. For O-ring chains, use an O-ring-safe cleaner and lubricant. Never use a wire brush it damages the O-rings.</p>

<p><strong>Belt:</strong> Belt drives do not require lubrication. In fact, applying lubricant to a belt will attract grit and cause rapid wear. Clean the belt with a soft brush and mild soapy water only. Inspect for cracks, missing teeth, and embedded stones.</p>

<h2>6. Checking Drivetrain Free Play</h2>
<p>Free play checks should be part of every service. With the rear wheel off the ground, rotate it by hand. It should spin freely with no binding, no grinding, and no lateral movement. Check:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rear wheel bearings:</strong> Grab the wheel at 12 and 6 o'clock and rock it. Any movement indicates worn bearings.</li>
<li><strong>Motor shaft bearings:</strong> With the chain removed, spin the motor sprocket. It should rotate smoothly with no roughness.</li>
<li><strong>Chain slack:</strong> Re-check after the wheel has been rotated several times.</li>
<li><strong>Axle adjuster lock nuts:</strong> Ensure they are tight a loose lock nut will allow the axle to move under load.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li> Battery isolator disconnected and key removed before any drivetrain work</li>
<li> Motor sprocket retaining bolt torqued to spec (2025 Nm M8 / 3545 Nm M10) with threadlocker</li>
<li> Rear sprocket bolts tightened in star pattern to 2530 Nm; flatness within 0.2 mm</li>
<li> Chain slack 1020 mm at midpoint of bottom run; belt tension 250450 N</li>
<li> Sprocket alignment verified with straight-edge or laser not swingarm marks</li>
<li> Axle nut torqued to 60100 Nm (per axle size); adjuster lock nuts tight</li>
<li> Chain lubricated (or belt cleaned); no excess lubricant on tyre or brake disc</li>
<li> Rear wheel spins freely with no binding, grinding, or lateral play</li>
</ol>
