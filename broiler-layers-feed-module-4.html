Module 4

🐔 Breeding Flock Nutrition

Feeding the birds that produce your chicks. Breeders are the only poultry class where over-feeding is as damaging as under-feeding, where the male and the female need different diets, and where the consequences of a nutrient shortfall show up in someone else's brooder house a week later. Covers male and female feeding, the fertility nutrients, what passes from hen to chick, body condition control, and breeder-specific health.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Explain why breeder males require separate feeding from females
  • Explain why breeder hens are fed to a target weight rather than to appetite
  • Name the vitamins and trace minerals with the strongest links to fertility and hatchability
  • Describe how the breeder hen's diet is transferred into the egg and the chick
  • Assess breeder body condition by weight and by hand without laboratory tools
  • Identify the breeder-specific health risks that show up as fertility or hatchability loss

Lesson 4.1~12 min

Breeder Male Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Explain why breeder males require separate feeding from females
  • Identify the nutrients that most affect semen quality and mating ability
  • Calculate the cost and value of protecting fertility through male management

In most small breeding flocks the male is an afterthought. He eats whatever the hens eat, nobody weighs him, and when fertility falls the hens get blamed. Yet one male serves roughly eight to twelve hens, so his condition is multiplied across every egg those hens lay. He is the highest-leverage bird on the farm.

The core problem is that males and females need different diets and, left together, will eat the same one. A breeder female is fed a controlled amount of a moderate-protein, high-calcium layer-type ration. A male fed that same ration gets far more calcium than he needs, and excess calcium over long periods is hard on the kidneys and does nothing for him. He also tends to over-eat if feed is freely available, gaining fat that reduces mating activity and physical mating ability. Overweight males are one of the most common causes of low fertility in small flocks, and the bird looks healthy right up until the hatch report arrives.

The practical solution used across the industry is separate-sex feeding. Female feeders are fitted with a grille or roll bar spaced so hens can put their heads through but the larger male head and comb cannot. Male feeders are hung higher than the hens can reach. On a smallholding this can be built from local materials for very little - a raised trough on brackets, and a bar across the female trough. The point is simply that each sex eats its own ration in its own amount.

What the male actually needs is a lower-calcium, moderate-protein ration with a strong vitamin and trace mineral supply. Calcium at the layer level of 3.8-4.2% is far above his requirement; a level nearer the 0.65-0.90% range used for growing and non-laying birds is appropriate, with available phosphorus in proportion at roughly a 2:1 calcium to phosphorus ratio, the optimum for non-laying poultry. Protein does not need to be high, and excess protein simply adds cost and house ammonia.

The nutrients that matter most to him are antioxidants and trace minerals. Vitamin E and selenium work together in the glutathione peroxidase system, and sperm cell membranes are rich in the unsaturated fats that oxidative damage attacks. The published pairing guidance is 10-20 IU of vitamin E per 0.3 mg of selenium, and combined supplementation outperforms either alone for antioxidant defence, immune response and tissue integrity - particularly under heat stress, which is exactly the condition in which African breeder males lose fertility. Zinc and manganese support reproductive tissue and skeletal soundness; Ross specifications for broilers give zinc 120 mg/kg and manganese 120 mg/kg, well above the NRC 1994 minimums of 35-45 and 20-60 mg/kg respectively. Lesson 3 covers the fertility nutrients in detail.

One ingredient must be watched carefully in male diets. Gossypol in cottonseed meal is specifically reproductively toxic, affecting the reproductive organs of both sexes, alongside liver enlargement and immune suppression. Keep finished diets below 0.1% gossypol, which generally means limiting cottonseed meal to 5-10% depending on the meal. If you are formulating for breeders and cottonseed meal is cheap locally, this is the ceiling to respect.

Leg and foot soundness is a nutritional issue with a direct fertility consequence. A male who cannot stand and balance cannot mate, however good his semen. Calcium to phosphorus ratios outside the 1.5-2.5:1 window increase skeletal problems, and vitamin D3 at breeder-appropriate levels supports bone mineralisation. Walk your males weekly and watch how they move.

Cost the leverage. Suppose you keep 100 breeder hens and 10 males, setting 1,800 eggs a month. If fertility is 82% and hatchability of fertile eggs is 88%, you hatch about 1,299 chicks. Lift fertility to 90% and you hatch 1,426 - 127 more chicks. At $0.80 each that is $102 a month. Building separate male feeders might cost $40 once, and a better vitamin premix perhaps $6 a month. The male side of the flock is where the cheapest fertility gains live.

A breeder unit in Bulawayo, Zimbabwe running 500 hens with 55 males had fertility drifting below 80%. Weighing the males showed them 18% heavier than the hens' proportional target and visibly fat. Installing raised male feeders and controlling male feed to a fixed daily allocation brought weights down over six weeks and fertility back above 88%.

Breeder Female Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Explain why breeder hens are fed to a target weight rather than to appetite
  • Set nutrient levels for a breeder hen in lay using published layer and breeder standards
  • Calculate a controlled daily feed allocation and its cost

A breeder hen is the one bird in poultry that you deliberately do not let eat as much as she wants. That single fact separates breeder management from everything else in this course. A broiler breeder carries the appetite of a meat bird and the job of a laying hen, and if you feed her to appetite she becomes overweight, ovulates erratically, produces double-yolked and misshapen eggs that cannot be set, and her fertility and hatchability fall. Controlled feeding to a body weight target is the whole discipline.

The principle is straightforward even where the exact breeder curve for your strain is not to hand. Weigh a sample of 30 hens weekly, compare the average against the target curve supplied with your stock, and adjust the daily allocation up or down in small steps. Never make large jumps. A hen that is over target is not slimmed by starving her; you simply hold the allocation flat and let her grow into it. A hen under target gets a small increase. The layer body weight shapes published by Lohmann give a useful sense of scale: 1.42 kg at 17 weeks at the end of rearing, stabilising at 1.80-2.00 kg through peak production, reaching about 2.06 kg at the end of a long cycle. Broiler breeders sit heavier than this, but the same principle of a controlled, steadily rising curve applies.

Uniformity is even more important in breeders than in commercial layers, because an uneven flock cannot be fed correctly by any single allocation. If you give the flock average allocation to a flock with 60% uniformity, the small birds stay small and the large birds get larger. Weigh 30 birds, calculate the percentage within plus or minus 10% of the average, and if you are below about 80% consider grading the flock into two pens fed separately.

Nutrient levels in lay follow layer logic with breeder emphasis. Calcium must support daily shell formation - Hy-Line gives about 4.0 g per bird per day at peak with a dietary concentration of 3.8-4.1%, rising to 4.7 g per day in decline at 3.92-4.70%, with a fine to coarse limestone split of 40:60 at peak moving to 25:75 later. A hatching egg with a weak shell is lost twice: it cracks in handling and it loses moisture badly in the setter. Available phosphorus in Lohmann layer phase 1 is 0.34-0.42%. Protein and amino acids follow the layer pattern, with NRC 1994 giving 690 mg lysine, 300 mg methionine and 580 mg methionine plus cysteine per day at 100 g intake, and Lohmann layer phase 1 digestible targets of 0.78% lysine, 0.39% methionine, 0.70% methionine plus cystine, 0.81% arginine and 0.68% valine at 110 g intake.

Because the hen is fed a restricted amount, every gram must count. This is the class of bird where a weak premix does the most damage, since she has no spare intake to compensate. Vitamins and trace minerals are covered in Lesson 3; the point here is that restriction and marginal supplementation are a bad combination.

Work the allocation. Suppose your target is 155 g per hen per day for 120 hens. Daily feed is 120 x 0.155 = 18.6 kg. At $0.52/kg that is $9.67 a day, or $290 a month. Now suppose you feed to appetite instead and the hens take 185 g. Daily feed becomes 22.2 kg, costing $11.54 - an extra $56 a month - and you have also bought yourself lower fertility, more unsettable eggs and a heavier flock. Over-feeding a breeder costs money twice.

Feed distribution technique matters as much as the number. A restricted allocation must reach every bird within a few minutes or the dominant hens take a double share. Use enough trough length that all birds can eat at once, distribute quickly, and feed at the same time each day. In hot climates feed early in the morning so the birds have consumed their allocation before the afternoon heat suppresses appetite.

A breeder farm outside Kumasi, Ghana running 400 hens fed ad libitum through a hopper system saw hatchability of set eggs at 71% and a high rate of double-yolked and oversized eggs. Moving to a weighed daily allocation delivered along a long trough at 6am, with weekly weighing of 30 hens, brought hatchability to 82% within two months at no extra feed cost - in fact the feed bill fell.

Vitamin and Mineral for Fertility

In this lesson
  • Name the vitamins and trace minerals with the strongest links to fertility and hatchability
  • Explain why breeder premix specification must exceed commercial layer levels
  • Cost a breeder premix upgrade against the hatchability it protects

Vitamins and trace minerals are where breeder nutrition differs most sharply from commercial layer nutrition. A commercial layer only has to produce an egg someone will eat. A breeder has to produce an egg that will build an entire chick from its own contents, with no further input, over 21 days in a sealed shell. Everything that chick needs to develop must already be inside the egg, and it got there from the hen's diet.

Start with the antioxidants, because reproduction is unusually vulnerable to oxidative damage. Vitamin E and selenium act together in the glutathione peroxidase enzyme system, and the published guidance is to pair 10-20 IU of vitamin E with each 0.3 mg of selenium. Combined supplementation is more effective than either alone for antioxidant defence during heat stress, immune response, gut integrity and intestinal microbiota stability. Ross 2022 broiler specifications set vitamin E at 80 IU/kg in starter falling to 55 in finisher, with selenium at 0.30 mg/kg throughout, against NRC 1994 ranges of just 5-25 IU/kg and 0.06-0.2 mg/kg. Breeder diets are conventionally specified at the higher end because the hen must supply the embryo as well as herself.

Vitamin A maintains epithelial tissue throughout the reproductive tract as well as gut and airway, and supports immune function. Ross 2022 gives 13,000 IU/kg in starter, 11,000 grower and 10,000 finisher, against an NRC 1994 range of 1,500-5,000 IU/kg. The gap is not an error: NRC describes the level below which deficiency appears while breeder specifications describe the level at which a modern bird performs.

Vitamin D3 governs calcium absorption and bone mineralisation, and in a breeder it does double duty - shell quality for the egg you set, and skeletal development in the embryo. Ross gives 5,000 IU/kg starter down to 4,000 finisher, against NRC's 200-1,100 IU/kg. The metabolite 25-OH D3 is absorbed more efficiently because it bypasses hepatic 25-hydroxylation, raising circulating levels faster, and is roughly four to five times more biologically active per unit weight, recommended at 2,760-5,529 IU/kg where it partly or fully replaces D3. Published work on maternal 25-OH D3 links it to improved bone strength, growth, immune status and muscle development in progeny.

Vitamin K at the NRC 1994 range of 0.5-1.75 mg/kg supports blood coagulation, bone mineralisation and epithelial integrity - all of which matter in a developing embryo. The B group act as cofactors in the rapid cell division of embryonic growth: NRC 1994 gives riboflavin 1.8-4.0 mg/kg, niacin 10-70, pantothenic acid 2-16, biotin 0.04-0.15, pyridoxine 2.0-3.0, cobalamin 0.002-0.004 and folate 0.5-1.0 mg/kg. Biotin and riboflavin in particular are classically associated with embryonic development, and folate and B12 with nucleotide synthesis and methylation - the machinery of building new cells.

Among trace minerals, manganese and zinc participate in skeletal and connective tissue formation and in shell matrix quality. Ross 2022 gives zinc 120 mg/kg, manganese 120, copper 16 and selenium 0.30, against NRC 1994 minimums of zinc 35-45, manganese 20-60, copper 4-8 and selenium 0.06-0.2. Watch the interactions: zinc above 150 mg/kg antagonises copper, iron and manganese absorption, and monensin may suppress copper absorption, so more is not automatically better.

Storage is the silent failure. Vitamins A, D3 and E degrade with heat, light, moisture and contact with trace mineral salts. A breeder premix sitting in an open sack under a tin roof at 35C is losing exactly the nutrients that matter most here. Buy quantities you will use within about a month, store sealed and shaded, and note that Module 10 covers feed storage and quality control in depth.

Cost the decision. Take 300 breeder hens setting 5,400 eggs a month. Upgrading from a generic layer premix to a properly specified breeder premix might add $0.008 per kilogram of feed. At 155 g a day, monthly feed is 300 x 0.155 x 30 = 1,395 kg, so the upgrade costs 1,395 x $0.008 = $11.16 a month. If it lifts hatchability of set eggs from 78% to 83%, that is 5,400 x 0.05 = 270 extra chicks. At $0.80 each that is $216 a month against $11 of premix.

A hatchery-supplying farm in Arusha, Tanzania could not get a straight answer on the vitamin A, D3, E and selenium levels in its premix, and hatchability sat stubbornly in the mid-seventies. Changing to a supplier who printed the figures on the label, and storing the premix in a sealed lidded drum in the coolest corner of the store, lifted hatchability by several points across two months with no other change.

Nutritional Impacts on Chick Quality

In this lesson
  • Describe how the breeder hen's diet is transferred into the egg and the chick
  • Score day-old chick quality using simple on-farm observations
  • Calculate the value of a chick quality improvement to your buyers and yourself

The chick that comes out of your incubator is made entirely from what was inside the egg, and what was inside the egg came from the hen. There is no other source. This is why breeder nutrition is judged not by the breeder flock's own performance but by the performance of birds in someone else's brooder house a week later. If your chicks die in week one, your reputation dies with them.

Three things pass from hen to chick. The first is nutrient deposition. Fat-soluble vitamins A, D3, E and K, the B vitamins, and trace minerals including selenium, zinc and manganese are deposited into the yolk in proportion to the hen's diet. A hen on a marginal premix produces an egg with marginal reserves, and the chick spends its first days short of exactly the antioxidant and immune nutrients it needs while its own gut is still developing. Published work on maternal 25-OH vitamin D3 links maternal supply to improved bone strength, growth performance, immune status and muscle development in the progeny - a direct demonstration that a chick inherits its mother's vitamin status.

The second is maternal antibody. The hen transfers immunity into the yolk, and this is what protects the chick through its first two to three weeks before its own immune system takes over. Anything that suppresses the hen's immune function reduces what she can pass on.

The third is toxins, and this is where breeders are handled more strictly than any other class. Ochratoxin A carries a regulatory limit of 0.01 ppm for breeder flocks against 0.10 ppm for broilers and layers - ten times stricter - because of reduced hatchability and teratogenic effects on the progeny. Aflatoxin is limited to 0.02 ppm for layers and breeders and causes immunosuppression, reduced growth and liver enlargement. Fumonisin for layers and breeders is 30 ppm, though at 18.6 ppm it has been shown to alter intestinal microflora and predispose birds to necrotic enteritis. Chronic low-level exposure to several mycotoxins at once is common in commercial operations and effects can be additive or synergistic, so testing for a single toxin is not enough. Module 10 covers grain sampling, testing and storage; the breeder-specific point is that the tolerance is far tighter.

Scoring chick quality takes ten minutes and needs no equipment. Take 50 day-old chicks and check each for a clean, fully healed navel with no string or scab; a dry, fluffy coat; bright alert eyes; legs and hocks that are clean and allow the chick to stand and walk without splaying; and a soft, properly absorbed abdomen rather than a hard distended one, which indicates an unabsorbed yolk sac. Count how many pass all five and express it as a percentage. Track that number batch to batch alongside your breeder feed changes and you have a working feedback loop.

Shell quality feeds directly into chick quality, and it is a nutritional output. A hen puts 2.2-2.5 g of calcium into each shell, mostly overnight, drawing on gizzard calcium and medullary bone. A thin shell loses moisture too fast in the setter and cracks in handling. The particle size rules from layer nutrition apply directly: 40% fine to 60% coarse limestone at peak, moving to 25% fine to 75% coarse in later production, with coarse particles of 2-4 mm retained in the gizzard for slow release.

Cost it. Take a breeder unit setting 5,000 eggs a month at 80% hatchability, producing 4,000 chicks. If 12% are second-quality and sell at $0.45 instead of $0.80, that is 480 chicks losing $0.35 each, or $168 a month. Cutting seconds to 5% recovers 280 chicks x $0.35 = $98 a month. Add the value of a hatchability improvement and the numbers grow quickly: a five-point rise on 5,000 eggs is 250 chicks, worth $200.

A hatchery in Ibadan, Nigeria supplying small producers began scoring 50 chicks from every hatch for navel healing and yolk absorption, and writing the score on the same board as the breeder feed batch number. Within three hatches they linked a run of poor navels and soft chicks to a particular maize delivery, tested it, and changed supplier. The record cost nothing and protected a customer base worth far more than the feed.

Managing Body Condition in Breeders

In this lesson
  • Assess breeder body condition by weight and by hand without laboratory tools
  • Adjust feed allocation in controlled steps against a target weight curve
  • Calculate the production and cost consequences of a flock drifting off target

Body condition is the control panel of a breeding flock. Almost every fertility and hatchability problem that is not a disease or a genetics problem traces back to birds that are too heavy, too light, or too uneven. Unlike feed formulation, condition management costs nothing but discipline.

There are two tools and you need both. The first is the scale. Weigh 30 hens and 15 males weekly, at the same time of day, ideally before the morning feed, using the same scale. Catch birds at random where you are standing rather than chasing the easy ones, because slow and weak birds are always easiest to catch and will drag your average down and mislead you. Calculate the average, and calculate uniformity as the percentage falling within plus or minus 10% of it. Plot the average against your strain's target curve.

The second tool is your hands. Run a finger along the keel bone. In a bird at correct condition the breast muscle rises smoothly on either side and the keel is felt as a firm ridge, not a sharp blade. A sharp, prominent keel with hollow breast means under-condition. A keel you cannot easily find, with a rounded breast and a soft fat pad in the abdomen just in front of the vent, means over-condition. Handle ten birds a week and your hands will tell you about condition changes before the scale confirms them.

Adjust in small steps. If the flock average is below target, add a few grams per bird per day and re-weigh in a week. If it is above target, hold the allocation flat and let the target curve catch up rather than cutting feed sharply. Sudden restriction on a laying breeder causes a production drop and can trigger a partial molt. The general shape published for layer growth gives a sense of the gradients involved: 0.20-0.25 kg per week of gain in the 4-8 week grower phase, 0.18-0.22 kg per week through the 9-17 week developer phase, 1.42 kg at 17 weeks, then stabilisation at 1.80-2.00 kg in peak production. Breeders are heavier but the principle of a steady controlled climb, never a jump, is the same.

Uniformity deserves its own attention. A flock at 80% or better can be managed on one allocation. Below about 70%, one allocation cannot serve both ends of the distribution and the spread widens every week. The practical answer is grading: weigh the flock, separate the lightest fifth into their own pen, and feed that pen a slightly higher allocation until they close the gap. This is a day of work that can be worth several points of hatchability.

Heat complicates everything. Above about 30C, appetite falls and birds may not consume their allocation, so a flock can drift below target without any change in what you offered. Feed early in the morning, check the trough two hours later to see whether the allocation has actually been eaten, and if it has not, note it - the record that matters is feed consumed, not feed offered. The dietary electrolyte balance bands from Module 1 Lesson 4 apply here as everywhere: 250-300 mEq/kg at 28-32C, 300-350 at 32-35C, and 350-400 above 35C.

Cost the drift. Take 200 breeder hens setting 3,600 eggs a month, hatching at 80% for 2,880 chicks at $0.80, giving $2,304. Suppose over-condition pulls hatchability to 72%: you get 2,592 chicks, worth $2,074 - a loss of $230 a month, and the over-fed flock also cost you more feed. If the hens ate 175 g instead of a target 155 g, that is 200 x 0.020 x 30 = 120 kg extra a month, another $62 at $0.52/kg. Nearly $300 a month lost, from a problem that a weekly scale and a disciplined allocation would have caught.

A breeder producer in Nakuru, Kenya with 350 hens weighed his flock only at housing and again at 45 weeks. By then the hens were well above target and hatchability had fallen from 84% to 74%. Introducing a Sunday weighing of 30 hens, a written allocation recorded on the wall, and a check two hours after feeding to confirm the allocation had been consumed, brought the flock back onto its curve over about ten weeks and hatchability to 81%.

Lesson 4.6~12 min

Flock Health and Disease Prevention

In this lesson
  • Identify the breeder-specific health risks that show up as fertility or hatchability loss
  • Apply the tighter mycotoxin ceilings that govern breeder feed
  • Build a simple breeder health record that links flock events to hatch results

Breeder health has a different definition of failure from every other class of poultry. A commercial broiler flock that is quietly unwell shows it in weight and conversion. A breeder flock that is quietly unwell may look and weigh perfectly normal while fertility, hatchability and chick quality fall away. The damage is exported to your customers before you see it. General disease prevention through nutrition - gut barrier, immune nutrients, coccidiosis and litter management - is covered in full in Module 9, and applies to breeders exactly as it does to any other flock. This lesson stays on what is specific to breeders.

The first breeder-specific issue is mycotoxin tolerance, which is far tighter than for any other adult bird. Ochratoxin A is limited to 0.01 ppm for breeder flocks, against 0.10 ppm for broilers, layers and turkeys - a tenfold difference driven by reduced hatchability and teratogenic effects on the progeny. Aflatoxin is limited to 0.02 ppm for layers and breeders, and causes immunosuppression, reduced antibody response, liver enlargement and greater disease susceptibility even below the threshold. T-2 and HT-2 trichothecenes are limited to 0.25 ppm in all poultry and cause oral lesions and lymphoid tissue atrophy. Fumonisin for layers and breeders is 30 ppm, but published work found that at 18.6 ppm - below the nominal safe level for broilers - intestinal microflora were altered and birds were predisposed to necrotic enteritis. Because chronic low-level multi-toxin exposure is the commercial norm and interactions can be additive or synergistic, a single-toxin test is not enough. In practice this means breeders get your cleanest grain, sourced and stored to the standards set out in Module 10, never the tail of the bin.

The second issue is that immunosuppression in a breeder is doubly costly. The hen transfers antibody into the yolk, and that maternal immunity is what protects the chick through its first two or three weeks. Anything that suppresses her immune system - mycotoxins, oxidative stress, a spent premix - reduces what she can pass on, so a chick from a compromised breeder flock is more fragile in its buyer's brooder even if it looked normal at hatch. The antioxidant nutrients from Lesson 3 are therefore health nutrients as well as fertility nutrients: vitamin E and selenium in the glutathione peroxidase system at 10-20 IU per 0.3 mg, vitamin A for epithelial integrity, and zinc at levels well above NRC minimums.

The third is heat. A breeder house above 32C loses fertility before it loses anything else, because males are affected quickly and semen quality is sensitive to heat and oxidative stress. Raise dietary electrolyte balance using the published bands - 250-300 mEq/kg at 28-32C, 300-350 at 32-35C, 350-400 above 35C - by increasing sodium, ideally as sodium bicarbonate, and reducing chloride. Excess chloride pushes blood towards acidosis so panting becomes less effective; raising DEB restores alkalinity and heat dissipation. Feed at dawn and dusk, keep males cool, and expect a fertility dip about a week after a heat event rather than during it.

The fourth is hygiene of the hatching egg itself, which is a breeder-only concern. Collect eggs frequently, at least three or four times a day, so they spend less time on the floor or in a soiled nest. Do not wash dirty eggs into the setter; a wet shell drives contamination through the pores. Store eggs cool and pointed-end down, and set them within about a week. Shell quality is your ally here, and it is nutritional - about 2.2-2.5 g of calcium per shell, delivered with a 40:60 fine to coarse limestone split at peak and 25:75 later.

Record-keeping is what ties this together. Keep one sheet per batch of eggs set, with the date, the breeder feed batch number, the flock's average weight that week, house temperature, any medication given, day-7 candling fertility, hatchability of set eggs, and the chick quality score from Lesson 4. Within three or four hatches you will start seeing which farm events precede which hatch results, and that is a diagnostic capability no laboratory can sell you.

A breeder farm in Lusaka, Zambia kept exactly this sheet and noticed hatchability dropping about ten days after every maize delivery from one particular trader during the rainy season. A mycotoxin panel confirmed the suspicion. They changed supplier and added a routine of rejecting any load with visible mould or a musty smell at the gate - a decision made entirely from their own written records.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Why should breeder males not eat the female breeder ration?

Layer-type breeder feed at 3.8-4.2% calcium is well above male requirement, and free access encourages fat gain that reduces mating ability.

2. Separate-sex feeding is achieved by:

A grille sized to let hen heads through but not the larger male head, plus male feeders raised above hen reach, keeps each sex on its own ration.

3. Which ingredient is specifically reproductively toxic and must be capped in breeder diets?

Gossypol affects the reproductive organs of both sexes and also causes liver enlargement and immune suppression. Keep finished diets below 0.1% gossypol.

4. Lifting fertility from 82% to 90% on 1,800 eggs a month at 88% hatch of fertile adds about:

1,800 x 0.82 x 0.88 = 1,299 chicks; 1,800 x 0.90 x 0.88 = 1,426. The difference is 127 chicks, worth about $102 at $0.80 each.

5. A male with poor leg soundness matters because:

Mating is a physical act. Skeletal problems, often driven by calcium to phosphorus ratios outside 1.5-2.5:1, take a male out of production without any visible illness.

6. Breeder hens are feed-restricted mainly because:

A broiler breeder has a meat bird's appetite and a layer's job. Fed to appetite she becomes overweight, with double-yolked and misshapen eggs and falling fertility and hatchability.

7. A breeder hen found above her target weight should be:

Large corrections are damaging. Holding the allocation flat while the target curve rises brings the bird back without the stress of restriction.

8. Flock uniformity below 80% is a problem in breeders because:

Controlled feeding depends on a single allocation being right for most birds. In an uneven flock the small birds stay small and the large ones grow larger, so grading into pens is often needed.

9. 120 hens on a 155 g allocation consume how much feed per day?

120 x 0.155 kg = 18.6 kg a day, costing $9.67 at $0.52/kg, or about $290 a month.

10. Why must a restricted allocation be distributed quickly along adequate trough length?

With a limited quantity there is no second chance. If all birds cannot eat at once, dominant hens over-eat and the rest fall below target, destroying uniformity.

11. Why must a breeder premix be specified more generously than a commercial layer premix?

A table egg only has to be edible. A hatching egg must build a complete chick from its own contents, and everything in it came from the hen's diet.

12. Vitamin E and selenium are paired at approximately:

Both work in the glutathione peroxidase antioxidant system, and the published optimal ratio is 10-20 IU of vitamin E per 0.3 mg of selenium.

13. Zinc above 150 mg/kg in the diet:

High dietary zinc competes with copper, iron and manganese for absorption, so raising one trace mineral without regard to the others can create a new deficiency.

14. 25-OH vitamin D3 is absorbed more efficiently than D3 because it:

Skipping the liver hydroxylation step raises circulating 25-OH D3 faster, an advantage that is largest in young birds and in maternal supply to the embryo.

15. What most commonly destroys the vitamins in a stored breeder premix?

Fat-soluble vitamins degrade under heat, sunlight and moisture and in contact with mineral salts, so buy monthly quantities and store sealed, shaded and off the floor.

16. Everything a developing chick needs comes from:

The egg is a sealed system for 21 days. Nutrient reserves and maternal antibody were deposited by the hen, which is why breeder diet quality shows up in chick quality.

17. The ochratoxin A limit for breeder flocks is:

Breeders are held at 0.01 ppm, ten times stricter than the 0.10 ppm for broilers and layers, because ochratoxin reduces hatchability and is teratogenic to progeny.

18. A hard, distended abdomen in a day-old chick indicates:

Proper yolk sac absorption leaves a soft abdomen. A hard distended belly is a standard marker of poor chick quality and predicts first-week losses.

19. Maternal 25-OH vitamin D3 supply has been linked in published work to:

The published maternal 25-OH D3 work reports progeny benefits in bone strength, growth performance, immune status and muscle development, showing direct inheritance of maternal vitamin status.

20. Testing feed for a single mycotoxin is insufficient because:

Multi-mycotoxin exposure is the norm in commercial operations, with additive, synergistic or antagonistic interactions, so a comprehensive panel is recommended.

21. A keel bone you cannot easily find, with a soft pad in front of the vent, indicates:

A buried keel with a rounded breast and an abdominal fat pad is the classic hand-assessment sign of an over-conditioned breeder.

22. A breeder flock found above its target weight should be corrected by:

Sharp restriction on a laying breeder causes a production drop and can trigger a partial molt. A flat allocation against a rising target curve corrects condition safely.

23. In hot conditions a flock can drift below target because:

Above about 30C birds may leave feed. Checking the trough two hours after feeding tells you what was consumed, which is the number that matters.

24. Uniformity below about 70% calls for:

One allocation cannot suit both ends of a wide spread. Separating the lightest fifth into their own pen on a higher allocation closes the gap.

25. Hatchability falling from 80% to 72% on 3,600 eggs a month costs about:

2,880 chicks fall to 2,592 - a loss of 288 chicks at $0.80, or $230, before counting the extra feed an over-conditioned flock consumed.

26. The ochratoxin A limit for breeder flocks compared with broilers is:

Breeders are limited to 0.01 ppm against 0.10 ppm for broilers and layers, because ochratoxin reduces hatchability and has teratogenic effects on progeny.

27. Why is immunosuppression in a breeder hen doubly costly?

Maternal antibody in the yolk protects the chick for its first two to three weeks. A compromised hen exports that weakness to her customers' chicks.

28. A breeder house running above 35C should target a dietary electrolyte balance of:

Severe heat stress above 35C calls for 350-400 mEq/kg, achieved by maximising sodium and potassium and minimising chloride so panting stays effective.

29. Dirty hatching eggs should be:

Washing wets the shell and can drive bacteria through the pores. Frequent collection and clean nests prevent the problem instead.

30. The most useful breeder health record links hatch results to:

One sheet per batch set, carrying feed batch number, weekly average weight, peak house temperature, medications, day-7 fertility, hatchability and chick quality score, reveals cause and effect within a few hatches.

Module 4 capstone

Run a four-week breeder condition and hatch audit on your own flock. Week 1: weigh 30 hens and 15 males separately, calculate each average and the uniformity percentage within plus or minus 10%, and record your current male to female ratio by counting both. Week 2: set a batch of at least 100 eggs and record fertility by candling at day 7 and hatchability of set eggs at hatch, then score 50 day-old chicks for navel healing, leg strength and yolk sac absorption, recording the percentage of first-quality chicks. Week 3: analyse your breeder feed against the figures in this module - vitamin E, selenium, manganese, zinc, vitamin D3 - and against the ochratoxin breeder limit of 0.01 ppm and aflatoxin limit of 0.02 ppm, and check whether your males are eating female feed. Week 4: cost one hatching egg and one saleable day-old chick at local prices, calculate what a five percentage point hatchability improvement would be worth per month, and set that against the cost of the corrections you have identified. Write a one-page plan with the three actions ranked by return.
