Lessons

1. Manual Peeling vs. Mechanical Peeling

Peeling removes the outer brown skin and the inner pinkish layer (cortex) of cassava roots. Manual peeling uses a sharp knife or machete. Hold the root firmly, cut off the ends, then slice lengthwise through the skin and peel it back. For 100 kg of roots, one person can peel about 20-30 kg per hour. Mechanical peelers are machines with abrasive drums or blades that rub off the skin. They can process 500-1000 kg per hour but cost $500-$3000. In Africa, manual peeling is common for small-scale operations because it is cheap and creates jobs. However, mechanical peeling is faster and reduces labour costs for large processors. When choosing, consider your daily root volume. If you process less than 500 kg per day, manual peeling is better. If more, a mechanical peeler pays off within months. Also, mechanical peelers may remove too much flesh, leading to starch loss. Manual peeling allows you to control depth. For starch extraction, you want to remove all skin and cortex because they contain bitter compounds and dirt. After peeling, roots should be processed within 24 hours to avoid spoilage. Always use clean, sharp knives to avoid bruising. Train peelers to remove only the skin and thin cortex, leaving the white flesh intact. In many African villages, groups of women peel together, sharing tips and increasing speed. You can also use a simple hand-cranked peeler made from a metal drum with rough interior. This costs less than $100 and speeds up peeling. Remember, the goal is clean, white roots ready for washing. Peeling is the first step to high-quality starch.

2. Washing and Sanitizing Roots

After peeling, cassava roots carry dirt, sand, and microbes. Washing removes these to prevent starch contamination. You need three basins or drums: first for rough washing, second for rinsing, third for sanitizing. Use clean water. For 100 kg of roots, use about 200 litres of water. In the first basin, add 1 tablespoon of salt per 10 litres to help remove dirt. Scrub roots with a soft brush or hands. Change water when it becomes muddy. In the second basin, rinse with clean water. In the third basin, add 1 tablespoon of bleach (sodium hypochlorite) per 10 litres of water. Soak roots for 5 minutes. Bleach kills bacteria and mould. Do not use too much bleach; it can affect starch quality. After sanitizing, rinse again with clean water. If you don't have bleach, use lemon juice or vinegar (1 cup per 10 litres) as a natural sanitizer. In rural Africa, many use river water, but it must be filtered or boiled first. Boiling kills microbes but takes fuel. Solar disinfection (putting water in clear bottles in sun for 6 hours) is a cheap alternative. After washing, roots should be white and free of smell. Wet roots are slippery, so handle carefully. Use a clean surface to drain. Do not let roots sit in dirty water; they absorb contaminants. For small operations, wash in a large plastic tub. For larger, build a concrete washing bay with sloped floor and drain. Always wash your hands and tools before starting. Clean water is key to safe starch. If you skip sanitizing, starch may ferment or smell bad. In markets, buyers pay more for clean, white starch. So invest time in washing.

3. Cutting and Slicing Roots for Grating

Whole cassava roots are too large for most graters. You must cut them into smaller pieces, usually 5-10 cm long and 2-3 cm thick. Use a sharp knife or machete on a clean wooden board. Cut roots crosswise into chunks, then slice lengthwise if needed. The size depends on your grater: manual graters need smaller pieces (2-3 cm), while motorized graters can take larger chunks (5-8 cm). For 100 kg of roots, cutting takes about 30-45 minutes with two people. Wear gloves to avoid cuts and skin irritation from cassava juice. Cut on a stable surface; never hold root in your hand while cutting. A common method is to split the root lengthwise into quarters, then cut into short lengths. Remove any woody core or fibrous parts. These can clog the grater. Also remove any dark spots or rotten areas. After cutting, pieces should be uniform for even grating. If pieces are too big, the grater may jam or produce coarse pulp. If too small, you waste time and get fine mush that is hard to press. In Africa, many use a simple wooden block with a knife attached, called a 'cassava cutter'. You can also use a mechanical chopper, but it costs more. For small scale, hand cutting is fine. Keep your knife sharp; a dull knife bruises the root and releases more water. Cut roots just before grating to prevent browning. If you must wait, soak pieces in clean water with a little salt (1 teaspoon per litre) to prevent discoloration. After cutting, proceed immediately to grating. Do not let cut pieces sit for more than 30 minutes. This step is simple but critical for efficient grating and high starch yield.

4. Removing Foreign Materials and Defects

Cassava roots can contain stones, sand, pieces of wood, and damaged parts. These must be removed before grating. Foreign materials damage graters and lower starch quality. After washing, inspect each root. Look for dark spots, soft areas, or holes from insects. Cut these out with a knife. Also remove any roots that are completely rotten or mouldy. They contain toxins that can spoil the whole batch. For 100 kg, expect to discard 5-10 kg of defective roots. Use a clean table or mat to spread roots. Work in good light. A simple method is to float roots in water: healthy roots sink, while some foreign materials float. But stones sink too, so also use your hands. For small stones, a mesh screen (1 cm holes) can help. Place roots on screen and shake; sand and small stones fall through. For larger operations, use a destoner machine, but it is expensive. Manual removal is fine for up to 1 tonne per day. Also check for metal pieces, glass, or plastic from packaging. These can break your grater. Train workers to be vigilant. Keep a bucket for waste. After removing defects, cut roots into pieces as described in Lesson 3. Inspect again after cutting; sometimes defects are hidden inside. Remove any brown or black streaks. The presence of foreign materials can cause off-flavours and may even damage equipment. In many African markets, starch with dark specks sells for less. So take time to clean. Use clean water to rinse roots after trimming. Change water frequently. A simple magnet can remove iron particles if you suspect them. For quality starch, this step is non-negotiable. It takes about 15-20 minutes per 100 kg. Do it well, and your grater will last longer and your starch will be purer.

5. Size Reduction: Grating and Rasping

Grating breaks cassava root cells to release starch granules. You can use a manual grater (a metal sheet with punched holes) or a motorized rasping machine. Manual grating: hold a piece of root and rub it against the rough surface. It takes about 1 hour to grate 10 kg. Motorized graters use a spinning drum with sharp teeth. They can process 500-1000 kg per hour. In Africa, many small processors use a locally made motorized grater powered by a 5-10 HP diesel engine or electric motor. The cost is $300-$1000. For manual grating, the root must be cut small (2-3 cm). For motorized, pieces can be larger (5-8 cm). The goal is to produce a fine pulp, not chunks. The finer the pulp, the more starch you extract. After grating, the pulp should be a smooth, wet mash. If it is too coarse, starch remains trapped in cells. You can run the pulp through the grater twice. Add a little water during grating to prevent overheating and to help starch release. For motorized graters, adjust the gap between the drum and the casing. Too tight causes jamming; too loose gives coarse pulp. Test with a handful of roots. In many villages, people use a 'cassava grater' made from a car wheel with nails hammered through, mounted on a frame. This works but is slow. A better option is a hammer mill with a screen, but it may bruise the starch. The best is a dedicated rasping machine with stainless steel teeth. After grating, the pulp is ready for pressing or settling. Work quickly; grated pulp darkens if left in air. Add 1 litre of water per 10 kg of pulp to keep it wet. If you are using a manual grater, take breaks to avoid blisters. Wear gloves. This step is the heart of starch extraction. A good grater can increase yield by 20%. So invest in a quality grater or maintain your manual one well.

6. Maintaining Equipment for Grating

Grating equipment must be cleaned and maintained daily. After use, rinse the grater with clean water to remove cassava pulp. Leftover pulp hardens and can break teeth or clog holes. For manual graters, use a stiff brush to scrub the rough surface. For motorized graters, open the casing and remove all pulp. Use a wooden scraper, not metal, to avoid damaging the drum. Wash with water and a little detergent. Rinse well. Dry with a cloth. Every week, check the teeth or blades for wear. Dull teeth produce coarse pulp and reduce starch yield. Sharpen or replace them. For a manual grater, you can sharpen the punched holes with a file. For motorized, replace the drum or teeth as recommended by the manufacturer. Lubricate bearings and moving parts with grease or oil every 50 hours of use. Check belts and tighten if loose. A loose belt slips and reduces grating speed. Also check the motor: clean air filters, change oil every 200 hours. In dusty African environments, clean the motor weekly. Store equipment in a dry place, covered with a tarpaulin. Rust is a major enemy. If you use a mild steel grater, coat it with cooking oil after drying. Stainless steel is better but more expensive. For manual graters, hang them on a wall. For motorized, keep in a shed. Train one person to be responsible for maintenance. Keep a logbook: date, hours used, maintenance done. Replace worn parts before they break. A broken grater stops production. In many projects, equipment fails because of neglect. Simple daily cleaning adds years to life. Also, ensure the power source is stable. If using a diesel engine, check fuel and oil daily. For electric, use a voltage stabilizer. With good care, a motorized grater can last 5-10 years. Manual graters can last 2-3 years if cleaned and sharpened. Remember, maintenance costs less than replacement. So make it a habit.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Cassava Root Preparation for Starch Extraction

In cassava starch extraction, root preparation is where margins are made or lost. Poor preparation increases starch losses, raises grating equipment wear, darkens starch colour, and drives up unit costs. For African processors from smallholder cooperatives in Nigeria and Ghana to medium-scale plants in Tanzania, Uganda, and Mozambique the preparation stage typically accounts for 2540% of total processing labour and a significant share of water and energy use. Getting it right is not technical perfectionism; it is direct profit protection.

This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into operational guidance: peeling method selection, washing and sanitising, cutting and slicing, defect removal, size reduction by grating/rasping, and equipment maintenance. It is written for plant managers, supervisors, and operators who need decisions they can act on today.

1. Peeling: Manual vs Mechanical The Cost Decision

Peeling removes the outer periderm and cortex, which contain little starch, high fibre, and enzymes that can darken the final product. In Africa, manual peeling remains dominant because of low capital cost and abundant labour. But labour cost per tonne rises with scale, and manual peeling is inconsistent deeper cuts waste starch-bearing parenchyma, while shallow cuts leave cortex that contaminates the mash.

Mechanical peelers (abrasive drum or knife type) raise throughput and consistency, but require reliable power, spare parts, and water. The decision is economic: compare cost per tonne peeled, not machine price alone.

2. Washing and Sanitising Roots

Washing removes soil, sand, and surface microorganisms. In starch extraction, soil is not just a hygiene issue it is an abrasive that accelerates grating drum wear and contaminates starch with ash. Effective washing typically uses a rotary washer or paddle washer with clean water, followed by a sanitising rinse where required for food-grade or export markets.

Water quality matters. Use potable or at least clean, low-iron water. High iron can cause discolouration. Chlorinated rinse water (typically 50100 ppm free chlorine, contact time per local food safety guidance) can reduce microbial load, but must be managed so it does not carry into starch with residual odour or flavour issues.

3. Cutting and Slicing Roots for Grating

Whole roots cannot be fed efficiently into most graters. Cutting or slicing into pieces (typically 515 cm chunks or 25 cm slices) improves feed control, reduces motor overload, and produces a more uniform mash. Uniformity is the key: mixed piece sizes cause uneven grating, higher fines loss, and inconsistent starch release.

Use clean, sharp stainless or food-grade blades. Carbon steel blades corrode and can introduce iron contamination, causing grey or dark starch. Set the cutter for consistent size and inspect daily.

4. Removing Foreign Materials and Defects

Foreign materials stones, sand, metal fragments, rope, plastic damage graters and contaminate starch. Defective roots rotten, insect-damaged, woody, or excessively fibrous reduce starch yield and quality. Removal should happen at multiple points: intake, after washing, and before grating.

Practical controls: a inspection table or conveyor with trained pickers; magnetic separators before the grater; and a reject bin with recorded weights. Track reject percentage it is a direct yield and quality KPI.

SAFETY WARNING Rotating Graters, Cutters, and Peelers

Rotating grating drums, cutter discs, and mechanical peelers can cause severe hand and arm injuries. Before any cleaning, jam clearing, or maintenance:

  • Isolate and lock out the power supply (lock-out/tag-out).
  • Wait for full stop never reach in while any part is moving.
  • Never bypass guards or emergency stops; report damaged guards immediately.
  • Use a tool, not hands, to clear blockages.
  • Wear cut-resistant gloves for manual peeling and handling of knives.

In many African plants, injuries occur during unplanned jam clearing. Make lock-out/tag-out a written, enforced procedure.

5. Size Reduction: Grating and Rasping

Grating (or rasping) ruptures cassava cells to release starch granules. The quality of this step determines starch recovery. Key variables: rasp/drum condition, speed, feed rate, and water addition. A worn rasp produces coarse mash with intact cells, lowering extraction efficiency. Excessive speed or feed can overheat the mash, causing starch damage and browning.

Target a fine, uniform mash not a paste. Add process water as needed to control temperature and viscosity. Inspect rasp surfaces every shift; replace or re-sharpen according to manufacturer guidance. In many African plants, the grater is the single highest-wear item; budgeting for rasps is a normal operating cost, not an emergency.

6. Maintaining Equipment for Grating

Maintenance is a profit centre. A well-maintained grater runs longer, uses less power per tonne, and produces better starch. Build a simple maintenance schedule:

  • Daily: clean, inspect rasps, check guards, check water flow, record downtime.
  • Weekly: check bearings, belt tension, alignment, electrical connections.
  • Monthly: replace worn rasps, inspect drum balance, review reject and yield data.
  • Annually: overhaul motor, replace worn parts, recalibrate feed system.

Keep a spare set of rasps, belts, and bearings on site. Downtime during harvest season is the most expensive downtime.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Process roots within 2448 hours of harvest to limit starch loss and browning.
  2. Peel consistently remove cortex without cutting deep into starch-bearing flesh.
  3. Wash with clean water until visible soil is removed; sanitise if required for your market.
  4. Cut to uniform size (515 cm chunks or 25 cm slices) before grating.
  5. Inspect and remove stones, metals, rot, and fibrous roots at intake, after washing, and before grating.
  6. Check grating rasps every shift; replace worn parts before they cut yield.
  7. Enforce lock-out/tag-out and guard checks before any cleaning or maintenance.
  8. Record rejects, downtime, and yield daily use the data to fix the biggest loss first.
