1. HACCP Principles in Flour Milling

HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) is a food safety system that prevents problems before they happen. In a flour mill, you identify where things can go wrong (hazards) and set up checks to stop them. There are 7 principles: conduct a hazard analysis, determine critical control points (CCPs), set critical limits, monitor CCPs, take corrective actions, verify, and keep records.

In a typical African flour mill, common hazards include stones, metal fragments, pests, and bacteria like Salmonella. Your CCPs might be: receiving wheat (check moisture and pests), milling (sifters and magnets), and packing (metal detectors). For each CCP, set limits. Example: metal detector must reject any piece larger than 2.0 mm. Monitor every 30 minutes by testing with a test piece.

If a CCP fails, you must act immediately. If the metal detector fails, stop packing, re-check all flour packed since the last good check, and divert suspect flour to animal feed or rework. Document everything: time, action, and who did it. Records prove your system works.

For small mills, start simple. Focus on three CCPs: receiving, sifting, and metal detection. Use a logbook and a calibrated thermometer. Train all staff on what to watch for. In many African countries, HACCP is required for export. Even for local sales, it builds trust and reduces recalls.

Remember: HACCP is a team effort. Form a food safety team with the mill manager, production supervisor, and quality officer. Review your plan every year or when you change equipment or suppliers. Keep it practical and written in a language everyone understands.

2. Sanitation and Pest Control

Sanitation means keeping the mill clean to prevent contamination. Pest control stops rodents, insects, and birds from getting into your flour. In Africa, warm climates make pests a year-round problem. A single rat can contaminate 10 kg of flour with urine and droppings, making it unsafe.

Start with a master sanitation schedule. Clean floors, walls, and equipment daily. Use food-grade detergents and sanitisers. For dry areas, sweep or vacuum; avoid water where it can cause mould. Clean sifters and magnets every shift. Use a 70% alcohol spray on food-contact surfaces. Store cleaning chemicals away from flour.

Pest control: seal all cracks larger than 6 mm. Install door sweeps and screens on windows. Keep a 1-metre clear zone around the buildingremove weeds and debris. Set up rodent bait stations every 10 metres along walls. Check them weekly. Use insect light traps for flies and moths. For stored product insects, fumigate with phosphine tablets only by trained personnel, following safety rules.

Monitor with sticky traps and pheromone traps. Record pest sightings in a log. If you see rodent droppings, clean and disinfect the area, then increase bait stations. Never use rat poison inside production areasuse only outside.

Train staff to report pests immediately. A clean mill is a safe mill. In many African mills, poor sanitation causes aflatoxin from mouldy grain. Keep wheat moisture below 13% to prevent mould. Regular cleaning also reduces dust explosions. Make sanitation a daily habit, not a once-a-month event.

3. Allergen Management and Cross-Contamination

Allergens are substances that cause severe reactions in some people. In flour milling, the main allergen is wheat (gluten). Other allergens like soy, milk, or egg may be present if you mill multiple products on the same lines. Cross-contamination happens when a small amount of allergen gets into a product that should not have it.

In Africa, many mills produce wheat flour, maize meal, and maybe soy flour. If you use the same equipment without proper cleaning, wheat residues can end up in maize meal. For someone with celiac disease, even 20 parts per million (ppm) of gluten can cause harm. That's about 20 grams of wheat flour in 1 tonne of maize meal.

To prevent cross-contamination, schedule production from least allergenic to most allergenic. For example, run maize first, then wheat. After wheat, do a full clean-out: vacuum, wipe with damp cloth, and flush with a small batch of the next product (then discard that flush). Use dedicated equipment where possibleseparate sifters, bins, and conveyors for allergens.

Label everything clearly. If a product contains wheat, list it in bold on the label. If there is a risk of cross-contamination, state 'may contain wheat'. Train staff on proper handwashing and changing uniforms between lines. Store allergenic ingredients in sealed, labelled containers.

Test for allergens using rapid test kits (e.g., ELISA for gluten). Swab surfaces and test. Keep records. If you find contamination, re-clean and re-test. For small mills, the simplest rule: never assume equipment is cleanverify it. Allergen management protects your customers and your business from costly recalls.

4. Traceability and Recall Procedures

Traceability means you can track any bag of flour from the wheat field to the customer's plate. If a problem occurs, you can quickly find and remove affected products. A recall is when you pull products from the market. In Africa, a recall can ruin a small mill if not handled well.

Start with lot numbers. Every bag of flour should have a code showing the production date, shift, and mill line. For example, '2025-03-15-A' means 15 March 2025, shift A. Record which wheat supplier and which silo went into that lot. Keep records for at least two years.

If a customer complains about stones in flour, you need to trace back. Check your lot number. Find the wheat supplier and the sifter settings. Then trace forward: which shops received that lot? Contact them immediately. In a recall, speed is critical. The faster you act, the fewer people get hurt.

Have a written recall plan. Include names and phone numbers of key staff, distributors, and local health authorities. Test your plan once a year with a mock recall. Pick a lot number, pretend it's contaminated, and see how long it takes to trace 100% of the product. Aim for under 4 hours.

If you must recall, notify authorities, collect returned products, and document everything. Find the root cause and fix it. For small mills, use a simple notebook or Excel sheet. The key is to record every batch and every sale. Traceability is not just for big companiesit's your safety net.

5. Auditing and Certification (BRC, SQF)

Auditing is a formal check of your food safety systems. Certification means an independent body confirms you meet a standard. BRC (British Retail Consortium) and SQF (Safe Quality Food) are two global standards. Many African exporters need one of these to sell to major supermarkets or overseas buyers.

A BRC audit covers senior management commitment, HACCP, quality management, site standards, product control, and process control. You get a grade: A, B, C, or D. SQF is similar but focuses on a food safety and quality plan. Both require documented procedures, trained staff, and regular internal audits.

Preparation is key. Start with a gap assessment: compare your current practices to the standard. Common gaps in African mills: no pest control records, no calibration logs, and no allergen testing. Fix these first. Write simple proceduresone page each. Train staff and keep training records.

During the audit, the auditor will walk through your mill, ask questions, and check records. Be honest. If you don't know an answer, say you'll find out. Show that you are committed to improvement. After the audit, you get a report with non-conformances. You must fix major ones within 28 days.

Certification costs money$2,000 to $10,000 USD for the audit plus annual fees. But it opens markets. For small mills, consider starting with a local standard like ISO 22000, then move to BRC. Join a cooperative or association to share costs. Remember: certification is not a one-time event. You must maintain the system every day.

6. Statistical Process Control in Milling

Statistical Process Control (SPC) uses data to keep your milling process stable. Instead of just checking final flour, you monitor key parameters during production. This helps you catch problems before they become defects. In flour milling, SPC is used for moisture, ash content, particle size, and extraction rate.

Start with a control chart. For example, measure flour moisture every hour. Plot the results. Calculate the average and the upper and lower control limits (usually 3 standard deviations). If a point falls outside the limits, or if you see a trend (7 points going up), stop and investigate. Maybe the wheat moisture changed, or the tempering time is off.

You can use a simple Excel sheet or a notebook. Record the time, measurement, and any adjustments. For small mills, focus on two or three critical parameters: moisture, ash, and protein. Moisture should be 13.5% to 14.5% for bread flour. Ash should be 0.50% to 0.60%. If ash is too high, your flour is too bran-contaminatedcheck the sifters.

SPC also helps with packaging. Weigh 10 bags every hour. If the average weight is below 50 kg (for a 50 kg bag), adjust the filler. Use a scale accurate to 10 grams. Keep a chart. This reduces give-away and ensures compliance.

Train your mill operators to take measurements and plot points. They don't need to be statisticiansjust follow the rules. When a chart shows a problem, they should call the supervisor. SPC saves money by reducing rework and complaints. In Africa, where margins are tight, SPC is a practical tool for consistent quality.

This Practical Reference consolidates the operational standards, tolerances, and verification steps that millers from Nairobi to Lagos to Durban use to protect brand value, satisfy buyers, and keep product moving. Treat it as a bench-level companion to the six lessons in this module.</p>

<h2>Sanitation, Pest Control, and Allergen Control</h2>

<p>Flour dust is a food source for insects and rodents, and a vector for cross-contamination. In humid African climates Mombasa, Lagos, Dar es Salaam pest pressure is year-round. A documented sanitation programme with frequency logs is mandatory for BRC and SQF. Allergen management is equally commercial: a mislabelled 50 kg bag of wheat flour containing soy traces can trigger a recall across an entire distribution network.</p>

<strong style="color:#7a1c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING Aflatoxin and Mycotoxin Risk in African Wheat and Maize Flour</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#5c1111;">In many African regions, wheat and maize can carry <strong>aflatoxins</strong> and <strong>deoxynivalenol (DON)</strong> above safe limits due to poor storage, high humidity, and delayed drying. Aflatoxin B1 is a Group 1 carcinogen. Never accept a truck load without a rapid test result. Reject, quarantine, and document any load exceeding 10 ppb aflatoxin or 750 ppb DON. Do not blend contaminated grain into clean stock this is illegal and unsafe. Store grain below 13% moisture and use aeration. Train intake staff to recognise mouldy, musty, or discoloured grain. Keep test kits calibrated and records for at least two years.</p>

<h2>Traceability, Recall, and Statistical Process Control</h2>

<p>Traceability is the backbone of a recall. In African distribution chains, flour often moves through multiple intermediaries wholesalers, open markets, and informal retailers. A one-up, one-down traceability system (identify immediate supplier and immediate customer) is the minimum required by Codex and BRC. Mock recalls should be conducted at least twice a year and completed within four hours. Statistical Process Control (SPC) complements this by monitoring key quality parameters moisture, ash, protein, and particle size using control charts. When a process drifts beyond 2 sigma, investigate before product goes out of specification.</p>

<p>For SPC in milling, the most practical variables are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Moisture content:</strong> target 13.5% 0.5% critical for shelf life and mould prevention.</li>
<li><strong>Ash content:</strong> target 0.55% 0.05% for white flour indicates bran contamination.</li>
<li><strong>Protein:</strong> target 11.5% 0.3% affects bread volume and buyer specifications.</li>
<li><strong>Particle size:</strong> target 120150 microns affects baking performance.</li>
</ul>
<p>Use X-bar and R charts. Plot every hour. When a point exceeds control limits, stop, investigate, and correct. Record the corrective action. This is the difference between a mill that passes audits and one that loses contracts.</p>

<h2>Auditing and Certification: BRC, SQF, and African Realities</h2>

<p>BRCGS Food Safety Issue 9 and SQF Edition 9 are the two most recognised certifications for flour mills supplying international buyers, supermarkets, and food manufacturers. In Africa, certification is increasingly required by retailers such as Shoprite, Carrefour, and export buyers in the EU and Middle East. The audit covers senior management commitment, HACCP, quality management, site standards, product control, process control, personnel, and food safety culture. Expect an unannounced audit option under BRCGS Issue 9. Prepare by conducting internal audits monthly, maintaining a corrective action log, and training staff on food safety culture.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>CCP monitoring:</strong> Verify metal detector and magnet checks every 30 minutes with test pieces; log results.</li>
<li><strong>Mycotoxin testing:</strong> Test every incoming wheat truck for DON and aflatoxin; reject non-compliant loads.</li>
<li><strong>Allergen changeover:</strong> Complete flush and clean; verify with label check and flush log.</li>
<li><strong>Pest control:</strong> Inspect bait stations and insect light traps weekly; trend sightings.</li>
<li><strong>Traceability:</strong> Maintain one-up, one-down records; conduct mock recall twice yearly within 4 hours.</li>
<li><strong>SPC:</strong> Plot moisture, ash, protein, and particle size hourly; act on out-of-control points.</li>
<li><strong>Sanitation:</strong> Clean per shift; verify with ATP swabs weekly; document.</li>
<li><strong>Internal audit:</strong> Audit monthly against BRC/SQF clauses; close corrective actions within 30 days.</li>
</ol>
