🐔 Organic and Alternative Feed Formulations

The formulation mechanics of organic, antibiotic-free and alternative-protein poultry rations - which ingredients are permitted, what the safe inclusion ceilings are, and exactly how the numbers move when you remove ionophores and synthetic amino acids from a standard broiler or layer diet.

Lesson 5.1~11 min

Organic Certification Requirements

In this lesson
  • Identify which feed inputs an organic standard permits, restricts and forbids
  • Rebuild a conventional mix sheet so that every line would survive an inspection
  • Calculate the ingredient cost penalty of converting one ration to certified organic

Organic certification is often talked about as a marketing decision. For the person mixing feed it is something much narrower and much more practical: a list of things that may go into the bag, a list of things that may not, and a paper trail proving which is which. This lesson deals only with those mechanics. Which certifying bodies operate in your country, what the inspection costs and whether the market will pay a premium are covered in Module 11 Lesson 3 - do not start converting a ration until you have read that lesson and confirmed a buyer.

Every organic standard in commercial use rests on the same four feed rules, whatever the wording.

  1. The feed ingredients themselves must be certified organic, grown without synthetic fertiliser or prohibited pesticide, on land with a documented conversion history. A bag of maize is not organic because the farmer says he used no chemicals; it is organic because it carries a certificate number.
  2. Genetically modified ingredients are excluded. In practice this rules out most imported soya bean meal traded in Africa unless it is specifically segregated and certified.
  3. Synthetic feed additives used to improve performance are excluded. This is the rule that changes the arithmetic most: it removes synthetic DL-methionine and L-lysine, all ionophore and synthetic coccidiostats, and antibiotic growth promoters.
  4. Solvent-extracted meals are usually excluded, because hexane is a synthetic processing aid. You are left with expeller or mechanically pressed cake, which carries more residual oil and less protein than the solvent meal in your normal formulation table.

What stays permitted matters just as much. Almost all standards allow the minerals and vitamins a bird physically cannot make for itself: limestone, dicalcium phosphate, salt, and a vitamin and trace mineral premix. Grit and coarse limestone are allowed. Feed enzymes such as phytase, probiotic cultures and most organic acids are permitted under most standards, though you must confirm the specific product is listed by your certifier before you buy a drum of it.

Now the consequence for the ration sheet. In a conventional broiler starter you would hit the Ross 2022 target of 1.32% digestible lysine and 1.00% digestible methionine plus cysteine partly by adding 1.5-2.5 kg of synthetic DL-methionine per tonne. Take that out and the only way back to 1.00% Met+Cys is more intact protein - more soya cake, more sunflower cake, more fish meal. Crude protein climbs above the NRC 1994 starter figure of 23.0% while you chase one limiting amino acid, and the surplus nitrogen you have paid for leaves the bird as ammonia in the litter. That is the central economic problem of organic poultry feeding, and every lesson in this module comes back to it.

Work the cost. Take a 1-tonne broiler grower batch. Conventional: 620 kg maize at $0.32/kg, 250 kg solvent soya meal at $0.62/kg, 60 kg maize bran, 25 kg fish meal, limestone, DCP, salt, premix, plus 2 kg synthetic methionine - roughly $455 per tonne at those illustrative prices. Certified organic: organic maize commonly trades at a 30-50% premium and organic expeller soya cake at 40-60%, and you must add roughly 40-60 kg more protein cake to replace the synthetic methionine. The same tonne lands nearer $640-$700. That is a rise of about 40-50% in feed cost, and feed is normally 65-75% of the total cost of producing a broiler.

A Ugandan example makes the point. A producer near Mukono running 400 birds on a local organic vegetable-box scheme converted his grower ration using certified organic maize from his own land and sunflower cake from a certified press in Lira. His feed cost per bird rose from about $2.10 to $3.05 across the cycle. The scheme paid him a 55% premium on live weight, so the conversion worked - but only because the buyer was agreed in writing before the first bag was mixed.

Write the mix sheet first, price it second, and sign the buyer third.

Organic Grain and Protein Sources

In this lesson
  • Rank the organic energy and protein ingredients available in African markets by nutrient value
  • Apply the published safe inclusion ceilings for cottonseed, rapeseed and canola cake
  • Balance an organic grower ration to the Ross energy specification using expeller cakes

Once the prohibited list is applied, an organic ration is built from a much shorter shopping list than a conventional one. The skill is knowing what each remaining ingredient actually delivers, and where it stops being safe.

Start with energy. Yellow maize is the reference at 3,350 kcal AMEn/kg and about 88% digestible, and it is the backbone of nearly every African poultry ration. Wheat sits lower at about 2,800 kcal/kg and only around 75% digestible, because its arabinoxylans get in the way. Barley is 2,600-2,700 kcal/kg at 73% digestibility, held back by beta-glucans, and oats lower again at 2,400-2,500 kcal/kg and 67% digestible. Maize bran, the cheapest energy source on most local markets, is a diluter: it carries fibre and some residual starch but nothing close to whole maize, so it belongs in layer and finisher rations rather than starter.

Now protein. Solvent soybean meal at 48% crude protein and 2,150 kcal/kg is the conventional workhorse, and it is the one you lose. Organic expeller soya cake retains more oil, so energy is higher but protein is typically several points lower - treat it as approximate and get your supplier's analysis rather than using a book figure. It is still the best amino acid profile available to you, with lysine standardised ileal digestibility of 85-95% and Met+Cys 80-90%. Fish meal at 60% crude protein delivers 2,700-2,900 kcal/kg with lysine digestibility of 90-98%, the highest of any ingredient in the table, plus almost no phytic acid (under 0.1%, against 1.0-1.3% in soybean meal). Where a certified or acceptable fish meal source exists, 3-5% inclusion does more for an organic starter than any other single decision.

The cakes carry ceilings you must respect.

  1. Cottonseed cake. Gossypol is the problem. Keep total gossypol below 0.1% of the finished diet, which in practice means limiting cottonseed cake to 5-10% of the ration depending on the gossypol content of the particular meal. Broilers are more sensitive than layers, and chicks more sensitive than older birds, so keep it out of starter entirely and use the low end of the range for young stock.
  2. Traditional rapeseed cake. High glucosinolate material is limited to 2-5% of the diet because of goitrogenic effects - thyroid enlargement, reduced feed intake from poor palatability, and growth depression.
  3. Modern low-glucosinolate canola meal is a different ingredient and can go to 5-15%, though it only supplies 1,900-2,000 kcal/kg at 65-70% digestibility, so it dilutes energy.
  4. Sunflower cake is widely available from small presses across East and Southern Africa. Its amino acid profile is methionine-adequate but lysine-poor, which makes it a useful partner for soya rather than a replacement.

Here is a worked organic broiler grower using expeller cakes only. Target: Ross 2022 grower energy of 3,050 kcal/kg and NRC 20% crude protein. Per 100 kg: maize 58 kg, expeller soya cake 24 kg, sunflower cake 8 kg, fish meal 4 kg, maize bran 2 kg, limestone 1.2 kg, DCP 1.6 kg, salt 0.3 kg, premix 0.3 kg, plus phytase and an NSP enzyme if your certifier lists them. Energy check: (58 x 3,350 + 8 x 1,950 + 4 x 2,800) all divided by 100 gives roughly 1,943 + 156 + 112 from those three lines alone, and with the soya cake contribution you land in the 2,950-3,050 kcal/kg band. Protein check: soya cake and fish meal do most of the work, and the ration comes in near 21% crude protein - deliberately above 20%, because without synthetic methionine you have to overshoot total protein to reach the amino acid target.

A Tanzanian example: a Njombe producer supplying an organic hotel contract runs exactly this shape of ration, buying sunflower cake from a village press 12 km away at about $0.30/kg and mixing it fresh every ten days because expeller cake with high residual oil goes rancid quickly in the heat. That last point is not a footnote. High-oil organic cakes have a short shelf life, and a rancid cake destroys vitamin A and E in the same bag.

Antibiotic-Free and Ionophore-Free Formulations

In this lesson
  • Describe what an ionophore does in the gut and what its removal changes
  • Rebuild a broiler programme around coccidiosis vaccination and gut-conditioning additives
  • Calculate the feed cost penalty of an antibiotic-free programme per bird

Antibiotic-free and ionophore-free are two different decisions, and mixing them up costs money. Antibiotic-free removes antibiotic growth promoters. Ionophore-free additionally removes monensin, lasalocid and salinomycin, which are the cheapest and most reliable coccidiosis control tools on the market. Many buyers who ask for antibiotic-free are perfectly happy with ionophores; ask before you strip them out.

Understand what you are taking away. Monensin, lasalocid and salinomycin are polyether ionophores. They transport sodium, potassium and hydrogen ions across the membrane of Eimeria parasites, causing osmotic imbalance, vacuolisation and cell death. Critically, they act in the initial phase of the parasite's life cycle, which lets a low level of infection through and allows the bird to build natural immunity. Nicarbazin works differently, attacking intracellular stages inside the gut epithelial cells, and amprolium is a thiamine antagonist that starves the parasite of thiamine rather than lysing it. All of them carry a 7-day withdrawal before slaughter, and the acceptable daily intake figures differ enormously - halofuginone at 0.00003 mg/kg body weight per day against nicarbazin at 0.2 mg/kg, which is among the highest.

Remove the ionophore and you must replace three separate things.

  1. Coccidiosis control. The mainstream replacement is a live coccidiosis vaccine given at day-old, which seeds a controlled infection so immunity develops. Vaccination only works if litter management is right - the oocysts must cycle through slightly damp litter to complete the immunity process, so a vaccinated flock on bone-dry or soaking litter fails. This is management work you did not previously have to do.
  2. The growth-promoting side effect. Ionophores also suppress gram-positive bacteria in the gut, which is part of why they improve feed conversion. Removing them raises the risk of Clostridium overgrowth and necrotic enteritis, especially where feed contains fumonisins - note that at 18.6 ppm, below the nominal 20 ppm broiler limit, fumonisins altered intestinal microflora and predisposed broilers to necrotic enteritis.
  3. Trace mineral balance. Monensin may suppress copper absorption, so a conventional programme sometimes carries a compensating copper adjustment. Take the ionophore out and revisit that adjustment rather than leaving it in blindly.

What goes in instead? The published evidence on natural alternatives is reassuring on performance and honest about cost. Trials of probiotic and herbal additives - oregano, garlic, thyme - showed body weight gain not significantly different from antibiotic controls over 0-42 days, feed intake maintained or slightly improved, and feed efficiency equivalent, with no increase in E. coli colonisation and no adverse blood chemistry. Garlic and thyme treatments significantly reduced ileal crypt depth, a positive gut-health marker. The trade-off was an increase in feed cost per kilogram of gain of 0.49-1.80% against antibiotics.

Organic acids do more of the heavy lifting. Formic and propionic at 0.5-1.0%, butyric at 0.3-0.8%, citric at 0.5-1.5% lower intestinal pH, suppress pathogens and improve mineral availability. Published efficacy ranges are 2-8% FCR improvement depending on acid type and gut health status, 1-3% better feed intake, and 3-5% better weight gain under suboptimal sanitation - which describes most small African houses in the wet season.

Cost it for 500 broilers eating 3.8 kg each, so 1,900 kg of feed. Ionophore inclusion typically costs $2-4 per tonne, call it $6 for the cycle. Replacing it: coccidiosis vaccine at roughly $0.03-0.05 per chick is $15-25; an organic acid blend at 0.5% inclusion with the acid at $1.80/kg adds about $17 per tonne, or $32 for the cycle. Total replacement cost is around $47-57 against $6. That is roughly $0.09-0.11 extra per bird. If the antibiotic-free buyer pays even $0.15/kg more on a 2.0 kg dressed bird, you are ahead.

A Ghanaian example: a Dormaa producer supplying a supermarket chain that demanded no antibiotics kept salinomycin (permitted under that buyer's rules), added butyrate at 0.4% and tightened litter management. FCR held at 1.72 and the supermarket premium covered the additive cost twice over. He asked what the buyer actually required before spending money on rules nobody imposed.

Plant-Based and Sustainable Proteins

In this lesson
  • Evaluate alternative protein ingredients against the published amino acid and energy standards
  • Set safe inclusion ceilings for leaf meals, insect meals and legume grains
  • Replace a portion of imported soya with locally grown protein without breaking the ration

Imported soybean meal is usually the most expensive line and the most exposed to currency swings on an African feed bill. Every producer eventually asks what can replace part of it. The answer depends on two things you can measure: amino acid quality and the anti-nutritional factor that sets the ceiling.

Judge every candidate against the same three yardsticks. Ross 2022 asks for 1.32% digestible lysine and 1.00% digestible Met+Cys in starter, falling to 1.08% and 0.86% in finisher. Layers at peak need about 0.92% SID lysine and 0.83% Met+Cys at 90 g intake on Hy-Line figures. And energy must still land near 2,975-3,100 kcal/kg for broilers. A protein source that hits crude protein but misses lysine, or that hits both but drags energy down, has not replaced soya.

Work through the realistic options.

  1. Sunflower cake. Widely pressed in East and Southern Africa. Relatively good in methionine, poor in lysine, and high in fibre so energy is modest. Use it to displace part of the soya, not all of it, and pair it with a lysine-rich ingredient such as fish meal.
  2. Cottonseed cake. Cheap wherever ginneries operate. Capped by gossypol at 5-10% of the diet so that finished-diet gossypol stays under 0.1%, and kept out of starter because chicks are more sensitive than older birds.
  3. Groundnut cake. Excellent palatability and useful protein, but it is the highest aflatoxin risk ingredient in African feed markets. Chicks tolerate only 0.005 ppm (5 ppb) aflatoxin, and broilers and layers 0.02 ppm. If you cannot test, do not put groundnut cake in starter feed.
  4. Grain legumes - cowpea, pigeon pea, lablab, soya beans grown on farm. All need heat treatment to destroy trypsin inhibitors. Roasting raw soya beans is standard practice, and under-roasted beans depress growth while over-roasted beans lose lysine. Note the general principle from the reference material: moderate heat processing also reduces phytic acid by roughly 20-30%, but excessive heat damages amino acids.
  5. Leaf meals - moringa, leucaena, cassava leaf, sweet potato vine. These are genuinely useful pigment and vitamin sources for layers and add some protein. Their fibre content limits them to low single-digit inclusions in commercial-type rations; published poultry inclusion ceilings vary widely by species and processing, so treat any figure you are given as approximate and introduce them gradually while weighing birds.
  6. Insect meals, chiefly black soldier fly larvae. Amino acid profile is broadly comparable to fish meal and the sustainability case is strong. Published poultry inclusion research is still moving, so treat inclusion rates as approximate, insist on a laboratory analysis from the producer, and start low.

The substitution arithmetic is what protects you. Suppose your grower uses 25 kg soya cake per 100 kg and you want to move 8 kg of it to sunflower cake. Soya cake carries far more lysine per kilogram than sunflower cake, so the swap loses lysine. You must either add roughly 1-2 kg of fish meal to put the lysine back, or accept a lower growth rate. Never substitute on crude protein alone - crude protein is a nitrogen measurement, and nitrogen is not lysine.

Also watch fibre. Sunflower cake, leaf meals and maize bran all add bulk. Push total fibre up and the bird fills its crop before it meets its energy requirement, which shows up as slow growth on a ration that looks correct on paper. In hot weather this is worse, because intake is already suppressed.

A Kenyan example: a Machakos producer with 800 layers replaced 6 kg of every 100 kg of soya cake with dried moringa leaf and 4 kg with sunflower cake, keeping fish meal at 3 kg to protect lysine. Yolk colour deepened enough for her to sell at the local hotel rate rather than the gate rate, and feed cost per dozen eggs fell by about $0.08. She weighed 20 birds every fortnight throughout the change, which is the only reason she knew it was working.

Probiotics and Enzyme Supplements

In this lesson
  • Match each enzyme class to the ingredient problem it actually solves
  • Calculate the phosphorus and energy value released by phytase and NSP enzymes
  • Decide whether an enzyme or probiotic pays back on your own ration

Enzymes and probiotics are the two additives most likely to pay for themselves in a small African feed operation, because they attack problems your ingredients genuinely have: high phytate, high fibre and a gut under pressure from heat and poor water.

Phytase first, because it is the clearest case. Phytic acid is present in every plant seed - maize at 0.7-1.0%, soybean meal at 1.0-1.3%, barley at 0.8-1.2%. It chelates minerals, cutting phosphorus bioavailability by 20-50%, zinc by 30-60%, and calcium, iron and copper by 15-40%, and it can complex with protein to reduce digestibility. Phytase degrades it and releases the bound minerals. Commercial products typically assay at 10,000 FTU per gram. The effective dietary range is 500-1,500 FTU/kg, and roughly 1,000 FTU/kg is equivalent to a 0.15-0.25% improvement in available phosphorus. The response is largest in young broilers, days 2-14 post-hatch, and it improves tibia ash, tibia calcium and tibia phosphorus - real bone strength, not just a paper saving.

Do the arithmetic. Ross 2022 asks for 0.50% available phosphorus in starter, 0.42% grower, 0.36% finisher. If phytase at 1,000 FTU/kg releases 0.20% available P, you can cut the dicalcium phosphate that was supplying it. DCP carries roughly 18% phosphorus, so 0.20% of the diet in available P needs a little over 1 kg of DCP per 100 kg. Removing 10-12 kg of DCP per tonne at $0.85/kg saves about $8.50-$10.20 per tonne. Phytase at 100 g/tonne of a 10,000 FTU/g product delivers 1,000 FTU/kg; if that 100 g costs $1.20-$2.00, the enzyme pays back four to eight times over. Do not remove all mineral supplementation - the reference is explicit that complete elimination is inadvisable.

NSP enzymes are the second case. A non-starch polysaccharidase complex combines xylanase (at least 12,500 VU/g), beta-glucanase (at least 8,600 VU/g) and arabinofuranosidase (46,000 VU/g). They break down hemicelluloses and pectins, releasing 96-265 kcal/kg of extra energy depending on the ingredient, and delivering 3-7% feed efficiency improvement in broilers at typical inclusion of 500-1,500 units/kg. The response is ingredient-specific and this matters for your buying decisions: wheat-based diets give the largest response because of arabinoxylans, barley and oats respond strongly because of beta-glucans, and maize-based diets give only modest improvements. If your ration is 60% maize, expect the low end. If you have moved to wheat or barley because maize prices spiked, the enzyme suddenly becomes far more valuable.

Protease is the third. At 500-1,500 units/kg it improves crude protein and amino acid digestibility, raises villus height and the villus-height-to-crypt-depth ratio, and works synergistically with phytase in nutrient-reduced diets. It is most attractive where you are using variable-quality expeller cakes and animal by-product meals.

Probiotics are a different kind of investment. The published performance picture is that probiotic and natural additives match or exceed antibiotic growth promoter controls on body weight gain, with no significant compromise in feed efficiency, no increase in E. coli counts and no adverse blood chemistry - at a feed cost increase of 0.49-1.80% per kilogram of gain. Gut structure improves: reduced ileal crypt depth and better villus integrity. So a probiotic does not usually make you money on a healthy, well-managed flock. It makes you money by narrowing the gap on the bad cycles - the wet season, the batch that arrives stressed, the house you cannot clean properly between flocks.

Storage rules matter more than most people admit. Enzymes are proteins and probiotics are living cultures. Both are damaged by heat and moisture, and both are damaged by pelleting temperatures unless the product is specifically heat-stable. On a smallholder mash operation this is an advantage - your cold mixing preserves activity that a commercial pellet mill would destroy.

A Nigerian example: an Ogun State producer mixing 4 tonnes a month for 1,200 broilers added phytase at 100 g/tonne and cut DCP from 18 kg to 8 kg per tonne. Feed cost fell about $9 per tonne, roughly $36 a month, against an enzyme cost near $7 a month. He kept limestone unchanged, because cutting calcium as well would have pushed the Ca:P ratio outside the 1.8-2.0:1 band Ross specifies.

Cost Comparison and Profitability

In this lesson
  • Build a side-by-side cost per tonne for conventional, antibiotic-free and organic rations
  • Convert a feed cost difference into the premium price you must negotiate
  • Decide which alternative programme your farm can actually finance

This is the lesson that decides whether the other five were worth reading. An alternative ration is only a good idea if the extra cost per bird or per dozen eggs is smaller than the premium you can actually collect. Module 11 Lesson 6 deals with how to find and price specialty markets; this lesson deals with the arithmetic on your side of the deal.

Build three costings for the same 1-tonne broiler grower batch. Use illustrative prices here and then replace every one with your own supplier quote.

Conventional: maize 620 kg at $0.32 = $198.40; solvent soya meal 250 kg at $0.62 = $155.00; maize bran 55 kg at $0.18 = $9.90; fish meal 25 kg at $1.10 = $27.50; limestone 12 kg at $0.09 = $1.08; DCP 16 kg at $0.85 = $13.60; salt 3 kg at $0.25 = $0.75; premix 3 kg at $2.60 = $7.80; DL-methionine 2 kg at $4.20 = $8.40; ionophore $3.00. Total about $425 per tonne.

Antibiotic-free and ionophore-free: same base, remove the ionophore (-$3.00), add coccidiosis vaccine costed separately at the chick, add butyrate at 4 kg/tonne at $3.50 = $14.00. Feed cost per tonne about $436, plus vaccine of roughly $0.04 per chick.

Certified organic: organic maize 620 kg at $0.44 = $272.80; organic expeller soya cake 290 kg at $0.92 = $266.80 (more cake, because there is no synthetic methionine); sunflower cake 30 kg at $0.30 = $9.00; fish meal 25 kg at $1.30 = $32.50; minerals, salt and premix as before at about $23.20. Total roughly $604 per tonne.

Now convert to per-bird numbers. A 42-day broiler eating 3.8 kg of feed: conventional $1.62, antibiotic-free $1.66, organic $2.30. On a 2.0 kg dressed weight, the organic ration costs $0.34 more per kilogram sold than conventional. That is your break-even premium before you have paid a single certification fee, and before allowing for the slower growth and higher mortality risk that come with removing coccidiostats.

For layers, run the same logic on a per-dozen basis. Hy-Line Brown figures give 1.23-1.28 kg of feed per dozen eggs from peak to 60 weeks, rising to 1.55-1.61 kg per dozen across a full 20-100 week cycle. Take 1.5 kg per dozen as a working figure for a small African flock. At a conventional layer ration of $0.40/kg, feed is $0.60 per dozen. At an organic layer ration of $0.56/kg, feed is $0.84 per dozen - $0.24 more. If organic eggs sell for $0.60 a dozen more, the margin per dozen improves by $0.36 before other costs.

Three traps swallow small producers who do this arithmetic badly.

  1. Ignoring the production penalty. Feed conversion and mortality usually worsen when you remove coccidiostats and synthetic amino acids. If FCR moves from 1.75 to 1.90 on a 2.0 kg bird, you have added 0.3 kg of feed per bird, which is another $0.55-$0.69 you did not budget for.
  2. Assuming supply. Certified organic maize is thin in most African markets. A producer who converts and then cannot buy certified grain for two months has lost the certification and the flock.
  3. Costing feed only. Certification fees, inspection travel, separate storage to prevent commingling, and the record-keeping time all sit outside the mix sheet.

A Zambian example worth copying: a Chisamba producer ran a 300-bird trial batch on the antibiotic-free ration alongside her normal 300-bird conventional batch in the same week, same chick source, same house layout. She recorded feed used, mortality and final weight for both. The antibiotic-free batch came in at FCR 1.84 against 1.76, mortality 6.5% against 5.0%, and $0.13 higher cost per bird sold. Her buyer was offering $0.25 per bird more. She converted the whole farm - on evidence, after one cycle, not on a brochure.

Run the trial batch. It is the cheapest research you will ever buy.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Which prohibited input causes the biggest formulation problem in organic broiler feed?

Methionine is the first limiting amino acid in maize-soya poultry diets. Removing the synthetic form forces you to add far more intact protein cake to reach the Ross 2022 target of 1.00% digestible Met+Cys in starter, raising both cost and crude protein.

2. Why does organic certification usually exclude solvent-extracted soybean meal?

Solvent extraction uses hexane, a synthetic processing aid excluded by most standards. Organic formulations use expeller or mechanically pressed cake, which has more residual oil and less protein.

3. Which of these is normally still permitted in a certified organic poultry ration?

Vitamins and trace minerals cannot be synthesised by the bird and are permitted under almost all standards, subject to the specific product being listed by your certifier.

4. A certified organic conversion raises feed cost by 40% and feed is 70% of total production cost. Roughly how much does total cost rise?

0.40 x 0.70 = 0.28, so total cost of production rises by roughly 28%. That is the minimum premium your buyer must pay before you break even.

5. What makes a bag of maize organic for certification purposes?

Certification is a documentary system. Inspectors follow the paper trail from certified land to your mixer, so an uncertified ingredient contaminates the whole batch regardless of how it was actually grown.

6. Cottonseed cake inclusion is capped mainly to control which compound?

Gossypol is a natural polyphenol in cottonseed causing reproductive toxicity, liver enlargement and growth depression. Keep total gossypol under 0.1% of the finished diet, which means roughly 5-10% cake inclusion.

7. Why is wheat a weaker energy source than maize in poultry diets?

Wheat delivers around 2,800 kcal/kg at roughly 75% digestibility because arabinoxylans interfere with digestion, against maize at 3,350 kcal/kg and about 88%.

8. Which ingredient contains the least phytic acid?

Fish meal contains under 0.1% phytic acid, so diets built partly on fish meal need less phytase and lose less zinc, iron and phosphorus to chelation.

9. What is the practical storage risk with expeller cakes in a hot climate?

Mechanical pressing leaves more oil in the cake than solvent extraction. That oil oxidises quickly in heat, and rancid fat destroys the fat-soluble vitamins stored alongside it.

10. Low-glucosinolate canola meal can be included at what level?

Modern low-glucosinolate canola meal is safe at 5-15% of the diet, whereas traditional high-glucosinolate rapeseed meal is limited to 2-5%.

11. How do ionophore coccidiostats kill Eimeria parasites?

Monensin, lasalocid and salinomycin are polyether ionophores. They move Na+, K+ and H+ across the parasite membrane, producing osmotic imbalance, vacuolisation and cell death.

12. Why does a coccidiosis vaccine require careful litter management?

Live vaccination seeds a controlled infection that must cycle to build immunity. Bone-dry litter stops the cycle; soaking litter overwhelms the bird. This management work replaces the ionophore.

13. Which coccidiostat is a thiamine antagonist rather than an ionophore?

Amprolium, in use since the early 1960s, blocks thiamine-dependent Eimeria metabolism rather than lysing the parasite cell directly.

14. Published trials of herbal additives against antibiotic controls found what for body weight gain over 0-42 days?

Body weight gain was not significantly different from antibiotic controls, feed efficiency was equivalent and E. coli counts did not rise. The measured trade-off was 0.49-1.80% higher feed cost per kg gain.

15. Before removing ionophores from your ration, what should you check first?

Many buyers requiring antibiotic-free production still permit ionophores. Removing them without being asked adds vaccine cost, additive cost and management work for no premium.

16. Why is crude protein alone a poor basis for substituting one protein ingredient for another?

Crude protein is a nitrogen figure. Two ingredients at the same crude protein can differ enormously in lysine or methionine, so a swap on crude protein alone can quietly break the amino acid specification.

17. Which ingredient carries the highest aflatoxin risk in African feed markets?

Groundnut is highly prone to aflatoxin contamination. With a chick limit of just 0.005 ppm, untested groundnut cake should not go into starter feed.

18. What happens if fibre from sunflower cake, leaf meals and bran pushes too high?

Bulky, fibrous rations limit intake physically. The bird stops eating before it has consumed enough energy, which looks like poor growth on a ration that appears correct on paper.

19. Raw soya beans grown on farm must be heat treated because they contain what?

Trypsin inhibitors in raw soya block protein digestion. Roasting destroys them, but under-roasting leaves them active and over-roasting destroys lysine.

20. How should you treat published inclusion rates for insect meals and leaf meals?

Poultry inclusion research on these ingredients is still developing and results vary with processing. Treat figures as approximate, get an analysis, start low and weigh birds through the change.

21. Roughly what available phosphorus improvement does 1,000 FTU/kg of phytase deliver?

Published equivalency puts 1,000 FTU/kg at roughly 0.15-0.25% improvement in available phosphorus, which is what allows a meaningful cut in dicalcium phosphate.

22. In which diet does an NSP enzyme complex give the largest energy response?

Wheat is high in arabinoxylans and barley and oats in beta-glucans, so those diets respond strongly. Maize-based diets show only modest improvements.

23. Why should you not remove all mineral supplementation when using phytase?

Phytase reduces reliance on supplementation but does not remove the need for it. Cutting mineral inputs entirely risks deficiency and skeletal problems.

24. When do probiotics most clearly earn their cost?

Probiotics match antibiotic controls on performance at 0.49-1.80% higher feed cost per kg gain. The return comes from narrowing the losses on bad cycles, not from beating a healthy flock.

25. Why does small-scale mash mixing suit enzymes and probiotics?

Enzymes are proteins and probiotics are live cultures; both are damaged by heat. Cold mixing on a smallholder operation preserves activity a pellet mill would reduce unless the product is heat-stable.

26. A ration costs $180 more per tonne and a broiler eats 3.8 kg. What is the extra cost per bird?

$180 per tonne is $0.18 per kilogram. Multiplied by 3.8 kg of feed per bird gives $0.684, so roughly $0.68 extra per bird before any production penalty.

27. Why does an organic broiler ration need more protein cake than a conventional one?

Without synthetic DL-methionine you can only reach the Ross target of 1.00% digestible Met+Cys by adding more intact protein, which raises both cost and total crude protein.

28. Which cost is most often left out of an organic conversion budget?

The mix sheet only captures ingredients. Inspection fees, travel, segregated storage to prevent commingling and administration time sit outside it and are frequently forgotten.

29. If FCR worsens from 1.75 to 1.90 on a 2.0 kg bird, how much extra feed is eaten?

1.90 x 2.0 = 3.80 kg against 1.75 x 2.0 = 3.50 kg. The 0.30 kg difference is real money that a feed-cost-only comparison hides.

30. What is the cheapest way to test whether an alternative programme works on your farm?

A parallel trial batch controls for chick quality, weather and house conditions, and gives you your own FCR, mortality and cost per bird within one cycle.

Module 5 capstone

Build two complete rations for the same bird and compare them honestly. Week 1: write down your current conventional ration as a 100 kg mix sheet with every ingredient, its inclusion percentage and its delivered price per kilogram, then calculate crude protein, metabolisable energy and cost per tonne. Week 2: rebuild the same ration under antibiotic-free and ionophore-free rules - remove the coccidiostat, remove synthetic methionine and lysine, and replace the lost amino acids with real protein ingredients, staying inside the inclusion ceilings in this module (cottonseed cake to hold gossypol under 0.1% of the finished diet, rapeseed 2-5%, low-glucosinolate canola 5-15%). Week 3: price both rations from at least two real local suppliers, including a phytase or NSP-enzyme quote and a probiotic or organic-acid quote, and calculate cost per tonne and cost per bird for a 42-day broiler eating 3.8 kg or a layer eating 110 g/day. Week 4: write one page stating the extra cost per bird of the alternative ration, the extra biosecurity and management work it forces on you, and the price premium per kilogram of meat or per dozen eggs you would need to break even. Use your own supplier quotes, not the example figures in the lessons.
