1. Temperature and Humidity Management

Temperature and humidity are the most critical environmental factors for broiler health and growth. Day-old chicks need a brooding temperature of 32-34Â°C at chick level. Reduce by 2-3Â°C each week until 20-22Â°C at 4-5 weeks. In Africa, use a thermometer at chick height. For heating, use charcoal stoves, gas brooders, or infrared bulbs. In hot climates, open curtains, use fans, and sprinkle water on the roof. Humidity should be 60-70% in the first week, then 50-60%. High humidity with high temperature causes heat stress; low humidity causes dehydration. Use a hygrometer. In dry areas, hang wet sacks or use misters. In humid coastal areas, increase ventilation. Monitor chicks: if they huddle under heat, they are cold; if they pant and spread out, they are hot. Adjust accordingly. Always provide cool, clean water. In extreme heat, add electrolytes. In cold, avoid drafts but allow fresh air. Record daily temperature and humidity to track patterns. Power outages are common; have backup heating. For small flocks, use a kerosene lamp inside a metal drum for emergency heat. Remember, day-old chicks cannot regulate their own body temperature for the first two weeks, so you must be their thermostat.

2. Air Quality and Ammonia Control

Ammonia gas from litter burns birds' eyes and lungs, causing blindness, respiratory disease, and poor growth. If you smell ammonia at human nose level, it is already too high for birds (above 25 ppm). Aim for less than 20 ppm. To control ammonia: remove wet litter, increase ventilation, and use litter amendments like superphosphate (0.5 kg per mÂ²) or zeolite. In open-sided houses, adjust curtains to allow airflow without drafts. In tunnels, ensure fans pull air from one end to the other. Never block vents to keep heat in; stale air is worse than cold. Check ammonia levels with a handheld meter or your nose. If your eyes water, act immediately. Also, carbon dioxide and dust are concerns. Dust from feed and feathers can cause respiratory issues. Use pelleted feed to reduce dust, and avoid overstocking. Stocking density: maximum 10-12 birds per mÂ² in open houses, 15-18 in controlled. In Africa, many farms are open-sided; use ridge vents and side curtains. During hot weather, open all vents. During cold, open only top vents. Stir litter daily to release trapped gases. If using gas brooders, ensure proper combustion; faulty heaters produce carbon monoxide. Never use charcoal stoves in sealed rooms. Provide fresh air without chilling chicks. A simple test: if you can see your breath, it's too cold and humid; if you cough, ammonia is high.

3. Litter Management and Moisture Control

Litter is the bedding on the floor. Good litter absorbs moisture, provides cushioning, and dilutes manure. Use wood shavings, rice hulls, or chopped straw. Avoid sawdust (too fine) and sand (too abrasive). Spread 5-10 cm deep. Litter should be dry and crumbly, not wet or caked. Wet litter causes ammonia, breast blisters, and footpad dermatitis. To control moisture: fix drinkers immediately. Use nipple drinkers or closed cups to prevent spillage. If using open drinkers, place them on raised platforms. Increase ventilation to remove moisture. In humid areas, add fresh litter regularly. Turn litter daily to aerate. If litter gets wet, remove clumps and add dry material. A simple test: squeeze a handful; it should not drip water. If it does, it's too wet. Litter moisture should be 20-25%. In Africa, many farmers use deep litter. To prevent caking, avoid overstocking and ensure drinkers are not too close to feeders. When litter becomes too wet, it can be replaced entirely, but that is costly. Instead, maintain it. After each flock, remove all litter, clean and disinfect, then add new litter. Used litter is good fertilizer for crops. Never let litter get too dry and dusty; that causes respiratory problems. Aim for a balance: not too wet, not too dry. Check litter daily by walking through the house; if it sticks to your boots, it's too wet.

4. Lighting Intensity and Duration Adjustments

Light controls feeding, growth, and reproduction. Broilers need light to find feed and water. For the first 7 days, provide 23 hours of light and 1 hour of darkness. Use 40-60 watt bulbs per 20 mÂ², hung 2-2.5 m high. Intensity should be bright enough to read a newspaper. After day 7, reduce to 20 hours of light and 4 hours of dark, or use intermittent lighting (e.g., 1 hour light, 2 hours dark) to save energy and reduce stress. In Africa, natural daylight may be enough in open houses, but supplemental light is needed in the morning and evening. Use LED bulbs to save power. Avoid sudden changes in lighting; it stresses birds. Keep light uniform; shadows cause crowding. Clean bulbs regularly; dust reduces output. In hot climates, provide light during cooler hours (early morning, late evening) so birds eat when it's cool. For heavy breeds, longer light increases feed intake and weight. For leanness, shorter light. But never go below 18 hours total light in the first 3 weeks. After 3 weeks, you can use 18 hours light, 6 hours dark. Some farmers use 12 hours light, 12 hours dark from day 21 to reduce sudden death syndrome. Always observe birds: if they are not eating, check lighting. If they are panting, reduce light intensity. Use a timer to automate. In areas with power cuts, use solar-powered lights or rechargeable lamps. Remember, darkness allows birds to rest and grow.

5. Noise and Stress Reduction

Noise stresses broilers, leading to reduced feed intake, poor growth, and increased disease. Sudden loud noises cause panic, crowding, and injury. Common sources: vehicles, generators, dogs, shouting, and equipment. Minimize noise by locating houses away from roads and noisy activities. Use sound barriers like trees or walls. Train workers to move calmly and speak softly. Avoid banging doors or dropping equipment. Turn off radios and loud machines. In open houses, noise from outside is hard to control, but you can mask it with soft background music? No, that may add noise. Better to reduce. Handle birds gently during vaccination or weighing. Use dim light during handling to keep them calm. Keep a routine: feed, water, and check at the same times daily. Birds thrive on predictability. If a noise occurs, don't panic; move slowly. Provide hiding spots? Not practical. Instead, ensure enough space so birds can spread out. Stocking density affects stress: overcrowding amplifies noise stress. Keep density at 10-12 birds/mÂ². In hot climates, noise plus heat is deadly. So during heat stress, minimize all disturbances. If you must move birds, do it at night. Use fans that are quiet. Fix squeaky doors. Train dogs to stay away. If you have children, keep them away from houses. Stress increases cortisol, which suppresses immunity. So, a quiet farm is a healthy farm. Also, consider the noise from your own operations: feed mixing, generator, etc. Schedule noisy tasks when birds are least active, or use ear protection for birds? No, just reduce noise at source. A simple test: stand inside the house; if it's uncomfortably loud, it's too loud for birds.

6. Waste Management and Environmental Impact

Broiler waste includes litter, manure, dead birds, and wastewater. Improper disposal pollutes soil, water, and air, and spreads disease. Manage waste to protect the environment and earn extra income. Litter and manure are valuable fertilizers. After each flock, remove litter, compost it, and sell to crop farmers. Composting: pile litter, keep moist, turn weekly for 4-6 weeks. This kills pathogens and weed seeds. Use compost on gardens or sell in bags. Dead birds: never throw in rivers or bushes. Use incineration, burial, or composting. Incinerators must be far from houses. Burial pits should be 1.5 m deep, covered with lime. Composting dead birds: layer with litter and straw, turn regularly. This is safe and produces fertilizer. Wastewater from cleaning: don't dump into streams. Use a soak pit or constructed wetland. In Africa, water is scarce, so recycle where possible. Reduce waste by using nipple drinkers (less spillage) and proper feed storage. Avoid overstocking to reduce waste volume. Also, consider flies and odours: keep litter dry, use fly traps, and plant aromatic herbs like lemongrass around houses. Environmental impact: broiler farming contributes to greenhouse gases (methane, nitrous oxide) from manure. Proper composting reduces emissions. Also, avoid antibiotics in feed; use alternatives. Finally, comply with local regulations. Many African countries have laws on waste disposal. Check with extension officers. A clean farm is a profitable farm. Sell compost as organic fertilizer; it's in demand. Use dead birds for biogas? Possible but requires investment. Start simple: compost and sell. This turns waste into wealth.
