1. HACCP basics for small food processors

HACCP stands for Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points. It is a food safety system that prevents problems before they happen. As a small processor in Africa, you can implement HACCP without expensive consultants. Start by identifying where things can go wrong in your process. For example, if you dry maize on the ground, birds and rodents can contaminate it. That is a hazard. List all steps from receiving raw materials to shipping. For each step, ask: what could make the food unsafe? Common hazards include bacteria like Salmonella, chemicals like aflatoxins, and physical objects like stones.

Next, find the critical control points (CCPs). These are steps where you must take action to prevent or eliminate a hazard. For a small fruit juice processor, pasteurization is a CCP. You must heat the juice to 72C for 15 seconds to kill E. coli and other pathogens. For a groundnut processor, roasting at 160C for 15 minutes can reduce aflatoxins. Set a critical limit: the minimum or maximum value that keeps food safe. For pasteurization, the limit is 72C for 15 seconds. If the temperature drops below that, you must stop and reheat.

Monitor each CCP. This means checking the temperature or time regularly. Use a simple thermometer that you calibrate monthly with ice water (0C) and boiling water (100C). Record the results in a logbook. If you find a deviation, take corrective action: reheat the juice, or discard the batch if it cannot be fixed. Keep records for at least two years; buyers and auditors will ask for them.

Finally, verify your HACCP plan works. Test your final product for bacteria like E. coli and Salmonella. Send samples to a lab every three months. If results show contamination, adjust your CCPs. Also, train your workers. They must know why handwashing after using the toilet is critical. A simple sign in the local language at the toilet exit helps. HACCP is not paperwork for its own sake; it is a practical way to keep your export business alive. Start with one product line, then expand.

2. Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) on a budget

Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) are the basic conditions and activities needed to produce safe food. You do not need a fancy factory. You need discipline and cheap solutions. Start with your building. Walls should be smooth and washable. If you have rough mud walls, cover them with plastic sheeting or cement plaster. Floors must be sloped to a drain so water does not pool. Fix leaks immediately; stagnant water breeds bacteria. Keep doors and windows screened to keep out flies and rodents. A screen costs less than $20 at a local market.

Personal hygiene is free. Workers must wash hands with soap and water before handling food, after using the toilet, and after touching garbage. Provide a handwashing station with a bucket, tap, and soap. No towel needed; air dry or use single-use paper. Workers with cuts or diarrhea must not handle food. Provide clean aprons and hairnets. If hairnets are expensive, use clean caps or scarves. No jewelry, no eating, no smoking in production areas.

Equipment and utensils must be cleaned and sanitized. Use a three-compartment sink: wash with detergent, rinse with clean water, then sanitize with a bleach solution (1 tablespoon of bleach per gallon of water). Soak utensils for 1 minute, then air dry. For cutting boards, use plastic, not wood, because wood harbors bacteria. Clean floors and walls daily. Keep a cleaning schedule posted on the wall. Assign one person per area.

Pest control is critical. Store raw materials and finished products off the floor on pallets. Keep garbage in covered bins and empty daily. Use traps for rats and flies. Do not use poison near food. If you see cockroaches, call a pest control company, but first seal cracks. Water quality matters: use potable water. If you use well water, test it for E. coli every six months. Boil or chlorinate if needed. These GMP steps cost little but prevent costly rejections at the port.

3. Controlling aflatoxins in groundnuts and maize

Aflatoxins are poisons produced by molds (Aspergillus flavus and A. parasiticus) that grow on groundnuts, maize, and other crops. They cause liver cancer and are strictly regulated. The European Union allows only 2 parts per billion (ppb) of aflatoxin B1 in groundnuts for direct human consumption, and 4 ppb total aflatoxins. For maize, the limit is 5 ppb for B1 and 10 ppb total. Many African exports are rejected because of aflatoxins. You can prevent this.

Start in the field. Use good agricultural practices: plant early, irrigate properly, and control insects. Drought stress increases aflatoxin risk. Harvest when crops are mature but before they over-dry. For groundnuts, harvest when 75% of pods have dark veins inside. Do not leave harvested crops on the ground. Dry on clean tarps or raised platforms, not on bare soil. Dry to safe moisture: groundnuts to 8-10%, maize to 13-14%. Use a moisture meter ($50-$100). If you cannot afford one, use the salt test: mix a handful of groundnuts with dry salt in a jar; if salt sticks to the nuts, they are too wet.

During storage, keep moisture low. Store in clean, dry jute bags on pallets. Keep the store ventilated and cool. Do not mix new and old crops. Inspect weekly for mold, insects, and moisture. If you see green or yellow mold, remove and destroy affected kernels. Never blend moldy with good crops; that spreads aflatoxins. For maize, you can use a simple hand sorter to remove discolored kernels. For groundnuts, hand-sort to remove shriveled, discolored, or moldy nuts.

Testing is essential. Buy a rapid test kit like AgraStrip or RidaQuick. They cost about $5 per test. Test each lot before shipping. If aflatoxin is above 4 ppb for groundnuts or 10 ppb for maize, do not export. You can also use biocontrol like Aflasafe, which is a natural product that reduces aflatoxin by 80-90% when applied in the field. Aflasafe is available in several African countries. Finally, keep records of your drying, storage, and testing. Buyers want proof.

4. Pesticide residue limits: what the EU really checks

The European Union sets Maximum Residue Limits (MRLs) for pesticides on food. If your export exceeds the MRL, it is rejected. MRLs are in milligrams per kilogram (mg/kg), also called parts per million (ppm). For example, the MRL for chlorpyrifos on groundnuts is 0.01 mg/kg. For maize, it is 0.05 mg/kg. These are very low. Many African farmers use pesticides that are banned in the EU, like chlorpyrifos and dimethoate. Even if you use a legal pesticide, you must follow the pre-harvest interval (PHI): the number of days between the last spray and harvest. For example, if the label says PHI is 14 days, you must not harvest for 14 days after spraying.

The EU checks pesticide residues by taking a sample of your shipment at the port of entry. They test for over 500 different pesticides. If they find one above the MRL, they notify the Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed (RASFF). Your name and shipment are published. That can destroy your reputation. To avoid this, keep a spray diary: record the date, pesticide name, dose, and PHI. Use only pesticides registered in your country and allowed in the EU. Check the EU Pesticides Database online. If you cannot access the internet, ask your extension officer for a list.

Use integrated pest management (IPM) to reduce pesticide use. Scout your field weekly. Use pheromone traps for moths. Plant resistant varieties. Encourage natural enemies like ladybugs. If you must spray, use the correct dose. Do not mix pesticides unless the label says so. Wear protective clothing. After spraying, triple-rinse the empty container and puncture it so it cannot be reused. Never spray near harvest. For groundnuts, stop all spraying 21 days before harvest. For maize, stop 30 days before harvest.

If you are a smallholder, join a farmer group that can afford a shared sprayer and training. Some exporters provide lists of approved pesticides. Also, consider organic production. Organic exports have zero pesticide residue limits, but you must be certified. That takes three years of no synthetic pesticides. Start with a small plot. Finally, test your crop for residues before shipping. Labs in Kenya, Ghana, and Nigeria offer this for $50-$100 per sample. It is cheaper than a rejected container.

5. Traceability: from farm to shipping container

Traceability means you can follow a food product from the farm to the consumer. If a buyer finds a problem, you must quickly identify the source. The EU requires traceability for all food imports. Without it, your shipment can be rejected. You do not need a computer. A simple paper system works. Start by assigning a unique code to each farmer or field. For example, use the farmer's initials and a number: JD-001. When you receive their harvest, write that code on the bag. Keep a logbook with the date, farmer code, weight, and any test results.

During processing, keep batches separate. If you mix groundnuts from different farmers, you lose traceability. Instead, process one farmer's lot at a time. If you must mix, record the codes of all farmers in that batch. Assign a batch number to your finished product, like BATCH-2025-03-15. Write this number on every bag or carton. In your logbook, link the batch number to the farmer codes. Also record the processing date, machine settings, and operator name. For example, if you roast at 160C for 15 minutes, write that down.

When you ship, create a shipping container code. For example, CONT-2025-04-01. Record which batches went into that container. If a buyer in Europe finds aflatoxin in one bag, you can trace it back to the batch and then to the farmer. That farmer's other bags can be recalled. Without traceability, you might have to recall the entire container, costing thousands of dollars. Keep all records for at least two years. Store them in a dry place. Use a simple filing cabinet or a plastic box.

Train your workers to fill in forms correctly. Use a checklist for each step. For example, when receiving raw materials, check: farmer code, weight, moisture, and visible mold. Sign and date. When shipping, check: batch numbers, container code, and destination. If you use a smartphone, you can take photos of forms and store them in Google Drive. But paper is fine. The key is consistency. Auditors will ask to see your records. If you cannot show them, you fail. So start today, even with a notebook. It is the cheapest insurance for your export business.

6. Preparing for a food safety audit

A food safety audit is an inspection by a buyer or certification body to check if you follow food safety standards. It can be scary, but with preparation, you can pass. First, know what the auditor will ask for. They will want to see your HACCP plan, GMP records, traceability logs, and test results. They will walk through your facility, look at your equipment, and interview workers. They may take samples. The audit usually takes 1-2 days. You will get a report with non-conformities (problems) that you must fix.

Start preparing at least one month before. Do a self-audit using the same checklist the auditor will use. For example, if you are audited against GlobalG.A.P. or BRC, download their checklist. Walk through your process and score yourself. Common non-conformities include: no handwashing station, missing pest control records, dirty equipment, and no calibration for thermometers. Fix these before the auditor arrives. Clean everything: floors, walls, ceilings, equipment, and storage areas. Remove all clutter. Make sure you have soap, paper towels, and bleach.

Organize your documents. Put them in a binder with tabs: HACCP plan, GMP records, cleaning schedules, pest control records, training records, traceability logs, and lab test results. Make sure they are up to date. For example, if you calibrate your thermometer monthly, the last calibration should be within the last month. If you test for aflatoxin, have results for each lot. Train your workers on what to say. They should know basic food safety: wash hands, no jewelry, report illness. Practice a mock audit with a friend playing the auditor. Ask them to ask tough questions.

On audit day, be honest. If you do not know an answer, say you will find out. Do not argue. Take notes on any non-conformities. After the audit, create a corrective action plan. For example, if the auditor says your floor drain is clogged, fix it within a week and send a photo as evidence. Most auditors allow 30 days to fix minor issues. If you fix everything, you get certified. Certification opens doors to high-value markets. It is worth the effort. Remember, the auditor is not your enemy; they help you improve. Many African exporters fail their first audit, but pass the second after fixing problems. So do not give up.

<h2>Practical Reference: Food Safety Standards for African Agro-Exporters</h2>

<p>This module gives small and medium African agro-exporters a working reference for the food safety standards that determine whether a shipment is accepted or rejected at destination. The focus is commercial: every standard here exists because a buyer, a border inspector or a retailer will check it. Getting these right protects revenue, opens premium markets and reduces the cost of rejected consignments.</p>

<p>African exporters face a specific reality. Most small processors operate with limited capital, unreliable cold chains and fragmented supply from many smallholder farms. The standards below are therefore presented with cost-conscious, practical implementation in mind. The goal is not certification for its own sake but access to markets such as the EU, the Gulf, the UK and increasingly intra-African markets under the AfCFTA.</p>

<h3>1. HACCP basics for small food processors</h3>
<p>HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) is the internationally recognised system for identifying and controlling food safety hazards. For a small African processor, the practical version has five steps: map your process from raw material to dispatch; identify where biological, chemical or physical hazards can enter; decide which steps are Critical Control Points (CCPs); set limits and monitor them; and keep records that prove you did it.</p>
<p>Typical CCPs in African agro-processing include roasting temperature and time for groundnuts, moisture content at drying, metal detection before packing, and pasteurisation or sterilisation for value-added products. Records matter more than paperwork volume. A simple daily logbook signed by the line supervisor is acceptable to most buyers if it is consistent and honest.</p>

<h3>2. Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) on a budget</h3>
<p>GMP covers the physical environment, personnel and operations. Low-cost priorities for African processors are: separate raw and finished product areas; potable water with a tested source; handwashing stations with soap; pest control through screening and trapping rather than chemicals; and cleanable surfaces. Many of these can be achieved with locally fabricated stainless or food-grade plastic equipment rather than imported lines.</p>
<p>Worker health and hygiene training is the cheapest high-impact intervention. A one-day training on handwashing, clean clothing and reporting illness typically reduces contamination risk more than any single piece of equipment.</p>

<h3>3. Controlling aflatoxins in groundnuts and maize</h3>
<p>Aflatoxins are the single biggest food safety barrier for African groundnut and maize exporters. They are produced by <em>Aspergillus flavus</em> and <em>Aspergillus parasiticus</em>, especially under drought stress, delayed harvest, poor drying and humid storage. The EU applies some of the strictest limits globally: 2 g/kg for aflatoxin B1 and 4 g/kg total aflatoxins in groundnuts for direct human consumption, with stricter limits for processed products.</p>
<p>Control is a chain of practices, not a single test: plant improved varieties, harvest at maturity, dry to below 10% moisture within 48 hours, store on pallets in ventilated dry conditions, and sort out discoloured or mouldy kernels. Aflatoxin testing at intake and before shipment is essential. Rapid test kits are affordable and can be used at buying centres, with confirmatory laboratory testing for export lots.</p>

<h3>4. Pesticide residue limits: what the EU really checks</h3>
<p>The EU sets Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) under Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. For many active substances used in Africa, the EU MRL is set at the limit of determination (LOD), effectively a default of 0.01 mg/kg unless a specific MRL exists. This means a product can be rejected even when the residue is below the level that would harm a consumer, because the EU has not approved that substance for that crop.</p>
<p>African exporters must therefore check the EU pesticide database before using any product. Common problem substances include chlorpyrifos, dimethoate and monocrotophos. The practical response is to shift to approved alternatives, respect pre-harvest intervals strictly, and test representative samples at an accredited laboratory before shipment.</p>

<h3>5. Traceability: from farm to shipping container</h3>
<p>Traceability is a legal requirement for EU food imports under Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 and is increasingly required by UK, Gulf and African retail buyers. For African exporters, a workable system links each lot to a farm or buying centre, a processing date and a container number. One lot should be traceable within four hours.</p>
<p>Low-cost tools include sequential lot codes, a paper register at intake and a simple spreadsheet or mobile app for container dispatch. The key is consistency: every bag, crate or container must carry the code that links it back to its source.</p>

<h3>6. Preparing for a food safety audit</h3>
<p>Buyers and certification bodies conduct audits against schemes such as GlobalG.A.P., BRCGS, FSSC 22000 or buyer-specific standards. Preparation follows a predictable pattern: document your procedures, implement them for at least three months, run an internal audit, correct findings, then book the external audit. The most common failures in African processing are missing records, inconsistent monitoring and inadequate pest control evidence.</p>
