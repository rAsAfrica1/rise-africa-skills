1. Short-Day, Intermediate, and Long-Day Varieties

Onion varieties are grouped by how many hours of daylight they need to form bulbs. This is called photoperiod. In Africa, most countries lie between the equator and 35S or 35N, so day length is fairly consistent year-round. Short-day varieties need 1012 hours of daylight to bulb. They are best for regions like Senegal, Mali, Sudan, and northern Nigeria where days are shorter. Intermediate varieties need 1214 hours and suit places like Kenya, Tanzania, and Zambia. Long-day varieties need 1416 hours and are only for far southern Africa like South Africa's Western Cape in summer. If you plant a long-day variety in a short-day region, it will grow leaves but never form a bulb. If you plant a short-day variety in a long-day region, it may bulb too early and stay small. So always match variety to your latitude. Ask your local agricultural extension officer for the day length in your area. A simple rule: if you are within 10 degrees of the equator, choose short-day types. Between 10 and 20 degrees, choose intermediate. Beyond 20 degrees, you can try long-day in summer. Examples: 'Red Creole' is short-day, 'Texas Grano 502' is intermediate, 'Yellow Globe' is long-day. Plant at the right time too: short-day onions should be planted in cool months so they bulb before hot weather. In Kenya, plant short-day varieties in AprilMay. In Nigeria, plant in OctoberNovember. Always check with local farmers what works.

2. Hybrid vs. Open-Pollinated Varieties

Hybrid onion seeds are produced by crossing two parent lines. They give uniform plants, higher yields, and better disease resistance, but you cannot save seed from themthe next generation will be mixed and poor. Hybrid seeds cost more: a 100g packet may cost $15$30, while open-pollinated (OP) seeds cost $5$10. Open-pollinated varieties are stable, so you can save seeds from your best bulbs and replant. They are cheaper and good for local markets, but yields may be 2030% lower than hybrids. For African smallholders, OP varieties like 'Red Creole' and 'Bombay Red' are popular because they are hardy and seed can be saved. Hybrids like 'Sivan' or 'Candy' give bigger bulbs but require good management: fertilizer, water, and pest control. If you are a beginner, start with OP varieties. If you sell to supermarkets or export, hybrids give the uniform size and color buyers want. To save OP seed, select the best 1020 bulbs at harvest, store them until the next season, then replant them in isolation from other onion varieties. Let them flower and produce seed. Collect seed when the heads dry and turn brown. Store in a cool, dry place. Never save seed from hybridsyou will waste time and money. Also, buy certified seed from reputable suppliers to avoid mixing. In many African countries, local seed companies like Seed Co, East-West Seed, and Kenya Seed Company sell both types. Check the label for 'F1 hybrid' or 'OP'.

3. Disease-Resistant and Storage Varieties

Onions suffer from diseases like purple blotch, downy mildew, and neck rot. Choosing resistant varieties reduces losses without chemicals. For purple blotch, look for 'Red Creole' and 'Texas Grano 502' which have some tolerance. For downy mildew, 'Candy' and 'Sivan' are resistant. In East Africa, 'Bombay Red' resists thrips and purple blotch. In West Africa, 'Violet de Galmi' is popular for its resistance to downy mildew. Storage ability is also key. Onions that store well have thick, dry skins and high dry matter. 'Red Creole' stores 34 months. 'Yellow Globe' stores 56 months. 'Copra' and 'Hytech' are hybrids that store 68 months. To store onions, cure them first: spread bulbs in a dry, shaded area for 1014 days until necks are tight and skins rustle. Then store in a cool, dark, well-ventilated place. Ideal temperature is 05C, but in Africa, use a ventilated shed or raised platform. Avoid piling onions more than 30 cm deep. Check weekly and remove rotten bulbs. If you plan to sell over several months, choose long-storage varieties. If you sell immediately after harvest, disease resistance matters more. Always buy seed labeled with resistance info. Ask your extension officer for the latest resistant varieties in your area. Some varieties like 'Red Creole' are dual-purpose: resistant and store well. But no variety is immune to all diseases, so rotate crops with maize or beans for at least 2 years.

4. Choosing Varieties for Your Climate

Your climate determines which onion variety will thrive. Onions grow best in mild temperatures (1525C) with plenty of sun. In hot, dry areas like northern Nigeria or Senegal, choose short-day varieties that mature fast, such as 'Red Creole' or 'Bombay Red'. They bulb in 90110 days. In cooler highlands like Kenya's Rift Valley or Ethiopia, intermediate varieties like 'Texas Grano 502' work well, maturing in 110130 days. In Mediterranean climates like South Africa's Western Cape, long-day varieties like 'Yellow Globe' are best. Rainfall matters too. Onions hate waterlogged soil. If your area gets heavy rain, plant on raised beds 2030 cm high. In dry areas, you need irrigation. Choose varieties that tolerate heat: 'Red Creole' and 'Violet de Galmi' are good. For cold areas, 'Yellow Globe' and 'Copra' tolerate frost. Also consider your soil. Onions prefer sandy loam with pH 6.06.8. If your soil is heavy clay, add compost and plant on ridges. Test your soil with a simple kit from an agro-dealer. Altitude matters: above 1500 m, choose intermediate or long-day types. Below 1000 m, choose short-day. Talk to nearby farmers about what works. Visit a local market and see which onions sell bestred, white, or yellow. Then choose a variety that matches both your climate and market demand. Always plant a small trial plot of a new variety before planting your whole field.

5. Seed Quality and Germination Testing

Good seed is the foundation of a good onion crop. Always buy certified seed from a reputable agro-dealer. Check the label for: variety name, lot number, germination percentage, and expiry date. Onion seed loses viability quicklygermination drops by 10% every year if stored poorly. Never use seed older than 2 years. To test germination at home, do this: count 100 seeds. Soak a clean cloth in water and wring it out. Place seeds evenly on the cloth, fold it, and put it in a plastic bag. Keep it in a warm place (2025C) for 1014 days. Keep the cloth moist but not dripping. After 14 days, count how many seeds have sprouted. If 85 or more sprouted, the seed is good. If 7084, use more seed per plot (increase seeding rate by 20%). If below 70, buy new seed. You can also do a float test: put seeds in a glass of water. Viable seeds sink; dead seeds float. But this is less reliable. Store your seed in an airtight container with a desiccant like silica gel or dry rice. Keep it in a cool, dark place. Never store seed in a plastic bag in the sun. For planting, use 45 kg of seed per hectare if germination is above 85%. If germination is lower, increase to 67 kg. Always sow fresh seed each season for best results.

6. Understanding Seed Catalogs and Suppliers

Seed catalogs list varieties with details like maturity days, disease resistance, and storage life. But many catalogs are written for large farms in Europe or America. As an African smallholder, you need to read between the lines. Look for varieties that match your day length and climate. Ignore terms like 'long-day' if you are near the equator. Check the 'days to maturity'this is from transplanting, not sowing. In Africa, add 1014 days for nursery time. Also check 'bolting resistance'onions that bolt (flower early) produce small bulbs. Choose varieties with good bolting resistance for your planting season. When buying from local suppliers, ask for the latest seed lot and check the germination percentage on the label. Reputable suppliers in Africa include: East-West Seed (East Africa), Seed Co (Southern Africa), Kenya Seed Company, and Technisem (West Africa). You can also order from online suppliers like Seedman or Baker Creek, but check if they ship to your country and if the variety suits your climate. Always buy from a supplier who stores seed properlynot in a hot warehouse. If you buy from a market vendor, ask to see the original packet. Avoid seed sold in unlabeled bottles or bags. Join a farmer group and buy in bulk to get discounts. Also, ask your extension officer for a list of recommended varieties for your region. Keep records of what you plant and how it performs, so you know what to buy next season.

<h2>Practical Reference: Onion Varieties and Selection for African Growers</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a working decision tool for African onion producers. Onion (<em>Allium cepa</em>) is one of the most commercially important vegetable crops in Africa, with major production in Egypt, Nigeria, Niger, Sudan, South Africa, Ethiopia, Kenya, and Tanzania. Yet yields across much of sub-Saharan Africa average 815 t/ha compared to 4060 t/ha in well-managed commercial systems. Variety choice matched to latitude, day-length, market, and storage needs is one of the lowest-cost, highest-impact decisions a grower makes. Get it wrong and no amount of fertilizer or irrigation will fix the mismatch.</p>

<p>This lesson gives you the reference material to make that decision with confidence, whether you are a smallholder selecting seed for a 0.25 ha plot or an agribusiness procuring seed for 50 ha of commercial production.</p>

<h3>Day-Length Classification at a Glance</h3>

<p>Onion bulbing is triggered by photoperiod (day length) interacting with temperature. Varieties are grouped into short-day, intermediate-day, and long-day types. In Africa, most production zones between 0 and 30 latitude use short-day varieties, while Mediterranean North Africa (Egypt, Morocco, Tunisia, Algeria) and high-altitude zones in East and Southern Africa can support intermediate or long-day types. The critical rule: <strong>a long-day variety planted in a short-day environment will produce leaves but no bulb</strong>; a short-day variety planted in a long-day environment will bulb too early and yield tiny bulbs.</p>

<h3>Hybrid vs. Open-Pollinated: The Business Case</h3>

<p>Open-pollinated (OP) varieties allow seed saving, cost less per kilogram, and are familiar to smallholders. Hybrids cost 38 more per kg but deliver 2040% higher yields, more uniform bulbs, better bolting resistance, and stronger disease tolerance. For commercial growers selling to supermarkets, processors, or exporters, hybrids are usually the profitable choice. For subsistence or low-input systems, well-adapted OPs remain viable. The break-even calculation: if hybrid seed costs an extra US$150/ha but yields an additional 5 t/ha at US$300/t, the extra revenue is US$1,500 a 10:1 return. Run this calculation for your own farm before deciding.</p>

<h3>Choosing Varieties for Your Climate: A Decision Framework</h3>

<p>Work through these four questions in order:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>What is my latitude and planting month?</strong> This determines the day-length your crop will experience during bulbing. Match it to the variety class.</li>
<li><strong>What is my target market?</strong> Fresh local market accepts red and purple OPs; supermarkets and exporters demand uniform hybrids, often with specific bulb size and skin colour.</li>
<li><strong>How long must the crop store?</strong> If you need to hold bulbs for 4+ months to capture off-season prices, choose a long-day or high-dry-matter intermediate variety with proven storage ratings.</li>
<li><strong>What diseases are endemic on my farm?</strong> If you have a history of pink root or Fusarium basal rot, prioritise resistant varieties and rotate with cereals.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Seed Quality and Germination Testing</h3>

<p>Poor seed is the silent killer of onion profits. Onion seed loses viability quickly germination can drop below 70% within 12 months in tropical storage. Before planting, run a simple germination test:</p>

<ol>
<li>Count 100 seeds at random from the lot.</li>
<li>Place on moist paper towel or clean cloth in a shallow tray.</li>
<li>Keep moist and at 2025C for 1014 days.</li>
<li>Count normal seedlings. <strong>Below 80% germination: reject or increase seeding rate proportionally.</strong></li>
</ol>

<p>For commercial plantings, buy seed with a stated germination percentage and test date. Store unused seed in airtight containers with silica gel at cool temperatures never in a hot store room.</p>

<h3>Understanding Seed Catalogs and Suppliers</h3>

<p>When reading a catalog or supplier listing, look for: variety name and type (short/intermediate/long-day), days to maturity, disease resistance codes (PR, FBR, DM), bolting resistance, recommended latitude range, germination percentage, lot number, and treatment status. Reputable suppliers in Africa include East-West Seed, Seed Co, Kenya Seed Company, Technisem, Seminis, Bejo, and Rijk Zwaan. For OP varieties, consult your national NARS (e.g. KALRO, IITA, NARO, EIAR) for locally adapted releases.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="line-height:1.7;">
<li> Confirm your latitude and planting date before choosing a day-length class.</li>
<li> Match variety to market: fresh, supermarket, export, or processing.</li>
<li> Check disease-resistance codes against your farm's history.</li>
<li> Verify storage life if you plan to hold bulbs for 3+ months.</li>
<li> Run a germination test on every new seed lot before planting.</li>
<li> Calculate hybrid vs. OP break-even using your own yield and price data.</li>
<li> Buy from a reputable supplier with lot numbers and test dates.</li>
<li> Never save seed from hybrid crops buy fresh F1 seed each season.</li>
</ul>
