1. Concrete Mix Proportions and Testing

Concrete is a mix of cement, sand, stone, and water. For farm buildings, a common mix for foundations is 1:3:5 (one part cement, three parts sand, five parts stone) by volume. For slabs and columns, use 1:2:4. For plastering, use 1:3 (cement to sand) without stone. Always measure using a consistent container like a bucket or a wooden box. Do not just guess. The water should be clean, not salty or muddy. Add water gradually until the mix is workable but not too wet. A good test is to squeeze a handful; it should hold shape without dripping.

Testing the mix: Do a slump test. Make a cone from sheet metal, 30 cm high, 10 cm top diameter, 20 cm bottom diameter. Fill it in three layers, each time tamping 25 times with a steel rod. Lift the cone carefully. The concrete should settle by about 5-10 cm for foundations, 2-5 cm for slabs. If it slumps too much, add more cement and sand. If too stiff, add a little water.

Another test is the cube test. Make cubes of 15 cm x 15 cm x 15 cm using the same mix. Cure them in water for 7 days, then send to a lab or test with a hammer. A good mix for farm buildings should reach at least 15 MPa (megapascals) after 28 days. If you cannot test, rely on the slump and your experience.

In Africa, many farmers use river sand and crushed stone. Avoid sand with too much clay or silt. Wash it if needed. Stone should be clean and between 10-20 mm. For small jobs, you can mix on a clean concrete floor or a metal sheet. Turn the dry mix three times, then add water and turn again. Use within 30 minutes of adding water. Never add water to a mix that has started to set. This will weaken the concrete.

Remember, the strength of your farm building depends on the mix. Use the right proportions, test as you go, and keep records. If you are unsure, ask a local mason or agricultural extension officer.

2. Formwork Construction for Foundations and Slabs

Formwork is the mould that holds wet concrete in place until it hardens. For farm foundations, you need timber boards, stakes, nails, and a hammer. Use boards that are straight and at least 25 mm thick. For a foundation trench, dig the trench to the required depth and width, then place boards along the sides. Hold them with wooden stakes driven into the ground every 1 metre. Nail the boards to the stakes. Make sure the top of the formwork is level. Use a spirit level or a water level (a clear plastic tube filled with water) to check.

For slabs, you need to build a box. The bottom must be firm and level. Use a layer of sand or hardcore, then a plastic sheet to stop water from escaping. Build the sides with boards, and support them with stakes. The height of the formwork should be the thickness of the slab, usually 100-150 mm for farm floors. For edges, you can use boards nailed to stakes. Make sure there are no gaps, or cement will leak out.

Before pouring concrete, oil the formwork with used engine oil or a commercial release agent. This stops the concrete from sticking and makes it easier to remove. Check that all nails are hammered in and no sharp edges are sticking out. The formwork must be strong enough to hold the weight of the concrete without bulging. For a 1 metre high wall, use boards that are at least 25 mm thick and stakes every 600 mm.

After pouring, leave the formwork in place for at least 24 hours for foundations and 48 hours for slabs. In hot weather, cover the concrete with wet sacks or plastic to stop it drying too fast. Remove the formwork carefully, using a crowbar. Do not damage the concrete edges. Clean the boards and store them for reuse. Good formwork saves money and gives a smooth finish.

In rural Africa, you can use local timber like eucalyptus or pine. Avoid termite-infested wood. If you cannot afford boards, you can use metal sheets or even strong plastic. But make sure they are rigid. Always check your formwork before pouring. A mistake here can waste a whole day's work and cement.

3. Reinforcement Placement and Tying

Reinforcement is steel bars (rebar) that give concrete strength to resist pulling and cracking. For farm buildings, common sizes are 6 mm, 8 mm, 10 mm, and 12 mm. For foundations, use 12 mm bars at the bottom. For slabs, use 8 mm or 10 mm bars in a grid. For columns, use 12 mm vertical bars and 6 mm ties. Always use deformed bars (with ribs) for better grip. Smooth bars are weaker.

Before placing, clean the bars with a wire brush to remove rust and dirt. Cut them to length using a hacksaw or bolt cutter. Bend them using a bending machine or a strong pipe. For corners, bend bars into L-shapes. The overlap (lap length) should be at least 40 times the bar diameter. For a 12 mm bar, that is 480 mm. Tie the bars together with soft iron wire (binding wire) using a hook. Twist the wire tightly so the bars do not move.

Placement: For foundations, put a 50 mm concrete cover (a layer of concrete) under the bars. Use small stones or plastic chairs to lift the bars. For slabs, place the grid in the middle or slightly towards the bottom. The spacing between bars should be 150-200 mm for farm floors. For columns, tie the vertical bars with ties every 150 mm. Make sure the bars are straight and not touching the formwork. Use a cover block (small concrete blocks) to keep the bars in place.

In Africa, rebar can be expensive. You can use bamboo or timber as reinforcement for small jobs, but it is not as strong. If you use rebar, buy from a reputable supplier. Avoid rusty or bent bars. Store them off the ground and cover with a tarp. When tying, work in a team. One person holds, another ties. Use a wire twister for speed. Check that all bars are tied before pouring. A missing tie can cause the bars to move and weaken the structure.

Remember, reinforcement must be surrounded by concrete. No bar should be exposed. After pouring, inspect the surface. If you see a bar sticking out, cover it with more concrete. Proper reinforcement makes your farm building last for decades.

4. Pouring, Compacting, and Curing Concrete

Pouring concrete is a race against time. Once water is added, you have about 30-60 minutes to place it. For foundations, pour in layers of 150-200 mm. Do not pour from a height greater than 1 metre, or the mix will separate. Use a wheelbarrow or a bucket to move concrete. For slabs, start at one corner and work your way across. Use a rake or shovel to spread it evenly. Never add water to the mix after it has started to set. If it is too stiff, add a little cement and water mix.

Compacting removes air pockets. Use a poker vibrator if available. Insert it vertically into the concrete, hold for 5-10 seconds, then slowly pull out. Space the insertions about 500 mm apart. If you have no vibrator, use a steel rod or a wooden stick. Push it up and down rapidly. Also tap the sides of the formwork with a hammer. This helps the concrete settle. For slabs, use a screed board (a straight plank) to level the surface. Move it in a sawing motion. Then use a float (a flat wooden or metal tool) to smooth the surface.

Curing is keeping the concrete wet so it gains strength. After pouring, cover the concrete with wet sacks, plastic sheets, or a layer of sand. Keep it wet for at least 7 days. In hot weather, wet it twice a day. In cold weather, once a day. Do not let it dry out. If you skip curing, the concrete will crack and be weak. For farm floors, you can also pond water on the surface. After 24 hours, you can walk on it, but not heavy loads. After 7 days, you can remove formwork and start using it for light work. After 28 days, it reaches full strength.

In Africa, water can be scarce. Use greywater or rainwater for curing. You can also use a curing compound, but it is expensive. A simple method is to cover with wet grass or banana leaves. Check daily. If the concrete looks dry, add water. Proper curing is the cheapest way to make strong concrete. Do not rush. A well-cured farm floor will last 20 years or more.

5. Brick and Block Laying Techniques

Bricks and blocks are the walls of your farm building. Common types are clay bricks, concrete blocks, and stabilized soil blocks. For farm buildings, use blocks that are at least 150 mm thick for load-bearing walls. Before laying, soak clay bricks in water for a few minutes. Do not soak concrete blocks. Mix mortar: 1 part cement to 4 parts sand for blocks, 1:5 for bricks. Add water to make a smooth paste. It should be workable but not runny.

Start at the corners. Build a corner lead about 4-5 courses high. Use a spirit level to check that it is plumb (vertical) and level. Stretch a string line between corners to guide the courses. Lay the first course on a full bed of mortar. For subsequent courses, spread mortar with a trowel. Place the block, then tap it down with the trowel handle. Check level and plumb. Remove excess mortar with the trowel. The mortar joint should be 10-12 mm thick. Do not make joints too thick, or the wall will be weak.

For strength, stagger the blocks (running bond). The vertical joint in one course should be in the middle of the block below. This is called a half-bond. For corners, use a whole block or cut one with a bolster chisel and hammer. For openings (doors and windows), leave a gap and build a lintel later. You can use a piece of timber or a concrete lintel. Reinforce every fourth course with wire mesh or a thin steel bar. This is called a bond beam. It prevents cracks.

In Africa, many farmers make their own blocks using a block-making machine. You can also buy from local suppliers. Always check that blocks are cured for at least 14 days before use. If you lay blocks in hot weather, wet them lightly. Work in a team: one person mixes mortar, one carries blocks, one lays. Keep the wall straight by checking with a plumb bob and level every course. After finishing for the day, cover the top of the wall with a plastic sheet to protect from rain. Proper bricklaying gives you a strong, straight wall that will hold your roof.

6. Plastering and Rendering of Walls

Plastering is covering walls with a cement-sand mix to give a smooth, durable finish. Rendering is the same but on external walls. For farm buildings, use a mix of 1 part cement to 3 parts sand for internal plaster, and 1:4 for external render. Use clean sand, not too fine. Sieve it to remove stones. Add water to make a thick paste. It should stick to the trowel without dripping.

Before plastering, prepare the wall. Remove dust and loose mortar. Wet the wall with water so it does not suck moisture from the plaster. For smooth concrete walls, make the surface rough with a hammer and chisel, or apply a bonding agent. For block walls, just wet them. Start at the bottom and work up. Use a trowel to throw the plaster onto the wall. Then use a float to spread it evenly. The first coat (scratch coat) should be 5-10 mm thick. Let it dry for 24 hours. Then apply the second coat (finish coat) of 5-10 mm. Total thickness should be 15-20 mm.

For a smooth finish, use a wooden float in a circular motion. Then use a steel trowel to polish. For a textured finish, use a sponge or a broom. Check that the plaster is level and plumb using a straight edge. Fill any cracks with more plaster. Corners should be sharp. Use a corner trowel. After plastering, cure the wall by wetting it gently for 3 days. Do not let it dry too fast, or it will crack.

In Africa, plastering is often done with a mix of cement and sand, but you can also use cow dung and clay for traditional buildings. However, for farm buildings that need to be strong, cement plaster is best. If you are on a budget, you can plaster only the inside and leave the outside rendered. Always wear gloves and a mask when mixing cement. Keep the work area clean. A well-plastered wall is easy to clean and protects the wall from rain and insects. It also looks professional.

<h2>Practical Reference: Concrete and Masonry Work for African Farm Buildings</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core trade knowledge you need to supervise or carry out concrete and masonry work on farm buildings across Africa. It is written for the person who must get a store, dairy, poultry house, grain shed, or livestock pen built to last through heat, termites, seasonal rains, and heavy use while controlling cost. Every figure and table here is drawn from published standards and field guidance, not guesswork.</p>

<p>Farm buildings fail for predictable reasons: weak concrete mixes, badly built formwork that bulges or collapses, reinforcement placed with too little cover, concrete poured and left to dry in the sun, and block walls laid without proper bond or mortar. Each of those failures costs money twice once to build, once to repair. The reference below is organised so you can check your work at each stage.</p>

<p><strong>Water-to-cement ratio.</strong> A practical field rule is that the water should be no more than about half the weight of the cement (w/c 0.5). <p><strong>Testing on site.</strong> Full laboratory cube testing is rarely available on a farm, but you can and should do two simple checks:</p>
<ul>

<li><strong>Cube samples:</strong> Cast at least three 100 mm or 150 mm cubes per pour, cure them under damp conditions, and send them to a local testing lab at 7 and 28 days. This is the only way to know the actual strength of what you placed. Many national standards bodies and universities offer low-cost cube testing.</li>
</ul>

<h3>2. Formwork for Foundations and Slabs</h3>

<p>Formwork holds concrete in shape until it sets. On farm sites, timber formwork is the norm. The two failure modes are <strong>bulging</strong> (insufficient bracing) and <strong>blowout</strong> (a panel gives way under wet concrete pressure). Both waste concrete and create unsafe conditions.</p>

<p>Key rules for farm formwork:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use straight, sound timber. Reject boards with large knots, splits, or rot they fail under load.</li>
<li>Support vertical faces with stakes at no more than 600900 mm centres, driven firmly into the ground.</li>
<li>Wale (horizontal) members should be fixed with bolts or heavy screws, not nails alone, where concrete depth exceeds about 600 mm.</li>
<li>Coat the face with a release agent (used engine oil is common but check local environmental rules; proprietary mould oil is better).</li>
<li>For slabs on grade, the "formwork" is often just edge boards; ensure they are level and securely pegged so the slab edge stays straight.</li>
<li>Strip formwork only when concrete has gained enough strength typically 2448 hours for vertical faces in warm African conditions, longer for cold or damp weather. Never strip load-bearing formwork before 7 days without engineering advice.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. Pouring, Compacting, and Curing</h3>

<p>Pour concrete as close as possible to its final position. Dropping it from height causes segregation the heavy aggregate separates from the mortar. Use a chute, a bucket, or a wheelbarrow, and keep the drop under about 1.5 m.</p>

<p><strong>Compacting.</strong> Every pour must be compacted to remove air pockets. Use a poker vibrator where available. On small farm jobs, rodding with a steel bar and tapping the formwork with a hammer is acceptable for thin sections. Compact in layers of 150300 mm. Do not over-vibrate it causes segregation.</p>

<p><strong>Curing.</strong> This is the single most neglected step on African farms, and it is the one that most affects strength and durability. Concrete does not "dry" to gain strength; it <em>cures</em> by retaining moisture. In hot, dry, or windy conditions, concrete can lose moisture faster than it gains strength, leading to surface cracking, dusting, and reduced durability.</p>

<ul>
<li>Start curing as soon as the surface can take it without damage usually within a few hours.</li>
<li>Keep the surface continuously damp for at least 7 days (1014 days for structural or watertight work).</li>
<li>Methods: ponding, wet hessian or burlap, plastic sheeting, or a curing compound. In hot climates, shade and windbreaks help.</li>
<li>Do not let concrete freeze (rare in most of Africa but possible in highland areas) and do not let it dry out.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Brick and Block Laying Techniques</h3>

<p>Masonry walls carry roof loads and resist wind and rain. The two essentials are <strong>bond</strong> (how units overlap) and <strong>mortar</strong> (the glue).</p>

<p><strong>Mortar mixes for masonry:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>1 : 3</strong> (cement : sand) strong mortar for foundations, retaining walls, and below damp-proof course. Use where high strength is needed.</li>
<li><strong>1 : 4 to 1 : 5</strong> general walling mortar for most farm buildings. Good balance of strength and workability.</li>
<li><strong>1 : 6</strong> weak mortar for internal non-load-bearing walls or where movement is expected. Do not use for structural walls.</li>
<li>Add lime (1 part lime to 68 parts sand, replacing some cement) to improve workability and reduce cracking in hot climates, if available.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Bond patterns.</strong> Stretcher bond (all units laid lengthwise) is fine for single-skin walls up to about 2.4 m high. For taller or load-bearing walls, use English bond (alternating courses of headers and stretchers) or Flemish bond. Always break vertical joints by at least a quarter of a unit length never let joints line up continuously, as this creates a crack path.</p>

<p><strong>Workmanship checks:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Use a spirit level and plumb line on every course. Check level at least every 3 courses.</li>
<li>Fill vertical joints fully voids let water in and weaken the wall.</li>
<li>Keep mortar joints 1012 mm thick. Thicker joints shrink and crack.</li>
<li>Wet clay bricks before laying in hot weather so they do not suck moisture from the mortar. Concrete blocks generally need less pre-wetting but should be clean and dust-free.</li>
<li>Do not build more than about 1.5 m of wall height in a day fresh mortar cannot carry the load.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Plastering and Rendering of Walls</h3>

<p>Plaster (internal) and render (external) protect masonry from rain, wind, and abrasion, and provide a cleanable surface for dairies, poultry houses, and food stores.</p>

<p><strong>Mix:</strong> A common farm mix is 1 : 4 to 1 : 5 cement : sand for the base coat, and 1 : 4 for the finish coat. For external render in wet areas, use 1 : 3 to 1 : 4 and consider adding a waterproofer. For internal plaster in dry areas, 1 : 5 to 1 : 6 is adequate.</p>

<p><strong>Application:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Prepare the wall: brush off dust, wet clay bricks, and fix any loose blocks. For smooth concrete surfaces, apply a bonding agent or a spatter dash coat.</li>
<li>Apply the base coat (scratch coat) 1015 mm thick, and scratch the surface with a nail float or wire brush to give the finish coat a key.</li>
<li>Let the base coat cure for at least 24 hours (dampen it if hot).</li>
<li>Apply the finish coat 58 mm thick. Use a wooden float for a textured finish or a steel trowel for a smooth finish.</li>
<li>Cure the plaster for at least 7 days by keeping it damp. This prevents shrinkage cracks.</li>
<li>Do not plaster in direct hot sun or strong wind the mortar dries too fast. Work in shade or early morning/late afternoon.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Mix by volume, not by eye.</strong> Use gauge boxes or buckets. Keep the water-to-cement ratio at or below 0.5.</li>
<li><strong>Test workability.</strong> Do a slump test on every significant pour; target 2575 mm for slabs and foundations.</li>
<li><strong>Build formwork strong.</strong> Stake at 600900 mm centres, brace against blowout, and coat with release agent.</li>
<li><strong>Place reinforcement with cover.</strong> Use chairs and spacers; minimum cover 4050 mm for most farm elements, 75 mm in soil.</li>
<li><strong>Compact every layer.</strong> 150300 mm layers, rod or vibrate, and tap formwork to release air.</li>
<li><strong>Cure for 7 days minimum.</strong> Keep concrete and plaster continuously damp; shade and windbreak in hot, dry conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Check masonry bond and level.</strong> Break joints, fill verticals, keep 1012 mm joints, and check plumb every 3 courses.</li>
<li><strong>Wear PPE.</strong> Gloves, boots, and eye protection for concrete and rebar work every time, no exceptions.</li>
</ol>
