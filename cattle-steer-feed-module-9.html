Module 9

🐄 Advanced Ruminant Physiology and Nutrition

How the rumen actually works, and how to use that knowledge to buy less feed and get more milk and meat out of what you already have.

  • Quantify the share of feed energy lost as enteric methane in beef and dairy cattle

Lesson 9.1~12 min

Rumen Microbial Ecology

In this lesson
  • Explain why you feed the rumen microbes before you feed the animal
  • Identify the pH and temperature range in which fibre-digesting microbes work best
  • Diagnose a underfed rumen from feed intake, dung and body condition rather than from a laboratory report

A cow does not digest grass. The microbes living in her rumen digest grass, and the cow digests the microbes. That single sentence changes how you buy feed. When you put a bag of maize bran or a load of Napier grass in front of a cow, you are really feeding a fermentation vat of about 100 to 200 litres, held at a steady body temperature near 39 degrees C, containing bacteria, protozoa and fungi that between them can break the cellulose bonds that no mammal can break alone.

The microbes fall into working groups. The fibre-digesting (fibrolytic) group attacks cellulose and hemicellulose in stems and leaves. They are slow, they are the reason a cow can live on crop residues, and they are fragile: they need a rumen pH between about 6.2 and 6.8 to work well. The starch and sugar fermenters (amylolytic) attack grain, molasses and cassava. They are fast, they produce acid quickly, and they tolerate a lower pH than the fibre group does. When you tip in a lot of grain at once, the fast group multiplies, the pH drops, and the slow fibre group stops working. The cow is then digesting less of the very forage you already paid for or grew.

The microbes need three things from you: fermentable energy, a nitrogen supply, and a stable environment. Nitrogen matters more than most small producers realise. The rumen bacteria build their own body protein from rumen-degradable protein and from ammonia, and that microbial protein is the main protein supply reaching the cow's small intestine. Below roughly 7.5 percent crude protein in the diet dry matter, which is the lowest level used in the NRC 2016 beef recommendations, the microbial population is nitrogen-starved. The visible result is not a protein problem, it is an intake problem: the cow eats less, because undigested fibre sits in the rumen and physically blocks her from taking another mouthful. This is the single most common and most expensive mistake in dry-season feeding across Africa. Farmers feed maize stover at maybe 3 to 5 percent crude protein, the rumen stalls, intake collapses, and the cow loses condition on a full stomach.

Worked example. A 350 kg Boran-cross cow is offered maize stover free choice. The stover tests at 4 percent crude protein and 72 percent NDF. Her intake capacity from that NDF is DMI = 120 / 72 = 1.67 percent of body weight, so 350 x 0.0167 = 5.8 kg dry matter per day at best, and in practice less because the microbes are nitrogen-limited. The diet supplies 5.8 x 0.04 = 0.23 kg of crude protein. Now add 0.5 kg of sunflower cake at about 30 percent crude protein: that adds 0.15 kg of protein, bringing the total to 0.38 kg from 6.3 kg of dry matter, which is 6.0 percent. Add 1.0 kg of cake instead and you reach 0.23 + 0.30 = 0.53 kg protein in 6.8 kg dry matter, 7.8 percent, just over the 7.5 percent floor. That one kilogram of cake does not just add protein. It restarts fibre digestion, so the cow eats more stover, and she extracts more from every kilogram she eats. If sunflower cake costs 0.35 USD/kg, you are spending 0.35 USD a day to unlock forage you already own.

Concrete African context. In the Ethiopian highlands, farmers feeding teff straw through the long dry season report the same pattern every year: cattle standing in a full feed trough losing weight. Teff straw is bulky and low in nitrogen. The fix used by extension services is not to buy more straw, it is to add a small nitrogen source such as noug (niger seed) cake, cottonseed cake or a urea-molasses block so that the rumen population can work. The cost is small; the effect on intake is large.

Practical rules that follow from rumen ecology:

  1. Never make a big diet change in one day. Give the microbial population 2 to 3 weeks to adapt to a new feed, and keep changes between feeding groups under about 10 to 15 percent of diet dry matter at a time.
  2. Feed the same feeds at the same times daily. Pulse-feeding grain once a day swings rumen pH far harder than splitting it into two or three meals.
  3. Keep clean water in front of the animals at all times. The rumen is a water bath; restricted water cuts intake immediately.
  4. Protect the fibre floor. Forage NDF must stay at or above 15 to 16 percent of total diet dry matter, which is the absolute floor before acidosis and milk fat depression begin.
  5. If your basal forage tests below 7.5 percent crude protein, buy nitrogen before you buy energy.

When you next look at a thin cow standing next to full feed, do not ask what is wrong with the cow. Ask what is wrong with her microbes.

Lesson 9.2~12 min

Volatile Fatty Acid Production and Energy Capture

In this lesson
  • Describe how rumen fermentation converts feed into the volatile fatty acids that supply most of a cow's energy
  • Calculate the energy value of a forage from its ADF using the published TDN and net energy equations
  • Choose between forage and concentrate on the basis of cost per Mcal rather than cost per bag

When rumen microbes ferment feed they do not hand the cow sugar. They hand her volatile fatty acids, or VFAs, absorbed straight through the rumen wall. These VFAs supply the large majority of a ruminant's energy. The three that matter are acetate, propionate and butyrate. On a high-forage diet the mix leans strongly toward acetate; as you add starch it shifts toward propionate. Published proportions vary by diet and are best treated as approximate, but the direction of the shift is reliable and it is the part you can act on.

Why the shift matters commercially: propionate is the main precursor the animal uses to make glucose. Glucose drives growth, drives milk lactose and therefore milk volume, and fills the energy gap of early lactation. Acetate is the main building block for milk fat. So a diet swung hard toward starch tends to give more milk volume and faster liveweight gain but risks depressed milk fat; a diet swung hard toward coarse forage supports butterfat but limits growth rate. Neither extreme is a moral position, it is a market decision. If you are paid by the litre, chase propionate. If you are paid on butterfat, or you sell to a cheese or ghee buyer, protect acetate.

Fermentation also leaks energy as methane, which is discussed in Lesson 5, and as heat. In beef cattle roughly 6 to 8 percent of gross energy intake leaves the animal as methane; in dairy cattle roughly 5 to 7 percent. That is feed you bought and burned.

You do not need a laboratory to put numbers on this. Two published equations do most of the work. First, estimate TDN from acid detergent fibre: TDN% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%). Then convert TDN to net energy, using NEL Mcal/kg = (0.0245 x TDN%) - 0.12 for lactating dairy, or NEg Mcal/kg = (0.0257 x TDN%) - 0.315 for growing beef.

Worked example, two forages side by side. A leafy Rhodes grass hay is tested at 33 percent ADF. TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 33) = 88.9 - 25.7 = 63.2 percent. NEL = (0.0245 x 63.2) - 0.12 = 1.548 - 0.12 = 1.43 Mcal/kg. A stemmy, late-cut Rhodes hay from the same field tests at 42 percent ADF. TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 42) = 88.9 - 32.7 = 56.2 percent. NEL = (0.0245 x 56.2) - 0.12 = 1.377 - 0.12 = 1.26 Mcal/kg. The two bales look similar and are sold at the same price, but the leafy bale delivers 1.43 Mcal per kg dry matter against 1.26, about 13 percent more energy. If you feed 8 kg dry matter of hay a day, that is 11.4 Mcal versus 10.1 Mcal, a gap of 1.3 Mcal, which is roughly the energy in half a kilo of dairy meal.

Now cost it. Suppose that hay costs 0.12 USD per kg as-fed at 88 percent dry matter, so 0.136 USD per kg DM. The leafy bale gives energy at 0.136 / 1.43 = 0.095 USD per Mcal. The stemmy bale gives 0.136 / 1.26 = 0.108 USD per Mcal. Compare with a dairy meal at 0.40 USD per kg as-fed, roughly 0.45 USD per kg DM, supplying around 1.7 Mcal NEL/kg: that is 0.45 / 1.7 = 0.26 USD per Mcal. Energy from good hay is roughly a third the price of energy from the bag. That single comparison is the most profitable calculation in this course, and most farmers have never done it.

Concrete African context. A dairy farmer in Nyeri, Kenya, with six crossbred cows was buying 90 kg of dairy meal a week at 0.42 USD/kg, about 38 USD a week, while feeding over-mature Napier cut at 2 metres. Napier cut at 2 metres is stemmy and high in ADF; cut at around 1 metre it is markedly more digestible. Shifting the cutting height, which costs nothing but discipline, raised the energy density of the basal forage and let him cut roughly 20 kg a week off the meal bill, about 8 USD a week or 416 USD a year, from six cows.

Practical points to carry away:

  1. Energy is the most expensive nutrient you buy. Always price it per Mcal, never per bag.
  2. Forage maturity is a bigger lever on energy than most supplement decisions.
  3. Split concentrate into at least two feeds a day to steady the acetate-propionate balance and protect butterfat.
  4. Sudden starch loading raises propionate fast and pH falls with it. Ramp up over 2 to 3 weeks.

Lesson 9.3~12 min

Protein Degradation and Synthesis in Rumen

In this lesson
  • Distinguish rumen-degradable protein from rumen-undegradable protein and match each to the right class of animal
  • Calculate the RDP and RUP supplied by a simple on-farm ration
  • Decide when bypass protein is worth buying and when it is money wasted

Protein for a cow arrives in two forms with two completely different jobs. Rumen-degradable protein, RDP, is broken down in the rumen and rebuilt into microbial protein. Rumen-undegradable protein, RUP or bypass protein, escapes rumen fermentation and is digested further down in the abomasum and small intestine, delivering amino acids directly to the animal. Getting the balance wrong in either direction costs money: too little RDP and the microbes stall, so forage digestion falls; too little RUP in a high-producing cow and she cannot make the milk her genetics allow, no matter how much crude protein is in the bag.

The published targets are clear. Growing and finishing beef cattle should be at roughly 60 to 70 percent RDP and 30 to 40 percent RUP of total crude protein. Low-producing dairy cows, under about 40 lb (roughly 18 kg) of milk a day, use 65 to 70 percent RDP effectively because microbial protein alone covers their needs. High-producing dairy cows above about 60 lb (roughly 27 kg) a day need the balance to shift toward RUP, sometimes to 50:50 or even 45:55, with an RUP requirement estimated at 7.2 to 7.5 percent of diet dry matter.

For most African smallholder dairy herds this is genuinely good news. A cow giving 8 to 15 litres a day does not need expensive bypass protein. Her microbes can do the job if you give them enough degradable nitrogen and fermentable energy. Bypass protein supplements only start paying when a cow's yield is high enough that microbial protein alone cannot keep up. Selling a smallholder an imported bypass product for a 10-litre cow is a transfer of money, not a nutrition strategy.

Feed sources differ sharply. Fresh forage and legumes are high in RDP. Soybean meal runs about 35 to 40 percent RUP of its crude protein; alfalfa haylage about 40 to 45 percent; heat-treated products such as blood meal and fish meal run 70 to 90 percent RUP. Note the mechanism: heat treatment is what creates bypass value. That is why an over-heated, scorched cake can be high in RUP but low in digestibility, and why gently toasted material is the useful kind.

Worked example. A 450 kg crossbred cow giving 12 litres a day is fed 9 kg dry matter of Napier grass at 9 percent crude protein and 2 kg dry matter of a home mix that is 1.4 kg maize bran at 11 percent CP and 0.6 kg cottonseed cake at 38 percent CP. Protein contributions: Napier 9 x 0.09 = 0.81 kg; maize bran 1.4 x 0.11 = 0.15 kg; cottonseed cake 0.6 x 0.38 = 0.23 kg. Total crude protein = 1.19 kg from 11 kg dry matter, which is 10.8 percent of diet DM. For a 12-litre cow that is a reasonable working level, and because Napier and bran are largely degradable, the RDP share is comfortably above 60 percent. This cow does not need a bypass supplement; she needs consistency.

A caution on cottonseed. Gossypol in cottonseed products is a real constraint. Lactating cattle tolerate up to about 1,050 mg/kg of diet dry matter, but calves and breeding cattle are affected at 400 mg/kg or above, with reduced growth and reduced fertility. Breeding bulls should be off cottonseed meal for 2 to 3 months before the breeding season, and where it is used for bulls the published guidance is roughly 3 to 5 lb per head per day. Whole cottonseed should not exceed about 25 percent of diet dry matter, or 0.5 percent of body weight daily.

Excess protein is also a loss. When RDP outruns the fermentable energy available, the surplus is deaminated to ammonia, absorbed, converted to urea in the liver at an energy cost, and urinated away. You paid for protein and bought yourself a metabolic bill. The signal in a dairy herd is high milk urea nitrogen; the signal on farm is a strong ammonia smell in the shed and expensive feed producing no extra milk.

Concrete African context. In Uganda's central dairy belt, farmers commonly feed lablab or Desmodium alongside Napier. Both legumes are high in degradable protein and, fed at 20 to 30 percent of the forage dry matter, they lift a 7 to 9 percent CP Napier diet into the 11 to 13 percent range at almost no cash cost once established. That is a better first move than buying cake. Grow the nitrogen before you buy it.

Rules of thumb:

  1. Under about 18 kg of milk a day, prioritise RDP and cheap fermentable energy.
  2. Above about 27 kg, start balancing for RUP toward 7.2 to 7.5 percent of diet DM.
  3. Never chase crude protein percentage alone; it hides which fraction you are buying.
  4. Legume forage is the cheapest protein most African farms can access.

Then check whether your highest-yielding cow is above or below roughly 27 litres a day, and decide honestly whether you need any bypass protein at all.

Lesson 9.4~12 min

Ruminal pH and Acidosis Prevention

In this lesson
  • Recognise the difference between acute and subacute ruminal acidosis and the farm signs of each
  • Calculate a safe maximum concentrate allowance for a given body weight
  • Build a step-up feeding plan that moves cattle onto concentrate without crashing rumen pH

Rumen pH is the single most useful number you cannot easily measure on a small farm, so you must manage it by design rather than by monitoring. Normal rumen pH sits between about 6.2 and 6.8. When rapidly fermentable starch enters faster than the rumen can buffer and absorb the resulting acid, pH falls. Below about 6.0 fibre digestion is impaired and you have subacute ruminal acidosis, usually called SARA. Far lower and you have acute acidosis, which is a life-threatening emergency.

Acute acidosis is dramatic and rare: an animal breaks into the grain store, eats several kilos of maize, and within hours is dull, has profuse watery dung, is dehydrated and may go down. It needs a veterinarian immediately. Subacute acidosis is the expensive one because it is invisible. There is no dead animal to point at. Instead you get erratic feed intake, cows sorting the ration and leaving the long fibre, loose bubbly dung with undigested grain visible, laminitis showing up weeks later as lameness, milk fat depression, and a herd that simply does not perform to the feed bill you are paying.

Prevention rests on four levers, all of which are free or cheap.

  1. Keep enough effective fibre in the diet. The published floor is 15 to 16 percent forage NDF of total diet dry matter, and this is an absolute minimum, not a target. Working targets by lactation stage are roughly 19 to 25 percent forage NDF in early lactation, 25 to 26 percent in mid lactation and 27 to 28 percent in late lactation, with total diet NDF in the 25 to 38 percent range. Fibre must also be long enough to stimulate chewing; finely chopped forage loses much of its protective effect.
  2. Cap the concentrate. A practical published ceiling is about 2 to 3 lb of concentrate per 100 lb of body weight per day, and the better your forage quality, the less concentrate you need to reach the same production.
  3. Step up slowly. Never move an animal by more than about 10 to 15 percent of diet dry matter between feeding groups, and take 2 to 3 weeks to bring cattle onto a high-concentrate finishing diet.
  4. Buffer where necessary. Sodium bicarbonate is the standard rumen buffer and is cheap relative to the losses it prevents. Ionophores such as monensin also stabilise fermentation, improving feed efficiency by about 6.4 percent and average daily gain by about 2.5 percent, with dry matter intake typically 1 to 2 percent lower. Check local registration and follow label directions and veterinary advice.

Worked example. A 400 kg steer is being brought onto a fattening ration. Body weight 400 kg equals about 880 lb. The concentrate ceiling of 2 to 3 lb per 100 lb body weight gives 880/100 x 2 = 17.6 lb at the conservative end and 880/100 x 3 = 26.4 lb at the top end, that is roughly 8 to 12 kg of concentrate per day, and only with good forage alongside and a proper adaptation period. Suppose his total dry matter intake will be 10 kg. The forage NDF floor of 15 percent means at least 1.5 kg of forage NDF must be present. If your hay is 65 percent NDF, that requires 1.5 / 0.65 = 2.3 kg of hay dry matter per day as an absolute minimum, and you should be feeding well above that.

Now the step-up. Start him at 2 kg concentrate and 8 kg forage dry matter. Increase concentrate by no more than about 1.0 to 1.5 kg every 4 to 5 days while dropping forage correspondingly, so that over roughly 3 weeks he reaches 7 to 8 kg concentrate with 3 to 4 kg forage. Skipping this step-up to save three weeks is the most common cause of ruined finishing cattle in small feedlots.

Concrete African context. A small feedlot outside Kaduna, Nigeria, buying in 250 kg White Fulani bulls, lost performance for two seasons by putting animals straight onto a maize-and-cottonseed-cake ration on arrival. Animals arrive stressed, dehydrated and often off feed after transport, which is exactly the wrong moment for a starch load. Changing to a protocol of good hay plus water only for 48 hours, then a 21-day step-up, removed the scouring and the early deaths without adding a single input cost.

Watch also for the interaction with byproducts. Distillers grains can go to 50 percent of diet dry matter but above that sulfur toxicity becomes a risk, and their phosphorus-to-calcium ratio is high, so calcium must be added. Wet brewers grains, widely available near African breweries, are useful at roughly 30 to 40 percent of diet DM, with storage losses around 15 percent and palatability problems above 30 percent.

Lesson 9.5~12 min

Methane Production and Mitigation

In this lesson
  • Quantify the share of feed energy lost as enteric methane in beef and dairy cattle
  • Rank practical mitigation options by cost and by realistic effect size on a small farm
  • Explain why improving forage quality reduces methane and feed cost at the same time

Methane from cattle is not only a climate issue. It is feed you bought, fermented, and vented. In beef cattle roughly 6 to 8 percent of gross energy intake leaves as methane; in dairy cattle roughly 5 to 7 percent. Note that an older figure of 2 to 3 percent circulates in some training material and is too low; the current published range is the one above. On a farm feeding 10 kg of dry matter a day, a 7 percent energy loss is a meaningful slice of the feed bill.

Methane is made by methanogens, which are archaea rather than bacteria, using hydrogen released during fermentation. Anything that changes fermentation changes methane output. The published drivers are consistent:

  1. Forage quality and composition. Higher NDF means more methane, because fermentation is slower and hydrogen accumulates. Lower digestibility means more methane. High-starch diets mean less methane, because fermentation is faster and shifts toward propionate, which consumes hydrogen.
  2. Dry matter intake. Higher intake raises absolute methane but lowers methane as a percentage of gross energy, which is why a well-fed productive animal is more efficient per kilo of product than a half-starved one.
  3. Animal factors. Higher-producing dairy animals emit more in absolute terms; larger cattle emit slightly more, scaled to metabolic body weight.
  4. Environment. Cold stress raises emissions slightly through higher maintenance; heat stress lowers them slightly through reduced intake.

That third point deserves emphasis for African producers, because it cuts against a common misunderstanding. Emissions per animal and emissions per kilo of milk or meat are different numbers. A cow producing 3 litres a day on poor forage may emit less methane in total than one producing 15 litres, but far more per litre. Raising productivity is itself a mitigation strategy, and it is the one that pays you.

Options ranked by realism for a small or medium producer:

  • Improve forage quality and cut forage earlier. Dietary management of this kind reduces methane in the range of 10 to 20 percent via the fermentation shift, and usually improves feed efficiency at the same time. Cost: nothing but timing discipline. This is the first move for every farm.
  • Raise per-animal productivity and cull unproductive stock. Fewer animals producing the same output cuts total emissions and total feed cost together.
  • Ionophores such as monensin. Designed for efficiency, they deliver a secondary methane reduction of roughly 3 to 5 percent alongside the 6.4 percent feed efficiency gain. Registration varies by country.
  • 3-nitrooxypropanol (3-NOP, sold as Bovaer). This inhibits the final step of methanogenesis. Meta-analysis puts mean reduction at 20 to 40 percent, with 39.0 plus or minus 5.4 percent at an optimised dose of 123 mg per kg of diet dry matter, and doses above 100 mg/kg giving the best results. It works best on high-starch diets, around 40 percent reduction, and less well on high-NDF forage diets, around 20 to 25 percent. High dietary fat reduces its efficacy. Dairy cattle respond more strongly than beef. No adverse effects on intake, digestibility or milk production have been reported. Availability and approval status vary by region, and the cost case usually depends on a carbon credit or premium market.
  • Seaweed (Asparagopsis). Research reports about 50 to 80 percent reduction, higher than 3-NOP, but palatability and consistency are variable and commercial availability is limited. Treat as promising, not as a purchasing decision.
  • Probiotics and enzyme additives. Typically 5 to 15 percent at best, with limited robust peer-reviewed support. Be sceptical of sales claims here.

Worked example. A herd of 20 dairy cows each eating 14 kg dry matter a day, with a diet at roughly 2.3 Mcal of metabolisable energy per kg DM, takes in about 32 Mcal each per day. At 6 percent lost as methane that is about 1.9 Mcal per cow per day, and across 20 cows about 38 Mcal a day. If you could recover a quarter of that through better forage and higher digestibility, you would free roughly 9.5 Mcal a day, which is in the region of the energy in 5 to 6 kg of dairy meal. Over a year, at 0.42 USD per kg of meal, that is on the order of 800 to 900 USD. The numbers are approximate, but the direction is not.

Concrete African context. Carbon-credit and low-emission dairy schemes are beginning to operate in Kenya, Rwanda and Ethiopia, generally paying on emissions intensity, that is emissions per litre, rather than emissions per cow. A smallholder who lifts a herd from 6 to 10 litres a cow through better forage and mineral supplementation improves emissions intensity by roughly a third without buying any additive at all. If you are approached about a methane scheme, ask on which basis you are paid before you sign anything.

Lesson 9.6~12 min

Milk Composition and Dietary Manipulation

In this lesson
  • Calculate the energy cost of milk from its fat content using the published NEL equations
  • Diagnose and correct milk fat depression using the forage NDF floor
  • Choose a feeding strategy that matches how your buyer actually pays you

Milk is not one product. It is water carrying fat, protein and lactose, and each of those responds differently to what you feed. If your buyer pays a flat price per litre, you should chase volume. If your buyer pays on butterfat, or you sell to a ghee, yoghurt or cheese maker, fat percentage is your income and you should feed accordingly. Many smallholders feed for one and get paid for the other.

Start with the energy arithmetic, because milk is expensive to make. Two published equations let you cost it. Where composition is known, NEL per kg of milk = (9.29 x kg fat) + (5.5 x kg crude protein) + (3.95 x kg lactose). Where only fat is measured, NEL per kg of milk = 0.360 + (0.0969 x fat percent).

Worked example. A crossbred cow gives 12 kg of milk a day at 4.2 percent fat. Energy in the milk = 0.360 + (0.0969 x 4.2) = 0.360 + 0.407 = 0.767 Mcal NEL per kg. For 12 kg that is 12 x 0.767 = 9.2 Mcal NEL per day just for lactation. Now add maintenance. The published dairy maintenance equation is NEm = 0.10 x body weight in kg raised to the power 0.75. For a 400 kg cow, 400 to the power 0.75 is about 89.4, giving roughly 8.9 Mcal per day. Note that published tables sometimes quote lower maintenance figures for the same weight class, so treat any single maintenance number as approximate and check it against your own herd's condition trend. Using the equation, her total requirement is about 9.2 + 8.9 = 18.1 Mcal NEL per day. If her forage supplies 1.35 Mcal NEL per kg DM and she eats 12 kg DM, forage delivers about 16.2 Mcal and she is roughly 2 Mcal short, which is about 1.2 kg of dairy meal. If her forage is stemmy at 1.15 Mcal per kg DM, forage delivers only 13.8 Mcal and the shortfall jumps to 4.3 Mcal, needing well over 2.5 kg of meal to close. Same cow, same litres, double the purchased feed, because of when the Napier was cut. Forage quality, not the meal bag, decides whether this cow milks profitably.

Milk fat depression is the most common composition problem and it is nearly always a fibre problem. Milk fat is built largely from acetate, and acetate comes from fibre fermentation. When forage NDF falls too low, or when forage is chopped too finely, or when concentrate is loaded too fast, rumen pH drops, the acetate-to-propionate ratio falls, and butterfat falls with it. The fix is structural: restore forage NDF above the 15 to 16 percent of diet DM floor, use working targets of roughly 19 to 25 percent forage NDF in early lactation rising to 27 to 28 percent in late lactation, keep chop length long enough to make cows chew, and split concentrate into two or more feeds.

Milk protein responds more to total amino acid supply reaching the intestine, which for most smallholder cows means microbial protein, which in turn means adequate rumen-degradable protein and fermentable energy. This is why a cow on good legume-supplemented forage often shows better protein percentage than one on a bran-heavy diet: the microbes are better fed.

Fat supplementation is a trap worth naming. Adding polyunsaturated oils raises the polyunsaturated fatty acid content of the milk but tends to reduce saturation and can depress milk fat percentage. Adding significant amounts of oil or oilseed also interferes with fibre digestion in the rumen. Individual cows respond differently to the same diet, which is why herd averages mislead and why you should track your best and worst cows separately.

Concrete African context. A dairy cooperative in Tanzania's Southern Highlands moved from flat-rate payment to a fat-and-protein based schedule. Several members saw their income fall despite unchanged litres, because they had been pushing maize bran hard and chopping Napier very fine to reduce refusals. The cooperative's advice was simple and free: chop coarser, feed the concentrate twice a day rather than once, and put a slab of dry hay in front of cows before the morning concentrate. Butterfat recovered over the following six weeks. Nothing was purchased.

Practical summary:

  1. Ask your buyer exactly how you are paid, in writing, before you change any feeding.
  2. Protect the forage NDF floor at 15 to 16 percent of diet DM at all times; treat 19 to 28 percent as the working range depending on lactation stage.
  3. Coarse chop and split concentrate feeding are the two cheapest butterfat interventions in existence.
  4. Track individual cows. Herd averages hide both your best animals and your problems.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. What is the optimal rumen pH range for fibre-digesting bacteria?

Cellulolytic efficiency depends on pH staying in the 6.2-6.8 range. Heavy grain feeding pushes pH lower and shuts fibre digestion down.

2. A cow is offered unlimited maize stover at 4% crude protein but keeps losing weight. What is the most likely explanation?

Below about 7.5% crude protein in diet dry matter, microbial growth is nitrogen-limited. Undigested fibre stays in the rumen and physically limits how much more she can eat.

3. The main source of protein reaching a cow's small intestine on a forage diet is:

Rumen microbes build their own body protein from degradable protein and ammonia; the cow then digests those microbes downstream.

4. What is the absolute floor for forage NDF as a share of total diet dry matter?

15-16% forage NDF of total diet DM is the published minimum to prevent acidosis and milk fat depression.

5. Why should diet changes between feeding groups be kept to 10-15% of diet dry matter?

The microbial population shifts slowly. Abrupt changes outrun that adaptation and cause intake and production losses.

6. Which volatile fatty acid is the main precursor for glucose production in cattle?

Propionate is the key gluconeogenic substrate, driving milk lactose and volume as well as liveweight gain.

7. A hay tests at 40% ADF. What is its estimated TDN?

TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 40) = 88.9 - 31.2 = 57.7%.

8. A forage is calculated at 60% TDN. What is its approximate NEL?

NEL = (0.0245 x 60) - 0.12 = 1.47 - 0.12 = 1.35 Mcal/kg.

9. Roughly what proportion of gross energy intake is lost as methane in beef cattle?

Published baselines are about 6-8% of gross energy for beef and 5-7% for dairy.

10. Why should concentrate be split into two or more feeds per day?

One large starch meal swings rumen pH sharply. Splitting it keeps fermentation and the VFA profile more stable.

11. For a dairy cow producing under about 18 kg of milk per day, what RDP share of crude protein can she use effectively?

Low-producing cows can use 65-70% RDP effectively because microbial protein synthesis covers their requirement.

12. Which feed group is highest in rumen-undegradable protein?

Blood meal, fish meal and similar heat-treated products run about 70-90% RUP of crude protein.

13. At what dietary gossypol level are calves and breeding cattle already at risk?

Lactating cattle tolerate up to about 1,050 mg/kg, but calves and breeding stock show reduced growth and fertility from 400 mg/kg.

14. What happens when rumen-degradable protein exceeds the fermentable energy available?

Surplus ammonia from deamination is detoxified to urea in the liver and excreted, so you pay for the protein and for the disposal.

15. Which RUP requirement applies to a high-producing dairy cow above roughly 27 kg milk per day?

Estimated RUP requirement for high producers is 7.2-7.5% of diet dry matter.

16. Which is the more economically damaging problem on most farms?

Acute acidosis is dramatic but rare. Subacute acidosis quietly reduces intake, milk fat, and gain across the whole herd while the feed bill continues.

17. A 500 kg (1,100 lb) cow is fed by the 2-3 lb per 100 lb body weight rule. What is her approximate upper concentrate limit?

1,100/100 x 2 to 3 = 22 to 33 lb, which is roughly 10 to 15 kg per day, and only alongside adequate forage.

18. Which visible sign points to subacute ruminal acidosis?

Loose bubbly dung with visible grain, milk fat depression and laminitis-driven lameness weeks later are the classic SARA picture.

19. How long should a step-up onto a high-concentrate finishing diet take?

The microbial population needs 2-3 weeks to adapt, with changes limited to about 10-15% of diet DM at a time.

20. Above what inclusion do distillers grains create a sulfur toxicity risk?

Distillers dried grain can be used up to 50% of diet DM; above that sulfur toxicity becomes a concern, and the high phosphorus to calcium ratio needs correcting with added calcium.

21. What share of gross energy intake is typically lost as methane in dairy cattle?

Published baselines are 5-7% of gross energy for dairy and 6-8% for beef cattle.

22. 3-NOP at its optimised dose of 123 mg/kg diet DM reduces methane by approximately how much?

Meta-analysis reports 39.0 plus or minus 5.4 percent reduction at that dose, within an overall 20-40% range.

23. On which type of diet is 3-NOP most effective?

Reduction reaches about 40% on high-starch diets but only 20-25% on high-NDF forage diets, and high dietary fat reduces efficacy.

24. Which mitigation option costs nothing but timing discipline?

Dietary management through better forage quality reduces methane 10-20% and usually improves feed efficiency at the same time.

25. Why does raising per-cow productivity count as methane mitigation?

Higher intake raises absolute methane but lowers it as a share of gross energy and, critically, per unit of product, which is how emissions intensity schemes pay.

26. A cow gives milk at 4.0% fat. Using NEL = 0.360 + (0.0969 x fat %), what is the energy content per kg of milk?

0.360 + (0.0969 x 4.0) = 0.360 + 0.388 = 0.748, about 0.75 Mcal NEL per kg.

27. Milk fat is built largely from which volatile fatty acid?

Acetate from fibre fermentation is the main precursor for milk fat, which is why low effective fibre depresses butterfat.

28. A herd suddenly drops in butterfat after the farmer started chopping Napier very finely. The most likely cause is:

Fine chopping reduces chewing and effective fibre. Rumen pH falls, the acetate to propionate ratio drops, and milk fat follows.

29. What effect does supplementing polyunsaturated oil usually have on milk?

PUFA supplementation increases milk PUFA but decreases saturation and can depress milk fat percentage.

30. Before changing a feeding strategy to raise butterfat, what should a producer establish first?

Feeding for fat when you are paid flat per litre, or for volume when you are paid on components, is a direct income loss.

Module 9 capstone

Rumen Performance Audit on your own herd. Step 1: pick five animals you can handle safely (for a dairy, five cows in the first 120 days of lactation; for a beef unit, five growing steers) and record each one's live weight, either on a scale or with a weigh-band, plus body condition score on a 1-5 scale. Step 2: write out everything those animals eat in 24 hours, ingredient by ingredient, with the weight fed as-fed and your best estimate of dry matter percentage (green forage roughly 20-25% DM, wilted or cut-and-carry roughly 30%, dry hay roughly 88%, concentrates roughly 88%). Convert every line to kilograms of dry matter and add them up. Step 3: work out the forage share of total dry matter, and check it against the floor for rumen health: forage NDF must not fall below 15-16% of total diet dry matter. Step 4: calculate expected dry matter intake capacity from your main forage using DMI (% of body weight) = 120 / NDF%, using either a lab NDF result or the closest published value you can find for that forage, and compare it with what the animals actually ate. Step 5: convert your main forage's ADF to TDN using TDN% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%), then to energy using NEL Mcal/kg = (0.0245 x TDN%) - 0.12 for dairy, or NEg Mcal/kg = (0.0257 x TDN%) - 0.315 for growing beef. Step 6: price every ingredient at what you actually paid at your local market in USD per kg of dry matter, and calculate the cost of one Mcal of energy and one kg of crude protein from each feed. Step 7: write a one-page conclusion naming the two cheapest sources of energy and protein available to you locally, the one ration change you will make next month, and the expected saving per animal per month in USD. Present the audit with your calculations shown, not just the answers.
