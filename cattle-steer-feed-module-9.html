Lessons

1. Grinding, rolling, and cracking grains

Grinding, rolling, and cracking are the first steps in feed processing. They break open the hard outer shell of grains like maize, sorghum, and millet so the animal's stomach can digest the nutrients. Without processing, up to 30% of grain passes through the animal undigested, wasting money. The goal is to increase surface area for digestion without making the feed too fine, which can cause acidosis or dust.

For cattle, the best particle size is like cracked pepper or coarse cornmeal. You can use a hammer mill with a 6-8 mm screen for grinding. A roller mill is better for cracking because it produces fewer fines. If you don't have a mill, you can use a mortar and pestle or even a clean, flat rock to crack grains by hand, though this is slow. In many African villages, farmers rent a hammer mill from a local entrepreneur for about $5 per 50 kg bag.

Rolling is done with a roller mill that crushes the grain between two metal rollers. This is ideal for barley, oats, and maize. The rollers should be set so the grain is cracked into 2-4 pieces, not powdered. Cracking is similar but uses a corrugated roller to break the grain into smaller pieces. For sorghum, cracking is preferred because it reduces bitterness.

After processing, store the feed in a dry place. Moisture above 14% causes mould. In humid areas like coastal West Africa, add a mould inhibitor like propionic acid at 0.5% of the feed weight. Always clean your mill between batches to avoid contamination. A well-maintained hammer mill with a 15-horsepower diesel engine can process 1 ton per hour. For a small herd of 10 cattle, you need about 50 kg of processed grain per day. Remember, processing costs money, but it pays back in better weight gain and milk production.

2. Pelleting and extrusion processes

Pelleting and extrusion are advanced processing methods that improve feed efficiency and reduce waste. Pelleting compresses ground feed into small, hard cylinders. Extrusion uses high heat and pressure to expand the feed, like making puffed snacks. Both methods are common in commercial feed mills but can be done on a small scale in Africa.

To make pellets, you need a pellet mill. The ground feed is mixed with steam and forced through a die with holes of 4-6 mm diameter. The pellets are then cooled and dried. For cattle, pellet size should be 6-8 mm. A small pellet mill with a 10-horsepower motor can produce 200-300 kg per hour. In Kenya, some farmer cooperatives share a pellet mill to reduce costs. Pellets reduce sorting by animals, so every bite has the same nutrition. They also reduce dust and improve handling.

Extrusion is more complex. It uses a machine called an extruder that cooks feed at 120-150C for 10-30 seconds. This gelatinizes starch, making it more digestible. Extrusion is useful for making calf starter or creep feed. However, it destroys some vitamins, so you must add a vitamin premix after extrusion. A small extruder costs about $2,000, which may be too expensive for individual farmers. Instead, you can buy extruded feed from a local mill.

In Africa, pelleting and extrusion are mostly done by commercial feed companies. But you can make simple pellets at home using a hand-cranked pellet press, which costs around $150. The process: mix ground feed with 5-10% water, press into pellets, and sun-dry for 2 days. This is labour-intensive but works for small herds. Always check pellet quality: they should be hard, not crumbly, and sink in water. Poor pellets mean poor binding, so add 2% molasses or cassava starch as a binder. Remember, pelleting and extrusion add cost, so use them only for high-value animals like dairy cows or finishing steers.

3. Mixing and batching procedures

Mixing and batching are about combining ingredients in the right proportions to create a balanced feed. Batching means weighing or measuring each ingredient accurately. Mixing means blending them uniformly. If you don't mix well, some cattle get too much protein and others too little, leading to poor growth and wasted money.

Start with a feed formula. For a simple cattle fattening diet, you might use: 50% maize, 20% soybean meal, 15% wheat bran, 10% molasses, 4% minerals, and 1% salt. For a 100 kg batch, you need 50 kg maize, 20 kg soybean meal, 15 kg wheat bran, 10 kg molasses, 4 kg minerals, and 1 kg salt. Weigh each ingredient on a scale. A hanging scale that measures up to 100 kg costs about $30. If you don't have a scale, use a 20-litre bucket: 1 bucket of maize weighs about 15 kg, but this is less accurate.

Mixing can be done by hand or with a machine. For small batches, spread the ingredients in layers on a clean concrete floor and turn them with a shovel at least 5 times. For larger batches, use a drum mixer or a cement mixer. A used cement mixer costs $200-$500 and can mix 100 kg in 5 minutes. Mix for at least 3 minutes after the last ingredient is added. Overmixing can cause separation, so stop when the colour is uniform.

Batching and mixing should be done in a dry, shaded area. Humidity causes ingredients to clump. Always mix the smallest ingredients first (minerals, salt) with a small amount of grain to make a premix, then add the rest. This prevents pockets of concentrated minerals. After mixing, store the feed in clean sacks. Label each sack with the date and formula. Use within 2 weeks to avoid mould. In Africa, many farmers mix feed weekly to keep it fresh. A well-mixed feed should look and smell the same in every handful. If you see different colours or textures, mix again.

4. Silage making and preservation

Silage is fermented, high-moisture fodder that cattle love, especially in the dry season when fresh grass is scarce. It is made from crops like maize, sorghum, and Napier grass. The process preserves nutrients through anaerobic fermentation, where lactic acid bacteria turn sugars into acid, preventing spoilage. Good silage smells like vinegar or apples, not like rotten eggs.

To make silage, harvest maize at the dough stage, when the kernels are soft but not dry. Chop the whole plant into 2-3 cm pieces. A hand chopper or a forage chopper (costing $500-$1,500) makes this easier. For 1 tonne of silage, you need about 1 tonne of fresh chopped maize. Spread the chopped material on a clean surface and wilt it for 4-6 hours to reduce moisture to 65-70%. To test: squeeze a handful; it should hold together but not drip water.

Next, pack the chopped material into a silo. A simple pit silo is a hole in the ground, 2 m deep and 2 m wide, lined with plastic. A bunker silo is a concrete wall structure. For small farms, use heavy-duty plastic bags (200-litre drums also work). Pack tightly in 15 cm layers, trampling each layer to remove air. This is critical: air spoils silage. Add 1 kg of salt per 100 kg of silage to improve fermentation. Then seal the top with a plastic sheet and weigh it down with tires or soil. The silo must be airtight.

Fermentation takes 3-6 weeks. After opening, feed 10-15 kg per cow per day. Remove only what you need and reseal immediately. In Africa, silage is often made from maize stover, but you can also use sweet potato vines, sorghum, or even sugarcane tops. A well-made silage pit of 2 m x 2 m x 2 m holds about 4 tonnes, enough to feed 10 cows for 40 days. If silage smells bad or is mouldy, do not feed itit can cause disease. Always keep the silo shaded and away from termites.

5. Total mixed ration (TMR) preparation

Total Mixed Ration (TMR) is a complete feed that mixes forages, grains, proteins, minerals, and vitamins into one uniform blend. Unlike feeding separate ingredients, TMR ensures every bite has the right balance, preventing cattle from sorting and getting too much of one thing. TMR is especially good for dairy cows and finishing steers, leading to better weight gain and milk production.

To make TMR, you need a mixer wagon or a stationary mixer. For small herds, you can mix on a concrete floor with a shovel. A typical TMR for a dairy cow producing 20 litres of milk per day might include: 20 kg maize silage, 5 kg alfalfa hay, 4 kg maize grain, 2 kg soybean meal, 1 kg molasses, 0.2 kg minerals, and 0.1 kg salt. This gives about 32 kg of fresh feed per day. For a steer gaining 1 kg per day, use: 10 kg maize silage, 3 kg grass hay, 5 kg maize grain, 1.5 kg cottonseed cake, 0.5 kg molasses, and 0.15 kg minerals.

Start by weighing each ingredient. If you don't have a scale, use a known container: a 20-litre bucket holds about 10 kg of silage or 15 kg of grain. Add the forages first, then grains, then protein meals, then molasses (diluted with water), and finally minerals and salt. Mix for at least 5 minutes until the colour is uniform. If using a mixer wagon, load in the same order and run for 10 minutes. In Africa, many farmers use a feed mixer mounted on a tractor, but you can also use a cement mixer for small batches.

Feed TMR twice a day, in the morning and evening. Provide fresh water always. Store leftover TMR in a cool, dry place and use within 24 hours to prevent spoilage. A well-prepared TMR should be slightly moist, like a wrung-out sponge, and smell sweet. If it heats up or smells sour, discard it. TMR reduces feed waste by 10-15% compared to separate feeding. For a herd of 20 cattle, you need about 500 kg of TMR per day, so plan your mixing schedule accordingly. Always adjust the ration based on animal weight, milk production, and weather.

6. Quality control in feed manufacturing

Quality control ensures that every batch of feed is safe, nutritious, and consistent. Poor quality feed wastes money, causes health problems, and reduces animal performance. In Africa, where feed fraud (like adding sand or sawdust) is common, quality control protects your cattle and your profits.

Start with ingredient inspection. Check for mould, insects, and foreign objects. Maize should be dry (moisture below 14%) and free of aflatoxin. Aflatoxin is a poison produced by mould; it can kill cattle. Test with a simple aflatoxin test kit (costing $20) or send a sample to a lab. Smell the ingredients: good maize smells sweet, while mouldy maize smells musty. Soybean meal should be light brown and free of lumps. Minerals should be fine and uniform.

During mixing, check uniformity. Take 10 handfuls from different parts of the batch. They should look and smell the same. If not, mix longer. After mixing, test for particle size: for cattle, feed should be like cracked pepper. Use a set of sieves (2 mm, 4 mm, 6 mm) to check. Too fine causes acidosis; too coarse causes sorting. Also, check for moisture: squeeze a handful; if it stays in a ball, it's too wet. Add dry ingredients or let it dry.

After processing, store feed in a clean, dry, rodent-proof room. Use pallets to keep bags off the floor. Label each bag with the date, formula, and batch number. Keep records of all ingredients and quantities. This helps trace problems. For pellets, check hardness: drop a pellet from 1 metre; it should not shatter. For silage, check pH: good silage has pH 3.8-4.5. You can use pH paper (costing $5). If pH is above 5, the silage is spoiled.

Finally, train your workers. They must wash hands, wear clean clothes, and not smoke near feed. A simple quality control checklist can prevent 90% of problems. In Africa, many feed mills follow the East African Community standards. For small-scale farmers, the key is consistency: use the same ingredients, same proportions, and same methods every time. If you suspect a problem, stop feeding and test. It is cheaper to test than to lose cattle.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Feed Processing and Manufacturing for African Cattle and Steer Operations



In African beef and dairy production, feed represents 6075% of total cost of production. How you process, mix, and preserve that feed determines whether your animals gain 0.8 kg/day or 0.4 kg/day and whether your business survives drought, currency swings, and dry-season price spikes. This practical reference distils the six core processing operations into decisions you can apply on-farm or in a small commercial feed mill today.





1. Grinding, Rolling, and Cracking Grains


Particle size is the single biggest lever on starch digestibility and feed conversion in African cattle operations. Whole grains pass through undigested (especially in maize and sorghum), wasting 1525% of the energy you paid for. Grinding to a coarse meal (24 mm) typically improves starch digestibility by 812% versus whole grain, but over-grinding to dust (<1 mm) raises the risk of rumen acidosis and costs you more in milling energy.


In practice: for maize and sorghum, a hammer mill with a 46 mm screen is the workhorse for small-to-medium operations. For barley and oats, rolling or cracking is preferred because it preserves fibre structure and reduces fines. In East Africa, the common "chaff cutter + hammer mill" combination (often petrol or diesel-driven, 515 hp) handles 300800 kg/hour sufficient for a 50150 head feedlot.

2. Pelleting and Extrusion Processes


Pelleting binds ground feed into uniform 48 mm cylinders. Benefits: less sorting by animals, reduced dust, higher bulk density (cheaper transport), and improved handling. In African conditions, pelleting is most justified for high-value dairy concentrates, poultry, and pig feeds not usually for beef finishing rations where TMR is more economical.


Extrusion (heat + pressure) gelatinises starch and deactivates anti-nutritional factors such as trypsin inhibitors in soybeans and gossypol in cottonseed. For smallholder operations, a simple steam-flaking or dry-roasting step on soybeans before inclusion is often more practical than full extrusion. If you buy extruded full-fat soy, verify the supplier's processing temperature (typically 120150C for 2030 seconds) under-processed soy reduces protein digestibility.



3. Mixing and Batching Procedures


Uniform mixing is where most African feed businesses lose money silently. A cow can sort a poorly mixed ration in minutes eating the maize and leaving the urea and minerals. The result: acidosis in some animals, deficiency in others, and inconsistent performance across the herd.


Rule of thumb: mix concentrates for 35 minutes after the last ingredient is added; mix a full TMR for 58 minutes. Always batch by weight, never by volume a "bucket of maize" varies 2030% in weight depending on moisture and compaction. Calibrate your scale monthly.

SAFETY WARNING UREA AND AFLATOXIN

Urea is the most common cause of cattle poisoning in African feedlots. Never exceed 1% of total diet DM, never feed to starving animals, and always adapt over 1014 days. Keep urea locked away from children and store separately from other ingredients. Equally serious: aflatoxin in maize and groundnut cake above 20 ppb causes liver damage, reduced growth, and milk residues. Test every incoming maize batch in humid zones (Kenya, Uganda, Nigeria, Mozambique) reject anything above 20 ppb for dairy, 100 ppb for beef.




4. Silage Making and Preservation


Silage is the cheapest way to carry green forage through the dry season and the most common failure point for African cattle businesses. The principle is simple: chop to 12 cm, pack tightly to exclude oxygen, seal, and let anaerobic fermentation drop pH to 3.84.2 within 3 weeks. Failures come from: chopping too coarse, poor packing, delayed sealing, and opening the pit too early.


Maize silage at 3035% DM is the gold standard for beef. In semi-arid zones, sorghum, Napier grass, and forage legumes (lablab, cowpea) work well. For smallholders, a 1 m pit or a 200 L drum with a tight lid is a realistic entry point. Add 23% molasses as a fermentation stimulant when forage DM is above 35%.



5. Total Mixed Ration (TMR) Preparation


TMR means every bite delivers the same nutrient profile no sorting, no slug feeding, no acidosis. It is the single most impactful practice for commercial African feedlots, and increasingly for medium-scale dairy. See Figure 1 for the workflow.


Sequence matters: load long forage first, then silage, then concentrates, then liquids (molasses), then minerals last. Mix 58 minutes. Target 23 cm chop length long enough to stimulate rumination, short enough to prevent sorting. Feed twice daily, push up 46 times, and clean refusals every morning. Refusals should be 35% of offered feed.



6. Quality Control in Feed Manufacturing


Quality control is a business discipline, not a laboratory luxury. The four controls that pay back fastest in African operations:



  • Incoming ingredient inspection: smell, colour, moisture (target <12% for grains), foreign matter. Reject mouldy or insect-damaged loads.

  • Moisture testing: a simple moisture meter (US$80150) prevents mycotoxin and storage losses worth many times its cost.

  • Scale calibration: monthly check with a known weight. A 2% scale error on a 500 kg batch is 10 kg of urea enough to poison a pen.

  • Mix uniformity: sample 5 points from the mixer and lab-test for crude protein; variation should be <5%.


Keep a one-page batch record: date, ingredients, weights, mixer time, operator. This is your defence when a client complains, and your data when you scale.



Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Grain ground to 24 mm, not dust check by sieving a handful.

  2. Batch by weight, never by bucket or volume.

  3. Mix concentrates 35 min; full TMR 58 min.

  4. Silage chopped 12 cm, packed tight, sealed within 24 hours.

  5. Urea never exceeds 1% of diet DM; adapt over 1014 days.

  6. Test maize and groundnut cake for aflatoxin every incoming batch.

  7. Calibrate scales monthly; keep a one-page batch record.

  8. Target 35% feed refusals more means overfeeding, less means underfeeding.
