1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Eggs are the fastest way to turn feed into cash on a small farm. A healthy layer hen eats about 120 grams of feed a day and gives you one egg. In most African markets, that egg sells for 200 to 500 shillings, or 50 to 150 naira, depending on your country and season. If you keep 100 hens, you collect about 80 to 90 eggs a day. That is real money every single morning. Unlike goats or cows that take months to grow, layers start paying you back at 18 to 20 weeks of age. You do not wait for a big sale. You sell daily to neighbours, shops, and hotels. That daily cash covers feed, transport, and school fees. It also protects you from price shocks because you are not depending on one big harvest. Many African farmers fail with poultry because they treat it as a side project. They buy chicks, then forget vaccination or feed. But if you follow a simple daily routine, eggs become your most reliable income. You need a secure house, clean water, balanced feed, and a market. The market is everywhere: every family eats eggs. Your job is to keep the hens healthy and collect eggs before they break. This module shows you exactly how to do that, step by step, with tools you can find locally. By the end, you will know why eggs are called daily cash and how to start with as few as 20 hens.

2. Tools and Materials

You do not need expensive equipment to start a layer flock. You need a few basic tools and materials that are available in any local market or agrovet shop. First, housing: a simple deep litter house with wire mesh walls and a corrugated iron roof. For 100 hens, you need about 2 square metres per bird inside, so a 10m by 20m house works. Use wood poles, offcuts, and nails. Second, feeders and drinkers. You can buy plastic feeders or make them from cut jerrycans. One 5-litre drinker serves 20 hens. Third, feed: layers mash or pellets. A 50kg bag costs between 25,000 and 35,000 shillings in East Africa, or 15,000 to 22,000 naira in Nigeria. You also need a feed trough that prevents spillage. Fourth, egg trays: plastic or cardboard trays hold 30 eggs. Buy at least 10 trays for 100 hens. Fifth, a record book and pen. Write down daily egg count, feed used, and deaths. Sixth, health items: vaccines for Newcastle and Gumboro, a small syringe, and disinfectant. Seventh, a source of clean water. Finally, a wheelbarrow and shovel for cleaning litter. You can start with 20 hens and scale up. The total starting cost for 20 hens is roughly 150,000 to 200,000 shillings, or 80,000 to 120,000 naira. Most of that is feed and chicks. Do not buy cheap feed; it wastes money. Buy from a trusted agrovet. Keep your tools clean and dry. A simple shelter, good feed, and clean water are the real tools of daily egg cash.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps every day to keep your layers producing and your cash flowing. Step 1: Early morning check. At 6am, open the house and check that all hens are active. Remove any dead birds immediately. Step 2: Feed and water. Give each hen 120g of layers mash. For 100 hens, that is 12kg per day. Put feed in clean troughs. Fill drinkers with fresh water. Step 3: Collect eggs. Eggs are usually laid in the morning. Walk through the house at 8am, 10am, and 2pm. Collect eggs in a clean bucket or tray. Do not leave eggs in the nest; they get dirty or broken. Step 4: Clean and sort. Wipe dirty eggs with a dry cloth. Never wash eggs with water; it removes the protective bloom and makes them spoil faster. Sort by size and colour. Step 5: Sell. Take eggs to your regular customers: shops, hotels, neighbours. Sell by the tray or singly. Keep a record of how many you sold and the price. Step 6: Afternoon check. At 4pm, check water again. Remove any wet litter. Step 7: Evening. At 6pm, give a small amount of feed if you use a split feeding system. Close the house to keep out predators. Step 8: Weekly tasks. Clean drinkers with disinfectant. Replace dirty litter. Vaccinate according to schedule. Step 9: Monthly tasks. Weigh a few hens to check condition. Count feed bags used. Adjust feed if eggs drop. Step 10: Record everything. Your record book tells you if you are making profit. If you follow these steps, your hens will lay 80 to 90 eggs per 100 hens daily. That is your daily cash.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many beginners lose money because of simple mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Feeding the wrong feed. Layers need layers mash with 16-18% protein and enough calcium. If you feed grower mash or maize alone, eggs drop or shells become soft. Fix: Buy proper layers mash from a reputable agrovet. Mistake 2: Dirty water. Hens refuse to drink dirty water, and then they stop laying. Fix: Wash drinkers daily with clean water and a little disinfectant. Mistake 3: Overcrowding. More than 4 hens per square metre causes stress, pecking, and disease. Fix: Provide at least 2 square metres per hen inside, and more if possible. Mistake 4: No vaccination. Newcastle disease can kill your whole flock in days. Fix: Follow a strict vaccination schedule: Newcastle at day 7, Gumboro at day 14, and boosters every 3 months. Mistake 5: Leaving eggs in nests. Eggs get broken, eaten, or dirty. Fix: Collect eggs at least three times a day. Mistake 6: Not keeping records. You cannot know your profit without records. Fix: Write down daily egg count, feed used, and sales. Mistake 7: Selling too cheaply. If you sell below cost, you lose money. Fix: Calculate your cost per egg: feed cost per day divided by eggs collected. Add a profit margin. Mistake 8: Ignoring predators. Snakes, rats, and hawks take eggs and chicks. Fix: Use wire mesh and close the house at night. Mistake 9: Using old feed. Mouldy feed causes disease. Fix: Store feed in dry, raised containers. Mistake 10: Giving up too early. Layers start laying at 18-20 weeks. Fix: Be patient and follow the routine. Avoid these mistakes and your daily egg cash will grow.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not just about you; it is about your hens, your customers, and your business. Always wear clean clothes and boots when entering the poultry house. Wash your hands before and after handling birds or eggs. Use a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance to prevent disease. Keep the house well ventilated but free from draughts. Ammonia from wet litter harms hens and workers. Remove wet litter daily and add dry wood shavings. Store feed in a cool, dry place, off the floor, to prevent mould and rats. Never mix new feed with old feed; use old feed first. Handle eggs gently. Cracked eggs sell for less or not at all. Use clean, dry egg trays. Do not wash eggs unless you are selling them immediately and will refrigerate them. For best practice, keep a closed flock. Do not bring in new birds without a 30-day quarantine. Limit visitors. If you must visit other farms, change clothes and boots before entering your house. Vaccinate on time. Keep a veterinary contact for emergencies. Sell eggs fresh. If you store eggs, keep them in a cool room, not in direct sun. In hot African climates, eggs lose quality fast. Sell within 3 days. Keep records of all treatments and sales. This helps you trace problems and prove quality to buyers. Finally, treat your workers fairly. Train them on safety and hygiene. A safe farm is a productive farm. Your daily cash depends on healthy hens and happy customers.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is time to put your knowledge into action. This exercise will help you plan your own daily egg sales system. Do this on paper or in a notebook. Step 1: Calculate your start-up cost. Decide how many hens you want to start with. For 20 hens, you need a house of about 40 square metres. List costs: chicks (20 x 5,000 shillings = 100,000), feed for 20 weeks (20 hens x 120g x 140 days = 336kg, about 7 bags at 30,000 = 210,000), vaccines and health (20,000), feeders and drinkers (30,000), egg trays (5,000). Total roughly 365,000 shillings. Adjust for your local prices. Step 2: Find your market. Talk to 5 potential customers: neighbours, a shop, a hotel, a school, a market vendor. Ask what price they pay per egg or per tray. Write down their answers. Step 3: Set your price. Calculate your cost per egg: daily feed cost for 20 hens (20 x 120g = 2.4kg, at 600 shillings per kg = 1,440 shillings). If you get 18 eggs, cost per egg is 80 shillings. Add 30% profit, so sell at 104 shillings. Round to 100 or 110. Step 4: Create a daily routine. Write a timetable: 6am open house, 6:30 feed and water, 8am collect eggs, 10am collect eggs, 2pm collect eggs, 4pm check water, 6pm close house. Step 5: Make a record sheet. Draw columns for date, eggs collected, eggs sold, price, feed used, and notes. Use it every day for one week. Step 6: Review. After one week, calculate your income and expenses. Did you make a profit? If not, find the problem: feed, price, or egg count. Adjust and try again. This exercise prepares you for the real business of daily egg cash.

<h2>Practical Reference: Daily Egg Sales in a Layer Flock System</h2>

<p>In a layer flock system, eggs are not a by-product they are the daily cash engine of the farm. Unlike broilers, which pay once at slaughter, layers convert feed into a saleable product every single morning. For small and medium-scale African poultry enterprises, this daily cash flow is what covers feed, labour, transport, and household needs without waiting for a lump sum at the end of a cycle. This Practical Reference consolidates the numbers, tools, and routines you need to run egg sales as a disciplined business, not a side activity.</p>

<p>The core principle is simple: <strong>a hen that lays today must be sold today or stored correctly today.</strong> Eggs are perishable, fragile, and highly sensitive to heat. In most African markets, demand is steady but margins are thin, so losses from breakage, spoilage, or under-pricing can erase a day's profit. Treat every egg as cash in a fragile shell.</p>

<h3 style="color:#a8324a;margin-top:0;"> Safety Warning: Egg Handling and Heat Risk</h3>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;"><strong>Never store eggs in direct sunlight or in a closed vehicle.</strong> In most African climates, ambient temperatures above 30C can cause egg quality to deteriorate within 2448 hours. Salmonella and other bacteria multiply rapidly in warm, dirty conditions.</p>
<ul style="margin:0;padding-left:20px;">
<li>Collect eggs at least twice daily morning and afternoon.</li>
<li>Discard cracked, leaking, or dirty eggs immediately; do not sell them.</li>
<li>Wash hands and use clean, dry trays. Never wash eggs with dirty water.</li>
<li>Store eggs in a shaded, ventilated room, off the floor, away from chemicals.</li>
<li>If refrigeration is unavailable, sell within 24 hours of collection.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;font-size:12px;color:#7a1f33;">Reference: WHO Five Keys to Safer Food; FAO, <em>Good Practices for the Egg Sector</em>.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist Daily Egg Sales Routine</h3>

<ol style="line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Morning collection:</strong> Collect eggs before 9 a.m., before heat builds up. Count and record.</li>
<li><strong>Grade and sort:</strong> Separate clean, intact eggs from cracked, dirty, or abnormal ones. Grade by size if your market pays by weight.</li>
<li><strong>Record production:</strong> Note eggs laid vs. eggs collected. A gap signals theft, predation, or hidden nests.</li>
<li><strong>Store correctly:</strong> Use clean trays in a shaded, ventilated space. Keep off the floor and away from strong smells.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm today's price:</strong> Check local market, hotel, or kiosk prices before selling. Do not guess.</li>
<li><strong>Sell and reconcile:</strong> Record eggs sold, cash received, and eggs remaining. Match cash to trays sold.</li>
<li><strong>Reinvest immediately:</strong> Use daily cash to buy feed, water treatment, or vaccines do not delay.</li>
<li><strong>End-of-day review:</strong> Note breakage, spoilage, unsold stock, and any customer feedback. Adjust tomorrow's plan.</li>
</ol>
