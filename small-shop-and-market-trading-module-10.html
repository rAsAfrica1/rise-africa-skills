1. Module 10 Lesson 1: Choosing the Right Location for Your Shop or Stall

Location is the single most important decision you will make as a small shop or market trader. A good location can make an average product sell quickly, while a poor location can make an excellent product sit on the shelf until it spoils. In many African markets, the difference between a profitable trader and one who struggles is often not the goods they sell but where they sell them. This lesson will help you think like a business person when choosing a spot, whether you are renting a kiosk, a container, a table in a market, or selling from a wheelbarrow or mat. Start by understanding your customer. Who will buy from you? Are they workers rushing to the bus park in the morning, mothers buying vegetables for the evening meal, students looking for airtime and snacks, or construction workers needing tools and food? Each group moves differently and buys at different times. A stall near a taxi rank does well with quick, cheap items like water, sweets, cigarettes, and phone credit. A spot near a residential area does well with household goods, fresh produce, and items people buy daily. A location near a school may sell snacks, exercise books, and pens. Write down the main customers you expect and what they need. Next, study the traffic. Foot traffic is the number of people who pass your spot. But not all traffic is equal. A hundred people passing quickly on their way to work may buy less than twenty people who walk slowly and stop to look. Watch the spot at different times of day. Morning, midday, evening, and weekend patterns can be very different. Count people for at least three days before you commit. Also check vehicle traffic if you sell bulky items like furniture, building materials, or sacks of maize. Can a customer park nearby? Can a supplier deliver to your door? If not, your costs rise and customers may go elsewhere. Competition is another factor. Some new traders fear competition, but a market with no competitors may have no customers either. If you sell the same thing as five other traders, you must compete on price, quality, or service. That is hard. A better approach is to sell something similar but different, or to offer better service, credit, or packaging. For example, if everyone sells loose cooking oil, you can sell it in clean, sealed bottles with a label. If everyone sells tomatoes, you can sell sorted tomatoes in small bowls ready for a meal. Look at what competitors are missing. Maybe they close early, or they do not give change quickly, or they are rude to customers. You can win by being open longer, being friendly, and being reliable. Rent and costs must be realistic. A prime spot may cost more, but it should bring more sales. Calculate how much you must sell each day just to cover rent, transport, licenses, and your own living costs. If the rent is too high, you will work hard and still make a loss. Negotiate the rent if possible. Ask about hidden costs like market association fees, security, electricity, water, and waste collection. In some markets, you must also pay local government levies. Know all these before you sign or agree. Accessibility matters. Can customers reach you easily? Is the path muddy in the rainy season? Is there shade from the sun? Is there light at night? Can you secure your goods when you close? A spot that floods or has no lockable door will cost you in damaged or stolen stock. Safety is also important. Avoid isolated spots where you or your customers may be robbed. Work in areas where other traders are nearby, because traders often help each other with security and information. Legal and official issues cannot be ignored. In many countries, you need a trading license, a health permit for food, or a market allocation letter. Selling without permission can lead to fines, confiscation, or forced removal. Visit the local council or market office to find out what is required. Keep your papers safe and pay your fees on time. A legal trader can grow and even get a loan, while an illegal one lives in fear. Finally, think about growth. Will this location still work in one year or three years? If you plan to add more products, do you have space? If you plan to buy a freezer or a sewing machine, is there electricity? If you plan to employ someone, can two people work there? Choose a spot that can grow with you. If you cannot find a perfect location, start with a smaller spot in a good area and expand later. Many successful traders began with a single table and moved to a shop. Before you commit, spend time at the spot. Talk to nearby traders. Ask them about sales, security, fees, and customer behavior. Visit at different times. Then make your decision with your eyes open. A good location is not just a place. It is a business asset. Treat it like one. Remember that location alone does not guarantee success. You still need good products, fair prices, and good service. But the right location gives you a fair chance. The wrong location makes every other part of your business harder. Choose carefully, and your shop or stall will have a strong foundation for growth.

2. Module 10 Lesson 2: Stock Selection and Buying for Small Shops and Market Stalls

What you stock and how you buy it decides whether you make a profit or just move goods around. Many small traders fail because they buy what they like, or what is cheap, instead of what customers will buy at a price that covers costs and leaves a margin. This lesson teaches you how to select stock and buy it well in an African context, where supply can be irregular, prices change quickly, and transport costs matter. Start with your customers. Your stock must match the people who pass your shop or stall. If you sell in a low-income area, expensive branded goods may sit for months. If you sell near offices, cheap low-quality items may not attract workers who want something that lasts. Make a list of the ten items your customers ask for most. These are your core products. Keep them in stock always. Then add a few items that customers ask for but you do not yet sell. These are your growth products. Finally, identify items that do not sell. Stop buying them, even if they look nice. Your money should work, not sleep on the shelf. Think about the range. Too few products and customers go elsewhere for the rest of their shopping. Too many products and your money is spread thin, and some items expire or go out of fashion. A good rule is to go deep in a few fast-moving items and wide only in items that customers expect to find together. For example, a small shop may stock many sizes of cooking oil, but only one or two brands of soap. A market food stall may stock many types of vegetables but only one type of salt. Group related items so customers can buy a full meal or a full task in one place. This increases the size of each sale. Quality is critical. Customers may forgive high prices if quality is good, but they will not forgive bad quality. Check every delivery. Open a bag of rice to smell it. Shake a bottle of drink to check for leaks. Look at expiry dates. Reject spoiled or damaged goods before you pay. If you buy from a wholesaler who refuses returns, you carry the loss. Build relationships with suppliers who stand behind their products. A good supplier will replace bad stock and warn you about price changes. Buying well means knowing your costs. For every item, calculate the landed cost. This includes the buying price, transport, loading and offloading, market fees, and any packaging you add. Then set a selling price that gives you a gross margin. A simple formula is selling price equals landed cost plus markup. In many small shops, a markup of twenty to fifty percent is common, but it depends on the product. Fast-moving staples like sugar and maize flour may have small margins because customers know the price. Luxury or convenience items can carry higher margins. Never guess your price. Write it down. Buying in bulk can lower your cost per item, but only if you can sell the bulk before it spoils or ties up your money. Perishable goods like tomatoes, fish, and milk must move fast. Non-perishable goods like soap, salt, and matches can be bought in larger quantities if you have safe storage. Consider group buying with other traders. When several shops or stalls combine orders, you can buy at wholesale prices and share transport. This is common in many African markets and can greatly improve your margin. But agree clearly on how to divide goods, who pays first, and what happens if someone fails to pay. Cash flow is the blood of a small business. Do not put all your money into stock. Keep a reserve for rent, transport, emergencies, and slow days. A common mistake is to buy too much stock and then have no cash to restock fast-selling items. Track your sales daily. Know which items finish first and which stay longest. Use a simple notebook or a phone app. Record what you bought, what you sold, and what is left. This record helps you buy better next time. Credit is a tool, not a gift. Many suppliers offer goods on credit, and many customers ask for credit. Use supplier credit only if you are sure you can sell the goods and repay on time. Customer credit can build loyalty, but it can also destroy your business if too many people owe you. Set clear rules. For example, credit only for regular customers, only up to a small amount, and only with a written record. Never give credit for perishable goods. If you must give credit, treat it as a loan and follow up politely but firmly. Seasonality affects stock. In many African countries, demand changes with rains, harvests, school terms, festivals, and holidays. During Ramadan, demand for dates, cooking oil, and sugar rises. During Christmas and New Year, demand for drinks, rice, and new clothes rises. During planting season, demand for seeds and fertilizer rises in farming areas. Plan ahead. Buy stock before the rush, but do not overbuy after the peak. After a festival, prices often fall, but customers may have less money. Adjust your stock to match. Storage protects your investment. Moisture, rats, insects, and sunlight destroy goods. Use pallets or shelves to keep goods off the floor. Keep food away from soap and chemicals. Store items in clean, dry containers. Rotate stock so older items sell first. This is called first in, first out. If you sell cold drinks, you need a fridge and a way to keep it running during power cuts. If you sell fresh produce, you need shade and ventilation. Good storage reduces waste, and waste is lost profit. Finally, review your stock every week. Ask yourself what sold well, what did not, what customers asked for, and what you will change next week. Buying is not a one-time decision. It is a cycle of listening, testing, and adjusting. The best small traders are not the ones with the most goods. They are the ones with the right goods at the right time, bought at the right price, and sold with a margin that keeps the business alive and growing.

3. Module 10 Lesson 3: Pricing, Costing, and Profit for Market Traders

Many small shop and market traders work hard all day and still have no money at the end of the month. Often the problem is not sales. It is pricing and costing. If you do not know your true costs, you cannot know your true profit. This lesson teaches you how to calculate costs, set prices, and protect your profit in an African trading environment where prices move, customers bargain, and hidden costs appear. Start with the difference between revenue, cost, and profit. Revenue is all the money you receive from sales. Cost is all the money you spend to buy goods and run the business. Profit is what remains after costs. If revenue is one hundred thousand shillings and costs are ninety thousand, profit is ten thousand. But many traders forget some costs. They count the buying price of goods but forget transport, market fees, rent, packaging, airtime, electricity, security, and their own food and transport. These are real costs. If you ignore them, you may think you are making profit when you are actually losing money. There are two types of costs. Direct costs are tied to each item you sell, such as the buying price, transport per item, and packaging. Indirect costs are fixed or shared, such as rent, licenses, and security. To price well, you must know both. A simple way is to calculate the landed cost per item. For example, if you buy a sack of rice for fifty thousand, pay five thousand transport, and two thousand market fees, your total cost is fifty-seven thousand. If the sack contains twenty-five bags, your landed cost per bag is two thousand two hundred and eighty. If you sell each bag for three thousand, your gross profit per bag is seven hundred and twenty. Multiply by twenty-five bags and your gross profit is eighteen thousand. From that, you must pay rent, your own transport, and other indirect costs. Only what remains is net profit. Markup and margin are different. Markup is the amount you add to cost. Margin is the percentage of the selling price that is profit. If an item costs one thousand and you sell it for one thousand five hundred, your markup is five hundred, or fifty percent of cost. Your margin is five hundred divided by one thousand five hundred, which is thirty-three percent. This matters because a fifty percent markup is not a fifty percent margin. Many traders confuse the two and overestimate their profit. Pricing is not just about cost. It is also about value. Customers pay more when they see value. Value can come from convenience, quality, cleanliness, packaging, credit, or friendly service. A trader who sells peeled and sliced pineapple near an office can charge more than one who sells whole pineapples at the market, because the customer saves time. A shop that opens early and closes late can charge slightly more because it is reliable. Look for ways to add value without adding much cost. Bargaining is common in many African markets. Set a minimum price below which you will not sell, and a starting price that gives room to negotiate. Know your minimum before the customer arrives. If you decide in the heat of the conversation, you may sell below cost. Practice saying no politely. A customer who walks away may return, but a sale at a loss always hurts you. For regular customers, you can offer a small discount or a free item, but only if it does not destroy your margin. Psychology matters in pricing. Prices ending in nine or five often feel cheaper. Round prices are easier for making change. In busy markets, customers value speed, so simple prices help. In some cultures, a price that is too low makes customers suspect the quality. A price that is too high drives them away. Watch what customers accept. Test small changes. If you raise the price of one item and sales continue, you have learned something. If sales fall sharply, you may have gone too far. Record your prices and review them when supplier prices change. Never absorb a cost increase silently. If your supplier raises prices by ten percent, you must either raise your price, reduce your packaging, or find a cheaper supplier. If you keep selling at the old price, your profit disappears. This is common with fuel, transport, and imported goods. Check your prices at least once a month. Promotions can increase sales, but they must be planned. A promotion that sells goods below cost brings customers but no profit. A better promotion is a bundle, such as buy two get a small item free, where the free item costs you little but feels valuable to the customer. Or offer a discount only on slow-moving stock to clear it and free your money. Always calculate the promotion before you announce it. Waste and theft are silent costs. If you sell food, unsold food at the end of the day is a loss. Track how much you throw away. Reduce it by buying less, preserving better, or selling at a discount near closing time. Theft by customers, employees, or even family members can eat your profit. Keep records, count stock regularly, and separate business money from personal money. If you take money from the till for personal use, record it as a withdrawal, not as a sale. Many small businesses fail because the owner treats the till as a personal wallet. Cash flow is not the same as profit. You can be profitable on paper but have no cash because customers owe you or because you bought too much stock. Keep a daily cash record. Know how much cash you have, how much you owe suppliers, and how much customers owe you. Pay yourself a small, fixed salary if possible. This keeps your personal and business money separate and shows the true health of the business. Finally, set profit goals. Decide how much profit you want each day, week, and month. Work backward to calculate how many items you must sell. If your goal is ten thousand profit per day and your margin per item is five hundred, you must sell twenty items. If you usually sell fifteen, you need to increase sales, raise margin, or reduce costs. This simple math turns hope into a plan. Pricing and costing are not complicated, but they require discipline. Write down your numbers. Review them often. Adjust when the market changes. A trader who knows their numbers will survive hard times and grow in good times.

4. Module 10 Lesson 4: Customer Care, Record Keeping, and Daily Business Discipline

A small shop or market stall can grow into a strong business if it is run with discipline. Two areas decide whether that happens: how you treat customers and how you keep records. This lesson covers both, with practical steps for African traders who may have limited education, limited time, and no expensive software. Customer care is not about being soft. It is about being reliable and respectful. Customers return to places where they feel welcome and where they get what they paid for. Greet every customer. In many African cultures, greeting is not optional. A simple good morning or how are you opens the door. Even if the customer does not buy today, they may return tomorrow. Listen more than you talk. Ask what they need. If you do not have it, say so honestly and, if possible, tell them when it will be available. Do not pretend to have something you do not have. That destroys trust. Be consistent. Open at the same time each day. Keep your prices clear. Give correct change. If you promise to keep an item for a customer, keep it. If you cannot, tell them early. Consistency builds a reputation that brings repeat customers. Handle complaints calmly. A customer who complains is giving you a chance to fix a problem. Listen without arguing. Apologize if you were wrong. Offer a fair solution, such as a replacement, a refund, or a discount on the next purchase. Never insult a customer, even if they are rude. In a market, word travels fast. One bad experience can cost you many future sales. Go the extra step when you can. Offer a bag for heavy items. Help an elderly customer carry goods to the taxi. Remember regular customers' names and what they usually buy. Small kindnesses cost little and create loyalty. But also protect yourself. Do not allow customers to take goods without payment unless you have a clear credit agreement. Do not allow a crowd to distract you while someone steals. Be friendly but alert. Record keeping is the memory of your business. Without records, you cannot know what you sold, what you spent, what you owe, or what you are owed. You do not need a computer. A simple exercise book and a pen are enough. Divide the book into sections. One section for daily sales. Write the date, items sold, quantity, price, and total. One section for purchases. Write what you bought, from whom, quantity, cost, and whether you paid cash or credit. One section for expenses. Write rent, transport, market fees, airtime, food, and any other cost. One section for debts. Write who owes you and who you owe. At the end of each day, total your sales and expenses. At the end of each week, review the totals. At the end of each month, calculate your profit. This simple habit will show you which products make money and which do not. It will also help you get a loan, because lenders want to see records. Keep your records safe from rain, fire, and theft. If you use a phone, back up your data or send a photo of the page to a trusted person. Separate business money from personal money. Use a cash box or a mobile money wallet for the business. When you take money for personal use, write it down as a withdrawal. This prevents the common problem of spending the stock money and then having nothing to restock. Count your cash at the start and end of each day. If the count does not match your records, investigate immediately. Small differences can become big losses if ignored. Stock control is part of record keeping. Count your stock regularly. Know how many items you have, how many you sold, and how many should remain. If the numbers do not match, you may have theft, damage, or a recording error. Find out which one. Use a simple stock card for each fast-moving item. Write the date, quantity in, quantity out, and balance. This helps you reorder at the right time and avoid running out of best sellers. Time discipline matters. Open and close at set times. Use the quiet hours to clean, arrange stock, update records, and plan purchases. Do not close early just because sales are slow. A customer who finds you closed may not come back. If you must leave, ask a trusted person to watch the stall, and leave clear instructions. Plan your day. Know what you will buy, what you will sell, and what tasks you must complete. A short list each morning keeps you focused. Money discipline is the hardest part. It is easy to spend business money on personal needs, especially when family or friends ask. Decide on a small, fixed salary for yourself and stick to it. If the business is new, you may need to take very little at first. Explain to your family that the business money is for the business. This is not selfish. It is how the business survives and grows to support them later. Build a small emergency fund. Save a little each week, even if it is very small. This fund helps you when a supplier raises prices, when a customer does not pay, or when you or a family member is sick. Without savings, one shock can close your shop. Review your business every month. Ask yourself what went well, what went wrong, and what you will change. Look at your best-selling items, your worst-selling items, your biggest expenses, and your debts. Set one or two clear goals for the next month. For example, increase sales of a slow item, reduce transport costs, or pay off a supplier debt. Write these goals down and check them weekly. Finally, treat your business as a business, not as a hobby or a fallback. Dress neatly. Keep your stall clean. Speak professionally. Arrive on time. These habits attract customers and respect. They also attract opportunities, such as supply contracts, loans, and partnerships. A disciplined trader with good records and happy customers can grow from a small table to a shop, from a shop to a store, and from a store to a distribution business. It starts with daily habits, not big dreams alone.

5. Module 10 Lesson 5: Growing Your Shop or Market Business in Africa

Once your shop or market stall is stable, the next question is how to grow. Growth does not always mean a bigger shop. It can mean more customers, more products, higher margins, or new ways of selling. This lesson gives practical growth strategies for African traders who want to move from survival to real business success. The first step is to strengthen what you already have. Before you expand, make sure your current operation is profitable and organized. Review your records. Are you making a real profit after all costs? Do you have reliable suppliers? Are your customers returning? If the answer is no, fix these first. Growth on a weak foundation will collapse. Increase sales to existing customers. It is easier and cheaper to sell more to a current customer than to find a new one. Offer related products. If you sell bread, add butter, jam, and tea. If you sell phone accessories, add chargers, earphones, and screen protectors. If you sell vegetables, add spices, onions, and cooking oil. This increases the average amount each customer spends. Train yourself and your workers to suggest one extra item politely. This is called upselling, and it works when it is helpful, not pushy. Add new products carefully. Test a small quantity first. If it sells, buy more. If it does not, stop. Do not tie up all your money in a new product just because you like it. Ask customers what they want that you do not have. Their answers are free market research. Also watch what competitors sell successfully. You do not need to copy everything, but you can learn from what works. Improve your margins. Look for cheaper suppliers without reducing quality. Buy in larger quantities when you can sell them. Reduce waste. Negotiate transport costs. Package goods in a way that adds value without adding much cost. For example, repackaging rice or sugar into clean, sealed bags can allow you to charge more than loose goods. If you sell food, improve hygiene and presentation. Customers pay more for clean, attractive food. Extend your hours or days. If your customers work during the day, open earlier or later. If you close on Sundays, test opening for a few hours. If the market is busy on certain days, make sure you are there. Small changes in availability can bring significant sales. Use mobile money and digital tools. In many African countries, mobile money is now common. Accepting mobile payments can increase sales because customers do not need cash. It also gives you a record of transactions. A simple phone can help you track sales, send messages to customers, and order stock. If you have internet, use social media to show your products. Even a WhatsApp status with today's prices can bring customers. Build a customer list. Write down phone numbers of regular customers, with their permission. Send them a message when you get new stock or when you have a special offer. Do not send too many messages, or they will block you. One or two useful messages a month is enough. This turns a market stall into a relationship business. Consider partnerships. Two or three traders can share transport, buy in bulk, or split a large order. A shop can partner with a delivery rider to reach customers who cannot come to the market. A food stall can partner with an office to supply lunch daily. Partnerships work when the terms are clear and written down, even in a simple notebook. Train and trust workers. If you employ someone, train them well. Teach them your prices, your standards, and how to treat customers. Pay them fairly and on time. A good worker can run the shop while you buy stock or open a second location. A bad worker can destroy your business. Start with one person and watch carefully. Give clear duties and check their work. Save and reinvest. Growth requires money. Save a portion of your profit each week. Use savings to buy more stock, improve your stall, or open a second location. Avoid taking large loans for expansion until you are sure of your sales. If you do take a loan, borrow only what you can repay from expected profit, and keep a reserve for slow months. Many small businesses fail because they borrowed too much too soon. Open a second location only when the first is stable. A second shop or stall doubles your costs and your management work. Make sure you have a trusted person to run it and that your records can track both locations separately. If you cannot manage two, consider a mobile selling route instead. A wheelbarrow, bicycle, or motorbike can take your goods to customers who do not come to the market. This can be cheaper than renting another stall. Go where the customers are. Markets, bus parks, schools, churches, mosques, and construction sites are all places where people gather. If your current location is quiet at certain times, take your goods to a busy place during those hours. This is common with food, drinks, and phone accessories. Protect your reputation. As you grow, more people will know your name. Keep your quality high. Pay your debts on time. Treat suppliers and customers fairly. In many African business communities, reputation is more valuable than advertising. A trader known for honesty can get credit, partnerships, and loyal customers that others cannot. Plan for risks. Theft, fire, flooding, sickness, currency changes, and sudden price increases can hurt any business. Keep some savings. Insure your stock if insurance is available and affordable. Keep copies of your records in a safe place. Have a plan for who will run the business if you are sick or away. Do not depend on one supplier or one product. Diversity reduces risk. Finally, think long term. What do you want this business to become in five years? A larger shop? A wholesale business? A small chain? A farm-to-market supplier? Write down your vision. Then break it into yearly, monthly, and weekly goals. Review your progress honestly. Celebrate small wins. Learn from mistakes. Ask for advice from successful traders, business associations, or local enterprise support programs. Many governments and NGOs offer training and small loans for traders. Use them. Growth is not luck. It is the result of good habits, careful planning, and consistent effort. Your shop or stall can be more than a daily hustle. It can be a real business that supports your family, employs others, and serves your community. Start where you are, use what you have, and grow step by step.

This Practical Reference lesson is the working toolkit for Module 10. Everything here is designed to be printed, pinned to the wall of the shop, or copied into the back of the exercise book you use for daily records. In small-scale retail across Africa, the difference between a business that survives and one that closes is rarely the size of the capital. It is the discipline of daily control: knowing exactly what came in, what went out, what is left, and what needs replacing before customers notice.

Use this page alongside Lessons 1 to 5. SAFETY WARNING FOOD, WATER AND CHEMICALS

If your shop or stall sells food, drinks, or any chemical product, the following rules are not optional. They protect your customers, your family and your licence to trade.

  • Never store pesticides, paraffin, bleach or other chemicals next to food, drinks or cooking oil. Vapours and spills can contaminate food even through packaging. Use a separate shelf, a separate cupboard, or a separate table.
  • Never sell food that has been in contact with flood water, sewage, dead animals or chemicals. Discard it. Do not wash it and re-sell it.
  • Keep drinking water and bottled drinks out of direct sunlight and off the floor. Heat speeds spoilage and can leach chemicals from some plastics.
  • Wash hands with soap before handling food, after using the toilet, and after handling money. Money carries bacteria. Use tongs or a clean scoop for bread, meat, fish and cooked food.
  • Cover all cooked and ready-to-eat food. Keep raw meat, poultry and fish separate from cooked food and from vegetables.
  • Check expiry dates every week. Remove expired stock from the shelf immediately and record the loss. Never re-label or re-date a product.
  • If you sell veterinary drugs or agricultural chemicals, follow the label and keep a disposal plan. Never repackage chemicals into drink bottles or food containers.

Sources: WHO, Five Keys to Safer Food; FAO/WHO, Codex Alimentarius General Principles of Food Hygiene; MSD Animal Health and national veterinary authorities for veterinary product handling.

How to use these tables in a real trading day

Morning, before the first customer. Count your float and write it down. Walk your shelves and note what is low. Check the expiry dates on the front row of every food and drink item. If you bought fresh produce yesterday, inspect it now: remove anything spoiled before it contaminates the rest.

During trading. Every sale goes into the book, even the smallest one. If you sell on credit, write the name, the amount and the date. Do not rely on memory. In busy markets, traders who record every sale typically find 510% more profit at the end of the month than those who do not, because leaks become visible.

Midday reconciliation. Count the cash in the till and compare it to the sales you have recorded plus your opening float. If the numbers do not match, find out why before the day ends. Common causes are: giving change incorrectly, a sale not recorded, a family member taking money, or a customer credit not written down.

Afternoon restocking. Buy only what you can sell within a realistic period. For perishables, that may be one day. For dry goods, one to two weeks. Rotate stock so that older items are sold first. This is called FIFO First In, First Out and it is one of the simplest ways to reduce losses.

Evening close. Count cash again. Separate your business money from your household money. Pay yourself a fixed daily or weekly amount, and record it as a business expense. Bank or save the surplus. If you have a mobile money float, reconcile it against your SMS records before you sleep.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Float counted and recorded before the first sale of the day.
  2. Every sale recorded in the book, including small sales and credit sales.
  3. Cash reconciled at least once during the day and again at closing.
  4. Expiry dates checked on all food, drinks and medicines; expired stock removed and recorded.
  5. Chemicals stored separately from food, drinks and cooking oil, with lids closed.
  6. Hands washed and food covered before handling any ready-to-eat product.
  7. Stock rotated so older items are sold first; low stock noted for tomorrow's buying.
  8. Business money separated from household money; owner's pay recorded as an expense.
  9. Credit list updated with names, amounts and dates; follow up on overdue credit weekly.
  10. Closing cash and mobile money balance written down and compared to records before locking up.

Common mistakes that quietly kill small shops

Mixing the till with the household purse. When the same pocket pays for stock and school fees, you cannot tell whether the business is profitable. Separate containers, separate mobile money wallets, separate records.

Buying in bulk to get a discount, without checking turnover. A discount on 50 bags of flour is worthless if you can only sell 5 bags a week and the rest spoils or ties up your cash. Buy for turnover first, discount second.

Trusting memory for credit. Even honest customers forget. A simple credit book with name, date, amount and a reminder date protects both of you.

Ignoring small losses. A spilled litre of oil, three cracked eggs, a bar of soap that walked away these add up to a significant share of profit over a month. Record them, then reduce them.

Selling expired or contaminated food. This is the fastest way to lose customers, invite a health inspection, and in serious cases cause harm. The reputational and legal cost is far greater than the value of the stock.
