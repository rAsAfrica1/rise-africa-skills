1. Storage Requirements: Temperature and Humidity

Proper storage is critical to reduce post-harvest losses, which can reach 30-50% in Africa if onions are not stored well. Onions store best at 0-4C with 65-70% relative humidity. However, in most African farms, cold storage is expensive and unreliable. Instead, you can use improved natural ventilation storage. Cure onions first: after harvest, leave them in the field for 2-3 days to dry, then move to a shaded, well-ventilated area for 2-3 weeks until necks are dry and skins rustle. For small-scale storage, use raised wooden pallets or shelves in a dark, dry room. Ensure good airflow: space onions in thin layers (no more than 15 cm deep) or hang in mesh bags. Avoid direct sunlight and rain. In humid areas, use a fan or dehumidifier if possible. Check regularly for soft or sprouting bulbs and remove them immediately. For larger scale, consider a solar-powered cold room; many African cooperatives share such facilities. Ideal storage life: 3-6 months for well-cured onions. Without proper storage, onions may sprout, rot, or lose weight. Keep rodents and insects out by sealing gaps and using traps, not poison near food. Remember: temperature and humidity control are your main tools to maintain quality and price.

2. Common Storage Diseases and Pests

Even with good storage, onions can be attacked by diseases and pests. The most common fungal diseases are neck rot (Botrytis), black mold (Aspergillus niger), and blue mold (Penicillium). Neck rot appears as soft, watery spots near the neck, often with gray mold. Black mold causes black powdery patches on outer scales. These spread in warm, humid conditions. To prevent, ensure onions are well-cured and stored dry. Remove infected bulbs immediately. Bacterial diseases like soft rot (Erwinia) cause foul-smelling, mushy bulbs; avoid wounding onions during harvest. Pests include onion thrips (tiny insects that cause silvery streaks), mites (which cause surface damage), and rodents. Thrips can be controlled by good ventilation and by hanging yellow sticky traps. For mites, keep humidity low. Rodents: use traps and keep storage area clean. In Africa, a common problem is the onion fly (Delia antiqua) in storage? Actually, onion fly is field pest, but in storage, beetles like the cigarette beetle can infest. Use diatomaceous earth (food grade) sprinkled around storage area to deter insects. Never use chemicals directly on onions intended for consumption. For organic control, neem leaves or garlic sprays can repel some pests. Regularly inspect stored onions every two weeks. Sort out soft, sprouting, or moldy bulbs. If you find many infected bulbs, sell them quickly for processing (e.g., puree) rather than losing all. Proper curing and dry storage are your best defenses.

3. Controlled Atmosphere Storage

Controlled atmosphere (CA) storage involves adjusting oxygen, carbon dioxide, and nitrogen levels to extend onion shelf life. Typically, onions are stored at 0-4C with 1-3% oxygen and 5-10% carbon dioxide. This slows respiration, sprouting, and decay. However, CA storage is expensive and requires airtight rooms and gas control equipment. For most African smallholders, this is not feasible. But you can mimic some benefits with modified atmosphere packaging (MAP). Use perforated plastic bags or liners that allow some gas exchange but reduce oxygen. For example, store cured onions in 5-10 kg perforated polyethylene bags; this can extend storage by 2-4 weeks. Another low-cost method is vacuum storage: seal onions in airtight containers with a small oxygen absorber packet. This is practical for small quantities for home use or high-value markets. In Kenya and Nigeria, some commercial farms use solar-powered cold rooms with CA for export. If you are a cooperative, consider investing in a shared CA facility. But for most, focus on proper curing, ventilation, and cool, dry storage. CA is not a replacement for good curing. Always cure onions first. Also, different onion varieties respond differently; red onions often store longer than white. Test small batches before scaling up. Remember, CA requires monitoring: check gas levels daily and remove any spoiled bulbs. If you cannot afford CA, don't worry; good traditional storage can still give you 3-5 months.

4. Market Channels: Wholesale, Retail, and Direct

Selling onions profitably means choosing the right market channel. Wholesale: You sell large quantities to traders or wholesalers at farm gate or local markets. Price is lower but you sell fast. In many African countries, wholesale prices fluctuate; for example, in Nigeria, a 100 kg bag may sell for 20,000-40,000 Naira depending on season. Retail: You sell directly to consumers in small quantities at markets or roadside stalls. You get higher prices but need time and transport. A 1 kg bunch may sell for 500-1000 Naira in cities. Direct: Selling to restaurants, hotels, schools, or through community-supported agriculture (CSA) or online platforms. You can negotiate contracts and get better prices. For example, a restaurant may buy 50 kg weekly at a premium. To access these, form relationships and ensure consistent quality. Cooperatives can aggregate produce to meet large orders. Also consider selling to processors for value-added products (see lesson 6). Key tips: grade onions by size and quality; uniform onions fetch higher prices. Use clean, labeled bags. For retail, display attractively. For wholesale, ensure timely delivery. In Africa, mobile money (M-Pesa, Airtel Money) simplifies payments. Always have a backup channel: if wholesale prices crash, retail or direct can save you. Keep records of sales to identify best channels. Join farmer groups to share transport and market information. Remember, consistent supply and quality build trust and repeat business.

5. Pricing Strategies and Market Analysis

Pricing onions right is key to profit. First, know your costs: seeds, fertilizer, labor, storage, transport, and packaging. Add a 20-30% profit margin. But market forces matter. Do market analysis: visit local markets, check prices on radio, or use apps like Esoko or M-Farm. In East Africa, onion prices peak in dry seasons when supply is low. For example, in Tanzania, prices may double from harvest to off-season. Strategies: 1) Cost-plus pricing: calculate total cost per kg and add margin. 2) Competitive pricing: match or slightly undercut nearby sellers. 3) Value-based pricing: if your onions are organic or premium, charge more. 4) Dynamic pricing: adjust prices based on demand (higher on weekends). 5) Bulk discounts: encourage large buyers. Also consider storage: if you can store until prices rise, you gain more. But storage costs and risks exist. Use a simple break-even analysis: if storage costs 1000 Naira per bag per month and price rises by 5000 Naira after two months, it's worth it. Keep records of prices over time to predict trends. Join cooperatives to negotiate better prices collectively. Avoid panic selling at harvest when prices are lowest. Instead, sell part and store part. For direct markets, you can set higher prices due to freshness and traceability. Always communicate value: tell customers your onions are well-cured and chemical-free. Use social media (WhatsApp, Facebook) to advertise. Bartering is common; know your bottom line. Finally, be flexible; prices change, so review weekly.

6. Value-Added Products: Powders, Pickles, and Sets

Turning onions into value-added products increases income and reduces waste. Onion powder: Dehydrate sliced onions in a solar dryer or oven at 50-60C for 6-8 hours until brittle. Grind into powder using a blender or mill. Store in airtight jars. 1 kg fresh onions yields about 100-150 g powder. Sell in 50g or 100g packets to supermarkets or online. Onion pickles: Slice onions, mix with vinegar, salt, sugar, and spices (e.g., turmeric, chili). Pack in sterilized jars. Pickles last 6-12 months. A 500g jar can sell for 3-5 times the price of fresh onions. Onion sets: Grow small bulbs (sets) for planting. Harvest when bulbs are 1-2 cm diameter, cure, and store. Sell to other farmers as planting material. Sets fetch high prices because they save farmers time. In Africa, demand for sets is growing. Also consider onion paste or puree: blend onions, cook, and package in sachets; sell to restaurants. For all products, ensure hygiene: wash hands, sterilize equipment, use clean water. Label with production and expiry dates. Get necessary permits from local authorities. Packaging matters: use attractive, sealed bags or jars. Market via local shops, social media, and farmer markets. Start small, test demand, then scale. Value-added products can double or triple your income from the same harvest. They also use smaller or damaged onions that would otherwise be discarded. Train yourself via online videos or local workshops. With simple tools, you can process onions at home. Remember, consistency in quality builds reputation.

<h2>Practical Reference: Onion Storage &amp; Marketing in Africa</h2>

<p>Onion farming in Africa rewards those who treat storage and marketing as a business, not an afterthought. Across the continent, post-harvest losses for onions can reach 3050% when curing, ventilation and pest control are poorly managed. That is lost revenue. This practical reference pulls together the operational numbers, disease threats, storage technologies and market routes that determine whether your harvest becomes profit or waste. Use it as a field-side decision tool: check temperature and humidity, inspect bulbs weekly, choose your market channel deliberately, and price with knowledge of what buyers actually pay.</p>

<p>Onions are a high-value, semi-perishable crop. Unlike grains, they continue to respire and can be stored for several months if cured and ventilated correctly. In West Africa, the dry season harvest from the Sahel (e.g., Sokoto, Kano, Diffa) supplies southern markets for months. In East Africa, Kenyan and Tanzanian onions move to Nairobi, Mombasa and Kampala. In Southern Africa, South African and Zambian production feeds regional trade. Your storage strategy should match your target market: fresh bulbs for urban retail need different handling than bulbs destined for dehydration or seed-set production.</p>

<h3>Storage Requirements: Temperature and Humidity</h3>
<p>Onions are best stored after proper curing: 1015 days of drying in the field or under shade until the neck is tight and outer scales are papery. For long-term storage, target 05C with 6570% relative humidity. In most African smallholder and medium-scale operations, refrigeration is costly or unavailable, so ambient ventilated storage is common. In that case, keep bulbs below 30C and 6070% RH, with strong night ventilation. High humidity encourages neck rot and bacterial soft rot; low humidity causes weight loss and shrivelling. Avoid storing onions near ethylene-producing crops (e.g., ripe bananas, tomatoes), which can trigger sprouting.</p>

<p>Table 1 summarises practical storage targets for different market intentions. Use a simple thermometer and hygrometer (available in most agricultural input shops) to monitor conditions daily. In the Sahel, night temperatures can drop enough for passive cooling; in humid coastal zones, forced ventilation or raised crates are essential.</p>

<h3>Common Storage Diseases and Pests</h3>
<p>The most damaging storage diseases in African onion systems are neck rot (<em>Botrytis</em> spp.), bacterial soft rot (<em>Erwinia</em> / <em>Pectobacterium</em>), black mould (<em>Aspergillus niger</em>), and fusarium basal rot. Insect pests include onion thrips (<em>Thrips tabaci</em>) and the onion fly. Rodents and birds can also cause losses in poorly sealed stores.</p>

<p>Prevention starts in the field: avoid excess nitrogen, control thrips, stop irrigation 23 weeks before harvest, and cure thoroughly. In storage, remove and destroy infected bulbs immediately; do not compost them near the store. Keep crates off the floor and away from walls to allow airflow. In humid conditions, consider a weak bleach solution (1%) for cleaning store surfaces before loading.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #c62828;background:#ffebee;padding:12px;margin:16px 0;">
<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong> Never store onions in the same room as pesticides, herbicides, or fertilisers. Onion bulbs absorb odours and chemical residues, making them unsafe for sale and consumption. Always follow label instructions for any post-harvest chemical treatment, and wear gloves and a mask when handling fungicides or fumigants. In confined stores, ensure adequate ventilation before entering carbon dioxide and fumigant vapours can be lethal.
</div>

<h3>Controlled Atmosphere Storage</h3>
<p>Controlled atmosphere (CA) storage reduces oxygen (to 13%) and increases carbon dioxide (to 510%), slowing respiration and sprouting. In Africa, CA is rare for onions due to capital and energy costs, but modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) is emerging for premium retail. For most commercial growers, the practical alternative is improved natural ventilation: raised slatted floors, chimney vents, and careful stacking. In Kenya and South Africa, some large operations use cold rooms with ethylene scrubbing for export-grade onions. If you cannot afford CA, invest in curing and airflow first they deliver 7080% of the benefit at 10% of the cost.</p>

<h3>Pricing Strategies and Market Analysis</h3>
<p>Onion prices in Africa are seasonal and volatile. In many markets, prices peak 35 months after harvest when local supply declines. If you can store safely, you can capture 3080% higher prices. However, storage costs, weight loss and spoilage must be subtracted. A simple break-even: if you lose 10% of bulbs and spend 5% of value on storage, you need a price increase of at least 15% to profit.</p>

<p>Track prices weekly from at least three sources: local wholesale market, a nearby urban retail market, and a regional price bulletin (e.g., FEWS NET, AMIS, national bureau of statistics). Calculate your cost of production per kilogram, then set a floor price. For direct sales, consider bundling: 1 kg, 5 kg, and 25 kg nets. For wholesale, grade by size (e.g., 4060 mm, 6080 mm) because buyers pay premiums for uniform bulbs. Contracts with processors can reduce risk but usually offer lower prices; use them for surplus or off-grade onions, not your best stock.</p>

<h3>Value-Added Products: Powders, Pickles, and Sets</h3>
<p>Value addition turns small or damaged bulbs into higher-margin products. Onion powder: slice, dry at 5060C for 812 hours, mill and package. A 10 kg batch of fresh onions yields roughly 11.5 kg of powder, which can sell for 510 times the fresh price per kilogram. Pickled onions: use a brine of vinegar, salt and sugar; package in sterilised jars. This requires food safety compliance and labelling. Onion sets: grow bulbs to 1525 mm, cure and sell to other farmers as planting material; this is a high-value niche in West and East Africa.</p>

<p>Before investing in value addition, secure a buyer. Restaurants, hotels, and supermarkets are common customers for powder and pickles. Check national food safety regulations (e.g., KEBS in Kenya, NAFDAC in Nigeria, SABS in South Africa) for packaging and labelling requirements.</p>
