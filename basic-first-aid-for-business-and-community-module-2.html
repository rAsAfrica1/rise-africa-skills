1. Recognizing Cardiac Arrest and Agonal Breathing

Cardiac arrest happens when the heart suddenly stops pumping blood. In Africa, it often occurs outside hospitals, in homes, markets, and workplaces. Every minute without CPR reduces survival by about 10%. You must act fast. The first step is to check for responsiveness. Tap the person firmly on the shoulder and shout, 'Are you okay?' If they do not respond, they may be in cardiac arrest. Next, check for normal breathing. Look at the chest for 5 to 10 seconds. A person in cardiac arrest may have no breathing or only gasping. Gasping, also called agonal breathing, looks like occasional snoring, gurgling, or slow, irregular gasps. It is not normal breathing. Many people mistake gasping for normal breathing and delay CPR. Do not be fooled. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, assume cardiac arrest. In Africa, common causes include heart attacks, electrocution, drowning, and severe allergic reactions. Business owners and community members should be trained to recognize these signs. Remember: unresponsive + no normal breathing = start CPR. Call for help immediately. If you are alone, use your mobile phone to call emergency services on speaker while you begin CPR. In many African countries, emergency numbers vary: 112 in Kenya, 10111 in South Africa, 199 in Nigeria. Know your local number. Do not waste time checking for a pulse; it is unreliable even for trained professionals. The key is to recognize the absence of normal breathing and act. Time is heart muscle. Every second counts.

2. Hands-Only CPR Technique for Adults

Hands-only CPR is CPR without rescue breaths. It is recommended for untrained bystanders or those unwilling to give breaths. It is simple, effective, and easy to remember. For adults, hands-only CPR can double or triple survival chances. Here is how to do it. First, ensure the scene is safe. Then, tap the person and shout. If no response and no normal breathing, call for help. If someone else is around, ask them to call emergency services and find an AED. If you are alone, call on speakerphone and start CPR. Position the person on their back on a firm, flat surface. Kneel beside their chest. Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, between the nipples. Place your other hand on top and interlock your fingers. Keep your arms straight, shoulders directly above your hands. Push hard and fast. Compress at least 5 cm (2 inches) deep. Push at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute. That is about 2 compressions per second. Allow the chest to come back up fully after each compression. Do not stop until help arrives, an AED is ready, or you are too exhausted to continue. In Africa, many people fear causing harm, but doing nothing is worse. Hands-only CPR is safe and does not require mouth-to-mouth. It is perfect for busy markets, offices, and community gatherings. Practice on a pillow or a CPR manikin if available. The rhythm of the song 'Stayin' Alive' by the Bee Gees is about 100 beats per minuteuse it to keep pace. Remember: push hard, push fast, and do not stop.

3. Chest Compressions: Depth, Rate, and Recoil

Chest compressions are the heart of CPR. They manually pump blood to the brain and vital organs. To be effective, you must get three things right: depth, rate, and recoil. Depth: For an average adult, push down at least 5 cm (2 inches) but not more than 6 cm (2.4 inches). If you are a small person, use your body weight to help. Too shallow and blood flow is poor; too deep and you may break ribs. Rib fractures are a risk, but they are better than death. Rate: Aim for 100 to 120 compressions per minute. That is faster than one per second. Count out loud: 'One and two and three and...' or use a metronome app. Many smartphones have free metronome apps. The song 'Stayin' Alive' has the correct beat. Recoil: After each compression, let the chest come all the way back up. Do not lean on the chest. This allows the heart to refill with blood. If you do not allow full recoil, blood flow decreases. In Africa, where ambulances may be far away, high-quality compressions are even more critical. You may need to continue for 10, 20, or 30 minutes. Switch with another rescuer every 2 minutes to avoid fatigue. Fatigue leads to shallow compressions. If you are alone, keep going as long as you can. Remember: 5 cm deep, 100-120 per minute, full recoil. Practice on a firm surface. Do not be afraid to push hard. The life you save could be a family member, a colleague, or a customer.

4. Rescue Breaths: When and How to Provide

Rescue breaths, also called mouth-to-mouth, provide oxygen to a person who is not breathing. In adult CPR, the current recommendation is to start with 30 chest compressions before giving 2 rescue breaths. This is called the 30:2 ratio. However, if you are untrained or uncomfortable, hands-only CPR is still effective. Rescue breaths are especially important for drowning, electrocution, and drug overdose, where lack of oxygen is the main problem. Here is how to give them. First, open the airway. Place one hand on the forehead and two fingers under the chin. Tilt the head back gently. This lifts the tongue away from the back of the throat. Pinch the nose shut with your thumb and index finger. Take a normal breath, not a deep one. Seal your mouth over the person's mouth. Blow steadily for about 1 second. Watch for the chest to rise. If it rises, give a second breath. If it does not rise, re-tilt the head and try again. Do not blow too hard or too fast; it can cause stomach inflation. After 2 breaths, immediately resume compressions. Continue cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths. In Africa, concerns about diseases like HIV and TB may make you hesitant. Use a CPR face shield or pocket mask if available. If not, you can still do hands-only CPR. The risk of transmission is very low. If you are unwilling or unable to give breaths, do not stop compressions. Something is better than nothing. Remember: 30 compressions, 2 breaths, repeat.

5. Using an AED: Pad Placement and Safety

An AED, or Automated External Defibrillator, is a portable device that checks the heart rhythm and can send an electric shock to restart the heart. It is safe and easy to use, even for untrained people. AEDs are increasingly available in African airports, malls, and large offices. If you see one, use it. First, turn on the AED. It will give you voice prompts. Expose the person's chest. Dry it if wet. Remove any medication patches. If the person has a lot of chest hair, shave it quickly with a razor from the AED kit. Place one pad on the upper right side of the chest, just below the collarbone. Place the other pad on the lower left side, below the nipple, on the side of the chest. The pads have pictures to guide you. Plug the connector into the AED if not already connected. The AED will analyze the heart rhythm. Do not touch the person during analysis. If a shock is advised, the AED will charge. Shout 'Clear!' and make sure no one is touching the person. Press the shock button. Immediately resume chest compressions after the shock. If no shock is advised, continue CPR. The AED will re-analyze every 2 minutes. Keep the pads on. In Africa, AEDs may be rare, but if one is available, it greatly improves survival. Do not be afraid to use it. It will not shock a normal heart. Remember: turn on, attach pads, clear, shock if advised, resume CPR.

6. Team CPR and Switching Roles

Cardiac arrest is a team effort. When more than one rescuer is available, you can provide better CPR with less fatigue. The key is to switch roles every 2 minutes, or sooner if tired. High-quality compressions require energy. After 2 minutes, even a strong person starts to push less deeply. So, plan your team. One person does compressions. Another gives rescue breaths. A third calls emergency services and finds an AED. A fourth can manage the crowd and keep time. If you have only two rescuers, one does compressions while the other gives breaths. Switch every 2 minutes. The switch should be quickless than 5 seconds. To switch smoothly, the rescuer doing compressions says, 'I'm getting tired, switch on my next count.' Then, at the end of a cycle, the new rescuer kneels on the opposite side, places hands on the chest, and continues. The old rescuer moves to the breathing position. If you are alone, you cannot switch, but you can call for help. In African communities, teamwork is natural. Use it. Appoint a timekeeper with a phone timer. Rotate roles. Keep encouraging each other. After the ambulance arrives, give a clear handover: what happened, when CPR started, how many shocks, and any changes. Remember: 2-minute switches, clear communication, and support. Team CPR saves more lives than solo CPR. Practice with your colleagues, family, or community group. You never know when you will need it.

<h2>Practical Reference: CPR and AED for Adults</h2>

<p>Sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) is a leading cause of death worldwide, and outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa are worsened by long emergency response times, limited ambulance coverage, and low bystander CPR rates. For businesses, this is not only a health issue but an operational and reputational one: a collapse in a workshop, bank, factory, market, church, or community hall can become a fatality within minutes if no one acts. This practical reference gives African business owners, safety officers, and community responders a clear, field-ready standard for adult CPR and AED use, aligned with international resuscitation guidelines and adapted to the realities of low-resource settings.</p>

<h3>Why this matters for African businesses</h3>
<p>In many African cities, ambulance response can exceed 2030 minutes, and in rural areas it may be hours or unavailable. Workplaces, markets, schools, and places of worship therefore become the first and often only link in the chain of survival. Bystander CPR can double or triple survival from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. For a business, a trained response team protects lives, reduces liability, and keeps operations running when an emergency occurs.</p>

<h3>Recognizing cardiac arrest and agonal breathing</h3>
<p>Cardiac arrest is recognised by unresponsiveness and absent or abnormal breathing. Agonal breathinggasping, snoring, or irregular slow breathsis common in the first minutes and is often mistaken for normal breathing. Treat any unresponsive adult with no normal breathing as being in cardiac arrest and start CPR immediately. Do not delay to feel for a pulse; lay rescuers are not required to check it.</p>

<h3>Hands-only CPR technique for adults</h3>
<p>Hands-only CPR (compression-only) is recommended for untrained bystanders and is effective for adult sudden collapse. It removes hesitation about rescue breaths and is easier to teach and remember. Place the heel of one hand on the centre of the chest, the other hand on top, interlock fingers, keep arms straight, and push hard and fast. For trained responders, conventional CPR with breaths is preferred, especially if the collapse was witnessed and not asphyxial.</p>

<h3>Chest compressions: depth, rate, and recoil</h3>
<p>High-quality compressions are the foundation of CPR. Push at least 5 cm but not more than 6 cm deep, at a rate of 100120 per minute, allowing full chest recoil between compressions. Minimise interruptions to less than 10 seconds. In African heat and humidity, rotate compressors every 2 minutes to maintain quality; fatigue degrades depth and rate quickly.</p>

<h3>Rescue breaths: when and how to provide</h3>
<p>Rescue breaths are recommended for trained responders, for paediatric arrests, and for adult arrests that are likely asphyxial (drowning, choking, drug overdose, smoke inhalation). Use a barrier device or pocket mask if available. Give 2 breaths after every 30 compressions, each over about 1 second, just enough to see the chest rise. Avoid excessive ventilation; it reduces cardiac output and increases gastric inflation.</p>

<h3>Using an AED: pad placement and safety</h3>
<p>An AED is safe and effective when used correctly. Turn it on, attach pads to the bare chest (right upper chest below the collarbone and left lower chest below the armpit), and follow the voice prompts. Ensure no one touches the patient during analysis and shock delivery. In wet environmentscommon during rainy seasonsdry the chest quickly before applying pads. Remove medication patches and jewellery from the pad area if possible.</p>

<h3>Team CPR and switching roles</h3>
<p>In a business or community setting, assign roles before an emergency: compressor, airway/breaths, AED operator, timekeeper, and recorder. Switch compressors every 2 minutes or sooner if quality drops. Use a closed-loop communication style: You are compressing, I am on AED, switch in 30 seconds. Practise with your team quarterly. In many African workplaces, staff rotate shifts and casual workers may be present; train a core team and keep a simple AED/CPR kit accessible.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Check scene safety and tap/shout for response.</li>
<li>Call emergency services and send for an AED.</li>
<li>Check breathing for no more than 10 seconds.</li>
<li>Start chest compressions: 100120/min, 56 cm deep.</li>
<li>Allow full chest recoil; minimise interruptions.</li>
<li>Use AED as soon as it arrives; follow prompts.</li>
<li>Switch compressors every 2 minutes.</li>
<li>Continue CPR until help arrives or the person recovers.</li>
</ol>
