1. Nutrient Requirements of Suckling and Weanling Piglets

Suckling piglets rely entirely on sow's milk for the first 2-3 weeks. Sow milk provides about 7.2 MJ digestible energy (DE) per kg, 55 g crude protein (CP) per kg, and high levels of lactose and fat. However, by 3 weeks, milk supply peaks and piglets need extra feed. In Africa, where sows often have limited milk due to poor nutrition, creep feeding is critical. Start offering a creep feed from day 7. Use a highly digestible diet: 14.5 MJ DE/kg, 20-22% CP, 1.5% lysine, and 0.9% calcium. Include cooked cereals (maize, rice), soybean meal, dried skim milk, and fish meal. Add 5-10% sugar or molasses to encourage eating. Piglets eat only 200-300 g/day at first, so freshness mattersoffer small amounts 3-4 times daily and remove leftovers.

Weaning usually happens at 4-6 weeks in African smallholder systems, but early weaning (3-4 weeks) is possible with proper nutrition. After weaning, piglets face stress and often stop eating. Use a weaner diet: 14.0-14.5 MJ DE/kg, 18-20% CP, 1.2-1.4% lysine, and 0.8% calcium. Replace some soybean meal with cooked eggs, whey, or yogurt if available. Add 0.3% salt and a vitamin-mineral premix. Feed 4-5 times daily, increasing gradually. Clean water is essentialpiglets drink 1-2 liters per day.

In Africa, common mistakes include feeding maize alone (too low protein and lysine) and using moldy feed (causes diarrhea). Mix your own: 55% maize, 25% soybean meal, 10% fish meal, 5% whey, 3% oil, 1% limestone, 1% premix. For 100 kg of feed, this gives about 20% CP and 1.4% lysine. If soybean meal is expensive, use 10% sunflower cake plus 0.2% synthetic lysine. Always provide shade and clean pens to reduce disease. Weigh piglets weekly; aim for 6-8 kg at weaning and 20-25 kg at 8 weeks. Adjust feed based on weight gainif they gain less than 300 g/day, increase protein and energy.

2. Nutrient Requirements of Growing-Finishing Pigs

Growing-finishing pigs are the backbone of pork production. They need balanced energy, protein, minerals, and vitamins to grow fast and produce lean meat. In Africa, local breeds like Mukota or indigenous pigs grow slower than exotic breeds, so adjust nutrient levels accordingly. For exotic breeds (Large White, Landrace, Duroc), aim for 13.5-14.5 MJ DE/kg and 16-18% CP from 20-50 kg (grower phase), then 13.0-13.5 MJ DE/kg and 14-16% CP from 50-90 kg (finisher phase). Lysine is critical: 1.0-1.1% for growers, 0.8-0.9% for finishers. Calcium should be 0.6-0.8% and available phosphorus 0.3-0.4%. For local breeds, reduce protein by 1-2% and energy by 0.5 MJ, but keep lysine at 0.9-1.0% for growers and 0.7-0.8% for finishers.

A typical African grower diet: 60% maize, 20% soybean meal, 10% wheat bran, 5% fish meal, 3% oil, 1% limestone, 1% premix. This gives about 17% CP and 14.2 MJ DE/kg. For finishers, use 65% maize, 15% soybean meal, 12% wheat bran, 4% fish meal, 2% oil, 1% limestone, 1% premixabout 15% CP. If soybean meal is scarce, replace with 10% groundnut cake plus 0.15% synthetic lysine. Always add 0.25% salt and a vitamin-mineral premix. Mix thoroughly and store in dry, cool place.

Feed intake matters: growers eat 1.5-2.0 kg/day, finishers 2.5-3.0 kg/day. Feed twice daily, and provide clean water at all times. In hot climates, pigs eat less, so increase nutrient density (more energy and protein per kg) by adding 2-3% oil. Weigh pigs every two weeks. Target daily gain: 600-800 g for exotic breeds, 400-600 g for local breeds. If gain is low, check for worms (deworm every 2-3 months) and improve feed quality. Avoid overfeeding finisherstoo much energy makes them fat, reducing carcass value. Use a simple feed chart: for every 10 kg increase in body weight, reduce CP by 1% and lysine by 0.1%. This saves money and prevents waste.

3. Nutrient Requirements of Gestating Sows

Gestating sows need nutrients mainly for fetal growth, body maintenance, and building reserves for lactation. Overfeeding causes fat sows and difficult births; underfeeding causes weak piglets and poor milk. In Africa, many sows are fed kitchen waste or maize alone, leading to deficiencies. A good gestation diet should have 12.0-13.0 MJ DE/kg, 12-14% CP, 0.5-0.6% lysine, 0.8% calcium, and 0.4% available phosphorus. Feed 2.0-2.5 kg per day for a 150-200 kg sow. In the first month after mating, feed only 2 kg/day to prevent embryo loss. In the last month, increase to 2.5-3.0 kg/day to support fetal growth. If the sow is too thin (body condition score 2 or less), feed 3 kg/day; if too fat (score 4 or more), feed 1.8 kg/day.

A simple gestation diet for Africa: 55% maize, 15% wheat bran, 10% soybean meal, 8% sunflower cake, 5% fish meal, 5% molasses, 1% limestone, 1% premix. This gives about 13% CP and 12.5 MJ DE/kg. Add 0.3% salt. If soybean meal is expensive, use 12% groundnut cake and 0.1% synthetic lysine. Provide plenty of clean watersows drink 10-20 liters daily. Also give 0.5 kg of green fodder (sweet potato vines, cassava leaves, or amaranth) per day for vitamins and fiber. Fiber prevents constipation and keeps sows content. Avoid moldy feedmycotoxins cause abortions.

Monitor body condition monthly using a scale of 1 (very thin) to 5 (very fat). Aim for 3 (ribs felt but not seen). Adjust feed accordingly. In the last two weeks before farrowing, switch to a lactation diet gradually to prepare the sow. Deworm sows 2 weeks before mating and treat for mange if needed. Provide a clean, dry farrowing pen with bedding. Gestating sows can be group-housed, but feed individually to prevent bullying. In Africa, many farmers keep sows in pens with limited exercise, so keep energy low to avoid fatness. A good rule: feed 1 kg for maintenance plus 0.3 kg per piglet in the litter (e.g., for 10 piglets, feed 2 kg + 3 kg = 5 kg? Nothat's for lactation. For gestation, total 2-3 kg). Remember, overfeeding is as harmful as underfeeding. Weigh feed, don't guess.

4. Nutrient Requirements of Lactating Sows

Lactating sows produce 5-10 liters of milk daily, so they need high energy and protein. A sow nursing 10 piglets can lose 1-2 kg of body weight per week if underfed. In Africa, poor lactation is a major cause of piglet deaths. A good lactation diet should have 13.5-14.5 MJ DE/kg, 16-18% CP, 0.9-1.0% lysine, 0.9% calcium, and 0.5% available phosphorus. Feed 2 kg for maintenance plus 0.4 kg per piglet. For 10 piglets, feed 2 + 4 = 6 kg/day. If the sow is thin, feed up to 7 kg. Split into 3-4 meals to avoid waste and digestive upset. Always provide unlimited clean watersows drink 20-30 liters daily.

A typical lactation diet: 55% maize, 20% soybean meal, 10% wheat bran, 5% fish meal, 4% oil, 3% molasses, 1% limestone, 1% premix, 0.3% salt. This gives about 17% CP and 14.0 MJ DE/kg. For cheaper mix, use 15% groundnut cake, 5% sunflower cake, and 0.2% synthetic lysine. Add 2% oil in hot weather to increase energy without bulk. Provide 1 kg of green fodder daily. Avoid moldy feed and ensure feed is freshsows refuse stale feed. In Africa, many farmers use swill (kitchen waste), but this can be unbalanced. If using swill, add 1 kg of concentrate (soybean meal + premix) per 5 kg swill.

Monitor sow body condition weekly. Aim to lose no more than 0.5 kg per week. If sow loses too much, increase feed. If she is too fat, reduce energy but keep protein high. Weigh piglets at birth and at 21 days. Target weaning weight (28 days) of 7-8 kg. If piglets are small, check sow for mastitis or poor milk. In hot climates, feed sows early morning and evening when cool. Provide shade and ventilation. After weaning, reduce feed to 2 kg/day for 3 days to dry off milk, then switch to gestation diet. Keep records: feed cost per piglet weaned is a key profit measure. In Africa, every extra 100 g of piglet weaning weight means faster growth to market. So invest in good lactation feed.

5. Nutrient Requirements of Boars

Boars are often neglected in African pig production, but their nutrition affects libido, semen quality, and serving ability. A boar needs a diet similar to a gestating sow but slightly higher protein and energy. Aim for 13.0-13.5 MJ DE/kg, 14-16% CP, 0.7-0.8% lysine, 0.8% calcium, and 0.4% available phosphorus. Feed 2.0-2.5 kg per day for an adult boar (150-250 kg). If the boar is working hard (serving 3-4 times per week), increase to 2.5-3.0 kg. Overfeeding makes boars fat and lazy, reducing libido. Underfeeding causes low sperm count and poor semen quality.

A simple boar diet: 60% maize, 15% wheat bran, 12% soybean meal, 5% fish meal, 5% molasses, 1% limestone, 1% premix, 0.3% salt. This gives about 15% CP and 13.2 MJ DE/kg. Add 0.5 kg of green fodder daily for vitamins. Provide plenty of clean waterboars drink 10-15 liters daily. In Africa, some farmers feed boars only maize or kitchen waste, leading to deficiencies. Always include a vitamin-mineral premix, especially zinc and selenium, which are important for sperm production. Add 0.2% salt. If soybean meal is scarce, use 10% groundnut cake plus 0.1% synthetic lysine.

Manage boars carefully: keep them in individual pens to avoid fighting. Allow moderate exercisea walk twice daily improves muscle tone and libido. Check body condition monthly: ribs should be felt but not seen. If boar is too thin, increase feed; if too fat, reduce energy and increase exercise. Deworm every 3-4 months. In hot weather, boars may eat less, so feed during cool hours. Provide shade and wallows. Collect semen if you do artificial insemination; poor nutrition shows in semen within 4-6 weeks. A good boar can serve 20-30 sows per year. In Africa, where boars are expensive, proper feeding extends their useful life. Avoid feeding moldy feedmycotoxins reduce fertility. Also, do not feed cottonseed cake (gossypol is toxic to boars). Keep records of serving dates and feed intake. A healthy boar is a profitable boar.

6. Adjusting Nutrient Specifications for Health Status and Environment

Pigs in Africa face heat, disease, and poor hygiene, so nutrient specifications must be adjusted. A healthy pig in a cool, clean pen needs standard nutrients. But if the pig has diarrhea, worms, or respiratory disease, it needs more protein, vitamins, and minerals to fight infection. For example, increase vitamin C and E by 50% and add 0.1% zinc oxide for 2 weeks during diarrhea. In hot weather (above 28C), pigs eat less, so increase energy density by adding 3-5% oil and increase protein by 1-2% to compensate. Also add electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride) to water. In humid conditions, mold grows fast; use mold inhibitors (0.1% propionic acid) and avoid wet feed.

In dusty or ammonia-heavy pens, pigs suffer respiratory stress. Increase vitamin A and E to support lung health. If pigs are crowded, increase lysine by 0.1% because stress reduces feed intake. For pigs with parasites, deworm first, then feed a high-protein diet (18% CP) for 2 weeks to recover. In areas with African swine fever, there is no treatment, so focus on biosecurity. But for other diseases like pneumonia, add 0.2% salt and 0.5% sodium bicarbonate to feed to maintain electrolyte balance. Always provide clean waterdirty water spreads disease.

Environment also affects nutrient requirements. In cold weather (below 15C), pigs need more energy for warmthincrease feed by 10-20% or add 2% oil. In free-range systems, pigs exercise more and may eat grass, so reduce feed by 10% but ensure minerals. In confinement, provide a premix with extra B-vitamins. For sows in hot climates, feed at night to reduce heat stress. In all cases, monitor pig performance: if growth is poor, adjust nutrients. Use a simple record book: note feed intake, weight gain, and health events. In Africa, many farmers cannot afford expensive additives, so use local alternatives: add 1% charcoal for diarrhea, 0.5% dried moringa leaves for vitamins, and 2% clay for minerals. Always consult a vet or extension officer before making major changes. Remember, prevention is cheaper than treatment. Good nutrition supports immunity.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Nutrient Specifications for Pig Classes in African Production Systems

Feed is the single largest cost in pig production, typically representing 6075% of total operating expenses in African commercial herds. Getting nutrient specifications right and adjusting them for the realities of your farm is therefore not a technical luxury but a direct driver of margin. This practical reference consolidates the nutrient requirements for all classes of pigs into working tables, shows how to adjust specifications for health status and environment, and gives you a checklist you can apply at the feed mill today.

The figures below assume a maizesoybean meal base diet, the dominant energyprotein matrix across Southern, East, and West Africa. Where local ingredients differ (cassava, palm kernel cake, cottonseed cake, fishmeal, brewers' spent grain), the nutrient targets remain the same; only the ingredient matrix changes. Always formulate to nutrient specifications, not to ingredient inclusion rates.

Note the shape of the curve: lysine (the first limiting amino acid in maize-based diets) is highest in the youngest pigs and falls sharply as the pig matures. Lactating sows sit between grower and weanling pigs because milk output is protein-intensive. This is why a single "pig feed" sold across all classes destroys profitability you either overfeed expensive protein to finishers or underfeed it to piglets.

SAFETY WARNING MEDICATED FEED AND WITHDRAWAL PERIODS

If you include antibiotics, coccidiostats, or other veterinary drugs in pig feed, you must observe the legal withdrawal period before slaughter. In most African jurisdictions this is 514 days depending on the drug and national regulation. Selling pigs inside the withdrawal window can result in residue-positive carcasses, rejection at abattoirs, fines, and loss of buyer contracts. Never mix medicated and non-medicated feed in the same mixer without a full clean-out cross-contamination can put residues into finisher feed. Always record batch numbers, drug name, inclusion rate, and withdrawal date in your feed register. Consult your national veterinary authority (e.g., KEPHIS in Kenya, DALRRD in South Africa, NAFDAC in Nigeria) for the current approved list.

How to Use These Tables in Practice

Start with Table 1 as your baseline specification. Then apply Table 2 adjustments according to your actual farm conditions. A piggery in the humid lowlands of Ghana or coastal Kenya, operating at 32C with sub-clinical respiratory challenge, should be running higher energy and amino acid density than a highland farm in Uganda at 22C with clean health status. This is not academic it is the difference between a 750 g/day and a 950 g/day average daily gain in the grower phase, which translates directly into days to market and feed cost per kilogram of gain.

For weanling piglets, the critical window is the first 14 days post-weaning. At this stage, feed intake is low and digestive capacity is immature. Use highly digestible ingredients (cooked cereals, fishmeal, skim milk powder where affordable) and keep lysine at 1.351.50%. Every gram of feed the piglet refuses in this window is a gram of growth lost permanently piglets do not fully compensate later.

For gestating sows, the objective is not maximum nutrition but controlled nutrition: maintain body condition (BCS 3.03.5) without excessive weight gain. Overfeeding gestating sows is one of the most common and costly errors in African piggeries it increases feed cost, causes farrowing difficulties, and reduces subsequent lactation feed intake. Restrict to 1.82.2 kg/day and monitor body condition monthly.

For lactating sows, the opposite applies: feed to appetite (4.56.0 kg/day) with high energy and lysine density. A sow nursing 1012 piglets produces 79 kg of milk daily and will mobilise body reserves if feed is restricted. Excessive mobilisation leads to delayed return to oestrus and smaller next litter a hidden cost that shows up 5 months later.

For boars, keep specifications moderate but consistent. Over-conditioned boars lose libido and have poor semen quality. Under-conditioned boars tire quickly. Target BCS 3.0 and feed 2.02.5 kg/day of a 12.513.0 MJ/kg diet with 0.550.65% lysine.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Formulate by class, not by convenience. Never feed one diet to all pigs the cost of mismatch exceeds the cost of separate mixing.
  • Anchor on lysine. It is the first limiting amino acid in maize-based diets; balance all other amino acids relative to it.
  • Test your ingredients. A single crude protein test on your maize and soybean meal prevents systematic over- or under-formulation.
  • Adjust for heat. Above 28C, increase energy and amino acid density by 510% to compensate for reduced intake.
  • Monitor body condition monthly. Use calipers or visual BCS on sows and boars; adjust feed, not the specification, first.
  • Keep a feed register. Record batch, date, class, ingredients, and any medication this is your traceability and your cost-control tool.
  • Observe withdrawal periods. Never sell pigs inside the medicated feed withdrawal window.
  • Review specifications quarterly. Ingredient quality, season, and health status change your formulation should follow.
