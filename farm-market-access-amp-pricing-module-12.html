Lessons

1. Keeping simple business records

Keeping simple records is the first step to making your farm a real business. You don't need a computer or expensive software. A notebook and a pen work perfectly. Start by recording every sale and every expense. For example, when you sell a bag of maize for 25,000 shillings, write the date, what you sold, the quantity, and the amount. When you buy fertilizer for 30,000 shillings, write that down too. At the end of each week, add up your total sales and total expenses. The difference is your profit or loss.

To make it even easier, use a simple cash book. Divide a page into two columns: money in and money out. Every time cash comes in, write it in the 'in' column. Every time you spend, write it in the 'out' column. At the end of the month, subtract the total out from the total in. If you have more money in, you made a profit. If you have more out, you made a loss. This helps you see if you are actually making money or just moving it around.

Also keep records of your crops and livestock. For each plot, write what you planted, the date, how much seed you used, and how much you harvested. For livestock, record births, deaths, and sales. These records help you plan for the next season. For instance, if you harvested 10 bags of maize from one acre last year, you can expect similar or better if you use the same methods. If you used improved seed and fertilizer, you might harvest 15 bags. This information guides your decisions.

Finally, keep receipts and invoices in a safe place. Even a simple receipt book can help you track payments. If you sell to a trader who promises to pay later, write down their name, the amount, and the date. Follow up to ensure you get paid. Good records also help you get loans from banks or cooperatives. They want to see that you are organized and can repay. So start today with a notebook and pen. Your farm business will grow stronger.

2. Planning for the next planting season

Planning for the next planting season starts right after harvest. Don't wait until the rains are about to start. Use your records from last season to decide what to plant and how much. For example, if you grew maize and beans, look at which crop gave you more profit. Suppose you spent 50,000 shillings on maize and earned 120,000, while beans cost 30,000 and earned 90,000. Maize gave a profit of 70,000, beans 60,000. But beans may take less time and leave the soil better. So you might plant both, but adjust the acreage.

Next, calculate your input needs. For one acre of maize, you might need 10 kg of improved seed, 100 kg of fertilizer (like DAP at planting and urea for top dressing), and labour for land preparation, planting, weeding, and harvesting. Write down the cost of each. If seed costs 5,000, fertilizer 30,000, and labour 20,000, your total cost is 55,000 shillings. If you expect to harvest 15 bags and sell each at 10,000, your income is 150,000, giving a profit of 95,000. This simple budget helps you know if you need a loan or can save from other income.

Also plan for risks. What if the rains come late? You might choose a drought-tolerant variety. What if pests attack? You might set aside money for pesticides. What if prices fall at harvest? You could store some grain and sell later when prices rise. For storage, you need a dry, safe place. You can use hermetic bags like PICS bags to protect against weevils without chemicals. They cost about 2,000 shillings per bag and can save your harvest.

Finally, make a calendar. Mark the expected start of rains, planting dates, weeding times, and harvest. Share this plan with your family so everyone knows what to do. If you work with other farmers, you can coordinate planting to attract buyers. A clear plan turns farming from a gamble into a business.

3. Diversifying crops and markets to reduce risk

Diversifying means not putting all your eggs in one basket. If you only grow maize and the price drops, you lose everything. But if you also grow beans, vegetables, or keep chickens, you have other income sources. For example, in many parts of Africa, maize prices fall after harvest because everyone sells at once. But if you have tomatoes, you can sell them when they are scarce and get higher prices. Or if you keep goats, you can sell one when you need cash urgently.

Start by choosing crops that do well in your area and have different harvest times. If you grow maize (3-4 months) and cassava (9-12 months), you have food and income throughout the year. You can also intercrop, like planting beans with maize. Beans add nitrogen to the soil and give you an extra crop. For markets, don't rely on one buyer. If you sell only to a local trader, they can set low prices. Instead, sell to multiple buyers: local traders, schools, restaurants, or at the market. You can also process your crops to add value. For example, turn cassava into gari or maize into flour. That way, you can sell to different customers.

Consider livestock as part of your diversification. A few chickens can provide eggs and meat. They need little space and feed. You can sell eggs weekly and chickens for special occasions. Goats are also good; they multiply quickly and can be sold when you need money. But remember, livestock need care: shelter, water, and feed. Start small and grow as you learn.

Diversifying markets means finding new places to sell. If you usually sell at the farm gate, try the local market. If you sell at the market, try selling to a school or hospital. You can also join a farmers' group that sells in bulk to bigger buyers. The key is to have at least three ways to earn from your farm. That way, if one fails, the others keep you going.

4. Working with other farmers for better prices

Working together with other farmers gives you power. When you sell alone, you are a small seller and buyers can offer low prices. But when you join with 10 or 20 farmers, you have a larger quantity. A trader who wants 100 bags of maize will pay more per bag if they can buy all from one group. You can also negotiate better. For example, if you each sell a bag for 8,000 shillings alone, together you might get 10,000 per bag. That's an extra 2,000 per bag, which adds up.

How do you start? Talk to your neighbours. Form a group with a clear name and rules. Choose a leader and a treasurer. Meet regularly to discuss prices, share information, and plan. You can also register the group with the local government or cooperative department. That opens doors to training and loans. Many NGOs and extension officers prefer to work with groups because they can train many farmers at once.

One big advantage is bulking. Instead of each farmer selling a few bags, you collect all the bags in one place. Then you can invite buyers to come and bid. You can also store together and sell when prices are high. For storage, you might rent a warehouse or use a hermetic storage facility. The group can also buy inputs in bulk. For example, if you each buy one bag of fertilizer, you pay retail price. But if you buy 20 bags together, you get a discount. You can also share equipment like a maize sheller or a water pump.

But groups need trust and good management. Keep clear records of who contributed what and how money is shared. Avoid conflicts by having a written constitution. If someone sells outside the group, they might break trust. So set rules and follow them. With a strong group, you can access better markets, get fair prices, and learn from each other. It's a key step to a sustainable farm business.

5. Accessing extension services and training

Extension services are support from government or NGOs that help farmers learn new methods. In many African countries, the government employs extension officers who visit farmers. They can advise on planting, pests, and marketing. But sometimes they are few and far between. So you need to be proactive. Find out where your nearest extension office is. Visit and ask for help. They can give you information on improved seeds, fertilizers, and market prices. They might also invite you to training sessions.

Training can be in many forms: field days, demonstrations, or short courses. For example, a field day might show you how to use hermetic bags to store maize without chemicals. A demonstration might teach you how to make compost. These are practical skills that increase your yield and reduce costs. Attend as many as you can. Also, listen to agricultural radio programs. Many stations have daily or weekly shows with tips and market prices. You can call in and ask questions.

Another way to access training is through farmer groups. As we discussed, groups often get priority for training. You can also use your mobile phone. Many services now send SMS messages with weather forecasts, market prices, and advice. For example, in Kenya, the Kenya Agricultural Commodity Exchange (KACE) sends market prices via SMS. In Nigeria, farmers can use the 'Farmers' Call Centre' to ask questions. Check if such services exist in your area. They are often free or low cost.

Don't forget about other farmers. Some of the best training comes from fellow farmers who have tried new things. Visit their farms and see what they do. Ask questions. If you see a method that works, try it on a small plot first. Also, share what you learn with others. That way, the whole community benefits. Extension and training are not just about getting information; they are about building a network of support for your farm business.

6. Setting goals for growth and profit

Setting goals turns your dreams into a plan. Without goals, you might farm the same way year after year. With goals, you decide where you want your farm to be in one, three, or five years. For example, a goal might be: 'By next year, I will increase my maize yield from 10 bags to 15 bags per acre.' Or 'I will save 200,000 shillings to buy a water pump.' Goals should be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART). So instead of 'I want more money,' say 'I will earn 500,000 shillings profit from my farm by December next year.'

Start with your current situation. Look at your records. How much did you earn last season? What were your costs? Then decide what you want to change. If you want to increase profit, you can either increase income or reduce costs. For example, you could grow a higher-value crop like tomatoes, or you could reduce fertilizer costs by using compost. Write down your goal and the steps to achieve it. If your goal is to earn 500,000 shillings, break it down: how much from maize, how much from beans, how much from chickens? Then plan the activities and inputs needed.

Review your goals regularly. Every month, check your progress. If you are behind, adjust your plan. For example, if you planned to sell 20 bags of maize but only harvested 15, you might need to sell some chickens to make up the income. Also, celebrate your achievements. When you reach a goal, set a new one. This keeps you growing.

Finally, think about long-term sustainability. A sustainable farm business does not just make money this year; it keeps making money in the future. That means taking care of your soil, water, and animals. It means saving some money for emergencies and reinvesting in your farm. It means building relationships with buyers and other farmers. With clear goals and a plan, you can build a farm that supports your family for years to come.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Building a Sustainable Farm Business

This Practical Reference lesson is designed as a hands-on guide for African smallholder and emerging commercial farmers who want to move from subsistence thinking to business thinking. Whether you farm one hectare or twenty, the principles here will help you keep better records, plan smarter, reduce risk, and earn more from every harvest. Use this module alongside the other lessons in Farm Market Access & Pricing to build a farm business that survives bad seasons and thrives in good ones.

1. Keeping Simple Business Records

Records are the foundation of every farm business decision. Without them, you are guessing. With them, you can see which crops make money, which costs are eating your profit, and where you can improve. You do not need expensive software. A notebook, a simple ledger book, or a free spreadsheet on a phone or computer is enough to start.

Keep at least four types of records: production records (what you planted, when, how much you harvested), financial records (income and expenses), input records (seeds, fertilizer, pesticides, labour), and market records (who you sold to, at what price, and when). Review your records weekly and summarise them monthly. This habit alone puts you ahead of most farmers in your area.

2. Planning for the Next Planting Season

Planning should start at least three months before the rains. Begin by reviewing last season's records. Which crops gave you the best return per hectare? What were your biggest costs? Then decide: what will you plant, on how much land, and with what inputs? Write a simple farm plan that lists each crop, the area, expected yield, expected price, and estimated costs. This becomes your budget.

In most African markets, prices are lowest right after harvest and highest during the lean season. If you can store safely, you may earn more by selling later. But storage has costs and risks. Your plan should include a storage and selling strategy, not just a production plan.

3. Diversifying Crops and Markets to Reduce Risk

Diversification means not putting all your eggs in one basket. If you grow only maize and the price crashes, you lose everything. If you grow maize, beans, and vegetables, and also keep a few goats, you spread your risk. Diversify across three dimensions: crops (different families and harvest times), markets (local, regional, institutional, export), and income streams (crop sales, livestock, value-added products, services).

4. Working with Other Farmers for Better Prices

One farmer selling 500 kg of maize has little bargaining power. Fifty farmers selling 25 tonnes together can negotiate with millers, schools, and processors. This is the power of collective marketing. Farmer groups, cooperatives, and savings groups can also share transport costs, buy inputs in bulk at a discount, and access training together.

To make collective marketing work, you need: a written agreement, transparent leadership, accurate weighing and grading, and a fair way to share costs and profits. Start small with a group you trust, and formalise as you grow.

5. Accessing Extension Services and Training

Extension officers, NGOs, and government programmes provide free or subsidised training on agronomy, post-harvest handling, and business management. In many African countries, you can also access digital extension services via mobile phone. These include weather forecasts, market prices, and pest alerts.

6. Setting Goals for Growth and Profit

Goals turn dreams into plans. Write down one goal for production, one for profit, and one for business development. Make them SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound. For example: "By December 2026, increase my maize yield from 2 tonnes to 3 tonnes per hectare by using improved seed and correct spacing, and increase my net profit by 30%."

Review your goals every three months. Adjust when necessary. Celebrate progress â it keeps you motivated.

⚠ SAFETY WARNING: Never store pesticides, herbicides, or treated seed in the same room or container as food, animal feed, or drinking water. Always wear protective clothing when applying agrochemicals, and wash hands and clothes immediately after use. In case of poisoning, contact your nearest health facility or poison control centre immediately. Store all chemicals in their original labelled containers, locked away from children and livestock.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • ☐ Keep a daily record of all income, expenses, and farm activities.
  • ☐ Write a simple farm plan and budget before each planting season.
  • ☐ Grow at least three different crops or enterprises to spread risk.
  • ☐ Sell to at least two different buyers or markets each season.
  • ☐ Join or form a farmer group for collective marketing and input buying.
  • ☐ Contact your local extension officer at least once per season.
  • ☐ Set one SMART production goal and one SMART profit goal each year.
  • ☐ Review your records and goals every three months and adjust.
