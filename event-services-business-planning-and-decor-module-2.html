Lessons

1. Estimating startup costs for decor and planning services in your area

Start by listing every item you need to begin. For decor, you might need fabric (e.g., 20 meters of satin at $3 per meter = $60), vases (10 at $5 each = $50), artificial flowers (20 bunches at $4 = $80), and a staple gun ($15). For planning, you need a notebook ($2), a phone with a data plan ($10 per month), and transport money for client meetings ($20 per month). Add a basic toolkit: scissors, glue gun, zip ties, and measuring tape ($30). Total for these basics: about $267. But you also need a way to get to events: if you don't have a car, budget for taxi or boda boda fares ($50 per event).

Next, think about where you'll store your materials. If you work from home, no extra cost. If you rent a small storage room, that could be $30 per month. Also, consider marketing: printing flyers ($10 for 100), a simple Facebook ad ($5), and business cards ($15). Don't forget licenses: in many African countries, a small business permit costs between $20 and $100 per year. Add these up: storage ($30), marketing ($30), license ($50) = $110. So total startup could be around $377.

But costs vary by country and city. In Nairobi, a decor starter kit might cost $400; in Accra, $350; in Lagos, $450. To estimate for your area, visit local markets (e.g., Katwe in Kampala, Balogun in Lagos) and price items. Ask other decorators what they spent. Use a notebook to record every price. Then add 10% for unexpected costs. This gives you a realistic startup figure.

Finally, decide how to fund it. You might save $50 per week for 8 weeks, borrow from family, or use a mobile savings group (like a chama). Start small: buy one item per week. You don't need everything on day one. Begin with what you have and reinvest profits. Your goal is to know your number so you can plan, not to have it all at once.

2. How to create a simple budget using a notebook or free phone app

A budget is just a plan for your money. You can use a notebook and pen, or a free app like 'Money Manager' (Android) or 'Spendee' (iOS). If using a notebook, draw three columns: Date, Description, Amount. For income, write positive numbers; for expenses, write negative numbers. At the end of each week, add up the columns to see your balance. For example: on 1st March, you receive a $100 deposit (income +100). On 2nd March, you buy fabric for $30 (expense -30). Your balance is $70.

For a monthly budget, list all expected income (deposits, final payments) and all expected expenses (materials, transport, airtime, rent). Use a simple table: Income (e.g., $500 from two events) minus Expenses (e.g., $200 materials, $50 transport, $20 airtime, $30 storage) = Profit ($200). If you use a phone app, create categories like 'Materials', 'Transport', 'Marketing'. Enter each transaction as it happens. The app will show you a pie chart of where your money goes.

Keep it simple. Don't track every single coin, but do track every business transaction. At the end of the month, review: Did you spend more than you earned? Which expense was highest? Can you reduce it? For example, if transport is high, try to combine client meetings or use public transport. If materials are high, buy in bulk or negotiate discounts.

Remember, a budget helps you avoid surprises. If you know you have a $50 license fee due in June, you can set aside $10 each month. Use your notebook to write reminders. If you use an app, set alerts. The goal is to always know how much money you have and where it's going. This habit will keep your business alive.

3. Pricing your services: per event, per hour, or package deals

Pricing is how you get paid. You can charge per event, per hour, or as a package. Per event: you quote a fixed price for the whole job. For example, decorating a wedding for $300. This is best when you know exactly what's needed. Per hour: you charge for your time, e.g., $10 per hour for planning consultations. This works for meetings or small tasks. Package deals: you bundle services, e.g., 'Wedding Decor + Planning = $500'. This attracts clients who want everything done.

To set your price, calculate your costs first. For a wedding, materials might cost $150, transport $30, and your time (10 hours at $10/hour) = $100. Total cost = $280. Add profit: aim for 20-30% profit, so price at $350-$400. Check what competitors charge. In Kampala, basic decor might be $200; in Johannesburg, $500. Don't underchargeyou'll work for free. Don't overchargeclients will go elsewhere.

For per hour, decide your hourly rate. If you want to earn $200 per week and work 20 hours, your rate is $10/hour. For packages, combine services and offer a small discount. E.g., if decor alone is $300 and planning alone is $200, package at $450 (save $50). This encourages clients to buy more.

Always write a simple contract. State the price, what's included (e.g., '10 table centerpieces, backdrop, and setup'), and payment terms (e.g., 50% deposit, 50% on event day). Get a signature or WhatsApp confirmation. Adjust prices as you gain experience. Start lower to get your first clients, then increase. Keep records of what you charged and how long each job took. This helps you price better next time.

4. Managing cash flow when clients pay deposits and final payments

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. For event services, clients usually pay a deposit (e.g., 50%) to book, and the balance on or before the event day. You need to manage this carefully so you can buy materials and pay your team without running out of money. Example: Client books a wedding for $400. They pay $200 deposit on 1st May. You need to buy fabric, flowers, and pay transport ($150) by 15th May. You have $50 left. Then you get the final $200 on 25th May. Your cash flow is positive if you time purchases right.

To manage, create a simple cash flow calendar. List each event, deposit date, final payment date, and when you need to pay for materials and labor. Use a notebook or a free app like 'Cash Flow' (Android). For each week, write money in and money out. If you see a week where money out is more than money in, you need to delay purchases or ask for a higher deposit. For example, ask for 60% deposit if your costs are high.

Always keep a small reserve for emergencies. If a client delays final payment, you still need to pay your suppliers. Set aside 10% of each deposit as a buffer. Also, follow up on final payments: send a reminder 3 days before the event. If they pay late, charge a small late fee (e.g., $10) as stated in your contract.

Don't mix business and personal money. Use a separate mobile money wallet or bank account. When a deposit comes in, transfer it to your business account. Pay business expenses from that account. This way, you always know your true cash position. If you're short, consider a small loan from a microfinance app like Tala or Branch, but only if you're sure you can repay from the final payment.

5. Opening a mobile money or bank account for your business

To run a real business, you need a separate account for business money. In most African countries, mobile money is the easiest. Examples: M-Pesa (Kenya, Tanzania), MTN Mobile Money (Uganda, Ghana, Cameroon), Airtel Money (Nigeria, Zambia). To open a mobile money account, visit an agent with your national ID and register. You'll get a SIM card and a wallet. You can deposit, withdraw, and pay bills. Most agents charge small fees (e.g., 1% for deposits). For a business account, some providers offer 'Lipa na M-Pesa' (paybill) or merchant codes, which let clients pay you directly. This costs a small monthly fee (e.g., $2) but builds trust.

If you prefer a bank, go to a local branch with your ID, business permit (if you have one), and proof of address. Banks like Equity Bank (East Africa), Absa (South Africa), or Zenith (Nigeria) offer small business accounts. Minimum balances vary: some require $10, others $100. Monthly fees range from $2 to $10. A bank account gives you a cheque book, a debit card, and online banking. But banks can be slow and require more paperwork.

Start with mobile money because it's fast and low-cost. Use it to receive deposits and pay suppliers. Keep a record of all transactions. If you want to save for equipment, open a separate savings wallet or a fixed deposit. As your business grows, open a bank account for larger sums. Always keep your business money separate from personal money. Don't use your personal wallet for business. This discipline helps you track profits and avoid confusion.

When choosing, compare fees: mobile money charges per transaction; banks charge monthly. Also consider accessibility: mobile money agents are everywhere; banks may be far. For most small event businesses, mobile money is enough to start.

6. Tracking expenses and profits for each event

To know if you're making money, you must track expenses and profits for each event. Use a notebook or a free app like 'Expense Manager' or 'QuickBooks Self-Employed'. For each event, create a page or a digital folder. Write the event name, date, and client. Then list all income: deposit and final payment. Then list all expenses: materials, transport, labor, rentals, airtime, and any other costs. Subtract expenses from income to get profit. Example: Wedding for Chidi on 10th June. Income: $500. Expenses: fabric $100, flowers $50, vases $20, transport $30, assistant $40, airtime $5. Total expenses = $245. Profit = $255.

At the end of the event, review your list. Did you forget any expense? Did you spend more than planned? If profit is low, see where you can cut costs next time. Maybe buy flowers in bulk or use reusable items. If profit is high, consider raising your prices or offering premium packages. Keep all receipts in an envelope or take photos and store them in a phone folder. This helps if a client disputes a charge.

Also track your time. If you spent 20 hours on the event, your profit per hour is $255/20 = $12.75. Is that worth it? If not, adjust your pricing or work faster. Compare events: which type is most profitable? Weddings, birthdays, or corporate events? Focus on the most profitable.

Finally, at the end of each month, add up profits from all events. This is your business income. Subtract any monthly overheads (storage, data, license) to get net profit. Use this to plan: save for equipment, pay yourself a salary, or reinvest. Without tracking, you might think you're making money when you're actually losing. So make it a habit: after every event, update your records immediately.

7. Practical Reference

Planning Your Event Business Finances

Rise AFRICA Skills — Event Services Business (Planning and Decor)

This practical reference module helps you set up the money side of an event planning and decor business in Africa. You will learn how to estimate startup costs, build a simple budget, price your services, manage deposits and final payments, open a mobile money or bank account, and track profit event by event. The goal is simple: know your numbers, protect your cash, and grow a business that pays you.

1. Estimating Startup Costs for Decor and Planning Services

Startup costs are the one-time or early expenses you pay before your business can deliver its first events. In event planning and decor, these costs are lower than in many businesses because you can rent or hire most items instead of buying them. But you still need to plan carefully.

Your main startup cost categories are:

  • Registration and legal: business name registration, local permits, and any required licences.
  • Tools and equipment: basic decor items, fabric, vases, lights, extension cables, a good tape measure, scissors, glue guns, and storage boxes.
  • Transport and logistics: hiring a van or pickup for event days, fuel, and loading help.
  • Marketing: phone data, printed business cards, a simple social media page, and sample photos.
  • Working capital: money set aside to pay for rentals, labour, and transport before a client pays you.
  • Business account: mobile money registration or bank account opening fees, where applicable.

A useful rule for beginners in African markets is to keep startup spending under the value of your first two or three confirmed events. This prevents you from sinking savings into items you may not need. Rent chairs, tents, drapes, and lighting from established suppliers until your own bookings are steady.

2. How to Create a Simple Budget Using a Notebook or Free Phone App

You do not need expensive software. A hardcover notebook with columns for date, description, money in, money out, and balance is enough to start. If you prefer a phone, free tools such as Google Sheets, WPS Office, or a simple expense tracker app work well and can be used offline in many areas.

Follow this method:

  1. List expected income for the month from confirmed events and deposits.
  2. List fixed costs that do not change, such as data bundles, transport to client meetings, and storage rent.
  3. List variable costs that change per event, such as flowers, fabric, rental chairs, and casual labour.
  4. Subtract costs from income to see your expected profit.
  5. Update daily with every payment received and every expense paid.

Keep two separate records: one for the business and one for your personal money. Mixing them is the fastest way to lose track of whether the business is actually profitable.

3. Pricing Your Services: Per Event, Per Hour, or Package Deals

Pricing is where many event businesses lose money. You must cover direct costs, overheads, your time, and a profit margin. Below are three common pricing models used across African markets.

Table 1: Common event pricing models compared
Model Best for Advantage Risk
Per event (flat fee) Weddings, birthdays, corporate launches Client knows the full price; easy to quote You may underquote if scope grows
Per hour Consulting, on-site coordination, set-up Fair when time is uncertain Client may rush you or dispute hours
Package deal Repeat clients, bundles (decor + planning) Higher average sale; clear value Must calculate costs carefully first

A simple pricing formula: Price = Direct costs + Overhead share + Your time + Profit margin. If direct costs are 40,000 KES and you want a 30% margin, your price should be at least 57,000 KES, not 40,000 KES. Many beginners forget to pay themselves.

4. Managing Cash Flow with Deposits and Final Payments

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. Event businesses often face a gap: you must pay suppliers and labour before the client pays the final balance. A deposit policy protects you.

Recommended practice:

  • Request a 30–50% deposit to confirm the booking.
  • Use the deposit to pay rentals, transport, and materials.
  • Collect the final payment on or before the event day, not weeks later.
  • Keep a small emergency fund for last-minute needs such as extra fabric or fuel.
  • Write the payment terms in a simple contract or WhatsApp confirmation.
⚠ SAFETY WARNING:

Never spend a client deposit on personal needs or on a different event. If you do, you may fail to deliver the current event, damage your reputation, and face legal or community consequences. Always keep event money separate from personal money. Use a dedicated mobile money wallet or bank account for the business. In many African markets, word of mouth is your biggest marketing tool — one broken promise can end your business.

5. Opening a Mobile Money or Bank Account for Your Business

Separating business and personal money is essential. In most African countries you can open a mobile money wallet (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money, Orange Money) or a small business bank account.

Table 2: Mobile money vs. bank account for small event businesses
Feature Mobile Money Bank Account
Opening speed Minutes, with ID Days to weeks
Access Anywhere with phone signal Branch or ATM
Fees Per transaction Monthly or per transaction
Best for Daily receipts, deposits Savings, larger clients, loans
Record keeping Statement via SMS/app Printed statement

Register the account in your business name where possible. Keep the SIM card and PIN secure. Record every transaction in your notebook or app the same day.

6. Tracking Expenses and Profits for Each Event

After every event, sit down and calculate the real profit. Use this simple structure:

  • Event income: deposit + final payment.
  • Direct costs: rentals, flowers, fabric, labour, transport, venue fees.
  • Overhead share: data, transport to meetings, storage, phone airtime.
  • Net profit: income minus all costs.

If an event made no profit, find out why: underpricing, overspending on decor, or too much unpaid labour. Adjust your prices or your supplier choices before the next event.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • ☐ List all startup costs and keep them under the value of your first 2–3 events.
  • ☐ Keep a daily notebook or free phone app for money in and money out.
  • ☐ Separate business money from personal money at all times.
  • ☐ Price using: direct costs + overhead + your time + profit margin.
  • ☐ Charge a 30–50% deposit and collect the final payment on event day.
  • ☐ Open a mobile money wallet or bank account in the business name.
  • ☐ Track profit after every single event, not just at month end.
  • ☐ Keep a small emergency fund for last-minute event needs.
