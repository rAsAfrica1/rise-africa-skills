1. Common Nursery Pests: Aphids, Whiteflies, Mites

Aphids are small, soft-bodied insects, usually green, black, or brown, that cluster on the undersides of young leaves and stems. They suck sap, causing leaves to curl, yellow, and stunt. They also excrete honeydew, a sticky substance that attracts ants and grows black sooty mold. Check your seedlings at least twice a week. Look for shiny honeydew or ants crawling on plants. A strong jet of water from a hose can knock aphids off. For small infestations, spray with a mix of 1 tablespoon of liquid soap (not detergent) in 1 litre of water. Repeat every 3 days for two weeks. Ladybugs and lacewings eat aphids, so encourage them by planting flowers like marigolds nearby.

Whiteflies are tiny, white, moth-like insects that fly up in a cloud when you disturb the plant. They also suck sap and transmit viruses. You will see yellow sticky traps catching them. Hang yellow sticky traps just above the seedling canopy, about 10 cm above. For control, spray with neem oil: mix 5 ml neem oil with 1 litre of water and a few drops of soap. Spray in the evening to avoid leaf burn. Repeat every 7 days. Remove heavily infested leaves and destroy them. Keep the nursery weed-free because whiteflies hide in weeds.

Spider mites are not insects but arachnids. They are tiny, red or brown, and spin fine webs on the undersides of leaves. You may see stippled, yellow dots on leaves. Mites thrive in hot, dry conditions. To check, tap a leaf over a white paper; you will see tiny moving specks. Increase humidity by misting plants. Spray with insecticidal soap or neem oil, covering both sides of leaves. For severe cases, use sulfur dust, but follow label instructions. Avoid broad-spectrum pesticides that kill beneficial insects. Rotate chemicals to prevent resistance.

Regular scouting is key. Walk through your nursery every morning. Inspect 10 random seedlings. If you find pests on more than 2 seedlings, treat immediately. Keep a notebook to record pest sightings and treatments. Healthy seedlings resist pests better, so water and fertilise properly. Remove and burn crop debris. These steps will keep aphids, whiteflies, and mites under control.

2. Common Diseases: Damping Off, Powdery Mildew

Damping off is a fungal disease that attacks seedlings at soil level. It causes stems to rot and seedlings to collapse. You may see a water-soaked lesion on the stem, then the seedling falls over and dies. It spreads quickly in wet, cool conditions and overcrowded nurseries. To prevent damping off, use clean containers and sterile potting mix. Never reuse old soil without sterilising it. You can sterilise soil by solarisation: cover moist soil with clear plastic for 4-6 weeks in full sun. Ensure good drainage; never let seedlings sit in water. Water in the morning so leaves dry before night. Space seedlings to allow airflow. If damping off appears, remove and destroy affected seedlings immediately. Drench remaining soil with a copper-based fungicide or a biological control like Trichoderma. Apply according to label. Improve ventilation and reduce watering.

Powdery mildew looks like white, powdery spots on leaves and stems. It thrives in warm, dry days and cool, humid nights. It reduces photosynthesis and weakens seedlings. To prevent, plant resistant varieties if available. Ensure good air circulation by spacing plants and pruning overcrowded leaves. Avoid overhead watering; water at the base. Remove infected leaves and destroy them. For control, spray with a mix of 1 tablespoon baking soda, 1 teaspoon liquid soap, and 1 litre of water. Spray every 7 days. Alternatively, use neem oil or sulfur-based fungicides. Apply in the evening to avoid leaf burn. Milk spray (1 part milk to 9 parts water) can also be effective. Repeat weekly until symptoms stop.

Sanitation is critical. Remove all plant debris from the nursery floor. Clean tools with 10% bleach solution between uses. Do not compost diseased plants; burn or bury them deep. Keep a footbath with disinfectant at the nursery entrance to prevent carrying pathogens in on shoes. Rotate crops if you grow in the same beds. Keep records of disease outbreaks and treatments. Early detection and quick action are your best defence. Healthy, strong seedlings are less susceptible, so provide optimal growing conditions.

3. Preventive Measures: Sanitation and Airflow

Sanitation is your first line of defence against pests and diseases. Start with a clean nursery site. Remove weeds, grass, and debris from around the nursery because they harbour pests. Keep the area free of standing water. Use clean pots, trays, and tools. Wash pots with soap and water, then rinse with a 10% bleach solution (1 part bleach to 9 parts water). Let them dry in the sun. Sterilise pruning shears and knives by dipping in alcohol or bleach solution between plants. Wear clean clothes and shoes; use a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance. Never bring diseased plants into the nursery. Quarantine new seedlings for at least 7 days before mixing with others. Dispose of diseased plants by burning or burying them at least 30 cm deep, away from the nursery.

Airflow is essential to prevent fungal diseases like damping off and powdery mildew. Ensure your nursery has good ventilation. If in a greenhouse, open vents and use fans to circulate air. Space seedlings so leaves do not touch; this allows air to move freely. Avoid overcrowding. Use benches or raised beds to improve air movement under pots. Water in the morning so foliage dries before evening. Avoid overhead watering; use drip irrigation or water at the base. If you must water overhead, do it early in the day. Keep humidity below 70% if possible. In humid climates, use a dehumidifier or increase ventilation. Prune lower leaves of larger seedlings to improve airflow. Remove any dead or yellowing leaves promptly.

Maintain a regular cleaning schedule. Sweep floors weekly. Remove plant debris daily. Clean benches and surfaces with soapy water. Keep a record of cleaning activities. Train workers on hygiene practices. Provide handwashing stations with soap and water. Encourage workers to wash hands before handling plants. Store tools clean and dry. Use clean growing media; never reuse old mix without sterilising. Solarise soil or use commercial sterile mixes. These practices reduce pest and disease pressure significantly. A clean, well-ventilated nursery produces strong, healthy seedlings that resist problems naturally.

4. Organic Pest Control Methods

Organic pest control uses natural materials and methods to manage pests without synthetic chemicals. It is safe for you, beneficial insects, and the environment. Start with physical controls: handpick large pests like caterpillars and beetles. Use a strong water spray to dislodge aphids and mites. Set up yellow sticky traps for whiteflies and winged aphids. Use barriers like row covers or insect netting to keep pests off seedlings. For soil pests, apply diatomaceous earth around stems; it cuts soft-bodied insects. Beer traps can catch slugs and snails. These methods are cheap and effective for small nurseries.

Botanical sprays are plant-based pesticides. Neem oil is versatile: mix 5 ml neem oil with 1 litre of water and a few drops of liquid soap. Spray in the evening, covering all leaf surfaces. It controls aphids, whiteflies, mites, and some fungi. Reapply every 7-14 days. Garlic and chili spray: blend 2 bulbs garlic, 2 hot peppers, and 1 litre water. Strain and add a few drops of soap. Spray weekly. It repels many insects. Soap spray: 1 tablespoon liquid soap in 1 litre water. It kills soft-bodied insects on contact. Test on a few leaves first to avoid burning. Always spray in the evening or on cloudy days to prevent leaf burn. Do not spray when beneficial insects are active.

Microbial pesticides include Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) for caterpillars. Mix according to label and spray on leaves. It is safe for beneficials. Trichoderma is a fungus that controls soil-borne diseases like damping off. Mix into potting soil or drench. Beauveria bassiana is a fungus that infects insects; use as a spray. These products are available at farm supply stores. Always follow label instructions. Store them properly.

Cultural practices support organic control. Rotate crops to break pest cycles. Plant companion plants like marigolds, basil, and coriander to repel pests and attract beneficials. Keep plants healthy with proper watering and fertilising. Remove and destroy infested plant parts. Sanitise tools and hands. Monitor your nursery daily. If you see pests, act quickly. Combine methods for best results. Organic control requires patience and persistence, but it builds a healthy ecosystem in your nursery.

5. Using Beneficial Insects

Beneficial insects are natural enemies of pests. They eat or parasitise pests, reducing their numbers without chemicals. Common beneficials include ladybugs, lacewings, parasitic wasps, and predatory mites. Ladybugs (ladybirds) eat aphids, whiteflies, and mites. One ladybug can eat 50 aphids a day. Lacewing larvae, called aphid lions, are voracious predators; each can eat 200 aphids or mites in a week. Parasitic wasps lay eggs inside aphids, whiteflies, or caterpillars; the larvae kill the pest from inside. Predatory mites attack spider mites and thrips. You can attract these beneficials by planting flowers like dill, fennel, marigolds, sunflowers, and alyssum. Also, avoid broad-spectrum pesticides that kill them.

To introduce beneficials, you can buy them from commercial suppliers. Ladybugs and lacewings are available as adults or larvae. Release them in the evening near pest infestations. Follow release rates: for ladybugs, release 1,500 per 100 square meters. For lacewings, release 1,000 eggs per 100 square meters. Predatory mites are released at 2,000 per 100 square meters. Ensure the nursery has adequate humidity and no ants, which protect pests. Provide a water source for beneficials. Do not release if you have used pesticides recently; wait at least 2 weeks. Monitor pest levels after release. It may take a few weeks to see results.

Conserve existing beneficials. Reduce pesticide use; use only selective, organic products. Provide habitat: leave some flowering weeds or plant insectary strips. Provide shelter like small piles of leaves or rocks. Avoid excessive tillage that destroys ground beetles. Create a diverse ecosystem. Keep records of beneficial sightings. If you see ladybugs, lacewings, or hoverflies, you know your pest control is working. Encourage them by not spraying when they are present. Use row covers to protect seedlings until beneficials establish. Combine beneficials with other organic methods for a robust pest management plan. Healthy, diverse nurseries naturally have fewer pest problems.

6. Safe Use of Pesticides in Nurseries

Pesticides can be dangerous if not used correctly. Always read the label before buying or using any pesticide. Choose the least toxic product that will solve your problem. Prefer organic or biological pesticides when possible. Wear protective clothing: long-sleeved shirt, long trousers, closed shoes, gloves, goggles, and a mask. Mix pesticides outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Never mix more than you need. Use clean water and accurate measuring tools. Follow the label rate exactly; more is not better. Mix in a dedicated container, never in food or drink containers. Keep children and animals away during mixing and spraying.

Apply pesticides in the evening or early morning when beneficial insects are less active and wind is calm. Spray when there is no rain forecast for at least 24 hours. Cover all plant surfaces, especially leaf undersides where pests hide. Use a fine spray to avoid runoff. Do not spray on windy days to prevent drift. Avoid spraying near water sources, wells, or streams. Do not eat, drink, or smoke while spraying. If you feel dizzy or sick, stop immediately and seek medical help. After spraying, wash your hands and face with soap and water. Wash contaminated clothing separately. Store pesticides in their original containers, locked away from children, food, and animal feed. Keep them in a cool, dry place.

Dispose of empty containers safely. Triple-rinse them, then puncture and bury them away from water sources. Never reuse pesticide containers. Follow local regulations for disposal. Keep a record of pesticide use: date, product, rate, area treated, and weather. This helps track effectiveness and safety. Consider integrated pest management (IPM): combine cultural, physical, biological, and chemical methods. Use chemicals only as a last resort. Rotate pesticides with different modes of action to prevent resistance. Do not spray the same product repeatedly. If you hire workers, train them on safe handling. Provide protective gear and ensure they follow safety rules. Always prioritise human health and environmental safety. Safe pesticide use protects you, your workers, your community, and your nursery.

<h2>Practical Reference: Pest and Disease Management in African Seedling Nurseries</h2>

<p>Every tray of seedlings you lose to pests or disease is money straight off your margin. In a nursery business, a 10% loss on 5,000 seedlings is 500 plants that consumed seed, substrate, labour, water and space and earned nothing. Pest and disease management is therefore not a "technical" sideline; it is a <strong>profit-protection function</strong>. This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the module: identify the problem fast, apply the cheapest effective control, and build prevention into your daily routine so you spend less on chemicals and lose fewer plants.</p>

<h3>1. Diagnose Before You Spend</h3>
<p>Misdiagnosis is the most expensive mistake in a nursery. A farmer who sprays insecticide for damping off wastes money, kills beneficial insects, and still loses the tray. Train your workers to answer three questions before any treatment:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Where is the damage?</strong> Leaves and stems = likely pest. Roots, collar, or the whole tray collapsing = disease.</li>
<li><strong>What pattern?</strong> Clustered on new growth = aphids or whiteflies. Fine webbing and stippling = mites. Patches of collapsed seedlings = damping off.</li>
<li><strong>What is the weather doing?</strong> Humidity and poor airflow drive fungal disease; dry, hot conditions drive mites.</li>
</ol>

<h3>4. Prevention: Sanitation and Airflow</h3>
<p>Prevention is the highest-return investment in any nursery. It costs labour, not chemicals.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Wash and disinfect trays</strong> between batches (soapy water, then a 10% bleach rinse, then dry in sun).</li>
<li><strong>Never reuse old soil</strong> without composting or sterilising damping off pathogens live in it.</li>
<li><strong>Space trays</strong> so air moves between them; stack only at the end of the day if necessary.</li>
<li><strong>Water at the base</strong>, in the morning, so leaves dry before night.</li>
<li><strong>Quarantine new seed or seedlings</strong> for 35 days before mixing with existing stock.</li>
<li><strong>Remove and destroy</strong> infected trays immediately do not compost diseased material.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Organic Pest Control Methods</h3>
<p>Organic methods keep input costs low and protect the beneficial insects that do free work. Reliable options in African nurseries include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Soap spray:</strong> 5 ml mild liquid soap per 1 L water; target aphids and whiteflies; reapply every 57 days.</li>
<li><strong>Neem oil:</strong> follow product label; acts on whiteflies, mites and aphids; avoid spraying in full sun.</li>
<li><strong>Garlic/chilli extract:</strong> locally made repellent; useful where commercial products are expensive or unavailable.</li>
<li><strong>Yellow sticky traps:</strong> cheap monitoring and mass-trapping for whiteflies and winged aphids.</li>
<li><strong>Wood ash or diatomaceous earth:</strong> barrier against crawling pests around nursery benches.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Using Beneficial Insects</h3>
<p>Beneficial insects are free labour. Ladybirds and their larvae eat aphids; lacewing larvae eat aphids, whiteflies and mites; predatory mites control spider mites; parasitic wasps attack whitefly nymphs. Protect them by:</p>
<ul>
<li>Spraying only when pest numbers justify it not on a calendar.</li>
<li>Using selective, short-residual products rather than broad-spectrum insecticides.</li>
<li>Planting flowering borders (e.g. marigold, sunflower) near the nursery to shelter adults.</li>
<li>Avoiding spraying in the middle of the day when pollinators and predators are active.</li>
</ul>
<p>Where commercial beneficial insects are unavailable, conserve what is already on your farm. A nursery surrounded by diverse vegetation suffers fewer pest outbreaks than one on bare ground.</p>

<h3>7. Safe Use of Pesticides in Nurseries</h3>
<p>If you must use a synthetic pesticide, treat it as a last resort and a business decision:</p>
<ul>
<li>Confirm the pest or disease first spraying blind wastes money.</li>
<li>Choose the product registered for that crop and that pest in your country.</li>
<li>Calibrate your sprayer; over-dosing burns seedlings and wastes product.</li>
<li>Spray in the early morning or late evening to protect beneficial insects and reduce evaporation.</li>
<li>Record every spray: date, product, rate, area, operator. Records protect your business and your market access.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Scout at least 10 trays every morning; record findings.</li>
<li>Identify pest vs disease before any treatment.</li>
<li>Disinfect trays and use clean media for every new batch.</li>
<li>Space trays and ventilate to keep foliage dry.</li>
<li>Water at the base in the morning, never late evening.</li>
<li>Use soap, neem or traps before any synthetic pesticide.</li>
<li>Protect and encourage ladybirds, lacewings and predatory mites.</li>
<li>Wear full PPE and keep spray records if chemicals are used.</li>
</ol>
