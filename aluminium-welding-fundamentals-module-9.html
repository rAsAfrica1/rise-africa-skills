1. Preheat and Its Limits

When you weld aluminium thicker than 6 mm, the metal pulls heat away from the weld zone so fast that your arc struggles to melt the base metal. Preheating solves this by raising the base metal temperature before you strike an arc. For 612 mm thick aluminium, preheat to 80120C. For 1225 mm, go to 120180C. Use a temperature stick or infrared thermometernever guess. In Africa, many workshops use a propane torch or charcoal brazier to preheat. Keep the flame moving so you don't overheat one spot. A simple check: spit on the metal; if it sizzles and dances, it's around 100C. But for critical work, buy a temp stick.

Preheat has limits. Never exceed 200C on heat-treatable alloys like 6061 or 7075. Above that, you risk over-ageing the alloy, which permanently reduces its strength. For 5083 or 5086 (non-heat-treatable), you can go up to 150C but no higher. If the metal starts to show a dull grey oxide or you see a rainbow tint, you've gone too far. Also, preheat the whole joint area, not just one spotat least 75 mm on each side of the weld. Uneven preheat causes distortion.

In the field, you might not have a thermometer. Use a 'temperature crayon' that melts at a set temperature (e.g., 120C). Mark the metal, heat until the mark melts. That's your cue. Another trick: hold a piece of paper against the metalit chars at about 200C, so if it browns quickly, back off. Remember, preheat is not a substitute for proper amperage. You still need enough current to melt the filler and base metal. For 10 mm aluminium, use 180220 amps with preheat. Without preheat, you'd need 250+ amps, which can blow through thin sections.

After welding, let the part cool slowly. Don't quench it. Rapid cooling can cause cracking in thick sections. Cover it with a fire blanket or dry sand to slow cooling. In hot African climates, ambient cooling is often fine, but avoid direct breeze. Preheat is a tooluse it wisely, and know when to stop.

2. Groove Preparation

Thick aluminium needs a groove so you can get full penetration. For 612 mm plate, a single-V groove with a 60 included angle works well. For 1225 mm, use a double-V or U-groove to reduce filler metal and distortion. The root face (land) should be 1.53 mm. Too thick a land and you won't penetrate; too thin and you'll burn through. Cut the groove with a plasma cutter, angle grinder, or milling machine. In many African workshops, a portable plasma cutter is common. After cutting, clean the groove with a stainless steel wire brush dedicated to aluminium. Never use a brush that has touched steelit causes contamination.

Remove all oxide just before welding. Aluminium oxide melts at 2072C while the base metal melts at 660C. If you don't remove it, the oxide skin will sit in the weld and cause lack of fusion. Use a carbide burr or a stainless steel brush. Then wipe with acetone or methyl ethyl ketone (MEK). In rural areas, you can use clean, high-proof alcohol (like isopropyl) if acetone is unavailable. Do not use petrol or dieselthey leave oily residue.

The groove should be dry and free of oil, grease, or moisture. Moisture causes porosity. Preheat the groove area to 80120C to drive off moisture. If you're welding 5083, you can preheat to 100C. For 6061, stay under 120C. Check the groove angle with a template or protractor. A common mistake is making the angle too narrowthen the arc can't reach the root. Too wide, and you use too much filler and get more distortion. For a 10 mm plate, a 60 V-groove with a 2 mm root face and 2 mm root gap is a good starting point.

Tack the plates every 150200 mm. Use the same filler as the weld. Tacks should be at least 50 mm long and thick enough to hold. If you're welding in position (vertical or overhead), reduce the groove angle to 4550 to help the puddle freeze faster. Always support the back of the groove with a backing strip if you can't get full penetration from both sides. A copper backing bar works wellit chills the weld and prevents burn-through. In Africa, you can use a thick aluminium backing plate, but preheat it too. Clean the groove again after tacking. Then you're ready to weld.

3. Root Pass and Fill Passes

The root pass is the most critical. For thick aluminium, use a keyhole technique or a backing strip. With a keyhole, you push the arc until you see a small hole at the leading edge of the puddle, then move forward and let the filler fill it. This ensures full penetration. Use a 3.2 mm or 4.0 mm tungsten electrode (2% thoriated or lanthanated) for DCEN (direct current electrode negative). For AC, use a balanced wave. Set amperage: for 10 mm plate, root pass at 180220 A. Use a 4.0 mm filler rod (4043 or 5356). 4043 flows better for root passes; 5356 is stronger but stiffer. In Africa, 4043 is widely available.

Start the arc on a run-on tab or 20 mm inside the groove. Don't strike on the base metalit causes tungsten inclusion. Move steadily. Keep the arc length short (23 mm). If you're using a foot pedal, feather the heat as you move. For the root, you want a slight reinforcement on the back side. If you're welding without backing, you need a keyhole. Watch the puddleit should be bright and fluid. If it looks sluggish, increase amperage. If it's too fluid, reduce.

After the root, clean it thoroughly. Use a stainless steel brush and acetone. Remove any oxide or spatter. Then do the fill passes. For a V-groove, you'll need 24 fill passes depending on thickness. Use a weave or stringer bead. For 12 mm plate, use 3.2 mm filler for fill passes. Amperage can be 200240 A. Keep interpass temperature below 120C for 6061, below 150C for 5083. Check with a temp stick. If it's too hot, wait. Fill passes should overlap by 50%. Don't leave valleysthey cause lack of fusion.

For the cap pass, you can use a slightly lower amperage (180200 A) and a wider weave to get a smooth finish. Use a 4.0 mm filler. The cap should be slightly convex, not flat or concave. If you're welding vertical-up, use a triangle weave and pause at the sides. In Africa, many welders use a 'whip' techniquemove forward and back quickly. But for thick aluminium, a steady forward motion with slight oscillation works best. Always inspect each pass. If you see porosity, grind it out and re-weld. Don't try to cover it up.

4. Interpass Temperature

Interpass temperature is the temperature of the weld area between passes. For thick aluminium, it's critical. If you let the part get too hot, you lose mechanical properties and risk cracking. For 6061-T6, keep interpass below 120C. For 5083, below 150C. For 7075, below 100Cbut 7075 is rarely welded. Use a temperature stick or infrared gun. In Africa, a simple 'touch test' is unreliablealuminium doesn't change colour. You need a tool. A temp stick that melts at 120C is cheap and effective. Mark the metal near the weld. When the mark melts, you've hit the limit. Stop and let it cool.

Why does it matter? Aluminium conducts heat away quickly, but in thick sections, heat builds up. If you keep welding, the base metal gets hotter and hotter. For heat-treatable alloys, excessive heat causes over-ageing: the strengthening precipitates grow and the alloy softens. You can lose 3050% of strength. For non-heat-treatable alloys, high interpass can cause hot cracking, especially in 5083 if you're using 5356 filler. The weld metal and base metal expand differently, and if the part is restrained, cracks form.

To control interpass, use cooling breaks. Weld one pass, then wait. You can speed cooling with compressed air (clean, oil-free) or a wet rag. Never quench with waterit can cause cracking. In a hot African workshop, ambient cooling may be slow. Use a fan. If you're welding a large part, alternate sides. Weld one side, then flip and weld the other. This balances heat. Another trick: use a heat sink. Clamp a thick aluminium or copper bar next to the weld. It draws heat away. Copper is better but expensive; aluminium works if it's thick.

Measure interpass at the weld toe, not the centre. The toe is where the next pass will start. If you're using a temp stick, mark the toe. Also, check the whole partsometimes one end is hotter. If you're welding 25 mm plate, you might need to wait 1015 minutes between passes. That's normal. Don't rush. A good welder plans the sequence: root, cool, fill 1, cool, fill 2, cool, cap. If you have multiple welders, stagger the work. In Africa, many jobs are done by one welder, so patience is key. Keep a bucket of water and a rag nearbybut only for cooling the part, not quenching the weld.

5. Post-Weld Cleaning

After welding aluminium, you must clean it. The weld leaves a black smut (soot) and oxide. If you don't remove it, you get corrosion and a poor appearance. First, let the part cool to room temperature. Then, use a stainless steel wire brushdedicated to aluminium only. Brush the weld and heat-affected zone (HAZ) until it's shiny. You can also use a carbide burr to remove spatter or rough edges. In Africa, a hand brush is common, but a wire wheel on a grinder works faster. Use a new wheelnever one that's been used on steel. Steel contamination causes rust spots.

Next, remove the smut. The black smut is magnesium oxide and other oxides. It's stubborn. Use a chemical cleaner: a mixture of 10% nitric acid and 90% water, or a commercial aluminium cleaner. In many African markets, you can find 'aluminium brightener' or 'aluminium cleaner' at hardware stores. Apply with a brush, let it sit for 23 minutes, then rinse with clean water. Wear gloves and eye protection. If you can't get acid, use a strong detergent and a Scotch-Brite pad. Scrub hard. Then rinse. For food-grade or marine applications, you must remove all smutit causes pitting.

After cleaning, you can passivate the surface. A light etch with a 5% nitric acid solution (or vinegar for a mild etch) helps. Then rinse and dry. If you're painting or anodising, you need a perfectly clean surface. Wipe with acetone. Don't touch with bare handsoils cause defects. In Africa, many welders skip cleaning. That's a mistake. Uncleaned welds fail early, especially in coastal areas with salt air. Salt accelerates corrosion on smut. A simple test: wipe with a white cloth. If it comes away black, clean again.

For structural welds, you may need to inspect after cleaning. Look for cracks, porosity, or undercut. Use a magnifying glass or dye penetrant. If you find defects, grind and re-weld. After re-welding, clean again. Finally, apply a protective coating if needed. A clear lacquer or paint designed for aluminium works. In rural areas, a coat of oil or grease can temporarily protect, but it attracts dust. Better to use a proper paint. Remember, cleaning is not optionalit's part of the weld. A clean weld lasts; a dirty one fails.

6. Post-Weld Heat Treatment Basics

Post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) for aluminium is not like steel. You don't just heat and cool. For heat-treatable alloys like 6061, you can restore some strength by solution heat treatment and artificial ageing. But this requires a furnace with precise temperature control. In most African workshops, you don't have that. So for 6061, the practical approach is to accept a reduction in strength in the HAZ. You can't fully restore it without proper equipment. However, you can do a 'stabilisation' treatment: heat the whole part to 150180C for 24 hours, then air cool. This relieves stresses and slightly improves properties. It's not a full T6, but it helps.

For non-heat-treatable alloys like 5083, PWHT is not about strength. It's about stress relief. You can heat to 200250C for 12 hours. This reduces residual stresses from welding. But be careful: 5083 is sensitive to sensitisation (formation of beta phase) if held between 65200C for too long. So keep time short. For most thick aluminium welds, stress relief is not mandatory unless the part is highly restrained. In Africa, many jobs are done without PWHT. If you're welding a pressure vessel or critical structure, you need it. Otherwise, you can skip it.

If you do PWHT, you need a furnace. A makeshift furnace can be made from firebricks and a propane burner, but temperature control is hard. Use a thermocouple and a digital controller. Ramp up slowly: 50100C per hour. Hold at temperature. Then cool slowlyfurnace cool or air cool. Never quench. For 6061, if you try to solution treat at 530C, you risk melting if you overshoot. That's dangerous. So don't attempt unless you have proper equipment and training.

In summary: for thick aluminium, PWHT is rarely done in small workshops. Instead, focus on preheat, interpass control, and proper welding. If you must do PWHT, use a certified furnace. In Africa, some large fabrication shops have them. For the rest, design your weld to minimise stress: use balanced welding, backstep, and peening. Peening (hammering the weld with a rounded tool) can reduce stress. But don't overdo itit can cause cracking. The best 'heat treatment' is good welding practice. Know your alloy, control your heat, and clean your weld. That's what keeps thick aluminium strong.

7. Practical Reference

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Welding Thick Aluminium</h2>

<p>Thick aluminium generally section thicknesses above 6 mm, and certainly above 12 mm behaves very differently from the thin sheet most welders meet first. It conducts heat away from the arc roughly five times faster than steel, it has no colour change before melting, and its oxide layer melts at around 2,072 C while the metal beneath melts at about 660 C. For an African fabrication business, this matters commercially: thick aluminium work is where the money is (trailer chassis, tanker barrels, marine hulls, bus body frames, structural gantries, cold-room panels, and mining equipment guards), and it is also where poor technique destroys margin through rework, gas waste, and rejected deliveries.</p>

<p>This reference module collects the practical numbers and habits you need to weld thick aluminium profitably. Use it alongside the other lessons in this module: <em>Preheat and Its Limits</em>, <em>Groove Preparation</em>, <em>Root Pass and Fill Passes</em>, <em>Interpass Temperature</em>, <em>Post-Weld Cleaning</em>, and <em>Post-Weld Heat Treatment Basics</em>.</p>

<h3>1. Preheat and its limits</h3>
<p>Preheat is a productivity tool, not a default. Its purpose is to drive off surface moisture and hydrocarbons, reduce the thermal gradient between the arc and the cold parent metal, and allow you to use a slightly lower current while still achieving fusion at the root. On a 20 mm 6082-T6 extrusion in a Nairobi workshop at 1,800 m altitude, a 100 C preheat can cut the number of passes required by 1520 % and reduce porosity caused by hydrogen from surface contamination.</p>

<p>Limits: never exceed the temperature at which the alloy's mechanical properties are affected. For 5xxx and 6xxx alloys, keep preheat between 80 C and 120 C. For 7xxx alloys (which are rarely welded), preheat is generally avoided. Measure with a contact thermometer or temperature crayon IR guns misread shiny aluminium badly. In hot African conditions, ambient metal may already be at 4050 C; account for that when setting the preheat target.</p>

<h3>2. Groove preparation</h3>
<p>Thick aluminium should almost never be welded square-butt. Use a single-V (60 included), double-V (60 included each side), or U-groove for thicknesses above 12 mm. Double-V halves the weld metal volume and distortion. A root face of 1.53 mm and a root gap of 23 mm are typical for GMAW; for GTAW, a slightly tighter gap works.</p>

<p>Cut with a plasma cutter, carbide saw, or router not an angle grinder if you can avoid it, because grinding smears aluminium and traps oxide. Degrease with acetone or a dedicated aluminium cleaner, then stainless-steel-wire brush the groove within 30 minutes of welding. Use a dedicated stainless brush for aluminium only; a brush that has touched steel will contaminate the weld with iron and cause black smut and porosity.</p>

<h3>3. Root pass and fill passes</h3>
<p>For thick sections, GMAW with 1.2 mm or 1.6 mm 5356 or 4043 filler is the workhorse. Root pass: short stringer beads, no weave, full penetration, 24 mm root reinforcement maximum. Fill passes: weave width no more than three times the electrode diameter, and stagger start/stop points so they do not align across passes. Clean each pass with a stainless brush before the next aluminium oxide re-forms within hours.</p>

<p>Push angle 1015, travel speed steady, and keep the wire at the leading edge of the puddle. If you hear a "bacon frying" sound you are close to correct; a harsh crackling means you are too cold or too fast.</p>

<h3>4. Interpass temperature</h3>
<p>This is the single most neglected variable in African workshops. On a hot afternoon in Lagos or Dar es Salaam, ambient temperature alone can push the workpiece above 150 C after only a few passes. Above that, 6xxx alloys lose strength in the heat-affected zone and 5xxx alloys become more prone to hot cracking. Measure between every pass. If you exceed the limit, stop, allow air cooling (never water quench), and resume.</p>

<h3>5. Post-weld cleaning</h3>
<p>Remove smut with a stainless brush and warm water, or a proprietary aluminium cleaner. Do not use steel wool or carbon-steel brushes. If the weld will be painted or anodised, a chemical etch (typically a dilute caustic followed by a nitric or proprietary deoxidiser) is required. In coastal African environments, salt-laden air accelerates galvanic corrosion at any iron contamination, so cleaning is not cosmetic it is a durability requirement.</p>

<h3>6. Post-weld heat treatment basics</h3>
<p>Aluminium does not respond to heat treatment the way steel does. 5xxx alloys are strain-hardened and cannot be strengthened by heat treatment; 6xxx and 7xxx are precipitation-hardened and can be partially restored by solution treatment and artificial ageing, but this requires a controlled furnace and is rarely economic for field fabrication. The realistic options for African fabricators are: (a) design so that the HAZ is not the critical section, (b) use 5083/5086 for welded structures where toughness matters more than peak strength, and (c) if heat treatment is required (e.g. for aerospace or pressure vessels), send the part to a certified heat treater do not attempt it in a workshop oven.</p>

<li><strong>Aluminium oxide fume and ozone:</strong> GMAW and GTAW on aluminium produce ozone and fine particulate. Weld only with local exhaust ventilation or in a well-ventilated area. In enclosed African workshops with corrugated roofs and no extraction, this is a genuine health risk.</li>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Verify alloy and temper</strong> before quoting 6082-T6 and 5083 need different fillers and different expectations.</li>
<li><strong>Preheat to 80120 C</strong> for sections above 12 mm; measure with a contact thermometer, not an IR gun.</li>
<li><strong>Cut a proper groove</strong> 60 included, 23 mm root gap, 23 mm root face; double-V above 20 mm.</li>
<li><strong>Degrease and stainless-brush</strong> the groove within 30 minutes of welding; keep aluminium brushes separate from steel brushes.</li>
<li><strong>Control interpass temperature</strong> never exceed 150 C; in hot ambient conditions, allow air cooling between passes.</li>
<li><strong>Clean every pass</strong> with a stainless brush before the next; re-oxidation is fast.</li>
<li><strong>Post-weld clean</strong> with a stainless brush and warm water or proprietary cleaner; chemical etch if painting or anodising.</li>
<li><strong>Do not attempt in-house heat treatment</strong> unless you have a calibrated furnace and certified procedure; design around the HAZ instead.</li>
</ol>
