1. Building a Queen Excluder Frame

A queen excluder keeps the queen out of the honey supers so she doesn't lay eggs there. You can buy one, but making your own saves money and lets you fit it to your hive. You need a wooden frame that fits between the brood box and the super. Use 2 cm thick timber, about 10 cm wide. Cut four pieces: two long sides equal to the hive's length (usually 48 cm) and two short sides equal to the width (usually 40 cm). Nail or screw them into a rectangle. The critical part is the mesh: use galvanized wire mesh with holes exactly 4.2 mm (or 4.14.5 mm). If holes are too big, the queen passes; too small, workers can't pass. You can find this mesh at agricultural supply shops or order it online. Cut the mesh 2 cm larger than the frame opening on all sides. Staple it to the frame using a staple gun or hammer and fencing staples, pulling it tight so it doesn't sag. Fold the edges over and staple again. For an African context, if you can't find 4.2 mm mesh, you can use a piece of plastic queen excluder cut to size, but ensure it's secured flat. Test the excluder by placing it on a hive and watching: workers should move through freely, but the queen (larger thorax) cannot. Check for gaps at corners; bees will glue them with propolis, but large gaps let the queen through. Paint the wooden frame with linseed oil or leave untreated; avoid toxic paints. In hot climates, metal mesh can get hot, so consider a wooden rim to shade it. This component costs little and lasts years if kept dry. Always remove the excluder during winter or when not using supers, as bees may cluster and starve if they can't reach honey.

2. Constructing a Feeder: Boardman and Division Board

Feeders are essential for feeding sugar syrup during dearth periods or to stimulate brood. Two common types are the Boardman feeder and the division board feeder. The Boardman feeder is an entrance feeder: a inverted jar screwed onto a wooden base that sits at the hive entrance. To build one, you need a glass jar (1 litre) with a metal lid. Punch 68 small holes (1 mm) in the lid. Make a wooden base from 2 cm thick timber, about 15 cm x 10 cm, with a raised rim to hold the jar upside down. The base has a slot that fits the hive entrance. When inverted, syrup drips slowly, and bees feed from the base. This feeder is cheap and easy, but in hot African climates, syrup can ferment or attract ants. The division board feeder replaces a frame inside the hive. Build a box that fits the brood chamber dimensions (e.g., 48 cm long, 5 cm wide, 25 cm deep). Use plywood or timber. Make a float from thin wood or styrofoam to prevent bees from drowning. Fill with syrup and place it in the hive where a frame would go. Bees access it from the top. This feeder is safer from robbers and ants. For both, use 1:1 sugar syrup (1 kg sugar to 1 litre water) for stimulation, or 2:1 for winter stores. In Africa, where cane sugar is available, this is practical. Clean feeders regularly to prevent mold. Build the division board from untreated pine; avoid treated wood. Seal joints with beeswax or food-grade silicone. A well-made feeder costs less than buying and can be repaired. Always provide a landing area for bees. In dry areas, also provide water nearby.

3. Building a Pollen Trap

A pollen trap collects pollen from foragers' legs as they enter the hive. This is valuable for selling or feeding. The simplest is a bottom-mounted trap that fits under the brood box. You need a wooden frame that matches the hive base, about 5 cm high. The key component is a screen with 5 mm holes (hardware cloth) that bees must squeeze through, knocking off pollen pellets. Below the screen, a drawer with a fine mesh (1 mm) collects the pollen. Build the frame from 2 cm timber. Cut a piece of 5 mm mesh to fit the hive entrance area. The bees enter through a 2 cm gap, then must pass through the mesh to reach the brood chamber. Pollen falls through the mesh into a tray. Make the tray from plywood with a fine screen bottom for ventilation. It should slide in and out for easy collection. In Africa, use locally available materials: mosquito netting for the fine mesh, and metal gauze for the 5 mm. Ensure the trap doesn't block the entrance completely; leave a side escape for drones and workers to bypass if needed. Collect pollen every 23 days to prevent mold. Dry pollen in shade before storing. A good trap can collect 50100 g per day in strong season. But don't leave it on all year; pollen is essential for brood. Use it for 12 weeks, then remove. Build two traps so you can alternate. This component requires precision: holes too large let pollen pass, too small trap bees. Test with a few bees. In hot climates, place the trap in shade to avoid overheating. Clean regularly to prevent pests.

4. Constructing a Swarm Trap or Bait Hive

A swarm trap is a lightweight box used to attract and capture wild swarms. In Africa, where swarms are abundant, this is a cheap way to get bees. Use a box made from plywood or thin timber, about 40 cm x 30 cm x 25 cm. It should have a volume of about 3040 litres, similar to a small hive. Cut a 3 cm entrance hole near the top. Inside, place 34 frames with foundation or just top bars. The trap should be watertight but ventilated. Use a lid that overlaps. To attract bees, bait the trap with lemongrass oil or old comb. Rub the inside with beeswax. Place the trap in a shady tree, 35 metres high, facing east to catch morning sun. In African savanna, hang traps near water and flowering trees. Check traps weekly during swarming season (often after rains). When bees move in, they will start building comb. Once established, close the entrance at night and move the trap to your apiary. Then transfer the frames to a permanent hive. Build the trap from untreated wood; avoid plywood with toxic glues. Use nails and exterior wood glue. Add a small landing board. For durability, paint the outside with light-coloured paint. A good swarm trap can be made from a discarded wooden crate. Ensure it's secure against monkeys and honey badgers. In some regions, use a traditional log hive as a trap. Always get permission if hanging on someone's land. This component costs almost nothing and can build your apiary quickly.

5. Building a Nuc Box for Splits

A nucleus (nuc) box is a small hive used to start new colonies from splits or to rear queens. It typically holds 45 frames. Build it from 2 cm thick timber, with dimensions to fit standard frames. For a 5-frame nuc, internal dimensions: 48 cm long, 20 cm wide, 25 cm deep. Cut two long sides (48 cm), two short sides (20 cm), a bottom (48x20 cm), and a lid. Assemble with nails and glue. The entrance should be 2 cm high and 5 cm wide, with a reducer to control traffic. Add a screened ventilation hole (5 cm diameter) on the side, covered with mesh. Inside, use frames with foundation or starter strips. Nuc boxes are essential for making splits: you take 23 frames of brood, 1 frame of honey, and 1 frame of bees from a strong hive, place them in the nuc, and add a new queen or let them raise one. In Africa, nuc boxes can be made from cheap plywood or even cardboard for temporary use, but wood lasts longer. Paint the outside with light colour. Place the nuc in a shady spot. Feed with sugar syrup until established. A nuc box allows you to increase your apiary without buying full hives. Build several so you can manage multiple splits. Ensure frames fit snugly to prevent burr comb. Use a follower board to adjust space. Check for pests like ants; place legs in oil cans. This component is simple and can be built in an hour.

6. Constructing a Winterization Wrap or Cozy

In many African regions, winters are mild, but in highlands or deserts, nights can be cold. A winterization wrap or cozy helps bees maintain brood temperature and reduces stress. Use breathable materials like burlap, hessian, or old blankets. Do not use plastic, which traps moisture. Cut a piece of burlap to wrap around the hive, leaving the entrance open. Secure with rope or tape. For extra insulation, add a layer of dry grass or straw between the wrap and the hive. In cold areas, you can build a wooden outer shell with insulation. Use 2 cm timber to make a box that fits over the hive with 5 cm gap, fill the gap with sawdust or vermiculite. The top can have a cushion of dry leaves. Ensure ventilation: drill 2 cm holes near the top to let moisture escape. In Africa, many beekeepers use a simple quilt box: a shallow box filled with wood shavings or dry grass, placed on top of the hive under the lid. This absorbs moisture and insulates. Build it from plywood, 10 cm deep, with a mesh bottom. The bees propolize it, but it's effective. Winterization is not needed in tropical lowlands, but in areas with frost, it can save colonies. Also reduce the entrance to 1 cm to keep out cold and robbers. Provide extra honey stores (23 frames) before winter. The wrap should be removed in spring. In hot climates, a reflective cover can reduce heat. Always allow ventilation; condensation kills bees. This component is cheap and can be made from recycled materials. Test the temperature inside: if it's too hot, remove insulation.

This practical reference module equips you with the technical specifications, materials sourcing strategies, and business considerations to construct these components using locally available materials while meeting international apiary standards.</p>

<h3>1. Queen Excluder Frame</h3>
<p>The queen excluder is a critical management tool that separates the brood chamber from honey supers, ensuring that honey comb remains free of brood and thus marketable at premium prices. In African contexts, commercial beekeepers in Tanzania and Ethiopia report 1525% higher honey prices when comb is brood-free and uniform in color. Construct a wooden frame from seasoned cypress, pine, or eucalyptus measuring 505 mm 425 mm (for Langstroth) or 460 mm 460 mm (for Kenya Top Bar). The internal aperture should accommodate a metal or plastic grid with 4.14.3 mm spacing wide enough for worker bees to pass but excluding the larger queen (thorax width ~4.8 mm).</p>
<p>Materials: 20 mm 5 mm flat aluminium or galvanized steel rods, or UV-stabilized plastic grids imported in bulk. Weld or rivet rods at precise intervals; inaccurate spacing either traps drones (reducing colony vigor) or allows the queen through (ruining honey supers). For small-scale enterprises, buying pre-fabricated plastic excluders from suppliers such as Beekeeping Supplies Africa (Nairobi) or Bee Equipment Ethiopia (Addis Ababa) may be more cost-effective than local fabrication, but for volumes above 200 units annually, local welding using jigs reduces unit cost by 3040%.</p>

<h3>2. Constructing a Feeder: Boardman and Division Board</h3>
<p>Supplementary feeding is essential during dearth periods dry seasons in the Sahel, cold spells in the Ethiopian highlands, or post-harvest in Southern Africa. The Boardman feeder is an entrance feeder consisting of a inverted jar (typically 1-litre) mounted on a wooden base that slots into the hive entrance. It is cheap, easy to inspect, but can stimulate robbing. The division board feeder replaces one frame inside the brood box, holding 24 litres of syrup behind a wooden or plastic float to prevent drowning. In African conditions where absconding is common during scarcity, division board feeders are superior because they are internal, reduce robbing, and allow feeding during rains.</p>
<p>Construct the division board from 12 mm plywood or seasoned timber: dimensions 460 mm 250 mm 40 mm internal depth. Seal joints with bee-safe silicone or beeswax. Add a float of thin timber or cork. For Boardman feeders, use food-grade plastic jars with perforated lids (23 mm holes). Never use containers that previously held pesticides, oils, or chemicals.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#b71c1c;">Never use containers, drums, or plastic vessels that have previously stored pesticides, herbicides, motor oil, or industrial chemicals. Residues are absorbed into plastic and leach into sugar syrup, killing bees and contaminating honey with compounds that can render it unsellable and unsafe for human consumption. Always use food-grade materials. When feeding, avoid spilling syrup near hives this triggers robbing, spreads disease, and can lead to colony death. In African markets, honey contaminated with chemical residues is rejected by exporters and premium buyers. Wear gloves and eye protection when cutting and sealing feeder components.</p>

<h3>3. Building a Pollen Trap</h3>
<p>Pollen trapping is a high-value enterprise. Bee pollen sells for USD 1540 per kilogram in African urban markets and export channels, and is used in nutraceuticals, cosmetics, and health foods. A pollen trap is fitted at the hive entrance and forces returning foragers to crawl through a grid (typically 4.85.0 mm) that knocks pollen pellets from their corbiculae into a collection tray below. Construct the trap body from 20 mm timber, with a removable tray of food-grade plastic or aluminium mesh (1 mm). The grid can be made from 4.8 mm wire or plastic. Ensure the trap has a bypass mechanism so it is not active continuously continuous trapping weakens colonies and reduces brood rearing.</p>
<p>Best practice: trap pollen for 23 days per week maximum, and only during peak flowering. In East Africa, pollen flow peaks after the long rains (JuneAugust) and short rains (NovemberJanuary). A well-designed trap can yield 50150 g per colony per day during peak flow.</p>

<h3>4. Constructing a Swarm Trap or Bait Hive</h3>
<p>Swarm traps are the cheapest way to grow colony numbers essential for business expansion. In Africa, where feral colonies are abundant, a well-placed bait hive can capture 38 swarms per season. Construct from 20 mm timber into a box of approximately 40 litres internal volume (e.g., 400 mm 300 mm 330 mm), with a 3040 mm entrance and a removable lid. Rub the interior with beeswax and lemongrass oil (a synthetic queen pheromone analogue). Position 35 metres high in a shaded tree, facing east to catch morning sun. In miombo and savannah regions, swarm season follows the first rains; place traps 46 weeks before expected swarming.</p>
<p>Business tip: A single bait hive costs USD 510 to build and can yield a colony worth USD 4080. Selling captured swarms or nucs to other farmers is a profitable side enterprise.</p>

<h3>5. Building a Nuc Box for Splits</h3>
<p>Nucleus (nuc) boxes are 5-frame hives used for queen rearing, mating, and colony splits. They are the engine of apiary multiplication and queen sales. Construct from 20 mm timber to internal dimensions of 505 mm 200 mm 250 mm (Langstroth nuc) or adapt to local frame sizes. Include a division board, a ventilated lid, and an entrance disc with multiple settings (open, queen excluder, closed) for transport and introduction. In Ethiopia, Kenya, and Nigeria, nuc boxes with mated queens sell for USD 60120, making them one of the highest-margin products in beekeeping.</p>
<p>Ensure tight joints to prevent absconding and pest entry. Use food-safe paint on exterior only. Provide a mesh ventilation panel (3 mm) on the lid for transport.</p>

<h3>6. Constructing a Winterization Wrap or Cozy</h3>
<p>Although much of Africa is tropical, highland regions Ethiopian highlands, Kenyan Rift Valley, Lesotho, and South African highveld experience cold nights and frost. Winterization wraps reduce colony stress, conserve honey stores, and prevent absconding. Construct a cozy from breathable materials: jute sacks, hessian, or woven polypropylene filled with dry grass, sawdust, or straw. Wrap around the hive leaving the entrance clear and top ventilation open to prevent condensation. A well-fitted cozy can reduce winter honey consumption by 2030%, directly improving profitability.</p>
<p>In hot lowland areas, the same cozy can be used as a shade wrap or moisture regulator during heavy rains. Use natural fibers to avoid plastic sweat and mold.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Use only seasoned, untreated timber (cypress, pine, eucalyptus) for all components.</li>
<li> Verify queen excluder bar spacing at 4.14.3 mm before installation.</li>
<li> Build division board feeders with food-grade sealant and internal floats.</li>
<li> Activate pollen traps only 23 days per week during peak flowering.</li>
<li> Rub swarm traps with beeswax and lemongrass oil; position 35 m high, east-facing.</li>
<li> Ensure nuc boxes have ventilated lids and adjustable entrance discs.</li>
<li> Use breathable natural fibers for winterization wraps; never seal the entrance.</li>
