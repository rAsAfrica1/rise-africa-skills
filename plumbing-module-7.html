1. Module 7 Lesson 1: Sizing Water Supply Systems for African Buildings

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on one of the most important skills a plumber in Africa can master: sizing water supply systems correctly. Whether you are working on a two-bedroom home in Kumasi, a three-storey apartment block in Nairobi, or a small hotel in Lusaka, the principles are the same. If you oversize, you waste money on pipes, fittings, and pumps. If you undersize, your client suffers low pressure, noisy pipes, and constant complaints. Your reputation and your profit depend on getting this right. Start by understanding demand. In most African settings, water supply is intermittent. Municipal water may come for only a few hours a day, so buildings rely on storage tanks and booster pumps. This changes how you calculate demand. You must size for peak flow when everyone draws water at the same time, usually in the morning between 5am and 8am. A typical household in sub-Saharan Africa uses between 50 and 100 litres per person per day, but in urban middle-class homes with flush toilets, showers, and washing machines, this can rise to 150 litres per person per day. For a commercial guesthouse, calculate 200 litres per guest per day. Use these figures as a starting point, then adjust for local habits. The first step is to count fixtures. List every tap, shower, toilet cistern, washing machine point, and garden tap. Each fixture has a fixture unit value. A wash basin tap is 1 fixture unit. A shower is 2. A toilet cistern is 3. A kitchen sink is 2. A garden tap is 2. A washing machine point is 3. Add all fixture units in the building. Then use a demand curve or table to convert total fixture units into litres per minute. A simple rule for small buildings: 1 fixture unit equals about 10 litres per minute at peak. So a house with 20 fixture units needs about 200 litres per minute at peak. That sounds high, but peak demand only lasts a short time. For storage, you need enough water for at least one day. If four people use 100 litres each, you need a 400-litre tank minimum. In practice, install a 1000-litre tank for a family of four because municipal supply is unreliable and you want a buffer. For a guesthouse with 20 guests, install at least 5000 litres of storage. Now size the pipe. The main pipe from the tank to the building should be sized so that water velocity does not exceed 1.5 metres per second. Higher velocity causes noise, erosion, and water hammer. Use the formula: flow rate in litres per second equals velocity in metres per second multiplied by pipe cross-sectional area in square metres, then multiplied by 1000. For a flow of 0.5 litres per second and a velocity of 1.5 metres per second, you need a pipe area of 0.000333 square metres, which is a diameter of about 20mm. So a 25mm pipe is safe. For branch pipes to individual fixtures, use 15mm or 20mm. For the main supply from the meter to the tank, use 25mm or 32mm depending on distance and pressure. Always account for friction loss. The longer the pipe and the more fittings, the more pressure you lose. A rough guide: for every 10 metres of pipe, you lose about 0.1 bar of pressure. Each elbow adds the equivalent of 0.5 metres of pipe. Each tee adds 1 metre. If your municipal pressure is only 1 bar, and you have 30 metres of pipe with 10 elbows, you lose about 0.3 bar from pipe and 0.5 bar from elbows, leaving only 0.2 bar at the tank inlet. That is not enough to fill a tank quickly. You may need a booster pump or a larger pipe. In Africa, always design for low and intermittent municipal pressure. Install a break tank at ground level, then a booster pump to fill the roof tank or pressurise the system. Choose pumps carefully. A typical booster pump for a home is 0.5 to 1.0 horsepower. For a small guesthouse, 1.5 to 2.0 horsepower. For a large apartment block, you may need a multi-stage pump or a variable speed drive. Always install a pressure tank and a pressure switch to avoid pump cycling. Set the cut-in pressure at 1.5 bar and cut-out at 3.0 bar for most buildings. Now consider material selection. In many African markets, PPR pipes are popular because they handle hot water and are easy to fuse. PVC pipes are cheaper but only for cold water and they become brittle in sunlight. PEX pipes are flexible and good for retrofits, but they need special fittings. Galvanised steel pipes are still found in older buildings, but they corrode and restrict flow. For new work, use PPR for hot and cold, or PEX where available. Always use brass or plastic fittings, not cheap zinc alloy, which cracks. When you install, support pipes properly. Use clips every 1 metre for horizontal runs and every 1.5 metres for vertical runs. Allow for expansion in hot water pipes by using expansion loops or offset bends. Insulate hot water pipes to save energy and prevent burns. Label all pipes clearly: blue for cold, red for hot. This helps future maintenance and impresses clients. Finally, test your system. Fill the tank, open all taps, and check for leaks. Measure pressure at the furthest fixture. It should be at least 0.5 bar for a shower to work well. If pressure is low, check for closed valves, clogged aerators, or undersized pipes. Document your calculations and leave a copy with the client. This builds trust and helps you charge fairly. Remember, a well-sized system saves water, saves energy, and saves your reputation. In the next lesson, we will look at drainage and venting. Before you move on, practice sizing a system for a three-bedroom house with two bathrooms, a kitchen, and a garden tap. Calculate fixture units, peak flow, tank size, and pipe diameters. Bring your calculations to the next session.

2. Module 7 Lesson 2: Drainage, Venting, and Waste Management in African Buildings

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 7. In this lesson we tackle drainage, venting, and waste management. This is where many plumbing systems in Africa fail. Poor drainage causes smells, blockages, and health hazards. Poor venting causes slow drains and gurgling. Poor waste management pollutes the environment. As a professional plumber, you must design and install systems that work reliably even when water supply is intermittent and maintenance is irregular. Start with the basics of gravity drainage. Waste water must flow downhill at a slope that carries solids without leaving them behind. The standard slope for a 100mm pipe is 1 in 40, which means 25mm drop per metre. For a 50mm pipe, use 1 in 30, or 33mm per metre. For a 75mm pipe, use 1 in 35. Do not use too steep a slope because water runs away and leaves solids behind. Do not use too shallow a slope because solids settle. Use a spirit level and a tape measure to set slopes accurately. In Africa, many buildings use PVC pipes for drainage because they are cheap and smooth. But PVC can become brittle in strong sunlight, so paint exposed pipes or use UV-resistant pipes. For underground drains, use PVC or concrete pipes. Cast iron is noisy but durable; it is rarely used now due to cost. Always lay drains on a firm bed of sand or fine gravel. Do not backfill with rocks or rubble because they can crack the pipe. Use a string line to keep the pipe straight. Install inspection chambers at every change of direction, at every junction, and every 15 metres on straight runs. An inspection chamber should be at least 600mm by 600mm for a small building, with a removable cover. In Africa, many blockages happen because inspection chambers are too small or missing. Build them properly with a concrete base and brick or concrete walls. The invert, which is the smooth curved bottom, must match the pipe slope so waste flows without obstruction. Now venting. Every drain needs air behind it to flow properly. Without venting, water traps get sucked dry, allowing sewer gas into the building. Sewer gas smells bad and can be explosive and unhealthy. Install a vent pipe, also called a soil vent pipe, that runs from the drain up through the roof. The vent must be at least 100mm above the roof and at least 3 metres away from any window or air intake. For a small house, one 50mm vent pipe is enough for the whole system. For a larger building, you may need multiple vents or a loop vent. Never install an air admittance valve as the only vent; they are useful for individual fixtures but not for the whole system. In Africa, many plumbers skip venting to save money. This is a false economy. A properly vented system lasts longer and needs fewer repairs. For toilets, use a 100mm pipe. For showers and basins, use 50mm. For kitchen sinks, use 50mm and install a grease trap. Grease is the number one cause of kitchen drain blockages. A grease trap is a small box, at least 20 litres, where grease floats and can be skimmed off. Install it between the sink and the drain. Clean it weekly. For restaurants and hotels, install a larger grease trap, at least 100 litres, and clean it daily. Now waste management. In many African cities, there is no central sewer. Buildings use septic tanks or pit latrines. A septic tank must be sized correctly. For a household of six, use a tank of at least 2000 litres. The tank should have two chambers: the first for solids to settle, the second for further digestion. The outlet goes to a soakaway or drain field. The soakaway must be at least 10 metres from any water source, and the soil must be permeable. If the soil is clay, you need a raised bed or a mound system. Never connect rainwater to the septic tank because it will overload it. Never use strong chemicals like bleach in large amounts because they kill the bacteria that break down waste. For pit latrines, ensure the pit is at least 30 metres from any well or borehole, and preferably downhill. Add a vent pipe to reduce smells and flies. Use a fly screen on the vent. In flood-prone areas, raise the latrine above the flood level. For greywater, which is water from sinks, showers, and laundry, do not mix it with blackwater from toilets. Greywater can be reused for irrigation after simple filtration. Install a greywater diverter to a garden or a reed bed. This saves water and reduces the load on the septic tank. In many African countries, water is scarce, so greywater reuse is a smart business practice. You can offer it as an added service to clients. Now consider materials and workmanship. Use solvent cement properly. Clean the pipe and fitting with cleaner, apply cement to both surfaces, push together with a quarter turn, and hold for 30 seconds. Do not use too much cement because it can create a ridge inside the pipe that catches waste. Do not use too little because the joint will leak. For threaded joints, use Teflon tape or paste, but not both. Tighten firmly but not so tight that you crack the fitting. Support horizontal drainage pipes every 1 metre and vertical pipes every 1.5 metres. Use brackets that do not crush the pipe. For underground pipes, bed them in sand and cover with a warning tape so future diggers do not hit them. Test your drainage system before closing walls or floors. Fill each fixture with water and let it drain. Check for leaks at every joint. Run a hose into the vent pipe to check for blockages. If you can, do a smoke test or a water test on the whole system. A water test involves plugging the lowest point and filling the system with water to at least 3 metres head. Let it stand for 15 minutes. If the level drops, you have a leak. Fix it before proceeding. Finally, think about maintenance. Give your client a simple maintenance schedule: clean grease traps weekly, inspect inspection chambers monthly, and flush unused drains every two weeks. Show them how to use a drain snake and a plunger. Warn them not to flush wipes, sanitary pads, or cooking oil. In Africa, many blockages come from these items. A little education goes a long way. In the next lesson, we will cover hot water systems. Before you move on, sketch a drainage layout for a two-bedroom house with one bathroom, a kitchen, and a laundry. Show pipe sizes, slopes, vent pipe, inspection chambers, and septic tank. Bring your sketch to the next session.

3. Module 7 Lesson 3: Hot Water Systems for African Homes and Businesses

Welcome to Lesson 3 of Module 7. In this lesson we focus on hot water systems. In many parts of Africa, hot water is a luxury, but demand is growing. Middle-class homes, guesthouses, hospitals, and restaurants all need reliable hot water. As a plumber, you can earn good money installing and maintaining hot water systems. But you must understand the options, the safety requirements, and the local context. Start with the types of hot water systems. The most common in Africa are electric storage heaters, instant electric heaters, solar water heaters, and gas water heaters. Each has advantages and disadvantages. Electric storage heaters are simple and cheap to buy, but expensive to run if electricity is costly. They come in sizes from 10 litres for a small sink to 200 litres for a large family. They work by heating water in a tank and storing it. The tank is insulated, so water stays hot for hours. Install a pressure relief valve and a vacuum breaker for safety. The pressure relief valve opens if the tank pressure gets too high, preventing an explosion. The vacuum breaker prevents the tank from collapsing if the water supply is cut and the tank cools. In Africa, many electric heaters fail because the pressure relief valve is missing or blocked. Always install it and test it every year. Instant electric heaters, also called tankless heaters, heat water only when you open the tap. They are small and save energy because they do not store hot water. But they need a large electrical supply, often 40 to 60 amps, and they can only supply one or two fixtures at a time. They are good for a single shower or a kitchen sink. They do not work well with low water pressure. If your client has a gravity-fed system with low pressure, an instant heater may not activate. In that case, use a storage heater or a pump. Solar water heaters are ideal for Africa because sunshine is abundant. They use solar collectors to heat water, and a tank to store it. There are two main types: flat plate collectors and evacuated tube collectors. Flat plate collectors are cheaper and work well in sunny areas. Evacuated tube collectors are more efficient in cloudy or cold conditions. Both need a backup electric element for rainy days. Install the tank above the collectors so that thermosiphon circulation works without a pump. If the tank cannot be above the collectors, use a pump and a controller. Solar water heaters save a lot of money on electricity. A family of four can save up to 70 percent of their water heating bill. But they need regular maintenance: clean the collectors, check the antifreeze if used, and inspect the tank for corrosion. In Africa, many solar heaters fail because of poor installation or lack of maintenance. Offer a maintenance contract to your clients. Gas water heaters are popular in areas with reliable gas supply. They heat water instantly and are cheaper to run than electric heaters in many countries. But they produce carbon monoxide, so they must be vented to the outside. Never install a gas heater in a bathroom or a closed room. Install a carbon monoxide detector nearby. Use a qualified gas fitter if you are not trained. In Africa, gas heaters are common in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa. Always check local regulations. Now sizing. To size a hot water system, calculate peak demand. A shower uses about 10 litres per minute of hot water, but it is mixed with cold, so the actual hot water draw is about 5 to 7 litres per minute. A basin tap uses 5 litres per minute. A kitchen sink uses 10 litres per minute. A bath uses 100 litres. For a family of four, assume two showers in the morning, one shower in the evening, and some kitchen use. Peak demand might be 150 litres over one hour. A 150-litre storage heater can handle this if it has a fast recovery time. Recovery time depends on the heating element size. A 3kW element heats about 50 litres per hour. A 4kW element heats about 70 litres per hour. So a 150-litre tank with a 3kW element takes three hours to heat from cold. That is fine if you heat at night and use in the morning. But if you need more, install a larger element or a larger tank. For a guesthouse with 10 rooms, each with a shower, peak demand could be 500 litres over two hours. Use two 300-litre heaters or a central system with a large tank and a boiler. For a hospital, you need hot water 24 hours a day. Use a central system with a storage tank and a recirculation loop. A recirculation loop keeps hot water ready at every tap, reducing wait time and water waste. Install a pump on the return line and a timer or thermostat to run it only when needed. Now installation. Always install a hot water heater on a solid wall or floor. Use a drain pan under the heater to catch leaks. Connect the cold inlet to the bottom and the hot outlet to the top. Install a shut-off valve on the cold inlet. Install a temperature and pressure relief valve on the hot outlet or on the tank itself. Run the discharge pipe to a safe place, preferably outside, and make sure it cannot scald anyone. Use copper or PPR pipes for hot water. Do not use PVC because it softens and can burst. Insulate all hot water pipes to save energy and prevent burns. Use pipe insulation with a high R-value. In Africa, many plumbers skip insulation to save money, but the client pays more in electricity. Explain the savings to your client. For solar systems, mount the collectors facing the equator. In the northern hemisphere, face south. In the southern hemisphere, face north. Tilt the collectors at an angle equal to your latitude plus 10 degrees for year-round performance. For example, in Nairobi, latitude is about 1 degree south, so tilt the collectors at 10 to 15 degrees. In Cape Town, latitude is 34 degrees south, so tilt at 44 degrees. Use a sturdy frame that can withstand wind. Connect the collectors to the tank with insulated pipes. Use a controller to manage the pump and the backup element. Set the backup element to turn on only when the tank temperature drops below 45 degrees Celsius. Now safety. Hot water can cause severe burns, especially for children and elderly people. Set the thermostat to 60 degrees Celsius to kill bacteria, but install a thermostatic mixing valve at each outlet to blend hot and cold water to a safe 45 degrees Celsius. This is especially important in hospitals, clinics, and homes with children. In Africa, many hot water systems are set too high, causing scalds. Always test the water temperature at the tap before you leave. For gas heaters, check for leaks with soapy water. Never use a flame to check for gas leaks. Install a carbon monoxide detector and test it. For electric heaters, check the earth wire and the circuit breaker. Use a residual current device to prevent electric shock. Finally, maintenance. Show your client how to drain the tank every six months to remove sediment. Sediment reduces efficiency and shortens the life of the heater. Replace the anode rod every two years if the tank has one. Check the pressure relief valve every year. For solar systems, clean the collectors every three months and check the antifreeze every year. For gas systems, clean the burner and check the flue every year. Offer a maintenance contract. This gives you recurring income and keeps your clients safe. In the next lesson, we will cover plumbing tools and materials. Before you move on, calculate the hot water demand for a six-room guesthouse. Choose a system, size the tank, and list the safety devices you will install. Bring your plan to the next session.

4. Module 7 Lesson 4: Plumbing Tools, Materials, and Costing for African Markets

Welcome to Lesson 4 of Module 7. In this lesson we focus on tools, materials, and costing. As a plumber in Africa, you need to know what tools to buy, how to maintain them, and how to price your work so you make a profit. Many plumbers fail not because they lack skill, but because they underprice their work or buy cheap tools that break. This lesson will help you run a profitable plumbing business. Start with essential hand tools. You need a pipe wrench, also called a Stillson wrench, in two sizes: 350mm and 450mm. Use it for gripping and turning pipes and fittings. You need an adjustable wrench for nuts and bolts. You need a set of screwdrivers, both flat and Phillips. You need a hammer, a chisel, and a hacksaw with spare blades. You need a tape measure, a spirit level, and a plumb bob. You need a pipe cutter for copper and plastic pipes. You need a deburring tool to smooth cut ends. You need a set of files, both flat and round. You need a drain snake, at least 5 metres long, for clearing blockages. You need a plunger for toilets and sinks. You need a bucket and a sponge for cleanup. You need safety gear: gloves, goggles, dust mask, and safety boots. Do not work without safety gear. In Africa, many plumbers get injured because they skip safety. A good pair of gloves costs little and saves your hands. Now power tools. A cordless drill with a set of masonry and wood bits is essential. A hammer drill is better for concrete walls. An angle grinder is useful for cutting pipes and tiles. A reciprocating saw is good for cutting pipes in tight spaces. A heat gun is useful for softening PVC and shrinking insulation. A pipe threading machine is expensive but useful if you do a lot of galvanised steel work. You can rent one when needed. A pressure test pump is essential for testing water lines. A drain camera is expensive but can help you find blockages and justify your charges. In Africa, many plumbers buy cheap power tools that fail after a few months. Buy good brands if you can, or buy from a reputable supplier with a warranty. Now materials. The most common pipes in Africa are PVC, PPR, PEX, and galvanised steel. PVC is cheap and easy to work with, but only for cold water and drainage. PPR is good for hot and cold water and is easy to fuse. PEX is flexible and good for retrofits. Galvanised steel is strong but corrodes. For fittings, use brass or plastic. Avoid cheap zinc alloy fittings because they crack. For valves, use brass gate valves, ball valves, and check valves. Use a pressure relief valve on every water heater. Use a stop valve on every fixture. Use a float valve on every tank. For tanks, use polyethylene tanks. They are cheap, durable, and easy to install. Buy from a reputable manufacturer because some tanks are made from recycled plastic that cracks. For septic tanks, use concrete or polyethylene. For soakaways, use concrete rings or plastic chambers. For pipes, buy in bulk to save money. But do not buy more than you need because storage can damage pipes, especially PVC in sunlight. Store pipes horizontally on supports, not on the ground. Cover them with a tarp. Now costing. To price a job, you must calculate three things: materials, labour, and overhead. Materials include pipes, fittings, valves, tanks, and any special items. Add 10 percent for waste and mistakes. Labour is your time and your helpers time. Calculate how many days the job will take. Pay yourself a fair daily rate. In many African countries, a plumber earns between 20 and 100 US dollars per day, depending on skill and location. Do not undercharge. If you charge too little, you cannot buy good tools or support your family. Overhead includes transport, fuel, phone calls, and wear and tear on tools. Add 15 percent to cover overhead. Then add profit. A reasonable profit is 20 to 30 percent. So if materials cost 1000, labour is 500, and overhead is 150, your total cost is 1650. Add 25 percent profit, which is 412, so your price is 2062. Round up to 2100. Always give a written quote. Include a description of the work, the materials, the timeline, and the payment terms. Ask for a deposit of 50 percent before you start. This protects you from clients who refuse to pay. In Africa, many plumbers work without a contract and then struggle to get paid. A simple written agreement signed by both parties is enough. Now, how to reduce costs without reducing quality. Buy materials in bulk when you can. Negotiate with suppliers for discounts. Keep a stock of common fittings so you do not have to rush to the shop. Maintain your tools so they last longer. Train a helper so you can do more work in less time. But do not cut corners on safety or quality. A leaky joint or a poorly installed pipe will cost you more in the long run. Now, let us talk about pricing for different types of jobs. For a small repair, like fixing a leaking tap, charge a minimum call-out fee plus the cost of parts. In many African cities, a call-out fee is 10 to 20 US dollars. For a bathroom installation, charge per fixture. For a toilet, charge 50 to 100 US dollars. For a shower, 100 to 200. For a basin, 50 to 100. For a full house rough-in, charge per square metre or per fixture. For a three-bedroom house, a full plumbing rough-in might cost 1500 to 3000 US dollars, depending on the market. For a commercial building, charge more because the work is more complex and the liability is higher. Always visit the site before quoting. Take photos and measurements. Ask about water pressure, water quality, and local regulations. Check for access issues, like narrow gates or stairs. These affect your labour time. Now, how to get paid. Ask for a deposit. Invoice at milestones. For a large job, invoice at 30 percent, 60 percent, and 100 percent completion. Do not hand over the keys or finish the job until you are paid in full. If a client refuses to pay, stop work and seek mediation. In Africa, many plumbers lose money because they trust clients who do not pay. Be professional but firm. Finally, keep records. Keep receipts for all materials. Keep a log of hours worked. Keep a copy of every quote and invoice. This helps you track your profit and improve your pricing. Use a simple notebook or a phone app. At the end of each job, review your costs. Did you make a profit? If not, why? Adjust your prices for the next job. In the next lesson, we will cover business skills and customer service. Before you move on, create a quote for a two-bedroom house that needs a new bathroom, a kitchen sink, and a water tank. List materials, labour, overhead, and profit. Bring your quote to the next session.

5. Module 7 Lesson 5: Business Skills, Customer Service, and Scaling Your Plumbing Business

Welcome to Lesson 5 of Module 7. In this final lesson, we focus on business skills, customer service, and how to scale your plumbing business. You already know the technical side of plumbing. Now you need to know how to make money, keep clients happy, and grow. Many skilled plumbers in Africa remain poor because they do not treat plumbing as a business. They wait for someone to call, they undercharge, and they do not follow up. This lesson will change that. Start with your mindset. You are not just a plumber. You are a business owner. You solve problems. You provide a service that people need. You deserve to be paid well. Do not compete on price alone. Compete on quality, reliability, and professionalism. In every African city, there are many plumbers. The ones who succeed are the ones who answer their phone, arrive on time, do clean work, and stand behind their work. That is your competitive advantage. Now, customer service. The first contact is usually a phone call or a WhatsApp message. Answer promptly. Be polite. Ask questions: What is the problem? Where is it? How long has it been happening? When can I come? Give a clear time window. If you are running late, call or text. Do not just show up late. When you arrive, greet the client respectfully. Put on shoe covers if you have them. Lay down a drop cloth to protect the floor. Keep your tools organised. Do not make a mess. If you need to turn off the water, tell the client first. If you need to move furniture, ask permission. Explain what you are doing as you work. This builds trust. When you finish, test everything in front of the client. Show them that the tap no longer leaks, the toilet flushes properly, and the water runs hot. Clean up all debris. Take away old parts if the client wants. Give them a simple written report: what you did, what parts you used, and what to watch for. Then ask for payment. If the client is happy, ask for a referral. Say: If you know anyone who needs a plumber, please give them my number. Word of mouth is the best marketing in Africa. Now, pricing and payment. Always give a written quote before starting work. For small jobs, you can give a verbal quote, but follow up with a WhatsApp message summarising the price. For larger jobs, use a simple contract. Include the scope of work, the price, the timeline, and the payment terms. Ask for a deposit. For small jobs, ask for payment on completion. For larger jobs, ask for 50 percent upfront, 30 percent at rough-in, and 20 percent at final. Do not be afraid to ask for a deposit. It shows you are professional. If a client refuses to pay a deposit, be cautious. They may not pay at all. Now, marketing. You do not need a big budget. Start with a simple business card. Include your name, phone number, and what you do. Give cards to hardware stores, real estate agents, and property managers. Create a WhatsApp Business profile with your services and photos of your work. Ask happy clients for a short testimonial. Post it on your status or a simple Facebook page. Join local business groups. Offer a small discount for referrals. In Africa, WhatsApp is the most powerful marketing tool. Use it well. Now, record keeping. You must know your numbers. Keep a simple book or spreadsheet with income and expenses. Record every job: date, client, description, amount charged, materials cost, and profit. At the end of each month, add up your income and expenses. Are you making a profit? If not, find out why. Maybe you are undercharging. Maybe you are spending too much on transport. Maybe you are not getting enough jobs. Fix the problem. Also, keep a separate bank account for your business. Do not mix business and personal money. This helps you see your true profit and saves you stress. Now, scaling. How do you grow from a one-person operation to a team? First, hire a helper. Train them well. Pay them fairly. Teach them your standards. Let them do simple tasks while you do the skilled work. As you get more jobs, hire more helpers. Eventually, you can have a team of plumbers. You can take on larger projects. You can specialise: some plumbers do only new installations, some do only repairs, some do only solar water heaters. Specialising can increase your rates because you become an expert. You can also add related services: drain cleaning, leak detection, water tank cleaning, pump installation, and bathroom renovations. <h2>Module 7 Lesson 6: Practical Reference Water Supply, Sanitation, and Costing Benchmarks for African Plumbing Contractors</h2>

<p>This practical reference lesson consolidates the technical, regulatory, and commercial benchmarks you need to size, price, and deliver plumbing work across African markets. It is written for the working plumber or small contractor who must quote a job on Monday, order materials on Tuesday, and pass inspection on Friday often in settings where piped water is intermittent, municipal pressure is unreliable, and clients pay in cash or mobile money. Use the tables and checklist as a field companion to Lessons 15 of this module.</p>

<h3>1. Why Benchmarks Matter More Than Rules of Thumb</h3>

<p>In many African cities, only a fraction of households receive continuous piped water. The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme reports that in 2022, 2.2 billion people globally lacked safely managed drinking water, and Sub-Saharan Africa accounted for the largest share of that gap. For a plumber, that statistic translates into daily design reality: you are rarely sizing for a 3-bar municipal main. You are sizing for a rooftop tank, a borehole with a booster pump, or a 24-hour storage reserve because the utility supplies water for only a few hours a day.</p>

<p>That reality changes every number in your quote: pipe diameters, tank volumes, pump head, and the labour hours needed to commission the system. The benchmarks below are drawn from FAO irrigation and livestock water guidance, WHO sanitation standards, and university extension publications on plumbing and small-business costing. They are starting points always verify against local authority requirements and the manufacturer's data sheet for the specific pipe or pump you are installing.</p>

<p>Once you have a daily demand, size the storage tank for at least one day of consumption plus fire or cleaning reserve. In areas with supply of only two to four hours per day, a two-day reserve is common practice. Then size the distribution pipe so velocity stays between 0.5 and 1.5 m/s in occupied buildings above 1.5 m/s you get noise and erosion; below 0.5 m/s you get sediment and stagnation.</p>

<p>When you quote, present three lines: materials, labour, and contingency. Clients in cash-based markets respond better to a single clear total than to an hourly rate they cannot verify. For repeat work landlords, schools, clinics, agro-processors offer a maintenance contract that covers tank cleaning, pump servicing, and leak inspection twice a year. That recurring revenue is what turns a plumber into a business.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #b00020; background:#fdecef; padding:16px; border-radius:6px; margin:20px 0;">
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-weight:bold; color:#b00020; font-size:1.05em;"> SAFETY WARNING WATER, SANITATION, AND CHEMICALS</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; color:#7a0016;">Water and sanitation work carries serious health risks. Before starting any job involving tanks, drains, or chemical treatment:</p>
<ul style="margin:0; color:#7a0016;">
<li>Never enter a storage tank, septic tank, or pit without confined-space training, a gas monitor, a harness, and a standby person outside. Hydrogen sulphide and methane can kill in seconds.</li>
<li>Wear gloves, eye protection, and a respirator when handling chlorine, lime, or drain chemicals. Follow the label; never mix products.</li>
<li>Disinfect and flush new pipework before commissioning. Contaminated water causes diarrhoeal disease, which WHO identifies as a leading cause of child mortality in Sub-Saharan Africa.</li>
<li>Keep drinking-water pipes and drainage pipes physically separated and clearly labelled. Cross-connections are a common and dangerous installation error.</li>
<li>Dispose of chemical containers and contaminated water according to local regulations never into storm drains or open ground.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>4. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Confirm the source and pressure:</strong> Ask how many hours per day water is available and measure static and residual pressure before sizing pipe or pump.</li>
<li><strong>Size storage for at least one day of demand</strong> (two days where supply is intermittent), plus a 2030% peak factor.</li>
<li><strong>Keep velocities between 0.5 and 1.5 m/s</strong> in occupied buildings to avoid noise, erosion, and stagnation.</li>
<li><strong>Separate potable and waste lines</strong> by the required distance and label every pipe; install non-return valves and air vents.</li>
<li><strong>Disinfect and flush</strong> all new or repaired potable pipework, and record the chlorine dose and contact time.</li>
<li><strong>Quote in three lines</strong> materials, labour, contingency and add a 510% warranty reserve.</li>
<li><strong>Offer a maintenance contract</strong> covering tank cleaning, pump service, and leak inspection twice a year.</li>
<li><strong>Photograph and document</strong> every installation, including hidden pipework, for warranty and future maintenance.</li>
</ul>
