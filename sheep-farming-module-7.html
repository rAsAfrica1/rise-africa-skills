1. Preparing for Lambing Season

Preparing for lambing starts at least six weeks before the first ewe is due. In most of Africa, lambing coincides with the end of the dry season or the start of the rains, so plan around your local climate. First, condition-score your ewes: they should be at 3 to 3.5 on a 5-point scale. Thin ewes (score 2 or less) need extra feedgive 250500 g of maize or sorghum bran per day, plus good hay. Fat ewes (score 4+) risk difficult births, so reduce concentrates. Vaccinate all ewes against pulpy kidney and tetanus 46 weeks before lambing. Also drench for internal parasites using a product like albendazole, following the dosage on the label.

Next, prepare a clean, dry lambing area. A 3 m x 3 m pen can hold 810 ewes. Spread dry straw or wood shavings. Provide a creep areaa small, sheltered corner with a heat lamp or a simple wooden box lined with dry grassfor newborn lambs to escape cold and drafts. In hot climates, ensure shade and ventilation. Have on hand: iodine for navels, a clean bucket, soap, towels, a stomach tube and syringe for feeding colostrum, and a lambing jack or ropes for difficult births. Keep a notebook to record dates and any problems.

Feed ewes well in late pregnancy. The last 6 weeks, the lamb grows 70% of its birth weight. Give 500 g1 kg of a mix of maize, cottonseed cake, and minerals per ewe per day, plus free-choice hay. Provide clean water at all times. Separate ewes that are close to lambing into a maternity pen at night. Check them every 34 hours during the day. Look for signs: restlessness, pawing the ground, a water bag appearing. Most ewes lamb without help, but be ready to assist if no progress after 30 minutes of straining. Keep records of which ewe lambs, when, and how many lambs. This helps you manage future breeding and culling.

2. Normal and Difficult Births

A normal birth (lambing) happens in three stages. Stage 1 lasts 26 hours: the ewe separates from the flock, paws the ground, and may bleat. Stage 2 is active labour: strong contractions, and the water bag appears. The lamb should be delivered within 3060 minutes. Normal presentation is two front feet first, with the head resting between them. Once the head is out, the rest follows quickly. Stage 3 is passing the placenta, usually within 24 hours. Do not pull on the placenta; let it come naturally. If the ewe strains for more than 30 minutes with no progress, or if you see a tail or only one foot, it is a difficult birth.

For a difficult birth, wash your hands and arms with soap and water. Use a mild disinfectant. Lubricate with vegetable oil or clean grease. Gently insert your hand to feel the lambs position. If the head is turned back, push the lamb slightly back and cup the head to bring it forward. If legs are bent, find the legs and pull them into the birth canal. Work slowly. If you cannot correct it in 10 minutes, call for help or a vet. Use a lambing rope or clean towel to grip the legs and pull gently downwards and towards the ewes hocks when she strains. Never pull with force; you can tear the ewe. After a difficult birth, give the ewe antibiotics (e.g., oxytetracycline) as directed by a vet to prevent infection.

After birth, clear the lambs nose and mouth of mucus. If the lamb is not breathing, rub it briskly with a towel or straw. Hold it upside down for a few seconds to drain fluid. Dip the navel in iodine. Ensure the lamb breathes within 30 seconds. For twins or triplets, help the ewe dry them and ensure each gets colostrum. Keep the ewe and lambs in a clean pen for 2448 hours. Watch for retained placenta (more than 12 hours) or a ewe that is off feedthese need veterinary attention. Record any difficult births to avoid breeding from that ewe again if the problem is hereditary.

3. Colostrum Management

Colostrum is the first milk a ewe produces after lambing. It is rich in antibodies, fat, and energy. Lambs are born with no immunity; they must absorb antibodies from colostrum within the first 6 hours of life. After 24 hours, the gut closes and antibodies cannot pass. So timing is critical: the lamb must suckle within 30 minutes to 1 hour of birth. A newborn lamb needs 50 ml of colostrum per kg of body weight in the first 6 hours. For a 4 kg lamb, that is 200 ml. Then feed 200 ml every 46 hours for the first 24 hours. If the lamb is weak, use a stomach tube. Measure the tube from mouth to last rib, mark it, and pass gently. Give colostrum at body temperature (3839C).

If the ewe dies or rejects the lamb, use colostrum from another ewe, or frozen colostrum from a previous lambing. You can also use cow colostrum, but it is less concentrated; add 1 egg yolk per 500 ml to boost energy. Commercial colostrum replacers are available in farm supply shops. Never feed boiled or heated colostrum above 40Cit destroys antibodies. If you must store colostrum, refrigerate for up to 24 hours or freeze in small bags for up to 6 months. Thaw in warm water, not a microwave.

For orphan lambs, feed colostrum via a bottle with a teat. If the lamb wont suck, tube-feed. After the first 24 hours, transition to milk replacer or goat milk. Feed 200250 ml per feeding, 4 times a day for the first week. Always sterilise bottles and tubes. Keep a chart for each lamb: birth weight, time of first colostrum, amount given. Lambs that get enough colostrum are 5 times less likely to die in the first month. In Africa, where diseases like pneumonia and scours are common, colostrum is your cheapest insurance. If a ewe has mastitis or no milk, treat the ewe and hand-feed the lamb. Never leave a lamb to starveit will die within 12 hours.

4. Orphan Lamb Care

Orphan lambs come from ewes that die, reject their lamb, or have no milk. In Africa, many orphan lambs are caused by poor nutrition, disease, or difficult births. Your goal is to keep the lamb warm, fed, and clean. First, move the lamb to a dry, draft-free pen. A simple wooden box with a heat lamp (or a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel) works. The lamb needs an environment of 2530C for the first week. If it is shivering, warm it slowlyrubbing with a towel and warm water bottles. Never feed a cold lamb; warm it first.

Feeding: Use a bottle with a lamb teat. Feed colostrum for the first 24 hours as described in Lesson 3. After that, use a milk replacer specifically for lambs, or goat milk. Mix according to the labelusually 1 part powder to 4 parts warm water (40C). Feed 200250 ml per feeding, 4 times a day for the first week, then 3 times a day. By week 4, reduce to 2 feedings and offer creep feed (a mix of maize, soybean meal, and minerals) and clean hay. Always feed at the same times. Keep a chart: date, time, amount. Weigh the lamb weekly. A healthy orphan gains 150200 g per day. If it loses weight, increase feed or check for disease.

Hygiene is critical. Wash bottles and teats after every feeding with hot soapy water. Rinse well. Store milk replacer in a cool, dry place. Orphan lambs are prone to scours (diarrhoea) and pneumonia. If scours occur, reduce milk amount for one feeding and give oral rehydration solution (1 teaspoon salt, 1 teaspoon sugar in 1 litre water). If pneumonia (coughing, rapid breathing) appears, call a vet for antibiotics. Also, orphan lambs need company. If possible, pen them with a gentle older lamb or a quiet ewe. Social isolation causes stress. After 2 weeks, introduce them to a small group of other lambs. By 8 weeks, they should be eating solid food and can be weaned. Record all treatments and weights. Good care turns a weak orphan into a productive sheep.

5. Weaning Strategies

Weaning is when you separate lambs from their mothers. In Africa, most lambs are weaned at 812 weeks of age, but it depends on breed and feed. For meat breeds like Dorper, wean at 810 weeks. For wool breeds, 1012 weeks. The lamb should weigh at least 1215 kg and be eating solid feed well. Weaning too early (before 6 weeks) causes stress and poor growth. Weaning too late (after 16 weeks) drains the ewe and delays her next pregnancy. Plan to wean when the lambs are strong and the pasture is good.

Before weaning, prepare the lambs. From 2 weeks of age, offer creep feeda high-protein mix (1618% protein) of maize, soybean meal, and minerals. Also provide clean, fresh hay. Lambs learn to eat by watching their mothers. By 6 weeks, they should be eating 200300 g of creep per day. At weaning, move the lambs to a clean, well-fenced paddock with good grass and shade. Leave the ewes behind. The lambs will bleat for 12 days, but they settle. Provide plenty of water and the same creep feed they are used to. Do not change feed suddenly. For the first week after weaning, feed 300400 g of creep per lamb per day, plus hay. After 2 weeks, you can reduce creep and let them graze.

Weaning management: Keep lambs in a group of similar size to reduce bullying. Check daily for signs of stress: droopy ears, off feed, or diarrhoea. If any lamb is sick, isolate and treat. Weigh a sample of lambs at weaning and again 4 weeks later. A good growth rate is 150200 g per day. If growth is poor, check for parasitesdrench with albendazole. Also, vaccinate lambs against pulpy kidney at weaning and give a booster 4 weeks later. Ewes should be dried off: reduce their feed and water for 35 days to stop milk production. Check udders for mastitis. After weaning, ewes can be prepared for the next breeding season. Record weaning weights and dates. This data helps you select the best lambs for breeding and cull poor performers.

6. Growth and Development Monitoring

Monitoring growth is how you know if your lambs are healthy and your management is working. Start by weighing lambs at birth. A normal birth weight for most African breeds is 2.54 kg. Weigh weekly for the first 8 weeks, then every 2 weeks until 6 months. Use a hanging scale or a bathroom scaleweigh yourself, then hold the lamb and subtract. Record weights in a notebook or on a simple chart. A healthy lamb gains 150250 g per day in the first month. By 8 weeks, it should weigh 1218 kg. If a lamb gains less than 100 g per day, investigate: is the ewe producing enough milk? Are parasites present? Is feed adequate?

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a hands-on tool. For lambs, feel the spine and ribs. A score of 3 (on a 5-point scale) is ideal. If you can feel sharp bones, the lamb is thinincrease feed. If you cannot feel bones, it is fatreduce concentrates. Also monitor height and body length: a lamb should double its birth weight by 8 weeks and triple by 16 weeks. Measure heart girth (around the chest just behind the front legs) with a tape measure. A gain of 1 cm in heart girth per week is good. Keep records for each lamb. This helps you spot slow growers early.

Health checks go with growth. Watch for signs of disease: coughing, diarrhoea, lameness, or dull coat. Common problems in lambs include coccidiosis (bloody diarrhoea), pneumonia, and internal parasites. Drench lambs for worms every 46 weeks if pasture is wet. Vaccinate for pulpy kidney and tetanus. If a lamb stops growing, isolate it and check its temperature (normal is 38.539.5C). Treat according to vet advice. At 3 months, select lambs for breeding or sale. Keep the fastest growers; cull the slow ones. In Africa, where feed can be scarce, monitoring growth ensures you invest in the best animals. Good records also help you plan sales and breeding. Weigh, score, and recordevery week.

<h2>Practical Reference: Lambing &amp; Lamb Rearing in African Sheep Enterprises</h2>

<p>This practical reference module consolidates the critical operational knowledge a sheep farmer needs during the lambing season and the first 12 weeks of a lamb's life. In African smallholder and emerging commercial systems, lamb mortality before weaning routinely ranges between 15% and 30% and in poorly managed flocks it can exceed 40%. Every percentage point recovered is direct income. A ewe that raises two healthy lambs instead of one transforms her annual contribution from a cost centre into a profit centre. This lesson is written for the farm manager, extension officer, or lead shepherd who must make fast, correct decisions with limited veterinary access, variable feed, and unpredictable weather.</p>

<p>The economics are simple: in most African markets a weaned lamb sells for between US$40 and US$120 depending on breed, weight, and country. A flock of 50 ewes losing 20% of lambs annually loses roughly 10 lambs a direct revenue loss of US$400 to US$1,200, before accounting for the feed, labour, and veterinary costs already invested in those pregnancies. Reducing mortality to 8% recovers more than half that loss. The practices below are the highest-leverage interventions available to any African sheep enterprise.</p>

<h3>Stage 1: Preparing for Lambing Season</h3>

<p>Preparation begins six weeks before the first expected lamb. In African systems where rams run with ewes year-round, accurate mating dates are often unknown so the practical approach is to condition-score the flock monthly and prepare facilities from the moment a ram is removed. Key actions: flush ewes on good-quality forage or a concentrate supplement (200300 g/day) for three weeks before mating and three weeks after; vaccinate against clostridial diseases (pulpy kidney, tetanus) four to six weeks pre-lambing so antibodies pass into colostrum; deworm strategically, not routinely, using a product appropriate to the local parasite profile; and prepare a clean, dry, draught-free lambing pen with individual bonding pens for ewes and newborns.</p>

<p>Feed the pregnant ewe correctly. In the last six weeks of gestation, 70% of foetal growth occurs. Underfeeding now produces small, weak lambs and poor colostrum; overfeeding produces oversized lambs and difficult births (dystocia). A ewe carrying twins needs roughly 1.5 the energy of a single-bearing ewe. In dry-season lambing, this means supplementary feeding is not optional it is the difference between a live lamb and a dead one.</p>

<h3>Stage 2: Normal and Difficult Births</h3>

<p>A normal birth (presentation) has the lamb coming front feet first, head resting on the knees, in a diving posture. Most ewes deliver unaided within 3060 minutes of active labour. Intervene only if: the ewe has been straining hard for more than 60 minutes with no progress; a water bag has been visible for over two hours; or the lamb is in an abnormal position. Always wash hands and arms, use obstetric lubricant or clean cooking oil, and work slowly. If you cannot correct the position within 15 minutes, call for veterinary assistance internal damage to the ewe is worse than a lost lamb.</p>

<h3>Stage 3: Colostrum Management</h3>

<p>Colostrum is the single most important feed a lamb will ever receive. It delivers immunoglobulins (antibodies), energy, and a laxative effect that clears meconium. The lamb's gut can absorb antibodies efficiently only in the first 6 hours; by 24 hours the window is effectively closed. Target: 50 ml/kg body weight within the first 2 hours, and 200 ml/kg over the first 24 hours. A 4 kg lamb needs roughly 200 ml in the first 2 hours and 800 ml in the first day.</p>

<p>In African conditions, where ewes may be thin or multiple-bearing, colostrum quality varies. If a ewe has insufficient colostrum, use frozen colostrum from a healthy single-bearing ewe, or a commercial colostrum replacer. Cow colostrum is a poor substitute but better than nothing. Never feed boiled colostrum heat destroys antibodies. If a lamb cannot suckle, use a stomach tube (oesophageal feeder) this is a life-saving skill every shepherd should master.</p>

<h3>Stage 4: Orphan Lamb Care</h3>

<p>Orphan lambs arise from ewe death, rejection, mastitis, or triplets. The economics of rearing an orphan are tight: milk replacer and labour can cost US$1525 per lamb, so only rear orphans if the lamb is healthy and the market price justifies it. Otherwise, foster onto a ewe with a single lamb. Fostering techniques: rub the orphan with the ewe's afterbirth or the foster lamb's fluids; confine in a bonding pen for 2448 hours; use a ewe hobble or headgate if she rejects the lamb. If fostering fails, bucket-rear with a teat feeder. Feed milk replacer at 37C, 45 times daily for the first two weeks, reducing to twice daily by week 6. Introduce creep feed from day 10. Wean at 68 weeks for orphans.</p>

<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Zoonotic and Handling Risks</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Brucellosis (<em>Brucella melitensis</em>) and <em>Chlamydia abortus</em> (enzootic abortion) are common in African sheep flocks and are transmitted to humans through contact with aborted foetuses, placentas, and uterine fluids. Always wear disposable gloves when assisting a birth or handling aborted material. Never handle abortion material with bare hands. Burn or deeply bury aborted foetuses and placentas. Disinfect boots, hands, and clothing after handling. Pregnant women and immunocompromised persons must not assist with lambing or handle abortion material. If you develop fever, sweats, or joint pain after handling livestock, seek medical attention and tell the clinician you work with sheep. Additionally, use proper restraint when handling rams they can cause serious injury during the breeding season.</p>
</div>

<h3>Stage 5: Weaning Strategies</h3>

<p>Weaning is the most stressful event in a lamb's life. Abrupt weaning at 8 weeks causes a growth check and increases disease risk. Better practice: wean at 1012 weeks, or when the lamb reaches 2.5 birth weight, whichever comes first. Reduce the ewe's feed 35 days before weaning to lower milk production and reduce mastitis risk. Move ewes away from lambs not the other way around so lambs stay in familiar pens. Keep lambs on the same creep feed for at least 10 days post-weaning. In African market systems where lambs are sold at 46 months, weaning at 12 weeks with continued creep feeding produces the fastest finishing and highest prices.</p>

<h3>Stage 6: Growth and Development Monitoring</h3>

<p>Weigh lambs weekly for the first 8 weeks, then fortnightly. Record birth weight, 4-week weight, 8-week weight, and weaning weight. Target daily liveweight gain: 150250 g/day for meat breeds, 100150 g/day for indigenous breeds under extensive management. A lamb that fails to gain or loses weight for two consecutive weeks is a red flag check for parasites (especially <em>Haemonchus contortus</em>), coccidiosis, pneumonia, or poor milk supply. Use a simple colour-coded record card per lamb. In low-resource settings, a hanging spring scale and a clean plastic crate are sufficient. Data drives decisions: without weights, you cannot know whether your interventions are working.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Colostrum within 2 hours of birth 50 ml/kg minimum; tube-feed if weak.</li>
<li> Dip navel in iodine or chlorhexidine immediately after birth.</li>
<li> Ensure lamb is dry, warm, and in a draught-free pen within 30 minutes.</li>
<li> Confirm suckling within 3 hours; check ewe's udder for mastitis or blind teats.</li>
<li> Record birth weight, date, dam ID, and litter size.</li>
<li> Introduce creep feed at day 1014; fresh clean water always available.</li>
<li> Weigh weekly; investigate any lamb failing to gain for two consecutive weeks.</li>
<li> Wean at 1012 weeks or 2.5 birth weight; keep lambs on familiar feed for 10 days.</li>
<li> Wear gloves for all birthing and abortion handling; disinfect after.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Business takeaway:</strong> Every lamb saved is pure margin. That is the difference between a subsistence flock and a profitable sheep enterprise.</p>
