1. Nominative, Accusative, and Genitive Cases

In Arabic, nouns and adjectives change their endings based on their role in the sentence. This is called 'i'rab' or case. The three main cases are nominative (raf'), accusative (nasb), and genitive (jarr). For example, 'the teacher wrote the lesson' is 'kataba al-mu'allimu ad-darsa'. Here 'al-mu'allimu' (the teacher) is nominative because it's the subject, ending in -u. 'Ad-darsa' (the lesson) is accusative because it's the object, ending in -a. If we say 'I went to the teacher', 'dhahabtu ila al-mu'allimi', 'al-mu'allimi' is genitive because it follows the preposition 'ila', ending in -i. In African business contexts, you might use these cases when writing emails or contracts. For instance, 'The company signed the contract' 'waqqa'at ash-sharikatu al-'aqda'. Here 'ash-sharikatu' is nominative (subject) and 'al-'aqda' is accusative (object). If you say 'The contract of the company', 'al-'aqdu ash-sharikati', 'ash-sharikati' is genitive because it's the second part of a possessive construction (idafa). To master this, practice with everyday sentences. Use flashcards with color-coded endings: red for -u (nominative), blue for -a (accusative), green for -i (genitive). In Swahili or Hausa, you don't have these endings, so it takes time. Start with simple nominal sentences: 'The merchant is honest' 'at-tajiru ameenun' (both nominative). Then add verbs: 'The merchant bought the goods' 'ishtara at-tajiru al-bida'a' (merchant nominative, goods accusative). Listen to Arabic news on BBC Arabic or Al Jazeera and note the endings. In 2 weeks, you'll notice patterns. Remember: prepositions like 'fi' (in), 'ila' (to), 'min' (from) always cause genitive. Verbs cause accusative for objects. Subjects are nominative. Practice with 10 sentences daily, focusing on one case at a time. Write them down, say them aloud, and record yourself. This builds muscle memory for correct endings.

2. Subject and Object Pronouns

Arabic pronouns are either attached (suffixes) or detached (separate words). Subject pronouns are usually detached: 'ana' (I), 'anta' (you, masculine), 'anti' (you, feminine), 'huwa' (he), 'hiya' (she), 'nahnu' (we), 'antum' (you plural), 'hum' (they). Object pronouns are attached to verbs: '-ni' (me), '-ka' (you, masc.), '-ki' (you, fem.), '-hu' (him), '-ha' (her), '-na' (us), '-kum' (you plural), '-hum' (them). For example, 'I wrote it' 'katabtuhu' (katabtu + hu). 'She saw me' 'ra'atni' (ra'at + ni). In African trade, you might say 'He sold it to me' 'ba'ahu li' but with pronouns: 'ba'ahu li' becomes 'ba'ahu li'? Actually, 'ba'a-hu li' (he sold it to me) uses attached -hu for 'it' and separate 'li' for 'to me'. But you can also say 'ba'ani' (he sold me) if 'me' is direct object. Practice with common verbs: 'akala' (eat), 'shariba' (drink), 'kataba' (write), 'qara'a' (read). Conjugate: 'akaltu' (I ate), 'akalta' (you ate), 'akala' (he ate), 'akalat' (she ate), 'akalna' (we ate), 'akaltum' (you plural ate), 'akalu' (they ate). Then add object suffixes: 'akaltuhu' (I ate it), 'akaltuka' (I ate you? unlikely, but grammatically), 'akalani' (he ate me? again odd). Better: 'I saw him' 'ra'aytuhu'. 'They helped us' 'sa'aaduna' (sa'aadu + na). Note that when a verb ends in a vowel, the suffix may change. For instance, 'huwa yara' (he sees) + 'ni' becomes 'yaraani' (he sees me). In Egyptian Arabic, these are simplified, but Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) keeps them. For business, learn to use object pronouns with 'to give' 'a'ta': 'a'tani' (he gave me), 'a'tahu' (he gave him), 'a'taha' (he gave her). Write 10 sentences daily using different subject-object combinations. Use apps like Duolingo or Memrise for drills. In 3 weeks, you'll handle basic conversations.

3. Possessive Pronouns and Idafa Construction

In Arabic, possession is expressed in two ways: using possessive pronouns (attached suffixes) or the idafa construction (a noun followed by another noun in genitive). Possessive suffixes are similar to object pronouns: '-i' (my), '-ka' (your, masc.), '-ki' (your, fem.), '-hu' (his), '-ha' (her), '-na' (our), '-kum' (your plural), '-hum' (their). For example, 'my book' 'kitabi' (kitab + i). 'Your pen' 'qalamuka' (qalam + ka). 'Her house' 'baytuha' (bayt + ha). The idafa construction is used for more complex possession: 'the teacher's book' 'kitabu al-mu'allimi' (book of the teacher). Here the first noun (kitab) loses its 'al-' and the second noun (al-mu'allim) takes genitive case (-i). So 'the president's office' 'maktabu ar-ra'isi'. In African business, you might say 'the company's profits' 'arbahu ash-sharikati'. Notice that the first noun can take any case depending on its role: 'I saw the teacher's book' 'ra'aytu kitaba al-mu'allimi' (kitaba is accusative because it's the object, but still first term of idafa). If you want to say 'my father's car', you can use both: 'sayyaratu abi' (car of my father) or 'sayyarat abi' but 'abi' already means 'my father', so idafa with a possessive suffix on the second noun is common: 'sayyaratu abi' (the car of my father). Actually, 'abi' is 'father + my', so it's 'the car of my father'. To say 'my father's car' you say 'sayyaratu abi'. If you want to say 'the car of the father', 'sayyaratu al-abi'. Practice with family and business terms: 'the merchant's shop' 'dukkanu at-tajiri'. 'The bank's manager' 'mudiru al-banki'. Note that idafa can chain: 'the manager of the bank of the city' 'mudiru banki al-madinati'. Write 10 idafa phrases daily, using words like 'kitab' (book), 'qalam' (pen), 'bayt' (house), 'sharika' (company), 'maktab' (office). Use them in sentences. This is essential for writing formal Arabic.

4. Relative Pronouns and Clauses

Relative pronouns connect a noun to a descriptive clause. In Arabic, the relative pronoun must agree with the noun in gender, number, and case. The main relative pronouns are: 'alladhi' (masculine singular), 'allati' (feminine singular), 'alladhina' (masculine plural), 'allati' (feminine plural, same as singular), and 'allati' for non-human plural. For example, 'the man who wrote the letter' 'ar-rajulu alladhi kataba al-kitaba'. Here 'alladhi' is masculine singular because 'rajul' is masculine singular. 'The woman who wrote the letter' 'al-mar'atu allati katabat al-kitaba'. 'The men who wrote' 'ar-rijalu alladhina katabu'. 'The women who wrote' 'an-nisa'u allati katabna'. Note that the relative pronoun can be omitted in some cases, but in formal Arabic it's usually present. The relative clause is called 'silah' (connection) and must contain a pronoun referring back to the antecedent. For example, 'the book that I read' 'al-kitabu alladhi qara'tuhu'. Here 'qara'tuhu' has the suffix '-hu' referring to 'al-kitab'. Without it, the sentence is incomplete. In African contexts, you might say 'the contract that we signed' 'al-'aqdu alladhi waqqa'nahu'. 'The goods that were sold' 'al-bida'atu allati bi'at'. Practice by combining two sentences: 'The merchant is honest. He sold me the goods.' becomes 'The merchant who sold me the goods is honest' 'at-tajiru alladhi ba'ani al-bida'ata ameenun'. Note that the relative clause comes right after the noun. Use relative clauses in business writing: 'The company that won the bid' 'ash-sharikatu allati fazat bil-munaqasa'. 'The employee who speaks Arabic' 'al-muwazzafu alladhi yatakallamu al-'arabiyya'. Write 10 sentences daily, using different relative pronouns. Listen to Arabic news and identify relative clauses. This will improve your comprehension and production.

5. Interrogative Pronouns and Particles

Interrogative pronouns and particles are used to ask questions. The main ones are: 'man' (who), 'ma' (what), 'ayna' (where), 'mata' (when), 'kayfa' (how), 'limadha' (why), 'kam' (how many), 'ayyu' (which). For example, 'Who is the manager?' 'man huwa al-mudir?' 'What is this?' 'ma hadha?' 'Where is the bank?' 'ayna al-bank?' 'When is the meeting?' 'mata al-ijtima'?' 'How are you?' 'kayfa haluka?' 'Why did you come?' 'limadha ji'ta?' 'How many books?' 'kam kitaban?' (note: 'kam' is followed by accusative singular). 'Which company?' 'ayyu sharikatin?' In African business, you might ask: 'Who is the client?' 'man huwa al-'amil?' 'What is the price?' 'ma huwa as-si'r?' 'Where is the office?' 'ayna al-maktab?' 'When is the deadline?' 'mata al-maw'id?' 'How do I register?' 'kayfa usajjilu?' 'Why is the shipment late?' 'limadha ash-shahnu muta'akhkhirun?' 'How many employees?' 'kam muwazzafan?' Note that 'ma' can also mean 'not' in some contexts, but as a question it's 'what'. 'Man' is for humans, 'ma' for non-humans. 'Ayyu' is used for choice among a group. Practice by asking and answering questions with a partner. Use flashcards with question words on one side and example sentences on the other. In 2 weeks, you'll be able to form basic questions. Remember that in Arabic, question particles like 'hal' can be used for yes/no questions: 'hal anta tajir?' (Are you a merchant?). 'Hal' has no meaning by itself but turns a statement into a question. So 'anta tajir' (you are a merchant) becomes 'hal anta tajir?' (Are you a merchant?). Use these in daily conversations. Write 10 questions daily and answer them.

6. Demonstrative Pronouns in Depth

Demonstrative pronouns point to things or people. In Arabic, they vary by gender, number, and proximity (near vs. far). Near: 'hadha' (this, masc. sing.), 'hadhihi' (this, fem. sing.), 'ha'ula'i' (these, plural). Far: 'dhalika' (that, masc. sing.), 'tilka' (that, fem. sing.), 'ula'ika' (those, plural). For example, 'This is a book' 'hadha kitabun'. 'This is a pen' 'hadhihi qalamun' (note: qalam is masculine, but here we use feminine because? Actually, 'hadhihi' is for feminine nouns, but 'qalam' is masculine, so it should be 'hadha qalamun'. Correction: 'hadhihi' is for feminine, so 'hadhihi sayyaratun' (this is a car). 'These are books' 'ha'ula'i kutubun'. 'That is a house' 'dhalika baytun'. 'That is a school' 'tilka madrasatun'. 'Those are teachers' 'ula'ika mu'allimuna'. In African business, you might say 'This contract is important' 'hadha al-'aqdu muhimmun'. 'These goods are expensive' 'ha'ula'i al-bida'atu ghaliyatan'. 'That company is closed' 'tilka ash-sharikatu muqhlatun'. 'Those clients are waiting' 'ula'ika al-'umala'u yantaziruna'. Note that demonstratives can be used as adjectives before a noun: 'this book' 'hadha al-kitabu'. The noun then takes the definite article. The demonstrative agrees in gender and number with the noun. For example, 'this woman' 'hadhihi al-mar'atu'. 'these women' 'ha'ula'i an-nisa'u'. Practice by pointing to objects around you and naming them: 'hadha maktabun' (this is an office), 'hadhihi shashatun' (this is a screen), 'dhalika babun' (that is a door). Write 10 sentences daily using demonstratives. Use them in dialogues: 'What is this?' 'ma hadha?' 'This is a pen.' 'hadha qalamun.' 'Is that your house?' 'hal dhalika baytuka?' 'Yes, that is my house.' 'na'am, dhalika bayti.' In 2 weeks, you'll master them. Listen to Arabic songs or news and note demonstratives.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Arabic Cases & Pronouns for African Agribusiness

This practical reference is designed for agribusiness professionals, traders, and field officers operating in Arabic-speaking markets across North and East Africa from Sudan and Egypt to Somalia, Djibouti, and the Sahel. Whether you are drafting a supply contract for sesame in Khartoum, negotiating a livestock deal in Mogadishu, or writing a formal letter to a Gulf buyer, correct use of Arabic cases and pronouns signals professionalism and reduces costly misunderstandings.

Arabic grammar is a case-based system. Nouns and adjectives change their ending depending on their grammatical role in the sentence. In Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) the language of contracts, invoices, and official correspondence these endings are usually written but not always pronounced in everyday speech. For business writing, mastering them is essential.

Understanding the Three Cases at a Glance

The three core cases are:

  • Nominative ( / al-marf) used for the subject (fil) and predicate (khabar). Marker: -u (amma) on singular nouns.
  • Accusative ( / al-manb) used for the direct object (mafl bihi), adverbs, and after certain particles. Marker: -a (fata).
  • Genitive ( / al-majrr) used after prepositions and in possessive (ifa) constructions. Marker: -i (kasra).

Subject and Object Pronouns

Arabic pronouns are either detached (independent words) or attached (suffixes). For business correspondence, attached pronouns are far more common and efficient.

Detached subject pronouns: (I), (you, m.), (you, f.), (he), (she), (we), (you pl.), (they).

Attached object pronouns: (-n, me), (-ka, you m.), (-ki, you f.), (-hu, him/it), (-h, her/it), (-n, us), (-kum, you pl.), (-hum, them).

Business example: "We sent you the invoice." Here -n = we (subject suffix on verb), -kum = you (object suffix after preposition).

Possessive Pronouns and Ifa Construction

The ifa () is the Arabic possessive construction. It links two nouns: the first (muf) takes no definite article and no case-ending nn, while the second (muf ilayhi) is always in the genitive case.

Examples for agribusiness:

  • the price of cotton
  • this year's harvest
  • the terms of the contract
  • Port Sudan harbour

Possessive suffixes attach directly to the noun: (my harvest), (your harvest), (his harvest), (her harvest), (our harvest), (your pl. harvest), (their harvest).

Relative Pronouns and Clauses

The main relative pronoun is (alladh, m. sg.), (allat, f. sg.), (alladhna, m. pl.), (allawt, f. pl.). These introduce descriptive clauses essential in contract specifications.

Example: "The company that supplies coffee."

Demonstrative Pronouns in Depth

Demonstratives are essential for pointing to specific goods, contracts, or locations:

  • (hdh) this (m. sg.)
  • (hdhihi) this (f. sg.)
  • (dhlika) that (m. sg.)
  • (tilka) that (f. sg.)
  • (huli) these (pl.)
  • (ulika) those (pl.)

Business example: "This shipment of wheat." Note the demonstrative + ifa combination, common in shipping documents.

SAFETY WARNING Contractual Accuracy

Incorrect case endings can change the legal meaning of an Arabic contract. For example, (I appointed the trader as agent accusative) vs. (incorrect, changes subject/object relationship). In agribusiness deals involving advance payment, export licensing, or quality specifications, a single wrong vowel can shift liability. Always have contracts reviewed by a certified Arabic legal translator before signing. This is especially critical in Sudan, Egypt, and Somalia where MSA case endings carry legal weight in courts.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Identify the subject it takes the nominative (-u). In " ", the farmer is the doer.
  2. Identify the object it takes the accusative (-a). In " ", maize is what is sold.
  3. Check prepositions after f, min, il, al, bi, li, the noun is genitive (-i).
  4. Apply ifa rules first noun: no al-, no tanwn; second noun: genitive case.
  5. Match pronouns to gender and number use -hu for him/it (m.), -h for her/it (f.), -hum for them (m. pl.).
  6. Use correct relative pronoun for m. sg., for f. sg., for m. pl.
  7. Verify demonstratives / for near, / for far; agree in gender.
  8. Double-check written vowels in formal business Arabic, case endings must be written and pronounced correctly.
