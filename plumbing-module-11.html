1. Water Supply Systems in African Towns and Cities

Welcome to Module 11, Lesson 1. This lesson sets the foundation for everything else in this module: understanding where water comes from, how it reaches a building, and how a plumber in an African town or city earns money by keeping that chain working. In most African urban and peri-urban areas, water reaches a property through one of four routes: a piped municipal connection, a borehole with a submersible pump, a rainwater harvesting tank, or a tanker delivery into a storage tank. Your job as a plumber is to know each route and to design, install, and repair the system so the customer has water at the tap every day. Start with the municipal connection. The utility pipe enters the plot and usually terminates at a meter. From the meter, water may go directly into the building or, more commonly in areas with intermittent supply, into a ground tank. Intermittent supply is the norm in many cities, so the plumber must plan for storage. A typical domestic setup is: municipal main, meter, non-return valve, ground tank, booster pump, roof tank or pressure tank, then distribution to taps. Each component has a purpose. The non-return valve stops dirty water from the tank flowing back into the mains. The booster pump lifts water to the roof tank or pressurises the line. The roof tank gives gravity pressure, roughly 0.1 bar for every metre of height. A tank 10 metres above the tap gives about 1 bar, enough for a shower but not for a long hose. Boreholes are the second major source. In many estates and rural trading centres, a borehole with a submersible pump is the only reliable supply. The plumber must understand the pump curve, the static water level, the drawdown, and the delivery head. If the pump is oversized, it cycles too often and burns out. If it is undersized, the customer complains of weak pressure. Always size the pump for the total dynamic head, which is the vertical lift plus friction losses plus the pressure required at the outlet. A simple rule: measure the depth to water, add the height to the highest tap, add 10 to 20 percent for friction, and add the desired pressure in metres. Then choose a pump that delivers the required flow at that head. Rainwater harvesting is growing fast, especially in areas with unreliable mains. The plumber installs gutters, first-flush diverters, a storage tank, and a filtration and disinfection point. The first-flush diverter discards the first rain, which carries dust and bird droppings from the roof. A simple sand and gravel filter or a cartridge filter removes particles. For drinking, the customer must boil or chlorinate. Never tell a customer that rainwater is automatically safe. Tanker delivery is common where neither mains nor borehole is available. The plumber installs a large storage tank, often 5000 to 10000 litres, with a float valve and a pump. The tank must be raised on a concrete plinth, protected from sunlight to reduce algae, and fitted with a screened vent and an overflow pipe that discharges safely away from the foundation. Now think about the business side. In Africa, plumbing is not only about pipes. It is about trust, speed, and clear pricing. When you arrive at a site, ask the customer three questions: where does the water come from, when is it available, and what is the biggest problem. Then inspect the whole system before quoting. A customer who says the tap has no water may actually have a blocked aerator, a closed valve, a failed pump, or an empty tank. Diagnose before you price. Charge for the diagnosis if it takes more than 30 minutes, and tell the customer in advance. Keep a simple toolkit: pipe wrench, adjustable spanner, hacksaw, deburring tool, PTFE tape, solvent cement for PVC, compression fittings for copper and PEX, a pressure gauge, a multimeter for pump circuits, and a torch. In many African markets, PVC and PPR pipes are the most common for cold water, while PPR or copper is used for hot water. Know the local sizes: 20mm, 25mm, 32mm, 40mm, and 50mm are standard. Always pressure-test new pipework before covering it. For PVC, fill the line, bleed air, and hold at 1.5 times the working pressure for one hour. For PPR, follow the manufacturer's heating and fusion times. A leak found before the wall is closed costs you nothing. A leak found after tiling costs you your reputation. Finally, document everything. Take photos of the meter reading, the tank, the pump label, and the pipe route. Write a simple invoice with parts, labour, and a one-line description of the work. Give a warranty: 30 days on labour, and the manufacturer's warranty on pumps and tanks. This builds repeat business. In the next lesson, we will go deeper into pumps and pressure systems. Before then, visit a local hardware store and note the prices of the five most common fittings you use. Knowing your costs is the first step to profitable pricing. Remember, in Africa, water is life, and the plumber who brings it reliably is a community asset. Build your skill, build your name, and the work will follow.

2. Pumps, Tanks, and Pressure Systems for Reliable Supply

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 11. In Lesson 1 we mapped the sources of water. Now we focus on the heart of the system: pumps, tanks, and pressure. This is where most plumbing businesses make their money, because pumps fail often and customers need them fixed fast. We will cover pump types, sizing, installation, common faults, and the business of pump service contracts. Start with pump types. The most common in African domestic and small commercial settings are the centrifugal pump, the submersible pump, the jet pump, and the booster pump. A centrifugal pump is used where water is already above ground, for example to move water from a ground tank to a roof tank. It is not self-priming, so it must be flooded. A jet pump can lift water from a shallow well, usually up to about 8 metres, and is self-priming. A submersible pump sits inside a borehole or a deep tank and pushes water up. A booster pump increases pressure in a line that already has water, for example after a gravity tank. Each has a job. Never use a centrifugal pump to suck water from a deep well; it will cavitate and fail. Sizing a pump is the most important technical skill in this lesson. You need three numbers: flow rate, total head, and power supply. Flow rate is litres per minute or cubic metres per hour. A typical house needs 20 to 40 litres per minute for comfortable use. A small hotel or guesthouse needs 100 to 200 litres per minute. Total head is the sum of static lift, friction loss, and pressure head. Static lift is the vertical distance from the water level to the highest outlet. Friction loss depends on pipe length, diameter, and fittings. As a rule of thumb, add 10 percent for every 100 metres of straight pipe, and add the equivalent length of fittings. Pressure head is the pressure you want at the tap, converted to metres: 1 bar equals 10 metres. So if you want 2 bar at the tap, that is 20 metres of head. Add everything, then choose a pump whose curve gives your required flow at that head. Do not choose a pump by horsepower alone. A 1 HP pump can be strong or weak depending on its impeller and speed. Always read the pump curve. If the supplier cannot show you a curve, go to another supplier. Installation matters as much as selection. For a submersible pump, the borehole must be straight and clean. The pump must sit above the bottom by at least 1 metre, and the water level must never drop below the pump inlet. Install a non-return valve on the pump outlet, a safety rope, and a torque arrestor if the pump is powerful. The cable must be waterproof and secured to the pipe every 3 metres. The control box must be above ground, protected from rain, with a proper earth connection. In many African countries, voltage fluctuates, so install a surge protector and, for three-phase pumps, a phase failure relay. These small devices save expensive motors. For surface pumps, mount them on a concrete base, align the suction pipe with a slight upward slope to the pump, and keep the suction pipe as short and straight as possible. Use a foot valve with a strainer. Prime the pump before starting. Never run a pump dry, even for a minute. Dry running destroys seals and impellers. Tanks are the next focus. In Africa, the most common tanks are polyethylene, galvanised steel, and concrete. Poly tanks are cheap, light, and easy to install, but they must be protected from direct sunlight to prevent algae and to extend their life. Steel tanks are strong but rust unless coated inside and out. Concrete tanks are durable but must be properly cured and waterproofed. Whatever the material, the tank must have an inlet, an outlet, an overflow, a drain, and a vent. The overflow must be screened to keep out insects and small animals, and it must discharge to a safe place, not onto the wall or foundation. The tank base must be level and strong enough to carry the full weight: one litre of water weighs one kilogram, so a 5000 litre tank weighs 5 tonnes. Never put a large tank on a wooden platform. Use a reinforced concrete slab or a steel stand designed by an engineer. Pressure systems are what make the tap feel normal. In a gravity system, pressure comes from height. In a pumped system, pressure comes from a pump and a pressure tank. The pressure tank, also called a hydro-pneumatic tank, stores water under pressure so the pump does not start every time a tap opens. Set the pressure switch to start the pump at the low setting and stop it at the high setting. A common setting is 1.5 bar to start and 3 bar to stop. The pressure tank pre-charge should be about 0.2 bar below the start pressure. If the tank is waterlogged, the pump cycles rapidly and fails. Check the pre-charge with a tyre gauge and replace the bladder if needed. Common faults and fixes: no water at the tap but pump runs, check for a closed valve, a blocked strainer, or a broken pipe. Pump hums but does not start, check the capacitor, the voltage, and the pressure switch. Pump runs but pressure is low, check for a worn impeller, a leaking foot valve, or a clogged filter. Pump starts and stops rapidly, check the pressure tank pre-charge and the pressure switch settings. Motor trips the breaker, check for a short circuit, a bad capacitor, or a seized bearing. Always switch off the power and lock it out before working on a pump circuit. In Africa, power can come back unexpectedly, so never rely on a switch alone. Now the business angle. Pumps are a service business. Offer an annual service contract: inspect the pump, clean the strainer, check the pressure tank, test the pressure switch, measure the current, and record everything. Charge a monthly or quarterly fee. This gives you predictable income and saves the customer from sudden breakdowns. Keep a stock of common spares: capacitors, pressure switches, foot valves, float valves, and pump seals. When you install a pump, label the breaker, write the installation date on the control box, and give the customer a simple manual. Teach them how to switch off the pump if a pipe bursts. A customer who understands the system will call you for service, not for emergencies. In the next lesson, we will look at drainage and sanitation, which is the other half of plumbing. Before then, practice sizing a pump for a two-storey house with a 3000 litre ground tank, a 2000 litre roof tank, and a highest tap 8 metres above ground. Write down your flow, head, and pump choice. Bring your notes to the next session.

3. Drainage, Sanitation, and Waste Systems

Welcome to Lesson 3 of Module 11. Water supply brings water in; drainage takes waste out. In Africa, poor drainage causes more disease and more property damage than poor supply. A plumber who understands drainage is a public health worker and a business owner. This lesson covers soil pipes, waste pipes, traps, vents, septic tanks, soakaways, and the common problems you will meet in towns, estates, and rural areas. Start with the difference between soil and waste. Soil pipes carry human waste from toilets. Waste pipes carry greywater from sinks, showers, and washing machines. In many African buildings, the two are combined into a single stack, but it is better to keep them separate where possible, because soil pipes need a larger diameter and a steeper fall. The minimum diameter for a toilet drain is 100mm, and for a sink or shower it is 40mm to 50mm. The fall, or gradient, is critical. A common rule is 1 in 40 for 100mm pipe and 1 in 30 for 50mm pipe. That means for every metre of pipe, the fall is 25mm for 100mm and 33mm for 50mm. Too little fall and solids settle. Too much fall and the water runs away from the solids, leaving them behind. Use a spirit level and a string line to set the fall accurately. Traps are the next essential. Every fixture must have a trap to hold a water seal that stops sewer gases from entering the building. The seal depth should be 50mm to 75mm. A trap can lose its seal through evaporation, through siphonage, or through back pressure. Siphonage happens when a large volume of water flows down the stack and sucks the water out of a nearby trap. Back pressure happens when the stack is blocked and air pushes back. The cure is proper venting. A vent pipe allows air into the system so the water can flow freely. The vent must rise above the roof, usually at least 300mm above the roof surface and away from windows. In a single-storey house, a 50mm vent is often enough. In a multi-storey building, you need a vent stack connected to each branch. If you cannot run a full vent, use an air admittance valve, which is a one-way valve that lets air in but not out. It is cheaper and easier, but it must be accessible for replacement. Now consider the septic tank. A septic tank is a watertight chamber that separates solids from liquids. The solids settle to the bottom and form sludge, while the liquids flow out to a soakaway or a drain field. A typical tank for a family of six is 2000 to 3000 litres, with two or three chambers. The inlet and outlet must have T-pipes or baffles to prevent scum and sludge from leaving. The tank must be vented, and it must be emptied every two to five years depending on use. Never build a septic tank with open joints or a porous floor; that lets untreated waste into the ground and can contaminate wells. The soakaway is where the liquid goes. It can be a pit filled with stones, a set of perforated pipes in trenches, or a seepage bed. The soil must be permeable. If the soil is clay, a soakaway will not work, and you need a mound system or a connection to a sewer. The soakaway must be at least 15 metres from any well or borehole, and downhill from it. In many African cities, the sewer network is unreliable or nonexistent, so on-site systems are the norm. As a plumber, you must know the local rules. Some councils require a permit for a septic tank. Some require a minimum distance from a boundary. Check before you dig. Common drainage problems and fixes: slow draining sink, usually a grease or hair blockage. Remove the trap and clean it. If the blockage is further down, use a drain snake or a pressure jet. Bad smell in the bathroom, usually a dry trap or a blocked vent. Pour water into the trap and check the vent. Gurgling sounds, usually a blocked vent or a partially blocked drain. Toilet backs up when you flush, usually a blockage in the soil pipe or a full septic tank. Check the tank level and pump it out if needed. Water pooling around the septic tank, usually a failed soakaway or a high water table. You may need to enlarge the soakaway or install a pump chamber. Sewage backing up into the house, usually a blocked main or a collapsed pipe. This is an emergency. Stop using water, open the inspection chamber, and clear the blockage. If the pipe is collapsed, you must dig and replace it. Safety is non-negotiable. Sewage contains bacteria, viruses, and parasites. Wear gloves, boots, and eye protection. Wash your hands with soap and clean water after every job. Never eat or drink while working on a drain. Use a disinfectant on tools and on the work area. If you cut yourself, stop, clean the wound, and cover it. If you feel sick after working with sewage, see a doctor and tell them what you were doing. Now the business side. Drainage work is often urgent, so customers will pay a premium for fast service. Offer a call-out service with a clear fee for the first hour and an hourly rate after that. Keep a drain machine, a set of rods, a plunger, and a bucket of disinfectant in your vehicle. For new installations, quote by the fixture and by the metre of pipe. A typical quote includes the pipe, fittings, traps, vent, labour, and the connection to the tank or sewer. Always test the system before you leave. Fill each fixture and watch the flow. Check for leaks at every joint. Run the tap and listen for gurgling. If the system is correct, the water should run away quickly and quietly. In the next lesson, we will cover bathroom and kitchen installation, which is where drainage and supply meet. Before then, inspect a septic tank and a soakaway in your area. Note the size, the distance from the well, and the condition. Write a short report. This practice will make you confident when a customer asks for a new system.

4. Bathroom and Kitchen Installation and Renovation

Welcome to Lesson 4 of Module 11. This lesson is about the work that customers see and pay for: bathrooms, kitchens, and the fixtures that make a house comfortable. In Africa, bathroom and kitchen renovations are a growing market, especially in cities where landlords upgrade rental units and homeowners improve their properties. A plumber who can install a complete bathroom or kitchen cleanly and on time will never lack work. We will cover planning, first fix, second fix, waterproofing, fixture installation, and the business of renovation. Start with planning. Before you buy anything, measure the room and talk to the customer about how they will use it. Ask about water pressure, hot water, storage, and drainage. A bathroom needs a toilet, a washbasin, a shower or bathtub, and a floor drain. A kitchen needs a sink, a tap, a dishwasher point if required, and a washing machine point if required. Draw a simple plan on paper. Mark the position of the soil pipe, the waste pipe, the water supply, and the electrical points. Remember that water and electricity must be separated. A socket must not be directly above a sink or a shower. In many countries, the rule is at least 600mm from a water source. If you are unsure, ask a qualified electrician. First fix is the work you do before the walls are plastered and tiled. This is where you run the pipes. For water supply, use PPR or copper for hot water and PPR, PVC, or PEX for cold water. For drainage, use PVC or uPVC. Run the pipes in straight lines, with clips every 600mm for horizontal runs and every 1 metre for vertical runs. Leave enough pipe sticking out of the wall for the fixture connection. Cap all open ends to keep out dirt and insects. Take photos of the pipe routes before the wall is closed. These photos will save you when a customer wants to hang a cabinet or drill a tile. Pressure-test the supply pipes before closing. Fill the line, bleed the air, and hold at 1.5 times the working pressure for one hour. For drainage, fill the pipe with water and check for leaks at every joint. Second fix is the work after tiling and painting. This is where you install the fixtures. Start with the toilet. The soil pipe must be at the correct height and angle. The toilet pan must sit level and be sealed to the floor with silicone or a wax ring. The cistern must be connected to the water supply with a flexible hose and an isolation valve. Check the flush and the fill. Adjust the float valve so the water stops at the marked level. Next, the washbasin. The waste must have a trap, and the trap must be accessible. The tap must be connected with an isolation valve so the customer can shut off the water for maintenance. The basin must be sealed to the wall or counter with silicone. Then the shower. The shower tray must be level and sealed to the wall. The waste must be connected to the drain with a trap. The shower mixer must be connected to hot and cold supplies, with the hot on the left and the cold on the right. Test the temperature and the flow. Finally, the kitchen sink. The sink must be set in a counter with a waterproof seal. The tap must have an isolation valve. The waste must have a trap and a connection to the drain. If there is a dishwasher or washing machine, install a standpipe with a trap and a water supply point with a valve. Waterproofing is the step that saves you from callbacks. In a bathroom, the floor and the walls around the shower must be waterproofed before tiling. Use a cement-based waterproofing membrane or a liquid applied membrane. Apply two coats, and let it dry according to the manufacturer's instructions. Turn the membrane up the wall at least 200mm, and 1.8 metres in the shower area. Seal all corners and pipe penetrations with a flexible sealant. If you skip waterproofing, water will leak into the room below, and the customer will blame you. Common installation mistakes and how to avoid them: pipes not clipped, causing noise and vibration. Traps not accessible, making future cleaning impossible. No isolation valves, so the customer must shut off the whole house for a small repair. Wrong fall on the waste pipe, causing slow drainage. Toilet not sealed, causing smells and leaks. Shower tray not level, causing water to pool. Tap not connected to the correct supply, causing scalding. Avoid these by working slowly and checking each step. Now the business of renovation. Renovation work is different from new build because you are working in an occupied house. The customer may be living there, so you must keep the work area clean and safe. Cover the floor, protect the furniture, and remove waste daily. Agree on a start time and a finish time. Give the customer a written schedule with milestones: first fix, waterproofing, tiling, second fix, testing, and handover. Ask for a deposit to buy materials, usually 50 percent. Buy quality materials from a reputable supplier. Cheap taps and toilets fail quickly and damage your reputation. Offer a warranty: one year on installation, and the manufacturer's warranty on fixtures. Keep a record of the serial numbers and the purchase dates. When you finish, test everything in front of the customer. Fill the basin, flush the toilet, run the shower, and check for leaks. Show the customer where the isolation valves are and how to use them. Give them a simple maintenance guide: clean the aerators, check the traps, and call you if they see a leak. In the next lesson, we will cover business skills, pricing, and customer care, which will help you turn your technical skill into a profitable company. Before then, plan a bathroom for a small house. Draw the layout, list the materials, and write a quote. Bring it to the next session for review.

5. Business Skills, Pricing, and Customer Care for Plumbers

Welcome to Lesson 5 of Module 11. You now have the technical knowledge: supply, pumps, drainage, and installation. This final lesson is about turning that knowledge into a profitable, respected plumbing business. In Africa, many skilled plumbers remain poor because they cannot price, cannot manage customers, and cannot keep records. This lesson will help you avoid that trap. We will cover pricing, quoting, invoicing, customer care, marketing, record keeping, and growth. Start with pricing. There are three common ways to charge: hourly, per job, and per project. Hourly is good for repairs and small jobs. Per job is good for installations like a toilet or a tap. Per project is good for a full bathroom or a building. Whatever method you use, you must know your costs. Your costs include materials, transport, tools, labour, and overhead. Overhead includes rent, phone, insurance, and the depreciation of your tools. Add a profit margin, usually 20 to 30 percent. Do not compete only on price. Compete on quality, speed, and reliability. A customer who chooses the cheapest plumber often pays twice. When you quote, be specific. List the materials, the labour, the time, and the warranty. State what is not included. For example, if the customer must buy the tiles, say so. If the work requires a helper, include the helper's cost. If the job may need a permit, say so. Give the quote in writing, and ask the customer to sign it. A signed quote protects both of you. For larger jobs, ask for a deposit. A common arrangement is 50 percent on acceptance, 40 percent on completion of the first fix, and 10 percent on handover. Never buy materials with your own money for a customer who has not paid a deposit. You are not a bank. Invoicing is the next step. An invoice must have your business name, your contact details, the customer's name, the date, the invoice number, a description of the work, the parts used, the labour, the total, and the payment terms. Keep a copy. If the customer does not pay, follow up politely but firmly. Send a reminder after seven days. If there is still no payment, stop work and seek advice. In many African countries, small claims courts can help, but prevention is better. Do not hand over the final work until the final payment is made. Customer care is what makes a customer call you again. Answer your phone. Arrive on time. If you are delayed, call. Wear clean clothes. Speak politely. Listen to the customer's problem without interrupting. Explain what you will do and how long it will take. Keep the work area clean. Do not play loud music. Do not ask for food or drinks. Do not use the customer's toilet without permission. When you finish, clean up and remove all waste. Ask the customer to inspect the work with you. If they are not satisfied, fix it before you leave. A happy customer tells three people. An unhappy customer tells ten. Marketing is how you get new customers. In Africa, word of mouth is the strongest marketing. Ask every satisfied customer for a referral. Give them a small discount on their next job if they refer someone. Keep a simple customer list with names, phone numbers, and the work you did. Send a message every few months to remind them of your service. Use a sign on your vehicle or your toolbox. Use social media if you have a smartphone. Post photos of your work, with the customer's permission. Join a local plumbers' association if one exists. Attend training and trade fairs. Build relationships with hardware stores, estate agents, and landlords. They can send work your way. Record keeping is the difference between a business and a hustle. Keep a notebook or a simple spreadsheet with every job: date, customer, description, materials, cost, price, and profit. At the end of the month, add up your income and your expenses. Are you making a profit? If not, find out why. Maybe your prices are too low. Maybe you are spending too much on transport. Maybe you are not getting enough jobs. Fix the problem. Keep your receipts for materials and tools. Separate your business money from your personal money. Open a bank account or a mobile money account for the business. Save a portion of every payment for taxes, tools, and emergencies. In many countries, you must register your business and pay tax. Do it. It protects you and helps you grow. Growth is the final topic. You cannot do everything alone. Train an apprentice. Teach them safety, cleanliness, and customer care. Pay them fairly. As your business grows, you can buy a vehicle, hire more plumbers, and take on larger projects. But do not grow too fast. Grow only when you have the work and the systems to support it. Keep learning. Technology changes: new pipes, new pumps, new taps. Read manuals. Watch videos. Attend workshops. The best plumbers are the ones who never stop learning. Let us review the whole module. Lesson 1 covered water sources and system design. Lesson 2 covered pumps, tanks, and pressure. Lesson 3 covered drainage and sanitation. Lesson 4 covered bathroom and kitchen installation. Lesson 5 covered business skills. Together, these lessons give you the technical and commercial foundation to succeed as a plumber in Africa. Your final assignment is to write a one-page business plan. Include your services, your target customers, your prices, your costs, and your marketing plan. Bring it to the next session. Remember, plumbing is not just a trade. It is a service that protects health, saves water, and builds communities. Do it well, and you will be respected and rewarded. Thank you for completing Module 11.

<h2>Practical Reference: Plumbing Materials, Pipe Sizing, and Water Quality for African Conditions</h2>

<p>This lesson is a working reference for the plumber who is quoting a job on Monday and installing on Tuesday. It covers the three decisions that most often determine whether a job makes money or loses money: which pipe material to specify, what diameter to run, and how to protect the customer's water quality. Get these wrong and you return for free repairs. Get them right and you build a reputation that fills your diary without advertising.</p>

<p>Across most African markets, the plumber who can confidently specify materials, read a sizing table, and explain water safety to a client wins the commercial and institutional work schools, clinics, guesthouses, small factories, and NGO-funded water points. That is where the margins are. Domestic repair work pays the bills; specification work pays the profit.</p>

<h3>Sizing: Getting the Diameter Right First Time</h3>

<p>Undersized pipe means poor pressure and unhappy customers. Oversized pipe wastes the client's money on materials. The rule of thumb: size for velocity, not for volume. Keep cold water below 1.5 m/s and hot water below 1.2 m/s to control noise, erosion, and pressure loss. <p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#a5281b"> SAFETY WARNING Water Quality and Chemical Handling</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;color:#7a1e14">Drinking water must meet WHO <em>Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality</em>. Never connect a plumbing line to a source of unknown quality without testing. Chlorine dosing must be calculated over-dosing causes burns and pipe damage; under-dosing leaves pathogens. Store chlorine and acid-based descalers in labelled, locked containers away from food and children. <h3>Water Quality: What the Plumber Must Protect</h3>

<p>Plumbers are the last line of defence between a water source and a family's health. WHO estimates that contaminated drinking water causes hundreds of thousands of diarrhoeal deaths each year, most in low- and middle-income countries. A plumber who installs a clean, correctly disinfected system prevents illness; one who cross-connects a tank to a sewer line causes it. Practical steps: flush and disinfect new mains with a chlorine solution (typically 50 mg/L free chlorine for 30 minutes, then flush), keep tank lids sealed, screen overflow and vent pipes against insects and rodents, and label every non-potable line clearly.</p>

<p>For boreholes and shallow wells, test for faecal coliforms, nitrate, iron, and in mining or industrial areas heavy metals. In many African countries, national standards bodies and ministries of water publish free testing guidance. Where no lab is nearby, field test kits for chlorine residual and turbidity give a fast indication of whether treatment is working.</p>

<h3>Business-First Notes</h3>

<p>Every technical decision in this lesson has a commercial consequence. Specify PPR for hot water and you avoid a callback when the uPVC fails at 60C. Size the branch correctly and the customer does not call you back complaining about weak showers. Disinfect the tank and the guesthouse owner recommends you to the next guesthouse. Build a simple quotation template that lists material, size, quantity, unit price, and labour separately clients trust itemised quotes and they protect you when prices move. Keep a small stock of the three most-used fittings in each size so you are not losing a day to the hardware shop.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Confirm the job type</strong> repair, renovation, or new build before quoting; new builds need sizing calculations, repairs usually do not.</li>
<li><strong>Measure static and residual pressure</strong> at the point of connection; if below 0.5 bar, discuss a tank and pump with the client before promising performance.</li>
<li><strong>Match material to duty</strong> uPVC for cold and drainage, PPR for hot, HDPE for buried mains, copper only where the budget and water chemistry allow.</li>
<li><strong>Size for velocity</strong> keep cold below 1.5 m/s and hot below 1.2 m/s; check the flow table before choosing diameter.</li>
<li><strong>Disinfect and flush</strong> every new or repaired drinking-water line and tank before handing over.</li>
<li><strong>Eliminate cross-connections</strong> with an air gap or approved backflow preventer; label all non-potable lines.</li>
<li><strong>Photograph the installation</strong> before closing walls or trenches it protects you and helps the next plumber.</li>
<li><strong>Issue an itemised quote and a written handover note</strong> with materials, sizes, test results, and a simple maintenance tip for the client.</li>
</ul>
