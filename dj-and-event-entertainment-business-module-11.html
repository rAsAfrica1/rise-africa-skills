Lessons

1. Weddings: Ceremony, Cocktail, and Reception

A wedding has three distinct phases, each needing a different DJ approach. During the ceremony, your job is to support the mood without overpowering it. Play soft instrumentals or acoustic covers at low volume (around 60-70 dB) as guests arrive. Use a wireless microphone for the officiant and ensure you have a backup wired mic. In Nigeria, popular ceremony songs include 'Onyame Betwi' by Ohemaa Mercy and 'Eze Ebube' by Neon Adejo. In Kenya, 'Malaika' by Fadhili Williams is a classic. Keep a playlist of 10-15 songs ready, but be prepared to fade out quickly if the couple wants a specific entrance song.

Cocktail hour is your chance to set a relaxed, social vibe. Move to jazz, soul, or Afrobeat instrumentals. Volume should allow conversationaround 70 dB. Use this time to test your reception sound system and check wireless mic batteries. In South Africa, deep house and amapiano instrumentals work well. Have a tablet or laptop with a DJ software like Serato or Virtual DJ, and a backup on a USB drive. Keep the energy low but engaging.

Reception is the main event. You'll need to coordinate with the MC and caterers. Play high-energy Afrobeat, amapiano, or hip-hop for the grand entrance and first dance. In Ghana, 'Kpanlogo' and highlife classics get everyone moving. Use a proper PA system with subwoofersat least 2000 watts for 200 guests. Have a wireless mic for toasts and speeches. Be ready to switch genres quickly: from romantic dinner music to dance floor bangers. Always have a 'do not play' list from the couple. Charge $300-$800 in Nairobi or Lagos for a full wedding, depending on equipment and hours. Always carry backup cables, a power strip, and a phone with offline music in case of internet failure.

2. Corporate Events and Conferences

Corporate gigs pay better and are more predictable than clubs. You'll typically DJ at conferences, product launches, award dinners, and team-building events. The key difference: you're background, not the star. Arrive at least 2 hours early to set up. For a conference for 100-300 people, you need a clean, professional setup: a laptop with Serato or Traktor, a controller like Pioneer DDJ-SB3, and a PA system with clear vocals. Avoid heavy bass during speeches. Use a mixer with separate volume controls for music and microphones.

During registration and breaks, play instrumental jazz, lo-fi, or Afrobeat without vocals. Volume should be around 65 dB so people can network. For a product launch, you might need to play a specific brand anthem or walk-up music. In Kenya, corporate clients often request 'Wimbo' by Sauti Sol or 'Nakupenda' by Nyashinski for upbeat moments. In Nigeria, 'Fall' by Davido or 'Ye' by Burna Boy work for energy. But always get the client's approval on any song with explicit lyrics.

Award dinners require tight timing. You'll work with an MC and event manager. Have stings and transition sounds readylike a 2-second applause or drum roll. Use a software like Virtual DJ with hot cues for quick access. For after-party or networking sessions, you can raise energy with amapiano or afrobeats. Charge $500-$1500 in Johannesburg or Lagos for a full-day corporate event. Always bring a backup laptop, extra RCA cables, and a power conditioner to protect against surges. Get a 50% deposit upfront and a signed contract. Corporate clients value punctuality and professionalism above all.

3. Club and Festival Performances

Club and festival sets are high-energy and demand quick mixing. In a club, you might play 2-4 hour sets. In festivals, you get 45-90 minutes. Know your crowd: in Lagos, expect afrobeats, amapiano, and hip-hop. In Nairobi, gengetone and dancehall are huge. In Cape Town, deep house and techno dominate. Bring your own headphones (e.g., Pioneer HDJ-X5) and a USB with Rekordbox-analyzed tracks. Most clubs have CDJ-2000s or 3000s, so prepare your music in Rekordbox or Serato.

For festivals, you need to adapt to large crowds and possibly play on a stage with a monitor speaker. Sound check is criticaltest your levels and EQ. Use a controller if the club doesn't have CDJs, but always have a backup USB. Mix in key using Camelot wheel to avoid clashes. For African festivals like Nyege Nyege in Uganda or Afrochella in Ghana, blend local sounds with global hits. Keep the energy high with quick cuts and drop swaps every 30-60 seconds. Watch the crowd: if they're not dancing, change the genre.

Pay varies: clubs pay $100-$300 per night in Nairobi or Accra. Festivals pay $500-$2000 for a set. Always negotiate travel and accommodation. Bring earplugs (e.g., Earasers) to protect your hearing. Stay soberyou're working. Have a bag with extra USB drives, a phone charger, and a small flashlight. Network with other DJs and promoters; they book future gigs. Record your sets (with permission) to promote yourself on Mixcloud or SoundCloud. After the show, get feedback from the promoter and ask for a testimonial.

4. School Dances and Community Events

School dances and community events are great for building your reputation and earning steady income. For a high school dance in Kenya or Nigeria, you'll play clean versions of popular songs. Expect a crowd of 100-500 students. Use a PA system with clear vocalsno subwoofers needed. Volume should be around 85 dB, but check with teachers. Play afrobeats, gengetone, and dancehall, but avoid explicit lyrics. Have a playlist of approved songs from the school administration. In South Africa, amapiano and gqom are popular. Charge $150-$400 for a 3-hour event.

Community events include fundraisers, cultural festivals, and national day celebrations. In Ghana, you might DJ at a Homowo festival or a church bazaar. Play highlife, gospel, and hiplife. For cultural events, research traditional music: in Nigeria, juju and fuji; in Kenya, benga and ohangla. Use a microphone to make announcements and keep the crowd informed. Bring a table for your equipment and a chair. Ensure you have a power extension cord and a surge protector.

For school events, you'll often work with a student committee. Get a written agreement with the school specifying hours, payment, and equipment. Arrive early to set up in the hall or outdoor field. Test your sound before students arrive. Have a backup for everything: extra cables, a spare laptop, and a phone with offline music. During the event, take requests but filter them for appropriateness. Keep the energy up with games like 'dance battles' or 'freeze dance'. After the event, pack up quickly and get a signature on your invoice. Word-of-mouth from students and teachers leads to more gigs. Always dress neatlyyou're a role model.

5. Private Parties and Milestone Celebrations

Private parties include birthdays, anniversaries, graduations, and retirement parties. These are often intimate, with 20-100 guests. The key is personalization. Before the event, ask the host for a list of must-play songs and do-not-play songs. For a 50th birthday in Lagos, you might play old-school juju, fuji, and highlife from the 80s and 90s. In Nairobi, expect rhumba, benga, and classic reggae. Use a compact PA system like a JBL EON or QSC K series. Volume should be comfortable for conversation and dancingaround 75-85 dB.

For milestone celebrations like a 21st birthday or a 40th anniversary, create a timeline with the host. You might need to play a slideshow with background music. Use a laptop with Virtual DJ and a projector if needed. Have a wireless mic for toasts and speeches. For a graduation party, play upbeat afrobeats and hip-hop. In South Africa, amapiano and kwaito are essential. Charge $200-$600 depending on hours and travel. Always bring a gift for the host? No, but a thank-you card is professional.

Set up in a corner or near a power source. Bring a small table for your gear. Use a laptop stand to keep your equipment safe. Have a playlist ready for dinner, then transition to dance music. Watch the crowd: if older guests are sitting, play slower songs; if younger guests arrive, switch to high-energy. Take requests but don't let one guest hijack the playlist. At the end, thank the host and ask for a testimonial. Private parties often lead to referrals for weddings and corporate events. Always have a contract and take a 50% deposit.

6. Niche Markets (LGBTQ+, Cultural, Themed)

Niche markets can be lucrative and build a loyal following. For LGBTQ+ events in Africa, safety and discretion are critical. In countries where same-sex relationships are illegal, these events are private and word-of-mouth. You might DJ at a house party or a discreet venue. Play a mix of pop, house, and queer anthems like 'Born This Way' by Lady Gaga or 'Flawless' by Beyonc. In South Africa, where LGBTQ+ rights are protected, you can play at Pride events in Johannesburg or Cape Town. Charge $300-$800. Always respect privacyno photos or social media posts without permission.

Cultural events celebrate specific ethnic groups. In Nigeria, a Yoruba wedding might require talking drum samples and juju music. In Kenya, a Kikuyu cultural day needs benga and mugithi. Research the music: listen to traditional songs, learn the artists, and understand the meaning. For a themed partylike a 90s hip-hop night or a Caribbean carnivalyou need to dress the part and play era-specific hits. Use props like a disco ball or LED lights. Create a playlist that tells a story. Charge $250-$700.

For all niche events, build a brand. Create a logo and social media pages that cater to that audience. Network with event planners who specialize in these markets. Always have a clear contract that includes a non-disclosure agreement if needed. Bring backup equipment and a phone with offline music. For LGBTQ+ events, have a safe word with the organizer in case of harassment. For cultural events, consult with community elders to avoid offensive mistakes. These gigs may not be your main income, but they can be the most rewarding. They also pay well because you're one of few DJs who understand the culture.

<h3>1. Weddings: Ceremony, Cocktail, and Reception</h3>
<p>In much of Africa, a wedding is not one event but three or four linked events, often across two families and sometimes across two countries. Your job is to translate a couple's taste into a timeline that respects elders, faith leaders, and the venue. The ceremony segment usually requires near-silent operation: processional and recessional cues, ambient microphone for officiant, and no bass bleed into the sanctuary or hall. Cocktail hour is your chance to show range afro-soul, highlife, jazz, amapiano instrumentals at conversational volume. The reception is the performance peak: grand entrance, first dance, family dances, cake, and the open floor.</p>
<p>Business practice: charge by segment, not by hour. A three-segment wedding is a full production day with a site visit, rehearsal attendance, and a backup DJ. Build a written run-of-show with the couple and the planner, and get a signed cue sheet. In markets such as Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa, wedding budgets can support premium pricing, but competition is fierce; differentiation comes from reliability, cultural fluency, and clean sound rather than from the biggest speaker stack.</p>

<h3>2. Corporate Events and Conferences</h3>
<p>Corporate work is the most profitable recurring segment for a disciplined operator. Clients include banks, telecoms, NGOs, government agencies, and multinationals with country offices. Deliverables typically include conference AV support, walk-in and walk-out music, awards-night hosting, gala dinner entertainment, and branded after-party sets. The buyer is often a procurement officer, not a creative director, so your quotation must be formal: company registration, tax compliance, insurance where required, and clear payment terms.</p>
<p>Technical demands differ from club work. Speech intelligibility matters more than bass. You will need lapel and handheld microphones, a digital mixer with scene recall, redundant playback, and a cue sheet timed to the minute. Many African conference venues from Lagos to Nairobi to Kigali have in-house AV, so your role may be integration and music direction rather than full supply. Price accordingly, and never let a corporate client discover a technical gap during a keynote.</p>

<h3>3. Club and Festival Performances</h3>
<p>Clubs and festivals build your brand and your content library, but they are the most physically and legally exposed work. Festival stages require weatherproofing, generator power planning, sound-pressure compliance, and coordination with stage managers and safety officers. Club residencies offer predictable income but can trap you in low fees; use them to test new edits and to record promotional content.</p>
<p>Across Africa, festival circuits such as Oppikoppi's successors, Nyege Nyege, Sauti za Busara, and Afro Nation have created real performance income for DJs who can read a diverse crowd. Protect your hearing, insure your gear, and confirm in writing who pays for transport, accommodation, and backline.</p>

<h3>4. School Dances and Community Events</h3>
<p>Schools, churches, mosques, youth centres, and municipal events are volume business with tight budgets but excellent referral value. The critical constraint is content appropriateness and child safeguarding. Confirm the age range, get written approval for your playlist policy, and never be alone with minors. Use edited versions of tracks where lyrics are explicit, and keep volume within limits that allow teachers to supervise.</p>
<p>Community events independence-day celebrations, cultural festivals, fundraisers often require multilingual MC work and sensitivity to local protocol. These bookings rarely pay premium rates, but they build the local trust that leads to weddings and corporate referrals.</p>

<h3>5. Private Parties and Milestone Celebrations</h3>
<p>Birthdays, anniversaries, graduations, retirement parties, and baby showers form a steady mid-market. Clients want a personalised experience: dedications, photo-montage cues, and a playlist that reflects their story. Discretion matters, especially for high-profile families who do not want footage online. Offer tiered packages basic DJ, DJ plus MC, DJ plus lighting and photo-booth and upsell add-ons rather than discounting your base fee.</p>

<h3>6. Niche Markets: LGBTQ+, Cultural, and Themed</h3>
<p>Niche specialization is the fastest route to premium pricing because it signals safety and competence. LGBTQ+ events require absolute discretion, vetted venues, and zero-tolerance crowd management; in jurisdictions where same-sex conduct is criminalised, you must understand the legal risk before accepting the booking. Cultural and themed events Igbo traditional weddings, Somali aroos, Ethiopian habesha celebrations, Indian-African fusion weddings, masquerade or decade parties require research into music, dress, and ritual sequence. Themed events reward preparation: build a reference playlist, a costume-appropriate look, and a lighting palette that matches the concept.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #b00020; background:#ffe6e6; padding:16px; margin:24px 0; border-radius:8px;">
<h3 style="color:#b00020; margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING Electrical, Crowd, and Legal Risk</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:8px;"><strong>Electrical:</strong> Never daisy-chain extension leads, never run mains cables through standing water, and never rely on a venue's unlabelled sockets for a full rig. Use an RCD/earth-leakage device, surge protection, and a generator with a qualified operator for outdoor festivals. In many African cities, voltage fluctuation and load-shedding are routine; a UPS for your mixer and laptop is not optional.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom:8px;"><strong>Crowd:</strong> At festivals and clubs, maintain clear escape routes, respect venue capacity, and stop the music immediately if a crush or fight begins. Never place speakers where they can topple onto dancers.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;"><strong>Legal:</strong> Confirm music licensing, noise-ordinance curfews, and for LGBTQ+ or politically sensitive events the legal environment and venue security plan before you accept. Your fee does not cover legal defence or personal injury. Carry public-liability insurance where available, and put safety responsibilities in writing with the client.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Confirm the segment and buyer:</strong> identify whether you are selling to a couple, a procurement officer, a promoter, or a community organiser, and price accordingly.</li>
<li><strong>Get the run-of-show in writing:</strong> cue times, names, pronunciations, and the exact moments you must hit.</li>
<li><strong>Do a site visit:</strong> check power, load-in access, acoustics, noise curfew, and escape routes before the event day.</li>
<li><strong>Carry redundancy:</strong> backup laptop, spare cables, spare microphone, and a UPS or generator plan for critical audio.</li>
<li><strong>Respect content rules:</strong> edited tracks for schools, culturally appropriate music for ceremonies, and written playlist approval for niche events.</li>
<li><strong>Protect yourself legally:</strong> signed contract, deposit, cancellation terms, insurance where available, and confidentiality clause for private or sensitive events.</li>
<li><strong>Document everything:</strong> photos, testimonials, and a short recap reel after each event to feed your marketing pipeline.</li>
<li><strong>Debrief and upsell:</strong> within 48 hours, thank the client, request a referral, and propose the next event in their calendar.</li>
</ul>
