1. Shona Greetings and Introductions for Business in Zimbabwe

Welcome to Module 12 of the Shona Language course. This lesson focuses on greetings and introductions, essential for building trust and rapport in Zimbabwean business settings. In Zimbabwe, greetings are not just polite formalities; they are the foundation of relationships. A proper greeting can open doors, while a rushed one can close them. Shona culture values respect (kuremekedza) and community (ukama). When you greet someone, you acknowledge their humanity and position. The most common greeting is 'Mhoro' or 'Mhoroi' for multiple people, used during the day. In the morning, you say 'Mangwanani' (good morning). In the afternoon, 'Masikati' (good afternoon). In the evening, 'Manheru' (good evening). For a general hello, 'Hesi' is also used, borrowed from English. But the most respectful greeting is 'Makadii' or 'Makadini' (how are you?) for an elder or superior, and 'Wakadii' or 'Wakadini' for a peer or younger person. The response is 'Ndiripo' (I am fine) or 'Ndakanaka' (I am good). Always use the plural form 'Makadii' when addressing someone older or of higher status, even if it is one person. This shows respect. When meeting for the first time, you might say 'Ndinoziva kuti ndiri kusangana nemi' (I know that I am meeting you) but more commonly, you simply say 'Makadii' and then introduce yourself. To ask someone's name, say 'Zita rako ndiani?' (What is your name?) for a peer, or 'Zita renyu ndiani?' for an elder. The response is 'Zita rangu ndi...' (My name is...). In business, you often exchange business cards. You can say 'Ndine kadhi rangu rebhizinesi' (I have my business card). When introducing yourself, state your name and your company. For example, 'Ndini John, ndinoshanda kuEconet' (I am John, I work at Econet). To ask where someone works, say 'Munoshanda kupi?' (Where do you work?). The response: 'Ndinoshanda ku...' (I work at...). Handshakes are common in business, but note that in rural areas or with elders, a slight bow or clapping hands may be appropriate. For women, a handshake is fine in urban business settings. When greeting a group, start with the eldest or most senior person. Use 'Mhoroi' to the group, then 'Makadii' to each individual if time permits. In a meeting, it is customary to greet everyone before starting. A simple 'Mangwanani kune vese' (Good morning to all) works. If you are late, apologize: 'Ndine urombo nekunonoka' (I am sorry for being late). To say goodbye, 'Chisarai' (stay well) to those remaining, and 'Fambai zvakanaka' (travel well) to those leaving. In business emails, start with 'Mhoro' or 'Mangwanani' and end with 'Ndini wenyu' (Yours sincerely) or 'Ndatenda' (Thank you). Remember, taking time to greet properly is an investment. It shows you value the person, not just the deal. Practice these phrases daily. In the next lesson, we will build on this with numbers and money. For now, greet everyone you meet in Shona and watch relationships grow.

2. Numbers, Money, and Negotiation in Shona

In this lesson, we tackle numbers, money, and negotiation, critical for business transactions in Zimbabwe. Shona numbers are straightforward. One to ten: poshi (1), piri (2), tatu (3), ina (4), shanu (5), tanhatu (6), nomwe (7), sere (8), pfumbamwe (9), gumi (10). Eleven is gumi neposhi, twelve gumi nepiri, and so on. Twenty is makumi maviri, thirty makumi matatu, forty makumi mana, fifty makumi mashanu, sixty makumi matanhatu, seventy makumi manomwe, eighty makumi masere, ninety makumi mapfumbamwe. One hundred is zana. One thousand is churu. For larger numbers, Shona often borrows from English, like 'miriya' for million. When discussing money, the currency is the US dollar (dhora) and the Zimbabwean dollar (ZWL). In business, you might say 'Mari' for money. To ask the price: 'Imarii?' (How much?). The response: 'Inosvika madhora makumi mashanu' (It costs fifty dollars). To negotiate, start with 'Ndingakwanise here kuwana mutengo uri nani?' (Can I get a better price?). Or 'Mutengo wacho unodhura' (The price is expensive). A common negotiation tactic is to ask for a discount: 'Ndipeiwo discount' (Give me a discount). But in Shona, it is polite to say 'Ndingakumbirewo kuti mudzikise mutengo' (I request that you lower the price). If you are selling, you might say 'Mutengo wacho ndiwo wakaderera' (The price is already low). To agree, say 'Ndabvuma' (I agree). To disagree, 'Kwete' (No) or 'Handibvumi' (I do not agree). When making a deal, you might say 'Tobvumirana' (We agree). For payment terms, 'Ndichabhadhara mangwana' (I will pay tomorrow). 'Ndine mari yekubhadhara' (I have money to pay). 'Ndingabhadhara necard here?' (Can I pay by card?). In Zimbabwe, cash is still king, but mobile money like EcoCash is widely used. You can say 'Ndingabhadhara neEcoCash here?' (Can I pay with EcoCash?). To ask for a receipt: 'Ndipeiwo risiti' (Give me a receipt). In negotiation, patience is key. Shona culture values long-term relationships over quick wins. Do not rush. Start with small talk, then move to business. A common phrase is 'Ngatitaure nezvebhizinesi' (Let us talk about business). When discussing quantities, use 'zvimedu' for units. For example, 'Ndinoda zvikamu makumi maviri' (I want twenty units). To ask for a sample: 'Ndipeiwo sample' (Give me a sample). To place an order: 'Ndinoda kuodha' (I want to order). In a market setting, bargaining is expected. Start at half the price and work up. But in formal business, prices are often fixed. Learn to read the situation. If you are in a shop, the price is the price. If you are in Mbare Musika, negotiate. Remember, 'Mutengo wakanaka' means a good price. 'Wakanyanya' means it is too much. 'Dzikisa' means reduce. 'Wedzera' means add. Practice these phrases with a partner. Role-play buying and selling. In the next lesson, we will cover directions and transportation. For now, count your money in Shona and negotiate with confidence.

3. Directions, Transportation, and Business Travel in Shona

This lesson covers directions, transportation, and business travel in Shona-speaking areas. Zimbabwe's business hubs like Harare, Bulawayo, and Mutare require you to navigate efficiently. Start with basic direction words: 'kuruboshwe' (left), 'kurudyi' (right), 'mberi' (forward), 'kumashure' (back), 'pamusoro' (up), 'pasi' (down). To ask for directions: 'Ndinoda kuenda ku...' (I want to go to...). 'Ndiratidzeiwo nzira yekuenda ku...' (Show me the way to...). The response might be 'Famba mberi, wobva waenda kuruboshwe' (Go forward, then turn left). To ask where something is: 'Chikoro chiri kupi?' (Where is the school?). 'Chiteshi chebhazi chiri kupi?' (Where is the bus station?). For transportation, 'bhazi' is bus, 'tekisi' is taxi, 'mota' is car, 'chitima' is train, 'ngarava' is truck. To hire a taxi: 'Ndinoda tekisi' (I need a taxi). 'Munditore ku...' (Pick me up at...). To ask the fare: 'Imarii kuenda ku...?' (How much to go to...?). In Zimbabwe, commuter omnibuses (kombis) are common. You can say 'Ndinoda kukwira kombi' (I want to board a kombi). To ask the conductor: 'Unosvika ku... here?' (Do you reach...?). The conductor might say 'Hongu' (Yes) or 'Kwete' (No). For business travel, you might need to book a flight: 'Ndinoda kubhuka ndege' (I want to book a flight). 'Ndege inosimuka nguvai?' (What time does the plane depart?). To rent a car: 'Ndinoda kuhaya mota' (I want to rent a car). 'Mota ine marii pazuva?' (How much is the car per day?). When driving, know these terms: 'mugwagwa' (road), 'muganhu' (border), 'chiteshi chepeturu' (petrol station), 'paki' (park). To ask for a petrol station: 'Peturu chiteshi chiri kupi?' (Where is the petrol station?). In business meetings, punctuality is appreciated but traffic in Harare can be heavy. Allow extra time. If you are lost, call your contact: 'Ndasvika asi ndarasika' (I have arrived but I am lost). They might say 'Mira ipapo, ndinouya' (Wait there, I am coming). For public transport, always have small change. In Shona, 'mari diki' means small money. To ask for change: 'Ndipeiwo mari diki' (Give me small change). When traveling to rural areas for business, like buying tobacco or cotton, you might use a 'bhazi renyika' (country bus). To ask the schedule: 'Bhazi rinosimuka nguvai?' (What time does the bus leave?). To buy a ticket: 'Ndinoda tikiti' (I need a ticket). 'Tikiti yekuenda ku...' (Ticket to...). In a new town, ask for the business district: 'Bhizinesi nzvimbo iri kupi?' (Where is the business area?). To find a bank: 'Bhangi riri kupi?' (Where is the bank?). To find a hotel: 'Hotera iri kupi?' (Where is the hotel?). Practice these phrases. Use a map of Harare and give directions in Shona. Next lesson, we will cover food and dining etiquette. For now, navigate with confidence.

4. Food, Dining, and Business Hospitality in Shona

This lesson explores food, dining, and business hospitality in Shona culture. In Zimbabwe, sharing a meal is a sign of trust and friendship. Business deals are often sealed over food. Start with basic food vocabulary: 'chikafu' (food), 'mvura' (water), 'tea' (tii), 'coffee' (kofi), 'sadza' (thick maize porridge), 'nyama' (meat), 'huku' (chicken), 'nyama yemombe' (beef), 'nyama yenguruve' (pork), 'hove' (fish), 'muriwo' (vegetables), 'bhinzi' (beans), 'mupunga' (rice), 'chingwa' (bread), 'mukaka' (milk), 'shuga' (sugar), 'munyu' (salt). To say you are hungry: 'Ndine nzara'. Thirsty: 'Ndine nyota'. To ask for food: 'Ndinoda chikafu'. To ask for water: 'Ndinoda mvura'. In a restaurant, you might say 'Ndinoda menyu' (I want the menu). To order: 'Ndinoda sadza nenyama' (I want sadza and meat). To ask for the bill: 'Ndipeiwo bhiri' (Give me the bill). In business hospitality, if you are invited to someone's home, bring a small gift like fruit or drinks. Say 'Ndatenda nekuuya' (Thank you for coming) when you arrive. The host might say 'Mauya' (Welcome). To compliment the food: 'Chikafu chakanaka' (The food is good). To say you are full: 'Ndakora'. To refuse more food politely: 'Ndatenda, ndakora' (Thank you, I am full). In a business lunch, let the host order first. If you have dietary restrictions, say 'Handidye nyama' (I do not eat meat) or 'Handinwi doro' (I do not drink alcohol). In Shona culture, it is polite to accept at least a little of what is offered. If you cannot eat something, say 'Ndinotenda asi handidye...' (I thank you but I do not eat...). For drinks, 'doro' is beer, 'waini' is wine, 'mvura' is water, 'jusi' is juice, 'soda' is soda. To toast: 'Mudzimu wakanaka' or simply 'Cheers' (cheers). In a business meeting, tea is often served. Accept it with both hands if possible. Say 'Ndatenda' (Thank you). To ask for more: 'Ndipeiwo imwe' (Give me another). To pass food: 'Ndipeiwo sadza' (Pass me the sadza). In rural areas, you might be offered 'mahewu' (fermented maize drink) or 'masese' (traditional beer). Accept politely. If you are hosting, ensure guests are served first. Say 'Kudyai' (Eat) or 'Mudye' (Eat, plural). To offer more: 'Wedzera' (Add more). In business, a shared meal breaks down barriers. Use it to build relationships. Learn to say 'Ndakafara kudya nesu' (I am happy to eat with you). After the meal, say 'Ndatenda nechikafu' (Thank you for the food). In the next lesson, we will cover business meetings and negotiations. For now, enjoy your meal and practice these phrases.

5. Business Meetings, Negotiations, and Professional Etiquette in Shona

This final lesson focuses on business meetings, negotiations, and professional etiquette in Shona. Zimbabwean business culture blends traditional respect with modern efficiency. Start with meeting vocabulary: 'musangano' (meeting), 'hurukuro' (discussion), 'chibvumirano' (agreement), 'kondirakiti' (contract), 'bhizinesi' (business), 'kambani' (company), 'mushandi' (worker), 'mukuru' (boss), 'mutengi' (customer), 'mubhadhari' (payer). To schedule a meeting: 'Ndinoda kusangana nemi' (I want to meet with you). 'Tingasangana rini?' (When can we meet?). The response: 'Mangwana masikati' (Tomorrow afternoon). To confirm: 'Ndichauya' (I will come). To reschedule: 'Ndinoda kuchinja nguva' (I want to change the time). In a meeting, greet everyone first. Use 'Mangwanani' or 'Masikati' depending on the time. Then 'Makadii kune vese' (How are you all?). To start the meeting: 'Ngatitangei' (Let us begin). To present your agenda: 'Nyaya dzatinoda kukurukura ndedzi...' (The issues we want to discuss are...). To ask for opinions: 'Maonero enyu ndeei?' (What are your views?). To agree: 'Ndinobvumirana nazvo' (I agree with that). To disagree politely: 'Ndinonzwisisa asi ndinofunga kuti...' (I understand but I think that...). In negotiations, use these phrases: 'Tinoda chibvumirano chakanaka kune vese' (We want a good agreement for all). 'Ndingakwanise here kuwana mutengo uri nani?' (Can I get a better price?). 'Tipeiwo nguva yekufunga' (Give us time to think). 'Ndichakupai mhinduro mangwana' (I will give you an answer tomorrow). To close a deal: 'Tobvumirana' (We agree). 'Ngatisainei' (Let us sign). 'Ndatenda nebasa rakanaka' (Thank you for the good work). In professional etiquette, dress formally. A suit is appropriate for men, and business attire for women. Punctuality is important, but be flexible. In Shona culture, relationships come first. Spend time on small talk before business. Ask about family: 'Vakadii vana?' (How are the children?). 'Hupenyu hwakanaka here?' (Is life good?). Avoid sensitive topics like politics. Use titles: 'VaMoyo' for Mr. Moyo, 'Amai Moyo' for Mrs. Moyo. For a young woman, 'Sisi' (sister), for a young man, 'Bhudi' (brother). In emails, start with 'Mhoro' or 'Mangwanani', then state your purpose. End with 'Ndatenda' and your name. In phone calls, say 'Hesi' then introduce yourself. To ask to speak to someone: 'Ndingataure na... here?' (May I speak to...?). To leave a message: 'Mudzidzise kuti ndakafona' (Tell him that I called). In meetings, take notes. Ask for clarification: 'Ndapota dzokororai' (Please repeat). 'Handina kunzwa zvakanaka' (I did not hear well). To summarize: 'Saka tabvumirana kuti...' (So we agreed that...). After the meeting, send a follow-up email in Shona or English. Say 'Ndatenda nemusangano' (Thank you for the meeting). In negotiation, be patient. Shona culture values consensus. Do not rush. Use silence as a tool. If you need to think, say 'Regai ndifunge' (Let me think). To make a counteroffer: 'Tinogona kukupa...' (We can give you...). To accept: 'Ndabvuma' (I agree). To reject: 'Hatigone' (We cannot). Remember, a good negotiator listens more than talks. Build trust by being honest and reliable. In the next module, we will cover advanced conversation. For now, practice these phrases in role-plays. You are now equipped to conduct business in Shona. Congratulations!

Practical Reference: Shona Poultry & Livestock Vocabulary for Agribusiness Deals in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe's agribusiness economy runs on livestock and poultry. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), agriculture contributes roughly 817% of Zimbabwe's GDP depending on rainfall, and livestock alone accounts for about 2030% of agricultural output value in most years. If you are negotiating with smallholder farmers, commercial feed suppliers, or veterinary service providers in Mashonaland, Midlands, or Matabeleland, you need more than greetings you need the precise Shona vocabulary for animals, health, feed, and transactions. This Practical Reference lesson equips you with that vocabulary, structured for business use.

SAFETY WARNING Animal Health & Chemical Handling

When discussing mushonga (veterinary medicine) or mishonga yezvipembenene (pesticides) with suppliers or farm workers, always confirm:

  • Withdrawal periods Shona: nguva yekumira mushonga usati watengesa nyama kana mazai. Selling meat or eggs before the withdrawal period ends creates drug residues that are illegal and dangerous.
  • Dosage accuracy Never estimate. Use the correct chiyero (measure) per kilogram of body weight.
  • Personal protective equipment (PPE) Gloves, masks, and boots when handling vaccines or chemicals. The WHO estimates that pesticide poisoning causes over 300,000 deaths annually in developing countries, many in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Cold chain Vaccines must stay between 28C. Confirm firiji (refrigeration) availability before purchase.
  • Report outbreaks Notify the Department of Veterinary Services immediately if chirwere (disease) spreads. Newcastle disease can wipe out an entire flock within days.

Source: WHO, Pesticide Residues in Food (2023); MSD Animal Health, Vaccine Handling Guidelines for Africa (2023).

Business Dialogue Practice: Buying Chicks from a Hatchery

You: Mhoro. Ndinoda kutenga machira mazana mashanu. Mutengo wacho wakadii?
(Hello. I want to buy 500 day-old chicks. What is the price?)

Supplier: Machira mazana mashanu anokwana madhora mazana maviri nemakumi mashanu. Unoda mushonga wekudzivirira here?
(500 chicks cost US$250. Do you need vaccines?)

You: Hongu. Ndipawo mushonga weNewcastle nemushonga weGumboro. Ndinodawo chikafu chemavhiki matatu ekutanga.
(Yes. Give me Newcastle and Gumboro vaccines. I also need feed for the first three weeks.)

Supplier: Zvakanaka. Chikafu chemavhiki matatu chinosvika makirogiramu mazana matatu. Unoda kuti tiendese kumba kwako here?
(Fine. Three weeks of feed comes to 300 kg. Do you want us to deliver to your home?)

You: Hongu, endesai. Ndichabhadhara ne mobile money. Ndinoda receipt yechibvumirano.
(Yes, deliver. I will pay by mobile money. I need a receipt for the contract.)

Quick Reference Checklist for Agribusiness Meetings

  • Greet properly first Use Mhoro/Mangwanani before business. Skipping greetings damages trust in Shona business culture.
  • Confirm animal type and quantity huku, mombe, mbudzi, nguruve never assume the species from context alone.
  • Verify vaccine names and cold chain Ask for mushonga weNewcastle, Gumboro, Fowl Pox by name, and confirm refrigeration.
  • Agree on feed quantity and type Starter, grower, finisher (chikafu chekutanga, chepakati, chekupedzisira).
  • State price per unit clearly Mutengo wechira rimwe (price per single chick) avoids disputes.
  • Confirm delivery date and location Zuva rekuendesa uye nzvimbo.
  • Request written contract or receipt Chibvumirano chakanyorwa kana risiti.
  • Ask about mortality guarantee Vachavimbisa here kuti machira haafe? Reputable hatcheries offer replacement for early deaths.
