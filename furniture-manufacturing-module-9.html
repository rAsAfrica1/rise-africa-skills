1. Production Planning and Scheduling

Production planning is deciding what to build, how many, and when. For a small furniture workshop, start with a simple weekly plan. On Monday morning, list all orders: for example, 5 dining tables, 10 stools, 3 wardrobes. Check your calendar for deadlines. If a customer needs a table by Friday, you must schedule it early in the week.

Break each job into steps: cutting, assembly, sanding, finishing, and drying. Estimate time per step. A basic dining table might take 2 hours to cut, 3 hours to assemble, 1 hour to sand, and 2 hours for finishing plus 24 hours drying. For 5 tables, that's 10 hours cutting, 15 hours assembly, etc. Total workshop hours per week: 40. So you can fit about 3 tables if you also do other jobs.

Use a whiteboard or a notebook as a Gantt chart. Write days of the week across the top, and list jobs down the side. Draw bars to show when each step happens. For example, Monday: cut all table parts. Tuesday: assemble. Wednesday: sand and start finishing. Thursday: finish and dry. Friday: deliver. This visual plan helps you avoid bottlenecks.

Schedule based on worker skills. If you have two workers, one good at cutting and one at assembly, assign tasks accordingly. Don't overload one person. Also, plan for material delivery. If your timber supplier takes 3 days, order early. Keep a buffer of 10% extra time for delays like power cuts or machine breakdowns.

Finally, review your plan daily. At the end of each day, check what was done. If you're behind, adjust: maybe work overtime or move a job to next week. Communicate with customers if delays happen. A simple plan written on paper is better than a perfect plan in your head.

2. Bill of Materials (BOM) and Cut Lists

A Bill of Materials (BOM) is a list of every part and material needed to build one item. A cut list is a detailed plan for cutting each part from raw stock. Together, they save time and reduce waste.

Start with a simple product: a wooden stool. BOM: 4 legs (3cm x 3cm x 45cm), 1 seat (30cm x 30cm x 2cm), 8 screws (4cm), 4 dowels (1cm diameter, 5cm long), wood glue, sandpaper, varnish. For 10 stools, multiply everything by 10: 40 legs, 10 seats, 80 screws, etc.

Now create a cut list. You buy timber in standard sizes: 2.4m long planks. For legs, each 45cm leg comes from a 2.4m plank. 2.4m / 0.45m = 5.33, so you get 5 legs per plank with 15cm waste. For 40 legs, you need 8 planks (40/5). For seats, each 30cm x 30cm piece: from a 2.4m x 1.2m plywood sheet, you can cut 8 seats (2 rows of 4). So 10 seats need 2 sheets (with waste).

Write your cut list on paper or a spreadsheet. Include part name, dimensions, quantity, and material. For example: Leg, 3x3x45cm, 40, Mahogany plank. Seat, 30x30x2cm, 10, Plywood. This helps you buy exactly what you need. Add 10% extra for mistakes.

When cutting, optimize. Group similar cuts. Use a stop block on your mitre saw to cut many pieces to the same length quickly. Label parts with a pencil or tape. Store cut parts in bins or stacks by product. This prevents confusion.

A good BOM and cut list reduce waste and speed up assembly. You can also use them to calculate costs. If a plank costs 5000 Naira, and you need 8 planks, material cost for legs is 40,000 Naira. Add labour and overhead to set your price.

3. Inventory Management and Material Handling

Inventory management means knowing what materials you have, where they are, and when to reorder. Without it, you waste time searching or run out of key items.

Start by organizing your storage. Use shelves or racks. Group similar items: all timber together, all hardware (screws, hinges) in bins, all finishes (varnish, paint) on a separate shelf. Label each shelf with the item name and minimum stock level. For example: 'Screws 4cm min 200'. When you take screws, write the new count on a clipboard or whiteboard.

Keep a simple inventory log. A notebook works. Every time you use or buy materials, record the date, item, quantity in or out, and balance. For example: '5 May used 40 screws balance 160'. When balance hits the minimum, reorder. For timber, keep at least enough for one week's production. If you use 10 planks a week, reorder when you have 15 left.

Material handling is how you move materials safely and efficiently. Use trolleys or hand carts for heavy planks. Don't carry alone if a plank is over 2 meters. Store timber off the floor on battens to prevent moisture and termites. Keep pathways clear. In a small workshop, arrange machines in a logical flow: cutting area near timber storage, assembly near cutting, finishing away from dust.

Use the FIFO method: First In, First Out. Older stock should be used first. Mark dates on timber when received. This prevents decay.

Finally, do a monthly stock take. Count everything. Compare with your log. Adjust. This helps you spot theft or waste. Good inventory management saves money and keeps production smooth.

4. Lean Manufacturing Principles in Furniture Production

Lean manufacturing is about eliminating waste to produce more with less. In furniture making, waste comes in many forms: overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, and defects. Here's how to apply lean in your workshop.

First, identify value from the customer's view. A customer wants a strong, beautiful table at a fair price, delivered on time. Anything that doesn't add value is waste. For example, sanding a part that will be hidden is waste. So, plan to only sand visible surfaces.

Second, map your process. Walk through your workshop and write down every step from receiving timber to shipping the product. Time each step. You'll likely find bottlenecks. For instance, if your only planer is slow, you may have a queue. Solution: schedule planing for early morning before other work, or buy a second planer if volume justifies.

Third, implement 5S: Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize, Sustain. Sort: remove unnecessary items from workbenches. Set in order: assign a place for every tool. Shine: clean machines and floors daily. Standardize: create checklists for cleaning and maintenance. Sustain: make it a habit. A clean workshop is safer and faster.

Fourth, reduce setup time. If changing a blade takes 20 minutes, do all cutting for one thickness before switching. Batch similar jobs. For example, cut all legs for 10 stools at once, then all seats.

Fifth, use pull production. Don't build 50 stools hoping to sell. Build to order or keep small buffer stock. This reduces inventory and cash tied up.

Finally, involve your workers. They know where waste happens. Ask for ideas. A simple suggestion like moving a screw bin closer can save minutes per day. Lean is continuous improvement, not a one-time fix.

5. Workstation Design and Ergonomics

Ergonomics is designing work to fit the worker, not the other way around. In furniture making, poor ergonomics leads to back pain, fatigue, and mistakes. Good design boosts productivity and health.

Start with workbench height. For assembly, the bench should be at elbow height when standing. For most people, that's about 90-110cm. If you do detailed work like carving, raise it to 110-120cm. If you do heavy planing, lower it to 80-90cm. Use adjustable legs or platforms. In Africa, many workshops use wooden benches; you can add blocks under legs to adjust.

Next, arrange tools within easy reach. The most used tools should be within arm's length, at waist to shoulder height. For a cutting station, keep your saw, pencil, tape measure, and square on a nearby shelf or in a holster. Avoid bending or twisting to grab tools. Use a tool belt if you move around.

Lighting is critical. Natural light is best, but if not enough, use LED bulbs. Position lights so they don't cast shadows on your work. For sanding and finishing, add a focused lamp. Poor light causes eye strain and poor quality.

Flooring: concrete is common, but standing all day on concrete hurts. Use anti-fatigue mats or rubber mats where you stand. If not available, wear cushioned shoes. Keep floors dry and free of offcuts.

For heavy lifting, use mechanical aids: trolleys, hoists, or a partner. Never lift a sheet of plywood alone if it's over 20kg. Bend your knees, not your back. Store heavy items at waist height, not on the floor or high shelves.

Finally, encourage breaks. Every hour, stand up, stretch, and walk for 2 minutes. Rotate tasks among workers to avoid repetitive strain. A well-designed workstation reduces injuries and keeps your team productive.

6. Quality Control and Inspection Procedures

Quality control (QC) ensures every piece meets your standards before it leaves the workshop. Without QC, you risk returns, bad reviews, and lost customers. Here's a practical QC system for a small furniture business.

First, set standards for each product. For a dining table: dimensions within 2mm, smooth surface (no scratches), joints tight (no gaps), finish even (no drips), and stable (no wobble). Write these down. Share with your team.

Second, inspect at three stages: incoming materials, in-process, and final. Incoming: check timber for cracks, knots, or moisture. Moisture should be below 12% for furniture. Use a moisture meter (cost about $30). Reject bad boards. For hardware, check screws and hinges for defects.

In-process: inspect after each major step. After cutting, check lengths and angles. After assembly, check squareness by measuring diagonals. They should be equal within 1mm. After sanding, run your hand over the surface; it should feel smooth. After finishing, check for runs, bubbles, or missed spots.

Final inspection: before delivery, test the product. Sit on the chair, open drawers, shake the table. Use a checklist. For example: Table 1) top flat? 2) legs level? 3) finish smooth? 4) hardware secure? If any fail, fix before delivery.

Use simple tools: tape measure, square, level, and your hands. For finishes, a bright light helps spot defects. Keep a log of defects and fixes. If a defect repeats, find the root cause. Maybe your saw blade is dull, or your sanding technique needs training.

Train your workers on QC. Empower them to stop and fix problems. A culture of quality saves money in the long run. Remember, it's cheaper to fix in the workshop than to replace a customer's table.

<h2>Practical Reference: Production Planning and Workflow in African Furniture Manufacturing</h2>

<p>This practical reference distils the six lessons of the Production Planning and Workflow module into field-ready guidance for African furniture businesses from single-bench carpentry shops in Kumasi and Nakuru to 40-worker workshops supplying hotels, schools, and export buyers. The focus is commercial: fewer rejected units, faster throughput, tighter cash tied up in stock, and safer, more profitable operations. Use it as a bench-side manual, a training handout, or a checklist for supervisors.</p>

<h3>1. Production Planning and Scheduling</h3>
<p>Planning converts confirmed orders into a dated sequence of operations. In African workshops, the biggest planning failures are over-promising on delivery and batching jobs by convenience rather than by due date. A simple weekly plan board (physical or WhatsApp-based) with columns for Order, Quantity, Material Ready?, Machine/Station, Start, Finish, and Dispatch Date is enough for most SMEs.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Backward scheduling:</strong> Start from the customer's delivery date, subtract finishing, assembly, machining, and material lead times. This reveals the true "latest start" date.</li>
<li><strong>Load check:</strong> Compare planned hours against available machine and labour hours per station (saw, planer, sander, assembly, spray booth).</li>
<li><strong>Buffer:</strong> Add 1015% time buffer for power cuts, drying delays, and material shortages common realities across the continent.</li>
</ul>

<h3>2. Bill of Materials (BOM) and Cut Lists</h3>
<p>A BOM lists every component, quantity, dimension, material, and finish required for one unit. The cut list converts the BOM into optimised cutting instructions per sheet or board. For a typical three-seat sofa frame or a school desk, the BOM prevents the classic error of discovering a missing rail after assembly has begun.</p>

<h3>4. Lean Manufacturing Principles in Furniture Production</h3>
<p>Lean is not a Japanese import reserved for large factories it is a mindset of removing waste. The seven wastes in furniture production are: overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, and defects. Practical African applications:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>5S on the shop floor:</strong> Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardise, Sustain. A clean bench reduces search time and injury risk.</li>
<li><strong>Standard work:</strong> Written one-page instructions for each operation (e.g., "How to cut a 45 mitre safely") reduce variation and training time.</li>
<li><strong>Kanban:</strong> Simple cards or chalk marks that signal when a downstream station needs more parts.</li>
<li><strong>Kaizen:</strong> Weekly 30-minute team meeting to fix one small problem a wobbly jig, a misplaced tool, a slow drying rack.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Workstation Design and Ergonomics</h3>
<p>Musculoskeletal injuries are common among African carpenters who work on the floor or at fixed-height benches. A well-designed workstation reduces fatigue, improves quality, and cuts absenteeism. Key principles:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Work height:</strong> Bench height should be elbow height for light work; lower for heavy work requiring force.</li>
<li><strong>Reach zone:</strong> Frequently used tools within 40 cm of the body; less-used items further away.</li>
<li><strong>Lighting:</strong> 500 lux minimum at the bench; natural light supplemented with LED task lighting.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilation:</strong> Dust extraction at source for saws and sanders; spray booths with extraction for finishing.</li>
<li><strong>Anti-fatigue matting:</strong> Reduces standing strain on concrete floors.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Quality Control and Inspection Procedures</h3>
<p>Quality failures in African furniture businesses often stem from lack of inspection at the right stages rather than lack of skill. Implement three inspection gates:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Incoming inspection:</strong> Check timber moisture content (1215% for interior furniture), board thickness, and hardware quantity against the BOM.</li>
<li><strong>In-process inspection:</strong> Check dimensions, squareness, and joint fit after machining and before assembly.</li>
<li><strong>Final inspection:</strong> Check finish, hardware function, stability, and packaging before dispatch. Use a simple checklist with pass/fail and a signature.</li>
</ul>
<p>Record defects on a Pareto chart or tally sheet. The top three defects usually account for 7080% of rework fix those first.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c00; background:#fff0f0; padding:12px 16px; margin:18px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#c00;"> SAFETY WARNING Dust and Chemical Hazards</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Wood dust is a known carcinogen (nasal and lung cancer) and a fire/explosion risk. In African workshops, dust extraction is often missing. <strong>Never</strong> operate saws, planers, or sanders without dust extraction or approved respiratory protection (N95 or better). Spray finishing must be done in a ventilated booth with organic vapour cartridges not in the open workshop. Keep a fire extinguisher within 10 metres of every finishing area. Adhesives and lacquers must be stored in a locked, fire-rated cabinet away from ignition sources. Train all workers on the material safety data sheet (MSDS) for every chemical on site.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Plan backward from delivery date</strong> subtract finishing, assembly, machining, and material lead times; add a 1015% buffer.</li>
<li><strong>Every job has a BOM and cut list</strong> checked against stock before cutting begins.</li>
<li><strong>Stock is FIFO and off the ground</strong> bin cards or spreadsheet updated weekly; reorder triggers set.</li>
<li><strong>5S is visible on the shop floor</strong> tools shadow-boarded, walkways clear, waste bins labelled.</li>
<li><strong>Workstations are ergonomic</strong> bench at elbow height, task lighting 500 lux, dust extraction at source.</li>
<li><strong>Three inspection gates are active</strong> incoming, in-process, final with signed checklists.</li>
<li><strong>Safety equipment is in use</strong> respirators, eye protection, hearing protection, fire extinguisher, MSDS accessible.</li>
<li><strong>Weekly Kaizen meeting held</strong> one small improvement logged and implemented every week.</li>
</ol>
