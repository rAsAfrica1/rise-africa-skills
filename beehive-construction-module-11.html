Lessons

1. Selecting an Apiary Site: Sun, Wind, Water

Choose a site that gets morning sun and afternoon shade. In hot African regions, hives facing east or northeast get early warmth to start foraging, but avoid full-day sun that melts comb. In cooler highlands, full sun is better to keep hives warm. Place hives where they are sheltered from strong winds. A windbreak of tall grass, shrubs, or a fence 23 meters away reduces wind stress and prevents hives from tipping. Avoid low-lying areas that flood; bees drown and hives rot. The site should be at least 100 meters from livestock pens and human footpaths to reduce disturbance. If you have a permanent water source like a stream or pond within 500 meters, that's ideal. Otherwise, provide a shallow water trough with stones for bees to land on. Never place hives under trees that drop sap or attract ants. Keep hives off the ground: use stands 4050 cm high with legs, and grease the legs or use ash to deter ants. In areas with honey badgers, use strong metal stands or hang hives from trees. For easy access, leave 12 meters of clear space around each hive. If you keep multiple hives, arrange them in a row or circle with entrances facing different directions to reduce drifting. Start with 25 hives for a beginner. Record the site's sun direction, wind direction, and distance to water. A good site means healthier bees and more honey.

2. Leveling and Securing Hives

A hive must sit level side-to-side and slightly tilted forward (23 cm) so rain runs out and not into the entrance. Use a spirit level or a clear bottle half-filled with water to check. If the ground is uneven, dig a shallow trench or use flat stones under the hive stand. For a permanent apiary, pour concrete pads or use treated wooden posts set in the ground. Secure hives to stands with metal straps or strong rope to prevent theft and wind damage. In windy areas, drive a stake at each corner and tie the hive down. For top-bar hives, ensure the bars are level and tight; gaps invite pests. If using Kenyan top-bar hives, the legs should be at least 40 cm high and made of metal or treated wood. Grease the legs with used engine oil or place them in tins filled with ash to stop ants. Check that the hive entrance is clear and not blocked by grass. After securing, observe the hive for a day: bees should fly in and out freely. If they cluster outside, the hive may be too hot or too small. For Langstroth hives, use a hive strap around the whole box. In areas with elephants or monkeys, build a strong fence or hang hives from thick branches. Always secure the roof so it doesn't blow off. A well-leveled and secured hive reduces absconding and makes inspections easier.

3. Installing Packages and Nucs

Install packages and nucs in the late afternoon when bees are less active. For a package (a box of bees with a mated queen in a cage), first prepare the hive: remove 45 frames from the center, leaving a gap. Spray the package lightly with sugar syrup (1:1 water to sugar) to calm bees. Shake the bees into the gap, then gently place the queen cage between two frames, candy side up. Close the hive and leave them for 35 days. The bees will eat through the candy and release the queen. For a nuc (a small hive with 45 frames of brood, bees, and a queen), simply transfer the frames into your hive, keeping them in the same order. Add empty frames to fill the box. Do not smoke heavily; a little smoke is fine. Feed the new colony with sugar syrup (1:1) for 23 weeks to help them draw comb. Use a feeder inside the hive or a bucket feeder on top. In hot climates, provide shade and ensure ventilation. Check after 3 days: the queen should be laying. If not, look for the queen or eggs. In Africa, install during a nectar flow if possible, or when flowering plants are abundant. Avoid installing during dearth or heavy rains. If you buy a nuc, inspect it for pests like small hive beetles or varroa (if present in your area). Always wear protective gear. After installation, reduce the entrance to 23 cm to help the bees defend against robbers. Leave them undisturbed for a week, then inspect.

4. Routine Inspections and Record Keeping

Inspect your hives every 710 days during the active season, and every 23 weeks during dearth. Open the hive on a sunny, calm day between 10 am and 4 pm when foragers are out. Use a smoker with dry grass or cow dung to calm bees. Stand to the side of the entrance, not in front. Remove the roof and inner cover gently. Use a hive tool to pry frames apart. Check for: eggs and larvae (queen is laying), queen cells (swarming or supersedure), food stores (honey and pollen), pests (ants, wax moths, small hive beetles), and diseases (foulbrood, chalkbrood). Count frames of brood. A strong colony should have 58 frames of brood. Look for the queen; if you don't see her, look for eggs (tiny white specks). If no eggs, the colony may be queenless. Keep records: date, hive number, number of frames of brood, food status, queen seen (yes/no), pests, and actions taken. Use a notebook or a simple form. For example: '15/03/2025, Hive 2: 6 frames brood, 3 frames honey, queen seen, no pests, added super.' Records help you track colony health, predict swarming, and plan harvests. In Africa, where seasons vary, records show when dearth starts so you can feed early. Always clean your hive tool between hives with a flame or alcohol to prevent disease spread. After inspection, close the hive carefully and secure the roof. Limit inspection to 1520 minutes to avoid chilling or overheating brood.

5. Repairing and Replacing Damaged Parts

Inspect hives monthly for damage. Common problems: broken frames, cracked boxes, holes in roofs, and rusted metal parts. Repair small cracks with wood filler or a mixture of sawdust and wood glue. For larger holes, cut a piece of wood to fit and nail it over the hole. Replace broken top bars or frames immediately; bees will build burr comb in the gap, making inspections difficult. Use a hive tool to scrape off propolis and wax before repairing. If a box is rotten, replace it with a new one. In Africa, termites are a major threat. Treat wooden stands and boxes with used engine oil or a borate solution (20% borax, 80% water) by painting or soaking. Check metal stands for rust; paint with anti-rust paint. Replace worn hive straps. If the roof is leaking, apply a new layer of tar felt or corrugated iron sheet. For Kenyan top-bar hives, ensure the bars are straight and not warped; replace warped bars. If a hive becomes infested with wax moths, remove affected frames and freeze them for 24 hours (if possible) or burn them. Clean the hive with a scraper and expose it to sunlight for a day. For small hive beetles, use traps or diatomaceous earth around the stand. Always keep spare parts: extra frames, boxes, roofs, and stands. A well-maintained hive lasts 1015 years. In the rainy season, check for leaks weekly. After repairs, reassemble the hive and ensure it is level and secure. If a colony is too weak to defend a damaged hive, consider combining it with a stronger colony.

6. Winterizing and Spring Buildup

In Africa, 'winter' refers to the cool, dry season or the rainy season, depending on your region. In highlands, temperatures can drop below 10C, and bees cluster. Prepare by reducing the entrance to 12 cm to keep out cold and robbers. Ensure the hive has 46 frames of honey; if not, feed thick sugar syrup (2:1) in a feeder. Wrap the hive with a black plastic sheet or sack to absorb heat, but leave ventilation holes. In hot, dry areas, 'winterizing' means providing shade and water. Use a white-painted roof to reflect heat. During the rainy season, tilt the hive forward more and ensure the roof is watertight. For spring buildup (or the start of the main nectar flow), stimulate the colony: feed 1:1 sugar syrup and provide pollen substitute (soy flour and brewer's yeast mixed with honey). Add a super or empty box when the colony has 78 frames of brood. Reverse boxes if the brood is in the lower box and honey above. Remove any queen cells if you don't want swarming, or make splits. Check for pests and diseases after the dearth. In Africa, buildup often coincides with flowering of key plants like acacia, citrus, or sunflower. Time your inspections to catch the flow. Provide clean water nearby. If you have multiple hives, equalize brood and food between strong and weak colonies. Record your actions. A strong spring buildup leads to a good harvest.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Hive Placement, Setup & Maintenance

This practical reference is written for the working African beekeeper whether you manage five hives behind a homestead in Kakamega, fifty hives on a commercial acacia block in Tanzania, or a pollination service contracted to a mango estate in Ghana. Every recommendation below is framed around the business of bees: healthy colonies produce more honey, more splits, more pollination revenue, and more value-added products (beeswax, propolis, royal jelly). Placement and maintenance are not hobby details they are the difference between a hive that pays for itself in one season and a hive that quietly dies and takes your capital with it.

Use this module alongside the seven lessons in the course. The tables, diagram, and checklist are designed to be printed and carried to the apiary.

Site Selection: The Business Case for Getting It Right First Time

Moving hives after installation costs labour, stresses colonies, and loses foraging days. A site chosen on the criteria below will typically out-produce a poorly sited apiary by 3060% in the first year, according to trials reported by the International Centre of Insect Physiology and Ecology (ICIPE) in Kenya. That is the difference between a marginal and a profitable enterprise.

Leveling, Securing and Installing Stock

Hives must sit perfectly level side-to-side and slightly tilted forward (23) so that rainwater and condensation run out of the entrance rather than pooling on combs. In African conditions the three biggest setup failures are: hives placed directly on the ground (termites, ants, toads), hives unsecured against wind and livestock, and hives installed without a stand which is the single cheapest upgrade with the highest return.

Install packages and nucleus colonies (nucs) in the late afternoon or early evening, when foragers are in and temperatures are falling. Feed 1:1 sugar syrup for the first 23 weeks if there is no strong nectar flow this is not a cost, it is an investment that determines whether the colony draws comb before the dearth arrives. In East Africa, install during the long-rains build-up (MarchApril) or short-rains build-up (OctoberNovember) so colonies peak with the flowering season.

Routine Inspections and Record Keeping

An inspection is a business audit. Every visit should answer five questions: Is the queen present and laying? Is there brood in all stages? Is there enough stored food? Are there signs of disease or pests? Does the colony need space or a super? Record all five in a hive log a paper notebook is fine, but a smartphone app such as BeeScanning or a simple spreadsheet will let you track yield per hive, per season, and calculate your true cost per kilogram of honey.

Repairing and Replacing Damaged Parts

Inspect stands, roofs, and boxes at every visit. Termite damage to stands is the most common structural failure in African apiaries treat timber with used engine oil or plant stands on metal/stone footings. Replace warped or cracked boxes before the rains, not after. Keep at least one spare complete hive per ten hives in service so that a swarm or an emergency split can be housed immediately rather than lost.

Winterizing and Spring Buildup

In most of tropical Africa "winterizing" means preparing for the cool, dry or highland cold period and the dearth that accompanies it: reduce entrances, ensure 1520 kg of stored honey or equivalent syrup, insulate the roof, and merge weak colonies. In Mediterranean-climate zones (Morocco, Tunisia, South Africa's Western Cape), true winter preparation applies: wrap or insulate, close ventilation, and check stores monthly. Spring buildup is the commercial growth phase feed, expand, and split. A colony that goes into the flow with 810 frames of bees will out-yield one that goes in with four.

SAFETY WARNING Africanized bees, agrochemicals, and fire
  • Africanized / aggressive colonies: Many African subspecies are highly defensive. Always work in pairs, wear a full suit and closed shoes, use smoke sparingly, and never open hives near homesteads, schools, or livestock. If a colony is persistently aggressive, requeen with a docile strain rather than tolerating it the risk to workers and neighbours is a legal and reputational liability.
  • Agrochemicals: Never inspect immediately after a neighbour has sprayed. Confirm spray schedules before harvest and keep a written record pesticide kills are the leading cause of sudden colony loss in commercial African apiaries, and documentation supports any compensation claim.
  • Fire: Smokers cause bush fires in dry savannah. Use a metal bucket for ash, never smoke in tall dry grass, and keep a fire extinguisher or water drum at the apiary.
  • Allergies: Keep an antihistamine and epinephrine auto-injector in the apiary first-aid kit; know each worker's allergy status before hiring.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Site scored 30+/40 using Table 1 before any hive is moved.
  2. Hives on stands 4060 cm high, level side-to-side, tilted 23 forward.
  3. Windbreak established on the windward side; hives not in a wind tunnel.
  4. Water source within 300 m with floating landing sticks.
  5. Hives secured against wind, livestock, and theft (straps, fence, or lock).
  6. Inspection log updated every visit: queen, brood, food, pests, space.
  7. Spare hive ready per ten hives for swarms and emergency splits.
  8. Safety kit on site: suits, smoker, water/fire control, first-aid, antihistamine.
