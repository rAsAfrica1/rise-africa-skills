Lessons

1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Ore extraction is the heart of your gold mining operation. It is the physical work of breaking gold-bearing rock from the ground and getting it to the surface. If you extract too little, you waste time and money. If you extract too much waste rock, you waste effort on processing material that has no gold. The goal is to extract ore efficiently and handle it carefully so that no gold is lost. In artisanal mining, most gold is fine particles locked in quartz veins or alluvial gravel. You need to break the rock and move it without scattering the gold. Poor extraction and handling can lose up to 30% of your gold before you even start processing.

In Africa, artisanal miners often work in remote areas with limited equipment. You might be using hand tools like picks, shovels, and hammers, or you might have a small jaw crusher or stamp mill. The methods you choose depend on your deposit type. For hard rock, you drill and blast or use manual methods to break the ore. For alluvial deposits, you dig and wash gravel. Handling includes loading, transporting, and stockpiling ore. Each step must be done with care to prevent gold loss. For example, when you blast, fine gold can fly away with dust. When you transport, spillage can occur. When you stockpile, rain can wash away fine gold.

This module will teach you practical techniques to extract and handle ore effectively. You will learn the tools you need, step-by-step methods, common mistakes, and safety practices. By the end, you will be able to run a more productive and profitable operation. Remember, gold is heavy and does not travel far on its own, but careless handling can move it away from you. Every gram counts. In artisanal mining, a gram of gold can be worth a day's wage. So treat your ore like the treasure it is.

2. Tools and Materials

To extract and handle ore efficiently, you need the right tools. For hard rock mining, you will need a pickaxe, sledgehammer, chisel, and crowbar. A pickaxe with a sharp point breaks rock, while a sledgehammer (4-8 kg) is for driving chisels or breaking large boulders. Chisels should be made of hardened steel, about 30 cm long, and you need several sizes. A crowbar (1.5 m) helps lever out loose rock. For drilling, you can use a hand drill (star drill) or a manual auger. If you have access to a compressor and jackhammer, that speeds up work, but many artisanal miners start with hand tools.

For alluvial mining, you need a shovel, pick, and a pan or sluice box. A good shovel with a long handle saves your back. A pick breaks compacted gravel. You also need buckets (20 L) to carry gravel, and a wheelbarrow or sacks for transport. For hauling ore from underground, a wheelbarrow or a small cart on rails is useful. If you work underground, you need a headlamp or torch, and a rope for hauling buckets up shafts.

Safety gear is essential: hard hat, safety glasses, gloves, steel-toed boots, and dust mask. In Africa, many miners skip these, but they prevent injuries. You also need a first aid kit. For processing, you will need a mortar and pestle or a stamp mill to crush ore, and a pan for panning. A sluice box with riffles captures fine gold. You can build one with wood and carpet. Always have a tarp to catch spills when handling ore. A magnet helps remove iron particles. Finally, a notebook to record where you extracted ore and how much gold you recovered. This helps you improve.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Identify the ore body. Look for quartz veins in rock or pay streaks in gravel. Test pan a sample to confirm gold. Mark the area with stakes. Step 2: Remove overburden. Clear soil, vegetation, and waste rock. Pile it away from your working area. Step 3: Break the ore. For hard rock, drill holes along the vein using a chisel and hammer. Space holes 30-50 cm apart. Fill with explosives if you have them, or use feathers and wedges to split rock. For manual breaking, hit the rock along natural cracks. Aim to produce pieces 10-20 cm across. Step 4: Reduce size. Use a sledgehammer to break larger pieces into smaller ones (5-10 cm). This makes transport easier. Step 5: Load ore. Use shovels and buckets to load ore into wheelbarrows or sacks. Do not mix waste rock with ore. Step 6: Transport. Move ore to your processing site. If underground, haul buckets up shafts with a rope and pulley. Use a cart if possible. Step 7: Stockpile. Place ore on a concrete pad or tarpaulin. Keep different grades separate (high-grade, low-grade). Cover with a tarp to protect from rain. Step 8: Crush and process. Crush ore to sand size using a stamp mill or mortar. Then pan or sluice to recover gold. Step 9: Clean up. Sweep the area and collect any spilled material. Pan the sweepings to recover fine gold. Step 10: Record. Note how much ore you extracted, where, and how much gold you got. This helps you plan next time. Remember, the goal is to extract only ore, not waste, and to handle it gently to avoid losing gold.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Extracting too much waste rock. Many miners break everything, hoping to find gold. This wastes time and processing capacity. Fix: Sample and test before extracting. Only break rock that shows gold or is near gold-bearing veins. Mistake 2: Losing fine gold during blasting. Explosives can blow fine gold into the air. Fix: Use smaller charges, cover the blast area with mats, and collect dust. Mistake 3: Spilling ore during transport. Carrying ore in open buckets or sacks can spill. Fix: Use closed containers or tie sacks securely. Place a tarp under loading areas. Mistake 4: Mixing high-grade and low-grade ore. This dilutes your high-grade and lowers recovery. Fix: Keep grades separate. Use different stockpiles and label them. Mistake 5: Stockpiling on bare ground. Gold particles can sink into soil or wash away. Fix: Build a concrete pad or use a thick tarpaulin. Cover with a roof or tarp. Mistake 6: Not crushing fine enough. If ore is too coarse, gold remains locked in rock. Fix: Crush to at least 2 mm (sand size). Use a stamp mill or ball mill if available. Mistake 7: Ignoring safety. Accidents stop work. Fix: Wear protective gear, support underground workings, and never work alone. Mistake 8: Not recording data. Without records, you cannot improve. Fix: Keep a logbook of extraction and recovery. Mistake 9: Using wrong tools. Using a pick on hard rock instead of a sledgehammer wastes energy. Fix: Match tools to the job. Mistake 10: Skipping cleanup. Gold dust settles on floors and equipment. Fix: Regularly sweep and pan cleanup material. Each fix is simple but can increase your gold recovery by 10-20%.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional. Mining is dangerous. In Africa, many accidents happen because miners take shortcuts. Always wear a hard hat, safety glasses, gloves, steel-toed boots, and a dust mask. Dust from crushing ore contains silica, which causes lung disease. Wet the ore before crushing to reduce dust. If you work underground, never go alone. Use wooden or steel supports to prevent cave-ins. Check the roof for loose rock before starting. Use a rope and harness when working in shafts. Ensure good ventilation. Use a canary or gas detector if you suspect bad air. For blasting, use only licensed explosives and follow safety rules. Keep bystanders away. Never relight a misfire immediately; wait 30 minutes. When handling ore, use proper lifting techniques. Bend your knees, not your back. Don't overload wheelbarrows. Keep your work area tidy to avoid trips and falls. Store tools safely. Have a first aid kit and know how to use it. Best practices include: plan your work, communicate with your team, and take breaks. Drink water to avoid heat exhaustion. In hot climates, work early morning or late afternoon. Maintain your tools; a dull chisel is more dangerous than a sharp one. Finally, respect the environment. Do not dump waste into rivers. Rehabilitate mined areas. Use mercury responsibly or avoid it altogether. By following these practices, you protect yourself, your team, and your community.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. This exercise will help you apply what you learned. You will need: a pickaxe, shovel, sledgehammer, chisel, buckets, wheelbarrow, tarpaulin, and a pan. Choose a small area with visible quartz veins or alluvial gravel. If you have a known gold-bearing spot, use that. Otherwise, test pan a few samples to find a good spot. Mark a 2m x 2m square. Remove overburden and set it aside. Dig or break the ore within the square. For hard rock, drill a few holes with a chisel and hammer, then break the rock with a sledgehammer. Aim for pieces 10-20 cm. For alluvial, dig gravel and pick out large rocks. Load all ore into buckets and carry to a tarpaulin. Weigh or estimate the volume. Crush a sample of ore to sand size using a mortar and pestle. Pan the crushed ore carefully. Count the gold particles or weigh the gold. Record your results: area, volume, and gold recovered. Now, repeat the process but with better handling: cover the ore with a tarp when transporting, keep high-grade separate, and sweep the area after. Compare gold recovery. You should see an improvement. Finally, clean up your site. Fill the hole, replace overburden, and dispose of waste properly. Write a short report: what worked, what didn't, and what you will do differently. This exercise teaches you the value of careful extraction and handling. Do it regularly to improve your skills.

Step-by-Step Method

  1. Assess the ore body. Map visible reef or alluvial gravel. Sample every 1â2 m. Record gold visible in panning to estimate grade before you commit labour.
  2. Prepare the face. Remove loose rock, install ground support in underground workings, and clear a level working floor so ore does not mix with mud.
  3. Drill and blast (or split). Use the minimum explosive or splitting force needed. Over-blasting creates fines that are easily lost and increases dilution.
  4. Sort at the face. Remove obvious waste rock and clay before loading. This single step often raises head grade by 20â30%.
  5. Load and haul. Use wheelbarrows, sacks, or a small winch. Keep ore and waste routes separate. Weigh and record every load.
  6. Stockpile correctly. Build a pad on hard, drained ground. Cover with tarpaulin. Never stockpile on bare soil where fines wash away.
  7. Record and reconcile. Log tonnes mined, tonnes processed, and gold recovered. Compare weekly to detect losses or theft.

Unsupported underground workings are the leading cause of death in African ASM. Never enter a shaft or pit without inspecting roof and walls, installing support, and checking ventilation. Dry drilling of quartz reef releases respirable crystalline silica, which causes silicosisâan incurable lung disease. Always wet-drill or use dust suppression, wear an N95 or better respirator, and never work alone. In Ghana, Tanzania, and South Africa, ASM fatality rates remain 5â10 times higher than large-scale mining. Your life and your licence depend on doing this right.

Safety and Best Practice

  • Ground control: Inspect and scale loose rock every shift. Install timber or steel supports in weak ground.
  • Ventilation: Ensure at least two openings for airflow. Use fans where natural ventilation is insufficient.
  • Water management: Dewater pits and shafts; never let water pool at the working face.
  • PPE discipline: Helmet, boots, gloves, goggles, and dust mask are non-negotiable.
  • Blasting safety: Only trained, licensed persons handle explosives. Clear the area and use a warning system.
  • Mercury awareness: If you use mercury downstream, do so only with retorts and in line with the Minamata Convention. Extraction itself should be mercury-free.
  • Formalisation: Register your operation, pay royalties, and sell through licensed channels. This unlocks finance, better prices, and legal protection.

Practical Exercise

Objective: Measure and improve the grade of ore delivered to your processing site over one week.

  1. Select one working face. Before loading, sort and remove visible waste rock into a separate pile.
  2. Weigh and record the sorted ore and the waste separately for five consecutive days.
  3. Pan a 5 kg representative sample from each day's ore and estimate visible gold content.
  4. At the end of the week, calculate: (a) total ore tonnes, (b) total waste tonnes, (c) ratio of ore to waste, (d) estimated grade change compared with your previous week.
  5. Compare your gold recovery at the processing stage with the previous week. Document the difference in your logbook.

Expected outcome: Most teams see a 15â30% improvement in head grade and a measurable increase in recovered gold within one week, simply from face sorting and disciplined handling.
