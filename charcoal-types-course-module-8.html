1. Blending Tools: Stumps, Tortillons, Brushes

Blending tools are essential for controlling charcoal. Stumps are tight rolls of paper with pointed ends, used to smudge and blend. Tortillons are similar but smaller and more pointed. In Africa, you can make your own stump by tightly rolling a piece of paper (like newspaper) into a pencil shape and taping it. For a tortillon, use a smaller strip. Brushes: soft brushes like fan brushes or makeup brushes can blend large areas. In many African markets, you can find cheap makeup brushes that work well. Use a stump to blend small areas like shadows, and a brush for larger areas like skies. Clean stumps by rubbing on sandpaper or a rough cloth. Avoid using your fingers for blending because oils from your skin can affect the charcoal. Practice blending gradients from dark to light. For a smooth finish, blend in small circles. For texture, blend in one direction. Keep a few different sizes: a large stump for big areas, a small tortillon for details. You can also use a chamois cloth (or soft leather) for blending large areas. In Africa, look for soft leather scraps from tailors. Remember: blending too much can make your drawing look muddy. Use blending tools sparingly to maintain contrast. A good starter set: one large stump, two tortillons, one soft brush. Store them in a small pouch to keep them clean.

2. Erasers for Charcoal

Charcoal is forgiving because you can erase it, but you need the right erasers. Kneaded erasers are like putty; they lift charcoal without smudging. You can shape them to a point for details. In Africa, if you can't find a kneaded eraser, use white bread (remove crust) rolled into a ball. It works similarly, though it crumbles. Vinyl erasers are firmer and can erase dark marks, but they can tear paper if you press too hard. Use them lightly. For fine details, use a pencil eraser or a battery eraser (if available). You can also use a clean, dry cloth to wipe away charcoal. To erase, dab, don't rub. Rubbing pushes charcoal into the paper. For highlights, use a kneaded eraser to lift charcoal. You can also use masking tape to lift charcoal. In Africa, many artists use a piece of soft rubber (from a bicycle inner tube) to erase. Test on scrap paper first. Keep erasers clean by kneading them or washing vinyl erasers with soap and water. Store kneaded erasers in a sealed container to prevent drying. Practice erasing to create highlights on a dark background. A good eraser can save a drawing. Always have at least one kneaded and one vinyl eraser.

3. Fixatives and Workable Fixatives

Charcoal smudges easily, so you need a fixative to protect your drawing. Fixative is a spray that holds charcoal in place. Workable fixative allows you to continue drawing after spraying. In Africa, commercial fixatives can be expensive or hard to find. You can make your own: mix 1 part skim milk with 3 parts water. Spray lightly. Or use hairspray (unscented, strong hold) as a cheap alternative. Test first on scrap paper. To apply, hold the can 30 cm away and spray in light coats. Too much will darken the drawing. Work in a ventilated area. For workable fixative, spray lightly, let dry, then draw more. You can also use a fixative between layers to prevent smudging. Store fixative away from heat. If you use hairspray, choose a brand that dries clear. In Africa, some artists use gum arabic dissolved in water (1 part gum to 10 parts water) as a fixative. Apply with a spray bottle. Always test on a sample. Fixative is especially important if you plan to frame or transport your drawing. Without fixative, your drawing will smudge. A good fixative will not change the color of the charcoal. Practice on a test piece before applying to your final work.

4. Charcoal Holders and Extenders

Charcoal sticks and pencils get shorter as you use them. Holders and extenders let you use every last bit. A charcoal holder is a clamp or screw mechanism that grips the charcoal. You can buy one at art shops, but in Africa, you can make your own. Use a metal pen tube or a piece of bamboo. Split one end, insert charcoal, and wrap with rubber band or wire. For pencils, use a pencil extender (a metal or plastic tube that holds the pencil stub). If you can't find one, use a thick straw or rolled paper. Another option: wrap the charcoal with tape to make it longer. For soft charcoal, you can hold it with a piece of cloth. Extenders help you control the charcoal and keep your fingers clean. They also allow you to use very short pieces. When making a holder, ensure the charcoal is secure but not crushed. Test the grip. For vine charcoal, which is very soft, use a holder that doesn't apply too much pressure. For compressed charcoal, a simple clamp works. You can also use a craft knife to sharpen the charcoal to a point before inserting. Store holders with the charcoal inserted to protect the tip. Clean holders with a dry cloth. A good holder can save you money by using all your charcoal.

5. Cleaning and Storage

Charcoal dust gets everywhere. Clean your tools regularly to keep them working. After each session, wipe stumps and tortillons on a piece of sandpaper or rough cloth to remove charcoal. If they get too dirty, you can wash them with mild soap and water, then let them dry completely. Brushes: wash with soap and water, reshape, and dry flat. Erasers: knead kneaded erasers until clean; wash vinyl erasers with soap and water. Fixative: keep cans clean and nozzles unclogged. Store all tools in a box or pouch. A simple pencil case works. For charcoal sticks, keep them in a rigid container to prevent breakage. You can use a mint tin or a small plastic box. Line it with foam or cloth. For pencils, store them tip-up in a cup or roll. Keep your drawing board clean. Wipe it with a damp cloth after each use. In Africa, humidity can cause charcoal to absorb moisture. Store charcoal in a dry place with a desiccant packet (like silica gel) if possible. If you live in a dusty area, cover your tools. Label your boxes. Keep a small brush for dusting off your paper. Never store charcoal with food. A clean workspace prevents smudges on your drawings. Set aside 10 minutes after each session to clean and store your tools.

6. DIY Charcoal Tools

You don't need expensive tools. You can make many charcoal tools yourself. Charcoal: make your own by burning hardwood (like acacia or mango) in a closed tin. Let it cool, then use. Stumps: roll newspaper tightly into a pencil shape, tape it. Tortillons: roll smaller paper strips. Erasers: use bread or rubber from a bicycle inner tube. Fixative: mix skim milk and water (1:3) or use hairspray. Holders: split a bamboo stick, insert charcoal, wrap with rubber band. Blending brushes: use makeup brushes or soft cloth. Sandpaper: use rough stone or concrete to sharpen charcoal. Drawing board: use a piece of plywood or hardboard. Clips: use clothes pegs to hold paper. Smudge guard: use a piece of paper under your hand to prevent smudging. Storage: use a cardboard box with dividers. You can also make a charcoal pencil by wrapping charcoal in paper and gluing. For a kneaded eraser, mix flour and water to make a dough, let it dry slightly. Test all DIY tools on scrap paper. In Africa, these materials are cheap and available. Making your own tools saves money and teaches you resourcefulness. Share your DIY ideas with other artists. A good artist can work with minimal tools.

<h2>Practical Reference: Tools and Accessories for Charcoal Work</h2>

<p>In the African charcoal economy, the difference between a hobbyist and a profitable producer often comes down to tools. Whether you are a fine artist blending charcoal for gallery work in Nairobi, a workshop owner in Kumasi training apprentices, or a producer pressing charcoal dust into briquettes for sale, your tools determine your output, your costs, and your margins. This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the essential tools and accessories covered in this module: blending tools (stumps, tortillons, brushes), erasers, fixatives and workable fixatives, charcoal holders and extenders, cleaning and storage, and DIY charcoal tools. It is written for practitioners who need to buy smart, maintain equipment, and turn tools into business advantage.</p>

<h3>1. Blending Tools: Stumps, Tortillons, Brushes</h3>
<p>Stumps and tortillons are tightly rolled paper tools that smudge and blend charcoal without the oils of a finger. Tortillons are the blunt, pencil-shaped version; stumps are thicker and pointed. In African workshops, stumps are often unavailable locally, so artists substitute rolled newspaper, cotton buds, or even clean cloth wrapped around a stick. Brushes soft fan brushes, flat hake brushes, and large mop brushes are used to sweep charcoal dust, soften edges, and create atmospheric washes. For briquette producers, a stiff brush is essential for cleaning press moulds between batches.</p>

<h3>2. Erasers for Charcoal</h3>
<p>Charcoal erasers are not just for mistakes they are drawing tools. A kneaded eraser lifts charcoal to create highlights without tearing paper. A vinyl or plastic eraser removes charcoal cleanly from smooth surfaces. A gum eraser crumbles as it works, making it gentle on textured paper. For charcoal producers, erasers matter less, but for artists selling finished work, the right eraser protects commissions and reduces rework. In the African market, kneaded erasers are often imported and expensive; a soft bread dough ball or a clean, slightly damp cloth can substitute in a pinch.</p>

<h3>3. Fixatives and Workable Fixatives</h3>
<p>Charcoal smudges. Fixative is a clear liquid sprayed or brushed that binds charcoal particles to the paper so a finished drawing survives handling, transport, and sale. Workable fixative does the same but allows further drawing on top. For African artists selling at markets or shipping work, fixative is not optional; it is a business requirement. In humid coastal cities like Mombasa or Lagos, final fixative should be applied in thin, multiple coats to avoid clouding. Workable fixative is best for staged drawings built up over several sessions.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020;background:#fdecec;padding:14px;margin:18px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Fixative Vapour</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;color:#333;">Fixatives contain solvents (often xylene, toluene, or acetone-based carriers) that are flammable and toxic when inhaled. Always spray outdoors or in a well-ventilated area never in a closed room, dormitory, or shared workshop. Keep away from open flames, charcoal stoves, and welding sparks. Wear a dust mask or respirator (N95 or better) and eye protection. Pregnant women and children should avoid the area during spraying. Store cans below 30C, away from direct sun critical in African climates where daytime temperatures routinely exceed 35C. Dispose of empty cans through licensed waste handlers; do not burn them.</p>
</div>

<h3>4. Charcoal Holders and Extenders</h3>
<p>A charcoal holder is a mechanical clamp often metal or wood that grips short pieces of charcoal so you can draw until the last millimetre. An extender (or bridge) supports your hand above the drawing surface to prevent smudging. Both tools save money: without a holder, artists discard charcoal at 3040% of its usable length. For producers, a holder is less relevant, but extenders and clamps are useful when testing briquette hardness or pressing samples. In many African studios, a split bamboo stick bound with rubber bands serves as a functional holder.</p>

<h3>5. Cleaning and Storage</h3>
<p>Charcoal dust is abrasive and hygroscopic it absorbs moisture from the air. In humid regions, stored charcoal and paper can soften, warp, or grow mould. Best practice: store charcoal in sealed plastic tubes or airtight boxes with a silica gel pack. Clean blending stumps by rubbing them on fine sandpaper or a rough cloth; never wash them with water, as paper stumps lose their shape. Brushes should be wiped clean after each session and stored bristle-up. Fixative cans should be capped tightly and stored upright. For workshops, a weekly cleaning schedule for moulds, presses, and tables reduces contamination and extends tool life.</p>

<h3>6. DIY Charcoal Tools</h3>
<p>DIY is not a compromise it is a competitive advantage in African markets where imported tools carry high markups. You can make:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rolled-paper stump:</strong> tightly roll a strip of newspaper or bond paper, tape the end, sharpen with sandpaper.</li>
<li><strong>Charcoal holder:</strong> split a bamboo stick, insert charcoal, bind with rubber bands or twine.</li>
<li><strong>Extender:</strong> a clean wooden ruler or dowel taped to a small block keeps your hand off the paper.</li>
<li><strong>Brush:</strong> cut a soft paintbrush to size for blending; wash and dry thoroughly before use.</li>
<li><strong>Storage box:</strong> repurpose a metal biscuit tin with a silica gel sachet.</li>
<li><strong>Briquette press:</strong> a simple lever or screw press built from local hardwood and scrap metal can cut equipment costs by 6080%.</li>
</ul>
