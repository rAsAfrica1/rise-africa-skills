1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Welcome to Module 4. In gold smelting and refining, your furnace is the heart of your operation. Without proper heating equipment, you cannot melt gold, separate impurities, or produce clean bars. In Africa, many small-scale miners and refiners lose money because they use makeshift fires or wrong equipment. This module teaches you the right furnaces and heating tools to get high recovery and safe work.

Gold melts at 1,064Â°C (1,947Â°F). That is very hot. A simple wood fire can reach 600â800Â°C, not enough. Charcoal with forced air can hit 1,200Â°C, but it is hard to control. That is why we use furnaces: propane, diesel, or electric. In Africa, propane furnaces are common because gas is available in many cities. Diesel furnaces work where gas is scarce. Electric furnaces are clean but need stable power.

Why does this matter? If your furnace is too cold, gold stays mixed with slag and you lose gold. If it is too hot, you burn crucibles and lose gold as vapour. If heating is uneven, your gold bar has holes and impurities. A good furnace gives steady, controllable heat. That means more gold recovered, less waste, and safer work.

You also need the right tools: crucibles, tongs, molds, and safety gear. In this module, you will learn to choose, set up, and use furnaces and heating equipment. We focus on practical methods used in African workshops, from Ghana to South Africa. By the end, you will know how to melt gold safely and efficiently, even with limited resources. Letâs get started.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to melt gold safely and efficiently. This method works for a propane furnace, but the principles apply to diesel or electric.

Step 1: Prepare your workspace. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated shed. Clear area of flammable materials. Set furnace on a firebrick or concrete floor. Have sand bucket and extinguisher nearby. Wear full safety gear: leather apron, face shield, gloves, boots.

Step 2: Preheat the furnace. Turn on propane and ignite the burner. Adjust air intake to get a blue flame with slight roar. Let furnace heat for 10â15 minutes until inside glows red (about 900Â°C). Use thermometer to check.

Step 3: Charge the crucible. Place gold and flux in crucible. For 1 kg gold, add 100 g flux (2:1 borax/soda ash). Do not fill crucible more than 2/3 full. Put crucible in furnace using tongs.

Step 4: Melt. Close furnace door. Increase heat to 1,100â1,200Â°C. Gold will melt in 20â40 minutes depending on quantity. Stir occasionally with a graphite rod to mix flux. Do not open door too oftenâheat escapes.

Step 5: Check purity. When gold is fully liquid, it should look like bright orange liquid. Impurities (slag) float on top. If slag is thick, add more flux. Skim slag with a graphite rod or steel spoon. Put slag in a metal containerâit may contain gold; reprocess later.

Step 6: Pour. Preheat mold to 200Â°C. Use tongs to lift crucible. Pour gold slowly into mold in one steady stream. Do not stopâit causes lines. Let bar cool for 10â15 minutes. Do not quench in waterâit can crack.

Step 7: Clean up. Turn off furnace. Let it cool completely. Remove slag and clean crucible for reuse. Store gold bar in safe place.

Remember: Always work with a partner. Never leave furnace unattended. If flame goes out, turn off gas immediately and ventilate before relighting. With practice, you can melt 1 kg gold in under 30 minutes.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced refiners make mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them.

Mistake 1: Using a wood or charcoal fire. It cannot reach 1,064Â°C reliably. Fix: Use a propane or diesel furnace. If you must use charcoal, use a forced-air burner (blacksmith style) and a clay furnace. But invest in a proper furnaceâit pays off.

Mistake 2: Wrong crucible. Using a steel pot or cheap clay pot. Steel melts at 1,400Â°C but reacts with gold; clay cracks. Fix: Use clay-graphite crucibles rated for 1,200Â°C+. Buy from reputable suppliers.

Mistake 3: No flux or wrong flux. Without flux, impurities stay in gold. Too much flux wastes gold. Fix: Use 2:1 borax/soda ash. For 1 kg gold, 100 g flux. Adjust if slag is thick.

Mistake 4: Overheating. Gold boils at 2,856Â°C, but at 1,300Â°C it starts to vaporise and you lose gold. Also, crucibles burn. Fix: Keep temperature at 1,100â1,200Â°C. Use a thermometer. If gold looks white-hot, itâs too hot.

Mistake 5: Wet mold. Water in mold causes steam explosion, spraying molten gold. Fix: Preheat mold to 200Â°C for 10 minutes. Never pour into a cold or wet mold.

Mistake 6: Pouring too fast or too slow. Fast pour causes turbulence and bubbles; slow pour causes uneven bars. Fix: Pour in one steady stream, about 2â3 seconds for 1 kg.

Mistake 7: No safety gear. Burns are common. Fix: Always wear leather apron, face shield, gloves, and boots. Keep a bucket of sand and fire extinguisher.

Mistake 8: Leaving furnace unattended. A gas leak or flare-up can cause fire. Fix: Never leave. If you must step away, turn off gas.

Mistake 9: Reusing crucible without cleaning. Leftover slag contaminates next melt. Fix: Clean crucible with warm water and wire brush after each use.

Mistake 10: Not reprocessing slag. Slag can contain 5â10% gold. Fix: Crush slag and pan it, or re-melt with more flux. You can recover extra gold.

By avoiding these mistakes, you will get higher recovery and safer work.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional when working with 1,200Â°C molten gold. Follow these rules to protect yourself and your community.

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE): Wear a leather apron, face shield (not just goggles), heat-resistant gloves (leather or Kevlar), and steel-toe boots. Never wear synthetic clothingâit melts and sticks to skin. Tie back long hair. Remove jewellery.

Ventilation: Melting gold releases toxic fumes (especially if impurities like lead or mercury are present). Work outdoors or in a shed with open doors and a fan. Never work in a closed room. If you feel dizzy, get fresh air immediately.

Fire Safety: Keep a dry powder fire extinguisher (ABC type) and a bucket of sand within armâs reach. Never use water on a gas fire. If gas leaks, turn off tank, ventilate, and do not ignite until smell is gone. Check hoses and connections with soapy water before each use.

Furnace Setup: Place furnace on a fireproof surface (concrete or firebricks). Keep away from walls, curtains, and flammable liquids. Maintain a 1-metre clearance. Secure propane tank upright outside the work area.

Handling Molten Metal: Use long tongs. Never touch crucible with bare hands. When pouring, keep face away from crucible. Pour slowly and steadily. Let mold cool before moving.

Emergency Procedures: If gold spills, let it cool completely before picking up. If you get a burn, cool with clean water for 10 minutes, then cover with sterile cloth. Seek medical help for serious burns.

Best Practices: Work with a partner. Keep a phone nearby for emergencies. Do not eat, drink, or smoke in the work area. Wash hands after handling flux and gold. Store gold and chemicals securely.

Environmental Responsibility: Dispose of slag and chemicals properly. Do not pour into rivers or soil. Collect slag and sell to recyclers or reprocess. Use flux sparinglyâexcess borax can harm plants.

Training: If possible, take a short course in furnace operation. Many mining associations in Africa offer training. Always learn from an experienced refiner before working alone.

6. Practical Exercise

Now itâs time to practice. This exercise will guide you through a safe gold melt using a propane furnace. You will need: 50 g gold (or gold-bearing concentrate), propane furnace, #1 clay-graphite crucible, tongs, 5 g flux (2:1 borax/soda ash), steel mold, thermometer, and full PPE.

Step 1: Set up. Outdoors or in a ventilated shed, place furnace on firebricks. Connect propane tank with regulator and hose. Check for leaks with soapy water. Put on PPE. Have sand bucket and extinguisher ready.

Step 2: Preheat. Turn on gas, ignite burner, adjust to blue flame. Let furnace heat to 900Â°C (check with thermometer). This takes 10â15 minutes.

Step 3: Charge. Mix 50 g gold with 5 g flux. Place in crucible. Using tongs, put crucible in furnace. Close door.

Step 4: Melt. Increase heat to 1,150Â°C. Wait 20â25 minutes. Open door briefly to stir with graphite rod. Gold should be liquid and orange. Skim slag with rod.

Step 5: Pour. Preheat mold to 200Â°C. Lift crucible with tongs. Pour gold into mold in one steady stream. Let cool 10 minutes.

Step 6: Weigh and record. Remove bar. Weigh it. Calculate recovery: (weight of bar / weight of gold input) x 100. For 50 g input, you should get 45â48 g bar (90â96% recovery). If less, check slag for gold.

Step 7: Clean up. Turn off furnace. Let cool. Clean crucible and mold. Store bar safely.

Repeat this exercise 3 times to build skill. Record temperature, time, and recovery each time. Aim for consistent 95% recovery. If you have access to a mentor, ask them to watch and give feedback. After mastering this, try larger quantities (100 g, 500 g) or different fluxes. Always prioritise safety.

Practical Reference: Furnaces and Heating Equipment for Gold Smelting and Refining

This practical reference consolidates the furnace and heating knowledge you need to run a small-to-medium gold smelting operation in Africa. It is written for the artisan, the small-scale miner, the jewellery workshop, and the emerging refiner who must make equipment decisions that balance cost, fuel availability, throughput, and safety. In gold smelting, the furnace is not a peripheral tool; it is the heart of the process. The wrong furnace choice can double your fuel bill, destroy your crucible, contaminate your gold, or put your team in hospital. The right choice can turn a marginal operation into a profitable one.

Across Africa, the most common smelting furnaces are the clay-lined pit furnace, the propane/gas crucible furnace, the diesel or waste-oil furnace, the induction furnace, and the electric resistance furnace. Each has a place depending on your scale, your location, your power supply, and your budget. This reference gives you the numbers, the diagrams, and the checklists to choose and operate correctly.

â ï¸ SAFETY WARNING: Carbon Monoxide and Metal Fume Hazard

Gold smelting furnaces produce carbon monoxide (CO), a colourless, odourless gas that kills within minutes in enclosed spaces. They also release metal fumes (lead, arsenic, mercury, cadmium) from impure gold and from fluxes. Never operate a furnace indoors without forced ventilation and a CO monitor. In African workshops, cases of CO poisoning and heavy-metal exposure are routinely reported where furnaces are run in unventilated rooms or under makeshift shelters. Always work outdoors or in a well-ventilated shed with cross-draught, and wear a P2/P3 respirator when charging, fluxing, or pouring. If you feel dizzy, headache, or nausea, stop immediately and move to fresh air.

Quick Reference Checklist: Furnace Setup and Operation

  1. Ventilation first: Position the furnace outdoors or in a shed with at least two open sides and a high roof vent. Never in a closed room.
  2. Refractory condition: Inspect lining for cracks, spalling, or glassy slag build-up before every heat. Replace or patch if cracks exceed 3 mm.
  3. Crucible check: Use only graphite-clay or silicon-carbide crucibles rated above your target temperature. Never use mild steel pots for gold melting.
  4. Burner alignment: Flame should swirl, not strike the crucible directly. Direct impingement shortens crucible life and causes hot spots.
  5. Temperature control: Use a thermocouple or optical pyrometer. Gold melts at 1064Â°C; do not exceed 1200Â°C unless alloying requires it.
  6. Flux discipline: Add borax and soda ash in measured amounts. Excess flux eats crucibles and creates hazardous slag.
  7. Pour safety: Preheat the mould, pour in one steady motion, and keep the pour area dry. Molten gold + moisture = steam explosion.
  8. Post-run shutdown: Turn off fuel, let the furnace cool naturally, and never quench a hot refractory lining with water.
