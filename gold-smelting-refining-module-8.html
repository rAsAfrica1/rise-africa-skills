1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Cyanidation is a chemical process used to extract gold from low-grade ore. It involves dissolving gold in a sodium cyanide solution, then recovering it. This method is dangerous because cyanide is highly toxic. In Africa, many small-scale miners use cyanidation without proper safety, leading to poisonings and environmental damage. For example, in Tanzania and Ghana, illegal cyanide use has contaminated rivers. But cyanidation is also important: it allows recovery of fine gold that panning or sluicing misses. In this module, you will learn the correct and safe way to do cyanidation, if you must. But remember: cyanide can kill you in minutes. Never work alone. Always have a safety plan. This method is not for beginners. If you can use gravity separation or mercury-free methods, do that instead. But if you have no choice, learn the right way. We will cover tools, step-by-step method, common mistakes, safety, and a practical exercise. This is dangerous work, but with knowledge, you can reduce the risk. Always prioritize life over gold.

2. Tools and Materials

To do cyanidation, you need specific tools and chemicals. First, sodium cyanide (NaCN) or potassium cyanide (KCN) in powder or briquette form. Buy from a licensed supplier; never buy from informal markets. You need a leaching tank: a plastic or stainless steel drum, at least 200 liters, with a lid. Do not use galvanized metal because cyanide reacts with zinc. You need a pH meter or pH paper to keep pH above 10.5. Lime (calcium hydroxide) to raise pH. You need a respirator with cyanide filters, chemical goggles, rubber gloves, and a rubber apron. Always have a buddy. For aeration, you need an air pump or a stirrer. For recovery, you need zinc dust or activated carbon. For testing, you need a gold pan and a scale. For safety, have a first aid kit and a cyanide antidote kit (amyl nitrite, sodium nitrite, sodium thiosulfate). Store chemicals in a locked, ventilated area away from acids. Never store cyanide near acids because it releases deadly hydrogen cyanide gas. Keep a bucket of bleach and water to neutralize spills. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated shed. Never work in a closed room. Have a hose with water for washing. All these tools are available in most African cities, but they cost money. Do not improvise with dangerous substitutes.

3. Step by Step Method

First, crush your ore to fine powder, like sand. You can use a ball mill or stamp mill. The finer the ore, the better the gold recovery. Next, mix water and ore in the leaching tank. Use 1 part ore to 3 parts water. Add lime to raise pH to 10.5-11.0. Test with pH paper. Then add sodium cyanide. A typical dose is 0.5 to 2 grams of cyanide per liter of water. For a 200-liter tank, start with 100 grams of cyanide. Stir gently for 24 to 48 hours. Keep the tank covered to prevent animals or people from drinking. After leaching, let solids settle. Siphon off the clear liquid (pregnant solution) into another tank. Now recover gold. Two methods: zinc precipitation or carbon adsorption. For zinc, add zinc dust to the solution. Gold precipitates as a black mud. For carbon, add activated carbon; gold attaches to carbon. Then burn the carbon or treat with caustic soda to recover gold. Finally, smelt the gold mud with borax and soda ash in a furnace. Always test the tailings to see if gold remains. If yes, repeat leaching. Never pour waste into rivers. Neutralize waste with bleach or hydrogen peroxide before disposal. Work in a well-ventilated area. Always wear full protective gear. Never eat, drink, or smoke during the process.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Using too much cyanide. This wastes money and increases danger. Fix: Use the correct dose, 0.5-2 g/L. Test with a cyanide test kit. Mistake 2: Not controlling pH. If pH drops below 10, hydrogen cyanide gas forms. Fix: Add lime regularly and check pH every hour. Mistake 3: Leaching for too short a time. Gold may not fully dissolve. Fix: Leach for at least 24 hours, preferably 48. Mistake 4: Using dirty water or acid-contaminated water. This consumes cyanide. Fix: Use clean water, and never mix with acid. Mistake 5: Not wearing protective gear. Cyanide can be absorbed through skin. Fix: Always wear gloves, goggles, and respirator. Mistake 6: Storing cyanide near acids. This creates deadly gas. Fix: Store separately in locked cabinets. Mistake 7: Pouring waste into the environment. This kills fish and people. Fix: Neutralize with bleach (1 part bleach to 10 parts waste) and test before disposal. Mistake 8: Working alone. If you collapse, no one helps. Fix: Always have a buddy who knows first aid. Mistake 9: Using metal containers that react with cyanide. Fix: Use plastic or stainless steel. Mistake 10: Not having an antidote kit. Fix: Keep amyl nitrite and sodium thiosulfate on site. Learn to use them. These mistakes are common in African small-scale mining. Avoid them to stay alive.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Cyanide is a fast-acting poison. It stops your cells from using oxygen. You can die within minutes. Safety is not optional. First, never work alone. Always have a trained buddy. Second, wear full protective equipment: respirator with cyanide cartridges, chemical goggles, rubber gloves, rubber apron, and rubber boots. Third, work in a well-ventilated area, preferably outdoors. Never work in a closed room. Fourth, have a safety plan. Know where the nearest clinic is and how to get there. Keep a cyanide antidote kit on site. Amyl nitrite ampoules, sodium nitrite, and sodium thiosulfate. Learn how to use them. Fifth, never eat, drink, or smoke while working. Wash hands thoroughly after work. Sixth, store cyanide in a locked, dry, ventilated area away from acids. Seventh, have a spill kit: bleach, water, and sand. If a spill happens, evacuate, ventilate, and neutralize with bleach. Eighth, test the air for hydrogen cyanide gas using a gas detector. If you smell bitter almonds, that is dangerous; but not everyone can smell it. Ninth, train everyone on the dangers. Tenth, keep records of chemical use. In Africa, many miners die because they ignore safety. Do not be one of them. If you feel dizzy, headache, or short of breath, get fresh air immediately and seek help. Always prioritize life over gold.

6. Practical Exercise

In this exercise, you will leach a small sample of gold ore using cyanide, under supervision. You will need: 1 kg of crushed ore, 3 liters of water, 5 grams of sodium cyanide, 10 grams of lime, a 10-liter plastic bucket, a stirrer, pH paper, gloves, goggles, respirator, and a buddy. First, put on all protective gear. Work outdoors. Mix water and ore in the bucket. Add lime until pH is 10.5-11.0. Then add cyanide slowly while stirring. Cover the bucket and stir every hour for 24 hours. After 24 hours, let solids settle. Carefully pour off the clear liquid into another bucket. Add 1 gram of zinc dust to the liquid. Stir for 30 minutes. A grey or black precipitate will form. Let it settle, then pour off the liquid. The precipitate is your gold concentrate. Dry it and smelt with borax. Weigh the gold. Calculate recovery: (gold recovered / gold in ore) x 100. For example, if ore had 0.1 grams and you recovered 0.08 grams, recovery is 80%. If recovery is low, check pH, cyanide dose, and leaching time. After the exercise, neutralize all waste with bleach (1 part bleach to 10 parts waste). Test with pH paper and cyanide test strips before disposal. Clean all tools with soap and water. Wash your hands and face. Report any spills or symptoms immediately. This exercise teaches you the process but remember: cyanide is deadly. Never do this without proper training and safety equipment.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Cyanidation for Gold Recovery Field Companion

Cyanidation is the most widely used chemical process for extracting gold from ore, and across Africa it is both an economic lifeline and a serious hazard. For small-scale and artisanal operators in Ghana, Tanzania, Burkina Faso, Zimbabwe, Mali, and the DRC, cyanide leaching can recover fine gold that gravity methods miss but it can also kill within minutes if mishandled. This Practical Reference gives you the numbers, tables, and checklists you need to run a controlled, compliant, and profitable cyanidation operation. It is written for the operator who intends to stay in business: safe, legal, and bankable.

Business-first framing: Cyanidation is only worth doing if the gold recovered exceeds the cost of reagents, containment, PPE, licensing, and waste treatment and if you can do it without a fatality or a regulatory shutdown. In most African jurisdictions, unlicensed cyanide use is a criminal offence and a licence-revocation trigger. Treat the safety and compliance sections below as your operating licence, not as optional reading.

1. Process Overview

Cyanidation dissolves gold in an alkaline cyanide solution in the presence of oxygen, forming a soluble gold-cyanide complex. The gold is then recovered by adsorption onto activated carbon (CIP/CIL) or by zinc precipitation (Merrill-Crowe). For small African operations, agitated tank leaching followed by carbon adsorption is the most common route; vat and heap leaching are used where ore volumes are large and capital is limited.

RED SAFETY WARNING CYANIDE IS LETHAL

Never add cyanide to a solution with pH below 10.5. Acidic cyanide releases hydrogen cyanide gas, which can kill an adult in minutes at concentrations above 100 ppm. Always add lime first, verify pH with a calibrated meter, and only then introduce sodium cyanide.

Never work alone. Cyanide exposure can incapacitate within seconds. A trained buddy with a radio, a cyanide antidote kit (amyl nitrite, sodium nitrite, sodium thiosulphate), and a clear evacuation route must be present at all times.

Never discharge untreated tailings. Free cyanide above 0.5 mg/L is illegal in most African jurisdictions and will contaminate community water sources downstream. Use alkaline chlorination, SO/air, or hydrogen peroxide destruction before release.

4. Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Licence and notification: Confirm you hold a valid cyanide use permit and have notified the relevant national environmental authority (e.g., EPA Ghana, NEMC Tanzania, NEMA Kenya).
  2. pH first, cyanide second: Add lime to reach pH 10.511.0 and verify with a freshly calibrated meter before any NaCN enters the tank.
  3. PPE is non-negotiable: Full-face respirator with cyanide-rated cartridges, chemical-resistant gloves and apron, rubber boots, and eye protection. No exceptions.
  4. Ventilation and monitoring: Forced ventilation in enclosed areas; HCN gas detector with audible alarm set at 4.7 ppm.
  5. Antidote kit on site: Amyl nitrite, sodium nitrite, and sodium thiosulphate, checked monthly for expiry; all staff trained in use.
  6. Containment and spill kit: Bunded tanks, impermeable floors, and a spill kit containing lime, absorbent, and PPE stored within 10 metres of the leach area.
  7. Tailings treatment: Destroy free cyanide to below 0.5 mg/L before discharge; test with a cyanide field kit and log results daily.
  8. Record keeping: Log NaCN consumed, pH, HCN readings, tailings cyanide, and any incidents. These records are your legal defence and your business intelligence.
