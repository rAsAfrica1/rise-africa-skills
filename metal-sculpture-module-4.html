1. Introduction to Blacksmithing: Forge Setup and Safety

Blacksmithing is the art of shaping metal using heat and hammer. In Africa, traditional blacksmiths have worked iron for centuries, and today you can set up a simple forge using locally available materials. A basic forge needs a heat source, an air supply, and a fireproof hearth. You can build a forge from a car brake drum, a metal bucket lined with clay, or even a hole in the ground lined with mud. The fire should be fueled with charcoal, which is widely available. For air, use a hand-cranked blower, a hair dryer, or a bellows made from leather and wood. The air pipe (tuyere) should enter the fire from the side, about 10 cm above the bottom.

Safety is critical. Always wear leather gloves, safety glasses, and a leather apron. Never wear synthetic clothingit melts. Work in a well-ventilated area, as forging produces carbon monoxide. Keep a bucket of water and a fire extinguisher nearby. The metal must be heated to 8001200C, which is bright orange to yellow. At this temperature, it becomes soft like clay. Use tongs to hold the metalnever your hands. Quench hot metal in water or oil to cool it. Remember that hot metal looks the same as cold metal; always test with a magnet or water drop before touching.

Start your fire with small pieces of charcoal, then add more as it catches. Use the blower to get a steady, hot fire. The ideal forge fire is a deep bed of coals with a bright center. Place your metal in the hottest part, called the 'sweet spot'. Heat it until it's the right color, then move quickly to the anvil. Work efficiently because the metal cools fast. A good forge setup is the foundation of all blacksmithing, so take time to build it right.

2. Basic Forging Techniques: Drawing Out, Upsetting, Bending

Drawing out means making metal longer and thinner. Heat your metal to bright orange (around 1000C). Place it on the anvil and strike with the hammer's flat face. Hit at a slight angle, moving the metal away from you. Turn the metal 90 degrees after each blow to keep it square. Use the horn of the anvil for curves. For a 1 cm square bar, you can draw it out to 6 mm thick in about 10 heats. Work from the center outward to avoid creating cold shuts (folds).

Upsetting is the opposite: making metal shorter and thicker. Heat a section of your bar, then strike the end against the anvil or a solid block. This forces the metal to bulge. For a decorative knob, heat the last 5 cm to yellow, hold it vertically, and hammer the end down. Upsetting is useful for making rivet heads or thickening edges. Be careful not to overheatif it sparks, it's burning.

Bending is simple but must be done at forging temperature. Use the anvil's horn or a bending fork. For a sharp bend, place the metal over the edge of the anvil and strike near the bend. For a smooth curve, use the horn. You can also bend cold metal in a vise, but it may crack. Always bend with the grain if possible. Practice these three techniques on scrap metal. A good blacksmith can draw out, upset, and bend with precision. In Africa, these skills are used to make tools, art, and household items. Remember: heat, hammer, and control.

3. Using Anvils, Hammers, and Tongs

The anvil is your main work surface. A good anvil is made of hardened steel and weighs 50150 kg. In Africa, you can find used anvils at scrap yards or make one from a piece of railway track. Mount it at knuckle height on a sturdy stump. The flat face is for general hammering. The horn is for bending and shaping curves. The hardy hole holds tools like cutters, and the pritchel hole is for punching. Keep the anvil clean and free of scale. Never hammer on the anvil's face with a hardened hammerit can chip.

Hammers come in various weights. For general forging, use a 12 kg cross-peen or ball-peen hammer. The cross-peen is for drawing out, and the ball-peen for texturing. Hold the hammer near the end of the handle for power, or near the head for control. Strike with a relaxed grip, letting the hammer's weight do the work. Don't grip too tightit causes fatigue. For heavy work, use a sledgehammer with a striker. Always wear safety glasses.

Tongs are essential for holding hot metal. You need several types: flat-jaw for square bars, bolt-jaw for round stock, and pick-up tongs for small pieces. Make your own tongs from mild steel by forging the jaws to fit your work. Adjust them by heating and bending. Never use pliersthey slip. When holding hot metal, keep the tongs parallel to the anvil. Your hammer hand should be free. Practice picking up and moving hot metal until it's second nature. Good tools make good work.

4. Heat Treating and Annealing Metal

Heat treating changes the hardness and toughness of steel. Annealing softens metal so you can work it. To anneal, heat the metal to non-magnetic (around 800C for high-carbon steel), then bury it in dry ash, sand, or vermiculite. Let it cool slowly overnight. This relieves stress and makes filing and drilling easier. In Africa, wood ash from a fire works well.

Hardening makes steel tough. Heat the metal to critical temperature (bright orange, non-magnetic), then quench it quickly in water, oil, or brine. Water cools fastest, oil slower. For most tools, use oil (motor oil or vegetable oil). Quench vertically to avoid warping. The metal becomes hard but brittle. Test with a fileit should skate without cutting.

Tempering reduces brittleness. After hardening, clean the metal and heat it gently until it turns a specific color. For a chisel, heat until it's straw yellow (220C); for a spring, blue (300C). Then quench again. This gives a tough, usable tool. Practice on scrap steel. Different steels require different temperatures. If you don't know the steel type, experiment. Heat treating is the difference between a tool that lasts and one that breaks. Always wear gloves and eye protection.

5. Twisting and Texturing Metal

Twisting adds decorative flair to metal. Heat a square bar to bright orange. Clamp one end in a vise and use a wrench or tongs to twist the other end. For a uniform twist, turn slowly and keep the heat even. A 1 cm bar can be twisted 23 full turns over 20 cm. If it cools, reheat. You can twist multiple bars together for a rope effect. In Africa, twisted iron is used in furniture, gates, and art. Practice on 6 mm square stock.

Texturing creates surface patterns. Use a hammer with a rounded face or a chisel to make marks. The ball-peen hammer gives dimples. A cross-peen makes lines. You can also use a punch to make dots. For a hammered look, strike the metal repeatedly with a ball-peen while it's at low orange heat. Work on a sandbag or wood to avoid flattening. Another method is to use a spring fuller or a guillotine tool to make grooves. Always work on a clean anvil.

For a rustic look, brush the hot metal with a wire brush to remove scale, then apply wax or oil while warm. This gives a black finish. You can also use a brass brush to add color. Texturing is about experimentation. Try different hammers and techniques. Remember to anneal if the metal gets too hard. These decorative techniques turn simple forgings into art.

6. Project: Forged Abstract Form

Now you will create an abstract sculpture using the skills you've learned. This project combines drawing out, bending, twisting, and texturing. You will need a 1 m length of 10 mm mild steel round bar, a forge, anvil, hammer, tongs, and a wire brush. Start by drawing out one end to a taper. Heat the last 10 cm to orange and hammer it to a point. This will be a leg or spike.

Next, create a twist in the middle. Heat a 15 cm section and twist it 3 full turns. Then bend the bar into a flowing curve using the anvil's horn. Heat as needed. Add a second taper on the other end. Now, upset one section to make a thicker node. Heat a 5 cm section and hammer it against the anvil to thicken it. This creates visual weight. Use the ball-peen hammer to texture the surface with dimples. Work while the metal is at low orange.

Finally, decide on a base. You can weld the sculpture to a metal plate or set it in a concrete base. For a free-standing form, ensure it balances. Clean the finished piece with a wire brush, then apply beeswax or linseed oil while warm. This protects it from rust. Display your abstract form. Remember, abstract art is about expressionthere are no mistakes, only variations. This project proves you can forge, shape, and finish metal. With these skills, you can create saleable art for local markets or commissions.

<h2>Practical Reference: Forging and Shaping for African Metal Sculpture Studios</h2>

<p>This practical reference supports the <strong>Forging and Shaping</strong> module of the <em>Rise Africa Skills</em> Metal Sculpture course. It is written for artisans, workshop owners, and apprentices who intend to turn metal sculpture into a reliable income stream not just a creative hobby. Every technique here is chosen because it is repeatable, teachable, and compatible with the tools and materials most commonly available in African urban and peri-urban workshops: scrap vehicle springs, railway steel, rebar, mild steel plate, and charcoal or gas forges.</p>

<p>Blacksmithing and forging sit at the foundation of decorative and functional metalwork across the continent from Ghanaian and Nigerian wrought-iron gate traditions to Senegalese and South African sculptural work sold to galleries and export buyers. The commercial logic is simple: forged work commands higher prices than welded assembly because it demonstrates skill, produces unique forms, and resists imitation. This reference consolidates setup, technique, tooling, heat treatment, surface work, and a project brief into one usable document.</p>

<h3>1. Forge Setup and Safety in an African Workshop Context</h3>

<p>Most successful sculpture workshops in Nairobi, Accra, Lagos, and Johannesburg start with a solid-fuel forge built from local materials: a car brake drum or wheel rim as the firepot, clay or firebrick lining, a steel pipe for the tuyere, and a hand-cranked or electric blower. Charcoal is widely available and cheaper than gas in many regions, though gas forges offer cleaner, faster heating for small work. Choose based on your electricity reliability and fuel logistics, not on prestige.</p>

<p>Site the forge with at least 3 metres of clear space, on a non-combustible floor, with cross-ventilation or an overhead hood. Keep quench water, a first-aid kit, and a dry-powder extinguisher within arm's reach. Good light matters for judging colour temperature the single most important skill in forging.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fff0f2; padding:14px; margin:18px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE LIGHTING THE FORGE</strong>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 18px;">
<li><strong>Carbon monoxide:</strong> never burn charcoal or coal in an enclosed room. CO is odourless and kills. Forge outdoors or under an open-sided shelter with a working flue.</li>
<li><strong>Hot metal looks like cold metal:</strong> below roughly 550 C, steel shows no glow. Always test with tongs, never with fingers.</li>
<li><strong>Eye protection:</strong> wear impact-rated goggles or a face shield during forging and grinding. Chipping scale causes permanent eye injury.</li>
<li><strong>Burns:</strong> keep a bucket of clean water and burn gel on site. Never quench a burn with forge water it is contaminated.</li>
<li><strong>Fuel storage:</strong> store charcoal, coal, and gas cylinders away from the forge, in a ventilated area, never near the fire.</li>
<li><strong>Hearing:</strong> anvil work exceeds 100 dB. Use ear plugs during heavy striking sessions.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>2. Basic Forging Techniques: Drawing Out, Upsetting, Bending</h3>

<p>These three operations are the alphabet of forged sculpture. Master them and you can make almost any abstract or figurative form.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Drawing out</strong> lengthening and thinning stock by striking with the hammer face at the anvil's horn or edge. Keep the work at a bright orange-yellow (roughly 9501150 C for mild steel). Rotate 90 between blows to avoid a "fish-mouth" end.</li>
<li><strong>Upsetting</strong> thickening or shortening by striking the end of the bar while it rests on the anvil face. It is the fastest way to build mass for a joint or a sculptural foot.</li>
<li><strong>Bending</strong> use the horn for curves, the edge for sharp angles, and a bending fork or scroll wrench for controlled radii. Bend while hot; cold bending cracks high-carbon scrap.</li>
</ul>

<p>For sculpture, the commercial trick is <em>consistency</em>: a buyer of a series of ten forged bowls or abstract forms expects each to match. Practice the same operation on offcuts until the results are repeatable before you commit expensive stock.</p>

<h3>3. Using Anvils, Hammers, and Tongs</h3>

<p>An anvil is the single largest tool investment in a forging studio. A 5080 kg London-pattern anvil, even a used one, is worth more than three cheap cast-iron substitutes because it rebounds the hammer and saves your arm. Mount it at knuckle height on a hardwood stump or a sand-filled steel drum this also reduces noise complaints in dense urban workshops.</p>

<p>Hammers: start with a 11.5 kg cross-peen for drawing out, a 1 kg ball-peen for texturing, and a lighter 0.5 kg hammer for detail. Tongs must match the stock flat-jaw for plate, bolt-jaw for square bar, and wolf-jaw for round. Make your own tongs from scrap spring steel; it is a core employability skill and a sellable product in itself.</p>

The rules below apply to the medium- and high-carbon steels commonly found in scrap vehicle springs, files, and bearings the same stock used for forged blades, chisels, and decorative tooling.</p>

<h3>5. Twisting and Texturing Metal</h3>

<p>Twisting and texturing add value at almost zero material cost. A plain bar becomes a sculptural element with 20 minutes of skilled work.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Twisting:</strong> heat a section evenly, clamp one end in a vice, and turn the other with a wrench or twisting fork. Count turns to keep a series consistent.</li>
<li><strong>Texturing:</strong> use a ball-peen hammer, a cross-peen, a chisel, or a home-made stamp to create scale, bark, or fabric-like patterns. Work at a dull red to avoid cracking.</li>
<li><strong>Surface finish:</strong> wire-brush hot, then apply beeswax, linseed oil, or a locally mixed patina. In humid coastal markets, a wax finish plus a clear lacquer extends outdoor life by years.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Costing and Pricing a Forged Piece</h3>

<p>African buyers local galleries, hotels, exporters, and diaspora customers pay for <em>story plus finish</em>. A forged abstract form priced only on material and hours will lose money. Use a simple costed model, then apply a margin that reflects skill and market positioning.</p>

<h3>7. Project: Forged Abstract Form</h3>

<p><strong>Brief:</strong> design and forge a freestanding abstract form, 4080 cm tall, using at least three of the techniques above (drawing out, upsetting, bending, twisting, texturing). The piece must stand unsupported, have a stable base, and carry a consistent finish.</p>

<p><strong>Suggested workflow:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Sketch three ideas; choose the one you can forge in 1014 hours.</li>
<li>Cut stock to length; normalise scrap spring steel before shaping.</li>
<li>Forge the base first it controls stability.</li>
<li>Build the main form, then add twisted or textured details.</li>
<li>Weld or forge-weld joints; grind clean.</li>
<li>Wire-brush hot, apply wax or oil finish.</li>
<li>Photograph in natural light for your portfolio and online sales.</li>
</ol>

<p>This project is deliberately commercial: it produces a photographable, shippable, repeatable product that can be sold as a one-off or as a small series. Document every step buyers and grant panels reward evidence of process.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Forge outdoors or under a working flue CO kills silently.</li>
<li> Wear goggles, ear plugs, leather apron, and closed boots.</li>
<li> Heat to bright orange-yellow before drawing out; never forge dull red.</li>
<li> Rotate the bar 90 between hammer blows to keep sections square.</li>
<li> Match tongs to stock shape flat, bolt-jaw, or wolf-jaw.</li>
<li> Normalise after heavy forging; anneal before drilling or filing.</li>
<li> Count twists and blows so a series stays consistent.</li>
<li> Cost every piece with material, fuel, labour, and overheads before quoting.</li>
</ul>
