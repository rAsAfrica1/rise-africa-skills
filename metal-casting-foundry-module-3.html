1. Introduction and Why It Matters

In metal casting, your furnace must reach the melting point of the metal you are pouring. Aluminium melts at around 660C, while cast iron needs about 1200C. The fuel you choose and how you burn it determines whether you succeed or waste time and money. In many African workshops, charcoal, coal, and firewood are common, but they have different heat values and burn rates. For example, charcoal burns hotter and cleaner than wood, but it is more expensive. Coal can produce high heat but often contains sulphur that can ruin your metal. Understanding fuels and combustion means you can pick the right fuel for the job, control the air supply to get complete burning, and avoid excess smoke or dangerous gases. This module will teach you how to select, store, and use fuels efficiently. You will learn to build a fire that reaches the right temperature, how to tell if combustion is complete by the flame colour, and how to save money by using less fuel. Whether you are melting scrap aluminium for cooking pots or casting bronze for art, mastering fuels and combustion is the difference between a successful pour and a failed one.

2. Tools and Materials

To work with fuels and combustion, you need more than just a match. Start with a metal drum or clay furnace. A 200-litre steel drum cut in half works well for small melts. You need a tuyere (a pipe that blows air into the fire), which can be made from a 2-inch steel pipe. A blower or bellows is essential: a hand-cranked blower from a blacksmith or a simple vacuum cleaner motor can work. For fuels, you need charcoal (preferably hardwood charcoal), coal (bituminous or anthracite), or coke. Avoid wet wood. You also need a fire rake or long-handled tongs to move fuel, a metal bucket for ash, and a pyrometer or thermocouple to measure temperature if you can afford one. A cheaper alternative is to learn the colour of hot metal: dark red is about 600C, bright orange is 900C, and yellow-white is 1200C. Safety gear: leather gloves, face shield, and a respirator mask for fumes. For storage, keep fuel dry in a covered shed. In Africa, you can often find charcoal at local markets, and coal from hardware stores or mining areas. Always have a fire extinguisher or sand bucket nearby. Remember, the right tools make the job faster and safer.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Prepare your furnace. Place it on a fireproof surface, away from walls. Step 2: Lay a bed of small kindling (dry wood shavings or paper) at the bottom. Step 3: Add charcoal or coal on top, leaving space for air. Step 4: Light the kindling from the bottom through the tuyere hole. Step 5: Once the fire catches, turn on the blower slowly. Too much air at first will blow out the fire. Step 6: Add more fuel gradually as the fire grows. Keep the fuel bed about 15-20 cm thick. Step 7: Watch the flame. A yellow-orange flame means incomplete combustion; add more air. A blue flame means complete combustion and maximum heat. Step 8: For melting aluminium, you need about 700C. For cast iron, 1200C. Use a pyrometer or watch the crucible colour. Step 9: Once the metal is molten, skim off dross (impurities) with a skimmer. Step 10: Pour carefully. Always keep the blower running until you finish. After pouring, shut off the air and let the fire die. Never pour water on a hot furnace. In Africa, many foundries use charcoal because it is locally available. But charcoal burns fast, so you must keep adding it. Coal burns longer but needs more air. Practice with small batches to learn your fuel's behaviour.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Using wet fuel. Wet wood or charcoal produces steam, lowers temperature, and creates smoke. Fix: Store fuel under cover for at least a week before use. Mistake 2: Not enough air. Without enough oxygen, fuel burns slowly and produces carbon monoxide. Fix: Check your blower and tuyere for blockages; increase air flow gradually. Mistake 3: Too much air. This blows heat away and can extinguish the fire. Fix: Adjust the air supply until you see a steady blue flame. Mistake 4: Using the wrong fuel for the metal. Charcoal may not reach 1200C for cast iron. Fix: Use coke or coal for high-melting metals. Mistake 5: Adding large chunks of fuel at once. This smothers the fire. Fix: Add small amounts frequently. Mistake 6: Ignoring smoke colour. Black smoke means incomplete combustion and wasted fuel. Fix: Improve air supply or use drier fuel. Mistake 7: Not cleaning ash. Ash blocks air flow. Fix: Remove ash after every melt. Mistake 8: Leaving fire unattended. Fix: Always have someone watching. In Africa, a common mistake is using charcoal made from softwood, which burns quickly. Fix: Buy hardwood charcoal from reputable sellers. Also, don't use petrol or kerosene to start the fire; it's dangerous. Use paper and kindling instead.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety starts before you light the fire. Wear cotton or wool clothing, not synthetic, which melts. Leather boots, gloves, and a face shield are must-haves. Set up your furnace in a well-ventilated area, ideally outdoors or with a chimney. Carbon monoxide is invisible and deadly; never work in a closed room. Keep a bucket of sand, a fire extinguisher, and a first-aid kit within reach. Before lighting, check that the tuyere and blower are clear. Never use petrol, diesel, or paraffin to start a fireuse paper and kindling. When adding fuel, use long-handled tongs; never use your hands. Stand upwind to avoid smoke and heat. If you smell sulphur (like rotten eggs) from coal, stop and move to fresh air; it can cause health problems. For best practice, preheat your crucible gradually to avoid cracking. Keep the fuel bed even. Monitor the flame: a blue flame is efficient and safe; a yellow flame means you are wasting fuel and creating soot. After the melt, let the fire burn out completely. Dispose of ash in a metal container, not plastic. In Africa, many foundries use charcoal, which produces less sulphur than coal. But charcoal still produces carbon monoxide. Always have a buddy systemnever work alone. Teach others about safety. Remember, a safe foundry is a productive foundry.

6. Practical Exercise

Exercise: Melt 1 kg of scrap aluminium using charcoal in a small drum furnace. You will need: a 100-litre drum, a 2-inch steel pipe for tuyere, a hand blower or hair dryer (on cold setting), 3 kg of hardwood charcoal, a crucible, tongs, and safety gear. Step 1: Set up the furnace on bare ground. Step 2: Fill the drum with a layer of kindling and 1 kg of charcoal. Step 3: Light the kindling and start the blower slowly. Step 4: After 5 minutes, add more charcoal. Step 5: Place the crucible with aluminium scrap in the fire. Step 6: Keep the blower running. Watch the flame: it should become blue. Step 7: After about 15-20 minutes, check if the aluminium is molten (it will look like shiny liquid). Step 8: Skim off dross. Step 9: Pour into a preheated mould. Step 10: Shut off the blower and let the fire die. Record how long it took and how much charcoal you used. Compare with coal: repeat the exercise using coal. Which fuel gave a faster melt? Which was cheaper? In Africa, charcoal is often easier to find, but coal may be cheaper per kilogram. Calculate your cost per melt. Also, observe the smoke: charcoal produces less smoke. This exercise teaches you to control air, fuel type, and temperature. Do it three times to improve your speed. Always keep safety first.

<h2>Practical Reference: Fuels and Combustion in African Foundries</h2>

<p>Whether you are running a small jua kali workshop in Gikomba, a metal fabrication cluster in Nnewi, or a growing foundry in Ogun State, the fuel you burn and how you burn it will determine your melt quality, your production cost, and your profit margin. This practical reference consolidates the technical data, conversion factors, and troubleshooting guidance you need to make fast, informed decisions on the foundry floor. It is written for the operator, the workshop owner, and the technician who must keep the furnace running and the costs down.</p>

<h3>Fuel Selection at a Glance</h3>

<p>African foundries typically rely on one of four fuel pathways: coke (metallurgical or foundry coke), charcoal, diesel, or liquefied petroleum gas (LPG). Each has different calorific values, ignition characteristics, and cost structures. The choice is rarely purely technical it is a business decision shaped by local supply chains, transport costs, and the scale of your operation.</p>

<h4 style="color:#b71c1c; margin:0 0 8px 0; font-family:Arial, sans-serif;"> SAFETY WARNING Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning</h4>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; color:#333;">Carbon monoxide is odourless, colourless, and potentially fatal. It is produced in large quantities during incomplete combustion in cupolas, crucible furnaces, and charcoal stoves. In enclosed or poorly ventilated workshops common in dense urban informal settlements CO can accumulate to dangerous levels within minutes.</p>
<p style="margin:0; font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; color:#333;"><strong>Mandatory controls:</strong> Never operate a fuel-fired furnace in a sealed room. Install roof vents or forced ventilation. Keep furnace doors open during warm-up. If you feel dizzy, nauseous, or develop a headache, shut down the furnace immediately and move to fresh air. Seek medical attention. Train every worker on CO awareness before they enter the foundry floor.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Check fuel quality before purchase:</strong> Reject wet, dusty, or high-ash fuel. Ask for a moisture content reading or do a simple squeeze test.</li>
<li><strong>Calibrate your air-to-fuel ratio:</strong> For coke, aim for 810 kg air per kg fuel. For LPG, follow the supplier's stoichiometric ratio (15.5:1 by mass).</li>
<li><strong>Preheat your charge:</strong> Use waste heat from the flue to warm scrap before charging. This saves 58% fuel per melt.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain a clean coke bed:</strong> Replace or screen coke before every heat. Fines and dust choke the bed and reduce airflow.</li>
<li><strong>Monitor flame colour:</strong> A neutral flame is blue-white with a short yellow tip. Yellow with black smoke means too much fuel or too little air.</li>
<li><strong>Record every melt:</strong> Log fuel weight, charge weight, melt time, and metal temperature. This data is your most powerful cost-control tool.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect tuyeres and blast pipes weekly:</strong> Blockages and leaks waste fuel and reduce melt rate.</li>
<li><strong>Train on CO safety monthly:</strong> Review symptoms, ventilation checks, and emergency shutdown procedures with all staff.</li>
</ol>
