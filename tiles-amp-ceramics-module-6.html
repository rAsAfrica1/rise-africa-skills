1. Making Glazes from Local Materials

You can make excellent glazes using materials found around you in Africa. The three main ingredients are a glass former (silica, from sand or quartz), a flux (to lower melting point, like wood ash, limestone, or soda ash from Lake Natron), and a stabilizer (like clay or alumina). A basic recipe by weight: 40% silica sand (washed and sieved to 80 mesh), 30% wood ash (from hardwood, sieved), 20% whiting (calcium carbonate from limestone), and 10% kaolin clay. Mix dry, then add water to a creamy consistency (like milk).

Test each glaze on a small tile before using on your work. Apply three coats by brushing, let dry, and fire to 1000-1100C in an electric kiln or a well-built wood kiln. If the glaze is too matte, add 5% more ash; if it runs, add 5% more kaolin. Keep records: date, recipe, firing temperature, and result. Local materials vary, so always test.

For a glossy finish, increase silica to 50% and reduce clay to 5%. For a satin finish, use 35% silica and 15% clay. You can also add 2-5% of a metal oxide (see Lesson 4) for colour. Sieve the mixed glaze through an 80-mesh screen to remove lumps. Store in a sealed bucket; glazes can be used for months if kept clean.

Safety: wear a dust mask when mixing dry materials. Wood ash must be washed to remove soluble salts: put ash in a bucket, add water, stir, let settle, pour off water, repeat three times, then dry. This prevents glaze defects like crawling or pinholing. With practice, you can create unique glazes that reflect your local geology and reduce costs.

2. Applying Glaze: Dipping, Pouring, and Spraying

Glaze application determines the final surface. The three main methods are dipping, pouring, and spraying. For dipping, mix glaze to a specific gravity of 1.4-1.5 (measure with a hydrometer or weigh 100 ml: it should weigh 140-150 g). Stir well. Hold the tile or pot with tongs, dip quickly (1-2 seconds), and remove. A single dip gives a thin coat; two dips (with a short drying time between) give a thicker coat. For even coverage, dip the piece at a slight angle and rotate.

Pouring is good for large tiles or irregular shapes. Place the piece on a turntable or a banding wheel. Pour glaze from a jug in a steady stream, starting at the centre and moving outward. Let excess drip off, then wipe the bottom with a sponge. For a two-tone effect, pour one colour, let dry, then pour a second colour over part of the piece.

Spraying gives a smooth, even coat. Use a spray gun or a mouth atomizer. Thin the glaze to the consistency of skim milk (specific gravity 1.2-1.3). Spray in light passes, 6-8 inches away, moving continuously. Build up 3-4 thin coats, letting each dry. Always spray in a ventilated area or outdoors, wearing a respirator. Clean the spray gun immediately after use.

After applying, wipe the foot of the pot or the edges of tiles with a damp sponge to prevent glaze from sticking to the kiln shelf. Let the glaze dry completely (at least 2 hours) before firing. If you are glazing tiles, leave a 2 mm border unglazed if they will be butted together. Test your application on a sample tile first to check thickness and coverage.

3. Decorative Techniques: Sgraffito, Mishima, and Slip Trailing

Sgraffito means 'scratched' in Italian. Apply a layer of coloured slip (clay mixed with water and oxide) or glaze to leather-hard clay. Let it dry to a matte finish. Use a loop tool, a nail, or a sharp stick to scratch through the slip to reveal the clay body underneath. Draw geometric patterns, African motifs, or animal shapes. The contrast between the slip colour and the body creates the design. For best results, the slip should be 1-2 mm thick. After scratching, clean the edges with a soft brush.

Mishima is a Korean technique. Incise lines into leather-hard clay using a sharp tool. Fill the lines with a contrasting slip (often white or black). Let the slip dry slightly, then scrape the surface with a metal rib to remove excess slip, leaving the slip only in the lines. This creates fine, inlaid patterns. You can also use this technique on bisque-fired tiles: paint slip into the lines, let dry, and wipe the surface with a damp sponge.

Slip trailing is like icing a cake. Mix slip to a thick, yogurt-like consistency. Put it in a squeeze bottle or a plastic bag with a small hole cut in the corner. Draw raised lines, dots, or patterns on leather-hard or bisque-fired clay. You can trail slip over glaze before firing for a textured effect. Keep the slip moving steadily; practice on a scrap tile first.

All three techniques can be combined. For example, sgraffito over a slip-trailed design. After decorating, let the piece dry slowly (cover with plastic for 24 hours) to prevent cracking. Then bisque fire to 1000C, apply a clear glaze if desired, and glaze fire to 1080-1100C. These techniques are perfect for telling African stories through your tiles.

4. Using Oxides for African Earth Tones

Metal oxides give glazes their colour. For African earth tones, the key oxides are iron oxide (red, brown, black), manganese dioxide (purple-brown, black), copper oxide (green, turquoise), and cobalt oxide (blue). Always add oxides to a base glaze by weight: 1-5% for soft colours, 5-10% for strong colours. For example, add 4% red iron oxide to a clear glaze for a warm terracotta; add 2% copper oxide and 1% cobalt oxide for a deep green.

To get earthy reds and browns, use iron oxide. A 6% addition to a matte glaze gives a rich reddish-brown; 10% gives a dark chocolate brown. For a speckled effect, use unwashed wood ash or add 2% granular ilmenite. Manganese dioxide at 3-5% gives a purple-brown; at 8% it is almost black. Copper oxide at 2-3% in an alkaline glaze gives turquoise; in a lead glaze it gives green. Cobalt is strong: 0.5% gives light blue, 2% gives deep blue.

Mix oxides into the glaze slurry after sieving. Wear a dust mask; oxides are fine powders. Test on tiles: apply three coats, fire to 1080C. Note that iron and copper can cause glaze running; add 2% kaolin to reduce fluidity. For a truly local look, use red clay from your area as a colourant: 10-20% red clay in a clear glaze gives a warm, earthy tone.

You can also blend oxides: 3% iron + 1% manganese gives a metallic bronze. Keep records of your recipes. African earth tones are often muted and warm, so start with low percentages and increase. Remember that firing temperature affects colour: higher temperatures (1100C) make iron redder, while lower temperatures (1000C) make it browner. Always test your oxide blends before using on a large piece.

5. Glaze Firing and Understanding Results

Glaze firing melts the glaze and bonds it to the clay. For earthenware tiles, fire to 1000-1100C. For stoneware, 1200-1280C. You need a kiln with a pyrometer or cones. Use cones (e.g., Orton cone 06 for 1000C, cone 04 for 1060C, cone 3 for 1160C) to confirm temperature. Place cones where you can see them through the peephole. Heat slowly: 100C per hour up to 600C (to burn out organics), then 150C per hour to top temperature. Soak at top temperature for 15-30 minutes to smooth the glaze.

Cooling matters. Fast cooling can cause crazing (fine cracks) or dunting (cracking). Let the kiln cool naturally with the door closed. Do not open the kiln above 200C. For crystal glazes, cool slowly (50C per hour) between 1100C and 900C to grow crystals. For matte glazes, cool normally.

Understand your results: a glossy glaze is smooth and reflective; a matte glaze is satin or dull; a satin glaze is in between. If the glaze is too thin, you see the clay body through it; too thick, it may run or crawl. If the glaze is bubbly, you fired too fast or the glaze was too thick. If the glaze is pinholed, there was dust or oil on the bisque. If the colour is weak, you need more oxide or a higher temperature.

Keep a firing log: date, kiln, temperature, cone, glaze recipe, and result. After firing, check the tiles for defects (see Lesson 6). If the glaze is underfired (dry, rough), refire to a higher temperature or add more flux. If overfired (runny, transparent), reduce temperature or add more clay. With practice, you will learn to control your kiln and get consistent results.

6. Troubleshooting Glaze Defects

Common glaze defects and their fixes: Crazing (fine cracks) happens when the glaze contracts more than the clay. Fix: add 5-10% silica to the glaze, or fire to a higher temperature, or use a clay body with lower expansion. Crawling (glaze pulls away in patches) is caused by too thick application, dusty bisque, or too much clay in the glaze. Fix: apply thinner, wipe bisque with a damp sponge, reduce clay by 5%.

Pinholing (small holes) comes from dust, oil, or air bubbles. Fix: clean bisque with a soft brush, apply glaze thinner, fire slower in the early stages (100C per hour to 600C). Pinholes can also be caused by too much gas in the clay; bisque fire longer. Blistering (large bubbles) is from firing too fast or too thick a glaze. Fix: fire slower, apply thinner.

Running (glaze drips down) happens when the glaze is too fluid. Fix: add 2-5% kaolin or alumina hydrate, or reduce flux (ash, whiting). If the glaze runs onto the kiln shelf, use a kiln wash (50% alumina, 50% kaolin) on shelves. Shivering (glaze flakes off) is the opposite of crazing: the glaze contracts less than the clay. Fix: add more flux or fire lower.

Dunting (cracks during cooling) is caused by fast cooling or thick glaze. Fix: cool slowly (close the kiln, do not open until below 200C). If you see a defect, note it in your log and adjust one variable at a time. Test on small tiles. Remember: most defects are caused by application thickness, firing speed, or glaze recipe. With careful testing, you can solve any problem. African clays and local materials may behave differently, so always test before a big firing.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Glaze Firing, Results & Troubleshooting for African Ceramics Studios

This reference module is written for working African ceramics businesses from small studio potters in Kumasi, Ghana, to tile workshops in Tunisia, and from cooperatives in Tigray, Ethiopia, to craft enterprises in Limpopo, South Africa. The goal is commercial: fewer failed firings, more consistent output, faster client delivery, and better margins.

Glaze firing is where a potter's raw materials, labour, and reputation are either locked into a sellable product or lost to a kiln load of defects. In African markets where gas, electricity, and imported chemicals can be expensive, unreliable, or subject to long supply chains mastering local materials and firing control is a direct business advantage.

Understanding Firing Results in an African Studio Context

Glaze firing is not just a technical step it is a business-critical process. In many African studios, kilns are fired with wood, gas, or diesel, and the cost of a failed firing can equal a week's profit. Understanding what happens at each temperature stage helps you diagnose defects and protect your margins.

Between 100200 C, remaining physical water leaves the clay and glaze. Between 500600 C, chemically combined water is driven off and quartz inversion occurs a rapid, risky expansion that can crack ware if you fire too fast. Between 9001000 C, glaze materials begin to sinter and melt. Between 11001280 C, the glaze matures and bonds to the clay body.

For African earth-tone glazes using local materials wood ash, granite dust, river clay, termite mound clay, and locally sourced oxides the maturation range is often narrower than commercial glazes. Test tiles are not optional; they are your insurance policy.

SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE FIRING

Glaze materials and kiln firing pose serious health and safety risks. In African studios, ventilation is often inadequate, and personal protective equipment (PPE) may be scarce or expensive. You must:

  • Wear a fitted respirator (N95 or better) when mixing dry glaze materials silica and metal oxides are hazardous.
  • Never fire in enclosed spaces without ventilation. Carbon monoxide from gas, wood, or charcoal kilns is lethal.
  • Keep children and untrained workers away from glaze mixing and kiln areas.
  • Store oxides and glaze chemicals in clearly labelled, sealed containers, away from food and water sources.
  • Wash hands and clothing after handling glaze materials lead and barium compounds are especially dangerous.
  • Check local regulations for kiln emissions and workplace safety standards.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test first: Always fire a test tile before committing a full production batch.
  2. Record everything: Note glaze recipe, application method, firing curve, and results.
  3. Control the ramp: Slow down between 500600 C to avoid cracking.
  4. Use pyrometric cones: They confirm actual heat work, not just kiln temperature.
  5. Check glaze fit: Crazing or shivering means the glaze and clay body are incompatible.
  6. Keep bisque clean: Dust and grease cause crawling and pinholes.
  7. Standardise materials: Buy oxides and clays from the same source for consistency.
  8. Cool slowly: Rapid cooling causes dunting and weakens ware.
