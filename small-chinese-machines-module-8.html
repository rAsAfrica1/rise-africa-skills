1. Building a Preventive Maintenance Schedule

A preventive maintenance schedule is a written plan that tells you what to check, clean, lubricate, and replace on your machineâand when. Without one, you only fix things after they break, which costs more and stops production. Start by listing every maintenance task your machine needs. Check the manual (even a Chinese manual can be translated with a phone app). Common tasks: daily clean dust from filters, weekly check belt tension, monthly grease bearings, quarterly change oil. For a typical small Chinese machine like a brick maker or maize mill, daily tasks take 10 minutes, weekly 30 minutes, monthly 2 hours.

Create a simple table on paper or a whiteboard. Columns: Date, Task, Done (â), Notes. Hang it next to the machine. Assign one person responsible. For example, at a maize mill in Tanzania, the operator checks the belt every morning before starting. If loose, he tightens it with a 14mm spanner. Every Friday, he greases the main bearing with a grease gun (2 pumps). Every first Monday, he changes the oil in the gearbox using 2 liters of 80W-90 gear oil.

Use a calendar or phone alarm to remind you. For daily tasks, tie them to start-up or shut-down routines. For weekly tasks, pick a fixed day like Saturday afternoon. For monthly tasks, use the first day of the month. Record everything. If a task is missed, note why. After three months, review: are tasks too frequent or too rare? Adjust based on wear. For example, if belts last only 3 months instead of 6, check alignment or increase tension checks.

Train your operator to do daily and weekly tasks. You or a mechanic handle monthly and quarterly tasks. Keep a small notebook with all maintenance historyâthis helps when ordering spares. A good schedule reduces breakdowns by up to 70% and extends machine life by years. It also makes your business more reliable, so customers trust you.

2. Sourcing Spare Parts from China and Locally

When your machine breaks, you need the right spare part fast. You have two main sources: China (original or aftermarket) and local suppliers. Each has pros and cons. From China, parts are cheap but take 2-6 weeks to arrive by air, or 2-3 months by sea. You also pay shipping and customs. Locally, parts are faster but often more expensive and may not fit perfectly. Your strategy: keep critical spares on hand, and for non-critical parts, order from China in advance.

To order from China, you need the machine model and part number. Look on the machine nameplate or in the manual. If you don't have it, take a photo of the part and send it to the supplier. Use platforms like Alibaba, Made-in-China, or contact the original manufacturer. For small orders, use a sourcing agent who consolidates goods and handles shipping. Payment is usually by bank transfer or Western Union. Always ask for a proforma invoice and check the part number carefully. A wrong part wastes weeks.

For local sourcing, build relationships with hardware shops, auto parts dealers, and other machine owners. In Nairobi, Kariobangi Light Industries has many spare parts for Chinese machines. In Lagos, Alaba International Market. In Accra, Abossey Okai. Bring your old part to match. Local mechanics can also fabricate simple parts like bushes, belts, or gaskets. For example, a maize mill roller bearing might be available at a local bearing shop (SKF or NSK brand) for $15, while the Chinese original costs $5 but takes 3 weeks. If you need it now, buy local.

Keep a list of suppliers with contact details and typical lead times. For critical parts like belts, bearings, filters, and fuses, always have one spare. For expensive parts like motors or control boards, you might not keep a spare but should know where to get one quickly. Join a WhatsApp group of machine owners in your areaâthey often share sources. Always compare prices: a part from China might cost $10 plus $20 shipping, while local is $40. If you can wait, order from China; if not, buy local. Plan ahead to avoid downtime.

3. Keeping a Critical Spare Parts Inventory

A critical spare parts inventory is a small stock of the parts that fail most often and would stop your machine if missing. You don't need to stock everythingâjust the items that cause the longest downtime if you have to order them. Start by tracking breakdowns for one month. Write down what part failed, how long it took to get a replacement, and how much production you lost. The parts that caused more than 2 hours of downtime are critical.

Common critical spares for small Chinese machines: belts (V-belts, timing belts), bearings (ball bearings, roller bearings), filters (air, oil, fuel), fuses, relays, contactors, switches, and lubricants (grease, oil). For a brick making machine, also keep spare moulds and mixing blades. For a maize mill, keep spare screens and hammers. For a generator, keep spare fuel filters and injectors. Quantities: at least one spare for each critical part. For fast-wearing items like belts, keep two. For cheap items like fuses, keep a pack of 10.

Store parts in a clean, dry box or cabinet. Label each part with its name, part number, and machine it fits. Keep a notebook or spreadsheet with: part name, quantity, minimum stock level, and supplier. When you use a part, write it down and reorder when stock hits the minimum. For example, if you have 2 belts and use one, reorder immediately. Don't wait until zero. Set a reorder point: for items with 4-week lead time, reorder when you have 2 left. For local items, reorder when you have 1 left.

Check your inventory every month. Look for rust, cracks, or expiration dates (for rubber parts). Rotate stock: use oldest first. Keep a small toolkit with the inventory: spanners, screwdrivers, grease gun, multimeter. Train your mechanic to use the inventory and record usage. A good inventory costs maybe $100-200 to set up, but saves thousands in lost production. It also reduces stressâyou know you can fix common problems immediately.

4. Training a Local Mechanic on Your Machine

Your machine will break down. When it does, you need someone who can fix it fast. Training a local mechanicâeither an employee or a trusted technicianâis one of the best investments you can make. Start by selecting someone with basic mechanical skills: can use spanners, understands how engines or motors work, and is willing to learn. A formal certificate is not necessary; attitude and problem-solving matter more.

First, get the machine manual (or download a similar model). Translate key sections using Google Translate. Sit with the mechanic and go through the machine: identify all parts, their functions, and common failure points. Show them the lubrication points, belts, filters, and electrical panel. Let them practice under your supervision. For example, have them change a belt, grease bearings, and check voltage with a multimeter. Teach them to use the preventive maintenance schedule and spare parts inventory.

Next, create a troubleshooting guide. For each common problem (machine won't start, unusual noise, overheating, poor output), list possible causes and solutions. For a maize mill: if it won't start, check fuse, then switch, then motor. If it overheats, check belt tension, lubrication, and ventilation. Practice these scenarios. Have the mechanic diagnose a simulated fault. Give them a small notebook to record all repairsâwhat was wrong, what they did, and parts used.

Encourage them to ask questions and contact you if unsure. Build a relationship with a local mechanic who specializes in Chinese machines. Pay them fairly for training and repairs. Consider sending them to a short course at a vocational school or to another business with the same machine. For example, in Kenya, the Kenya Industrial Research and Development Institute (KIRDI) offers training on small machinery. In Nigeria, the Industrial Training Fund (ITF) has programs. After training, let them handle routine maintenance while you oversee major repairs. A well-trained mechanic reduces downtime from days to hours and saves you money on external technicians.

5. Extending Machine Lifespan

A small Chinese machine can last 5-10 years if you take care of it. Neglect it, and it might fail in 1-2 years. The key is consistent maintenance, proper operation, and using quality parts. Start with daily care: clean the machine after every use. Dust and debris cause overheating and wear. For a brick machine, wash off clay and sand. For a maize mill, blow out flour dust with compressed air or a brush. Check and top up lubricants daily. Use the right oil and greaseâcheap alternatives may save money now but damage bearings later.

Operate within limits. Don't overload the machine to meet a big order. For example, a maize mill rated at 200 kg/hour should not run at 300 kg/hour continuously. Overloading causes belts to slip, motors to overheat, and bearings to fail. Follow the manual's speed and load recommendations. Also, ensure stable power supply. Voltage fluctuations damage motors and control boards. Use a voltage stabilizer or surge protector (cost $20-50). If you run a generator, keep it well maintained.

Lubrication is critical. Use the correct grease: lithium-based for high-speed bearings, calcium-based for water-resistant areas. Grease bearings every 50 hours of operation (about weekly for daily use). Over-greasing can also damage seals, so follow the manual. Change oil in gearboxes and engines every 200-500 hours or as specified. Use a funnel and clean container to avoid contamination. Check belts monthly: a belt that is too tight wears bearings; too loose slips and wears itself. Correct tension: about 1 cm deflection when pressed with thumb.

Store the machine properly when not in use. Cover it to keep out dust and rain. If it will sit for months, drain fuel and oil, and grease exposed metal to prevent rust. Keep a log of all maintenance and repairs. After one year, review: what broke most? Adjust your schedule. Also, train operators to listen for unusual sounds and report them immediately. A small noise today can become a major failure tomorrow. By following these steps, you'll get many years of reliable service from your machine.

6. The Cost of Downtime and How to Avoid It

Downtime is when your machine is not working. It costs you money in lost production, wasted labour, and unhappy customers. For a small business, even one day of downtime can be devastating. Calculate your downtime cost: (Daily production in units) x (Profit per unit) + (Daily labour cost) + (Fixed costs like rent). For example, a maize mill produces 1,000 kg per day, profit $0.10/kg, so $100 profit. Labour $20, rent $10. Total downtime cost = $130 per day. If a breakdown takes 3 days to fix, you lose $390 plus customer trust.

The biggest causes of downtime are: lack of preventive maintenance, missing spare parts, untrained operators, and power problems. To avoid downtime, first implement the preventive maintenance schedule. Second, keep critical spares in stock. Third, train your operator and mechanic. Fourth, have a backup plan for power: a generator or inverter. Also, keep a list of emergency contacts: mechanic, electrician, spare parts supplier. For example, if your maize mill motor burns out, you need a replacement fast. If you have a spare motor, downtime is 2 hours. If not, it could be 2 weeks.

Track downtime. Write down every time the machine stops, why, and how long. After a month, you'll see patterns. Maybe belts break every 3 weeksâthen buy better belts or fix alignment. Maybe power cuts happen dailyâthen invest in a stabilizer. Use a simple notebook or a phone app. Share this data with your team. Set a goal: reduce downtime by 50% in 3 months.

Have a contingency plan. If the main machine fails, can you use a smaller backup? Can you outsource to another business? Can you shift production to another time? For example, a brick maker might keep a manual mould to make a few bricks while the machine is down. Also, build relationships with other machine ownersâthey might lend you a part or help with repairs. Finally, insure your machine if possible. Some micro-insurance policies cover equipment breakdown. The cost of prevention is always less than the cost of downtime. Spend a little time and money now to keep your machine running.

Practical Reference: Maintenance and Spare Parts for Small Chinese Machines

In African agribusiness, a broken machine is not just a mechanical problem â it is a cash-flow problem. A hammer mill that sits idle for two weeks during maize harvest can cost a small processor more than the machine itself. This module gives you a practical reference for keeping Chinese-made machines (hammer mills, rice threshers, oil presses, feed mixers, water pumps) running, sourcing parts without being overcharged, and building the local capacity to fix things before they fail.

The core principle is simple: maintenance is a business expense, not a technical chore. Every hour of planned maintenance buys you several hours of unplanned downtime avoided. Every spare part you stock is insurance against a lost contract.

A preventive maintenance (PM) schedule is a written plan that says what to check, when to check it, and who is responsible. For small Chinese machines used in African agribusiness, the schedule should be built around operating hours, not calendar days, because usage is often seasonal.

2. Sourcing Spare Parts from China and Locally

Most small Chinese machines in Africa are imported through traders who buy from platforms like Alibaba, Made-in-China, or directly from factories in Zhejiang, Shandong, and Henan provinces. The spare parts supply chain mirrors this: some parts are cheap and easy to find locally, others must be imported.

Rule of thumb: source locally anything that is generic (bearings, belts, filters, bolts, oil seals). Import from China only what is machine-specific (injection pumps, cylinder heads, custom gears, electronic controllers).

3. Keeping a Critical Spare Parts Inventory

You do not need to stock everything. You need to stock the parts that stop production when they fail and take longer than 48 hours to replace. This is your critical spares list.

For a typical 10â20 HP hammer mill or rice thresher, a critical spares kit might include:

Store parts in a dry, locked cabinet. Label everything. Keep a simple inventory card or notebook â or use a free app like Google Sheets on a phone. Review stock before each season.

5. Extending Machine Lifespan

Chinese machines are not inherently short-lived â they are often misused. The same 10 HP diesel engine that lasts 2 years in one farm can last 8 years in another. The difference is discipline.

Key lifespan extenders:

  • Clean fuel and oil. Dirty fuel destroys injector pumps. Use a funnel with a filter. Change oil on schedule.
  • Correct load. Do not overload a hammer mill with wet maize or a thresher with green stalks. Match the feed rate to the machine's rated capacity.
  • Cooling. Keep cooling fins and radiators clean. In dusty African conditions, clean the air filter daily.
  • Storage. Store under cover. Moisture causes rust in cylinders and electrical systems.
  • Operator discipline. One trained operator who follows the checklist is worth more than three careless ones.
