Lessons

1. Brainstorming Business Ideas

Start by looking around you. What problems do people complain about daily? In Lagos, traffic jams mean people need quick phone charging services. In Nairobi, many small shops lack delivery options. Write down 10 problems you see in your community. For each, ask: Can I solve this with a product or service? Example: A young man in Kigali noticed his neighbours struggled to find affordable fresh vegetables. He started a mobile vegetable stall, buying from farmers at 500 RWF per kg and selling at 800 RWF per kg. That's a 60% markup.

Next, think about your skills. Can you cook, repair phones, sew, or teach? List 5 things you are good at. Combine skills with problems. If you can bake, and your area has no bakery, that's an idea. If you can fix bicycles, and many students ride to school, that's another. Don't dismiss simple ideas. A woman in Accra started a laundry service for busy workers; she now employs 3 people.

Use the SCAMPER method to improve existing ideas: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse. For example, a chapati seller in Dar es Salaam substituted wheat flour with cassava flour to cut costs by 30%. Combine a hair salon with a small caf. Adapt a Kenyan mobile money model to a new country.

Write everything down. Use a notebook or free tools like Google Keep or Trello. Aim for 20-30 ideas. Then filter: Which ideas require less than $500 to start? Which can break even within 6 months? Which use local materials? Eliminate those needing expensive imports or special licenses.

Finally, test your top 3 ideas with 10 potential customers. Ask: Would you buy this? How much would you pay? If 7 out of 10 say yes, you have a strong candidate. Don't fall in love with one idea too early. Brainstorming is about quantity first, then quality.

2. Evaluating Market Opportunities

A market opportunity exists when there are many potential customers who have a need, money to spend, and limited competition. Start by counting your potential customers. In a city like Kampala, if you plan to sell school bags, count the number of primary schools within 5 km. Each school might have 500 students. That's 2,500 potential customers. If you capture just 10%, that's 250 bags per year. At 20,000 UGX profit per bag, that's 5 million UGX.

Next, check competition. Walk around and see who else sells similar products. If there are 10 vendors selling the same thing, the market may be saturated. But if they are all far away or sell poor quality, you have a gap. For example, in a Tanzanian town, there were many phone repair shops but none offered same-day screen replacement. One entrepreneur filled that gap and charged a 50% premium.

Look for trends. Is the population growing? Are incomes rising? Is there new infrastructure like a road or market? A new road in rural Kenya can bring more customers to your shop. Also consider seasonality. Selling umbrellas in a rainy season is smart, but you need a backup product for dry months.

Use free data: national statistics offices, local government offices, and Google Trends. For example, search 'buy maize flour online' in your country to see if interest is growing. Talk to local leaders, market chairpersons, and other business owners. Ask them: What products are in high demand? What is missing?

Finally, calculate the size of the opportunity. Estimate total sales in your area. If you sell shoes, and there are 1,000 potential customers who buy 2 pairs per year at 15,000 TZS each, the market is 30 million TZS. If you can get 5% market share, that's 1.5 million TZS in revenue. If your costs are 1 million TZS, you make 500,000 TZS profit. That's a viable opportunity. If the numbers are too small, move on.

3. Conducting a Feasibility Analysis

A feasibility analysis answers: Can I actually do this? It has four parts: technical, financial, market, and organisational. Start with technical. Do you have the skills, equipment, and materials? For a poultry farm, you need a coop, feed, vaccines, and knowledge of chicken diseases. If you lack skills, can you learn in 2 weeks? If you need a machine that costs $2,000, can you afford it or rent it?

Financial feasibility is about numbers. Estimate start-up costs: equipment, rent, licenses, initial stock. For a small retail shop in Malawi, you might need 200,000 MWK for stock, 50,000 MWK for shelves, 30,000 MWK for a business license, and 100,000 MWK for rent deposit. Total: 380,000 MWK. Then estimate monthly costs: rent 50,000, electricity 10,000, transport 15,000, your salary 80,000. Total monthly costs: 155,000 MWK. If your average profit per item is 500 MWK, you need to sell 310 items per month to break even. Can you sell that many?

Market feasibility: Will people buy? Test with a small pilot. Sell for one week from a temporary spot. If you sell 50 items in a week, that's 200 per month, below your break-even. You might need to adjust price or find cheaper suppliers.

Organisational feasibility: Do you have the time? Can you manage employees? Do you need a partner? If you work full-time elsewhere, you may need a trusted family member to run the shop. Also check legal requirements: business registration, tax, health permits. In many African countries, you can register a sole proprietorship online for less than $50.

Finally, do a sensitivity check. What if sales are 20% lower? What if costs rise 10%? If the business still survives, it's feasible. If not, revise your plan or choose another idea. Feasibility is not about being perfect; it's about avoiding obvious failure.

4. Identifying Your Target Market

Your target market is the specific group of people most likely to buy from you. Don't say 'everyone'that's too broad. Instead, define by age, gender, location, income, and needs. For example, a hair salon in Johannesburg might target women aged 25-40 who work in offices and earn 15,000-30,000 ZAR per month. They need quick, professional styling before work.

Start by looking at your current customers (if any) or survey 20 people. Ask: How old are you? Where do you live? How much do you earn? What do you buy? Why? Group them into segments. Common segments in African markets include: students, young professionals, mothers, farmers, small business owners, and tourists. Each has different needs and spending power.

Create a customer profile. Give your typical customer a name: 'Chidi, 32, a teacher in Enugu, earns 80,000 NGN per month, has two children, buys in bulk once a month, cares about quality and price.' This helps you make decisions: where to advertise, what price to set, what packaging to use.

Use free tools to find your market. Facebook Audience Insights shows you the age, gender, and interests of people in your area. Google Analytics on a simple website can show who visits. But nothing beats talking to people. Visit markets, churches, mosques, and community meetings. Ask: What products do you wish were available? What do you dislike about current options?

Once you know your target, focus your efforts. If you sell baby clothes, don't waste money advertising to teenagers. Instead, partner with clinics, maternity wards, and parenting groups on WhatsApp. A tailor in Nairobi targeted pregnant women through a local hospital and got 30 orders in one month. Be specific, and your marketing will be cheaper and more effective.

5. Defining Your Value Proposition

Your value proposition is why a customer should buy from you instead of your competitor. It's not just what you sellit's the benefit. For example, a food vendor in Kano doesn't just sell rice; she sells 'hot, home-style meals ready in 5 minutes for busy workers.' That's a value proposition.

Start by listing what makes you different. Use the four Ps: Product, Price, Place, Promotion. Product: Is yours fresher, larger, or more durable? Price: Are you cheaper or offer better value? Place: Are you closer or open longer? Promotion: Do you offer free delivery or a loyalty card? Pick one or two strengths. Trying to be everything to everyone weakens your message.

Test your value proposition with customers. Say: 'We offer fresh bread delivered to your door by 7am for 500 KES.' Ask: Does that appeal to you? Would you pay 500 KES? If they hesitate, adjust. Maybe they want delivery by 6am, or they'd pay 400 KES. A bakery in Mombasa changed its delivery time from 8am to 6am and saw a 40% increase in orders.

Write a value proposition statement. Format: 'For [target customer] who [need], we provide [product/service] that [key benefit]. Unlike [competitor], we [difference].' Example: 'For busy mothers in Lilongwe who need affordable childcare, we provide a daycare centre that offers flexible hours and nutritious meals. Unlike other centres, we charge per hour and are open until 8pm.'

Finally, make sure your value proposition is clear in all your marketing: signboards, WhatsApp status, business cards. A customer should understand it in 5 seconds. If you can't explain it simply, you don't have a strong value proposition yet. Keep refining until it's sharp and compelling.

6. Refining Your Business Concept

Your business concept is a one-page summary of what you do, who you serve, and how you make money. Refining it means making it clearer and more focused. Start by writing a draft in plain language. Example: 'I sell second-hand clothes in Gaborone.' That's too vague. Refine: 'I sell quality second-hand children's clothing (ages 2-10) at affordable prices in Gaborone West, with a buy-one-get-one-free offer on Saturdays.' Now it's specific.

Use the Business Model Canvas, a free tool from Strategyzer. It has 9 boxes: customer segments, value proposition, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners, cost structure. Fill it out for your idea. For a mobile money agent in Uganda: customers are local traders; value proposition is fast, safe cash deposits; channels are a physical kiosk and WhatsApp; revenue is commission per transaction; costs are rent, float, and phone. This forces you to think through all parts.

Next, test your concept with a small pilot. Run it for one week. For example, if you plan to sell grilled maize, set up a stand for 3 days. Track: How many customers? How much did they buy? What did they say? If you sell 100 cobs in 3 days at 500 UGX each, that's 50,000 UGX. If your costs were 30,000 UGX, you made 20,000 UGX. That's a positive signal. If you sold only 20 cobs, refine: change location, price, or product.

Get feedback from 5 potential customers. Ask: What do you like? What would make you buy? What price is fair? Adjust your concept based on their answers. A juice seller in Accra originally offered only orange juice. Customers asked for a mix of pineapple and ginger. She added it and sales doubled.

Finally, write your refined concept in one paragraph. Include: business name, what you sell, target customer, unique benefit, and how you make money. Keep it under 100 words. This becomes the foundation of your business plan. Review it monthlyas you learn more, your concept may evolve.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Defining Your Business Idea

Every viable agribusiness in Africa starts with a clear business idea not a vague aspiration, but a specific answer to three questions: What problem am I solving? For whom? Why am I the right person to solve it? This practical reference consolidates the tools, checklists, and data you need to move from a raw idea to a refined business concept you can test in the market. It is designed to be used alongside the six lessons in this module and revisited as your thinking sharpens.

In African agribusiness, ideas often emerge from real friction: post-harvest losses, unreliable input supply, fragmented market access, or unmet demand for processed food. The strongest concepts are built on observed problems, not imported templates. Use this reference to pressure-test your idea before you invest time, money, or reputation.

1. Brainstorming Business Ideas Africa Context

Start with the value chain around you. In most African economies, agribusiness opportunities cluster in five zones: input supply (seed, fertiliser, agrochemicals), production (crops, livestock, aquaculture), aggregation and logistics, processing and value addition, and market access (retail, export, digital platforms). A useful brainstorming frame is the "problem-first" approach: list ten problems you have personally observed in the last 12 months, then rank them by frequency, severity, and your ability to act.

2. Evaluating Market Opportunities

An opportunity is not the same as an idea. An opportunity is an idea that meets a real, reachable, and paying demand. Evaluate using four filters: size (is the addressable market big enough?), growth (is demand rising?), accessibility (can you reach buyers at a viable cost?), and competition (can you differentiate?). 3. Conducting a Feasibility Analysis

Feasibility is where enthusiasm meets arithmetic. Test four dimensions: technical (can you produce or deliver at the required quality?), financial (do unit economics work at realistic prices?), market (will buyers actually pay?), and organisational (do you have the team, licences, and partners?). The financial test is the most common failure point. Use the table below as a quick feasibility scorecard.

SAFETY WARNING Financial and Legal Risk

Do not commit savings, land, or family assets to an agribusiness idea before completing a feasibility analysis and confirming demand with real buyers. Many African agri-ventures fail because founders skip this step and over-invest in production before securing off-take. Also verify land tenure, cooperative registration, and phytosanitary/export licences before signing contracts informal agreements are often unenforceable. If your idea involves agrochemicals, livestock health products, or export commodities, consult your national regulator (e.g., KEPHIS in Kenya, NAFDAC in Nigeria, or your Ministry of Agriculture) before trading.

4. Identifying Your Target Market

"Everyone" is not a target market. Segment by geography, income, purchase behaviour, and channel. In African agribusiness, common segments include: smallholder farmers (input buyers), urban middle-class consumers (fresh produce and processed foods), institutional buyers (schools, hotels, processors), and export markets (EU, Middle East, intra-African under AfCFTA). Choose one primary segment for your first year, then expand. Define your buyer persona: age, location, income, buying frequency, price sensitivity, and the trigger that makes them buy.

5. Defining Your Value Proposition

Your value proposition is the single sentence that explains why a specific customer chooses you over the alternative. Use this template: "We help [target customer] achieve [outcome] by providing [offer] unlike [alternative], because [proof]." In African agribusiness, strong propositions often rest on reliability, price, proximity, or trust not novelty. Example: "We supply urban restaurants in Nairobi with same-day, traceable leafy greens, unlike open-air markets, because we operate a cold-chain hub within 20 km of the city."

6. Refining Your Business Concept

Refinement is iterative. Write a one-page concept note covering: problem, solution, target market, value proposition, revenue model, key partners, and critical risks. Share it with five potential customers and three industry peers. Their objections are your cheapest form of market research. Revise weekly until the concept survives scrutiny. Only then move to full business plan development.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. I have listed at least 10 real problems observed in my value chain in the last 12 months.
  2. I have confirmed demand with at least 3 potential paying customers (not friends or family).
  3. I have sized my addressable market using credible data, not assumptions.
  4. I have completed a four-dimension feasibility check (technical, financial, market, organisational).
  5. I have defined one primary target segment with a written buyer persona.
  6. I have written a value proposition in one sentence and tested it with 5 buyers.
  7. I have identified all regulatory licences and land/tenure issues relevant to my idea.
  8. I have a one-page concept note that has survived review by customers and peers.
