1. Types of Metals: Steel, Aluminum, and Alloys

In metal packaging, the three main materials you will work with are steel, aluminum, and their alloys. Steel is strong and cheap, made from iron and carbon. Tinplate is steel coated with a thin layer of tin to stop rust. It is used for food cans, paint tins, and bottle caps. Tin-free steel (TFS) uses chromium instead of tin; it is cheaper and good for beer and beverage cans. Aluminum is lightweight, does not rust, and conducts heat well. It is perfect for drink cans, aerosol cans, and foil trays. Alloys mix metals to improve properties. For example, aluminum 3004 is used for can bodies because it is strong and easy to shape. In Africa, you can source steel from local scrap or import from South Africa, Egypt, or Nigeria. Aluminum is recycled locally in many countries. When choosing, consider cost, corrosion resistance, and weight. Steel is heavier but cheaper; aluminum is lighter but costs more. For food, use tinplate or coated steel. For drinks, aluminum is best. Always check the metal grade: for cans, look for 'tinplate' or 'aluminum 3004'. Test with a magnet: steel sticks, aluminum does not. This simple test helps you sort metals at the workshop.

2. Can Manufacturing: Two-Piece and Three-Piece

Can manufacturing has two main methods: two-piece and three-piece. In three-piece cans, you make a body by rolling a rectangular sheet into a cylinder and welding the side seam. Then you attach a top and bottom end. This is common for food cans like tomato paste or corned beef. The body is usually tinplate, and the ends are also tinplate. The weld is done with a copper wire electrode; you must control the current to avoid burn-through. In two-piece cans, you start with a circular blank of aluminum or steel. A press draws it into a cup, then a second draw stretches it into a tall can. There is no side seam, so no leaks. This is how beverage cans are made. The bottom is formed in the same piece. The top end is seamed on later. For a small-scale operation in Africa, three-piece can making is more practical because you can use a manual roller and a spot welder. Two-piece requires expensive deep-drawing presses. However, you can buy pre-formed two-piece cans and fill them. Key tools: sheet metal shears, roller, welder, seamer. For three-piece, cut body blanks to size, roll into cylinder, weld seam, then flange both ends. For two-piece, you need a press. Always test for leaks by filling with water and checking for drips. In Africa, many small canneries use imported two-piece cans and focus on filling and seaming.

3. Coating and Lining Technologies

Metal cans need a coating or lining to protect the food or drink from the metal, and to protect the metal from acids. Without a lining, acidic foods like tomatoes or pineapple will react with the tinplate, causing off-flavors and rust. The most common linings are epoxy, phenolic, and acrylic. Epoxy is used for acidic foods and beverages. Phenolic is for meats and fish. Acrylic is for beer and some fruits. You can apply linings by spraying, roller coating, or electrocoating. In small workshops, spray coating is easiest. First, clean the metal with a solvent like acetone. Then spray a thin, even coat of food-grade epoxy. Cure it in an oven at 200C for 10-15 minutes. The coating must be 5-10 microns thick. Too thin, it fails; too thick, it cracks. For cans, you also need to coat the seams and ends. Always use food-safe coatings that meet FDA or equivalent standards. In Africa, you can buy epoxy resin from chemical suppliers in Kenya, Nigeria, or South Africa. Test the coating by filling the can with a saltwater solution and leaving it for 48 hours. If you see rust or blisters, the coating is bad. Also, check for pinholes by holding the can up to a light. A good lining is smooth and shiny, with no gaps.

4. Ends, Easy-Open Ends, and Seaming

Can ends are critical for sealing and opening. There are two main types: standard ends and easy-open ends (EOE). Standard ends require a can opener. Easy-open ends have a scored line and a tab that you pull to open. The tab is riveted to the end. To make an EOE, you start with a coated aluminum or tinplate sheet. You stamp out a circular blank, then form the curl and the countersink. The score line is made with a sharp die that cuts partway through the metal. The tab is made from a separate piece and attached with a rivet. For small-scale production, you can buy pre-made EOEs and seam them onto your cans. Seaming is the process of attaching the end to the can body. You use a seaming chuck and rolls. The end is placed on the can, then the first operation roll curls the edge of the end around the can flange. The second operation roll tightens the seam. The seam must be airtight and leak-proof. A good seam has a thickness of about 1.2 mm and a width of 2.5 mm. You can check with a seam micrometer. In Africa, manual seaming machines are available for small canneries. Always test seams by immersing filled cans in hot water (80C) for 5 minutes and looking for bubbles. If bubbles appear, the seam is bad. Adjust the rolls and try again.

5. Aerosol and Specialty Metal Packaging

Aerosol cans are used for products like insect spray, paint, deodorant, and cooking spray. They are made of tinplate or aluminum. The can must withstand internal pressure of 3-6 bar. Aerosol cans have a concave bottom for strength and a valve on top. The valve is crimped onto the can. The propellant is a gas like butane or propane, mixed with the product. For small-scale production in Africa, you can buy empty aerosol cans and a filling machine. The filling process: first, the product is put into the can, then the valve is crimped, then the propellant is injected through the valve. You need a pressure tester to check for leaks. Specialty metal packaging includes tubes for toothpaste or ointments, trays for food, and drums for chemicals. Aluminum tubes are made by impact extrusion: a slug of aluminum is punched into a die to form a tube. The tube is then coated inside and outside. For drums, steel is rolled and welded. In Africa, metal drums are often reconditioned and reused for chemicals or water. Always check for corrosion and clean thoroughly. For aerosols, safety is key: never fill near a flame, and use proper ventilation. Test every batch for pressure and leaks. A simple soap bubble test can find leaks: spray soapy water on the valve and look for bubbles.

6. Corrosion and Compatibility Testing

Corrosion is the enemy of metal packaging. It happens when metal reacts with oxygen, water, or acids. For food and drinks, you must test that the product does not corrode the can and that the can does not spoil the product. Common tests include: salt spray test (ASTM B117), which exposes the can to a salt mist for 24-72 hours to see if rust forms. For compatibility, you fill the can with the actual product and store it at 40C for 3 months. Then you check for off-flavors, color changes, and metal dissolution. You can also do a simple test: fill the can with the product, seal it, and leave it at room temperature for 2 weeks. Open it and look for rust, pitting, or a metallic taste. For acidic products like tomato sauce, use a coated can. For salty products, use tinplate with a lacquer. In Africa, you can do a basic corrosion test by burying a can in wet soil for a week; if it rusts, the coating is poor. Always keep records of your tests. If a can fails, check the coating thickness, the seam, and the metal grade. A good can should last at least 2 years on the shelf. Remember: corrosion is faster in hot, humid climates, so store cans in a cool, dry place. Test each new batch of cans and each new product formula.

<h2>Practical Reference: Metal Packaging for African Food &amp; Beverage Processors</h2>

<p>This lesson is a working reference for packaging managers, QA officers, and production supervisors in African food, beverage, and agro-processing operations. Metal packaging steel (tinplate and tin-free steel), aluminium, and their alloys remains the backbone of long-shelf-life products across the continent: tomato paste, canned fish, evaporated milk, fruit juices, carbonated soft drinks, beer, edible oils, and aerosol products. The commercial logic is simple: metal gives you 1836 months of ambient shelf life without a cold chain, which in markets where cold-chain coverage is under 30% is often the difference between a product that can be distributed nationally and one that cannot leave the factory gate.</p>

<p>This reference covers the practical decisions you will actually face: which metal to specify, how cans are made and where they fail, coating selection for aggressive products, seaming tolerances, aerosol specifics, and corrosion testing. Use it alongside your supplier specifications and your own plant trial data.</p>

<h3>1. Types of Metals: Steel, Aluminium, and Alloys</h3>

<p>Three base materials dominate African canning lines. <strong>Tinplate</strong> is low-carbon steel (0.130.30 mm) coated with 1.011.2 g/m tin; the tin is cosmetic and corrosion-inhibiting, not structural. <strong>Tin-free steel (TFS / ECCS)</strong> uses a chromium/chromium-oxide layer instead of tin cheaper, excellent for lacquer adhesion, but cannot be soldered, so it is welded or cemented. <strong>Aluminium</strong> (typically 3104 alloy for can bodies, 5182 for ends) is lightweight, non-rusting, and ideal for carbonated and aerosol products, but it is softer and more expensive per tonne than steel, and it reacts with acidic and salty products unless lacquered.</p>

<p>For African processors, the practical trade-off is usually cost versus corrosion resistance versus recyclability. Steel is cheaper and locally more available in some markets; aluminium is lighter (lower freight per can, relevant for long inland distribution) and has strong scrap value, which supports informal collection networks in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa.</p>

<h3>2. Can Manufacturing: Two-Piece and Three-Piece</h3>

<p><strong>Three-piece cans</strong> are made from a rectangular body blank (welded into a cylinder) plus two ends. They are flexible for low-to-medium volumes and are common in African tomato paste, fish, and evaporated milk operations where batch sizes vary. The welded side seam is the critical control point a poor weld is a leak or a corrosion pathway.</p>

<p><strong>Two-piece cans (DWI drawn and wall-ironed)</strong> are formed from a single disc of aluminium or steel, drawn into a cup and ironed to final wall thickness. There is no side seam, so leak risk is much lower. DWI dominates beverage canning globally and is increasingly the standard for carbonated drinks produced in Nigeria, Kenya, Egypt, and South Africa. The trade-off: high tooling cost and minimum order volumes, which favour large producers.</p>

<p>For a small or mid-sized African processor, the realistic choice is often three-piece welded cans sourced from a regional can maker, or imported two-piece cans where volumes justify it. Local can manufacturing capacity exists in Egypt, Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa; importing empty cans is expensive because you ship air.</p>

<h3>3. Coating and Lining Technologies</h3>

<p>Internal lacquer is what stands between your product and the metal. Choose it by product chemistry, not by price alone.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Epoxy-phenolic:</strong> general-purpose, good for acidic and neutral products, moderate sulphur resistance.</li>
<li><strong>Organosol / vinyl:</strong> for high-acid products (tomato, citrus) and products needing a thicker film.</li>
<li><strong>Polyester:</strong> good for beverages and beer; often used in two-piece DWI cans.</li>
<li><strong>Oleoresin / C-enamel:</strong> specifically for sulphur-bearing proteins (fish, meat) to prevent sulphide staining.</li>
<li><strong>Zinc oxide or aluminium-pigmented lacquer:</strong> for sulphur-staining products and some vegetables.</li>
</ul>

<p>The external coating matters too: base coat plus print plus varnish must survive retort temperatures (121130 C) without blistering, and must resist scuffing in distribution. In hot, humid African conditions, external rust on tinplate cans is a real risk specify a lacquer with good humidity resistance and protect cans from condensation in transit.</p>

<h3>4. Ends, Easy-Open Ends, and Seaming</h3>

<p>Easy-open ends (EOE) are a commercial expectation in retail, especially for beverages, fish, and dairy. They use a scored aluminium or TFS end with a riveted tab. The score depth must be controlled to a fraction of a millimetre: too shallow and the consumer cannot open it; too deep and it leaks or bursts in retort.</p>

<p>Seaming is where most field failures happen. A double seam is formed in two operations (first and second roll) and produces a 5-thickness interlock. Key parameters to monitor every shift:</p>

<h3>5. Aerosol and Specialty Metal Packaging</h3>

<p>Aerosols add pressure and propellant to the equation. Bodies are typically one-piece aluminium or three-piece tinplate, tested to withstand 1218 bar at 55 C. Critical controls: valve crimping, gasket compatibility with the propellant and product, and pressure testing of every batch. In African markets, aerosol insecticide, deodorant, and spray oil production is growing, but regulatory oversight on pressure testing is uneven. If you fill aerosols, invest in a water-bath pressure test and keep records.</p>

<p>Specialty formats include <strong>easy-open foil-lid cans</strong> for dry products, <strong>pails and drums</strong> for edible oils and bulk ingredients, and <strong>metal tubes</strong> for pastes. Each has its own closure and compatibility requirements.</p>

<h3>6. Corrosion and Compatibility Testing</h3>

<p>Never assume a can will work with your product. Run a compatibility trial before commercial production:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Accelerated storage:</strong> incubate filled cans at 37 C and 55 C for 212 weeks and inspect for internal corrosion, lacquer blistering, and product discolouration.</li>
<li><strong>Electrochemical testing:</strong> for high-value or high-risk products, use electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) to measure coating performance.</li>
<li><strong>Sulphide staining test:</strong> for fish and meat, check for black iron sulphide staining at the headspace and seam.</li>
<li><strong>Migration testing:</strong> confirm that lacquer components do not migrate into the food above regulatory limits (e.g. BPA/BADGE limits under EU 10/2011, referenced in many African import standards).</li>
<li><strong>Seam and pressure testing:</strong> confirm the closure survives your retort or pasteurisation process.</li>
<li><strong>Field trial:</strong> ship a batch through your actual distribution route heat, humidity, and handling are the real test.</li>
</ol>

<p>Document every trial. When you scale, your compatibility data is your defence against recalls and your evidence for buyers and regulators.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Confirm substrate (tinplate, TFS, or aluminium) against product acidity, salt, and sulphur content.</li>
<li> Verify internal lacquer type and film weight from the supplier's certificate of analysis.</li>
<li> Check incoming cans for dents, rust, seam damage, and lacquer scratches before filling.</li>
<li> Measure seam thickness, width, body hook, end hook, and overlap at least four times per shift.</li>
<li> Record retort time, temperature, and pressure for every batch; retain records for shelf life.</li>
<li> Run an accelerated compatibility trial (37 C / 55 C) before commercial launch.</li>
<li> Store cans dry, off the floor, away from condensation and direct sun; rotate stock FIFO.</li>
<li> Train at least two operators per shift on seaming inspection and retort record-keeping.</li>
</ul>
