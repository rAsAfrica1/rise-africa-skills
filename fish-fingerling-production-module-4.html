1. Natural vs. Induced Spawning Techniques

Natural spawning is the simplest and cheapest way to get fish to breed. In Africa, many farmers use earthen ponds where tilapia, catfish, or carp spawn on their own when conditions are right. For tilapia, you need water temperatures between 2530C, dissolved oxygen above 3 mg/L, and a pond bottom with sand or gravel for nest building. Stock one male for every two to three females. Within a few weeks, females will mouthbrood eggs. You can collect fry by seining the pond edges with a fine-mesh net (1 mm) every 1014 days. Natural spawning works well for Nile tilapia and North African catfish (Clarias gariepinus) in outdoor ponds, but you cannot control timing or quantity.

Induced spawning gives you control. You trigger breeding by manipulating environment or hormones. Environmental induction: for catfish, you simulate the rainy season by changing water level and temperature. Drain the pond partially, then refill with fresh water (23C cooler) and let it overflow. This mimics floods and often makes catfish spawn within 2448 hours. For tilapia, you can induce spawning by increasing water temperature to 2830C and adding fresh water.

Hormonal induction is the most reliable method for catfish. You inject a hormone (e.g., Ovaprim, or carp pituitary extract) into the females muscle or body cavity. A typical dose for Clarias gariepinus is 0.5 ml per kg of female weight. Males get half that dose. After injection, keep the fish in a clean tank with gentle water flow. Females usually ovulate 1012 hours after the second injection (if using a two-dose protocol).

Which method to choose? If you are a beginner with limited funds, start with natural spawning in ponds. It requires no hormones, no injections, and no special tanks. But if you need large numbers of fry on a schedule, induced spawning is better. You will need a spawning tank (e.g., 5001000 L), aeration, and a source of hormones. In many African countries, Ovaprim is available from aquaculture supply shops, but it can be expensive. Some farmers use local alternatives like dried pituitary glands from donor fish, but this requires practice. Always handle broodfish gently to avoid stress, which can block spawning.

2. Hormonal Induction of Spawning

Hormonal induction is the backbone of commercial catfish fingerling production in Africa. It lets you spawn fish on demand, even outside the natural breeding season. The most common hormone is Ovaprim, a synthetic salmon gonadotropin-releasing hormone. You can also use human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) or carp pituitary extract (CPE). For Clarias gariepinus, the standard dose is 0.5 ml Ovaprim per kg of female body weight. For males, use 0.25 ml per kg. Always inject into the dorsal muscle (above the lateral line) or into the body cavity behind the pelvic fin. Use a 2 ml syringe with a 0.8 x 40 mm needle. Disinfect the injection site with iodine or alcohol before injecting.

A two-dose protocol works best for catfish. First, give a priming dose: 10% of the total dose. Wait 6 hours. Then give the resolving dose: the remaining 90%. This mimics the natural hormone surge. After the second injection, keep the female in a spawning tank with clean, well-aerated water at 2628C. Ovulation usually occurs 1012 hours later. Check for eggs by gently pressing the abdomen near the genital papilla. If eggs flow out easily, the female is ready. Strip the eggs into a dry plastic bowl. Avoid water contact before fertilization.

For males, you can sacrifice the male to collect milt (sperm). Make a cut along the belly, remove the testes, and macerate them in 0.9% saline solution (about 5 ml per testis). Keep the milt solution cool (4C) and use within 30 minutes. Alternatively, some farmers use a live male and gently press the abdomen to release milt directly onto the eggs. But this is less reliable.

Timing is critical. If you leave eggs in the female too long after ovulation, they become overripe and won't fertilize. If you strip too early, eggs won't come out. Practice with a few fish before doing a large batch. Also, hormone quality matters. Store Ovaprim in a refrigerator (28C). Never freeze it. Expired hormone gives poor results. In Africa, you can buy Ovaprim from companies like Syndel or from local aquaculture distributors. If you cannot afford it, learn to make carp pituitary extract: collect pituitary glands from mature carp, dry them in acetone, and store in a cool, dark place. Grind and mix with saline before injection. It works, but dose varies (usually 24 mg per kg female).

3. Egg Collection and Incubation Methods

After spawning, you must collect eggs quickly and move them to an incubator. For catfish, strip the female by holding her head up and gently pressing the abdomen from front to back. Collect eggs into a clean, dry plastic bowl. Do not add water yet. For tilapia, you collect eggs from the mouth of the female. Hold the fish gently, open her mouth, and rinse the eggs into a bowl with clean water. Tilapia eggs are sticky and attach to surfaces.

Fertilization: For catfish, add milt solution to the eggs and mix gently with a feather or plastic spoon for 12 minutes. Then add clean water (about 100 ml per 100 g eggs) and stir for another minute. The eggs will swell and become sticky. For tilapia, fertilization happens in the mouth, so you don't need to add milt. But if you strip eggs from a female that hasn't spawned naturally, you must add milt from a male.

Incubation methods vary by species. Catfish eggs are adhesive and need flowing water. Use an incubation trough or a plastic basin with a mesh bottom. Place eggs in a single layer on the mesh. Set the trough in a larger tank with a gentle flow of water (0.51 L/min). Keep water temperature at 2628C. Eggs hatch in 2436 hours. Tilapia eggs are mouthbrooded, but for artificial incubation, use a funnel-shaped incubator (like a McDonald jar) with upflow water. Or place eggs in a fine-mesh basket suspended in a well-aerated tank. Hatch time is 35 days at 28C.

Water quality is critical. Use clean, dechlorinated water. If you use tap water, let it sit for 24 hours or add a dechlorinator. Maintain dissolved oxygen above 5 mg/L. Remove dead eggs daily with a pipette or siphon, because they grow fungus. For catfish, you can use a simple flow-through system: a 20 L bucket with a hole near the top, a mesh screen inside, and a slow drip of water. Place eggs on the screen. The water flows out the hole. This costs almost nothing. In Africa, many farmers use this method successfully. For tilapia, a 50 L plastic tank with a sponge filter and gentle aeration works well. Always keep incubators shaded to prevent algae growth.

4. Egg Disinfection and Fungal Treatment

Eggs are vulnerable to bacteria and fungi, especially in warm African waters. Fungal infections (like Saprolegnia) appear as white cotton-like patches on dead eggs. These spread fast and can kill the whole batch. Disinfection is not optional if you want high hatch rates. The most common treatment is formalin (37% formaldehyde solution). Use a dose of 0.51 ml formalin per litre of water for a 15-minute bath. Do this once daily until eggs hatch. For catfish, you can also use methylene blue at 23 mg/L as a continuous bath. It turns water blue and prevents fungus. Another option is hydrogen peroxide (3% solution) at 0.5 ml/L for 10 minutes daily. Always use gloves and eye protection when handling formalin.

For tilapia eggs, mouthbrooding females often get fungal infections on their eggs. You can treat the female with a formalin bath (0.5 ml/L for 30 minutes) or use a salt bath (3% NaCl for 5 minutes). But be careful: too much stress can make the female spit out eggs. If you incubate eggs artificially, treat them the same as catfish eggs.

A simple, low-cost method used in many African hatcheries is the salt bath. Dissolve 3 grams of non-iodized salt per litre of water. Soak eggs for 5 minutes. This reduces bacteria and fungus without chemicals. Repeat every 12 hours. Another natural option is tannin from tea leaves. Brew strong black tea (10 g leaves per litre), cool it, and use as a 10-minute bath. The tannins inhibit fungus. However, formalin is more reliable for heavy infections.

Prevention is better than cure. Always remove dead eggs immediately. Dead eggs turn opaque white within a few hours. Use a pipette or a turkey baster to suck them out. Keep water clean: filter it, and change 50% daily. Avoid overcrowding eggs: no more than one layer thick. Maintain water temperature stable (2628C). Fluctuations stress eggs. If you see fungus starting, treat immediately. Do not wait. In Africa, formalin is available from veterinary or aquaculture supply stores. Store it in a sealed container away from children. If you cannot get formalin, use salt baths and daily removal of dead eggs. Many farmers also add a few drops of methylene blue to the incubator water as a preventive measure.

5. Monitoring Embryonic Development

Once eggs are in the incubator, you must check them regularly to know if development is normal. Use a magnifying glass or a simple hand lens (10x). A smartphone camera with zoom can also work. At 2628C, catfish embryos develop quickly. Within 2 hours after fertilization, you can see the first cell division (2 cells). After 4 hours, 4 cells. After 8 hours, a ball of cells (morula). After 12 hours, the embryo starts to form a head and tail. After 24 hours, you can see a beating heart and black eyes. Hatching occurs at 2436 hours. For tilapia, development is slower: first cell division at 3 hours, eyes at 48 hours, hatching at 35 days.

What to look for: Healthy eggs are transparent or slightly amber. They are round and firm. Unfertilized eggs turn opaque white within 612 hours. Dead embryos also turn white and may grow fungus. If you see many white eggs, something is wrong: poor water quality, wrong temperature, or poor fertilization. Check your fertilization technique. Did you add milt properly? Was the male fertile? For catfish, if more than 30% of eggs turn white, consider improving your stripping and fertilization.

Keep a simple record. Write down the time of fertilization, water temperature, and what you see. For example: "10:00 fertilized, 12:00 2-cell stage, 18:00 morula, 08:00 next day: eyes visible." This helps you predict hatching time. If development stops at a certain stage, you can identify the problem. For example, if eggs die at gastrulation (around 10 hours), it might be a temperature shock. If they die at hatching, it could be low oxygen.

Adjust conditions if needed. If water is too warm (above 30C), embryos may develop too fast and hatch weak. If too cold (below 24C), development slows and fungus risk increases. Keep temperature stable. Use a simple thermometer. If you see fungus, treat as described in the previous lesson. Also, check water flow: eggs need gentle movement but not strong currents. For catfish, a slow drip is enough. For tilapia in jars, ensure upflow is not too strong. Observe daily. With practice, you will know within hours whether a batch is healthy. This saves you from wasting time on doomed eggs.

6. Hatching and Larval Emergence

Hatching is the moment larvae break out of the egg shell. For catfish, this happens 2436 hours after fertilization at 2628C. The first sign is the embryo rotating inside the egg. Then the tail breaks free, followed by the head. Newly hatched larvae are tiny (34 mm) and have a yolk sac attached to their belly. They cannot swim well yet. They wriggle and settle on the bottom of the incubator. For tilapia, hatching takes 35 days. Larvae are about 45 mm and also have a yolk sac.

Once hatching starts, stop all water flow for 1015 minutes to let larvae settle. Then gently collect them with a fine-mesh net or a siphon. Do not use a strong current. Move larvae to a nursery tank. A simple 100 L plastic tank works. Fill with clean, dechlorinated water at the same temperature as the incubator (2628C). Add gentle aeration. Do not feed for the first 23 days. The larvae live on their yolk sac. During this time, they are very fragile. Keep water clean: change 20% daily, siphoning out waste. Avoid handling them.

After yolk absorption (23 days for catfish, 45 days for tilapia), larvae become free-swimming and need food. Start with live feed: rotifers, moina, or newly hatched brine shrimp (Artemia). In Africa, you can culture moina in a small pond with cow dung. Or buy Artemia cysts and hatch them yourself: add 1 g cysts per litre of salt water (30 g/L), aerate for 24 hours, and collect the orange nauplii. Feed small amounts 46 times daily. Do not overfeed, as uneaten food fouls the water.

Survival rates vary. With good management, you can get 7080% hatch from fertilized eggs. But larval survival to 10 days may be only 5060%. The main killers are poor water quality, sudden temperature changes, and starvation. Keep temperature stable. Use a heater if needed, but in most African climates, ambient temperature is fine. If you see larvae dying, check ammonia and nitrite. Do a 50% water change. Also, avoid direct sunlight, which can overheat the tank. After 1014 days, larvae become fry and can be moved to larger nursery ponds. At this stage, they can eat finely ground fish feed. But that is the next module.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Spawning and Egg Handling for African Fish Fingerling Enterprises

This practical reference consolidates the operational knowledge required to move from broodstock conditioning to the point where hatchlings are ready for nursing. It is written for commercial fingerling producers in Africa who must deliver predictable volumes of quality fry to tilapia, catfish, and carp grow-out farms. Every technique below is assessed for its effect on survival rate, labour cost, and marketable output.

In African hatcheries, the difference between profit and loss is often decided in the first 72 hours after spawning. Egg quality, water temperature stability, and handling hygiene determine whether a batch yields 60% hatch or 90% hatch. A 30% improvement in hatch rate on a 100,000-egg batch is worth roughly 30,000 extra fingerlings at USD 0.050.15 per fingerling, that is USD 1,5004,500 of additional revenue per cycle. The procedures below are designed to protect that margin.

1. Natural vs. Induced Spawning: Choosing the Right Method

Natural spawning relies on environmental cues temperature, photoperiod, substrate, and water flow to trigger reproduction. It is the default for Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) in hapas and ponds across East and West Africa. Induced spawning uses hormonal or physical intervention to synchronise and increase egg output, and is standard for African catfish (Clarias gariepinus) and for tilapia when seed demand is high and timing must be controlled.

The business decision is straightforward: natural spawning has lower input costs but unpredictable timing and lower egg density per female. Induced spawning requires hormone, syringes, and trained staff, but delivers 310 times more eggs per female catfish and allows batch scheduling that matches customer orders. For a hatchery selling 50,000 fingerlings per month, induced spawning is usually the only way to meet contractual delivery dates.

2. Hormonal Induction of Spawning

For African catfish, the standard protocol uses either carp pituitary extract (CPE) or a synthetic GnRH analogue such as Ovaprim. The female receives a single intramuscular injection at 0.5 ml/kg body weight; the male receives a half dose to increase milt volume. Latency is 1012 hours at 2729 C. Below 25 C, latency extends and egg quality falls; above 31 C, egg mortality rises sharply.

In tilapia, hormonal induction is less common because natural spawning in hapas is efficient, but where all-male fry are required, sex-reversal protocols begin with synchronised spawning. For Oreochromis species, 17-methyltestosterone is added to feed after first feeding, not to the spawning tank a distinction that matters for food safety compliance and export certification.

3. Egg Collection and Incubation Methods

Egg collection must be fast and gentle. Catfish eggs are adhesive and clump quickly; leaving them in the spawning tank reduces fertilisation and increases fungal risk. Strip eggs into a clean dry plastic bowl, add milt, mix for 6090 seconds, then add 200300 ml of clean water to activate sperm. After 23 minutes, rinse and transfer to incubation.

Incubation systems in African hatcheries fall into three categories: flow-through troughs, Zug jars, and improvised basins with aeration. Flow-through troughs are the most reliable for catfish because they keep eggs moving and oxygenated. Zug jars work well for non-adhesive eggs but require careful flow calibration. Basins with airstones are low-cost but prone to dead spots and fungal blooms acceptable for small volumes but not for commercial contracts.

Water quality during incubation is non-negotiable. Dissolved oxygen must stay above 5 mg/L, ammonia below 0.05 mg/L, and temperature within 1 C of target. In many African hatcheries, the biggest cause of poor hatch is not disease but temperature fluctuation between day and night. Insulating tanks or using a simple solar-heated water reservoir can stabilise temperature and lift hatch rates by 1020%.

SAFETY WARNING HORMONE AND CHEMICAL HANDLING

Ovaprim, carp pituitary extract, formalin, and methyltestosterone are hazardous substances. Always wear nitrile gloves, eye protection, and a lab coat or apron when handling. Never eat, drink, or smoke in the hatchery. Store hormones refrigerated at 28 C, locked away from feed and food. Formalin is a suspected carcinogen use only in a ventilated area and never dispose of treated water into natural water bodies without neutralisation. Methyltestosterone is a controlled steroid; in many African countries its use requires veterinary prescription and must be documented for export certification. Pregnant staff must not handle hormones or formalin. Keep a written SOP and a first-aid kit on site.

4. Egg Disinfection and Fungal Treatment

Fungal infection (Saprolegnia spp.) is the single largest cause of egg loss in African catfish hatcheries. The fungus colonises dead eggs first, then spreads to healthy ones. Disinfection within 1015 minutes of fertilisation reduces the initial load; daily treatment during incubation controls spread.

Two treatments are widely used and affordable in Africa:

  • Salt bath: 3% NaCl (30 g/L) for 510 minutes daily. Cheap, safe, and effective for mild infections. Do not exceed 10 minutes or eggs may dehydrate.
  • Formalin: 100200 ppm (0.10.2 ml/L of 37% formalin) for 1530 minutes daily. More effective against established fungus but requires careful dosing and ventilation.

Remove dead eggs manually with a siphon or pipette whenever possible. Dead eggs turn opaque white within 24 hours; removing them reduces fungal substrate and improves water flow around live eggs. In flow-through troughs, a daily 10-minute inspection and dead-egg removal is the highest-return labour task in the hatchery.

5. Monitoring Embryonic Development

Embryonic development is temperature-dependent. At 2729 C, catfish eggs hatch in 1824 hours; tilapia eggs hatch in 35 days. A simple microscope or hand lens allows daily staging:

  • 02 h: Fertilised eggs show a clear perivitelline space and cell division.
  • 46 h: Morula and blastula stages; unfertilised eggs remain translucent and fail to divide.
  • 812 h: Gastrulation and early embryo; eye spots appear around 1416 h in catfish.
  • 1620 h: Embryo movements visible; heart beating.
  • 1824 h: Hatching begins; larvae emerge tail-first.

Record hatch time, temperature, and survival for every batch. Over 36 cycles, this data lets you predict output and schedule sales with confidence a critical advantage when supplying grow-out farms that plan stocking weeks in advance.

6. Hatching and Larval Emergence

Newly hatched catfish larvae are about 34 mm long and live on yolk for 23 days. Do not feed them during this period. Transfer them gently to a nursery tank with clean, well-oxygenated water at 2729 C. Stocking density for yolk-sac larvae should not exceed 50,000100,000 per m in flow-through systems. Once the yolk is absorbed and the swim bladder inflates, begin feeding with Artemia nauplii or a fine starter diet.

For tilapia, fry are usually left with the female in hapas until they are free-swimming, then collected and transferred to nursery ponds or tanks. Sex reversal, if required, begins at first feeding and continues for 2128 days.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Select broodstock at least 0.8 kg (catfish) or 150 g (tilapia), free of lesions and with visible maturity.
  2. Inject females in the evening; expect spawning 1012 hours later at 2729 C.
  3. Strip eggs into a dry bowl, add milt, mix 6090 seconds, then activate with clean water.
  4. Disinfect eggs within 15 minutes using 3% salt bath or 100200 ppm formalin.
  5. Incubate in flow-through troughs at 25 kg eggs/m with DO above 5 mg/L.
  6. Remove dead eggs daily and treat for fungus once per day.
  7. Monitor temperature every 4 hours; keep within 1 C of target.
  8. Transfer larvae to nursery 1824 hours post-hatch (catfish) or after yolk absorption (tilapia).
  9. Record batch data: egg count, fertilisation rate, hatch rate, and survival to first feeding.

Bottom line for African hatchery operators: spawning and egg handling is where technical precision directly converts to revenue. Invest in temperature control, hygiene, and record-keeping before investing in more broodstock. A hatchery that reliably hatches 80% of good eggs will outsell one that hatches 50% of twice as many eggs and it will do so with lower feed, labour, and chemical costs.
