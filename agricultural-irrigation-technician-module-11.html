1. Drip Irrigation System Design and Installation for African Smallholder Farms

Drip irrigation is one of the most efficient water delivery methods available to African farmers, achieving water use efficiency of 85 to 95 percent compared to 40 to 60 percent for furrow irrigation. As an irrigation technician, your ability to design and install drip systems correctly determines whether a farmer earns a return on investment or loses money on failed infrastructure. This lesson covers the complete workflow from field assessment to commissioning, with emphasis on conditions common across sub-Saharan Africa. Start with a site assessment. Measure the field dimensions using a tape or GPS app. Record the water source: borehole yield in litres per hour, river flow during dry season, or tank capacity. Test water quality for suspended solids, iron, and salinity, because drip emitters clog easily. A simple field test involves filling a clear bottle with source water and letting it settle for two hours; visible sediment means you need filtration beyond a standard screen filter. Measure the elevation difference across the field using a dumpy level or a water-filled hose with clear ends. A 10 metre elevation rise costs roughly 1 bar of pressure, which directly affects emitter performance. Next, determine crop water requirements. Use local reference evapotranspiration data, typically 4 to 7 mm per day in East Africa and 5 to 8 mm per day in the Sahel. Multiply by the crop coefficient, which ranges from 0.4 for young seedlings to 1.15 for maize at tasselling. For a one hectare tomato field at 6 mm per day, that equals 60 cubic metres per day, or 60,000 litres. If the farmer irrigates for four hours daily, the system must deliver 15,000 litres per hour, which is 15 cubic metres per hour. This flow rate drives every downstream decision. Select emitters based on soil type and crop spacing. Sandy soils need higher flow rates, 2 to 4 litres per hour, because water moves vertically and laterally slowly. Clay soils can use 1 to 2 litres per hour because water spreads widely. For row crops like maize and tomatoes, use inline drip tape with emitters every 30 centimetres. For tree crops like mango or citrus, use pressure-compensating drippers at 4 to 8 litres per hour, one or two per tree. Pressure-compensating emitters maintain uniform flow across a pressure range of 0.5 to 4 bar, which is essential on undulating African terrain. Calculate the number of emitters per hectare. For tomatoes at 1.2 metre row spacing and 0.4 metre plant spacing, you have roughly 20,800 plants per hectare. One emitter per plant gives 20,800 emitters. At 2 litres per hour each, total demand is 41,600 litres per hour, which exceeds the example above. This means you must either zone the field into blocks or use lower flow emitters. Zoning is the practical solution. Divide the field into four blocks of 0.25 hectares each. Irrigate one block at a time, so the pump only needs to supply 10,400 litres per hour. This reduces pump size and cost. Design the pipe network. Mainlines carry water from the pump or tank to the field. Use 50 mm or 63 mm PVC or HDPE pipe for mains. Sub-mains distribute to zones, typically 32 mm or 40 mm. Laterals are the drip lines themselves, usually 16 mm diameter. Size pipes to keep friction loss below 0.5 bar across the system. A simple rule: for 10,000 litres per hour in a 50 mm pipe, friction loss is about 0.3 bar per 100 metres. For 5,000 litres per hour, it drops to 0.1 bar per 100 metres. Always add a filter, a pressure gauge, and a valve at the head of each zone. Filtration is non-negotiable. Use a sand filter for river or dam water, followed by a screen filter of 120 mesh for drip. For borehole water with low sediment, a single 120 mesh screen filter suffices. Install a bypass so the farmer can flush the filter without shutting down the system. Include a chemical injection port for fertigation, which allows the farmer to apply soluble fertiliser through the drip lines, saving labour and improving nutrient use efficiency by 30 to 50 percent. Installation sequence matters. First, mark the mainline trench and lay pipe. Second, install the pump, filter, and valves at the head. Third, flush the mainline before connecting laterals. Fourth, connect laterals to sub-mains using barbed fittings or grommets. Fifth, flush each lateral for five minutes before closing end caps. Sixth, pressure test the entire system at 1.5 times working pressure for one hour. Seventh, install emitters if they are not pre-installed. Commissioning involves measuring flow at the first and last emitter on each lateral. Flow variation should be within 10 percent. If variation exceeds this, check for clogged emitters, leaks, or excessive friction loss. Train the farmer on daily filter cleaning, weekly lateral flushing, and monthly chlorination if organic matter is present. A well-designed drip system costs between 1,500 and 3,000 USD per hectare in East Africa, and pays back in one to two seasons through increased yields and reduced labour. Your role is to deliver that return, not just pipes and emitters.

2. Solar-Powered Pumping Systems: Selection, Sizing, and Maintenance

Solar-powered irrigation pumps have transformed water access across Africa, where diesel costs can consume 40 percent of a farmer's gross income and grid electricity is often unavailable or unreliable. As a technician, you must be able to size, install, and maintain solar pumping systems that deliver reliable water for five to ten years. This lesson gives you the practical steps. Begin with the water requirement. Calculate daily water demand in cubic metres. For a one hectare vegetable plot in Kenya or Tanzania, demand is typically 40 to 70 cubic metres per day during peak season. Convert this to a flow rate based on pumping hours. Solar pumps work best between 9 am and 3 pm, giving about six peak hours. If demand is 60 cubic metres per day, the pump must deliver 10 cubic metres per hour, which is 10,000 litres per hour. Next, determine total dynamic head, or TDH. TDH equals static water level plus drawdown plus elevation rise plus friction loss. Static water level is the distance from ground level to the water surface when the pump is off. Drawdown is the drop in water level when the pump runs, typically 1 to 5 metres for boreholes and 0.5 to 2 metres for open wells. Elevation rise is the vertical distance from the water source to the highest point of discharge. Friction loss depends on pipe length and diameter. For a 50 mm pipe carrying 10,000 litres per hour, friction loss is about 0.3 bar per 100 metres, which equals 3 metres of head per 100 metres. Add all these values. For example, static level 20 metres, drawdown 3 metres, elevation rise 10 metres, pipe length 200 metres with friction loss of 6 metres, total TDH equals 39 metres. Now select the pump. Solar pump manufacturers provide performance curves showing flow rate versus head for different solar array sizes. You need a pump that delivers 10,000 litres per hour at 39 metres head. This typically requires a 1.5 to 2.2 kilowatt solar array, or about 6 to 8 panels of 330 watts each. Use a pump with a brushless DC motor for efficiency and low maintenance. Submersible pumps are best for boreholes deeper than 10 metres. Surface pumps work for shallow wells and rivers but lose suction above 7 metres. Choose a pump controller with maximum power point tracking, or MPPT, which extracts 20 to 30 percent more power from the solar array than a simple direct connection. The controller also provides dry-run protection, which shuts down the pump when water level drops, preventing motor burnout. Install the solar array facing the equator: south in the northern hemisphere, north in the southern hemisphere. Tilt angle should equal the site latitude for year-round performance. In Kenya, latitude is near zero, so a tilt of 10 degrees is sufficient. In South Africa, latitude is 30 degrees south, so tilt panels 30 degrees north. Use galvanised steel or aluminium mounting frames that withstand wind loads of 120 kilometres per hour. Bury cables in conduit or use UV-resistant cable. Connect panels in series to match the controller input voltage, typically 100 to 400 volts DC. Add a DC circuit breaker and a surge protector. For the water side, install a float switch in the tank to stop the pump when the tank is full. This prevents overflow and saves water. Install a non-return valve to prevent backflow. Use a pressure tank if the system feeds a pressurised drip or sprinkler network. Maintenance is straightforward but must be regular. Clean panels weekly with a soft cloth and water. Dust reduces output by 10 to 25 percent in dry season. Check panel mounting bolts every month. Inspect cables for rodent damage every three months. Clean the pump intake screen monthly. Test the controller for error codes monthly. Replace the pump motor brushes every two years if applicable, though brushless motors need no brush replacement. Check the float switch quarterly. Keep a logbook of daily water output, because a gradual decline indicates clogging or panel degradation. Common failures include dry running, which burns the motor; lightning damage, which destroys the controller; and theft of panels. Mitigate theft by mounting panels on a high roof or inside a fenced enclosure with a lock. Use anti-theft bolts. For lightning, install a surge arrestor and a good earth ground. For dry running, ensure the water source has adequate recharge and install a low-level sensor. Solar pumps cost between 2,000 and 6,000 USD for a complete system, compared to 1,000 to 3,000 USD for a diesel pump. But solar has zero fuel cost and lower maintenance, so payback is typically 18 to 30 months. A farmer irrigating one hectare of tomatoes can earn an additional 3,000 to 5,000 USD per year with reliable water. Your job is to make that reliability real through correct sizing and disciplined maintenance.

3. Furrow and Basin Irrigation: Improving Traditional Methods for Higher Efficiency

Furrow and basin irrigation remain the most common methods in Africa because they require low capital and fit smallholder budgets. However, traditional practices waste 40 to 60 percent of applied water through deep percolation, runoff, and uneven distribution. As a technician, you can improve these systems with simple, low-cost changes that raise efficiency to 60 or 70 percent and increase yields by 20 to 40 percent. This lesson shows you how. Start with soil infiltration rate. Sandy soils absorb water at 20 to 30 millimetres per hour. Clay soils absorb at 5 to 10 millimetres per hour. Loam soils absorb at 10 to 20 millimetres per hour. This rate determines furrow length and flow rate. For sandy soil, use short furrows of 30 to 50 metres with high flow of 2 to 3 litres per second. For clay soil, use long furrows of 100 to 150 metres with low flow of 0.5 to 1 litre per second. For loam, use 60 to 100 metres with 1 to 2 litres per second. Measure infiltration with a simple ring infiltrometer: a metal ring 30 centimetres diameter driven 10 centimetres into soil, filled with water, and timed as the water level drops. Record millimetres per hour. Next, design the furrow layout. Furrows should run down the slope, not across it, to avoid overtopping. Maximum slope for furrows is 2 percent, or 2 centimetres per metre. On steeper land, use contour furrows or switch to basin irrigation. Furrow spacing depends on crop. For maize, 75 centimetres between furrows. For tomatoes, 60 to 90 centimetres. For onions, 30 to 40 centimetres. The furrow should be 15 to 25 centimetres deep and 20 to 30 centimetres wide at the top. Construct furrows with a hand hoe, ox-drawn ridger, or tractor. For basin irrigation, level the basin to within 2 centimetres. Basins are best for rice, vegetables, and tree crops. Sizes range from 1 metre by 1 metre for vegetables to 5 metres by 5 metres for trees. Build bunds 20 to 30 centimetres high and 30 centimetres wide. Compact the bunds to prevent leakage. Water application is where most farmers fail. They open the gate and let water run until the field is flooded, which causes runoff at the top and deep percolation at the bottom. Instead, use cutback irrigation. Apply water at a high rate for the first quarter of the furrow, then reduce flow by half for the remaining time. This matches infiltration and reduces runoff by 30 to 50 percent. For a 100 metre furrow on loam soil, apply 2 litres per second for 20 minutes, then 1 litre per second for 40 minutes. Total water applied is 2 times 20 times 60 plus 1 times 40 times 60, which equals 2,400 plus 2,400, or 4,800 litres per furrow. This delivers about 60 millimetres of water over the furrow area. Use a simple siphon tube from a head ditch to control flow. A 50 millimetre diameter plastic pipe 1 metre long works well. Place one siphon per furrow. Adjust the number of siphons to match the flow rate. For 2 litres per second, use two siphons of 50 millimetres each, because each siphon delivers about 1 litre per second. Measure flow with a bucket and stopwatch. Fill a 20 litre bucket and record the time. If it takes 10 seconds, flow is 2 litres per second. Train farmers to do this weekly. Irrigation scheduling is critical. Do not irrigate by calendar. Use soil moisture. A simple method is the feel test. Dig 15 centimetres into the soil. Squeeze a handful. If it forms a ball that holds together, moisture is adequate. If it crumbles, irrigate. If it drips water, stop irrigating. For more precision, use a tensiometer. Install at 15 and 30 centimetres depth. Irrigate when the reading reaches 40 to 50 centibars for vegetables, 50 to 60 for maize. Tensiometers cost 50 to 100 USD each and last three to five years. They pay back in one season through water savings. Maintenance of furrow and basin systems is simple but essential. Repair bunds after every irrigation. Remove sediment from furrows. Keep head ditches clean. Control weeds on bunds. For basin systems, re-level every two seasons. For furrow systems, reshape furrows every season. A well-maintained furrow system can achieve 60 to 70 percent efficiency, compared to 30 to 40 percent for neglected systems. The cost of improvement is mostly labour, not materials. A farmer with one hectare can improve furrows in two days of work. The yield increase from proper irrigation scheduling and cutback flow is 20 to 40 percent for maize, 30 to 50 percent for vegetables. For a farmer earning 2,000 USD per hectare from maize, that is an extra 400 to 800 USD per season. Your role is to demonstrate these techniques on a small plot, measure the results, and let the farmer see the difference. Seeing is believing.

4. Water Source Assessment and Pump Selection for African Farms

Before you install any irrigation system, you must assess the water source and select a pump that matches both the source and the field demand. Many irrigation failures in Africa come from mismatched pumps: too small, the crop suffers; too large, the pump cycles on and off, burns out, or drains the source. This lesson gives you a systematic method for assessment and selection. Start with source type. Common sources are boreholes, hand-dug wells, rivers, streams, dams, and tanks. Each has different characteristics. Boreholes are drilled, typically 50 to 150 metres deep, with a casing diameter of 100 to 200 millimetres. Yield is measured in litres per hour and tested by pumping for four to eight hours while measuring water level drawdown. A safe yield is the flow rate at which drawdown stabilises. If water level continues to drop, the borehole is over-pumped. Hand-dug wells are 5 to 20 metres deep, 1 to 2 metres diameter, and yield 1,000 to 10,000 litres per hour. Rivers and streams vary seasonally; dry season flow may be 10 percent of wet season flow. Dams store water but lose 1 to 2 metres per year to evaporation in hot climates. Tanks store pumped water and allow the pump to run at optimum flow for fewer hours. Measure source yield accurately. For a borehole, use a calibrated bucket and stopwatch at the discharge. For a well, bail or pump for one hour and measure recovery rate. For a river, measure cross-sectional area and velocity. Area equals width times average depth. Velocity is measured by timing a floating object over 10 metres. Flow equals area times velocity times 0.8 for a rough channel. For example, width 3 metres, average depth 0.5 metres, area 1.5 square metres. Time for float over 10 metres is 20 seconds, so velocity is 0.5 metres per second. Flow equals 1.5 times 0.5 times 0.8, which is 0.6 cubic metres per second, or 600 litres per second. That is a large river. Most farm sources are much smaller. Next, assess water quality. Test for salinity, measured as electrical conductivity in decisiemens per metre. Below 0.7 is safe for all crops. 0.7 to 3.0 requires salt-tolerant crops and leaching. Above 3.0 is unsuitable for most crops. Test for iron and manganese, which clog drip emitters. Iron above 0.5 milligrams per litre requires aeration and filtration. Test for pH; 6.5 to 8.0 is acceptable. Test for suspended solids by filtering one litre through a coffee filter and drying the residue. More than 50 milligrams per litre requires a sand filter. Now select the pump. There are three main types: centrifugal, submersible, and helical rotor. Centrifugal pumps are surface-mounted, good for shallow wells and rivers, with suction lift up to 7 metres. They deliver high flow at low head. Submersible pumps are placed inside the borehole or well, good for deep sources, and deliver high head. Helical rotor pumps are positive displacement, good for low flow and high head, and handle sandy water better than centrifugal pumps. Choose based on TDH and flow. Calculate TDH as static level plus drawdown plus elevation rise plus friction loss. Then use pump curves. A pump curve shows flow on the horizontal axis and head on the vertical axis. The operating point is where the system curve intersects the pump curve. The system curve starts at static head and rises with flow due to friction. For a borehole with static level 30 metres, drawdown 5 metres, elevation rise 5 metres, and friction loss 5 metres at design flow, TDH is 45 metres. If you need 10,000 litres per hour, find a pump that delivers 10,000 litres per hour at 45 metres. That is typically a 2.2 kilowatt submersible pump. Check the pump efficiency. Good pumps are 50 to 70 percent efficient. Efficiency equals water power divided by shaft power. Water power in kilowatts equals flow in litres per second times head in metres times 9.81 divided by 1000. For 10,000 litres per hour, which is 2.78 litres per second, and 45 metres head, water power equals 2.78 times 45 times 9.81 divided by 1000, which is 1.23 kilowatts. If the pump is 60 percent efficient, shaft power is 1.23 divided by 0.6, or 2.05 kilowatts. Add 10 percent for motor losses, so 2.25 kilowatts. A 2.2 kilowatt motor is marginal; choose a 3 kilowatt motor for reliability. Match the pump to the power source. Diesel engines are robust but need fuel and maintenance. Electric motors need grid or solar power. Solar is best for remote sites with good sunshine. For solar, use a brushless DC pump with an MPPT controller. For grid, use an AC pump with a starter or variable frequency drive. Install protection devices: dry-run protection, over-voltage protection, and lightning surge protection. Dry-run protection uses a level sensor or a flow switch. Over-voltage protection uses a voltage relay. Lightning protection uses a surge arrestor and a good earth ground. Maintenance schedule: check pump intake monthly, check pressure and flow monthly, check electrical connections quarterly, service diesel engines every 250 hours, replace solar controller batteries every three to five years if applicable. Keep a logbook. A well-selected and maintained pump lasts 8 to 15 years. A poorly selected pump fails in 1 to 2 years. The difference is your skill.

5. Irrigation Scheduling and Water Management for Maximum Profit

Irrigation scheduling is the decision of when to irrigate and how much water to apply. It is the single most important factor in profitability for African irrigated farms, because water is often the limiting resource and energy costs are high. A farmer who irrigates by calendar wastes 30 to 50 percent of water and may reduce yields through waterlogging or drought stress. This lesson teaches you practical scheduling methods that any farmer can use. Start with crop water requirement. Reference evapotranspiration, or ET0, is the water lost from a well-watered grass surface. It ranges from 3 to 5 millimetres per day in cool highlands to 6 to 9 millimetres per day in hot lowlands. You can get ET0 from a local weather station, a simple evaporation pan, or an online service like the FAO AquaCrop or a national meteorological app. If no data is available, use a Class A pan. Place the pan in an open area, fill with water, and measure the drop every 24 hours. ET0 equals pan evaporation times a pan coefficient, typically 0.7 to 0.8. For example, if pan evaporation is 8 millimetres per day, ET0 is 8 times 0.75, or 6 millimetres per day. Multiply ET0 by the crop coefficient, Kc, to get crop water requirement. Kc varies by crop and growth stage. For maize: 0.3 at emergence, 0.7 at knee height, 1.15 at tasselling, 0.9 at grain fill, 0.5 at maturity. For tomatoes: 0.4 at transplant, 0.8 at flowering, 1.15 at fruit set, 0.9 at harvest. For onions: 0.4 at emergence, 0.7 at bulb formation, 1.0 at bulbing, 0.6 at maturity. For a maize field at tasselling with ET0 of 6 millimetres per day, crop water requirement is 6 times 1.15, or 6.9 millimetres per day. That is 69 cubic metres per hectare per day, or 69,000 litres. If the farmer irrigates every three days, apply 207 cubic metres per hectare, which is 207,000 litres. But do not apply all at once if the soil cannot hold it. Check soil water holding capacity. Sandy soil holds 50 to 80 millimetres per metre depth. Loam holds 150 to 200 millimetres per metre. Clay holds 200 to 250 millimetres per metre. If root depth is 0.5 metres, sandy soil holds 25 to 40 millimetres, loam holds 75 to 100 millimetres. Applying 207 millimetres to sandy soil with 40 millimetres capacity loses 167 millimetres to deep percolation. That is waste. So irrigate more frequently on sandy soil: every day or every two days. On loam, every three to five days. On clay, every five to seven days. Use the feel test or a tensiometer to confirm. A tensiometer at 15 centimetres depth reads soil moisture tension. Irrigate when tension reaches 40 to 50 centibars for vegetables, 50 to 60 for maize, 60 to 70 for cotton. Install two tensiometers per field: one at 15 centimetres, one at 30 centimetres. Read them every morning. Record readings in a logbook. After one season, you will see patterns and can predict irrigation needs. Another practical method is the water balance method. Track rainfall and irrigation inputs and ET0 outputs. Soil moisture equals previous moisture plus rainfall plus irrigation minus ET0. If soil moisture drops below 50 percent of available water, irrigate. Available water is the difference between field capacity and permanent wilting point. For loam, field capacity is 30 percent by volume, wilting point is 15 percent, so available water is 15 percent. For a root depth of 0.5 metres, available water is 15 percent times 500 millimetres, or 75 millimetres. Irrigate when 50 percent is used, which is 37.5 millimetres. Apply 37.5 millimetres, which is 375 cubic metres per hectare, or 375,000 litres. Measure irrigation application accurately. For drip, check emitter flow and number of emitters. For sprinkler, use catch cans. Place 10 cans in a grid, run the system for 30 minutes, measure average depth. For furrow, use a siphon and a bucket to measure flow, and time the application. Apply only what the soil can hold. Over-irrigation leaches nutrients, wastes energy, and can waterlog roots. Under-irrigation stresses crops and reduces yield. The profit difference is large. A farmer with one hectare of tomatoes using proper scheduling can save 30 percent of water and increase yield by 20 percent. At a tomato price of 0.5 USD per kilogram and a yield of 20,000 kilograms per hectare, a 20 percent increase is 4,000 kilograms, worth 2,000 USD. Water savings of 30 percent on 6,000 cubic metres per season is 1,800 cubic metres, worth 0.10 USD per cubic metre in pumping cost, or 180 USD. Total benefit is 2,180 USD per hectare per season. Your role is to train farmers to keep simple records: date, crop stage, ET0, rainfall, irrigation amount, tensiometer reading. After one season, they will make better decisions than any calendar. Teach them to look at the soil, the plant, and the sky, and to trust measurements over habit. That is the foundation of profitable irrigation in Africa.

<h2>Practical Reference: Irrigation System Maintenance, Troubleshooting, and Water Safety for African Farms</h2>

<p>This practical reference lesson consolidates the field knowledge an Agricultural Irrigation Technician needs to keep water flowing profitably across an African smallholder or commercial farm. Irrigation is a business input, not a hobby: every blocked emitter, leaking joint, or contaminated water source is money leaking out of the farm enterprise. A well-maintained system can raise water-use efficiency by 3050% and extend pump life by years, directly improving gross margins per hectare. This module gives you the numbers, the checks, and the safety rules to protect both your crop and your customers.</p>

<h3>Why Maintenance Is a Business Decision</h3>
<p>Across sub-Saharan Africa, irrigation schemes frequently underperform not because of poor design but because of neglected maintenance. A drip line with 20% clogged emitters delivers 20% less water to the root zone, stressing crops during critical growth stages and cutting yields. For a one-hectare tomato crop in Kenya or Nigeria, a 15% yield loss at market price can mean hundreds of US dollars lost per season far more than the cost of the filters and labour needed to prevent it. Treat maintenance as a revenue-protection activity, scheduled on the farm calendar just like planting and fertiliser application.</p>

<h3>Water Source and Water Quality: The Foundation</h3>
<p>Before any pump or pipe is sized, the water source must be assessed for quantity, reliability, and quality. Surface water (rivers, dams, canals) and shallow groundwater are the most common sources for African smallholders, but both carry sediment, pathogens, and sometimes salinity. Sediment destroys pumps and clogs emitters; pathogens create food-safety and human-health risks when water contacts edible crops. Always test water at the start of the season and after heavy rains.</p>

<h3>Pump and Power System Checks</h3>
<p>Pumps fail for predictable reasons: cavitation from a blocked intake, dry running, worn impellers, or incorrect voltage on solar systems. For solar-powered pumping, the most common fault is undersized array relative to pump head, causing low flow in cloudy weather. Track daily flow rate against the design figure a 10% drop over a week signals a problem. Keep a simple logbook: date, pump hours, flow rate, fuel or solar yield, and any noise or vibration.</p>

<h3>Troubleshooting Common Faults</h3>
<p><strong>Low pressure at the last emitters:</strong> usually caused by a clogged filter, a partially closed valve, or a leak upstream. Fix filters first, then walk the line.</p>
<p><strong>Uneven crop growth along a drip row:</strong> indicates emitter clogging or pressure variation. Flush and treat with chlorine or acid as appropriate.</p>
<p><strong>Pump runs but no water:</strong> check the intake screen, foot valve, and water level. Dry running destroys seals within minutes.</p>
<p><strong>Solar pump weak in the afternoon:</strong> check panel tilt, dust, and shading; verify controller settings and battery state if hybrid.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Test water quality (EC, pH, coliforms) at the start of each season and after floods.</li>
<li>Backwash sand/screen filters weekly; flush drip laterals monthly.</li>
<li>Log pump hours, flow rate, and any unusual noise or vibration daily.</li>
<li>Check solar panels for dust and shading every week during the dry season.</li>
<li>Inspect valves, joints, and emitters for leaks or clogging every two months.</li>
<li>Wear PPE when handling chlorine, acids, or chemigation chemicals.</li>
<li>Keep a spare set of seals, emitters, and a filter element on the farm.</li>
<li>Train at least two workers on emergency shutdown and first aid.</li>
</ul>
