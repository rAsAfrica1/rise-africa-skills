1. Why Culling Matters on African Layer Farms

Culling is the deliberate removal of unproductive, sick, injured or uneconomic birds from your flock. On a commercial layer farm in Africa, culling is not a cruel afterthought. It is a financial discipline. Every bird in the house eats feed, drinks water, occupies space, uses labour and consumes medication. If that bird is not laying, it is a cost centre. In a business where feed can be 65 to 75 percent of total production cost, carrying dead weight is one of the fastest ways to destroy margin. Consider a simple calculation. A layer eats roughly 110 to 120 grams of feed per day. If a bird has stopped laying for 30 days, it has consumed about 3.3 to 3.6 kilograms of feed with zero egg income. At a farm-gate feed cost of 0.45 to 0.60 US dollars per kilogram in many African markets, that is 1.50 to 2.20 dollars lost per bird. Multiply that by 200 non-layers in a 5,000-bird flock and you have lost 300 to 440 dollars in a single month. That is money that should have paid wages, electricity or loan instalments. Culling also protects flock health. In Africa, disease pressure is high. Newcastle disease, infectious bronchitis, fowl typhoid, coccidiosis and internal parasites are common. A chronically sick bird is a reservoir. It sheds organisms into litter, water and air. Removing it reduces the pathogen load for the whole house. This is especially important where vaccination coverage is imperfect, where biosecurity is difficult because of open-sided houses, and where wild birds and rodents have easy access. Culling is therefore both a health tool and an economic tool. There are four main categories of birds to cull. First, the sick and injured. These include birds with swollen heads, respiratory distress, diarrhoea, paralysis, prolapse, cannibalised vents, broken legs or tumours. Second, the non-layers. These are mature hens that show no laying signs. You identify them by handling. A laying hen has a wide, moist, flexible vent, a soft and pliable abdomen, a bright comb and wattles, and a pubic bone gap of two to three fingers. A non-layer has a tight, dry vent, a hard abdomen, a pale or shrunken comb, and a pubic gap of one finger or less. Third, the poor producers. These are birds laying thin-shelled, misshapen, tiny or dirty eggs. They may also be very small or very large birds that fall outside the profitable range. Fourth, the aged birds at the end of their economic lay. In Africa, many farms keep layers to 72, 80 or even 90 weeks. This can be profitable if feed is cheap and egg prices are strong, but it must be a conscious decision, not neglect. Timing of culling matters. The best time is during the late afternoon or evening when birds are calm and easy to handle. Do not cull during the hottest part of the day. Heat stress makes handling dangerous for both birds and workers. Do not cull immediately after vaccination or during a disease outbreak unless you are removing clinically sick birds under veterinary advice. Always cull before feeding, or at least two hours after feeding, to reduce stress and contamination. The method of culling must be humane and practical. In many African settings, cervical dislocation is the most common method for small numbers. For larger numbers, use a captive bolt device or approved gas. Whatever method you choose, it must be quick, certain and performed by a trained person. Inhumane culling damages worker morale, creates community complaints and is increasingly unacceptable to buyers and regulators. If you sell culled birds, you must follow food safety rules. Do not sell birds that were treated with antibiotics within the withdrawal period. Do not sell birds with obvious disease lesions. If you cannot sell safely, incinerate or bury deep with lime. Never throw carcasses into a river or open pit near the farm. Record keeping is essential. Keep a simple culling register. Record date, house, number of birds removed, reason, method and destination. This data helps you track disease trends, calculate mortality and culling rates, and make better decisions about breed, feed and management. A farm that culls 0.5 percent per month is very different from one that culls 3 percent per month. The difference is usually management, not bad luck. Finally, culling must be linked to replacement planning. Every bird you cull creates space. That space should be filled by a productive pullet at the right time. If you cull without a replacement plan, you reduce flock size and egg output. If you replace without culling, you overcrowd and spread disease. The two activities must be planned together. In the African context, where cash flow is often tight and feed is expensive, culling is one of the highest-return management activities available to you. It costs almost nothing to remove a non-productive bird, but it saves feed, reduces disease risk and improves flock uniformity. Make it a weekly routine. Train your workers to identify non-layers. Pay them a small incentive for accurate culling. Review your culling data every month. Over a year, disciplined culling can improve feed conversion by 5 to 10 percent and increase net income by 10 to 20 percent. That is the difference between a farm that survives and a farm that grows.

2. Moulting: Natural, Forced and Business Decisions

Moulting is the natural process where a hen stops laying, sheds old feathers and grows new ones. In commercial layer production, moulting is also a management tool. It can be used to extend the productive life of a flock, reduce replacement costs and improve egg quality after a rest period. But moulting is not a magic bullet. It must be planned, managed and costed carefully. In Africa, where pullet prices are high and feed is expensive, moulting can be attractive. However, it also carries risks. This lesson explains the biology, the methods and the business case. Natural moulting occurs when day length shortens, when feed quality drops, when birds experience stress or when they reach the end of a laying cycle. In most African countries near the equator, day length is fairly constant, so natural moulting is less common. But it can still happen because of heat stress, disease, poor nutrition or water shortages. When a hen moults naturally, she stops laying for 8 to 16 weeks. She loses feathers from the head, neck, breast and finally the back. New feathers grow in and she gradually returns to lay. The problem with natural moulting is that it is uncontrolled. You do not know when it will start, how long it will last or how well the birds will come back into production. Forced moulting is a deliberate management practice. You intentionally induce a rest period by changing feed, water and light. The most common method worldwide is the feed restriction method. You withdraw feed for 7 to 14 days, depending on bird condition and climate. You may also reduce water for short periods, but this is risky in hot African climates. A safer method is to feed a low-protein, low-energy ration or a high-fibre ration such as wheat bran or rice bran for 2 to 4 weeks. You also reduce light to 8 hours per day. The goal is to cause the birds to lose 25 to 30 percent of their body weight, stop laying and drop feathers. Then you gradually bring them back. You increase feed and light over 4 to 6 weeks. Birds should start laying again 6 to 8 weeks after the moult begins. The second cycle usually lasts 20 to 30 weeks. Egg production in the second cycle is typically 10 to 15 percent lower than the first cycle, but egg size is larger and shell quality is often better. This can be valuable in markets that pay a premium for large eggs. The business case for forced moulting depends on three numbers. First, the cost of a replacement pullet. In many African countries, a ready-to-lay pullet costs 4 to 8 US dollars. Second, the cost of the moult. This includes feed, labour, reduced egg income during the rest period and possible mortality. Third, the value of the second cycle eggs. If egg prices are high and feed is available, moulting can be profitable. If egg prices are low or feed is scarce, it is usually better to cull and replace. A simple rule of thumb: if the cost of moulting is less than 60 percent of the cost of buying and rearing a replacement pullet, moulting is worth considering. But you must also consider cash flow. During the moult, you have no egg income for 6 to 8 weeks. If your farm cannot survive that gap, do not moult. Forced moulting is controversial. Many consumers, retailers and animal welfare organisations oppose it. In some countries it is banned or restricted. In Africa, regulations are less strict, but buyers are becoming more aware. If you supply supermarkets, hotels or export markets, check their policies before you moult. Some buyers will reject eggs from moulted flocks. Others will accept them if the moult is done humanely. There is also a welfare-friendly alternative called non-fasting moult. Instead of withdrawing feed, you feed a low-density ration with high fibre, such as 50 percent wheat bran or 40 percent rice bran, plus limestone and vitamins. This causes a slower moult but avoids starvation. Mortality is lower and welfare is better. The rest period is longer, often 10 to 12 weeks, but the birds are healthier. This method is increasingly recommended in Africa because it fits smallholder and medium-scale farms where labour is available and feed ingredients like bran are cheap. Managing a moult requires attention to detail. During the rest period, keep the house clean and dry. Remove sick and weak birds immediately. Provide plenty of clean water. Do not vaccinate during the moult unless there is a disease outbreak. Reduce light to 8 hours per day. Keep noise and handling to a minimum. Weigh a sample of birds every week. If they lose more than 30 percent of body weight, you have gone too far. If they lose less than 20 percent, the moult may not be effective. When you bring birds back into lay, use a pre-lay or layer ration with high calcium. Provide oyster shell or limestone grit free choice. Increase light gradually, by 30 minutes per week, until you reach 16 hours. Watch for egg peritonitis, prolapse and cannibalism. These are common after a moult. Record everything. Record the date the moult started, the method used, weekly body weights, mortality, feed intake, the date the first egg appeared and the peak production in the second cycle. This data will tell you whether moulting is profitable on your farm. It will also help you compare moulting with replacement. In conclusion, moulting is a tool, not a default. It can extend the life of a flock, improve egg size and save money on pullets. But it also costs money, carries welfare risks and may not suit your market. In Africa, where feed and pullets are expensive, moulting can be a smart business decision if you have the cash flow, the feed and the market. If you do not, culling and replacement is usually the better path. Always calculate before you moult. Never moult because it is traditional or because a neighbour does it. Moult because the numbers say it will make you money.

3. Flock Replacement: Planning, Pullet Selection and Timing

Flock replacement is the process of bringing new pullets into your layer house to replace culled or spent hens. It is one of the most important decisions in layer production because it determines your egg output, feed cost, disease risk and cash flow for the next 18 to 24 months. In Africa, replacement is often done poorly. Farmers buy pullets when they see a gap, not when the plan says so. They buy from unreliable sources. They mix ages. They overcrowd. The result is disease, poor production and lost money. This lesson gives you a practical replacement plan. Start with a production calendar. Work backwards from your target egg market. If you want to sell eggs every week of the year, you need flocks at different ages. This is called multi-age or staggered production. For example, if you have three houses, you can have one flock at 20 weeks, one at 45 weeks and one at 70 weeks. Each flock peaks at a different time. This smooths your egg supply and your cash flow. It also reduces the risk of total loss from a single disease outbreak. But multi-age farms have higher biosecurity risk. Younger birds can get infected by older birds. If you choose multi-age, you must have strict rules: separate workers, separate equipment, separate boots and separate air spaces. If you cannot manage that, use an all-in-all-out system. All-in-all-out means you empty the house, clean and disinfect it, then bring in a new flock. This is safer for disease control. It is easier to manage. But it means you have no eggs from that house for 4 to 6 weeks. You must plan your cash flow for that gap. The next decision is pullet source. You have three options. First, buy day-old chicks and rear them yourself. This gives you control over genetics, feed, vaccination and management. It is cheaper per bird if you have good rearing facilities. But it requires skill, time and capital. Second, buy ready-to-lay pullets at 16 to 18 weeks. This is faster and simpler. You get eggs in 2 to 4 weeks. But it is more expensive. You also inherit the health status and vaccination history of the rearer. Third, buy point-of-lay pullets from a reputable hatchery or rearer with a health certificate. This is the safest option for most African commercial farms. Whatever source you choose, inspect the pullets before you buy. Look for uniform size, bright eyes, clean vents, dry litter, good feathering and active behaviour. Ask for vaccination records. Ask about mortality in the rearing house. Ask about feed and lighting programme. Do not buy pullets from a farm with a history of disease. Do not buy pullets that are more than 20 weeks old and have not started laying. They may be stunted or diseased. Do not mix pullets from different sources in the same house. They will fight, spread disease and never become a uniform flock. Timing of replacement is critical. The ideal time to bring in new pullets is 2 to 4 weeks before you cull the old flock. This gives you time to prepare the house, clean and disinfect, and adjust feed and lighting. But you must not put new pullets in the same house as old hens. They must be in a separate house or a separate section with a solid wall. If you use the same house, you must remove all old birds, clean and disinfect, and wait at least 2 weeks before bringing in new birds. This is called downtime. Downtime reduces disease pressure. It is especially important for coccidiosis, worms and respiratory diseases. In hot African climates, plan replacement for the cooler months if possible. Pullets that arrive during extreme heat suffer more stress, eat less and start laying later. If you must replace during the hot season, do it early in the morning or late in the evening. Provide electrolytes and vitamins in the water for the first 3 days. Feed a good quality layer mash or crumble. Do not change feed abruptly. Mix the old feed with the new feed over 5 to 7 days. Lighting is another key factor. Pullets need 10 to 12 hours of light during rearing. When they reach 18 weeks, increase light by 30 minutes per week until you reach 16 hours. If you bring pullets into a house with long light, they may start laying too early, produce small eggs and prolapse. If you bring them into a house with short light, they may delay laying and cost you money. Always match the lighting programme of the rearer with your own. Ask for the lighting schedule. If the rearer used 12 hours, you must start at 12 hours and increase gradually. Do not jump to 16 hours. Body weight is the best indicator of readiness. A pullet should reach 1.4 to 1.6 kilograms at 18 weeks, depending on breed. If she is underweight, delay light stimulation and feed a higher-energy diet. If she is overweight, reduce feed slightly and increase exercise. Weigh a sample of 50 to 100 birds every week from 16 weeks. Record the data. Do not rely on age alone. A 20-week-old pullet that weighs 1.2 kilograms is not ready to lay. She will produce small eggs, prolapse and die. A 17-week-old pullet that weighs 1.5 kilograms is ready. Finally, plan your replacement budget. Include the cost of pullets, transport, feed, vaccination, labour, cleaning and lost egg income during downtime. Compare this with the expected income from the new flock. A good layer flock should produce 320 to 340 eggs per bird over 72 weeks. If your replacement cost is too high, you may need to moult instead. If your egg price is too low, you may need to delay replacement. In Africa, where markets can be volatile, flexibility is key. But flexibility must be based on numbers, not guesswork. Keep records. Review them monthly. Adjust your plan. Replacement is not an event. It is a cycle. Manage it well and your farm will produce eggs profitably for years.

4. Practical Culling and Replacement Records for African Farms

Records are the backbone of profitable layer farming. Without records, you are guessing. With records, you can see problems before they become disasters. This lesson focuses on the specific records you need for culling, moulting and flock replacement. It gives you simple formats that work on African farms, whether you keep 200 birds or 20,000. Start with a daily flock sheet. Every house should have one. Record the date, house number, flock age, number of birds at the start of the day, number of eggs collected, number of cracked eggs, number of dirty eggs, number of dead birds, number of culled birds, feed consumed in kilograms, water consumed in litres, and any medication or vaccination given. This sheet takes 5 minutes to fill. It gives you the raw data for everything else. From the daily sheet, calculate weekly and monthly summaries. The most important numbers are mortality rate, culling rate, lay percentage, feed conversion ratio and egg weight. Mortality rate is dead birds divided by birds at the start of the period, multiplied by 100. A good flock loses less than 0.5 percent per month after 20 weeks. If you lose more than 1 percent per month, investigate. Culling rate is culled birds divided by birds at the start of the period, multiplied by 100. A good flock culls 0.3 to 0.5 percent per month. If you cull more than 1 percent per month, you have a problem with breed, feed, disease or management. Lay percentage is eggs collected divided by birds alive, multiplied by 100. A good flock peaks at 92 to 95 percent and stays above 80 percent for 40 weeks. Feed conversion ratio is kilograms of feed divided by kilograms of eggs. A good ratio is 2.0 to 2.2 kilograms of feed per kilogram of eggs. If your ratio is above 2.5, you are losing money. Egg weight is measured by weighing a sample of 30 eggs every week. It should increase from 50 grams at 20 weeks to 65 grams at 40 weeks and stay stable. Now, specific culling records. Keep a culling register. Record date, house, bird identification if you use leg bands or wing tags, reason for culling, method used, and destination. Reasons should be coded. Use simple codes: S for sick, I for injured, N for non-layer, P for poor producer, A for aged. Destination codes: S for sold, H for home consumption, B for buried, C for incinerated. This register helps you see patterns. If most culls are N, your flock has a high number of non-layers. This could be due to poor rearing, poor feed or disease. If most culls are S, you have a health problem. If most culls are A, you are keeping birds too long. Review the register monthly. Calculate the culling rate by reason. Compare it with your targets. Take action. For moulting, keep a moult record. Record the date the moult started, the method used, the target body weight loss, the actual weekly body weights of a sample of 20 birds, the date the first egg appeared, the date the flock reached 50 percent lay, the peak lay percentage in the second cycle, and the total eggs produced in the second cycle. Also record mortality during the moult. A good moult loses less than 3 percent of birds. If you lose more, the method was too severe or the birds were not healthy enough. Compare the cost of the moult with the cost of replacement pullets. Include feed, labour, reduced egg income and mortality. If the moult cost more than 60 percent of replacement cost, do not moult again. For replacement, keep a pullet record. Record the source, breed, date of hatch, date of arrival, age at arrival, body weight at arrival, vaccination history, mortality during the first 4 weeks, age at first egg, and body weight at 18 weeks. Also record the cost per pullet, including transport. Compare this with the performance of the previous flock. If the new flock performs worse, investigate the source. Maybe the pullets were underweight. Maybe they were vaccinated incorrectly. Maybe they came from a farm with disease. Do not buy from that source again. Finally, keep a financial record. Record all income from eggs and culled birds. Record all expenses for feed, pullets, labour, medication, electricity, water and transport. Calculate your profit per bird and profit per egg. This is the ultimate measure of success. In Africa, many farms fail because they do not know their cost of production. They sell eggs at a price that seems good but is actually below cost. With good records, you can calculate your break-even price. Then you can negotiate better prices with buyers. You can also see which flock is most profitable and why. Records do not have to be complicated. A notebook and a pen are enough. A simple spreadsheet on a phone or computer is better. The key is consistency. Fill the records every day. Review them every week. Act on them every month. Train your workers to fill the daily sheet correctly. Check their work. Pay a small bonus for accurate records. In the long run, good records will make you more money than any single input. They will help you cull better, moult smarter and replace at the right time. They will turn your farm from a gamble into a business.

5. Integrating Culling, Moulting and Replacement into a Profitable Cycle

Culling, moulting and replacement are not separate activities. They are three parts of one continuous cycle. If you manage them together, you can keep your flock productive, your costs low and your income steady. If you manage them separately, you will create gaps, waste money and lose birds. This lesson shows you how to integrate them into a practical annual plan for an African layer farm. Start with a clear objective. What is your target? Do you want maximum eggs per bird? Maximum profit per bird? Maximum profit per house? Steady weekly income? Your objective determines your strategy. For example, if you want maximum eggs per bird, you might keep birds to 90 weeks and moult once. If you want maximum profit per house, you might cull at 72 weeks and replace quickly. If you want steady income, you might use multi-age flocks and replace one house at a time. Write down your objective. Share it with your workers. Make sure everyone understands it. Next, create a flock calendar. This is a simple table with columns for house, flock number, hatch date, arrival date, expected peak date, expected cull date, expected moult date if any, and expected replacement date. Fill it in for the next 24 months. This calendar is your roadmap. It tells you when to order pullets, when to prepare houses, when to cull and when to expect eggs. Without a calendar, you will always be reacting. With a calendar, you will be planning. Now, set your culling policy. Decide the criteria for culling. For example: cull any bird with a prolapse, any bird with a swollen head, any bird that has not laid for 21 days, any bird with a body weight below 1.2 kilograms after 30 weeks, and any bird with a broken leg. Write these criteria on the house wall. Train your workers to apply them. Review culling every week. Do not wait for the end of the flock. Cull continuously. This keeps the flock uniform and reduces feed waste. Set your moulting policy. Decide whether you will moult at all. If yes, decide the method, the age, the duration and the target body weight loss. For example: moult at 72 weeks, use the non-fasting method with wheat bran, target 25 percent body weight loss, rest for 10 weeks, then bring back into lay for 25 weeks. Write this policy down. Calculate the expected cost and return. If the return is not at least 20 percent higher than replacement, do not moult. Set your replacement policy. Decide the source of pullets, the breed, the age at arrival, the body weight target and the vaccination protocol. For example: buy 18-week-old pullets from a certified hatchery, target 1.5 kilograms body weight, vaccinate for Newcastle, infectious bronchitis, fowl pox and Marek's disease. Order pullets 6 months in advance. Prepare the house 4 weeks before arrival. Clean, disinfect and rest the house for 2 weeks. Now, integrate the three. Here is a sample cycle for a 5,000-bird house. Week 0: order pullets. Week 18: pullets arrive. Week 20: start laying. Week 26: peak lay at 93 percent. Week 40: lay at 88 percent. Week 60: lay at 78 percent. Week 72: lay at 65 percent. Decision point: moult or cull. If moult, start moult at week 73. Rest for 10 weeks. Week 83: start bringing back into lay. Week 90: lay at 70 percent. Week 110: lay at 60 percent. Week 115: cull all birds. If cull, cull at week 73. Clean house. Week 75: bring in new pullets. Week 77: start laying. This cycle repeats. The key is to plan the decision point 8 weeks in advance. At week 64, you should already know whether you will moult or cull. You should have ordered pullets if you will cull. You should have bought bran if you will moult. You should have booked the buyer for spent hens. You should have arranged labour for cleaning. No surprises. Now, consider the African context. Feed prices fluctuate. Egg prices fluctuate. Disease outbreaks happen. Power cuts happen. Water shortages happen. Your plan must be flexible. Build a buffer into your budget. Keep 3 months of feed cost in reserve. Keep a relationship with two pullet suppliers, not one. Keep a relationship with two egg buyers, not one. Train two workers to do culling, not one. If one leaves, you are not stuck. Also, consider the market. In some African countries, spent hens are a valuable product. They are sold for meat, often at a good price. In others, there is no market. If you can sell spent hens, culling is easier. If you cannot, you must budget for disposal. Moulting produces eggs that may be rejected by some buyers. Check your market before you moult. Replacement pullets may be scarce or expensive at certain times of year. Order early. Pay a deposit. Confirm the delivery date. Finally, measure your success. Calculate your profit per bird per cycle. Compare it with the previous cycle. If it is falling, find out why. Maybe your feed quality dropped. Maybe your culling rate increased. Maybe your egg price fell. Maybe your pullet cost rose. Fix the problem. If your profit is rising, find out why. Do more of what works. Share the results with your workers. Pay a bonus when the flock exceeds targets. This creates a team that cares about culling, moulting and replacement. In conclusion, culling, moulting and replacement are the three gears of a profitable layer farm. Culling removes the unproductive. Moulting extends the productive. Replacement renews the productive. When you integrate them, you create a cycle that runs smoothly year after year. You reduce disease. You reduce feed waste. You increase egg output. You increase profit. In Africa, where margins are thin and risks are high, this integration is not optional. It is the difference between a farm that struggles and a farm that thrives. Start today. Write your policy. Make your calendar. Train your workers. Keep your records. Review your results. Adjust your plan. Your flock will thank you with eggs, and your bank account will thank you with profit.

<p>The break-even row is the most important line in this table for African producers. If a hen is eating 115 g of feed per day and laying 3.5 eggs per week, she is roughly covering her feed cost at typical 2024 feed-to-egg price ratios in Ghana, Nigeria and Kenya. Below 3.5 eggs per week, the bird is a net drain she is consuming feed that could go to a productive hen or a pullet. This is the mathematical basis for culling.</p>

<div style="background:#ffebee;border-left:6px solid #c62828;padding:16px;margin:20px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c62828;"> SAFETY WARNING HEALTH, FEED AND CHEMICAL HAZARDS</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Forced moulting by feed and water withdrawal is a significant animal welfare and health risk. If you moult, follow a recognised protocol (e.g. Aviagen or university extension) that limits withdrawal to a maximum of 1014 days, never removes water, and provides a balanced re-feeding programme. Do not use salt, zinc oxide or other chemical moulting agents without veterinary supervision incorrect dosing causes kidney damage, mortality and drug residues in eggs. Cull birds sold for meat must be handled hygienically; do not sell birds that died of unknown causes. Always wear gloves when handling sick or dead birds, and wash hands after culling. If mortality exceeds 0.05% per day for three consecutive days, stop culling and consult a veterinarian before continuing this may indicate infectious disease (e.g. Newcastle, avian influenza) that requires official reporting.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Weigh and count weekly:</strong> Record hen-day production, daily mortality, feed intake per bird and egg weight every week no decision is better than the data behind it.</li>
<li><strong>Identify non-layers:</strong> A bird with pale comb, small vent, dry skin and no egg in the pelvic check for 2 consecutive weeks is a cull candidate.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate break-even:</strong> Divide weekly feed cost per bird by egg price. If a bird lays fewer eggs than that number, she is losing money.</li>
<li><strong>Check age before moulting:</strong> Moulting works best at 7085 weeks. Moulting a flock older than 90 weeks or with &gt;10% mortality is usually uneconomic.</li>
<li><strong>Time replacement with pullet availability:</strong> Order pullets 1820 weeks before you plan to cull the old flock pullet shortages are common in many African markets.</li>
<li><strong>Record every cull:</strong> Date, bird ID (or batch), reason, sale price or disposal method. This record is your audit trail and your cost data.</li>
<li><strong>Watch the market:</strong> Cull bird prices and egg prices move seasonally. Cull into high meat demand periods; moult into high egg price periods.</li>
<li><strong>Never moult a sick flock:</strong> If mortality is rising or disease is present, fix health first. Moulting a diseased flock multiplies losses.</li>
</ul>
