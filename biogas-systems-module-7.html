1. Biogas Cleaning: Removal of H2S, Moisture, and CO2

Raw biogas from a digester contains about 60% methane (CH4) and 40% carbon dioxide (CO2), plus traces of hydrogen sulfide (H2S) and water vapor. H2S smells like rotten eggs and corrodes metal pipes, engines, and gas burners. Moisture causes rust and can freeze in cold lines. CO2 reduces energy content. Cleaning these out is the first step before using biogas for cooking, electricity, or upgrading to biomethane.

H2S removal: The cheapest method is dry scrubbing with iron oxide. You can build a simple filter using steel wool or iron filings in a sealed drum. Biogas passes through, and H2S reacts with iron to form iron sulfide. When the filter turns black, expose it to air to regenerate. A 200-liter drum with 20 kg of iron filings can treat biogas from a small farm digester for several weeks. For larger systems, use activated carbon impregnated with potassium iodide. Alternatively, inject a small amount of air (2-5%) into the digester; bacteria oxidize H2S to elemental sulfur. This is called micro-aeration.

Moisture removal: Water vapor condenses in pipes. Install a water trapa simple T-junction with a valve at the lowest pointand drain it daily. For better drying, pass biogas through a bed of silica gel or a refrigerated dryer. In Africa, a cheap method is to run the gas through a long pipe buried underground; the cool soil condenses water, which you drain off.

CO2 removal: CO2 is not corrosive but takes up space. For cooking, you can leave it init just lowers the flame temperature. For vehicle fuel or grid injection, you must remove it. Common methods include water scrubbing (CO2 dissolves in water under pressure) and membrane separation. Small-scale farmers often skip CO2 removal unless selling biomethane.

Always install a pressure relief valve and a flame arrestor. Test for leaks with soapy water. In Kenya, the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) recommends H2S below 10 ppm for engine use. A simple lead acetate paper test turns black in the presence of H2S; use it to check your filter.

2. Upgrading Technologies: Water Scrubbing, PSA, Membrane Separation

Upgrading biogas means removing CO2 to get biomethane with 95-98% methane. Three main technologies are used: water scrubbing, pressure swing adsorption (PSA), and membrane separation. Each has pros and cons for African conditions.

Water scrubbing: CO2 dissolves in water more easily than methane. Biogas is compressed to 6-10 bar and fed into a column where water flows downward. The CO2 dissolves and exits with the water, while methane-rich gas leaves the top. You can regenerate the water by releasing pressure and stripping CO2 with air. Water scrubbing is simple and robust, but requires a lot of water (about 0.1 m3 per m3 of biogas) and a pump. In Africa, where water may be scarce, you can recirculate the water after stripping. A small unit for a farm digester costs around $2,000-$5,000. It works well in warm climates.

Pressure swing adsorption (PSA): PSA uses adsorbent materials like zeolites or activated carbon that preferentially trap CO2 under high pressure (4-10 bar). When pressure drops, CO2 is released. PSA can achieve 97% methane but requires clean, dry gas (H2S and water must be removed first). The valves and controls are complex, and maintenance can be tricky. PSA units are typically $5,000-$15,000 for small scale. They are sensitive to impurities, so good pre-cleaning is essential.

Membrane separation: Membranes are polymer hollow fibers that allow CO2 and water to pass through faster than methane. Biogas is compressed to 5-20 bar and passed through the membrane. Methane stays on one side, CO2 on the other. Membranes are compact, have no moving parts, and are easy to operate. However, they are expensive ( $8,000-$20,000 for small scale) and may need multiple stages to reach high purity. They work best with clean gas. In South Africa, some pilot projects use membranes for landfill gas upgrading.

For African entrepreneurs, water scrubbing is often the most practical because it is low-tech and can be built locally. PSA and membranes are better for larger, well-funded operations. Always compare capital cost, operating cost, water availability, and maintenance skills before choosing.

3. Biomethane Quality Standards for Grid Injection and Vehicle Fuel

Biomethane (upgraded biogas) must meet strict quality standards before it can be injected into natural gas grids or used as vehicle fuel. These standards ensure safety, protect equipment, and maintain energy content. In Africa, standards are still developing, but many countries follow European or ISO norms.

For grid injection, the key parameters are methane content, calorific value, water dew point, and impurities. Typical European standard (e.g., EASEE-gas) requires methane 95%, CO2 2%, O2 0.5%, H2S 5 mg/m3, and water dew point -10C at 70 bar. In South Africa, SANS 1535 for natural gas allows similar limits. In Kenya, KEBS is working on biomethane standards. For vehicle fuel, the standard is stricter: methane 97%, CO2 1.5%, H2S 5 ppm, and water 32 mg/m3. This is similar to compressed natural gas (CNG) quality.

Why these limits? CO2 reduces energy density and can cause corrosion when combined with water. H2S poisons catalytic converters and corrodes engines. Water can freeze and block valves. Oxygen can cause explosions in compressed systems. Siloxanes (from cosmetics in sewage) form abrasive deposits in engines; they must be removed with activated carbon.

To meet standards, you need a gas analyzer. A portable biogas analyzer (e.g., Geotech Biogas 5000) costs about $2,000 and measures CH4, CO2, O2, H2S. For continuous monitoring, install a fixed analyzer. In Africa, you can send samples to labs like SGS or Intertek for certification.

If you inject into a grid, you need a connection agreement with the utility. They will require a gas chromatograph and telemetry. For vehicle fuel, you need a compressor to 200-250 bar and a dispenser. In Uganda, the Kigali bus rapid transit pilot uses biomethane from a landfill; they follow ISO 15403-1 for CNG quality. Always check local regulations and consult the national gas authority.

4. Direct Use of Biogas for Cooking, Lighting, and Heating

Direct use of biogas is the simplest and most common application in Africa. After basic cleaning (H2S and moisture removal), biogas can be used in stoves, lamps, and heaters. This saves money on firewood, charcoal, and kerosene, and reduces indoor air pollution.

Cooking: Biogas stoves are similar to LPG stoves but have larger jets because biogas has lower energy content (about 6 kWh/m3 vs 25 kWh/m3 for LPG). A typical family needs 1-2 m3 of biogas per day for cooking. A 6 m3/day digester (e.g., from 4-6 cows) is enough. Stoves cost $20-$50. Adjust the air intake for a blue flame; a yellow flame means too little air. In Ethiopia, the National Biogas Programme has installed over 20,000 biogas stoves. Keep the stove clean and check for leaks with soapy water.

Lighting: Biogas lamps use a mantle (like a camping lantern) and consume about 0.1-0.2 m3/hour. They give light equivalent to a 60-watt bulb. However, they produce heat and humidity, and mantles break easily. LED lights powered by a biogas generator are more efficient, but for off-grid homes, a biogas lamp is a cheap option. In Tanzania, some schools use biogas lamps for evening study.

Heating: Biogas can power water heaters, space heaters, and incubators. For poultry, a biogas brooder can replace charcoal. A simple biogas water heater uses a burner under a tank. For space heating, use a radiant heater with a flue to exhaust combustion gases. In South Africa, some farms use biogas for heating piglet pens. Ensure proper ventilation to avoid carbon monoxide buildup.

Efficiency tips: Insulate pipes, use a gas pressure regulator, and turn off appliances when not in use. A biogas refrigerator (absorption type) can be run on biogas, but they are expensive and rare. For cooking, use a pressure cooker to save gas. Always install a condensate trap and drain it regularly. With good management, a family biogas system can save $200-$500 per year on fuel.

5. Electricity Generation: CHP Engines and Generators

Biogas can generate electricity using a generator set (genset) powered by a spark-ignition or dual-fuel engine. Combined heat and power (CHP) systems capture waste heat for heating or drying, boosting overall efficiency to 80-90%. In Africa, biogas electricity is used on farms, in agro-processing plants, and for mini-grids.

Engine types: Standard diesel engines can be converted to run on biogas (dual-fuel) with a small amount of diesel (10-20%) for ignition. Dedicated biogas engines (e.g., from MAN, Caterpillar, or Chinese brands like Weichai) are more efficient but costlier. For small scale (10-100 kW), converted diesel engines are common. For example, a 30 kW genset can run on biogas from a 100-cow dairy farm. In Kenya, the Gorge Farm uses a 2.4 MW biogas plant from vegetable waste to power its operations.

Fuel requirements: The engine needs clean biogas: H2S < 200 ppm (ideally < 50 ppm), moisture removed, and CO2 can remain. CO2 lowers power output but is not damaging. Methane number should be > 130. Install a gas mixer, pressure regulator, and flame arrestor. The engine oil must be changed more often (every 200-300 hours) because biogas burns cleaner but can contaminate oil with acids.

Heat recovery: CHP captures heat from the exhaust and engine jacket. You can use it to heat the digester (which improves gas production), dry crops, or heat water. A heat exchanger and a thermal storage tank are needed. For a 30 kW engine, you can recover about 50 kW of heat.

Grid connection: For selling electricity, you need a generator with synchronizing controls and a power purchase agreement (PPA) with the utility. In South Africa, the REIPPPP program supports biogas. In Nigeria, the Rural Electrification Agency promotes mini-grids. For off-grid, use a battery bank and inverter to stabilize power.

Costs: A 10 kW biogas genset costs $5,000-$15,000. Maintenance is key: check spark plugs, air filter, and oil regularly. Train a local technician. Always follow the manufacturer's manual and local electrical codes.

6. Case Study: Biogas-to-Biomethane Project

The Bio2Watt project in Bronkhorstspruit, South Africa, is a successful biogas-to-biomethane case. It uses cattle manure from a feedlot and organic waste from supermarkets to produce biogas, which is upgraded to biomethane and injected into the national gas grid. This project shows how African entrepreneurs can turn waste into a valuable commodity.

Project details: Bio2Watt operates a 4.6 MW biogas plant that processes 40,000 tons of organic waste per year. The digester produces about 2,000 m3 of biogas per hour. The biogas is cleaned (H2S removal with iron oxide, moisture removal with a chiller) and then upgraded using water scrubbing to 96% methane. The biomethane is compressed and injected into the Sasol gas pipeline. The project cost about $15 million and was financed by the Industrial Development Corporation (IDC) and private investors.

Technology: The upgrading unit uses high-pressure water scrubbing. Water is recycled, which is important in water-scarce South Africa. The plant also has a CHP unit that generates electricity for the grid and heat for the digester. The H2S removal uses a biological trickling filter, which is low-cost and robust.

Lessons for Africa: First, secure a long-term gas sales agreement. Bio2Watt has a 10-year PPA with Sasol. Second, use local waste streamsfeedlots, markets, and food processing plants are reliable sources. Third, invest in proper pre-treatment: H2S and moisture removal are critical for upgrading. Fourth, train local operators; the plant employs 30 people, mostly from the community. Fifth, government incentives help: South Africa's renewable energy feed-in tariff made the project viable.

Challenges: High initial capital, complex permitting, and grid access. In many African countries, gas grids are limited. However, biomethane can also be used as vehicle fuel. In Kenya, the Kigali project produces biomethane for buses. For small-scale entrepreneurs, start with direct use (cooking) and gradually move to upgrading as you gain skills and market access. The Bio2Watt model can be replicated in other African countries with strong waste management and energy policies.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Biogas Upgrading, Utilisation and Business Decisions

This practical reference module is built for African agribusiness operators, project developers, financiers and technical managers who need to move biogas from a waste-treatment cost centre to a revenue-generating product line. The core message is simple: raw biogas is a low-value fuel; cleaned and upgraded biogas (biomethane) is a premium energy commodity with multiple monetisation pathways. The decision to clean, upgrade or use biogas directly should be driven by three business questions: (1) What is the highest-value end use I can reliably serve? (2) What is the cost per cubic metre of removing HS, moisture and CO? (3) What quality standard must I meet to sell into that market?

Biogas typically contains 5070% methane (CH), 3050% carbon dioxide (CO), 0.13% hydrogen sulphide (HS), water vapour and trace contaminants. For cooking, lighting and heating, only HS and moisture removal are usually required. For electricity generation in CHP engines, HS must be reduced to below 200500 ppm to protect engine components. For grid injection or vehicle fuel, CO must be removed to achieve 9598% methane content, and HS to below 510 ppm. Each step adds capital and operating cost, so the business case must be built from the revenue side backwards.

SAFETY WARNING HS AND METHANE HAZARDS

Hydrogen sulphide (HS) is highly toxic and can cause unconsciousness or death at concentrations above 500 ppm. Methane is flammable and explosive at 515% concentration in air. Never enter a digester, gas holder or confined space without proper gas monitoring, forced ventilation and a rescue plan. In African field conditions, always use calibrated HS and CH detectors, and ensure all electrical equipment in gas zones is explosion-proof (ATEX or equivalent). Biogas upgrading systems operate at elevated pressures inspect relief valves and pressure vessels regularly. Train all operators on emergency shutdown procedures and provide personal HS monitors.

Direct Use vs. Upgrading: A Business Decision Framework

For many African agribusinesses, direct use of biogas for cooking, lighting and heating remains the fastest path to cost savings. A 100 m/h biogas plant can replace LPG, firewood or diesel for on-site thermal needs, with payback periods of 24 years. However, the highest revenue per cubic metre of methane comes from biomethane sold as vehicle fuel or injected into a gas grid. In countries such as South Africa, Kenya, Nigeria and Egypt, emerging bio-CNG markets for buses, trucks and municipal fleets offer premium prices. The decision hinges on: local gas prices, distance to grid or filling station, capital availability, and technical capacity to operate upgrading equipment.

Electricity generation through CHP engines is a middle path. It requires HS removal but not CO separation, and can use biogas directly. In Africa, CHP is attractive where grid electricity is unreliable or expensive, and where waste heat can be used for pasteurisation, drying or greenhouse heating. The key business metric is the levelised cost of electricity (LCOE) compared to grid tariffs or diesel generator costs. Typical LCOE for biogas CHP in Africa ranges from USD 0.080.15/kWh, competitive with diesel at USD 0.250.40/kWh.

Case Study: Biogas-to-Biomethane Project Kenya

A commercial flower farm in Naivasha, Kenya, installed a 500 m/h anaerobic digester treating flower waste and cattle manure. The raw biogas was initially used for cooking and heating. In 2022, the farm added biological HS removal, condensation drying and a membrane separation unit to produce 250 m/h of biomethane (96% CH). The biomethane is compressed and sold to a local transport company for bio-CNG buses. The project cost USD 1.8 million, with a payback of 4.5 years. Revenue comes from fuel sales, carbon credits and reduced waste disposal costs. Key success factors: consistent feedstock, skilled operators, and a nearby offtake partner. Challenges included high humidity requiring robust drying, and initial membrane fouling from siloxanes solved by adding activated carbon pre-treatment.

Quick Reference Checklist 8 Points

  1. Characterise your biogas: Measure CH, CO, HS, moisture and siloxanes before designing any upgrading system.
  2. Match technology to end use: Cooking/heating needs only HS and moisture removal; vehicle fuel and grid injection require CO separation.
  3. Prioritise HS removal: Protect engines, membranes and downstream equipment; biological scrubbers are lowest cost in Africa.
  4. Dry the gas thoroughly: Tropical humidity demands condensation and desiccant drying to prevent corrosion and freezing.
  5. Verify quality standards: Confirm local grid or vehicle fuel specifications before investing in upgrading.
  6. Secure an offtake agreement: Biomethane is only valuable if someone buys it lock in buyers early.
  7. Plan for maintenance: Membranes, adsorbents and scrubber media need replacement; budget 510% of capex annually.
  8. Train operators and enforce safety: HS and methane monitoring, explosion-proof equipment and emergency drills are non-negotiable.
