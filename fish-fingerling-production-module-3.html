1. Selection of Healthy Broodstock

Selecting healthy broodstock is the first step to producing quality fingerlings. Start by choosing fish that are 812 months old for tilapia and 610 months for catfish, as these ages give good egg and sperm quality. Look for fish with a body weight of at least 200300 g for tilapia and 500800 g for catfish. Avoid fish that are too fat or too thin; a healthy broodfish has a rounded belly and a thick base to the tail.

Examine each fish closely. The eyes should be clear and bright, not cloudy. The skin should be smooth, shiny, and free of wounds, spots, or parasites. The fins should be intact, not ragged or bloody. Gills should be bright red, not pale or brown. Check the vent (the opening near the tail) it should be clean and slightly pink, not red or swollen. Any fish showing signs of disease, deformity, or slow movement should be removed immediately.

Source your broodstock from a reputable hatchery or from your own best-performing ponds. Never mix fish from different farms without quarantine, as this spreads diseases. In Africa, many farmers use Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) or African catfish (Clarias gariepinus). For tilapia, select fish that are 68 months old and 150250 g; for catfish, 812 months and 8001200 g. Always choose more females than males, usually 23 females for every male.

Before stocking, weigh and measure each fish. Record the data in a notebook. A good broodfish should gain weight steadily. If you are buying from a hatchery, ask for health certificates and check for common diseases like tilapia lake virus or catfish iridovirus. Quarantine new fish in a separate tank for 14 days, treating with salt (35 g per litre) to reduce stress and kill external parasites.

Finally, handle fish gently with wet hands or a soft net. Rough handling causes stress, which lowers egg quality. Keep broodstock in clean, well-aerated water with a temperature of 2630C and a pH of 6.58.5. A simple test kit for ammonia, nitrite, and pH is essential. By selecting only the best fish, you set the foundation for strong, fast-growing fingerlings.

2. Broodstock Nutrition and Feeding Regimes

Proper nutrition is critical for broodstock because it directly affects egg quality, sperm count, and the health of the fry. Broodfish need a diet with 3540% protein, 68% fat, and added vitamins and minerals. You can use commercial broodstock pellets, but many African farmers make their own mix. A simple recipe: 40% fishmeal, 30% soybean meal, 20% maize or wheat bran, 5% cassava flour, and 5% vitamin-mineral premix. Add 2% cod liver oil or vegetable oil for essential fatty acids.

Feed broodfish twice a day, at 89 am and 45 pm. Give them 23% of their total body weight per day. For example, if you have 10 kg of broodfish, feed 200300 g of feed daily. Adjust the amount based on water temperature: in warm months (2830C), feed more; in cool months (below 24C), reduce to 1% body weight. Overfeeding causes fat deposits that reduce fertility; underfeeding leads to small, weak eggs.

Supplement with natural foods. In ponds, broodfish will eat algae, insects, and small crustaceans. You can also add chopped earthworms, termites, or small fish once a week. For tilapia, add 1020% of feed as spirulina or chlorella to boost colour and egg quality. For catfish, add 5% squid meal or shrimp meal. Always store feed in a cool, dry place and use within 3 months to prevent mould.

Water quality affects feeding. Keep dissolved oxygen above 5 mg/L and ammonia below 0.5 mg/L. If fish gasp at the surface, stop feeding and increase aeration. Use a simple aeration pump or splash water. During spawning season, increase protein to 40% and add 1% garlic powder to stimulate appetite and prevent disease.

Record feed amounts daily. A 100 g female tilapia should gain 23 g per week. If she is not gaining, check for disease or poor water quality. For catfish, a 1 kg female should gain 510 g per week. Adjust feed based on condition you should be able to feel a slight fat layer but not a thick belly. Always remove uneaten feed after 30 minutes to avoid fouling the water. Healthy, well-fed broodstock produce 2030% more eggs than poorly fed ones.

3. Broodstock Conditioning and Spawning Induction

Conditioning prepares broodfish for spawning. Start 46 weeks before you want eggs. Move selected males and females to separate conditioning tanks. Use clean, well-aerated water at 2830C for tilapia and 2628C for catfish. Keep pH at 6.58.0 and ammonia below 0.1 mg/L. Feed a high-protein diet (40% protein) twice daily. For tilapia, condition at a density of 510 fish per cubic metre; for catfish, 35 fish per cubic metre.

For natural spawning, tilapia can be conditioned in harem tanks with one male to three females. Provide nesting sites like flat stones or plastic trays. After 23 weeks, females will show a swollen genital papilla and males will dig nests. For catfish, natural spawning is harder; you usually need hormone injection. Use a synthetic hormone like Ovaprim or human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG). For catfish, inject females with 0.5 ml Ovaprim per kg body weight, and males with half that dose. Inject into the muscle near the dorsal fin using a 1 ml syringe.

After injection, place one male and one female in a spawning tank with a substrate (like a piece of net or banana leaves) for catfish to lay eggs on. Keep water at 2628C and aerate gently. Eggs are released 1012 hours after injection. For tilapia, you can also use hormonal induction if you want synchronized spawning. Use 17-methyltestosterone? No, that is for sex reversal. For spawning, use HCG at 1000 IU per kg for females.

Monitor fish closely. Remove eggs after spawning to prevent parents from eating them. For tilapia, females mouthbrood eggs for 1421 days. You can strip eggs from the female's mouth after 34 days and incubate them artificially in a hatchery jar. For catfish, eggs hatch in 2436 hours at 28C. Keep water clean and free of fungus by adding methylene blue at 2 mg/L.

Record all dates, hormone doses, and water temperatures. A simple notebook works. After spawning, return broodfish to conditioning tanks and feed them well. They can be used again after 46 weeks. Always have backup males and females in case some do not respond.

4. Genetic Improvement and Selective Breeding

Genetic improvement means choosing the best fish to breed so that future generations grow faster, resist disease, and taste better. Without selection, fish become stunted and weak after a few generations. Start by tagging or marking your best fish. Use small plastic tags or fin clips. Record growth rate, body shape, and survival. Select only fish that reach 200 g in 4 months (for tilapia) or 500 g in 6 months (for catfish).

Choose a minimum of 50 males and 150 females as your breeding population. Never breed brother to sister; that causes inbreeding and deformities. Instead, use a rotational system: divide your broodstock into 34 families. Each year, breed males from family A with females from family B, then B with C, and so on. This keeps genetic diversity high. In Africa, many farmers use the GIFT strain of Nile tilapia, which grows 30% faster than local strains.

Select for specific traits. For growth, weigh each fish at 4 and 8 months. Keep only the top 20% by weight. For disease resistance, expose fish to a mild disease challenge (like Streptococcus) and keep survivors. For body shape, choose fish with a deep body and small head. For colour, if you want red tilapia, select only red or pink fish. Always record data in a logbook: tag number, weight, length, date, and family.

Manage your breeding stock carefully. Keep each family in a separate tank or hap (a net cage). When they are ready to spawn, mix males and females from different families. After spawning, return them to their family tanks. Replace broodfish every 1218 months to avoid old, poor-quality eggs. A good selective breeding programme can increase growth by 1015% per generation.

Avoid common mistakes: do not select only the largest fish if they are sick or deformed. Do not use too few breeders. Do not mix strains without a plan. Work with other farmers to exchange males every 2 years. Simple records and consistent selection will give you a big advantage. Many African hatcheries now sell improved strains; buy from them if you cannot run your own programme.

5. Health Management of Broodstock

Broodstock are valuable, so disease can be costly. Prevent problems by keeping water clean. Test water daily for temperature (2630C), pH (6.58.5), ammonia (00.5 mg/L), and nitrite (00.1 mg/L). Use a simple test kit. Change 2030% of water every 23 days. Keep a separate quarantine tank for new fish. Quarantine for 14 days and treat with salt (35 g/L) or potassium permanganate (2 mg/L) for 30 minutes.

Common diseases in African broodstock include bacterial infections (Aeromonas, Streptococcus), fungal infections (Saprolegnia), and parasites (Trichodina, Ichthyophthirius). Signs: white spots, frayed fins, red ulcers, cloudy eyes, or rubbing against walls. For bacterial infections, use oxytetracycline at 50 mg/kg fish for 7 days in feed. For fungus, use methylene blue at 23 mg/L as a bath for 3 days. For parasites, use formalin at 25 ml per 1000 L for 1 hour, or salt baths (10 g/L for 30 minutes).

Vaccination is possible for some diseases. In Africa, vaccines for Streptococcus in tilapia are available. Vaccinate fish at 1015 g size by injection. Keep records of vaccination dates. Also, use probiotics in feed (1 g/kg) to boost immunity. Add garlic (5 g/kg feed) and vitamin C (500 mg/kg feed) during stressful periods like spawning.

Handle fish gently to avoid stress. Stress lowers immunity. Do not overcrowd. Keep density at 510 kg per cubic metre for tilapia, 35 kg for catfish. Remove dead fish immediately. Disinfect nets and tanks with chlorine (10 mg/L) between uses. If disease occurs, isolate affected fish and treat the whole tank. Do not use antibiotics without veterinary advice; misuse causes resistance.

Record all treatments, dates, and doses. A healthy broodstock programme includes regular health checks every 2 weeks. Look at gills, skin, and fins under a magnifying glass. If you see problems, act fast. A sick broodfish produces poor eggs or none at all. By keeping your broodstock healthy, you ensure a steady supply of quality fingerlings.

6. Record Keeping and Broodstock Performance Evaluation

Record keeping is the backbone of good broodstock management. Without records, you cannot tell which fish perform best. Use a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on a phone. For each broodfish, record: tag number, species, sex, date of birth, weight every month, length, feed amount, spawning dates, number of eggs, hatch rate, and any treatments. Also record water quality daily: temperature, pH, ammonia, and nitrite.

At spawning, record the male and female tag numbers, date, hormone dose (if used), time of spawning, number of eggs, and number of fry hatched. Calculate hatch rate: (number of fry / number of eggs) 100. A good hatch rate for tilapia is 7080%; for catfish, 6070%. Calculate fecundity: number of eggs per gram of female body weight. For tilapia, expect 24 eggs per gram; for catfish, 1020 eggs per gram.

Evaluate performance every 3 months. For each female, calculate average eggs per spawn, average hatch rate, and survival of fry to 30 days. For each male, check fertility by looking at egg hatch rate. Cull any female that produces less than 1 egg per gram or has hatch rate below 50%. Cull any male that sires eggs with hatch rate below 50%. Replace them with better fish from your selection programme.

Use a simple scoring system. Give each fish a score: growth (15), survival (15), and egg quality (15). Total score out of 15. Keep only fish scoring 10 or above. Record all scores. This helps you decide which fish to breed next season. Also track feed cost per fry produced. If feed cost is high, adjust feeding or cull poor performers.

Share records with other farmers or extension officers. They can help you spot trends. For example, if hatch rate drops in the dry season, you may need to adjust water temperature or feeding. Keep records for at least 2 years. This data is your best tool for improving your hatchery. A farmer who keeps good records can double production in 23 years. Start simple: a notebook and a pen. Then move to a phone app like Excel or Kobo Collect. The key is consistency.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Broodstock Management for Fish Fingerling Production

Broodstock management is the single highest-leverage activity in a fingerling business. The quality, fecundity, and health of your broodstock determine hatch rates, fry survival, growth performance, and ultimately the price premium you can command for your fingerlings. In Africa's rapidly expanding aquaculture sector led by Nigeria, Egypt, Ghana, Kenya, Uganda, and Zambia hatcheries that treat broodstock as a managed asset rather than a byproduct consistently out-earn those that do not. A well-managed broodstock unit can produce 2040% more viable fry per female per cycle, reduce disease-related losses, and shorten the production cycle by weeks. This lesson consolidates practical protocols for selection, nutrition, conditioning, spawning induction, genetic improvement, health management, and performance tracking with an emphasis on what works under African farm conditions.

1. Selection of Healthy Broodstock

Select broodstock from fast-growing, disease-free populations with documented performance. For Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) and catfish (Clarias gariepinus), the following criteria are non-negotiable:

  • Body condition: Full, rounded belly (females), robust musculature, no spinal deformities.
  • Size: Tilapia females 150300 g, males 200400 g; catfish females 800 g2 kg, males 13 kg.
  • Age: 618 months for tilapia; 824 months for catfish. Avoid over-mature fish (low egg quality).
  • Health: Clear eyes, intact fins, no lesions, no gill discoloration, active feeding response.
  • Genetics: Use certified improved strains (e.g., GIFT, Abbassa, or locally selected lines) rather than random pond fish.

Source fish from reputable hatcheries or national research institutes. Mixing sources without quarantine is a common and costly mistake.

2. Broodstock Nutrition and Feeding Regimes

Broodstock nutrition directly influences egg size, larval quality, and spawning frequency. Underfeeding delays spawning; overfeeding causes fat accumulation and reduced fertility. A practical African hatchery regime:

3. Broodstock Conditioning and Spawning Induction

Conditioning prepares broodstock physiologically for spawning. Key steps:

  1. Sex separation: Hold males and females separately for 23 weeks to control spawning timing.
  2. Temperature and photoperiod: Maintain 2629C and 12:12 light cycle for tilapia. Catfish require 2730C.
  3. Hormonal induction: For catfish, use Ovaprim (0.5 mL/kg female) or carp pituitary extract (4 mg/kg). Inject intramuscularly near the dorsal fin.
  4. Latency period: 1012 hours for catfish at 27C; strip eggs and fertilize with macerated male testes.
  5. Tilapia: Natural spawning in hapas is preferred; hormonal induction is rarely economic at small scale.

SAFETY WARNING Hormone Handling & Fish Welfare

Ovaprim and carp pituitary extracts are biological agents. Always wear gloves, eye protection, and a lab coat when handling. Store at 4C, never freeze. Dispose of syringes in a sharps container never reuse. Overdosing causes female mortality; underdosing causes failed spawning and wasted broodstock. Handle broodstock gently: rough handling during injection is a leading cause of post-spawning mortality and fungal infection in African hatcheries.

4. Genetic Improvement and Selective Breeding

Without deliberate selection, hatchery broodstock degrade within 34 generations due to inbreeding depression reduced growth, lower survival, and increased deformity. Practical genetic management for African hatcheries:

  • Tag and track: Use PIT tags or fin clipping to identify individual broodstock.
  • Family-based selection: Maintain 50+ families per generation to avoid inbreeding (Ne > 50).
  • Selection traits: Prioritize growth rate (weight at 6 months), survival, and disease resistance.
  • Cross-breeding: Rotate males between tanks every 2 generations to maintain genetic diversity.
  • Record pedigree: Never spawn siblings. A simple notebook or spreadsheet pedigree prevents catastrophic inbreeding.

Programs like GIFT (Genetically Improved Farmed Tilapia) and Abbassa (Egypt) demonstrate 1015% genetic gain per generation when properly managed. Small hatcheries can achieve 58% annual gain with disciplined selection.

5. Health Management of Broodstock

Broodstock are your most valuable fish disease here is catastrophic. Core protocols:

  • Quarantine: 21 days in separate tanks with 2% salt dips (5 min) on entry.
  • Water quality: Dissolved oxygen >5 mg/L, ammonia <0.05 mg/L, pH 6.88.0.
  • Biosecurity: Foot baths, dedicated nets per tank, no sharing equipment between quarantine and production.
  • Vaccination: Where available (e.g., Streptococcus vaccines for tilapia), vaccinate broodstock 4 weeks pre-spawning.
  • Parasite control: Monthly formalin bath (25 mL/m for 1 hour) or potassium permanganate (2 mg/L).
  • Culling: Remove any fish showing lesions, erratic swimming, or emaciation immediately.

6. Record Keeping and Broodstock Performance Evaluation

Records convert broodstock management from guesswork into a business. Track per female and per tank:

  • Tag ID, species/strain, date acquired, source
  • Weight and length at each spawning
  • Spawning date, hormone dose, latency period
  • Egg count, egg weight, fertilization rate
  • Hatch rate, fry count, 30-day survival
  • Feed consumed, mortality events, treatments

Calculate Return on Broodstock Investment (ROBI) = (Value of fry sold Broodstock cost) Broodstock cost. A well-managed tilapia female producing 1,500 fry at $0.05 each generates $75 per cycle against a feed cost of ~$35. The economics strongly favor disciplined record keeping.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Select broodstock 150 g (tilapia) or 800 g (catfish), disease-free and well-conditioned.
  • Feed 3545% protein diet at 1.53% body weight daily with vitamin C and E supplementation.
  • Condition 1430 days pre-spawning; separate sexes to control timing.
  • Use Ovaprim (0.5 mL/kg) for catfish; allow 1012 h latency at 27C.
  • Maintain 50 families and tag every broodfish to prevent inbreeding.
  • Quarantine all new stock 21 days; never mix sources without screening.
  • Record every spawning event: eggs, hatch rate, fry survival, feed cost.
  • Calculate ROBI per female per cycle; cull underperformers ruthlessly.
