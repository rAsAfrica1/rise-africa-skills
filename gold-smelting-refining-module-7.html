Lessons

1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Mercury amalgamation is a method used by small-scale gold miners across Africa to capture fine gold from crushed ore or concentrates. It works because mercury binds with gold particles to form an amalgam, a soft, silvery-gold blob. This method is popular because it is cheap, fast, and requires little equipment. In many African countries like Ghana, Tanzania, Zimbabwe, and Burkina Faso, miners use mercury in sluice boxes, pans, or small ball mills. For example, a miner might add 10 grams of mercury to a bucket of concentrate and stir for 20 minutes. The amalgam is then squeezed through a cloth to remove excess mercury, leaving a hard ball that is later burned to evaporate the mercury, leaving raw gold. However, mercury is highly toxic. It damages the brain, kidneys, and lungs, and it never breaks down in the environment. When burned, it becomes an invisible vapor that can be inhaled by families, including children. In water, bacteria convert it to methylmercury, which builds up in fish and can poison entire communities. Despite these dangers, many miners continue because they lack safer alternatives or training. This module will teach you the correct techniques to minimize risk, but more importantly, it will show you why you should consider moving away from mercury altogether. You will learn how to use it safely if you must, but also how to protect yourself, your family, and your community. By the end, you will understand the full process, common mistakes, and best practices. Remember: mercury is not just a toolit is a poison. Treat it with extreme respect.

2. Tools and Materials

To do mercury amalgamation safely and effectively, you need the right tools and materials. First, you need mercury itself. In Africa, it is often sold in small plastic bottles or syringes at mining supply shops. A typical amount is 100 grams, which costs about $10$20 USD depending on the country. You also need a clean, non-porous container for mixinga plastic bucket or a glass bowl works well. Avoid using aluminum or zinc containers because mercury reacts with them. You will need a sturdy stirring rod, preferably plastic or wood, not metal. For squeezing the amalgam, get a clean cotton cloth or a piece of chamois leather. A small rubber squeezer or a syringe without a needle can help remove excess mercury. For burning the amalgam, you need a retort or a fume hood. A simple retort can be made from a metal pipe and a condenser, but it is better to buy a proper one from a supplier. If you cannot get a retort, you must burn outdoors away from people and use a fan to blow vapors away. You also need personal protective equipment: nitrile gloves (not latex), safety goggles, and a respirator with a mercury vapor cartridge. A plastic apron is also good. For weighing, a small digital scale (0.1 g accuracy) helps measure mercury and gold. For storing mercury, use a sealed glass or plastic container with a tight lid, kept in a cool, dark place. Never store mercury in open containers or near food. Finally, have a spill kit ready: sulfur powder, a plastic scoop, and a sealed waste container. Sulfur binds with mercury to make it safer to clean up. All these tools should be kept away from children and animals. In many African mining communities, these items are available at local hardware stores or through mining cooperatives. If you cannot afford a retort, at least use a fume hood or burn outside. Your health is worth the investment.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps carefully to amalgamate gold with mercury. First, prepare your work area. Choose a well-ventilated spot outdoors, away from homes, children, and animals. Lay down a plastic sheet to catch spills. Put on your gloves, goggles, and respirator. Second, concentrate your gold. Mercury works best on fine, clean gold particles. Pan or sluice your ore to remove as much dirt and sand as possible. You want a heavy concentrate. Third, measure your mercury. A good ratio is 1 part mercury to 1 part gold by weight. For example, if you expect 5 grams of gold, use about 5 grams of mercury. Too much mercury makes the amalgam soft and hard to squeeze; too little leaves gold behind. Fourth, mix. Put your concentrate in a plastic bucket. Add the mercury. Add a small amount of water to cover the material. Stir gently but thoroughly for 1520 minutes. The mercury will form a silvery paste with the gold. You will see the amalgam as a shiny, heavy blob. Fifth, separate the amalgam. Pour off the water and lighter material. The amalgam will settle at the bottom. Use your hands (with gloves) to pick up the amalgam. Sixth, squeeze. Put the amalgam in a cotton cloth. Twist the cloth tightly to squeeze out excess mercury. Do this over a container to catch the mercury. Keep squeezing until the amalgam is a hard, silver-gold ball. Seventh, burn. This is the most dangerous step. Place the amalgam in a retort or on a metal plate. Heat it with a torch or over a fire. The mercury evaporates, leaving gold. If using a retort, the mercury vapor is captured and can be reused. If not, do it outdoors with a fan blowing vapors away from you. Never breathe the fumes. Eighth, cool and weigh your gold. After burning, you have a sponge-like gold button. Melt it in a crucible to get pure gold. Remember: always work with a partner, and never eat, drink, or smoke during the process.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many miners make mistakes that reduce gold recovery or increase mercury poisoning. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Using too much mercury. This makes the amalgam runny and hard to squeeze, and you lose gold in the excess mercury. Fix: Use only 1:1 ratio of mercury to expected gold. Weigh if possible. Mistake 2: Not cleaning the concentrate. Dirty ore coats gold particles and prevents mercury from touching them. Fix: Pan or sluice your material until it is mostly black sand and gold. Mistake 3: Stirring too little or too little water. Mercury needs water to move freely and contact gold. Fix: Add enough water to cover the material and stir for at least 15 minutes. Mistake 4: Squeezing with bare hands. Mercury absorbs through skin. Fix: Always wear nitrile gloves. Mistake 5: Burning without a retort or in a closed room. This releases toxic vapor that you and your family inhale. Fix: Use a retort or burn outdoors with a fan. Never burn indoors. Mistake 6: Storing mercury in open containers. It evaporates at room temperature. Fix: Keep mercury in a sealed glass or plastic bottle. Mistake 7: Not cleaning up spills. Small mercury beads can hide in cracks and continue to vaporize. Fix: Use sulfur powder on spills. It turns mercury into a black compound that is less dangerous. Sweep it up and put it in a sealed container. Mistake 8: Letting children play with mercury. It looks like a shiny toy. Fix: Keep all mercury and amalgam away from children. Educate your family about the dangers. Mistake 9: Reusing mercury without filtering. Dirty mercury works poorly. Fix: Filter mercury through a fine cloth or coffee filter before reuse. Mistake 10: Ignoring health symptoms. Tremors, memory loss, and mouth sores are signs of mercury poisoning. Fix: See a doctor immediately and stop using mercury. By avoiding these mistakes, you will get more gold and stay healthier. Remember, mercury is a cumulative poisonsmall exposures add up over time.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Mercury is one of the most toxic metals on Earth. It attacks the nervous system, kidneys, and lungs. In Africa, many miners suffer from mercury poisoning because they do not take safety seriously. This lesson will teach you best practices to protect yourself and your community. First, always wear personal protective equipment (PPE). This includes nitrile gloves (not latex, which mercury passes through), safety goggles, and a respirator with a mercury vapor cartridge. A simple dust mask is not enough. Second, work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Mercury vapor is heavier than air and can accumulate in low places. Use a fan to blow vapors away from you. Never work in a closed room or near sleeping areas. Third, use a retort. A retort captures mercury vapor and turns it back into liquid mercury, which you can reuse. This saves money and prevents pollution. If you cannot afford a retort, burn outdoors away from people and animals. Fourth, never eat, drink, or smoke while working with mercury. Wash your hands and face thoroughly with soap and water after handling mercury, even if you wore gloves. Fifth, store mercury safely. Keep it in a sealed glass or plastic container, labeled clearly. Store away from food, water, and children. Sixth, clean up spills immediately. Use sulfur powder to bind mercury, then sweep it into a sealed container. Never use a vacuum cleanerit spreads mercury vapor. Seventh, dispose of mercury waste properly. Never pour mercury down a drain or into a river. Collect waste in a sealed container and take it to a hazardous waste facility if available. In many African countries, mining cooperatives or NGOs can help with safe disposal. Eighth, educate your family and community. Mercury poisoning affects everyone, especially children and pregnant women. Methylmercury builds up in fish, so avoid eating fish from contaminated waters. Ninth, consider alternatives. There are mercury-free methods like gravity separation, borax smelting, or direct smelting. These are safer and increasingly affordable. Tenth, monitor your health. If you experience tremors, memory problems, or mouth sores, see a doctor and stop using mercury. Best practice is to stop using mercury altogether. But if you must use it, follow these rules strictly. Your life and your community's health depend on it.

6. Practical Exercise

This practical exercise will help you practice mercury amalgamation safely and effectively. You will need: 10 grams of mercury, 10 grams of gold concentrate (or clean gold flakes), a plastic bucket, a stirring rod, a cotton cloth, nitrile gloves, safety goggles, a respirator, a digital scale, and a retort or outdoor burning area. Work outdoors away from people. Step 1: Put on your PPE. Gloves, goggles, and respirator. Step 2: Weigh 10 grams of mercury and 10 grams of gold concentrate. If you don't have a scale, use a small syringe to measure mercury (10 grams is about 0.74 mL). Step 3: Place the concentrate in the bucket. Add enough water to cover it. Add the mercury. Step 4: Stir gently for 20 minutes. You will see the mercury turn into a silvery paste. Step 5: Pour off the water and lighter material. The amalgam will settle at the bottom. Step 6: Pick up the amalgam with gloved hands. Put it in the cotton cloth. Step 7: Twist the cloth tightly to squeeze out excess mercury. Collect the mercury in a container for reuse. Keep squeezing until the amalgam is a hard ball. Step 8: Weigh the amalgam. It should weigh about 1518 grams (gold plus some mercury). Step 9: Burn the amalgam. If you have a retort, use it. If not, place the amalgam on a metal plate and heat it with a torch outdoors, with a fan blowing vapors away. The mercury will evaporate, leaving a gold button. Step 10: Cool and weigh the gold. You should recover about 910 grams of gold. Step 11: Clean up. Use sulfur powder on any spills. Put waste in a sealed container. Wash your hands and face. Step 12: Record your results. How much gold did you recover? Did you lose any mercury? What could you improve? Remember: this exercise uses real mercury. Do not do it indoors. Do not let children watch. If you feel dizzy or have a headache, stop and get fresh air. Practice this exercise only under supervision if you are new. The goal is to learn the method while minimizing danger. In many African mining communities, you can practice with a local cooperative that has safety equipment. Always prioritize safety over speed.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Mercury Amalgamation in African Gold Processing

Mercury amalgamation remains one of the most widely usedand most dangerousmethods for recovering fine gold in artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM) across Africa. From the ashanti goldfields of Ghana to the copper-gold belts of Zambia, the tin-gold regions of Nigeria, and the alluvial deposits of Tanzania and Zimbabwe, mercury is still used because it is cheap, portable, and effective at capturing free gold from concentrates. For the African agribusiness entrepreneur or processing cooperative, however, mercury is not just a technical inputit is a liability, a regulatory risk, and a public health hazard that can destroy a business and a community.

This practical reference module is designed to give operators, trainers, and cooperative managers a business-first understanding of mercury amalgamation: what it is, how it is done, where it fails, and how to protect workers, communities, and commercial viability. It assumes you are already familiar with the step-by-step method covered in earlier lessons and now need a consolidated reference for field use, training, and decision-making.

Why Mercury Amalgamation Still Matters in African ASM

Mercury forms an alloyan amalgamwith gold when the two are brought into contact. The amalgam is then heated to evaporate the mercury, leaving behind a gold-mercury sponge that is further refined. In Africa, this method is used because:

  • Low capital requirement: A retort, a pan, and a few grams of mercury cost far less than a gravity separation plant or a cyanidation circuit.
  • Portability: Mercury can be carried to remote sites where electricity and water are unreliable.
  • Speed: A skilled operator can recover visible gold from a concentrate in under an hour.
  • Familiarity: Generations of miners have used mercury, creating a knowledge baseand a false sense of safety.

But the business case is weakening. Mercury is toxic, increasingly regulated under the Minamata Convention on Mercury, and a growing number of buyersincluding refiners in South Africa, the UAE, and Europeare refusing mercury-contaminated gold. For African agribusinesses that process gold as a side enterprise or as a community service, the reputational and legal risks now outweigh the short-term convenience.

SAFETY WARNING: Mercury Vapour Is Odourless, Invisible, and Deadly

Heating mercury amalgam in open air releases mercury vapour that can cause permanent brain damage, kidney failure, and death. Never burn amalgam indoors, near food, or without a retort. A single gram of mercury can contaminate a 20-acre lake. In African ASM communities, mercury poisoning often presents as tremors, memory loss, and kidney diseasesymptoms frequently misdiagnosed as malaria or stress. If you suspect mercury exposure, stop work immediately, move to fresh air, and seek medical attention. Report spills to local environmental authorities.

Quick Reference Checklist for Safe and Compliant Mercury Handling

  1. Use a retort: Always heat amalgam in a closed retort with a condenser. Never open-burn amalgam.
  2. Wear PPE: Nitrile gloves, goggles, and a respirator with mercury vapour cartridges. Change gloves after every session.
  3. Work outdoors or ventilated: Never handle mercury in enclosed rooms, kitchens, or near children.
  4. Store mercury safely: Keep in sealed, labelled, unbreakable containers away from heat and food.
  5. Capture spills: Use sulphur powder or zinc dust to bind spilled mercury; never vacuum or sweep dry.
  6. Monitor health: Annual urine mercury testing for all workers handling mercury.
  7. Document and report: Keep records of mercury purchased, used, and recovered. Report to national Minamata focal points.
  8. Plan transition: Set a business timeline to move to gravity, borax, or cyanidation alternatives within 1224 months.

Business-First Considerations for African Operators

Mercury amalgamation is not just a technical choiceit is a commercial one. Buyers in Johannesburg, Dubai, and Zurich are increasingly requiring mercury-free certification. Banks and development finance institutions are screening for environmental compliance. Insurance companies are refusing to cover mercury-related liabilities. For African agribusinesses that want to scale, the question is not whether to abandon mercury, but how quickly and at what cost.

The good news is that mercury-free alternatives are proven and increasingly affordable. Gravity separation using shaking tables and centrifuges works well for coarse gold. Borax smelting is a low-cost, low-toxicity method for fine gold. Cyanidation, when properly regulated, offers the highest recovery rates. Training programmes funded by the Global Environment Facility, UNEP, and the African Development Bank are available across Ghana, Tanzania, and Zimbabwe.

For cooperatives, the smart move is to phase out mercury while building a premium brand around responsible sourcing. Mercury-free gold can attract a price premium of 515% in ethical markets. That premium, combined with lower health costs and reduced regulatory risk, makes the transition a business casenot just a moral one.
