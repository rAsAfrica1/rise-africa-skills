1. Planning a Layout for a Two-Tap House (Kitchen and Bathroom)

Before you cut any pipe, draw a simple plan on paper. For a two-tap houseâone tap in the kitchen, one in the bathroomâyou need to decide where the water enters the house (usually from a meter or tank) and how to run pipes to each tap with the fewest bends. A typical layout: main line enters near the kitchen, then branches to the bathroom. Use a T-junction to split the line. Keep the bathroom branch as short as possible to reduce pressure loss.

Measure the distance from the entry point to each tap along the walls and floors. Add 10% extra for bends and mistakes. For example, if the kitchen tap is 5 meters away and the bathroom is 8 meters, total pipe needed is about 14.3 meters. Buy 15 meters of 20mm PVC pipe for the main line and 15 meters of 15mm pipe for the branches to each tap. You will also need elbows, tees, and adapters.

Decide whether pipes will run along walls (surface-mounted) or inside walls (concealed). Surface mounting is easier for beginners: use pipe clips every 1 meter to hold pipes straight. Concealed pipes look better but require chasing walls and careful leak testing before closing. For a simple house, surface mounting is recommended.

Plan for a shut-off valve at the entry point and another before each tap. This lets you isolate a leak without cutting water to the whole house. Also, install a drain valve at the lowest point to empty the system for repairs. Sketch the layout showing pipe sizes, fittings, and valve locations. This plan will guide your installation and help you buy the right materials.

2. Calculating Pipe Sizes for Adequate Water Pressure

Water pressure drops as it flows through pipes, especially through small pipes or many fittings. To ensure both taps get good flow, you need to choose the right pipe diameter. For a typical two-tap house with a gravity tank 5 meters above the taps, use 20mm (3/4 inch) pipe for the main line and 15mm (1/2 inch) for each branch. If the tank is lower than 3 meters, use 25mm main line to reduce friction loss.

Calculate pressure loss using simple rules: each 10 meters of 15mm pipe loses about 0.1 bar (1.5 psi) at a flow of 10 liters per minute. Each elbow or tee adds the equivalent of 0.5 meters of pipe. For example, if the bathroom branch is 8 meters with 4 elbows, total equivalent length is 8 + (4 x 0.5) = 10 meters. At 10 L/min, pressure loss is about 0.1 bar. If your tank provides 0.5 bar (5 meters height), you have 0.4 bar left at the tapâenough for a shower.

Use a pressure gauge to check actual pressure. If pressure is below 0.2 bar at the tap, increase pipe size or raise the tank. Avoid using too many fittings; each one adds loss. Also, keep pipes straight and avoid sharp bends. For gravity systems, a larger tank outlet (25mm) helps.

If you have a pressurized system (from a municipal main), pressure is usually 2-4 bar, so 15mm pipes are fine. But if pressure is high (above 4 bar), install a pressure-reducing valve to protect pipes and fittings. Always test pressure after installation. A simple bucket test: fill a 10-liter bucket and time it. If it takes more than 30 seconds, flow is poorâcheck for blockages or undersized pipes.

3. Installing a Water Meter and Main Shut-Off Valve

The water meter measures your usage and the main shut-off valve lets you stop water in an emergency. Install them at the point where water enters the house, usually after the municipal connection or tank outlet. First, dig a small pit or mount a bracket on the wall. The meter must be horizontal and in line with the pipe. Use a meter coupling to connect it to the pipe. Apply Teflon tape to all threaded connections to prevent leaks.

Shut-off valve: install a gate valve or ball valve immediately after the meter. A ball valve is better because it opens and closes with a quarter turn. Make sure the valve handle is accessible. If the valve is outside, protect it with a small box. For a gravity system from a rooftop tank, install the valve at the tank outlet.

Before installing, flush the pipe to remove dirt. Close the valve, then open a tap downstream to release pressure. Cut the pipe squarely with a pipe cutter or hacksaw, and deburr the edges. Dry-fit all parts first. Use PVC solvent cement for plastic pipes: apply a thin coat to both the pipe and fitting, push together, and hold for 30 seconds. Do not twist. Allow 24 hours to cure before turning on water.

After installation, slowly open the main valve and check for leaks. If you see a drip, tighten threaded connections or re-cement. Test the shut-off valve by closing it and opening a tapâwater should stop completely. If it doesn't, the valve may be faulty or installed backwards. Always keep a spare valve and washers on hand for repairs.

4. Running Pipes Through Walls and Floors

Running pipes through walls and floors requires careful planning to avoid damage and leaks. For surface-mounted pipes, you simply clip them to the wall. But if you want concealed pipes, you must chase grooves in the wall or drill holes through floors. Use a hammer and chisel for soft walls (blockwork) or an angle grinder with a diamond blade for concrete. Wear safety glasses and a dust mask.

For walls: cut a groove 1.5 times the pipe diameter deep and wide enough to allow a protective sleeve. Wrap the pipe in foam insulation or a plastic sleeve to allow for expansion and prevent friction. Secure the pipe with clips every 50 cm. Then fill the groove with mortar or plaster. Before closing, pressure-test the pipe (see Lesson 5).

For floors: drill holes through wooden floors using a spade bit slightly larger than the pipe. For concrete floors, use a hammer drill and masonry bit. Insert a sleeve (a short piece of larger pipe) to protect the pipe from movement. Seal around the pipe with silicone or mortar to prevent water seeping through. Never run pipes under a floor without a sleeveâthey can crack from floor movement.

When passing through external walls, slope the pipe slightly downward toward the outside so water doesn't run back into the house. Use a wall sleeve and seal with expanding foam. If you must bend a pipe, use a heat gun to soften PVC and bend it gently, or use elbow fittings. Avoid sharp bends that restrict flow. Always mark pipe locations on a plan so you don't drill into them later.

5. Testing the System for Leaks Before Closing Walls

Before you cover any pipes with plaster or floorboards, you must pressure-test the system. This step saves you from breaking open walls later. The easiest method is the water pressure test. Close all taps and the main shut-off valve. Connect a pressure test pump to a tap or a test point. Fill the system with water, then pump until the pressure reaches 1.5 times the working pressure (for gravity systems, test at 1 bar; for mains, test at 6 bar).

Watch the gauge for 30 minutes. If pressure drops more than 0.1 bar, you have a leak. Walk along the pipes and check every joint, elbow, and tee for drips. Use a dry cloth to wipe jointsâif it gets wet, that's the leak. Mark the leak with a pencil. If no leaks, the pressure should hold steady. For a simple gravity system, you can also do a bucket test: fill the tank, open all taps, and check for continuous flow without air bubbles.

If you find a leak, drain the system, dry the area, and re-cement the joint. For threaded connections, tighten or add more Teflon tape. Never try to patch a leak with tape or glue from the outsideâit will fail. After repairs, retest. Once the system passes, you can close walls and floors. But leave a small access panel near valves for future maintenance.

Also test the shut-off valves: close each one and open a downstream tap. Water should stop. If not, replace the valve. Record the test results and date. This simple step prevents costly water damage and mold growth in walls.

6. Case Study: Installing a Gravity-Fed System from a Rooftop Tank

In a village near Kisumu, Kenya, a family wanted running water in their kitchen and bathroom. They had a 1000-liter plastic tank on the roof, 5 meters above the taps. The main line from the tank to the house was 10 meters of 25mm PVC pipe. Inside, they used 20mm pipe for the main line and 15mm branches to each tap. They installed a ball valve at the tank outlet and another before each tap. Total cost: about 8,000 KES (80 USD) for pipes, fittings, and valves.

Installation steps: First, they mounted the tank on a strong wooden platform on the roof, with the outlet 10 cm above the tank bottom to avoid sediment. They ran the 25mm pipe down the outside wall, using clips every 1 meter. At the entry point, they installed a shut-off valve and a drain valve. Inside, they used a T-junction to split to the kitchen and bathroom. All pipes were surface-mounted to save labor.

They pressure-tested the system at 1 bar for 30 minutesâno leaks. Then they opened the main valve. The kitchen tap filled a 10-liter bucket in 25 seconds (flow rate 24 L/min). The bathroom shower had good pressure. Total project took two days. The family saved money by doing the work themselves after a short training.

Key lessons: Use a larger main line (25mm) to reduce pressure loss. Keep the tank at least 3 meters above the highest tap. Install valves for easy isolation. Always test before closing walls. This system now provides reliable water, reducing the need to carry buckets from a distant well.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Designing and Installing a Simple Water Supply System

In much of urban and peri-urban Africa, a reliable water supply system is not just a household convenience â it is a business asset. Whether you are a plumber serving clients, a landlord installing fittings for tenants, or an entrepreneur building a guesthouse, the ability to design, install, and maintain a simple water supply system determines your reputation and your revenue. This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the core competencies from the module: planning layouts for a two-tap house (kitchen and bathroom), sizing pipes for adequate pressure, installing a water meter and main shut-off valve, routing pipes through walls and floors, leak testing before closing walls, and a case study on gravity-fed systems from rooftop tanks. Each section is written with the African context in mind â variable municipal pressure, frequent power interruptions, and the need for cost-effective, locally available materials.

1. Planning the Layout for a Two-Tap House

A two-tap house â one tap at the kitchen sink and one at the bathroom â is the minimum viable plumbing layout for most low- to middle-income housing in Africa. The design principle is simple: minimise pipe runs, keep the main line accessible, and group wet areas where possible. In practice, the kitchen and bathroom are often on opposite sides of the house, which means the main supply line must be routed efficiently.

Start by identifying the water entry point (usually from the municipal meter at the boundary or from a rooftop tank). Mark the position of the kitchen sink and the bathroom washbasin or shower. Draw a single-line diagram showing the main line, the branch to the kitchen, and the branch to the bathroom. Use 25 mm (1 inch) nominal bore (NB) for the main line and 20 mm (Â¾ inch) for branches where pressure allows. In many African municipalities, incoming pressure is between 1 and 3 bar, but it can drop below 0.5 bar during peak hours or load shedding. Design for the worst case, not the best.

2. Calculating Pipe Sizes for Adequate Water Pressure

Pipe sizing is where many installations fail. Undersized pipes cause low pressure at the tap; oversized pipes waste money and material. The goal is to deliver at least 0.5 bar (5 metres head) at the most remote tap during peak demand. In Africa, where municipal pressure fluctuates, this often means designing for gravity or a booster pump.

Use the following rule of thumb for small domestic systems: 25 mm NB for the main line, 20 mm NB for branches to individual taps, and 15 mm NB for final connections to fixtures. If the run from the meter to the farthest tap exceeds 30 metres, increase the main line to 32 mm NB. Flow velocity should not exceed 1.5 m/s to avoid noise and erosion; 1.0 m/s is ideal.

3. Installing a Water Meter and Main Shut-Off Valve

The water meter and main shut-off valve are the two most important components after the pipe itself. The meter measures consumption and is usually installed by the utility at the boundary. The shut-off valve, however, is the installer's responsibility and should be placed immediately after the meter, inside the property, in an accessible location. In many African cities, utility meters are installed at the boundary wall, and the shut-off valve is placed in a small chamber or on an accessible wall.

Install the meter horizontally with the flow arrow pointing toward the house. Use unions on both sides so the meter can be removed for repair without cutting pipes. The shut-off valve should be a full-bore ball valve or gate valve â never a globe valve, which restricts flow. Label it clearly. In rental properties, this valve is your first line of defence against leaks and disputes.

4. Running Pipes Through Walls and Floors

In African construction, walls are often masonry (brick, block, or concrete) and floors are either concrete slab or suspended timber. Running pipes through these requires planning to avoid structural damage and future leaks. Always use sleeves â short lengths of larger pipe â where pipes pass through walls or floors. This allows movement and protects the pipe from abrasion. Seal sleeves with silicone or mortar to prevent water and pests from entering.

For buried pipes, use HDPE or PVC with a minimum cover of 300 mm in traffic areas and 150 mm in gardens. In areas with high water tables or rocky soil, consider running pipes above ground in a service duct or along the wall. In East Africa, it is common to see pipes surface-mounted on exterior walls â this is acceptable if UV-resistant PVC or painted pipes are used, and if they are insulated where frost or extreme heat is a concern.

â  SAFETY WARNING: Never run water pipes through load-bearing beams, columns, or foundations without engineering approval. Cutting reinforcement steel or creating deep chases in structural elements can cause collapse. In seismic or expansive soil zones, use flexible connections at entry points. Always isolate the water supply before cutting into any pipe, and wear eye protection when working with PVC solvent cement â the fumes are toxic and flammable.

5. Testing the System for Leaks Before Closing Walls

Leak testing is non-negotiable. Once walls are closed, finding a leak means demolition. The standard test is a hydrostatic pressure test: cap all outlets, fill the system with water, and pressurise to 1.5 times the working pressure (minimum 6 bar for domestic systems) for at least 30 minutes. A drop of more than 0.5 bar indicates a leak. In gravity-fed systems, test by filling the tank and observing the system under static head for 24 hours.

For PVC systems, solvent-welded joints must cure for at least 24 hours before testing. For threaded joints, use PTFE tape and tighten firmly â but not so tight that you crack the fitting. In Africa, where water is precious, even a slow drip wastes hundreds of litres per month and damages walls. Test every branch, every joint, and every fixture connection.

6. Case Study: Installing a Gravity-Fed System from a Rooftop Tank

In many parts of Africa, municipal supply is intermittent. A rooftop tank solves this by storing water during supply hours and delivering it by gravity during outages. Consider a two-tap house in Kampala, Uganda, where municipal water runs from 6 AM to 10 AM only. The installer mounts a 1,000-litre tank on a 3-metre stand on the roof. The tank outlet is 25 mm NB, feeding a main line that drops to the kitchen and bathroom.

With a 3-metre head (tank bottom to tap), the static pressure is approximately 0.3 bar â enough for a kitchen tap and a shower, but marginal for a washing machine. The installer uses 25 mm NB for the main line and 20 mm NB for branches, keeping runs short. A float valve controls tank filling, and a shut-off valve at the tank outlet allows maintenance. The system cost approximately 40% less than a pumped system and requires no electricity â critical in areas with load shedding.

The business lesson: gravity-fed systems are a viable, low-cost product for African plumbers. They require careful sizing and a good tank stand, but they are reliable, cheap to maintain, and highly valued by customers. Market them as âpower-free water security.â
