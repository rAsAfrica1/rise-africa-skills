Lesson 1

Why Groundnut Commands a Premium

In this lesson
  • Explain why groundnut prices are 2–4x other legumes
  • Understand the dual food-and-oil market
  • Recognise the aflatoxin risk that comes with the premium

Groundnut sells for $0.60–1.20/kg at the local market, up to $2.00/kg in urban retail, and $2.50–4.00/kg as export-grade kernels. That is 2–4x the price of common bean and 3–5x the price of cowpea. The reason is simple: groundnut is both a food and an oil crop, and the world always wants both.

It is grown across the Sahel, West Africa, East Africa and parts of southern Africa. Nigeria, Senegal, Sudan, Tanzania, Malawi and Zambia are the largest producers. The global market for peanut products — oil, butter, flour, snacks — is worth tens of billions of dollars, and Africa supplies a fraction of what it could.

The premium is real. So is the reason many African farmers miss it: aflatoxin. One bad test and the entire crop — up to 10 tonnes — is rejected by the processor or exporter. Module 4 is dedicated entirely to preventing aflatoxin, and every module that follows returns to it.

Lesson 2

The Four Types — Virginia, Spanish, Valencia, Runner

In this lesson
  • Distinguish the four groundnut types by use and market
  • Match the type to your rainfall zone
  • Choose the type that fits your buyer

Virginia

Large-seeded, spreading or bunch type. 120–150 days. Higher yielding in good rainfall. Preferred for in-shell roasting, confectionery and premium kernel markets. Most common in West Africa and parts of East Africa.

Spanish

Small-seeded, upright (bunch). 90–110 days. Drought-tolerant. Preferred for oil extraction and peanut butter. Common in Senegal, Nigeria and southern Africa.

Valencia

Three-seeded pods. 100–130 days. Sweeter, favoured for direct consumption and roasting. Smaller market but premium prices.

Runner

Spreading type with high yield. 130–150 days. Grown in the USA for peanut butter. Some improved African varieties use runner genetics.

Choosing your type

  • Short rains (300–500 mm): Spanish types (maturity escape)
  • Medium rainfall (500–800 mm): Virginia or Valencia
  • Long rains, high yield: Virginia or Runner
  • Oil market: Spanish
  • Premium in-shell market: Virginia or Valencia
Lesson 3

Yield Reality and the African Gap

In this lesson
  • Understand the African average vs achievable yield
  • Identify the four yield-closing factors
  • Set a realistic first-season target

African average groundnut yield sits at 0.6–0.9 t/ha of shelled kernels. Improved varieties with calcium, seed treatment and disease control reach 1.5–2.5 t/ha. Under irrigation and full management, 3+ t/ha is recorded.

Four factors that close the gap

  1. Certified seed. African smallholders often plant seed saved for 3+ seasons with virus and fungal load. Certified seed yields 30–60% more.
  2. Calcium at pegging. Groundnut is unique — pods form underground, and calcium must reach the pegging zone. Gypsum is the answer. Missing this single input caps yield at 60% of potential.
  3. Rapid drying after lifting. Slow drying creates aflatoxin and destroys the crop's market value. Covered here and in Module 5.
  4. Disease control in the first 6 weeks. Early leaf spot and rosette virus are the biggest threats. Module 4 covers them.

Lesson 4

Aflatoxin — the Sentence Every Buyer Reads

In this lesson
  • Understand what aflatoxin is and why it matters
  • Know the regulatory limits
  • Understand when aflatoxin forms — and when it does not

Aflatoxin is a toxin produced by Aspergillus fungi. Two species — Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus — grow on groundnut and produce aflatoxin when conditions allow. Aflatoxin causes liver cancer in humans, stunting in children, and chronic illness in livestock. It is one of the most dangerous food contaminants in the world.

Regulatory limits

  • EU (food): total aflatoxins 4 ppb, aflatoxin B1 2 ppb
  • USA (food): total aflatoxins 20 ppb
  • Codex (international): 15 ppb for processed peanuts
  • Most African countries: follow Codex or their national standard

An African farmer selling to a food processor or exporter who tests at 15 ppb has a crop if the reading is 8 ppb and no crop if the reading is 20 ppb. One failed test can cost the entire season.

When does aflatoxin form?

Aflatoxin does not form in a healthy field at harvest. It forms in five situations:

  1. Drought stress during pod fill, when the pod cracks and the fungus enters
  2. Pod damage from termites, white grubs, weevil, or poor lifting
  3. Slow drying after lifting — pods piled wet for days
  4. Pest damage during storage — insects create entry points
  5. Moisture in storage — above 9% kernel moisture, fungus grows

Fix all five and aflatoxin does not form. That is the whole strategy: prevent stress, prevent damage, dry fast, store dry.

The rule: aflatoxin is a preventable problem, not a random one. Farmers who lose crops to aflatoxin are usually harvesting too late, drying too slowly, or storing too damp. Fix those three and you fix the problem.
Lesson 5

The Five Markets for Groundnut

In this lesson
  • Name the five buyer types for groundnut
  • Match grade to buyer
  • Understand the price ladder from farm gate to retail

Market 1 — Local market (in-shell and kernels)

Households, market vendors, roadside sellers. Cash. Prices $0.60–1.20/kg. The easiest channel and the baseline price.

Market 2 — Urban retailers and supermarkets

Buy cleaned, graded kernels in 500g or 1kg packs. Price $1.20–2.00/kg. Require business registration and standard packaging.

Market 3 — Peanut butter and snack processors

Buy kernels in 5–50 tonne lots. Price $0.80–1.40/kg. Require aflatoxin test below their limit (usually 10–20 ppb). Payment on delivery or 30 days.

Market 4 — Oil millers

Buy lower-grade kernels for oil extraction. Price $0.60–1.00/kg. Accept higher aflatoxin for oil (toxin stays in the cake, not the oil) but require specific moisture and oil content.

Market 5 — Export

Buy in 20-tonne containers. Price $2.50–4.00/kg. Require aflatoxin below 4 ppb (EU) or below 15 ppb (Middle East/Asia). Require uniform grade, moisture below 7%, and phytosanitary documentation.

Knowledge check — Module 1

1. What is the African average groundnut yield?

Correct: 0.6–0.9 t/ha. The achievable figure is 1.5–2.5 t/ha.

2. What single input caps yield at 60% of potential if missing?

Correct: gypsum. Calcium must reach the pegging zone.

3. What is the EU regulatory limit for total aflatoxin in food?

Correct: 4 ppb. The strictest major market.

4. When does aflatoxin form?

Correct: all four conditions. Prevention is a season-long discipline.

5. Which groundnut type is preferred for oil extraction?

Correct: Spanish. High oil content and drought tolerance.
