1. Routine Maintenance Schedule

A solar system needs regular care to keep working for years. In Africa, dust, heat, and rainy seasons make maintenance even more important. Follow this schedule to avoid surprises.

Daily: Check your system's basic indicators. Look at the charge controller or inverter display. If it shows a red light or error code, write it down. Listen for unusual sounds like buzzing or clicking. At the battery bank, smell for rotten eggs (that means battery acid is boiling).

Weekly: Clean the solar panels if you see dust buildup. In dry, dusty areas like the Sahel, clean them every 3-4 days during harmattan. Use a soft cloth or sponge with plain water. Never use soap or abrasive cleaners. Also check that all cables are tight and not chewed by rodents.

Monthly: Inspect the battery terminals for corrosion (white or green powder). Clean with a wire brush and apply a thin layer of petroleum jelly. Check battery water level if you have flooded lead-acid batteries. Top up with distilled water only, never tap water. Test the system voltage with a multimeter: a 12V battery should read 12.6V when full. Measure panel output: on a sunny day, a 100W panel should produce about 18V open-circuit.

Quarterly: Tighten all mounting bolts on panels and racking. Check the inverter and charge controller for dust inside; blow it out with a dry cloth or soft brush. Inspect all cable insulation for cracks or sun damage.

Yearly: Do a full system audit. Check the battery capacity by doing a load test. Replace any worn cables. Clean the panel frames and remove any bird nests. Re-torque all electrical connections to the manufacturer's specs (usually 1.5-2 Nm for small terminals).

Write every action in a logbook with date, time, and what you did. This helps you spot patterns and proves to customers you are professional. Remember: a well-maintained system can last 10-15 years for panels and 5-7 years for batteries. Skipping maintenance cuts that in half.

2. Cleaning and Inspecting Solar Panels

Dirty panels can lose 20-30% of their power. In Africa, dust, bird droppings, and leaves are the main culprits. Cleaning is simple but must be done correctly to avoid damage.

Best time to clean: early morning or late afternoon when panels are cool. Never clean hot panels with cold waterthe glass can crack. Use a soft sponge or microfiber cloth and plain water. If water is scarce, use a dry soft brush first, then a damp cloth. For stubborn bird droppings, soak with water for 5 minutes, then wipe gently. Do not use abrasive pads, soap, or detergentsthey leave residue that attracts more dust.

How to clean: Start at the top and work down. Rinse the panel with water to remove loose dirt. Wipe in straight lines, not circles, to avoid streaks. Rinse again. For a 300W panel (about 1m x 1.6m), you need about 2 liters of water. If you have many panels, a small bucket and a long-handled soft brush work well.

Inspection checklist: After cleaning, look for:
- Cracks or chips in the glass. Even a small crack lets water in and corrodes the cells.
- Hot spots: feel the panel surface (with the back of your hand, not palm) for unusually hot areas. Hot spots mean cell damage.
- Discoloration: brown or yellow patches indicate overheating or failing cells.
- Frame and mounting: check for rust, loose bolts, or bent rails. Tighten bolts to 1.5 Nm.
- Cables and connectors: look for frayed wires, cracked MC4 connectors, or exposed copper. Replace any damaged part immediately.
- Junction box: ensure the lid is sealed. Water inside means a short circuit.

If you find a cracked panel, cover it with a tarp and disconnect it from the system. A damaged panel can drag down the whole array. Replace it with a panel of the same voltage and wattage. For small cracks, you can use silicone sealant as a temporary fix, but plan to replace within a month.

Frequency: In dusty regions, clean weekly. In rainy season, clean after each dust storm. Always inspect after heavy rain or strong winds. Keep a cleaning log: date, panels cleaned, and any damage found. This protects your investment and your reputation.

3. Checking and Replacing Components

Solar system components wear out. Knowing how to test and replace them saves money and downtime. You need a digital multimeter (cost: $10-20), a screwdriver set, and replacement parts.

Batteries: Test voltage with a multimeter. A 12V battery at rest should read 12.6V (full), 12.2V (50%), 11.8V (empty). If it reads below 11.8V after charging, it's failing. Check water level in flooded batteries: plates should be covered by 1 cm of distilled water. If water is low, top up. If a battery is swollen, cracked, or smells like rotten eggs, replace it immediately. When replacing, match voltage and amp-hour (Ah) rating. For a 100Ah system, use a 100Ah battery. Connect positive to positive, negative to negative. Tighten terminals to 5 Nm.

Charge Controller: Check the display for error codes. Common codes: E1 (battery low), E2 (overvoltage), E3 (overload). If the controller is hot to touch (above 60C), it's overloaded. Test with a multimeter: input from panels should be 18-22V for a 12V system. Output to battery should be 13.8-14.4V during charging. If no output, replace the controller. Match the controller's amp rating to your panel array. A 30A controller handles up to 360W of panels at 12V.

Inverter: Test AC output with a multimeter set to AC volts. It should read 220-240V (or 110-120V depending on region). If it reads 0V, check the fuse first. If the fuse is good, the inverter is dead. Also listen for loud hummingthat means transformer failure. Replace with an inverter of same or higher wattage. For a 1kW inverter, do not load more than 800W continuous.

Cables and Connectors: Look for blackened or melted insulation. Test continuity with a multimeter: touch probes to both ends of a cable; a good cable reads near 0 ohms. If it reads infinite, the cable is broken. Replace with same gauge wire. For a 10A circuit, use at least 4mm cable. MC4 connectors: pull apart, check for corrosion. Clean with contact cleaner, then reconnect until you hear a click.

Always turn off the system (disconnect panels first, then battery) before replacing any component. After replacement, test the whole system: panel voltage, battery voltage, and load operation. Keep spare fuses, MC4 connectors, and a spare charge controller on hand.

4. Diagnosing System Faults

When a solar system stops working, don't guess. Follow a step-by-step diagnosis. You need a multimeter, a notebook, and patience.

Step 1: Talk to the user. Ask: When did it stop? Was there a storm? Did you add a new appliance? What lights are on? This gives clues.

Step 2: Check the obvious. Is the inverter switch on? Are the panels shaded by a new tree or building? Is the battery switch on? Are any circuit breakers tripped? Reset breakers once. If they trip again, there's a short.

Step 3: Test the panels. Disconnect panels from the charge controller. Measure open-circuit voltage (Voc) with a multimeter. For a 12V panel, Voc should be 18-22V in full sun. If it reads 0V, check for broken cables or a blown fuse in the combiner box. If it reads low (e.g., 10V), a panel may be shaded or damaged. Test each panel individually.

Step 4: Test the battery. Measure voltage at the battery terminals. If below 11.8V, charge it with a grid charger or another source. If it won't hold charge (drops quickly under load), replace it. Also check specific gravity with a hydrometer for flooded batteries: full charge is 1.265, half is 1.190. If cells differ by more than 0.050, battery is failing.

Step 5: Test the charge controller. With panels connected and battery connected, measure voltage at controller's battery terminals. It should be 13.8-14.4V during charging. If not, check controller settings (bulk, absorption, float). If settings are correct but no output, replace controller.

Step 6: Test the inverter. Measure AC output. If 0V, check DC input at inverter terminals. Should match battery voltage. If DC is good but no AC, inverter is faulty.

Step 7: Test the load. Plug in a known good appliance (e.g., a phone charger). If it works, the original appliance is faulty. If not, check wiring from inverter to outlets.

Common faults in Africa: dust on panels (low power), loose battery terminals (intermittent), corroded MC4 connectors (high resistance), and over discharged batteries (dead). Write down every measurement. Compare with normal values. If you find a fault, fix it and retest. If you can't find it, call a more experienced technician. Never leave a system in a fault stateit can damage other components.

5. Using Monitoring Systems

Monitoring systems tell you how your solar system is performing without climbing on the roof. They range from simple built-in displays to phone apps. In Africa, where internet may be limited, choose a system that works offline or with SMS.

Basic monitoring: Most charge controllers and inverters have a small LCD screen. It shows battery voltage, charging current, and error codes. Check it daily. Write down the numbers in your logbook. For example, a 12V system should show 13.5-14.4V during charging and 12.6V at rest. If you see 11.5V, the battery is low.

Smart monitoring: Devices like Victron BMV or Renogy Bluetooth modules connect to your phone. They log data and send alerts. Cost: $30-100. They work via Bluetooth (up to 10m) or Wi-Fi. If you have internet, you can check from anywhere. For off-grid, some use GSM modules that send SMS alerts for low battery or fault codes. Set alerts for: battery below 11.8V, inverter overload, and panel voltage low.

What to monitor:
- Battery voltage: should be 12.6V (full) to 11.8V (empty).
- Charging current: on a sunny day, a 100W panel gives about 5-6A at 12V.
- Load current: know your appliances. A 10W LED bulb draws 0.8A at 12V. A 50W TV draws 4A.
- Energy produced: daily kWh. A 300W system in good sun produces about 1.2-1.5 kWh per day.
- Error codes: write them down and look up in manual.

How to use data: If you see battery voltage dropping faster than usual, something is wrong. Maybe a cloudless day but low charge? Check for dirty panels. If load current is higher than normal, a new appliance may be drawing too much. If error codes appear, address them immediately.

For community systems: Assign one person to check the monitor every morning. Record data in a shared logbook. If the system has remote monitoring, set up weekly reports via SMS or WhatsApp. This helps catch faults early and reduces downtime. Remember: monitoring is not a replacement for physical inspection. Still clean panels and check connections monthly.

6. Creating Maintenance Logs

A maintenance log is a written record of everything you do to your solar system. It helps you remember what was done, when, and by whom. It also shows customers you are professional. In Africa, where systems are often shared, logs prevent arguments and keep everyone accountable.

What to include: Use a simple notebook or a printed template. For each entry, write:
- Date and time
- Your name
- What you did (e.g., cleaned panels, checked battery water)
- Measurements (battery voltage, panel voltage, load current)
- Any problems found (e.g., cracked MC4 connector)
- Actions taken (e.g., replaced connector)
- Parts used (e.g., 1 MC4 connector)
- Next scheduled maintenance

Example entry: "12/05/2025, 8:00 AM, John. Cleaned 4 panels (dusty). Battery voltage 12.4V. Found corrosion on negative terminal. Cleaned with wire brush, applied petroleum jelly. Next clean: 19/05/2025."

Why it matters: Logs help you spot trends. If battery voltage is slowly dropping each week, you know it's time to replace. If you see repeated inverter errors, you can check if it's overloaded. Logs also help when you sell the systembuyers want to see maintenance history. For community systems, a logbook in a central place (e.g., school office) lets everyone see the system is cared for.

How to keep it simple: Use a table with columns: Date, Task, Readings, Notes. Keep a pen tied to the logbook. For smartphone users, use a free app like Google Sheets or a simple notes app. Take photos of any damage and attach them. At the end of each month, review the log. If you see many faults, consider upgrading components.

Training others: Teach one person per community to fill the log. Make it part of their daily routine. Reward good record-keeping. A well-kept log is proof of a well-maintained system. It can also help you get paid maintenance contracts. Remember: if it's not written down, it didn't happen.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Maintenance & Troubleshooting for Solar Installations in African Communities

In off-grid and weak-grid African markets, a solar installation is only as valuable as its uptime. A household system that fails during the rainy season means lost lighting, missed phone charging income, spoiled vaccines at a clinic, or a cold refrigerator at a rural shop. For the technician or micro-entrepreneur running a solar business, maintenance is not a cost centre it is the core of your reputation, your referral pipeline, and your recurring revenue.

This Practical Reference consolidates the seven lessons in this module into field-ready procedures. It is written for the realities of African deployment: dusty harmattan seasons, high ambient temperatures, variable load growth, limited spare-part supply chains, and customers who pay for reliability, not for theory.

1. Routine Maintenance Schedule

Build the schedule around the customer's usage and the local climate. In dusty Sahelian or semi-arid zones, panel cleaning moves from monthly to fortnightly. In humid coastal areas (Lagos, Mombasa, Accra), corrosion inspection on terminals and enclosures becomes the priority. The schedule should be agreed in writing at installation and priced into an annual service contract this is where recurring revenue lives.

2. Cleaning and Inspecting Solar Panels

Dust, bird droppings, and harmattan haze can cut yield by 1530% within weeks. Clean early morning or late afternoon never pour cold water on hot panels. Use a soft brush or microfibre cloth with plain water; avoid abrasive pads and detergents that leave residue. Inspect for micro-cracks, hot spots (use an IR thermometer), and shading from new construction or growing trees.

3. Checking and Replacing Components

Components fail in a predictable order in African field conditions: batteries first, then charge controllers, then inverters, then panels. Keep a small stock of fuses, MC4 connectors, cable glands, and one spare controller for your most common system size. Replace like-for-like; upgrading a component without checking the rest of the system causes cascading failures.

4. Diagnosing System Faults

Use a structured method: Observe Measure Isolate Verify. Start at the load (what is not working?), move to the battery (voltage under load), then the controller (error codes), then the array (open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current). Most "dead system" calls in the field trace to a blown fuse, a loose terminal, or a deeply discharged battery not a failed panel.

SAFETY WARNING ELECTRICAL & BATTERY HAZARDS

PV arrays generate DC voltage whenever exposed to light they cannot be "switched off" at the panel. Always cover panels or work at dawn/dusk before disconnecting DC circuits. Battery banks can deliver hundreds of amps into a short circuit and can explode if a tool bridges terminals; remove metal jewellery, use insulated tools, and never smoke near lead-acid batteries (they vent hydrogen). Vent battery rooms. Use appropriate PPE: insulating gloves, safety glasses, and non-conductive footwear. If you are not trained and certified for the voltage class you are working on, stop and refer the job to a licensed electrician.

5. Using Monitoring Systems

Monitoring turns reactive repairs into proactive service. Even a basic Bluetooth charge controller app that logs daily yield and battery state-of-charge lets you spot a failing battery weeks before the customer calls. For larger systems, GSM-enabled monitoring with SMS alerts is now affordable across most of Africa and pays for itself in reduced site visits.

6. Creating Maintenance Logs

The maintenance log is your business asset. It documents warranty claims, proves service delivery to the customer, supports financing applications for inventory, and reveals patterns (e.g., every battery in a village failing at year three switch supplier). Keep both a paper copy on site and a digital copy on your phone or laptop. A simple log includes: date, technician, task performed, readings (voltage, current, SoC), parts replaced, and next service date.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm customer's reported fault and safety status before touching any conductor.
  2. Cover panels or work at dawn/dusk before disconnecting DC circuits.
  3. Clean panels with plain water and soft cloth; inspect for cracks and shading.
  4. Check and torque battery terminals; look for corrosion and swelling.
  5. Read charge controller and inverter fault logs; record error codes.
  6. Measure open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current on each string.
  7. Replace only like-for-like components; verify the rest of the system.
  8. Complete the maintenance log, photograph the site, and schedule the next visit.
