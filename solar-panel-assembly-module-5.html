1. Preparing EVA Encapsulant and Backsheet

EVA (ethylene vinyl acetate) and backsheet are the protective layers that seal your solar cells. In Africa, humidity and dust are your enemies, so preparation is critical. Start by checking that your EVA roll is within its expiry dateold EVA turns yellow and won't cross-link properly. Cut EVA and backsheet to size: they should extend 5-10 mm beyond the glass edges on all sides. For a standard 60-cell panel (1.6 m x 1.0 m), cut EVA sheets of 1.62 m x 1.02 m. Use a clean, dry tablewipe it with isopropyl alcohol. Wear gloves to avoid oils from your fingers. Store EVA in a sealed bag with desiccant; if it absorbs moisture, it will bubble during lamination. Backsheet (usually white or black TPT) must be cut with the shiny side facing up (the side that goes against EVA). Check for wrinkles or creasesthese cause delamination. In dusty areas, work in a clean room or use a plastic sheet tent. Cut with sharp scissors or a rotary cutter. Label each piece with the panel ID. Prepare a stack: glass, EVA, cells, EVA, backsheet. Keep EVA covered until layup to prevent dust. If you're in a coastal region, pre-bake EVA at 50C for 30 minutes to remove moisturebut don't exceed 60C or it will start cross-linking. Always have spare EVA and backsheet; mistakes happen. For 10 panels, order 15% extra. Remember: clean hands, clean surfaces, no moisture.

2. Layering Glass, EVA, Cells, EVA, and Backsheet

Layering is like making a sandwich: glass on the bottom, then EVA, then solar cells (stringed and interconnected), then another EVA, then backsheet on top. Work on a flat, clean surfacea dedicated layup table is best. First, place tempered glass (3.2 mm thick) with the textured side (the side that faces the sun) down. Wipe glass with a lint-free cloth and alcohol. Next, lay the first EVA sheet on the glass, frosted side up. Smooth out any air pockets with a roller. Now carefully position your interconnected cell strings. Cells must be centered with equal spacingtypically 2-3 mm between cells. Use a jig or marks on the glass to align. Connect busbars if not already done. Once cells are placed, lay the second EVA sheet on top, frosted side down. Again, roll out air. Finally, place the backsheet on top, shiny side down (the side that bonds to EVA). The backsheet should overhang the glass by 5 mm on all sides. Check alignment: all layers must be flush. If you're in a windy area, work in a screened area or use a temporary cover. Avoid touching EVA with bare handsuse gloves. For large panels, get a helper. After layup, trim any excess EVA or backsheet with a sharp blade. Then, before moving to the laminator, secure the stack with heat-resistant tape at the corners. This prevents shifting. Remember: any dust or hair between layers will show as a defect. Work fast but carefully. A typical layup takes 10-15 minutes for a 60-cell panel.

3. Setting Up and Operating a Laminator

A solar laminator is a large vacuum oven with a flexible membrane. In Africa, many small workshops use second-hand Chinese laminators (e.g., 2.2 m x 1.2 m). First, check the machine: clean the chamber, inspect the membrane for holes, and ensure vacuum pump oil is clean. Set the temperature controller to 150C for standard EVA. Preheat the laminator for 30 minutes. Place your layered panel on the loading tray, ensuring it's centered. The glass should be on the bottom. Close the lid and start the vacuum pump. The vacuum removes air from between layers. Typical vacuum is -0.1 MPa (absolute). Once vacuum is reached (about 2-3 minutes), the membrane presses down. Now, the heating cycle begins: ramp up to 150C and hold for 8-10 minutes. During this time, EVA melts and cross-links. Watch the temperature and vacuum gaugesif vacuum drops, you have a leak. After the hold, start cooling: turn off heaters and keep vacuum on. Cooling can be accelerated with a fan or water coils. Cool to below 60C before releasing vacuum. Then, open the lid and remove the panel. For a 60-cell panel, total cycle time is 20-25 minutes. In hot climates, cooling may take longer; use a timer. Always wear heat-resistant gloves. If you're off-grid, ensure your generator can handle the laminator's load (typically 3-5 kW). Keep a log of each cycle: date, panel ID, temperature, vacuum, time. This helps troubleshoot. Never open the lid while hotit causes warping. After 50 cycles, clean the membrane with silicone spray.

4. Controlling Temperature and Vacuum During Lamination

Temperature and vacuum are the two knobs that determine lamination quality. Too little heat: EVA won't cross-link, leading to delamination. Too much heat: bubbles, yellowing, or cell damage. Standard EVA cures at 150C for 8-10 minutes. But in Africa, ambient temperatures can be 35C, so your laminator may overshoot. Use a calibrated thermocouple placed on the glass surface, not just the machine's display. Vacuum must be strong enough to remove all air. A vacuum of -0.1 MPa (or 100 kPa) is ideal. If you see bubbles, vacuum is likely insufficient. Check for leaks: worn gaskets, dirty seals, or a torn membrane. During the heating phase, vacuum should be maintained continuously. As EVA melts, it releases volatiles; vacuum pulls them out. If vacuum drops, air enters and causes bubbles. Use a vacuum gauge with a needle valve to monitor. For thick backsheets or large panels, you may need a two-stage vacuum: first -0.05 MPa for 2 minutes, then full vacuum. Temperature ramp rate: 5-10C per minute. Too fast causes uneven curing. Hold at 150C for 8 minutes for 3.2 mm glass. Then cool under vacuum to 60C. In humid conditions, pre-dry EVA at 50C for 30 minutes. If you're using a fast-cure EVA, follow the datasheetsome cure in 5 minutes. Always keep a log. If you see bubbles after lamination, you can sometimes re-laminate, but it's risky. Prevention is better: calibrate your controller monthly with an external thermometer. Use a vacuum pump with a capacity of at least 100 m/h for large panels. In dusty environments, cover the laminator when not in use.

5. Cooling and Removing Laminated Panels

After the heating cycle, your panel is at 150C. Do not open the laminator! Cooling must happen under vacuum to prevent bubbles. Turn off the heaters and keep the vacuum pump running. The panel will cool naturally, but you can speed it up with a fan blowing on the laminator's exterior or by circulating cool water through coils. In hot African climates, cooling from 150C to 60C can take 15-20 minutes. Use a timer and check the temperature with an infrared thermometer through a viewing port if available. Never spray water directly on the hot glassit will crack. Once the panel reaches 60C or below, release the vacuum slowly. If you release too fast, the sudden pressure change can cause delamination. Open the lid and carefully lift the panel using heat-resistant gloves. Place it on a clean, flat surface to cool further. The panel is still warm; let it cool to room temperature before handling cells. Inspect the edges: EVA should be slightly squeezed out, indicating good flow. Trim excess EVA with a sharp knife. If you see any bubbles or delamination now, mark the panel for rework. Stack cooled panels with paper or foam between them to prevent scratches. In dusty areas, cover panels immediately. For quality control, record the cooling time and final temperature. If you're producing many panels, a cooling rack with forced air can reduce cycle time. Remember: patience here saves you from costly defects. A properly cooled panel will have clear, bubble-free encapsulation.

6. Inspecting Laminated Panels for Bubbles and Delamination

Inspection is your last chance to catch defects before the panel is framed and sold. Work in a well-lit area, preferably with a bright LED light. First, visual inspection: look for bubbles, delamination, and foreign particles. Bubbles appear as small air pockets under the glass or between layers. Delamination is when layers separate, often at edges. Use a flashlight at a low angle to highlight bubbles. For a 60-cell panel, any bubble larger than 2 mm is a reject. Check edges: EVA should be continuous, no gaps. If you see white spots, that's moisture or incomplete cross-linking. Next, tap test: gently tap the panel with a coin. A dull thud indicates delamination; a clear ring means good bond. For critical applications, use a handheld vacuum leak detector or a thermal camerabubbles show as hot spots. If you find bubbles, you can sometimes re-laminate, but it's often cheaper to reject. Document defects with photos and panel ID. Common causes: dirty EVA, insufficient vacuum, or wrong temperature. In Africa, dust is a major culpritinspect in a clean room. Also check for cell cracks: use an electroluminescence (EL) tester if available, but visual cracks are often visible. If a panel passes, clean it with isopropyl alcohol and apply edge sealant (silicone) to prevent moisture ingress. Store panels in a dry place. For every 10 panels, expect 1-2 defects when starting out. Keep a log of defects and review weekly to improve your process. Remember: a bubble today becomes a failed panel in the field.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Lamination Process for Solar Panel Assembly

Lamination is the step where a solar panel stops being a stack of loose materials and becomes a single, weather-sealed power generator. For solar assembly businesses across Africa from Lagos to Nairobi to Accra this is where your margin is won or lost. A failed laminate means wasted EVA, wasted cells, wasted labour, and a customer who will not pay. Getting lamination right the first time is not just a technical requirement; it is a cash-flow requirement. This practical reference gives you the numbers, settings, and checks that experienced assembly teams use to keep yield high and rework low.

Why Lamination Matters for African Assembly Businesses

In Europe or China, lamination failures are absorbed by large factories. In a 550 kW/month assembly workshop in Africa, a 10% laminate reject rate can wipe out your profit on a whole batch. Common failure drivers in hot, humid, and dusty environments include:

  • Moisture ingress before lamination EVA absorbs water in humid climates, causing bubbles and delamination during curing.
  • Dust contamination a single particle between layers creates a stress point and a visible defect.
  • Temperature overshoot cheap or uncalibrated laminators can exceed 160 C, degrading EVA and backsheet.
  • Inconsistent vacuum power fluctuations (common on grid-tied workshops) reduce vacuum pump performance.

The practical reference below gives you the operating window that works reliably in tropical and semi-arid African conditions.

Preparing EVA Encapsulant and Backsheet

EVA (ethylene-vinyl acetate) is hygroscopic. In a coastal workshop in Mombasa or Dar es Salaam, ambient humidity above 60% RH will degrade EVA within hours of opening the sealed roll. Store EVA in sealed foil bags with desiccant. Once opened, use within 8 hours in humid conditions or 24 hours in dry conditions (Sahel, highland Kenya, Botswana).

Backsheet (typically TPT or PET-PE-PET) is less sensitive but must be kept clean and free of creases. Cut both EVA sheets and backsheet to size with at least 1015 mm overhang beyond the glass edge. This overhang prevents glass-to-glass contact and allows the EVA to flow and seal the edges.

Layering Glass, EVA, Cells, EVA, and Backsheet

Layering is a clean-room discipline even in a small workshop. Work on a stainless steel or glass layup table wiped with isopropyl alcohol. The sequence is fixed:

  1. Glass tempered, low-iron, 3.2 mm standard. Place textured side (the side that will face the sun) down. Check for chips or cracks.
  2. Bottom EVA place over glass, smooth out wrinkles by hand or with a soft roller.
  3. Solar cells stringed and laid with busbars aligned. Handle by edges only; oils from fingers cause delamination.
  4. Top EVA place over cells, again smoothing wrinkles.
  5. Backsheet matte or shiny side orientation per manufacturer; typically the white side faces outward.

Use a layup template or jig to keep cells aligned. In dusty workshops, use a portable HEPA filter or at minimum a clean, enclosed layup tent. A single dust particle under the glass is a permanent visible defect.

Setting Up and Operating a Laminator

Most small-to-medium African assembly businesses use a single-chamber or dual-chamber laminator. Key setup steps:

  • Level the machine a 1 tilt causes uneven pressure and EVA flow.
  • Calibrate temperature use an external thermocouple or IR thermometer; do not trust the display alone.
  • Test vacuum with an empty tray, pull vacuum and check the gauge reaches < 1 mbar within 60 seconds.
  • Clean the diaphragm EVA residue from previous runs causes surface defects.
  • Preheat bring the chamber to 100 C before loading the panel.

For a typical fast-cure EVA, the lamination cycle is:

  • Pump down: 60120 seconds to < 1 mbar
  • Heat ramp: to 140150 C at 510 C/min
  • Cure hold: 812 minutes at 140150 C
  • Cool down: to < 60 C before opening (natural or forced air)

SAFETY WARNING High Temperature and Vacuum Hazard

Laminator chambers reach 150 C. Never open a hot chamber. Always wear heat-resistant gloves (rated to 200 C) and safety glasses. The vacuum pump and chamber can implode if the diaphragm is damaged inspect before every run. In workshops with unstable grid power, use a surge protector and never bypass the emergency stop. EVA fumes at high temperature can irritate lungs ensure ventilation or extraction. Keep a dry powder fire extinguisher within 3 metres of the laminator.

Controlling Temperature and Vacuum During Lamination

Temperature and vacuum are the two variables that determine whether your panel passes or fails. In African workshops, both are under threat: ambient temperatures of 3545 C can push chamber temperature above setpoint, and voltage drops can slow the vacuum pump.

Temperature control: Use a PID controller with an external thermocouple placed on the glass surface, not just the chamber air. Target 140150 C for fast-cure EVA. Above 155 C, EVA degrades and yellowing begins. Below 135 C, cure is incomplete and delamination follows within months.

Vacuum control: Target < 1 mbar during cure. A vacuum of 510 mbar leaves air pockets that become bubbles. If your pump cannot reach < 1 mbar, check for leaks in the diaphragm, seals, and hose connections. In areas with frequent power cuts, consider a small UPS for the vacuum pump and controller.

Cooling and Removing Laminated Panels

Do not rush cooling. Removing a panel above 60 C causes warping, cell cracking, and EVA flow that exposes edges. Allow natural cooling in the chamber or use a forced-air cooling station. Once below 60 C, release vacuum slowly a sudden release can pull the backsheet away from the EVA.

Remove the panel with clean gloves. Trim excess EVA and backsheet with a sharp blade, leaving a 23 mm edge. Do not pull or tear this can lift the encapsulant from the glass.

Inspecting Laminated Panels for Bubbles and Delamination

Inspection is your last chance to catch defects before the panel leaves the workshop. Use a well-lit inspection table and, if possible, an electroluminescence (EL) tester for cell cracks. Visual inspection checklist:

  • Bubbles: any visible air pocket larger than 1 mm is a reject or rework.
  • Delamination: check edges and busbar areas for lifting or white haze.
  • Cell cracks: visible as dark lines or irregular shapes under light.
  • Discolouration: yellowing indicates overheating during cure.
  • Foreign particles: dust, hair, or solder splatter under the glass.
  • Edge seal: EVA should flow to the edge without voids.

For a business-first approach, track your reject rate per batch. A well-run African workshop should achieve < 3% laminate reject rate. Above 5%, investigate EVA storage, vacuum performance, and operator technique.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. EVA stored sealed with desiccant; used within 8 h (humid) or 24 h (dry).
  2. Layup table cleaned with isopropyl alcohol; gloves worn at all times.
  3. Layer sequence correct: glass EVA cells EVA backsheet.
  4. Laminator calibrated with external thermocouple; vacuum < 1 mbar.
  5. Cure at 140150 C for 812 min; never exceed 155 C.
  6. Cool to < 60 C before releasing vacuum and removing panel.
  7. Inspect for bubbles > 1 mm, delamination, cracks, and particles.
  8. Record batch reject rate; target < 3%.
