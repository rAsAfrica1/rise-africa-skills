1. Checking market prices via SMS and apps

To get the best price for your maize, beans, or tomatoes, you need to know what buyers are paying in different markets. On a basic phone, you can dial *433# (or similar USSD code) in many African countries to get daily market prices. For example, in Kenya, dial *433# to get prices from major markets like Wakulima, Eldoret, and Mombasa. In Nigeria, use the AgroMall app or dial *347*33# for commodity prices. These services are often free or cost a few cents per query. Write down the prices in a notebook. Compare at least three markets near you. If you are in Uganda, dial *256*14# to get prices for maize, beans, and coffee from Kampala, Gulu, and Mbale. The price difference can be 20-30% between markets, so checking saves you money. For smartphone users, download apps like Esoko, MFarm, or FarmCrowdy. These apps show current prices, buyers, and even weather. Make it a habit to check prices every Monday and Thursday morning before you go to the market. If you see a high price in a distant market, calculate transport cost. For example, if maize is 1,200 KES in Nairobi but 900 KES in Kitale, and transport is 200 KES per bag, you still gain 100 KES per bag. Always confirm the price with two different sources. Some SMS services are outdated. Call a friend in that market to confirm. Use your phone to take a photo of the price board and share it with your farmer group. This simple step can increase your income by 10-15% per harvest. Remember, the buyer who knows the market price has the power. Don't accept the first offer without checking.

2. Joining farmer WhatsApp groups for price updates

WhatsApp groups are the fastest way to share market prices among farmers. Ask your local extension officer or cooperative leader to add you to a farmer group in your area. If none exists, create one with 10-15 nearby farmers. Name it something clear like 'Maize Farmers - Nakuru West'. In the group, agree on a daily price update. For example, every morning at 7am, one person checks the market price and posts it: 'Today maize at Nakuru market: 2,800 KES per 90kg bag. Beans: 6,500 KES per 90kg.' Use the voice note feature if typing is hard. Record a 30-second voice note saying the prices. This helps members who cannot read. Share photos of price boards or receipts. When you sell, post the actual price you received. This builds trust. In Ghana, groups like 'Farmers Market Hub' have over 500 members sharing tomato and pepper prices from Accra, Kumasi, and Takoradi. In Tanzania, 'Wakulima wa Mbogamboga' shares vegetable prices. Be careful not to share fake prices. Only post what you have confirmed. If you see a price that seems too high, ask for a photo of the market board. Use the group to negotiate together. If 10 farmers have 100 bags of maize, you can call one buyer and get a better price than selling alone. Also, use the group to find transport. Post: 'Who is going to Nairobi tomorrow with a lorry? I have 20 bags.' This saves money. Keep the group focused on prices and selling. Avoid too many jokes or unrelated messages. Set rules: no politics, no forwards. If someone posts a scam, remove them. A good WhatsApp group can raise your income by 20% because you stop selling to the first low offer. Start today by asking three farmers for their numbers.

3. Using mobile money for payments

Mobile money is safer and faster than cash. In East Africa, M-Pesa, Airtel Money, and Tigo Pesa are common. In West Africa, use MTN MoMo, Orange Money, or Wave. To receive payment from a buyer, give them your mobile money number. For example, if you sell 10 bags of beans at 6,000 KES each, total 60,000 KES, the buyer sends it to your phone. You will get an SMS confirmation. Check the SMS carefully: it shows the amount, sender, and transaction code. Do not release goods until you see the SMS on your own phone. Fake SMS can be sent by scammers. Always dial *334# (Safaricom) or your provider's code to check your balance. Withdraw cash at an agent. Agents charge a small fee, about 1% (e.g., 60 KES for 60,000 KES). That is cheaper than transport to a bank. You can also pay for transport, inputs, and labor using mobile money. For example, send 500 KES to a tractor driver. Keep a record: write down the transaction code in a notebook. If there is a problem, call customer care with that code. In Uganda, use *165# for MTN MoMo. In Nigeria, use *737*1*1# for GTBank or *894# for FirstMonie. To avoid fraud, never share your PIN with anyone. No buyer needs your PIN. If someone calls saying they are from the mobile money company and asks for your PIN, hang up. Also, do not send money to a buyer who claims they will pay you later. Use 'Send Money' only for people you trust. For large sales, ask the buyer to send to your mobile money and then you transfer to your bank account if needed. Mobile money has a daily limit, often 150,000 KES. For bigger sales, split into two days or use a bank. Always keep your phone charged and have a backup power bank. A dead phone means no payment. Mobile money reduces theft and gives you proof of payment.

4. Advertising your produce on social media

You can sell your produce directly to buyers using Facebook, WhatsApp Status, and Instagram. Start by taking clear photos of your produce. Use natural light, show the quantity, and include a size reference (like a hand or a ruler). For example, take a photo of a heap of 50 tomatoes with a 1-litre bottle next to it. Post on Facebook groups like 'Farmers Market Kenya', 'Buyers and Sellers Nigeria', or 'Agri Marketplace South Africa'. Write a simple post: 'I have 100 bags of maize, 90kg each, at 2,500 KES per bag. Located in Kitale. Transport can be arranged. Call 0712345678.' Use WhatsApp Status: post the same photo and text. Your contacts will see it. Ask friends to share. On Instagram, use hashtags like #FarmersMarket #MaizeForSale #KenyaAgriculture. If you have a smartphone, use free apps like Canva to add text to your photo. For example, write 'TOMATOES FOR SALE - 50 KES PER KG' in big letters. Post every two days until sold. Respond quickly to comments and calls. If someone asks for a lower price, say 'The price is 2,500. If you buy 20 bags, I can do 2,400.' Never post your location exactly if you are alone. Meet buyers in a public place or at the market. In Ghana, farmers use Facebook groups like 'Ghana Agriculture and Farming' to sell cocoa, cassava, and plantain. In Tanzania, 'Soko la Wakulima' on Instagram has thousands of followers. You can also use TikTok to show your farm and produce. A 30-second video of you holding a big bunch of bananas can get buyers. Always include your phone number. If you don't have a smartphone, ask a friend to post for you and share the commission. Social media advertising is free and reaches many buyers. It cuts out middlemen who pay low prices. One farmer in Uganda sold 200kg of honey in three days after posting on Facebook. Try it this week.

5. Avoiding online scams and fake buyers

Scammers target farmers because they know you want to sell quickly. Common scams: A fake buyer calls or messages, offers a high price, and asks you to send money for 'transport' or 'registration'. Never send money to a buyer. The buyer pays you, not the other way. Another scam: They send a fake mobile money SMS saying 'You have received 50,000 KES' but it is not from the official number. Always check your balance by dialing your provider's code. If the money is not in your account, it is a scam. Another: They ask for your PIN or ask you to dial a code to 'confirm' payment. Never share your PIN. Never dial codes from strangers. Another: Fake Facebook profiles with no friends or posts. Check the profile: when was it created? Do they have real photos? Ask for their phone number and call it. If they refuse, block them. Another: They send a cheque or fake bank transfer. Wait for the money to reflect in your account before releasing goods. In Nigeria, '419' scams are common. In Kenya, 'wash wash' scams. If a deal sounds too good, it is. For example, someone offers 10,000 KES per bag of maize when the market price is 2,500 KES. That is a scam. Also, be careful with online marketplaces that ask for a fee to list your produce. Some are genuine, but many are fake. Use known platforms like Esoko, MFarm, or Twiga Foods. If you are unsure, ask your farmer group. Post the buyer's number in the group and ask if anyone has dealt with them. Meet buyers in a public place, like a market or police station. Take a friend. Do not go to a private house alone. If you feel threatened, call 112 or 999. Report scams to your mobile provider and the police. Keep records of all messages and calls. Scammers rely on your urgency. Slow down. Verify everything. A real buyer will not rush you or ask for money upfront.

6. Saving important contacts and records on your phone

Your phone can be your farm office. Save all important contacts: buyers, transport drivers, input suppliers, extension officers, and mobile money agents. Use clear names: 'Maize Buyer - Nairobi - John' or 'Transport - Kipchoge'. Add a second number if they have one. For each contact, add a note: what they buy, typical price, and location. For example, 'Tomato buyer, buys 50 crates weekly, pays 1,500 KES per crate.' This helps you remember. Use your phone's contact app or a free app like Google Contacts. Back up your contacts to your Google account or SIM card. If you lose your phone, you can restore them. For records, use a notebook and your phone. Take photos of receipts, mobile money messages, and handwritten agreements. Save them in a folder called 'Farm Records'. On a smartphone, use Google Drive or Dropbox (free 15GB). On a basic phone, use the memory card. Create a simple record for each sale: date, buyer, quantity, price, total, amount paid, balance. For example: '12/05/2025 - Sold 20 bags maize to John - 2,500 each - total 50,000 - paid 30,000 - balance 20,000.' Take a photo of this page. Also, save mobile money transaction codes. If a buyer claims they paid, you can check. At the end of each month, count your sales and expenses. This tells you if you are making a profit. Use a calculator on your phone. If you have a smartphone, use a free app like 'Farm Manager' or 'AgriTracker'. But a notebook works fine. Keep your phone charged and have a small notebook and pen always. When you meet a new buyer, save their number immediately. Send them a WhatsApp message: 'This is Mary, the maize farmer from Kitale. Nice to meet you.' This keeps the connection. Also, save the numbers of your farmer group. If you change phone, update your group. A well-organized phone saves you time and money. You can call the best buyer instead of waiting for a low offer.

<h2>Practical Reference: Using Mobile Phones for Market Information</h2>

<p>In much of sub-Saharan Africa, the mobile phone is no longer just a communication tool it is the primary business infrastructure for smallholder farmers and agripreneurs. With mobile penetration exceeding 80% in many countries and mobile money transactions growing at double-digit rates annually, a farmer with a basic handset can now access real-time market prices, receive payments, join buyer networks, and build a digital reputation. This practical reference consolidates the six core skills in this module into actionable guidance you can apply today.</p>

<p>Whether you are selling maize in Nakuru, tomatoes in Tamale, or coffee in Mbale, the principles are the same: get accurate price information fast, connect with verified buyers, transact safely, and keep records that protect your margins.</p>

<h2>1. Checking Market Prices via SMS and Apps</h2>

<p>Price information is the single most valuable piece of data a farmer can have before selling. In Kenya, the Kenya Agricultural Market Information System (KAMIS) provides daily commodity prices via SMS shortcode 21222. In Ghana, Esoko sends price alerts to registered farmers. Across the continent, platforms like MFarm, AgroCenta, and Twiga Foods provide app-based price discovery.</p>

<p><strong>Business action:</strong> Subscribe to at least two price sources. Compare the farm-gate price offered by a trader against the wholesale market price in the nearest urban centre. The difference minus transport and handling is your potential gain from selling directly or aggregating with neighbours.</p>

<h2>2. Joining Farmer WhatsApp Groups for Price Updates</h2>

<p>WhatsApp groups have become the de facto trading floor for many African farmers. A well-run group of 50200 farmers in a district can share daily prices, coordinate bulk sales, and vet buyers collectively. The key is to join groups that are moderated, focused on your commodity, and populated by active traders and farmers not just acquaintances.</p>

<p><strong>Business action:</strong> Join at least one commodity-specific group per crop you grow. Introduce yourself with your location, volume, and quality. When you receive a price, cross-check it against your SMS source. If a group consistently shares inaccurate prices, leave it bad data costs money.</p>

<h2>3. Using Mobile Money for Payments</h2>

<p>Mobile money platforms M-Pesa (Kenya, Tanzania, DRC), MTN MoMo (Ghana, Uganda, Rwanda), Airtel Money (Zambia, Malawi, Niger), and Orange Money (Senegal, Mali, Cte d'Ivoire) allow farmers to receive payment instantly, avoid cash-handling risks, and build a transaction history that can support access to credit.</p>

<p><strong>Business action:</strong> Register your mobile money account in your own name and business name where possible. Always confirm the buyer's identity before releasing produce. Request payment before or at the point of collection. Keep your PIN secret no legitimate buyer needs it.</p>

<h2>4. Advertising Your Produce on Social Media</h2>

<p>Facebook Marketplace, WhatsApp Status, and increasingly TikTok and Instagram are used by farmers to advertise produce directly to consumers, restaurants, and institutional buyers. A clear photo, your location, quantity available, and a phone number are the minimum requirements for an effective post.</p>

<p><strong>Business action:</strong> Post at least twice a week during harvest season. Use local language and English. Include a price range, not just "call for price." Respond to inquiries within one hour during business hours. Save every serious buyer's number and follow up after delivery.</p>

<h2>5. Avoiding Online Scams and Fake Buyers</h2>

<div style="background:#fff3f3; border-left:6px solid #c0392b; padding:16px; margin:20px 0; border-radius:4px;">
<h3 style="color:#c0392b; margin-top:0;"> RED SAFETY WARNING: Protecting Yourself from Fraud</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:8px;"><strong>Never release produce before confirming payment.</strong> Common scams include:</p>
<ul style="margin:0; padding-left:20px;">
<li><strong>Fake buyers</strong> who request delivery, then disappear or refuse payment.</li>
<li><strong>Overpayment scams</strong> where a "buyer" sends too much mobile money and asks you to refund the difference the original transfer is later reversed.</li>
<li><strong>Phishing SMS</strong> claiming to be from M-Pesa or your bank, asking for your PIN or to "confirm" your account.</li>
<li><strong>Advance-fee fraud</strong> where you are asked to pay a "registration fee" or "transport deposit" to access a buyer.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin-top:8px; margin-bottom:0;"><strong>Rule:</strong> Verify the buyer's identity through your WhatsApp group or a known intermediary. Use a written agreement (even a WhatsApp message confirming quantity, price, and delivery terms). Meet in a public place for first transactions. Never share your mobile money PIN with anyone.</p>
</div>

<h2>6. Saving Important Contacts and Records on Your Phone</h2>

<p>Your phone is your business ledger. Save every buyer, transporter, input supplier, and extension officer with a clear label for example, "Tomato Buyer Tamale Market Mr. A." Use your phone's notes app or a simple spreadsheet app (Google Sheets, WPS Office) to record sales, expenses, and debts.</p>

<p><strong>Business action:</strong> Back up your contacts to your Google account or SIM card monthly. Keep a written backup in a notebook. Record every transaction the same day amount, buyer, quantity, and payment method. This record is your evidence in a dispute and your data for negotiating better terms next season.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul style="line-height:1.8;">
<li> Subscribe to at least two price information sources (SMS + app or WhatsApp group).</li>
<li> Join one commodity-specific WhatsApp group with active traders and farmers.</li>
<li> Register a mobile money account in your own name and keep your PIN secret.</li>
<li> Confirm payment before releasing produce no exceptions.</li>
<li> Post produce ads with clear photos, location, quantity, and price range.</li>
<li> Verify every new buyer through a group or known contact before transacting.</li>
<li> Save and label all business contacts; back them up monthly.</li>
<li> Record every sale and expense the same day in your phone or notebook.</li>
</ul>
