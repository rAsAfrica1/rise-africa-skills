1. Overview of Polymers Used in Packaging

Plastic packaging in Africa relies on a handful of polymers, each with distinct properties and recycling codes. The most common is PET (polyethylene terephthalate, code 1), used for beverage bottles and food jars. It is strong, clear, and lightweight. HDPE (high-density polyethylene, code 2) is opaque, tough, and chemical-resistant, making it ideal for milk jugs, detergent bottles, and jerrycans. LDPE (low-density polyethylene, code 4) is soft and flexible, used for shrink wrap, grocery bags, and squeezy bottles. PP (polypropylene, code 5) is heat-resistant and rigid, found in yogurt tubs, bottle caps, and woven sacks. PS (polystyrene, code 6) is brittle and often foamed (Styrofoam) for trays and cups. PVC (code 3) is rarely used for food packaging due to safety concerns but appears in some cling films and pipes. Others (code 7) include PC, PLA, and multi-layer laminates.

In Africa, local availability and cost drive polymer choice. PET is widely recycled in countries like South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria, with informal collectors earning 510 KES per bottle. HDPE is often recycled into crates and pipes. LDPE is problematic because it is lightweight and easily littered, but some entrepreneurs melt it into bricks or fuel. PP is increasingly used for reusable containers because it withstands boiling water and sunlight. PS is less common due to its fragility and litter impact.

When selecting a polymer, consider the product: oily foods need HDPE or PP; acidic drinks need PET; frozen items need LDPE or PP. Check the resin identification code (the triangle with a number) on the bottom. For a small-scale packer, buying virgin resin pellets from local suppliers (e.g., in Nairobis Industrial Area or Lagoss Apapa) is common, but recycled flakes can cut costs by 3050% if you have a grinder and extruder. Always test a sample: fill, seal, drop, and store for a week to see how the plastic performs in your climate.

Safety note: Never use PVC or PS for hot, fatty foods. Avoid reusing PET bottles for long-term storage because they scratch and harbor bacteria. Work with suppliers who provide food-grade certificates.

2. Extrusion and Molding Processes

Extrusion and molding turn plastic pellets into packaging. The most basic machine is a single-screw extruder: you feed pellets into a hopper, they melt in a heated barrel (150280C depending on polymer), and a screw pushes the melt through a die. For film, the die is a circular ring and you blow air into the bubble (blown film) or cast it onto a chill roll (cast film). For sheets, a flat die produces a continuous sheet that is cooled and rolled. For pipes and profiles, the die shapes the melt. A small extruder for LDPE film costs about $5,000$15,000 new, but used machines are common in African markets.

Injection molding makes rigid items like caps, tubs, and crates. Pellets are melted and injected under pressure into a closed mold. The mold is cooled, opened, and the part ejected. Cycle times are 1030 seconds. A typical 100-ton machine can produce 500 bottle caps per hour. In Africa, many small workshops use hand-operated injection machines for caps and small containers, costing $2,000$8,000. For bottles, blow molding is used: a preform (a test-tube-shaped piece) is heated and inflated inside a mold. PET preforms are often imported from China or made locally by injection molding. A manual blow molding machine for 5-litre bottles costs around $3,000.

Thermoforming is common for trays and clamshells: a sheet of PP or PET is heated until soft, then sucked onto a mold. You can start with a simple vacuum former made from a oven and a vacuum cleaner, costing under $500. Rotational molding makes large water tanks and drums: plastic powder is placed in a hollow mold, heated while rotating, then cooled. This is ideal for 2005,000 litre tanks.

Key parameters: temperature, pressure, cooling time, and screw speed. For HDPE film, set barrel zones at 180C, 200C, 210C, and die at 220C. For PP injection, use 220260C. Always pre-dry PET and PC pellets at 120C for 4 hours to avoid bubbles. In dusty African environments, keep pellets covered and clean. Start with simple products: LDPE bags, PP caps, or PET preforms. Train one operator per machine and keep a logbook of settings for each product.

3. Film and Flexible Packaging

Flexible packaging includes bags, pouches, sachets, and wraps. It is the cheapest and fastest-growing category in Africa because it is lightweight, uses little material, and can be printed attractively. The base material is usually LDPE, LLDPE, or PP film, often in multiple layers for strength and barrier. A single-layer LDPE bag of 3050 microns thickness is common for bread, produce, and general retail. For longer shelf life, you need barriers: PET/PE laminates for snacks, or metallized PET for coffee. These are made by co-extrusion (multiple extruders feeding one die) or lamination (gluing layers together).

Small-scale production starts with a blown film extruder. You feed LDPE pellets, melt them, and blow a bubble that is cooled and rolled. A 45 mm extruder can produce 3050 kg per hour of film, enough for 10,00020,000 bags per day. You then cut and seal bags on a heat sealer (impulse sealer costs $50$200). For printing, flexographic printing uses rubber plates and fast-drying inks. A one-colour flexo press costs $2,000$5,000. In Kenya, many informal converters buy plain film rolls and print with stencils or digital printers for small runs.

Pouches and sachets are made on form-fill-seal (FFS) machines. Vertical FFS (VFFS) machines form a tube from film, fill product, and seal. A basic VFFS for 1 kg bags costs $8,000$20,000. Horizontal FFS (HFFS) is for flat sachets, like shampoo or single-serve milk. For very low volume, you can buy pre-made pouches and seal them with a vacuum sealer ($200$500).

Key quality checks: seal strength (peel test by hand), thickness (micrometer), and print rub test (tape test). In hot, humid climates, store film in a cool, dry place away from sunlight. LDPE film degrades in UV light, so add 12% UV masterbatch for outdoor use. For food contact, use food-grade LDPE and avoid recycled content unless it is approved. A common African challenge is static electricity causing film to stick; use an ionizing bar or ground the machine. Start with plain bags for local markets, then add printing as you grow.

4. Rigid Plastic Containers: Bottles, Jars, and Tubs

Rigid containers protect and display products. Bottles are mainly PET (for drinks) or HDPE (for milk, oil, chemicals). Jars are often PET or PP, while tubs are PP or HDPE. The choice depends on clarity, barrier, heat resistance, and cost. PET bottles are made in two steps: injection molding a preform (weighing 2030 g for a 500 ml bottle), then blow molding the preform into a bottle. A manual blow molding machine with a single mold can produce 100200 bottles per hour. Preforms cost $0.05$0.15 each, depending on weight and resin price. In Nigeria, local preform makers sell in Lagos and Kano. For HDPE bottles, extrusion blow molding is used: a tube of melted plastic is extruded, then clamped in a mold and inflated. This is common for 15 litre oil and detergent bottles.

Jars and tubs are injection molded or thermoformed. PP tubs for yogurt and margarine are injection molded with a thin wall (0.51 mm). A 100-ton injection machine can make 1,000 tubs per hour. Molds cost $2,000$10,000, so many small businesses buy ready-made containers from local manufacturers. For example, in Nairobi, you can buy 500 ml PP tubs for 1525 KES each. In Accra, PET jars for cosmetics cost 25 GHS.

Design tips: For bottles, keep the neck diameter standard (e.g., 28 mm for PET water, 38 mm for juice) so you can use common caps. For tubs, use a rim that accepts a snap-on lid or a foil seal. Add a tamper-evident band if needed. Wall thickness should be uniform to avoid weak spots. For hot-fill products (like jam), use PP or HDPE, not PET, because PET deforms above 60C. For carbonated drinks, PET must be thicker (e.g., 30 g for 500 ml) to withstand pressure.

Quality control: check drop strength (drop filled bottle from 1.2 m onto concrete), top-load strength (stack 5 filled bottles), and seal integrity (squeeze bottle). In Africa, dust and humidity can affect molding; keep molds clean and dry. Start with a single product and a single size. Buy containers if volumes are below 5,000 per month; invest in a machine only when you have steady demand.

5. Closures and Dispensing Systems

Closures are the lid, cap, or dispenser that seals a container. They are mostly made of PP or HDPE because these plastics are flexible, chemical-resistant, and cheap. Common types: screw caps (for bottles), snap-on lids (for tubs), flip-top caps (for shampoo), disc-top caps (for ketchup), pumps (for lotion), and sprayers (for perfumes). A simple 28 mm screw cap weighs 23 g and costs $0.01$0.03 in bulk from Asian or local suppliers. In Africa, local injection molders produce caps in Kenya, South Africa, Egypt, and Nigeria. A small hand-operated injection machine (50100 ton) can make 5001,000 caps per hour. The mold costs $1,500$5,000.

For bottles, the neck finish must match the cap. The most common is the 28 mm PCO (plastic closure only) for PET water and soda. For juices, a 38 mm cap is common. For oil, a 38 mm or 48 mm cap. Always measure the neck diameter and thread pitch. If you buy caps locally, bring a sample bottle to test. For tubs, snap-on lids are made of LDPE or PP and require a matching rim. A lid for a 500 ml tub costs $0.02$0.05. Foil seals (aluminum/PE) are used for yogurt and medicine; they are heat-sealed with a induction sealer or a simple hot plate.

Dispensing systems add value. A flip-top cap for dish soap costs $0.03$0.06. A lotion pump costs $0.10$0.30. A trigger sprayer costs $0.20$0.50. These are usually imported from China, but some assembly happens in Africa. For small-scale production, you can buy ready-made dispensers and focus on filling. If you mold your own, start with a simple screw cap or snap-on lid. Design for a tight seal: add a liner (foam or PE) inside the cap for liquids. Test by filling with water, inverting, and squeezing.

Common problems: caps that leak because of wrong thread, lids that crack because of too much force, and pumps that clog with thick products. Use a torque tester (or hand-tighten consistently) to avoid over-tightening. For carbonated drinks, use a cap with a liner that vents pressure. In dusty environments, keep caps in sealed bags before use. A good closure can make a cheap container look professional. Always order 10% extra caps for waste and damage.

6. Recycling and Identification Codes for Plastics

Every plastic container has a resin identification code (RIC) inside a triangle, from 1 to 7. Code 1 is PET (bottles), 2 is HDPE (milk jugs), 3 is PVC (pipes), 4 is LDPE (bags), 5 is PP (caps, tubs), 6 is PS (foam), and 7 is other (PC, PLA, multi-layer). In Africa, informal waste pickers collect PET and HDPE because they have value. A kilogram of PET bottles fetches 1020 KES in Kenya, 2040 NGN in Nigeria, or 25 ZAR in South Africa. HDPE is similar. LDPE and PP are less valuable because they are bulky and hard to recycle. PS is often littered.

Recycling steps: collect, sort by code, wash, shred, and melt. For PET, you remove caps and labels, then shred into flakes. Flakes are washed in hot water with caustic soda to remove glue and dirt, then dried. They can be sold to local recyclers or melted into new products like fiber, strapping, or sheets. A small PET washing line costs $5,000$20,000. For HDPE, you can shred and melt into pellets using an extruder with a pelletizer. A basic pelletizer costs $3,000$8,000. In Nairobi, Mr. Green Africa and other recyclers buy sorted plastic. In Lagos, Wecyclers collects from communities.

For a packaging business, recycling can cut costs. You can use 2030% recycled PET in new bottles if you have food-grade approval (requires hot washing and testing). Recycled HDPE is fine for non-food items like crates and pipes. LDPE bags can be made with 1020% recycled content, but they lose strength. PP tubs can use recycled PP for non-food uses. Always label recycled content if required by law. In Rwanda, plastic bags are banned, so focus on reusable PP bags.

To identify plastics without a code, do a simple test: PET is clear and strong, floats in water; HDPE is opaque, floats; LDPE is soft, floats; PP is rigid, floats; PS is brittle, sinks; PVC is soft, sinks. Burn test: PET smells sweet, HDPE smells like candle, PP smells like diesel, PS smells like gas, PVC smells like acid (and green flame). Never burn PVC indoors. For a small business, start by sorting your own waste and selling to recyclers. Then consider making a product like a recycled plastic lumber or a paving block. Keep records of weights and prices to know your margins.

<h2>Practical Reference: Plastic Packaging in African Agribusiness</h2>

<p>Plastic packaging is the workhorse of African food and agribusiness supply chains. From 500 ml PET cooking-oil bottles in Nigeria to 25 kg woven polypropylene maize sacks in Kenya and 1 L HDPE jerrycans of palm oil in Ghana, plastics protect products, extend shelf life, carry branding, and move goods across borders. For a processor, packer, or exporter, choosing the right polymer, wall thickness, closure, and end-of-life pathway is a commercial decision not a technical luxury. This lesson is a business-first reference on plastic packaging materials, processes, and compliance for African markets.</p>

<p>Globally, plastics account for roughly 4045% of all packaging materials by value, and in many African markets the share is higher for fast-moving consumer goods and agricultural inputs. The practical challenge is not whether to use plastic, but how to specify it so it survives heat, humidity, transport, and regulatory scrutiny while keeping unit cost low and meeting tightening Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) rules now active in South Africa, Kenya, Nigeria, Rwanda, and Ghana.</p>

<strong style="color:#c62828"> SAFETY WARNING Food-Contact Plastics and Recycled Content</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0">Never use recycled plastic for direct food contact unless it is certified food-grade and compliant with the destination market's rules. In the EU, recycled PET for food contact must meet EU Regulation 10/2011 and be produced under an EFSA-approved process. In South Africa, food-contact plastics must comply with SANS 1853 and the Foodstuffs, Cosmetics and Disinfectants Act 54 of 1972. Non-food-grade recycled HDPE (e.g., from detergent bottles or pesticide containers) must never be used for edible oil, milk, or water packaging. Pesticide jerrycans are a particular hazard they must be triple-rinsed, punctured, and handled through registered collection schemes (e.g., CropLife's container management programmes in Kenya, South Africa, and Ghana).</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol style="font-size:14px;line-height:1.7">
<li><strong>Match polymer to product:</strong> PET for clarity and O barrier; HDPE for toughness and moisture barrier; PP for heat resistance; multi-layer for long shelf life.</li>
<li><strong>Verify food-contact compliance:</strong> Confirm SANS 1853 (South Africa), KEBS standards (Kenya), NAFDAC (Nigeria), or FDA/EU 10/2011 for export markets before purchase.</li>
<li><strong>Check the RIC code:</strong> Ensure the correct resin identification code is molded or printed on the pack required in several African EPR schemes.</li>
<li><strong>Optimise wall thickness:</strong> Reduce grammage without compromising drop-test performance a 510% resin saving directly improves margin.</li>
<li><strong>Test in real conditions:</strong> Run drop, stack, and 40 C/75% RH humidity tests that reflect African transport and storage, not European lab conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Plan for EPR:</strong> Register with the national producer responsibility organisation (e.g., SAWIC in South Africa, KEPRO in Kenya, EPRON in Nigeria) and budget the levy.</li>
<li><strong>Design for recyclability:</strong> Use mono-material structures, avoid PVC labels on PET, and keep caps attached where required by new regulations.</li>
<li><strong>Secure supply:</strong> Identify at least two local or regional converters to reduce dependence on imported resin and finished packs.</li>
</ol>
