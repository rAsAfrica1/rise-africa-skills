1. Crush and Strain Method for Small Batches

For beekeepers with just a few hives, the crush and strain method is the simplest and cheapest way to extract honey. You don't need an extractorâjust a clean bucket, a knife, a potato masher or clean hands, and a straining cloth. First, harvest frames only when they are at least 80% capped. Use a sharp knife to cut the comb away from the frame into a clean food-grade bucket. If the comb is thick, cut it into smaller pieces. Then crush the comb thoroughly with a potato masher or your hands (wear food-safe gloves). The goal is to break all the wax cells so honey can flow out. Next, set up a straining system: place a fine mesh bag or cheesecloth over a second bucket, secure it with a string or rubber band, and pour the crushed comb mixture into the cloth. Let it drain for 24 to 48 hours at room temperature (25â30Â°C). Do not squeeze the wax too hardâyou'll get more honey but also more wax particles and impurities. After draining, the honey will be in the bottom bucket. For every 10 kg of crushed comb, you can expect about 7â8 kg of honey, depending on how well it drains. The leftover wax can be rinsed and saved for making candles or cosmetics. This method is ideal for 1â5 hives and produces high-quality honey, though it's labour-intensive. In Africa, where equipment costs are high, crush and strain is often the most practical choice. Always work in a clean, fly-free room and use stainless steel or food-grade plastic buckets. Avoid using buckets that previously held chemicals or oils.

2. Using a Manual Centrifugal Extractor

A manual centrifugal extractor uses spinning motion to pull honey out of the comb without destroying the wax. This allows the bees to reuse the comb, saving them energy and giving you more honey in the long run. Extractors come in 2-frame, 4-frame, and 8-frame sizes. For a small-scale beekeeper in Africa, a 4-frame stainless steel extractor is a good balance between cost and efficiency. Before extracting, uncap the honeycomb with a sharp uncapping knife or a hot air gun. Work over a uncapping tray to catch wax shavings. Then place the uncapped frames into the extractor basket, making sure they are balancedâif you put one frame on the left, put another on the right. Turn the crank slowly at first, then increase speed. Honey will fly out of the comb and hit the inside wall, then run down to the bottom. After 3â5 minutes of spinning, stop, reverse the frames, and spin again to extract the other side. For thick combs, you may need to spin for 8â10 minutes total. A manual extractor can process about 20â30 kg of honey per hour with two people. After extraction, open the honey gate at the bottom and let the honey flow through a double sieve into a settling tank or bucket. Let it settle for 2â3 days so air bubbles and fine wax particles rise to the top. Then skim off the foam. Clean the extractor immediately with warm water and a soft brushânever use soap, as residue can contaminate future honey. Store the extractor dry and upside down. This method is faster and cleaner than crush and strain, and it preserves the comb for reuse.

3. Extracting Honey from Stingless Bee Hives

Stingless bees (Meliponini) are common in Africa, and their honey is highly valued for its medicinal properties and unique tangy taste. However, extracting from stingless bee hives is different from honeybee hives. Stingless bees store honey in small pots made of cerumen (a mix of wax and plant resin), not in large combs. You cannot use a centrifugal extractor because the pots are fragile and the honey is thin and runny. Instead, use the crush and strain method, but with extra care. First, harvest only the honey pots, leaving the brood and pollen pots intact. Use a clean spoon or syringe to scoop out the honey and cerumen mixture into a clean bucket. Avoid crushing the pots too much, as this releases more cerumen particles. Then, strain the mixture through a fine mesh cloth or a double layer of cheesecloth. Because stingless bee honey is more liquid, it drains fasterâusually within 12â24 hours. Do not squeeze the cloth; let gravity do the work. The yield is lower: a strong stingless bee colony may produce only 1â2 litres of honey per harvest, compared to 20â30 litres from a honeybee hive. After straining, let the honey settle for 24 hours, then skim off any floating wax. Store in clean glass jars. Note that stingless bee honey ferments easily due to its high water content (often 25â30%). To reduce spoilage, you can gently warm the honey to 35Â°C (not higher) to evaporate some water, or keep it refrigerated. Always leave enough honey for the bees to survive, especially during dry seasons. In Africa, stingless beekeeping is growing, and proper extraction ensures you get the best price for this premium product.

4. Dealing with Wax Cappings and Comb

When you uncap honeycombs, you create a mixture of wax cappings and honey. Don't throw this awayâit's valuable. First, let the cappings drain in a colander over a bucket for 24 hours. You'll collect 1â2 kg of honey from every 10 kg of cappings. Then, rinse the cappings with clean water to remove leftover honey. Now you have raw wax. To purify it, melt the wax in a double boiler or a solar wax melter. Never melt wax over direct fireâit can catch fire and darken the wax. Heat to about 70Â°C. Once melted, strain through a fine cloth or a kitchen sieve to remove debris like bee parts or propolis. Pour the clean wax into moulds (e.g., silicone muffin trays or plastic containers) and let it cool. You can sell this wax for candle making, cosmetics, or to other beekeepers for foundation sheets. For comb that is too old or damaged, you can also render it into wax. But if the comb is only slightly damaged, you can repair it with a hot knife and return it to the hive. When dealing with cappings, always work in a bee-proof room to avoid robbing. In Africa, wax is a valuable secondary productâ1 kg of clean beeswax can sell for $5â$15, depending on quality. Store dry wax in a cool place; it keeps for years. Also, never mix wax from different hives without melting, as diseases can spread. Proper handling of cappings and comb increases your overall income and keeps your apiary healthy.

5. Minimizing Contamination During Extraction

Honey is a natural product, but it can easily be contaminated during extraction. The biggest risks are dirt, wax particles, bee parts, and moisture. To minimize contamination, follow strict hygiene. First, work in a clean, enclosed room with screened windows to keep out flies and dust. Wear clean clothes and wash your hands with soap and water before starting. Use only food-grade stainless steel or plastic buckets, and sanitize them with a weak bleach solution (1 tablespoon per 4 litres of water), then rinse thoroughly with clean water. Never use buckets that held pesticides, fuel, or chemicals. During extraction, keep the honey covered as much as possible. If using a centrifugal extractor, make sure it is clean and dry before loading frames. For crush and strain, use a clean straining cloth that has been boiled and dried. Avoid touching the honey with bare hands if you have cuts or sores. Also, control moisture: extract only capped honey (below 20% moisture) to prevent fermentation. Use a refractometer to check moisture content. If honey is too wet, you can dehumidify the room or use a honey dryer. After extraction, filter the honey through a double sieve (coarse then fine) to remove wax and debris. Let it settle, then skim off foam. Never add water or any other substance. Store honey in clean, dry glass or food-grade plastic containers with airtight lids. In Africa's warm climate, contamination can happen fast, so always keep your equipment spotless and your workspace tidy. A clean operation also gets you better prices from buyers.

6. Post-Extraction Cleanup and Storage

After extraction, clean up immediately. Honey left on equipment attracts bees, ants, and rodents, and it hardens, making cleaning harder. Start by scraping off excess wax and honey from the extractor, buckets, and tools. Then wash everything with warm water (not hot, to avoid melting wax) and a soft brush. Do not use soap unless you rinse extremely wellâsoap residue can taint future honey. For stainless steel extractors, a pressure washer on low setting works well. After washing, dry all equipment completely with a clean cloth or let it air dry in the sun. Store extractors upside down to keep out dust. For honey storage, use food-grade buckets or glass jars with airtight lids. Fill containers to the top to reduce air space, which can cause crystallization or moisture absorption. Label each container with the harvest date and hive number. Store in a cool, dark placeâideally below 25Â°C. In hot African climates, a shaded room or a cellar is best. If honey crystallizes, it's not spoiled; just warm the jar in a water bath at 40Â°C to liquefy. Never store honey in metal containers other than stainless steel, as it can react and cause off-flavours. Keep honey away from strong-smelling items like onions or petrol. For long-term storage, keep honey in sealed buckets in a dry room. Proper cleanup and storage ensure your honey stays high-quality for months, and you can sell it at premium prices. Also, clean your wax cappings and store them separately for future rendering.

Practical Reference: Honey Extraction Techniques for African Beekeepers

Honey extraction is where beekeeping stops being a hobby and becomes a business. Every drop left in the comb, every gram of wax lost, and every batch spoiled by contamination is money out of your pocket. Across Africaâfrom the miombo woodlands of Zambia and Tanzania to the acacia belts of Kenya, Ethiopia, and Nigeriaâbeekeepers work with two very different insect species: the introduced Apis mellifera and various indigenous stingless bees (Meliponula, Dactylurina, Plebeina, Liotrigona). The extraction method you choose depends on your colony type, your volume, your budget, and your target market.

This practical reference consolidates the six core lessons of this module into a field-ready guide. It assumes you are extracting for saleânot just for household useâand that buyers care about moisture content, cleanliness, and consistent quality. Method Selection: Crush & Strain vs. Centrifugal

The crush and strain method is the default for stingless bee honey and for small Apis mellifera harvests where frames are not built on standard Langstroth dimensions. It is cheap, requires no specialized equipment, and works with any comb shape. The trade-off is that you destroy the comb, so the bees must rebuild, and your wax recovery is mixed with honey residue.

The manual centrifugal extractor is the commercial standard for Apis mellifera once you have ten or more hives. It preserves drawn combâsaving the colony roughly 6â8 kg of honey for every kilogram of wax they would otherwise need to rebuild. In African conditions where nectar flows are seasonal and unpredictable, preserving comb is a direct profitability gain.

Dealing with Wax Cappings and Comb

When you uncap frames before centrifugal extraction, you produce cappingsâa mixture of wax and honey. Do not throw these away. Rinse them in a clean bucket with a small amount of warm water (not exceeding 40Â°C), then strain the rinse water through a fine mesh. The honey-water solution can be fed back to bees during dearth periods, and the clean cappings can be melted into wax blocks for sale or for making foundation sheets.

In the crush and strain method, the leftover comb after draining still contains 10â15% honey. The most profitable approach is to melt this comb in a solar wax melter. The honey residue settles at the bottom and can be skimmed off, while the wax rises and solidifies. A simple solar wax melterâa wooden box with a glass lid and a metal trayâcosts under $30 to build and can process 5â10 kg of comb per sunny day.

Minimizing Contamination During Extraction

Contamination is the silent profit killer. Buyers in export markets reject honey for antibiotic residues, pesticide traces, and excessive moisture. Even local buyers notice off-flavours from smoke, metal, or dirty equipment. The following practices are non-negotiable for commercial extraction:

  • Use food-grade equipment only. Stainless steel, food-grade plastic, or glass. Never use galvanized buckets or rusty containersâzinc and iron leach into honey.
  • Extract in a screened room or tent. Bees rob open honey, and robbing bees carry dirt and other hive material.
  • Wash hands and tools with clean water before starting and between batches. Do not use soap residueâit taints honey flavour.
  • Keep smoke to a minimum. Smoke from uncapping enters honey and creates a medicinal off-flavour. Use a cool, brief puff only when necessary.
  • Filter through food-grade mesh (600â800 microns). This removes wax fragments and bee parts without stripping pollen, which many buyers value.
  • Do not overheat honey. If you warm honey to reduce viscosity, keep it below 40Â°C. Higher temperatures destroy enzymes and darken colour.

Post-Extraction Cleanup and Storage

Cleanup is not the end of the jobâit is the start of the next harvest. Honey residue left on extractors, buckets, and tools attracts ants, beetles, and rodents, and it ferments, creating off-odours that contaminate future batches.

Wash all equipment with warm water (no soap if possible), rinse thoroughly, and dry in direct sunlight. Store extractors upside down so dust does not settle inside. For honey storage, use food-grade plastic buckets or glass jars with airtight lids. Store in a cool, dark placeâideally below 25Â°C. In hot African climates, this may mean a shaded storeroom or a below-ground cellar.

Moisture content is the single most important storage variable. Honey above 20% moisture will ferment. If you do not have a refractometer, use the "thumb test": a drop of honey on your thumb should hold its shape and not spread. If it spreads, dry it further before storageâeither by leaving it in a shallow tray in a dry room or by using a simple solar dehydrator.
