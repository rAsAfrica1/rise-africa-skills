1. Preparing Colonies for Winter

In many African regions, winter is not a deep freeze but a cool, dry season with little to no nectar flow. Bees still need preparation. Start by assessing each colony's strength 6-8 weeks before the cool season. A strong colony has at least 8 frames covered with bees and a laying queen. Weak colonies (fewer than 5 frames) should be combined with a stronger hive using the newspaper method: place a sheet of newspaper between two brood boxes, make a few slits, and stack the weak colony on top of the strong one. The bees will chew through and merge peacefully.

Reduce the entrance to about 2-3 cm using a wooden cleat or grass bundle. This helps the bees defend against robbers and keeps out cold drafts. Remove any empty supers and unused frames to reduce the space the bees must heat. In areas with cold nights, consider moving hives to a spot that gets morning sun and is sheltered from wind. If you use top bar hives, close the ends with a follower board and insulate the roof with dry grass or a sack.

Ensure the hive has adequate honey stores: a strong colony needs 15-20 kg of honey to survive a 3-4 month dearth. If stores are low, feed thick sugar syrup (2 parts sugar to 1 part water) in a feeder placed inside the hive to prevent robbing. Stop feeding when the bees stop taking it or when brood rearing slows. Also, treat for varroa mites before winter"use approved strips or oxalic acid dribbling according to local guidelines. A healthy colony with low mite load survives better.

Finally, tilt the hive slightly forward so condensation runs out, and ensure the roof is watertight. In African savannahs, termites can be a problem; place hive stands in oil cans or use ash around the legs. With these steps, your colonies will be ready for the cool season.

2. Winter Hive Insulation and Ventilation

In African winters, temperatures may drop to 5-10C at night, especially in highlands. Bees cluster to stay warm, but they also need ventilation to avoid moisture buildup. Insulation helps them conserve energy. Use locally available materials: dry banana leaves, grass, or old blankets. Wrap the hive body with a layer, leaving the entrance clear. For Langstroth hives, place a quilt box on top: a shallow box filled with wood shavings or dry rice husks, covered with a breathable cloth. This absorbs moisture and adds insulation. In top bar hives, stuff dry grass between the follower board and the end wall.

Ventilation is critical. Without it, warm moist air from the cluster condenses on cold surfaces, dripping on bees and causing chill or disease. Drill a 2-3 cm hole near the top of the hive body (above the brood nest) and cover it with wire mesh to prevent robbers. In Langstroth hives, you can also prop the outer cover up slightly with a twig to allow air exchange. But avoid strong drafts directly on the cluster. The entrance should remain open but reduced to 2-3 cm. If you use a screened bottom board, keep it open for ventilation; in very cold areas, you may close it partially.

Monitor humidity inside the hive. If you see mold or wetness on inner covers, increase ventilation. A simple hygrometer can help, but visual checks suffice. On sunny days, bees may take cleansing flights; ensure the entrance is not blocked by dead bees or debris. Remove any snow or frost from the entrance. In areas with cold winds, place a windbreak of grass or sticks on the windward side. Remember, bees generate heat by eating honey and shivering; proper insulation and ventilation reduce honey consumption and stress. This means more bees survive to spring buildup.

3. Winter Feeding and Moisture Control

Winter feeding is often necessary when honey stores are below 10 kg. Use thick sugar syrup (2:1 sugar to water by volume) or fondant. Syrup can ferment or freeze in cold weather, so fondant is safer. To make fondant, mix 4 kg sugar with 1 liter water and 1 teaspoon cream of tartar; heat gently until dissolved, then cool and knead into a stiff dough. Place it on top of the frames, over the cluster, so bees can reach it. For syrup, use an inside feeder (like a division board feeder) to prevent robbing. Never feed outside the hive in winter.

Moisture control is as important as feeding. Bees produce water vapor from respiration and from consuming honey. If this moisture condenses and drips, bees can chill and die. Use a quilt box or moisture-absorbing material like dry newspaper or burlap on top of the frames. Replace it when damp. Ensure the hive has a slight forward tilt so condensation runs down and out the entrance. In humid regions, increase ventilation by opening the top hole more. Avoid sealing the hive completely; air must circulate.

Check stores every 2-3 weeks on warm days (above 10C). Lift the hive from the back; if it feels light, add fondant. Do not open the hive fully in cold weather; just lift the cover enough to add feed. If you see dead bees at the entrance, clear them. Also, ensure the entrance is not blocked by ice or snow. In some African areas, winter is dry, so provide a water source nearby if bees are active. But avoid open water that can drown bees; use a shallow dish with stones. With proper feeding and moisture management, your colonies will emerge strong in spring.

4. Spring Buildup and Swarm Prevention

Spring signals the start of nectar flow and rapid brood rearing. As soon as you see bees bringing in pollen and the queen increasing laying, it's time for management. First, inspect the hive on a warm day (above 15C). Check for the queen, brood pattern, and stores. If the colony survived winter but is weak (fewer than 4 frames of bees), consider combining it with another weak colony or giving it a frame of capped brood from a strong hive. This boosts numbers.

Swarm prevention is crucial in spring. Swarming happens when the hive becomes crowded and the queen has nowhere to lay. To prevent it, provide space early. Add a super or an empty box with frames when the brood nest has 7-8 frames of bees. If using a top bar hive, add empty bars at the back. Also, remove any queen cells you find during inspections"but only if you can do it every 7-10 days. Alternatively, make a split: remove 2-3 frames of brood with bees and a queen cell to a new hive, and give the original hive new frames to draw. This mimics swarming but keeps your apiary growing.

Ensure the queen has room to lay. If she is old (over 2 years) and brood pattern is spotty, replace her with a new mated queen. In African beekeeping, local queens are often available from other beekeepers. Also, watch for signs of disease like chalkbrood or foulbrood. If you see mummified larvae, remove and burn the frames. Feed 1:1 sugar syrup to stimulate brood rearing if nectar flow is slow. But stop feeding once honey supers are on to avoid contaminating honey. With these steps, your colonies will build up strong and be ready for the main flow.

5. Summer Management for Honey Flow

Summer is the main honey flow in many African regions, especially after rains. Your goal is to maximize honey production while keeping colonies healthy. Before the flow, ensure each hive has a laying queen and at least 10 frames of bees. Add honey supers (for Langstroth) or extra bars (for top bar) when the first nectar appears. Use a queen excluder if you want honey without brood, but in Africa, many beekeepers skip it to allow the queen to move up; just harvest only capped honey frames. Inspect every 7-10 days to check for swarm cells and to add more space as needed.

During the flow, avoid frequent full inspections; it disturbs bees and may reduce yield. Instead, do quick checks from the back: lift the lid, look at the top bars, and see if bees are working. If the super is 80% full, add another. Harvest honey only when frames are at least 80% capped. Use a bee brush or gentle smoke to remove bees. Never harvest uncapped honey; it will ferment. Leave at least 10 kg of honey for the bees, especially if a dearth follows. In hot climates, ensure hives are shaded during the hottest part of the day; place them under trees or use a shade cloth. Provide water nearby.

Monitor for pests: ants, wax moths, and small hive beetles. Keep hives off the ground and use ant barriers like grease or ash. If you see wax moth larvae, remove affected frames and freeze them for 24 hours. For small hive beetles, use traps or diatomaceous earth. Also, watch for signs of American foulbrood: sunken, perforated cappings. If found, burn the hive and equipment. After the flow, when nectar slows, remove honey supers and store them dry to prevent moths. Reduce entrances to prevent robbing. With good summer management, you can harvest 20-30 kg per hive in a good year.

6. Fall Preparations and Disease Check

Fall (autumn) is the time to prepare colonies for winter and to do a thorough disease check. In many African regions, fall is the end of the rainy season and the start of a dearth. After harvesting honey, inspect each hive carefully. Look for signs of disease: American foulbrood (sunken, dark cappings with a ropy mass), European foulbrood (twisted larvae), chalkbrood (white or black mummies), and nosema (dysentery). If you suspect foulbrood, isolate the hive and consult an extension officer. For chalkbrood, improve ventilation and replace the queen. For nosema, ensure good ventilation and feed clean syrup.

Check varroa mite levels using a sugar roll or alcohol wash. If you find more than 3 mites per 100 bees, treat with approved methods: oxalic acid dribbling (in broodless periods) or formic acid pads. In Africa, many beekeepers use traditional methods like tobacco smoke, but these are not effective. Use only approved treatments. Also, check for tracheal mites if bees have K-winged wings. Treat with menthol crystals if available.

Assess honey stores. A colony needs 15-20 kg to survive winter. If stores are low, feed thick syrup (2:1) until they stop taking it. Combine weak colonies with strong ones. Reduce entrances to 2-3 cm and add insulation. Remove any empty supers and store them properly to prevent wax moths. Clean the bottom board of debris. Finally, ensure the hive is watertight and tilted forward. In areas with cold winters, wrap the hive with insulation. With these fall preparations, your bees will be healthy and ready for the next season.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Overwintering and Seasonal Management for African Beekeepers



In Africa, "overwintering" rarely means surviving snow. For most beekeepers south of the Sahara, the real survival challenge is the dry season " a dearth of nectar and pollen, high evaporation, bush fires, and (in highland and Mediterranean-climate zones such as the Ethiopian highlands, Kenya's Aberdares, and South Africa's Western Cape) genuinely cold, wet winters. This lesson is written as a business tool: every management decision here is framed around colony survival, honey yield, and cash flow. A colony that dies in the dearth is a lost asset; a colony that survives it strong is your first honey harvest of the next season.



Work through the six lessons in this module " Preparing Colonies for Winter, Winter Hive Insulation and Ventilation, Winter Feeding and Moisture Control, Spring Buildup and Swarm Prevention, Summer Management for Honey Flow, and Fall Preparations and Disease Check " using this page as your field reference. Keep it on your phone, print it, and take it to the apiary.





Why Seasonality Beats Temperature in Africa


Most African honey bee colonies (Apis mellifera scutellata, adansonii, monticola, capensis and others) respond to rainfall and forage, not to a fixed winter. In equatorial and savanna zones there are typically two dry seasons and two rainy seasons per year; in highland and Mediterranean zones there is one long cold/wet season. Your management calendar must be built from your own local flowering pattern, not copied from a European textbook.


From a business standpoint, this means:



  • Your honey harvest dates move every year. Track them. After three seasons you can predict your flow with confidence and schedule labour and buyers accordingly.

  • Your biggest loss risk is the dearth, when colonies abscond or starve. Absconding is the African bee's answer to stress " and it wipes out your investment in that hive overnight.

  • Every hive you keep through the dearth costs money (sugar, labour, fuel for transport). Only carry colonies you expect to repay that cost.



Preparing Colonies for the Dearth / Winter


Start 6"8 weeks before the flow ends. The colony needs: a young, laying queen; 4"6 frames of bees; and at least 8"12 kg of stored honey or its equivalent in feed. Reduce the entrance, remove empty supers, and close any cracks. In highland areas where night temperatures drop below 10 C, cluster the hives together and face entrances away from prevailing cold wind and rain.



Insulation and Ventilation


African hives fail in the cold/wet season more often from condensation than from cold. Warm, moist air rises, hits a cold lid, and drips back onto the cluster. Solutions that work across the continent:



  • Add a 5"8 cm layer of dry grass, straw, or banana-leaf thatch above the inner cover, under a waterproof outer lid.

  • Tilt the hive 5"10 forward so any water runs out of the entrance, not into the brood nest.

  • Keep a small upper ventilation gap (2"3 mm) " but in savanna zones with small hive beetle or ants, screen it with mesh.

  • Do not wrap hives in plastic in humid zones. It traps moisture and kills colonies.



Winter Feeding and Moisture Control


Feed thick sugar syrup (2 parts sugar : 1 part water by weight) or fondant when stores fall below 5 kg. Never feed thin syrup in the dearth " it ferments, triggers robbing, and can cause dysentery. Add a pollen substitute (soy flour + brewer's yeast, 3:1) only if you are deliberately building brood before a known flow.



Spring Buildup and Swarm Prevention


When the first rains arrive, inspect weekly. Add supers before the bees need them. Swarming is the single biggest cause of lost honey yield in African apiaries. Prevent it with: young queens, adequate space, drawn comb ready, and " where legal and locally appropriate " timely splits. Every split is also a saleable nuc colony: a swarm prevented is a product created.



Summer Management for the Flow


During the main flow, your job is to keep supers ahead of the colony, harvest only fully capped frames, and keep records. Shade hives with living trees or shade cloth; provide a clean water source close by. In hot lowland zones, hives in full sun can reach lethal temperatures.



Fall Preparations and Disease Check


After harvest, inspect every colony for Varroa destructor (where present), American and European foulbrood, Nosema, chalkbrood, and small hive beetle. Treat only with registered products, follow withdrawal periods, and remove and burn badly diseased comb. Store drawn comb in a dry, dark, airy place and protect it from wax moth with ventilation and, where permitted, Bacillus thuringiensis var. aizawai.

SAFETY WARNING " Pesticides, Fire and Aggressive African Bees



  • Never apply agricultural pesticides near hives during the flow or when crops are flowering. African bees forage widely; a single spray event can kill a whole apiary and destroy your season's income. Coordinate with neighbouring farmers before they spray, and ask them to spray at dusk on windless days.

  • Never use unregistered or expired miticides or antibiotics. Residues end up in your honey and can disqualify you from export and supermarket contracts.

  • Bush fire: in dry-season savanna, clear a 3"5 m firebreak around the apiary and keep hives on stands. Fire is the leading cause of total apiary loss in parts of West and Southern Africa.

  • Aggression: A. m. scutellata is highly defensive. Always wear a full suit and veil, work in pairs, use cool smoke sparingly, and never open hives at night or in the rain. Keep a clear escape path.

  • Hive theft and honey theft peak in the dearth. Brand hives, keep them visible from a homestead, and consider a community watch arrangement.




Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Know your local flow. Record first and last flowering dates for three seasons before you trust a calendar.

  2. Inspect every 7"10 days during buildup, and every 2"3 weeks during the dearth.

  3. Keep supers ahead of the bees " never wait for congestion before adding space.

  4. Feed thick syrup (2:1) or fondant whenever stores drop below 5 kg in the dearth.

  5. Ventilate and insulate the top; tilt the hive forward to drain condensation.

  6. Check for Varroa, foulbrood and small hive beetle after every harvest " treat only with registered products.

  7. Store drawn comb dry, dark and airy to defeat wax moth.

  8. Keep a hive-level profit record. Cull or unite colonies that don't repay their feed cost.
