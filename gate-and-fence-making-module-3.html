1. Clearing and Marking the Fence Line

Before you dig any hole, you must clear the fence line. Walk the boundary with your client and agree on the exact path. Use a machete or slasher to cut tall grass, small bushes, and saplings. For bigger trees, you may need a bow saw or axe. Remove all debris and stack it away from the line. If the ground is uneven, use a hoe to scrape the topsoil level along a 1-metre-wide strip.

Once cleared, mark the line using wooden pegs (about 3040 cm long) and a strong string or rope. Drive the first peg at the starting corner, then run the string to the next corner. Pull it tight and tie it to a second peg. Check that the string is straight by sighting along it. For long runs, place intermediate pegs every 35 metres to keep the string from sagging.

Use a tape measure to mark the positions of gate posts and corner posts. Standard fence post spacing is 2.5 to 3 metres for chain-link or wooden fences. For a gate, the opening should be at least 1 metre wide for a pedestrian gate, or 34 metres for a vehicle gate. Mark these with a different colour peg or a piece of cloth.

If the ground slopes, you will need to step the fence. That means each post hole is dug to the same depth, but the posts will be at different heights above ground. The string line should follow the slope, not be level. Mark the ground where each post will go with a small stake or a splash of white paint.

Finally, take a photo of the marked line and note the measurements in a notebook. This helps if you need to leave and return later. Clear the line of any sharp rocks or roots that could damage your tools. A clean, well-marked line saves hours of confusion later.

2. Using String Lines and Batter Boards

A string line is your best friend for keeping a fence straight and at the right height. You will need strong nylon string (not cotton, which stretches when wet), wooden pegs or steel rods, and a hammer. For corners, you need batter boards: two wooden stakes driven into the ground about 1 metre apart, with a horizontal board nailed across them. The board should be at least 50 cm above ground and level.

Set up batter boards at each corner of the fence. Drive the stakes firmly so they don't move. Use a spirit level to make the crossboard level. Then run a string line between the batter boards, tying it to small nails or notches on the boards. This string represents the outside edge of your fence. For a corner, you need two strings crossing at 90 degrees. Check the corner with a 3-4-5 triangle: measure 3 metres along one string, 4 metres along the other, and adjust until the distance between those points is exactly 5 metres. That gives you a perfect right angle.

For long straight runs, set intermediate batter boards every 1520 metres. The string should just touch the top of each board. If the string sags, tighten it or add more supports. Use a line level (a small spirit level that hangs on the string) to check that the string is level. If the ground slopes, you can set the string at a constant height above the ground, but for a fence, it's often better to follow the ground contour so the fence looks even.

Once the strings are set, you can measure post positions along them. Mark each post centre with a small nail or a dab of paint on the string. The string also gives you the height reference: the top of your posts should be a set distance below or above the string, depending on your design. Keep the string tight and check it daily. A loose string means a crooked fence.

3. Establishing Level and Plumb References

Level means horizontal, plumb means vertical. For a fence, every post must be plumb, and the top of the fence should be level (or follow a steady slope). You need a spirit level (at least 60 cm long), a plumb bob (a weight on a string), and a line level for long distances.

To set a post plumb, hold a spirit level against the side of the post. Adjust the post until the bubble is centred in the vial. Check two sides: one at 90 degrees to the other. If the post is round, you can use a plumb bob: hang it from the top of the post and see if the string touches the bottom centre. If it hangs away, the post is out of plumb. For wooden posts, you can also use a carpenter's square against a straight edge.

For level, you need a reference line. Set up a string line at the desired top height of the fence. Use a line level to make it level. Then measure down from the string to each post hole to get the correct post height. If the ground is uneven, you can use a water level: a clear plastic hose filled with water. Hold one end at the reference point and the other end at the new post location. When the water stops moving, the two ends are at the same level. Mark that height on a stake.

For long fences, a dumpy level or laser level is faster, but they are expensive. A simple alternative is a straight edge and a spirit level: place a long straight board on two posts and check with a level. Adjust until level, then mark the third post. Repeat. Always double-check your work. A post that is 1 cm out of plumb at the bottom can be 5 cm out at the top. Small errors multiply. Take your time and check every post.

4. Locating Underground Utilities

Before you dig, you must find out what is buried. Hitting a water pipe, electrical cable, or fibre optic line can cause injury, cost a lot of money, and cut off services. In many African countries, there is no central 'call before you dig' service, so you must ask locally.

Start by talking to the property owner and neighbours. Ask where the water line, septic tank, and electrical cables run. In rural areas, water pipes are often plastic and only 3050 cm deep. Electrical cables may be direct-buried or in conduits. If the area has a history of mining or old foundations, ask about those too. Look for signs on the surface: patches of different soil, concrete markers, or small metal covers.

If you have access to a metal detector, use it along the fence line. It can find metal pipes, cables, and rebar. For plastic pipes, you can use a thin metal rod or a piece of stiff wire to probe gently into the ground. Push it in slowly; if you hit something solid, stop and dig carefully by hand. Never use a pickaxe or post-hole digger until you are sure there is nothing buried.

If you suspect a utility, dig a small test hole (30 cm x 30 cm) by hand with a trowel or spade. Expose the utility and note its depth and direction. If you find an electrical cable, do not touch it. Call a licensed electrician. For water pipes, you can often route the fence around them or protect the pipe with a sleeve. In some countries, you can hire a private utility locating company, but it may be expensive. Always assume there is something buried until you prove otherwise. Safety first.

5. Digging Post Holes to Correct Depth

The depth of your post hole depends on the post material and fence height. For a 1.8-metre high fence, a wooden post should be buried at least 60 cm in firm soil, or 90 cm in sandy or loose soil. Metal posts for chain-link are usually set 6075 cm deep. A good rule: bury one-third of the total post length. So a 2.4-metre post goes 80 cm into the ground.

Use a post-hole digger (also called a clam-shell digger) for narrow holes. For wider holes, use a spade and a crowbar. If the ground is rocky, you may need a digging bar and a sledgehammer. Start by marking the hole centre with a peg. Dig straight down, keeping the hole vertical. Check with a spirit level as you go. The hole should be about 3 times the width of the post. For a 10 cm post, dig a 30 cm diameter hole.

In clay soil, the hole may hold water. If it does, bail it out before setting the post. In sandy soil, the hole may collapse. You can use a temporary sleeve made from a plastic pipe or a piece of metal sheet to hold the sides. For very loose soil, you may need to dig a wider hole and backfill with concrete or gravel.

Depth is critical. Too shallow, and the post will lean or pull out. Too deep, and you waste concrete and time. Measure the depth with a tape or a marked stick. The bottom of the hole should be flat and firm. If you hit a large rock, you can break it with a crowbar or move the hole slightly. Never set a post on loose soil; tamp the bottom with a piece of wood or a metal bar. After digging, place the post in the hole and check that it is at the right height. Mark the post where it meets the ground, then remove it and dig a little deeper if needed. Repeat for every hole.

6. Setting Posts in Concrete

Concrete is the strongest way to set posts, especially for gates and corners. Use a mix of 1 part cement, 2 parts sand, and 3 parts gravel (or 1:2:3). For a 30 cm diameter hole, 60 cm deep, you need about 0.04 cubic metres of concrete, which is roughly 2 bags of 50 kg cement for every 10 posts. Mix dry ingredients first, then add water until it is like thick porridge. Do not make it too wet.

Place the post in the hole. Check that it is plumb with a spirit level on two sides. You can brace it with wooden stakes and string to hold it straight. Fill the hole with concrete in layers of about 20 cm. Tamp each layer with a stick or a piece of rebar to remove air bubbles. The concrete should slope away from the post at the top so water runs off. Mound it slightly above ground level.

For wooden posts, treat the buried part with tar or bitumen to prevent rot. For metal posts, paint the buried part with anti-rust paint. Let the concrete cure for at least 24 hours before removing braces. Do not hang a gate or pull a fence tight for at least 3 days. In hot weather, cover the concrete with a plastic sheet or wet cloth to slow drying and prevent cracks. In cold weather, protect it from frost.

If you are setting many posts, mix concrete in a wheelbarrow or on a clean hard surface. Wear gloves and a dust mask when handling cement. Work in the morning or evening to avoid extreme heat. After curing, backfill any gaps with soil and compact it. Check each post again for plumb. If a post is slightly off, you can adjust it before the concrete fully hardens. Once hard, it is very difficult to move. So take your time and get it right the first time.

<h2>Practical Reference: Site Preparation and Layout for Gate and Fence Making</h2>

<p>In African agribusiness, a fence is not simply a boundary it is an asset that protects livestock, secures high-value crops, defines grazing rotations, and signals land ownership. Whether you are enclosing a 2-hectare poultry unit in Ogun State, a mango orchard in Machakos, or a 40-head cattle kraal in Chiredzi, the profitability of the enterprise depends on doing site preparation and layout correctly the first time. A poorly set post fails within two rainy seasons; a correctly set post can last fifteen years or more. This lesson gives you the practical field method used by commercial fencing contractors across the continent, adapted for small and medium agribusiness operators working with hand tools and limited equipment.</p>

<h3>Why Layout Is a Business Decision</h3>
<p>Every metre of fence you build has a cost: posts, wire, cement, labour, and critically the opportunity cost of land taken out of production along the fence line. A 1.5 m error in a corner post placement on a 100 m boundary can force you to buy 34 extra posts and 20 m of extra wire, and it can create a gap that livestock will find. In commercial terms, accurate layout is the cheapest quality control you will ever perform. Budget roughly 1015% of total fence cost for site preparation and layout labour, and never skip it to "save time."</p>

<h3>Step 1: Clearing and Marking the Fence Line</h3>
<p>Walk the boundary with the landowner, the farm manager, and where relevant the local authority or chief's representative, and agree the line on the ground before any cutting begins. Clear a working corridor of 1.01.5 m on each side of the centre line. In savanna and bushveld conditions, cut grass and shrubs to ground level with a slasher or brush cutter; in forest or riverine areas, fell small trees and treat stumps with a recommended herbicide to prevent regrowth. Do not burn indiscriminately in many countries, unmanaged burning is illegal and destroys soil organic matter.</p>
<p>Mark the line with pegs at every corner, every gate opening, and at intervals of no more than 30 m along straight runs. Use durable pegs: treated timber offcuts, steel reinforcing bar (Y12 or Y10), or painted hardwood. Paint the tops white or fluorescent orange so they are visible in tall grass. Record the GPS coordinates of corner pegs and gate posts in a farm notebook or phone app this becomes your legal and maintenance record.</p>

<h3>Step 2: Using String Lines and Batter Boards</h3>
<p>A string line is a tensioned cord stretched between pegs to define the exact fence centre line. Use braided nylon mason's line (not cotton twine, which sags when wet). For long runs, support the line every 1520 m with intermediate pegs so it does not sag or catch on grass. Batter boards horizontal boards fixed to pairs of stakes set back 11.5 m from the corner allow you to re-establish the corner position after digging, when the corner peg itself is removed. This is essential for gate openings, where a 50 mm error in post spacing can make a gate impossible to hang.</p>
<p>For square or rectangular enclosures, use the 3-4-5 method to establish a true right angle: measure 3 m along one line, 4 m along the other, and adjust until the diagonal between the two points is exactly 5 m. Scale up (6-8-10, 9-12-15) for larger corners to reduce error. For irregular boundaries, set out the longest straight run first, then work around the perimeter.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Establishing Level and Plumb References</h3>
<p>On sloping African terrain common in the highlands of Ethiopia, Rwanda, and Kenya a fence that follows the ground contour post-by-post will have uneven wire tension and will sag or snap at the steepest points. Establish a level reference using a dumpy level, a laser level, or, for small jobs, a clear water-filled hose (the "water level" method). Mark the level line on a corner post or a temporary stake, then measure down to each post position. On a 15% slope, a post spacing of 3 m may need to be reduced to 2.4 m to keep the wire within the strainer's capacity.</p>
<p>Plumb is equally important: every post must be vertical. Use a spirit level or a plumb bob (a stone or bolt on a string) against the post on two faces. A post leaning 5 off vertical loses roughly 15% of its pull-out resistance and will lean further with each wet season. For gate posts, plumb is non-negotiable a 10 mm lean over a 1.5 m gate post translates into a gate that drags or will not latch.</p>

<h3>Step 4: Locating Underground Utilities</h3>
<p>Before you drive a single post, identify buried services. In African agribusiness settings, these commonly include: water mains and irrigation lines, buried electrical cables (including solar pump feeds), telecoms fibre, and gas lines near agro-processing facilities. Contact the relevant utility provider for example, the local water authority, the national power utility (Kenya Power, Eskom, ZESCO, etc.), or the telecoms operator and request a locate service. Where no formal service exists, hand-dig test pits to 600 mm depth along the line at 5 m intervals and probe carefully with a blunt rod, never a sharpened stake.</p>

<div style="border-left:6px solid #c00; background:#fff2f2; padding:12px 16px; margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c00;"> SAFETY WARNING Buried Services</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Striking a buried electrical cable or gas line with a post driver or auger can cause electrocution, explosion, or fire, and is often fatal. <strong>Never</strong> use powered augers or mechanical post drivers until you have written confirmation of the location of all buried services. Hand-dig with a blunt-nosed spade, and if you uncover an unidentified cable or pipe, <strong>stop work immediately</strong>, mark the spot, and do not resume until the utility owner has inspected and cleared the area. Keep a minimum horizontal clearance of 600 mm from any known service, or the greater distance specified by the utility. Ensure at least one worker on site holds current first-aid and electrical-safety awareness training.</p>
</div>

<h3>Step 5: Digging Post Holes to Correct Depth</h3>
<p>Post hole depth is determined by post height above ground and soil type. As a rule of thumb, bury one-third of the total post length. In loose sandy soils, increase to 40%; in heavy clay, 30% is usually sufficient. Hole diameter should be 2.53 times the post diameter to allow proper concrete or soil compaction around the post.</p>

<h3>Step 6: Setting Posts in Concrete</h3>
<p>Concrete is the most reliable method for strainer, corner, and gate posts, and is increasingly used for intermediate posts in high-value enterprises such as poultry and dairy. Use a 1:3:5 mix (cement : sand : coarse aggregate) or a 1:4:8 mix for non-structural intermediate posts. In many African markets, a 50 kg bag of cement (CEM II 32.5) is the standard unit; one bag makes approximately 0.035 m of concrete at 1:3:5, enough for 34 intermediate posts or 12 gate posts.</p>

<ul>
<li> Boundary agreed in writing with landowner, neighbours, and local authority before any clearing.</li>
<li> Working corridor cleared 1.01.5 m each side of centre line; stumps treated against regrowth.</li>
<li> Corner and gate pegs placed, painted, and GPS coordinates recorded.</li>
