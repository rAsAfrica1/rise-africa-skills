1. Macronutrients and Micronutrients for Cabbage

Cabbage needs three main macronutrients: nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Nitrogen makes leaves grow big and green. A cabbage head that is small and pale often lacks nitrogen. Phosphorus helps roots and head formation. Potassium gives firm, heavy heads and disease resistance. In Africa, a common fertilizer is NPK 15-15-15 or 12-24-12. Apply 200 kg per hectare (about 2 kg per 10 m x 10 m bed) before planting. For a small garden, use 2 tablespoons per plant.

Cabbage also needs calcium and magnesium. Calcium prevents tip burn (brown edges on inner leaves). If your soil is acidic (pH below 6.0), add agricultural lime at 2 tonnes per hectare (200 g per square meter) two months before planting. Magnesium deficiency shows as yellowing between veins on older leaves. Use Epsom salts (magnesium sulfate) at 1 tablespoon per liter of water as a foliar spray.

Micronutrients are needed in tiny amounts but are critical. Boron helps head formation; deficiency causes hollow stems. Molybdenum helps nitrogen use; deficiency causes narrow, whippy leaves. Zinc and iron help leaf color. In most African soils, boron and molybdenum are often low. You can buy a micronutrient mix (like 'Multifeed' or 'Omex') and spray 1 ml per liter every 2 weeks.

Organic sources: well-rotted cattle manure (10-20 tonnes per hectare) supplies all nutrients slowly. Wood ash is rich in potassium and calcium. Bone meal adds phosphorus. Seaweed extract provides micronutrients. Test your soil every 2 years through your local agricultural extension office. Without a test, watch your plants: yellow older leaves = nitrogen; purple leaves = phosphorus; brown leaf edges = potassium. Adjust accordingly.

Remember, too much fertilizer burns roots. Always water after applying. For cabbage, a balanced approach with both organic and mineral fertilizers gives the best heads.

2. Timing of Fertilizer Applications

Cabbage grows in three stages: seedling (0-3 weeks), vegetative (3-8 weeks), and head formation (8-12 weeks). Fertilizer timing must match these stages. At planting, apply a base fertilizer. Use NPK 12-24-12 or 15-15-15 at 200 kg per hectare. For a 10 m x 10 m plot, that's 2 kg. Mix it into the top 15 cm of soil. If you use manure, apply 2 weeks before planting so it doesn't burn seedlings.

Three weeks after transplanting, the cabbage needs a nitrogen boost. This is called side-dressing. Apply urea (46-0-0) at 100 kg per hectare, or 1 kg per 10 m x 10 m plot. Place it 10 cm from the plant base and cover with soil. Water immediately. In sandy soils, split this into two applications: 3 weeks and 5 weeks after transplanting.

When heads start to form (about 8 weeks), apply potassium and phosphorus. Use NPK 0-0-60 (potassium chloride) at 100 kg per hectare, or 1 kg per plot. This makes heads firm and heavy. If you see small heads, you missed the timing. Do not apply nitrogen after head formation starts; it causes loose, leafy heads and delays maturity.

In Africa, rainfall patterns matter. Apply fertilizer just before rain, not during heavy downpour, or nutrients wash away. In dry season, irrigate before and after applying. For sandy soils, apply little and often: every 2 weeks instead of one big dose.

A simple calendar: Week 0: base NPK. Week 3: urea side-dress. Week 5: urea side-dress (if sandy soil). Week 8: potassium. Week 10: foliar spray with micronutrients. This schedule gives high yields. Adjust based on plant color and growth. Always keep records of what you applied and when.

3. Side-Dressing and Top-Dressing Techniques

Side-dressing means placing fertilizer beside the plant, not on the leaves. Top-dressing means spreading fertilizer on the soil surface around plants. For cabbage, side-dressing is better because it puts nutrients near roots. Use a hoe or stick to make a shallow furrow 10-15 cm from the plant stem. Sprinkle fertilizer in the furrow, then cover with soil. This prevents nitrogen loss to the air.

For 100 cabbage plants, you need about 500 g of urea for one side-dressing. That's 5 g per plant, about one teaspoon. Do not touch the stem with fertilizer; it burns. If you have a small garden, use a bottle cap: one capful per plant. After applying, water the soil well. If no rain, irrigate the next day.

Top-dressing is used when plants are dense and you cannot reach the soil. Broadcast urea or NPK evenly over the bed, then water. But this wastes fertilizer because some lands on leaves and burns them. If fertilizer gets on leaves, wash it off with water immediately. Top-dressing works best with soluble fertilizers like calcium nitrate or potassium nitrate.

In Africa, many farmers use a 'ring' method: make a ring of fertilizer around each plant, 10 cm away. This is a type of side-dressing. For cabbage, ring side-dressing with urea at 3 and 5 weeks after transplanting gives excellent results.

Another technique is fertigation: mixing soluble fertilizer in irrigation water. Use a watering can with 1 tablespoon of NPK 20-20-20 per 10 liters of water. Apply 1 liter per plant every week. This is precise and saves fertilizer.

Always wear gloves when handling fertilizer. Store fertilizer in a dry place, off the ground. After side-dressing, check plants after 3 days: if leaves turn dark green, it worked. If they stay yellow, apply again.

4. Foliar Feeding for Quick Nutrient Uptake

Foliar feeding means spraying liquid fertilizer directly onto leaves. Plants absorb nutrients through their leaves faster than through roots. This is useful when cabbage shows deficiency symptoms or when roots are damaged. For example, if you see yellow leaves between veins (magnesium deficiency), a foliar spray of Epsom salts works in 2-3 days.

To make a foliar spray, use a knapsack sprayer or a hand sprayer. For 10 liters of water, add 20 g of urea (2 tablespoons) for nitrogen. For micronutrients, use a commercial mix like 'Multifeed' or 'Omex' at 5 ml per liter. For calcium, use calcium nitrate at 10 g per liter. Always test on a few leaves first to avoid burning.

Spray early morning or late evening when it's cool. Never spray in hot sun (above 30C) because leaves burn. Spray the undersides of leaves too, where stomata are. The spray should drip off leaves slightly. Repeat every 7-10 days until symptoms disappear.

Foliar feeding is not a replacement for soil fertilizer. It's a quick fix. Use it when: plants are young and roots are small, soil is too dry or too wet for root uptake, or you see a specific deficiency. For cabbage, a foliar spray at head formation with potassium and boron improves head size and firmness.

In Africa, you can make your own foliar feed: soak 1 kg of fresh cow manure in 10 liters of water for 3 days, strain, and spray. This gives some nitrogen and microbes. Or use wood ash: 1 cup in 10 liters of water, strain, and spray for potassium.

Always clean your sprayer after use. Store chemicals away from children. Wear a mask and gloves when spraying. Foliar feeding is a powerful tool when used correctly.

5. Organic Fertilizers and Amendments

Organic fertilizers come from plants, animals, or minerals. They release nutrients slowly, improve soil structure, and feed soil life. For cabbage, the best organic fertilizer is well-rotted cattle or poultry manure. Apply 10-20 tonnes per hectare (1-2 kg per square meter) two weeks before planting. Fresh manure burns plants; always compost it first.

Compost is made from crop residues, kitchen waste, and manure. Build a heap 1.5 m high, turn it every 2 weeks, and keep it moist. In 2-3 months, it becomes dark, crumbly compost. Apply 2-4 kg per square meter. Compost adds nutrients and holds water.

Wood ash is a good source of potassium and calcium. Spread a thin layer (1 cup per square meter) around cabbage plants. Do not mix ash with nitrogen fertilizers; it causes ammonia loss. Bone meal provides phosphorus; apply 100 g per square meter at planting.

Green manures are plants grown to improve soil. Plant cowpeas or sunhemp before cabbage, then plough them in before flowering. They add nitrogen and organic matter.

In Africa, other local amendments include: chicken feathers (slow-release nitrogen), crushed eggshells (calcium), and banana peels (potassium). Use them in compost.

Organic farming is not just about nutrients. It builds soil health. A soil rich in organic matter holds water better, resists erosion, and supports beneficial microbes. For cabbage, combine organic and mineral fertilizers for best results. For example, apply manure at planting, then side-dress with urea at 3 weeks. This gives both slow and fast nutrients.

Always test manure for contamination. Avoid manure from animals treated with antibiotics. Store organic fertilizers under cover to prevent nutrient leaching. With good organic management, you can reduce mineral fertilizer by half.

6. Monitoring Plant Health and Adjusting Nutrition

Monitoring means watching your cabbage plants regularly to see if they need more or less fertilizer. Walk through your field every 3 days. Look at leaf color, leaf size, and head formation. A healthy cabbage has dark green, glossy leaves and a firm, round head.

Yellow lower leaves mean nitrogen deficiency. Apply urea side-dress at 5 g per plant. If the whole plant is pale, spray foliar urea at 20 g per 10 liters. Purple leaves mean phosphorus deficiency, often in cold, wet soil. Add bone meal or NPK. Brown leaf edges (tip burn) mean calcium deficiency. Add lime or spray calcium nitrate. Yellow between veins on older leaves means magnesium deficiency. Spray Epsom salts.

Keep a notebook. Record date, what you saw, what you applied, and the result. For example: '10 May: lower leaves yellow. Applied 5 g urea per plant. 13 May: leaves green again.' This helps you learn.

Use a soil test kit or send samples to an extension lab. Test pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium. Cabbage likes pH 6.0-6.8. If pH is below 6.0, add lime. If above 7.0, add compost or sulfur.

Adjust based on weather. In heavy rain, nitrogen leaches away; apply more. In drought, nutrients cannot move to roots; irrigate first. In cool weather, phosphorus uptake slows; add foliar phosphorus.

If plants are too leafy and heads are small, you applied too much nitrogen. Stop nitrogen and apply potassium. If heads crack, it's uneven watering, not nutrition.

Monitoring is not a one-time thing. It's a habit. The best cabbage farmers check their crops daily. They adjust small amounts often, rather than large amounts once. This saves money and gives better yields. Always compare your crop to a healthy reference plant. Take photos to track progress.

<h2>Practical Reference: Crop Nutrition and Fertilization for Cabbage</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core lessons of the <strong>Crop Nutrition and Fertilization</strong> module into a field-ready guide for African cabbage growers. Cabbage (<em>Brassica oleracea</em> var. <em>capitata</em>) is a heavy feeder. It removes large quantities of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, and boron from the soil, and it is sensitive to both deficiency and excess. For commercial growers supplying urban markets, hotels, schools, and processing buyers, nutrition management is not a background activity it is a direct driver of head weight, uniformity, shelf life, and profit per hectare.</p>

<p>In African production systems, cabbage is grown across a wide range of conditions: highland areas of Kenya, Ethiopia, Tanzania, and Rwanda; peri-urban farms around Accra, Lagos, and Nairobi; and irrigated dry-season plots in Senegal and Zimbabwe. Soil fertility varies from volcanic loams to sandy coastal soils and heavily weathered laterites. The practical implication is that blanket fertilizer recommendations fail. A business-first approach means soil testing, targeted application, and frequent crop monitoring to protect every unit of investment.</p>

<h3>Understanding Macronutrients and Micronutrients</h3>

<p>Cabbage requires six macronutrients in large quantities: nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), and sulfur (S). It also needs micronutrients boron (B), zinc (Zn), manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), molybdenum (Mo), and chlorine (Cl) in small but essential amounts. A crop producing 60 tonnes per hectare typically removes approximately 180250 kg N, 4060 kg PO, and 200280 kg KO per hectare, depending on yield, variety, and soil type.</p>

<p>Nitrogen drives leaf expansion and head size. Phosphorus supports root development and early establishment. Potassium regulates water balance, head firmness, and storage quality. Calcium prevents tip-burn, a major market-reject defect. Boron is critical for head formation; deficiency causes hollow stem and brown heart. Sulfur supports flavor compounds and nitrogen use efficiency. In many African soils, especially sandy and eroded ones, boron, zinc, and sulfur are the hidden constraints that limit yield even when NPK is applied.</p>

<h3>Timing of Fertilizer Applications</h3>

<p>The most profitable fertilizer program for cabbage follows crop demand. Apply phosphorus and potassium as a basal dressing at or before transplanting, because these nutrients move slowly in soil and must be in the root zone early. Apply nitrogen in split doses: a starter dose at transplanting, a side-dress at 34 weeks, and a top-dress at 68 weeks just before head initiation. This split reduces leaching losses, especially on sandy soils and under heavy rainfall.</p>

<p>In African conditions, two factors often disrupt timing: unreliable rainfall and cash flow. Farmers may delay fertilizer until they have money, which can coincide with the crop's peak demand period. A business-first response is to plan fertilizer purchases with the same discipline as seed purchases, and to use smaller, more frequent applications rather than one large application. Where irrigation is available, fertigation allows precise timing and can improve nitrogen use efficiency by 2030% compared with broadcasting.</p>

<h3>Side-Dressing and Top-Dressing Techniques</h3>

<p>Side-dressing places fertilizer near the root zone but not directly against the stem. For cabbage, band fertilizer 1015 cm from the plant row and 58 cm deep, then cover lightly with soil. This reduces volatilization of urea and places nutrients where roots can access them. Top-dressing is similar but is usually applied later, between rows, when the canopy is closing. Avoid broadcasting urea on wet leaves in hot sun it can cause leaf burn and ammonia loss.</p>

<p>On smallholder plots, farmers often apply fertilizer by hand. The key discipline is even distribution. Uneven application creates variability in head size, which reduces market value. For larger farms, calibrated fertilizer spreaders or injectors improve uniformity and reduce labor. In all cases, apply when soil is moist but not waterlogged, and never apply nitrogen immediately before heavy rain.</p>

<h3>Foliar Feeding for Quick Nutrient Uptake</h3>

<p>Foliar feeding is a rapid correction tool, not a replacement for soil fertilization. It is most useful for calcium, boron, magnesium, and micronutrients, which are needed in small quantities and can be absorbed through leaves within hours. Apply foliar sprays in early morning or late afternoon to avoid evaporation and leaf burn. Use a surfactant where recommended, and ensure the spray reaches the underside of leaves where stomata are concentrated.</p>

<p>For cabbage, a calcium-boron foliar spray at head initiation and again 1014 days later can significantly reduce tip-burn and hollow stem, especially in crops growing on sandy or high-pH soils. Foliar nitrogen (urea at 12%) can be used as a quick boost if the crop shows nitrogen deficiency and soil application would be too slow. Do not exceed recommended concentrations more is not better and can cause leaf burn.</p>

<p style="margin: 8px 0 0 0;">Always wear gloves, boots, and a face mask when handling fertilizers, especially urea, ammonium nitrate, and micronutrient powders. Store fertilizers in a dry, ventilated place away from children, livestock, food, and seed. Never mix fertilizers with bare hands or in food containers. Wash hands and exposed skin immediately after application. If fertilizer contacts eyes, rinse with clean water for at least 15 minutes and seek medical attention.</p>

<h3>Organic Fertilizers and Amendments</h3>

<p>Organic inputs are central to sustainable cabbage production in Africa. Well-decomposed cattle manure, poultry litter, compost, and green manures improve soil structure, water-holding capacity, and microbial activity. They also supply slow-release nutrients and micronutrients. Apply 510 tonnes per hectare of good compost or manure 23 weeks before transplanting. Poultry litter is richer in nitrogen and should be used carefully to avoid salt burn.</p>

<p>Biochar, wood ash, and lime are useful amendments. Lime raises pH and supplies calcium, which helps prevent tip-burn. Wood ash supplies potassium and raises pH but should be used in moderation. Legume green manures such as lablab, cowpea, or mucuna can be grown before cabbage to fix nitrogen and add organic matter. In acid soils common in highland areas, liming to pH 6.06.8 is one of the highest-return investments a cabbage grower can make.</p>

<h3>Monitoring Plant Health and Adjusting Nutrition</h3>

<p>Scout the crop at least twice a week. Look for leaf color, leaf size, head formation, and symptoms of deficiency or toxicity. A simple leaf color chart, available from extension services in several African countries, helps assess nitrogen status. Tissue testing, where available, gives precise information. Soil testing before planting remains the foundation of any fertilizer plan.</p>

<p>Keep a field record book: date, product, rate, method, and observed crop response. This data turns each season into a business learning cycle. If heads are small and pale, nitrogen may be limiting. If leaves have scorched margins, potassium may be deficient or salinity may be high. If inner leaves show tip-burn, calcium and boron need attention, often combined with more consistent irrigation. Adjust the next application based on evidence, not habit.</p>
