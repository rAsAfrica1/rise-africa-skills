1. Identifying Your Customers: Households, Restaurants, Schools, and Industries

Before you make one more briquette, you need to know who will buy it. Your customers fall into four main groups, and each has different needs. Households buy in small amounts, maybe 520 kg per week. They care about price, smoke, and how long the briquette burns. Restaurants and hotels buy 50200 kg per week. They need consistent heat and low ash, because they cook for many people. Schools and institutions buy in bulk, sometimes 500 kg or more per month. They often buy through a tender or procurement officer. Industries like brick kilns or tea factories need tonnes per month and care about energy value and reliable supply.

Start by listing every potential customer within 5 km of your workshop. Walk around your neighbourhood and note: How many restaurants? How many schools? Are there any factories? For each, write down what they currently use for cooking or heating charcoal, firewood, or electricity. Then estimate how much they spend per week. For example, a small restaurant might spend 3,000 KES (about $25) on charcoal each week. If your briquettes cost 20% less and burn longer, you have a strong pitch.

Talk to at least 10 potential customers before you produce a large batch. Ask them: What do you dislike about your current fuel? How much do you pay? Would you try a sample? Their answers will tell you which customer group to target first. Many successful briquette businesses start with restaurants because they buy regularly and give quick feedback. Schools are good for large orders but take longer to close. Households are the biggest market but require door-to-door selling or retail shops.

Once you know your customers, you can tailor your product. For households, make small, easy-to-light briquettes. For restaurants, make larger, longer-burning ones. For schools, offer bulk discounts and reliable delivery. For industries, provide a consistent calorific value and a contract. Write down your top 20 target customers with their contact details and weekly fuel spend. This list becomes your sales plan. Without knowing your customer, you are just guessing. With this list, you have a clear path to your first sale.

2. Pricing Strategies for Profit in Local Markets

Price too high, and no one buys. Price too low, and you work for nothing. To find the right price, you must first know your cost per kilogram. Add up all your costs: raw materials (sawdust, charcoal dust, binder), labour, rent, transport, and packaging. For example, if you produce 100 kg of briquettes in a day and your total daily cost is 2,000 KES, your cost per kg is 20 KES. You must sell above that to make a profit.

Next, check what your competitors charge. Visit local charcoal sellers and ask their price per kg or per sack. In many African markets, a 50 kg sack of charcoal sells for 1,5002,500 KES. That is 3050 KES per kg. If your briquettes cost 25 KES per kg to make, you could sell at 3540 KES per kg and still be cheaper than charcoal. But remember: briquettes may burn differently, so customers compare value, not just price.

Use a simple pricing formula: Cost + Desired Profit Margin = Selling Price. If you want a 30% profit margin, multiply your cost by 1.3. So 20 KES cost 1.3 = 26 KES selling price. But also consider volume discounts. For a restaurant buying 100 kg per week, offer 10% off if they pay cash on delivery. For a school buying 500 kg per month, offer 15% off and free delivery. This encourages larger orders and builds loyalty.

Test your price with real customers. Start with a slightly higher price and see if sales drop. If they do, lower it. If customers don't complain, you might be too cheap. Also, watch your competitors' prices and adjust. Never compete only on price compete on quality, reliability, and service. A customer will pay a little more if you always deliver on time and your briquettes burn well. Keep a simple record of your costs and sales each week. After one month, you will know your true profit per kg and can adjust your strategy. Remember, the goal is not just to sell, but to make a living.

3. Selling at Markets, Trade Fairs, and Door-to-Door

Markets and trade fairs are the fastest way to meet many customers in one day. At a local market, rent a small stall for 5001,000 KES per day. Bring 50100 kg of briquettes, a small cooking stove, and a sample pot. Set up a demonstration: light a few briquettes and boil water or cook a simple dish like beans. Let people see the low smoke and long burn. Hand out small samples (23 briquettes) in paper bags with your phone number. Sell 5 kg bags for a special market price, e.g., 150 KES instead of 175 KES. Keep a notebook to record names and phone numbers of interested buyers. Follow up with a WhatsApp message the next day.

Trade fairs, like agricultural shows or county trade fairs, attract bigger buyers restaurants, schools, and NGOs. A booth may cost 2,0005,000 KES for a week, but it is worth it if you get bulk orders. Prepare a professional signboard with your business name, phone number, and the words 'Clean, Cheap, Long-Burning Briquettes'. Bring a price list for bulk orders: 50 kg for 1,200 KES, 100 kg for 2,200 KES. Have a sign-up sheet for people who want a free sample delivered. Collect business cards from procurement officers. After the fair, call every contact within 48 hours.

Door-to-door selling works well in residential areas. Start with your own neighbourhood. Knock on doors, introduce yourself, and offer a free sample of 2 briquettes. If they like it, sell a 5 kg bag for 150 KES. Ask for referrals: 'If you like it, tell your neighbour and I will give you 10% off your next bag.' Spend 23 hours per day, three days a week. Track how many doors you knock, how many samples you give, and how many sales you make. A good conversion rate is 1 sale for every 10 doors. Over time, you build a regular customer base. Always wear clean clothes, be polite, and carry a receipt book. Word-of-mouth is powerful one happy customer can bring five more.

4. Using WhatsApp, Facebook, and Radio for Promotion

WhatsApp is the most powerful free tool for selling briquettes in Africa. Create a WhatsApp Business account with your business name, logo, and a catalog of your products. Post clear photos of your briquettes burning in a stove, with a pot of food. Write short captions: '5 kg for 150 KES. Burns 3 hours. Low smoke. Order now.' Save all your customers' numbers in a broadcast list. Send a weekly message with a special offer, e.g., 'Buy 2 bags, get 1 kg free this Friday.' Use WhatsApp Status to show behind-the-scenes videos of production. Reply to every message within 1 hour. Use the 'Quick Replies' feature for common questions like price and delivery. Ask happy customers to send a voice note or photo testimonial that you can forward to others.

Facebook helps you reach a wider audience. Create a Facebook Page for your business. Join local buy-and-sell groups in your town or city. Post your product with a price and a photo. For example: 'Clean briquettes for restaurants and homes. 50 kg sack at 1,200 KES. Free delivery within 5 km. Call 07XX-XXXXXX.' Boost a post for as little as 500 KES to reach 1,000 people in your area. Use Facebook Marketplace to list your briquettes for sale. Respond to comments quickly. Share customer testimonials and photos of your briquettes being used in a school or restaurant. This builds trust.

Radio is still king in many rural areas. Local radio stations charge 5002,000 KES for a 30-second advert. Write a simple script: 'Tired of smoky charcoal? Try EcoBriquettes from [Your Business]. They burn longer, cost less, and are clean. Call 07XX-XXXXXX for a free sample. That's 07XX-XXXXXX.' Run the ad 3 times a day for a week. Also, ask to be a guest on a local talk show to talk about clean cooking. Combine radio with WhatsApp: give a special code word like 'RADIO10' for 10% off if they call within 7 days. Track which promotion brings the most sales by asking every caller: 'How did you hear about us?' Then double down on what works.

5. Demonstrating Your Product: Cooking Tests and Samples

People will not buy briquettes they have never seen burn. A live cooking test is your best salesperson. Choose a visible spot a market, a busy street corner, or a customer's kitchen. Bring a small charcoal stove (jiko), 1 kg of your briquettes, a pot, water, and some dry beans or maize. Light 45 briquettes. While they catch, talk to the crowd about the benefits: less smoke, no sparks, longer burn. Time how long it takes to boil 1 litre of water. Compare with charcoal if possible. Let people see that your briquettes produce steady heat for 23 hours. Cook a simple dish and offer small tastes. This builds trust and creates a crowd.

Samples are the key to converting interest into sales. Prepare small sample packs of 23 briquettes in paper bags or old newspaper. Hand them out at markets, door-to-door, and to restaurants. Always include a small card with your phone number and price list. For restaurants, offer a free 5 kg sample to test for one week. Ask them to compare with their current fuel: How much did they use? How long did it burn? How much ash? Follow up after 3 days. If they are happy, ask for a first order of 50 kg. For schools, offer a free demonstration in their kitchen and a 10 kg sample. If the cooks like it, they will recommend it to the procurement officer.

Keep records of every sample you give. Write down the date, name, phone number, and quantity. Follow up within 2 days with a WhatsApp message: 'Hello, did you try the briquettes? How did they work? I can deliver 10 kg tomorrow for 300 KES.' If they say no, ask why. Their feedback helps you improve. A good sample program can convert 3050% of recipients into paying customers. Always carry a few sample bags with you you never know when you will meet a potential buyer. And always demonstrate with confidence: your belief in the product is contagious.

6. Building Relationships with Retailers and Distributors

Retailers and distributors can multiply your sales without you doing all the work. A retailer is a shop, kiosk, or hardware store that sells your briquettes to walk-in customers. A distributor buys in bulk and sells to many retailers or large institutions. To get a retailer, visit local shops that already sell charcoal or cooking supplies. Introduce yourself and offer a free 5 kg sample for them to try. Explain your price structure: you sell to them at 120 KES per 5 kg bag, and they can sell at 150 KES, making 30 KES profit per bag. Offer to take back any unsold bags within 30 days this reduces their risk. Provide a simple display stand or poster with your phone number. Check in every week to restock and collect payment.

For distributors, you need a clear contract. Agree on a minimum order, e.g., 500 kg per month. Offer a 20% discount off your retail price. Provide delivery within 20 km. Set payment terms: cash on delivery or 7-day credit for trusted partners. Give them marketing materials: posters, price lists, and sample bags. Train their sales staff on how to demonstrate and sell. A good distributor can sell 23 tonnes per month, giving you steady income and freeing you to produce more.

Maintain these relationships with regular communication. Send a WhatsApp message every Monday with stock availability and any promotions. Visit in person once a month. Ask for feedback: What do customers say? What sizes sell best? Adjust your production accordingly. Offer incentives: if a retailer sells 100 kg in a month, give them a free 10 kg bag. If a distributor exceeds 1 tonne, give a 5% bonus. Always deliver on time and be honest about stock levels. Trust is everything. When a retailer or distributor trusts you, they will push your product to their customers. Over time, you can build a network of 1020 retailers and 23 distributors, which can grow your business from a small workshop to a regional supplier.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Marketing and Selling Your Briquettes & Pellets

You have produced clean-burning briquettes or pellets. Now the business only survives if someone pays for them repeatedly. This reference lesson gives you the practical marketing and sales tools for African markets: who buys, what they will pay, where to sell, how to promote cheaply, how to prove your product works, and how to keep retailers and distributors loyal. Treat this as your field manual. Every decision here affects your margin, your cash flow, and your reputation.

Briquette and pellet enterprises across Africa from Kenya and Uganda to Ghana, Zambia, and Senegal succeed when they treat marketing as seriously as production. The market is not waiting for you. You must go to it with samples, prices, proof, and follow-up.

2. Pricing Strategies for Profit in Local Markets

Price too low and you kill the business. Price too high and you lose the market. Start by calculating your true cost per kilogram: raw material, binder, labour, fuel for the press, transport, packaging, and a share of your equipment. Then add your target margin.

Pricing tactics that work:

  • Anchor against charcoal. If charcoal sells at X per sack, price your briquettes so the cost per cooking session is equal or lower then sell the smoke and health benefit.
  • Bundle packs. A 5 kg pack at a round price is easier to sell than loose weight.
  • Give volume discounts to restaurants and schools, but never below your total cost.
  • Review monthly. Raw material and transport costs move. Adjust prices with notice, not silently.

3. Selling at Markets, Trade Fairs, and Door-to-Door

Markets and trade fairs give you foot traffic and credibility. Door-to-door builds trust block by block. At a market, bring a working stove, light a batch, and let people see the flame and smell the difference. At trade fairs, collect names and phone numbers a fair without follow-up is just a show.

Door-to-door works best in the late afternoon when people are cooking. Carry small sample packs, a price list, and a notebook. Record every interested household. Return within three days with stock.

4. Using WhatsApp, Facebook, and Radio for Promotion

These three channels cover most African markets at low cost.

  • WhatsApp: Create a business profile with your location, price list, and product photos. Use status updates to show production, deliveries, and customer cooking tests. Build broadcast lists for restaurants and retailers. Respond fast within an hour if possible.
  • Facebook: Post short videos of your briquettes burning. Join local buy-and-sell groups. Use Marketplace for small-pack sales. Tag your town and district so nearby buyers find you.
  • Radio: Community and vernacular radio reach rural and peri-urban customers who are not online. Buy short spots or sponsor a farming/cooking programme. Mention your WhatsApp number and market days.

5. Demonstrating Your Product: Cooking Tests and Samples

Nobody buys a fuel they have not seen burn. Demonstration is your strongest sales tool.

  • Boil one litre of water on your briquettes and on charcoal side by side. Time both. Show the smoke difference.
  • Give free 12 kg samples to influential cooks, restaurant owners, and school bursars. Ask for honest feedback.
  • Keep a simple feedback sheet: ignition time, burn duration, ash, smell, any problems.
  • Use feedback to improve the product and use positive quotes in your WhatsApp and Facebook posts.

6. Building Relationships with Retailers and Distributors

Retailers multiply your reach. Treat them as partners, not just buyers.

  • Offer a clear wholesale price and a fair retail margin (typically 2035%).
  • Start with small consignment stock if they are hesitant, then move to cash sales.
  • Provide branded packaging, price stickers, and a simple poster.
  • Visit regularly, replace slow-moving stock, and solve complaints quickly.
  • Pay commissions or bonuses for volume targets to keep them motivated.

SAFETY WARNING Carbon Monoxide and Fire Risk

Briquettes and pellets still produce carbon monoxide (CO), a colourless, odourless gas that can kill. Never demonstrate or use briquettes in a closed room without ventilation. Always burn in a well-ventilated space or outdoors. Keep a bucket of water or sand near any demonstration fire. Store stock away from open flames, and never let children handle burning stoves. When demonstrating indoors for a customer, open windows and doors and keep the session short. If anyone feels dizzy, headachy, or nauseous, move them outside immediately and seek medical help.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Know your cost per kg before you set any price never sell below it.
  2. Match pack size to the customer: small for households, bulk for schools and industries.
  3. Carry samples and a working stove to every market, fair, and door-to-door visit.
  4. Post product photos, prices, and cooking videos on WhatsApp and Facebook at least twice a week.
  5. Run a side-by-side cooking test against charcoal for every new customer group.
  6. Collect phone numbers and follow up within three days every time.
  7. Give retailers a fair margin, branded packaging, and regular restocking visits.
  8. Review prices and customer feedback monthly and adjust your plan.
