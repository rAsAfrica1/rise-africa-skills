1. Clothing and Colors

In Swahili, clothing items have specific names that you'll use daily in markets. A shirt is 'shati', trousers are 'suruali', a dress is 'gauni', and a skirt is 'sketi'. For shoes, say 'viatu', and a hat is 'kofia'. When shopping, you'll often ask for sizes: 'Ninaitaji shati la kati' means 'I need a medium shirt'. Remember that Swahili nouns belong to classes, so adjectives must agree. For colors, common ones include: -ekundu (red), -eusi (black), -eupe (white), -a kijani (green), -a bluu (blue), -a njano (yellow). The prefix changes with the noun: 'shati jekundu' (red shirt), 'suruali nyeusi' (black trousers). Practice by describing what you see. In many African markets, like Kariakoo in Dar es Salaam or Balogun in Lagos, you'll bargain for clothes. Start by asking 'Bei gani?' (How much?). If you want a different color, say 'Ninaitaji rangi nyingine' (I need another color). To ask for a larger size: 'Una ukubwa mkubwa?' (Do you have a bigger size?). For a smaller size: 'Una ukubwa mdogo?' (Do you have a smaller size?). When trying on, ask 'Naweza kujaribu?' (Can I try it?). Vendors may say 'Inakufaa' (It suits you). Learn to negotiate: 'Punguza kidogo' (Reduce a little). If the price is too high, say 'Ni ghali sana' (It's too expensive). Always check quality: 'Hii ni nzuri?' (Is this good?). In East Africa, second-hand clothes (mitumba) are common and affordable. You can find them in open-air markets. For new clothes, prices are higher. Knowing colors helps you get exactly what you want. Practice with a friend: point to clothes and name them in Swahili. This builds vocabulary fast. Also, note that some colors have cultural meanings. For example, white is often worn for celebrations, while black is for mourning. But in shopping, focus on practical use. When you pay, you might hear 'Asante' (Thank you). Respond with 'Karibu' (You're welcome). Now you're ready to shop for clothes in Swahili-speaking Africa.

2. Numbers 11-100

Counting in Swahili from 11 to 100 is straightforward once you know the pattern. Eleven is 'kumi na moja' (ten and one), twelve is 'kumi na mbili', and so on up to nineteen: 'kumi na tisa'. Twenty is 'ishirini', thirty is 'thelathini', forty is 'arobaini', fifty is 'hamsini', sixty is 'sitini', seventy is 'sabini', eighty is 'themashini', ninety is 'tisini', and one hundred is 'mia moja'. For numbers in between, combine the tens with 'na' (and) plus the unit. For example, twenty-one is 'ishirini na moja', thirty-five is 'thelathini na tano', forty-seven is 'arobaini na saba'. Note that 'na' is used for all. When bargaining, you'll need these numbers constantly. If a vendor says 'Elfu kumi' (ten thousand), you might counter with 'Elfu nane' (eight thousand). In many African countries, prices are in thousands due to inflation. For instance, in Tanzania, a shirt might cost 'elfu kumi na tano' (15,000 shillings). In Kenya, 'mia tano' (500 shillings). Practice saying numbers aloud. Start with 11-20, then 21-30, and so on. Use a chart or flashcards. When shopping, ask 'Bei ni ngapi?' (What is the price?). The answer will be a number. Repeat it back to confirm: 'Elfu kumi na mbili?' (Twelve thousand?). If you don't understand, say 'Samahani, sikuelewa' (Sorry, I didn't understand). You can also ask them to write it down: 'Andika bei, tafadhali' (Write the price, please). For larger numbers, like 100,000, say 'laki moja' in East Africa (from Indian influence). So 200,000 is 'laki mbili'. In some areas, 'mia mbili' means 200, but for 100,000, 'laki' is common. Learn to count money quickly. If an item is 45,000, say 'arobaini na tano elfu'. Break it down: 40 (arobaini) + 5 (tano) + thousand (elfu). Practice with real prices from local markets. This skill will save you money and earn respect from vendors. Remember, fluency in numbers builds confidence in bargaining.

3. Shopping at the Market

African markets are vibrant, crowded, and full of opportunities to practice Swahili. Start by greeting the vendor: 'Habari ya asubuhi' (Good morning) or 'Habari za mchana' (Good afternoon). A respectful greeting opens the door to better prices. Then state what you need: 'Natafuta nyanya' (I'm looking for tomatoes) or 'Nahitaji kuni' (I need firewood). In Swahili-speaking regions, common market items include: 'mboga' (vegetables), 'matunda' (fruits), 'nyama' (meat), 'samaki' (fish), 'mchele' (rice), 'mahindi' (maize), 'maharage' (beans), and 'maziwa' (milk). When you see something you like, ask 'Hii ni bei gani?' (How much is this?). The vendor will give a price. Don't accept the first price; it's usually inflated for foreigners. Counter with a lower offer. For example, if they say 'elfu tano' (5,000), you can say 'Nitatoa elfu tatu' (I'll give 3,000). They might say 'Punguza kidogo' (Reduce a little) meaning you should come up. Negotiate until you agree. If you can't agree, walk away slowly; sometimes they'll call you back with a better price. Always check the quality: 'Hii ni mbichi?' (Is this fresh?) for produce. For meat, ask 'Nyama ni ya ng'ombe?' (Is it beef?). For fish, 'Samaki ni wabichi?' (Are the fish fresh?). Bring your own bag: 'Nina mkoba wangu' (I have my own bag). In many markets, you can buy in bulk for a discount: 'Nikitaka kiasi kingi, utapunguza?' (If I want a large quantity, will you reduce?). Also, learn to ask for a receipt if needed: 'Nipe risiti, tafadhali' (Give me a receipt, please). In some countries, like Kenya, you might use M-Pesa for payment. Say 'Ninalipa kwa M-Pesa' (I'm paying by M-Pesa). Always count your change: 'Hii ni chenji yangu?' (Is this my change?). If shortchanged, say 'Nimepungukiwa' (I've been shortchanged). Be polite but firm. Building rapport with vendors can lead to better prices and fresher goods over time. Remember, market shopping is a social activity. Take your time, chat, and enjoy the experience.

4. Bargaining and Negotiating Prices

Bargaining is an art in African markets, and Swahili has the phrases you need to succeed. Start by asking the price: 'Bei gani?' or 'Ni bei gani?' The vendor will give an opening price, often high. Your first response should be 'Ni ghali sana!' (It's very expensive!) or 'Punguza!' (Reduce!). Then make a counteroffer. For example, if they say 'elfu kumi' (10,000), you might say 'Naweza kutoa elfu saba' (I can give 7,000). Use numbers you learned. The vendor may say 'Punguza kidogo' meaning you should increase your offer a bit. You can respond with 'Ni bei ya mwisho?' (Is that your final price?) If they say 'Ndiyo' (Yes), you can decide to accept or walk away. Walking away is a powerful tactic. Say 'Nitafikiria' (I'll think about it) and start leaving. Often they'll call 'Rudi! Rudi!' (Come back! Come back!) and offer a lower price. Another phrase: 'Nipe bei nafuu' (Give me a good price). If you're buying multiple items, ask for a discount: 'Nikinunua vitu viwili, utapunguza?' (If I buy two items, will you reduce?). For a bundle, say 'Kwa vyote, bei gani?' (For all, what price?). Sometimes vendors use 'Bei ya jioni' (evening price) as a joke for a final discount. In some cultures, bargaining is expected, but in others, like supermarkets, prices are fixed. Know the difference. In open markets, always bargain. In malls, don't. Also, be respectful. Don't insult the item or the vendor. Use polite language: 'Tafadhali' (Please) and 'Asante' (Thank you). If you agree on a price, say 'Sawa, nitanunua' (Okay, I'll buy it). Then pay. If you can't agree, say 'Asante, nitakuja kesho' (Thank you, I'll come tomorrow). This leaves the door open. Practice bargaining with a friend. Set a role-play: one is vendor, one is buyer. Use real prices from your local market. This builds confidence. Remember, bargaining is a conversation, not a fight. Smile, be friendly, and you'll get a fair price.

5. Money and Currency

Understanding money in Swahili is crucial for shopping. The word for money is 'pesa'. In East Africa, common currencies include Tanzanian Shilling (TZS), Kenyan Shilling (KES), Ugandan Shilling (UGX), and Rwandan Franc (RWF). In Swahili, you'll say 'shilingi' for shilling. For example, 'shilingi elfu kumi' means 10,000 shillings. Coins are 'sarafu' and notes are 'noti' or 'pesa za karatasi'. When asking for change, say 'Nipe chenji, tafadhali' (Give me change, please). If you need to break a large note, ask 'Una chenji ya elfu kumi?' (Do you have change for 10,000?). Common phrases: 'Nina pesa' (I have money), 'Hakuna pesa' (No money), 'Pesa ngapi?' (How much money?). In markets, you might hear 'Pesa mkononi' (Cash in hand). Mobile money is huge in Africa. In Kenya, M-Pesa is common; in Tanzania, M-Pesa, Tigo Pesa, and Airtel Money. To pay by mobile money, say 'Ninalipa kwa M-Pesa' or 'Nitatoa kwa simu' (I'll pay by phone). You'll need to know your PIN: 'PIN yangu ni...' When receiving money, 'Nimepokea pesa' (I have received money). For budgeting, learn to say 'Nina bajeti ya elfu hamsini' (I have a budget of 50,000). If something is too expensive, 'Imepita bajeti yangu' (It's over my budget). Also, be aware of counterfeit notes. Ask 'Hii ni pesa halisi?' (Is this real money?). In some countries, prices are in thousands, so 'elfu' is used often. For example, 'elfu tano' = 5,000. In Tanzania, 'laki' means 100,000. So 'laki moja' = 100,000. Practice counting money in Swahili. Use real notes or coins. Name them: 'noti ya elfu moja' (1,000 note), 'sarafu ya mia tano' (500 coin). When you travel, exchange money at banks or forex bureaus. Ask 'Bei ya kubadilisha pesa ni gani?' (What is the exchange rate?). Always keep small notes for easy transactions. Knowing money vocabulary makes shopping smoother and helps you avoid being cheated. Practice with a friend: one acts as cashier, one as customer. This builds real-world skills.

6. Common Shopping Phrases

Here are essential Swahili phrases for shopping, organized for quick reference. Greetings: 'Habari' (Hello), 'Shikamoo' (Respectful greeting to elders), 'Marahaba' (Response to Shikamoo). Asking for items: 'Nahitaji...' (I need...), 'Natafuta...' (I'm looking for...), 'Una...?' (Do you have...?). For example, 'Una sukari?' (Do you have sugar?). Asking prices: 'Bei gani?' (How much?), 'Ni bei gani?' (What is the price?), 'Punguza' (Reduce). Bargaining: 'Ni ghali sana' (It's very expensive), 'Nipe bei nafuu' (Give me a good price), 'Bei ya mwisho?' (Final price?), 'Nitatoa...' (I'll give...). Agreeing: 'Sawa' (Okay), 'Nitanunua' (I'll buy it), 'Nakubali' (I agree). Paying: 'Ninalipa kwa pesa taslimu' (I'm paying cash), 'Ninalipa kwa kadi' (I'm paying by card), 'Ninalipa kwa simu' (I'm paying by phone). Asking for change: 'Chenji yangu?' (My change?), 'Nimepungukiwa' (I've been shortchanged). Asking for receipt: 'Nipe risiti, tafadhali' (Give me a receipt, please). Checking quality: 'Hii ni nzuri?' (Is this good?), 'Hii ni mbichi?' (Is this fresh?). Sizes: 'Ukubwa gani?' (What size?), 'Una ukubwa mkubwa?' (Do you have a larger size?), 'Una ukubwa mdogo?' (Do you have a smaller size?). Trying on: 'Naweza kujaribu?' (Can I try it?). Colors: 'Rangi gani?' (What color?), 'Ninaitaji rangi nyekundu' (I need red). Quantities: 'Kiasi gani?' (How much quantity?), 'Nipe kilo moja' (Give me one kilo). Saying goodbye: 'Asante' (Thank you), 'Kwaheri' (Goodbye), 'Karibu tena' (Come again). If you don't understand: 'Samahani, sikuelewa' (Sorry, I didn't understand), 'Sema polepole' (Speak slowly). Asking for help: 'Naweza kupata msaada?' (Can I get help?). These phrases will get you through any market. Practice them daily. Role-play with a friend: one is vendor, one is buyer. Use real items and prices. The more you speak, the more natural it becomes. Remember, in Africa, a smile and a greeting go a long way. Don't be afraid to make mistakes. Vendors appreciate the effort. Soon you'll be bargaining like a local.

<h3>Numbers 11100: The Negotiation Backbone</h3>
<p>Numbers are the single most important asset in bargaining. In Swahili, numbers 1119 are formed by prefixing <strong>kumi na</strong> (ten and) to the unit: <em>kumi na moja</em> (11), <em>kumi na mbili</em> (12), up to <em>kumi na tisa</em> (19). Tens are built with the prefix <strong>m</strong> or <strong>mi</strong>: <em>ishirini</em> (20), <em>thelathini</em> (30), <em>arobaini</em> (40), <em>hamsini</em> (50), <em>sitini</em> (60), <em>sabini</em> (70), <em>themanini</em> (80), <em>tisini</em> (90), <em>mia moja</em> (100). Compound numbers link with <strong>na</strong>: <em>ishirini na tano</em> (25), <em>sitini na tatu</em> (63).</p>

<h3>Money and Currency Across the Region</h3>
<p>Swahili is spoken across multiple currency zones. Knowing the local currency term is essential. In Tanzania, the currency is <strong>Shilingi ya Tanzania (TZS)</strong>. In Kenya, <strong>Shilingi ya Kenya (KES)</strong>. In Uganda, <strong>Shilingi ya Uganda (UGX)</strong>. In the DRC (eastern Swahili-speaking zones), the <strong>Faranga ya Kongo (CDF)</strong> is used alongside the US dollar. In Rwanda and Burundi, the <strong>Faranga</strong> (RWF/BIF) is standard. Common phrases: <em>Nina shilingi ngapi?</em> (How many shillings do I have?), <em>Bei ni shilingi elfu tano</em> (The price is five thousand shillings), <em>Una chenji?</em> (Do you have change?), <em>Nina noti ya elfu kumi</em> (I have a ten-thousand note).</p>

<h3>Clothing, Colors, and Market Vocabulary</h3>
<p>Clothing trade is a major agribusiness-adjacent sector in East Africa from mitumba (second-hand clothing) supply chains to uniform procurement for cooperatives. Key terms: <em>nguo</em> (clothes), <em>shati</em> (shirt), <em>suruali</em> (trousers), <em>kanga</em> (traditional wrap), <em>kitenge</em> (printed fabric), <em>viatu</em> (shoes), <em>kofia</em> (hat). Colors: <em>nyekundu</em> (red), <em>bluu</em> (blue), <em>kijani</em> (green), <em>njano</em> (yellow), <em>nyeusi</em> (black), <em>nyeupe</em> (white), <em>kahawia</em> (brown). Example negotiation: <em>"Shati hili la kijani ni bei gani?"</em> (What is the price of this green shirt?) <em>"Ni shilingi elfu kumi na tano."</em> <em>"Punguza, natoa elfu kumi na mbili."</em></p>

<h3>Common Shopping Phrases for Business</h3>
<p>These phrases are your transactional toolkit. Practice them until they are automatic:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Naweza kupata...?</strong> Can I get...?</li>
<li><strong>Bei ya jumla ni ngapi?</strong> What is the wholesale price?</li>
<li><strong>Nahitaji kiasi kikubwa</strong> I need a large quantity.</li>
<li><strong>Unaweza kunipatia risiti?</strong> Can you give me a receipt?</li>
<li><strong>Nitakuja kesho</strong> I will come tomorrow.</li>
<li><strong>Hii ni ghali sana</strong> This is very expensive.</li>
<li><strong>Nipe bei ya mwisho</strong> Give me your final price.</li>
<li><strong>Tunashirikiana kwa muda mrefu</strong> We will do business for a long time.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Greet before you bargain.</strong> Always open with "Shikamoo" or "Habari" skipping greetings is considered rude and weakens your negotiating position.</li>
<li><strong>Know your numbers cold.</strong> Practice 11100 daily; hesitation on a price signals inexperience and invites inflated quotes.</li>
<li><strong>Anchor low, but respectfully.</strong> Open at 6070% of the asking price, then concede gradually.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm currency and unit.</strong> Ask "kwa kilo au kwa gunia?" (per kilo or per sack?) before agreeing.</li>
<li><strong>Check every note.</strong> Verify watermarks and threads; refuse torn or taped notes.</li>
<li><strong>Request a receipt (risiti).</strong> Essential for agribusiness bookkeeping, tax compliance, and dispute resolution.</li>
<li><strong>Build the relationship.</strong> Repeat buyers get better prices use "Tunashirikiana kwa muda mrefu."</li>
<li><strong>Walk away if needed.</strong> "Nitafikiria" (I will think about it) is a legitimate and powerful bargaining move.</li>
</ol>
