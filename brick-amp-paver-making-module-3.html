1. Making Wooden or Metal Brick Moulds in Standard Sizes

A brick mould is a simple box without a bottom or top. You can make it from hardwood planks (like mahogany or iroko) or from 2 mm mild steel sheet. The standard brick size in most African countries is 220 mm long, 105 mm wide, and 75 mm high. For pavers, common sizes are 200x100x60 mm or 200x100x80 mm. Make the mould slightly larger than the finished brick because clay shrinks when drying and firing. Add 10% to each dimension: for a 220x105x75 mm brick, build the mould interior to 240x115x80 mm.

For a wooden mould, use 25 mm thick planks. Cut four pieces: two long sides (240 mm) and two short sides (115 mm). Nail or screw them together with 50 mm nails or 40 mm screws. Make sure the inside corners are square and smooth. Sand the inside surfaces and rub them with used engine oil or soapy water to prevent clay from sticking. Metal moulds last longer and give sharper edges. Weld or rivet 2 mm steel plates into a box shape. Grind the inside welds flat. Drill two 10 mm holes in opposite sides for handles or attach metal strips as handles.

A single mould makes one brick at a time. For higher production, make a mould with two, three, or four compartments. A three-brick mould is common: interior dimensions 240x115x80 mm for each cavity, separated by 20 mm dividers. The mould should have a sturdy rim at the top so it does not bend when you strike off clay. Test your mould by filling it with wet soil and turning it out. If the brick sticks or breaks, smooth the inside more or add more oil.

Keep at least two moulds so one can be cleaned while the other is in use. Store moulds upside down in a dry place. Repair cracks or loose joints immediately. A good mould can last for thousands of bricks if handled well.

2. Preparing the Moulding Table and Water for Smooth Release

The moulding table is a flat, sturdy surface where you fill and turn out bricks. It should be about 1 metre high so you can work without bending too much. Use a wooden pallet, a strong table, or a concrete slab. The table top must be perfectly flat and smooth. Cover it with a thin layer of sand or fine ash to stop wet bricks from sticking. Do not use rough concrete directly; bricks will break when you lift them.

Water is critical for smooth release. You need two containers: one for cleaning the mould and one for wetting the table. Use clean water, not muddy or oily water. For each brick, dip the mould in water or spray it lightly. The inside should be wet but not dripping. If the mould is too dry, clay sticks. If too wet, the brick slumps. A plastic basin or bucket works well. Change the water when it becomes muddy; muddy water leaves a film that makes bricks rough.

Before you start, arrange your workspace. Put the clay heap on one side of the table, the mould and water basin on the table, and the drying area on the other side. You need a wooden board or metal sheet (the pallet) to carry wet bricks to the drying area. Sprinkle sand on the pallet so bricks do not stick. Keep a stick or trowel for striking off. Wear gloves if the clay is rough.

Test the setup: make one brick. If it sticks to the mould, the mould is not wet enough or the clay is too wet. If the brick cracks, the table is too dry or the clay is too stiff. Adjust by adding a little water to the clay or wetting the mould more. With practice, you will find the right balance. A smooth release means the brick slides out with a gentle tap, keeping sharp corners.

3. Techniques for Filling, Compacting, and Striking Off the Mould

Filling the mould correctly is the difference between strong bricks and weak ones. Take a lump of prepared clay (about the size of a large mango) and throw it into the mould with force. The clay should be stiff enough to hold its shape but soft enough to compact. Throw the lump so it fills the corners first. Then press the clay down with your thumbs, working from the centre outwards. Fill the mould slightly above the top.

Compacting removes air pockets and makes the brick dense. Use your fists or a wooden tamper to press the clay firmly into all corners. You should feel the clay resist. Do not just pat the surface; push down hard. For a three-brick mould, fill and compact each cavity separately. After compacting, the clay level will drop. Add more clay and compact again until the mould is full and slightly heaped.

Striking off is the act of removing excess clay. Use a straight wooden stick or a metal straight edge. Hold it flat against the top of the mould and draw it across with a sawing motion. This cuts off the extra clay and leaves a flat top. Do not press too hard or you will pull clay out of the corners. Wipe the stick clean after each strike. The top of the brick should be level with the mould rim.

Now the brick is ready to turn out. Do not wait; clay dries quickly. If you delay, the brick shrinks and sticks. A good strike-off leaves a smooth surface with sharp edges. Practice with different clay moisture levels. If the clay is too wet, it will smear when struck. If too dry, it crumbles. Aim for a consistency like firm dough. With 20 to 30 bricks, you will develop the right feel.

4. Turning Out Wet Bricks Without Breaking Corners

Turning out a wet brick is the most delicate step. If you rush, you break corners and lose the brick. First, make sure the mould is wet and the table is sanded. Place a wooden pallet (a flat board) next to the mould. The pallet should be slightly larger than the brick. Sprinkle fine sand on the pallet. Hold the mould with both hands, thumbs on the top edges. Lift it straight up about 5 cm, then turn it upside down quickly but smoothly over the pallet. Do not twist or tilt.

Lower the mould onto the pallet. Tap the top of the mould gently with your hand or a wooden mallet. Tap all four sides once. This loosens the brick from the mould. Then lift the mould straight up. The brick should stay on the pallet. If it sticks, tap again or wet the mould more. Never pull the brick out; let gravity and a gentle tap do the work. If a corner breaks, the clay is too wet or the mould is too dry.

After turning out, do not move the brick immediately. Let it sit on the pallet for 10 to 15 minutes to firm up. Then you can lift the pallet and carry it to the drying area. Carry the pallet flat, not tilted. If you must move a single brick, slide a thin metal sheet under it. Do not pick it up with your fingers; you will crush the corners.

For large production, use a pallet that holds 10 to 20 bricks. Turn out each brick onto the pallet, leaving 2 cm gaps between them. When the pallet is full, carry it to the shade. This saves time and reduces breakage. Practice turning out with a few bricks first. Speed comes later. A steady hand and a wet mould are your best tools.

5. Setting Bricks to Dry in the Shade Before Sun Drying

Fresh bricks are fragile. If you put them directly in the hot sun, they crack and warp. First, dry them in the shade for 2 to 3 days. Find a shaded area under a tree, a shed, or a roof. The ground should be level and dry. Place the pallets with bricks in rows, leaving 50 cm between rows for air movement. Do not stack bricks yet. Keep them on the pallet for the first 24 hours.

After 24 hours, the bricks are firm enough to stand on edge. Carefully lift each brick and stand it on its long side, like a book. This exposes more surface to air. Leave 5 cm between bricks. Do not stack them. Keep them in the shade for another 1 to 2 days. If the weather is very dry, cover them with a light cloth or grass mat to slow drying. If it is humid, increase air flow with a fan or by opening sides of the shed.

After 3 days in the shade, the bricks are leather-hard. They can now be moved to the sun. Choose a sunny spot with a clean, flat surface (concrete or dry ground). Lay the bricks flat or on edge, in rows. Leave 10 cm between bricks and rows. Turn them over every day so both sides dry evenly. Sun drying takes 5 to 10 days, depending on weather. In hot, dry climates, 5 days is enough. In humid areas, it may take 2 weeks.

Test for dryness: tap two bricks together. A dry brick gives a clear ring. A wet brick gives a dull thud. Do not fire or use bricks until they are completely dry. If rain is coming, cover the bricks with a plastic sheet or move them under shelter. Proper shade drying prevents cracks and gives stronger bricks.

6. Daily Production Rate: How Many Bricks Can One Person Mould?

A single person working steadily can mould 300 to 500 bricks per day using a single mould. With a three-brick mould, you can reach 800 to 1,200 bricks per day. The exact number depends on your speed, clay preparation, and drying space. A beginner might make 150 to 200 bricks in the first week. With practice, you will double that.

Time yourself. Filling, compacting, striking, and turning out one brick takes about 1 to 2 minutes with a single mould. That is 30 to 60 bricks per hour. If you work 8 hours with breaks, you get 240 to 480 bricks. With a three-brick mould, each cycle takes 2 to 3 minutes, producing 3 bricks. That is 60 to 90 bricks per hour, or 480 to 720 in 8 hours. Add time for clay preparation (2 hours) and carrying bricks to the drying area (1 hour). So a realistic full day is 300 to 500 bricks for a single mould, 800 to 1,200 for a three-brick mould.

To increase production, prepare clay the day before. Have two or three moulds ready. Use a pallet system so you do not walk far. Work in a team: one person prepares clay, one moulds, one carries bricks. A team of three can produce 2,000 to 3,000 bricks per day. Keep water and sand close. Do not stop to clean moulds every few bricks; clean them every 20 to 30 bricks.

Quality matters more than speed. Broken bricks waste clay and time. Aim for a steady, consistent pace. Track your daily output in a notebook. After one week, you will know your true rate. Set a target and try to beat it by 10% each week. With simple tools and good organisation, one person can supply enough bricks for a small house in a few weeks.

3. Techniques for Filling, Compacting, and Striking Off the Mould

This is where production speed meets quality. The standard sequence is:

  1. Wet the mould â dip or brush with water so the mix slides out cleanly.

  3. Fill with prepared mix (soil-cement or clay) in two layers.
  4. Compact each layer with a tamper or your thumbs, working from edges to centre to expel air pockets.
  5. Strike off the excess with a straight edge (a steel ruler or wooden straightedge) using a sawing motion.
  6. Level the surface with a trowel if needed.

Consistency is everything. A mix that is too dry will crumble; too wet will slump. The "ball test" â squeeze a handful of mix; it should hold shape without dripping â is a reliable field check.

4. Turning Out Wet Bricks Without Breaking Corners

The turn-out is the moment of truth. A single jerky movement can ruin an otherwise perfect brick. The technique:

  • Place a pallet board or thin steel sheet over the mould top.
  • Hold the board firmly against the mould with both hands.
  • Flip the mould and board together in one smooth, confident motion.
  • Lift the mould straight up â do not tilt or drag it.
  • Tap the mould lightly on the sides if the brick sticks.

Corners break when the mould is lifted unevenly or when the mix is too dry. If corners are consistently breaking, add a little more water or check your mould taper.

Freshly moulded bricks are fragile. They must be left in the shade for 24â48 hours (longer in hot, dry climates) to gain enough strength to be handled. Cover them with plastic sheeting, banana leaves, or damp cloth to slow drying and prevent surface cracking.

After the initial shade cure, move bricks to the sun for 5â7 days (soil-cement) or 3â5 days (clay). Turn them on edge after 2â3 days to ensure even drying. For soil-cement bricks, begin water curing after 24 hours â sprinkle daily for 7 days to reach full strength.
