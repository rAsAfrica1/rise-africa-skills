1. Sourcing and Preparing Local Scrap Steel in African Markets

Welcome to Module 8, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on the single most important input for any blacksmith business in Africa: your steel. Whether you are working in Kumasi, Kano, Nairobi, or a small town in Limpopo, the quality and cost of your raw material will decide whether you make profit or loss. Most African blacksmiths do not buy new billets from a mill. They buy scrap. That is not a weakness. It is a business model. But it must be managed like a business. Start by mapping your local scrap sources. The best sources are usually vehicle springs, axles, drive shafts, leaf springs, coil springs, torsion bars, railway clips, and old agricultural implements. These are medium carbon and high carbon steels that harden well and hold an edge. Second best are construction rebar, angle iron, flat bar offcuts, and water pipe. These are mostly low carbon and good for gates, grills, stands, and decorative work, but poor for knives, hoes, and chisels. Third are mixed mystery steels from dumpsites. Use these only for non-critical items or sell them to others. Build relationships with mechanics, welders, truck repair yards, farm cooperatives, and demolition contractors. Visit them weekly. Bring a small gift, buy them tea, and pay a fair price. Do not haggle to the point of insult. A steady supply at a fair price is worth more than one cheap load that disappears. Learn to identify steel by spark testing. Take a grinder or a piece of broken grinding stone and touch it to the metal in a dark corner. Low carbon steel gives long, straight, yellow-orange sparks with few bursts. Medium carbon gives shorter sparks with more bursts. High carbon gives short, bright, bushy sparks with many star bursts near the wheel. Cast iron gives dull red short sparks and is brittle. This test takes practice but costs nothing and saves you from wasting coal and time on bad steel. Cut and sort your scrap as soon as it arrives. Use a chalk or paint marker to label piles: spring steel, tool steel, mild steel, cast iron, unknown. Store them under cover. Rust is not a disaster, but deep pitting means you must grind more away, which wastes material and time. Keep a notebook. Record date, source, type, weight, price, and what you made from it. After three months you will know which source gives the best steel for knives and which gives the best steel for gates. That knowledge is money. Now think about cost. In many African cities, scrap steel sells by the kilogram or by the piece. A complete truck leaf spring might cost the same as ten small offcuts. Buy the complete spring when you can. You get more steel per shilling, and you control the cutting. But you need a hacksaw, a chisel, or an angle grinder to break it down. If you do not have power, use a hot cut. Heat the spring to orange and cut with a hot chisel and hammer. This is slower but builds skill and saves electricity. Always calculate your cost per finished item, not per kilogram. If a knife takes two kilograms of spring steel and sells for a certain price, you need to know your steel cost, coal cost, grinding cost, and your time. Many blacksmiths undercharge because they only count the steel. Do not be one of them. Set a minimum price that covers all costs plus at least thirty percent profit. For bulk buyers, offer a discount only if they pay cash and collect. For retail customers, hold your price. Your skill is valuable. Finally, consider forming a buying group with other blacksmiths in your area. Pool your money, buy a whole truckload of scrap, and share it by weight. This lowers your unit cost and gives you bargaining power. You can also share transport. In many African markets, transport is a hidden killer of profit. A buying group solves that. Keep records of every member contribution and every kilogram taken. Transparency prevents conflict. In summary, treat scrap as a strategic resource. Map your sources, test your steel, sort and store it well, record your costs, and cooperate with others. Do this and you will have a stable, profitable supply while your competitors run around looking for metal.

2. Forge Design, Fuel Efficiency, and Air Control for African Workshops

Welcome to Lesson 2. Your forge is the heart of your workshop. In Africa, most blacksmiths use either a ground forge with a side blast or a simple raised forge with a bottom blast. Both work, but fuel efficiency and air control separate the profitable smith from the struggling one. Charcoal and coal are expensive. In many countries, charcoal prices rise every year because of deforestation and regulation. Every lump you waste is money lost. Start with the fire pot. It should be made of thick steel or cast iron, about twenty to thirty centimeters across and ten to fifteen centimeters deep. A shallow pot wastes heat sideways. A deep pot concentrates heat but can choke the fire if the air is weak. The tuyere, which is the pipe that brings air into the fire, should enter from the side, slightly above the bottom of the pot. This is the side blast design. It is common in West and East Africa and it works well with charcoal. The bottom blast design, where air comes from below through a grate, works better with coal but clogs easily with charcoal ash. Choose based on your fuel. If you use charcoal, side blast. If you use coal, bottom blast with a clinker breaker. Now think about air. You do not need a powerful electric blower. You need controlled air. A hand-cranked blower, a bellows, or a small centrifugal fan with a gate valve will do. The key is to be able to increase and decrease air smoothly. Too much air blows heat away and burns your steel. Too little air gives a dull fire and slow work. Learn to read the fire. A good forging fire is bright yellow-orange in the center with a slightly darker edge. If the fire is white and sparkling, it is too hot and you are burning steel and fuel. If it is red and smoky, it is too cold and you are wasting time. Adjust the air and the fuel together. Add fuel around the edges, not in the center, so the center stays hot and the edges coke up. Build a mound of fuel and keep the center open. This is called a cave fire and it saves fuel. Fuel choice matters. Charcoal is light, burns clean, and is easy to light, but it burns fast. Coal is heavier, burns longer, and gives more heat per kilogram, but it produces smoke and clinker. In many African cities, coal is cheaper per kilogram but harder to find. Charcoal is everywhere but more expensive over time. If you can, use coal for heavy forging and charcoal for finishing and small work. Store fuel dry. Wet fuel wastes heat and creates steam. Keep your fuel under a roof or a tarpaulin. Raise the forge off the ground if possible. A raised forge saves your back and allows you to work longer. Use a table height that lets you hold the hammer with a relaxed arm. If the forge is too low, you bend and tire quickly. If it is too high, you lose control. A good height is about seventy to eighty centimeters from the ground to the top of the fire pot. Now consider the chimney or hood. In a closed workshop, smoke will kill your lungs and drive away customers. Build a hood over the forge and a chimney to take smoke outside. Use local materials: mud brick, sheet metal, or even a fifty-five gallon drum cut and shaped. The hood should be wide enough to catch smoke from the whole fire. The chimney should be taller than the roof and have a cap to keep rain out. A good chimney also improves draft, which pulls air through the fire and saves fuel. If you work outdoors, position your forge so the wind does not blow smoke into your face. Build a simple windbreak with poles and palm mats or zinc sheets. This also keeps rain off your fire. Water is another tool. Keep a bucket of water near the forge for cooling tongs and for quenching. But do not quench hot steel in dirty water if you are making tools. Use clean water or oil depending on the steel. We will cover that in a later lesson. Finally, maintain your forge. Clean out ash and clinker every day. A clogged tuyere wastes fuel and time. Check your blower or bellows for leaks. Repair the fire pot before it burns through. A well-maintained forge can last ten years. A neglected one lasts one season. In summary, build a forge that matches your fuel, control your air, keep your fuel dry, raise your work to a comfortable height, and remove smoke. These simple steps will cut your fuel cost, increase your output, and protect your health. That is practical business.

3. Hammer Technique, Anvil Use, and Efficient Forging Sequences

Welcome to Lesson 3. In this lesson we focus on the physical skill that turns steel into money: hammer technique and anvil use. Many blacksmiths in Africa work hard but produce slowly because they use too many heats and too many light blows. Efficiency is not about working faster with worse quality. It is about doing the right thing at the right temperature with the right force. Start with your stance. Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, left foot slightly forward if you are right-handed. Grip the hammer near the end of the handle, not in the middle. A loose grip at the start of the swing and a firm grip at impact gives you control and power. Do not squeeze the handle all the way through the swing. That tires your forearm and reduces speed. Let the hammer do the work. The anvil should be at knuckle height when you stand straight. If it is too low, you bend and lose power. If it is too high, you cannot swing freely. Mount your anvil on a solid stump or a steel stand. A bouncing anvil wastes energy. Pack dirt or sand under the stump to absorb vibration. Now think about heat. Steel forges best at a bright yellow-orange. At this temperature it moves easily under the hammer. If it is dark red, it is too cold and you will crack it or wear yourself out. If it is white or sparkling, it is too hot and you will burn it. Learn to watch the color in the shade, not in direct sunlight. Build a simple shade over your forge area so you can see the color accurately. Use the right hammer for the job. A heavy hammer, around two to three kilograms, is for drawing out and moving metal fast. A lighter hammer, around one kilogram, is for finishing and detailed work. Do not use a heavy hammer for finishing. You will leave deep marks and waste time grinding. Do not use a light hammer for drawing out. You will take forever and lose heat. Most African blacksmiths use one hammer for everything. Buy or make a second hammer. It will pay for itself in a month. Learn the basic blows. The flat blow is for smoothing and setting. The drawing blow is for lengthening. Strike with the face slightly angled so the metal flows along the anvil. The fullering blow uses a rounded or blunt tool to spread metal. The cutting blow uses a hot chisel. The punching blow uses a punch. Practice each blow on scrap until it becomes automatic. A good blacksmith can draw out a taper in one heat. A beginner takes three. That difference is fuel, time, and profit. Plan your sequence before you put steel in the fire. Ask: what is the first step, what is the second, what tools do I need, and how many heats will it take? For a simple knife, the sequence might be: forge the tip, forge the tang, flatten the blade, normalize, grind, harden, temper, handle. Do not stop in the middle to look for a tool. Have everything ready. This is called mise en place, a French cooking term that means everything in its place. It works in the forge too. Use the horn of the anvil for curves and rings. Use the step for shouldering and cutting. Use the hardy hole for holding tools and for bending. Use the pritchel hole for punching. Do not use the anvil face as a cutting table. You will chip it. Cut on a separate piece of scrap steel or on the hardy. Keep your anvil face clean. Wipe it with a rag. A rusty or dirty anvil marks your work and costs you grinding time. Now think about teamwork. In many African workshops, one person heats and one person hammers. This is called striker and smith. It can double your output if you communicate well. The striker must watch the smith and the steel. The smith must call the blows: hard, light, stop, turn. Agree on signals before you start. A good team can forge a hoe in twenty minutes. A poor team takes an hour. If you work alone, use tongs that fit your work. Loose tongs waste time and cause accidents. Make or buy three or four pairs: flat jaws, round jaws, and a pickup pair. Keep them near the forge. Do not waste heat looking for tongs. Finally, practice. Skill comes from repetition. Set aside one hour each week to practice a new technique on scrap. It might be a twist, a scroll, a weld, or a punch. Do not sell practice pieces. Give them to apprentices or use them as samples. Over a year, you will add many skills that let you charge more. In summary, stand right, grip right, heat right, use the right hammer, plan your sequence, and work as a team. These habits turn hard work into profitable work.

4. Heat Treatment, Edge Geometry, and Quality Control for African Tools

Welcome to Lesson 4. Heat treatment is where many African blacksmiths lose customers. A tool that looks good but dulls quickly or breaks will not be bought again. A tool that holds an edge and lasts will build your reputation. This lesson covers hardening, tempering, and quality control using simple, affordable methods. First, know your steel. If you are using leaf spring, it is usually medium carbon steel, around 0.5 to 0.6 percent carbon. It hardens well but needs a fast quench. If you are using coil spring or bearing steel, it may be higher carbon and can crack if quenched too fast. If you are using rebar or mild steel, it will not harden at all. Do not waste time trying to harden mild steel. Use it for gates and grills. Use spring steel for tools. Before hardening, normalize the steel. Heat it to a bright red, then let it cool in still air. Do this two or three times. Normalizing refines the grain and relieves stress from forging. It makes the steel tougher and less likely to crack. Do not skip this step. It costs only time and fuel, and it saves tools. Now harden. Heat the cutting edge to a bright orange, slightly hotter than forging temperature. Do not heat the whole tool if only the edge needs to be hard. Heat about two to three centimeters of the edge. Then quench. For spring steel, use warm oil. Engine oil, palm oil, or groundnut oil works. Warm oil is better than cold oil because it cools slightly slower and reduces cracking. For higher carbon steel, use water or brine, but be careful. Brine is salt water, about one tablespoon of salt per liter. It cools faster than plain water. Quench by plunging the edge straight down, not sideways. Move it slightly in the oil or water to break the vapor jacket. Hold it there until it stops smoking or bubbling. Then check with a file. A hard edge will skate a file. A soft edge will cut. If the file cuts, reheat and quench again. After hardening, the steel is too brittle. You must temper it. Temper by heating the tool to a lower temperature and holding it there. The traditional method is to clean the steel with sandpaper or a stone, then heat the back of the tool or the spine until the edge shows a color. Straw yellow is for razors and fine knives. Bronze or brown is for wood chisels. Purple is for axes and heavy choppers. Blue is for springs and tools that need toughness. Watch the color carefully. It changes fast. When you see the right color, quench the whole tool in oil or water to stop the process. If you overshoot, reharden and start again. For a more controlled temper, use a charcoal fire with a pipe or a small oven. You can also use a bed of hot sand or ash. The key is to hold the temperature for at least thirty minutes for small tools and one hour for large tools. This reduces brittleness without softening the edge too much. Now think about edge geometry. A knife for cutting soft materials should have a thin edge, about fifteen to twenty degrees per side. A knife for chopping wood should have a thicker edge, about twenty-five to thirty degrees per side. A hoe or axe should have a convex edge, like a rounded wedge. This gives strength. A chisel should have a flat back and a beveled front. The angle should match the wood or metal it cuts. Do not make every tool the same. Ask the customer what they will cut. Then grind the edge to match. Grinding generates heat. If the edge turns blue or purple, you have softened it. Cool the blade in water every few seconds while grinding. Use a slow speed grinder or a hand file if you do not have power. A hand file is slower but safer. It also gives you more control. Quality control is simple. After heat treatment, test the tool. For a knife, cut a piece of hardwood or bone. The edge should not roll or chip. For a hoe, chop into a dry log. The edge should bite and not fold. For an axe, chop into a knot. The edge should hold. If it fails, redo the heat treatment. Do not sell a tool that fails. Your reputation is worth more than one sale. Also check the handle. A loose handle is dangerous. A rough handle causes blisters. Sand it smooth and oil it with linseed or palm oil. Fit it tight with a wedge. For hoes and axes, use a metal wedge or a hardwood wedge. For knives, use rivets or a strong epoxy. If you use epoxy, roughen the tang and the handle scales first. Finally, mark your work. A simple stamp with your initials or your workshop name tells customers who made it. It also helps you track quality. If a customer complains, you can identify the batch and fix the problem. In summary, normalize, harden, temper, grind with care, test every tool, and mark your work. These steps turn a piece of scrap into a trusted tool that customers will pay for again and again.

5. Pricing, Marketing, and Scaling a Blacksmith Business in Africa

Welcome to Lesson 5. You now have the skills to make good tools and products. But skill alone does not pay the bills. This lesson is about turning your forge into a business that grows. We will cover pricing, marketing, customer relationships, and scaling. Start with pricing. Many African blacksmiths price by guessing or by copying the neighbor. That is a race to the bottom. Instead, calculate your cost for every product. List the steel, the fuel, the grinding discs, the sandpaper, the handle material, the transport, and your time. Add a percentage for workshop rent, tools, and electricity. Then add profit. A simple formula is: material cost plus labor cost plus overhead plus profit equals price. If a knife takes two hours of labor and you pay yourself a fair hourly rate, include that. If you do not pay yourself, you are not running a business. You are volunteering. For retail customers, set a price and hold it. For wholesale buyers, offer a discount only if they buy in bulk and pay cash. Do not offer credit to strangers. If you must offer credit, write it down, take a deposit, and set a date. In many African markets, credit kills small businesses. Be firm but polite. Now marketing. You do not need a big budget. You need to show your work. Keep a clean display area at your workshop. Put your best knives, hoes, axes, and decorative pieces where people can see them. Hang a sign with your name and what you make. If you have a smartphone, take clear photos in natural light. Post them on WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, or TikTok. Show the process: fire, hammer, sparks, finished tool. People love to see how things are made. Use local language and English. Ask satisfied customers to refer you. Give them a small discount on their next purchase if they bring a friend. Build relationships with hardware stores, agricultural supply shops, and market vendors. Offer them a wholesale price and let them sell for you. This expands your reach without you leaving the forge. Attend local trade fairs, agricultural shows, and church or community events. Bring samples and a small forge if you can. Demonstrations attract crowds. A crowd attracts buyers. Also consider tourism. In many African countries, tourists want authentic handmade crafts. Knives, letter openers, bottle openers, and small sculptures sell well. Work with hotels, lodges, and tour guides. Give them a commission for bringing customers. Now customer relationships. Remember names. Ask about their farm, their family, their work. A customer who feels known will come back. If a tool breaks, repair it or replace it. Do not argue. A small loss now can create a loyal customer for years. Keep a record of every sale. Write the customer name, the product, the date, and the price. This helps you see what sells and what does not. If a product does not sell in three months, stop making it or change the design. If a product sells fast, make more. This is called product focus. Do not try to make everything. Be known for one or two things first. Then expand. Now scaling. Scaling does not always mean a bigger workshop. It can mean better systems. Start by training an apprentice. Choose someone reliable and patient. Teach them safety first, then fire management, then hammer technique, then heat treatment. Pay them fairly and give them responsibility. A good apprentice frees you to do the work that earns the most money, like talking to customers and designing new products. Next, standardize your products. If you make a knife, make it the same size and shape every time. This makes training easier, quality more consistent, and production faster. Use jigs and templates. A simple wooden template for a knife profile saves time and reduces mistakes. Consider buying a small power hammer or a hydraulic press if your volume grows. But only buy if the machine pays for itself within a year. Do not go into debt for a machine you do not need. Also think about location. A workshop near a market or a main road gets more walk-in customers. A workshop far away may be cheaper but you must travel to sell. Calculate the trade-off. If you can, open a small retail point in town while keeping the forge outside. This separates the dirty work from the clean sales area. Finally, protect your health and your money. Use safety glasses, gloves, and ear protection. Blacksmithing is dangerous. An injury can destroy your business. Save money every month. Open a bank account or join a savings group. Keep records of income and expenses. Pay taxes if required. A registered business can get loans and contracts that informal businesses cannot. In summary, price for profit, market your work, build relationships, train apprentices, standardize products, and save money. Do these things and your blacksmith business will not just survive. It will grow.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Workshop Safety, Input Sourcing, and Tool Economics for African Blacksmiths

This practical reference module consolidates the operational knowledge a working blacksmith in Africa needs to run a safe, profitable forge. It is written for the artisan who already knows how to swing a hammer and wants to protect their health, source materials reliably, and price work so the business survives. Everything here is framed around money: lost days to injury cost income, poor fuel choices raise unit costs, and underpriced tools destroy margins. Use the tables and checklist as a bench-side reference you can tape to the wall.

Why Safety Is a Business Issue

Every day lost to a burn, eye injury, or respiratory illness is a day of unbilled work. A blacksmith producing 20 hoes a week at a market price of KES 450 each generates roughly KES 36,000 in weekly revenue; two lost days can wipe out KES 10,000 or more in sales and damage customer trust. The World Health Organization estimates that household and workplace air pollution from solid fuels contributes to millions of premature deaths annually, and forge coal fires release carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide, and fine particulate matter in confined spaces. The cheapest insurance is a cross-draught fire and a well-ventilated shelter.

SAFETY WARNING: Carbon Monoxide, Metal Fumes, and Quench Hazards

  • Never forge inside a closed room or a shelter without cross-ventilation. Carbon monoxide from a coal or charcoal fire is odourless and can kill in minutes. Keep the fire downwind of your breathing zone and open two opposing walls.
  • Galvanised steel, painted scrap, and leaded alloys release toxic zinc, lead, and cadmium fumes when heated. Do not forge them. Strip coatings or reject the metal.
  • Quenching hot steel in oil can flash-fire. Keep a fitted lid beside the trough and never lean over the quench.
  • Wear eye protection when grinding, chipping slag, or quenching. Hot scale causes permanent eye damage.
  • Keep a bucket of clean water and a burn dressing within arm's reach of the fire at all times.

Scrap Sourcing: Where the Steel Comes From

In most African markets, the blacksmith's raw material is not bought from a mill but recovered from the economy around them. The best sources, ranked by reliability and quality:

  • Vehicle leaf springs and coil springs (5160 / 9260 steel): excellent for edged tools because they are tough, harden well, and resist shock. Found at garages, matatu parks, and scrap yards.
  • Railway clips and fishplates: high-carbon, ideal for chisels and punches. Often available near rail depots.
  • Construction rebar: low-carbon, good for tongs, hinges, and non-edged work, but will not hold an edge.
  • Discarded farm machinery: plough shares, harrow discs, and bearing races. Bearing races (52100) make excellent knives.
  • Building demolition steel: angle iron, flat bar, and pipe. Useful for jigs and stands, not for blades.

Build relationships with two or three garages and one scrap dealer. Pay a small premium for clean, sorted steel and reject galvanised, painted, or unknown alloys. Sorting at source saves hours of grinding and prevents toxic fume exposure.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Ventilate before you light: confirm cross-draught airflow and that the fire is downwind of your face.
  2. Sort scrap before it enters the shop: reject galvanised, painted, and leaded metal; mark known alloys.
  3. Wear eye protection for all grinding, chipping, and quenching operations.
  4. Keep water and a burn kit within one step of the firepot.
  5. Track fuel per tool for one week each month to catch cost creep.
  6. Price from material + fuel + labour + 40% margin, never from what the neighbour charges.
  7. Quench oil away from the fire, with a lid on hand for flash fires.
  8. Record every sale and every scrap purchase in a simple notebook or phone app.
