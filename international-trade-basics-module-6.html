1. Commercial Invoice and Packing List

The commercial invoice is the core document in any export deal. It tells the buyer, bank, and customs exactly what you sold, for how much, and on what terms. In Africa, a missing or wrong invoice can delay your goods at ports like Mombasa or Lagos for weeks. A proper invoice includes: seller and buyer names and addresses, invoice number and date, description of goods (be specific: "100 bags of Arabica coffee beans, 60 kg each" not just "coffee"), quantity, unit price, total value, currency (USD, EUR, or local), Incoterm (e.g., FOB Mombasa), payment terms, and country of origin. For example, a Kenyan exporter shipping to the UK must show the invoice value in GBP or USD and state "Origin: Kenya" to qualify for duty-free access under the UK's Developing Countries Trading Scheme. Always sign and stamp the invoice.

The packing list is the invoice's twin. It lists how the goods are packed: number of packages, type (cartons, pallets, drums), weight (net and gross), dimensions, and marks/numbers on each package. Customs uses it to verify the shipment. If your invoice says 100 bags but your packing list says 98, you have a problem. A Ghanaian shea butter exporter might pack 500 kg in 20 cartons, each 25 kg, with marks like "GH-SHEA-001 to 020". The packing list must match the invoice and the actual load.

Use tools like Excel or Google Sheets to create templates. Include a reference number that ties invoice and packing list together. For African exporters, always keep three copies: one for your records, one for the bank, one for customs. Many banks in Nigeria and South Africa require the invoice to be stamped by a chamber of commerce. Double-check HS codes (Harmonized System) a wrong code can mean higher tariffs. For instance, raw cocoa beans (HS 1801) have different duty rates than cocoa paste (HS 1803).

Finally, digitize. Platforms like TradeMap or the African Trade Gateway help you generate compliant invoices. But even a handwritten invoice works if it has all the details. The key is accuracy: one typo can cost you demurrage fees of $100 per day at the port.

2. Bill of Lading and Air Waybill

The Bill of Lading (B/L) is the most important transport document for sea freight. It is a receipt from the shipping line, a contract of carriage, and a document of title meaning whoever holds the original B/L owns the goods. In Africa, major ports like Durban, Tema, and Abidjan handle millions of containers, and the B/L is your key. A typical B/L shows shipper, consignee (buyer or bank), notify party, vessel name, port of loading (e.g., Mombasa), port of discharge (e.g., Rotterdam), description of goods, number of containers, and freight terms (prepaid or collect). There are three types: original B/L (requires surrender at destination), telex release (electronic), and sea waybill (non-negotiable). For a letter of credit, banks usually demand an original B/L. Always check for "clean on board" meaning the goods were loaded without damage.

If you ship by air, you use an Air Waybill (AWB). It is not a document of title; it is a receipt and contract, but the airline releases goods to the consignee named on the AWB without needing the original. This is faster but riskier for the seller. An AWB has 11-digit number (e.g., 176-12345678), airport codes (NBO for Nairobi, JNB for Johannesburg), and shows chargeable weight. For high-value, perishable goods like fresh flowers from Ethiopia to Europe, AWB is standard. But for expensive electronics, you might prefer sea freight with a B/L to retain control.

In practice, African exporters often use freight forwarders to prepare these documents. For example, a Tanzanian coffee exporter might book through a forwarder who issues a House B/L (from the forwarder) and a Master B/L (from the shipping line). The House B/L is what the bank sees. Always verify that the B/L matches the commercial invoice exactly: same goods, same weights, same consignee. A mismatch can cause the bank to reject your documents under a letter of credit, costing you time and money. Keep copies for at least five years for tax and customs audits.

3. Certificate of Origin

A Certificate of Origin (COO) proves where your goods were produced. It is essential for customs clearance and for claiming preferential tariffs under trade agreements like the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), the EU's Everything But Arms (EBA) scheme, or the US African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA). Without a COO, your buyer pays the full most-favoured-nation duty, which can be 10-30% higher. For example, a Kenyan exporter of cut flowers to the EU pays 0% duty with a valid COO under EBA, but without it, the duty could be 8.5%. That is a huge difference.

In Africa, COOs are issued by chambers of commerce, customs authorities, or export promotion councils. In Kenya, the Kenya National Chamber of Commerce and Industry issues them; in Nigeria, the Nigerian Association of Chambers of Commerce, Industry, Mines and Agriculture (NACCIMA). The process: you submit your commercial invoice, packing list, and sometimes a declaration of origin. The chamber stamps and signs the COO. There are different types: a non-preferential COO (for general trade) and a preferential COO (for specific trade agreements, like the AfCFTA Certificate of Origin). For AGOA, you need a specific AGOA visa stamp. For the EU, you may use a statement on origin on the invoice if the value is under 6,000.

Common mistakes: not matching the HS code on the COO with the invoice, using the wrong form, or missing the chamber's stamp. A Ghanaian cocoa exporter once lost a $50,000 order because the COO listed "Ghana" as origin but the invoice said "Made in Cte d'Ivoire" a clerical error. Always double-check. Also, some countries require legalization by the embassy of the destination country, which costs extra and takes time. Start the COO process early. Digitally, some countries now accept electronic COOs through platforms like the AfCFTA's e-Tariff Book. But for now, paper originals are still king.

4. Letters of Credit: Types and Uses

A Letter of Credit (L/C) is a bank's promise to pay the seller once the seller presents compliant documents. It reduces risk for both parties: the seller knows they will be paid if they ship correctly, and the buyer knows the bank won't pay unless the documents are in order. In African trade, L/Cs are common for large transactions, like a Nigerian importer buying $200,000 of machinery from China. The importer's bank issues the L/C, and the exporter's bank in Africa advises it. The exporter ships, presents documents (invoice, B/L, packing list, COO, insurance) to their bank, which forwards them to the issuing bank. If all documents match the L/C terms exactly, the issuing bank pays.

There are several types. An irrevocable L/C cannot be changed without all parties' consent this is the most common. A confirmed L/C adds a second bank's guarantee, usually a bank in the exporter's country, so the exporter is protected against the importer's bank or country risk. For example, a Zimbabwean exporter might ask for a confirmed L/C from a South African bank because of currency volatility. A sight L/C pays immediately upon document presentation; a usance or term L/C pays after 30, 60, or 90 days. A transferable L/C allows the exporter to transfer part of the credit to a supplier. A back-to-back L/C is used by intermediaries. A revolving L/C is for multiple shipments.

The key is document compliance. Banks examine documents strictly a misspelled name or a missing signature can cause rejection. The Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP 600) governs most L/Cs. African exporters should use a checklist: does the invoice match the L/C? Is the B/L "clean on board" and consigned as required? Is the COO in the right format? If documents are rejected, you can ask for a waiver, but that costs time and fees. Many African SMEs fail at this stage. To avoid problems, work with a bank that has trade finance expertise, like Ecobank, Standard Bank, or Access Bank. Also, consider L/C insurance if the issuing bank is in a high-risk country. Always read the L/C carefully before shipping once you ship, you cannot change the terms.

5. Documentary Collections

Documentary collections are a cheaper alternative to letters of credit. Instead of a bank guaranteeing payment, the seller's bank sends documents to the buyer's bank, which releases them to the buyer only after payment (D/P Documents against Payment) or after the buyer accepts a bill of exchange (D/A Documents against Acceptance). The bank does not guarantee payment; it only acts as an intermediary. This is common in Africa for smaller, trusted transactions. For example, a South African exporter selling $15,000 of wine to a Kenyan distributor might use D/P: the exporter ships, sends documents to the bank, and the buyer pays at the bank to get the B/L and clear the goods. If the buyer refuses to pay, the exporter still owns the goods but faces costs to return or resell them.

D/P is safer for the seller because the buyer must pay before getting the documents. D/A is riskier: the buyer gets the documents by accepting a time draft (e.g., 60 days), promising to pay later. If the buyer defaults, the seller has little recourse. In Africa, D/A is used for established relationships, like a Ghanaian cocoa exporter selling to a European chocolate maker. The costs are lower than an L/C typically 0.1-0.5% of the transaction value versus 1-2% for an L/C. But the risk is higher. You can mitigate by using credit insurance (see next lesson) or by requiring a partial advance payment.

How to do it: the exporter prepares a collection letter with instructions, attaches the documents (invoice, B/L, packing list, COO, insurance), and gives them to their bank. The bank sends them to its correspondent bank in the buyer's country. The buyer's bank notifies the buyer, who pays or accepts. The bank then releases documents. The whole process can take 2-4 weeks. Common pitfalls: missing documents, incorrect instructions, or the buyer refusing to pay because of quality disputes. Always specify who pays bank charges usually the buyer, but clarify. For African SMEs, documentary collections work best when you have a long-standing relationship with the buyer and you are confident in their creditworthiness. Otherwise, stick to L/Cs or advance payment.

6. Export Credit Insurance and Guarantees

Export credit insurance protects you against the risk that your foreign buyer does not pay. In Africa, where currency shortages and political instability can delay payments, this is vital. For example, a Kenyan exporter of tea to Egypt might face a 90-day payment delay because of Egypt's foreign exchange crisis. With export credit insurance, the insurer covers up to 90% of the loss. In Africa, you can get this from the African Trade Insurance Agency (ATI), which operates in 20+ countries, or from private insurers like Euler Hermes or Coface. ATI, for instance, covers political and commercial risks for transactions up to $50 million. Premiums range from 0.2% to 1.5% of the insured value, depending on the buyer's country and creditworthiness.

There are two main types: short-term (up to 180 days) for consumer goods, and medium-to-long-term (1-5 years) for capital goods. You can insure a single shipment or your entire export turnover. For a Nigerian exporter selling to a buyer in Angola, ATI might charge 0.8% for a 90-day credit term. The process: you apply with your buyer's details, ATI checks the buyer's credit, and issues a policy. If the buyer defaults, you file a claim with proof of non-payment (e.g., bank notices, legal demand letters).

Export guarantees are different: they are bank instruments that assure the buyer you will perform. A performance guarantee (or bond) is issued by your bank to the buyer's bank, promising to pay if you fail to fulfill the contract. For example, a South African construction company bidding on a road project in Zambia might need a 10% performance guarantee. The bank charges a fee (1-3% per year) and may require collateral. An advance payment guarantee protects the buyer's advance payment if you don't deliver. These guarantees are common in government tenders.

For African SMEs, the key is to start small. Use ATI or your local export promotion agency to get credit insurance on one buyer. Then expand. Always read the policy exclusions many don't cover quality disputes or currency inconvertibility. And keep good records: invoices, shipping documents, and payment reminders. Without insurance, one bad debt can wipe out your profit. With it, you can offer competitive credit terms and win more orders.

This lesson is a practical reference. It summarises the key documents, the finance instruments that sit behind them, and the checks that experienced African trade finance officers apply before releasing goods or signing off on a transaction.</p>

<h3>1. Commercial Invoice and Packing List</h3>
<p>The commercial invoice is the exporter's legal demand for payment. It must match the sales contract, the LC (if any), the bill of lading, and the customs declaration line by line. In African agribusiness exports, mismatches on description, quantity, unit price or Incoterms are the single most common cause of bank rejection under UCP 600. A packing list describes what is physically in each package, carton, pallet or container. It matters for customs valuation, for warehouse handling at destination, and for insurance claims.</p>

<p><strong>Practical checks:</strong> use the same HS description as the customs declaration; state Incoterms 2020 (e.g. FOB Tema, CIF Mombasa); show net and gross weight; sign and date; and never describe goods as "as per proforma invoice" on an LC shipment banks reject that.</p>

<h3>2. Bill of Lading and Air Waybill</h3>
<p>The bill of lading (B/L) is a receipt, a contract of carriage, and a document of title. Because it is a document of title, the holder can claim the goods which is why banks accept it as security. An air waybill (AWB) is <em>not</em> a document of title; goods can be released to the named consignee without surrender of the original. For high-value perishables (flowers, fresh produce, fish), the AWB is common but the exporter loses the security that a B/L provides.</p>

<h3>3. Certificate of Origin</h3>
<p>The certificate of origin (COO) proves where the goods were produced. It unlocks preferential tariffs under AGOA (US), EU-ACP Economic Partnership Agreements, AfCFTA, COMESA, ECOWAS Trade Liberalisation Scheme, and bilateral agreements. A wrong or missing COO can add 1030% to the importer's duty and the importer will pass that cost back to you next season.</p>

<p>Common issuers in Africa: national chambers of commerce, customs authorities, and export promotion agencies. For AGOA, the exporter typically files a self-certification or a chamber-issued COO; for the EU GSP/EPA, a EUR.1 or a statement on origin is required.</p>

<h3>4. Letters of Credit: Types and Uses</h3>
<p>A letter of credit (LC) is a bank's irrevocable undertaking to pay against compliant documents. It shifts payment risk from the importer to the importer's bank. Under UCP 600, banks deal in documents, not goods so the exporter is paid only if the documents strictly comply.</p>

<h3>5. Documentary Collections</h3>
<p>Documentary collections (D/P documents against payment; D/A documents against acceptance) are cheaper than LCs but riskier for the exporter. The banks handle documents but do not guarantee payment. Under URC 522, the exporter instructs its bank to release documents to the importer only on payment or acceptance of a draft. D/P is safer than D/A, but both leave the exporter exposed if the importer refuses the goods. For African exporters shipping to new buyers, D/P with a 30% advance is a common compromise.</p>

<h3>6. Export Credit Insurance and Guarantees</h3>
<p>Export credit insurance protects the exporter against non-payment due to commercial or political risk. In Africa, key providers include the African Trade & Investment Development Insurance (ATIDI, formerly ATI), national export credit agencies (e.g. ECIC in South Africa, NEXIM in Nigeria, EGF in Egypt), and private insurers such as Euler Hermes and Coface. Guarantees performance bonds, advance payment guarantees, retention bonds are issued by banks and support tender and contract work.</p>

<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Fraud and document risk</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Trade document fraud is a real and recurring risk in African trade corridors. Never release original bills of lading, blank-endorsed documents, or signed invoices to an unverified counterparty. Do not accept an LC from a bank you cannot verify on the SWIFT network. Be alert to "amended" LC terms that arrive by email rather than through the advising bank these are a common fraud vector. Never ship against an LC that has not been advised through a bank in your own country. If a buyer pressures you to bypass the advising bank, stop and escalate to your trade finance officer.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Confirm the payment instrument (LC, D/P, D/A, open account) <em>before</em> production or shipment.</li>
<li>Check that LC terms match the sales contract and can realistically be met (dates, ports, documents).</li>
<li>Verify the issuing bank and confirm the LC through an African bank where possible.</li>
<li>Reconcile invoice, packing list, B/L or AWB, COO and insurance certificate same names, quantities, descriptions.</li>
<li>Use the correct Incoterms 2020 and state them on the invoice.</li>
<li>Obtain the right COO for the destination scheme (AGOA, EU EPA, AfCFTA, COMESA).</li>
<li>Present documents to the bank within the LC validity and the 21-day UCP 600 default, whichever is earlier.</li>
<li>Insure the shipment and check the policy covers the full CIF value plus 10%.</li>
</ol>
