Lessons

1. Deciding whether to focus on weddings, corporate, or cultural events

As your event business grows, you cannot be everything to everyone. Specializing helps you become known for one thing, charge more, and work more efficiently. Start by looking at your past 10 events. Which type gave you the most profit? Which did you enjoy most? Which brought the least stress? For example, if you made 500,000 Naira profit on a wedding but only 200,000 on a corporate event, weddings might be your niche. But consider the market: in many African cities, corporate events pay faster and have bigger budgets, while weddings are frequent but clients may negotiate hard. Cultural events like traditional marriages or festivals can be lucrative but seasonal. Talk to 5 potential clients in each category. Ask what they look for and what they would pay. If you choose weddings, you might invest in bridal showroom partnerships. For corporate, you need professional presentation, invoices, and reliability. For cultural, you need deep knowledge of local customs and fabrics. Pick one primary focus and one secondary for slow periods. For instance, focus on corporate events but take high-end weddings in December. This focus lets you buy specific decor (e.g., stage backdrops for corporate, floral arches for weddings) and train your team deeply. Remember, specialization does not mean you never do other events; it means your marketing and investments point in one direction. Write a one-page plan: your chosen focus, why, and three steps to dominate that niche in your area. Review it every six months.

2. Adding services: catering, photography, or MC services

Adding services increases your revenue per event and makes you a one-stop shop. But do not rush. First, survey your existing clients. Ask: 'Would you have paid extra for catering at your last event?' If 7 out of 10 say yes, it is worth exploring. Start with the service that requires the least new equipment and staff. Photography: you can partner with a freelance photographer and take a 20% commission. You do not need to buy a camera. MC services: if you are confident speaking, you can MC yourself for an extra 50,000 to 150,000 Naira per event. Catering is more complex: you need a food handler's permit, a kitchen, and staff. Begin by subcontracting to a local caterer you trust. You handle the client and decor, they handle food, and you add a 15-25% markup. For example, if a caterer charges 3,000 Naira per plate, you quote 3,750 Naira. Test one new service for three events before adding another. Track your profit margin per service. Photography might give 30% margin, MC 80%, catering 20%. Focus on high-margin services that do not distract from your core decor business. Also, train your team on upselling: when a client books decor, the team should mention photography or MC. Create simple packages: 'Decor + MC' or 'Decor + Photography'. Use a notebook or a free app like Wave to track which add-ons sell best. After six months, drop any service that does not make at least 100,000 Naira profit or bring in new clients.

3. Hiring and training a reliable team

You cannot scale alone. You need a team you can trust to set up decor while you manage clients. Start by hiring part-time helpers for busy weekends. Pay them a daily rate: in many African cities, 5,000 to 10,000 Naira per event day is standard for setup crew. Look for people who are punctual, honest, and physically strong. Test them on a small event first. For a core team, hire one assistant coordinator who can lead when you are away. Pay a monthly salary of 80,000 to 150,000 Naira depending on experience. Train your team on your standards: how to fold chair sashes, arrange centerpieces, and pack equipment without damage. Create a one-page checklist for each event type. Hold a 30-minute debrief after every event: what went well, what broke, what to improve. Use WhatsApp groups to share photos of perfect setups and mistakes. Invest in simple uniforms: branded T-shirts (2,000 Naira each) make your team look professional. Offer incentives: if a client gives a 5-star review, give the team a bonus of 5,000 Naira each. This reduces turnover. Keep a backup list of 5 freelancers you can call when you have two events on the same day. Never hire family just because they are family; hire for skill and attitude. Document everything: contracts, pay records, and training notes. A reliable team means you can take on bigger events and eventually step back from manual labor.

4. Investing in better equipment and a vehicle

Better equipment saves time and impresses clients. Start by replacing broken or cheap items. For example, invest in sturdy metal frames for backdrops instead of wooden ones that break. Buy 50 high-quality chair covers (500 Naira each) instead of 20 low-quality ones. A good quality tent (e.g., 10m x 20m) costs 500,000 to 1,500,000 Naira but can be rented out when you are not using it. For lighting, buy LED uplights (15,000 Naira each) that change colors and last for years. For a vehicle, you need a van or pickup to move decor. A used Toyota HiAce or Nissan Urvan costs 2,500,000 to 4,500,000 Naira. If you cannot buy, rent a van for 30,000 to 50,000 Naira per event. But buying saves money long-term: after 50 events, rental would cost 2,000,000 Naira. Consider a bank loan or a savings group (like ajo or chama) to fund the purchase. Before buying any equipment, calculate the payback period. If a new tent costs 800,000 Naira and you rent it for 100,000 Naira per event, it pays back in 8 events. Keep a maintenance log: clean tents after each use, store lights in dry boxes, and service the vehicle every 5,000 km. Insurance is important: insure your vehicle and equipment against theft or damage. With better equipment, you can charge 20-30% more because your setups look more professional.

5. Partnering with other small businesses for larger events

You cannot do everything alone. Partnering lets you bid on larger events without hiring more staff or buying more equipment. Identify complementary businesses: caterers, photographers, DJs, florists, and rental companies. Approach them with a clear proposal: 'I bring you clients, you give me a 15% referral fee, and we both deliver excellent service.' For example, if a client needs a 500-guest wedding, you handle decor, a partner handles catering, another handles photography. You act as the lead planner and take a 10-20% coordination fee on the total budget. Create a simple partnership agreement: who is responsible for what, payment terms, and what happens if something goes wrong. Use a WhatsApp group for each event to coordinate. Test partnerships on small events first. If a caterer is always late, do not use them for a big wedding. Build a network of 3 reliable partners in each category. This way, if one is unavailable, you have backups. Also, share marketing: post each other's services on social media. For larger events, you can jointly pitch to corporate clients. For example, a corporate end-of-year party for 300 staff might need decor, catering, MC, and photography. Together, you can quote 2,000,000 Naira, while alone you could only quote 500,000 Naira for decor. Always be transparent about your markup. Trust is key. After each event, meet with partners to review and improve.

6. Setting growth goals for the next year

Without goals, your business drifts. Set specific, measurable goals for the next 12 months. Start with revenue: if you made 2,000,000 Naira this year, aim for 4,000,000 Naira next year. Break it down: how many events at what average price? For example, 20 weddings at 150,000 Naira profit each = 3,000,000 Naira, plus 5 corporate events at 200,000 Naira = 1,000,000 Naira. Then set goals for team, equipment, and marketing. Maybe you want to hire 2 full-time staff, buy a van, and get 50 new Instagram followers per week. Write these goals on a board where you see them daily. Every month, review your progress. Use a simple spreadsheet or notebook to track: events done, revenue, expenses, profit, and client feedback. Adjust as needed. If you are behind on revenue by June, you can run a promotion or take on smaller events. Also, set learning goals: attend a workshop on floral design or take an online course on event marketing. Save 10% of your profit for reinvestment. For example, if you make 4,000,000 Naira profit, save 400,000 Naira for new equipment or training. Celebrate small wins: when you hit 50% of your goal, treat your team to a meal. Finally, set a personal goal: maybe you want to work less on weekends or take a vacation. Growth is not just money; it is freedom. Share your goals with a mentor or business group for accountability. Review and revise every quarter.

<h2>Practical Reference: Scaling and Specializing Your Event Business</h2>

For the African event entrepreneur, the question is no longer <em>whether</em> to grow, but <em>how</em> to grow profitably without collapsing under operational strain.</p>

<strong> SAFETY WARNING Food Handling &amp; Catering</strong><br>
If you add catering, you MUST comply with national food safety regulations. In Nigeria, this means NAFDAC registration for packaged food and state health permits for kitchens. In Kenya, KEBS certification and county public health licenses are mandatory. Failure to comply can result in fines exceeding $5,000, business closure, or criminal liability if a guest falls ill. Never prepare food in unlicensed facilities. Always maintain cold-chain storage and train staff on HACCP principles.

<h2>3. Hiring and Training a Reliable Team</h2>

<p>Your first hire should be an <strong>operations coordinator</strong>someone who can manage logistics while you focus on sales and client relationships. In most African markets, a competent coordinator costs $150$400/month depending on the country and city.</p>

<p><strong>Hiring principles for African event businesses:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hire for attitude, train for skill.</strong> Event work is stressful; punctuality and honesty matter more than experience.</li>
<li><strong>Use trial periods.</strong> Pay per-event for the first 3 events before offering a retainer.</li>
<li><strong>Document everything.</strong> Create a simple operations manual (even 5 pages) so that new hires can be trained quickly.</li>
<li><strong>Pay fairly and on time.</strong> Word travels fast in African event circles; a reputation for late payment will destroy your ability to recruit.</li>
<li><strong>Cross-train.</strong> Every team member should know at least two roles (e.g., decor + MC assistant; logistics + photography assistant).</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Training resources:</strong> The ILO's <em>Start and Improve Your Business (SIYB)</em> program is available in most African countries through local business development service providers. The African Management Institute also offers low-cost online courses in team management.</p>

<h2>4. Investing in Better Equipment and a Vehicle</h2>

<p>Equipment and transport are the two biggest capital expenditures for a scaling event business. The decision to buy versus rent depends on your event frequency.</p>

<p><strong>Rule of thumb:</strong> If you use an item more than 8 times per month, buy it. If fewer, rent it. A used Toyota Hiace or Nissan Urvan (common in Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana) costs $8,000$18,000 and can serve as both transport and storage. A dedicated cargo van or small truck costs $15,000$30,000.</p>

<p><strong>Priority equipment list for scaling:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Reliable vehicle (van or small truck) highest priority</li>
<li>Portable sound system with wireless microphones</li>
<li>LED lighting kit (battery-powered for areas with unstable electricity)</li>
<li>Durable tent and staging materials</li>
<li>Backup generator (25 KVA)</li>
</ol>

<p>Financing options include SACCOs in Kenya, cooperative societies in Nigeria, and asset financing from banks like Equity Bank (Kenya), GTBank (Nigeria), and Absa (Ghana, South Africa). Interest rates range from 1228% annually.</p>

<h2>5. Partnering with Other Small Businesses for Larger Events</h2>

<p>Partnerships allow you to bid on larger events without owning every asset. A typical partnership model in African markets is the <strong>consortium bid</strong>where 35 complementary businesses (decor, catering, sound, photography, venue) submit a joint proposal.</p>

<p><strong>Partnership best practices:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Sign a simple written agreement (even a one-page MOU) covering scope, payment split, and liability.</li>
<li>Agree on a lead partner who handles client communication.</li>
<li>Set clear quality standards; one partner's failure damages everyone's reputation.</li>
<li>Share client feedback openly after each event.</li>
<li>Review the partnership every 6 months.</li>
</ul>

<p>In South Africa, the <strong>Supplier Development Programme</strong> under the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition (the dtic) actively supports small business partnerships. In Nigeria, the <strong>SMEDAN</strong> cluster program helps event businesses form cooperatives.</p>

<h2>6. Setting Growth Goals for the Next Year</h2>

<p>Use the <strong>SMART-ER</strong> framework: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound, Evaluated, Reviewed. Here is a sample 12-month growth plan for an event business moving from Stage 2 to Stage 3:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Q1:</strong> Complete 6 events, hire 1 part-time coordinator, register business formally.</li>
<li><strong>Q2:</strong> Add MC services (low-cost, high-margin), reach 8 events/month.</li>
<li><strong>Q3:</strong> Purchase used van, sign 2 partnership MOUs, train 3 staff.</li>
<li><strong>Q4:</strong> Add photography service, reach 12 events/month, achieve 25% profit margin.</li>
</ul>

<p>Track these numbers monthly: events completed, revenue per event, cost per event, client satisfaction score, and repeat/referral rate.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li> Choose ONE primary event segment and dominate it before expanding.</li>
<li> Add only services with &gt;40% margin and manageable risk.</li>
<li> Hire an operations coordinator before you hire creatives.</li>
<li> Buy equipment used more than 8 times/month; rent the rest.</li>
<li> Secure a reliable vehicle before bidding on large events.</li>
<li> Formalize at least 2 partnerships with written agreements.</li>
<li> Set quarterly SMART-ER goals and review monthly.</li>
<li> Comply with food safety, tax, and business registration laws.</li>
</ul>
