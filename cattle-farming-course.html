Cattle Farming

A complete 6-month program — pasture, buying, tick control, breeding and pen fattening — that takes you from empty kraal to your first profitable sale.

⏱️ 6 Months (8–12 hrs/week)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? Cattle are the slowest, largest investment in livestock — so this program uses 6 months to take you from pasture assessment to your first animals bought right, kept healthy, and either bred or pen-fattened for sale. Months 1–3 build the foundation (land, feed, buying); Months 4–6 run the herd (health, breeding, fattening and marketing).

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each month → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen at the kraal, the dip tank and the auction, not the screen.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Ticks are the #1 killer of African cattle — tick-borne diseases (heartwater, anaplasmosis/gall sickness, babesiosis/redwater, theileriosis/January disease) kill more animals than drought. Your dipping calendar is life insurance.
  • Foot-and-Mouth and anthrax are notifiable diseases — sudden multiple deaths or blistered mouths/feet must be reported to veterinary services immediately, and anthrax carcasses must NEVER be opened or eaten.
  • The dry season is planned in the wet season. Cattle that starve in October were failed in March. Month 3 is your feed-security month.
MONTH 1 — Cattle Science, Breeds & Systems ▾

Lesson 1.1 — The cattle economy

  • Cattle are Africa's savings account, tractor, milk factory and meat business in one animal — but only managed herds beat inflation; neglected herds shrink.
  • Two profit engines: beef (weight gain sold per kg) and dairy (daily milk income). This course majors on beef with a dairy-basics lesson; the full dairy path is the rAs Dairy Production course.
  • Key numbers to hold: gestation ~283 days (9.5 months); heat every ~21 days; calves weaned 6–8 months; steers finished at 18–30 months depending on system.

Lesson 1.2 — Breeds for African conditions

Breed typeExamplesStrengthsTrade-offs
Indigenous (Sanga/Zebu)Mashona, Nguni, Boran, Tuli, AnkoleTick and heat tolerance, thrive on poor grazing, fertile, easy calvingSmaller frame, slower growth
Exotic beefBrahman, Simmental, Angus, HerefordBig frames, fast growth, feedlot performanceNeed better grazing, dipping and management
Crosses (the smart middle)Brahman × Mashona, Simmental × NguniHybrid vigour: indigenous toughness + exotic growthKeep records or crossing direction gets lost
DairyHolstein/Friesian (volume), Jersey (butterfat, hardiness)15–30+ L/day well fedHigh feed, water and health demands
  • Beginner rule: buy the toughness of local blood first; add exotic growth by using a good crossbred or exotic bull later.

Lesson 1.3 — Production systems

  • Extensive grazing (veld/rangeland): lowest cost, growth follows the rains; the base system for most of Africa.
  • Semi-intensive: grazing plus strategic supplementation (the profitable sweet spot this course teaches).
  • Pen fattening (feedlot): buy lean steers, feed intensively 90–120 days for 0.8–1.5 kg/day gains — a trading business built on feed maths (Month 6).
  • Cow–calf breeding: the long game — sell weaners yearly; success = calving rate (target 80%+ vs the communal average of ~45–55%).

Lesson 1.4 — Judging an animal (stockmanship 101)

  • Body condition score (BCS) 1–5: ribs clearly visible = 2 (thin), ribs felt but not seen = 3 (good working condition), blocky and smooth = 4–5 (fat/finished).
  • Age by teeth: 2 permanent incisors ≈ 2 years, 4 ≈ 2.5–3 years, 6 ≈ 3.5 years, full mouth (8) ≈ 4+ years; worn/gapped = old.
  • Health at a glance: shiny coat, moist muzzle, alert ears, smooth walk, no coughing, dung firm not watery.
  • Weight without a scale: heart-girth tape (girth just behind the front legs) estimates within ±5–8% — every buyer and seller should own one.

Lesson 1.5 — Water and land requirements

  • Water: an adult beast drinks 30–60 L/day (more when hot or lactating). Water security decides herd size before grass does.
  • Stocking rate reality: African rangeland commonly carries 1 livestock unit per 3–10+ hectares depending on rainfall — your local extension office has the figure for your area; overstocking is the slow death of both grass and herd.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Visit a dip day or auction: practise BCS-scoring and teeth-ageing on 10 animals, and record prices against condition. (2) Make a heart-girth weigh tape and test it on any available animal. (3) Get your area's official stocking rate and dipping schedule from the extension/veterinary office.

MONTH 2 — Grazing, Pasture & Infrastructure ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Grass is the factory

  • Grass value collapses as it matures: young green grass can carry 10–15% protein; dry standing winter grass falls to 2–4% — below maintenance needs. This single fact explains the African cattle year.
  • Rotational grazing: split grazing into paddocks (fences or herding), graze short (days–2 weeks), rest long (4–8 weeks) — rested grass regrows; hammered grass dies and bush takes over.
  • Never graze below ~5–10 cm stubble; the leaf left behind is the solar panel that regrows the pasture.

Lesson 2.2 — Improving the feed base

  • Planted pasture/fodder where land allows: Rhodes grass, star grass; napier/bana grass for cut-and-carry (see rAs Pasture Management).
  • Fodder trees and legumes: leucaena, acacia pods, velvet bean, lablab, mucuna — protein banks for the dry season.
  • Crop residues are gold: maize stover, groundnut haulms, bean straw — collect and stack at harvest, don't let them burn.

Lesson 2.3 — The infrastructure a herd needs

  • Kraal/holding pen: strong rails 1.5 m high; night kraaling protects from theft and predators and concentrates manure.
  • Crush/race: the single most useful structure on a cattle place — dosing, dipping (spray race), pregnancy checks, tagging all become one-person jobs. Pole crush: two converging fences ending 70–75 cm apart.
  • Water points: trough space ~60 cm per 10 head with clean water always; site troughs away from erosion-prone spots.
  • Fencing vs herding: fences cost capital, herders cost wages — most starters combine a fenced night/handling area with day herding.

Lesson 2.4 — The seasonal grazing plan

  • Wet season: rotate hard, cut and store surplus grass as hay (see rAs Hay Making), make silage where maize is grown.
  • Late wet: wean strategically so cows regain condition before winter.
  • Dry season: ration the saved veld, feed stored roughage, supplement protein (Month 3) and sell the animals you planned to sell BEFORE prices crash with everyone else's desperation sales.
✅ Iron-Clad Price Check: fencing wire, poles, cement for troughs and crush timber — 3 quotes each; fencing materials carry some of the widest price spreads in agriculture.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 2): (1) Map your grazing land into 4+ paddocks on paper with water access for each. (2) Build or fully cost a pole crush and night kraal. (3) Stack at least one tonne of crop residues or cut hay this month — dated and covered.

MONTH 3 — Nutrition, Supplementation & Buying Your Stock ▾

Lesson 3.1 — What cattle must get

  • Dry matter intake: cattle eat roughly 2–3% of body weight in dry matter daily — a 300 kg animal needs 7–9 kg DM (≈ that much hay, or more of wet feed).
  • Protein: growing stock need 12–14% CP in the diet; dry cows maintain on 7–8%; dry-season grass alone cannot supply either.
  • Minerals: phosphorus is Africa's classic deficiency — bone-chewing cattle are telling you; a lick with salt + P + trace minerals belongs in every kraal year-round.

Lesson 3.2 — Dry-season survival feeding

  • Protein supplementation transforms dry grass: as little as 0.5–1 kg/day of a protein source (cottonseed/sunflower/soya cake or a commercial protein lick at 200–500 g/day) lets rumen microbes digest low-quality roughage instead of the animal starving on a full belly.
  • Urea-based winter licks: powerful and cheap protein substitute — but UREA IS TOXIC when eaten too fast or wet; use only commercial formulations, keep licks dry, never feed to hungry animals unaccustomed to it, and keep out of reach of calves and other species.
  • Survival ranking when feed is short: sell first, feed the breeding core (cows and heifers) first, and never let oxen destined for sale eat the cows' winter feed.

Lesson 3.3 — Buying cattle without being cheated

  • Where: registered auctions (price discovery + movement papers), reputable farms, and — with care — communal sales. Every purchase needs the legal movement/ownership documents your country requires; stolen-stock accusations end farms.
  • What to buy first: 2–3 in-calf cows or cow-calf pairs of local/cross blood (breeding path) OR lean 18–30-month steers of BCS 2–2.5 (fattening path).
  • Apply Month 1 skills: BCS, teeth, girth tape, health signs — and buy at the END of the dry season when prices are lowest if your feed store can carry the animals to the rains.
  • Arrival protocol: quarantine 2–3 weeks apart from any existing stock, dip on arrival, deworm, and vaccinate per the Month 4 calendar.

Lesson 3.4 — Dairy basics (one-lesson version)

  • A crossbred dairy cow on good feeding gives 8–15 L/day; pure Holsteins 20–30 L but demand feeding and health management most starters can't yet supply.
  • Milk = water + feed: 60–80 L drinking water/day and 3–5 kg of dairy meal (16–18% CP) on top of forage for every 10 L of milk.
  • Hygiene sells milk: clean hands, clean udder, clean sealed containers, milk cooled or delivered within 2–3 hours. Full program: rAs Dairy Production course.
✅ Iron-Clad Price Check: protein cake, licks and the animals themselves — 3 quotes/valuations each. On buying day, the tape measure and teeth check beat every seller's story.

MONTH 4 — Herd Health: Ticks, Vaccines & Daily Care ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Tick-borne disease: know the big four

DiseaseCarrierClassic signsNote
HeartwaterBont ticksHigh fever, nervous signs — high-stepping, circling, convulsionsFast killer; vet treatment early or death
Anaplasmosis (gall sickness)Blue ticksFever, pale/yellow gums, constipation, weaknessCommon in stressed/moved adults
Babesiosis (redwater)Blue ticksRed/coffee-coloured urine, fever, anaemiaName says it: check the urine
Theileriosis (incl. January disease)Brown ear ticksSwollen lymph nodes, laboured breathing, feverRegionally devastating; vaccination exists in some countries
  • Control = dipping/spraying on your veterinary office's LOCAL schedule (commonly weekly in the rainy season, every 2 weeks to monthly in the dry), plus hand-dressing ears, tail base and udder where ticks hide.

Lesson 4.2 — The vaccination and treatment calendar

  • Core vaccines to discuss with your vet office (availability and law vary by country): blackleg/botulism/anthrax combinations annually; brucellosis for heifer calves (often legally controlled); lumpy skin disease before the rains; FMD where government programs run them.
  • Deworm strategically: young stock at weaning and rain-season intervals; adults on need (condition/dung) — blanket over-dosing breeds resistant worms.
  • Record every treatment (animal ID, product, dose, date) and obey slaughter/milk withdrawal periods on every label.

Lesson 4.3 — Notifiable emergencies

  • Anthrax: sudden death, bloody discharges, carcass bloats fast — DO NOT open, move, or eat; report immediately; the site must be handled by authorities (spores persist for decades).
  • Foot-and-Mouth (FMD): drooling, blisters on mouth/feet, lameness through the herd — report immediately; movement bans protect the whole district.
  • Sudden multiple deaths of any pattern: isolate the area, call veterinary services — never rush carcasses to the butcher.

Lesson 4.4 — The daily and weekly routine

  • Daily: count the herd, watch them walk and graze (a straggler is your early warning), check water.
  • Weekly at the crush: tick inspection/hand-dress, condition notes on the breeding cows, hoof and eye checks.
  • Calf care: navel iodine at birth, colostrum within 6 hours, dehorn and castrate early (first weeks — least stress) using proper tools; ear-tag and record.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 4): (1) Write your farm's 12-month health calendar with the district vet office's actual local schedule — laminate it. (2) Attend a dip day; learn the acaricide mixing rate used and why. (3) Save the veterinary emergency reporting number and rehearse your anthrax/FMD response on paper.

MONTH 5 — Breeding & Herd Growth ▾

Lesson 5.1 — The reproduction numbers

  • Heat every ~21 days lasting 6–18 hours: mounting/standing to be mounted, clear mucus, restlessness, milk drop in dairy cows.
  • Serve heifers at 60–65% of mature weight (≈ 250–280 kg for medium breeds), usually 18–24 months — breeding small heifers gives dead calves and ruined cows.
  • Gestation ~283 days; aim calving at the START of the rains so peak milk meets peak grass.
  • Bull power: one sound bull per 25–30 cows; test the bull's soundness (feet, testicle tone/size, libido) BEFORE the season, not after empty cows appear.

Lesson 5.2 — Controlled breeding beats year-round bulls

  • A defined 2–3 month bulling season = a tight calving season = uniform calves, one weaning, one vaccination batch, and cows that can be condition-managed as one group.
  • Target ≥ 80% of cows in calf; pregnancy-check (vet/AI tech, 2–3 months after bull removal) and CULL repeat-empty cows — a dry cow eats a calf's worth of grass yearly.
  • AI (artificial insemination) option: top genetics without keeping a bull; needs sharp heat detection and a reachable technician.

Lesson 5.3 — Calving and the first month

  • Signs of calving: udder fills, vulva swells, tail-head ligaments soften; most cows deliver unaided within 2–4 hours of straining — front feet + nose first is normal.
  • Intervene (experienced help/vet) if hard straining passes 2 hours with no progress, or presentation is wrong.
  • After birth: colostrum within 6 hours (the calf's only immunity), navel iodine, watch the cow cleanse (retained afterbirth past 24 h = vet call).
  • Weaning at 6–8 months: separate beyond fence-line contact, feed the weaner (this is when supplementation pays most), and the cow rebuilds for the next calf.

Lesson 5.4 — Records: the herd book

  • Per animal: ID/tag, birth date, dam and sire, weights (birth, weaning, yearly), treatments, calvings.
  • The two herd KPIs: calving % (calves born ÷ cows exposed) and weaning weight — both are bred AND fed.
  • Cull list logic each year: empty cows, bad mothers, chronic sick, wild temperament — sell them at good condition, not as skeletons later.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 5): (1) Set your bulling season dates so calving meets your rains — write the full backwards calendar (bull in, bull out, preg-check, calving, weaning). (2) Open a herd book with a page per animal. (3) Practise heat detection: observe a herd at dawn/dusk for 3 days and log every mounting event.

MONTH 6 — Pen Fattening, Marketing & Your Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Pen fattening: the 90-day cattle trade

  • The model: buy lean steers cheap (late dry season, BCS 2–2.5), feed a high-energy ration 90–120 days, sell finished at BCS 3.5–4 when prices peak.
  • Gains of 0.8–1.5 kg/day are realistic on a ration of roughly 65–75% energy (maize/hominy chop/molasses), 10–15% protein source (cake), 10–20% roughage, plus minerals — commercial pen-fattening meals simplify this for starters.
  • Adapt animals to grain over 2–3 weeks stepwise; sudden full grain causes acidosis (bloat, laminitis, death). Clean water and roughage always available.
  • Feed maths first, sentiment never: a steer eating 10 kg/day for 100 days consumes 1 tonne of feed — if feed cost per kg of GAIN exceeds the meat price per kg, the pen loses money before it starts.

Lesson 6.2 — Selling channels and carcass maths

  • Channels: registered auctions (transparent price/kg live), abattoirs (price per kg carcass by grade), butcheries (negotiate on carcass estimate), and private/festive live sales (often the best single-animal prices).
  • Dressing percentage 50–55% in grass/veld cattle, up to ~58% pen-finished: a 450 kg steer ≈ 230–255 kg carcass — every negotiation starts from this number.
  • Grades pay: fat cover and youth move an animal up grade bands worth real money per kg — this is what the fattening pen buys you.
  • Weigh or tape EVERY animal before pricing; sell on numbers, and always with movement papers in order.

Lesson 6.4 — Risk management

  • Theft: branding/tagging registered with authorities, night kraals, community watch — and insurance where offered.
  • Drought plan written in advance: trigger dates for early sales, feed purchase price ceilings, minimum core herd to protect.
  • Disease shock: the Month 4 calendar plus a cash reserve of one animal's value for veterinary emergencies.

Lesson 6.5 — Final project: your bankable cattle plan

  • From six months of records assemble: land and water assessment, chosen system (breeding, fattening or both), stocking plan vs official carrying capacity, feed-security calendar, health calendar, buying/selling strategy with real local prices, risk plan, and a 3-year growth ladder.
  • Submit with photos and records summary for your rAs certificate — the same file speaks to banks, partners and livestock schemes.

📖 Glossary

  • BCS — body condition score, 1 (emaciated) to 5 (fat).
  • Heifer — female before first calf; steer — castrated male; weaner — calf newly separated (6–8 months).
  • Dressing % — carcass ÷ live weight (≈50–58%).
  • Acaricide — tick-killing dip/spray chemical.
  • Notifiable disease — one the law requires you to report (anthrax, FMD).
  • Bulling season — the defined months the bull runs with cows.
  • Preg-check — pregnancy diagnosis by vet/technician after the season.
  • Acidosis — rumen collapse from sudden grain feeding.
  • Lick — mineral/protein block or meal offered free-choice.
  • Carrying capacity — hectares needed per animal on your rangeland.
