1. Introduction to Welding Processes: MIG, TIG, Stick

Welding joins metal by melting it. In Africa, you'll mostly use three processes: Stick (SMAW), MIG (GMAW), and TIG (GTAW). Each has a different setup, cost, and use. Stick welding is the most common in rural workshops because it's cheap and works outdoors. You need a stick welder (like a 200-amp inverter), electrodes (e.g., E6013 for general work), a chipping hammer, and a wire brush. MIG welding uses a wire feeder and shielding gas (usually CO2 or Argon mix). It's faster and easier for beginners but needs a gas cylinder and regulator. TIG welding uses a tungsten electrode and a separate filler rod, giving precise, clean welds on thin metal or aluminium. It's slower and requires more skill. For sculpture, Stick is good for thick steel frames; MIG for quick fabrication; TIG for detailed or visible joints. Always wear a welding helmet (shade 10-13), leather gloves, and a leather apron. Work in a well-ventilated areanever in a closed room. In many African cities, you can buy a 200-amp Stick welder for around $100-$200. MIG setups start at $300 with gas. TIG is most expensive. Start with Stick: it teaches you heat control and patience. Practice on scrap steel plates 3-5 mm thick. Set your amperage: 3.2 mm electrode at 90-120 amps. Strike an arc like a match, then maintain a tight arc. Listen to the soundit should crackle like frying bacon. If it hums, your amps are too low. Keep a consistent travel speed. Clean your welds with a wire brush after each pass. Remember, welding is a skill built by repetition. Spend at least 30 minutes daily on scrap before you weld a sculpture.

2. MIG Welding: Setup and Basic Beads

MIG welding (Metal Inert Gas) is like a hot glue gun for metal. You pull the trigger, wire feeds out, and an arc melts it into the joint. It's the easiest process to learn for sculpture because you can use two hands. First, set up your machine: a MIG welder (e.g., 180-amp), a gas cylinder (75% Argon / 25% CO2 for steel), a regulator, and a wire spool (0.8 mm or 0.9 mm ER70S-6 wire). Connect the ground clamp to your workbench. Set voltage and wire speed. For 3 mm steel, start with voltage at 18-19V and wire speed at 4-5 (on a 1-10 dial). The gas flow should be 10-15 CFH. Too much gas wastes it; too little gives porous welds. Before welding, clean metal to bare shine with a grinder. Position your gun at a 10-15 degree push angle. Stick out the wire about 10 mm. Wear a auto-darkening helmet (shade 10-11). Practice on a flat plate: make straight beads. Keep a consistent travel speedabout 3-5 mm per second. Listen for a steady sizzle. If it pops, increase wire speed or voltage. For a basic bead, move in a slight zigzag or straight drag. Don't weave too widekeep beads 6-8 mm wide. After each bead, let it cool, then chip slag if any (MIG has little slag). Clean with a wire brush. In Africa, MIG wire and gas are available in major cities like Nairobi, Lagos, and Johannesburg. A 5 kg spool of wire costs $15-$25. Gas refill is $30-$50. Practice making 10 beads a day. Once you can run a straight bead without burn-through, try butt joints on 3 mm plates. That's your foundation for sculpture.

3. TIG Welding: Setup and Basic Beads

TIG welding (Tungsten Inert Gas) gives you the most control and the cleanest welds. It's perfect for visible joints on sculptures, thin sheet metal, and aluminium. But it's slower and needs two hands: one for the torch, one for the filler rod. Setup: a TIG welder (AC/DC for aluminium, DC for steel), a tungsten electrode (2% thoriated for steel, pure tungsten for aluminium), a gas lens, and 100% Argon gas. For steel, use DCEN (electrode negative). Grind the tungsten to a sharp point. Set amperage: for 1.6 mm steel, use 60-80 amps. Gas flow: 15-20 CFH. Use a foot pedal if you have one to control heat. Wear a TIG glove (thin leather) and a helmet with shade 10-12. Start by making a puddle on a scrap plate without filler. Hold the torch at a 70-degree angle, close to the metal (2-3 mm arc length). Once you have a shiny puddle, dip the filler rod (1.6 mm ER70S-2 for steel) into the front edge of the puddle. Move forward, dip, move, dip. Keep a rhythm. For a basic bead, travel speed should be slow and steadyabout 2-3 mm per second. The weld should look like stacked dimes. If the puddle gets too big, reduce amps or move faster. For aluminium, use AC and a green tungsten. Clean aluminium with a stainless brush before welding. TIG is less common in rural Africa due to cost, but many technical colleges in cities like Accra and Durban teach it. A basic TIG welder costs $500-$800. Argon gas is $50-$80 per refill. Practice on 2 mm steel plates. Make 10 beads a day. Once you can control the puddle, try lap joints. TIG will make your sculptures stand out.

4. Brazing and Soldering Techniques

Brazing and soldering join metal without melting the base metal. They use a filler metal with a lower melting point. Soldering uses filler below 450C (lead-free solder for electronics, or silver solder for jewellery). Brazing uses filler above 450C (like brass or bronze rods). For sculpture, brazing is great for joining copper, brass, and steel without warping thin sheets. You need a torch (propane or oxy-acetylene), flux, and filler rods. For brazing steel, use a bronze rod (e.g., silicon bronze) and white flux. Clean the metal to bare shine. Apply flux to the joint. Heat the joint evenly with a neutral flame (blue cone). When the flux bubbles and turns clear, touch the rod to the joint. It should melt and flow like honey. Don't heat the rod directlyheat the metal. Capillary action pulls the filler into the joint. For a strong joint, keep the gap between parts at 0.05-0.1 mm. After brazing, clean off flux with hot water and a wire brush. Soldering is for small, delicate work. Use a soldering iron (30-60W) or a small torch. For copper sculptures, use silver solder (hard solder) with borax flux. Heat the joint, apply solder, and let it flow. In Africa, brazing rods and flux are available at hardware stores in cities. A propane torch kit costs $30-$50. Oxy-acetylene is more expensive ($200+). Practice on scrap copper pipes. Make lap joints and T-joints. Brazing is ideal for repairing cast iron or joining dissimilar metals. It's also safer than welding for thin materials. Remember: ventilation is criticalflux fumes are toxic. Work outdoors or use a fan. With practice, you can create seamless joints that look like solid metal.

5. Mechanical Fastening: Bolts, Rivets, and Screws

Not every joint needs welding. Mechanical fastening is strong, removable, and doesn't require heat. For sculpture, it lets you assemble and disassemble, or combine materials like wood and metal. Bolts: use grade 8.8 or stainless steel. For 6 mm bolts, drill a 6.5 mm hole. Tighten with a spanner or socket set. Add washers to spread load. For a permanent but low-profile joint, use rivets. Pop rivets (blind rivets) are easy: drill a hole, insert rivet, squeeze with a rivet gun. They work on thin sheets (up to 6 mm total thickness). For stronger joints, use solid rivetsheat them, insert, and hammer the tail. Screws: self-tapping screws cut their own threads in pilot holes. For metal, use machine screws with nuts. In Africa, bolts and rivets are sold in most hardware stores. A box of 100 pop rivets (4 mm) costs $5-$10. A rivet gun is $15-$25. For sculpture, consider using bolts as design featuresleave them exposed. Use lock nuts (nylon insert) to prevent loosening. When joining dissimilar metals (e.g., steel and aluminium), use nylon washers to prevent galvanic corrosion. Always drill pilot holes slightly smaller than the screw for self-tapping. For rivets, the hole should match the rivet diameter. Practice on scrap: make a lap joint with bolts, then with rivets. Test the strength by pulling apart. Mechanical fastening is reversible, so it's great for prototypes. In rural areas where welding gas is scarce, fastening is a reliable alternative. Keep a set of drill bits (3 mm to 10 mm), a hand drill or drill press, and a good rivet gun. Your sculpture can be a mix of welded and bolted parts for both strength and visual interest.

6. Project: Welded Geometric Sculpture

Now you'll build a welded geometric sculpture using the skills from this module. Choose a simple design: a cube frame, a pyramid, or an abstract star. For a cube, you need 12 pieces of 10 mm square steel bar, each 30 cm long. Cut them with an angle grinder or hacksaw. Clean ends with a grinder. Set up a jig: use a flat steel table or a thick wooden board with nails to hold pieces at 90 degrees. Tack weld each corner firstsmall 5 mm welds on opposite sides to prevent distortion. Use your Stick or MIG welder. For 10 mm steel, set Stick amps to 110-130 with a 3.2 mm electrode, or MIG at 19-20V and wire speed 5. Check squareness with a carpenter's square. After tacking all corners, weld full joints. Alternate sides to balance heat. Let it cool, then grind welds smooth if desired. For a pyramid, use 4 pieces for base, 4 for sides, and 1 for top. Tack and weld. For an abstract star, cut 5 pieces of 8 mm round bar, each 40 cm, and weld them at the center. Use a metal pipe as a central hub. After welding, clean with a wire brush and apply a rust-preventive primer. If you want a finish, use black spray paint or clear lacquer. This project takes 4-6 hours. Work in a ventilated area. Wear all safety gear. In Africa, you can source steel bars from local scrap yards or hardware stores. A 6 m length of 10 mm square bar costs $8-$12. This sculpture can be a small table piece or a garden ornament. Once you master the cube, try more complex polyhedra. The key is to measure twice, cut once, and tack before full welding. Show your finished piece to your instructor or post it online for feedback. Congratulationsyou're now a metal sculptor.

<h2>Practical Reference: Welding and Joining for Metal Sculpture</h2>

<p>This practical reference is written for working metal sculptors, artisanal fabricators, and smallshop operators across Africa who need to select, set up, and troubleshoot welding and joining processes quickly. The emphasis is on commercial viability: choosing the lowestcost process that still delivers the required strength, appearance, and corrosion resistance for a saleable sculpture. Whether you are welding mild steel armatures in Kumasi, brazing copper leaf forms in Bulawayo, or assembling stainless steel public art in Nairobi, the tables and checklist below are designed to be printed and pinned to the workshop wall.</p>

<p>Metal sculpture in Africa spans a huge range of scales and materials. In many markets, mild steel is the default because it is cheap, widely available as rebar, angle iron, and plate offcuts, and forgiving to weld. Copper and brass are common in West African and Central African art traditions, where brazing and soldering often replace fusion welding. Aluminium is increasingly used for outdoor and architectural pieces because it resists rust, but it demands TIG or MIG with the correct polarity and filler. Your process choice is therefore a business decision as much as a technical one: a MIG set that runs on a 5 kVA generator may be more valuable than a TIG set that needs stable threephase power, even if TIG produces prettier beads.</p>

<h3>Process Notes for Sculptors</h3>

<p><strong>MIG Welding: Setup and Basic Beads.</strong> MIG is the workhorse for mild steel sculpture because it is fast and forgiving. Set wire speed and voltage so the arc sounds like frying bacon, not popping. For 2 mm steel, start at 18 V and 80 A, then adjust. Push or drag? For sculpture, a slight push angle (1015) gives a flatter bead that is easier to grind. Keep your contacttiptowork distance at about 10 mm; too far and the arc becomes unstable. In dusty or windy outdoor conditions, fluxcore wire without gas is more reliable than solid wire with gas.</p>

<p><strong>TIG Welding: Setup and Basic Beads.</strong> TIG is the choice for aluminium, stainless, and copper where the bead will be seen. Set the machine to AC for aluminium, DCEN for steel and stainless. Sharpen your tungsten to a point for DC, ball it slightly for AC. Use a foot pedal if you have one, or set the amperage on the machine and control heat by travel speed. The classic TIG bead is made by dipping filler rod into the leading edge of the puddle in a rhythmic motion. For sculpture, a "stack of dimes" look is often desirable it signals handmade quality and can justify a higher price.</p>

<p><strong>Brazing and Soldering.</strong> Many African sculptors work with copper, brass, and bronze, and here brazing is often better than welding. Brazing at 450900 C produces a strong joint without melting the base metal, which preserves thin sheet and intricate forms. Use a flux appropriate to the alloy; boraxbased flux works for many copper alloys. Silver solder (hard soldering) is stronger and neater but more expensive. Clean the joint to bright metal before heating, and heat the joint, not the filler let the filler flow into the heat.</p>

<p><strong>Mechanical Fastening.</strong> Not every joint should be welded. Knockdown sculptures for export or transport are often bolted or riveted. Bolts allow disassembly for repair and reduce the risk of heat distortion. Rivets give a permanent, lowprofile joint and are traditional in some metalwork. Screws are useful for attaching sheet panels or mixedmaterial elements. Use antiseize on stainless bolts in aluminium to prevent galvanic corrosion, and always drill pilot holes to avoid cracking thin plate.</p>

<p><strong>Project: Welded Geometric Sculpture.</strong> A practical first commercial project is a geometric sculpture made from 25 25 mm mild steel square tube or 8 mm rebar. Design a form that can be built from straight cuts and simple joints cubes, tetrahedra, or stacked rings. Cut all pieces first, deburr, and lay out the assembly on a flat surface. Tackweld every joint before final welding to control distortion. Then weld, grind, and finish with a wire brush and clear lacquer or a patina. Price the piece by calculating material cost, consumables, labour hours, and a 3050% margin. This is the businessfirst approach: a repeatable design that can be produced in batches for galleries, hotels, and public spaces.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Material identified?</strong> Confirm steel type (mild, stainless, rebar) and thickness before choosing process and settings.</li>
<li><strong>Power supply adequate?</strong> Check generator kVA or mains breaker against Table 2; use an RCD.</li>
<li><strong>Metal clean?</strong> Grind or wirebrush to bright metal within 100 mm of the joint; remove mill scale, paint, oil, and rust.</li>
<li><strong>PPE on?</strong> Autodarkening helmet, leather gloves, apron, closed shoes, and eye protection for grinding.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilation?</strong> Work outdoors or use forced extraction; never weld in a sealed room with shielding gas.</li>
<li><strong>Settings tested?</strong> Run a test bead on scrap of the same material and thickness before welding the sculpture.</li>
<li><strong>Tack first?</strong> Tackweld all joints, check squareness and alignment, then complete welds in a balanced sequence to reduce distortion.</li>
<li><strong>Finish and price?</strong> Clean slag, grind if needed, apply finish, and calculate cost + labour + margin before quoting.</li>
</ol>
