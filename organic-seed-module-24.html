Financial Management - Cash Flow, Profit, and the Numbers That Matter

~15 min read30 min gameAdvanced
Shared phone

🧠 Brain Opener

You can be profitable on paper and still go broke. Most farms that fail are profitable farms that ran out of cash. Understanding cash flow is the difference between a business that survives and one that does not.

  • What is the difference between profit and cash flow?
  • Why do farms run out of cash in the middle of the season?
  • What are the three financial statements every seed producer needs?
🎁 Reveal a strong answer

Profit is revenue minus costs, measured over a period. Cash flow is money in minus money out, measured over time. A farm can be profitable for a season but run out of cash in month 4 because costs come before revenue. The three financial statements are income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement.

📖 Simba Runs Out of Cash

Simba had a good plan. He would plant 1 hectare of organic tomato seed. Cost: 800 dollars. Revenue at harvest: 3,200 dollars. Profit: 2,400 dollars.

He started with 500 dollars in cash. He borrowed 300 from a relative.

He spent 350 dollars on foundation seed and 100 on compost. Then he needed 50 dollars for a watering can. Then 80 dollars for pesticides and spray bottles. Then 60 dollars for fencing wire to keep animals out. Then 40 dollars for casual labour during weeding.

By month 3, he had spent 680 dollars. His tomato plants were flowering. Harvest was still 4 months away.

He needed water. His borehole pump broke. Repair: 120 dollars.

He had 120 dollars in cash. He paid for the repair. Then he had nothing.

Two weeks later, his plants needed water and he could not pay for fuel. Two weeks after that, a neighbour casually watered his field once. It was not enough. Half the crop withered.

Simba harvested 8 kilograms of tomato seed instead of 40. Revenue: 640 dollars. His cost: 800 dollars. Net loss: 160 dollars.

His plan had shown a profit. His reality showed a loss. The difference was cash flow.

His aunt, an accountant, explained it to him over tea.

"Your plan showed profit over the whole season. But cash flow is monthly. You did not have money in month 5 when you needed it. A profitable plan can still fail on cash."

Simba redesigned. The next season, he planted only half the plot. He kept half his land fallow. He produced less, but he had cash reserve. When the pump broke again, he paid for the repair and did not miss a watering.

His revenue dropped to 1,800 dollars. But his costs dropped to 500. And his profit rose to 1,300 dollars - more than the failed season made in revenue.

💡 The Big Idea

Profit is a score. Cash is air.

You can survive a year without profit. You cannot survive a week without cash. Every seed business must be planned around cash flow first, profit second. Know what you will spend, when you will spend it, and when the money will come in. If there is a gap, plan for it before it arrives.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

The three financial statements

Income statement. Revenue minus costs equals profit. Measured over a season or year. Tells you if the business made money.

Balance sheet. Assets minus liabilities equals equity. Measured at a point in time. Tells you what you own and what you owe.

Cash flow statement. Money in minus money out. Measured weekly or monthly. Tells you if you have money to pay the next bill.

Most small farms only track the first. That is why most small farms fail. The third is the one that keeps you alive.

2

How to build a cash flow plan

Draw a table with 12 columns, one per month. Label the rows: opening cash, cash in, cash out, closing cash.

Fill in what you expect each month. Be conservative - revenue later than you think, costs earlier than you think.

Every month where closing cash is negative is a problem. You must either delay a cost, accelerate a revenue, or find bridge financing.

Update the plan every month with real numbers. The plan becomes more accurate over time.

Cash flow planning is not accounting. It is a tool for making decisions. Ask: can I afford to buy that? Can I hire that person? Can I plant one more hectare?

3

The mid-season cash gap

Every seed business has a mid-season cash gap. Inputs are bought at the start. Labour is paid during the season. Harvest revenue arrives at the end. The gap is 3 to 6 months.

Five ways to bridge the gap. First, produce in smaller batches so revenue comes sooner. Second, take a deposit from pre-committed buyers. Third, offer to sell some seed at a discount for early payment. Fourth, keep a cash reserve equal to 3 months of expenses. Fifth, use short-term credit for specific, high-return expenses only.

The most common failure is spending the harvest revenue on personal needs before the next season starts. Separate your business cash from your household cash. Two accounts. Different banks if possible.

🛠 Hands-On - Build Your 12-Month Cash Flow

You need

  • A notebook or a spreadsheet
  • A pen
  • Your last season numbers
  1. Draw a table: 12 columns (months), 4 rows (opening, in, out, closing).
  2. Start with month 1. Write how much cash you have today.
  3. Write expected cash in for each month.
  4. Write expected cash out for each month.
  5. Calculate closing cash for each month.
  6. Mark every month where closing is negative.
  7. For each negative month, write three actions to fix it.
  8. Review the plan with a family member or friend. Adjust.
Why it works: One hour of cash flow planning prevents a season of panic.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Zimbabwe seed cooperative cash flow

A Zimbabwean seed cooperative failed in 2019 not because of poor yields but because of a cash gap. Their input costs fell in month 1-2, labour costs in months 3-5, revenue in months 8-9. By month 5 they had no cash. They fixed this in 2020 by taking deposits from buyers at planting time.

Kenya dairy cash flow model

Kenyan dairy cooperatives pay farmers weekly instead of monthly. Farmers have continuous cash flow and rarely run out. The seed business equivalent is selling in batches throughout the season rather than waiting for one big harvest sale.

🏆 Project Challenge

Run a Cash Flow Test

Build and use a 12-month cash flow plan for one season.

  1. Write your last season costs and revenues by month.
  2. Add 20 percent to costs. Reduce revenues by 20 percent.
  3. This pessimistic plan is more realistic than your optimistic one.
  4. Mark every negative-cash month.
  5. Write three actions to fix each negative month.
  6. Open a separate bank or mobile money account for business.
  7. Move business money only through that account.
  8. Review actual vs plan every month. Adjust.
