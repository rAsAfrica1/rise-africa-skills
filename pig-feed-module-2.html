🐷 Piglet Nutrition (0-8 weeks)

The eight weeks that decide whether a pig is profitable. Covers colostrum and the first day of life, feeding the sow so she actually milks, whether creep feed pays on your farm, what weaning does to a piglet's gut and appetite, how to build and cost a three-phase nursery ration from local ingredients, and the nursery routine that prevents problems without reaching for medicine.

  • Describe what colostrum contains and why the first hours of life cannot be repeated
  • State the published energy, lysine and mineral targets for a lactating sow
  • Describe what creep feed is for and what it realistically achieves before weaning
  • Explain the three simultaneous shocks weaning imposes on a piglet
  • Build a three-phase nursery programme with the correct lysine and energy for each phase
  • Set a written feed budget in kilograms per pig for each nursery phase

Lesson 2.1~12 min

Colostrum and Early Development

In this lesson
  • Describe what colostrum contains and why the first hours of life cannot be repeated
  • Adjust late gestation feeding to raise colostrum volume and quality
  • Plan iron provision for piglets born onto concrete or hard floors

A piglet is born with almost no immune protection of its own and very little energy reserve. Everything it needs in the first day comes from colostrum, the thick first milk the sow produces around farrowing. Colostrum carries maternal immunoglobulins, mainly IgG, and the piglet's gut can only absorb these intact for a short window after birth. Miss that window and no amount of later feeding replaces it.

Colostrum is a different substance from milk. The published composition targets are:

  • Solids: 20 to 25 percent, against 12 to 14 percent for ordinary milk from day 4 to 21
  • Fat: 4 to 8 percent
  • Protein: 5 to 15 percent, of which immunoglobulins are roughly 5 to 10 percent
  • Energy: 1.0 to 1.2 Mcal per kg

Two things drive colostrum quality, and one of them is entirely in your hands. The first is the sow's own immune history: vaccination and prior exposure determine which antibodies she can pass on, and younger gilts of parity 0 to 2 produce colostrum with fewer immunoglobulins than mature multiparous sows. The second is late gestation nutrition. From day 90 to day 112 of pregnancy the mammary gland goes through its main development, and the published daily energy requirement jumps from 5.9 Mcal ME in early and mid gestation to 8.8 Mcal ME in that final stretch, with SID lysine rising from roughly 8 to 10 g a day to 12 to 14 g. Underfeed there and the standards are explicit about the outcome: reduced colostrum volume and quality, and increased piglet mortality.

That jump is the most commonly missed feeding change on smallholdings. Producers feed dry sows a flat ration for the whole pregnancy because it is simple. Simple costs piglets.

Practical management in the first day:

  1. Be present at farrowing if you can. Dry each piglet and make sure it finds a teat within the first hour.
  2. Split-suckle large litters. Shut the biggest half away for an hour so the smallest piglets get uncontested access to colostrum.
  3. Keep the creep area warm. A cold piglet uses its energy reserve keeping warm instead of finding the udder.
  4. Do not move piglets between sows in the first hours if you can avoid it; the colostrum they need is the one their own dam produced against the pathogens in that farrowing house.

Iron is the other early decision. A newborn piglet carries about 40 mg of total body iron and needs roughly 7 mg a day for growth and maintenance. On soil, piglets root and find iron. On concrete or slats they do not, and anaemia follows within days. The published solutions are injectable iron dextran, dosed at 100 mg for piglets weaning at three weeks or under and 150 to 200 mg for later weaning, given intramuscularly on days 1 to 4 and about 95 percent effective; or oral supplementation through a creep tray, for which a published homemade recipe is 88 percent sow lactation ration, 10 percent ferrous sulfate and 2 percent corn oil, offered in shallow trays. The target haemoglobin is above 9 g per 100 ml of blood.

Worked example. A producer in Gulu, Uganda, runs 8 sows farrowing twice a year, averaging 11 born alive, so about 176 piglets a year. She loses 14 percent before weaning, that is 25 piglets. She decides to change two things: lift the late gestation ration from day 90, and inject iron on day 3.

  • Extra late gestation feed: the energy step from 5.9 to 8.8 Mcal ME per day is roughly a 49 percent increase. If she was feeding 2.2 kg a day, she moves to about 3.3 kg for the last 22 days, an extra 1.1 kg x 22 = 24.2 kg per sow per litter
  • Across 16 farrowings a year: 24.2 x 16 = 387 kg of sow feed. At an illustrative 0.40 USD/kg that is 155 USD
  • Iron dextran, illustrative cost 0.12 USD per piglet: 176 x 0.12 = 21 USD
  • Total additional cost: 176 USD a year

If those two changes cut pre-weaning mortality from 14 percent to 10 percent, she saves 7 piglets a year. At an illustrative weaner value of 40 USD that is 280 USD against 176 USD spent, and every surviving piglet also weans heavier. Run this with your own numbers before you commit.

Lesson 2.2~12 min

Lactation Performance in Sows

In this lesson
  • State the published energy, lysine and mineral targets for a lactating sow
  • Calculate a sow's daily feed requirement and identify why intake falls short
  • Cost the difference between a well-fed and an underfed lactation

The lactating sow is the hardest-working animal on your farm. She is producing milk for a litter that may double or triple its birth weight in three weeks, and she cannot physically eat enough to cover it. The published energy partition tells the story: 65 to 80 percent of her total energy goes to milk production, 15 to 25 percent to maintenance, and 5 to 15 percent comes out of her own body, with an expected loss of 50 to 150 pounds of bodyweight over a 21-day lactation. Your job is to keep that mobilisation within the healthy range rather than letting her strip herself down to a frame.

The published targets for a high-producing sow are specific:

  1. Energy density: about 3,362 kcal ME per kg of diet
  2. Target daily feed intake: about 14 pounds, which is 6.4 kg per day, giving roughly 21,500 to 21,700 kcal ME per day
  3. SID lysine: about 60 g per day, which works out anywhere between 0.77 and 1.34 percent of the diet depending on how much she eats
  4. Crude protein: 14 to 16 percent, with digestible protein not below about 14 percent
  5. Calcium 0.85 to 0.95 percent, STTD phosphorus 0.38 to 0.42 percent, with at least 22 g of STTD phosphorus a day
  6. Essential fatty acids: linoleic acid 125 g per day and alpha-linolenic acid 10 g per day, the latter supporting piglet neurological development through colostrum
  7. Crude fibre kept to 3 to 4 percent, because fibre displaces the energy she desperately needs

Note how the lysine specification is written. Because intake varies so much, the sow's requirement is stated per day, not per kilogram of feed. A sow eating 4 kg needs a much richer diet to reach 60 g of lysine than a sow eating 6.4 kg. Work the arithmetic in that direction: divide 60 g by her actual measured intake in kg to get the percentage her ration must carry.

Feeding rules that follow directly from the standards:

  • Feed ad libitum. Remove every restriction during lactation.
  • Sows eating under 14 pounds a day at peak lactation risk inadequate litter growth and increased mastitis, metritis and agalactia.
  • Water must be unlimited; dehydration cuts feed intake, which is the failure mode covered in Module 1 lesson 6.
  • In heat above about 29 degrees Celsius expect a 15 to 30 percent intake drop, and respond by raising energy density to 3,500 or more kcal ME per kg with added fat, plus modest sodium and potassium increases.

Worked example. A producer in Nakuru, Kenya, keeps 12 sows and measures actual lactation intake for a week by weighing what goes in and what is left. Her average sow is eating 4.6 kg a day, not the 6.4 kg target.

  • Lysine she must carry to reach 60 g/day: 60 g / 4.6 kg = 13.0 g per kg, which is 1.30 percent SID lysine, near the top of the published 0.77 to 1.34 percent range
  • Her current lactation ration is 0.95 percent SID lysine, so she is delivering 4.6 x 9.5 = 43.7 g of lysine a day, a shortfall of 16.3 g
  • Energy delivered at 3,362 kcal/kg: 4.6 x 3,362 = 15,465 kcal against a target near 21,500, a deficit of about 6,000 kcal a day

She has two routes. Route one is to fix intake: feed three times a day instead of twice, wet the feed, drop the house temperature with shade and water, fix the drinkers, and take feed out of the trough as soon as it goes stale. Route one is nearly free and should always be tried first. Route two is to enrich the ration, adding 3 percent fat and lifting lysine to 1.30 percent, which on an illustrative basis raises ration cost from 0.44 to 0.52 USD/kg. Over a 21-day lactation at 4.6 kg a day that is 96.6 kg of feed and an extra 96.6 x 0.08 = 7.73 USD per sow, or 93 USD a year across 12 sows farrowing twice.

Against that, a sow whose litter gains poorly weans lighter piglets, and every kilogram of weaning weight is worth roughly 1.50 to 2.00 USD of downstream gain value. If the change adds even 0.7 kg to each of 11 piglets, that is 11 x 0.7 x 1.75 = 13.5 USD per litter. Do both routes.

Lesson 2.3~12 min

Pre-Weaning Creep Feeds

In this lesson
  • Describe what creep feed is for and what it realistically achieves before weaning
  • Calculate whether creep feed pays on your own farm at your own piglet value
  • Specify feed form, particle size and feeder placement for high creep intake

Creep feed is solid feed offered to piglets while they are still suckling, in a protected area the sow cannot reach. It has two purposes, and only one of them is growth. The first is to add a little weight before weaning. The second, and more valuable, is to teach the gut and the piglet that solid feed is food, so that the weaning transition does not become a starvation event.

Be honest about the size of the growth effect. The published figures put creep intake at 0.5 to 2.0 kg per piglet across roughly four weeks to weaning, and that range is enormous because it depends on sow milk quality, feed form and house temperature. The growth response is a modest 10 to 20 g per day of extra pre-weaning gain, and only where creep intake reaches 0.8 kg per piglet or more. The larger prize is the transition: piglets that ate creep show 5 to 15 percent lower post-weaning diarrhoea incidence than piglets that did not.

Specification matters more than brand. The published guidance:

  1. Target SID lysine 1.30 to 1.50 percent, extrapolated from early nursery phase 1, because no separate creep tables exist in the standard references.
  2. Amino acid ratios as a percentage of lysine: methionine 28 to 32, methionine plus cysteine 56 to 62, threonine 62 to 65, tryptophan 18 to 20, valine 65 to 75, isoleucine 60 to 70, leucine 85 to 100, histidine 30 to 35.
  3. Feed form: pellets of 3 to 4 mm are preferred for initial creep because they promote consumption and cut waste. Crumble is an acceptable intermediate. Meal is the weakest option for palatability and intake, though it is about 10 percent cheaper.
  4. Pellet quality: durability index above 80 percent and fines below 20 percent at the feeder. Fines are what pigs refuse and what you sweep out.
  5. Pelleting temperature below 170 degrees Fahrenheit, to avoid scorching ingredients and heat damage to proteins.
  6. Fat at 3 to 4 percent, which protects nutrients during pelleting and improves palatability.

Feeding practice that actually raises intake: offer small amounts several times a day rather than filling a hopper once; site the tray where piglets already lie, close to the heat source and away from the dunging corner; remove and discard anything not eaten within a few hours because stale feed teaches piglets that feed tastes bad; and use a shallow tray, not a deep trough. If you are also using the homemade oral iron mixture from lesson 1, the same shallow tray serves both purposes.

Worked example. A producer in Bamenda, Cameroon, weans 190 piglets a year at 28 days and is deciding whether to start creep feeding.

  • Creep feed price, published range 0.50 to 0.80 USD/kg. His local agro-dealer quotes 0.72 USD/kg.
  • Realistic intake on a well-managed unit: assume 0.9 kg per piglet, which is above the 0.8 kg threshold where the growth response appears
  • Cost per piglet: 0.9 x 0.72 = 0.65 USD. Across 190 piglets: 123 USD a year
  • Growth response: 15 g/day extra over the last 18 days of a 28-day lactation = 0.27 kg extra weaning weight
  • Value of extra weaning weight: the standards value each additional kilogram at weaning at roughly 1.50 to 2.00 USD of downstream gain. At 1.75 USD: 0.27 x 1.75 = 0.47 USD per piglet
  • Diarrhoea benefit: if creep cuts post-weaning scour incidence by 10 percentage points, and he currently treats 30 piglets a year at an illustrative 1.40 USD of medicine and labour each, he avoids about 19 treatments, saving 27 USD, plus the growth those pigs lose

Growth value alone, 0.47 against 0.65, does not pay. Add the scour benefit and the reduced weaning check and it becomes roughly break-even to modestly positive. The published decision factors say the same: creep pays best where sow milk quality is poor, where genetics have high growth potential, where weaning is later at 28 to 32 days, and where creep intake is genuinely high. If your piglets barely touch the tray, stop buying creep and spend the money on the lactation ration instead, because that is where the milk comes from.

Weaning and Post-Weaning Stress

In this lesson
  • Explain the three simultaneous shocks weaning imposes on a piglet
  • Sequence fibre type through the first three weeks after weaning
  • Set vitamin E and vitamin D strategy for the post-weaning period

Weaning is the most violent thing that happens to a pig. In a single day the piglet loses its milk supply, loses its mother, and is usually mixed with strangers in a new building. Feed intake collapses, the gut lining shortens, and the bacteria that were held in check by milk and by a full gut start to multiply. Almost every nursery problem you will ever have traces back to this one event.

Three shocks arrive together:

  1. Nutritional. Highly digestible warm liquid milk is replaced with dry solid feed the piglet may never have eaten. Intake often falls to near zero for a day or two, and a gut with nothing in it loses villus height, which is exactly the absorptive surface the piglet needs to recover.
  2. Immunological. Maternal antibody protection from colostrum and milk fades just as exposure to new pathogens rises. Weaning is usually timed right in that gap.
  3. Social and environmental. Mixing, transport, noise, a colder or draughtier pen and a new water system all add stress on top.

The physiological consequences are measurable. The reference standards note that young pigs show reduced blood 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 status after weaning despite adequate dietary D3, because stress interferes with absorption and metabolism. They also set a stress level for vitamin E of 44 IU per kg and above, against a standard growing-pig level of 11 to 16 IU per kg, with research using 100 to 200 IU per kg, and note that vitamin E only delivers where selenium is adequate through the glutathione peroxidase system. So the nursery diet is not simply a smaller grower diet; it is a diet formulated for an animal under stress.

Fibre is the most useful and least expensive lever you have, and the published strategy is sequenced rather than fixed:

  • Days 0 to 7 after weaning: insoluble fibre at 2 to 3 percent is preferred. It speeds passage through the gut, which limits the time available for pathogenic bacteria to colonise and produce toxins.
  • Days 7 to 14: a roughly 50-50 mix of soluble and insoluble fibre, allowing the microbiota to adapt.
  • After day 14: increase soluble fibres, which ferment to volatile fatty acids including butyrate that feed the cells lining the gut and support tight junction development.

Note the trade-off. Insoluble fibre reduces nutrient digestibility in pigs under 10 kg because their enzyme systems are immature, and may depress growth if it goes above 5 percent in weaner diets. Soluble fibre increases digesta viscosity and can slow passage, which in some trials made scour worse. This is why the sequence matters more than any single number.

Management that reduces the shock, in order of cost:

  • Wean in groups by size, not by whim, so small piglets are not competing with large ones
  • Keep the nursery pen warmer than the finishing house and free of draughts
  • Feed the same diet for the first days that they met as creep, so the taste and form are familiar
  • Offer feed as a wet gruel two or three times a day for the first 48 hours to bridge the gap from liquid to dry
  • Give feed little and often, and throw away anything stale
  • Check every drinker on the day of weaning, not the week after

Worked example. A producer in Tamale, Ghana, weans 40 piglets a month at 28 days and loses on average 3 to post-weaning scour and setbacks. He costs the low-tech interventions for one month's group:

  • Gruel feeding for two days: no extra feed cost, about 30 minutes a day of labour
  • Raising vitamin E in the nursery premix from 16 to 44 IU/kg: at an illustrative 2.00 USD extra per tonne of feed, and 40 piglets eating 10 kg each in phase 1, that is 400 kg, so 0.80 USD for the whole group
  • Adding 2.5 percent milled millet hulls as an insoluble fibre source in the first week: displaces a similar weight of maize, roughly cost-neutral
  • Total cost: under 1 USD plus labour

If those changes save even one piglet a month at an illustrative 40 USD, that is 480 USD a year for less than 12 USD of inputs. Weaning management is the highest-return work on a pig farm precisely because the interventions are so cheap.

Lesson 2.5~12 min

Post-Weaning Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Build a three-phase nursery programme with the correct lysine and energy for each phase
  • Select specialty nursery ingredients and judge whether their cost is justified
  • Calculate the cost per piglet of moving from a single nursery diet to phase feeding

Nursery feed is the most expensive feed on the farm per kilogram and the cheapest per kilogram of gain, because feed conversion is at its lifetime best in this window. The published approach is phase feeding: three diets that step down in specification as the piglet's gut matures, rather than one compromise diet.

The published three-phase programme:

  1. Phase 1, early nursery, 5 to 7.5 kg, lasting 2 to 3 weeks. SID lysine 1.30 to 1.40 percent, energy about 3,350 kcal ME/kg. High lactose around 8 percent, spray-dried porcine plasma around 5 percent, zinc oxide at pharmacological level.
  2. Phase 2, mid nursery, 7.5 to 11.5 kg, lasting 2 to 3 weeks. SID lysine 1.20 to 1.30 percent, energy about 3,300 kcal ME/kg. Lactose reduced to around 5 percent, plasma around 3 percent, zinc oxide tapering.
  3. Phase 3, late nursery, 11.5 to 23 kg, lasting 2 to 4 weeks. SID lysine 1.08 to 1.15 percent, energy about 3,200 kcal ME/kg. Minimal lactose, specialty proteins reduced, moving toward the grower formulation and phasing zinc oxide out.

Calcium and phosphorus for the 5 to 11 kg weaner are total calcium 0.85 percent, total phosphorus 0.70 percent, STTD phosphorus 0.45 to 0.63 percent, at a ratio near 1.2 to 1. Vitamin A is 2,000 to 2,200 IU/kg, vitamin E 30 to 44 IU/kg, choline 0.60 g/kg, iron 80 to 100 mg/kg, zinc baseline 100 ppm and copper 15 to 20 ppm on typical nursery specifications.

The specialty ingredients are where nursery budgets are won or lost. The published profiles:

  • Spray-dried porcine plasma: over 85 percent standardised digestibility with useful immunoglobulin content, included at 3 to 5 percent, at a cost premium of 0.80 to 1.20 USD per kg
  • Fish meal: 88 to 92 percent protein digestibility, omega-3 fatty acids, excellent palatability, 3 to 5 percent inclusion, with a fishy odour in waste
  • Dried whey: 85 percent lactose plus protein with a prebiotic effect, 5 to 8 percent typical
  • Soy protein concentrate: reduced allergenicity compared with ordinary soybean meal, 3 to 8 percent, with a cost advantage over plasma
  • Enzymatically treated soybean meal: reduced oligosaccharides and better digestibility, at roughly 95 percent of ordinary soybean meal cost

Lactose deserves a note for African producers. It is the expensive part of a phase 1 diet and often the hardest to source. The published inclusion is 5 to 10 percent in creep and early nursery, reduced through phases 2 and 3 as intestinal maltase and isomaltase develop. Where dried whey is unavailable or absurdly priced, soy protein concentrate and good fish meal carry more of the load, and the phase 1 period is kept short.

Worked example. A producer in Arusha, Tanzania, currently feeds one nursery diet at 1.15 percent SID lysine from weaning to 25 kg. He weans 300 piglets a year. He compares that with a three-phase programme.

Single diet, illustrative cost 0.55 USD/kg. Feed to take a piglet from 6 to 25 kg at an FCR of 1.7: 19 kg gain x 1.7 = 32.3 kg. Cost per piglet 32.3 x 0.55 = 17.77 USD.

Three-phase programme:

  • Phase 1, 6 to 7.5 kg, 1.5 kg gain at FCR 1.3 = 2.0 kg of feed at an illustrative 0.95 USD/kg = 1.90 USD
  • Phase 2, 7.5 to 11.5 kg, 4 kg gain at FCR 1.5 = 6.0 kg at 0.68 USD/kg = 4.08 USD
  • Phase 3, 11.5 to 25 kg, 13.5 kg gain at FCR 1.8 = 24.3 kg at 0.48 USD/kg = 11.66 USD
  • Total feed: 32.3 kg. Total cost: 1.90 + 4.08 + 11.66 = 17.64 USD per piglet

Almost identical cost for the same feed weight, but the expensive specification is now delivered only where it is needed and the phase 3 diet is far cheaper per kilogram. In practice the phased pigs also convert better in phase 1 and 2, so the real comparison favours phasing. The reason so many producers avoid it is the extra mixing and bin space, not the money. Substitute your own prices and check.

Lesson 2.6~12 min

Health Management Through Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Set a written feed budget in kilograms per pig for each nursery phase
  • Apply feed hygiene and feeder management rules that prevent nursery setbacks
  • Use simple nursery records to decide when to change feed rather than reach for medicine

This lesson is about the nursery routine, not about disease treatment. Module 9 covers diagnosis, gut-health additives, zinc oxide and copper, and mycotoxin control in detail, and Module 10 covers antibiotic-free production. What follows is the layer underneath all of that: the feeding discipline that keeps nursery pigs out of trouble in the first place.

Start with the feed budget. Phase feeding fails on most small farms not because the formulas are wrong but because pigs are moved between diets by guesswork. A feed budget fixes that by specifying kilograms per pig, not days. Using the published phase weights, a workable budget is:

  1. Phase 1, from weaning to 7.5 kg: roughly 2 kg of phase 1 feed per pig
  2. Phase 2, 7.5 to 11.5 kg: roughly 6 kg of phase 2 feed per pig
  3. Phase 3, 11.5 to 23 kg: roughly 20 to 25 kg of phase 3 feed per pig

Multiply by the number of pigs in the group, weigh out that quantity, and move to the next diet when the budgeted feed is gone. This means a slow group stays on the richer diet longer, which is exactly what it needs, and a fast group moves on sooner, which saves money. It also stops the common and expensive error of feeding phase 1 specification to 20 kg pigs.

Feeder and hygiene rules that prevent nursery setbacks:

  • Adjust feeders so a thin, continuous layer of feed is visible but the pan is not flooded. Feed on the floor of a nursery pen becomes dung-contaminated within hours.
  • Empty, scrape and dry feeders between groups. Old feed in the corners of a feeder goes rancid, and fat that has gone rancid destroys vitamin activity.
  • Never store nursery feed for more than a few weeks. High-fat, high-milk-product diets spoil far faster than grower feed.
  • Store bags off the floor on pallets, away from walls, in a dry room. Damp grain is where the mould toxins covered in Module 9 lesson 5 begin.
  • Wash and disinfect nursery pens between groups and let them dry completely. All-in, all-out beats continuous flow every time.
  • Flush drinker lines before a new group arrives, because water sitting in warm pipes builds biofilm.

Use appetite as your instrument. Feed refusal is the earliest signal you have of a problem, and it is often a feed problem rather than an infection. Deoxynivalenol begins cutting voluntary feed intake at 1 to 3 parts per million, well below the 5 ppm treated as the swine maximum. For young pigs the aflatoxin limit is 20 parts per billion, the same as for breeding animals, and much stricter than the 200 ppb allowed for finishers over about 45 kg, precisely because young pigs are more susceptible. If a nursery group stops eating and the pigs are otherwise bright, look at the feed bin before you look for a syringe.

Keep four numbers per group and nothing more: pigs in, pigs out, total feed used, and days. From those you get mortality, average daily gain and feed conversion, and those three tell you whether last month's change worked.

Worked example. A producer in Kaduna, Nigeria, runs nursery groups of 45 piglets. He introduces feed budgeting and all-in, all-out cleaning.

  • Phase 1 budget: 45 x 2 kg = 90 kg. He weighs out exactly 90 kg and does not open the next bag until it is gone.
  • Phase 2 budget: 45 x 6 = 270 kg. Phase 3 budget: 45 x 22 = 990 kg.
  • Previously he fed phase 2 feed until roughly 20 kg by eye. Estimated over-feeding of the dearer diet: about 250 kg per group at an illustrative price difference of 0.20 USD/kg = 50 USD per group
  • With 8 groups a year, that is 400 USD saved with no change to any formula
  • Cleaning cost per group: disinfectant and labour at an illustrative 9 USD, so 72 USD a year

He also finds that in his first budgeted group, phase 1 feed lasted 11 days rather than the 7 he assumed, which told him his weaning weights were lower than he believed and sent him back to the lactation ration in lesson 2. That is the real value of a written budget: it turns a vague feeling into a number you can act on.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Why can colostrum not be replaced by feeding milk or milk replacer a day later?

Absorption of intact immunoglobulins closes within hours of birth. After that, colostrum given later provides nutrition but not the same systemic immune transfer.

2. What happens to the sow's daily energy requirement between day 90 and day 112 of gestation?

Mammary development peaks in late gestation, lifting the published requirement from 5.9 to 8.8 Mcal ME per day.

3. Which sows produce colostrum with the lowest immunoglobulin content?

Younger animals have had less immune exposure, so gilts and parity 1 to 2 sows produce less immunoglobulin-rich colostrum than mature sows.

4. What is the target haemoglobin level for suckling piglets?

Levels below 9 g per 100 ml indicate borderline anaemia, at which growth is already being lost.

5. In the published homemade oral iron creep recipe, what makes up the bulk of the mixture?

The recipe is 88 percent sow lactation ration, 10 percent ferrous sulfate and 2 percent corn oil, fed in shallow trays inside the creep area.

6. What share of a lactating sow's total energy goes into milk production?

Milk production takes 65 to 80 percent of total energy, with maintenance at 15 to 25 percent and the balance mobilised from body reserves.

7. A sow eats only 4.6 kg a day. What SID lysine percentage must her ration carry to deliver 60 g of lysine daily?

60 g divided by 4.6 kg is 13.0 g per kg, which is 1.30 percent of the diet, near the top of the published 0.77 to 1.34 percent range.

8. Why is crude fibre held to only 3 to 4 percent in lactation diets when gestating sows tolerate 6 to 8 percent?

In gestation fibre usefully provides satiety. In lactation the sow is already in energy deficit, so bulk that dilutes energy directly reduces milk output.

9. What is the correct feeding rule for a sow during lactation?

The standards call for ad libitum feeding in lactation. Restriction at this stage limits milk yield and litter growth.

10. Which two essential fatty acid targets are published for lactating sows?

Linoleic acid at 125 g per day supports milk fat and immune function, and alpha-linolenic acid at 10 g per day supports piglet neurological development via colostrum.

11. Above what level of creep intake does the published pre-weaning growth response appear?

The published response of plus 10 to 20 g per day of pre-weaning gain is tied to creep intake of at least 0.8 kg per piglet.

12. What is the main benefit of creep feeding, beyond extra weaning weight?

Piglets that have already learned to eat solid feed transition through weaning with less gut disruption and less scour.

13. Which feed form is preferred for initial creep feed?

Pellets of 3 to 4 mm give the best palatability and intake with the least waste, with crumble acceptable as an intermediate stage.

14. What is the published upper limit for fines at the creep feeder?

Pellet quality guidance is above 80 percent durability index and below 20 percent fines at the feeder, because fines are refused and wasted.

15. In which situation is creep feeding least likely to pay?

Creep feed only earns its cost if it is eaten. Where intake is minimal, the same money is better spent improving the lactation ration.

16. Which fibre type is preferred in the first week after weaning?

Insoluble fibre at 2 to 3 percent accelerates digesta passage in days 0 to 7, limiting the time available for pathogen colonisation.

17. Why does a weaned piglet that stops eating lose absorptive capacity?

Feed in the gut maintains the villi. A feed intake collapse shortens villi, which then makes recovery of intake harder, creating a downward spiral.

18. What happens to blood 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 status after weaning?

Stress interferes with absorption and metabolism, so young pigs show reduced 25(OH)D3 status post-weaning despite adequate dietary vitamin D3.

19. When should soluble fibre be increased in the nursery programme?

After about day 14 the microbiota has adapted, and soluble fibre then promotes volatile fatty acid production that supports the gut lining.

20. Which weaning-day action costs almost nothing and directly protects intake?

Water restriction cuts feed intake immediately, and weaning day is when a blocked or unfamiliar drinker does the most damage.

21. What is the published SID lysine specification for nursery phase 1 at 5 to 7.5 kg?

Phase 1 carries the highest lysine specification of any diet on the farm, at 1.30 to 1.40 percent SID lysine.

22. Why is dietary lactose reduced across the nursery phases?

The piglet starts able to digest milk sugar. As starch-digesting enzyme capacity develops, expensive lactose can be replaced by cereal starch.

23. Which nursery ingredient is described as having reduced allergenicity compared with ordinary soybean meal?

Soy protein concentrate has reduced allergenicity and is included at 3 to 8 percent, offering a cost advantage over spray-dried plasma.

24. What is the approximate protein digestibility of good quality fish meal in nursery diets?

Fish meal is published at 88 to 92 percent protein digestibility, with excellent palatability and useful omega-3 content.

25. What is the main practical barrier to phase feeding on a small farm?

Worked at real prices the total feed cost is similar or better, because expensive specifications go only where needed. The obstacle is labour and storage, not money.

26. Why is a nursery feed budget written in kilograms per pig rather than days on feed?

Phase specifications are tied to bodyweight. Budgeting by weight of feed consumed tracks growth automatically, while a fixed number of days does not.

27. What is the aflatoxin limit for young growing pigs?

Young pigs and breeding animals are held to 20 ppb, while finishers over about 45 kg are allowed 200 ppb because of better metabolic clearance.

28. Why should nursery feed not be stored for long periods?

Nursery diets are rich in fat and dairy products, which go rancid quickly, degrading both palatability and vitamin content.

29. A nursery group suddenly goes off feed but the pigs appear bright and alert. What should you check first?

Feed refusal with otherwise normal pigs points to the feed itself, and mould toxins reduce intake well before any clinical sign appears.

30. Which four records give you mortality, daily gain and feed conversion for a nursery group?

Those four figures are enough to calculate all three key performance measures, which is why the record sheet should stay short enough to actually be filled in.
