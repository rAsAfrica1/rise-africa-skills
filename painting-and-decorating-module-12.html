1. Surface Preparation in Tropical and Arid Climates

Welcome to Module 12, Lesson 1. In Africa, the success of any painting or decorating job is decided long before the first coat of paint is opened. Surface preparation is where you win or lose the contract, protect your reputation, and avoid costly callbacks. This lesson gives you a practical, business-first approach to preparing surfaces in the varied climates you will meet from Lagos to Nairobi, Accra to Kigali, Cairo to Cape Town. First, understand your enemy. In humid coastal cities such as Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, and Douala, moisture is constant. In arid regions such as Windhoek, Khartoum, and the Sahel, dust, heat, and intense sun dominate. In highland areas such as Addis Ababa, Nairobi, and Kigali, cool nights and warm days create movement in substrates. Each condition demands a different preparation routine. Start with a proper site assessment. Walk the building with the client or main contractor. Note the substrate: cement plaster, concrete, wood, metal, or previously painted surfaces. Check for cracks, efflorescence, mould, rust, and flaking. Use a moisture meter if available. In coastal areas, moisture content above 12 to 15 percent means you must wait or apply a breathable system. In dry areas, check for dust and loose sand that will prevent adhesion. Your quote must reflect the true preparation cost. Many African painters lose money because they price only the paint and labour, not the scraping, sanding, filling, and priming. A good rule is that preparation is 60 to 70 percent of the total job time. Price it that way. For new cement plaster, the surface must cure for at least 21 to 28 days in hot dry conditions, and longer in humid conditions. Do not rush this. Alkali in fresh cement will burn through cheap paint and cause blistering. Test with a pH strip or a simple vinegar drop. If it fizzes, the surface is still too alkaline. Wash the wall with clean water to remove salts and dust. In coastal areas, efflorescence is common. Brush off the white powder, then wash with a dilute acid solution only if the client approves and you have protective equipment. Rinse thoroughly and allow to dry. For previously painted surfaces, scrape all loose and flaking paint with a putty knife or scraper. Sand the edges smooth so the new paint blends. In humid regions, check for mould. Treat with a fungicidal wash, not just bleach, because bleach leaves a food source for new mould. Allow to dry completely. In dusty regions, dust is the biggest enemy. A dusty wall will reject paint. Wash with water and a stiff brush, then rinse. If water is scarce, use a damp cloth and compressed air, but never dry brush dust into the air and leave it on the surface. For wood surfaces, especially in areas with termites and borers, treat with an insecticidal primer or wood preservative. Sand with the grain, fill cracks with a flexible filler, and prime all knots with a knotting solution. In hot dry areas, wood expands and contracts. Use flexible fillers, not hard cement-based fillers, to avoid cracking. For metal surfaces, remove rust by wire brushing, sanding, or using a rust converter. In coastal areas, rust returns fast. Apply a zinc-rich primer or a red oxide primer within hours of cleaning. Do not leave bare metal overnight in humid conditions. For concrete floors and walls, check for laitance, a weak cement layer that must be removed by grinding or acid etching. In many African markets, you can hire a grinding machine or use a wire brush and muriatic acid with proper safety gear. Always neutralise acid with a baking soda solution and rinse. Safety is part of preparation. In Africa, heat, dust, and chemicals demand respect. Wear a dust mask, goggles, gloves, and boots. Provide clean water for washing. On site, keep a first aid kit. If you employ workers, train them on chemical safety and provide protective equipment. This protects lives and reduces your liability. Business tip: offer surface preparation as a separate line item in your quotation. Explain to clients that skipping preparation means repainting in one or two years instead of five to seven. Show them photos of failed jobs caused by poor preparation. This builds trust and justifies your price. Also, consider offering a preparation-only service for clients who want to paint themselves. This creates income even when you do not supply paint. Finally, document everything. Take before and after photos. Note the products used, the date, and the weather conditions. This record protects you if a client complains later. In Africa, word of mouth is powerful. A well-prepared surface leads to a durable finish, happy clients, and repeat business. Remember, preparation is not the boring part. It is the professional part. Master it, and you will stand out in a crowded market.

2. Choosing and Mixing Paints for African Conditions

Welcome to Lesson 2. In this lesson, we focus on choosing and mixing paints that perform in African conditions. The wrong paint choice wastes money, fails early, and damages your reputation. The right choice gives you a durable finish, happy clients, and referrals. Start with the basics. Paints are made of binder, pigment, solvent, and additives. In Africa, the binder and pigment quality matter most because of intense ultraviolet light, heat, and moisture. Cheap paints use less binder and more filler. They look good for a few months, then chalk, fade, or peel. As a professional, you must know the difference and explain it to clients. For exterior walls in hot, sunny regions such as Botswana, Namibia, and northern Nigeria, choose acrylic or styrene acrylic paints with UV stabilisers. These resist fading and chalking. In humid coastal areas such as Mombasa, Lagos, and Libreville, choose breathable acrylic paints that allow moisture to escape. Avoid oil-based exterior paints in humid areas because they trap moisture and blister. For interior walls, emulsion paints are common. Vinyl matt is affordable and good for low-traffic areas. Vinyl silk or acrylic eggshell is washable and better for corridors, kitchens, and clinics. In dusty areas, choose a washable finish so walls can be cleaned without damage. For wood, use alkyd or acrylic enamel. In hot dry areas, acrylic enamel is more flexible and resists cracking. In humid areas, alkyd enamel gives a hard finish but needs good ventilation during application. For metal, use direct-to-metal acrylics or alkyd enamels with rust inhibitors. In coastal areas, a two-coat system with a zinc-rich primer is best. For floors, use two-pack epoxy or polyurethane coatings. These are expensive but last for years in garages, workshops, and hospitals. Now, mixing. Never mix different brands or types unless the manufacturer approves. Chemical incompatibility causes lumps, poor drying, and failure. When mixing two-pack products, measure accurately. Use a scale or measuring cups. Do not guess. In African markets, some painters add kerosene, diesel, or water to stretch paint. This is unprofessional and ruins the finish. If a client pressures you to reduce cost, offer a lower-grade but correct product instead of adulterating a good one. For water-based paints, add clean water only if the manufacturer allows, and never more than 10 percent. For solvent-based paints, use the recommended thinner. In hot weather, paint dries fast. Add a retarder if the manufacturer provides one, or work in the early morning and late afternoon. In cold highland areas, paint dries slowly. Allow more time between coats. Stir paint thoroughly before use. Pigment settles at the bottom. Use a flat stirrer, not a stick that splashes. For small quantities, use a drill mixer on low speed. Do not shake cans violently because it introduces air bubbles. Strain paint through a mesh bag or nylon stocking to remove skins and lumps. This is especially important when using leftover paint. Colour mixing is a business opportunity. Many clients want custom colours. Learn to use tinting machines or universal colorants. Start with a white base and add colorant slowly. Record the formula so you can repeat it. In Africa, colour trends vary. In some markets, earthy tones such as terracotta, ochre, and olive are popular. In others, bright colours such as turquoise, yellow, and coral are preferred. Offer a colour consultation service. Bring a fan deck and show how colours look in different lighting. Remember that colour looks different in bright sun, shade, and artificial light. Paint a sample board and let it dry before the client decides. This avoids disputes. Quantity estimation is critical. Measure the area, then subtract doors and windows. One litre of paint covers about 8 to 12 square metres per coat, depending on the surface and product. Rough surfaces need more paint. Always buy 10 percent extra for touch-ups. For large projects, buy in 20-litre drums to save money. Store paint correctly. Keep cans sealed, upside down for solvent-based paints to prevent skin formation. Store in a cool, shaded place. In hot climates, do not store paint in direct sun or in metal containers that heat up. Rotate stock. Use older paint first. If paint has skin, remove it and strain the rest. If it smells sour or has lumps that do not mix, discard it. Never use spoiled paint on a client's property. Health and safety matter. Paints contain volatile organic compounds. Work in ventilated spaces. Wear a mask when spraying or using solvents. Keep paint away from children and animals. Dispose of empty cans and leftover paint responsibly. Do not pour paint into drains or rivers. In many African cities, waste collection is limited, so arrange proper disposal. Business tip: build relationships with reputable paint suppliers. Ask for technical data sheets and safety data sheets. Negotiate trade discounts. Offer clients a choice of good, better, and best paint systems. Explain the warranty differences. A five-year warranty versus a two-year warranty justifies a higher price. Also, consider becoming a certified applicator for a major brand. This gives you training, marketing support, and access to leads. Finally, keep learning. Paint technology changes. New products for heat reflection, anti-mould, and anti-graffiti are entering African markets. Attend supplier workshops and read technical bulletins. The painter who knows products wins the job. The painter who guesses loses money. Choose and mix paints like a professional, and your work will speak for itself.

3. Application Techniques for Walls, Wood, and Metal

Welcome to Lesson 3. This lesson covers practical application techniques for walls, wood, and metal in African conditions. Good technique separates a professional from an amateur. It affects coverage, finish, durability, and speed. Start with tools. For walls, you need brushes, rollers, and sprayers. A good quality brush holds paint and leaves few marks. For cutting in edges, use a 50mm or 75mm angled sash brush. For large areas, use a roller with a suitable nap. A short nap is for smooth surfaces, a medium nap for light texture, and a long nap for rough surfaces. In dusty areas, use a roller with a sealed end to keep dust out. For sprayers, airless is best for large projects. It is fast and gives a uniform finish. But it requires masking, ventilation, and skill. In many African markets, airless sprayers are available for hire. If you use one, practise on a sample board first. For wood, use a brush with natural bristles for oil-based paints and synthetic bristles for water-based paints. For metal, use a brush or roller that resists solvents. Now, walls. Start with the ceiling, then walls, then trim. Work in sections. Cut in edges first with a brush, then roll the field. Maintain a wet edge to avoid lap marks. In hot weather, paint dries fast, so work quickly and in smaller sections. Do not paint in direct sun on exterior walls because the surface is too hot. Paint the shaded side first. For exterior walls, apply two coats. The first coat can be thinned slightly if the manufacturer allows, to penetrate the surface. The second coat is full strength. For interior walls, apply one primer coat and two finish coats. In humid areas, allow extra drying time between coats. If the first coat is still tacky, do not apply the second. For textured surfaces, use a roller with a long nap and work the paint into the texture. Do not over-roll because it leaves marks. For smooth surfaces, use a short nap roller and apply light pressure. For corners and edges, use a brush and blend with the roller while the paint is wet. For wood, preparation is key. Sand with the grain, remove dust, and prime. Apply paint in the direction of the grain. Use thin coats, not thick ones. Thick coats wrinkle and crack. For doors, remove them if possible and paint horizontally. Paint the edges first, then the panels, then the stiles and rails. For windows, mask the glass or use a paint shield. Do not paint moving parts. For metal, apply primer immediately after cleaning. Use thin coats. For railings and gates, paint from top to bottom to avoid drips. For corrugated metal roofs, use a roller or sprayer. Be careful of heat and slip hazards. For structural steel, follow the specification. Often you need a primer, an intermediate coat, and a topcoat. Now, special techniques. In Africa, decorative finishes are popular. These include sponging, ragging, stippling, and colour washing. These can earn you more money than plain painting. Learn them. Practise on boards. Offer them as premium services. Another technique is stencilling. You can cut stencils from plastic sheets and apply patterns to walls. This is popular in homes, hotels, and restaurants. Also, consider textured finishes such as tyrolean, skimming, and Venetian plaster. These require skill and special materials. If you master them, you can charge high prices. For exterior walls in hot climates, consider heat-reflective paints. These reduce indoor temperatures and save energy. They are more expensive but clients recover the cost through lower air conditioning bills. Explain this to clients. For roofs, use reflective coatings. For walls, use light colours. Now, quality control. Check your work in good light. Look for runs, sags, missed spots, and uneven sheen. Fix defects immediately. Do not leave them for the client to find. Use a checklist. Check edges, corners, behind doors, under windows, and around pipes. Clean up as you go. Remove masking tape while the paint is still slightly wet for a clean edge. For solvent-based paints, remove tape after the paint skins over. For water-based paints, remove tape before the paint dries completely. Protect floors and furniture with drop sheets. In dusty areas, dampen the floor to keep dust down. In humid areas, use dehumidifiers if available, or schedule work for drier days. Safety again. Use ladders correctly. Do not overreach. Use scaffolding for high walls. In Africa, many accidents happen from falls. Secure your ladder. Wear non-slip shoes. Use a harness when working at height. Keep the site tidy. Store tools safely. Now, productivity. Time is money. Organise your team. One person cuts in, another rolls, another prepares. Use the right tools. A good roller covers more area than a brush. A sprayer covers more than a roller. But do not sacrifice quality for speed. Train your team. Teach them to load a brush correctly, to roll without splashing, and to clean tools properly. Clean tools last longer. At the end of the day, wash brushes and rollers with the correct solvent. For water-based paints, use warm soapy water. For oil-based paints, use the recommended thinner. Do not leave brushes standing in paint or solvent. Business tip: offer a maintenance package. After a project, offer to return in six or twelve months to inspect and touch up. This creates recurring income and keeps you in contact with the client. Also, take photos of your work. Use them in your portfolio and on social media. In Africa, WhatsApp and Facebook are powerful marketing tools. Post before and after photos. Ask satisfied clients for referrals. A referral is cheaper than advertising. Finally, remember that application is a craft. The more you practise, the better you become. Watch videos, attend workshops, and learn from experienced painters. Do not be afraid to ask questions. The best painters never stop learning. Apply your skills with pride, and your work will open doors to bigger and better projects.

4. Health, Safety, and Environmental Responsibility on Site

Welcome to Lesson 4. This lesson covers health, safety, and environmental responsibility on painting and decorating sites in Africa. These are not optional extras. They protect you, your workers, your clients, and the environment. They also protect your business from lawsuits, fines, and lost contracts. Start with the law. Many African countries have occupational health and safety laws. In South Africa, the Occupational Health and Safety Act applies. In Kenya, the Occupational Safety and Health Act applies. In Nigeria, the Factories Act and other regulations apply. Even if enforcement is weak, you should follow the law. It is the right thing to do, and it makes your business more professional. Also, many international clients and NGOs require proof of safety compliance. If you want those contracts, you need a safety record. Begin with a risk assessment. Before starting work, walk the site and identify hazards. These include working at height, electrical wires, sharp edges, dust, chemicals, heat, and traffic. Write down the risks and the controls. Share this with your team. Review it daily. For working at height, use proper ladders and scaffolding. Do not use chairs, buckets, or makeshift platforms. Secure ladders at the top and bottom. Use a harness when working above two metres. In Africa, falls are a leading cause of construction deaths. Do not become a statistic. For electrical safety, stay away from power lines. If you must work near them, ask the utility company to isolate the power. Use non-conductive ladders near electricity. Do not use metal ladders near wires. For dust, use dust masks. In dry areas, dust is everywhere. Wet surfaces before sanding. Use sanders with dust extraction if available. For chemical safety, read the safety data sheets. Wear gloves, goggles, and masks. Do not eat, drink, or smoke while handling paint. Wash hands after work. Store chemicals in labelled containers. Never put paint or solvent in a drink bottle. This has caused poisoning deaths in many countries. For heat stress, drink water often. Take breaks in the shade. Schedule heavy work for cooler hours. In hot regions such as the Sahel and Kalahari, heat exhaustion is real. Watch for dizziness, headache, and nausea. Move the person to a cool place and give water. For fire safety, keep solvents away from flames. Do not smoke near paint. Store oily rags in a sealed metal container because they can self-ignite. Keep a fire extinguisher on site. Know how to use it. For first aid, keep a kit with bandages, antiseptic, eye wash, and gloves. Train at least one person in basic first aid. Keep emergency numbers posted. Now, environmental responsibility. Paint and solvents are hazardous waste. Do not pour them down drains, into rivers, or on the ground. In many African cities, there are no special collection points. You may need to store waste and take it to a licensed facility. Check with local authorities. For leftover paint, try to use it on the same project or donate it to community projects. Do not mix different paints together. For empty cans, crush them and recycle if possible. In some countries, scrap metal dealers buy cans. For brushes and rollers, clean them in a bucket, not under a running tap. Let the solids settle, then dispose of the sludge properly. Reuse the solvent after filtering. For dust and debris, collect it and dispose of it responsibly. Do not sweep it into the street. In many African communities, street drains block easily. Blocked drains cause flooding and disease. Be a good neighbour. Also, consider the environmental impact of your products. Choose low-VOC paints when possible. They are better for indoor air quality and worker health. Choose water-based paints over solvent-based where suitable. They have less odour and lower fire risk. Choose durable paints because they last longer and reduce waste. Now, worker welfare. Pay your workers fairly and on time. Provide clean drinking water, shade, and breaks. Provide protective equipment. Do not charge workers for safety gear. That is your responsibility. Treat workers with respect. A motivated team works better and safer. Train your team regularly. Hold a toolbox talk every morning. Discuss the day's tasks, hazards, and controls. Keep a record. This shows clients and inspectors that you take safety seriously. Also, consider insurance. Public liability insurance protects you if you damage a client's property or injure someone. Workers compensation insurance protects your team. In many African countries, these are affordable. Ask an insurance broker. Business tip: use safety as a marketing tool. Tell clients that your team is trained and insured. Show them your safety records. Many clients, especially hotels, hospitals, schools, and international companies, will pay more for a safe contractor. Also, safety reduces downtime. An accident stops work, costs money, and damages your reputation. A safe site is a productive site. Finally, think about community impact. In many African neighbourhoods, painting projects attract attention. Keep noise down during rest hours. Keep the site clean. Greet neighbours. If you are working in a community, consider hiring local labour. This builds goodwill. Also, be careful with cultural sensitivities. Ask before painting over religious symbols or important marks. Respect the client's wishes. In summary, health, safety, and environmental responsibility are not just rules. They are part of being a professional. They protect your business, your workers, and your community. Make them a habit. Lead by example. Your team will follow. Your clients will notice. And your business will grow.

5. Costing, Quoting, and Growing Your Painting Business

Welcome to Lesson 5. This lesson is about the business side of painting and decorating in Africa. Many skilled painters struggle because they do not know how to cost, quote, and grow. This lesson gives you practical tools to make a profit and build a sustainable business. Start with costing. Costing is different from quoting. Costing is what the job costs you. Quoting is what you charge the client. If you do not know your costs, you cannot quote profitably. List all your costs. These include labour, paint, primers, fillers, sandpaper, masking tape, drop sheets, brushes, rollers, solvents, transport, food, accommodation, and safety equipment. Also include overheads: rent, vehicle maintenance, phone, internet, insurance, and marketing. Add a contingency of 10 to 15 percent for unexpected problems. Now, calculate labour. Decide how many workers you need and how many days the job will take. Pay fair wages. In many African countries, daily rates vary. Check the local market. Do not underpay because it leads to theft, poor work, and high turnover. A good worker is an asset. Train them and keep them. For paint, measure the area accurately. Use the coverage rates on the product data sheet. Add 10 percent for waste and touch-ups. For rough surfaces, add more. For multiple colours, add extra for cleaning tools between colours. For transport, calculate fuel, tolls, and vehicle wear. If the site is far, add accommodation and food. For equipment, charge a rental fee if you own it. If you hire, include the hire cost. Now, quoting. A good quote is clear, professional, and detailed. It should include your business name, contact details, client name, site address, date, and quote number. It should describe the work: surface preparation, products, number of coats, colours, and timeline. It should list what is included and what is not. For example, state that moving furniture is not included, or that scaffolding is included. It should state the price, payment terms, and validity period. It should state the warranty, if any. In Africa, many clients want a verbal quote. Avoid this. Always give a written quote. It protects you and the client. Use a simple template. You can create one on a phone or computer. If you do not have a printer, write neatly and take a photo. Send it by WhatsApp or email. Now, pricing strategies. There are three main approaches. Cost-plus pricing adds a markup to your costs. This is simple and safe. Market-based pricing looks at what competitors charge. This is risky because competitors may be cutting corners. Value-based pricing charges what the job is worth to the client. This is best for premium work. For example, a hotel that loses money when rooms are unavailable will pay more for a fast, high-quality job. Use value-based pricing when you can. Do not compete only on price. There is always someone cheaper. Compete on quality, reliability, and professionalism. Offer a warranty. A two-year warranty on workmanship builds trust. If you use good products and prepare well, you will rarely need to return. Now, payment terms. Ask for a deposit. In Africa, a 50 percent deposit is common for small jobs. For larger jobs, use milestones. For example, 30 percent on signing, 30 percent when preparation is complete, 30 percent when painting is complete, and 10 percent on final inspection. Never fund a job from your own pocket. If the client refuses a deposit, be careful. They may not pay. Get a signed contract. Keep records of all payments. Issue receipts. Now, managing cash flow. Cash flow is the biggest killer of small businesses. You may be profitable on paper but run out of cash. To manage cash flow, get deposits, pay suppliers on time, and follow up on invoices. Do not buy expensive equipment until you have steady work. Hire equipment for large jobs. Buy paint in bulk only when you have a confirmed job. Keep a simple cash book. Record money in and money out. Use a notebook or a phone app. Review it weekly. Know your break-even point. That is the amount you must earn to cover your costs. Anything above that is profit. Now, growing your business. Start by doing excellent work. Your reputation is your best marketing. Ask satisfied clients for referrals. Offer a small discount for referrals. Build a portfolio. Take before and after photos. Keep them on your phone. Create a simple website or social media page. In Africa, WhatsApp Business, Facebook, and Instagram are powerful. Post regularly. Show your work. Share tips. Respond to messages quickly. Network with other trades. Architects, engineers, real estate agents, and interior designers can refer work to you. Attend trade shows and supplier events. Join a painters association if one exists. Consider diversifying. You can offer related services such as wall papering, tiling, screeding, waterproofing, and ceiling installation. This increases your income per client. You can also offer colour consultation, decorative finishes, and maintenance contracts. Another growth path is to become a supplier. Sell paint, brushes, and sundries to other painters. This adds a new income stream. Or become a trainer. Teach young people the trade. In many African countries, youth unemployment is high. A training centre can be profitable and socially impactful. Now, managing clients. Set clear expectations. Communicate regularly. If there is a delay, tell the client early. Do not hide problems. If the client changes the scope, issue a variation order with a new price. Do not do extra work for free. Be professional. Arrive on time. Wear clean clothes. Keep the site tidy. Speak politely. Do not argue in front of the client. If there is a dispute, stay calm. Refer to the contract. If needed, use a mediator. Now, managing money. Separate business and personal money. Open a business bank account or mobile money account. Pay yourself a salary. Save for taxes. In many African countries, small businesses must pay tax. Check the rules. Keep receipts. Hire an accountant if you can afford one. If not, use a simple accounting app. Avoid debt. If you must borrow, borrow for equipment or a specific job, not for daily expenses. Now, think about the future. Set goals. Where do you want your business to be in one year, three years, five years? Write them down. Review them monthly. Invest in training. Learn new skills. Learn about new products. Learn about business management. Read books. Watch videos. Attend workshops. Finally, remember why you started. Painting and decorating is a noble trade. You improve people's homes, offices, and communities. You create beauty and comfort. Be proud of your work. Treat every job as a reference. Treat every client as a long-term relationship. With good costing, quoting, and business habits, you can build a successful painting business in Africa. The opportunities are there. Go and take them.

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-size:16px; font-weight:bold; color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING CHEMICAL AND HEALTH HAZARD</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-size:14px; color:#1a1a1a;">Solvent-based paints, thinners, and two-pack epoxy or polyurethane systems contain volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and isocyanates. In high ambient temperatures, evaporation rates increase and confined spaces stairwells, roof voids, water tanks, pit latrines, and unventilated rooms can reach dangerous vapour concentrations within minutes. Isocyanate exposure can cause occupational asthma, and repeated solvent exposure causes permanent neurological damage.</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-size:14px; color:#1a1a1a;">Mandatory controls: forced ventilation or extraction in all enclosed spaces; organic vapour or combination respirators (not dust masks) for solvent and two-pack work; nitrile gloves; eye protection; no eating, drinking, or smoking on site; separate storage for solvents away from living areas and ignition sources. Never use petrol, paraffin, or diesel as a thinner or cleaning agent this is a common and lethal practice in informal painting work.</p>
<p style="margin:0; font-size:14px; color:#1a1a1a;"><strong>Source:</strong> WHO, <em>Chemical Safety and Occupational Health Guidance for Paint and Solvent Exposure</em> (WHO, 2019).</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Measure gross area, then deduct openings.</strong> Never quote on a visual estimate. Use a laser measure or tape and record every wall, door, and window.</li>
<li><strong>Identify the substrate before pricing.</strong> New plaster, block, timber, steel, and galvanised metal each change the paint quantity and the preparation method.</li>
<li><strong>Apply the correct absorption factor.</strong> Add 1540% for new or high-absorption surfaces. This is not optional; it is the difference between profit and loss.</li>
<li><strong>Quote on practical spread rate, not theoretical.</strong> Use the manufacturer's practical figure or your own measured experience, never the laboratory number.</li>
<li><strong>Add 10% waste allowance.</strong> Covers spillage, touch-ups, roller loss, and the last half-litre that never quite finishes the wall.</li>
<li><strong>Check temperature and humidity before application.</strong> Above 32C or below 40% relative humidity, reduce spread expectations and work in shaded periods.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm primer compatibility.</strong> Alkali-resistant primer on new plaster, etch primer on galvanised steel, knotting on timber. Wrong primer equals failed topcoat.</li>
<li><strong>Document everything in the quote.</strong> Substrate, preparation method, number of coats, product brand, and coverage assumptions. This protects you when the client questions the final invoice.</li>
</ol>
