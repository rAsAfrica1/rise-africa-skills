WELDING FUNDAMENTALS · MODULE 7 OF 12

Striking and Running a Bead

The four things you control while welding: arc length, travel angle, work angle, and speed. Get these right and the rest follows.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Strike an arc by both the scratch and tap methods, and say when each is used
  • Set and hold arc length correctly
  • Explain travel angle, work angle and their effects
  • Read your own bead and diagnose what you did wrong
  • Practise in a way that produces improvement rather than repetition
  • Explain why the weld pool, not the electrode, is what you actually watch
Lesson 7.1

Striking the arc

Welding starts with a controlled short circuit that establishes the arc. Two methods. Both need practice.

Scratch start

Drag the electrode tip lightly across the work surface, as if striking a match, then lift it a few millimetres to establish the arc.

  • Easier to learn. The scratching motion naturally produces a start
  • Works on rustier or dirtier metal. The scratching helps break through
  • Risk: sticking. Scratch too slowly and the electrode sticks to the work. This is called "stubbing". If it happens, twist the holder sharply to release; do not pull straight back

Tap start

Bring the electrode straight down onto the work, touch, and lift straight up a few millimetres.

  • Cleaner start. Once learned, it produces a tidier arc strike with less mess
  • Preferred for finished work. Scratch marks can be left on polished or visible surfaces; tap start leaves less
  • Harder to learn. The timing between touching and lifting is short, and beginners often either stick or pull away without establishing the arc

Where you strike matters

Do not strike the arc on the joint itself unless the strike will be consumed by the weld. A strike point that is outside the weld becomes a small hardened spot — a potential crack initiation point.

Strike a short distance ahead of where you intend to start welding, on a piece of scrap attached to the work, or in a spot that will be welded over. If you strike on the joint itself and miss, that mark is now a defect.

What happens when the electrode sticks

  1. Do not panic and yank. The electrode will come free when you twist the holder or the arc re-establishes
  2. Twist the holder sharply — this breaks the small weld between the electrode tip and the work
  3. If twisting does not free it, switch off the machine and free the electrode by hand. Then clean the tip and restart
  4. After freeing, check the electrode. A stuck electrode often has a damaged coating at the tip. If the coating is missing, the next arc will be unstable

The stuck electrode that keeps welding

A stuck electrode is a dead short. The machine is delivering full current into the contact point. The electrode and the metal at that point heat up fast.

If it happens on thin metal, you will burn a hole. If it happens with a poor connection, the lead or holder can overheat. Free it quickly, and if it happens often, reduce your current or improve your technique — do not just keep burning through electrodes.

Lesson 7.2

Arc length

Arc length is the distance between the tip of the electrode and the surface of the molten pool. It is the single most important thing you control continuously while welding.

What changes with arc length

Arc lengthEffect
Too shortThe electrode dips into the pool and sticks. The coating does not burn properly and shielding fails
CorrectStable arc, good shielding, smooth crackling sound, controlled pool
Too longArc becomes unstable, spatters more, and the pool is exposed to air. Bead becomes wide, flat and often porous. Shielding gas from the coating spreads too thin

What "correct" actually is

For stick welding, the correct arc length is roughly the diameter of the core wire of the electrode. For a 3.2mm electrode, that is about 3mm of arc. Slightly longer for higher currents, shorter for lower.

The problem is that you cannot see the arc length — the arc is far too bright and the pool is between you and the tip. What you use instead are the sound and the behaviour of the pool:

  • A sharp, consistent crackling sound like frying bacon — arc length is correct
  • A soft hissing sound — arc too long. The shielding is failing
  • A harsh sputtering sound — arc too short, or electrode is about to stick
  • A violent, popping sound — arc way too long or electrode coating is damaged

Learners who listen to the arc improve much faster than learners who only look at it.

Holding arc length as you weld

The electrode is consumed as it welds, so the tip moves away from the pool. If you do not compensate, the arc lengthens as you weld and the bead quality degrades from start to finish.

You must feed the electrode in as it burns away. The motion is a slow, steady descent, at exactly the rate the electrode shortens. This is the single thing beginners find hardest to do.

The trick that helps

Rest the heel of your electrode-holding hand on the work, or on a guide, and use your wrist and fingers to feed the electrode in. Do not hold the holder in mid-air — you have no reference and no fine control.

Bracing your hand against the work is the single biggest improvement a beginner can make to arc length control. It costs nothing.

Lesson 7.3

Travel angle and work angle

Two angles matter, and they are often confused because both are "the angle of the electrode". They are separate.

Travel angle

The angle between the electrode and the direction of travel, viewed from the side. It is measured in the plane that contains the direction of motion.

  • Push angle — the electrode leans forward in the direction of travel. Heat is pushed ahead of the pool, penetration is shallower and the bead is flatter. Common in MIG
  • Drag angle — the electrode leans back, tip trailing the pool. Heat goes deeper, penetration is greater, the bead sits higher. Common in stick
  • Perpendicular — the electrode is straight up and down. Middle ground

Typical travel angles are in the range of 5–15° from perpendicular, in either direction. For stick welding in a groove, a slight drag angle is standard. For MIG, a slight push angle is common. The exact figure depends on process, joint and preference.

Work angle

The angle between the electrode and the work, viewed from the front — the plane perpendicular to the direction of travel.

  • For a butt joint or a bead on flat plate: electrode perpendicular to the work, or very slightly angled
  • For a lap or tee joint: electrode angled about 45° between the two plates, so the arc heats both equally. For a tee joint, tilt slightly away from the vertical plate — the vertical plate needs more heat than the flat one, because it acts as a heat sink and the arc has to climb slightly
  • For a corner joint: 45° between the plates

Getting the work angle wrong makes one side of the joint receive more heat than the other. You see it as uneven fusion and a bead that sits to one side.

Lesson 7.4

Travel speed

Travel speed is how fast you move along the joint. Together with current and arc length, it sets the heat input.

What travel speed does

SpeedEffect on beadEffect on penetration
Too slowWide, high bead; excess metal piled up; heat soaks into the surrounding metalDeep but often too hot — risk of burn-through on thin material
CorrectConsistent width, even ripples, correct height relative to the jointCorrect for the thickness
Too fastNarrow, low bead; ripples become sharp and spaced outShallow. Risk of lack of fusion at the edges

How to judge it

Watch the pool. On stick, the pool should be elongated behind the arc and the solidified bead should appear a consistent width behind it. On MIG, the pool should stay a consistent size and the bead should have a smooth, even profile.

What you are aiming for: a consistent width and height of bead. Ripples that are evenly spaced. A smooth transition at the toes of the weld — where the bead meets the parent metal — with no undercut.

Speed and current interact

If you weld too fast and turn the current up to compensate, you will get a hotter, more penetrating bead but with a narrower fusion zone. If you weld too slow and turn the current down, you get a cold, wide bead with poor penetration at the edges.

Set the current for the material and electrode, and adjust travel speed for the bead you want. Do not adjust both at once.

Speed on thin material

Thin material has less heat capacity. Travel speed has to be higher to prevent burn-through — but not so high that penetration fails. This balance is what makes thin sheet harder than thick plate. Practise on 3mm or thicker until you have some control, and only move to thinner material when you can hold a steady arc.

Lesson 7.5

Reading the bead

After every weld, look at what you produced. The bead is the record of what you did.

What a correct bead looks like

  • Consistent width along the whole length
  • Consistent height — the bead sits a reasonable amount above the parent metal but not excessively
  • Evenly spaced ripples — a sign of steady travel speed
  • Smooth tie-in at both edges — no sharp notch where the bead meets the metal
  • No visible porosity or holes
  • Uniform slag coverage on stick — slag should be continuous and easy to chip

What common faults look like

LookMeans
Bead too wide and flat, edges not tied inArc too long, or current too low, or travelling too slow with high current
Bead too narrow and tallTravelling too fast, or current too low
Sharp V-shaped groove at the edge (undercut)Current too high, or wrong travel angle, or arc too long
Visible holes in the surface (porosity)Contamination, moisture in electrodes, or shielding failure
Spatter everywhereArc too long, or current too high, or wrong polarity
Bead sits unevenly, one side better fused than the otherWrong work angle
Bead wanders, not straightNot watching the pool, or unsteady hand position

Cut it in half

The only honest test of penetration

Module 2 said you cannot see penetration from outside. The way to find out is to cut a test weld in half and look at the cross-section. The etched or just-cut face shows you exactly how deep the fusion went.

Do this regularly while practising. Produce a test piece, cut it with a grinder, and examine the section. Adjust your settings based on what you see. This is what turns practice into improvement.

Diagnose one change at a time

When a bead is wrong, do not change everything at once. Change one thing, weld another bead, and see the effect. That is how you learn what each control does.

  1. Weld a bead with current too low. Weld another with current too high. Notice the difference
  2. Weld a bead with arc too short. Weld another with arc too long. Notice
  3. Weld with a push angle, then with a drag angle. Notice
  4. Weld too fast, then too slow. Notice

Four experiments, one afternoon, and you will know more than a month of unfocused practice.

Lesson 7.6

Practice that actually improves you

Welding is a physical skill. It improves through deliberate practice — not repetitive practice, and not simply doing more of it.

What deliberate practice is

  1. Have a specific goal for each session. Not "practise welding" but "hold arc length steady for a full 200mm bead"
  2. Work on one thing at a time. Trying to fix current, angle, and speed in the same bead fixes none of them
  3. Get feedback. Look at the bead. Cut a test piece. Ask someone who welds better than you
  4. Adjust and try again. Change one thing, weld another bead, observe
  5. Stop when tired. Physical fatigue shows in the bead. Better to stop and continue fresh than push through and practise bad habits

What beginners most often get wrong

  • Holding the holder in mid-air. Brace your hand on the work
  • Watching the electrode instead of the pool. The pool tells you what is happening
  • Not feeding the electrode in as it burns. Arc length drifts longer and longer over the bead
  • Going too fast. Beginners usually hurry
  • Changing settings instead of technique. The settings were probably fine; the technique was not
  • Ignoring the sound. The arc tells you what is going on

A starter practice routine

  1. Get a piece of 6mm mild steel plate, roughly 200mm × 300mm
  2. Clean one face to bright metal
  3. Set your machine to the middle of the recommended range for a 3.2mm rutile electrode
  4. Weld a straight bead along the plate, watching the pool
  5. Let it cool, chip the slag, and look at the bead
  6. Weld a second bead parallel to the first, trying to hold the same width and speed
  7. Continue, filling the plate with parallel beads
  8. When the plate is full, cut it across at several points and look at the penetration
  9. Adjust one thing — current, or speed — and start again on a fresh face

Do that for a week and you will be welding. There is no shortcut.

Why this course will not teach you to weld

This module explains what the controls do. It does not turn you into a welder. Welding is learned with a machine in front of you, over many hours, with someone correcting your mistakes.

What this course does: it tells you which mistakes to look for, what causes them, and how to think about fixing them. That makes your practice efficient rather than wasteful. It does not replace practice.

Practical assignment — Module 7

If you have access to a machine and supervision, do the practical work. If not, complete the observation and planning parts only.

  1. Ten beads. On a clean 6mm plate, weld at least ten straight beads. Photograph the plate. Chip the slag off stick welds before photographing
  2. Cut and inspect. Cut the plate across at three points. Photograph the cut faces. State what the penetration looks like and whether it is adequate
  3. One diagnostic example. Take one bead that has a visible fault — undercut, porosity, uneven width, whatever. Describe what the fault is, and give two possible causes from the fault table in Lesson 7.5. State which is more likely
  4. Your sound observation. During welding, listen for the difference between a correct arc and a long arc. Describe both sounds in your own words
  5. Your practice plan. State three specific things you intend to practise in the next month, each with a specific goal
  6. Your fault log. Keep a written log of every bad bead you produce for one week: what you were trying to do, what actually happened, and what you changed next time

Points 3 and 6 carry the most marks. Point 3 assesses whether you can read a bead. Point 6 assesses whether you are practising deliberately or just repeating.

Module 7 knowledge check

1. What is the correct arc length for stick welding?

  1. As long as possible for maximum penetration
  2. Roughly the diameter of the electrode's core wire
  3. Whatever keeps the arc stable
  4. About 10mm
Show answer

b. For a 3.2mm electrode that means around 3mm. You judge it by sound and pool behaviour, not by sight — the arc is far too bright to look at directly.

2. What does a soft hissing sound from the arc indicate?

  1. Correct arc length
  2. Arc too long — the shielding is failing
  3. Arc too short
  4. The machine is faulty
Show answer

b. A correct arc gives a sharp crackling sound. A soft hiss means the arc is too long and shielding is compromised. A harsh sputter means too short. Learning to identify these by ear is one of the most useful skills a welder develops.

3. What is a drag (trailing) travel angle and what effect does it have?

  1. The electrode leans forward; shallower penetration and a flatter bead
  2. The electrode leans back, tip trailing the pool; deeper penetration and a higher bead
  3. The electrode is perpendicular; no effect
  4. The electrode points sideways; affects only the width
Show answer

b. Dragging the electrode pushes more heat into the root and produces deeper penetration and a higher bead. Pushing the electrode flattens the bead and reduces penetration. Standard practice in stick welding on grooves is a slight drag angle.

4. What is the correct work angle for a tee joint?

  1. Perpendicular to the base plate
  2. About 45° between the two plates, tilted slightly away from the vertical plate
  3. Parallel to the vertical plate
  4. Straight down the joint line
Show answer

b. The 45° angle heats both plates equally. The slight tilt away from the vertical plate compensates for the fact that the vertical plate acts as a heat sink and needs more heat.

5. Why should you cut test welds in half and look at the cross-section?

  1. To check the weld colour
  2. To see penetration — the one property that cannot be judged from the bead surface
  3. To check for slag
  4. It is required by law
Show answer

b. Penetration is the most important property of a weld and is invisible from outside. A neat bead with no penetration is a decoration, not a weld. Cutting a test piece in half is the only honest check available to a beginner.
