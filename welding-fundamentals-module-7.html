1. Oxy-Fuel Equipment and Safety

Oxy-fuel welding uses a mixture of oxygen and a fuel gasâusually acetyleneâto create a flame hot enough to melt steel. In Africa, you'll find this setup in small workshops from Lagos to Nairobi. The basic kit includes two cylinders (oxygen and acetylene), regulators, hoses, a torch handle, and tips. Oxygen cylinders are typically black with white shoulders; acetylene cylinders are maroon or red. Always use a flashback arrestor on both regulatorsâthese small devices prevent flames from traveling back into the hoses. Hoses are color-coded: green for oxygen, red for acetylene. Never interchange them.

Safety starts before you strike a spark. Store cylinders upright and secured with a chain or strap. Keep them at least 6 meters from any flame or heat source. Acetylene is unstable above 15 psi (1 bar), so never set the acetylene regulator above 15 psi. Oxygen regulators can go higher, but for welding, 2â5 psi is typical. Check for leaks with soapy waterânever a flame. A leak will bubble. If you smell acetylene (like garlic), ventilate immediately.

Wear the right gear: leather gloves, a leather apron, safety glasses, and steel-toe boots. In many African workshops, flip-flops are common, but they are dangerousâmolten metal and hot slag cause severe burns. Use goggles or a face shield with a #4 or #5 shade for welding. For cutting, a #3 or #4 shade is fine. Always have a fire extinguisher (dry chemical) nearby and a bucket of sand or water.

Before lighting, purge the hoses. Open the oxygen valve on the torch for a few seconds to blow out air, then do the same for acetylene. Set the working pressure: acetylene at 5 psi, oxygen at 10 psi for a neutral flame on 3 mm steel. Light the acetylene first, then slowly add oxygen. Never use a lighter with a flint near the tipâuse a striker. When finished, shut off the acetylene valve first, then oxygen, then bleed the hoses. This prevents flashback.

2. Lighting and Adjusting the Flame

Lighting the torch is a skill that takes practice. First, open the acetylene valve on the torch handle about a quarter turn. Then open the oxygen valve just a crackâtoo much oxygen will blow out the flame. Use a spark striker to ignite the acetylene. The flame will be yellow and smoky. Slowly open the oxygen valve until the flame becomes neutralâa sharp blue inner cone with a slightly rounded tip. That neutral flame is what you want for most welding and cutting.

There are three flame types: carburizing (excess acetylene), neutral, and oxidizing (excess oxygen). Carburizing has a feathery white inner cone and is used for welding aluminum or hardfacing. Neutral is for steel. Oxidizing has a short, pointed inner cone and is used for cutting or brazing. To adjust, start with acetylene only, then add oxygen until the feather disappears. For a neutral flame, the inner cone should be about twice as long as the feather was. If you add too much oxygen, the flame will hiss and the inner cone becomes pointedâthat's oxidizing.

In Africa, many workshops use a 'welding torch' for both welding and cutting by changing the tip. Always use the correct tip size for the metal thickness. For 1.5 mm steel, use a 0.6 mm tip; for 3 mm, a 1.2 mm tip; for 6 mm, a 2.4 mm tip. The tip size determines the flame size and heat output. If the flame pops or backfires, shut off the torch immediately. Backfires are usually caused by touching the tip to the work or by too low gas pressure. A flashback arrestor will stop a flashback from reaching the cylinders.

Practice lighting and adjusting until you can do it in under 10 seconds. Keep the tip clean with a tip cleanerâa small file with wires. A dirty tip causes an uneven flame. Always light with a striker, never a match or cigarette lighter. After lighting, adjust the flame to neutral. Listen: a neutral flame is quiet; an oxidizing flame hisses. When you're done, shut off the acetylene first, then oxygen. This sequence prevents a pop. Remember: acetylene is flammable at 2.5% to 100% in air, so never light near a leak.

3. Oxy-Fuel Welding Techniques

Oxy-fuel welding is a fusion process where you melt the base metal and add filler wire to create a joint. It's ideal for thin steel (0.5 mm to 6 mm), especially in rural areas where electricity is unreliable. You'll need a welding torch with a neutral flame, a filler rod (usually mild steel, 1.6 mm or 2.4 mm diameter), and a wire brush. Clean the metal to bare shineâremove rust, paint, and oil. In Africa, many jobs involve repairing farm equipment, gates, and water tanks.

Start with a butt joint on 1.5 mm steel. Hold the torch at a 45-degree angle to the work, with the inner cone about 2 mm from the metal. Move the torch in a small circular or back-and-forth motion to create a molten pool. Dip the filler rod into the leading edge of the pool, not the flame. The rod should melt and flow into the joint. Keep a consistent speedâtoo fast and you get poor fusion; too slow and you burn through. For a 1.5 mm sheet, use a 0.6 mm tip and 1.6 mm filler. For 3 mm, use a 1.2 mm tip and 2.4 mm filler.

There are two main techniques: forehand (push) and backhand (pull). Forehand is when the torch points in the direction of travel; backhand is when it points back toward the finished weld. Forehand gives better penetration on thin metal; backhand gives a smoother bead on thicker metal. For beginners, forehand is easier. Use a slight weaving motion to distribute heat evenly. If the pool gets too large, pull the torch away briefly. If it's too small, move slower or use a larger tip.

After welding, clean the joint with a wire brush and inspect for cracks or porosity. A good weld has a smooth, rippled bead with no holes. If you see holes, you may have used too much oxygen or moved too fast. Practice on scrap metal first. In many African workshops, you'll weld without a jig, so use clamps or vise grips to hold parts. Remember: never weld on a closed containerâit can explode. Always ventilate; the fumes from galvanized steel are toxic. Use a respirator if welding galvanized metal.

4. Oxy-Fuel Cutting Techniques

Oxy-fuel cutting uses a cutting torch with a special tip that has a center hole for oxygen. The process: heat the metal to a bright red (about 1000Â°C), then press the oxygen lever to blow a jet of pure oxygen through the metal. The oxygen burns the iron, creating a cut. This works on steel, not on aluminum or stainless steel (they don't oxidize the same way). In Africa, this is common for cutting scrap, pipes, and plates.

Use a cutting tip sized for the metal thickness. For 6 mm steel, use a 1.2 mm tip; for 12 mm, a 2.4 mm tip; for 25 mm, a 3.2 mm tip. Set acetylene at 5 psi and oxygen at 20â40 psi, depending on thickness. Light the torch with a neutral flame, then adjust to a slightly oxidizing flame (short inner cone). Hold the torch perpendicular to the metal, with the preheat flames just touching the surface. When the spot turns bright red, press the oxygen lever slowly. A stream of sparks will shoot out the bottomâthat's the cut.

Move the torch steadily along your cut line. Keep the tip about 3â5 mm above the metal. If you move too fast, the cut will stop; too slow, and you'll melt the edges. For a clean cut, use a straight edge or a guide. In many African workshops, you'll cut freehandâpractice on scrap. If the cut is ragged, your tip may be dirty or your oxygen pressure too low. Clean the tip with a tip cleaner. If the metal is rusty, grind it clean first.

Safety: always wear cutting goggles (shade #4 or #5), leather gloves, and a face shield. Sparks fly everywhereâprotect your feet. Never cut on a concrete floor; it can explode from the heat. Use a steel table or sand. After cutting, shut off the oxygen lever, then the acetylene valve, then oxygen valve on the torch. Bleed the hoses. Remember: never cut a sealed containerâit will explode. If you must cut a tank, fill it with water first. For thick steel, you may need to preheat the whole area with a rosebud tip before cutting.

5. Heating and Bending with Oxy-Fuel

Oxy-fuel heating uses a rosebud tip or a large welding tip to heat metal for bending, straightening, or loosening rusted parts. Unlike welding, you don't melt the metalâyou heat it to a dull red (about 600â700Â°C) so it becomes malleable. This is useful for bending pipes, straightening bent chassis, or removing seized bolts. In Africa, this is a common repair technique for farm implements and vehicle frames.

Use a rosebud tipâit has many small holes and produces a large, soft flame. Set acetylene at 10 psi and oxygen at 20 psi. Light with a neutral flame, then adjust to a slightly carburizing flame (feathery) to avoid oxidation. Hold the torch 10â15 cm from the metal and move it in circles to heat evenly. Don't concentrate heat in one spotâyou'll burn a hole. For bending, heat the area to a dull red, then bend slowly with a pipe wrench or hammer. For straightening, heat the bent area and apply pressure with a hydraulic jack or press.

When heating, watch the color: dark red is about 600Â°C, bright red is 800Â°C, orange is 1000Â°C. For bending steel, you want a bright red. For aluminum, heat until it just starts to change colorâaluminum doesn't glow red, so use a temperature crayon or soap (soap turns black at about 300Â°C). Never overheatâit weakens the metal. After bending, let it cool slowly in air or bury it in sand to prevent hardening. If you quench in water, it may become brittle.

Safety: heating produces no sparks, but the metal stays hot for a long time. Mark hot metal with a chalk or a sign. Wear leather gloves and a face shield. In many African workshops, you'll heat with the same torch you use for weldingâjust change the tip. Always have a fire extinguisher nearby. If you're heating a container that held fuel, purge it with water or inert gas first. Remember: never heat galvanized metal without a respiratorâthe zinc fumes cause metal fume fever.

6. Storage and Handling of Compressed Gases

Compressed gas cylinders are bombs if mishandled. Oxygen cylinders are black with white shoulders; acetylene cylinders are maroon or red. Store them upright and secured with a chain or strap so they can't fall. Keep them in a cool, dry place away from sunlight and heat. In Africa, many workshops store cylinders outside under a shade treeâthat's okay if they're upright and chained. Never store oxygen and acetylene together; keep them at least 6 meters apart or separated by a fire wall.

Never drop or roll a cylinder. Use a cylinder cart or trolley. If you must move one, tip it slightly and roll it on its bottom edgeânever drag it. Keep the valve protection cap on when moving or storing. When in use, open the valve slowlyâif you open it fast, the sudden pressure can ignite the grease or dust on the valve. Never use oil or grease on oxygen fittings; oxygen plus oil equals fire. Use only approved regulators and hoses. Check hoses for cracksâreplace them if damaged. Use flashback arrestors on both regulators.

Acetylene cylinders contain a porous mass and acetone. Never lay an acetylene cylinder on its sideâacetone can leak into the valve and cause problems. If you must lay it down, stand it upright for at least 30 minutes before use. Never use acetylene above 15 psi. Oxygen cylinders can be used at higher pressures, but for welding, keep it under 40 psi. Always close the cylinder valve when not in use, even if you're just taking a break. Bleed the hoses after shutting off the cylinders.

Inspect cylinders regularly for damage, rust, or leaks. If you smell gas, shut off the valve, ventilate, and use soapy water to find the leak. Never use a flame. If a cylinder leaks and you can't stop it, move it outdoors and call the supplier. Store empty cylinders with valves closed and caps on. Mark them 'EMPTY' to avoid confusion. In many African countries, refilling is done locallyâmake sure the supplier is reputable. Never refill a cylinder yourself. Always transport cylinders in a vertical position, secured in a vehicle. Remember: a full cylinder is under pressureâtreat it with respect.

4. Oxy-Fuel Welding Techniques â Business Notes

For agribusiness fabrication, the two practical techniques are leftward (forehand) welding for thin sheet (animal feeders, poultry drinkers, water troughs) and rightward (backhand) for thicker plate (implement frames, plough beams). Leftward gives better control and less distortion on thin metal; rightward gives deeper penetration and higher deposition on 3 mm and above.

Filler rod selection matters for cost. Use RG45 / ER70S-6 mild steel rod for general work. Flux is essential on rusty or galvanised stock â common in second-hand African farm equipment. Always grind galvanised coatings back 50 mm from the joint; zinc fumes cause metal fume fever.

Distortion control is a commercial issue: a warped implement frame is a rejected job. Use tack welds every 100â150 mm, alternate welding direction, and allow slow cooling. Do not quench mild steel â it embrittles the weld.

5. Oxy-Fuel Cutting Techniques

Cutting is the highest-volume oxy-fuel operation in most workshops â cutting rebar, angle iron, sheet, and scrap. The rule: preheat to cherry red, then press the oxygen lever. The oxygen jet does the cutting; the flame only starts the reaction. Move steadily; too fast leaves dross and a ragged edge, too slow overheats and rounds the top edge.

For plate over 25 mm, start the cut at the edge, not mid-plate, and use a slight drag angle. For piercing holes, tilt the torch and preheat the spot to red before pressing the lever. On farm scrap with paint, rust, or concrete residue, expect slower cuts and higher gas usage â price this into quotations.

6. Heating and Bending

Oxy-fuel heating is used to straighten bent plough beams, loosen seized nuts, and bend pipe or flat bar. Use a neutral or slightly carburising flame with a large multi-jet heating tip. Heat evenly across the bend zone to a dull cherry red (approx. 700â900 Â°C for mild steel). Bend slowly; do not hammer cold or black-hot steel. For pipe bending, pack with dry sand and cap ends to prevent collapse â a standard technique in rural African workshops without a pipe bender.

Never heat galvanised or painted steel without respiratory protection (P2/P3 mask) and forced ventilation. Zinc and paint fumes are the most common workshop health hazard after burns.
