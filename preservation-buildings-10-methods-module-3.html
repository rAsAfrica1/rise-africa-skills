Module 3

Method 2 — Smoking Houses and Kilns

Smoking is drying with chemistry added. The smoke deposits phenols and other compounds on the food that slow oxidation and inhibit bacteria. A proper smokehouse controls three things: temperature, airflow, and smoke density.

Lesson 3.1

How smoke preserves

Smoke is not just flavoured air. It carries four classes of chemical compound that act on food:

  1. Phenols — antioxidants. They slow the rancidity of fat.
  2. Formaldehyde and organic acids — antibacterial. They inhibit surface microbial growth.
  3. Carbonyls — colour and flavour. They give smoked product its characteristic brown surface.
  4. Heat — from the fire itself. Dries the surface, forms a pellicle (a glossy skin), and reduces water activity.

Smoke also dries the product. Most of the preservation effect of smoking comes from the drying, with the antimicrobial compounds adding 10–20% extra shelf life on top of drying alone.

Because of that, smoking is essentially fast drying with flavour. The product must be dried thoroughly, not just smoke-flavoured.

The rule: smoking without drying is not preservation. It is flavour. If you want shelf life, dry the product as well as smoking it.

Lesson 3.2

For a small African processor, hot-smoking is the correct choice. It is simpler, faster, safer, and produces a ready-to-eat product with a shorter but adequate shelf life if properly packaged. Cold-smoking is a specialty technique used for salmon, hams and cheeses in Europe, and it requires a level of temperature control that is impractical at smallholder scale.

Lesson 3.3

Designing a smokehouse

A smokehouse has four parts: the firebox, the chamber, the vent, and the access door. Get the proportions right or the fire smokes badly and the product spoils.

Proportions for a small hot-smoker

ElementDimensionNotes
Chamber interior1,000 × 1,000 × 2,000 mmHolds 50–100 kg of product on shelves
Firebox500 × 500 × 600 mmExternal to the chamber, connected by a pipe or tunnel
Firebox-to-chamber tunnel3–4 m long, 200 mm diameterCools the smoke before it enters the chamber
Vent200 mm diameter, adjustableAt the top of the chamber, opposite the tunnel
Shelves5–8 tiers of steel meshSpace 200–250 mm apart
Access doorFull height of the chamber, opens outwardInsulated if the smoker runs in cool conditions

The firebox

The firebox is separate from the chamber — never directly underneath. Heat and smoke travel through a tunnel, cooling as they go. This gives:

  • Uniform heat (no hotspots from direct fire)
  • Controllable temperature (you can add fuel without opening the chamber)
  • Cooler smoke, which is what carries the flavour compounds

The chamber

The chamber walls are typically brick, block, or insulated metal. Brick is best for a fixed installation — heavy, cheap, fire-resistant, and holds heat. The chamber must be sealed at the top except for the vent. Air comes in at the bottom, drawn by the chimney effect of the vent, passes over the product, exits at the top.

The vent

Adjustable, at the top, on the opposite side of the chamber from the tunnel. The vent controls airflow. Open more and the fire burns hotter and dries faster. Close it and the fire smolders, producing more smoke but less heat and less airflow. For most products, a partially open vent (50%) is correct.

Lesson 3.4

Fuel, temperature and management

Fuel choice

Smoke quality depends on what you burn. Best fuels:

  • Hardwood offcuts — mango, acacia, mukwa, mukau. Slow-burning, clean smoke.
  • Fruit wood — apple, cherry, guava. Sweet smoke.
  • Corn cobs and coconut shell — widely used in Africa, produce a rich smoke.
  • Sawdust — used for cold-smoking and for adding smoke without heat.

Never burn:

  • Softwoods (pine, eucalyptus) — high resin, harsh bitter smoke, potentially toxic compounds.
  • Treated or painted timber — preservatives and paint release toxic chemicals into the food.
  • Rubber, plastics, or glue-bonded composite boards.
  • Rotten or mouldy wood.

Temperature management

  • Measure chamber temperature with a thermometer at shelf level. Do not guess.
  • Hot-smoking temperature: 60–85 °C. Above 90 °C and the surface burns. Below 60 °C and it is cold-smoking without the technique.
  • Temperature control: damper on the vent controls airflow and thus temperature. Add small quantities of fuel to maintain the target.
  • Do not open the chamber to check temperature — the temperature drops 20 °C in seconds. Install a probe thermometer that reads from outside.

Smoke density

  • Thin, blue smoke is ideal. It carries flavour without depositing creosote.
  • Thick, white or black smoke is bad. It deposits creosote (a bitter, potentially carcinogenic compound) on the surface.
  • A smoldering fire makes thick smoke. A small hot fire with restricted air makes thin smoke.

Lesson 3.5

Food safety and packaging

Pre-smoking prep

  • Brine or salt the product first for most meats and fish. This seasons, adds shelf life, and starts the drying process.
  • Air-dry the surface for 30–60 minutes before smoking. A tacky surface (pellicle) is needed for smoke to adhere.
  • Do not smoke spoiled product. Smoking does not fix spoilage, it hides it. Every batch must be fresh.

Post-smoking cooling

  • Cool in a clean, dry space at ambient temperature for 1–2 hours.
  • Do not refrigerate hot product. It sweats and moulds.
  • Do not leave product at room temperature for more than 4 hours total.

Packaging

  • Vacuum pack for maximum shelf life (2–4 weeks at 4 °C, or 3 months frozen).
  • Modified atmosphere for longer shelf life at refrigeration (see Module 10).
  • Simple overwrap or paper for short shelf life (3–5 days).
  • Always label with date, product type, and use-by date.
The hot-smoking rule: the product must reach 70 °C internal temperature (or 75 °C for poultry) to be safe. If you do not have a probe thermometer, you cannot hot-smoke commercially. Buy one before your first sale.

Knowledge check — Module 3

1. What is the main preservation mechanism in smoking?

Correct: drying + antimicrobial smoke compounds. Most of the preservation comes from drying, with smoke chemistry adding 10–20% more shelf life.

2. What is the correct chamber temperature for hot-smoking?

Correct: 60–85 °C. Above 90 °C burns the surface; below 60 °C is cold-smoking.

3. Why is the firebox separate from the chamber?

Correct: cooling through the tunnel. Direct fire makes hotspots; external firebox with a tunnel gives uniform, controllable heat.

4. What fuel should you NEVER burn in a smokehouse?

Correct: treated or painted timber. Preservatives and paint release toxic chemicals into the food.

5. What internal temperature must hot-smoked poultry reach to be safe?

Correct: 75 °C. Meat 70 °C. Use a probe thermometer — guessing is how food poisoning happens.

Practical assignment — Module 3

Design a hot-smoking facility for a specific product.

  1. Which product are you smoking? (Fish, meat, poultry, sausage.)
  2. Your weekly throughput in kg and the number of smoke batches per week.
  3. Your smokehouse design — dimensions of the chamber, firebox, tunnel, and vent. Include a dimensioned sketch.
  4. The fuel you will use, and why.
  5. Your temperature management plan — how you will measure chamber temperature and internal product temperature.
  6. The pre-smoking prep process (brine or salt, air-drying time).
  7. Post-smoking cooling, packaging and shelf life for your product.
  8. Two supplier quotations for a probe thermometer and for any packaging materials you will need.
  9. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest food safety risk in your operation and how you will control it.

Marks are weighted toward points 3 and 9. The design is the structure; the paragraph is the safety discipline.
