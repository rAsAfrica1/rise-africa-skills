Lessons

1. Principles of Forge Welding

Forge welding is the ancient art of joining two pieces of metal by heating them to a white-hot temperature and hammering them together. In Africa, blacksmiths have used this technique for centuries to make tools, weapons, and art. To succeed, you need to understand the principle: when steel or iron reaches a specific temperature rangearound 1,200C to 1,400C (2,200F to 2,600F)the metal becomes plastic and, when pressed or hammered, the atoms on the surfaces can bond. This is not melting; it's a solid-state weld. The key is clean surfaces, the right heat, and speed.

In your forge, you'll know the metal is ready when it glows a bright yellow-white, almost sparkler-like. If you see tiny sparks shooting off, that's a sign it's burningtoo hot. You want the metal to look like a glowing lemon. At this temperature, you have only a few seconds to act before it cools. That's why preparation and speed are critical.

African forges often use charcoal or coal, which can reach these temperatures with a good air blast. You'll need a tuyere (air pipe) and a steady hand on the bellows or blower. The principle is simple: heat, clean, flux, hammer. But each step must be precise. If the metal is too cold, it won't weld; too hot, it burns and becomes useless. Practice judging the color in different lightingshade your eyes or work in a dimly lit area to see the true glow.

Remember, forge welding is not just about heat; it's about pressure. A single, firm hammer blow can fuse the metal, but you must strike while the metal is at welding heat. Many beginners fail because they hesitate. With practice, you'll develop a rhythm: heat, brush, flux, heat again, then quick, confident hammer strikes. This skill will allow you to create strong joints without electricity or modern welding machines, using only your forge, hammer, and anvil.

2. Preparing Surfaces for Welding

Before you even think about heating metal, you must prepare the surfaces. Dirty, rusty, or scaled metal will not weld. The surfaces must be clean, bare, and shaped to fit together tightly. Start by using a wire brush or a file to remove all rust, dirt, and old scale. If you have a grinder, use it, but a hand file works too. In many African workshops, a simple wire brush and a piece of broken grinding stone are used. Scrub until you see shiny metal.

Next, shape the ends. For a basic lap weld, you can simply overlap two flat bars. But for a stronger joint, you'll want to scarf the endstaper them so they fit together like puzzle pieces. A scarf joint increases surface area and helps the weld hold. To make a scarf, heat the end and hammer it to a taper, then bend it slightly so the two pieces interlock. This is done before the final welding heat.

After shaping, clean the surfaces again. Any scale that formed during shaping must be removed. Then, apply flux immediately before heating. Flux, such as borax, melts and coats the metal, preventing oxidation (scale) from forming at welding heat. Without flux, the oxygen in the air will create a layer of iron oxide that stops the weld. You can buy borax at any market or pharmacy; it's cheap and effective. Some blacksmiths also use fine sand or clay, but borax is best.

Finally, make sure your hammer and anvil are clean. A dirty hammer face can introduce impurities. Have your tools ready: hammer, anvil, tongs, wire brush, and flux. Speed is essential once the metal is hot, so arrange everything within arm's reach. In Africa, where forges are often outdoors, wind can cool the metal quicklywork close to the forge and have a helper if possible. With clean surfaces and good preparation, your weld has a much higher chance of success.

3. Using Flux: Borax and Commercial Fluxes

Flux is a chemical compound that cleans the metal and prevents oxidation during welding. In Africa, the most common flux is borax, which is readily available and inexpensive. Borax is a white powder that melts at around 743C (1,370F) and forms a glassy coating on the hot metal. This coating dissolves any remaining oxides and keeps air away from the surfaces, allowing the metal atoms to bond. You can also use commercial fluxes, which are often a mixture of borax and other chemicals like boric acid. These may work at slightly lower temperatures or be more effective on certain steels, but borax alone is sufficient for most blacksmithing.

To use borax, simply heat the metal to a black heat (about 400C) and dip it into the powder, or sprinkle the powder onto the hot metal. The borax will stick and melt as you bring the metal to welding heat. You can also mix borax with a little water to make a paste and brush it on. Apply flux to both surfaces that will be joined. As you heat, the flux will bubble and flow, carrying away impurities. When the metal reaches welding heat, the flux should be liquid and clear, like melted glass.

Be careful not to inhale borax dust; it can irritate your lungs. Work in a well-ventilated area. Also, borax can corrode your forge lining over time, so clean up any spills. If you're using commercial flux, read the instructionssome require specific temperatures. In many African markets, you can find borax in the laundry section or at chemical suppliers. It's often sold as a cleaning agent. A small bag will last a long time.

Remember, flux is not a substitute for clean metal. It helps, but you still need to brush off scale before applying. And don't overuse flux; a light coating is enough. Too much can create a thick layer that prevents the metal from touching. Practice with borax until you understand how it behaves. Once you master flux, your welds will be stronger and more consistent.

4. The Forge Welding Process Step-by-Step

Now we put it all together. Follow these steps carefully, and practice each one until it becomes second nature. First, prepare your metal: clean it, shape the ends (scarf or lap), and have your flux ready. Light your forge and bring it to a steady heat. You'll need a bright, clean firecharcoal or coal works well. Have your hammer, anvil, tongs, and wire brush within reach.

Step 1: Heat the metal. Place the prepared ends in the hottest part of the fire, just above the tuyere. Pump the bellows steadily. Watch the color. When it turns a bright orange, take it out and brush off any scale with your wire brush. Then apply flux. Return it to the fire.

Step 2: Bring to welding heat. Continue heating until the metal is a bright yellow-white, like a glowing lemon. If you see sparks, it's too hotpull it out immediately. The flux should be liquid and bubbling. This takes practice; in a charcoal forge, it might take 2-3 minutes. Don't rush.

Step 3: Remove and weld. Quickly take the metal from the fire and place it on the anvil. If you're doing a lap weld, overlap the ends. If a scarf weld, interlock them. Strike with firm, quick hammer blows. Start with light taps to set the weld, then increase force. Work from the center outward. You have only a few seconds before the metal cools below welding temperature. If it cools, reheat and try again.

Step 4: Finish and check. After welding, brush off excess flux and inspect the joint. A good weld will show no seam. If you see a crack, reheat and hammer again. Let it cool slowlydo not quench, as rapid cooling can crack the weld. In Africa, many smiths bury the piece in ash or sand to cool slowly. With practice, you'll complete a weld in under a minute. Remember, speed and heat are your friends.

5. Practice Welds: Scarf Joints and Lap Welds

The two most common forge welds are the lap weld and the scarf weld. The lap weld is simplest: you overlap two pieces of metal and weld them together. It's good for flat bars or when you need a quick joint. To make a lap weld, clean both surfaces, apply flux, heat to welding temperature, and hammer the overlap. The weld strength depends on how well you forge the jointyou should hammer from the center out to push out slag and flux. A lap weld is not as strong as a scarf weld because the stress is concentrated at the edges, but it's fine for many applications.

The scarf weld is stronger and more traditional. You taper the ends of both pieces so they fit together like a V or a tongue-and-groove. This increases surface area and distributes stress. To make a scarf, heat the end and hammer it to a gradual taper, then bend it slightly. When you bring the two pieces together, they should interlock. Weld as usual: heat, flux, and hammer. The scarf weld is ideal for making chains, rings, or repairing tools. In many African blacksmithing traditions, scarf welds are used for making hoes, knives, and decorative ironwork.

Practice with scrap metal first. Cut a few pieces of mild steel bar, about 1 cm thick. Try a lap weld on one pair, and a scarf weld on another. Use borax as flux. Keep your forge hot and work quickly. After welding, test the joint by bending it in a vise or hammering it. A good weld will not break; it will bend. If it breaks, examine the break: if it's clean, the metal didn't reach welding heat; if it's dirty, you didn't clean or flux enough.

Remember, forge welding takes practice. Don't be discouraged if your first attempts fail. In Africa, apprentices often spend weeks perfecting this skill. Start with small pieces and simple joints. As you gain confidence, try more complex shapes. With time, you'll be able to weld without hesitation, creating strong, beautiful joints that will last for generations.

6. Troubleshooting Failed Welds

Even experienced blacksmiths sometimes have welds fail. The key is to diagnose the problem and correct it. Here are common issues and solutions. First, if the weld doesn't stick at all, the metal likely wasn't hot enough. Forge welding requires a bright yellow-white heat. If the metal was orange or red, it's too cold. Solution: heat longer and use a hotter fire. In a charcoal forge, ensure good air blast and use small pieces of charcoal to concentrate heat.

Second, if the weld sticks but breaks easily, the surfaces were dirty or not fluxed properly. Scale, rust, or dirt prevents bonding. Always clean with a wire brush and apply flux immediately before heating. If you see black spots in the weld, that's trapped scale. Solution: clean more thoroughly and use fresh flux.

Third, if the metal burns (sparks fly off), it's too hot. Burnt metal is ruinedit becomes brittle and cannot be welded. Solution: watch the color carefully. Remove the metal as soon as it reaches bright yellow. Don't let it sit in the fire. Use a lower air blast if necessary.

Fourth, if the weld looks good but cracks later, you may have cooled it too quickly. Quenching a fresh weld in water can cause cracking. Instead, let it cool slowly in air or bury it in ash. Also, if you hammered too cold, the weld can crack. Always hammer at welding heat; if it cools, reheat.

Fifth, if the joint is weak, you may not have used enough pressure or the joint design was poor. For a lap weld, make sure you hammer the entire overlap, not just the edges. For a scarf weld, ensure the tapers fit tightly. Use a wire brush to remove flux between heats. In Africa, where humidity can cause rust, store your metal dry.

Finally, practice makes perfect. Keep a notebook of your attempts: note the heat color, flux type, and hammer blows. Over time, you'll learn to read the fire and metal. Don't fear failure; every failed weld teaches you something. With persistence, you'll master this ancient skill.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Forge Welding for the African Blacksmith Workshop

Forge welding is one of the oldest metal-joining techniques in the world, and it remains a commercially valuable skill for blacksmiths across Africa. In markets from Kano to Kumasi, from Nairobi's Kamukunji to Johannesburg's informal industrial zones, the ability to join two pieces of mild steel or wrought iron into a single, solid mass without electricity or expensive consumables gives a workshop a genuine competitive edge. This practical reference consolidates the key knowledge from the module into a format you can pin to the wall of your forge, consult before a job, and use to train apprentices. It is written for working smiths who need results they can sell: gates, grills, tools, agricultural implements, and repair work.

Forge welding is not merely a traditional skill; it is a business capability. A smith who can reliably weld can take on repair jobs that others refuse, fabricate larger pieces from smaller stock, and reduce waste by joining offcuts. In many African markets, steel stock is expensive and transport costs are high, so the ability to extend material through welding directly improves margins. The following reference covers principles, surface preparation, flux selection, process steps, practice joints, troubleshooting, and the safety and business considerations that matter most in a real workshop.

Core Principles at a Glance

Forge welding succeeds when three conditions are met simultaneously: the steel is hot enough to become plastic, the mating surfaces are clean and oxide-free, and the two surfaces are pressed together with enough force to cause atomic diffusion. In practice, the smith controls these by managing the fire, using flux, and striking quickly and accurately. The window between "hot enough to weld" and "burned beyond recovery" can be as short as five to ten seconds in a charcoal forge, so preparation and rhythm matter more than raw strength.

Surface Preparation and Flux

Before the steel goes into the fire, the mating surfaces should be ground, filed, or wire-brushed to bright metal. Any scale, rust, paint, or oil will burn into the joint and cause a cold shut. In many African workshops, a simple hand file or angle grinder is sufficient. The scarf or lap should be shaped so that the two pieces make full contact over a broad area, and the joint should be slightly convex so that the first hammer blows drive the centres together.

Flux performs three jobs: it dissolves oxides, excludes air from the joint, and lowers the melting point of surface contaminants so they can be squeezed out. Borax is the most widely available flux in Africa and works well for mild steel. Commercial fluxes (often borax-based with additives) are more forgiving but cost more. Table 1 compares the options.

The Process Step-by-Step

Work in a clean, bright fire with a deep bed of coke or charcoal and a good blast. Bring both pieces to a bright yellow-white heat (approximately 1,2001,300 C for mild steel). Apply flux early, then again as the steel approaches welding heat. When the surfaces look "wet" and begin to sparkle, remove them, tap off loose scale, bring them together, and strike firm, quick blows starting at the centre of the joint and working outward. Reheat and repeat if the weld does not take. Table 2 summarises the process parameters.

SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE LIGHTING THE FORGE
  • Burns: Welding heat steel causes instant, deep burns. Wear leather gloves, a leather apron, and closed leather boots. Never wear synthetic clothing near the forge.
  • Eyes: Wear safety glasses or goggles at all times. Hot scale and flux can fly when struck.
  • Fumes: Borax flux produces irritating fumes. Work outdoors or with strong cross-ventilation. Do not lean over the fire.
  • Fire risk: Charcoal and coke forges throw sparks. Keep a water bucket and sand bucket within arm's reach. Clear dry grass, paper, and fuel containers from the work area.
  • Hot metal: Never leave hot steel where customers, children, or apprentices can step or sit on it. Mark it clearly.
  • Tools: Inspect hammer heads and tongs before each session. A loose hammer head is a fatal accident waiting to happen.

Practice Welds: Scarf and Lap

The scarf joint is the traditional blacksmith's weld. Each bar is tapered to a matching wedge, overlapped, and welded. It is strong, visually clean, and ideal for extending bars, making rings, and repairing tools. The lap weld is simpler: two bars are overlapped and welded along the contact area. It is faster but leaves a visible step, so it is best for structural work where appearance is secondary. Apprentices should practise both until they can complete a sound weld in a single heat. A useful exercise is to weld a chain link from two pieces of 10 mm mild steel rod this builds heat control, flux timing, and hammer accuracy at the same time.

Troubleshooting Failed Welds

Most failures fall into four categories. First, cold shuts the surfaces touched but did not fuse, usually because the steel was not hot enough or the surfaces were dirty. Second, burned steel the metal sparked and became brittle; the only fix is to cut off the burned section and start again. Third, incomplete fusion at the edges often caused by hammering too hard at the start or by flux trapping. Fourth, cracking after cooling common in higher-carbon steels that were quenched or cooled too quickly; normalise by reheating and cooling slowly in ash or vermiculite. In every case, the diagnostic question is the same: was the metal hot enough, was the surface clean, and was the first blow accurate?

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Fire is clean, deep, and at welding heat before steel enters.
  2. Mating surfaces ground or filed to bright metal no scale, rust, or oil.
  3. Scarf or lap shaped for full contact; joint slightly convex.
  4. Flux applied at cherry-red, reapplied at welding heat.
  5. Steel brought to bright yellow-white; watch for the "wet" look, not sparks.
  6. Scale tapped off; pieces brought together quickly and accurately.
  7. First blows light and central, then firm and outward along the joint.
  8. Weld tested, reheated if needed, and cooled slowly for carbon steels.

Business Notes for the African Workshop

Forge welding lets a small workshop compete on jobs that would otherwise require a welder and electricity. In rural areas where grid power is unreliable, this is a decisive advantage. Marketing the skill is straightforward: display a welded chain link, a repaired hoe, or a seamless gate ring, and explain that the joint is solid metal, not a glued or bolted connection. Price repair work by the joint, not by the hour, so customers understand value. Train one apprentice to prepare surfaces and manage the fire while you weld this doubles output without doubling fuel cost. Finally, keep a small stock of borax and a dedicated welding hammer; these are the two tools that most reliably turn a failed weld into a paid job.
