Lesson 6.1~12 min

Pasture Establishment and Management

In this lesson
  • Choose a forage species suited to your rainfall, soil and intended class of cattle
  • Cost a pasture establishment from land preparation to first grazing
  • Manage grazing timing so that forage is harvested at the stage where quality peaks

Pasture is the cheapest feed you will ever produce and the one most often treated as free. Land that grows nothing but volunteer grass, grazed continuously, delivers a fraction of what the same land could deliver under a chosen species and a grazing plan. Establishment is a real capital project with a real payback period, and it should be costed like one.

Species choice comes first and is driven by rainfall, soil and purpose. Napier or elephant grass is a cut-and-carry crop for zero-grazing and small plots and gives very high yields per unit area under fertility and moisture. Rhodes grass and the Chloris types are hay grasses that cure well and tolerate cutting. Brachiaria types are grazing grasses with good drought tolerance. Legumes such as Desmodium, lablab and Lucerne raise protein and calcium and reduce the purchased protein bill; Module 10 Lesson 3 works through the legume substitution arithmetic in detail. The general rule is to match longevity to your tenure: do not establish a five-year perennial on land you may lose next season.

Establishment costs money before it earns any. Worked example for one hectare, using assumed local prices that you must replace with quotes from your own supplier.

  1. Land clearing and first ploughing: 60 USD.
  2. Second ploughing and harrowing to a fine seedbed: 45 USD.
  3. Seed or planting material: 55 USD.
  4. Planting labour: 40 USD.
  5. Basal fertiliser or manure: 70 USD.
  6. First weeding: 35 USD.
  7. Total establishment: 305 USD per hectare.

Now the payback. If that hectare produces 8 tonnes of dry matter a year and the alternative was buying hay at 110 USD per tonne of dry matter delivered, the pasture displaces 880 USD of purchases in a full production year. Even allowing for annual maintenance of 100 USD and a two-thirds yield in the establishment year, the project returns its capital inside the first twelve months. Write that calculation down before you plant, because it is also the number that tells you when a pasture has degraded far enough to be worth renovating.

Stocking rate is the constraint that ruins good pasture. Published figures for extensive pasture-based beef systems put land requirement at roughly 2 to 5 acres per head depending on forage productivity, against 150 to 500 square feet per head in a feedlot. Those numbers are a warning: pasture systems are land-hungry, and the commonest cause of pasture failure in Africa is running more animals than the land can carry through the dry season, not the wet season. Set the stocking rate on dry-season carrying capacity and conserve the wet-season surplus.

Grazing timing controls quality, and the arithmetic is brutal. Forage ADF rises roughly 2 to 4 percentage units per week as it matures. Because TDN% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%), each ADF point costs 0.78 points of digestibility. Three extra weeks of standing at 3 points a week is 9 ADF points, which is 9 x 0.779 = 7.0 points of TDN gone. A pasture at 30 percent ADF is 65.5 percent TDN; the same pasture three weeks later at 39 percent ADF is 58.5 percent TDN, and it will also have more NDF so the animal eats less of it. You lose on both digestibility and intake at the same time.

Rotational grazing is how you control that. Divide the grazing area, move the animals through it, and give each block time to recover. Two published safety rules apply to the timing. Delay spring or post-rain grazing until the forage is at least 6 inches tall, both because that protects regrowth and because very lush young forage carries high potassium and low magnesium and creates grass tetany risk. And keep nitrogen fertiliser moderate: applications above about 100 lb of nitrogen per acre, particularly in drought conditions and on ponded ground, are a listed nitrate accumulation risk, with forage above 1.5 percent nitrate on a dry matter basis being unsafe to graze.

Concrete African context. A 40 hectare beef unit near Mbeya, Tanzania, was grazing one open block continuously and buying hay for four months a year. Splitting the block into six paddocks with a single strand of electric fence and a solar energiser cost under 900 USD and allowed a 30-day rest between grazings. The visible change was that the animals were grazing leaf rather than stem for most of the year, and the hay buying window shrank from four months to under two.

Practical checks on any pasture:

  1. Look at what the cattle are eating, not what is standing. Stem left behind is quality you already lost.
  2. Graze or cut on a calendar tied to plant growth stage, not to when the animals look hungry.
  3. Rest every block long enough for regrowth; continuous grazing selects out your best species.
  4. Set the stocking rate for the driest month of the year.

Hay Production and Harvesting

In this lesson
  • Select a cutting date that maximises tonnes of digestible dry matter rather than tonnes of bulk
  • Calculate the real cost of a bale in USD per tonne of dry matter delivered
  • Store hay so that dry matter, vitamins and money are not lost between baling and feeding

Hay is the simplest form of conservation and the one that suits most African smallholders, because it needs sun rather than capital. Its weakness is that quality is decided in a single irreversible moment: the day you cut. Everything after that is damage limitation.

Cutting stage is the whole game. Forage ADF rises roughly 2 to 4 percentage units per week as the crop matures, and TDN% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%). Yield rises with maturity while quality falls, so there is a crossover point. Consider two cutting dates on the same block.

  1. Cut early. Yield 3.0 tonnes of dry matter per hectare at 30 percent ADF. TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 30) = 65.5 percent. Digestible dry matter harvested: 3.0 x 0.655 = 1.97 tonnes.
  2. Cut three weeks later. Yield 4.0 tonnes per hectare at 39 percent ADF. TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 39) = 58.5 percent. Digestible dry matter harvested: 4.0 x 0.585 = 2.34 tonnes.
  3. So the later cut wins on total digestible dry matter per hectare, by 0.37 tonnes.
  4. But now apply the intake test. If the early cut is 52 percent NDF, DMI = 120 / 52 = 2.31 percent of body weight; if the late cut is 66 percent NDF, DMI = 120 / 66 = 1.82 percent. A 400 kg cow eats 9.2 kg a day of the early hay and only 7.3 kg of the late hay.
  5. On early hay she gets 9.2 x 0.655 = 6.0 kg of TDN a day. On late hay she gets 7.3 x 0.585 = 4.3 kg.

That is the resolution. If you are feeding dry cows on maintenance, cut late and take the extra tonnes. If you are feeding milkers, growing stock or late-gestation cows, cut early, because the late hay physically cannot deliver the daily energy those animals need no matter how much of it you own. Decide what the hay is for before you decide when to cut.

Curing is next. Hay must reach a moisture content low enough to store without heating or moulding; the general storage guidance for feed is to keep it below 12 to 14 percent moisture, which is the same as roughly 86 to 88 percent dry matter, and standard dry hay is treated as about 88 percent dry matter in ration calculations. Bale too wet and you get heating, mould and mycotoxin risk, with aflatoxin limited to 20 micrograms per kg in feed and just 0.5 micrograms per kg in milk. Bale too dry and brittle and you shatter the leaf, which is where most of the protein sits.

Worked example: real cost per tonne of dry matter. Assumed local prices.

  1. Cutting, by hand or contractor: 45 USD per hectare.
  2. Turning and raking labour: 25 USD.
  3. Baling at 0.45 USD per bale for 160 bales: 72 USD.
  4. Transport to store: 30 USD.
  5. Total: 172 USD for 160 bales.
  6. If each bale weighs 18 kg at 88 percent dry matter, that is 160 x 18 x 0.88 = 2,534 kg of dry matter.
  7. Cost per tonne of dry matter: 172 / 2.534 = 68 USD.

Now compare that with a delivered purchase price of, say, 110 USD per tonne of dry matter. Making your own is worth 42 USD per tonne, and on 2.5 tonnes that is about 106 USD per hectare. If the numbers came out the other way, you should be buying hay and using the land for something else. Most producers have never run this calculation and therefore cannot answer the question.

Storage losses are real and budgetable. Published guidance is to account for about 3 percent loss on dry feed. Vitamins fall much faster than dry matter: beta-carotene degrades 33 to 50 percent per year of storage, so year-one hay retains 50 to 67 percent of its original vitamin A and year-two hay only 25 to 50 percent, and brown hay is effectively vitamin A free. Vitamin E declines 2 to 3 IU per lb per year. This is why cattle wintered on stored hay need supplemental vitamin A, especially breeding females, for whom reproductive failure is a listed deficiency sign.

Concrete African context. Rhodes grass hay producers around Nanyuki, Kenya, sell into the Nairobi dairy market and price per bale. Buyers who ask only one question, when was this cut, capture most of the available information without a laboratory. Producers who cut on a fixed calendar tied to heading rather than waiting for a convenient week consistently sell at a premium into the dairy trade, while the late-cut bales go to the beef and maintenance market at a lower price. Same field, same grass, different date.

Store hay off the ground, under cover, oldest at the front, and feed the oldest first.

Silage Production and Fermentation

In this lesson
  • Decide whether silage suits your rainfall, labour and capital better than hay
  • Cut and pack a crop at the dry matter content where fermentation succeeds
  • Cost a silage store per tonne of dry matter and compare it against buying feed

Silage is conservation by fermentation rather than by drying, which makes it the right answer where the harvest window is wet and the wrong answer where capital and labour are short. It preserves a crop cut at a high-quality stage even when the sun will not cooperate, and it stores a wet-season surplus that would otherwise be wasted.

The principle is simple. Chop the crop, pack it hard enough to exclude air, seal it, and let lactic acid bacteria drop the pH until nothing else can grow. Everything that goes wrong in silage is air. Air left in the pit means aerobic spoilage, mould, heating and dry matter loss. Air readmitted at feed-out means the face heats and spoils faster than you can feed it.

Dry matter at ensiling is the single control point. Silage is typically 28 to 35 percent dry matter, against 88 percent for dry hay, and that difference has to be remembered in every ration calculation you do. Too wet and you get a sour, effluent-losing fermentation; too dry and you cannot pack out the air. The field test that costs nothing is the squeeze test: a handful of chopped material squeezed hard should hold its shape and release little or no juice.

Worked example: how much crop do you actually need? A 15-cow herd is to be fed 20 kg of silage per head per day for 120 dry-season days.

  1. Fresh silage required: 15 x 20 x 120 = 36,000 kg, or 36 tonnes.
  2. Convert to dry matter at 30 percent: 36 x 0.30 = 10.8 tonnes of dry matter.
  3. Add conservation and storage losses. High-moisture feed carries about 15 percent loss, so you must ensile 10.8 / 0.85 = 12.7 tonnes of dry matter.
  4. Back to fresh weight at 30 percent dry matter: 12.7 / 0.30 = 42.3 tonnes of fresh crop to be cut and packed.
  5. If your maize or Napier yields 30 tonnes of fresh material per hectare, you need 42.3 / 30 = 1.4 hectares.

That is the calculation that stops a pit running out in week ten of a sixteen-week dry season, which is the most common silage failure on small farms. Notice that the 15 percent loss on high-moisture feed is not optional pessimism; it is published guidance and it is roughly two weeks of feeding on this example.

Costing the store. Assumed local prices for a 40 tonne pit or tube system.

  1. Crop production and cutting: 320 USD.
  2. Chopping, hired chopper or manual: 180 USD.
  3. Plastic sheeting and tubes: 210 USD.
  4. Molasses at 3 to 5 litres per tonne to support fermentation, 150 litres at 0.45 USD: 68 USD.
  5. Labour for filling and packing: 160 USD.
  6. Total: 938 USD for 40 tonnes fresh, which at 30 percent dry matter is 12 tonnes of dry matter.
  7. Cost per tonne of dry matter: 938 / 12 = 78 USD.

Compare that with hay at 68 USD per tonne of dry matter from the previous lesson, or bought hay at 110 USD. Silage is dearer per tonne than home-made hay here but cheaper than buying, and it buys you the ability to conserve a crop in a wet harvest window when hay would simply rot. That is what you are paying the extra 10 USD a tonne for.

Two safety matters belong to silage specifically. Sorghum and sudangrass carry cyanogenic glycosides that release prussic acid; the published management is that hydrogen cyanide dissipates after three to four weeks of ensiling, so sorghum silage must not be opened early, and green sorghum must never be fed as fresh forage because the safety margin is extremely narrow. Second, nitrate in drought-stressed maize and sorghum does not disappear in the pit the way prussic acid does, so a drought crop should be tested: below 0.5 percent of dry matter is safe, 0.5 to 1.5 percent requires at least 2 lb of energy grain per head daily, 1.5 to 3.0 percent is unsafe and above 3.0 percent must not be fed. Judging finished fermentation quality, and deciding what spoiled silage to discard, is covered in Module 10 Lesson 6.

Concrete African context. Dairy farmers in the Nandi hills of Kenya widely use tube silage, packing chopped Napier with molasses into heavy polythene tubes of about one tonne each. The attraction is that capital scales with need: a farmer can make three tubes this year and ten next, without ever building a pit. The discipline that separates the successful users from the rest is simple record keeping on the tubes themselves, marking each with its filling date so that no tube is opened before its fermentation is complete and none is left standing for two seasons.

Pack hard, seal completely, mark the date, and open nothing early.

Crop Residue Utilization

In this lesson
  • Identify which crop residues on your farm can carry cattle and which cannot without treatment
  • Calculate the supplement needed to lift a residue-based diet above the crude protein floor
  • Apply the safety checks that make residues from a drought year fit to feed

Maize stover, sorghum stalks, wheat and rice straw, bean haulms and groundnut tops are already on the farm and already paid for. In most African cattle systems they are the difference between a herd that walks into the rains and a herd that does not. Used badly they are also the reason cattle lose condition while eating all day.

The nutritional problem with residues is specific and easy to state. They are high in fibre, so ADF is high, digestibility is low, and NDF is high so intake is low. Their crude protein sits below the 7.5 percent of diet dry matter that is the lowest level used in NRC recommendations, which means the rumen microbes run short of nitrogen, fibre digestion slows, and intake falls further. The animal is not just eating a poor feed; she is eating less of a poor feed than she otherwise could. Fixing the nitrogen supply is therefore worth more than it looks, because it lifts both digestion and intake at once.

Worked example: what stover alone does to a cow. Assume maize stover at 48 percent ADF and 74 percent NDF.

  1. Energy: TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 48) = 88.9 - 37.4 = 51.5 percent.
  2. Intake: DMI = 120 / 74 = 1.62 percent of body weight. A 350 kg cow eats 350 x 0.0162 = 5.7 kg of dry matter per day.
  3. TDN delivered: 5.7 x 0.515 = 2.9 kg per day, which is 6.5 lb.
  4. Compare with requirement. A lactating cow at even 10 lb of milk needs 12.8 lb of TDN. A dry cow needs roughly maintenance, about 6 to 7 Mcal NEm.
  5. The stover is not close for a producing animal. It is a survival feed for a dry, non-pregnant cow and nothing more.

Now add a supplement and watch what happens. Add 1.5 kg of cottonseed cake at about 40 percent crude protein and roughly 70 percent TDN.

  1. Protein added: 1.5 x 0.40 = 0.60 kg of crude protein.
  2. If stover is 4 percent crude protein, it supplies 5.7 x 0.04 = 0.23 kg. Total 0.83 kg from 7.2 kg of total dry matter, which is 11.5 percent crude protein, comfortably above the 7.5 percent floor.
  3. Energy added: 1.5 x 0.70 = 1.05 kg of TDN, lifting the total from 2.9 to 3.95 kg, or 8.7 lb.
  4. Cost: 1.5 kg at 0.40 USD = 0.60 USD per head per day.

The supplement more than doubles the value of a feed you already own. That is the whole case for strategic supplementation of residues, and it is why buying a small quantity of a concentrated protein source usually beats buying a large quantity of mediocre hay.

Physical and chemical treatment is the other route. Chopping residues to a shorter length reduces sorting and waste. Urea treatment of straw and stover, in which the material is moistened, treated with a urea solution and sealed for several weeks, is widely practised across East and Southern Africa to raise the nitrogen content and the digestibility of low-quality roughage; the technique is well established, but the size of the response varies with material and method, so treat any single published response figure with caution and evaluate it on your own farm by weighing intake before and after. Molasses improves palatability and adds fermentable energy. None of these substitute for the arithmetic above; they change the inputs to it.

Safety gates matter more with residues than with any other feed, because residues concentrate the risks of a bad year. Nitrate accumulates in drought-stressed maize, sorghum and oats and does not disappear in storage: below 0.5 percent of dry matter is safe, 0.5 to 1.5 percent requires a minimum of 2 lb of energy grain per head daily, 1.5 to 3.0 percent is unsafe for grazing, and above 3.0 percent must not be fed. Nitrate concentrates in the lower stalk, so raising the cutting height on a drought crop lowers the risk. Sorghum and sudangrass regrowth carries prussic acid, with young and frost-damaged and rapidly regrowing plants at the highest risk and leaves more hazardous than stems; allow regrowth to reach 18 to 24 inches before grazing. And mouldy residues carry mycotoxins, with aflatoxin limited to 20 micrograms per kg in feed and 0.5 micrograms per kg in milk, and deoxynivalenol advised at 5 mg per kg of grain in diets where grain is no more than 40 percent.

Concrete African context. In the maize belt of central Malawi, stover is traditionally left in the field to be grazed communally after harvest, by which time rain and trampling have destroyed much of its value. Farmers who cut, carry and stack stover under a simple grass roof immediately after harvest, then feed it chopped with a small daily allowance of a protein cake, keep cattle in far better condition through the dry months on exactly the same crop residue their neighbours are wasting.

Treat residues as a bulk carrier that needs a nitrogen partner, and never feed a drought crop without asking about nitrate.

Forage Quality Testing and Analysis

In this lesson
  • Take a representative core sample from a hay lot or a silage pit without biasing the result
  • Specify exactly which analyses to request and what they should cost
  • Handle, label and dispatch samples so the laboratory result describes the feed you actually own

A forage analysis is only as good as the handful of material that reaches the laboratory. The most common reason a lab report does not match what the cattle are doing is not laboratory error; it is that somebody pulled one attractive handful from the front of the stack and posted it. This lesson is about the physical act of sampling and submitting. Reading and using the numbers that come back, including ADF, NDF, RFV and RFQ, is covered in Module 10 Lesson 1.

Start with the definition of a lot. A lot is forage that shares a field, a species, a cutting date and a harvest condition. Never mix two cuttings, two fields or two pits into one sample. If you have three cuttings of Rhodes grass hay in one shed, that is three lots and three samples, or the result describes nothing.

Sampling hay. The correct tool is a hay probe or core sampler, a hollow tube driven into the bale by hand brace or drill, which cuts through leaf and stem in the proportion they actually occur. Grab samples pulled by hand always over-represent leaf, which is the highest-protein part, so a hand sample flatters the hay and you will over-value it.

  1. Select at least 10 to 20 bales at random across the lot, from different parts of the stack, not just the accessible face.
  2. Core each bale in the centre of the end of a rectangular bale, or angled into the curved side of a round bale, to full probe depth.
  3. Empty every core into one clean bucket.
  4. Mix the combined cores thoroughly.
  5. Take a subsample of about 500 g from the mixed material.
  6. Seal it in a plastic bag, press the air out, and label it.

Sampling silage. Silage is more variable than hay and cannot be sampled through the plastic without ruining the seal, so sample at feed-out.

  1. Take handfuls from at least 10 points across the exposed face, top to bottom, avoiding the outer spoiled layer.
  2. Mix in a bucket and take a 500 g subsample.
  3. Silage is wet and will ferment or mould in transit. Either freeze it immediately and post it frozen in an insulated box, or get it to the laboratory within 24 hours.
  4. If the lab is more than a day away and freezing is impossible, ask whether they accept a dried subsample, and dry it in the shade rather than in direct sun.

Sampling standing pasture. Cut at grazing height, not ground level, from at least 10 points along a walked transect, because cattle do not eat the bottom two inches and neither should your sample.

Labelling is where samples die. Every bag needs the farm name, the sample date, the forage species, the cutting or filling date, the field or pit identity and your contact details, written on a label inside a second bag as well as on the outside, because ink runs. Keep a duplicate record in your own book.

What to request. For a routine decision, ask for dry matter, crude protein, ADF and NDF. That is enough to calculate digestibility, intake and relative feed value. Add calcium and phosphorus if you are building a mineral programme. Add a nitrate test on any drought-stressed maize, sorghum or oats, and on any heavily fertilised forage. Add a mycotoxin screen only if the material is discoloured, musty or was baled or ensiled wet, because screens are expensive and are a diagnostic rather than a routine test.

Costs and choices. As a working figure, a routine forage analysis is commonly in the region of 20 to 40 USD per sample, and a test at that price routinely changes a purchasing decision worth hundreds. Two laboratory methods exist. Wet chemistry is the reference method and is slower and dearer. NIRS, near infrared reflectance spectroscopy, predicts ADF, NDF, crude protein, IVDMD and digestibility from light reflectance and is faster and cheaper, with accuracy of roughly plus or minus 2 to 5 percent. Its limitation is that its prediction equations are calibration-dependent and vary by regional forage type and weather, so before you accept a NIRS result on Napier, Brachiaria or maize stover, ask the laboratory directly whether their equations are calibrated for tropical forages. If the answer is no, pay for wet chemistry.

Worked example: is testing worth it? You are offered 20 tonnes of hay at 105 USD per tonne, a 2,100 USD purchase. A 35 USD test comes back at 43 percent ADF, giving DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 43) = 55.4 percent against the 64.8 percent of the hay you already own at 31 percent ADF. On a digestible dry matter basis the offered hay is worth 105 x (55.4 / 64.8) = 90 USD per tonne to you. The test cost 35 USD and identified 300 USD of overpricing on one load.

Concrete African context. A commercial dairy near Jinja, Uganda, sends one core sample of every Napier silage tube batch to a laboratory in Kampala, and files the report with the tube's filling date on a clipboard in the feed store. Over two seasons the file itself became the most valuable document on the farm, because it showed which fields and which cutting intervals consistently produced the better silage, without any additional testing cost.

Seasonal Feeding Strategies

In this lesson
  • Build a month-by-month feed budget that exposes the dry-season gap before it arrives
  • Choose between conserving, treating, buying and destocking on cost per tonne of dry matter
  • Adjust the supplement programme as forage quality falls through the season

Every cattle enterprise in Africa runs on the same annual shape: a surplus of poor-to-good forage in the rains and a deficit of very poor forage at the end of the dry season. Almost every serious loss of condition, fertility and calf value happens in the last eight to twelve weeks of that deficit. The remedy is not a product; it is a budget written before the gap arrives.

Build the budget in tonnes of dry matter, because that is the only unit in which hay, silage, stover and pasture can be compared. Demand first.

  1. Count animals and assign each a body weight.
  2. Estimate intake capacity from the forage you will actually feed, using DMI as a percentage of body weight = 120 / NDF%.
  3. Multiply out. Twenty cattle averaging 350 kg on a 65 percent NDF dry-season forage: DMI = 120 / 65 = 1.85 percent, so 350 x 0.0185 = 6.5 kg per head per day, and 20 x 6.5 = 130 kg per day for the herd.
  4. Over a 120-day gap: 130 x 120 = 15,600 kg, or 15.6 tonnes of dry matter.

Now supply. Count your standing hay, your bales at 88 percent dry matter, your silage at 28 to 35 percent dry matter, and your stackable crop residues, and subtract storage losses of about 3 percent for dry feed and 15 percent for high-moisture feed. Suppose you have 60 bales at 18 kg, which is 60 x 18 x 0.88 = 950 kg, plus 12 tonnes of fresh silage at 30 percent dry matter less 15 percent loss, which is 12 x 0.30 x 0.85 = 3.06 tonnes. Total 4.0 tonnes against a 15.6 tonne requirement. The gap is 11.6 tonnes of dry matter and you now know it in October rather than discovering it in February.

Closing a gap has exactly four options and they should be priced against each other in USD per tonne of dry matter.

  1. Conserve more of your own surplus. From Lesson 2, home-made hay came out at about 68 USD per tonne of dry matter and from Lesson 3 silage at about 78 USD. Usually the cheapest option, but it must be decided in the wet season.
  2. Treat residues. Stover you already own costs only the labour of cutting, stacking and treating, so it is often the cheapest tonne available, but it needs a protein partner as shown in Lesson 4.
  3. Buy in. Price delivered per tonne of dry matter, not per bale, and adjust for quality using the digestible dry matter method from Module 10 Lesson 1.
  4. Destock. Selling animals reduces demand instead of raising supply. A cow you cannot feed through the gap will lose condition, lose a breeding cycle and sell badly at the worst point of the market, which is exactly when everyone else is also destocking.

The supplement programme has to change through the season because forage quality changes. In the rains, forage is young and highly digestible, crude protein is adequate, and the main risks are grass tetany on lush growth and nitrate on fertilised regrowth; magnesium becomes the priority mineral, with lactating cows needing 18 to 21 g per day against 7 to 9 g in late pregnancy. As the season dries, ADF rises 2 to 4 percentage units per week during growth and continues to climb as forage stands and weathers, digestibility falls by 0.779 points for every ADF point, NDF rises so intake falls, and crude protein drops below the 7.5 percent floor. At that point the priority switches to nitrogen and energy: a small daily protein supplement is worth more than a large quantity of extra roughage, because it restores both digestion and intake.

Vitamins are the quiet seasonal casualty. Cattle on dormant or stored forage lose their vitamin A supply, because beta-carotene degrades 33 to 50 percent per year of storage and dormant winter or dry-season pasture is a listed high-risk condition. A gestating 1,200 lb cow needs 34,000 IU per day and a lactating one 54,000 IU. If your cattle have been on brown feed for six weeks, supplement.

Worked example: what does closing the gap cost per head? An 11.6 tonne gap for 20 cattle over 120 days is 11,600 / 20 / 120 = 4.8 kg of dry matter per head per day to find. If treated stover at an all-in 45 USD per tonne of dry matter covers 3 kg of that and a 1.5 kg protein and energy supplement at 0.40 USD per kg covers the rest, the daily cost is (3 x 0.045) + (1.5 x 0.40) = 0.135 + 0.60 = 0.74 USD per head per day, or 1,776 USD for the herd across the gap. Now that is a number you can compare against the value of the condition, calves and milk it protects, and against the alternative of selling four animals.

Concrete African context. Pastoral and agropastoral producers around Kaduna, Nigeria, face a dry season in which standing forage is abundant in bulk but far below the protein floor. Producers who reserve a groundnut haulm stack purely for the last eight weeks, rather than feeding it from the start of the dry season, keep cows cycling through the hardest period. The feed is the same; the timing is the whole intervention.

Write the budget in tonnes of dry matter, write it early, and decide in the wet season what the dry season will eat.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Pasture is left standing three extra weeks and ADF rises 9 points. How much TDN is lost?

Each ADF point costs 0.779 TDN points, so 9 x 0.779 = 7.0 points of TDN.

2. Why should grazing be delayed until forage reaches about 6 inches?

Delaying grazing to 6 inches or more protects regrowth and reduces the grass tetany risk associated with very lush, high-potassium, low-magnesium forage.

3. Roughly how much land does an extensive pasture system need per head?

Published figures give 2 to 5 acres per head for pasture systems, against 150 to 500 square feet per head in a feedlot.

4. Which nitrogen fertiliser situation most increases nitrate accumulation risk?

Heavy nitrogen application, especially above about 100 lb per acre in drought and on ponded ground, produces maximum nitrate accumulation.

5. What should stocking rate be set against?

Setting the stocking rate on dry-season carrying capacity and conserving the wet-season surplus is what prevents pasture failure.

6. Hay is cut at 30% ADF. What is its TDN?

TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 30) = 88.9 - 23.4 = 65.5%.

7. Why can late-cut hay fail a milking cow even when it yields more tonnes per hectare?

Higher NDF lowers intake capacity (DMI = 120 / NDF%), so the cow physically cannot consume enough of a mature hay to meet her daily energy needs.

8. 160 bales weigh 18 kg each at 88% dry matter. How much dry matter is that?

160 x 18 = 2,880 kg fresh; 2,880 x 0.88 = 2,534 kg of dry matter.

9. How much vitamin A does two-year-old hay typically retain?

Beta-carotene degrades 33 to 50% per year of storage, leaving year-two hay with 25 to 50% of its original vitamin A.

10. What is the aflatoxin limit in cattle feed?

Feed is limited to 20 micrograms per kg of aflatoxin, while the milk safety limit is far lower at 0.5 micrograms per kg.

11. What is the typical dry matter content of silage?

Silage is typically 28 to 35% dry matter, compared with about 88% for dry hay.

12. How long must sorghum silage be sealed before prussic acid risk falls?

Hydrogen cyanide dissipates after three to four weeks of ensiling; sorghum silage must not be opened earlier.

13. A herd needs 36 tonnes of fresh silage. Allowing 15% loss, how much must be ensiled in dry matter terms if silage is 30% DM?

36 x 0.30 = 10.8 tonnes DM required; 10.8 / 0.85 = 12.7 tonnes DM must be ensiled to allow for 15% loss.

14. Does ensiling remove nitrate from drought-stressed forage?

Unlike prussic acid, nitrate persists through ensiling, so drought-stressed crops must be tested against the published thresholds.

15. What causes almost every silage failure?

Aerobic spoilage from air in the pit or at the feed-out face is the underlying cause of mould, heating and dry matter loss.

16. Maize stover tests at 48% ADF. What is its TDN?

TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 48) = 88.9 - 37.4 = 51.5%.

17. Why does low crude protein reduce intake of crop residues?

Below the 7.5% crude protein floor the rumen microbes are nitrogen-limited, fibre digestion slows and voluntary intake falls further.

18. How tall should sorghum regrowth be before grazing?

Allow sorghum regrowth to reach 18 to 24 inches before grazing, because young rapid regrowth carries the highest prussic acid risk.

19. Where does nitrate concentrate in a drought-stressed crop?

Nitrate concentrates in the lower stalk, so raising cutting height on a drought crop reduces the nitrate in the harvested feed.

20. A 350 kg cow eats residue at 74% NDF. How much dry matter can she consume daily?

DMI = 120 / 74 = 1.62% of body weight; 350 x 0.0162 = 5.7 kg.

21. Why is a hand-grabbed hay sample misleading?

Hand sampling picks up loose leaf preferentially; a core sampler cuts leaf and stem in their true proportion.

22. How should a silage sample taken at feed-out be handled if the lab is two days away?

Wet silage ferments and moulds in transit, so freeze it and ship frozen, or deliver within 24 hours.

23. Which four analyses form the routine minimum request?

Dry matter, crude protein, ADF and NDF are enough to calculate digestibility, intake capacity and relative feed value.

24. What must you check before trusting a NIRS result on Napier or maize stover?

NIRS equations are calibration-dependent and vary by regional forage type, so calibration for the forage in question must be confirmed.

25. When should you add a mycotoxin screen to a submission?

Mycotoxin screens are expensive diagnostics; request them when the material is discoloured, musty or was baled or ensiled wet.

26. In what unit should a seasonal feed budget be written?

Only tonnes of dry matter allow hay at 88% DM, silage at 28 to 35% DM, stover and pasture to be compared on the same basis.

27. Twenty cattle at 350 kg eat forage at 65% NDF. What is daily herd dry matter demand?

DMI = 120 / 65 = 1.85% of body weight; 350 x 0.0185 = 6.5 kg per head; 20 x 6.5 = 130 kg per day.

28. Which of these reduces demand rather than increasing supply?

Selling animals lowers the herd's dry matter requirement; the other three options add feed.

29. As the dry season advances, which supplement becomes the priority?

Dry-season forage falls below the 7.5% crude protein floor, so a small daily protein and energy supplement restores both digestion and intake.

30. 12 tonnes of fresh silage at 30% DM, after 15% loss, gives how much feedable dry matter?

12 x 0.30 = 3.6 tonnes DM; 3.6 x 0.85 = 3.06 tonnes after a 15% high-moisture storage loss.

Module 6 capstone

Twelve-Month Forage Budget and Production Plan. Step 1: measure or pace out every block of land you can cut or graze and record its area, its forage species and its condition. Step 2: count your animals and convert them into daily dry matter demand using DMI (% of body weight) = 120 / NDF% applied to the forage you actually feed, then multiply by 365 to get an annual herd dry matter requirement in tonnes. Step 3: estimate what each block produces per year in tonnes of dry matter, using your own past bale counts or silage pit volumes wherever possible and clearly marking any figure you had to estimate. Step 4: lay demand and supply on a month-by-month chart and identify the gap months, which in most of Africa are the last eight to twelve weeks of the dry season. Step 5: quantify the gap in tonnes of dry matter and price three ways to close it - conserving more of your own wet-season surplus as hay or silage, treating crop residues, and buying in - using a delivered price in USD per tonne of dry matter for each. Remember hay is roughly 88 percent dry matter and silage 28 to 35 percent, and budget conservation losses of about 3 percent on dry feed and 15 percent on high-moisture feed. Step 6: take one proper core sample of your main conserved forage following the sampling protocol in Lesson 5, send it for ADF, NDF and crude protein, and use TDN% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%) to check whether it can actually carry the class of animal you intend to feed. Step 7: write a one-page plan giving the cutting dates, the conservation method, the tonnes to be stored and the date by which each store must be full.
