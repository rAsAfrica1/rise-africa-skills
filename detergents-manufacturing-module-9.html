Lessons

1. Blending of liquid ingredients

Blending liquid detergent starts with clean water. In most African cities, tap water is fine, but if you use borehole or river water, let it settle and filter through a cloth to remove sand. Measure 70 litres of water into a 100-litre plastic drum. Add 2 kg of caustic soda (sodium hydroxide) slowly while stirring with a long wooden paddle. Never add water to caustic sodaalways add caustic to water. Stir until fully dissolved. This mixture will get hot; let it cool to room temperature.

Next, add 5 kg of sulphonic acid (LABSA) slowly while stirring. The mixture will thicken and heat up. Stir for 10 minutes until no lumps remain. Then add 1 kg of soda ash (sodium carbonate) to neutralise the acid. Check the pH with a pH strip; it should be between 7 and 8. If too acidic, add a little more soda ash; if too alkaline, add a little more sulphonic acid.

Now add 500 ml of perfume and 200 ml of colourant (if desired). Stir well. Then add 1 kg of salt (sodium chloride) dissolved in 2 litres of water. Salt thickens the mixture. Add slowly and stir for 15 minutes. Finally, add 500 ml of preservative (formalin or sodium benzoate) and stir for 5 minutes. Let the mixture rest for 24 hours before adjusting viscosity or filling. Always wear gloves, goggles, and a mask when handling chemicals. Work in a well-ventilated area. Keep children away.

2. Adjusting viscosity and clarity

After blending and resting, your liquid detergent may be too thin or too thick, or it may look cloudy. To thicken, dissolve 100 g of salt in 1 litre of water and add slowly while stirring. Stir for 10 minutes and check. Repeat until you reach the desired thicknesslike cooking oil. Do not add too much salt at once; it can thin the mixture again. If it becomes too thick, add a little water and stir.

To improve clarity, first check pH. If pH is above 8, add a small amount of sulphonic acid (10 ml at a time) until pH is 7-8. If pH is below 7, add soda ash. Cloudiness can also come from undissolved particles. Let the mixture settle for 48 hours, then siphon the clear top layer into another drum. You can also add 200 ml of urea per 100 litres to clear the detergent. Urea helps dissolve ingredients and brightens the liquid.

For a professional look, add 100 ml of optical brightener (like Tinopal) per 100 litres. This makes clothes look brighter. If foam is too much, add 50 ml of antifoam. Always test a small sample: put 5 ml in a bottle with water, shake, and see if it looks like a commercial product. Adjust until you are satisfied. Record every addition so you can repeat the formula. In Africa, many successful producers start with simple formulas and adjust based on customer feedback.

3. Filtration and purification

Filtration removes dirt, undissolved chemicals, and particles that make your detergent look dirty. Start by letting the mixture settle for at least 24 hours in a tall drum. The clear liquid will be on top, and sediment at the bottom. Use a siphon hose to carefully draw the clear liquid from the top into a clean container. Do not disturb the sediment. This simple step removes most impurities.

For better filtration, use a cloth filter. Stretch a clean cotton cloth (like a bedsheet) over a second drum and secure it with a rope. Pour the liquid through the cloth. The cloth catches fine particles. You can also use a nylon mesh or a coffee filter for small batches. For larger operations, build a sand filter: a drum with layers of gravel, coarse sand, and fine sand. Pour the detergent through it. This removes even tiny particles.

If the detergent still looks cloudy, add 500 ml of hydrogen peroxide (3%) per 100 litres and stir. This oxidises impurities and brightens the liquid. Let it stand for 2 hours. Then add 200 ml of sodium metabisulphite to remove excess peroxide. Stir and let settle. Finally, check pH again and adjust if needed. Always filter before filling. Clean filters regularlyreplace cloth or sand when it gets dirty. Proper filtration makes your product look professional and increases customer trust.

4. Filling and packaging lines

Filling and packaging turn your liquid detergent into a sellable product. For small-scale production, you can fill by hand using a funnel and a measuring jug. Use clean plastic bottles or jerrycans of 500 ml, 1 litre, 5 litres, or 20 litres. Rinse bottles with clean water and let them dry. Place a funnel in the bottle and pour the detergent to the desired level. Leave a small air gap at the top. Cap tightly.

For medium-scale, use a manual filling machine. These are available in many African cities for 50,000 to 200,000 Naira. They have a nozzle and a foot pedal. You set the volume, press the pedal, and it fills the bottle. This speeds up work and gives consistent fills. For large-scale, consider an automatic filling line, but start manual and grow.

Labeling is key. Print labels with your brand name, ingredients, volume, and contact details. Use waterproof labels or print on sticker paper and cover with clear tape. In Africa, attractive labels with bright colours sell better. Include instructions in English and local languages if possible. After filling, pack bottles in cartons or crates for transport. Store in a cool, dry place away from sunlight. Always keep records of production date and batch number. This helps if a customer complains. Good packaging protects the product and makes it easy to sell.

5. Preservation and microbial control

Liquid detergents can grow bacteria, mould, and yeast, especially in hot, humid African climates. These microbes cause bad smell, discoloration, and spoilage. To prevent this, add a preservative. Common preservatives are formalin (formaldehyde solution) and sodium benzoate. Formalin is very effective but toxic; use only 0.1% (100 ml per 100 litres). Sodium benzoate is safer; use 0.2% (200 g per 100 litres). Add preservative after all other ingredients and stir well.

Maintain good hygiene. Always use clean equipment and bottles. Wash drums and paddles with soap and water, then rinse with a bleach solution (1 tablespoon bleach per litre of water). Let them dry in the sun. Never mix old detergent with new; this introduces bacteria. Store the final product in a cool, dark place. Heat and sunlight break down preservatives and encourage growth. If you see mould or smell a rotten egg odour, discard the batch. Do not try to fix it.

For extra protection, adjust pH to 7-8. Most microbes do not grow well in this range. Also, avoid adding too much water; use clean water only. If you use natural ingredients like palm oil or coconut oil, they can go rancid. Add 0.1% of vitamin E or BHT as an antioxidant. Test your preservative system by keeping a sample for 2 weeks in a warm place. If it stays clear and smells fine, your preservation is working. Always label with a shelf life of 12-24 months.

6. Troubleshooting common issues

Even experienced producers face problems. Here are common issues and fixes. If your detergent is too thin, add salt solution (100 g salt per litre water) slowly while stirring. If it becomes too thick, add water. If it is cloudy, check pH and adjust to 7-8, then let settle and siphon. If it separates, you may have added too much salt or not stirred enough. Stir well and let rest; if still separated, add 100 ml of urea per 100 litres.

If the detergent smells bad, you likely have bacterial growth. Discard the batch and clean all equipment with bleach. Next time, add preservative correctly. If it does not foam well, you may have too little sulphonic acid or too much salt. Add 200 ml of sulphonic acid per 100 litres and stir. If foam is too much, add 50 ml antifoam. If the colour fades, add more colourant or store away from sunlight.

If bottles leak, check caps and threads. Use new bottles or add a liner. If labels peel off, use waterproof labels or cover with clear tape. If customers complain about skin irritation, reduce caustic soda or add a mildness agent like glycerin (500 ml per 100 litres). Always keep a notebook of your formula and changes. When you solve a problem, write down what you did. Share with your team. In Africa, successful producers learn by doing and adjusting. Don't be afraid to experiment on small batches before scaling up.

<h2>Practical Reference: Liquid Detergent Production</h2>

<p>Liquid detergent manufacturing is one of the most accessible entry points into Africa's fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) sector. Unlike bar soap or powder detergent, liquid detergent requires lower capital expenditure, shorter production cycles, and can be manufactured in batches of 2005,000 litres using basic mixing tanks. From Lagos to Nairobi, Accra to Kampala, small and medium enterprises (SMEs) are capturing market share from multinationals by offering affordable, locally scented, and sachet-packaged liquid detergents tailored to local water conditions and washing habits.</p>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six core lessons of the module into a business-first operational guide. It covers blending, viscosity and clarity control, filtration, filling and packaging, preservation, and troubleshooting with data tables, an equipment diagram, a safety warning, and a quick-reference checklist designed for shop-floor use.</p>

<h3>1. Blending of Liquid Ingredients</h3>
<p>The blending stage determines the active matter content, foam profile, and cleaning power of your detergent. The standard formulation for a general-purpose liquid detergent uses Linear Alkylbenzene Sulphonic Acid (LABSA) as the primary anionic surfactant, neutralised with sodium hydroxide (NaOH) or monoethanolamine (MEA). Sodium Lauryl Ether Sulphate (SLES) is often added as a secondary surfactant for viscosity and foam stability.</p>

<p><strong>Batch sequence (for 1,000 L batch):</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Charge 600 L of treated water (softened or deionised) into the mixing tank.</li>
<li>Slowly add 80100 kg LABSA with gentle agitation (6080 rpm). Avoid splashing.</li>
<li>Neutralise with 3040 kg NaOH (50% w/v solution) until pH reaches 7.58.5. Temperature will rise to 4050C monitor.</li>
<li>Add 4060 kg SLES (70% active) and mix for 15 minutes.</li>
<li>Add builders (sodium citrate, 2030 kg), hydrotropes (sodium xylene sulphonate, 1015 kg), and chelating agents (EDTA, 25 kg).</li>
<li>Add fragrance (510 kg), dye (0.52 kg), and preservative (as per supplier recommendation).</li>
<li>Top up to 1,000 L with water. Mix for 30 minutes at 80100 rpm.</li>
</ol>

<p><strong>Business note:</strong> In East Africa, LABSA is imported at $1,2001,500/tonne (CIF Mombasa), while SLES is $1,0001,300/tonne. A 1,000 L batch with 8% active matter costs approximately $450600 in raw materials, yielding 200 x 5 L bottles retailing at $34 each a gross margin of 4055%.</p>

<h3>3. Filtration and Purification</h3>
<p>Filtration removes undissolved particulates, gel lumps, and foreign matter that can clog sprayers and cause consumer complaints. For SMEs, a two-stage filtration setup is recommended:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Stage 1:</strong> 100150 micron bag filter removes large particles and gel lumps.</li>
<li><strong>Stage 2:</strong> 2550 micron cartridge filter polishes the product for clarity.</li>
</ul>
<p>In high-hardness water regions (e.g., parts of northern Nigeria, Ghana, and Kenya's Rift Valley), add a water softening step using ion-exchange resin or a simple lime-soda process before blending. This prevents calcium soap formation, which appears as white scum and reduces cleaning efficiency.</p>

<h3>4. Filling and Packaging Lines</h3>
<p>Packaging choice is a strategic business decision in Africa. Sachets (single-use 3050 ml pouches) dominate the low-income segment, while 500 ml5 L bottles and jerricans serve middle-income households and institutional buyers (hotels, restaurants, schools).</p>

<h3>5. Preservation and Microbial Control</h3>
<p>Liquid detergents are water-based and therefore vulnerable to microbial growth especially in humid tropical climates. Common preservatives include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Formaldehyde donors</strong> (DMDM hydantoin, imidazolidinyl urea) broad spectrum, low cost, but restricted in some markets.</li>
<li><strong>Isothiazolinones</strong> (MIT/CMIT blends) effective at 1050 ppm, widely used in Africa.</li>
<li><strong>Phenoxyethanol</strong> mild, compatible with most surfactants, used at 0.51.0%.</li>
<li><strong>Sodium benzoate / potassium sorbate</strong> effective at pH below 5.5, less suitable for alkaline detergents.</li>
</ul>
<p>Conduct a preservative efficacy test (ISO 11930 or USP &lt;51&gt;) before commercial launch. Store finished product away from direct sunlight and keep bulk storage tanks covered.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c62828;background:#ffebee;padding:16px;border-radius:8px;margin:20px 0;">
<strong style="color:#b71c1c;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING CHEMICAL HANDLING</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;color:#b71c1c;">LABSA is highly corrosive and causes severe skin burns and eye damage. NaOH (caustic soda) is extremely corrosive. Always wear chemical-resistant gloves (nitrile), safety goggles, and a face shield when handling these materials. Never add water to concentrated NaOH always add NaOH slowly to water. Ensure the mixing area is well-ventilated. Keep an eyewash station and emergency shower within 10 metres of the mixing tank. In case of skin contact, flush with copious water for at least 15 minutes and seek medical attention. Store acids and bases separately in clearly labelled, locked cabinets.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Troubleshooting Common Issues</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Product too thin:</strong> Check active matter content. Add NaCl in 2 kg increments with mixing. If pH is above 9, reduce with citric acid.</li>
<li><strong>Product too thick / gelled:</strong> Add urea or a small amount of water. Check for over-neutralisation or excessive NaCl.</li>
<li><strong>Cloudy product:</strong> Filter through 25 micron cartridge. Check water hardness. Add hydrotrope (sodium xylene sulphonate).</li>
<li><strong>Phase separation:</strong> Usually caused by incompatible fragrance or insufficient mixing. Re-mix at 60C and add a solubiliser.</li>
<li><strong>Poor foaming:</strong> Active matter too low or water too hard. Increase LABSA/SLES ratio or add a foam booster (cocamide DEA).</li>
<li><strong>Foul odour after storage:</strong> Preservative failure. Review preservative type and dosage. Check water quality and tank hygiene.</li>
<li><strong>Colour fading:</strong> Dye incompatible with preservative or pH. Switch to a pH-stable dye and store away from sunlight.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Verify raw material certificates of analysis (CoA) before each batch especially LABSA active matter and NaOH concentration.</li>
<li>Use softened or deionised water; test hardness before blending.</li>
<li>Neutralise LABSA slowly; monitor pH and temperature continuously.</li>
<li>Measure viscosity and pH on every batch; record in batch log.</li>
<li>Filter through 100 micron and 25 micron stages before filling.</li>
<li>Add preservative at the correct dosage and conduct annual challenge tests.</li>
<li>Calibrate filling equipment weekly; check fill weights every 30 minutes.</li>
<li>Retain a 500 ml sample from every batch for 12 months for traceability.</li>
</ol>
