1. Preventive Maintenance Schedules

Preventive maintenance means fixing small problems before they stop your mill. In Africa, most mill breakdowns happen because nobody checked the machine. A simple schedule saves you money and time. Start with a daily checklist: before starting, check oil levels in the gearbox and bearings. Look for loose bolts on the frame and motor. Listen for unusual noise. After milling, clean the machine: remove husk and bran from screens, rollers, and belts. Use a brush and compressed air if available. This takes 15 minutes but prevents rust and clogging.

Weekly tasks: grease all bearings with a grease gun. Check belt tension press the belt midway; it should deflect about 1 cm. If too loose, tighten. Inspect rubber rollers for wear if grooves are less than 1 mm deep, plan to replace them. Check the huller blades for dullness. Sharpen or replace if they don't cut cleanly. Also check the separator screens for holes or tears.

Monthly: change oil in the gearbox if you run the mill more than 8 hours a day. Use SAE 90 gear oil. Check alignment of the motor and main shaft with a straight edge misalignment causes vibration and bearing failure. Inspect all electrical connections for looseness or burning. Tighten terminal screws. Clean the motor cooling fan.

Yearly: replace all belts, bearings, and rubber rollers as a set, even if they look okay. Overhaul the huller and polisher. Replace worn shafts. This costs money but prevents unexpected breakdown during harvest season when you need the mill most.

Keep a logbook. Write down every maintenance action and the date. If you notice a part wearing faster than expected, investigate why maybe the feed rate is too high or the paddy is too wet. A good schedule turns maintenance from a crisis into a routine. Train one person to be responsible for daily checks. In many African mills, a wall chart with pictures helps workers remember steps. For example, draw a grease gun, a belt, and a roller. Tick each day. This simple system can double the life of your machine.

2. Common Milling Machine Faults and Repairs

When your mill stops working, don't panic. Most faults have simple causes. Here are the common ones and how to fix them. First, the mill won't start. Check the power supply: is there electricity? Test with a multimeter. If power is okay, check the circuit breaker reset it. If it trips again, there's a short circuit. Look for burnt wires or a faulty motor. If the motor hums but doesn't turn, the capacitor may be bad. Replace it with the same microfarad rating. In rural areas, low voltage is common use a voltage stabilizer if needed.

Second, the mill runs but no rice comes out. This often means the feed gate is closed or blocked. Open the gate and clear any jam. If the paddy is too wet, it sticks and blocks the chute. Dry paddy to 14% moisture before milling. Also check the huller if the rubber rollers are worn, they won't grip the paddy. Replace them.

Third, excessive vibration. Stop the machine immediately. Check for loose foundation bolts tighten them. If vibration continues, the main shaft may be bent or the bearings worn. Replace bearings. Also check for unbalanced pulley a chipped pulley causes vibration. Replace it.

Fourth, rice breaks too much. This is a common complaint. Causes: roller gap too tight, roller speed too high, or paddy too dry. Adjust the roller gap for most mills, a gap of 0.5 to 1 mm is good. Reduce engine speed if possible. If paddy is below 12% moisture, it becomes brittle. Add moisture by soaking? No better to harvest at right time.

Fifth, the mill overheats. Check lubrication dry bearings overheat. Grease them. Also check if the cooling fan is working. Clean dust from the motor. If the mill is overloaded, feed less paddy per minute.

Sixth, unusual noise. A knocking sound means a loose part tighten all bolts. A squealing sound means a dry or worn bearing replace it. A grinding sound means metal rubbing stop and inspect.

For repairs, you need basic tools: spanners, screwdrivers, pliers, a hammer, and a set of Allen keys. Keep spare parts: bearings, belts, rubber rollers, and fuses. In Africa, many millers wait for a technician from town that can take days. Learn to do minor repairs yourself. If a part is broken and you can't fix it, call a qualified technician. Never put your hand inside a running machine. Always switch off and unplug before repairing. With practice, you can fix 80% of faults in under an hour.

3. Replacement of Worn Parts (Rollers, Screens)

Worn parts reduce milling efficiency and quality. Rubber rollers and screens are the most common wear items. Replacing them is a skill every miller should learn. First, when to replace rollers: check the grooves. New rollers have deep grooves (about 2-3 mm). When the grooves are less than 1 mm, or the rubber is smooth, replace them. Also, if rice breakage increases or husking efficiency drops below 90%, it's time. Rollers usually last 800 to 1,200 hours of operation. In Africa, with dusty conditions, they may wear faster. Keep a spare pair.

To replace rollers: switch off and lock out power. Remove the belt guard. Loosen the tensioner and remove the drive belt. Take off the feed hopper. You will see two rollers: one fixed, one adjustable. Remove the bolts holding the bearing caps. Slide out the old roller. Clean the shaft and bearing housing. Inspect bearings if they are rough or noisy, replace them too. Slide the new roller onto the shaft. Make sure the keyway aligns. Tighten the bearing caps evenly. Adjust the gap between rollers to 0.5-1 mm using a feeler gauge. Turn the rollers by hand to check for smooth rotation. Reassemble in reverse order. Run the mill empty for 2 minutes to check for vibration.

Screens: The screen separates broken rice from whole grain. Holes get clogged or torn. A torn screen lets broken rice mix with whole rice, lowering grade. Replace screens when you see tears or when more than 5% broken rice passes through. To replace: remove the screen frame. Note how it sits take a photo. Remove old screen. Clean the frame. Install new screen usually a perforated metal sheet or wire mesh. Secure with clamps or bolts. Ensure it is tight loose screens vibrate and tear. The screen size depends on rice type: for long grain, use 1.8-2.0 mm holes; for short grain, 1.5-1.6 mm.

Other worn parts: belts (replace when cracked or glazed), bearings (replace when noisy or hot), and huller blades (sharpen or replace when dull). Always use original or high-quality spare parts. Cheap parts fail quickly. In many African markets, counterfeit bearings are common buy from a trusted supplier. Keep a record of when you replace each part. This helps you predict future replacements. With practice, replacing rollers takes 1 hour; screens take 30 minutes. Doing it yourself saves money and downtime.

4. Lubrication and Alignment Procedures

Lubrication and alignment keep your mill running smoothly. Without them, bearings fail, belts wear out, and energy consumption rises. Start with lubrication. Use the right grease and oil. For bearings, use lithium-based grease (NLGI 2). For gearboxes, use SAE 90 gear oil. For chains, use chain oil. Never mix different greases they can react and lose effectiveness.

How to lubricate: clean the grease fitting with a rag before attaching the grease gun. Pump grease until you see old grease coming out of the bearing seal. That means fresh grease has pushed out the old. Wipe off excess. Do this weekly or every 50 hours of operation. For gearboxes, check oil level daily with the dipstick. Top up if below the mark. Change oil every 500 hours or every 3 months, whichever comes first. Drain oil while warm it flows better and carries away dirt. Refill with the correct amount usually 1 to 2 liters for small mills.

Alignment: Misalignment between the motor and the main shaft causes vibration, bearing failure, and belt wear. Check alignment with a straight edge or a laser alignment tool. For most African mills, a straight edge (a steel ruler) works. Place it across the motor pulley and the main pulley. There should be no gap. If there is a gap, adjust the motor position. Loosen the motor mounting bolts. Tap the motor gently with a rubber mallet until the straight edge touches both pulleys evenly. Tighten bolts. Recheck. Also check pulley alignment the belts should run in a straight line. If belts are misaligned, they wear quickly and may jump off.

Belt tension: Correct tension is critical. Press the belt midway between pulleys. It should deflect about 1 cm (10 mm) for a V-belt. If more, tighten. If less, loosen too tight damages bearings. Use a belt tension gauge if available. Otherwise, use your thumb.

Shaft alignment: For the main shaft, check runout with a dial indicator. If runout exceeds 0.05 mm, the shaft is bent replace it. Also check coupling alignment if the mill has one. Use a feeler gauge to check gap at four points (top, bottom, left, right). Adjust shims until gaps are equal.

After alignment, run the mill empty for 10 minutes. Listen for unusual noise. Check for vibration by placing your hand on the bearing housing it should be warm, not hot. If hot, stop and recheck. Proper lubrication and alignment reduce downtime by 50% and extend bearing life by years. Train one person to do these tasks. Keep a log of when you lubricate and align. In dusty African conditions, do it more often every 30 hours instead of 50.

5. Troubleshooting Low Milling Yield

Low milling yield means you get less rice from the same amount of paddy. For example, you expect 65 kg of milled rice from 100 kg paddy, but you get only 50 kg. This is a big loss. Let's find the causes and fixes. First, check paddy quality. If paddy is too wet (above 14% moisture), it sticks to rollers and screens, causing loss. Dry it to 14%. If too dry (below 12%), grains break easily. Soak? No harvest at right maturity. Also, remove stones, mud, and immature grains before milling. Use a pre-cleaner.

Second, check the huller. Worn rubber rollers don't grip paddy well, so some paddy passes through without being husked. That unhusked paddy goes to the polisher and is lost as bran or broken. Replace rollers when grooves are shallow. Also, set the roller gap correctly 0.5 to 1 mm. Too wide, no husking; too narrow, breakage.

Third, check the polisher. If the polisher is overworking, it removes too much bran, reducing yield. Adjust the pressure less pressure means less bran removal. Also, check the screen in the polisher if clogged, rice stays too long and breaks. Clean screens daily.

Fourth, check the separator. A worn or wrong-sized screen lets good rice go to the bran bin. For example, if screen holes are too large, whole rice falls through. Use the correct screen size: 1.8-2.0 mm for long grain. Also, check the aspiration too much air blows away good rice. Reduce air speed.

Fifth, check feed rate. Feeding too fast overloads the mill, causing breakage and poor separation. Feed slowly and steadily. The ammeter should read about 80% of full load. If it's at 100%, reduce feed.

Sixth, check machine settings. The clearance between the huller and polisher, the angle of the separator, and the speed of the aspirator all affect yield. Read the manual. If no manual, experiment: change one setting at a time and measure yield. Keep records.

Seventh, maintenance. Worn bearings cause vibration, which breaks rice. Misaligned belts reduce speed. Lubricate and align regularly.

To measure yield, weigh 100 kg paddy. Mill it. Weigh milled rice. Calculate: (milled rice weight / 100) x 100%. Good mills get 65-68%. If below 60%, investigate. Common quick fixes: replace rollers, clean screens, adjust feed, and dry paddy. In Africa, many millers blame the paddy, but often it's the machine. With proper troubleshooting, you can increase yield by 10% or more. That's extra income for you.

6. Record Keeping and Maintenance Logs

Record keeping is not paperwork for its own sake. It saves you money. A simple logbook tells you when you last changed oil, how much rice you milled, and what repairs you did. Without records, you repeat mistakes and forget maintenance. Start with a notebook and a pen. If you can use a smartphone, use a simple spreadsheet app. But paper works fine.

What to record daily: date, hours run, paddy milled (kg), milled rice produced (kg), and any breakdowns. Also record fuel or electricity used. This helps you calculate cost per kg and profit. At the end of the day, write down any unusual noise, vibration, or smell. If you noticed a loose bolt, write it down and fix it.

Weekly: record maintenance done greasing, belt tension check, cleaning. Note the condition of rollers, screens, and belts. For example, 'Rollers: grooves 1.5 mm, okay.' 'Screen: small tear, replace next week.' This helps you plan.

Monthly: record oil changes, alignment checks, and parts replaced. Note the cost of parts and labor. Also record yield: (milled rice / paddy) x 100. If yield drops, look back at records to see what changed. Maybe you changed a setting or used different paddy.

Yearly: summarize. How many hours did the mill run? How many breakdowns? What was the total maintenance cost? Which parts wore out fastest? This helps you budget for next year.

Keep a separate log for each machine if you have more than one. Use a table with columns: Date, Hours, Maintenance Done, Parts Used, Cost, Remarks. For example:

| Date | Hours | Maintenance | Parts | Cost | Remarks |
| 1/6 | 8 | Greased bearings | Grease | 500 | Normal |
| 2/6 | 10 | Replaced roller | Roller | 20000 | Grooves worn |

At the end of each month, review the log. Ask: Did I do all scheduled maintenance? If not, why? What can I improve? In Africa, many millers keep records in their head, but that fails when you have many workers. Train one person to be the record keeper. Keep the logbook near the mill, not in an office. Use a wall chart for daily checks workers tick boxes.

Records also help when you sell the mill a buyer pays more for a well-maintained machine with logs. And if you apply for a loan, records show you are serious. Start today. Even a simple exercise book is enough. The goal is to know your machine's history so you can predict and prevent problems.

<h2>Practical Reference: Rice Mill Maintenance and Troubleshooting</h2>

<p>In African rice milling, downtime is money lost. A mill that stops for a broken roller, a clogged screen, or a misaligned belt can lose several tonnes of throughput in a single day. For small and medium-scale millers in Nigeria, Tanzania, Senegal, Ghana, and Cte d'Ivoire, the difference between profit and loss often comes down to how well the mill is maintained and how fast faults are diagnosed. This practical reference gives you the schedules, fault tables, replacement procedures, lubrication and alignment steps, yield troubleshooting logic, and record-keeping formats you need to keep a rice mill running.</p>

<p>Use it as a bench reference: print it, laminate it, and keep it near the mill. The tables are designed so an operator or mechanic can act immediately without waiting for a specialist.</p>

<h3>Replacement of Worn Parts (Rollers, Screens)</h3>

<p>Rubber rollers and screens are the most frequently replaced wear parts. In a typical African mill, rubber rollers last 80120 hours depending on paddy variety and moisture; screens last 200400 hours. Replacing them correctly restores milling efficiency and reduces broken grains.</p>

<p><strong>Roller replacement procedure:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Lock out the motor and disconnect power.</li>
<li>Remove the roller cover and loosen the tension bolts.</li>
<li>Slide out the worn roller; note the direction of rotation.</li>
<li>Clean the shaft and check for wear or burrs.</li>
<li>Slide the new roller on, ensuring the arrow matches rotation direction.</li>
<li>Set the gap between rollers to 0.51.0 mm using a feeler gauge.</li>
<li>Tighten bolts, replace cover, and test-run without paddy for 2 minutes.</li>
</ol>

<p><strong>Screen replacement procedure:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Stop the machine and isolate power.</li>
<li>Open the screen frame access panel.</li>
<li>Remove the retaining clips and lift out the old screen.</li>
<li>Clean the frame and check for cracks or distortion.</li>
<li>Fit the new screen, ensuring the perforation size matches the mill specification (typically 1.01.5 mm for whitening).</li>
<li>Secure clips, close panel, and run a test batch.</li>
</ol>

In many African mills, accidental start-up while a mechanic is inside the machine has caused severe injury. Use a padlock and keep the key with the person doing the work. <h3>Lubrication and Alignment Procedures</h3>

<p>Lubrication reduces friction and heat; alignment ensures power is transmitted efficiently. Both are simple tasks that, if done correctly, extend bearing life and reduce energy consumption.</p>

<p><strong>Lubrication:</strong> Use the grease specified by the manufacturer. For most rice mill bearings, a lithium-based grease (NLGI 2) is suitable. Apply 23 strokes from a grease gun every 50 operating hours. Over-greasing can damage seals, so do not overfill. Keep a lubrication chart on the wall showing each point, grease type, and frequency.</p>

<p><strong>Alignment:</strong> Misalignment between the motor pulley and the mill pulley causes vibration, belt wear, and bearing failure. Use a straight edge or laser alignment tool. The allowable offset is 0.05 mm per 100 mm of pulley diameter. Check alignment after any motor removal or belt replacement.</p>

<h3>Troubleshooting Low Milling Yield</h3>

<p>Low milling yield means less head rice and more broken grains. In African mills, yield losses of 1020% are common and directly reduce income. The main causes are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Paddy quality:</strong> High moisture (&gt;14%), immature grains, or excessive foreign matter. Solution: dry paddy to 1214% moisture, clean before milling.</li>
<li><strong>Roller condition:</strong> Worn or glazed rubber rollers. Solution: replace rollers, check gap.</li>
<li><strong>Screen condition:</strong> Torn or blocked screens. Solution: replace or clean screens.</li>
<li><strong>Whitener pressure:</strong> Too much pressure breaks grains. Solution: reduce pressure, adjust feed rate.</li>
<li><strong>Operator skill:</strong> Inconsistent feed or speed. Solution: train operators, use a feed controller.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Step-by-step yield check:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Measure 1 kg of paddy; record weight.</li>
<li>Mill it and weigh head rice, broken rice, bran, and husk.</li>
<li>Calculate head rice recovery = (head rice weight / paddy weight) 100.</li>
<li>If recovery is below 55% for local varieties, investigate the causes above.</li>
<li>Adjust one variable at a time and re-test.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Record Keeping and Maintenance Logs</h3>

<p>Records turn maintenance from guesswork into management. A simple logbook kept at the mill should capture daily operation, maintenance actions, parts replaced, and yield. This data helps you plan replacements, negotiate with suppliers, and prove quality to buyers.</p>

<p><strong>Minimum records for an African rice mill:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Daily production log:</strong> date, paddy milled (kg), head rice (kg), broken rice (kg), downtime (minutes), operator name.</li>
<li><strong>Maintenance log:</strong> date, task, component, parts used, cost, mechanic name.</li>
<li><strong>Parts inventory:</strong> rollers, screens, belts, bearings quantity in stock, reorder level.</li>
<li><strong>Yield log:</strong> weekly head rice recovery percentage.</li>
</ul>

<p>Keep logs for at least 12 months. Review them monthly to spot trends: if roller life is dropping, check paddy quality or alignment. If yield is falling, check screens and whitener settings.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li> Disconnect and lock out power before any maintenance.</li>
<li> Check belt tension and roller gap daily.</li>
<li> Clean screens and aspirator after every shift.</li>
<li> Grease bearings every 50 operating hours.</li>
<li> Replace rubber rollers at 100 hours or when wear exceeds 2 mm.</li>
<li> Inspect screens weekly for tears or blocked perforations.</li>
<li> Record daily production and maintenance actions in the logbook.</li>
<li> Review yield and downtime monthly; adjust maintenance plan as needed.</li>
</ul>
