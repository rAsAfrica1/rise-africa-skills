Market Research and Product Selection

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Easy difficulty

1 Local demand survey

Before you buy a single piece of equipment, spend a week talking to potential customers. You're looking for one clear answer: what product do people already buy regularly, and what do they pay for it?

Walk into 10 shops that sell processed food — supermarkets, tuckshops, market stalls. Ask the owner: "What processed food product sells fastest here? What brand? What price?" Write everything down. Do the same at 5 restaurants and 5 hotels.

At the end of the week, you'll have a list of 5–10 products with real prices. The one that appears most often, at a price you think you can beat, is your candidate. Don't guess. Ask.

2 Raw material availability check

It's no good making tomato sauce if tomatoes are only available two months a year at prices that kill your margin.

For your top 3 product candidates, investigate:

  • Where does it come from? Local farms? Markets? Imports?
  • Is it seasonal? If yes, you'll either process during the season and store, or buy dried/frozen year-round at higher cost.
  • How much does 1 kg cost? In season vs out of season. This is your biggest cost input.
  • Can you buy direct from farmers? Usually 30–50% cheaper than market price.

You want raw materials that are available year-round at a predictable price. If a crop is seasonal, plan to buy in bulk at harvest and process in batches throughout the year.

💡 Cheapest raw materials come from farmer contracts, not open markets. Talk to 3 farmers before you commit to any product.

3 Pricing power station

Your selling price is not what you want it to be. It's what the market will pay. Work backwards:

  1. Retail price of the competitor product = $2.00 for 500 g
  2. Wholesale price (what shops pay) = $1.40
  3. Your price to shops = $1.30 (undercut by 7%)
  4. Your cost to make must be under $1.00 to leave profit

If your ingredients, packaging, and labour add up to more than $1.00, you have a problem — either source cheaper, make a bigger pack for the same price, or pick a different product. Never launch a product at a loss hoping to fix it later.

4 Competition analysis

Drive or walk within 20 miles of your location. List every business making the same or similar product.

For each one, note:

  • Price — take a photo of the shelf tag
  • Packaging — glass jar, plastic bottle, sachet?
  • Quality — buy a sample and taste it
  • Availability — always stocked, or often out?

The gap you're looking for: a product that is often out of stock, or expensive, or poor quality. That's your opportunity. Don't compete on price alone — compete on freshness, local sourcing, or a specific flavour.

5 Seasonality planning

Most produce has a season. This shapes your entire business:

  • Mangoes: November to February in southern Africa
  • Tomatoes: Year-round under irrigation, peak in dry season
  • Peanuts: Harvest March to May
  • Maize: Harvest April to June

Two strategies work:

Strategy A — Match the season. Make mango juice only when mangoes are cheap. Sell for 6 months. Switch to a different product when the season ends.

Strategy B — Buy and hold. Buy in bulk at harvest (cheap), process in batches through the year, sell year-round. Requires storage and cash upfront.

Most small processors start with Strategy A and add B as they grow.

6 Product range decision

Start with one product only. Not three, not five. One.

Why? Because each product needs its own recipe, its own packaging, its own labelling, its own customer education. Doing one well beats doing five badly.

Pick a product that:

  1. Sells in high volume in your area
  2. Uses raw materials you can source year-round
  3. Has a simple process you can learn in 2 weeks
  4. Needs equipment under $3,000 to start

Examples of good first products: peanut butter, tomato sauce, fruit jam, roasted peanuts, cassava flour.

Once you're selling $500/month of product 1, add product 2. Not before.

7 Regulation check

Before you make anything, check what the law requires for food businesses where you operate:

  • Registration — most countries require you to register as a food business
  • Premises — separate areas for raw and finished product, washable surfaces, hand-washing sink
  • Labelling — mandatory elements on every pack (ingredients, weight, best-before, contact)
  • Testing — some countries require lab tests for shelf life or nutrient claims
  • Tax — VAT registration may be required above a threshold

In Zimbabwe: register with the local council and ZIMRA. In the UK: Food Standards Agency and local council. In Nigeria: NAFDAC. In Kenya: KEBS.

Compliance is not optional — selling unlabelled food can get you shut down and fined.

8 Business plan for your product

Once you've picked a product, write a 2-page plan. Not a 50-page one. Just:

  1. Product: what you're making, pack size, price
  2. Raw material: source, cost per unit, seasonal notes
  3. Equipment: what you need to buy, cost
  4. Space: where you'll work, rent or your own
  5. Cost per unit: ingredients + packaging + labour + overhead
  6. Selling price: to shops and direct
  7. Break-even: how many units a month to cover costs
  8. First 10 customers: names of shops or buyers you've already spoken to

If you can't fill in all 8 lines with real numbers, you're not ready to start. Spend another week on research.

✅ The business plan lives on one sheet of paper on your wall. Update it every month as numbers change.
✅ Module 1 checkpoint: You have a one-page plan with a real product, real price, real cost, and at least 3 potential buyers named. Move to Module 2 when that's done.
