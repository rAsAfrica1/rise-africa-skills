Cassava Processing

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Tubers & Roots

1 Cassava varieties and cyanide levels

Cassava comes in two main groups — sweet (low-cyanide) and bitter (high-cyanide). This is the single most important thing to know before you start processing.

Sweet varieties (e.g. TME 419, TMS 30572) have under 50 mg of cyanide per kg of fresh root. You can boil and eat them with simple processing. They're the best choice for a small processor starting out.

Bitter varieties (e.g. TMS 30001, local "bankye") have 100–500 mg/kg. They need proper fermentation, soaking, and pressing to be safe. Get it wrong and the product can poison customers.

Always ask the farmer which variety they're selling. If unsure, do a quick test: peel a small piece, grate it, and smell. Strong bitter-almond smell = bitter variety = full processing required.

⚠ Selling cassava products made from bitter varieties without proper detoxification is illegal in most countries and dangerous. Always process fully.

2 Peeling, washing and grating

Fresh cassava roots spoil within 24–48 hours of harvest. Process the same day or the day after, or the roots turn black and lose starch.

Peeling: Cut off both ends, then use a sharp knife to slice the skin away in strips. Don't leave any dark inner layer — it holds most of the cyanide. Wear gloves; the raw juice irritates skin.

Washing: Rinse peeled roots in clean water. Change water every 30 minutes when processing continuously.

Grating: You can grate by hand (slow — 5 kg/hour) or with a motorised grater (150–500 kg/hour). Most small processors start with a motorised grater at $200–$600. This is your first equipment purchase.

Grated cassava is called "cassava mash". It goes quickly to the next step — pressing.

3 Pressing and dewatering

Fresh cassava mash is about 65% water. You need to bring it down to 40–45% before fermentation or drying.

Hand press (starting): Put mash into cloth bags and press with a screw press or heavy stones for 4–6 hours. Slow but cheap ($50–$200).

Hydraulic press (scaling): A 20-ton hydraulic jack press handles 50 kg per batch in 30 minutes. Cost $300–$800.

Continuous press: For 1 tonne/hour and up — $2,000–$6,000.

The liquid that comes out ("cassava water") carries cyanide. Never pour it onto gardens or into streams — dig a soakaway pit at least 30 m from any water source.

💡 Save the pressed liquid in a drum for 3 days. The cyanide breaks down, and the water is then safe to use on crops.

4 Fermentation for garri

Fermentation is what gives garri its sour taste and makes it safe. It also breaks down more cyanide.

  1. Pack the pressed mash into a clean sack or fermenting bin
  2. Cover with a weight to keep it packed
  3. Leave for 2 to 3 days at room temperature
  4. The mash will warm slightly and smell sour when ready
  5. Fermenting too long (over 5 days) makes it too sour and mouldy

In hot weather (above 30°C), fermentation takes 2 days. In cool weather, 3 days. Test by smell — sharp sour smell, not putrid.

5 Frying garri (toasting)

Frying removes the remaining moisture and creates the dry, crunchy garri that stores for months.

Equipment: A wide shallow pan over a fire, or a mechanised garri fryer. A hand fryer does 20 kg per hour; a motorised fryer does 100–200 kg per hour.

Method:

  1. Heat the pan until a drop of water dances and evaporates instantly
  2. Add 2–3 kg of fermented mash at a time
  3. Stir continuously with a wooden paddle — never stop, or it burns
  4. After 15–25 minutes the garri turns pale yellow-white and feels dry
  5. Sieve out any lumps while hot — a fine sieve (2 mm)
  6. Cool on a clean surface, then pack

Yield: 100 kg fresh cassava → about 25 kg garri. So one tonne of roots = 250 kg garri.

6 Drying and milling to flour

For cassava flour (used in baking and as a thickener), you skip fermentation and instead dry the pressed mash fully, then mill it.

Drying: Sun-dry on clean mats or racks for 3–5 days until it snaps when bent (moisture under 12%). Or use a solar dryer — 2 days instead of 5.

Milling: Feed dried chips or chunks into a hammer mill. Sieve through a 250–300 micron screen for fine flour. A basic hammer mill is $400–$1,200 and does 200–500 kg/hour.

High-quality cassava flour (HQCF): Grade A — used by food companies and bakeries. Requires cleaner processing, no sand, no black specks, moisture under 10%.

💰 HQCF sells for 2–3× the price of regular cassava flour. If you can get a bakery contract, this is where the real money is.

7 Cassava starch extraction

Cassava starch is a premium product used in food, textiles, paper, and cosmetics. It sells for $600–$1,500 per tonne on world markets.

Process:

  1. Grate fresh cassava very finely
  2. Mix with water (1 part mash to 4 parts water)
  3. Sieve through a fine screen (100–150 microns) to remove fibre
  4. Let the milk-like liquid settle in a tank for 4–6 hours
  5. Pour off the water — the white starch is at the bottom
  6. Wash the starch twice more with fresh water
  7. Dry in the sun or oven until it cracks (10–12% moisture)
  8. Mill and sieve to fine powder

Yield: 1 tonne fresh cassava → 150–250 kg starch. The exact yield depends on variety and freshness.

8 Packaging, pricing and shelf life

Packaging:

  • Garri: 1 kg and 5 kg polythene bags with printed labels
  • Flour: 1 kg, 5 kg, 25 kg paper or poly-lined bags
  • Starch: 25 kg multi-wall paper bags for industry; 500 g jars for retail

Shelf life:

  • Garri — 6–12 months in sealed bags, dry storage
  • Flour — 6–12 months
  • Starch — 2 years or more if kept dry

Pricing (African markets, 2026):

  • Garri retail: $1.50–$3.00 per kg
  • Cassava flour: $1.20–$2.50 per kg
  • HQCF: $2.50–$4.50 per kg (bakery grade)
  • Starch: $0.60–$1.50 per kg in bulk
✅ Add value: sell flavoured garri (with sugar and groundnut), pre-mixed cassava fufu, or instant cassava porridge for higher margins.
