1. Milking Techniques and Hygiene

Milking a goat properly takes about 5 minutes per goat once you are skilled. Wash your hands with soap and clean water before touching the udder. Use a stainless steel bucket, not plastic, because plastic scratches hide bacteria. Clean the udder with a warm damp cloth, then dry with a clean towel. Discard the first few squirts of milk into a separate cup because they contain the most bacteria. Squeeze the teat gently but firmly between thumb and forefinger, then roll your fingers down to push milk out. Do not pull or yank. Use a full-hand grip, not just two fingers. Keep a calm environment; goats milk best when relaxed. In Africa, many farmers milk twice a day: early morning and late afternoon. Leave the kid with the mother at night if you milk in the morning, or separate them for 12 hours before milking. Always milk at the same times daily. After milking, dip teats in a teat dip solution (iodine-based) to prevent mastitis. Strain the milk through a clean cloth or filter into a clean container. Wash all equipment with hot water and dish soap, then rinse with clean water and air dry upside down. Never use the same cloth for cleaning udders and equipment. Keep records: date, goat ID, amount of milk. A good dairy goat gives 2-3 litres per day. If milk is bloody or lumpy, that goat has mastitis; treat it and do not sell that milk.

2. Milk Handling and Storage

Fresh goat milk spoils within 2 hours in African heat if not cooled. After straining, put milk in a clean glass or food-grade plastic container with a tight lid. Cool it immediately by placing the container in a bucket of cold water, or in a fridge if you have one. If no fridge, use a clay pot (zeer) or a wet sack: put the milk container inside a larger pot, fill the gap with wet sand, and keep it in the shade. Change the wet sand every few hours. This can keep milk cool for up to 12 hours. Never store milk in metal containers like aluminium pots because they give a metallic taste. Keep milk away from strong smells like onions, diesel, or soap. Use within 48 hours if refrigerated, or within 8 hours if only cooled with wet sand. For longer storage, boil the milk: heat to 85C (just before boiling), hold for 15 seconds, then cool quickly. This pasteurisation kills most bacteria and gives 5-7 days in a fridge. If you sell raw milk, label it clearly and tell customers to boil before drinking. Always use clean containers: wash with hot water and soap, then rinse with boiling water. Never mix morning and evening milk unless both are cooled to the same temperature. Test milk before selling: smell it (should be fresh, slightly sweet), look at it (no lumps), and taste a drop (not sour). If it smells sour or looks stringy, discard it.

3. Milk Processing: Cheese and Yogurt

To make yogurt: heat 1 litre of goat milk to 85C, then cool to 45C (you can test with a clean finger it should feel warm but not hot). Add 2 tablespoons of plain yogurt with live cultures as a starter. Stir well, cover, and keep warm (around 40-45C) for 6-8 hours. In Africa, wrap the pot in a blanket or put it in a thermos. When it thickens, refrigerate. For cheese: heat 4 litres of milk to 32C. Add 1/2 teaspoon of rennet diluted in 1/4 cup of cool water. Stir gently for 30 seconds, then leave for 45 minutes until curds form. Cut curds into 1 cm cubes, then heat slowly to 40C while stirring. Drain whey through a cheesecloth. For a simple fresh cheese (like chvre), hang the curds in cloth for 6 hours, then add salt and herbs. Press for a firmer cheese. Yield: 4 litres of milk gives about 500g of fresh cheese. Always use clean pots and utensils. For a soft cheese, you can also add lemon juice (1/4 cup per litre) to hot milk to curdle it. That gives paneer-style cheese. Store cheese in a fridge or cool place for up to 2 weeks. Yogurt lasts 1-2 weeks refrigerated. Sell in clean jars with lids. Label with date and type.

4. Dairy Goat Management

Dairy goats need better feed than meat goats. A milking doe needs 16-18% protein in her diet. Give her 1 kg of dairy meal (commercial or homemade: maize bran, cottonseed cake, and minerals) per 2 litres of milk produced. Also provide free-choice hay or fresh legume fodder like lucerne, desmodium, or sweet potato vines. Clean water at all times: a milking doe drinks 10-15 litres daily. Without water, milk drops fast. Minerals are critical: provide a salt lick with copper and selenium. In Africa, copper deficiency is common in goats, causing poor milk and health. Keep goats in a clean, dry house with 1.5 square metres per adult goat. Dirty bedding causes mastitis and foot rot. Trim hooves every 3 months. Vaccinate against CCPP and enterotoxaemia. Deworm every 3-6 months depending on load. Breed dairy goats like Toggenburg, Saanen, or their crosses with local goats for adaptability. A good doe produces 2-4 litres per day for 10 months after kidding. To keep milk flowing, milk twice daily at 12-hour intervals. Dry off the doe 2 months before next kidding. Keep records of milk per goat per day. Cull does that give less than 1 litre per day after their second kidding. Provide shade and ventilation; heat stress reduces milk by up to 30%.

5. Milk Quality Testing

You can test milk quality without a lab. First, the alcohol test: mix equal parts milk and 68% ethanol (or strong local gin) in a clean bottle. If curds form, the milk is acidic or has mastitis reject it. Second, the clot-on-boiling test: boil a small sample. If it curdles, it is not fresh. Third, the lactometer test: use a lactometer to check density. Pure goat milk reads 1.026-1.034 at 20C. If lower, it may be watered down. Fourth, the sediment test: filter 500 ml through a white cloth. Dirt or manure means poor hygiene. Fifth, smell and taste: fresh goat milk has a slightly sweet taste and no strong smell. Sour smell means bacteria. For mastitis, use the California Mastitis Test (CMT): mix milk with CMT reagent, look for gel formation. Or simply strip milk onto a dark surface lumps, blood, or watery milk indicate mastitis. Always test before selling or processing. Keep a thermometer to check milk temperature: should be below 4C for cold chain. If you sell to a dairy, they will test with these methods. Train your workers to do the alcohol test daily. Record results per goat. If a goat fails, treat and do not mix her milk with the bulk tank.

6. Marketing Dairy Products

Sell milk, yogurt, and cheese where people already buy food: local markets, schools, hospitals, and restaurants. In many African cities, fresh goat milk sells for 2-3 times the price of cow milk because it is easier to digest and good for children and the elderly. Target health-conscious customers and those with cow milk allergies. Package milk in 500 ml or 1 litre clean bottles with a label showing your farm name, date, and 'keep refrigerated'. For yogurt, use 250 ml or 500 ml jars. Sell plain and flavoured (vanilla, strawberry). Cheese: sell fresh chvre in 200g rounds wrapped in paper or vacuum bags. Price your products: calculate cost of feed, labour, packaging, and transport, then add 30-50% profit. For example, if 1 litre of milk costs 50 KES to produce, sell at 80-100 KES. For yogurt, 1 litre of milk makes 1 litre of yogurt; sell at 150-200 KES. Build a brand: give free samples at markets. Use WhatsApp groups and Facebook Marketplace to take orders. Partner with local shops that take a 10-15% commission. Deliver on time and keep products cold. Always keep records of sales and expenses. Reinvest profits into better feed and more goats. As you grow, consider selling to supermarkets or hotels they need consistent supply and quality. Never sell spoiled products; it destroys trust.

This Practical Reference brings together the operational numbers, hygiene standards, and processing options you need to run a profitable dairy goat enterprise.</p>

<h2>1. Milking Techniques and Hygiene</h2>

<p>Milk is 87% water and an ideal bacterial growth medium. Every 10 minutes of delay at ambient temperature in the tropics doubles the bacterial load. The commercial consequence is simple: buyers reject high-bacterial milk, processors pay less, and shelf life collapses from 3 days to under 12 hours.</p>

<p>Milking routine for a 24 litre doe takes 46 minutes by hand. Use a clean, dry, quiet pen. Wash hands and udder with potable water (chlorinated at 200 ppm if from a borehole), dry with a clean cloth used for that doe alone, and strip the first 23 squirts into a black-bottomed strip cup to check for clots, blood, or watery milk the first sign of mastitis. Milk with a full-hand squeeze, not thumb-and-finger pinching, which damages teat tissue. After milking, dip teats in iodine or chlorhexidine solution. Feed the doe immediately after milking so she remains standing and the teat canal closes before she lies down.</p>

<p>Milk twice daily at 12-hour intervals. Once-daily milking is possible for low-yielding local does but reduces yield by 2030% in improved breeds. Always milk kids-first in the first 3 days to ensure colostrum intake, then switch to twice-daily for the doe.</p>

<h2>2. Milk Handling and Storage</h2>

<p>The single most important rule: cool milk to 4C within 2 hours of milking and keep it there. In off-grid African conditions, use a chest freezer with frozen water bottles, a charcoal evaporative cooler (zeer pot), or a solar-powered milk chiller. Stainless steel or food-grade aluminium cans are the standard; plastic jerrycans are the leading cause of high bacterial counts and off-flavours on smallholder farms.</p>

<h2>3. Milk Processing: Cheese and Yogurt</h2>

<p>Processing roughly doubles the value of a litre of milk. A litre that sells raw for USD 0.80 becomes 1.2 kg of fresh cheese worth USD 3.004.00, or 1 litre of yogurt retailing at USD 1.502.00. Start with yogurt lower capital, faster turnover, and a ready market in urban and peri-urban areas. Cheese follows once you have consistent cold-chain and a buyer.</p>

<p><strong>Yogurt:</strong> Pasteurise milk at 85C for 15 minutes (kills pathogens and denatures whey proteins for a firmer set), cool to 43C, inoculate with 2% active culture (or 100 ml of previous batch per 5 L), incubate at 4245C for 46 hours until set, then chill to 4C. Flavour with sugar, fruit, or vanilla. Package in 250 ml or 500 ml cups with a foil seal. Shelf life: 1421 days refrigerated.</p>

<p><strong>Fresh cheese (chvre-style):</strong> Pasteurise, cool to 22C, add rennet (1 ml per 10 L) and mesophilic culture, let set 1218 hours, cut the curd, drain in muslin for 1224 hours, salt at 11.5%, and pack. Yield: 1 L milk 200250 g fresh cheese. Shelf life: 714 days chilled.</p>

<li>If you or a worker develop recurring fever after handling raw milk, seek medical testing for brucellosis immediately.</li>

<h2>4. Dairy Goat Management</h2>

<p>A dairy doe needs 1618% crude protein concentrate plus good-quality forage (Napier grass, lucerne, sweet potato vines, or tree fodder such as Leucaena and Calliandra) to produce 3 litres/day. Feed 0.4 kg concentrate per litre of milk above 1 litre maintenance. Provide clean water ad libitum a milking doe drinks 812 litres/day. Minerals (especially calcium and selenium) and a salt lick prevent milk fever and white muscle disease.</p>

<p>Breeding: aim for a 12-month kidding interval. Wean kids at 3 months, rebreed the doe 6090 days after kidding, and dry her off 60 days before the next kidding. Keep records of milk yield, kidding dates, and health treatments this is what turns a goat herd into a business.</p>

<h2>5. Milk Quality Testing</h2>

<p>Three field tests every smallholder can run:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Alcohol test (68% ethanol):</strong> Mix equal parts milk and alcohol. If it clots, the milk is acidic reject or process immediately into cheese. Cost: under USD 5 for a year's supply.</li>
<li><strong>Clot-on-boiling test:</strong> Boil 5 ml of milk. If it curdles, the milk is already sour and unsuitable for sale or yogurt.</li>
<li><strong>Lactometer / density test:</strong> Goat milk should read 1.0261.034 at 20C. Low readings indicate added water; high readings may indicate added solids or mastitis milk.</li>
</ul>

<p>For a co-op or processor, add a somatic cell count (SCC) test healthy goat milk is below 1,000,000 cells/ml, and premium milk is below 500,000. Also test for antibiotic residues if you treat does; withdrawal periods are 35 days for most intramammary antibiotics.</p>

<h2>6. Marketing Dairy Products</h2>

<p>Your market determines your processing. Three realistic channels in Africa:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Neighbourhood / direct retail:</strong> Highest price per litre (USD 0.801.20), no cold chain needed if sold within hours. Best for raw milk where legal, and for yogurt in urban estates.</li>
<li><strong>Co-operative / processor:</strong> Lower price (USD 0.400.70) but guaranteed volume and payment. Requires consistent quality and daily delivery. Examples: Brookside (Kenya), Lamex (Ethiopia), Pearl Dairy (Uganda).</li>
<li><strong>Value-added / branded:</strong> Yogurt, cheese, and flavoured milk sold under your own label to hotels, supermarkets, and schools. Highest margin (USD 1.504.00 per litre equivalent), but requires packaging, cold chain, and food-safety compliance.</li>
</ul>

<p>Price your product against the local alternative, not against your cost. A 500 ml yogurt cup that replaces a USD 1.20 imported brand can sell for USD 0.90 and still return 3 the raw milk price. Build a brand around freshness, hygiene, and local sourcing these are the three things African consumers pay a premium for.</p>
