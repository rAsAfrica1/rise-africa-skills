1. Choosing color schemes that reflect African fabrics and landscapes

Start by looking at the event's purpose. A wedding needs warm, joyful colors; a funeral calls for subdued, respectful tones. Go outside and study the landscape: the red earth of Ghana, the green hills of Kenya, the blue ocean of Zanzibar. These natural palettes always work. For a wedding, try burnt orange (like sunset), deep indigo (like night sky), and gold (like savanna grass). For a funeral, use black, white, and muted brown or grey. Corporate events need clean, professional colors: navy, grey, and white with one accent like teal or burgundy.

Next, pick three colors: one dominant (60% of decor), one secondary (30%), and one accent (10%). For example, a Ghanaian wedding might use kente-inspired gold as dominant, deep green as secondary, and red as accent. Write these percentages down. If you're using African fabrics, pull colors directly from them. Take a photo of the fabric and use a free app like Color Grab to identify the exact shades. Then buy paint, ribbon, or cloth in those shades.

Don't forget lighting changes color. Candles and warm fairy lights make colors look richer; solar lamps give a cooler, whiter light. Test your fabric under the actual lighting you'll use. Also consider the time of day: daytime events can handle bold contrasts, while evening events need softer, deeper tones. If you're on a budget, use one strong color and let white or beige do the rest. You can rent colored overlays for lights instead of buying new fabrics.

Finally, involve the client. Show them three sample boards made from local materials: a piece of ankara, a swatch of mud cloth, a photo of a landscape. Ask which one feels right. This avoids expensive mistakes. Remember, a simple two-color scheme done well looks better than five colors fighting each other. Practice by creating a palette for a fake event every week. In no time, you'll choose colors as naturally as you breathe.

2. Using local materials: kente, ankara, mud cloth, and woven baskets

Kente cloth is woven in Ghana by the Ashanti and Ewe people. It comes in strips about 10 cm wide. You can buy it by the yard at markets in Kumasi or Accra. For decor, use kente as table runners, chair sashes, or backdrop panels. A single strip can wrap a vase. Because kente is expensive, use it as an accent, not the main fabric. For example, a 2-meter strip costs about 50-100 cedis. Cut it into 10 cm pieces for napkin rings.

Ankara (also called Dutch wax) is printed cotton, sold in 6-yard pieces. It's cheaper and comes in bright patterns. Use ankara for tablecloths, cushion covers, or draped ceilings. One 6-yard piece can cover a 1.5m round table. To avoid overwhelming patterns, mix ankara with plain white or black fabric. Mud cloth (bogolanfini) from Mali is hand-painted with fermented mud. It has earthy brown and white geometric patterns. Use it for wall hangings, floor runners, or as a photo backdrop. It's heavier than ankara, so sew a rod pocket if hanging.

Woven baskets are everywhere: Ghana's Bolga baskets, Rwanda's agaseke, Kenya's kiondo. Use them as centerpieces, filled with fruit, flowers, or candles. A medium Bolga basket costs 30-50 cedis. For a rustic look, hang small baskets from a ceiling. For a modern look, spray-paint them gold or white. You can also use baskets as lampshades by cutting a hole in the bottom and fitting a bulb.

When sourcing, go to local markets early in the morning for the best prices. Buy in bulk with other event planners to get discounts. Always wash new fabrics in cold water to prevent bleeding. Iron on low heat. Store mud cloth away from direct sunlight to prevent fading. If you're renting, check for tears and stains before signing. A good rule: use at least one local material in every event. It tells a story and supports local artisans.

3. Designing table settings for weddings, funerals, and corporate events

For a wedding, start with a round table (1.5m diameter) that seats 8-10. Use a floor-length tablecloth in your main color (e.g., ivory or gold). Add a runner in kente or ankara. Place a centerpiece: a low arrangement of local flowers (protea, hibiscus, or roses) in a woven basket. Add two candles in glass holders. For each guest, set a charger plate, a white dinner plate, a folded napkin (tie with a strip of ankara), and a simple cutlery set. A small place card with the guest's name is a nice touch. Keep it low so people can see each other. Budget: 200-500 cedis per table if you rent linens and buy local flowers.

Funeral table settings are more subdued. Use white or black tablecloths, no bright patterns. Centerpiece: a single white candle and a small arrangement of white flowers (lilies or chrysanthemums) or greenery. Avoid glitter or loud colors. Napkins should be plain white or black. Place a framed photo of the deceased on a side table, not the dining table. For the family table, add a special cloth (e.g., a plain kente strip) as a sign of respect. Keep the setting simple and dignified. Budget: 100-200 cedis per table because you can reuse plain linens.

Corporate events need a clean, professional look. Use rectangular tables (1.8m x 0.75m) with white or grey tablecloths. Add a branded runner with the company logo if possible. Centerpiece: a small potted plant (aloe or snake plant) or a bowl of fresh fruit. Include a notepad, pen, and water glass for each attendee. For a conference, use a table for registration with a branded backdrop. For a gala dinner, add a small vase of white roses and a table number. Budget: 150-300 cedis per table. Always confirm the number of guests and table sizes with the venue. Arrive 3 hours early to set up. Use a checklist: linens, plates, glasses, cutlery, napkins, centerpieces, candles, place cards. Test the height of centerpieces by sitting at the table â they should not block eye contact.

4. Creating backdrops with rented or DIY fabric drapes

A backdrop is the focal point behind the head table, cake, or photo area. You can rent or make one. Renting from a local event company costs 500-1500 cedis for a 3m x 2.5m pipe-and-drape setup. If you rent, confirm delivery, setup, and takedown times. Check for stains or tears. For DIY, you need a frame: PVC pipes (2.5cm diameter) or wooden poles. Build a rectangle 3m wide and 2.5m tall. Join corners with elbow connectors. Secure the base with sandbags or water bottles. Cost: about 200-300 cedis for pipes and connectors.

For fabric, use lightweight, opaque materials. Ankara is too heavy for draping; use it as an accent panel. Instead, buy 6-8 meters of plain cotton or polyester in your main color. Sheer fabrics like organza or tulle add elegance but need doubling. To drape, start at the top center, staple or tie the fabric to the pipe, then pull it to one side in soft folds. Use zip ties or safety pins. For a gathered look, bunch the fabric every 30 cm. For a flat panel, pull tight and staple. Leave the bottom 10 cm above the floor to avoid dirt.

Add layers: a solid backdrop with a contrasting fabric swag across the top. Or hang a mud cloth panel in the center. For a photo backdrop, make it wider (4m) so groups fit. Add a small bench or two chairs. Lighting will make or break it: place two uplights (rented or DIY with LED bulbs) at the base pointing up. If using candles, put them in glass hurricanes on the floor. For outdoor events, anchor everything well; wind will pull down a loose backdrop. Test by gently shaking the frame.

DIY saves money but takes time. Practice setting up in your yard before the event. Time yourself: a 3m backdrop should take 30-45 minutes with two people. Always have a repair kit: extra fabric, zip ties, scissors, duct tape. If the event is far, transport pipes in a pickup truck. Charge clients for backdrop rental as a separate line item â 800-2000 cedis depending on size and fabric. This is a profit center.

5. Incorporating lighting: candles, fairy lights, and solar lamps

Lighting sets the mood. For weddings, use warm white fairy lights (2700K-3000K). A 10-meter string with 100 LEDs costs 50-100 cedis. Drape them along backdrops, table edges, or ceiling beams. Use a timer plug to turn them on at dusk. For a romantic look, combine fairy lights with candles. Unscented pillar candles (5cm diameter) in glass holders are safe and cheap: 10-20 cedis each. Place them on tables, along walkways, or in woven baskets. Never leave candles unattended. For outdoor events, use citronella candles to keep insects away.

Solar lamps are perfect for areas without electricity. A good solar lantern (e.g., d.light or Sun King) costs 200-500 cedis and gives 4-8 hours of light. Use them for pathways, parking areas, or outdoor dining. They are bright enough for safety but not for reading. For a softer glow, wrap the lamp in a white cloth or place it inside a frosted glass jar. Solar string lights (with a small panel) cost 300-500 cedis for 10 meters. They charge during the day and run at night. No wiring needed.

For corporate events, use clean, white light. LED panel lights (rented) give even illumination. Avoid colored lights unless it's a party. For funerals, use dim, warm light. Candles in white holders are respectful. Avoid flashing or colored lights. For any event, test all lights the night before. Check batteries, bulbs, and connections. Have spare bulbs and extension cords. Use black cable ramps to prevent tripping. If using generators, ensure they are far from guests and refueled before the event.

Calculate your needs: for a 100-guest wedding, you need about 20 meters of fairy lights, 30 candles, and 10 solar lamps for pathways. Rent a generator (5000-10000 cedis per day) only if you have high-wattage lights. Otherwise, battery and solar are cheaper. Always have a backup: a box of matches, extra batteries, and a torch. Practice turning everything on and off. A well-lit event feels professional; poor lighting ruins even the best decor.

6. Sketching your decor layout on paper before the event

Before you buy or rent anything, draw your layout. Use graph paper (1 square = 0.5 meter) and a pencil. Start with the venue's floor plan. Measure the room or outdoor space: length, width, and height. Note doors, windows, pillars, and power outlets. Draw these to scale. For a 10m x 15m hall, use 20 x 30 squares. Mark the entrance, stage, and guest tables. If you don't have a floor plan, pace out the dimensions â one step is about 0.75 meter.

Next, place the key elements. For a wedding: head table at the center back, dance floor in front, guest tables around. Leave 1.5m between tables for walking. Mark the backdrop behind the head table. For a funeral: coffin or photo area at one end, family tables near it, guest tables further back. For corporate: stage with lectern, rows of chairs, registration table near entrance. Use different colors for different elements: red for tables, blue for stage, green for walkways. Write dimensions on the sketch: table diameter, backdrop width, aisle width.

Now add decor details. Draw where each centerpiece, candle, or basket will go. Mark the path of fairy lights. Note where you need power for lights or music. If you're renting, list items with quantities: 10 round tables (1.5m), 80 chairs, 1 backdrop (3m x 2.5m), 20 meters of fairy lights. This becomes your shopping list. Share the sketch with your client and team. Adjust based on their feedback. A sketch prevents buying too much or too little. It also helps you set up faster because you know exactly where everything goes.

Finally, make a timeline. For a 6pm event, start setup at 10am. Sketch the order: backdrops first, then tables, then linens, then centerpieces, then lighting. Assign tasks to your team. Keep the sketch with you on event day. After the event, note what worked and what didn't. Save the sketch for future events. With practice, you'll sketch a full layout in 20 minutes. This simple step separates amateurs from professionals.

3. Designing Table Settings for Weddings, Funerals, and Corporate Events

Table settings are where your profit margin lives. A well-designed table uses fewer, larger elements rather than many small ones â this reduces both cost and setup time. The three event types have different emotional registers:

  • Weddings: celebratory, layered, textured. Use kente runners, ankara napkins, tall floral or basket centerpieces, and warm lighting.
  • Funerals: dignified, restrained, respectful. Avoid bright reds and loud patterns. Use muted mud cloth, white or cream linen, and green foliage. In many African traditions, white and black are the appropriate colors; check with the family.
  • Corporate events: branded, clean, functional. Use corporate colors in napkins and signage, minimal centerpieces that do not block sightlines, and reliable lighting for presentations.

4. Creating Backdrops with Rented or DIY Fabric Drapes

The backdrop is the most photographed element of any event. Clients remember it, and it drives referrals. You have two business models:

  • Rented pipe-and-drape systems: fast to set up, professional finish, but requires capital outlay (USD 400â1,500 for a starter kit) and storage.
  • DIY fabric drapes: lower capital, higher labor. Use local fabrics, PVC pipes, bamboo, or wooden frames. Best for smaller venues and repeat clients.

Practical rule: For every 3m of backdrop width, allow 6â9m of fabric (2â3x fullness). Always weigh down the hem with sandbags or hidden weights â African venues are often open-air and windy. Steam or iron fabric the day before; wrinkles are the fastest way to look amateur.

5. Incorporating Lighting: Candles, Fairy Lights, and Solar Lamps

Lighting transforms cheap decor into premium decor. In African venues, power supply can be unreliable, so a mixed lighting strategy is both aesthetic and practical.

  • Candles: Warm, intimate, cheap. Use LED candles where open flame is unsafe or prohibited. Never leave flames unattended.
  • Fairy lights: Best value for money. Battery and USB versions avoid generator dependence. Use warm white (2700K) rather than cool white for events.
  • Solar lamps: Ideal for outdoor and rural venues. Charge during the day, deploy at dusk. Increasingly affordable across Africa; a good solar lantern costs USD 10â30.

Business tip: Invest in rechargeable LED uplights (USD 15â40 each). They are reusable, safe, and let you charge a premium for "mood lighting" without generator fuel costs.

Open-flame candles near fabric drapes, kente, ankara, or dried foliage can ignite in seconds. Always keep flames at least 30cm from any fabric, use non-flammable candle holders, and never leave a venue with candles still burning. Do not overload sockets or run generators indoors â carbon monoxide kills. Secure all pipe-and-drape frames against wind; an unweighted backdrop can collapse onto guests. Check that rented lighting carries a valid electrical safety certificate. If a venue has no fire extinguisher, bring one. Your reputation and your client's safety are worth more than any booking.

6. Sketching Your Decor Layout on Paper Before the Event

Never set up without a plan. A simple hand sketch saves hours and prevents costly mistakes. Your sketch should show:

  • Venue dimensions (measure, don't guess)
  • Entrance, stage/backdrop, head table, guest tables, buffet, and dance floor
  • Power points and lighting positions
  • Walkways and emergency exits
  • Where your team will stand and store equipment

Photograph the venue at the same time of day as the event. This reveals natural light direction and shadow patterns that will affect your design. Share the sketch with the client for written approval before you buy a single meter of fabric.
