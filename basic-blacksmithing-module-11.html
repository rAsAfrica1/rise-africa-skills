1. Selecting Steel for Blades

For a blade that holds an edge and survives hard use, you need steel with enough carbon to harden. In most African markets, the easiest source is an old leaf spring from a truck or car. These are usually 5160 or similar spring steel, with around 0.6% carbon. Another good option is a bastard file they are often 1095 or W1 steel, with 0.8-1.0% carbon. Avoid mild steel (like rebar or angle iron) because it will not harden. You can test a piece: touch it to a grinding wheel. If it throws long yellow sparks with a starburst at the end, it has high carbon. If sparks are short and red, it's mild steel.

When you find a candidate steel, cut a small piece and heat it to bright orange (about 800C), then quench in water. Put it in a vise and try to bend it with a hammer. If it snaps or cracks, it's hardenable. If it bends without cracking, it's likely mild steel. For consistent results, try to get a full leaf spring from a single vehicle. Clean off all rust, dirt, and paint before forging. Use a wire brush or soak in vinegar overnight.

In many African towns, scrap yards and mechanics are your best sources. Ask for 'lorry spring' or 'motor spring'. A single leaf spring can yield 5-10 knife blanks. If you can't find spring steel, buy a new piece of 1080 or 5160 from a steel supplier but that costs more. Always keep a few known pieces for reference. Mark your steel with a paint pen so you don't mix up mild and high-carbon.

Remember: the steel's carbon content determines how hard the blade can get. Too little carbon, and the edge rolls. Too much carbon (over 1.2%), and the blade becomes brittle and hard to forge. For a beginner, 5160 or 1080 is ideal. They are forgiving in the forge and heat treat. Start with one type and learn its behavior before experimenting.

2. Forging a Simple Knife Profile

Start with a piece of steel about 20 cm long, 3 cm wide, and 5 mm thick. Heat it in your forge until it's bright orange (around 900C). Use tongs to hold it on the anvil. First, draw out the tang: hammer the last 5 cm to make it thinner and longer this will go into the handle. Work from the tip of the tang back toward the blade. Keep the steel hot; if it turns dark red, put it back in the fire.

Next, form the blade. For a simple drop-point knife, hammer the tip to a point. Hold the steel at an angle and strike the edge to draw it out. You want the blade to be about 15 cm long and 2.5 cm wide at the base, tapering to a point. Use light, controlled hammer blows. Don't try to shape everything in one heat. Reheat as needed. If the steel starts to crack, stop and reheat cold steel cracks.

After rough shaping, use a cross-peen hammer to set the bevels. The bevel is the sloping part that goes down to the cutting edge. Strike at a 45-degree angle along the edge, first on one side, then the other. Keep the center of the blade thick (about 3 mm) so it doesn't warp during heat treat. The edge should be about 1 mm thick. Check your work often by eye you want a symmetrical profile.

Once you have a basic knife shape, use a file to clean up the profile while the steel is still annealed (soft). You can also use a belt grinder if you have one. Forge the blade as close to final shape as possible to save grinding time. A good forging leaves only 1-2 mm of material to remove. When done, let the blade cool slowly in ash or vermiculite. This is normalizing, which we'll cover next.

3. Normalizing and Annealing Blades

After forging, the steel has internal stresses that can cause cracks or warps during heat treat. Normalizing relieves these stresses and refines the grain. To normalize, heat the blade to 850C just above non-magnetic (when a magnet no longer sticks). Hold it at that temperature for 2-3 minutes, then take it out and let it cool in still air. Do this three times. Each cycle makes the grain smaller and the steel more uniform. For a leaf spring, 850C is a bright orange. Do not quench just air cool.

Annealing is different: it makes the steel as soft as possible for grinding and filing. Heat the blade to 850C again, then bury it in dry wood ash, lime, or vermiculite. The key is slow cooling over several hours. In Africa, a common method is to put the blade in a metal box filled with ash, then place it in the dying fire. Leave it overnight. The next morning, the blade should be soft enough to file easily. If it's still hard, repeat the process.

You can also anneal by heating to 850C and then slowly cooling in a charcoal fire that is allowed to die down. Do not use water or oil. Slow cooling lets the carbon form soft pearlite instead of hard martensite. After annealing, the blade will be grey and covered in scale. Clean it with a wire brush or sandpaper before grinding.

Why does this matter? If you skip normalizing, the blade may warp or crack during quenching. If you skip annealing, grinding takes forever and you'll burn through belts. These steps are not optional they are the foundation of a good blade. In many African workshops, smiths normalize three times and anneal once. That's a good rule. Always test with a file: if it bites into the steel, it's annealed.

4. Grinding and Shaping the Blade

With the blade annealed and soft, you can grind the final shape. If you have a belt grinder, use a 60-grit belt for rough grinding, then 120, then 220. If you don't, a file and sandpaper will work it just takes longer. Start by refining the profile: make sure the edge is straight and the tip is centered. Use a file to remove the forging scale and smooth the curves. Work slowly and check symmetry by eye.

Next, grind the bevels. The bevel is the sloping part that leads to the cutting edge. For a beginner, a flat grind is easiest: hold the blade at a consistent angle against the belt or file. You want the bevel to go from the edge up to about half the blade's width. On a 2.5 cm wide blade, the bevel should be about 1.2 cm wide. Use a marker to draw a line down the center of the edge this is your guide. Grind until you almost reach the line, then flip and do the other side. Keep the edge thickness about 0.5-1 mm before heat treat.

Don't overheat the blade while grinding. If it gets too hot to touch, dip it in water. Overheating can ruin the anneal and cause cracks. For a file, use slow strokes and keep the file clean with a wire brush. For a belt grinder, use fresh belts and don't push too hard. After rough grinding, move to finer grits to remove scratches. A 220-grit finish is good before heat treat.

Finally, check the edge for straightness. Hold it up to a light you should see a thin, even line. If it's wavy, go back to the file. A little time here saves a lot of trouble later. Once the blade is ground, clean it with acetone or soap and water to remove any oil or dirt before heat treating.

5. Heat Treating a Blade

Heat treating makes the blade hard and tough. You need a forge, a quench tank, and a way to measure temperature. For most simple carbon steels (5160, 1080, 1095), heat to 800-850C non-magnetic. Use a magnet: when it stops sticking, you're there. Don't overheat if the steel sparkles, it's too hot. Hold at that temperature for 1-2 minutes to ensure even heating. If the blade is uneven, it will warp.

Quench in warm oil. Canola oil, peanut oil, or used motor oil works. Heat the oil to about 50-60C warm to the touch but not smoking. Quench the blade edge-first, then submerge completely. Move it around gently to break the vapor barrier. Hold it in the oil until it's cool enough to touch (about 10-15 seconds). For 1095, you may need a faster quench like water, but that risks cracking. For beginners, oil is safer.

After quenching, the blade is hard but brittle. You must temper it. Clean off the oil and heat the blade in an oven or over a fire to 200-220C for one hour. Do this twice. This relieves stress and gives toughness. You can check tempering color: a light straw color (200C) is good for knives. If you don't have an oven, put the blade in a charcoal fire and watch the colors but this is tricky. A kitchen oven is best.

Test the blade: after tempering, a file should skate across the edge without biting. If it bites, the blade is too soft re-harden and temper again. If it chips, it's too hard temper at a higher temperature (230C). With practice, you'll get a feel for it. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. Quenching oil can flare up, so have a lid nearby.

6. Attaching a Handle: Basic Techniques

A good handle makes the knife safe and comfortable. The simplest handle is a stick tang: the tang goes into a block of wood. Start by drilling a hole through a hardwood block (like mahogany, ebony, or camel thorn) that matches the tang's width. The block should be about 12 cm long and 3 cm thick. Use a drill press or hand drill. If you don't have a drill, you can burn the hole with a heated rod but that's messy. Fit the tang into the hole with epoxy. Make sure the blade is centered.

For a full-tang knife, you need two handle scales. Cut two pieces of wood or bone to match the tang's shape, leaving a little extra. Roughen the tang and the inside of the scales with sandpaper for better glue adhesion. Mix two-part epoxy and clamp the scales to the tang. Use C-clamps or a vise. Wipe off excess glue with acetone. Let it cure for 24 hours.

After the glue dries, shape the handle with a file, rasp, or belt grinder. Round the edges for comfort. Sand progressively from 80 to 400 grit. For a smooth finish, wet the wood and sand again to raise the grain, then sand with 600 grit. You can oil the handle with linseed oil or beeswax to protect it from moisture. In Africa, many smiths use animal fat or palm oil but these can go rancid. Linseed is better if available.

Finally, add a bolster or guard if you want. A simple brass or steel plate between the blade and handle adds strength and looks. Drill a hole for the tang and epoxy it in place. For a stick tang, you can peen the end of the tang over a washer to lock the handle. File it smooth. Test the handle by gripping hard it should not move. A well-attached handle will last for years.

<h2>Practical Reference: Blade Smithing Fundamentals</h2>
<p>This practical reference is written for working blacksmiths, artisan toolmakers, and smallscale metal workshops across Africa who want to move from general forging into making saleable knives, machetes, and agricultural blades. It assumes you already have basic forge skillsfire control, hammer technique, and simple tool making. The focus here is on repeatable results that customers will pay for: consistent hardness, clean grinds, and handles that survive real use. In many markets, a wellfinished knife or canecutting blade sells for 310 times the price of a rough forged blank, so the time spent on steel selection, heat treatment, and finishing is direct profit.</p>

<p>Blade smithing is a business of small tolerances. A blade that is 23 HRC too soft will roll its edge in the field; one that is too hard will chip and break. A handle that is not sealed will crack in the first rainy season. The procedures below are deliberately lowtech and use materials that are available in most African towns: leafspring steel, files, charcoal or coal, motor oil, and local hardwoods. Where possible, we cite published sources and standards so you can verify the numbers and adapt them to your own shop.</p>

<h2>Selecting Steel for Blades</h2>
<p>In Africa, the most reliable and affordable blade steels are recycled: vehicle leaf springs (5160 or similar), coil springs, old files (W1/W2 or 1095like), and agricultural discs. These are knownbyuse steelsyou can test them with a file and a spark test before committing to a blade. Avoid unknown galvanised or painted steel; the zinc coating is toxic when heated. For consistent results, buy new stock when possible: 1075, 1080, 5160, or 80CrV2 are common in South African and Kenyan steel suppliers. The table below gives practical guidance.</p>

<h2>Forging a Simple Knife Profile</h2>
<p>Start with a bar 46 mm thick, 3040 mm wide, and 200250 mm long. Forge the tang first, then draw out the blade. Keep the spine thick (34 mm) and the edge thin (0.51 mm before grinding). Work at a bright orange (850950C) and avoid burning the steel. In many African shops, a simple open forge with charcoal and a handcranked blower is enough. Use a crosspeen hammer to move metal quickly, then a flat hammer to true the profile. The goal is a blade that is straight, with a consistent bevel and no cold shuts.</p>

<h2>Normalizing and Annealing Blades</h2>
<p>After forging, the steel has internal stresses and large grains. Normalizingheating to 830870C and aircoolingrefines the grain. Do this 23 times. Annealingheating to 750800C and cooling slowly in ash, vermiculite, or limesoftens the steel so it can be ground and drilled. In Africa, a common method is to bury the blade in a bucket of dry wood ash overnight. This is not a full anneal but is sufficient for most leafspring and file steels. If you have access to a kiln, a controlled anneal at 760C for 1 hour then slow cool is better.</p>

<h2>Grinding and Shaping the Blade</h2>
<p>Grinding is where the blade becomes saleable. Use a belt grinder if available; otherwise, a bench grinder with a water dip to avoid overheating. Keep the edge coolif it turns blue, you have lost hardness. Grind the primary bevel to 0.51 mm at the edge, then sharpen. For African hardwoods and bone, a 2025 edge angle is a good compromise. Finish with 120400 grit, then a fine stone or diamond plate. A clean grind can double the perceived value of a knife.</p>

<h2>Heat Treating a Blade</h2>
<p>Heat treatment is the heart of blade smithing. For 5160 leaf spring: heat to 830860C (nonmagnetic plus a little more), quench in warm oil (canola, motor oil, or commercial quench oil). For files: 780820C, quench in water or brinebut this is risky; oil is safer. After quenching, temper immediately at 180220C for 1 hour, twice. This gives 5860 HRC for most knives. A simple tempering oven can be a charcoal fire with a thermometer or a kitchen oven. Test with a file: a good blade will skate a file at the edge.</p>

<div style="border: 4px solid #b30000; background: #ffe6e6; padding: 16px; margin: 20px 0;">
<h3 style="color: #b30000; margin-top: 0;"> SAFETY WARNING: Quenching and Heat Treatment</h3>
<p><strong>Never quench in water or oil without proper protective equipment.</strong> Hot oil can flash fire; water can explode into steam and throw hot steel. Always wear a face shield, leather apron, and gloves. Quench outdoors or in a wellventilated area. Keep a fire extinguisher or sand bucket nearby. In many African workshops, quench oil is stored in open drumsthis is a fire hazard. Use a metal container with a lid. Also, never heat galvanised steel; the zinc fumes cause metal fume fever. If you feel dizzy or have flulike symptoms after forging, stop and get fresh air. <strong>Your health and your workshop are your businessprotect them.</strong></p>
</div>

<h2>Attaching a Handle: Basic Techniques</h2>
<p>A good handle is as important as the blade. For a simple stick tang, use a hardwood block (e.g., African mahogany, mukwa, or ebony) and epoxy. Drill a hole slightly larger than the tang, fill with epoxy, insert tang, and pin with brass or steel rod. For a full tang, shape two scales, epoxy and pin with 23 pins. In Africa, many makers use local hardwoods like <em>Pterocarpus angolensis</em> (mukwa) or <em>Combretum</em> species. Seal the handle with linseed oil or polyurethane to prevent cracking. A wellfinished handle is a major selling point.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Steel identified:</strong> Spark test and file test done; no galvanised or painted steel.</li>
<li><strong>Forge temperature:</strong> Work at orange (850950C); never burn the steel.</li>
<li><strong>Normalize 23 times:</strong> Heat to 830870C, air cool.</li>
<li><strong>Anneal:</strong> Heat to 750800C, bury in ash or lime overnight.</li>
<li><strong>Grind cool:</strong> Dip in water often; never let the edge turn blue.</li>
<li><strong>Harden:</strong> Nonmagnetic plus 3050C; quench in warm oil.</li>
<li><strong>Temper immediately:</strong> 180220C for 1 hour, twice.</li>
<li><strong>Handle sealed:</strong> Epoxy and pin; seal with oil or varnish.</li>
<li><strong>Test edge:</strong> File skate, chop test, and visual inspection.</li>
</ul>
