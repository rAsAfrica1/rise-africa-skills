1. Calculating Cost of Goods Sold

To make a profit, you must first know exactly what each item costs you. Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) is the total cost of the products you sell. For a kiosk, this includes the purchase price of goods, transport to bring them to your stall, and any packaging you use. For example, if you buy a crate of 24 bottles of soda for 2400 KES, each bottle costs 100 KES. If you pay 100 KES for transport, add that to the total cost: 2400 + 100 = 2500 KES. Now each bottle costs 2500 / 24 = about 104 KES. Always calculate COGS per unit so you know your break-even price.

Keep a simple notebook or use a free app like Excel or Google Sheets on your phone. Record every purchase: date, item, quantity, unit cost, and total cost. At the end of each day, calculate your COGS by adding up the cost of all items sold. For instance, if you sold 10 sodas, 5 packets of biscuits, and 20 sweets, you need the cost per unit for each. If biscuits cost 50 KES per packet and sweets cost 5 KES each, your COGS for the day is (10 x 104) + (5 x 50) + (20 x 5) = 1040 + 250 + 100 = 1390 KES. Subtract this from your total sales to get your gross profit.

Remember to include hidden costs like electricity for a fridge, airtime for mobile money, or spoilage. If milk expires, that cost must be spread over the remaining units. For example, if you buy 20 litres of milk at 60 KES each and 2 litres spoil, your effective cost per litre becomes (20 x 60) / 18 = 66.67 KES. Always round up to the nearest shilling to stay safe. Review your COGS weekly to spot price changes from suppliers. If the price of cooking oil rises, adjust your selling price quickly. Without accurate COGS, you might think you are making profit when you are actually losing money. So, start today: list your top 10 products, calculate their unit costs, and write them on a card you keep at your stall.

2. Setting Profit Margins and Markups

Profit margin and markup are two ways to set your selling price. Markup is the amount you add to the cost price to get the selling price. Profit margin is the percentage of the selling price that is profit. For example, if an item costs 100 KES and you sell it for 150 KES, your markup is 50 KES (50% of cost). Your profit margin is 50/150 = 33.3%. Many kiosk owners use a simple rule: double the cost price (100% markup) for fast-moving items like sweets, and add 20-30% for staples like bread or milk. But be careful: high markup may scare customers away, while low markup may not cover your expenses.

To set a markup, first know your COGS per unit. Then decide on a target profit margin based on your total monthly expenses. Suppose your rent, transport, and airtime cost 10,000 KES per month. If you expect to sell 500 items, you need 20 KES profit per item just to cover these costs. So if an item costs 80 KES, you must sell it for at least 100 KES. That is a 25% markup. For luxury or impulse items like chocolate, you can charge 50-100% markup because customers are less price-sensitive. For competitive items like maize flour, keep markup low (10-15%) to attract customers.

Use a simple formula: Selling Price = Cost Price + (Cost Price x Markup Percentage). For a 40% markup on a 50 KES item: 50 + (50 x 0.4) = 70 KES. Always round to a friendly number like 70 or 75. Review your markups every month. If a supplier increases prices, recalculate. If you sell in bulk, you can lower markup per unit but earn more total profit. For example, selling a single sweet at 2 KES with 100% markup gives 1 KES profit. Selling a pack of 10 sweets for 15 KES (cost 10 KES) gives 5 KES profit, but the markup is only 50%. The key is to balance volume and margin. Track your best-selling items and adjust markups to maximise total profit, not just per-unit profit.

3. Competitive Pricing Techniques

In a competitive market, your prices must attract customers while covering costs. Start by checking what nearby kiosks and shops charge for the same items. Walk around your area and note prices for your top 10 products. If a competitor sells a 500ml soda for 50 KES, you can price yours at 50 KES to match, or 48 KES to undercut slightly. But never go below your COGS. If your cost is 45 KES, selling at 48 KES gives only 3 KES profittoo low unless you sell huge volumes. Instead, focus on differentiation: offer cold drinks when others sell warm ones, or open earlier when others are closed.

Use a strategy called 'loss leader': price a few popular items very low, even at break-even, to draw customers. Once they come for the cheap bread, they may buy other items with higher margins. For example, sell a loaf of bread at 55 KES when competitors sell at 60 KES. You make only 5 KES on bread, but customers also buy milk, sugar, and airtime. Another technique is bundle pricing: sell a packet of biscuits and a soda together for 80 KES instead of 85 KES separately. This increases total sales and clears slow-moving stock.

Monitor competitors weekly. If they lower prices, don't panic. First, check if you can negotiate lower prices from your supplier. Buy in larger quantities to get discounts. If not, emphasise value: clean stall, friendly service, or credit for trusted customers. In many African markets, customers value relationships over a few shillings. You can also offer a loyalty card: buy 10 sodas, get one free. This locks in customers without lowering your regular price. Remember, competing only on price is a race to the bottom. Compete on convenience, quality, and trust. Keep a small notebook of competitor prices and your own, and review it every two weeks.

4. Psychological Pricing Tactics

Psychological pricing uses human psychology to make prices feel more attractive. The most common tactic is charm pricing: set prices just below a round number, like 99 KES instead of 100 KES, or 49 KES instead of 50 KES. Customers perceive 99 as significantly cheaper than 100, even though it's only 1 shilling less. In many African markets, prices like 95, 98, or 99 work well. For very small items, like sweets, you can use 5 KES, 10 KES, but for items above 20 KES, charm pricing is effective. For example, a packet of tea that costs you 70 KES can be sold at 99 KES instead of 100 KESit feels like a bargain.

Another tactic is anchoring: place a higher-priced item next to a lower-priced one to make the lower price seem like a great deal. For instance, display a large bag of rice for 500 KES next to a smaller bag for 250 KES. The 250 KES bag looks affordable. You can also use 'buy one get one free' or '3 for 100 KES' to encourage bulk purchases. For example, if a single soap costs 40 KES, offer 3 for 100 KES (33.33 KES each). Customers feel they are saving, and you sell more units. But ensure the bundle price still covers your costs and gives a profit.

Use round prices for premium items. A fancy chocolate bar at 100 KES feels more luxurious than 99 KES. For everyday items, use charm pricing. Also, consider the 'rule of 100': for items under 100 KES, a percentage discount (e.g., 20% off) sounds better; for items over 100 KES, a shilling discount (e.g., 50 KES off) sounds better. For example, a 200 KES item with '50 KES off' is more appealing than '25% off'. Finally, always display prices clearly. Handwritten signs with big numbers work well. Avoid too many decimalsround to the nearest shilling. Test different prices on a few items and see what sells best. Small changes can increase your profit without losing customers.

5. Discounts and Promotions Without Losing Profit

Discounts and promotions can boost sales, but they can also destroy your profit if not planned. The golden rule: never discount below your COGS plus a minimum profit. Before offering any discount, calculate your break-even price. For example, if an item costs 80 KES, and you need at least 10 KES profit per unit to cover overheads, your minimum selling price is 90 KES. So a discount from 120 KES to 100 KES is safe, but to 85 KES is not. Use discounts to clear slow-moving stock or attract new customers, not on your best-sellers that already sell well.

Common promotions include 'buy 2 get 1 free', '10% off on weekends', or 'free airtime with purchase of 500 KES'. For 'buy 2 get 1 free', the effective price per unit drops. If a soda costs 50 KES, selling 3 for 100 KES means each is 33.33 KES. If your COGS is 40 KES, you lose money. So only run this if your COGS is below 33 KES. Instead, try 'buy 3 get a small sweet free'the sweet costs you 2 KES, but you sell 3 sodas at full price. That's a better promotion. Another idea: offer a discount only on a bundle. For example, a packet of maize flour and cooking oil together for 200 KES instead of 220 KES separately. Your total cost might be 170 KES, so you still make 30 KES profit.

Time-limited promotions create urgency. 'Today only: 10% off all biscuits' can bring in customers who then buy other items. But avoid daily discountscustomers will wait for them. Use promotions for special events like holidays or back-to-school. Track the results: did sales increase enough to offset the lower price? If you sold 20 units at 100 KES (profit 20 KES each = 400 KES) versus 30 units at 90 KES (profit 10 KES each = 300 KES), you made less profit despite more sales. So always calculate. Keep a simple record of promotion sales and profits. If a promotion doesn't increase total profit, stop it. Remember, the goal is profit, not just revenue.

6. Adjusting Prices Based on Demand

Demand for products changes based on time, weather, and events. Adjusting prices based on demand can increase your profit. For example, on a hot day, cold drinks are in high demand. You can charge 10-20% more for a cold soda than on a cool day. If a 500ml soda normally sells for 50 KES, on a hot day you can sell it for 60 KES. Customers are willing to pay for relief from the heat. Similarly, during Ramadan or Christmas, demand for certain foods like dates or biscuits rises. You can increase prices slightly, but be careful not to exploit customersthey may resent it and go elsewhere.

Another demand factor is time of day. In the morning, people rush to work and buy breakfast items like bread and tea. You can charge a premium for convenience. In the evening, demand for snacks and airtime increases. If you notice a product selling out quickly, you can raise its price. But test small increases: raise by 5 KES and see if sales hold. If sales drop too much, revert. Use a simple demand diary: note daily sales for each product and weather conditions. After a month, you'll see patterns. For example, you might find that on rainy days, umbrellas and hot tea sell more, so you can stock up and price slightly higher.

Also, consider seasonal demand. During school holidays, more children buy sweets and snacks. You can create small 'holiday packs' at a higher price. During planting season, farmers may have less cash, so demand for cheap items rises. You can lower prices on staples to keep volume, but maintain prices on treats. Always communicate price changes politely: 'Due to high demand, cold drinks are 60 KES today.' Most customers understand. Never change prices too oftenit confuses customers. Adjust based on clear patterns, not guesses. Keep your cost in mind: even with high demand, never sell below cost. The goal is to maximise profit when demand is high, and maintain sales when demand is low.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Pricing Strategies for Profit in a Small Kiosk or Tuck Shop

In a small kiosk or tuck shop, pricing is not a one-time decision. It is a daily discipline. Every item on your shelf has a cost, a market price, a shelf life, and a customer expectation. If you price too low, you work hard for nothing. If you price too high, customers walk to the next stall. This practical reference gives you the tools to calculate, set, test, and adjust prices so your business stays profitable in an African market context.

Use this module alongside the lessons on Cost of Goods Sold, Profit Margins, Competitive Pricing, Psychological Pricing, Discounts, and Demand Adjustment. The goal is simple: know your numbers, know your customer, and protect your profit.

1. Calculating Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) for a Kiosk

COGS is the true cost of the item you sell, not just the price you paid at the wholesaler. For a kiosk, COGS includes:

  • Purchase price per unit
  • Transport to bring stock to your stall
  • Spoilage, breakage, or theft
  • Packaging or repackaging costs
  • Any market fees or permits per item (if applicable)

Formula: COGS per unit = (Total purchase cost + transport + spoilage allowance + packaging) Number of sellable units.

Example: You buy 100 sachets of milk at 20 KES each = 2,000 KES. Transport is 200 KES. Two sachets leak and are unsellable. Packaging bags cost 100 KES. Sellable units = 98. COGS = (2,000 + 200 + 100) 98 = 2,300 98 = 23.47 KES per sachet. If you sell at 30 KES, your gross profit is 6.53 KES per sachet.

2. Setting Profit Margins and Markups

A markup is the amount added to COGS. A margin is the percentage of the selling price that is profit. Many kiosk owners confuse the two and underprice their goods.

Markup formula: Markup % = (Selling price COGS) COGS 100.

Margin formula: Margin % = (Selling price COGS) Selling price 100.

Example: COGS = 23.47 KES. Selling price = 30 KES. Markup = (30 23.47) 23.47 100 = 27.8%. Margin = (30 23.47) 30 100 = 21.8%.

For a small kiosk, aim for a minimum gross margin of 1525% on fast-moving goods and 3050% on slow-moving or specialty items. Airtime and mobile money may have very thin margins but bring foot traffic.

3. Competitive Pricing Techniques

You do not need to be the cheapest. You need to be competitive where it matters. Use these techniques:

  • Price matching on key items: Match the price of bread, milk, or airtime to nearby kiosks. Customers use these as reference points.
  • Bundle pricing: Sell bread + milk as a breakfast bundle at a small discount. This increases basket size.
  • Differentiation: If your kiosk is cleaner, opens earlier, or offers credit to trusted customers, you can charge a small premium.
  • Location advantage: Near a school, bus stop, or construction site, convenience justifies a slightly higher price.
  • Avoid price wars: Competing only on price destroys margins. Compete on availability, trust, and service.

4. Psychological Pricing Tactics

Customers do not always calculate. They react to how a price looks and feels.

  • Charm pricing: Price at 49 KES instead of 50 KES. The left-digit effect makes it feel significantly cheaper.
  • Round pricing for convenience: For airtime or bus fare, round numbers are easier and build trust.
  • Anchoring: Display a larger, premium pack next to a smaller one. The smaller pack looks like better value.
  • Per-unit framing: "20 KES per sachet" sounds cheaper than "100 KES for 5 sachets" even when the total is the same.
  • Loss leaders: Sell one item at a very low margin to attract customers who then buy other profitable items.

5. Discounts and Promotions Without Losing Profit

Discounts are powerful but dangerous. A 10% discount on a 20% margin cuts your profit in half. Follow these rules:

  • Discount only items with a margin above 30%.
  • Use time-limited promotions: "Today only" or "Until stock lasts."
  • Bundle slow-moving stock with fast-moving items instead of cutting prices.
  • Offer quantity discounts: "Buy 5, get 1 free" only if the COGS of the free item is covered by the extra volume.
  • Track promotion results. If sales volume does not increase enough to cover the discount, stop it.

Safety Warning: Do Not Sell Below Cost

Never price an item below its COGS to "beat" a competitor. This is a direct loss on every sale. It attracts customers who will leave as soon as your stock runs out, and it trains your regular customers to expect unrealistic prices. If a competitor is selling below cost, they are either buying cheaper, selling expired stock, or losing money. Focus on your own numbers. Also, never use credit or discounts as a way to clear expired food, medicine, or baby products. Selling expired goods is illegal and dangerous. Check expiry dates weekly and remove unsafe stock immediately.

6. Adjusting Prices Based on Demand

Demand changes by time of day, season, weather, and local events. Your prices should respond, but carefully.

  • Peak hours: Morning and evening commuters may pay a small premium for convenience. Keep prices stable but ensure stock availability.
  • Rainy season: Demand for umbrellas, hot drinks, and instant noodles rises. Adjust stock, not necessarily price.
  • School terms: Near a school, demand for snacks and stationery rises during term time. Plan promotions accordingly.
  • Festive seasons: Demand for soft drinks, rice, and cooking oil increases. You can raise prices slightly if competitors do the same, but avoid exploiting customers.
  • Slow days: Use small discounts or bundles to move perishable stock rather than throwing it away.

Review your prices at least once a month. When wholesale prices rise, adjust your selling prices within a week. When demand drops, consider reducing order quantities before reducing prices.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Calculate COGS for every item, including transport, spoilage, and packaging.
  2. Know your markup and margin percentages for each product category.
  3. Check competitor prices on 510 key items every week.
  4. Use charm pricing (e.g., 49 instead of 50) on impulse items.
  5. Never discount below cost. Protect at least a 10% margin on any promotion.
  6. Track daily sales and stock movement to identify demand patterns.
  7. Review prices monthly and after any wholesale price change.
  8. Remove expired or damaged stock immediately and record the loss in your COGS.
