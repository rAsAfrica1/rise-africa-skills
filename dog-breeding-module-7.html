1. Early Pregnancy Diagnosis Methods

Knowing whether your mare is pregnant as early as possible saves money and prevents accidents. In Africa, where vet access may be limited, you can use several practical methods. The first is teasing: from day 14 after covering, bring the mare near a stallion daily. A pregnant mare will usually refuse him, pin her ears, and kick. This is free but not 100% reliable; some mares show false heat. A better method is rectal palpation by a skilled technician. From day 18 to 21, a trained hand can feel a small, fluid-filled swelling in the uterus. By day 35, you can feel the fetus. This requires a vet or experienced AI technician, but it is cheap and quick. Ultrasound is the gold standard. A portable scanner with a 5 MHz linear probe can detect a pregnancy as early as day 14. You see a black fluid-filled circle (the embryonic vesicle) in the uterus. By day 25, you can see the heartbeat. In many African countries, mobile vet services offer ultrasound for a fee. Blood tests for equine chorionic gonadotropin (eCG) can be done from day 40, but they are less common. Urine tests are not reliable in horses. For small-scale breeders, the best plan is: tease daily for 21 days, then have a vet do rectal palpation or ultrasound at day 30. If positive, start proper care. If negative, rebreed on the next cycle. Early diagnosis prevents feeding a non-pregnant mare expensive supplements and allows you to manage a pregnant one correctly.

2. Ultrasound Monitoring of Fetal Development

Ultrasound is the most powerful tool for monitoring pregnancy in mares. It uses sound waves to create a picture of the uterus and fetus. In Africa, portable ultrasound machines are increasingly available through veterinary clinics or AI services. You should scan at key intervals. First scan at day 14-16: confirm pregnancy by seeing a black, fluid-filled vesicle. Also check for twins. Twins are dangerous because mares rarely carry both to term; one or both die, and the mare may abort. If you see two vesicles, your vet can pinch one or give a drug to reduce it. Second scan at day 25-30: you should see the embryo with a heartbeat. The heartbeat is a flickering motion. If no heartbeat, the pregnancy is not viable. Third scan at day 45-60: check for fetal sex (if desired) and ensure the fetus is developing normally. The placenta should be attached properly. After day 60, scans are less frequent unless problems arise. A healthy fetus grows steadily. By day 90, you can see the skeleton. By day 150, the fetus looks like a small horse. Ultrasound also helps detect uterine fluid, which may indicate infection or imminent abortion. In hot African climates, dehydration can reduce image quality, so ensure the mare is well hydrated before scanning. Always use a clean probe and lubricant. If you cannot access ultrasound, rectal palpation at day 30 and 60 can still give useful information, but ultrasound gives you early warning of problems.

3. Endocrine Changes During Pregnancy

Hormones are chemical messengers that control pregnancy. In the pregnant mare, several key hormones change. Understanding them helps you know what is normal and when to worry. Progesterone is essential to maintain pregnancy. It is produced by the corpus luteum on the ovary for the first 35-40 days. After that, the placenta takes over. If progesterone drops too low, the mare may abort. In Africa, where stress from heat, poor nutrition, or transport can affect hormones, monitoring progesterone via blood tests can be useful. However, tests are not always available. A practical sign of low progesterone is a mare that shows signs of heat (oestrus) during early pregnancy. If that happens, call a vet; she may need progesterone injections. Equine chorionic gonadotropin (eCG) is produced by the placenta from day 40 to 120. It helps form accessory corpora lutea that produce more progesterone. High eCG levels can cause ovarian cysts, which are harmless. Oestrogens increase from day 60, peaking around day 200. They help relax the cervix and prepare the uterus. Prolactin rises near foaling to prepare milk production. Relaxin softens the pelvic ligaments. In late pregnancy, cortisol rises to trigger labour. For the African breeder, the main takeaway: if a mare shows heat after day 40, or has a bloody discharge, suspect hormone imbalance. Ensure she gets good nutrition and low stress. Regular vet checks are best, but if not possible, keep her calm and well-fed.

4. Nutritional Requirements of the Pregnant Mare

A pregnant mare needs extra feed, but not too much. Overfeeding can cause problems like laminitis or a difficult birth. Underfeeding leads to weak foals. In Africa, where good pasture may be seasonal, you must adjust the diet. For the first 5 months of pregnancy (early gestation), the mare needs only about 10% more energy than a dry mare. If she is on good grass or hay, that is enough. Provide free-choice mineral salt, especially calcium and phosphorus. From month 6 to 8 (mid-gestation), increase energy by 20%. Add 0.5 to 1 kg of a balanced concentrate per day. Good sources in Africa include maize, sorghum, and cottonseed cake. Avoid moldy feed; it can cause abortion. From month 9 to 11 (late gestation), the fetus grows fast. Increase energy by 30-40%. A 500 kg mare may need 2-3 kg of concentrate daily, plus good hay. Protein should be 12-14% of the diet. Add 30-50 grams of calcium and 20-30 grams of phosphorus daily. If you cannot afford commercial supplements, crushed bone meal or limestone flour can help. Provide fresh water always; a pregnant mare drinks 40-60 litres per day. In hot climates, she may drink more. Add salt to feed (30-50 g/day) to encourage drinking. Also ensure she has access to shade. Overweight mares are at risk of foaling problems, so monitor body condition: you should feel ribs but not see them. Keep her active with light exercise. If pasture is poor, supplement with lucerne (alfalfa) hay or leaves from legume trees like leucaena (in moderation). Always transition feed changes slowly over 7-10 days.

5. Managing High-Risk Pregnancies

Some pregnancies are high-risk. You must identify them early and take action. High-risk mares include: those with twins, older mares (over 15 years), mares with a history of abortion, mares with poor body condition, and mares with uterine infections. Twins are the most common problem. If ultrasound at day 14-16 shows two vesicles, your vet should reduce one manually or with drugs. If not reduced, 90% of twin pregnancies end in abortion or stillbirth. Another risk is placentitis (infection of the placenta). Signs include premature udder development, discharge from the vulva, or a relaxed cervix. If you see these, call a vet immediately; antibiotics and anti-inflammatories can save the foal. In Africa, tick-borne diseases like piroplasmosis can cause abortion. Keep mares tick-free. Also, avoid stress: transport, extreme heat, and rough handling can trigger abortion. If a mare is high-risk, keep her in a quiet, shaded paddock. Feed her high-quality hay and a balanced concentrate. Monitor her daily: check for discharge, udder changes, and behaviour. If she starts to show signs of labour before day 320, call for help. A vet may use drugs to stop contractions. For mares with a history of abortion due to progesterone deficiency, weekly progesterone injections from day 20 to 120 can help. In areas with poor vet access, you can still reduce risk by good nutrition, tick control, and avoiding stress. If you cannot reduce twins, be prepared for the mare to lose them; she can be rebred next cycle. Always consult a vet if possible.

6. Prevention of Pregnancy Loss

Pregnancy loss (abortion) is heartbreaking and costly. Most losses happen in early pregnancy (before day 60) or late (after day 300). You can prevent many. First, ensure the mare is healthy before breeding. Treat any uterine infection. Vaccinate against equine herpesvirus (EHV-1) and equine viral arteritis (EVA) if available in your country. In Africa, these vaccines may be limited, so quarantine new horses for 3 weeks. Second, manage nutrition. Avoid moldy hay or grain; mycotoxins cause abortion. Provide balanced minerals, especially selenium and vitamin E. In selenium-deficient areas (common in parts of Africa), supplement. Third, control stress. Mares are sensitive to heat, transport, and sudden changes. Keep them in a familiar herd and routine. Avoid overcrowding. Fourth, control ticks and other parasites. Ticks spread diseases like piroplasmosis that cause abortion. Use tick grease or dips regularly. Deworm according to vet advice, but avoid certain dewormers (like ivermectin) in the first 60 days? Actually, most are safe, but check. Fifth, monitor for signs of impending abortion: vaginal discharge, relaxed tailhead, udder development before day 300. If you see these, call a vet. Sometimes bed rest and progesterone can help. Sixth, handle mares gently. No rough rectal exams, no chasing. If you must transport, do it in a well-ventilated trailer and avoid extreme heat. Finally, keep records. If a mare aborts, send the fetus and placenta to a lab if possible to find the cause. Prevent the same problem next time. With good care, most mares carry to term.

Practical Reference: Pregnancy Diagnosis and Management in the African Mare



For the African horse breeder â whether operating a peri-urban stable in Nairobi, a stud farm in the Western Cape, or a working-horse operation in Ethiopia â pregnancy diagnosis is not merely a veterinary formality. It is a business decision gate. Every open (non-pregnant) mare that is fed, housed, and managed for months without producing a foal is a direct loss of feed, labour, and opportunity cost. Conversely, a confirmed pregnancy that is lost at day 60 after a costly service fee represents wasted stud fees, transport, and a full reproductive cycle. This practical reference consolidates the diagnostic methods, monitoring schedules, and management actions that protect both the mare and the breeder's margin.



1. Early Pregnancy Diagnosis Methods



Three diagnostic routes are available to African breeders, ranked by cost and accuracy:




  • Transrectal palpation (day 16â18 onwards): The cheapest field method where a skilled veterinarian is available. Detects uterine tone and the embryonic vesicle. Requires a crush or safe restraint. In much of Africa, the limiting factor is the availability of a veterinarian competent in equine palpation, not the technique itself.

  • Transrectal ultrasound (day 14â16 onwards): The gold standard. Confirms viability (heartbeat by day 25), detects twins, and identifies the corpus luteum. Portable battery-powered units now travel well to remote farms.

  • Blood or urine tests: Oestrone sulphate (from day 60â70) and equine chorionic gonadotrophin (eCG, from day 40) can be measured. These are useful when a vet cannot reach the farm, but they give no information on viability or twinning.





The business rule is simple: diagnose by day 16â18, re-check by day 28â30 to confirm viability, and re-check again at day 45â60 to detect late embryonic loss and twins. Each re-check costs a fraction of a lost pregnancy.



2. Ultrasound Monitoring of Fetal Development



Ultrasound is the backbone of professional equine breeding. Beyond confirming pregnancy, it allows the breeder to:




  • Identify twins early (day 14â16) and reduce one â a critical intervention, as twin pregnancies in mares almost always end in abortion or stillbirth.

  • Assess fetal heartbeat at day 25, the single most reliable indicator of viability.

  • Monitor fetal growth, placental thickness, and fluid volume through mid and late gestation.

  • Determine fetal sex between day 60 and 90 (operator-dependent).



For African breeders, the practical constraint is equipment and power. Portable ultrasound units with battery packs and solar charging are now viable on off-grid farms. Where ultrasound is unavailable, sequential palpation plus blood testing at day 40 and 60 provides a workable substitute.

5. Managing High-Risk Pregnancies



Flag a mare as high-risk if she has any of the following:




  • History of abortion, stillbirth, or premature foaling.

  • Twin pregnancy (even after reduction).

  • Age over 18 years, or maiden mare over 15.

  • Poor body condition score (below 3/9) or laminitis history.

  • Placental thickening or fluid abnormalities on ultrasound.

  • Previous dystocia or caesarean.



High-risk mares need: monthly veterinary checks from month 7, a foaling alarm or night watch from day 300, a clean foaling box, and a written foaling plan with the nearest equine vet's number posted. In many African settings, the nearest competent equine vet may be hours away â so the breeder must be trained in basic foaling assistance and know when to call for help.



6. Prevention of Pregnancy Loss



Most pregnancy loss in mares occurs in the first 60 days. The main causes are:




  • Twinning â reduce one vesicle by day 16â25.

  • Corpus luteum insufficiency â supplement with altrenogest (Regu-Mate) or progesterone where indicated.

  • Infection â endometritis, placentitis. Treat with veterinary-prescribed antibiotics and anti-inflammatories.

  • Stress and transport â avoid long-distance transport in the first 45 days and again after day 300.

  • Nutritional deficiency â especially iodine, selenium, and vitamin E.

  • Toxins â mycotoxins in mouldy feed, fescue endophytes, and some plants (e.g., Senecio species).



Business-first prevention: The cheapest pregnancy loss is the one you prevent. A single Regu-Mate course or a twin reduction costs a fraction of a lost foal. Budget for these interventions at the time of breeding, not after a problem appears.
