1. Major Insect Pests: Cassava Mealybug and Green Mite

Cassava mealybug (Phenacoccus manihoti) is a small, pinkish-white insect that sucks sap from the growing tips of cassava. It injects a toxin that causes stunted growth, short internodes, and a 'candle-stick' appearance. The honeydew it excretes attracts ants and sooty mould, reducing photosynthesis. Yield losses can reach 80% if untreated. Green mite (Mononychellus tanajoa) is a tiny, yellowish-green mite that feeds on the underside of leaves, causing yellow speckling, leaf drop, and stunted growth. It thrives in dry conditions.

Scout your farm weekly, starting 4 weeks after planting. Check 20 random plants. For mealybug, look at shoot tips and undersides of young leaves. For green mite, tap a leaf over a white sheet of paperâmites will fall and appear as moving specks. Economic thresholds: treat mealybug if you find 10 or more mealybugs per shoot tip on 20% of plants; treat green mite if 5 or more mites per leaflet on 30% of plants.

Control mealybug with the parasitic wasp Apoanagyrus lopezi, which is available from national biological control programmes in Nigeria, Ghana, and Kenya. Also, prune and burn heavily infested shoots. For green mite, use neem oil (3% solution) or insecticidal soap, spraying undersides of leaves. Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides that kill natural enemies.

Prevent outbreaks by planting clean cuttings, intercropping with cowpea or maize, and maintaining soil fertility. Resistant varieties like TMS 30572 (mealybug-tolerant) and TME 419 (mite-tolerant) are available from IITA and national research institutes. Always remove and destroy crop residues after harvest to break the pest cycle.

2. Cassava Diseases: Mosaic, Bacterial Blight, and Root Rot

Cassava mosaic disease (CMD) is caused by viruses spread by whiteflies and infected cuttings. Symptoms: yellow-green mosaic patterns on leaves, leaf distortion, and stunted growth. Yield loss can be 20-90%. Bacterial blight (Xanthomonas axonopodis pv. manihotis) causes angular water-soaked leaf spots, gum exudate on stems, and wilting. It spreads through rain splash and infected tools. Root rot (various fungi like Fusarium and Pythium) causes soft, brown, foul-smelling roots; plants wilt and die. It is worse in poorly drained soils.

Prevention is key. For CMD, plant resistant varieties such as TMS 91/02324 or TME 419, and control whiteflies with neem oil or yellow sticky traps. Remove and burn infected plants immediately. For bacterial blight, use clean cuttings, rotate with cereals for 2 years, and disinfect tools with 10% bleach between plants. For root rot, improve drainage by planting on ridges, avoid overwatering, and rotate with maize or sorghum. Apply wood ash (a handful per planting hole) to suppress soil fungi.

If you see disease, act fast. For CMD, uproot and burn severely infected plants; do not compost them. For bacterial blight, prune infected branches and apply copper-based fungicide (e.g., copper oxychloride at 3 g/L) every 10 days. For root rot, remove affected plants and drench soil with Trichoderma (a beneficial fungus) at 5 g/L. Always wash hands and tools after handling diseased plants.

After harvest, plow deeply to bury crop residues and expose soil to sun. Plant cover crops like mucuna to improve soil health. Keep records of disease outbreaks to plan rotations. Remember: healthy planting material is your first defence.

3. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Strategies

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) combines cultural, biological, physical, and chemical methods to control pests and diseases with minimal harm to people and the environment. For cassava, IPM starts with prevention: use certified disease-free cuttings, plant resistant varieties, and practice crop rotation. Rotate cassava with maize, cowpea, or groundnut for at least one season to break pest cycles.

Monitor your field weekly. Use a scouting plan: walk a zigzag path, inspect 20 plants, and record pest numbers. Set action thresholds: for mealybug, treat if 20% of plants have 10+ mealybugs per tip; for green mite, treat if 30% of leaves have 5+ mites. For diseases, remove any plant showing mosaic or blight symptoms immediately.

Cultural controls: intercropping cassava with cowpea reduces whitefly populations and improves soil. Mulch with dry grass to conserve moisture and suppress weeds. Plant on ridges in wet areas to prevent root rot. Sanitation: burn or bury infected plant parts; never compost them. Clean tools with 10% bleach between fields.

Biological controls: conserve natural enemies like ladybirds, lacewings, and parasitic wasps. Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides. If needed, use biopesticides: neem oil (3%), Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) for caterpillars, or Trichoderma for root rot. Chemical controls: use only as a last resort. Apply imidacloprid (0.5 ml/L) for mealybug, or copper oxychloride (3 g/L) for bacterial blight. Always follow label instructions.

Record everything: planting date, variety, pest sightings, treatments. This helps you learn and adapt. IPM saves money, protects health, and yields better harvests.

4. Biological Control and Resistant Varieties

Biological control uses living organisms to suppress pests. For cassava, the most successful example is the parasitic wasp Apoanagyrus lopezi, introduced across Africa to control cassava mealybug. The wasp lays its eggs inside mealybugs, killing them. It is now established in over 20 African countries, reducing mealybug populations by 70-90%. You can get it from national biological control unitsâcontact your local agriculture extension officer.

Other biocontrol agents: predatory mites (e.g., Typhlodromalus aripo) feed on green mites. Ladybirds and lacewings eat mealybugs and whiteflies. Encourage them by planting flowering plants like sunflower or marigold around your field, and avoid spraying broad-spectrum insecticides.

Resistant varieties are your best long-term defence. For cassava mosaic disease, plant TMS 91/02324, TMS 30572, or TME 419. For bacterial blight, use TMS 92/0326 or TMS 4(2)1425. For green mites, TME 419 and TMS 30572 show tolerance. These varieties are available from IITA, national research institutes (e.g., NRCRI in Nigeria, CSIR-CRI in Ghana), and certified seed sellers. Always buy certified cuttingsâthey cost a bit more but save you from total crop loss.

Combine biocontrol with resistant varieties for best results. For example, plant TME 419 and release A. lopezi wasps if mealybug appears. Also, practice crop rotation and sanitation. Never save cuttings from diseased plants. If you cannot find resistant varieties, ask your extension officer about local tolerant landraces. Remember: prevention through resistant varieties is cheaper than curing a sick field.

5. Safe Use of Pesticides and Application Equipment

Pesticides can harm you, your family, and the environment if misused. Always read the label before opening a container. Wear protective gear: long-sleeved shirt, long trousers, rubber boots, gloves, goggles, and a nose mask. Never eat, drink, or smoke while spraying. Mix pesticides in a well-ventilated area away from children and animals. Use clean water and measure accuratelyâmore is not better.

Choose the right equipment. For small farms, a 15-litre knapsack sprayer is ideal. Check for leaks before use. Calibrate it: spray water on a 10 m x 10 m area, measure how much you used, and adjust to apply 200-300 L per hectare. Use a fan nozzle for foliar sprays and a cone nozzle for stem treatments. Clean the sprayer after each use: rinse with water three times, then with a little detergent, and rinse again. Never clean sprayers in a stream or near a well.

Apply pesticides in the early morning or late evening when wind is low and bees are less active. Spray undersides of leaves where pests hide. Do not spray when rain is expected within 6 hours. For mealybug, use imidacloprid at 0.5 ml/L; for green mite, use abamectin at 0.5 ml/L; for bacterial blight, use copper oxychloride at 3 g/L. Never mix pesticides unless the label says it is safe.

Store pesticides in a locked cabinet, away from food and feed. Keep a bucket of soapy water and clean clothes nearby in case of spills. If you feel dizzy or nauseous, stop spraying, wash exposed skin, and seek medical help immediately. Dispose of empty containers by puncturing them and burying them 50 cm deep, far from water sources. Never reuse pesticide containers for food or water.

6. Quarantine and Phytosanitary Measures

Quarantine and phytosanitary measures prevent the spread of pests and diseases across borders and regions. When you move cassava cuttings, you risk carrying mealybugs, mites, viruses, or fungi to new areas. Many African countries have strict rules: you cannot carry fresh cassava cuttings across international borders without a phytosanitary certificate. This certificate confirms that your plants have been inspected and are free from pests.

If you buy cuttings from another region, ensure they come from a certified clean seed system. In Nigeria, the National Agricultural Seeds Council (NASC) certifies cassava cuttings. In Ghana, the Plant Protection and Regulatory Services Directorate (PPRSD) does the same. Ask for the certificate and keep it. If you receive cuttings without certification, isolate them for at least 3 weeks and inspect for symptoms before planting.

On your farm, practice internal quarantine. If you see a new pest or disease, report it immediately to your extension officer. Do not move infected plants or soil to other fields. Mark the affected area and restrict access. For example, if you find cassava brown streak disease (a virus), uproot and burn all affected plants, and do not plant cassava there for 6 months.

Clean your tools and boots before leaving an infected field. Use a footbath with 10% bleach at the field entrance. After harvest, destroy crop residues by burning or deep ploughing. If you sell cuttings, only sell from healthy fields. Quarantine may seem like a hassle, but it protects your farm, your neighbours, and the entire cassava industry. Cooperate with government inspectorsâthey are there to help.

Practical Reference: Pest and Disease Management in African Cassava Enterprises

Cassava (Manihot esculenta Crantz) is the backbone of food security and rural income across sub-Saharan Africa, cultivated by an estimated 40 million smallholder households. Yet yield losses from insect pests and diseases routinely reach 30â50%, and in severe outbreaks can wipe out an entire season's investment. For agribusiness operators â whether you run a 50-hectare commercial farm, a village-level processing unit, or a certified seed multiplication scheme â pest and disease management is not a "cost centre." It is a profit-protection function. Every naira, cedi, shilling or rand lost to cassava mosaic disease (CMD) or cassava mealybug is margin lost from your business, not just yield lost from your field.

This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons of the Pest and Disease Management module into a single field-and-office tool. Use it alongside your scouting records, spray logs, and buyer contracts. The economics are simple: a well-managed hectare of improved cassava in Nigeria or Ghana can return 20â30 tonnes of fresh roots, while an unmanaged field may yield 8â12 tonnes. The gap is your competitive advantage â or your competitor's.

â  SAFETY WARNING â PESTICIDE HANDLING

Cassava is a food and feed crop. Never apply a pesticide to cassava unless the label explicitly lists cassava and the target pest, and never within the stated pre-harvest interval (PHI). Always wear the full PPE specified on the label â gloves, boots, long sleeves, eye protection and a respirator for Class II products. Never eat, drink or smoke during application. Store pesticides in a locked, ventilated, clearly labelled cabinet away from food, feed, seed and children. Triple-rinse empty containers and puncture them before disposal; never reuse pesticide containers for water or food. In case of poisoning, contact your national poison information centre immediately and take the product label with the patient. Banned or restricted products (e.g. paraquat, most WHO Class Ia/Ib insecticides) must never be used on cassava.

Operational Playbook for Agribusinesses

1. Clean seed is cheaper than rescue sprays. A certified clean stem cutting costs roughly US$0.05â0.10; a CMD-infected field costs US$600â2,100 per hectare in lost revenue. Buy only from certified multipliers and keep batch records for traceability â buyers and processors increasingly demand this.

2. Scout weekly, log everything. Assign one trained scout per 20â30 hectares. Record pest counts, disease incidence, and weather. This data feeds into your IPM decisions and is required evidence for any certification scheme (GlobalG.A.P., organic, or processor supply contracts).

3. Protect natural enemies. The classical biological control programme against cassava mealybug, led by IITA with the parasitoid Anagyrus lopezi (formerly Epidinocarsis lopezi), is one of the most successful in African agricultural history, saving an estimated US$9 billion in avoided losses across the continent (Neuenschwander, 2001). Broad-spectrum insecticide sprays destroy this free service. Reserve chemical intervention for confirmed threshold breaches.

4. Rotate and rogue. Practise at least a two-year rotation with cereals or legumes on fields with a history of root rot. Rogue out (uproot and burn) CMD- and CBB-symptomatic plants early â this is the cheapest disease control available to any farm.

5. Quarantine compliance protects your market access. Moving cassava stems or roots across regional borders without phytosanitary certification can spread CBSD, CMD strains, or new mealybug biotypes, and can result in confiscation, fines and loss of export licences. Obtain a phytosanitary certificate from your national plant protection organisation (NPPO) before any cross-border movement.
