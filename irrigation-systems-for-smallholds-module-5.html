1. Designing a Gravity-Flow System from a Tank

To design a gravity-flow system, you need a water source higher than your field. A tank on a stand, a rooftop tank, or a small dam on a hill works. The key is elevation difference, called head. For every 1 meter of drop, you get about 0.1 bar of pressure. Most drip lines need 1 bar, so you need at least 10 meters of head. If your tank is only 2 meters above the field, you can still use furrow or basin irrigation, which needs very little pressure.

Start by measuring the vertical distance from the water surface in your tank to the highest point in your field. Use a string and a line level, or a simple water-filled hose as a level. If the drop is less than 1 meter, gravity flow will be too slow for sprinklers but may work for short furrows. Next, calculate the flow rate you need. For a 1-hectare field with maize, you might need 30,000 liters per day. If you have 10 hours to irrigate, that's 3,000 liters per hour, or 50 liters per minute. A 50mm (2-inch) pipe can carry about 100 liters per minute at low pressure, so it's enough.

Choose your pipe size based on flow and distance. A 32mm (1.25-inch) pipe can carry about 30 liters per minute over 50 meters. If you need more, use 50mm. Always use larger pipes for the main line and smaller for laterals. Install a filter if you use drip, and a valve at the tank to control flow. Bury pipes 30cm deep to protect them from sun and animals. At the field, you can use a manifold to split into smaller lines.

Test your system before planting. Fill the tank and open the valve. Check that water reaches the farthest point with enough pressure. If it's too slow, raise the tank or increase pipe size. A well-designed gravity system saves fuel and labor, and works even when there's no electricity.

2. Calculating Head and Flow Rates

Head is the vertical distance water falls. It creates pressure. Flow rate is how much water comes out per minute. To calculate head, measure from the water surface in your tank to the outlet. If your tank is on a 3-meter stand and the pipe outlet is at ground level, head is 3 meters. If the outlet is 0.5 meters above ground, head is 2.5 meters. Use a tape measure or a long stick. For accuracy, use a water level: a clear plastic hose filled with water, held at both ends, shows the same level.

Flow rate depends on head and pipe size. A simple formula: flow (liters per minute) = 0.6 x diameter squared (in mm) x square root of head (in meters). For a 25mm pipe with 2 meters head: 0.6 x 625 x 1.41 = 529 liters per minute? That's too high; actual flow is less due to friction. Better to use a bucket test. Hold a bucket under the outlet for 10 seconds, measure liters, multiply by 6 to get liters per minute. Do this at the start and end of your mainline to see pressure loss.

Pressure loss in pipes is real. For every 10 meters of 25mm pipe, you lose about 0.1 bar at 20 liters per minute. So if you have 50 meters of 25mm pipe, you lose 0.5 bar. That's why you need extra head. If your drip system needs 1 bar, and you lose 0.5 bar in pipes, you need 1.5 bar total, which is 15 meters of head. Use larger pipes to reduce loss. A 32mm pipe loses half as much as a 25mm.

To measure head easily, use a pressure gauge at the end of the line. If you don't have one, attach a clear vertical tube to a T-joint. The water will rise to the pressure height. For example, if water rises 5 meters, you have 0.5 bar. Keep records of head and flow for different settings so you can adjust as crops grow. This helps you water evenly without wasting water.

3. Using Hoses and Pipelines for Distribution

For gravity systems, you need pipes and hoses that can handle low pressure without bursting. Polyethylene (PE) pipe is best: it's cheap, flexible, and comes in 20mm to 63mm sizes. Use 50mm for the main line from the tank, 32mm for sub-mains, and 16mm or 20mm for lateral lines to the crops. Lay pipes on the surface or bury them 20-30cm deep. Burying protects from sun and prevents damage by animals or foot traffic. If you can't bury, cover with grass or move them regularly.

Connect pipes with barbed fittings and metal clamps. For 32mm pipe, use 32mm barbed tees and elbows. Heat the pipe end in hot water to soften it, then push onto the fitting. Tighten with a hose clamp. For drip lines, you need a filter: a screen filter with 120 mesh is good. Install it after the main valve. Also install a valve at the start of each lateral so you can shut off water to one section while working on another.

Hoses are useful for moving water short distances. A 25mm (1-inch) lay-flat hose can carry 30-50 liters per minute. Use quick-connect couplings so you can move the hose easily. For sprinklers, you need at least 1 bar pressure, so use a 20mm hose with a sprinkler that works at low pressure, like a mini-sprinkler. For furrows, you can use a simple 50mm hose with holes punched at intervals, but this is less even.

To distribute water evenly, keep laterals at the same elevation. If one lateral is lower, it will get more water. Use a manifold with equal-length tubes to each lateral. If your field slopes, run laterals across the slope, not down it. At the end of each lateral, install a flush valve to clean out sediment. Check for leaks weekly and repair with pipe repair tape or a new section. With good pipes and fittings, your gravity system will last for years.

4. Furrow Irrigation: Layout and Management

Furrow irrigation is the oldest and cheapest gravity method. You dig small channels (furrows) between crop rows and let water flow from a head ditch or pipe. It works best on flat or gently sloping land (0.1% to 0.5% slope). For maize, make furrows 75cm apart, 15-20cm deep, and 30-50 meters long. For vegetables, use closer spacing, like 50cm. The water moves down the furrow, soaking into the soil. You need a gentle slope so water reaches the end without ponding.

To set up, first level your field roughly. Use a shovel and a string line to mark furrows. Dig the head ditch at the top of the field, connected to your tank or pipe. Make a small opening (a cut in the ditch bank) for each furrow. Control flow with a piece of cloth or a small board. Water should reach the end of the furrow in about 1/4 of the total irrigation time. For example, if you irrigate for 2 hours, water should reach the end in 30 minutes, then soak for 1.5 hours.

Manage furrows by checking soil moisture. Push a stick into the soil; if it goes in easily to 15cm, you have enough water. If not, irrigate longer. Avoid overwatering: it wastes water and leaches nutrients. In sandy soil, furrows should be shorter (20-30m) because water moves fast. In clay soil, longer furrows (50-70m) are fine. You can also use surge irrigation: alternate wetting and drying to improve efficiency.

After each irrigation, check for erosion. If water breaks through the furrow, repair it. Keep furrows clean of weeds. At the end of the season, reshape them. Furrow irrigation uses more water than drip, but it's cheap and easy. With good management, you can save up to 30% water by using shorter furrows and controlling flow. Always water early morning or evening to reduce evaporation.

5. Basin and Border Irrigation Techniques

Basin irrigation is perfect for crops planted in flat beds, like rice, vegetables, or young trees. You build a flat area surrounded by a small earthen bund (wall) to hold water. Basins can be square, rectangular, or round. For a 1m x 1m basin around a tree, make the bund 15cm high and 20cm wide. For a vegetable bed 1m wide and 5m long, make a basin with bunds on all sides. Water is poured or piped into the basin and left to soak. This method is very efficient because water doesn't run off.

To build a basin, first level the ground inside. Use a rake or a board. Then dig soil from around the edges to form the bund. Pack the bund with your feet or a shovel. Make an inlet on one side where water enters. For a gravity system, connect a hose or a small pipe to the inlet. Fill the basin to a depth of 5-10cm, depending on crop. For rice, keep 5cm standing water. For tomatoes, fill to 10cm and let it soak in. The basin should empty within 2-4 hours. If it takes longer, your soil is too clayey; add organic matter.

Border irrigation is like basin but for larger areas. You divide a field into long strips (borders) separated by low bunds. Each border is 3-10 meters wide and 30-100 meters long, with a slope of 0.1% to 0.3%. Water flows from the top of the border to the bottom, soaking in as it goes. This works well for wheat, barley, and fodder crops. To manage, open the inlet and let water flow. Stop when water reaches the end. The water should advance steadily, not too fast.

Both methods need good land leveling. Use a laser level if available, or a simple water level and a shovel. Keep bunds repaired after each irrigation. In basins, you can mulch to reduce evaporation. In borders, avoid overwatering the top end. You can use a cut-out in the bund to drain excess water. These techniques are low-cost and work well with gravity flow, especially where water is scarce but land is flat.

6. Maintaining Gravity Systems for Even Watering

Even watering means every plant gets the same amount of water. For gravity systems, this requires regular maintenance. Start with your tank: check for leaks, cracks, and sediment. Clean the tank every 3 months. Remove leaves and dirt that can block pipes. If you have a screen filter, clean it weekly. A clogged filter reduces flow and pressure, causing uneven watering. Also check the tank outlet for roots or debris.

Pipes and hoses need attention. Walk your lines every week. Look for leaks, kinks, and animal damage. Repair leaks immediately with pipe repair tape or by cutting out the damaged section and joining with a coupling. For drip lines, flush the ends once a month by opening the flush valve for 5 minutes. This removes sediment that can clog emitters. If you use furrows, check that water flows evenly. Adjust the size of the inlet openings so each furrow gets the same flow. Use a small stick to measure the water depth in each furrow.

Pressure is key. Install a pressure gauge at the end of your mainline. If pressure drops, check for blockages. A sudden drop means a major leak or a closed valve. A gradual drop means clogging. For gravity systems, keep the tank clean and the pipes clear. If you have multiple laterals, make sure they are all at the same height. If one is lower, it will get more water. You can install a valve on each lateral to balance flow.

Keep records. Note when you clean filters, repair leaks, and adjust valves. This helps you spot problems early. At the start of each season, test your system: fill the tank, open all valves, and check that water reaches every part of the field within the expected time. If it doesn't, find the cause. With simple maintenance, your gravity system will give even watering for many years, saving you water and increasing yields.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Gravity-Fed Irrigation Systems for Smallholder Profitability

Gravity-fed irrigation is the backbone of smallholder horticulture across Africa, from the terraced hills of Rwanda and Ethiopia to the floodplains of the Senegal River and the market gardens of Kenya's Rift Valley. The principle is simple: water stored at elevation flows downhill through pipes, hoses, and channels to crops, using gravity instead of fuel or electricity. For a smallholder, this means lower operating costs, fewer breakdowns, and a system that can be built and repaired with locally available materials. But gravity alone does not guarantee profit. The difference between a system that irrigates 0.2 hectares reliably and one that starves the crop at the top of the field is design: head, flow rate, pipe sizing, and layout.

This practical reference gives you the numbers, tables, and field checks you need to design, operate, and maintain a gravity system that pays. Treat it as a working document: measure your own site, fill in the blanks, and adjust before you buy pipe or dig furrows.

1. Sizing the system: head and flow

Head is the vertical distance from the water surface in the tank to the highest point of discharge. Flow is the volume delivered per unit time (litres per second, L/s). In gravity systems, flow depends on head, pipe diameter, length, and friction losses. A common mistake is to size the pipe for the tank outlet rather than for the furthest furrow. 2. Distribution: hoses, pipelines, furrows, basins

For smallholders, the cheapest reliable distribution is a buried or surface mainline (PVC or HDPE) from the tank to a manifold, then flexible hoses or open furrows to the crop. Keep the mainline as short and straight as possible. Every bend, narrow section, and leak steals head. Use 50 mm mainline for runs over 30 m, and step down to 32 mm or 25 mm laterals only where the flow is split.

Furrow irrigation suits row crops on slopes of 0.1â2%. Layout furrows along the contour, not up and down the slope, to prevent erosion and uneven watering. Furrow length depends on soil: sandy soils 20â30 m, loamy 50â80 m, clay 80â120 m. Cut a small notch or siphon from the manifold into each furrow so you can control flow per row. Basin irrigation is better for tree crops and paddy: level basins with bunds, filled from a head ditch. Border irrigation sits between the two: strips bounded by low ridges, good for fodder and cereals.

Business tip: group crops by water need. A single manifold feeding tomatoes, onions, and maize at the same time will over-water onions and under-water maize. Use separate blocks or valves and irrigate the highest-value, most water-sensitive crop first.

3. Even watering: the field checks that protect yield

Uneven watering is the most common cause of poor gravity irrigation results. Water arrives at the top of the furrow first and reaches the bottom last, so the top gets more and the bottom gets less. You can manage this by:

  • Shortening runs on sandy soils or steep slopes.
  • Using cut-back flow: start with a high flow to wet the whole furrow, then reduce to a maintenance flow.
  • Alternate furrow irrigation: water every other furrow, then switch. Saves 25â30% water with little yield loss in many crops.
  • Checking with a spade: dig 15 cm down at the top, middle, and bottom of the field. If moisture differs by more than a hand-span, adjust layout.
  • Recording run times in a notebook. What gets measured gets managed â and financed.

For a business, even watering means predictable harvests, which means contracts with buyers, hotels, and processors. A gravity system that waters unevenly cannot supply consistent volumes. Fix the system before you chase the market.
