1. Wood Species: Hardwoods vs. Softwoods and Their Properties

In furniture making, wood is the classic material. But not all wood is the same. Hardwoods come from broad-leaved trees (like mahogany, oak, iroko, teak) and softwoods from conifers (like pine, cedar, cypress). Despite the name, hardness varies: balsa is a hardwood but very soft; yew is a softwood but harder than some hardwoods. In Africa, you'll find excellent hardwoods such as iroko (often called African teak) â it's durable, resistant to termites, and has a beautiful golden-brown colour. Mahogany (like Khaya) is prized for its rich reddish-brown tone and easy workability. Softwoods like cypress and pine are cheaper, lighter, and easier to cut, but they dent easily and need protection from moisture.

When selecting wood, check three things: grain pattern, moisture content, and defects. Grain can be straight, interlocked, or wavy. Interlocked grain (common in iroko) can tear during planing â use a sharp blade and slow feed. Moisture content should be 8-12% for indoor furniture. You can buy a moisture meter for about $30, or use the oven-dry method: weigh a small sample, dry it in an oven at 100Â°C for 24 hours, then weigh again. The difference gives moisture content. Defects like knots, splits, and insect holes weaken the wood. Avoid them in structural parts.

For durability, hardwood like iroko or mahogany lasts decades outdoors if finished properly. Softwoods like pine need treatment against termites and rot. In West Africa, termites are a major threat â always use treated wood or naturally resistant species. For carving and turned legs, mahogany and ebony are excellent. For hidden frames, use cheaper softwoods or engineered wood. Always store wood flat, off the ground, in a dry, ventilated area. Stack with stickers (thin strips) between layers to allow air circulation. This prevents warping and mould. When buying, ask for 'kiln-dried' wood â it's more stable than air-dried. If you must use air-dried, let it acclimatise in your workshop for two weeks before cutting.

2. Engineered Wood Products: Plywood, MDF, Particleboard, and OSB

Engineered wood products are made by gluing wood fibres, particles, or veneers together. They are cheaper, more uniform, and less prone to warping than solid wood. Plywood is made of thin layers (plies) of wood glued with grains alternating at 90 degrees. This cross-lamination gives strength and stability. Good plywood has no voids and uses waterproof glue (phenolic) for exterior use. In Africa, marine plywood (made from mahogany or okoume) is common for boat building and kitchen cabinets. It's expensive but durable. Standard plywood is 4mm to 25mm thick. Use 18mm for cabinet carcasses.

MDF (Medium Density Fibreboard) is made from fine wood fibres mixed with resin and pressed. It's dense, smooth, and easy to machine. It takes paint and veneer well. But it's heavy and swells when wet â never use MDF in bathrooms or outdoors unless it's moisture-resistant (green core). MDF is ideal for painted doors, drawer fronts, and decorative panels. It dulls tools quickly because of the glue content â use carbide-tipped blades. Particleboard (chipboard) is made from wood chips glued and pressed. It's the cheapest and weakest. It sags under load, so use it for horizontal shelves only with support every 60cm. It's often covered with melamine or veneer. OSB (Oriented Strand Board) is made from large strands glued in layers. It's strong and used for structural sheathing, but not for fine furniture. It's ugly and hard to finish.

When buying engineered wood, check the edges for voids, the surface for smoothness, and the glue type. For kitchen cabinets in humid areas, use marine plywood or moisture-resistant MDF. For office desks, 18mm plywood or particleboard with laminate is fine. Always seal all edges with edge banding or paint to prevent moisture ingress. Store sheets flat on a dry surface. Cut with a fine-tooth blade (80 teeth for 250mm saw) to reduce chipping. For MDF, use a slow feed rate and sharp blades. A 2440mm x 1220mm sheet is standard. Calculate your cuts to minimise waste â a cutting layout can save 20% material.

3. Metal Alloys and Components in Furniture

Metal brings strength, modernity, and durability to furniture. Common metals are steel, aluminium, and brass. Steel is strong and cheap but rusts unless coated. Stainless steel (grade 304) resists rust and is used for outdoor furniture and kitchen fittings. Aluminium is lightweight, rust-proof, and easy to cut, but softer than steel. Brass and bronze are decorative, corrosion-resistant, but expensive. In Africa, you'll find steel from scrap yards and aluminium from window fabricators. Use them for frames, legs, handles, hinges, and brackets.

For structural frames, use square steel tubing (25mm x 25mm x 1.5mm wall) or round tubing (25mm diameter). Cut with an angle grinder or hacksaw, then weld or bolt. If welding, clean the joints first with a wire brush. For a rust-proof finish, apply red oxide primer then two coats of enamel paint. Aluminium can be bolted or riveted â use stainless steel bolts to avoid galvanic corrosion. For legs, consider 50mm x 50mm aluminium angle. For decorative elements, brass handles and hinges add a premium look. Brass tarnishes â polish with Brasso.

When joining metal to wood, use threaded inserts or T-nuts. Drill a pilot hole, screw in the insert, then bolt through. For hinges, use stainless steel piano hinges for cabinets â they last longer than cheap iron ones. In coastal areas, salt accelerates rust â use marine-grade stainless steel (316) or aluminium. Always wear safety glasses and gloves when cutting or grinding metal. Spark from angle grinders can start fires â keep a fire extinguisher nearby. For a clean cut, use a metal cutting disc (1mm thick) and let the tool do the work. Deburr edges with a file. A simple metal frame can transform a wooden top into a modern table. Measure twice, cut once â metal is unforgiving.

4. Plastics, Composites, and Advanced Materials

Plastics and composites are changing furniture design. They offer lightness, weather resistance, and bold shapes. Common plastics: acrylic (Perspex), polycarbonate, PVC, and nylon. Acrylic is clear, shatter-resistant, and can be heated and bent. Use it for chair backs, table tops, and decorative panels. Polycarbonate is stronger â used for bulletproof glass â but scratches easily. PVC is cheap and used for edge banding and pipes. Nylon is used for glides, rollers, and hinges. In Africa, plastic furniture (like monobloc chairs) is ubiquitous â but for custom work, you can buy acrylic sheets from sign makers.

Composites combine two materials for better properties. Fibreglass (glass-reinforced plastic) is strong, light, and rust-proof. It's made by layering glass fibre mat with polyester resin. You can mould it into curved chair shells. Carbon fibre is stronger and lighter but very expensive â used in high-end racing furniture. Wood-plastic composite (WPC) is made from wood flour and recycled plastic. It looks like wood, doesn't rot, and is used for outdoor decking and benches. In Africa, WPC is gaining popularity for outdoor furniture because it resists termites and moisture.

Working with plastics: cut acrylic with a fine-tooth blade (60 teeth) or score and snap. Drill with a sharp bit at slow speed to avoid cracking. Heat-bend acrylic with a heat gun or in an oven at 150Â°C for 10 minutes â wear gloves. Glue acrylic with dichloromethane (specialist glue) â it welds the joint. For fibreglass, work in a ventilated area, wear a respirator and gloves. Mix resin and hardener per instructions (usually 1-2% hardener). Apply gel coat first for a smooth finish. Sand with wet-and-dry paper (400 to 1200 grit) then polish. For WPC, cut and screw like wood, but pre-drill to avoid splitting. Always wear eye protection â plastic shards are sharp. Store sheets flat, away from heat.

5. Adhesives, Finishes, and Coatings: Types and Applications

Adhesives hold furniture together. The right glue depends on material, strength, and moisture. PVA (white glue) is for wood-to-wood indoor joints. It's strong, easy to clean, and sets in 30 minutes (clamp for 1 hour). For outdoor or wet areas, use polyurethane glue (Gorilla Glue) â it foams, fills gaps, and is waterproof. Epoxy is the strongest, gap-filling, and works on wood, metal, glass, and plastic. Mix two parts equally, apply within 20 minutes. Contact adhesive (like Bostik) is for laminates and veneers â apply to both surfaces, wait until tacky, then press. For metal to wood, use epoxy or construction adhesive (like No More Nails). Always clamp joints â use bar clamps, F-clamps, or rope tourniquets. Clean excess glue with a damp cloth before it sets.

Finishes protect and beautify. Oils (linseed, tung) penetrate wood, enhance grain, and are easy to repair. Apply with a cloth, wait 15 minutes, wipe off excess. Reapply 3 coats. They offer low protection against water â not for kitchen tables. Varnish (polyurethane) forms a hard film. Brush on 3 coats, sanding lightly (220 grit) between coats. It's durable and water-resistant. Lacquer is sprayed, dries fast, and gives a professional finish â but needs a spray booth. Paint (enamel or acrylic) covers wood and MDF. Prime first, then two topcoats. For a smooth finish, sand between coats with 320 grit. Wax (paste wax) gives a soft sheen and some protection â reapply monthly.

In Africa, humidity and sun are tough on finishes. For outdoor furniture, use marine varnish with UV inhibitors, or teak oil. For indoor, polyurethane varnish is fine. Always work in a dust-free area. Stir finishes, don't shake. Apply thin coats â thick coats wrinkle. Use a good brush (natural bristle for oil, synthetic for water-based). Clean brushes with mineral spirits (oil) or soapy water (water-based). Store finishes sealed, away from heat. A well-finished piece lasts years and adds value. Test on scrap first â different woods absorb differently.

6. Material Selection Criteria for Durability and Aesthetics

Choosing the right material is the difference between furniture that lasts and furniture that fails. Start with function: will it be indoors or outdoors? Will it hold heavy loads? Will it get wet? For outdoor furniture in Africa, use iroko, teak, or marine plywood with stainless steel fittings. For indoor, mahogany, pine, or MDF are fine. Consider load: a bookshelf needs strong shelves â use 18mm plywood or solid wood. A chair needs strong joints â use hardwood and epoxy. A table top needs scratch resistance â use hardwood with polyurethane finish or laminate.

Durability also depends on climate. In humid coastal areas, metal rusts fast â use aluminium or stainless steel 316. In dry areas, wood cracks â use well-seasoned wood and oil finishes. Termites are a major threat â use naturally resistant wood (iroko, camphor) or treated wood. For budget projects, particleboard with melamine is cheap but weak â reinforce with solid wood edges. For high-end, use solid mahogany with brass fittings. Always consider maintenance: oiled wood needs re-oiling yearly; varnished wood every 3-5 years; plastic needs little.

Aesthetics matter for selling. Grain pattern, colour, and texture create appeal. Mahogany's deep red, iroko's golden brown, and ebony's black are prized. Mixing materials â wood with metal, glass with wood â adds modern flair. For a rustic look, use reclaimed wood with knots and nail holes. For a sleek look, use MDF with high-gloss paint. Consider the market: in urban Africa, customers like modern, easy-to-clean furniture. In rural areas, traditional carved hardwood is valued. Always match material to design. A delicate chair in heavy iroko will be clumsy; a heavy table in balsa will break.

Cost is critical. Calculate material cost per project. A 2440x1220x18mm plywood sheet costs about $30-50. Solid iroko plank (25mm x 150mm x 2000mm) costs $20-40. Compare strength-to-cost. For hidden parts, use cheaper materials. For visible parts, invest in quality. Buy from reputable suppliers â check for moisture, defects, and grading. Always buy 10% extra for waste. Store materials properly. A good selection process saves money and builds reputation.

1. Hardwoods vs. Softwoods: The African Reality

The botanical distinction is simple: hardwoods are angiosperms (broad-leaved), softwoods are gymnosperms (conifers). But the commercial reality matters more. In Africa, the dominant furniture timbers are tropical hardwoods â Iroko, Mahogany, Teak, Ebony, and movingui. Imported softwoods such as pine and spruce arrive as sawn timber or as engineered panels.

For a workshop in Ghana, Kenya, or South Africa, the practical question is not "which is harder?" but "which gives me the best strength-to-cost ratio for this product?" Iroko (Milicia excelsa) is dense, durable, and resistant to termites â ideal for outdoor and hotel furniture, but hard on tooling and slow to dry. Pine is cheap, easy to machine, and takes paint well â good for painted cabinetry and budget flat-pack, but dents easily and needs protection in humid conditions.

Plywood, MDF, particleboard, and OSB now dominate volume production across Africa because they are dimensionally stable, available in large sheets, and cost less per square metre than solid timber. Understanding their limitations is where profit is made or lost.

3. Metal Alloys and Components

Metal in African furniture production is usually functional rather than decorative: hinges, drawer slides, bolts, frame reinforcement, and outdoor frames. The two alloys you will encounter most are mild steel (cheap, strong, but rusts fast in coastal and humid zones) and aluminium (light, corrosion-resistant, ideal for outdoor and coastal markets). Stainless steel 304 is the premium choice for coastal hotels and restaurants â it resists salt air but costs significantly more.

Practical rule: for any furniture destined for Mombasa, Lagos, or Durban coastal markets, specify galvanised or powder-coated steel, or aluminium. Bare mild steel hinges will seize within one rainy season.

4. Plastics, Composites, and Advanced Materials

PVC edge banding, ABS edges, polypropylene chair shells, and glass-reinforced plastic (GRP) are increasingly common. In Africa, the most commercially relevant advanced material is probably recycled plastic lumber â made from post-consumer HDPE â which is now produced in South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria. It is rot-proof, termite-proof, and ideal for outdoor benches, school furniture, and marine applications. It is heavier than timber and has lower stiffness, so it needs thicker sections.

5. Adhesives, Finishes, and Coatings

Adhesive choice determines whether your product survives the climate. PVA (white glue) is cheap and works well for interior, dry applications. Polyurethane (PU) adhesives are waterproof and gap-filling â essential for outdoor and kitchen furniture. Contact adhesive is fast for laminates but has poor heat resistance. Epoxy is strongest but expensive and slow.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Formaldehyde and Wood Dust

MDF, particleboard, and many plywoods contain urea-formaldehyde resins that off-gas formaldehyde, a known carcinogen. Cutting, sanding, and routing these materials releases fine dust that causes respiratory damage. In African workshops with poor ventilation, this risk is severe.

  • Always cut and sand MDF/particleboard with local exhaust ventilation or in open-air conditions.
  • Wear a P2/P3 respirator â a simple cloth mask is not adequate.
  • Store sheet goods in ventilated areas for 48 hours before machining.
  • Specify low-emission (E0/E1 or CARB Phase 2) boards where available.
  • Never burn MDF or treated offcuts â toxic fumes.

6. Material Selection Criteria for Durability and Aesthetics

Selection is a business decision. Use this hierarchy: application â climate â load â finish â cost. A hotel in Accra needs moisture-resistant MDF for bathroom vanities, PU adhesive, and two-pack polyurethane finish. A school in Nairobi needs solid hardwood or metal frames, PU adhesive, and powder coating. A budget flat-pack line in Johannesburg can use particleboard with melamine and PVA â provided it stays indoors.

Aesthetics matter for margin. African consumers and export buyers increasingly value visible grain, natural oil finishes, and locally sourced species. Iroko, mahogany, and movingui offer premium aesthetics; pine and MDF are neutral substrates for paint or veneer. Match the material to the price point â do not over-specify.
