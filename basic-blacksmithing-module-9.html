Lessons

1. Forging a Basic Chisel from Coil Spring

Coil springs from old cars or trucks are excellent sources of high-carbon steel, often 5160 or similar. Find a coil spring at a scrapyard or mechanic's shop. Use an angle grinder to cut one coil free, then heat it in your forge until it's bright orange (about 900C). Straighten it with your hammer on the anvil. Cut a piece about 15 cm long. This will be your chisel.

Hold the steel with tongs and bring it to a bright yellow heat (1000-1100C). Hammer one end into a flat, tapered edge. Work on both sides to create a symmetrical cutting edge. The edge should be about 2-3 mm thick at the tip and widen gradually to the body. Use a cross-peen hammer to draw out the metal. Keep the forging temperature high; if it turns dark red, stop and reheat.

Once the shape is formed, use a file or grinder to refine the cutting edge. For a wood chisel, a 25-degree bevel is good; for metal, 30-40 degrees. Then heat treat: heat the cutting end to non-magnetic (about 780C), quench in warm oil (canola or motor oil). Test with a file; it should skate. Temper by heating the back of the chisel until the cutting edge turns straw yellow (230C), then cool. Finally, sharpen on a stone.

Handle: you can leave it bare or add a wooden handle. For a bare chisel, round the struck end to prevent mushrooming. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. This chisel will cut hot metal, wood, or cold metal if tempered properly. In Africa, coil springs are plentiful and cheap, making this a sustainable tool-making method.

2. Creating a Punch from Tool Steel

Tool steel like O1, W1, or even old files and ball bearings can be used to make a punch. If using a file, anneal it first: heat to 800C and cool slowly in ash or vermiculite. This softens it for shaping. Cut a piece about 12-15 cm long and 1-2 cm thick. For a punch, you want a tapered point that will create holes in hot metal.

Heat the steel to bright orange and hammer one end into a taper. The point should be slightly rounded, not sharp, to avoid cracking. The taper should be gradual, about 5-7 cm long, ending in a tip of 2-3 mm diameter. Use a hammer and anvil to forge it. Keep the body straight. If you have a hardy hole, you can use a bolster to shape the taper. Reheat as needed. Once shaped, let it cool slowly.

Grind or file the tip to smooth it. Then heat treat: heat the tip to non-magnetic (780C), quench in oil. Temper the tip to a light straw color (230C) by heating the body until the tip changes color, then cool. This gives a hard tip with a tough body. For a hot punch, you might temper to a slightly higher temperature (purple, 280C) to avoid cracking in hot metal.

Use your punch to make holes in hot metal for rivets, tongs, or decorative work. Always punch on a heavy anvil or a block of steel. Strike the punch with a heavy hammer. In Africa, old car axles and leaf springs are good sources of tool steel. This punch will last many years if properly heat treated.

3. Making Tongs from Flat Bar

Tongs are essential for holding hot metal. You can make a simple pair from 1/2 inch (12 mm) flat bar, about 60 cm long. You'll need two pieces. Start by forging the jaws: heat one end of each bar to orange and hammer it into a tapered, slightly curved shape. The jaws should be about 10 cm long and curve inward to grip round or square stock. Use a cross-peen hammer to draw out the metal.

Next, forge the pivot boss. About 15 cm from the jaw end, upset the metal to create a thicker section. This will be the hinge. Heat that area and hammer it to make it wider and thicker. Then punch or drill a hole through both pieces at that point. The hole should be about 6-8 mm diameter. If you punch, do it hot and use a drift to size the hole.

Rivet the two pieces together with a piece of round bar (6 mm) or a bolt. Peen the rivet ends to secure. The tongs should open and close smoothly. Now forge the handles: draw out the remaining length to about 1 cm thick and 30 cm long. Curve the handles outward slightly so they don't pinch your hand. You can also forge a loop at the end for hanging.

Test the tongs on a piece of hot metal. Adjust the jaws by heating and bending if needed. The jaws should grip securely without wobbling. For different stock sizes, you can make multiple tongs or adjust the jaws. In Africa, flat bar is readily available at hardware stores. This project teaches you basic forging, punching, and riveting. With practice, you can make tongs in about two hours.

4. Forging a Hardy Tool for the Anvil

A hardy tool is a shank that fits in the hardy hole of your anvil, used for cutting, fullering, or bending. Start with a piece of square bar that fits your anvil's hardy hole. Measure the hole: typically 2-3 cm square. Cut a piece of mild steel or tool steel about 15 cm long. One end will be the shank, the other the tool.

For a hot cut hardy, forge the top into a sharp, chisel-like edge. Heat the steel to orange and hammer the end into a wedge shape. The edge should be about 5 cm wide and taper to a sharp edge. Keep the shank square and slightly tapered so it wedges into the hole. You can also make a fuller hardy by forging a rounded edge, or a bending fork by cutting two prongs.

Heat treat the cutting edge if using tool steel: heat to non-magnetic, quench in oil, temper to straw. For mild steel, it won't harden much, but it's fine for hot cutting. The shank should be a loose fit; don't force it. File the shank to fit if needed.

To use, drop the shank into the hardy hole. The tool should sit securely. For cutting, place hot metal on the edge and strike with a hammer. The hardy takes the place of a chisel, leaving your hands free. Always wear safety glasses. In Africa, you can make a hardy from an old axle or leaf spring. This tool expands your anvil's versatility and is a great beginner project.

5. Heat Treating Your Homemade Tools

Heat treating hardens and tempers steel so it holds an edge without being brittle. You need a forge, oil quench tank, and a way to measure temperature. If you don't have a thermometer, learn to judge by color: non-magnetic is about 780C (bright red). Heat your tool's cutting edge to that temperature. Use a magnet to test; when it stops sticking, it's ready.

Quench immediately in warm oil (about 50-60C). Canola, peanut, or motor oil works. Vegetable oil is common in Africa. Submerge the tool vertically, edge first, and move it around to cool quickly. Don't use water for most tool steels; it can crack them. After quenching, the steel is hard and brittle. Test with a file: it should skate without cutting.

Now temper: this reduces brittleness. Clean the steel to bare metal. Heat the tool away from the edge, letting heat travel to the edge. Watch for oxide colors: straw (230C) for cutting tools, brown (260C) for punches, purple (280C) for springs. When the edge reaches the desired color, quench in oil to stop the process. Alternatively, use an oven at 200-250C for one hour.

Always temper after hardening. Test your tool on scrap metal. If it chips, temper more; if it bends, harden again. Practice on scrap pieces first. In Africa, you can use a charcoal forge and a metal bucket for quench oil. Heat treating is the difference between a tool that works and one that fails. Take your time and be consistent.

6. Testing and Refining Your Tools

After making and heat treating your tools, test them on actual work. For a chisel, try cutting cold mild steel or hot metal. It should cut without chipping or rolling. If the edge rolls, it's too soft; re-harden and temper to a lower temperature. If it chips, it's too hard; temper to a higher temperature. For a punch, punch a hole in hot metal; the tip should not deform.

For tongs, test grip on various stock sizes. If the jaws don't hold, heat and adjust the curve. The rivet should allow smooth movement but not be too loose. For a hardy, ensure it fits snugly but can be removed. Test cutting hot metal; the edge should stay sharp.

Refining: use a grinder or file to sharpen edges. For chisels, maintain a bevel angle of 25-30 degrees. For punches, keep the tip slightly rounded. Dress the striking end of tools to remove mushrooming. Always wear safety glasses when testing; pieces can fly.

Keep a log of your heat treating colors and results. Adjust as needed. In Africa, you can test on local materials like hardwood or scrap metal. Share your tools with other smiths for feedback. A well-made tool lasts years. If a tool fails, don't discard it; reforge and re-treat. Practice makes perfect. Your skills will improve with each tool you make.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Making Your Own Tools

In African blacksmithing workshops from the crowded lanes of Kariakor in Nairobi to the roadside forges of Kumasi and the metalworking clusters of Nnewi the ability to make your own tools is not a hobby. It is a business survival skill. Imported tools are expensive, freight-heavy, and often the wrong shape for local work. A coil spring from a scrap truck, a length of leaf spring from a broken axle, or a piece of tool steel from a bearing race can become a chisel, punch, or set of tongs that earns money the same week.

This practical reference consolidates the seven lessons in this module: forging a chisel from coil spring, creating a punch from tool steel, making tongs from flat bar, forging a hardy tool for the anvil, heat treating your homemade tools, and testing and refining them. Treat it as a bench-side document. Read it before you light the forge, and keep it within reach while you work.

Why Tool-Making Is a Business Decision

A working blacksmith in most African markets faces three constraints: capital, supply chains, and customer expectations. Making your own tools addresses all three.

  • Capital: A new imported set of tongs can cost the equivalent of several days' earnings. Flat bar offcuts and scrap spring steel are often free or near-free.
  • Supply chains: When a chisel breaks mid-job, waiting weeks for an import is not an option. If you can forge a replacement in an hour, you keep the contract.
  • Customer expectations: Customers judge a smith by the finish of the work. Sharp, well-tempered chisels and punches produce cleaner cuts, which command higher prices for gates, hoes, knives, and agricultural implements.

Tool-making also creates a product line. Many successful African smiths sell tongs, punches, and hardy tools to other smiths and to vocational training centres. This turns a cost centre into a revenue stream.

Material Selection: Reading Scrap Correctly

The single biggest cause of failed homemade tools is wrong material choice. Not all scrap steel is hardenable. Use these field tests before you commit forge time.

Forging the Core Tools

Chisel from Coil Spring

Cut a 200250 mm length of coil spring. Straighten it at bright yellow heat (approx. 1,1001,200C). Do not straighten at low heat spring steel cracks. Forge the cutting end to a taper, then draw the edge to about 23 mm thickness. The shank should remain thicker than the edge to absorb hammer blows. Quench and temper as described below.

Punch from Tool Steel

A punch is a short, stout tool. Start with a 150180 mm length of bearing race or axle steel. Forge a taper to the working end, then shape the tip: round for drifting holes, square for slot punching. Keep the body thick. A punch that bends is worse than a punch that breaks it wastes the hole.

Tongs from Flat Bar

Tongs are the smith's hands. Forge them from 25 6 mm or 30 8 mm flat bar. The jaw should be shaped to grip your most common stock round bar, flat bar, or square. The reins should be long enough to keep your hand out of the heat. A well-made pair of tongs lasts years and can be sold to other smiths.

Hardy Tool for the Anvil

The hardy hole is the square hole in the anvil. A hardy tool a hot cut, a bottom fuller, or a bending fork sits in that hole and multiplies what your anvil can do. Forge the shank to fit your hardy hole with a slight taper so it seats firmly. The working end should be forged from medium-carbon steel and heat treated.

Heat Treating Your Homemade Tools

Heat treatment is where most homemade tools succeed or fail. The principle is simple: harden the working end, leave the rest tough.

  1. Normalise: Heat the whole tool to non-magnetic (approx. 750780C) and let it cool in air. This relieves forging stresses.
  2. Harden: Heat only the working end to non-magnetic, then quench vertically in warm water or oil. Warm water (4060C) reduces cracking in spring steel. Oil is safer for high-carbon bearing steel.
  3. Temper: Polish the hardened end and watch the oxide colours. For chisels and punches, aim for a straw-to-bronze colour (approx. 220260C). For hot cuts, temper to a darker purple (approx. 280300C).
  4. Cool and test: Cool in water, then test on scrap metal before using on paid work.

SAFETY WARNING Molten Metal, Quench Burns, and Toxic Fumes

  • Never quench hot steel in a plastic container. The steel can melt through and spill scalding water on your feet. Use a metal bucket or drum.
  • Warm water quench can flash to steam instantly. Keep your face and hands back. Wear a leather apron, closed leather boots, and eye protection.
  • Galvanised, painted, or chromed scrap releases toxic fumes (zinc, lead, chromium) when heated. Do not forge it indoors. Work outdoors or with strong cross-ventilation, and wear a respirator if fumes are visible.
  • Spring steel can shatter during quenching if overheated or quenched cold. Always normalise first and use warm water or oil.
  • Keep a water bucket and sand bucket within arm's reach at all times. In many African workshops, fire risk from dry thatch, wooden benches, and stored fuel is high.

Testing and Refining Your Tools

A tool that has not been tested is not a tool it is a guess. Test every homemade tool before it goes into paid work.

  • Chisel test: Cut a 6 mm mild steel plate. The edge should bite and curl a chip, not skate or chip. If it skates, it is too soft re-harden and temper slightly cooler. If it chips, it is too hard temper to a darker colour.
  • Punch test: Drive a hole through 5 mm mild steel. The punch tip should stay straight and sharp. A mushroomed head means the shank is too soft; a bent tip means the temper is too soft or the steel is low-carbon.
  • Tongs test: Grip a round bar and a flat bar. The jaws should hold without slipping. The reins should not flex painfully in your hand. Adjust the jaw gap while hot.
  • Hardy test: Seat it in the anvil and strike it with a hammer. It should not wobble. Cut a hot bar to check the edge holds at temperature.

Refine in small steps. A tool that is almost right is worth adjusting; a tool that is badly made should be cut up and reforged. In a business context, a tool that fails on a customer's job costs you the customer, not just the steel.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Identify your steel source before forging spring, bearing, or axle steel, not rebar, for anything that cuts.
  2. Forge spring steel only at bright yellow heat; never straighten it cold or at dull red.
  3. Normalise the whole tool before hardening to relieve stress and prevent cracking.
  4. Harden only the working end; leave the shank tough to absorb hammer blows.
  5. Quench vertically in warm water (spring steel) or oil (bearing steel); keep your face back.
  6. Temper to straw/bronze for cold chisels and punches, purple for hot cuts and hardy tools.
  7. Test on scrap steel before using on paid work; adjust temper rather than starting over.
  8. Store finished tools dry and lightly oiled rust destroys edges faster than work does.
