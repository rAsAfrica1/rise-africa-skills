1. Finding buyers: local traders, breweries, schools, and NGOs

Your sorghum and millet harvest is money waiting to happen, but only if you find the right buyer. Start with local traders at your nearest market. They buy in bulk and pay cash, but prices are often low. Visit the market on different days to learn who pays best. For example, in many parts of Nigeria, a trader might offer 25,000 per 100kg bag of sorghum, while another offers 28,000. Always ask around before selling.

Breweries are a major buyer for sorghum. Companies like Nigerian Breweries, Guinness, and SABMiller use sorghum for beer and malt drinks. They often contract farmers directly, providing seeds and buying the harvest. Contact their local office or agricultural department to ask about contract farming. They require clean, high-quality grain, so proper drying and storage matter. Payment is usually by bank transfer after delivery.

Schools and universities with boarding facilities buy millet and sorghum for porridge and meals. Approach the school bursar or catering officer with a sample and your price. They prefer consistent supply and may sign a term contract. NGOs and food aid organisations also buy for relief programmes. For instance, the World Food Programme (WFP) purchases locally from smallholder farmers. Register with farmer groups or check tender notices in district offices.

Don't ignore local processors who make flour, beverages, or animal feed. They may buy regularly. To find all these buyers, attend agricultural shows, join farmer associations, and use radio announcements. Keep a list of buyers with their contact details and typical prices. Always get a written agreement or receipt for large sales. Building relationships with several buyers reduces risk. Never rely on one buyer; if they delay payment or lower prices, you have alternatives.

2. Negotiating prices and understanding market cycles

Negotiating a good price starts with knowing the market. Sorghum and millet prices follow seasonal cycles. Right after harvest, when many farmers sell, prices drop. For example, in Burkina Faso, millet might sell for 150 CFA francs per kg at harvest, but rise to 250 CFA francs per kg during the lean season (June-August). If you can store your grain safely, you can sell later for more. But storage costs and spoilage risk must be considered.

Before you negotiate, check current prices. Use your mobile phone to call other farmers, traders, or listen to market radio programmes. In Kenya, the Kenya Agricultural Commodity Exchange (KACE) sends SMS price alerts. In Ghana, Esoko provides similar services. Knowing the price gives you confidence. When a trader offers a low price, you can say, 'I know the price at Techiman market is 200 cedis per bag; can you match that?'

Negotiation is not a fight; it's a conversation. Be polite but firm. Start by asking the buyer's best price, then counter with a slightly higher figure. Be ready to walk away if the offer is too low. If you have quality grain, emphasise that: 'My sorghum is well-dried and clean, no stones or mould. That's worth a premium.' For large volumes, ask for a better price per bag. Also discuss payment terms: cash on delivery is best. If they want credit, agree on a written date and possibly a higher price to cover risk.

Market cycles also depend on demand from breweries, schools, and export. For instance, when breweries increase production, sorghum demand rises. Keep informed through farmer groups and extension officers. Plan to sell when prices are high, but don't hold too long if you need cash. A good strategy: sell half at harvest to cover immediate costs, store half for later. Always calculate your cost of production to know your minimum price. Never sell below that.

3. Grading and sorting for premium prices

Grading and sorting your sorghum and millet can increase your income by 10-30%. Buyers pay more for clean, uniform grain. Start by removing foreign matter: stones, sand, chaff, and damaged grains. Use a simple sieve or winnowing basket. For millet, you can also use a destoner. After cleaning, sort by size and colour. For sorghum, uniform red or white grains are preferred. For millet, uniform pearl or finger millet grains.

Different grades have different prices. For example, Grade 1 sorghum (less than 1% foreign matter, moisture below 12%) might sell for 20% more than Grade 3. Breweries and processors often specify grade standards. Ask your buyer for their requirements. Common grades: Grade 1 for premium food and brewing, Grade 2 for general food, Grade 3 for animal feed. Use a moisture meter to check dryness; if you don't have one, bite a grainit should be hard and crack, not soft.

Packaging also matters. Use clean, new bags (50kg or 100kg). Label with your name, grade, and weight. For premium markets, you might pack in 1kg or 5kg branded bags for direct sale to consumers. This adds value but requires investment in packaging and marketing. Start small: sell graded grain in bulk to traders and processors, then explore value addition later.

To get the best price, take samples to buyers before delivery. Let them see the quality. If they agree to a higher price, deliver the same quality. Consistency builds trust. Join a cooperative to access better grading equipment and training. Many NGOs and projects offer grading and sorting workshops. Invest a little time in cleaning; the returns are worth it. Remember, a clean product opens doors to high-value buyers like breweries and export markets.

4. Using mobile phones and apps to check market prices

Your mobile phone is a powerful marketing tool. You can check market prices without leaving your farm. In many African countries, there are services that send price information via SMS or USSD. For example, in Kenya, dial *654# to access M-Farm, which gives prices for maize, sorghum, and other crops. In Ghana, Esoko offers a similar service. In Nigeria, the Nigeria Commodity Exchange has price updates. Ask your extension officer or fellow farmers about the service in your area.

Smartphone apps are also available. Download apps like 'Farmerline' (Ghana), 'Kilimo Salama' (Kenya), or 'AgriApp' (Nigeria). These apps show prices from different markets. They also provide weather forecasts and farming tips. If you don't have a smartphone, you can still use SMS. For instance, send a text to a short code with the crop name and market, and receive the current price. Some services charge a small fee per message, but it's worth it.

Beyond apps, use social media. Join WhatsApp or Telegram groups for farmers in your region. Members often share prices and buyer requests. Facebook groups like 'Sorghum Farmers Association of Nigeria' can be useful. You can also call traders directly to ask their price. Build a network of contacts: other farmers, traders, extension officers, and cooperative leaders. They are your eyes and ears.

When you have price information, compare across markets. Sometimes it's worth transporting to a distant market if the price difference covers transport costs. For example, if sorghum is 200 birr per quintal in your village and 250 birr in the next town, and transport costs 30 birr per quintal, you gain 20 birr. Use simple arithmetic. Also, check prices regularly to understand trends. Keep a notebook or use your phone's notes app to record prices weekly. Over time, you'll know when to sell. Remember, information is power; the more you know, the better you negotiate.

5. Forming or joining a farmer cooperative for better sales

A farmer cooperative is a group of farmers who work together to buy inputs, share knowledge, and sell their harvest. Joining or forming one can increase your bargaining power. When you sell alone, you might get a low price because traders know you have limited volume. But a cooperative can aggregate hundreds of tonnes, attracting big buyers like breweries and NGOs. For example, the Sorghum Farmers Cooperative in Kano, Nigeria, sells directly to Nigerian Breweries at a premium price.

To form a cooperative, start with at least 10-15 farmers in your area. Hold a meeting to discuss goals and rules. Elect leaders: chairperson, secretary, treasurer. Register with the government's cooperative department to get legal status. This allows you to open a bank account and sign contracts. Many NGOs and government programmes provide training and grants to new cooperatives. For instance, the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) supports cooperative development in many African countries.

As a member, you benefit in several ways. You can access bulk inputs like improved seeds and fertiliser at lower prices. You can share equipment like threshers and storage facilities. You can get training on grading, post-harvest handling, and marketing. When selling, the cooperative negotiates as one. They can demand higher prices, arrange transport, and ensure timely payment. Some cooperatives also add value by processing flour or packaging for retail.

But cooperatives require commitment. You must follow the rules, contribute fees, and attend meetings. There may be challenges like mismanagement or delayed payments. Choose leaders wisely and demand transparency. If you prefer not to join, you can still collaborate informally with neighbours to sell together. But a formal cooperative opens more doors. Check with your local extension officer about existing cooperatives in your area. Joining one is often easier than starting from scratch. Remember, unity is strength; together you can get better prices and services.

6. Transport and logistics: reducing post-harvest losses to market

Getting your harvest to market without losses is crucial. Post-harvest losses for sorghum and millet can be 10-20% if not handled well. Losses happen during harvesting, drying, storage, and transport. To reduce them, start with proper drying. Dry your grain to below 12% moisture before storage. Use a moisture meter or the salt test: mix salt with grain in a bottle, shake, and if salt sticks, it's too wet. Dry on a clean tarpaulin, not on the ground, to avoid contamination.

For storage, use hermetic bags like Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS) bags. These bags kill insects without chemicals and keep grain safe for months. They cost about $2-3 each and can store 100kg. Alternatively, use metal silos or clean drums with tight lids. Add a layer of ash or neem leaves to deter pests. Never store grain on the floor; use pallets to avoid moisture. Check regularly for signs of insects or mould.

When transporting, use clean, dry bags. Don't overload vehicles; stack bags properly to avoid tearing. Cover with tarpaulin if it might rain. For short distances, use a bicycle or motorcycle with a carrier. For longer distances, hire a truck. If you're part of a cooperative, you can hire a truck together, reducing costs. For example, a 10-tonne truck from Kano to Lagos might cost 200,000, but shared among 20 farmers, it's affordable.

Plan your transport to avoid delays. Harvest when dry, and sell when roads are passable. In rainy season, roads may be bad, increasing costs and losses. Consider selling to a nearby market if transport is risky. Always get a receipt for transport and keep records. If you deliver to a buyer, ensure they inspect and accept the grain before unloading. Any rejected grain must be brought back or sold elsewhere. Reducing losses means more money in your pocket. Invest in good bags and storage; it pays off.

<h2>Practical Reference: Marketing and Selling Your Sorghum &amp; Millet Harvest</h2>

<p>Sorghum and millet are no longer just subsistence crops. Across the Sahel, East Africa, and Southern Africa, demand is rising from breweries, feed mills, schools, NGOs, and urban consumers who want gluten-free and climate-smart grains. But producing a good harvest is only half the business. The money is made or lost in how you find buyers, negotiate, grade, and move your grain to market. This practical reference gives you the tools, numbers, and checklists to turn your sorghum and millet into reliable income.</p>

<h3>1. Finding Buyers: Local Traders, Breweries, Schools, and NGOs</h3>
<p>Your first step is to map every possible buyer within reach. There are four main buyer categories, and each has different price, volume, and payment behaviour.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Local traders and aggregators:</strong> They buy in small volumes, pay cash quickly, but often offer the lowest price. Good for immediate cash flow after harvest.</li>
<li><strong>Breweries and processors:</strong> Companies such as East African Breweries, SABMiller/AB InBev, and local millers buy sorghum in bulk for clear beer, porridge flour, and animal feed. They pay premium prices but require consistent quality, volume, and formal contracts.</li>
<li><strong>Schools, hospitals, and institutions:</strong> They buy millet and sorghum for school feeding programmes and patient meals. Contracts are often seasonal and require reliable delivery.</li>
<li><strong>NGOs and relief agencies:</strong> WFP, FAO, and local NGOs buy for food assistance. They pay well but demand strict quality standards, traceability, and sometimes certification.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Business tip:</strong> Do not rely on one buyer. Sell 60% to a contract buyer (brewery or institution) for stable income, and 40% to local traders for flexibility and quick cash.</p>

<h3>2. Negotiating Prices and Understanding Market Cycles</h3>
<p>Prices for sorghum and millet follow predictable seasonal cycles. Immediately after harvest (typically OctoberDecember in the Sahel, JuneAugust in East Africa), supply floods the market and prices drop 2040%. By the lean season (MarchJuly), prices can double. The business skill is to sell part of your harvest at harvest for cash flow, and store the rest to sell when prices rise.</p>
<p><strong>Negotiation rules:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Never accept the first offer. Ask for 1520% above the quoted price and negotiate down.</li>
<li>Know the current market price before you negotiate. Use mobile apps (see section 4).</li>
<li>Sell in graded, uniform lots buyers pay more for clean, sorted grain.</li>
<li>Negotiate volume discounts for buyers who take larger quantities.</li>
<li>Get payment terms in writing: cash on delivery, 7-day, or 30-day payment.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. Grading and Sorting for Premium Prices</h3>
<p>Grading is the single cheapest way to increase your income. A farmer who sells unsorted grain may receive 2030% less than one who sells clean, graded, uniform lots. Buyers pay for consistency they need to know that every bag meets the same standard.</p>
<p><strong>Grading steps:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Clean:</strong> Remove stones, sand, chaff, and foreign matter. Use a sieve or winnowing.</li>
<li><strong>Sort:</strong> Remove broken, discoloured, and mouldy grains. These lower the entire lot's value.</li>
<li><strong>Dry:</strong> Ensure moisture content is below 1213% for safe storage and premium pricing.</li>
<li><strong>Grade by size and colour:</strong> Uniform grain commands higher prices, especially for breweries.</li>
<li><strong>Package:</strong> Use clean, labelled bags (50 kg or 100 kg). Brand your bags with your cooperative name.</li>
</ol>

<h3>4. Using Mobile Phones and Apps to Check Market Prices</h3>
<p>Information is power. Farmers with access to real-time market prices negotiate 1025% better prices than those without. Several free and low-cost tools are available across Africa:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>MFarm (Kenya):</strong> SMS and app-based market prices for grains, including sorghum and millet.</li>
<li><strong>Esoko (Ghana, Nigeria, and West Africa):</strong> Provides commodity prices, weather, and buyer connections via SMS.</li>
<li><strong>Agri-Business Africa / Kilimo (Tanzania):</strong> Market price alerts and buyer directories.</li>
<li><strong>WhatsApp and Telegram groups:</strong> Many cooperatives and traders run price-sharing groups. Join local grain trader groups.</li>
<li><strong>Radio and SMS:</strong> Even without a smartphone, you can subscribe to SMS price alerts from national bureaus of statistics or agricultural ministries.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>How to use price information:</strong> Check prices weekly. Compare at least three sources. When you know the market price, you can confidently reject low offers and time your sales.</p>

<h3>5. Forming or Joining a Farmer Cooperative for Better Sales</h3>
<p>Individual farmers selling 500 kg cannot access brewery contracts or NGO tenders. Cooperatives aggregate volume, improve bargaining power, and reduce transaction costs. A cooperative of 50 farmers with 50 tonnes of sorghum can negotiate 3050% higher prices than individual farmers.</p>
<p><strong>Steps to form or join a cooperative:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Register with the national cooperative authority (e.g., Kenya's Commissioner for Cooperatives, Nigeria's Federal Department of Cooperatives).</li>
<li>Elect a management committee and a treasurer. Open a bank account.</li>
<li>Set quality standards and a grading system for all members.</li>
<li>Appoint a marketing officer to contact buyers and negotiate contracts.</li>
<li>Pool transport and storage to reduce costs.</li>
<li>Share profits based on volume and quality delivered not equally.</li>
</ol>

<h3>6. Transport and Logistics: Reducing Post-Harvest Losses to Market</h3>
<p>Post-harvest losses for sorghum and millet in Africa range from 1030%, according to FAO. Most losses happen during transport, storage, and handling. Reducing these losses is directly equivalent to increasing your income.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Use clean, dry bags:</strong> Never transport grain in dirty or wet sacks. Use polypropylene bags with liners for moisture protection.</li>
<li><strong>Load carefully:</strong> Avoid overloading. Use tarpaulins to protect from rain and dust.</li>
<li><strong>Transport during dry hours:</strong> Early morning or late afternoon to avoid heat and rain.</li>
<li><strong>Store properly:</strong> Use raised pallets, hermetic storage bags (e.g., Purdue Improved Crop Storage PICS bags), and keep storage dry and ventilated.</li>
<li><strong>Pool transport:</strong> Cooperatives can hire a truck for 10 tonnes and share costs, reducing per-bag transport cost by 4060%.</li>
<li><strong>Insure your load:</strong> For large volumes, consider transport insurance against accidents and theft.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7b241c;">Never sell or transport grain with moisture above 14% or with visible mould. Buyers in formal markets (breweries, NGOs, schools) will reject contaminated grain and may blacklist you. Always dry grain to below 1213% moisture before storage or sale. Test with a moisture meter if available. If grain smells musty or shows green/yellow mould, do not sell it for human consumption use it for animal feed only after testing, or discard it. Your reputation is your most valuable business asset.</p>
