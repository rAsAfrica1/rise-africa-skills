1. Recognizing Allergic Reactions and Anaphylaxis

An allergic reaction happens when your body overreacts to something harmless like dust, peanuts, or bee stings. Mild reactions include sneezing, itchy eyes, a runny nose, or a rash. These are uncomfortable but not life-threatening. You can often treat them with antihistamines like chlorpheniramine (Piriton) or cetirizine. In Africa, common triggers are insect stings, certain foods (peanuts, shellfish, eggs), and medications like penicillin.

But some reactions become severe quickly. Anaphylaxis is a life-threatening allergic reaction that affects the whole body. It usually starts within minutes of contact with the trigger. Watch for these signs: swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat; difficulty breathing or swallowing; a hoarse voice; wheezing; a sudden drop in blood pressure causing dizziness or fainting; a widespread rash or hives; vomiting; and a feeling of impending doom. In children, they may become very pale, floppy, or complain of a 'frog in the throat'.

Anaphylaxis is a medical emergency. Without treatment, it can kill within minutes. The first and most important treatment is epinephrine (adrenaline) given by auto-injector into the outer thigh. If you don't have epinephrine, call for emergency help immediately. In many African settings, ambulance services may be limited, so you must be prepared to transport the person to the nearest clinic or hospital yourself. Do not wait to see if symptoms improve.

Remember: anaphylaxis can happen even if the person has only had mild reactions before. The severity can change from one exposure to the next. If you suspect anaphylaxis, act fast. Keep the person lying flat with legs raised to maintain blood pressure, unless they are vomiting or having trouble breathing, in which case let them sit up. Never let them stand or walk. Be ready to give CPR if they stop breathing.

After giving epinephrine, you must still get the person to a hospital because symptoms can return hours later (biphasic reaction). Document what triggered the reaction, the time, and what treatment was given. This information helps doctors and helps the person avoid future exposure.

2. Using an Epinephrine Auto-Injector

An epinephrine auto-injector is a device that delivers a single dose of adrenaline into the muscle. Common brands include EpiPen, Jext, and Emerade. In Africa, availability varies; some private clinics and pharmacies in cities like Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg stock them, but in rural areas you may need to rely on vials of adrenaline and a syringe. However, for community and business first aid, an auto-injector is easiest to use.

Here is how to use one. First, check the expiration date and look at the liquid through the viewing window. It should be clear. If it is cloudy or discolored, do not use it. Pull off the blue safety cap (or the cap indicated on your device). Do not touch the needle end. Hold the device in your fist like a pen, with the needle pointing down.

Press the device firmly against the outer thigh, about halfway between the knee and the hip. You can give it through clothing if necessary. You will hear a click and feel a prick. Hold it there for 3 seconds (or as instructed on the device) to ensure the full dose is delivered. Then remove it and massage the area for 10 seconds. Note the time.

If the person is still having severe symptoms after 5 minutes, and you have a second injector, give a second dose in the other thigh. If you only have adrenaline vials and syringes, the adult dose is 0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1:1000 solution) into the outer thigh. For children, the dose is 0.01 mg per kilogram of body weight, up to 0.5 mg.

After injecting, you must get the person to a hospital immediately, even if they feel better. Keep the used auto-injector to show the doctor. Never put your thumb over the needle end of the device. If you accidentally inject yourself, go to a hospital right away. Store auto-injectors at room temperature, away from extreme heat or cold. In hot African climates, do not leave them in a car. Check expiration dates regularly and replace as needed.

3. Asthma Attacks: Recognition and Assistance

Asthma is a long-term condition where the airways become inflamed and narrow, making it hard to breathe. In Africa, asthma is common, especially in urban areas with air pollution, and in rural areas where people cook with biomass fuels like wood and charcoal. An asthma attack can be triggered by dust, smoke, pollen, exercise, cold air, or infections.

Recognizing an asthma attack: The person may cough, wheeze (a whistling sound when breathing out), and complain of chest tightness. They may be short of breath and unable to speak full sentences. You might see them using extra muscles in the neck and chest to breathe. In severe attacks, their lips or fingernails may turn blue (cyanosis), and they may become drowsy or confused. Children may stop playing and sit hunched forward.

What to do: Stay calm and reassure the person. Help them sit upright; do not let them lie down. If they have a reliever inhaler (usually blue, containing salbutamol), help them use it. A spacer device, if available, makes it easier. Give 4 to 10 puffs of the inhaler, one puff at a time, with a breath in between. Wait 4 minutes. If there is no improvement, give another 4 to 10 puffs. If they still do not improve, or if they are too breathless to use the inhaler, call for emergency help or take them to a clinic immediately.

If you have no inhaler, you can help by removing the trigger (e.g., open windows, move away from smoke). Do not give any medication by mouth unless prescribed. Do not give sedatives. If the person stops breathing, start CPR.

After an attack, the person should see a doctor to review their treatment. For businesses, keep a reliever inhaler in the first aid kit if any staff member has asthma. Ensure the workplace is smoke-free and well-ventilated. In communities, teach people to avoid burning rubbish and to use clean cookstoves to reduce asthma triggers.

4. Insect Sting Allergies

Insect stings from bees, wasps, hornets, and ants can cause allergic reactions. In Africa, bee stings are common, especially in rural areas where beekeeping is practiced. Some people are severely allergic to the venom, and a single sting can trigger anaphylaxis.

For most people, a sting causes pain, redness, and swelling at the site. This is normal. But for those with an allergy, symptoms can appear within minutes: hives all over the body, swelling of the face or throat, difficulty breathing, dizziness, or a drop in blood pressure. If you see these signs, treat it as anaphylaxis and give epinephrine if available.

First aid for a normal sting: Remove the stinger if it is still in the skin. Scrape it out sideways with a credit card or fingernail; do not squeeze it, as this can release more venom. Wash the area with soap and water. Apply a cold compress to reduce swelling and pain. You can give an antihistamine like chlorpheniramine to reduce itching. Avoid scratching to prevent infection.

If the person is stung in the mouth or throat, swelling can block the airway. Give them ice to suck and get them to a hospital immediately.

Prevention: Wear shoes outdoors, avoid wearing bright colors or strong perfumes, and do not swat at bees. If you are allergic, carry an epinephrine auto-injector at all times. In workplaces, keep a sting kit with antihistamines and epinephrine. In communities, educate people about the difference between a normal sting and an allergic reaction.

If someone has a known insect sting allergy, they should inform coworkers and have an action plan. After any severe reaction, follow up with a doctor. Allergy testing and immunotherapy (allergy shots) can reduce sensitivity over time, but these are not widely available in all African countries.

5. Food Allergies in the Workplace

Food allergies are a growing concern in African workplaces, especially in cities where people eat together in canteens or share meals. Common allergens include peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, eggs, milk, wheat, and soy. In some African cultures, peanuts are a staple, so peanut allergy can be particularly challenging.

In the workplace, food allergies can cause mild reactions like hives or stomach upset, but they can also cause anaphylaxis. The risk comes from accidental exposure: shared utensils, cross-contamination on surfaces, or food brought in for celebrations.

What employers can do: Create an allergy-aware culture. Ask staff to disclose allergies voluntarily. Label foods in shared kitchens. Provide separate preparation areas if possible. Encourage handwashing before and after eating. If someone has a known food allergy, keep an epinephrine auto-injector in the first aid kit and ensure several staff are trained to use it.

If a reaction occurs: If the person has mild symptoms (itchy mouth, hives), give an antihistamine. If they have severe symptoms (difficulty breathing, swelling, dizziness), give epinephrine immediately and call for emergency help. Do not wait. Keep the person lying flat with legs raised. If they vomit, turn them on their side.

For employees: If you have a food allergy, tell your manager and close coworkers. Bring your own food if necessary. Read labels carefully. Avoid foods that may contain traces of your allergen. Carry your own epinephrine auto-injector.

In community settings, such as schools or markets, educate food handlers about allergens. Simple steps like using separate cutting boards and cleaning surfaces can prevent reactions. Remember, even a tiny amount of an allergen can trigger a severe reaction in a highly sensitive person.

6. Creating an Allergy Action Plan

An Allergy Action Plan is a written document that tells everyone what to do if someone has an allergic reaction. It is essential for businesses, schools, and community groups. The plan should be simple, clear, and posted where everyone can see it.

Step 1: Identify the person and their allergies. Include their name, photo, and specific allergens (e.g., peanuts, bee stings). Note any other medical conditions like asthma.

Step 2: List the signs of a reaction. Separate mild symptoms (hives, itching, runny nose) from severe symptoms (difficulty breathing, swelling of throat, dizziness, vomiting). Use plain language.

Step 3: State the actions. For mild symptoms: give an antihistamine (e.g., cetirizine 10 mg for adults). For severe symptoms: give epinephrine auto-injector into the outer thigh, call emergency services, and keep the person lying flat. Include the location of the auto-injector and who is trained to use it.

Step 4: Emergency contacts. List the person's doctor, family contacts, and local emergency numbers. In many African countries, the ambulance number may be 112 or 999, but response times vary. Identify the nearest clinic or hospital and how to get there.

Step 5: Training and review. Ensure all staff know the plan. Practice with a trainer auto-injector. Review the plan every 6 months and after any reaction.

Step 6: Document. After an incident, write down what happened, the time, symptoms, treatment given, and outcome. This helps improve the plan.

A good Allergy Action Plan saves lives. It removes confusion and ensures quick, correct action. For businesses, it also shows you care about employee safety. In communities, share the plan with local health workers. Adapt the plan to your setting: in rural areas, include transport arrangements to the nearest health facility. Keep it simple and practical.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: First Aid for Allergies and Anaphylaxis

Allergic emergencies are sudden, frightening, and in the case of anaphylaxis potentially fatal within minutes. For African businesses, from agro-processing plants and food markets to offices, farms, and hospitality venues, the ability to recognize and respond to severe allergic reactions is a core safety competency. This practical reference consolidates the essential knowledge from this module into a single, field-ready guide. It is written for managers, supervisors, first aiders, and community responders operating in resource-constrained settings where access to emergency medical services may be delayed.

Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction. It typically develops within minutes of exposure to a trigger most commonly foods, insect stings, medications, or latex. The global prevalence of food allergy is rising, and in Africa, common triggers include peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, eggs, and insect stings. In workplace settings, cross-contamination in shared kitchens, canteens, and food-handling areas is a frequent cause. The first aider's priority is simple: recognize the emergency, remove the trigger if safe, administer epinephrine if available, and call for help.

Recognizing Allergic Reactions vs. Anaphylaxis

Mild allergic reactions are common and usually self-limiting. They may involve localized itching, a few hives, or sneezing. Anaphylaxis, by contrast, is a medical emergency. The key differentiator is the involvement of multiple body systems or the presence of severe respiratory or circulatory compromise. A reaction that begins with hives but progresses to throat tightness, wheezing, or dizziness is anaphylaxis until proven otherwise.

In African workplace and community settings, symptoms may be mistaken for other conditions. Wheezing may be attributed to asthma or dust; vomiting and diarrhea may be attributed to food poisoning; dizziness may be attributed to heat exhaustion. First aiders must maintain a high index of suspicion, especially when symptoms appear within minutes to two hours after a known or suspected exposure.

Table 1: Differentiating mild allergic reaction from anaphylaxis
Feature Mild Allergic Reaction Anaphylaxis
Skin Localized hives, itching Widespread hives, flushing, swelling of face/lips/tongue
Respiratory Mild nasal congestion, sneezing Throat tightness, hoarse voice, wheezing, stridor, difficulty breathing
Circulation Normal Pale/clammy skin, rapid pulse, dizziness, collapse, low blood pressure
Gastrointestinal Mild nausea Severe vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhea
Onset Gradual, often minutes to hours Rapid, usually within minutes; can be sudden
Action Antihistamine, monitor Epinephrine IM, call emergency services, monitor

Source: World Allergy Organization (WAO) Anaphylaxis Guidance 2020; Resuscitation Council UK Anaphylaxis Guidelines 2021.

Using an Epinephrine Auto-Injector

Epinephrine (adrenaline) is the only life-saving treatment for anaphylaxis. It works by reversing airway swelling, constricting blood vessels to raise blood pressure, and relaxing bronchial muscles. In many African countries, auto-injectors may be unavailable or prohibitively expensive; however, where they are available, every first aider should know how to use them. The standard dose for adults and children over 30 kg is 0.3 mg intramuscular (IM) into the outer thigh. For children under 30 kg, the dose is 0.15 mg.

Auto-injectors are designed for simplicity. The steps are: (1) remove the safety cap, (2) place the device against the outer thigh at a 90-degree angle, (3) press firmly until a click is heard, (4) hold for the recommended time (usually 310 seconds, depending on the device), (5) remove and massage the site for 10 seconds, and (6) note the time and keep the device for disposal. If symptoms persist after 515 minutes, a second dose may be given from a new device.

SAFETY WARNING: Never administer epinephrine into a digit, buttock, or intravenous line. Always use the outer thigh. Do not delay epinephrine to wait for antihistamines or for medical advice in anaphylaxis, minutes matter. If epinephrine is unavailable, do not attempt to substitute with antihistamines alone; call emergency services immediately and monitor the airway continuously. In settings where auto-injectors are not available, trained personnel may draw up 0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1:1000 solution) for adults using a syringe and needle, but this requires specific training and is not recommended for untrained first aiders.

Asthma Attacks: Recognition and Assistance

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition of the airways, and acute attacks can be triggered by allergens, exercise, cold air, smoke, or stress. In African workplaces, exposure to dust, fumes, and biomass smoke is a common trigger. An asthma attack presents with wheezing, chest tightness, shortness of breath, and coughing. Severe attacks may present with difficulty speaking, blue lips, and exhaustion.

First aid for asthma includes: sitting the person upright, encouraging slow breathing, and helping them use their reliever inhaler (usually salbutamol). If the person has no inhaler, or if symptoms worsen, call for emergency help. Unlike anaphylaxis, asthma does not typically involve skin or gastrointestinal symptoms, though the two can coexist. If in doubt, treat as anaphylaxis if epinephrine is available.

Table 2: First aid actions for common allergic and respiratory emergencies
Emergency Key Signs Immediate First Aid Do NOT
Anaphylaxis Hives, swelling, wheeze, collapse Epinephrine IM, call emergency, lie flat Do not stand or sit upright; do not delay epinephrine
Asthma attack Wheezing, chest tightness, cough Sit upright, use reliever inhaler, call if severe Do not lay flat; do not leave alone
Insect sting (local) Pain, redness, swelling at site Remove stinger, cold compress, monitor Do not squeeze stinger; do not scratch
Food reaction (mild) Itching, few hives, mild nausea Stop eating, antihistamine, monitor Do not ignore progression to severe symptoms

Source: World Health Organization (WHO) Basic Emergency Care Course 2018; American Red Cross First Aid/CPR/AED Guidelines 2020.

Insect Sting Allergies

Bee, wasp, and ant stings are common in many African regions, particularly in agricultural and outdoor work. For most people, stings cause local pain and swelling. For those with venom allergy, a single sting can trigger anaphylaxis. In Africa, the most dangerous stings come from honeybees, paper wasps, and certain ant species. Fire ants are less common but present in some regions.

First aid for a sting: remove the stinger by scraping sideways with a card or fingernail (do not squeeze), wash the area, apply a cold compress, and monitor for signs of systemic reaction. If the person has a known allergy and carries an auto-injector, be prepared to assist. If they develop difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, or dizziness, treat as anaphylaxis.

Food Allergies in the Workplace

In African workplaces, food allergies are a growing concern, especially in canteens, catering, and food processing. Common allergens include peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, eggs, milk, wheat, and soy. Cross-contamination is a major risk: shared utensils, cutting boards, and frying oil can transfer allergens. Businesses should implement allergen management plans, label food clearly, and train staff to recognize and respond to reactions.

For first aiders, the priority is to identify the trigger, remove the person from the area if necessary, and provide appropriate care. If the person has a known allergy and carries medication, assist them. If they are unconscious, check breathing and be prepared to perform CPR. In all cases, document the incident and review the workplace allergy plan.

Creating an Allergy Action Plan

An allergy action plan is a written document that outlines how to prevent, recognize, and respond to allergic reactions in a specific setting. It should include: a list of known allergens and at-risk individuals; prevention measures (e.g., separate food preparation areas, insect control); a clear response protocol; designated first aiders; location of epinephrine and other medications; and emergency contact numbers. The plan should be reviewed annually and after any incident.

In African businesses, the plan must account for local realities: limited ambulance services, distance to clinics, and availability of medications. It is advisable to establish a relationship with the nearest health facility and to maintain a well-stocked first aid kit. Community-based plans should also consider traditional remedies and cultural practices, while emphasizing evidence-based first aid.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Recognize the signs: Hives, swelling, wheezing, difficulty breathing, dizziness, or collapse after exposure.
  2. Call for help early: Activate emergency services or the nearest clinic immediately.
  3. Give epinephrine: If available, administer IM into the outer thigh without delay.
  4. Position the patient: Lay flat with legs raised; if breathing is difficult, allow sitting upright.
  5. Monitor continuously: Check breathing, pulse, and consciousness; be ready for a second dose after 515 minutes.
  6. Remove the trigger: If safe, remove the stinger or stop food exposure.
  7. Do not give food or drink: Avoid oral intake if the airway is compromised.
  8. Document and review: Record the incident and update the allergy action plan.
