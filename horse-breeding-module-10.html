1. Common Venereal Diseases in Horses

Venereal diseases in horses spread mainly through mating. The three most common in Africa are contagious equine metritis (CEM), equine viral arteritis (EVA), and dourine. CEM is caused by the bacteria Taylorella equigenitalis. It causes inflammation of the uterus and can lead to infertility. Infected stallions often show no signs, but mares may have a pus-like discharge and fail to conceive. EVA is a virus that causes fever, swelling of the legs, and abortion in pregnant mares. It spreads through semen and respiratory droplets. Dourine, caused by Trypanosoma equiperdum, is a protozoan disease that causes swelling of the genitals and paralysis. It is common in parts of North and West Africa.

To prevent these diseases, always test new horses before breeding. For CEM, take swabs from the clitoral fossa and uterus of mares, and from the urethra and semen of stallions. Laboratories in Kenya, South Africa, and Nigeria can run these tests. For EVA, blood tests detect antibodies. Dourine is diagnosed with blood smears or serology. If a horse tests positive, do not breed it. Treat CEM with antibiotics like penicillin, but carrier stallions may need surgery to remove infected tissue. There is no treatment for EVA or dourine; infected animals must be culled or isolated.

In Africa, many breeders skip testing because of cost. But one infected stallion can ruin an entire herd. A single CEM outbreak can cost thousands of dollars in lost foals and vet bills. Work with your local veterinary office to set up a testing schedule. For EVA, vaccinate stallions and mares before breeding. The vaccine is available in South Africa and Kenya. For dourine, control tsetse flies and use trypanocidal drugs like diminazene in endemic areas. Always use artificial insemination with certified disease-free semen when possible.

Remember, venereal diseases are silent. A horse can look healthy but still spread infection. Regular testing and strict hygiene during mating are your best defence.

2. Diagnosis and Treatment of Endometritis

Endometritis is inflammation of the lining of the uterus. It is a major cause of infertility in mares. In Africa, it often follows a difficult birth, poor hygiene during mating, or an infection like CEM. Mares with endometritis may show a discharge from the vulva, stain their tail, or simply fail to conceive after several cycles. Some mares have a 'fluid-filled' uterus that can be seen on ultrasound.

Diagnosis starts with a thorough history and physical exam. Your vet will use a speculum to look at the cervix and vagina. They will take a swab from the uterus for culture and sensitivity. This tells you which bacteria are present and which antibiotics will kill them. A uterine biopsy is the gold standard: a small piece of tissue is taken and sent to a lab. It shows how damaged the uterus is and helps predict the chance of pregnancy. In many African countries, biopsy is available at veterinary schools like those in Pretoria, Nairobi, and Ibadan.

Treatment depends on the cause. For bacterial infections, the vet will flush the uterus with sterile saline and infuse antibiotics. Common antibiotics include penicillin, gentamicin, and ceftiofur. Do this daily for 3-5 days. For mares that accumulate fluid, use oxytocin to help the uterus contract and expel fluid. Give 20 IU of oxytocin intramuscularly after breeding. Some mares need repeated lavage with a dilute povidone-iodine solution. If there are fibrous adhesions, surgery may be needed.

Prevention is key. Always use clean equipment and gloves when breeding. Wash the mare's perineum with mild soap before mating. Avoid breeding a mare with a poor perineal conformation (a 'sunken' anus) without surgical correction. After foaling, check the placenta for completeness; retained pieces cause infection. In Africa, where vet access can be limited, train yourself to recognise the signs early. A simple ultrasound scan by a trained technician can save a mare's breeding career.

3. Managing Abortion and Infectious Causes

Abortion in mares is the loss of a foal before 300 days of gestation. In Africa, infectious causes are common. The main culprits are equine herpesvirus-1 (EHV-1), equine viral arteritis (EVA), and bacteria like Streptococcus equi subspecies zooepidemicus. EHV-1 is the most frequent cause of abortion storms, where many mares abort within weeks. The virus spreads through nasal secretions and can also cause neurological disease. EVA causes abortion in late pregnancy. Bacterial infections often ascend from the vagina.

When a mare aborts, act fast. Collect the foetus, placenta, and uterine fluid in clean plastic bags. Refrigerate, do not freeze, and send to a lab within 24 hours. A post-mortem on the foetus can identify the cause. Blood tests from the mare can detect EHV-1 or EVA. In many African countries, the Central Veterinary Laboratory in Harare, Zimbabwe, or the Onderstepoort Veterinary Institute in South Africa can run these tests. If you cannot reach a lab, at least examine the placenta for white patches (placental separation) and the foetus for swollen organs.

To control an outbreak, isolate the aborting mare for at least 3 weeks. Disinfect stables with a 1:100 dilution of bleach. Do not breed the mare again until she has a clean uterine culture. For EHV-1, vaccinate all pregnant mares at 5, 7, and 9 months of gestation. The vaccine is available in South Africa and Kenya. For EVA, vaccinate stallions and mares before breeding. There is no vaccine for dourine, but trypanocidal drugs can prevent abortion in endemic areas.

In Africa, many breeders lose foals to abortion because they do not vaccinate or test. A single EHV-1 outbreak can cost you 10 or more foals. Work with your vet to set up a vaccination schedule. Keep records of every abortion. If a mare aborts twice, do not breed her again without a full reproductive exam. Remember, abortion is not just a loss; it is a warning sign of disease in your herd.

4. Vaccination and Biosecurity in Breeding Operations

Vaccination and biosecurity are your first line of defence against reproductive diseases. In Africa, the most important vaccines for breeding horses are for equine herpesvirus-1 (EHV-1), equine viral arteritis (EVA), and tetanus. Tetanus is not a venereal disease but is common after foaling or castration. Vaccinate all horses annually with tetanus toxoid. For EHV-1, vaccinate pregnant mares at 5, 7, and 9 months of gestation. For EVA, vaccinate stallions and mares before breeding. The EVA vaccine is available in South Africa and Kenya. In some countries, you may need a permit to import it.

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out. Start with a quarantine area. Any new horse must be isolated for 30 days. Test for CEM, EVA, and dourine before introducing them to the herd. Use separate buckets, feed troughs, and grooming tools for each horse. Disinfect stables with a 1:100 bleach solution or a commercial disinfectant like Virkon. Control visitors: they should wear clean boots and overalls. Do not let them touch your horses without washing hands. Keep a log of all visitors and their contact with horses.

During breeding, use artificial insemination (AI) with certified disease-free semen. If you use natural mating, wash the mare's perineum and the stallion's penis with mild soap before and after. Use disposable gloves and a new breeding roll for each mating. After breeding, clean the stallion's penis with warm water and a mild antiseptic. Never breed a mare that has a discharge or a stallion that has a swollen testicle.

In Africa, many breeders operate on small farms with limited resources. But biosecurity does not have to be expensive. Simple steps like quarantining new horses, vaccinating, and cleaning equipment can prevent outbreaks that cost thousands. Work with your local vet to create a herd health plan. Keep records of all vaccinations, tests, and treatments. If you sell horses, provide a health certificate. A reputation for clean, healthy horses will earn you more money in the long run.

5. Parasite Control in Breeding Herds

Parasites can cause poor condition, anaemia, and even abortion in breeding horses. In Africa, the most important internal parasites are strongyles (bloodworms), roundworms (ascarids), and tapeworms. External parasites like ticks, lice, and mange mites also affect reproduction. Ticks can transmit diseases like equine piroplasmosis, which causes abortion. A heavy worm burden in a pregnant mare can lead to a weak foal or abortion.

Control starts with good management. Do not overcrowd pastures. Rotate grazing every 2-3 weeks to break the parasite life cycle. Remove manure from stables and paddocks daily. Compost manure away from grazing areas. Harrow pastures in dry season to expose larvae to sun. For mares and foals, use a faecal egg count (FEC) every 8-12 weeks. A FEC of more than 500 eggs per gram means you need to deworm. In Africa, common dewormers include ivermectin, fenbendazole, and pyrantel. Use them strategically: deworm pregnant mares 4-6 weeks before foaling, and deworm foals at 2, 4, 6, and 8 months of age. Rotate dewormer classes yearly to prevent resistance.

For external parasites, use tick control. In tick-infested areas, spray or dip horses every 2 weeks with acaricides like cypermethrin. Check ears, tail, and under the belly for ticks. For lice and mites, use ivermectin injections or topical powders. Keep bedding clean and dry. If you see a horse rubbing its tail or biting its skin, treat immediately.

In Africa, many breeders underestimate parasites. A mare with a heavy worm load will not conceive or will abort. A foal with roundworms can die from colic. Set up a parasite control calendar with your vet. Use faecal egg counts to guide treatment, not just routine deworming. Remember, healthy pastures and clean stables are as important as drugs. Train your workers to recognise signs of parasite infestation: dull coat, weight loss, diarrhoea, and colic. Early action saves foals.

6. Reproductive Surgery and Interventions

Sometimes, breeding problems need surgery. In Africa, common reproductive surgeries for horses include caesarean section, correction of uterine torsion, and removal of ovarian tumours. A caesarean is needed when a mare cannot deliver a foal naturally. This can happen if the foal is too large or wrongly positioned. The surgery must be done by a qualified vet in a clean environment. After surgery, the mare needs antibiotics and pain relief. In many African countries, only referral hospitals like those in Pretoria or Nairobi can perform this. If you cannot reach a hospital, you may lose both mare and foal.

Uterine torsion is when the uterus twists on itself. The mare shows severe colic and a distended abdomen. This is an emergency. The vet may try to roll the mare to untwist the uterus, but often surgery is needed. Ovarian tumours, like granulosa cell tumours, cause stallion-like behaviour in mares. They can be removed by surgery or treated with hormones. Another common intervention is Caslick's operation. This is done when a mare has a poor vulval seal, which allows air and bacteria into the vagina. The vet stitches the upper part of the vulva closed, leaving a small opening for urine. This prevents endometritis. The stitches are removed before foaling.

Artificial insemination (AI) is not surgery but a key intervention. It allows you to use superior stallions without moving the mare. In Africa, AI is becoming more common. You need a trained technician, a speculum, and a syringe. The semen is deposited into the uterus. AI reduces the risk of venereal diseases and injuries. Embryo transfer is also available in some African countries, but it is expensive. It allows a valuable mare to produce multiple foals per year.

After any surgery, give the mare rest. Do not breed her for at least 2-3 months. Keep the wound clean and watch for infection. In Africa, where vet services may be far away, prevention is better than surgery. Good nutrition, regular exercise, and proper breeding hygiene reduce the need for interventions. If you must operate, choose a vet with experience in equine reproduction. Ask for references. A successful surgery can save a mare's life and her breeding future.

<h2>Practical Reference: Reproductive Health &amp; Disease Management in African Horse Breeding</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the operational essentials of the module <strong>Reproductive Health and Disease Management</strong>. It is written for stud managers, equine veterinarians, and breeding-farm owners across Africa who need to protect fertility, reduce foal losses, and keep stallion and mare value intact. This lesson gives you the diagnostic, treatment, and biosecurity framework to act early and commercially.</p>

<h3>1. Common Venereal Diseases and Their Business Impact</h3>

<p>The two diseases that most often shut down breeding operations are <strong>Contagious Equine Metritis (CEM)</strong>, caused by <em>Taylorella equigenitalis</em>, and <strong>Equine Viral Arteritis (EVA)</strong>. CEM is a notifiable disease in many countries and is transmitted venereally through contaminated stallion genitalia, semen, and fomites. EVA spreads both venereally and via the respiratory route and can cause abortion storms, neonatal mortality, and carrier stallions that shed virus in semen for life.</p>

<p>African context matters here. South Africa has an established CEM control framework through the national Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development (DALRRD), and live cover of mares across farms requires negative culture status. In Kenya and Zimbabwe, EVA outbreaks have been documented in Thoroughbred populations and can force cancellation of breeding seasons. In North Africa, movement of horses between Morocco, Tunisia, and Algeria creates cross-border risk. For the business, a single CEM-positive stallion can halt an entire stud book, trigger quarantine costs, and destroy the market value of a breeding licence.</p>

<h3>2. Diagnosis and Treatment of Endometritis</h3>

<p>Endometritis is the single most common cause of subfertility in mares and is classified as <strong>acute infectious</strong>, <strong>chronic infectious</strong>, or <strong>persistent post-breeding</strong>. Diagnosis follows a stepwise protocol: history, transrectal palpation and ultrasonography, speculum examination, endometrial cytology, and culture with antimicrobial sensitivity. A guarded endometrial swab taken during oestrus gives the most reliable culture. Cytology showing neutrophils confirms active inflammation even when culture is negative.</p>

<p>Treatment depends on cause. For bacterial endometritis, the core approach is <strong>uterine lavage</strong> with sterile saline, followed by <strong>intrauterine antibiotic infusion</strong> based on culture and sensitivity. Oxytocin (1020 IU) or prostaglandin F2 analogues are used to promote uterine clearance. For mares with a history of retained fluid, post-breeding lavage plus ecbolic therapy is standard. Fungal endometritis, more common in hot humid African coastal regions, requires specific antifungal therapy such as nystatin or clotrimazole infusion after identifying the organism.</p>

<p>Commercially, the cost of a 35 day treatment cycle is small compared to the value of a successful pregnancy in a proven mare. However, indiscriminate antibiotic use without culture creates resistance and wastes money. Build a farm-level treatment log and review sensitivity patterns annually with your veterinarian.</p>

<h3>3. Managing Abortion and Infectious Causes</h3>

<p>Abortion in mares is defined as loss of pregnancy before day 300 of gestation. In African breeding operations, the most important infectious causes are <strong>EHV-1</strong>, <strong>EVA</strong>, <strong>equine viral arteritis</strong>, <strong>salmonellosis</strong>, <strong>leptospirosis</strong>, and <strong>nocardioform placentitis</strong>. Non-infectious causes include twin pregnancy, umbilical cord torsion, and maternal stress.</p>

<p>Every abortion is a biosecurity event. The aborting mare should be isolated immediately, and the foetus, placenta, and uterine swabs submitted to a veterinary laboratory. In South Africa, the <strong>Onderstepoort Veterinary Research</strong> laboratory and provincial state veterinary services provide diagnostic support; in Kenya, the <strong>Central Veterinary Laboratories</strong> in Kabete; in Nigeria, the <strong>National Veterinary Research Institute</strong> in Vom. Prompt diagnosis protects the rest of the herd and prevents a repeat event the following season.</p>

<h3>4. Vaccination and Biosecurity in Breeding Operations</h3>

<p>Vaccination is the cheapest insurance in a breeding programme. Core vaccines for breeding herds include <strong>EHV-1/EHV-4</strong> (killed or modified-live, given at 5, 7, and 9 months of gestation), <strong>EVA</strong> (for stallions and seronegative mares), <strong>tetanus</strong>, <strong>equine influenza</strong>, and <strong>African horse sickness</strong> where endemic. In South Africa, AHS vaccination is compulsory in designated control zones and must be timed outside the breeding season for mares in foal.</p>

<p>Biosecurity is the operational backbone. New arrivals must be quarantined for at least 2130 days, tested for CEM, EVA, and EIA (equine infectious anaemia), and vaccinated according to farm protocol. Breeding equipment phantom, AV, speculum, sleeves must be disinfected between mares. Stallions should never cover a mare that has not been declared clean. Handlers should move from young stock to pregnant mares, never the reverse.</p>

<h3>5. Parasite Control in Breeding Herds</h3>

<p>Internal parasites especially <em>Strongylus vulgaris</em>, cyathostomins, and <em>Parascaris equorum</em> in foals cause colic, poor condition, and reduced fertility. In breeding herds, the goal is not zero worms but <strong>strategic control that protects foals and pregnant mares without driving anthelmintic resistance</strong>. Use faecal egg counts (FEC) every 812 weeks to identify high shedders, and treat selectively. Moxidectin and ivermectin are effective against most strongyles; praziquantel is added for tapeworm in high-risk areas. In hot, humid African regions, strongyle transmission is year-round, so a targeted interval programme based on FEC is better than calendar dosing.</p>

<h3>6. Reproductive Surgery and Interventions</h3>

<p>Surgical and manual interventions are sometimes necessary to preserve breeding value. Common procedures include <strong>Caslick's vulvoplasty</strong> for mares with pneumovagina, <strong>manual reduction of twin pregnancy</strong> before day 35, <strong>cervical repair</strong> after foaling trauma, and <strong>ovariectomy</strong> for granulosa-theca cell tumours. In stallions, <strong>castration</strong> is routine for non-breeding animals, while <strong>penile and preputial surgery</strong> may be required for traumatic injuries. These procedures require trained equine surgeons and sterile facilities; in many African regions, referral to a university veterinary teaching hospital (Onderstepoort, Nairobi, Ibadan, Khartoum) is the practical route.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul style="font-family:Verdana, sans-serif; font-size:13px; line-height:1.6;">
<li><strong>Test before you breed:</strong> CEM, EVA, and EIA status confirmed for every mare and stallion at least 30 days before the season starts.</li>
<li><strong>Quarantine every new arrival</strong> for 2130 days and keep them away from pregnant mares.</li>
<li><strong>Culture and cytology</strong> any mare with discharge, repeat breeding, or a history of endometritis treat on sensitivity, not guesswork.</li>
<li><strong>Vaccinate pregnant mares</strong> against EHV-1 at 5, 7, and 9 months; keep AHS and influenza boosters current.</li>
<li><strong>Isolate and investigate every abortion</strong> submit foetus, placenta, and swabs to a recognised laboratory.</li>
<li><strong>Disinfect all breeding equipment</strong> between mares; use sterile sleeves and lubricant for every AI procedure.</li>
<li><strong>Run faecal egg counts</strong> every 812 weeks and treat only high shedders to slow anthelmintic resistance.</li>
<li><strong>Keep written records</strong> of treatments, vaccinations, and breeding dates this protects your stud book and your market value.</li>
</ul>
