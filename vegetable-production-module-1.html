Lesson 1

Why Vegetables Beat Field Crops on Cash

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Explain why vegetables generate more cash per square metre than maize or sorghum
  • Understand the short cycle as an advantage — and a discipline
  • Know the risk — vegetables fail without water and skill

A hectare of maize earns $500–1,000 gross once per year. A hectare of tomatoes under irrigation earns $4,000–15,000 gross, and can produce 2–3 crops per year. That is a 5–20x difference in cash per hectare per year. It is why almost every successful smallholder in Africa who has water has moved at least part of the farm into vegetables.

The advantage is not the price per kilogram. Vegetables often sell for less per kilogram than grain at harvest. The advantage is cycle speed and land efficiency. A 3-month tomato cycle on 1,000 m² can gross $1,200–2,000 four times a year. Grain cannot do that.

The trade-off is real. Vegetables need:

  • Water — every day, not just in the rainy season
  • Labour — daily attention, not seasonal
  • Skill — pests and diseases are the main threat, and diagnosis matters
  • Market — vegetables spoil. You must sell quickly.

Get all four right and vegetables are the best smallholder cash business in Africa. Miss any one and the crop rots in the field or on the shelf.

Lesson 2

Water First — Reliable Supply Is the Entry Bar

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Estimate the water need of a small vegetable plot
  • Choose between borehole, river, dam, tank and rainwater harvesting
  • Plan irrigation method (drip, sprinkler, furrow, watering can)

Vegetables need 4–7 mm of water per day in hot dry weather — 4,000–7,000 litres per day per hectare. Most small plots are 1,000 m², so 400–700 litres per day. That is 2–3 full watering cans per bed per day in the dry season.

Water sources ranked by reliability

  1. Borehole with solar or electric pump — most reliable, most expensive. $3,000–15,000.
  2. River or dam with pump — reliable if the source does not dry, requires permitting in most countries. $500–3,000 for pump + pipe.
  3. Farm pond / rainwater tank — seasonal, fills in the rains, empties slowly. $300–3,000. Best combined with a borehole for the dry season.
  4. Wells or shallow pits — hand-dug, reliable to a depth of 5–10 m in many areas. $200–800. Best with a hand pump or a small petrol pump.
  5. Watering can from a nearby source — cheapest, most labour-intensive, works for plots under 300 m².

Lesson 3

The Five Buyers of Vegetables

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Name the five buyer types and what each one pays
  • Match crops to the correct buyer
  • Understand the payment and rejection risks of each channel

Buyer 1 — The local market vendor

Buys daily or every other day at the market. Cash on delivery. Price is market-set and fluctuates. Convenient for small volumes, but pays the lowest price and rejects anything not visually perfect.

Buyer 2 — The urban supermarket

Buys on contract, weekly or fortnightly. Requires consistent quality, grading, packaging, and 30–60 day payment terms. Pays 20–50% above market vendors. Requires a registered business and invoices.

Buyer 3 — The restaurant and hotel

Buys weekly at higher prices for specific crops (tomatoes, lettuce, herbs, spinach). Payment weekly or monthly. Loyal if you deliver on time and to spec. Rejection is possible if quality drops.

Buyer 4 — The processor

Buys tomatoes, onions, chillies for paste, sauce, or drying. Pays 30–50% below market vendor price because they absorb gluts, but they take volume and are not quality-fussy on appearance. Cash on delivery or short credit.

Buyer 5 — The institutional buyer (school, hospital, army)

Buys on tender. Large volumes, 30–60 day payment, strict quality and delivery schedule. Requires business registration and often collateral or references. Highest total contract value but hardest to win.

Smart vegetable farmers sell to two or three channels at once. The market vendor takes the bulk. The restaurant takes the premium grade. The processor absorbs the occasional surplus. No single buyer can destroy the season.

Best beginner crop: leafy greens (kale, spinach, Swiss chard). They finish in 30–60 days, tolerate regular harvesting (cut-and-come-again), and sell every day in every market. Start there. Learn irrigation and pest management on greens before moving to tomato.

Best cash crop once you have skill: tomato. Highest gross per plot, universal market, but the pest and disease system (Module 4) is the whole battle.

Lesson 5

The Three Entry Points — 200 m², 1,000 m², or 1 ha

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Choose the entry scale that matches your water, labour and market
  • Estimate capital and expected gross at each scale
  • Scale up only after the first crop is sold

Entry point 1 — 200 m² (home garden)

  • Capital: $50–200 (seeds, tools, watering cans, manure)
  • Water: 100–200 L per day, can be carried
  • Labour: 1 person, part-time
  • Gross revenue: $150–500 per cycle
  • Best for: first learning, household + surplus sale

Entry point 2 — 1,000 m² (quarter acre)

  • Capital: $400–1,500 (drip or sprinkler, tank, seeds, fertiliser)
  • Water: 400–700 L per day, needs a tank or a pump
  • Labour: 1 full-time person + casual help at peak
  • Gross revenue: $800–2,500 per cycle, 2–3 cycles/year
  • Best for: a proper market vegetable business

Entry point 3 — 1 hectare

  • Capital: $3,000–10,000 (borehole or pump, drip lines, storage, packhouse)
  • Water: 4,000–7,000 L per day, requires a borehole or large dam
  • Labour: 3–5 full-time staff
  • Gross revenue: $8,000–30,000/year
  • Best for: a commercial vegetable operation with contracts

The rule: start at the scale your water and your market can support, not the scale your ambition wants. A 1,000 m² plot with reliable water and a buyer beats a 1-hectare plot that fails for lack of water every time.

Knowledge check — Module 1

1. Why do vegetables beat field crops on cash per hectare?

Correct: short cycles and higher value per m². A 3-month cycle can produce 2–3 crops per year.

2. What is the entry bar for a vegetable business?

Correct: reliable water. Without daily water, no vegetable plot can survive the dry season.

3. Which buyer type pays the highest price for vegetables?

Correct: restaurants, hotels, supermarkets. 20–50% above market, but require consistency.

4. What is the best beginner crop?

Correct: leafy greens. 30–60 day cycle, tolerant of regular harvest, easy to sell.

5. What is the daily water need of a 1,000 m² vegetable plot in hot weather?

Correct: 400–700 litres. At 4–7 mm/day per hectare, that is the pro-rata for 1,000 m².

Module 1 capstone

Write a one-page entry plan for your vegetable business. Include: (1) your entry scale (200 m², 1,000 m², or more) and why; (2) your water source and how many litres per day you can guarantee; (3) your first crop and why you chose it; (4) your two target buyers and what they will pay; (5) your expected gross revenue per cycle.
