1. Introduction and Why It Matters

TIG welding, or Tungsten Inert Gas welding, is a precise method that uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode to create an arc that melts the base metal. A separate filler rod is added by hand. This gives you control that stick or MIG welding can't match. In Africa, TIG is in high demand for food-grade stainless steel in dairy and brewery plants, aluminium in auto repair and window fabrication, and thin sheet metal in artisan workshops. For example, in Kenya's growing solar panel industry, TIG is used to weld aluminium frames that must be watertight and corrosion-resistant. Learning TIG opens doors to higher-paying jobs because it requires skill and patience. Unlike stick welding, TIG produces clean, spatter-free welds that often need no grinding. That saves time and money. But it's slower and demands good hand-eye coordination. You'll need a TIG machine with high-frequency start, a gas cylinder of 100% argon, and a regulator. In many African cities, you can find used TIG machines for around $300-$600. The gas costs about $50-$80 per refill, but a cylinder lasts many hours. TIG is also versatile: you can weld steel, stainless steel, aluminium, copper, and even titanium. For a self-employed welder, TIG means you can take on repair jobs for restaurants, dairies, and factories that need sanitary welds. This module will teach you from the ground up: tools, technique, fixing mistakes, safety, and a practical exercise. By the end, you'll be able to lay a clean bead on mild steel and stainless steel, and you'll know how to troubleshoot common problems. TIG is a skill that pays for itself quickly if you practice. Let's get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To TIG weld, you need the right equipment. First, a TIG welder. Look for one with adjustable amperage (at least 200A for aluminium) and high-frequency start. Inverters are lighter and more energy-efficient. Brands like Miller, Lincoln, or cheaper Chinese units like Everlast are common in Africa. Next, a tungsten electrode. For steel and stainless, use 2% thoriated (red) or ceriated (grey) tungsten. For aluminium, pure tungsten (green) works but lanthanated (gold) is better. You'll need a tungsten grinder or a dedicated grinding wheel to sharpen the tip. Never use the same wheel for steel. Shielding gas: 100% argon for most metals. For thicker aluminium, you can use a helium-argon mix, but argon is fine up to 6mm. Gas flow rate: 10-15 cubic feet per hour (CFH) for most jobs. A flowmeter is essential. Filler rods: for mild steel, use ER70S-6; for stainless, ER308L; for aluminium, ER4043 or ER5356. Sizes: 1.6mm, 2.4mm, and 3.2mm. You'll also need a welding helmet with a shade 9-13 lens, TIG gloves (thin and flexible), and a torch with a collet body, collet, and ceramic cup. Cups come in sizes #4 to #8; #6 is a good all-rounder. A foot pedal is highly recommended for amperage control. For cleaning, have a stainless steel wire brush (dedicated to aluminium only) and acetone or alcohol for wiping oil. In Africa, you can source these from welding supply shops in major cities or online. A basic TIG setup costs around $500-$1000. Don't skimp on gas quality; moisture in gas causes porosity. Keep your tungsten sharp and your metal clean. That's half the battle.

3. Step by Step Method

Start by cleaning your metal. Remove all oil, paint, and rust with acetone and a stainless brush. For aluminium, brush with a dedicated stainless brush until shiny. Set up your machine: for 3mm mild steel, set amperage to 80-100A; for 3mm stainless, 60-80A; for 3mm aluminium, 120-150A. Set gas flow to 12 CFH. Sharpen your tungsten to a point (for steel) or a balled tip (for aluminium). Clamp your work lead to the table or workpiece. Put on your helmet and gloves. Hold the torch like a pencil, with the cup resting on the metal if possible. Press the foot pedal to start the arc. Keep a short arc length (1-2mm). For steel, make a small puddle, then dip the filler rod into the leading edge of the puddle. Move forward steadily, dipping and moving. For a bead on plate, travel speed should be about 2-3mm per second. For a butt joint, tack the ends first, then weld from one end to the other. Use a slight forward push angle (10-15 degrees). For aluminium, you need to move faster and use more heat. The puddle should be shiny and fluid. Dip the filler as soon as the puddle forms. If you see black soot, you're too hot or moving too slow. For stainless, keep the heat low and move quickly to avoid warping. After welding, let the post-flow gas run for a few seconds to cool the tungsten. Practice on scrap metal first. Make 10 beads on a flat plate before trying joints. Remember: TIG is about consistency. Keep your arc length constant and your dip rhythm steady. A good exercise is to weld a series of overlapping beads, then cut and etch to see penetration. In Africa, many welders learn by doing repairs on pots, pans, and gates. Start with thin material and work up.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Tungsten touching the puddle. This contaminates the tungsten and causes a sputtery arc. Fix: Sharpen the tungsten again and restart. Keep a short arc but don't dip the tungsten. Mistake 2: Porosity (holes in the weld). Causes: dirty metal, low gas flow, or too much arc length. Fix: Clean with acetone, increase gas to 15 CFH, and shorten arc. Mistake 3: Lack of penetration. You're moving too fast or amps too low. Fix: Increase amps by 10-20A and slow down. For 3mm steel, you need at least 80A. Mistake 4: Burn-through on thin metal. Too many amps or too slow. Fix: Reduce amps, use a faster travel speed, or use a heat sink (copper backing). Mistake 5: Tungsten balling up on steel. This happens if you use pure tungsten on steel. Fix: Use 2% thoriated or ceriated tungsten. Mistake 6: Arc wandering. This is often due to a bent tungsten or wrong grind angle. Fix: Grind tungsten longitudinally, not radially, and keep it straight. Mistake 7: Black soot on aluminium. This is from too much heat or not enough cleaning. Fix: Reduce amps, increase travel speed, and brush the aluminium right before welding. Mistake 8: Cracking in stainless. This can happen from too much heat or fast cooling. Fix: Use less amps, backstep weld, or use a chill block. Mistake 9: Gas leaks. Check hose connections with soapy water. Replace cracked hoses. Mistake 10: Inconsistent bead. This is from poor hand position. Fix: Rest your hand on a block or use a torch guide. Practice makes perfect. In Africa, where materials can be expensive, always test on scrap first. Learn to read the puddle: it should be a calm, shiny pool. If it's turbulent, something is wrong. Keep a notebook of settings that work for different thicknesses. That's how you become a pro.

5. Safety and Best Practice

TIG welding is safer than stick welding because there's no slag and less spatter, but hazards still exist. The arc emits intense ultraviolet (UV) and infrared light. Always wear a welding helmet with a proper shade. For TIG at 100A, use shade 10; for 200A, shade 12. Never look at the arc with naked eyes. Wear flame-resistant clothing, leather gloves, and a leather apron. TIG gloves are thin for dexterity, but they still protect from heat and UV. Ensure good ventilation. TIG welding produces ozone and nitrogen oxides, especially on aluminium. In a small workshop, use a fume extractor or open windows and doors. Never weld in a closed room. Argon gas is heavier than air and can displace oxygen. If you're welding in a pit or confined space, use a respirator and have someone watch you. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. The arc can ignite flammable materials. Clear the area of paper, cloth, and solvents. Electrical safety: check your cables for damage. Don't weld in wet conditions. Use a GFCI if possible. Ground the workpiece properly. When not welding, turn off the machine and close the gas valve. Store gas cylinders upright and secure them with a chain. Never roll a cylinder on its side. In Africa, power fluctuations can damage inverters. Use a surge protector or voltage stabilizer. Best practices: Keep your tungsten sharp and clean. Use the right gas flow. Don't overheat the metal; it can warp or crack. Let the weld cool before handling. Clean your work area daily. Wear safety glasses when grinding tungsten. And always practice good posture to avoid back strain. TIG welding can be done for hours, so take breaks. Drink water. Remember, safety is not just for you; it's for everyone in the shop. A safe welder is a productive welder.

6. Practical Exercise

For this exercise, you'll need: a TIG welder, 3mm mild steel plate (100mm x 100mm), ER70S-6 filler rod (2.4mm), argon gas, a #6 cup, 2% thoriated tungsten (2.4mm), a stainless brush, acetone, and a grinder. First, clean the steel with acetone and brush off any rust. Set your machine to DCEN (direct current electrode negative) for steel. Set amperage to 90A. Set gas flow to 12 CFH. Sharpen your tungsten to a sharp point. Clamp the plate to your table. Put on your helmet and gloves. Step 1: Make a bead on plate without filler. Practice moving the torch in a straight line, creating a puddle. Keep arc length 2mm. Do this for 5 passes. Step 2: Add filler. Hold the filler rod at a 15-degree angle. Dip the rod into the leading edge of the puddle, then move forward. Dip every 2-3mm. Aim for a consistent bead width of 4-5mm. Do 10 beads. Step 3: Butt joint. Cut two plates, place them with a 1mm gap, tack both ends. Weld from one end to the other. Use the same dip technique. Check penetration by cutting the weld crosswise and polishing with a grinder. You want full penetration without burn-through. Step 4: T-joint. Stand one plate on another and weld the corner. This is harder; use 80A and move faster. Step 5: Aluminium practice (optional). If you have aluminium, set to AC, use pure tungsten, and 120A. Clean with a stainless brush, weld a bead. After each step, inspect your weld. Look for undercut, porosity, and consistency. Write down the settings that worked. Repeat until you can make a clean bead without stopping. In Africa, you can use scrap metal from car panels or roofing sheets. Practice for at least 2 hours. This exercise builds muscle memory. When you can weld a butt joint on 3mm steel that passes a bend test, you're ready for real jobs.

This Practical Reference consolidates the tools, parameters, defect remedies and safety controls you need to quote, weld and deliver TIG work profitably. <div style="border:2px solid #c0392b;background:#fdecea;padding:14px;border-radius:6px;margin:20px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING TIG welding hazards</strong>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 18px;padding:0;">
<li><strong>Electric shock:</strong> TIG uses high-frequency (HF) starting. Never weld in wet boots or on damp ground common during rainy seasons. Use a dry insulating mat and check the earth clamp.</li>
<li><strong>UV radiation:</strong> The TIG arc emits intense UV. Always use a shade 1013 helmet with correct filter. Bare skin will burn within minutes.</li>
<li><strong>Argon asphyxiation:</strong> Argon is heavier than air and displaces oxygen in confined workshops, pits or tanks. Ventilate enclosed spaces and never weld inside a sealed vessel without forced extraction.</li>
<li><strong>Fumes:</strong> Stainless welding releases hexavalent chromium and nickel. Use local exhaust ventilation or a powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR).</li>
<li><strong>Fire:</strong> Sparks can ignite dry grass, maize husks or diesel spills common in agri workshops. Keep a dry-powder extinguisher within 3 metres and clear combustibles from the bench.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prepare the metal:</strong> Degrease with acetone, grind to bright metal, bevel joints &gt;3 mm.</li>
<li><strong>Select tungsten:</strong> 2% lanthanated for AC/DC general work; grind longitudinally to a sharp point.</li>
<li><strong>Set gas flow:</strong> 812 L/min argon; increase to 14 L/min for aluminium or outdoor draughts.</li>
<li><strong>Check polarity:</strong> DCEN for steel and stainless; AC for aluminium.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain arc length:</strong> 23 mm, short enough to hear a steady hiss, not a crackle.</li>
<li><strong>Feed filler consistently:</strong> dip at the leading edge of the pool, withdraw before moving.</li>
<li><strong>Control heat input:</strong> use foot pedal or amperage control to taper off and avoid crater cracks.</li>
<li><strong>Post-weld:</strong> brush stainless with a dedicated stainless brush; inspect for porosity before signing off.</li>
</ul>
