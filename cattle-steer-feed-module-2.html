Lesson 2.1~12 min

Colostrum and Early Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Explain passive transfer and why the first hours of life decide it
  • Set a written colostrum protocol that any worker on the farm can follow
  • Identify the vitamin and mineral gaps a newborn calf carries from its dam

A calf is born with almost no immune protection of its own. It has a working immune system but no antibodies in circulation, because the cow's placenta does not pass them across during pregnancy. Everything the calf has to fight infection with in its first weeks comes in through the gut, in colostrum, in a window that closes fast. Nutritionists call this passive transfer, and it is the single largest controllable factor in whether calves live or die in the first month.

Two things are happening in that window. The colostrum has to be there, and the gut has to still be open. The calf's intestine can absorb whole antibody molecules for only a short period after birth, and that ability falls away steadily over the first day of life. A calf that gets excellent colostrum a day late gets a good meal and very little immunity. This is why speed matters more than perfection: get clean colostrum into the calf as soon as it can swallow.

The reference standards behind this course do not publish a fixed volume figure, so treat any litres-per-calf number as approximate and adjust to your own breed and calf size. What they do give is the composition target for the newborn's diet: a milk-only diet should supply 20 to 24 percent crude protein on a dry matter basis. That is far higher than anything the animal will need again in its life, and it reflects a body that is almost all growing tissue.

Colostrum also carries fat-soluble vitamins that the calf is genuinely short of at birth, because these do not cross the placenta well either. Vitamin E and selenium matter most. Published guidance treats the periparturient window - roughly five weeks before calving through three weeks after - as the critical period for vitamin E and selenium supplementation of the dam, with an optimal ratio of 1 mg selenium per 100 IU of vitamin E for maximum immune benefit. Selenium requirement is 0.10 mg/kg of diet dry matter, with a maximum tolerable level of 5 mg/kg. Get this wrong in the dry cow and the calf is born into a deficit: selenium-responsive white muscle disease, weak calves that will not stand to suck, and poor immune response.

Worked example on colostrum economics. Say you run 30 cows and lose four calves a year in the first month, which is a 13 percent loss. If a weaned calf is worth 220 USD, those four calves are 880 USD of lost revenue plus the cost of carrying the cows. Now cost the fix. A clean bucket and teat set, a thermometer, navel iodine and gloves might run 60 USD once. A vitamin E and selenium supplement for 30 dry cows through the critical window might run 45 USD. If that programme saves two of the four calves, the return is 440 USD against roughly 105 USD spent, in the first year. Very little else on a cattle farm pays like that.

The practical protocol, written so anyone can follow it:

  1. Be present at calving where you can. A calf that is dried, breathing and standing quickly will suck sooner.
  2. Dip or spray the navel with iodine at birth. The navel is an open door straight into the abdomen.
  3. Get colostrum in as fast as possible after birth, and top up again the same day. If the calf will not suck, feed it by bottle or by trained tube feeder.
  4. Use clean equipment every time. Colostrum is a perfect bacterial culture medium; a dirty bucket turns the best colostrum on the farm into an infection dose.
  5. Keep spare colostrum from a healthy, well-fed cow frozen in labelled bags for the calf whose dam has none.
  6. Feed the dry cow properly. Colostrum quality is made in the last weeks of pregnancy, not on the day of calving.

A named African case. In smallholder dairy schemes in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, extension services found that calf mortality was concentrated in households where calving happened unattended in the field overnight. The technical fix was not a new feed. It was moving close-up cows to a clean paddock near the homestead, dipping navels, and having a frozen colostrum reserve. Farms that adopted the routine reported far fewer first-month losses without spending on additional purchased inputs.

Colostrum is the cheapest veterinary product on your farm, and it has a shelf life measured in hours.

Liquid Feeding Strategies

In this lesson
  • Compare whole milk, milk replacer and restricted versus generous feeding on cost and growth
  • Convert a liquid feeding plan into a daily cost per calf in USD
  • Manage feeding hygiene, temperature and timing to prevent digestive upsets

After colostrum, the calf lives on liquid feed until its rumen is developed enough to run on solids. How you handle those weeks decides both the calf's growth and a surprisingly large share of your cash cost, because milk is expensive - either you buy replacer or you forgo milk you could have sold.

Three choices are on the table for most African producers. Whole milk from the dam is the simplest and gives excellent nutrition, but every litre fed is a litre not sold, so its true cost is your farm-gate milk price. Commercial milk replacer is usually cheaper per litre reconstituted but its quality varies enormously and a poor replacer built on plant protein rather than milk protein will not perform in a very young calf. Nurse feeding, where the calf suckles the dam directly, costs no labour and virtually eliminates hygiene problems, but you cannot measure intake and you cannot easily wean.

The nutrient target is the same whichever route you take: a milk-only diet supplies 20 to 24 percent crude protein on a dry matter basis. Read the replacer label against that. A bag showing 18 percent crude protein is not a young-calf product whatever the picture on the front says.

The reference standards here do not publish a single fixed daily litres figure, and the right allowance depends on breed, calf weight, ambient temperature and whether you want fast early growth or the cheapest possible weaning. Treat the numbers in any feeding chart as approximate starting points and judge by the calf. What is not negotiable is consistency: the same volume, at the same temperature, at the same times each day. Most liquid-phase scours on smallholder farms trace back to variation - a cold feed, a doubled feed after a missed one, or a bucket washed with pond water.

Worked example on cost. Compare two plans for one calf over an eight-week liquid phase, using a farm-gate milk price of 0.45 USD per litre and a milk replacer costing 2.40 USD per kg that reconstitutes at 125 g per litre.

Plan A, whole milk at 4 litres a day: 4 x 0.45 = 1.80 USD per calf per day. Over 56 days that is 100.80 USD of forgone milk sales. Plan B, milk replacer at 4 litres a day: 4 x 0.125 = 0.5 kg powder, at 2.40 USD/kg that is 1.20 USD per day, or 67.20 USD over 56 days.

Plan B saves 33.60 USD per calf, roughly a third. Across 20 calves a year that is 672 USD - real money on a smallholder unit. But the saving only holds if the replacer is a genuine milk-protein product at 20 to 24 percent crude protein and is mixed at the stated rate with clean water at the stated temperature. A cheap replacer that costs you one dead calf worth 220 USD has wiped out six calves' worth of savings.

Practical management points:

  1. Feed at body temperature, near 39 degrees C. Cold milk chills the calf and slows digestion.
  2. Split the daily allowance into at least two feeds. Large single feeds overload the abomasum.
  3. Wash and dry every bucket, teat and mixing jug after every use. Hygiene is the whole game in the liquid phase.
  4. Offer clean fresh water from the first week, separately from milk. Calves need free water to start eating starter and developing a rumen; milk alone does not substitute.
  5. Never increase or change the liquid feed abruptly. Step changes of 10 to 15 percent are the general rule for diet change in cattle, and a young calf is less forgiving than an adult.
  6. Weigh or heart-girth tape calves fortnightly. It is the only honest feedback you will get.

A named African case. Peri-urban dairy producers around Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, face a sharp version of this trade-off because fluid milk prices are high and the temptation to restrict calf milk is strong. Farms that cut liquid feed aggressively to sell more milk reported calves reaching weaning weight several weeks late and entering their first lactation later, which pushed the whole herd's returns backwards. The producers who did best treated the milk fed to a heifer calf as an investment in an asset rather than as lost sales, while using replacer where they could source a reliable milk-protein product.

The liquid phase is short. Its consequences are not.

Starter Feed Formulation for Young Calves

In this lesson
  • Explain how dry starter intake builds the rumen that milk cannot build
  • Formulate a starter to a 16-18% crude protein target from locally available ingredients
  • Screen starter ingredients for aflatoxin and gossypol risk before feeding

A newborn calf is functionally a simple-stomached animal. Milk bypasses the rumen through the oesophageal groove and is digested in the abomasum. The rumen at birth is small, smooth-walled and non-functional. It becomes a working fermentation vat only when dry feed ferments inside it, and the calf that has not eaten dry feed cannot be weaned safely no matter how old it is.

That is the whole reason for calf starter. Its job is not primarily to feed the calf - the milk is doing that - it is to grow the rumen wall, establish a microbial population, and build the capacity that will let the animal live on forage afterwards. The wider physiology of rumen development is treated in Module 9; here we are concerned with what to put in the bucket.

The composition target is clear. Published guidance puts pre-weaning calves at 16 to 18 percent crude protein of diet dry matter for optimal gain, dropping to 14 to 16 percent post-weaning for continued growth. Starter should be palatable, dry, and offered fresh in small amounts daily so it never goes stale or mouldy. Calves are fussy; a handful of fresh starter eaten beats a kilogram of stale starter refused.

Worked formulation. Suppose you can buy, per kg as-fed: maize meal at 0.30 USD and about 9 percent crude protein, wheat bran at 0.22 USD and about 15 percent, sunflower cake at 0.28 USD and about 30 percent, and molasses at 0.25 USD and about 4 percent. You want roughly 17 percent crude protein. Try a mix of 45 percent maize meal, 20 percent wheat bran, 30 percent sunflower cake and 5 percent molasses.

Crude protein by weighted average: (0.45 x 9) + (0.20 x 15) + (0.30 x 30) + (0.05 x 4) = 4.05 + 3.00 + 9.00 + 0.20 = 16.25 percent. Slightly low. Shift 5 percentage points from maize to sunflower cake: 40 percent maize, 20 percent bran, 35 percent cake, 5 percent molasses gives (0.40 x 9) + (0.20 x 15) + (0.35 x 30) + (0.05 x 4) = 3.60 + 3.00 + 10.50 + 0.20 = 17.30 percent. That is inside target.

Cost per kg: (0.40 x 0.30) + (0.20 x 0.22) + (0.35 x 0.28) + (0.05 x 0.25) = 0.120 + 0.044 + 0.098 + 0.013 = 0.275 USD per kg. Add roughly 2 percent of a calf mineral and vitamin premix on top and you are near 0.30 USD per kg of a starter you formulated yourself. Compare that against the bagged commercial starter price in your town before deciding which route to take - sometimes the commercial bag wins on convenience and consistency, and sometimes it is double the price for the same specification.

Two ingredient hazards deserve specific attention in a calf starter. Aflatoxin is the first: the published feed limit is 20 micrograms per kg (ppb), and the risk comes from heat and moisture in maize, groundnuts and cottonseed meal, with visible mould not always present. Aflatoxin in a calf shows as feed refusal, diarrhoea and immune suppression, and in a dairy situation it carries into milk, where the safety limit is 0.5 micrograms per kg. Gossypol is the second: cottonseed products are toxic to calves at 400 mg/kg of diet dry matter and above, causing reduced growth. Cottonseed cake is a good adult protein feed and a poor calf ingredient. Use sunflower, groundnut or soya cake in starter instead.

A named African case. Around Nakuru, Kenya, dairy farms buying bagged calf starter through cooperative stores have generally seen calves reach weaning starter intakes earlier than farms mixing maize bran and milk by hand, mostly because the bagged product is consistent and palatable rather than because its formula is superior. The producers who did equally well on home mixes were the ones who kept ingredients dry, mixed small fresh batches weekly, and included a proper calf premix rather than adult cattle mineral.

Practical rules: offer starter from the first week, fresh daily, in small amounts. Throw out what is not eaten. Keep clean water beside it at all times. Store ingredients dry below roughly 12 to 14 percent moisture and rotate stock so nothing sits long enough to mould.

Transition to Forage-Based Diets

In this lesson
  • Set a weaning decision rule based on starter intake rather than calendar age
  • Apply the ADF and NDF equations to check whether a forage can support a weaned calf
  • Identify the forage toxicity risks that specifically threaten young stock

Weaning is not a date. It is a physiological state: the calf can be weaned when its rumen is developed enough to extract what it needs from dry feed. The single best field indicator is consistent, substantial daily starter intake over several consecutive days. A calf eating starter reliably is a calf whose rumen is working. A calf of the same age eating almost nothing is not ready, and weaning it produces a growth check that can take months to recover.

The nutrient target steps down at weaning. Pre-weaning calves need 16 to 18 percent crude protein of diet dry matter; post-weaning the published target is 14 to 16 percent for continued growth. That is still far above the 7.5 percent floor and far above what most tropical grass hay supplies on its own, which is the crux of the problem: a freshly weaned calf cannot live on the same forage that maintains an adult cow.

Here is where the two forage equations do real work. Take a weaned calf of 120 kg being offered Rhodes grass hay at 38 percent ADF and 66 percent NDF.

Digestible dry matter: DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 38) = 88.9 - 29.6 = 59.3 percent. Intake capacity: DMI = 120 / 66 = 1.82 percent of body weight, so 120 x 0.0182 = 2.18 kg of dry matter per day.

If that hay is 7 percent crude protein, the calf receives 2.18 x 0.07 = 0.15 kg of crude protein, which is 7.0 percent of the diet. Below the floor, and nowhere near the 14 to 16 percent a growing weaned calf needs. To reach 15 percent from 2.18 kg of intake you need 2.18 x 0.15 = 0.33 kg of crude protein, a shortfall of 0.18 kg. Sunflower cake at 30 percent crude protein supplies that in 0.18 / 0.30 = 0.60 kg. At 0.28 USD per kg, that is 0.17 USD per calf per day. Note also that adding the supplement lifts total intake above 2.18 kg, because the rumen now digests fibre faster - the calculation understates the benefit.

Manage the transition itself in steps. The general rule for cattle is that diet changes should not exceed 10 to 15 percent of diet dry matter at a time, with two to three weeks for the microbial population to adapt. In practice: keep starter available for several weeks after the milk stops, introduce the new forage alongside the old feed rather than in place of it, and do not wean, move, dehorn and vaccinate in the same week. Stack those stresses and you get the classic post-weaning slump.

Forage toxicity risks fall hardest on young stock, because a small animal reaches a toxic dose on a smaller amount of feed. Two matter most in African systems. Prussic acid from sorghum, sudangrass, sorghum-sudan hybrids and especially johnsongrass is released when the plant is chewed. The published lethal dose is around 4.4 mg HCN per kg of body weight per hour, and the risk is highest in young plants, frost-damaged plants and rapid regrowth, with leaves more hazardous than stems. Published management is to allow regrowth to reach 18 to 24 inches before grazing, to ensile sorghum for three to four weeks so the HCN dissipates, or to hay-cure it well. Do not turn weaned calves onto short sorghum regrowth.

Nitrate is the second. Drought-stressed and heavily fertilised forages accumulate it. Below 0.5 percent of dry matter is safe; 0.5 to 1.5 percent needs caution and at least 2 lb per head daily of energy grain; 1.5 to 3.0 percent is unsafe for grazing; above 3.0 percent must not be fed at all.

A named African case. Around Arusha in northern Tanzania, weaned calves turned out onto young sorghum regrowth after early rains have historically been the classic prussic acid casualty. Farms that hold calves back until the regrowth reaches knee height, or that graze mature crop residue first, avoid the losses on the same fields. The forage is not the problem; the growth stage is.

Growth Targets and Body Condition

In this lesson
  • Calculate maintenance energy requirement from metabolic body weight
  • Score body condition on a 1-5 scale and convert a score change into energy terms
  • Set a written weight target for each stage and measure against it fortnightly

You cannot manage what you do not measure, and on most smallholder cattle units nobody weighs anything. That single habit change - fortnightly weights and monthly condition scores written in a book - does more for growth than any feed additive on the market.

Start with the energy arithmetic, because it explains why targets are met or missed. Maintenance energy for beef cattle is calculated from metabolic body weight: NEm = 0.077 x BW to the power 0.75 Mcal per day, where BW is in kilograms. For dairy cattle the published coefficient is higher at 0.10 x BW to the power 0.75, reflecting modern high-production genetics. Published worked values include roughly 3.5 Mcal NEm per day for a 500 lb steer and 6.0 to 7.0 Mcal per day for a 1,200 lb cow, and for dairy about 2.8 Mcal per day for a 500 kg cow and 3.4 Mcal for a 650 kg cow.

Worked example. A 150 kg weaned calf. Metabolic body weight is 150 to the power 0.75. Take it in steps: the square root of 150 is 12.25, and the square root of 12.25 is 3.50, so 150 to the power 0.25 is 3.50 and 150 to the power 0.75 is 150 / 3.50 = 42.9. Maintenance is therefore 0.077 x 42.9 = 3.30 Mcal NEm per day. Everything above that is available for growth. Published figures put a 500 lb steer gaining 1.5 lb a day at 3.2 Mcal NEg per day on top of maintenance, so growth roughly doubles the energy bill. That is why an animal on a maintenance-only ration simply stands still, and why the last kilogram of feed is the one that produces the gain.

Body condition score is your low-cost check on whether the energy is arriving. Score on a 1 to 5 scale by feel over the ribs, spine and tail head - 1 being emaciated, 3 being moderate, 5 being over-fat. The published energy value of condition is useful: one body condition score unit is roughly 9.4 percent of body weight in energy storage, with an energy value of 6.3 Mcal of retained energy per kg and a requirement of 5.6 Mcal NEL per kg to put it on. So on a 300 kg animal, one condition score is about 28 kg of body tissue, and replacing it takes roughly 28 x 5.6 = 157 Mcal of net energy. That is weeks of surplus feeding. Losing condition is fast and cheap; regaining it is slow and expensive, which is the strongest argument there is for feeding through the dry season rather than after it.

Practical targets to write down for a growing calf: a birth weight, a weaning weight and a target weight at 12 months, each specific to your breed and market. Then measure. A weigh-band or heart-girth tape costs a few dollars and, used the same way each time by the same person, is accurate enough to detect a stall. Weigh fortnightly, plot on paper, and act when the line flattens rather than three months later when you notice the animals look thin.

Worked example on cost of a stall. A 150 kg calf that should be gaining 0.6 kg a day but is gaining 0.2 kg is losing 0.4 kg of gain a day. Over a 90-day dry season that is 36 kg of live weight. At a live weight price of 2.20 USD per kg, that is 79 USD per animal. Supplementing 0.6 kg of sunflower cake a day at 0.28 USD would cost 0.17 x 90 = 15 USD. The comparison is not close, and it holds for most African dry seasons in most years.

A named African case. Under Rwanda's Girinka programme, households receiving a heifer are trained to record growth and condition. Where recording was actually done, extension officers could identify underfeeding early and intervene during the dry season. Where no records were kept, the same problem was usually only noticed at the point of a failed first service, by which time the heifer's productive life had already been delayed by a year.

Measure, write it down, and act on the flat part of the line.

Lesson 2.6~12 min

Health Management Through Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Trace calf scours and early losses back to specific nutrition and hygiene failures
  • Set vitamin A, D and E provision for calves against published requirements
  • Build a nutrition-led prevention checklist for the first 90 days of life

Most calf deaths on smallholder farms are not caused by exotic disease. They are caused by a short list of preventable failures, and nutrition sits underneath nearly all of them. The metabolic diseases of adult cattle - milk fever, ketosis, grass tetany - are dealt with in Module 11. This lesson stays with the calf.

Start with the three that kill. Failure of passive transfer is first: the calf that did not get enough clean colostrum fast enough has no antibody protection and will meet everything on the farm undefended. Navel infection is second: the umbilical stump is an open route into the abdomen, and iodine at birth closes it. Scours is third, and it is where nutrition and hygiene meet. Digestive scours comes from inconsistency - cold milk, doubled feeds, abrupt changes, dirty buckets. Infectious scours comes from pathogen load in a calf with weak defences. The two feed each other: a gut irritated by inconsistent feeding is easier to colonise, and an infected calf stops eating and loses condition fast.

The nutritional side of prevention is specific and cheap. Vitamin A requirement for a 500 lb growing calf is 12,500 IU per day, rising to 15,000 IU per day for stressed calves under health challenge. Dietary concentration runs 1,000 to 1,773 IU per lb of dry feed depending on class. This matters far more in Africa than most producers realise, because vitamin A comes from beta-carotene in green forage and stored hay loses it fast: published figures show beta-carotene degrading 33 to 50 percent per year of storage, so hay is at 50 to 67 percent of original vitamin A after one year and 25 to 50 percent after two, with brown hay severely depleted. Any calf on dry-season hay or dormant standing pasture needs supplementation. The earliest sign of deficiency is night blindness, followed by poor coat, skeletal problems and immune compromise.

Vitamin E follows the same pattern. Growing cattle need 16 IU per lb of feed dry matter, but stressed calves need 400 to 500 IU per day - a very large step up. Stored feed loses 2 to 3 IU per lb per year. Pair it with selenium at 0.10 mg/kg of diet dry matter, keeping the 1 mg selenium per 100 IU vitamin E ratio in mind, and remembering the maximum tolerable level of 5 mg/kg. Vitamin D at 125 IU per lb of diet dry matter prevents rickets; calves with normal sun exposure make their own, and housed calves do not. B vitamins and vitamin K are made by rumen microbes once the rumen is working, so they are rarely a concern except during severe illness or antimicrobial treatment - but note that a pre-weaning calf has no functioning rumen yet, which is one more reason its liquid diet must be complete.

Feed safety is the other half. The aflatoxin limit in feed is 20 micrograms per kg, and calves show feed refusal, diarrhoea and immune suppression. Gossypol is toxic to calves at 400 mg/kg of diet dry matter and above. Store feed below roughly 12 to 14 percent moisture, inspect visually for discoloured grain, mould and off odours, and rotate stock. Water quality counts too: the nitrate standard is 10 mg/L of nitrate-nitrogen.

Worked example on the economics of prevention. Twenty calves a year, 15 percent first-month mortality means three deaths. At 220 USD each that is 660 USD. A prevention package of navel iodine, clean feeding equipment, a calf-specific vitamin and mineral premix and stressed-calf vitamin E and selenium might total 120 to 150 USD across all twenty calves. Halving the loss returns 330 USD against that spend, and the surviving calves also grow faster because they are not fighting infection.

A named African case. Dairy cooperatives around Mbarara in south-western Uganda ran calf-mortality audits and found losses concentrated in the first three weeks, dominated by scours and navel infection rather than by any specific pathogen. The intervention that moved the numbers was a written feeding routine, individual clean feeding equipment and navel dipping - not more medicine.

The checklist for the first 90 days: clean colostrum fast, iodine the navel, feed the same amount at the same temperature at the same times, wash everything, offer fresh starter and clean water daily, supplement vitamins A, D and E where feed is dry or stored, and weigh fortnightly so you see trouble before it becomes a loss.

Do this today: check your calf feeding equipment for cleanliness and your feed store for mould and moisture, then read your mineral and vitamin product label for vitamin A, D and E levels and compare them with the published calf requirements.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Why is a calf born without circulating antibodies?

Bovine placentation does not pass antibodies to the fetus, so all early immunity arrives through colostrum and must be absorbed across the gut.

2. The published crude protein target for a milk-only newborn diet is:

A milk-only diet should supply 20-24% crude protein on a dry matter basis.

3. Why does speed matter more than volume perfection with colostrum?

The absorptive window closes progressively after birth. Excellent colostrum given late delivers nutrition but very little immunity.

4. The optimal published ratio of selenium to vitamin E for immune benefit is:

Published guidance gives 1 mg selenium per 100 IU vitamin E as the optimal ratio.

5. Selenium deficiency in young calves classically causes:

White muscle disease is a selenium-responsive myopathy of young stock, and the deficit usually starts in the dam's diet.

6. The true cost of feeding whole milk to a calf is best measured as:

Every litre fed is a litre not sold, so forgone revenue is the real cost and it is what you compare replacer against.

7. A milk replacer label shows 18% crude protein. For a very young calf this is:

A milk-only diet should supply 20-24% crude protein of dry matter; 18% falls short.

8. Why should calves have free clean water from the first week?

Water is required for dry starter intake and rumen fermentation to begin. Milk alone bypasses the rumen and does not substitute.

9. At 4 litres a day and 0.45 USD per litre, the milk cost of one calf over 56 days is:

4 x 0.45 = 1.80 USD per day, and 1.80 x 56 = 100.80 USD.

10. Most liquid-phase scours on smallholder farms trace back to:

Consistency and hygiene dominate. Cold feeds, doubled feeds after a missed one, and dirty equipment are the usual causes.

11. What is the main purpose of calf starter?

Milk bypasses the rumen. Only fermentation of dry feed builds the rumen wall and microbial population needed for weaning.

12. The published pre-weaning crude protein target is:

16-18% crude protein of diet dry matter pre-weaning, falling to 14-16% post-weaning.

13. A mix of 40% maize (9% CP), 20% bran (15% CP), 35% sunflower cake (30% CP) and 5% molasses (4% CP) gives roughly:

3.60 + 3.00 + 10.50 + 0.20 = 17.30% crude protein by weighted average.

14. Why is cottonseed cake a poor calf starter ingredient?

Gossypol reduces growth in calves at 400 mg/kg of diet DM and above; cottonseed suits fattening stock far better.

15. The published aflatoxin limit in cattle feed is:

20 micrograms per kg (ppb) is the published feed limit; the milk safety limit is far tighter at 0.5 micrograms per kg.

16. The best practical signal that a calf is ready to wean is:

Weaning readiness is physiological. Reliable dry feed intake shows the rumen is functioning; age alone does not.

17. A hay at 38% ADF has a digestible dry matter of:

DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 38) = 88.9 - 29.6 = 59.3%.

18. Sorghum regrowth should reach what height before grazing?

Published management calls for 18-24 inches of regrowth to reduce prussic acid risk, or ensiling for 3-4 weeks.

19. Forage at 4% nitrate on a dry matter basis should be:

Above 3.0% of dry matter, nitrate is classed as highly toxic and must not be fed.

20. Why should weaning, moving, dehorning and vaccinating not happen in the same week?

Each is a stressor that reduces intake. Together they compound into the classic post-weaning slump that takes months to recover.

21. Maintenance energy for beef cattle is calculated as:

NEm = 0.077 x BW(kg) to the power 0.75 Mcal per day for beef cattle under NRC 2016.

22. A 150 kg calf has a metabolic body weight of about 42.9. Its maintenance requirement is therefore:

0.077 x 42.9 = 3.30 Mcal NEm per day, before any allowance for growth.

23. One body condition score unit represents roughly what share of body weight?

Published work puts one BCS unit at about 9.4% of body weight in energy storage.

24. Why is preventing condition loss better than recovering it?

Loss is fast; regain requires 5.6 Mcal NEL per kg of tissue, so recovery feeding costs far more than maintenance feeding would have.

25. The dairy maintenance coefficient of 0.10 compared with beef's 0.077 reflects:

NRC Dairy 2001 raised the coefficient from the earlier 0.08 standard to reflect modern high-production genetics.

26. The three commonest causes of early calf loss on smallholder farms are:

Adult metabolic diseases are a separate topic. Early calf losses are dominated by failed passive transfer, navel infection and scours.

27. Vitamin A requirement for a stressed 500 lb calf is:

12,500 IU/day is the baseline for a 500 lb growing calf, boosted to 15,000 IU/day under health challenge.

28. Beta-carotene in stored hay degrades by roughly:

Published figures put the loss at 33-50% per year, leaving one-year-old hay at 50-67% of its original vitamin A.

29. Why are B vitamins normally not supplemented in cattle?

Rumen microbes synthesise most B vitamins and vitamin K, so supplementation is rarely needed except during severe illness or antimicrobial treatment.

30. Digestive scours in the liquid phase is most often caused by:

Inconsistency and poor hygiene irritate the gut and raise pathogen load, and the two effects compound each other.

Module 2 capstone

Calf Rearing Protocol and Cost Sheet for your own unit. Step 1: write a one-page colostrum protocol naming who feeds the newborn, within how many hours, how much, how it is checked for cleanliness and how the navel is treated - and pin it where the person doing the work can read it. Step 2: build a liquid feeding plan for one calf from birth to weaning showing, week by week, the litres or milk replacer offered per day, the target calf weight, and the daily feed cost in USD. Step 3: specify a calf starter using ingredients you can actually buy, and calculate its crude protein by weighted average, showing the arithmetic; the target is 16-18% crude protein of dry matter pre-weaning. Step 4: set your weaning rule in writing as a starter intake threshold rather than a date, and state the post-weaning crude protein target of 14-16% of dry matter. Step 5: for the forage the calf will move onto, calculate DDM% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%) and DMI% of body weight = 120 / NDF%, and check whether a 120 kg calf can physically eat enough of it. Step 6: total the full cost of rearing that calf to weaning in USD - milk, starter, minerals, vaccines, labour - and divide by the weaning weight to get cost per kg of weaned calf. Step 7: list the three things on your farm most likely to cause a calf death in the first month, and the nutrition-led step you will take against each.
