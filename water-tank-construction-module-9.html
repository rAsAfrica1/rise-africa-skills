1. Lesson 1: Water Quality Fundamentals and Treatment Planning for Tank Owners

This lesson sets the commercial and technical foundation for water treatment in tank systems across Africa. If you are building tanks for schools, clinics, hotels, estates or farms, treatment is not an add-on. It is the difference between a tank that sells and a tank that gets abandoned after the first rainy season. Your client is not buying a tank. They are buying safe, reliable water. That is the business insight. In Kenya, a 10,000 litre plastic tank costs between KES 85,000 and KES 140,000 depending on brand and wall thickness. A ferrocement tank of the same volume built on site costs KES 45,000 to KES 75,000 in materials and labour. A reinforced concrete tank of 20,000 litres costs KES 180,000 to KES 350,000. In Nigeria, a 1,000 gallon (about 4,500 litre) plastic tank sells for NGN 250,000 to NGN 450,000. In Ghana, a 5,000 litre poly tank is GHS 3,500 to GHS 6,000. In South Africa, a 5,000 litre JoJo tank is ZAR 6,500 to ZAR 11,000. These numbers matter because treatment choices must match the tank material, the water source and the client budget. Water quality problems in African tanks fall into four groups. First, microbial contamination from bacteria, viruses and protozoa. Second, turbidity from silt, clay and organic matter. Third, chemical problems such as iron, manganese, fluoride, nitrate and salinity. Fourth, aesthetic problems such as taste, odour and colour. Your job as a tank builder is to diagnose these and design a treatment train that fits. A treatment train is simply the sequence of steps: source protection, pre-filtration, sedimentation, filtration, disinfection and storage. For a typical borehole feeding a concrete tank, the train might be: borehole, aeration for iron removal, 5 micron sediment filter, activated carbon filter, chlorination, then storage. For a rainwater harvesting system feeding a ferrocement tank, the train might be: roof catchment, first flush diverter, gutter mesh, 100 micron pre-filter, sedimentation in the tank, then chlorination or boiling at point of use. For a river or dam source feeding a brick tank, the train might be: intake screen, roughing filter, coagulation and flocculation with alum, sedimentation, rapid sand filter, chlorination and storage. Each step costs money and needs maintenance. A 5 micron sediment filter cartridge costs KES 800 to KES 1,500 in Nairobi and needs replacement every 1 to 3 months. A 10 inch activated carbon block filter costs KES 2,500 to KES 4,500 and lasts 3 to 6 months. A basic inline chlorinator for a 10,000 litre tank costs KES 15,000 to KES 35,000 installed. A dosing pump system costs KES 60,000 to KES 150,000. In Nigeria, a 10 inch filter housing is NGN 25,000 to NGN 45,000. In Ghana, a UV unit for 1,000 litres per hour is GHS 4,000 to GHS 8,000. In South Africa, a domestic reverse osmosis unit is ZAR 4,000 to ZAR 12,000. Your treatment plan must be written and signed with the client. It should state the source, the tank material and volume, the daily demand, the treatment steps, the chemicals and doses, the maintenance schedule, the responsible person and the cost. This protects you from blame when the client fails to maintain the system. It also creates a recurring revenue stream. You can sell a service contract for tank cleaning and chlorination at KES 8,000 to KES 20,000 per visit in Kenya, NGN 40,000 to NGN 100,000 in Nigeria, GHS 800 to GHS 2,000 in Ghana and ZAR 1,500 to ZAR 4,000 in South Africa. Do two visits per year per tank and you build a business that is not dependent on new construction. Safety is non-negotiable. Chlorine is a hazardous chemical. Never mix chlorine with acid or ammonia. Always wear gloves, goggles and a respirator when handling chlorine granules or gas. Store chlorine in a cool, dark, ventilated place away from fuel, fertiliser and children. Have a first aid plan and a spill kit. For confined space entry into tanks, never enter alone. Test for oxygen and chlorine gas. Use a harness and a standby person. These rules are not optional. They are the difference between a professional operation and a tragedy. Finally, know the standards. Kenya has KS EAS 12:2018 for drinking water. Nigeria has NIS 554:2017. Ghana has GS 175-1:2017. South Africa has SANS 241:2015. These standards set limits for E. coli, total coliforms, turbidity, free chlorine, arsenic, fluoride, nitrate and other parameters. You do not need to be a laboratory, but you should partner with one. A basic bacteriological test costs KES 2,500 to KES 5,000 in Kenya, NGN 15,000 to NGN 30,000 in Nigeria, GHS 300 to GHS 700 in Ghana and ZAR 500 to ZAR 1,200 in South Africa. A full chemical test costs two to four times more. Build these tests into your quotes and your service contracts. That is how you sell trust, not just tanks.

2. Lesson 2: Sedimentation, Filtration and Clarification Systems for African Tanks

Sedimentation and filtration are the workhorses of water treatment. They remove the dirt that chlorine cannot easily kill because particles shield bacteria. A turbid tank is a failed tank in the eyes of the client. This lesson shows you how to design, build and price these systems for ferrocement, concrete, plastic and brick tanks. Start with sedimentation. The principle is simple: slow the water down so particles settle. In a tank, you can create a stilling zone or a separate settling basin. For a 10,000 litre tank, a settling zone of 1,000 to 1,500 litres is enough if the water is not heavily loaded. The detention time should be at least 2 to 4 hours. For river water with high silt, you need 6 to 12 hours. You can build a settling basin in concrete or brick. A 3,000 litre brick settling basin with cement plaster costs KES 25,000 to KES 40,000 in Kenya, NGN 120,000 to NGN 200,000 in Nigeria, GHS 2,500 to GHS 4,500 in Ghana and ZAR 5,000 to ZAR 9,000 in South Africa. Add a sludge drain valve at the bottom. Add a floating outlet or a baffle so only the clearer top water flows to the next stage. For ferrocement tanks, you can cast a internal baffle wall to create a settling chamber. For plastic tanks, you can use a smaller pre-settling tank upstream. The key numbers are surface overflow rate and detention time. For plain sedimentation, use a surface overflow rate of 0.5 to 1.0 cubic metres per square metre per hour. For a flow of 1,000 litres per hour, you need 1 to 2 square metres of surface area. That is a basin 1 metre by 1 to 2 metres. Depth should be 1.5 to 2.5 metres. Next, coagulation and flocculation. This is for water that will not settle on its own, such as muddy river water or water with fine clay. You add a coagulant such as aluminium sulphate (alum), ferric chloride or polyaluminium chloride (PAC). Alum is the cheapest and most common. In Kenya, a 25 kg bag of alum costs KES 3,500 to KES 5,500. In Nigeria, it is NGN 18,000 to NGN 30,000. In Ghana, GHS 350 to GHS 600. In South Africa, ZAR 700 to ZAR 1,200. The dose for typical turbid water is 20 to 60 mg per litre. That means for 1,000 litres you need 20 to 60 grams. You must jar test to find the right dose. A simple jar test uses six jars, a stirring rod and a stopwatch. Add different doses, stir fast for 1 minute, stir slow for 15 minutes, then let settle for 30 minutes. Choose the dose that gives the clearest water with the least sludge. Build a flocculation chamber with gentle stirring. You can use a hand-cranked paddle or a small solar-powered mixer. A 200 litre plastic drum with a paddle costs KES 8,000 to KES 15,000 to set up. After flocculation, the water goes to sedimentation. Then filtration. Filtration is the most important barrier against particles and many pathogens. There are three main types you should know: slow sand filters, rapid sand filters and pressure filters. Slow sand filters are excellent for small communities. They use fine sand, a gravel bed and a biological layer called the schmutzdecke. Flow rate is low, about 0.1 to 0.3 cubic metres per square metre per hour. A slow sand filter for 2,000 litres per day needs about 1 to 2 square metres of surface area. Build it in concrete or brick. A 2 square metre slow sand filter in concrete costs KES 60,000 to KES 120,000 in Kenya, NGN 300,000 to NGN 600,000 in Nigeria, GHS 6,000 to GHS 12,000 in Ghana and ZAR 12,000 to ZAR 25,000 in South Africa. The sand must be clean, washed river sand with an effective size of 0.15 to 0.35 mm. The gravel layers go from 5 mm at the bottom to 40 mm at the top. The filter needs cleaning every 1 to 3 months by scraping the top 1 to 2 cm of sand. Rapid sand filters use coarser sand and higher flow rates, 4 to 10 cubic metres per square metre per hour. They need backwashing, which requires pumps and valves. They are better for larger systems, above 20,000 litres per day. A rapid sand filter for a school or small estate costs KES 250,000 to KES 600,000 in Kenya. Pressure filters are compact and work under pump pressure. A 10 inch or 20 inch cartridge filter is the simplest pressure filter. A 20 inch big blue filter housing costs KES 8,000 to KES 15,000 in Kenya, NGN 40,000 to NGN 80,000 in Nigeria, GHS 800 to GHS 1,500 in Ghana and ZAR 1,500 to ZAR 3,000 in South Africa. Cartridges cost KES 1,500 to KES 4,000 each and last 1 to 6 months depending on sediment load. For iron and manganese removal, use aeration followed by filtration. Iron gives water a metallic taste and stains laundry and fixtures. Manganese gives a black stain. Aeration can be as simple as a cascade, a spray nozzle or a small air compressor. Then filter through manganese greensand or a catalytic carbon filter. A 10 inch catalytic carbon cartridge costs KES 3,000 to KES 6,000 in Kenya. For fluoride removal, use activated alumina or bone char. This is a specialist area. A household bone char filter costs KES 3,000 to KES 8,000. For salinity, you need reverse osmosis or distillation. A small RO unit for 200 litres per day costs KES 25,000 to KES 60,000 in Kenya, NGN 150,000 to NGN 350,000 in Nigeria, GHS 3,000 to GHS 7,000 in Ghana and ZAR 5,000 to ZAR 12,000 in South Africa. When you design filtration, always include a bypass and a sampling point. The bypass lets you service the filter without stopping supply. The sampling point lets you test before and after. Label every valve and pipe. Train the client on backwashing, cartridge replacement and filter cleaning. Write a simple logbook. A filtration system without a logbook is a system that will fail quietly. Your business can sell the filters, the housings, the installation and the maintenance. A typical installation markup is 30 to 50 percent on materials. A maintenance contract for filters is KES 5,000 to KES 15,000 per visit in Kenya, NGN 30,000 to NGN 80,000 in Nigeria, GHS 500 to GHS 1,500 in Ghana and ZAR 1,000 to ZAR 3,000 in South Africa. That is recurring revenue. That is the business.

3. Lesson 3: Chlorination Methods, Dosing and Safety for Tank Systems

Chlorination is the most common and most cost-effective disinfection method for tanks in Africa. It kills bacteria and viruses, and it leaves a residual that protects water during storage and distribution. But chlorine is also a chemical that can harm people if misused. This lesson gives you the practical numbers, methods and safety rules. First, understand chlorine demand and residual. When you add chlorine to water, some of it is consumed by organic matter, iron, manganese, ammonia and other impurities. That is the chlorine demand. The amount left over is the free residual chlorine. The target for drinking water is a free residual of 0.2 to 0.5 mg per litre at the point of use, and 0.5 to 1.0 mg per litre in the tank. In an emergency, you can go up to 2.0 mg per litre, but not for long-term drinking. The World Health Organization guideline is 5 mg per litre maximum, but taste and odour become a problem above 1 mg per litre for many people. The three main chlorine products are: sodium hypochlorite solution (liquid bleach), calcium hypochlorite granules or tablets, and chlorine gas. For tank systems in Africa, liquid bleach and calcium hypochlorite are the most practical. Chlorine gas is for large municipal plants and requires special training and equipment. Sodium hypochlorite is sold as household bleach, usually 3.5 to 5 percent chlorine, or as industrial bleach at 10 to 15 percent. In Kenya, a 20 litre jerrycan of 10 percent sodium hypochlorite costs KES 2,500 to KES 4,500. In Nigeria, it is NGN 15,000 to NGN 30,000. In Ghana, GHS 250 to GHS 500. In South Africa, ZAR 500 to ZAR 1,000. Calcium hypochlorite is sold as granules or tablets, usually 60 to 70 percent chlorine. A 1 kg container of 65 percent calcium hypochlorite costs KES 1,500 to KES 3,000 in Kenya, NGN 10,000 to NGN 20,000 in Nigeria, GHS 150 to GHS 300 in Ghana and ZAR 300 to ZAR 600 in South Africa. Tablets are convenient for small tanks and for point-of-use treatment. A 1 gram tablet of 60 percent calcium hypochlorite can treat 20 to 30 litres of clear water. Now the dosing formula. The amount of chlorine product needed equals: volume of water in litres multiplied by desired dose in mg per litre, divided by the concentration of the product as a decimal, multiplied by 1000 if you want grams. For example, to dose 10,000 litres at 2 mg per litre using 65 percent calcium hypochlorite: 10,000 x 2 = 20,000 mg of chlorine needed. 20,000 divided by 0.65 = 30,769 mg of product = 30.8 grams. So you need about 31 grams of 65 percent calcium hypochlorite. For liquid bleach at 10 percent: 20,000 divided by 0.10 = 200,000 mg = 200 grams or 200 ml of bleach. That is about one cup. Always add chlorine to water, never water to chlorine. Use a clean plastic bucket. Stir well. Let it stand for 30 minutes before use. For tank shock chlorination, use a higher dose. For a clean tank, use 50 mg per litre. For a dirty or new tank, use 100 to 200 mg per litre. For a 10,000 litre tank at 100 mg per litre, you need 1,000,000 mg of chlorine = 1 kg of pure chlorine. With 65 percent calcium hypochlorite, that is 1,000 divided by 0.65 = 1,538 grams, about 1.5 kg. With 10 percent bleach, that is 10,000 grams or 10 litres of bleach. Shock chlorination is for cleaning and disinfection, not for drinking. You must flush the tank after shock chlorination until the free residual is below 2 mg per litre. Now the methods. The simplest method is manual dosing. You measure the chlorine, mix it in a bucket, and pour it into the tank. This works for small tanks and for clients with limited budgets. It requires a trained person and a logbook. The next method is a floating chlorinator or a chlorine tablet feeder. This is a plastic device that holds tablets and releases chlorine slowly. It costs KES 5,000 to KES 15,000 in Kenya, NGN 25,000 to NGN 60,000 in Nigeria, GHS 500 to GHS 1,200 in Ghana and ZAR 1,000 to ZAR 2,500 in South Africa. It is good for tanks that are filled regularly. The third method is a dosing pump. This is a small electric pump that injects chlorine solution into the water line at a set rate. It costs KES 60,000 to KES 150,000 in Kenya, NGN 300,000 to NGN 800,000 in Nigeria, GHS 6,000 to GHS 15,000 in Ghana and ZAR 12,000 to ZAR 30,000 in South Africa. It is the best method for larger systems, hotels, clinics and estates. It needs a power supply and regular calibration. The fourth method is inline chlorination using a erosion feeder or a venturi injector. This is common in South Africa and is gaining ground in Kenya and Nigeria. It costs KES 25,000 to KES 60,000 for a venturi system. Safety is critical. Chlorine gas and dust can burn your eyes, throat and lungs. Always wear chemical splash goggles, a face mask or respirator, gloves and an apron. Work in a well-ventilated area. Never mix chlorine with acid, ammonia, or other cleaning chemicals. That can create toxic chlorine gas. Store chlorine in a cool, dark, dry place. Keep it away from children, fuel, fertiliser and food. Have a spill kit with sand, a bucket and plenty of water. If chlorine gets on your skin, rinse with plenty of water for 15 minutes. If you inhale chlorine, move to fresh air immediately and seek medical help. If you swallow chlorine, do not induce vomiting. Drink water and seek medical help. For tank entry, never enter a tank that has been chlorinated without testing the air. Chlorine gas is heavier than air and can accumulate at the bottom. Use a gas detector. Use a harness and a standby person. Ventilate the tank for at least 30 minutes before entry. Test for oxygen and chlorine. The safe oxygen range is 19.5 to 23.5 percent. The safe chlorine level is below 0.5 mg per litre. Your business should sell a chlorination service. A basic tank cleaning and chlorination for a 10,000 litre tank costs KES 8,000 to KES 20,000 in Kenya, NGN 40,000 to NGN 100,000 in Nigeria, GHS 800 to GHS 2,000 in Ghana and ZAR 1,500 to ZAR 4,000 in South Africa. Include a free chlorine test before and after. Include a written report with photos. That report is your marketing. It proves you are a professional. It also justifies the next service visit. Do two visits per year. That is KES 16,000 to KES 40,000 per tank per year in Kenya. With 100 tanks, that is KES 1.6 million to KES 4 million per year. That is a real business.

4. Lesson 4: Tank Cleaning, Disinfection and Maintenance Programs by Tank Type

A tank is not a one-time sale. It is a long-term asset that needs cleaning and disinfection. This lesson covers how to clean and maintain ferrocement, concrete, plastic and brick tanks, with costs and schedules for Africa. Start with the cleaning frequency. For a tank storing treated municipal or borehole water, clean and disinfect every 6 to 12 months. For rainwater tanks, clean every 3 to 6 months, especially after the first rains. For river or dam water, clean every 2 to 3 months. For tanks serving clinics, schools and food businesses, clean every 3 to 6 months and keep a logbook. The cleaning process has seven steps. Step one: drain the tank. Use the water for irrigation or cleaning, not for drinking. Step two: remove sludge and sediment. Use a plastic shovel and buckets. Never use metal tools that can scratch the tank lining. Step three: scrub the walls and floor. Use a stiff brush and a mild detergent. Do not use strong acids or caustic soda on concrete or ferrocement. Step four: rinse thoroughly. Step five: inspect for cracks, leaks, algae, rust and damaged inlets or outlets. Step six: disinfect with a chlorine solution. Step seven: refill and test the free chlorine residual. For ferrocement tanks, the surface is cement-rich and can be slightly alkaline. New ferrocement tanks should be filled and drained two or three times before use, or dosed with a small amount of vinegar or citric acid to neutralise the surface. For disinfection, use a chlorine solution of 50 to 100 mg per litre. For a 10,000 litre ferrocement tank, that is about 1.5 kg of 65 percent calcium hypochlorite or 10 litres of 10 percent bleach. Scrub the walls with the solution. Let it sit for 30 minutes. Then drain and rinse. Refill and check the residual. For concrete tanks, the same process applies. Concrete tanks are heavy and durable but can crack. Inspect the joints and the floor. Repair cracks with a non-toxic waterproof mortar or an epoxy approved for drinking water. A 5 kg pack of drinking-water-safe epoxy costs KES 4,000 to KES 8,000 in Kenya, NGN 20,000 to NGN 40,000 in Nigeria, GHS 400 to GHS 800 in Ghana and ZAR 800 to ZAR 1,500 in South Africa. For plastic tanks, cleaning is easier. They are smooth and lightweight. But they can be damaged by sunlight and by rough handling. Use a soft brush and a mild detergent. Do not use abrasive pads. Do not use chlorine concentrations above 100 mg per litre for long periods, as it can degrade some plastics. Check the inlet, outlet, overflow and vent. Check the tank base for cracks and the tank for UV degradation. A UV-stabilised tank lasts 10 to 20 years. A non-UV tank can fail in 3 to 5 years. In Kenya, a 10,000 litre UV-stabilised tank costs KES 85,000 to KES 140,000. In Nigeria, a 1,000 gallon tank costs NGN 250,000 to NGN 450,000. In Ghana, a 5,000 litre tank costs GHS 3,500 to GHS 6,000. In South Africa, a 5,000 litre JoJo tank costs ZAR 6,500 to ZAR 11,000. For brick tanks, the mortar joints and plaster are the weak points. Inspect for efflorescence, which is a white salt deposit that indicates water movement. Repair cracks and re-plaster with a waterproof cement mix. Disinfect the same way as concrete. Brick tanks are common in Nigeria, Ghana and parts of Kenya. They are cheap to build but need more maintenance. A 10,000 litre brick tank costs NGN 150,000 to NGN 300,000 in Nigeria and GHS 3,000 to GHS 6,000 in Ghana. Now the maintenance program. A good program has four parts: daily checks, monthly checks, quarterly checks and annual checks. Daily checks: check the tank level, check for leaks, check the pump and the inlet. Monthly checks: check the free chlorine residual, check the filter pressure, check the first flush diverter, check the gutters. Quarterly checks: clean the pre-filter, test the water for bacteria, inspect the tank interior if possible. Annual checks: full cleaning and disinfection, full chemical test, inspect and repair the tank structure, service the pump and the chlorinator. Write this program into a contract. Price it as an annual service. In Kenya, an annual service contract for a 10,000 litre tank is KES 20,000 to KES 45,000. In Nigeria, it is NGN 100,000 to NGN 250,000. In Ghana, it is GHS 2,000 to GHS 5,000. In South Africa, it is ZAR 4,000 to ZAR 10,000. That includes two cleaning visits, four chlorine tests, one bacteriological test and a written report. Your cost for a bacteriological test is KES 2,500 to KES 5,000 in Kenya, NGN 15,000 to NGN 30,000 in Nigeria, GHS 300 to GHS 700 in Ghana and ZAR 500 to ZAR 1,200 in South Africa. Your cost for chlorine test strips is low. A pack of 100 DPD1 test strips costs KES 1,500 to KES 3,000 in Kenya. A digital chlorine meter costs KES 15,000 to KES 35,000. That meter pays for itself in one or two contracts. Safety during cleaning is critical. Never enter a tank alone. Use a harness and a tripod. Test the air for oxygen and chlorine. Ventilate for at least 30 minutes. Use a blower if available. Wear a respirator, gloves, goggles and boots. Have a standby person outside. Have a rope and a first aid kit. If you feel dizzy, get out immediately. Do not use petrol or diesel generators inside or near the tank. Do not smoke. Do not use open flames. For clients, give a simple one-page maintenance guide. Use pictures. Use local language where possible. Show them how to check the chlorine residual, how to clean the first flush diverter and how to call you. A client who understands the system is a client who renews the contract. A client who does not understand will blame you when the water tastes bad. That is the difference between a one-time sale and a long-term business.

5. Lesson 5: Business Models, Pricing and Compliance for Water Treatment Services

This final lesson turns water treatment into a profitable, compliant business. You will learn how to price, package, market and protect your services in Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana and South Africa. Start with the three business models. Model one: treatment as part of tank construction. You build the tank and include a basic treatment package: a first flush diverter, a pre-filter and a manual chlorination kit. This adds KES 15,000 to KES 40,000 to the project in Kenya, NGN 80,000 to NGN 200,000 in Nigeria, GHS 1,500 to GHS 4,000 in Ghana and ZAR 3,000 to ZAR 8,000 in South Africa. Your margin is 25 to 40 percent. Model two: treatment retrofit. You add treatment to an existing tank. This is a growing market because many tanks were built without treatment. A retrofit package with a sediment filter, a carbon filter and a chlorinator costs KES 35,000 to KES 90,000 in Kenya, NGN 180,000 to NGN 450,000 in Nigeria, GHS 3,500 to GHS 9,000 in Ghana and ZAR 7,000 to ZAR 18,000 in South Africa. Your margin is 30 to 50 percent. Model three: service contracts. You clean, test and maintain tanks on a recurring basis. This is the most valuable model because it creates predictable income. A contract for a 10,000 litre tank is KES 20,000 to KES 45,000 per year in Kenya, NGN 100,000 to NGN 250,000 in Nigeria, GHS 2,000 to GHS 5,000 in Ghana and ZAR 4,000 to ZAR 10,000 in South Africa. Your cost is labour, transport, chemicals and tests. Your margin is 40 to 60 percent if you route your visits efficiently. Now pricing. Never price on guesswork. Use a cost-plus model. List your direct costs: chemicals, filters, cartridges, test kits, transport, labour, protective equipment and disposal. Add your overhead: office, phone, insurance, tools, vehicle, marketing. Add your profit margin. For construction and retrofit, a 30 to 50 percent margin is normal. For service contracts, a 40 to 60 percent margin is normal. For emergency call-outs, add 50 to 100 percent. For remote areas, add transport and accommodation. For clinics, schools and NGOs, you can charge more because the compliance requirements are higher. For example, a clinic in Kenya may need monthly bacteriological testing. That is KES 2,500 to KES 5,000 per test. Add it to the contract. A school may need quarterly testing. A hotel may need weekly chlorine checks. Package these into three tiers: Basic, Standard and Premium. Basic: one cleaning per year, two chlorine tests, one bacteriological test. Standard: two cleanings per year, four chlorine tests, two bacteriological tests, filter replacement. Premium: four cleanings per year, monthly chlorine tests, quarterly bacteriological tests, filter replacement, emergency response within 24 hours. Price Basic at KES 20,000, Standard at KES 35,000 and Premium at KES 60,000 in Kenya. In Nigeria, Basic NGN 100,000, Standard NGN 180,000, Premium NGN 300,000. In Ghana, Basic GHS 2,000, Standard GHS 3,500, Premium GHS 6,000. In South Africa, Basic ZAR 4,000, Standard ZAR 7,000, Premium ZAR 12,000. These are guides. Adjust for tank size, water source and location. Compliance is your competitive advantage. In Kenya, the Water Services Regulatory Board and the Ministry of Health oversee drinking water quality. The standard is KS EAS 12:2018. In Nigeria, the Nigerian Industrial Standard NIS 554:2017 applies, and the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control regulates chemicals and packaged water. In Ghana, the Ghana Standards Authority GS 175-1:2017 applies, and the Water Resources Commission regulates water use. In South Africa, SANS 241:2015 applies, and the Department of Water and Sanitation oversees compliance. You do not need to be a laboratory, but you must partner with an accredited one. Use a laboratory that is recognised by the national accreditation body. In Kenya, that is KENAS. In Nigeria, that is NiNAS. In Ghana, that is GAC. In South Africa, that is SANAS. Keep copies of your test reports. They protect you and they prove your value to the client. Marketing is simple. Do three things. First, document every job with before-and-after photos, chlorine test results and a short report. Second, ask for a testimonial and a referral. Third, target the sectors that need compliance: clinics, schools, hotels, restaurants, food processing, estates, churches and NGOs. These clients have budgets and they fear bad water. A single outbreak of cholera or typhoid can shut down a school or a hotel. Your service is insurance. Price it that way. Safety and insurance. Get public liability insurance. In Kenya, a basic policy for a small water services business costs KES 30,000 to KES 80,000 per year. In Nigeria, NGN 150,000 to NGN 400,000. In Ghana, GHS 1,500 to GHS 4,000. In South Africa, ZAR 3,000 to ZAR 8,000. Train your staff on chlorine safety, confined space entry and first aid. Keep records of training. If an accident happens, your records show you did the right thing. Finally, think about scale. Start with one vehicle, one team and one service area. Add a second team when you have 50 to 80 contract tanks. Add a third when you have 120 to 150. Use a simple customer management system. A notebook works at first. Then move to a spreadsheet or a phone app. Track every tank, every visit, every test result and every payment. Send reminders one month before the service is due. Offer a 5 to 10 percent discount for annual prepayment. That improves your cash flow. In Kenya, a well-run water treatment service business with 200 contract tanks can generate KES 4 million to KES 9 million per year in recurring revenue. In Nigeria, 200 tanks can generate NGN 20 million to NGN 50 million. In Ghana, GHS 400,000 to GHS 1 million. In South Africa, ZAR 800,000 to ZAR 2.4 million. That is before construction and retrofit work. That is a real business. That is the opportunity. Water treatment is not a cost. It is a service. Sell it as a service. Price it as a service. Deliver it professionally. That is how you build a brand that lasts.
