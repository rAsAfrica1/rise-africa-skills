1. The Role of Sugar in Preservation

Sugar preserves food by pulling water out of microorganisms through osmosis. When you make jam, you add sugar to fruit, and the sugar binds with water, making it unavailable for bacteria, yeast, and mould. For safe preservation, your final product needs at least 60% sugar by weight. That means for every 1 kg of fruit pulp, you add 600 g to 800 g of sugar. If you use less, the jam may spoil quickly, even in the fridge. In Africa, we often use white or brown sugar, but you can also use honey or palm sugarjust adjust for their water content. Honey has about 20% water, so use slightly more.

Sugar also adds flavour, colour, and texture. It helps fruits keep their shape during cooking and prevents them from turning mushy. When sugar is heated with fruit acids, it forms a gel, which gives jam its thick consistency. Without enough sugar, the gel won't set properly. However, too much sugar can crystallise. To avoid this, add a little lemon juice or citric acidabout 1 tablespoon per kg of fruit. The acid also helps the gel set and brightens the colour.

In African markets, you'll find many fruits perfect for sugaring: mangoes, guavas, pineapples, papayas, and berries. These fruits have different natural pectin levels. Pectin is the substance that makes jam thick. Underripe fruits have more pectin than ripe ones. So, when making jam, mix ripe fruit for flavour with a few underripe pieces for pectin. If your fruit is low in pectin (like strawberries or cherries), you can add commercial pectin or use a natural source like grated apple or lemon peel.

Always use clean, dry utensils and jars. Sugar alone doesn't kill all microbes; it just stops them from growing. So, start with clean fruit and equipment. Boil your jars for 10 minutes to sterilise them. After filling, process jars in a hot water bath for 10 minutes to seal. This ensures your preserves last for months, even in warm climates. Remember, sugar is your friend, but it must be used correctly.

2. Making Jam from Local Fruits

Jam is made by cooking crushed fruit with sugar until it thickens. Start with 1 kg of ripe fruitmango, guava, pineapple, or a mix. Wash, peel, and remove seeds. Cut into small pieces. For every 1 kg of fruit, add 600800 g of sugar and 2 tablespoons of lemon juice. If you want a softer set, use 600 g; for a firmer jam, use 800 g. Put fruit and sugar in a heavy-bottomed pot. Let it sit for 12 hours so the sugar draws out the juice. This helps the fruit keep its shape.

Cook over medium heat, stirring often to prevent burning. Once it boils, reduce heat and simmer. Skim off foam with a spoon. The jam is ready when it reaches 105C on a candy thermometer, or when a drop placed on a cold plate wrinkles when you push it with your finger. This takes 2040 minutes, depending on the fruit's water content. Pineapple and papaya are watery, so they take longer. Mango and guava set faster. Stir constantly near the end to avoid sticking.

For a smoother jam, mash the fruit with a potato masher before cooking. For a chunkier texture, leave some pieces whole. You can also add spices like cinnamon, ginger, or vanilla for extra flavour. In West Africa, people often add a little chilli or black pepper to mango jam for a kick. Just add 1/4 teaspoon of ground spice per kg of fruit. Taste before jarringadjust sugar or lemon if needed.

Pour hot jam into sterilised jars, leaving 1 cm headspace. Wipe rims, seal with lids, and process in a boiling water bath for 10 minutes. Cool, label, and store in a dark, cool place. Jam lasts 612 months. Once opened, refrigerate and use within 3 weeks. If you see mould, discard the whole jar. Making jam is a great way to use up surplus fruit and earn income. Sell at local markets or to neighbours. A 250 ml jar can fetch 5001000 Naira or 2050 KES, depending on the fruit and packaging.

3. Making Jelly and Marmalade

Jelly is made from fruit juice, not pulp. It's clear and firm. Use fruits high in pectin: guava, green apples, citrus peels, or unripe pawpaw. To make jelly, wash and chop 1 kg of fruit. Add 1 litre of water and simmer for 30 minutes until soft. Strain through a muslin cloth or clean cotton shirt. Don't squeezelet it drip overnight. You should get about 500 ml of juice. For every 500 ml juice, add 400 g sugar and 1 tablespoon lemon juice. Boil until it reaches 105C or sheets off a spoon. Pour into sterilised jars and process for 10 minutes. Jelly should be clear and wobble when set.

Marmalade is a citrus preserve made from the whole fruitpeel, pith, and juice. Oranges, lemons, grapefruits, and mandarins work well. In Africa, we have bitter oranges and tangerines. To make marmalade, wash 1 kg of citrus. Cut in half, squeeze juice, and save. Slice the peel and pith thinly. Soak the sliced peel in 1 litre of water overnight to reduce bitterness. Next day, boil the peel in the soaking water for 30 minutes until soft. Add the juice and 800 g sugar. Simmer until setting point, about 3045 minutes. Skim foam. Pour into jars and process.

For a quicker marmalade, use a food processor to chop the fruit finely. But don't pureeyou want texture. You can also add a little ginger or whisky for flavour. Test set by placing a spoonful on a cold plate; it should wrinkle when pushed. If it's too runny, boil longer. If too thick, add a little water. Remember, marmalade sets firmer than jam because of the pectin in the peel.

Both jelly and marmalade need sterilised jars. Use jars with tight lids. Process in a boiling water bath for 10 minutes. Store in a cool, dark place. They last up to a year. Marmalade is popular on toast and with cheese. You can sell it in small jars at breakfast markets or to hotels. A 250 ml jar of marmalade can sell for 8001500 Naira or 3060 KES. Jelly is often used as a glaze for meats or as a sweet spread. Package attractively with fabric covers and labels.

4. Preserving Fruits in Syrup

Preserving fruits in syrup means keeping whole or sliced fruit in a sugar solution. This method works for firm fruits like pineapple, mango, papaya, guava, and peaches. Start with 1 kg of ripe but firm fruit. Wash, peel, and cut into even pieces. For the syrup, mix 500 g sugar with 1 litre of water. Bring to a boil, then simmer for 5 minutes. You can add lemon juice (1 tablespoon per litre) or spices like cinnamon sticks or cloves. The syrup should be clear.

Pack fruit tightly into sterilised jars. Pour hot syrup over the fruit, leaving 1 cm headspace. Use a knife or chopstick to remove air bubbles. Wipe rims, seal, and process in a boiling water bath for 15 minutes for pints or 20 minutes for quarts. This heat kills microbes and seals the jars. After processing, let jars cool undisturbed for 12 hours. Check sealslids should be concave and not pop when pressed. If a jar didn't seal, refrigerate and use within a week.

For a lighter syrup, use 300 g sugar per litre of water. For a heavier syrup, use 700 g. Heavier syrup preserves better but is sweeter. You can also use honey or palm sugar. If using honey, reduce water by 20% because honey has water. Always use clean, ripe fruit. Overripe fruit will turn mushy. Underripe fruit may be tough. Mix ripe and slightly underripe for best texture.

You can also preserve fruits in their own juice. Just pack fruit with a little sugar and let it sit for 2 hours. The sugar draws out juice, then process as above. This is called 'preserving in own juice' and is lower in sugar. Store jars in a cool, dark place. They last 1218 months. Once opened, refrigerate and eat within 2 weeks. Serve syrup fruits as dessert, over yoghurt, or in fruit salads. Sell them in decorative jars at gift shops or during holidays. A 500 ml jar can sell for 15002500 Naira or 50100 KES. This is a high-value product because it looks premium.

5. Testing for Set and Storage

Testing for set is criticalif your jam, jelly, or marmalade doesn't set, it will spoil or be runny. The most reliable test is the temperature test: use a candy thermometer and cook until the mixture reaches 105C (220F) at sea level. At higher altitudes, subtract 1C for every 300 metres. If you don't have a thermometer, use the plate test. Put a small plate in the freezer for 10 minutes. Drop a teaspoon of hot jam on the cold plate. Let it cool for 30 seconds, then push it with your finger. If it wrinkles and holds its shape, it's set. If it runs, keep boiling and test again every 5 minutes.

Another test is the spoon test: dip a cold metal spoon into the jam. Hold it up and let it drip. If the drops fall in a sheet or a single blob, it's set. If they drip like water, it's not ready. For jelly, use the sheet test: the juice should fall from the spoon in a sheet, not drops. Always remove the pot from heat while testing to avoid burning. Once set, skim foam and pour into jars immediately. Overcooking makes the jam hard and dark.

Storage is just as important. Use glass jars with new lids. Sterilise jars by boiling for 10 minutes. Fill while hot, leaving 1 cm headspace. Wipe rims with a clean cloth. Seal and process in a boiling water bath for 10 minutes (add 5 minutes for every 1000 metres above sea level). Cool undisturbed for 1224 hours. Check seals: press the centre of the lidif it doesn't pop, it's sealed. Label with date and fruit type. Store in a cool, dark, dry placeideally below 25C. A cupboard away from the stove is good. Avoid damp places.

Properly sealed and stored preserves last 1218 months. Once opened, refrigerate and use within 34 weeks. If you see mould, bubbles, or a bad smell, discard the whole jar. Never eat from a jar with a broken seal. For selling, store jars in crates in a cool room. Rotate stockuse older jars first. In hot African climates, consider storing in a refrigerator if you have one, or a cool cellar. You can also process jars in a water bath for longer (20 minutes) to ensure safety. Always keep records of batch dates and fruit sources.

6. Packaging and Selling Sweet Preserves

Packaging turns your preserves into a sellable product. Start with clean, attractive jars. Glass jars with metal lids are bestthey're reusable and show the product. You can buy new jars or collect and sterilise used ones. Remove old labels and glue. For a professional look, use jars of the same size and shape. Common sizes: 250 ml for individual use, 500 ml for families, and 1 litre for bulk. Fill jars to 1 cm from the top. Wipe any spills. Seal tightly.

Labels must include: product name, ingredients, net weight, production date, and your contact. Add a 'best before' dateusually 12 months from making. Use waterproof labels or write with a permanent marker on the lid. For a rustic look, cut circles from brown paper and tie with string. You can also add a fabric cover over the lid. In Africa, colourful African print fabrics make beautiful jar covers. Sell at local markets, schools, churches, or online via WhatsApp or Facebook. Price competitively: check what others charge. A 250 ml jar of mango jam might sell for 5001000 Naira or 2050 KES. Marmalade and syrup fruits can fetch more.

For market stalls, display jars neatly on a table with a clean cloth. Offer free samples on crackers or bread. Tell customers how to use the producton toast, in yoghurt, as a glaze. Highlight that it's homemade, natural, and has no preservatives. Offer bulk discounts for 5 jars or more. Keep a record of sales and customer feedback. Adjust sweetness or fruit combinations based on what sells. You can also approach local hotels, guesthouses, and cafes to supply them regularly. They often buy in bulk and pay well.

For online selling, take clear photos in natural light. Show the jar, the label, and the product texture. Post on social media with your price and delivery options. Use clean packaging for deliverywrap jars in bubble wrap or newspaper. Consider offering gift baskets with 34 small jars. During holidays, sales increase. Plan ahead and make extra. Always follow food safety: wash hands, sterilise jars, and don't sell any jar with a broken seal. With good packaging and marketing, your sweet preserves can become a steady income.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Sugaring, Jams, Jellies & Preserves

Sugaring is one of the oldest and most reliable food preservation methods in Africa. From Moroccan majoun and Egyptian fruit preserves to West African guava jam, East African mango chutney, and Southern African marmalade made from naartjies, sugar does more than sweeten it preserves. For the African agribusiness entrepreneur, sweet preserves turn cheap, seasonal, and often wasted fruit into shelf-stable, high-margin products with long shelf life, low capital requirements, and strong local and export demand.

This Practical Reference module covers the science, production, testing, packaging, and commercialisation of jams, jellies, marmalades, and fruit preserved in syrup. It is written for small and medium enterprises (SMEs), cooperatives, women's groups, and youth agribusinesses operating in African markets.

Why Sugar Preserves Food

Sugar preserves by reducing water activity (aw). Microorganisms bacteria, yeasts, and moulds need free water to grow. When sugar is dissolved into fruit pulp or juice at high concentration (typically 6065% soluble solids), the available water becomes chemically bound to sugar molecules, starving microbes of the moisture they need. This is why a properly made jam with 65 Brix will not spoil at room temperature, even without refrigeration.

Sugar also works alongside acid (from fruit or added lemon juice) and heat (boiling and hot-filling) to create a multi-hurdle preservation system. The acid lowers pH below 4.6, which inhibits Clostridium botulinum and most pathogens, while boiling sterilises the product and drives off oxygen.

Key Product Categories and Legal Definitions

Understanding the difference between jam, jelly, marmalade, and preserves matters for labelling, pricing, and compliance with national standards and export requirements.

Fruit Selection for African Markets

Choose fruit that is ripe but not overripe, free from mould, and affordable in bulk during peak season. High-pectin fruits set easily; low-pectin fruits need added pectin or blending with a high-pectin fruit.

  • High pectin: guava, sour apple, citrus peel, tamarind, unripe pawpaw, baobab fruit pulp
  • Medium pectin: mango (slightly underripe), pineapple, plum, apricot
  • Low pectin: strawberry, ripe mango, papaya, melon, banana add pectin or blend with guava/apple

In Africa, the best business opportunities often lie in underutilised indigenous fruits: baobab, marula, mobola plum, African mango (Irvingia gabonensis), wild loquat (Uapaca kirkiana), and tamarind. These command premium prices in urban and export markets because they are unique, nutrient-dense, and culturally valued.

Making Jam: Step-by-Step

  1. Prepare fruit: Wash, peel, deseed, and cut. For guava and mango, remove skins and stones. Weigh pulp.
  2. Cook fruit: Simmer pulp with a little water until soft (1020 minutes).
  3. Add sugar: Add 4555% sugar by weight of pulp. Stir until dissolved.
  4. Add acid and pectin: Add lemon/lime juice and pectin (pre-dissolved in warm water).
  5. Boil rapidly: Cook to 104105 C or until 65 Brix, stirring to prevent burning.
  6. Test for set: Use the plate/freeze test or a refractometer.
  7. Skim and fill: Remove foam, fill hot into sterilised jars, leaving 6 mm headspace.
  8. Seal and process: Cap immediately; optionally water-bath process for 1015 minutes.

Making Jelly and Marmalade

Jelly requires clear juice. Cook fruit with water, strain through a muslin cloth (do not squeeze, or the jelly will be cloudy), then cook juice with sugar and acid to set. Marmalade adds thinly sliced citrus peel, simmered until tender, then cooked with juice and sugar. For naartjie or orange marmalade, use 1 part peel to 3 parts juice and 4 parts sugar.

Preserving Fruit in Syrup

Fruit in syrup is a high-value product for supermarkets and export. Use firm, ripe fruit. Prepare syrup at 3040 Brix (light) or 5060 Brix (heavy). Pack fruit into jars, cover with hot syrup, and process in a water bath (85100 C) for 1525 minutes depending on jar size. This creates a commercially sterile product with 1224 months shelf life.

⚠ SAFETY WARNING BOTULISM AND MOULD RISK

Low-acid fruit preserves (pH > 4.6) that are not properly acidified or heat-processed can support Clostridium botulinum, which causes botulism a life-threatening illness. Always acidify with lemon/lime juice or citric acid to pH < 4.6, and water-bath process all sealed jars. Never sell or taste preserves with mould growth, off-odours, or bulging lids discard immediately. Never reduce sugar below 60% in jam recipes, as this raises water activity and permits spoilage. If selling commercially, test pH and Brix for every batch and keep records. Follow your national food safety authority (e.g., KEBS, NAFDAC, SABS, Ghana FDA) and Codex Alimentarius requirements.

Testing for Set and Storage

Set tests:

  • Refractometer: Most accurate. Jam should read 65 Brix at 20 C.
  • Plate/freeze test: Place a drop on a chilled plate; if it wrinkles when pushed, it is set.
  • Sheet test: Lift a spoon; if the jam falls in a sheet rather than drops, it is ready.
  • Thermometer: 104105 C at sea level; adjust for altitude (subtract 1 C per 300 m).

Storage: Store in a cool, dark, dry place (1525 C). Unopened jars last 1224 months. Once opened, refrigerate and consume within 46 weeks. Avoid direct sunlight, which fades colour and degrades flavour.

Packaging and Selling Sweet Preserves

Packaging is your silent salesperson. For African markets:

  • Glass jars: 200500 ml for retail; recyclable, premium perception. Ensure food-grade lids with plastisol lining.
  • Labelling: Include product name, ingredients (descending order), net weight, batch number, production and expiry dates, storage instructions, and your contact/business registration number. Bilingual labels (English/French/Portuguese/local language) increase market reach.
  • Branding: Tell the story "Made in Ghana from sun-ripened mangoes" or "Women-led cooperative, Kenya." African origin and indigenous fruit are strong selling points.
  • Pricing: Calculate cost of goods (fruit, sugar, jars, labels, labour, energy), add 3050% margin for wholesale, 100200% for direct retail. A 250 ml jar of premium baobab jelly can retail for USD 38 in urban or export markets.
  • Channels: Local markets, supermarkets, hotels, restaurants, gift shops, online (Jumia, Kilimall, Takealot), and export via AfCFTA opportunities.
  • Compliance: Register with your national standards body, obtain a food handling certificate, and comply with labelling and additive regulations.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. ☐ Use ripe, undamaged fruit; weigh accurately.
  2. ☐ Maintain 6065% sugar and pH < 4.6 (add lemon/lime or citric acid).
  3. ☐ Add pectin for low-pectin fruits; pre-dissolve to avoid lumps.
  4. ☐ Cook to 104105 C or 65 Brix; stir constantly to avoid burning.
  5. ☐ Test set with refractometer, plate, or sheet test before filling.
  6. ☐ Sterilise jars and lids; hot-fill, leave 6 mm headspace, seal immediately.
  7. ☐ Water-bath process sealed jars (1025 min) for safety and shelf life.
  8. ☐ Label with batch, dates, ingredients, and storage instructions; store cool and dark.
