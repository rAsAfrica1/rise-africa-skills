Lessons

1. Principles of Multiple-Effect Evaporation

In sugar processing, evaporation removes water from clarified juice to concentrate it to about 60-65 Brix. Multiple-effect evaporation uses a series of vessels (effects) where steam heats juice, and the vapour produced in one effect heats the next effect. This saves energy because each kilogram of steam evaporates multiple kilograms of water. The first effect uses live steam from the boiler, typically at 120-130C. The vapour from the first effect (at about 100C) goes to the second effect, and so on. Each subsequent effect operates at lower pressure and temperature. A common setup in African sugar factories is a quadruple or quintuple effect evaporator. For example, a factory crushing 100 tonnes of cane per hour produces about 100 tonnes of juice at 15 Brix. To concentrate to 65 Brix, you need to evaporate about 77 tonnes of water per hour. With a quintuple effect, steam economy can be 4.5 kg water evaporated per kg steam, so you need only about 17 tonnes of steam per hour. That saves huge amounts of bagasse, which can be used for cogeneration. The key principle: the number of effects determines steam economy. More effects mean better fuel efficiency but also higher capital cost and more complexity. In Africa, where bagasse is often used as fuel, a 4 or 5 effect evaporator is typical. The temperature difference between live steam and the last effect condenser drives the evaporation. The last effect usually operates under vacuum at about 55-60C to avoid caramelisation and to use low-pressure vapour. Understanding this cascade is essential for any operator.

2. Evaporator Design and Operation

Evaporators in sugar factories are usually of the Robert or falling-film type. Robert evaporators have vertical tubes with juice inside and steam outside. Falling-film evaporators have juice falling as a thin film inside tubes, which gives better heat transfer and shorter residence time. In Africa, many older factories still use Robert evaporators, but new installations often choose falling-film for energy efficiency. Key design parameters include heating surface area, tube length and diameter, and vapour space. For a 100 t/h factory, total heating surface might be 3000-5000 m across all effects. The first effect may have 800 m, and the last effect 1200 m because the vapour is less dense. Operation requires careful control of juice level, steam pressure, and vacuum. Juice level should cover the tubes but not flood the vapour space. If the level is too low, tubes dry out and scale bakes on. If too high, juice carries over into the vapour line, contaminating condensate. Steam pressure to the first effect is typically 1.5-2.5 bar gauge. Vacuum in the last effect is maintained by a condenser and vacuum pump, usually at 0.15-0.2 bar absolute. Operators must monitor temperatures, pressures, and Brix at each effect. A common problem is entrainment, where juice droplets get carried with vapour. This is prevented by installing demister pads or cyclones. Regular checking of condensate for sugar (using a refractometer or simple taste) helps detect entrainment early. In many African factories, evaporator operators manually adjust valves, but modern plants use PLC control. Training operators to read gauges and respond to changes is critical for consistent syrup quality.

3. Steam Economy and Energy Efficiency

Steam economy is the kilograms of water evaporated per kilogram of steam supplied to the first effect. In a well-run quintuple evaporator, economy can reach 4.5 to 5.0. That means 1 tonne of steam evaporates 4.5 tonnes of water. If your factory uses 17 tonnes of steam per hour to evaporate 77 tonnes of water, your economy is 4.5. To improve economy, you can add more effects, but there are limits. The temperature difference between live steam and last effect vapour must be enough to drive heat transfer. If you have too many effects, the temperature drop per effect becomes too small, and boiling point elevation (due to dissolved solids) reduces available driving force. In Africa, bagasse is often the fuel. Saving steam means more bagasse for cogeneration or sale. Simple measures can improve efficiency: (1) Insulate steam lines and evaporator bodies to reduce heat loss. (2) Remove air from steam spacesair is a poor conductor. Install air vents and ensure they work. (3) Clean tubes regularlyscale acts as insulation. (4) Maintain vacuum in the last effectpoor vacuum raises boiling temperature and reduces economy. (5) Use vapour bleeding: take vapour from middle effects to heat juice heaters or for pan boiling. This reduces steam demand. For example, bleeding 2 tonnes/h of vapour from the third effect to a juice heater saves 2 tonnes/h of live steam. Monitor steam flow, juice flow, and Brix to calculate economy daily. A drop in economy indicates a problem: scale, air leaks, or entrainment. In many African factories, steam economy is not measured, so operators miss opportunities. Train staff to record data and calculate economy weekly. Even a 5% improvement can save thousands of dollars in bagasse or fuel oil.

4. Scaling and Cleaning of Evaporators

Scale is the hard deposit that forms on evaporator tubes when dissolved solids precipitate. In sugar juice, scale is mainly calcium sulphate, calcium carbonate, silica, and organic matter. Scale reduces heat transfer, so you burn more steam to evaporate the same water. A 1 mm scale layer can increase steam consumption by 10-15%. In Africa, where juice quality varies, scaling can be severe. Symptoms include rising steam pressure, falling evaporation rate, and higher Brix in later effects. To control scale, first ensure proper clarificationremove as much calcium and impurities as possible. Add scale inhibitors if needed, but many African factories rely on regular cleaning. Cleaning methods: (1) Mechanical: for Robert evaporators, operators may use brushes or scrapers, but this is labour-intensive and can damage tubes. (2) Chemical: circulate dilute hydrochloric acid (2-5%) or sulphamic acid to dissolve carbonate and sulphate scale. Caustic soda (1-2%) removes organic scale. Always neutralise and rinse well after cleaning. (3) Boiling out: fill with water and add soda ash, then boil to soften scale. Frequency depends on juice quality. Some factories clean every 2 weeks, others every 3 months. A common practice is to clean when steam economy drops by 10% or when evaporation rate falls. In African conditions, high silica in cane (from soil) can cause hard scale. Use of a pre-evaporator or juice softening can help. After cleaning, inspect tubes for cracks and replace if needed. Safety: acid cleaning requires goggles, gloves, and ventilation. Never add water to acidadd acid to water. Train a cleaning team and keep records of cleaning dates and results. Regular cleaning is one of the cheapest ways to save energy.

5. Monitoring Brix and Concentration Control

Brix measures the concentration of dissolved solids in juice or syrup. In evaporation, you must control the final syrup Brix to about 60-65 Brix. Too low, and you waste energy in the pans; too high, and you risk crystallisation in the evaporator or sugar loss. The most common tool is a refractometer. In African factories, handheld refractometers are used to check Brix at each effect. For continuous monitoring, inline refractometers or density meters are installed. Operators should take samples from each effect every hour. The Brix should increase gradually: first effect maybe 20 Brix, second 30, third 40, fourth 50, fifth 60-65. If Brix is not rising as expected, there may be scale, low steam, or high juice flow. Control is done by adjusting juice feed rate or steam pressure. If final Brix is too low, reduce feed rate or increase steam. If too high, increase feed. In many African factories, the evaporator operator manually controls a valve on the juice feed. This requires constant attention. A simple rule: if the last effect Brix is below 60, you are evaporating too much water later in the pans, wasting energy. If above 65, you may get sugar colour problems and scaling. Also monitor syrup temperatureit should be about 60-65C. Use a thermometer. Record Brix, temperature, and steam pressure every hour in a logbook. This data helps troubleshoot. For example, if Brix drops suddenly, check for a leak in the juice line or a drop in steam pressure. Train operators to trust their refractometer and to calibrate it daily with distilled water. A clean prism is essential. In Africa, where ambient temperatures are high, refractometers should be temperature-compensated. Consistent Brix control leads to better sugar quality and lower energy costs.

6. Troubleshooting Evaporator Performance

When an evaporator is not performing, you see symptoms: low evaporation rate, high steam consumption, low Brix, or high Brix in early effects. Start by checking the basics: steam pressure, vacuum, juice level, and feed rate. Common problems and solutions: (1) Low steam pressure: check boiler and steam valves. If pressure is below 1.5 bar, evaporation drops. (2) Poor vacuum in last effect: check condenser water flow, vacuum pump, and air leaks. A vacuum of 0.2 bar absolute is typical. If vacuum is lost, boiling point rises, reducing temperature difference. (3) Scale: if steam pressure is normal but evaporation is low, scale is likely. Clean tubes. (4) Entrainment: if condensate tastes sweet or refractometer shows Brix in condensate, juice is carrying over. Check demister, reduce juice level, or lower steam pressure. (5) Air in steam space: air reduces heat transfer. Open air vents and ensure they are not blocked. (6) High juice level: floods tubes, reduces evaporation, and causes entrainment. Adjust level control. (7) Low juice level: tubes dry, scale bakes, and tubes can crack. Maintain level. (8) Blocked tubes: in Robert evaporators, tubes can block with scale or debris. Clean mechanically. (9) Condensate blockage: if condensate cannot drain, steam space floods. Check traps and valves. (10) Brix too high: reduce feed or increase juice flow. In African factories, power cuts are common, causing vacuum loss and juice sitting in tubes. After a power cut, restart carefully: restore vacuum, then steam, then feed. Keep a troubleshooting chart near the evaporator. Train operators to identify the most likely cause first: scale and air leaks are the most common. Regular maintenance and record-keeping prevent many problems. If performance drops suddenly, check for a recent change in juice quality or a mechanical failure. A systematic approach saves time and energy.

<h2>Practical Reference: Evaporation and Concentration in African Sugar Processing</h2>

<p>Evaporation is the thermal heart of every sugar factory. In a typical African cane sugar mill, the evaporator station receives clarified juice at 1218 Brix and must concentrate it to 6068 Brix before crystallisation while consuming as little steam as possible. For a 5,000 TCD (tonnes cane per day) factory in Kenya, Tanzania, Nigeria, or Zambia, the evaporator station can account for 2540% of total process steam demand. Getting evaporation right directly affects sugar recovery, bagasse surplus for cogeneration, and factory profitability.</p>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the operating knowledge you need on the factory floor: multiple-effect principles, design choices, steam economy, scaling control, Brix monitoring, and troubleshooting. It is written for shift supervisors, process chemists, and technical managers who must make decisions in real time.</p>

<h3>Why Multiple-Effect Evaporation Matters in Africa</h3>

<p>Single-effect evaporation uses roughly 1 kg of steam per kg of water evaporated. Multiple-effect evaporation reuses the vapour from one vessel to heat the next, achieving steam economy of 35 kg water per kg steam in a quadruple or quintuple effect set. For African factories where bagasse is either surplus (for cogeneration income) or scarce (forcing fuel oil purchase), every kilogram of steam saved has a direct financial value.</p>

<p>In a Kenyan coastal factory selling surplus bagasse to a nearby cogeneration plant, improving steam economy from 3.2 to 3.8 reduces bagasse consumption by roughly 15%, freeing tonnes of bagasse per day for sale. In a Nigerian factory burning fuel oil during cane off-season, the same improvement cuts fuel bills proportionally.</p>

<h3>Scaling and Cleaning: The Hidden Profit Killer</h3>

<p>Scale formation on evaporator tubes is the single largest cause of lost steam economy in African factories. Calcium sulphate, calcium carbonate, silica, and organic deposits insulate the heating surface, forcing higher steam pressure and reducing evaporation rate. A 1 mm scale layer can increase steam consumption by 1015%.</p>

<p>In factories using cane varieties with high silica content common in parts of Tanzania and Mozambique scale can build within 812 hours of operation, requiring daily boiling-out. In contrast, well-controlled factories in Kenya and Eswatini may run 57 days between chemical cleans.</p>

<h3>Monitoring Brix and Concentration Control</h3>

<p>Syrup Brix leaving the last effect should be 6068 Brix. Too low, and you waste steam in the pans; too high, and you risk crystallisation in the evaporator and syrup lines. In African factories, refractometers and density meters are the workhorses. Manual refractometer checks every 30 minutes remain common, but inline refractometers with automatic control are increasingly affordable.</p>

<p>Key control points:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Juice feed Brix:</strong> 1218 Brix after clarification. Lower Brix means more water to evaporate check clarifier performance.</li>
<li><strong>Syrup Brix:</strong> 6068 Brix. Adjust steam or feed rate to maintain target.</li>
<li><strong>Vacuum in last effect:</strong> 0.150.25 bar absolute. Poor vacuum raises boiling point and reduces economy.</li>
<li><strong>Condensate quality:</strong> Check for sugar carryover indicates entrainment and product loss.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Troubleshooting Evaporator Performance</h3>

<p>When steam economy drops or syrup Brix falls, work through this diagnostic sequence:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Check steam pressure and temperature.</strong> Low exhaust steam pressure reduces evaporation rate. Verify boiler and turbine extraction.</li>
<li><strong>Check vacuum.</strong> Condenser water flow, vacuum pump, and air leaks are common culprits. A 0.05 bar vacuum loss can cut economy by 5%.</li>
<li><strong>Check for scale.</strong> Compare steam pressure required for target Brix against baseline. Rising pressure = scaling.</li>
<li><strong>Check entrainment.</strong> Sugar in condensate means product loss and fouling. Inspect entrainment separators.</li>
<li><strong>Check juice feed distribution.</strong> Uneven feed causes local overheating and scaling.</li>
<li><strong>Check non-condensable gases.</strong> Poor venting causes vapour blanketing and reduces heat transfer.</li>
<li><strong>Check condensate drainage.</strong> Traps and lines must remove condensate freely. Waterlogging kills performance.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist Shift Supervisor</h3>

<ul style="line-height:1.8;">
<li> Record steam pressure, temperature, and flow at start of shift.</li>
<li> Check vacuum in last effect target 0.150.25 bar absolute.</li>
<li> Verify syrup Brix every 30 minutes with calibrated refractometer.</li>
<li> Monitor condensate for sugar carryover report any sweetness immediately.</li>
<li> Check juice feed Brix and flow confirm clarifier is delivering 1218 Brix.</li>
<li> Inspect sight glasses for foaming, entrainment, or scaling indicators.</li>
<li> Confirm boil-out schedule is followed do not skip cleaning.</li>
<li> Log all deviations and report to process chemist before shift handover.</li>
</ul>
