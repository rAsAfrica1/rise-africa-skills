1. Pre-Flight Checklist and Procedures

Before every flight, run a systematic pre-flight check. In Africa, dust, heat, and uneven terrain make this critical. Start with a physical inspection: check propellers for cracks or nicks, ensure motors spin freely, and confirm landing gear is secure. Inspect the battery: look for swelling, check voltage with a multimeter (e.g., 4.2V per cell for LiPo), and ensure it's charged to at least 50% for short flights. For a DJI Phantom 4, battery should read 15.2V or higher. Next, check the controller: charge level above 50%, antennas positioned correctly, and sticks move smoothly. Calibrate the compass if you've moved more than 100 km or changed locations significantly. In dusty areas, calibrate the IMU every 10 flights. Power on the drone and controller in the correct order: controller first, then drone. Wait for GPS lock: at least 10 satellites for stable hover. Check home point is set. Use a pre-flight checklist app like 'Drone Deploy' or a printed list. In rural areas, ensure no power lines or tall trees within 30 meters. Check weather: wind below 15 km/h, no rain, visibility above 5 km. For night flights, attach strobe lights. Finally, announce 'Takeoff' and clear the area. This 5-minute routine prevents 90% of crashes.

2. Basic Maneuvers: Hovering, Takeoff, Landing

Master these three skills before anything else. Takeoff: Stand 3-5 meters away, face the drone. Push both sticks down and inward (or use auto-takeoff). For manual takeoff, throttle up slowly until the drone lifts 1 meter, then hold. In windy conditions, take off into the wind. Hovering: The goal is to keep the drone in one spot. Use small, gentle stick movements. If the drone drifts left, tap right stick slightly right. Practice in an open field with no obstacles. In Africa, watch for sudden gustsâcompensate by lowering altitude to 2 meters where wind is weaker. Use 'Atti' mode (attitude) to learn manual control; GPS mode is easier but builds bad habits. Landing: For a safe landing, bring the drone down slowly. Reduce throttle to descend at 1 m/s. At 0.5 meters, reduce further. When it touches down, hold throttle down for 3 seconds to stop motors. In dusty areas, land on a mat or tarmac to avoid debris. Practice landing on a 1x1 meter square. Do 20 takeoffs and landings daily. For a Mavic Air 2, battery lasts about 20 minutesâso you can do many cycles. Always land with 20% battery remaining. If you can't land, hover and wait for a safe spot. In emergencies, use auto-land. These basics build muscle memory.

3. Advanced Maneuvers: Figure Eights, Banked Turns

Once you can hover confidently, learn figure eights and banked turns. Figure eight: Imagine two circles side by side. Start at the center, fly forward and right, then curve left to complete a circle, then cross the center and curve right to complete the other circle. Use coordinated turns: add a bit of rudder (left stick) with aileron (right stick). Keep altitude constant at 5 meters. In Africa, practice in a large open field like a soccer pitch. Banked turns: Instead of flat turns, tilt the drone into the turn. For a right bank, push right stick right and slightly down, and add left rudder. This makes turns smoother and faster. Practice at 30-degree bank angle. For a DJI Mavic 2, switch to 'Sport' mode for sharper turns, but be carefulâit's faster. Do 10 figure eights and 10 banked turns per session. Use cones or trees as markers. In windy conditions, bank into the wind to maintain course. These maneuvers are used in mapping, search and rescue, and cinematography. In African agriculture, drones use banked turns to cover fields efficiently. Always keep line of sight. If you lose orientation, switch to GPS mode and hover. Practice until you can do figure eights without thinking. This takes about 10 hours of flight time.

4. Flying in Different Weather Conditions

Africa's weather varies from dusty harmattan to coastal humidity. Learn to fly safely. Wind: Most consumer drones handle up to 30 km/h, but for beginners, stay below 15 km/h. Check wind with a handheld anemometer or weather app. In strong wind, fly into the wind on the way out and with it on return to save battery. Dust: In Sahel regions, dust reduces visibility and can clog motors. Use propeller guards and avoid flying in dust storms. After flight, clean with compressed air. Rain: Never fly in rainâmost drones are not waterproof. If caught, land immediately and dry with silica gel. Heat: In 40Â°C heat, batteries overheat. Fly in early morning or late afternoon. Keep batteries shaded. Cold: In highlands like Ethiopia, cold reduces battery life. Warm batteries to 20Â°C before flight. Humidity: Coastal areas cause condensation. Let drone acclimate before flying. Thunderstorms: Never flyâlightning can strike. Use a weather app like 'UAV Forecast' to check conditions. In all cases, maintain visual line of sight. If you can't see the drone, use the map. For night flying, attach strobes. In harmattan, use a filter to protect the lens. Always have a landing plan. If conditions worsen, land immediately. Practice in different conditions gradually. Start in calm mornings, then move to windy afternoons.

5. Emergency Procedures and Failsafes

Emergencies happen. Know how to react. First, set failsafes: In your drone app, set 'Return to Home' (RTH) altitude to 30 meters above the tallest obstacle. Set battery failsafe to trigger RTH at 20% and land at 10%. For a DJI drone, go to 'Main Controller Settings' > 'Failsafe'. Loss of signal: The drone will hover, then RTH if signal not regained. Do not panicâwait. If it doesn't return, use the map to locate it. Fly-away: If the drone drifts away, switch to 'Sport' mode and try to bring it back manually. If that fails, use 'Find My Drone' in the app. Motor failure: If a motor stops, the drone will spin. Cut throttle and let it dropâit's better than a fly-away. In Africa, emergency landing spots are often bushes. Practice emergency landings by simulating motor failure at 10 meters. Battery fire: If a battery swells or catches fire, move away and use a fire extinguisher (Class D) or sand. Never use water. Lost link: If controller dies, the drone will RTH. Always carry spare batteries for controller. Crash: If you crash, power off, check for damage. Do not fly again until repaired. Use a checklist for emergencies. In remote areas, carry a first aid kit and satellite phone. Practice these procedures monthly. For a Mavic 2, RTH can be triggered by pressing the RTH button. Know your drone's behavior. In high wind, RTH may not workâland manually. Always fly with a spotter.

6. First-Person View (FPV) Flying

FPV flying gives you a drone's-eye view. You'll need goggles, a drone with FPV camera, and a controller. In Africa, FPV is used for racing, inspection, and wildlife monitoring. Start with a simulator like 'VelociDrone' or 'Liftoff' on a computer. Practice 10 hours before real flight. For real FPV, use a drone like the DJI FPV or a custom build. Put on goggles, ensure antenna is connected. Take off in 'Normal' mode, then switch to 'Sport' or 'Manual'. In manual mode, the drone does not self-levelâyou must control everything. Start in an open field. Fly forward slowly, use small stick movements. To turn, bank and yaw simultaneously. In Africa, watch for birds and power lines. FPV range is limitedâstay within 500 meters. Use a spotter to watch the drone. For racing, use a 5.8GHz video transmitter. In dusty areas, protect goggles from dust. Practice figure eights in FPV. If you feel dizzy, land immediately. FPV flying is immersive but disorienting. Always have a failsafe: if signal lost, drone will cut motors (for racing) or RTH (for camera drones). In African wildlife, FPV can stress animalsâkeep distance. For inspection, use a gimbal for stable video. After flight, remove goggles and check drone. Clean lenses. FPV is not for beginnersâfirst master line-of-sight flying. Then transition. Always follow local regulations. In many African countries, FPV requires a spotter. Never fly over people. Practice in a designated area.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Drone Piloting and Flight Training

This practical reference is written for African agribusiness operators who need to move from owning a drone to using it profitably. Whether you are scouting maize in Kaduna, spraying tomatoes in Naivasha, or mapping cotton in Northern Ghana, the difference between a useful flight and a wasted battery is procedure. This module gives you the field-ready checklists, manoeuvre standards, and failure responses that keep aircraft flying, crops protected, and clients paying.

Why procedure beats talent in African agribusiness

In Europe or North America, a drone crash is an insurance claim. In many African markets, it is a season-ending loss. A single DJI Agras T40 costs more than a smallholder's annual income, and replacement parts can take weeks to clear customs. That reality makes checklist discipline a commercial survival skill, not a bureaucratic ritual. Operators who log every flight, inspect every propeller, and respect battery limits win repeat contracts from cooperatives and agri-input dealers. Operators who improvise lose aircraft and credibility.

Pre-flight checklist and procedures

The pre-flight check is your insurance policy. Do it the same way every time, in the same order, so that a missed step becomes obvious. For African field conditions, add three local checks: dust on motor vents, insect nests in air intakes, and loose wiring caused by rough transport on murram or feeder roads.

Basic manoeuvres: hover, takeoff, landing

Hovering is the foundation of every spray pass and every mapping grid. A drone that drifts in hover will produce overlapping spray bands and blurred orthomosaics. Practise hovering at 3 m, 10 m, and 30 m with the nose pointing in four directions. In the African context, practise in crosswinds, because steady laminar air is rare on open farmland.

Takeoff should be vertical, deliberate, and to a safe altitude before any lateral movement. Landing should be into the wind where possible, with a spotter confirming the landing zone is clear of children, livestock, and curious bystanders â a real risk at cooperative demonstration days.

Advanced manoeuvres: figure eights and banked turns

Figure eights train smooth control input and spatial awareness. They matter commercially because they mirror the turns at the end of each survey pass. Banked turns, used correctly, keep the camera or spray nozzle stable through the turn and reduce gimbal stress. Used incorrectly, they stall the aircraft. For fixed-wing mapping drones common in large-scale maize and wheat operations, banked turns are mandatory; for multirotors, they are optional but improve footage quality.

Flying in different weather conditions

African weather is not a single condition; it is a set of regional risks. Coastal operators in Mombasa or Lagos face salt-laden humidity and sudden squalls. Sahelian operators face Harmattan dust that abrades motors and reduces visibility. Highveld operators in South Africa face afternoon thunderstorms and rapid wind shifts. The rule is simple: if you would not fly a kite, do not fly a drone.

Heat is the silent killer. Lithium batteries above 45Â°C lose capacity and can swell. Motors running in 38Â°C ambient air with 90% humidity cool poorly. Schedule spraying for early morning and late afternoon, both for battery health and for spray efficacy â many pesticides volatilise in midday heat.

SAFETY WARNING: Never fly a drone beyond visual line of sight (BVLOS) without explicit approval from your national civil aviation authority. In most African jurisdictions â including Kenya (KCAA), Nigeria (NCAA), South Africa (SACAA), and Ghana (GCAA) â BVLOS operations require a special permit, and flying near airports, military installations, or populated areas without authorisation can lead to confiscation of equipment, fines, or arrest. Always carry your registration, remote pilot certificate, and flight log. Safety is not optional; it is the licence to operate.

Emergency procedures and failsafes

Every commercial operator should have three failsafe layers configured before takeoff: return-to-home (RTH) on signal loss, auto-land on critically low battery, and manual override on the controller. Test them during training, not during a paid job.

Common emergencies and responses:

  • Signal loss: Aircraft should RTH automatically. Do not panic-switch modes; wait for reconnection.
  • Compass error: Land immediately in a clear area and re-calibrate away from metal and magnets.
  • Battery failure: Descend and land at the nearest safe point; do not attempt a long return.
  • Bird attack: Climb steadily and move away; raptors are territorial near nesting sites.
  • Fly-away: Trigger RTH, note last coordinates, and alert local authorities if over sensitive areas.

First-person view (FPV) flying

FPV is increasingly used in African agribusiness for crop scouting, livestock monitoring, and infrastructure inspection. It gives immersive situational awareness but removes the peripheral vision that keeps you safe. FPV pilots must have a visual observer (VO) who maintains line of sight and calls out obstacles and other aircraft. In Kenya and South Africa, FPV operations are generally permitted only with a VO and within VLOS limits.
