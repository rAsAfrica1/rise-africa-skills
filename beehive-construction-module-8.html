1. Purpose of Inner Covers and Ventilation

An inner cover sits directly on top of the top bars of your hive, just under the outer cover. Its main jobs are to provide insulation, prevent the bees from gluing the outer cover down with propolis, and allow moisture to escape. Without an inner cover, condensation from the bees' breath and nectar ripening can drip back onto the cluster, chilling bees in cold weather or causing mold in humid conditions. In hot African climates, an inner cover with a ventilation notch also helps hot air rise and exit, reducing bearding and fanning.

You need an inner cover that fits snugly but not too tight. A common design uses a 10 mm thick plywood or solid wood board cut to the same length and width as your hive's top opening. For a standard Langstroth hive, that's 508 mm by 413 mm. Cut a rectangular hole in the centre, about 100 mm by 150 mm, and cover it with 3 mm wire mesh (hardware cloth) to stop bees from passing through while letting air move. On one short side, cut a notch 20 mm wide and 10 mm deep into the edge; this becomes an upper entrance and ventilation gap when the outer cover is on.

In Africa, many beekeepers use local materials: offcuts of plywood from carpentry shops, flattened metal drums, or even sturdy cardboard sealed with propolis. Whatever you use, the inner cover must be flat and free of gaps that let rain in. If you use a solid board without a notch, drill two 25 mm holes near opposite corners and screen them. This gives cross-ventilation. During honey flow, you can leave the notch open; in cool highland areas, close it with a small wedge to retain warmth.

Proper ventilation also reduces absconding. Bees in a hot, stuffy hive with no air movement will often leave. An inner cover with a mesh centre and a notch gives them a chimney effect: hot air rises through the mesh and out the notch, drawing cooler air in from the entrance. Check your inner cover every few months. Scrape off propolis with a hive tool, and replace mesh if it's torn. A well-made inner cover costs little but pays you back in healthier, calmer colonies.

2. Constructing an Inner Cover with Bee Escape

A bee escape is a small device that lets worker bees go down but not back up, useful for clearing bees from a honey super before harvest. You can build an inner cover with a built-in bee escape using simple tools: a hammer, saw, drill, and 6 mm plywood. Start with a board 508 mm by 413 mm. Cut a central hole 100 mm by 150 mm. Instead of mesh, you will fit a bee escape. You can buy a plastic or metal escape (like a Porter escape) or make one from a 20 mm thick wooden block with a 19 mm tunnel and a small wire grid.

To make a wooden escape: take a block 80 mm by 80 mm by 20 mm. Drill a 19 mm hole through the centre. On the top face, cut a slot 3 mm wide and 15 mm deep across the hole. Insert a piece of 3 mm wire mesh over the slot, stapled down. The bees enter the hole from below, walk through the tunnel, and exit through the mesh into the super above. Because the mesh is flat, they cannot figure out how to go back down. This is a one-way valve.

Fix the escape over the central hole of your inner cover using four 25 mm screws. Make sure the escape sits flush and there are no gaps around it. On the short side of the cover, cut a 20 mm by 10 mm notch for ventilation and as an upper entrance when the escape is not in use. Sand rough edges. Paint the cover with linseed oil or food-grade paint, avoiding the escape area.

When using the escape, place the inner cover with escape under the honey super you want to clear. The bees will move down into the brood box overnight. After 24 to 48 hours, most workers will be gone. Then remove the super. In Africa, where small hive beetles and ants are common, a bee escape can also help reduce pests by letting bees concentrate in the brood nest. Clean the escape after each use; propolis can block the tunnel. Store the cover dry. This tool saves you from shaking bees off frames, which stresses the colony and loses honey.

3. Building a Telescoping Outer Cover

The telescoping outer cover is the roof of your hive. It slides over the inner cover and the top edges of the hive body, protecting against rain, sun, and wind. It must be at least 50 mm larger than the hive on all sides so it overhangs and sheds water away from the walls. For a standard 10-frame Langstroth hive, the outer cover is about 560 mm long, 460 mm wide, and 100 mm tall. In Africa, you can adapt these dimensions to your local hive size, but keep the overhang.

Build the cover from 20 mm thick timber. Cut two side pieces 560 mm long and two end pieces 460 mm long, each 100 mm wide. Nail or screw them together into a rectangular frame. Then cut a top board 560 mm by 460 mm from 12 mm plywood or solid wood. Fix it to the frame with 40 mm screws. For extra strength, add a 20 mm by 20 mm batten along the inside edges of the frame. This frame creates a dead air space that insulates the hive from the hot sun.

Next, cover the top with metal roofing. You can use galvanised iron sheet, aluminium, or even flattened tin cans. Cut the metal 50 mm larger than the top board on all sides. Bend the edges down over the sides by 50 mm and nail them to the frame with roofing nails. This metal reflects sunlight and keeps rain out. In hot areas, paint the metal white or silver to reflect heat. In cooler highlands, black paint absorbs heat, but white is safer for bees.

Finally, add a ventilation gap. Drill two 25 mm holes in the end pieces, near the top, and cover them with 3 mm mesh to stop bees from entering. This lets hot air escape. The telescoping cover should fit loosely enough to lift off easily but not so loose that wind blows it off. You can add a brick or strap on windy sites. Check annually for rust and repaint. A good outer cover lasts 5 to 10 years and is the first line of defence for your colony.

4. Cutting and Fitting Metal Roofing

Metal roofing for your hive cover can be cut from old corrugated iron sheets, flat galvanised sheet, or aluminium flashing. You need tin snips (aviation snips) for straight cuts, a measuring tape, a marker, and gloves to protect your hands from sharp edges. For a telescoping cover of 560 mm by 460 mm, cut the metal 660 mm by 560 mm, giving a 50 mm overhang on all sides to bend down.

Mark your cut lines with a marker. Use the tin snips to cut along the lines. If the metal is thick, use a hacksaw with a fine-tooth blade. For corrugated sheets, cut along the ridges to keep the sheet strong. After cutting, file the edges smooth with a metal file. This prevents cuts and helps the metal bend without tearing. Wear gloves and eye protection; metal shards fly.

Bend the overhang down over the sides of the wooden cover. You can do this by hand for thin aluminium, but for thicker galvanised iron, use a hammer and a block of wood. Place the metal on the cover, align it, and tap the edges down gently. Work from the centre of each side outward to avoid wrinkles. At the corners, make a diagonal cut from the corner of the top board to the corner of the metal, then fold the flaps over each other and nail. This creates a neat, watertight corner.

Fix the metal to the wooden frame with roofing nails or screws with rubber washers. Space them every 100 mm along the edges. Nail through the top of the metal into the frame, not through the bent sides, to avoid leaks. For extra waterproofing, run a bead of silicone sealant under the metal edges before nailing. In Africa, where intense sun can make metal expand, leave a 2 mm gap between sheets if you use more than one piece. Paint the metal with anti-rust paint every two years. A well-fitted metal roof keeps your hive dry and cool, and it can be removed for inspection without disturbing the bees.

5. Adding Insulation and Moisture Control

Insulation in the hive cover helps keep temperatures stable. In hot African regions, it reduces heat stress; in cool highlands, it stops condensation from dripping on the bees. The simplest insulation is a 25 mm layer of rigid foam board (like Styrofoam) cut to fit inside the telescoping cover, between the top board and the inner cover. You can also use dry grass, sawdust, or shredded paper in a cloth bag, but foam is cleaner and lasts longer.

To install foam: cut it to the inside dimensions of your outer cover, about 510 mm by 410 mm for a Langstroth. Place it on top of the inner cover, then slide the outer cover over. The foam should not block the ventilation holes in the outer cover. If your outer cover has mesh vents, cut the foam to leave those areas open. In very hot areas, add a second layer of foam or use a reflective foil facing upward. In humid areas, moisture is the bigger enemy.

Moisture control: bees produce water vapour from respiration and from ripening nectar. If that moisture condenses on a cold inner cover, it drips back. To prevent this, ensure your inner cover has a ventilation notch and a mesh centre. You can also add a moisture quilt: a shallow box (50 mm deep) placed between the inner cover and outer cover, filled with wood shavings or dry rice husks. The shavings absorb moisture and let it evaporate through the top vents. In Africa, rice husks are cheap and available. Replace them when they feel damp, about every 2 to 3 months.

Another trick: tilt the hive slightly forward so condensation runs out the entrance, not onto the cluster. A 5 mm tilt is enough. Also, avoid sealing the hive completely. Bees need air movement. In the rainy season, check the inner cover for mold. If you see black spots, scrape and sun-dry the cover. A dry hive is a healthy hive. Insulation and moisture control are not expensive, but they can mean the difference between a colony that survives the season and one that absconds.

6. Weatherproofing Covers for Longevity

Your covers face sun, rain, wind, and insects. Without weatherproofing, wood warps, metal rusts, and bees suffer. Start with the wood. Use hardwood or treated pine for the frame. If you use untreated wood, paint it with two coats of exterior latex paint or linseed oil. Avoid creosote or toxic paints; bees are sensitive. Paint all surfaces, including edges and inside the frame, but do not paint the inner cover's bee-contact areas if you use a bee escape. Let it dry for 48 hours before putting it on the hive.

For the metal roof, apply a rust-resistant paint. In coastal Africa, salt air rusts metal fast, so use galvanised or aluminium sheets and paint them with zinc-rich paint. Check for scratches every few months. Sand any rust spots and touch up with paint. If you use corrugated iron, make sure nails have rubber washers and are not overdriven. Replace missing nails. Seal seams with roofing silicone.

Add a wind strap. In windy areas, a simple strap over the outer cover, anchored to the hive stand, prevents the cover from blowing off. You can use a ratchet strap or a piece of inner tube. Also, ensure the cover overhangs enough so rain drips off away from the hive body. A 50 mm overhang is minimum; 75 mm is better in heavy rain.

Finally, inspect your covers every season. Look for cracks, rot, rust, and loose mesh. Repair small problems immediately. A cracked inner cover lets rain in; a rusted roof leaks. Keep a spare cover ready so you can swap out a damaged one without exposing the colony. With proper care, a well-built telescoping cover lasts 8 to 10 years in African conditions. This saves you money and keeps your bees productive. Remember, the cover is the hive's umbrella and its winter coat. Treat it well, and your bees will reward you with honey.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Building Inner Covers and Telescoping Top Covers

In African beekeeping enterprises, the inner cover and telescoping top cover are not mere accessories they are the roof over your honey factory. A well-built inner cover regulates ventilation, prevents comb attachment to the lid, and provides a clean bee-free space for feeding and inspection. A properly fitted telescoping cover protects the entire hive from rain, sun, dust, and pests. Together, they can extend hive life by 35 years and reduce colony absconding during heat stress. This practical reference gives you the specifications, materials, and construction sequence to build these components using locally available timber and galvanised sheet, with business-first decisions on cost, durability, and scalability.

Purpose and Performance Standards

The inner cover sits directly on the top box. Its functions are: (1) to prevent bees from propolising the outer cover to the hive body; (2) to provide an insulating air gap that reduces heat gain under African sun; (3) to allow controlled ventilation through a central slot or screened hole; and (4) to provide a bee-free space for placing feeders or escape boards. The telescoping cover fits over the inner cover and overhangs the hive body by at least 25 mm on all sides. This overhang is the single most important rain-shedding feature without it, water runs down the hive walls and into the brood nest.

In business terms, a hive with a poor top cover loses colonies, requires replacement timber, and produces less honey. A hive with a well-built top cover can be worked in the rainy season, reducing downtime and increasing annual yield per hive by 1525%. The materials cost for one inner cover and one telescoping cover in most African markets is between USD 6 and USD 14, depending on timber and metal prices. This is a high-return investment.

SAFETY WARNING Cutting and Fitting Metal Roofing

Galvanised sheet edges are razor-sharp. Always wear cut-resistant gloves and safety glasses when cutting, folding, or handling metal roofing. Use tin snips or a hand shear never an angle grinder with a cutting disc on thin sheet, as it throws hot metal fragments and can shatter. When nailing metal to timber, wear ear protection. Secure all sheets on a flat surface before cutting. In hot African conditions, metal surfaces can reach 6070C allow to cool before handling. Keep children and livestock away from the work area. Dispose of off-cuts in a covered container; sharp metal in soil injures humans and animals.

Construction Sequence

Step 1 Inner cover. Cut the 10 mm plywood to 510 410 mm. Cut a central ventilation slot 100 20 mm and cover it with 3 mm wire mesh stapled from the underside. Drill a 25 mm bee escape hole near one end. Nail and glue a 20 20 mm rim around the perimeter. The rim creates a 20 mm bee space above the frames, which prevents burr comb and allows bees to move freely. Sand all edges; apply two coats of linseed oil or food-safe wood sealer. Do not use creosote or motor oil these contaminate honey.

Step 2 Telescoping cover. Cut the top board to 560 460 mm. Cut four side rails: two at 560 mm and two at 460 mm, each 75 mm deep and 20 mm thick. Assemble as a shallow box with waterproof wood glue and galvanised nails. The internal dimensions must be at least 10 mm larger than the hive body on all sides so the cover slides on and off without jamming. Check fit before adding metal.

Step 3 Metal roofing. Cut galvanised sheet to 600 500 mm. Fold down 25 mm on all four edges using a folding bar or hammer over a straight edge. Place over the cover and nail with galvanised roofing nails at 50 mm intervals along the edges. Apply a bead of roofing sealant under the metal edge on the windward side. In coastal or high-rainfall areas, use aluminium or pre-painted sheet for longer life.

Step 4 Insulation and moisture control. In hot, dry regions, fit a 10 mm rigid foam or sisal mat inside the cover before nailing the top board. This reduces heat transfer into the hive by up to 5C. In humid regions, drill two 10 mm ventilation holes in the side rails and cover them with mesh to prevent condensation. Never seal the hive completely bees need airflow to ripen honey and reduce humidity.

Step 5 Weatherproofing. Paint all external timber with two coats of exterior-grade paint or wood preservative. Pay special attention to end grain, which absorbs water fastest. Re-apply sealant around nails and metal edges annually. In termite-prone areas, treat the timber with borate-based preservative before assembly. Store spare covers flat and off the ground.

  • Central ventilation slot 100 20 mm covered with 3 mm wire mesh.
  • Bee escape hole 25 mm diameter, fitted with an 8-way escape if used for honey harvesting.
  • Telescoping cover overhangs hive body by at least 25 mm on all four sides.

  • Insulation fitted in hot regions; ventilation holes fitted in humid regions.

  • Annual maintenance: check sealant, nails, and metal edges before rainy season.
