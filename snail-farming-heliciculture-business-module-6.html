1. Common Diseases and Parasites in Snails

Snails in Africa commonly suffer from bacterial, fungal, and parasitic infections. The most frequent bacterial disease is shell rot, caused by Pseudomonas and Aeromonas species. It appears as dark, soft spots on the shell that spread and emit a foul smell. Another major issue is fungal infections like Aspergillosis, which cause white fuzzy growth on the shell or body, often after prolonged damp conditions. Parasites include nematodes (roundworms) that infest the gut, causing weight loss and poor growth, and mites that attach to the snail's body, leading to irritation and secondary infections. In West Africa, the giant African land snail (Achatina achatina) is particularly susceptible to these problems when kept in overcrowded, dirty pens.

Recognize diseases early. Shell rot starts as a small brown spot; within days it can penetrate the shell, causing death. Fungal infections appear as cotton-like patches on the shell or mantle. Nematode infections show as thin, lethargic snails with diarrhoea-like waste. Mites look like tiny moving dots on the snail's skin. These problems thrive in wet, dirty environments with poor ventilation. In a typical backyard farm in Nigeria or Ghana, you might see 10-20% mortality per month if left unchecked.

Prevention is cheaper than cure. Keep the pen clean: remove leftover food and waste daily. Use well-drained soil (loamy, not clay) and provide hiding places like broken pots or banana leaves. Avoid overcrowding: stock no more than 20-30 adult snails per square meter. Quarantine new snails for 2 weeks before adding them to the main pen. If you spot disease, isolate affected snails immediately. For shell rot, gently clean the shell with a soft brush and apply a dilute solution of potassium permanganate (1 gram per 10 litres of water) once daily for a week. For fungal infections, improve ventilation and reduce moisture; you can dust the shell with agricultural lime. For mites, dip snails in a mild salt solution (1 teaspoon per litre) for 5 minutes, then rinse. Always wear gloves and wash hands after handling sick snails.

Remember, healthy snails are active, have smooth, shiny shells, and eat well. Sick ones hide, stop eating, and have dull or damaged shells. Regular observation is your best tool. In Africa, where veterinary services for snails are rare, you must be your own doctor. Keep records of any disease outbreaks to learn patterns. With good management, you can reduce mortality to under 5% per month.

2. Preventive Health Care and Biosecurity

Preventive health care is the backbone of profitable snail farming. Biosecurity means keeping diseases out and stopping them from spreading. In Africa, many farmers lose snails to preventable causes like dirty pens, poor feed, and introduced infections. Start with a clean site: choose a shaded area away from stagnant water and other livestock. Build pens with smooth walls to prevent predators and pests. Use mesh covers to keep out rats, ants, and birds. Before introducing snails, disinfect the pen with a 2% formalin solution (20 ml formalin per litre of water) and let it dry for 3 days. Then rinse with clean water.

Daily routines matter. Feed snails with fresh, clean vegetables and fruitscabbage, pawpaw, mango, and cucumber are good. Remove uneaten food after 24 hours to prevent mould. Provide a calcium source like ground oyster shell or limestone; sprinkle it on the soil weekly. Keep soil moisture at 70-80% (squeeze a handfulit should hold together but not drip). Too wet causes fungal growth; too dry stresses snails. Use a simple hygrometer or just feel the soil. Clean water in shallow dishes must be available; change it daily. Avoid using tap water with high chlorine; let it sit for 24 hours first.

Quarantine new snails for at least 14 days in a separate pen. Observe them for signs of illness. Never mix new snails with your main stock until cleared. Control pests: ants can be deterred by placing a ring of wood ash around the pen. Rats and snakes need physical barriers. If you have other animals, keep them away from snail pens. Visitors should not handle snails without washing hands. Use dedicated tools (gloves, buckets) for each pen and disinfect them between uses.

Vaccines are not available for snails, so hygiene is your only defence. Keep records of cleaning schedules, feed sources, and any disease events. In a study in Ghana, farms that followed strict biosecurity had 30% higher survival rates. Simple steps like washing hands, using clean feed, and isolating sick snails can save your business. Remember, a healthy snail is a productive snail. Invest time in prevention rather than cure.

3. Recognizing Signs of Illness and Stress

Snails cannot tell you when they are sick, so you must learn to read their body language. A healthy snail is active, especially at night or after rain. It has a smooth, shiny shell, a moist body, and responds to touch by retracting quickly. It eats well and leaves normal droppings (dark, cylindrical). Sick or stressed snails show clear signs. They may hide in the shell for long periods, even during active hours. The shell may become dull, cracked, or discoloured. The body may look shrivelled or swollen. They stop eating, and their droppings become watery or absent. You might see mucus that is thick or discoloured (yellowish or greenish).

Stress can be caused by poor environment: overcrowding, extreme temperatures, dry or waterlogged soil, loud noises, or frequent handling. In Africa, heat stress is common during dry season. Snails may aestivate (seal themselves in the shell) to survive, but if forced to stay active in high heat, they weaken. Check for signs like excessive mucus, pale body, or slow movement. Another sign is shell thinning, which happens when calcium is lacking. If you see snails clustering on top of each other, it may be a sign of overcrowding or lack of hiding spaces.

Parasites and diseases have specific signs. Shell rot: dark spots that enlarge. Fungal infection: white fuzzy patches. Nematodes: weight loss despite eating. Mites: tiny moving dots on the body. Bacterial infections: foul smell and rapid death. Keep a daily log: note any snail that is inactive, has abnormal shell, or is isolated. Early detection is key. If you see one sick snail, isolate it immediately. Check water quality and soil moisture. Adjust feeding. Sometimes stress is relieved by simply adding more hiding places or reducing stocking density.

Train yourself to do a quick health check every morning. Pick up a few snails gently; they should feel heavy and firm. If they feel light or soft, they may be ill. Look at the shell under light. A healthy shell is glossy and uniform. In a typical pen of 100 snails, you might find 2-5 sick ones weekly. Remove them, treat if possible, and record the cause. Over time, you will recognise patterns and prevent outbreaks. Remember, stressed snails are more vulnerable to disease, so reducing stress is disease prevention.

4. Treating Snail Ailments: Natural and Chemical Options

When prevention fails, you need to treat sick snails. Always isolate affected snails first. For shell rot, clean the shell gently with a soft toothbrush and lukewarm water. Then apply a dilute potassium permanganate solution (1 g per 10 L water) using a cotton swab. Do this once daily for 5-7 days. Alternatively, use a 1% copper sulphate solution, but be carefulit can be toxic if overused. For fungal infections, improve ventilation and reduce moisture. Dust the shell with agricultural lime or powdered charcoal. You can also use a mild solution of baking soda (1 teaspoon per litre) as a dip for 2 minutes. For mites, dip snails in a salt solution (1 teaspoon per litre) for 5 minutes, then rinse with clean water. Repeat every 3 days until mites are gone.

Natural remedies are often safer and cheaper. For bacterial infections, neem leaf extract works: boil 100 g of neem leaves in 1 litre of water for 10 minutes, cool, and use as a dip for 5 minutes daily. Garlic extract (crush 5 cloves in 1 litre water, strain) can also be used as a dip. For internal parasites like nematodes, feed snails with pumpkin seeds (ground) mixed into their feed2 g per snail per day for a week. Papaya seeds also have deworming properties. These natural options are readily available in Africa and reduce chemical residues.

Chemical treatments should be a last resort. Antibiotics like oxytetracycline can be used for bacterial infections, but dosage is tricky. A common approach: dissolve 1 g oxytetracycline in 1 litre of water and spray on feed, but only for 3 days. Overuse leads to resistance. For fungal infections, a 0.5% formalin solution can be sprayed on the pen, not on snails. Always follow safety: wear gloves, mask, and wash hands. Never use insecticides or herbicides near snails. After treatment, keep treated snails in a separate recovery pen with clean soil and fresh feed. Monitor for 2 weeks before returning to main pen.

Record all treatments: date, symptoms, treatment, and outcome. This helps you learn what works. In Africa, many effective remedies are traditional. For example, in Nigeria, farmers use ash from burnt cocoa pods to treat shell rot. In Kenya, aloe vera gel is applied to wounds. Combine local knowledge with scientific methods. Remember, stressed snails heal slowly, so provide optimal conditions: 25-28C, 75% humidity, and plenty of calcium. If a snail does not improve in a week, cull it to prevent spread. Treating individual snails is labour-intensive, so focus on prevention.

5. Quarantine Procedures for New Stock

Quarantine is a must for any new snails you bring to your farm. It protects your existing stock from diseases and parasites. In Africa, many farmers buy snails from markets or other farms, which often carry hidden infections. A proper quarantine lasts at least 14 days, preferably 21 days. Set up a separate pen away from your main snail houseat least 10 metres away. The quarantine pen should have its own tools, gloves, and water source. Never share equipment between quarantine and main pens without disinfecting.

When you receive new snails, inspect each one. Look for shell damage, abnormal mucus, or signs of mites. Weigh a sample and record. Place them in the quarantine pen with clean, moist soil (sterilised by baking in an oven at 100C for 30 minutes, or solarised under black plastic for a week). Provide fresh feed and water. Keep the pen covered with mesh. For the first 3 days, minimise disturbance. Then observe daily: are they eating? Moving? Any deaths? If any snail dies, remove it immediately and send for diagnosis if possible. If you see symptoms, treat the whole group. After 14 days, if all snails are healthy, you can introduce them to the main pen. But first, take 2-3 snails from quarantine and place them in a small cage with 2-3 snails from your main stock. Observe for 5 days. If no disease appears, you can mix all.

During quarantine, maintain strict hygiene. Feed quarantine snails last, after you have tended to main stock. Wash hands and change gloves between pens. Disinfect boots by stepping in a footbath with 2% formalin. Keep a quarantine log: date in, source, number, health observations, treatments, date out. In a case in Uganda, a farmer lost 200 snails because he skipped quarantine and introduced infected stock. The disease spread within a week. Quarantine also helps snails adapt to new conditions and recover from transport stress. It is a small investment of time that saves huge losses. Always buy from reputable sources, but still quarantine. Never mix new and old snails immediately.

6. Record-Keeping for Health and Productivity

Record-keeping is not paperwork for its own sakeit is a tool to make better decisions. Without records, you cannot know if your snails are healthy or if your treatments work. In Africa, many small-scale farmers keep everything in their heads, but that leads to repeated mistakes. Start with a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone. Record daily: date, temperature, humidity, number of snails, feed given, and any deaths. For health, note any sick snails, symptoms, treatment given, and outcome. Also record when you clean the pen, when you add calcium, and when you quarantine new stock.

Use a simple system. For example, a table with columns: Date, Pen ID, Snail Count, Deaths, Symptoms, Action Taken, Notes. At the end of each week, count your snails. Calculate mortality rate: (deaths / starting count) x 100. If mortality is above 5% per month, investigate. Track growth: weigh 10 snails monthly. Healthy snails gain weight steadily. If growth stalls, check feed and health. Record feed types and amounts. If you notice a disease outbreak, you can look back to see what changednew feed, new snails, weather. This helps you prevent future outbreaks.

Records also help with productivity. Note egg-laying dates, hatch rates, and time to maturity. For example, Achatina achatina matures in 9-12 months. If your snails take longer, something is wrong. Keep financial records: costs of feed, treatments, and labour, and income from sales. This tells you if your snail business is profitable. In Ghana, a farmer who kept records for 6 months discovered that a certain feed (pawpaw leaves) caused bloating, so he switched. Without records, he would have kept losing snails.

Make record-keeping a habit. Spend 5 minutes each morning. Use a simple form or a notebook. At the end of each month, review your records. Look for patterns: are deaths higher in dry season? Do certain pens have more disease? Adjust your management. Share records with other farmers to learn together. In Kenya, farmer groups that shared records reduced mortality by 20%. Your records are your farm's memory. They turn experience into knowledge. Start todayeven a simple tally on a wall works. But be consistent. The best farmers are those who learn from their own data.

<h2>Practical Reference: Snail Health &amp; Disease Management for African Snail Farms</h2>

<p>In African heliciculture, disease rarely kills a farm outright it erodes profit margins. A single <em>Pseudomonas</em> outbreak in a grow-out pen can cut marketable stock by 2040%, while chronic <em>Angiostrongylus</em> contamination in a poorly managed hutch can destroy buyer trust and trigger regulatory shutdowns. This module is written for the commercial snail farmer the entrepreneur selling <em>Achatina achatina</em>, <em>A. fulica</em>, or <em>Archachatina marginata</em> to hotels, processors, and export markets. Every health decision is a business decision: quarantine protects your capital, record-keeping protects your margins, and biosecurity protects your reputation.</p>

<p>Snails are molluscs, not livestock in the mammalian sense, and their immune systems are primitive. They rely on environmental hygiene, moisture balance, and calcium availability to resist pathogens. When those fail, disease moves fast often within 72 hours. This reference gives you the diagnostic and treatment framework used by commercial farms in Ghana, Nigeria, Kenya, and Cte d'Ivoire.</p>

<h3>2. Preventive Health Care and Biosecurity Your Cheapest Insurance</h3>

<p>In snail farming, prevention is not just cheaper than cure it is the only economically viable strategy. Chemical treatments are expensive, often unregistered for molluscs, and can leave residues that disqualify stock from export markets. Biosecurity is your margin protection.</p>

<p><strong>Core biosecurity practices for African snail farms:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pen hygiene:</strong> Remove uneaten feed and dead snails daily. Dead snails decompose fast and spread <em>Pseudomonas</em> within hours.</li>
<li><strong>Substrate management:</strong> Replace soil/bedding every 46 weeks or when it becomes compacted or odorous. Sterilise reused soil by solarisation or heat treatment.</li>
<li><strong>Water quality:</strong> Use clean, chlorine-free water. If using borehole or stream water, test for <em>Angiostrongylus</em> and coliforms. Chlorine at 0.51 ppm is acceptable but must be off-gassed before use.</li>
<li><strong>Feed safety:</strong> Never feed spoiled or mouldy produce. Wash all leafy greens in salt water (5%) for 10 minutes to remove parasite eggs and pesticide residue.</li>
<li><strong>Traffic control:</strong> Limit visitors. Use footbaths (copper sulphate or Virkon) at pen entrances. Dedicate tools per pen never share between quarantine and production units.</li>
<li><strong>Rodent and insect control:</strong> Rats are intermediate hosts for <em>Angiostrongylus</em>. Use traps, not rodenticides (snails may consume bait). Screen pens with mosquito netting to exclude flies and mites.</li>
<li><strong>Stocking density:</strong> Keep grow-out pens at 2030 snails/m for <em>A. achatina</em>; overcrowding stresses snails and increases disease transmission.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. Treating Snail Ailments: Natural and Chemical Options</h3>

<p><strong>Natural treatments (first line):</strong> For fungal shell rot, dilute potassium permanganate (1 g per 10 L water) as a dip for 5 minutes. For mite infestations, neem leaf extract (100 g leaves boiled in 1 L water, cooled, sprayed on substrate) is effective and residue-free. For bacterial infections, improve drainage and reduce humidity <em>Pseudomonas</em> thrives in wet, stagnant conditions. Calcium supplementation with crushed oyster shell or limestone is the single most cost-effective health intervention.</p>

<p><strong>Chemical treatments (last resort, vet-supervised):</strong> Oxytetracycline (1020 mg/L bath for 30 minutes) can treat bacterial septicaemia, but withdrawal periods apply typically 1421 days before sale. Copper sulphate footbaths (2% solution) are for equipment and pen surfaces, never for direct snail contact. No chemical treatment is registered for snails in most African jurisdictions, so use is off-label and requires veterinary oversight.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #c00; background:#fff0f0; padding:12px; margin:16px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<p style="margin:0; font-weight:bold; color:#900; font-size:1.05em;"> SAFETY WARNING ZOONOTIC AND CHEMICAL RISKS</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#600;">Snails can carry <em>Angiostrongylus cantonensis</em> (rat lungworm), which causes eosinophilic meningitis in humans. <strong>Never eat raw or undercooked snails. Always wear gloves when handling sick or dead snails, and wash hands with soap after any contact.</strong> Chemical treatments (oxytetracycline, copper sulphate, potassium permanganate) are hazardous. Wear gloves, eye protection, and a mask when mixing. Store chemicals away from feed and out of reach of children. Observe withdrawal periods strictly residue-contaminated snails can fail export tests and harm consumers. If you or a worker develop severe headache, stiff neck, or nausea after handling snails, seek medical attention immediately and inform the doctor of snail contact.</p>
</div>

<h3>5. Quarantine Procedures for New Stock</h3>

<p>Every new snail whether purchased, gifted, or wild-caught is a potential disease vector. A 2130 day quarantine is non-negotiable for commercial farms. Quarantine pens should be physically separate (minimum 5 m from production pens), with dedicated tools, separate feeding, and separate record sheets.</p>

<p><strong>Quarantine protocol:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Inspect each snail on arrival. Reject any with shell damage, abnormal mucus, or lethargy.</li>
<li>Bath new snails in salt water (5%) for 5 minutes to remove surface parasites and debris.</li>
<li>Place in quarantine pen with fresh, sterilised substrate and clean water.</li>
<li>Feed a standard diet; observe daily for 2130 days.</li>
<li>Monitor for: shell changes, feeding behaviour, mucus production, mortality.</li>
<li>Test for <em>Angiostrongylus</em> if wild-caught (faecal smear or snail tissue test via veterinary lab).</li>
<li>Only after 30 days with zero symptoms, introduce to production pens and only in the evening when snails are active.</li>
<li>Record all observations in the quarantine log (see Section 6).</li>
</ol>

<h3>6. Record-Keeping for Health and Productivity</h3>

<p>Record-keeping is where most African snail farms lose money silently. Without records, you cannot trace a disease outbreak to its source, calculate mortality rates, or prove health status to buyers and regulators. A simple notebook or spreadsheet is enough but it must be consistent.</p>

<p><strong>Essential records:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Daily log:</strong> Date, temperature, humidity, feed given, mortality count, observed symptoms.</li>
<li><strong>Quarantine log:</strong> Source of stock, arrival date, observations, release date.</li>
<li><strong>Treatment log:</strong> Date, pen ID, product used, dose, withdrawal period, outcome.</li>
<li><strong>Productivity log:</strong> Monthly weight sampling (10 snails per pen), egg clutches, hatch rates, feed conversion.</li>
<li><strong>Mortality log:</strong> Date, pen, number, suspected cause, action taken.</li>
<li><strong>Sales and traceability log:</strong> Batch ID, date sold, buyer, health status at sale.</li>
</ul>

<p>These records let you calculate your true cost of production per kg, identify your best-performing pens, and provide buyers with traceability a growing requirement for export to EU and Middle Eastern markets.</p>
