1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Bread is not just food; it is a daily cash machine. In almost every African community, from Lagos to Nairobi to Accra, people buy bread every single day. Unlike a wedding cake or a birthday cake, bread is bought by the same customers over and over. That means if you bake good bread, you don't need to find new customers every day. You just need to keep your regulars happy.

Think about your own street. How many people eat bread for breakfast? How many buy it on the way to work? How many send children to the shop for a loaf in the evening? In many areas, a small bakery can sell 200 to 500 loaves a day. At a profit of 50 to 100 naira per loaf, that is 10,000 to 50,000 naira profit daily. That is real money.

But why do some bakeries fail? They fail because they treat bread as a side business. They bake when they feel like it. They don't measure. They don't keep records. They don't show up. The bakery business is a daily business. Your customers expect bread today, tomorrow, and the next day. If you miss a day, they will buy from someone else. If you miss a week, they will forget you.

This module will teach you the foundation: why bread is daily cash, what tools you need, how to bake step by step, common mistakes, safety, and a practical exercise. By the end, you will understand that bread is not just a productit is a system. A system of buying ingredients, mixing, baking, and selling every day. When you master the system, you can feed your family, pay school fees, and even employ others.

Start thinking like a baker, not a hobbyist. Your oven is your ATM. Your ingredients are your investment. Your customers are your daily income. Let's begin.

2. Tools and Materials

To start a small bakery, you don't need a million naira. You need the right tools and materials. Here is what you need for a basic daily bread production of 50 to 100 loaves.

First, the oven. A gas oven is best for consistent heat. A single-deck gas oven can cost 150,000 to 300,000 naira. If that is too much, you can start with a charcoal oven or even a local mud oven. But gas is cleaner and easier to control. You also need baking trays. Get at least 10 trays so you can bake in batches. Each tray holds about 6 to 8 loaves.

Next, mixing tools. You need a large mixing bowl or a dough mixer. A 20-litre bowl is good for small batches. A hand mixer or a wooden spoon for mixing. You also need a weighing scale. This is not optional. Bread requires exact measurements. A digital scale that measures up to 5 kg is fine. You need measuring spoons and cups for yeast, salt, and sugar.

Ingredients: flour, yeast, salt, sugar, water, and oil or butter. For 50 loaves, you need about 25 kg of flour, 250 g of yeast, 500 g of salt, 1 kg of sugar, and 2 litres of oil. You also need waterclean water. And you need packaging: bread bags, twist ties, and a marker to write dates.

Other tools: a dough scraper, a rolling pin (if you make shaped bread), a cooling rack, and a storage bin for flour. You also need a table for shaping and a clean apron and hairnet. Don't forget a fire extinguisher and a first aid kit.

You can buy these tools gradually. Start with the oven, trays, scale, and basic ingredients. As you grow, add a mixer and more trays. The key is to start. Many successful bakers started with one oven and one tray. What matters is that you have the essentials to bake consistently.

3. Step by Step Method

Baking bread is a science, but it is also simple. Follow these steps for a basic white bread recipe that makes 10 loaves.

Step 1: Gather and measure. For 10 loaves, you need 5 kg of flour, 50 g of yeast, 100 g of salt, 200 g of sugar, 2.5 litres of warm water, and 100 ml of oil. Use your scale. Don't guess.

Step 2: Activate the yeast. In a small bowl, mix yeast, sugar, and 1 cup of warm water (not hot, or it will kill the yeast). Let it sit for 10 minutes until it foams. This tells you the yeast is alive.

Step 3: Mix the dough. In a large bowl, put flour and salt. Make a well in the center. Pour in the yeast mixture, remaining water, and oil. Mix with your hands or a spoon until a sticky dough forms.

Step 4: Knead. Turn the dough onto a floured table. Knead for 10 to 15 minutes. Push, fold, turn, repeat. The dough should become smooth and elastic. If it sticks, add a little flour. If it's too dry, add a little water.

Step 5: First rise. Put the dough in a lightly oiled bowl, cover with a damp cloth, and let it rise in a warm place for 1 to 2 hours. It should double in size.

Step 6: Punch down and shape. Punch the dough to release air. Divide into 10 equal pieces. Shape each into a loaf and place in greased loaf pans.

Step 7: Second rise. Cover and let rise again for 30 to 45 minutes.

Step 8: Bake. Preheat oven to 200C (400F). Bake for 25 to 30 minutes until golden brown. The bottom should sound hollow when tapped.

Step 9: Cool and package. Remove from pans and cool on a rack. Once cool, bag and label with date. Sell same day for best quality.

Remember, consistency is key. Use the same recipe, same measurements, same oven temperature. That way, your customers get the same great bread every day.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced bakers make mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them.

Mistake 1: Yeast not foaming. You mix yeast with water and nothing happens. Fix: Your water was too hot (above 45C) or too cold, or the yeast is expired. Use lukewarm water and check the expiry date. Always proof your yeast before mixing.

Mistake 2: Dough too dry or too sticky. You add too much flour or too little water. Fix: Measure accurately. If dough is sticky, add flour one tablespoon at a time. If dry, add water one tablespoon at a time. Don't dump.

Mistake 3: Bread doesn't rise. The dough stays flat. Fix: Your yeast was old, water too hot, or room too cold. Use fresh yeast, warm water, and let it rise in a warm place (like near the oven or in a closed cupboard).

Mistake 4: Bread is dense and heavy. Fix: You didn't knead enough or didn't let it rise long enough. Knead for at least 10 minutes and give it the full rising time. Also, check your flourold flour can cause dense bread.

Mistake 5: Bread burns outside, raw inside. Fix: Oven too hot. Reduce temperature to 180C and bake longer. Use an oven thermometer to check actual temperature.

Mistake 6: Bread tastes bland. Fix: You forgot salt or used too little. Salt is not just for taste; it controls yeast. Use the right amount.

Mistake 7: Inconsistent sizes. Fix: Use a scale to divide dough into equal pieces. Customers notice when one loaf is smaller.

Mistake 8: Selling stale bread. Fix: Bake daily. If you have leftovers, sell them as day-old at a discount or make breadcrumbs. Never sell stale bread as fresh.

Mistake 9: Poor packaging. Fix: Use clean bags, tie them well, and label with date. Presentation matters.

Mistake 10: Not keeping records. Fix: Write down how much flour you used, how many loaves you sold, and your profit. This helps you improve and grow.

Learn from these mistakes. Every mistake is a lesson. Fix them and your bread will be better, and your customers will keep coming back.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in the bakery is not just about avoiding accidents; it is about producing clean, safe bread that won't make your customers sick. Here are the key safety and best practices.

Personal hygiene: Always wash your hands with soap before handling dough. Wear a clean apron, hairnet, and if possible, gloves. Tie back long hair. Don't bake if you are sick, especially with cough, cold, or stomach issues. Cover any cuts with a waterproof bandage.

Clean workspace: Keep your tables, tools, and floor clean. Wash mixing bowls, trays, and utensils after every use. Use food-safe sanitizer. Don't let raw dough sit on surfaces for hours. Clean up spills immediately to avoid slips.

Food safety: Store flour in a dry, cool place in sealed containers. Keep yeast in the fridge or a cool dark place. Check expiry dates on all ingredients. Don't use mouldy flour or expired yeast. Use clean water. Don't mix old dough with new dough unless you know what you're doing (some bakers use old dough as a starter, but it must be handled properly).

Oven safety: Gas ovens can leak. Check for gas smells. Never light an oven with a match if you smell gas. Use the built-in igniter or a long lighter. Keep flammable items away from the oven. Use oven mitts when handling hot trays. Have a fire extinguisher nearby and know how to use it.

Knife and tool safety: Keep knives sharp; dull knives slip. Cut away from your body. Store knives safely. Don't leave tools on the floor.

Best practices for quality: Measure accurately. Use a thermometer to check water temperature (35-40C for yeast). Keep a baking log: date, time, temperature, and results. This helps you repeat success. Cool bread completely before packaging to prevent sweating and mould. Label with date and price. Rotate stock: sell older bread first.

Customer safety: Never sell bread that is burnt, undercooked, or dropped on the floor. If in doubt, throw it out. Your reputation is your business. A single case of food poisoning can destroy your bakery.

Finally, train your workers. Everyone who touches dough must know these rules. Safety is a habit. Make it part of your daily routine.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to put what you've learned into practice. This exercise will help you understand your local market and plan your daily bread business.

Exercise 1: Market survey. Visit 5 shops or bakeries near you that sell bread. Ask: How many loaves do you sell per day? What is your selling price? What size are the loaves? What time do customers buy the most? Write down your findings. This tells you the demand and the going price.

Exercise 2: Cost calculation. Using the recipe from Lesson 3 (10 loaves), find the current prices of flour, yeast, salt, sugar, oil, and gas/electricity. Calculate the total cost. Divide by 10 to get cost per loaf. Now compare with the selling price you found. What is your profit per loaf? If profit is less than 30% of selling price, you need to adjustmaybe buy in bulk or reduce waste.

Exercise 3: Bake a test batch. If you have access to an oven, bake 10 loaves using the step-by-step method. If you don't have an oven, find a local bakery and ask to watch or assist for a day. Note the steps, the timing, and the challenges. Write down what you would do differently.

Exercise 4: Identify your customers. Who will buy your bread? Will you sell from home, supply shops, or go door-to-door? List 10 potential customers or shops. Talk to them. Ask if they would buy from you if you supplied fresh bread daily. Get their phone numbers.

Exercise 5: Create a daily plan. Write a schedule: what time you wake up, mix dough, bake, cool, package, and sell. Include time for cleaning and record-keeping. A sample: 4am start, 4:30 mix, 5:30 shape, 6:30 bake, 7:30 cool, 8am sell. Adjust to your market.

Exercise 6: Set a goal. How many loaves do you want to sell daily in one month? Write it down. Then write the steps to get there: buy tools, practice, find customers, etc.

Do these exercises before moving to Module 2. They will give you a real foundation. Remember, a business plan on paper is good, but action is better. Start small, learn fast, and grow daily.

<h3>Why Daily Bread Equals Daily Cash</h3>

<p>Bread has three business advantages that few other food products share:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>High frequency:</strong> People buy bread daily or every few days. This means your cash register rings every day, not once a month.</li>
<li><strong>Low unit cost:</strong> A loaf of bread is affordable to almost every household, so your customer base is large.</li>
<li><strong>Short production cycle:</strong> From mixing to selling, a simple bread batch takes 35 hours. You can bake, sell, and repeat the same day.</li>
</ol>

<p>In many African cities, the informal bread market is dominated by small bakeries and roadside vendors. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), bread and bakery products are among the most consumed processed foods in sub-Saharan Africa, with demand growing alongside urbanisation (FAO, 2021). This is not a niche it is a mass market.</p>

<h3>Step-by-Step Method: From Dough to Daily Cash</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Plan your daily batch:</strong> Decide how many loaves you will bake based on yesterday's sales. Start with 50100 loaves if you are new.</li>
<li><strong>Weigh ingredients accurately:</strong> Use a scale. Inaccurate measurements cause inconsistent bread and waste.</li>
<li><strong>Mix and knead:</strong> Combine flour, yeast, salt, sugar, oil, and water. Knead for 1015 minutes until the dough is smooth and elastic.</li>
<li><strong>First proof:</strong> Cover the dough and let it rise in a warm place for 12 hours until doubled in size.</li>
<li><strong>Shape and second proof:</strong> Divide, shape into loaves, place in greased tins, and proof for 3060 minutes.</li>
<li><strong>Bake:</strong> Preheat oven to 200220C. Bake for 2535 minutes until golden brown and hollow-sounding when tapped.</li>
<li><strong>Cool and pack:</strong> Cool on racks for at least 30 minutes before packing to prevent soggy bread.</li>
<li><strong>Sell and record:</strong> Sell the same day. Record every sale and every expense in a notebook or phone app.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Common Mistakes and Fixes</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Mistake:</strong> Baking too many loaves and having leftovers. <strong>Fix:</strong> Track daily sales for one week and bake only what you can sell.</li>
<li><strong>Mistake:</strong> Using expired yeast. <strong>Fix:</strong> Check expiry dates and store yeast in a cool, dry place.</li>
<li><strong>Mistake:</strong> Not recording costs. <strong>Fix:</strong> Use a simple cash book. Know your cost per loaf.</li>
<li><strong>Mistake:</strong> Selling on credit. <strong>Fix:</strong> Encourage cash sales. If you must give credit, keep a strict ledger and follow up.</li>
<li><strong>Mistake:</strong> Ignoring hygiene. <strong>Fix:</strong> Wash hands, clean surfaces, and cover dough. Customers buy with their eyes first.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="list-style-type:square;">
<li> Calculate cost per loaf before setting your price.</li>
<li> Bake only what you can sell in one day.</li>
<li> Keep a daily cash book record every sale and expense.</li>
<li> Store flour, yeast, and sugar in dry, sealed containers.</li>
<li> Check oven temperature with a thermometer if possible.</li>
<li> Cool bread completely before packing.</li>
<li> Keep fire safety equipment within reach.</li>
<li> Reinvest a portion of daily profit into tomorrow's ingredients.</li>
</ul>
