1. Calculating the true cost per block: materials, labour, and overhead

Many block makers guess their costs and end up losing money. To know your true cost per block, you must add up three things: materials, labour, and overhead. Let's use a standard 6-inch block (15cm x 20cm x 40cm) as an example. A bag of cement costs about $8 and can make 30 blocks if you use a 1:6 mix (1 part cement to 6 parts sand). So cement per block = $8 / 30 = $0.27. Sand: a tipper of sand costs $60 and can make 200 blocks, so sand per block = $0.30. Water: negligible, say $0.01. Total materials = $0.58 per block.

Labour: If you pay a worker $10 per day and they make 100 blocks, labour per block = $0.10. If you work yourself, still count your timepay yourself a wage. Overhead: This includes rent for your yard, tools (shovels, wheelbarrows, moulds), curing water, and transport of materials. Suppose monthly overhead is $200 and you make 2,000 blocks per month, overhead per block = $0.10.

Now add them up: materials $0.58 + labour $0.10 + overhead $0.10 = $0.78 per block. That is your true cost. You must also account for breakages and wasteadd 5%: $0.78 x 1.05 = $0.82. So each block costs you about $0.82 to produce. If you sell at $0.90, your profit is only $0.08 per block. That is too small. You need to either reduce costs or increase price.

To reduce costs: buy cement in bulk for a discount, negotiate sand prices, improve your mix to use less cement (but keep strength), or increase daily production to spread overhead. Keep a notebook and record every expenseeven small ones like transport. After one month, calculate your actual cost per block. Many makers are surprised to find they are selling at a loss. Do this calculation every month because prices change.

2. Setting a selling price that covers costs and gives profit

Once you know your true cost per block, you can set a selling price that gives you a healthy profit. A common mistake is to copy a neighbour's price without knowing if it covers your costs. Your price must cover all costs plus a profit margin. A good rule for small block makers is to aim for a 20-30% profit margin on top of your total cost. If your cost per block is $0.82, a 25% profit means you add $0.21, so selling price = $1.03. Round it to $1.00 or $1.05 for easy selling.

But you must also consider what the market will pay. Check the prices of other block makers in your area. If they sell at $0.90, you cannot easily sell at $1.05 unless your blocks are stronger, better cured, or you offer delivery. You might need to reduce your costs to compete. For example, if you can get your cost down to $0.70 by buying materials in bulk, then selling at $0.90 gives you $0.20 profit per blocka 28% margin.

When setting your price, think about your target customer. Are you selling to individual home builders who buy 50 blocks at a time, or to contractors who buy 1,000 blocks? For small orders, you can charge a higher price per block because your overhead per block is higher. For large orders, you can offer a lower price but still make a profit because you save on labour and overhead per block. Also consider your competition: if there are many block makers, price wars can happen. Instead of lowering price, add value: offer free delivery for orders above 500 blocks, or guarantee your blocks for strength.

Finally, write your price list and stick to it. Do not change prices every day. Review your prices every three months or when material costs change. Always calculate your profit per block and per day. If you sell 200 blocks a day at $1.00 and your cost is $0.82, your daily profit is $36. That is a good income. But if you sell at $0.85, your profit is only $6 per daynot enough to grow.

3. Comparing prices with other block makers in your area

To stay competitive, you must know what other block makers are charging. But don't just look at the pricefind out what they offer for that price. Visit their yards or send a friend to ask. Note their block size, cement mix, curing method, and whether they deliver. Some makers sell cheap blocks but use too little cement, so the blocks break easily. You can use that to your advantage by selling stronger blocks at a slightly higher price.

Make a simple table in your notebook: columns for name, price per block, block size, delivery included, and quality notes. For example, Maker A sells 6-inch blocks at $0.85, no delivery, but blocks are weak. Maker B sells at $0.95, includes delivery for 500+ blocks, and blocks are strong. You can position yourself in the middle: sell at $0.90, offer delivery for 1,000+ blocks, and guarantee strength. That gives customers a reason to choose you.

Also consider the location. If you are far from town, customers may prefer a maker closer to the main road even if your price is lower, because transport costs them money. So you might need to offer free delivery within a certain radius or reduce your price to compensate. On the other hand, if you are near a growing neighbourhood with many construction sites, you can charge a premium because of convenience.

Don't engage in a price war. If a competitor drops their price below your cost, let them. They will soon go out of business. Instead, focus on quality and service. Talk to customers, ask why they chose you or another maker. Use that feedback to improve. For example, if many say they want blocks delivered, buy a used truck or partner with a transporter. If they want different sizes, add a new mould. By knowing the market, you can set a price that is fair to you and attractive to customers.

4. Offering discounts for bulk orders without losing money

Bulk orders are good because you sell many blocks at once, reducing your selling effort and often your overhead per block. But if you give too big a discount, you can lose money. The key is to calculate your profit at the discounted price. Suppose your normal price is $1.00 per block and your cost is $0.82. Your profit is $0.18 per block. For a bulk order of 1,000 blocks, you might offer a 10% discount, selling at $0.90. Your profit drops to $0.08 per block. Is that acceptable? Yes, if you still make a profit and the order helps you cover fixed costs. But if you discount to $0.80, you lose $0.02 per blocknever do that.

A smart way to discount is to base it on your cost savings. For large orders, you save on labour (you can produce more blocks per worker per day) and overhead (you use the same moulds and yard). So your cost per block might drop to $0.75. Then selling at $0.90 gives you $0.15 profitstill good. Calculate your cost for the bulk order separately, considering any extra costs like delivery. If you must deliver 1,000 blocks 10 km away, that might cost $30 in transport. So your total cost becomes $750 + $30 = $780, and revenue at $0.90 is $900, profit $120. That's fine.

Always set a minimum order quantity for discounts, such as 500 blocks. Offer tiered discounts: 5% for 500-999 blocks, 10% for 1,000-1,999, 15% for 2,000+. But never go below your cost. Use a simple formula: Discounted price = Cost per block + (Desired profit per block). For example, if cost is $0.82 and you want at least $0.10 profit, your minimum price is $0.92. So your maximum discount from $1.00 is 8%. Write this down and stick to it. Also, ask for a deposit (e.g., 50%) before starting a bulk order to avoid cash flow problems. And deliver on time to build trust for repeat business.

5. Tracking daily income and expenses in a simple notebook

You cannot grow a business if you don't know where your money is going. A simple notebook is enough to track daily income and expenses. At the end of each day, write down: date, number of blocks sold, price per block, total income, and all expenses (cement, sand, labour, transport, water, tools, etc.). Also note any money you took out for personal use. This takes only 10 minutes but gives you full control.

Create columns: Date | Description | Income | Expense | Balance. Start with your opening cash. For example, on Monday you sell 200 blocks at $1.00 = $200 income. You buy 2 bags of cement for $16, pay a worker $10, and buy water for $2. Total expenses = $28. Your net for the day = $172. Add that to your balance. At the end of the week, total your income and expenses. If your expenses are more than 80% of income, your profit margin is too thin. Look for ways to reduce costs or increase price.

Also track your production: how many blocks you made each day. This helps you calculate your cost per block accurately. If you made 500 blocks in a week and spent $400 on materials and labour, your cost per block is $0.80. Compare that to your selling price. If you sell at $1.00, your profit is $0.20 per block. Multiply by number of blocks sold to get weekly profit.

Use a separate page for debts: who owes you money and who you owe. Follow up on debts weekly. Never mix business money with personal money. Pay yourself a fixed salary from the business, and record it as an expense. This way, you know if the business is truly profitable. At the end of the month, review your notebook. Identify your biggest expenses and see if you can negotiate better prices or reduce waste. Many block makers fail because they don't trackdon't be one of them.

6. Reinvesting profits to grow your block yard

Profit is not just for spendingit is for growing your business. To expand your block yard, you need to reinvest a portion of your profits. A good rule is to reinvest at least 30% of your monthly profit. For example, if you make $500 profit in a month, put $150 back into the business. Use it to buy more moulds, a larger curing area, or a second wheelbarrow. More moulds mean you can produce more blocks per day, increasing your income.

Start by identifying what limits your production. If you can only make 100 blocks a day because you have one mould, buy another mould. If you lack space for curing, rent a bigger yard or build simple racks. If you often run out of cement, buy in bulk to get a discount. Reinvesting in bulk materials can lower your cost per block, increasing your profit margin. For example, buying 50 bags of cement at once might cost $7.50 per bag instead of $8, saving $0.50 per bag. That adds up.

Another way to reinvest is to improve quality. Buy a vibrating table to make stronger blocks, or a mixer to save labour time. These tools cost money but pay back quickly. For instance, a $200 mixer can reduce mixing time by half, allowing you to produce 50 more blocks per day. At $0.20 profit per block, that's $10 extra per day$300 per month. The mixer pays for itself in less than a month.

Also consider marketing: paint a signboard, print simple flyers, or create a WhatsApp Business account. These are low-cost ways to get more customers. As your business grows, you might hire more workers, buy a truck for delivery, or open a second yard. But grow step by step. Always keep some cash reserve for emergencies, like a machine breaking down or a slow sales month. Reinvesting wisely turns a small block yard into a thriving business that supports your family and creates jobs.

<h2>Practical Reference: Pricing, Costing, and Profit for Block Makers</h2>

<p>In block making, the difference between a business that survives and one that collapses is rarely the quality of the blocks alone. It is whether the maker knows the true cost of every block leaving the yard, and whether the selling price is set to recover that cost plus a margin. This reference module gives you the calculations, benchmarks, and record-keeping habits that turn a block yard into a profitable enterprise. It is written for the African context, where cement prices fluctuate, transport is expensive, and competition is often informal and price-driven.</p>

<p>The core principle is simple: <strong>you cannot price what you cannot cost.</strong> Every lesson below builds toward a single habit knowing your numbers daily, not monthly.</p>

<h3>1. Calculating the True Cost Per Block</h3>

<p>Most block makers calculate only cement and sand. That is a fatal error. The true cost per block has three layers: <strong>materials</strong>, <strong>labour</strong>, and <strong>overhead</strong>. Overhead includes rent, water, electricity or diesel, mould depreciation, transport of raw materials, and your own time if you are not drawing a separate salary.</p>

<h3>2. Setting a Selling Price That Covers Costs and Gives Profit</h3>

<p>Once you know your true cost, set your price using a <strong>markup on cost</strong>, not a guess. A standard target in small-scale block making is 1530% net margin, depending on competition and volume. If your true cost is KSh 103.80 and you want a 25% margin, your price is KSh 129.75 round to KSh 130.</p>

<p>The formula: <strong>Selling Price = True Cost (1 + Target Margin)</strong>. Never price below true cost, even to win a tender. A loss-making contract drains cash and kills the business slowly.</p>

<h3>3. Comparing Prices With Other Block Makers</h3>

<p>You must know your competitors' prices, but you must not copy them blindly. Many competitors are pricing below true cost because they ignore overhead or labour. Walk or call five block yards within 10km. Record their prices for 6-inch, 8-inch, and solid blocks. Then compare against your cost floor.</p>

<p>If your cost is higher than their price, investigate: Are you buying cement retail while they buy wholesale? Is your labour productivity low? Are you paying rent they are not? Fix the cost, not the price. If your cost is lower, you have room to undercut slightly or to match price and earn a higher margin.</p>

<h3>4. Offering Discounts for Bulk Orders Without Losing Money</h3>

<p>Bulk discounts are a powerful sales tool, but only if you calculate the <strong>minimum acceptable price</strong>. Your floor is true cost plus a small margin (say 5%) to cover the risk of a large order. For a 1,000-block order, your floor is KSh 103.80 1.05 = KSh 108.99, round to KSh 109.</p>

<p>Offer tiered discounts: 500+ blocks at 5% off, 1,000+ at 8%, 5,000+ at 12%. Always show the customer the normal price and the discounted price, and state that the discount applies only for full payment on delivery or a deposit. Never discount below your floor.</p>

<h3>5. Tracking Daily Income and Expenses in a Simple Notebook</h3>

<p>You do not need accounting software. A hardcover notebook with four columns per day is enough: <strong>Date | Description | Money In | Money Out</strong>. Record every bag of cement bought, every block sold, every transport payment, every casual wage. At the end of each day, total the columns and write the running balance.</p>

<p>At the end of the week, calculate: total income, total expenses, and net profit. At the end of the month, divide total profit by number of blocks sold to get your actual profit per block. Compare it to your target. If it is lower, find out why usually it is unrecorded expenses or underpricing.</p>

<h3>6. Reinvesting Profits to Grow Your Block Yard</h3>

<p>Profit is not for spending. In the first 1224 months, reinvest at least 50% of net profit into the business. Priorities in order: (1) buy raw materials in bulk to reduce unit cost, (2) buy a second mould or a vibrating table to increase output, (3) build a curing shed to reduce breakage, (4) hire and train one reliable worker so you can focus on sales, (5) buy a small delivery trolley or partner with a boda-boda for transport.</p>

<p>Reinvestment compounds. A block yard that adds one mould per quarter can double output in a year without increasing overhead proportionally.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Know your true cost per block</strong> materials + labour + overhead, recalculated every time cement or sand prices change.</li>
<li><strong>Set price as cost (1 + target margin)</strong> never below cost, even for large orders.</li>
<li><strong>Check five competitors monthly</strong> know their prices, but compete on cost efficiency, not on price alone.</li>
<li><strong>Set a bulk discount floor</strong> cost + 5% minimum, and require deposit or full payment.</li>
<li><strong>Record every transaction daily</strong> four columns: Date, Description, Money In, Money Out.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate weekly and monthly profit</strong> compare actual profit per block to target.</li>
<li><strong>Reinvest at least 50% of profit</strong> bulk materials first, then equipment, then labour.</li>
<li><strong>Wear PPE every day</strong> mask, goggles, gloves; wash cement contact immediately.</li>
</ul>
