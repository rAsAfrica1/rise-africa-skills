AGRICULTURE · FLAGSHIP

Ostrich Farming

A complete 6-month program for the three-product bird — leather, meat and feathers. Safe handling of a 150 kg animal, hide-protecting fencing, the make-or-break chick phase, 42-day incubation and honest capital economics.

⏱️ 6 Months (8–12 hrs/week)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? Ostriches are a long-horizon, high-value business: a chick hatched in Month 1 reaches slaughter age at 10–14 months, and breeding birds take 2–3 years to mature. So this program uses six months differently — Months 1–3 build the knowledge, infrastructure and legal foundation, and Months 4–6 run real birds through their most dangerous growth phase (0–3 months, where most beginners lose their flock) while you build the three-product business plan: meat, leather and feathers.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each month → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen at the camps and the abattoir office.

⚠️ Read first: ostriches can kill you

  • An adult ostrich is 100–150 kg, 2.5 m tall, and kicks FORWARD with a 10 cm claw that can disembowel. Breeding males in season are genuinely dangerous. Never enter a breeding camp without an escape route, a thorn branch or board as a shield, and a second person watching.
  • This is a licensed, capital-heavy business in most countries — ostriches are often controlled species requiring permits for keeping, movement and slaughter, and the meat is export-regulated. Confirm every requirement in Month 1 BEFORE spending.
  • Never start with chicks alone and no mentor. Chick mortality of 30–50% is normal for beginners and near-zero for the disciplined. Month 4 is the difference.
MONTH 1 — The Ostrich Business, Biology & Legal Groundwork ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Three products from one bird

  • Leather: the real prize — ostrich hide (the quill-pattern crown) is among the world's most valuable leathers, used by luxury fashion houses. Often 40–50% of a slaughter bird's value.
  • Meat: red meat, low fat and cholesterol, high iron — sold as premium fillet/steaks to restaurants, lodges and export markets. A slaughter bird yields roughly 30–45 kg of meat.
  • Feathers: plumes for fashion and carnival, and body feathers for industrial dusters (they hold static) — a smaller but steady stream.
  • Plus: eggs (empty shells carve and sell to tourists; one egg = ~24 chicken eggs), and breeding-stock sales, often the fastest early income.

  • Ostriches are ratites — flightless, with a digestive system that ferments fibre; they graze like livestock, not like poultry, and can use lucerne pasture as the bulk of their diet.

Lesson 1.3 — The legal and market reality check

  • Ask your wildlife/agriculture authority, in writing: is a permit needed to keep, breed, move or slaughter ostriches? Which abattoirs are certified? Are hides/feathers export-controlled (CITES-related paperwork applies in some jurisdictions)?
  • The market truth: ostrich value is realised through PROCESSORS (tanneries and export abattoirs). Before buying a single bird, secure a written indication of interest from a processor or established buyer — farms that raise ostriches with nowhere to sell them fail expensively.
  • If no processor is reachable, the viable local model is meat + eggshell craft + breeding stock + agri-tourism — plan around what your market can actually absorb.

Lesson 1.4 — Entry routes and honest capital

  • Buy day-old/3-month chicks and grow to slaughter: lowest capital, fastest cycle, but chick-rearing skill is everything.
  • Buy proven breeding trios: expensive (a proven trio can cost as much as a car) but produces your own chicks yearly.
  • Contract growing for an established farm: the smart beginner route where it exists — they supply chicks and buy back birds.
  • Be clear-eyed: ostrich farming is NOT a low-capital start like rabbits or poultry. Fencing, camps, incubation and stock make it a serious investment with a 1–3 year payback.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Get written answers from your wildlife/agriculture authority on permits, movement and slaughter. (2) Contact two processors/buyers (abattoir, tannery, restaurant group) and record their requirements and prices. (3) Visit an ostrich farm; record camp sizes, feed regime, mortality experience and their honest advice.

MONTH 2 — Camps, Fencing & Handling Safety ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Camp design by age group

GroupSpaceNotes
Chicks 0–3 months1–2 m² indoors + small runHeated brooder area, non-slip floor
Growers 3–6 months10–20 m² per birdGroups of 20–40, shade essential
Finishers 6–14 months50–100 m² per birdRoom to run — exercise builds meat and prevents leg problems
Breeding trio1,000–2,500 m² (¼ acre+) per trioPrivacy between camps; males fight through fences

Lesson 2.2 — Fencing that holds a two-metre bird

  • Height 1.8–2 m, smooth wire or mesh (NO barbed wire — panicking ostriches tear their valuable hides on it, destroying the most profitable product).
  • Strong straining posts; no protruding wire ends or sharp objects anywhere a bird can reach — every scratch is a leather downgrade.
  • Rounded camp corners where possible: frightened ostriches run into corners and pile up, breaking legs and necks.
  • Solid or shade-cloth screens between breeding camps so males can't see and challenge each other constantly.

Lesson 2.3 — Handling without injury (yours or theirs)

  • The kick is FORWARD and downward — never stand directly in front of an adult. Approach from the side or rear.
  • The hood: a dark cloth hood over the head calms an ostrich almost instantly — the standard restraint tool for moving, treating or loading birds; two people, one on the neck, one steering the body.
  • Carry a thorn branch, board or panel as a shield in breeding camps; keep a fence/gate within a few strides at all times, and never work alone.
  • Watch the male's display: puffed wings, red shins/beak, booming call and a kneeling "kantling" display mean he is in breeding mode and dangerous.

Lesson 2.4 — Water, shade and the camp furniture

  • Water: 8–12 L/bird/day, in sturdy troughs that can't be tipped; birds also drink after every feed.
  • Shade over feeding areas (heat stress cuts growth), and a dust-bathing patch for feather condition.
  • Feed troughs raised to chest height so birds don't ingest sand — sand impaction is a classic ostrich killer.
  • Remove ALL foreign objects: ostriches swallow stones, wire, nails, plastic — impaction from swallowed junk kills birds routinely. A clean camp is a survival measure.

MONTH 3 — Nutrition Through the Life Stages ▾

Lesson 3.1 — The ration ladder

StageProteinFibreFeed/day
Chick starter (0–3 months)20–22%Low–moderate0.1 → 1.0 kg rising
Grower (3–6 months)16–18%Moderate1.2–2.0 kg
Finisher (6–14 months)14–16%Higher (lucerne-based)2.0–2.5 kg
Maintenance (off-season adults)12–14%High1.5–2.0 kg + grazing
Breeder (in season)16–18% + calciumModerate2.0–2.5 kg
  • Lucerne (alfalfa) — green, hay or pellet — is the backbone of ostrich feeding worldwide; a mature bird can take much of its intake from good lucerne pasture, slashing costs.

Lesson 3.2 — Feeding rules that prevent deaths

  • Change feeds gradually over 7–10 days — abrupt ration changes cause impaction and gut upsets.
  • Grit/small stones: birds need SOME grit for the gizzard, but must not have access to piles of stones or rubbish — provide controlled grit, not a scrapyard.
  • Never feed mouldy lucerne or damp pellets; never let feed run out then refill (gorging causes impaction).
  • Water beside every feeding point — dry feed without water is an impaction recipe.

Lesson 3.3 — Pasture as the cost lever

  • Establish lucerne or a good mixed pasture in the finisher camps: feed can fall from 100% bought pellets to 40–60% bought, transforming the economics of a 10–14 month grow-out.
  • Rotate camps to protect the pasture and break parasite cycles; rest each camp 4–8 weeks.

Lesson 3.4 — Growth monitoring

  • Weigh or measure monthly: rough targets are ~10 kg at 1 month, 30–40 kg at 3 months, 60–70 kg at 6 months, 90–100 kg at 10–12 months.
  • Leg problems (bent/rotated legs, splayed hocks) come from too-fast growth on high-protein feed with too little exercise plus slippery floors — the fix is fibre, space and grip, not more protein.
✅ Iron-Clad Price Check: ostrich pellets (specialist feed — check availability EARLY), lucerne bales and seed — 3 quotes each; feed availability may decide whether ostriches are viable in your area at all.

MONTH 4 — Chicks: the Make-or-Break Phase ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Why chicks die (and how yours won't)

  • Beginner mortality of 30–50% in the first 3 months is normal; disciplined farms achieve 85–95% survival. The killers, in order: chilling, yolk-sac infection, impaction (sand/objects), leg problems from slippery floors, and stress/overcrowding.
  • Every rule below exists because it kills chicks when broken.

Lesson 4.2 — Brooding protocol

  • Temperature: 30–32°C at chick level in week 1, dropping ~3°C per week to about 20°C by week 4–5; read the chicks (huddling = cold, panting/spread = hot).
  • Floor: NON-SLIP always — rubber matting, hessian or rough concrete; smooth surfaces cause splayed legs within days and those chicks never recover.
  • Space: 1–2 m² per chick indoors with access to a sunny run for exercise from day 3–5 — sunlight and exercise build straight legs.
  • Hygiene: disinfect the brooder before arrival, dry bedding daily, no visitors; the navel/yolk sac is an open wound for the first days.

Lesson 4.3 — Feeding chicks

  • Chicks live on the absorbed yolk sac for the first 3–5 days — offer water and starter, but don't panic at low intake; force-feeding causes more harm than hunger does.
  • Then: chick starter (20–22% CP) plus finely chopped fresh lucerne/greens which teach pecking; older chicks copy — a few older chicks or a "teacher" bird boosts intake.
  • Absolutely no access to sand, gravel, wire, string or plastic — chicks peck at everything and impact easily.

Lesson 4.4 — Chick health watchlist

(3) Keep a daily chick register (numbers, deaths with suspected cause, weights weekly) — this becomes your survival-rate KPI.

MONTH 5 — Breeding, Eggs & Incubation ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Breeding pairs and trios

  • Compatibility matters: ostriches choose. An incompatible trio simply doesn't produce — watch for the male's display being accepted (the female drops her wings and squats) rather than fled from; swap birds if no laying starts.
  • Season: typically 6–8 months (spring into late summer in each hemisphere); the male's shins and beak turn bright red when in condition.
  • A productive female lays every second day in bouts — 40–80 eggs a season; collect eggs at least twice daily.

Lesson 5.2 — Egg handling (where hatch rates are won)

  • Collect with clean hands, mark with date and camp, and NEVER wash a dirty egg in cold water (it draws bacteria through the shell) — dry-clean gently with fine abrasive paper or a lightly damp cloth in warm disinfectant per hatchery advice.
  • Store pointed end DOWN at 15–18°C, turning daily, for no more than 7 days before setting — hatchability falls sharply after that.
  • Never keep cracked or badly soiled eggs — they explode in the incubator and contaminate every other egg.

Lesson 5.3 — Incubation

  • 42 days at roughly 36.0–36.5°C, 20–30% relative humidity (drier than chicken eggs), turning at least 4–8× daily.
  • Target 13–16% weight loss across incubation — weigh eggs and adjust humidity; too little loss drowns chicks, too much dehydrates them.
  • Candle at day 14 and day 28: remove clear (infertile) and dead-germ eggs. Move to the hatcher around day 39 with humidity raised.
  • Natural incubation (letting the trio sit) is possible and free but yields far fewer chicks than machine incubation with egg collection.

Lesson 5.4 — Hatching

  • Hatching takes up to 48 hours from first pip — assist only if a chick is clearly stuck beyond that and only with hatchery guidance; premature "help" kills chicks by rupturing unabsorbed blood vessels.
  • Leave chicks in the hatcher until dry and active (6–24 h), then to the pre-heated brooder; disinfect navels.
  • Record every egg: set date, candling result, hatch outcome — hatchability (chicks ÷ fertile eggs) is your breeding KPI, with 60–80% being a strong result.

(2) Visit or arrange access to an incubator; log its temperature/humidity settings and turning regime. (3) Create your egg register template with candling and hatch columns.

MONTH 6 — Slaughter, Products, Economics & Your Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Getting birds to market

  • Slaughter at 10–14 months (roughly 90–110 kg) — the window where hide quality peaks; older birds develop thicker, less desirable skin and cost more feed for little gain.
  • Slaughter must occur at a licensed/certified abattoir in almost all jurisdictions, especially for meat sold commercially or for export.
  • Transport: hood and load calmly, low density, night/cool travel, no sharp edges in the vehicle — a panicked transport ruins hides worth hundreds of dollars.

Lesson 6.2 — The three products in detail

  • Hide: graded on the crown (quill-follicle area) and on the absence of scratches, scars and holes. Every thorn, barb and fight is money lost — which is why Month 2's fencing rules exist. Sold to tanneries; premium hides feed luxury goods.
  • Meat: fillets and steaks from the thigh/drum (ostrich has no breast meat worth selling — the value is in the legs). Sold to restaurants, lodges, butcheries and export processors; biltong/jerky is the value-add for local markets.
  • Feathers: harvested by clipping/plucking mature birds on schedule (a live-bird income) and at slaughter; graded by type — plumes for fashion, body feathers for dusters.
  • Eggshells: blown, cleaned and carved/painted for the craft and tourist trade — a small business inside the business.

Lesson 6.4 — Risk and scale

  • Risks to plan for: chick mortality (the biggest), market/processor dependency, avian influenza controls (movement bans can freeze sales), theft, and injury to staff.
  • Scaling ladder: 20 grow-out birds under contract → own breeding trio(s) + incubator → 100+ birds with staff → agri-tourism/eggshell/biltong lines for cash flow between slaughter batches.

Lesson 6.5 — Final project: your bankable business plan

  • Assemble: permits and legal findings in writing, camp/fencing plan and costs, feed strategy with pasture, chick-rearing protocol and your survival-rate target, breeding/incubation plan, the signed or written buyer interest, full costings, risk plan and a 3-year ladder.
  • Submit with photos and records for your rAs certificate — for ostriches this plan is genuinely essential: no bank or partner funds this enterprise on enthusiasm.

📖 Glossary

  • Ratite — flightless bird group including ostrich, emu, rhea.
  • Trio — one male with two females, the standard breeding unit.
  • Kantling — the male's kneeling wing-waving breeding display.
  • Crown — the quill-follicle area of the hide that carries the value.
  • Hooding — covering the head to calm a bird for handling.
  • Candling — lighting an egg to check development.
  • Hatchability — chicks hatched ÷ fertile eggs set.
  • Impaction — blocked gut from swallowed sand/objects; a major killer.
  • Splay leg — chick leg deformity from slippery floors.
  • Plume — the premium fashion feather.
