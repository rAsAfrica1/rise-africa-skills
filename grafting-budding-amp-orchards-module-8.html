1. When and how to remove grafting tape without damaging the graft

Grafting tape holds the scion and rootstock tightly together so the cambium layers can fuse. But if you leave it on too long, it can girdle the tree, cutting off water and nutrients. In most African conditions, remove the tape once the graft has taken and new growth is 1015 cm long. This usually happens 48 weeks after grafting, depending on the species and season. For mango, wait 68 weeks; for citrus, 46 weeks; for avocado, 810 weeks. Check weekly by looking for green shoots emerging from the scion. If the scion is still green and plump, the graft is alive. If it shrivels or turns brown, it failedremove the tape anyway and regraft later.

To remove tape, use a sharp, clean knife or razor blade. Do not pull the tape offyou might tear the tender callus tissue. Instead, cut the tape lengthwise along the side of the graft, away from the bud or shoot. For plastic tape, make a single cut and peel it back gently. For biodegradable tape, you can leave it if it breaks down within 3 months, but check regularly. If the tape is stuck to the bark, moisten it with water for 10 minutes before cutting.

After removal, inspect the graft union. It should look like a smooth, slightly swollen seam. If you see any constriction marks, the tape was left too longthe tree may recover, but watch for weak growth. Do not remove tape on a windy day; the graft is fragile. If you must remove tape early because of girdling, do it in the cool of the evening and provide shade for a few days.

In dry areas like the Sahel, tape can dry and crack, falling off on its own. In humid areas like the Congo Basin, tape may rot and attract fungus. After removal, spray the graft union with a copper-based fungicide (e.g., copper oxychloride at 3 g per litre) to prevent infection. Always sterilise your knife with methylated spirits or fire between trees to avoid spreading disease.

2. Managing water and shade for newly grafted trees

New grafts are like newborn babiesthey need consistent moisture and protection from harsh sun. For the first 2 weeks after grafting, water the rootstock every 23 days if there is no rain. Give 510 litres per tree, depending on soil type. Sandy soils dry out faster, so water daily in hot weather. Clay soils hold water, so every 34 days is enough. Never let the soil dry out completely, but do not waterlog itroots need air. A simple test: dig 10 cm down; if the soil feels like a wrung-out cloth, it's perfect. If it's bone dry, water. If it's soggy, wait.

Shade is critical. Direct sun can cook a tender graft. In open fields, build a shade structure using palm fronds, banana leaves, or shade net (50% shade). Place it on the sunny side, about 50 cm above the graft. For a single tree, a simple tripod of sticks with a thatch roof works. In agroforestry systems, plant living shade like pigeon pea or moringa 12 metres away, but prune them so they don't compete for water. Remove shade gradually over 34 weeks once new leaves are firm and dark green. Sudden exposure can scorch them.

Mulching is your best friend. Spread a 510 cm layer of dry grass, straw, or maize stover around the base, but keep it 5 cm away from the trunk to prevent rot. Mulch reduces evaporation by up to 70% and keeps soil temperature stable. In very hot areas, water in the early morning or late evening to reduce loss. If you have drip irrigation, run it for 30 minutes daily. Otherwise, use a watering can or bucket.

During the rainy season, ensure good drainagedig a shallow channel if water pools. Too much water causes root rot, which kills grafts faster than drought. After 46 weeks, when the graft is established, reduce watering to once a week, but increase amount to 1520 litres. Always check the graft union for signs of stress: wilting leaves, brown tips, or cracks. Adjust water and shade accordingly.

3. Fertilizing young grafts with locally available organic matter

Young grafts need gentle, balanced nutritionnot a heavy dose of chemical fertilizer that can burn tender roots. Start fertilizing 46 weeks after grafting, once new shoots are 1015 cm long. Use well-rotted manure, compost, or kitchen ash. For each tree, apply 23 handfuls (about 200300 g) of compost or aged cow dung. Spread it in a ring 1520 cm from the trunk, then cover with a thin layer of soil. Do not pile it against the stemit invites termites and rot.

If you have poultry manure, use lessone handful (100 g) per tree, because it is strong. Mix it with wood ash (a source of potassium) at a ratio of 3:1. For phosphorus, crush bones from your kitchen, burn them, and grind into powder. Add one tablespoon (10 g) per tree. For potassium, banana peels and wood ash are excellent. Soak banana peels in water for a week, then pour the liquid around the tree. This 'banana tea' also adds minor nutrients.

In many African soils, nitrogen is low. Plant leguminous cover crops like cowpea, groundnut, or lablab between your trees. They fix nitrogen from the air. When they flower, cut them and leave the residues as mulch. This slowly releases nitrogen. Alternatively, make a liquid fertilizer: fill a sack with fresh animal manure (cow, goat, or chicken), tie it, and soak in a 200-litre drum of water for 2 weeks. Dilute 1 part tea to 3 parts water and apply 2 litres per tree every 2 weeks.

Do not fertilize during the dry season when the tree is dormantwait for the rains. Also, avoid fresh manure; it heats up and burns roots. If leaves are yellow, add a bit more nitrogen (e.g., urine diluted 1:10). If leaves are dark green but few flowers later, reduce nitrogen and add ash. Always water after applying any fertilizer. Keep records of what you apply and whenthis helps you learn what works in your area.

4. Controlling pests and diseases on new grafts

New grafts are vulnerable to pests and diseases because their tissues are soft and wounds are open. Scout your trees twice a week. Look for holes in leaves, sticky sap, curled shoots, or powdery spots. Common pests include aphids, whiteflies, mealybugs, and termites. Aphids cluster on new shoots and suck sap. Spray with a soap solution: mix 1 tablespoon of liquid soap in 1 litre of water, add a pinch of chilli powder, and spray in the evening. Repeat every 3 days until gone. For whiteflies, yellow sticky traps workpaint a piece of cardboard yellow, coat with grease, and hang near the tree.

Termites are a major threat in dry savannahs. They attack the graft union and roots. To prevent, keep the area around the tree clean of dead wood. Apply wood ash in a ring around the base. If you see termite mounds nearby, destroy them or pour a mixture of Neem oil and water (10 ml per litre) into the mound. For active infestations, use a bait of cardboard soaked in insecticide (e.g., fipronil) placed near the mound, but handle chemicals carefully.

Diseases like powdery mildew, anthracnose, and bacterial wilt often appear in humid conditions. Powdery mildew looks like white flour on leaves. Spray with a baking soda solution: 1 teaspoon baking soda, 1 litre water, a few drops of soap. For anthracnose (dark sunken spots on leaves and stems), remove infected parts and burn them. Spray copper fungicide (3 g per litre) every 10 days. Bacterial wilt causes sudden wiltingthere is no cure, so remove and burn the tree to save others.

Prevention is better than cure. Keep tools clean. Avoid overhead watering, which spreads spores. Plant resistant rootstocks if available. Encourage natural enemies like ladybirds and spiders by planting flowers such as marigolds and sunflowers nearby. If you must use chemical pesticides, choose those with low toxicity, like neem-based products, and follow label instructions. Never spray during flowering to protect bees.

5. Training the new shoot into a strong tree structure

Once your graft has produced a strong shoot (3040 cm tall), you need to train it into a tree that will bear heavy crops without breaking. The goal is a single, straight trunk with well-spaced branches. Start by removing any shoots that grow from the rootstock below the graft unionthese are suckers and will steal nutrients. Cut them cleanly with a sharp knife when they are small (less than 10 cm). Check weekly.

If the main shoot is growing sideways, tie it to a straight stick (bamboo or eucalyptus) using soft cloth or banana fibre. Do not use wireit cuts the bark. Loosen the tie every month as the stem thickens. For fruit trees like mango, avocado, and citrus, aim for a central leader (one main stem) with 35 side branches. When the main stem reaches 6080 cm, pinch off the tip to encourage branching. This is called 'heading'. Choose side branches that are spaced 1520 cm apart and point outward at a wide angle (4560 degrees). Remove branches that grow inward or rub against others.

In the first year, remove flowers and fruits. This feels wasteful, but it lets the tree put energy into roots and branches. For example, pluck off all mango flowers in the first season. In the second year, allow a few fruits to test, but still remove most. By the third year, you can let it bear normally. For grafted trees, the rootstock may send up vigorous shootsremove them promptly, or they will dominate.

Prune during the dry season when growth is slow, using clean, sharp secateurs. Make cuts at a slight angle, just above a bud, facing outward. Seal large cuts (bigger than 2 cm) with pruning paint or mud to prevent disease. Train your tree to a open centre or vase shape for easy harvesting and good air flow. This reduces fungal diseases. Always step back and look at the tree from all sidesimagine a bird flying through it. If branches block the centre, remove them. A well-trained tree lives longer and yields more.

6. Hands-on: Inspecting and caring for your grafts after 2 weeks

Two weeks after grafting, it's time for a thorough inspection. Grab your notebook, a sharp knife, and a roll of grafting tape in case you need to rewrap. Walk to your tree in the morning when it's cool. First, look at the scion. Is it still green and plump? If yes, the graft is likely alive. If it's shrivelled, brown, or black, it has failed. Use your fingernail to gently scratch the bark of the scionif you see green underneath, it's alive. If it's brown and dry, remove the tape and cut the scion off cleanly. You can regraft on the same rootstock if it's healthy.

Next, check the graft union. Gently feel for swellingthat's callus tissue forming. Do not poke or prod too much. Look for any signs of rot, mould, or insect damage. If you see white cottony masses, that's mealybugs. Dab them with a cotton bud dipped in methylated spirits. If the union looks dry and cracked, wrap a new layer of grafting tape around it, but not too tight. Water the tree if the soil is dry. If it's been raining heavily, check for waterlogged soil and improve drainage.

Remove any shoots growing from the rootstock below the graft. These are easy to spotthey grow fast and have different leaves. Cut them off at the base. Also remove any weeds within 30 cm of the trunk; they compete for water and nutrients. Apply a handful of compost or well-rotted manure in a ring around the tree, then water it in. If you have shade netting, make sure it's still in place and not touching the leaves.

Record everything in your notebook: date, scion condition, union appearance, pests seen, actions taken. Take a photo if you can. This record helps you track progress and spot problems early. If the graft has taken, you'll see new leaves unfolding in the next week. If not, plan to regraft in the next season. Remember, aftercare is the difference between a successful graft and a dead stick. Keep observing weekly until the shoot is at least 30 cm tall.

<h2>Practical Reference: Aftercare of Grafts and Buds</h2>

<p><strong>Business case:</strong> A graft is a capital investment. A single successful mango, avocado, or citrus graft can produce fruit worth 1040 the cost of the rootstock and scion within 35 years. But 3060% of grafts fail in the first 6 weeks not because the technique was wrong, but because aftercare was neglected. This lesson gives you the field protocols to protect that investment, reduce nursery losses, and bring trees into production faster. Every action below is designed to increase survival rate, shorten the juvenile phase, and lower your cost per established tree.</p>

<h3>1. When and How to Remove Grafting Tape Without Damaging the Graft</h3>

<p>Grafting tape, parafilm, or polythene strips hold the cambium layers in contact and prevent moisture loss. But if left too long, they girdle the growing union, trap moisture that breeds rot, and distort the new shoot. The timing depends on the species and the union's healing speed.</p>

<p><strong>Rule of thumb:</strong> Remove tape when the union has callused and the new shoot has produced 23 true leaves typically 36 weeks after grafting for mango, avocado, and citrus; 24 weeks for passion fruit and guava; 48 weeks for cashew and macadamia. In hot, dry African conditions, healing is faster but dehydration risk is higher, so remove in the early morning or late afternoon.</p>

<p><strong>Technique:</strong> Use a sharp, clean blade or fingernail to slit the tape lengthwise away from the union never pull it across the graft. If the tape sticks, moisten it with clean water and wait 5 minutes. Cut in one direction, then peel back gently. Inspect the union: it should be green-white and firm, not brown or mushy. If you see rot, remove the tape, clean the wound with a 1% bleach solution, and allow it to dry.</p>

<h3>2. Managing Water and Shade for Newly Grafted Trees</h3>

<p>New grafts have no functional root-to-shoot connection until the union heals. Water stress kills callus formation; excess water causes rot. The target is consistent moisture not wet, not dry.</p>

<p><strong>Water:</strong> Irrigate lightly every 23 days for the first 2 weeks, then every 35 days. In sandy soils, water more frequently; in clay, less. A simple test: squeeze a handful of soil from 10 cm depth it should hold together but not drip. If it crumbles, water. If it drips, stop. In drylands (Sahel, Kalahari, Horn of Africa), use a 2-litre bottle with a small hole in the cap as a drip irrigator per tree.</p>

<p><strong>Shade:</strong> Direct sun can cook a new graft. Use 5070% shade cloth, palm fronds, or a simple stick-and-grass shelter for the first 34 weeks. Remove shade gradually 2 hours more sun per day over 7 days to harden the shoot. In East Africa, many nurseries use banana leaves as temporary shade; in West Africa, woven raffia mats work well.</p>

<h3>3. Fertilizing Young Grafts with Locally Available Organic Matter</h3>

<p>Do not fertilize at the moment of grafting the tree is healing, not growing. Wait until the new shoot has 46 leaves (usually 46 weeks). Then feed gently. Synthetic fertilizers can burn new roots; organic matter is safer and often free.</p>

<p><strong>Local options:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Well-rotted cattle or goat manure:</strong> 23 kg per tree, mixed into the top 10 cm of soil, 15 cm from the trunk.</li>
<li><strong>Compost:</strong> 35 kg per tree, applied as a mulch ring.</li>
<li><strong>Poultry litter:</strong> 0.51 kg per tree stronger, so use less and keep away from the stem.</li>
<li><strong>Wood ash:</strong> A handful per tree in acidic soils; provides potassium and calcium.</li>
<li><strong>Legume biomass:</strong> Tithonia, pigeon pea, or lablab leaves chop and bury 1 kg per tree.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Timing:</strong> Apply at 6 weeks, then every 810 weeks during the first year. Always water after applying. In dry season, reduce quantity but keep frequency.</p>

<h3>4. Controlling Pests and Diseases on New Grafts</h3>

<p>New grafts are vulnerable because the wound is open and the shoot is tender. The main threats in Africa are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Termites:</strong> Attack the rootstock and union. Control: keep soil dry around the base, apply wood ash or neem cake, remove dead wood.</li>
<li><strong>Aphids and whiteflies:</strong> Suck sap and transmit viruses. Control: neem oil spray (5 ml/L), soap solution, or introduce ladybirds.</li>
<li><strong>Fungal rot at union:</strong> Caused by excess moisture. Control: improve drainage, remove tape on time, dust with wood ash.</li>
<li><strong>Borer beetles:</strong> Enter through the graft wound. Control: keep the area clean, paint the trunk with a clay-and-manure slurry.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Preventive routine:</strong> Inspect grafts every 3 days for the first month. Remove any diseased tissue immediately. Keep the nursery floor free of weeds and debris. Avoid overhead watering that wets the union.</p>

<strong style="color:#c62828;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong> Never use unregistered or banned pesticides on food crops. In many African countries, products like monocrotophos and methamidophos are illegal for fruit trees. Always wear gloves, a mask, and long sleeves when handling any agrochemical. Store chemicals away from children and livestock. If you use neem oil or soap spray, test on one leaf first some plants burn. Wash hands after handling manure to avoid parasitic infections.

<h3>5. Training the New Shoot into a Strong Tree Structure</h3>

<p>A grafted tree's future shape is decided in the first 6 months. The goal is a single strong leader with 34 well-spaced branches not a bushy mess.</p>

<p><strong>Steps:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>When the new shoot reaches 3040 cm, remove any side shoots below it.</li>
<li>Allow the leader to grow to 6080 cm, then pinch the tip to encourage branching.</li>
<li>Select 34 branches evenly spaced around the trunk, at 4560 angles.</li>
<li>Remove all other shoots. Tie the leader to a stake if it bends.</li>
<li>Repeat pinching every 30 cm of new growth until the tree has a clear framework.</li>
</ol>

<p><strong>Business note:</strong> A well-trained tree produces fruit 12 years earlier and yields 2030% more per tree over its life. That is direct profit.</p>

<h3>6. Hands-On: Inspecting and Caring for Your Grafts After 2 Weeks</h3>

<p>At 2 weeks, you will know if the graft has taken. Here is the field protocol:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Check the union:</strong> Green and firm = success. Brown, mushy, or black = failure. Remove failed grafts immediately to prevent disease spread.</li>
<li><strong>Check the tape:</strong> If the shoot has 23 leaves, slit and remove tape. If not, wait one more week.</li>
<li><strong>Check moisture:</strong> Soil should be moist at 10 cm. Water if dry.</li>
<li><strong>Check shade:</strong> If leaves are scorched, increase shade. If pale and stretched, reduce shade.</li>
<li><strong>Check pests:</strong> Look for ants, termites, aphids, or holes. Treat with neem or soap spray.</li>
<li><strong>Record:</strong> Write date, graft type, survival, and any action taken. This data drives your business decisions.</li>
</ol>
