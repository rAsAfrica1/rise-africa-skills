Lessons

1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Gravity concentration is the step where you separate heavy gold particles from lighter sand, rock, and dirt using water and motion. Before you smelt, you want your material as rich in gold as possible. Smelting raw ore is wasteful: you burn extra fuel, create more slag, and lose gold in the process. By concentrating first, you increase the gold content, sometimes from a few grams per tonne to hundreds of grams per tonne. This makes smelting faster, cheaper, and cleaner.

In Africa, many small-scale miners already use simple gravity methods like panning and sluicing. But if you rush or do it wrong, you leave gold behind. For example, fine gold particles smaller than 0.5 mm can float away if you use too much water or don't classify your material properly. Studies in Ghana and Tanzania show that proper gravity concentration can recover over 90% of gold, while poor methods recover less than 50%.

Gravity concentration works because gold is about 19 times denser than water and 6-7 times denser than common rock. When you swirl a pan or run a sluice, lighter materials wash away while gold settles. The key is to use the right equipment, classify your material into similar sizes, and control water flow. You don't need expensive machines. A simple pan, a sluice box, or a spiral concentrator can do the job if you understand the principles.

Before you invest in a furnace or smelting chemicals, spend time on gravity concentration. It saves money on fuel and chemicals, reduces health risks from fumes, and gives you a cleaner gold button. In this module, you will learn the tools, step-by-step methods, common mistakes, safety, and a practical exercise to master gravity concentration. This knowledge will make your smelting operation more profitable and sustainable.

2. Tools and Materials

To do gravity concentration, you need a few basic tools. The most common is a gold pan. In Africa, you can buy a plastic or metal pan from mining supply shops for about $10-$20. A 14-inch pan is good for beginners. You also need a bucket or two for carrying water and classified material. A 20-litre bucket works well. For larger operations, a sluice box is better. You can build one with wood or buy a aluminum sluice for $50-$150. It should be at least 1 meter long and 30 cm wide, with riffles (bars) to catch gold.

You will also need a classifier or sieve set. Classifying means separating your material into different size ranges. Gold recovery is best when particles are similar in size. A set of sieves with mesh sizes like 2 mm, 1 mm, and 0.5 mm is ideal. You can make your own with mosquito net or wire mesh. A shovel and a pick are needed to dig and move ore. A small trowel helps with feeding material into the pan or sluice.

Water is essential. You need a steady supply, either from a river, a drum, or a recirculating system. In dry areas, you can use a small pump to recycle water. A hose or pipe helps direct water flow. For cleanup, a snuffer bottle (a plastic bottle with a tube) is used to suck up fine gold. You can make one from a 500 ml bottle and a rubber tube. A magnet is useful to remove black sand (magnetite) from your concentrate.

Finally, safety gear: gloves, boots, and eye protection. Mercury is sometimes used after gravity concentration, but we will not cover that here. For gravity alone, you don't need chemicals. Keep your tools clean and in good condition. A well-maintained pan or sluice will last for years. Always work on a stable surface and avoid losing fine gold by using a catch basin under your equipment.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Dig and crush. Collect ore from your mining site. If it's hard rock, crush it to sand-sized particles (less than 2 mm) using a hammer or stamp mill. For alluvial deposits, you may only need to break up clay. The smaller the particles, the better the gold liberation. Aim for 80% passing a 2 mm sieve.

Step 2: Classify. Use your sieves to separate material into sizes: coarse (2-1 mm), medium (1-0.5 mm), and fine (less than 0.5 mm). Process each size separately. This prevents small gold from being washed away with large rocks. For panning, start with the medium size.

Step 3: Panning. Fill your pan halfway with classified material. Add water and swirl gently. Break up lumps with your fingers. Tilt the pan slightly and let the lighter material wash off. Use a circular motion to let gold settle to the bottom. Repeat until only black sand and gold remain. Then use a snuffer bottle to collect the gold. A good panner can process 20-30 pans per hour.

Step 4: Sluicing. For larger volumes, set up a sluice box at a slight angle (about 5-10 degrees). Feed classified material at the top while water flows through. The riffles catch heavy gold. Check the sluice every 30 minutes. Clean out the riffles into a bucket. Then pan the concentrate to get your gold. A sluice can process 1-2 cubic meters per hour.

Step 5: Clean and upgrade. After panning, you will have a concentrate of gold and black sand. Use a magnet to remove magnetite. Then do a final panning to get a pure gold concentrate. If you have a spiral concentrator, run the concentrate through it for higher purity. The final product should be 70-90% gold. Now it's ready for smelting. Always save your tailings and re-process them; you might recover more gold.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not classifying. Many miners dump all material into a sluice or pan without separating sizes. This causes fine gold to wash away. Fix: Always use sieves to classify into at least three size ranges. Process each separately.

Mistake 2: Too much water flow. In a sluice, if water is too fast, it scours the riffles and carries gold out. Fix: Adjust the slope and water volume. The water should flow smoothly, not violently. A good rule: the water depth should be about twice the height of the riffles.

Mistake 3: Overloading the pan. Putting too much material in a pan makes it hard to separate gold. Fix: Fill the pan only halfway. Work in small batches. It's better to pan 10 small batches than one huge one.

Mistake 4: Using dirty water. Muddy water reduces visibility and makes it hard to see gold. Fix: Let water settle in a drum before use, or use a settling pond. Clean water helps you spot gold.

Mistake 5: Losing fine gold. Fine gold (less than 0.1 mm) can float on water surface tension. Fix: Add a drop of dish soap to break surface tension. Also, use a snuffer bottle and check your tailings.

Mistake 6: Not checking equipment. Worn riffles or cracked pans lose gold. Fix: Inspect your sluice riffles regularly. Replace worn rubber matting. For pans, avoid deep scratches that trap gold.

Mistake 7: Skipping tailings. Many miners throw away tailings that still contain gold. Fix: Re-run tailings through a finer sluice or pan. You can recover an extra 10-20% gold. Always sample your tailings to see if they are worth re-processing.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Gravity concentration is safer than chemical methods, but you still need to be careful. Always wear gloves and boots. Wet rocks and slippery surfaces cause falls. Use eye protection when crushing ore, as flying chips can injure your eyes. If you use a hammer or stamp mill, keep your fingers clear. Never work alone; have a partner nearby in case of accidents.

Water safety is important. If you work near a river, be aware of strong currents and flash floods. In dry areas, don't overexert yourself in the heat. Drink plenty of water and take breaks. If you use a pump, check for electrical safety and fuel spills. Keep children away from your work area.

Best practice for gravity concentration: Work clean. Keep your tools clean and free of oil or grease. Oil makes gold particles float and lose. Use only clean water. After each session, clean your pan, sluice, and buckets. Store your concentrate in a sealed container to prevent loss.

Another best practice is to sample your ore before processing. Take a 10 kg sample, crush it, and pan it to estimate gold content. This tells you if the ore is worth processing. Keep records of your recovery. Weigh your gold concentrate before and after smelting to calculate recovery rate. Aim for over 90% recovery from gravity concentration.

Finally, respect the environment. Don't dump tailings into rivers. Build a settling pond for muddy water. Reclaim your mining site after use. In many African countries, environmental regulations require this. By following safety and best practices, you protect yourself, your community, and your future profits.

6. Practical Exercise

In this exercise, you will practice gravity concentration on a sample of ore. You will need: 20 kg of ore, a set of sieves (2 mm, 1 mm, 0.5 mm), a gold pan, a bucket, water, a snuffer bottle, a magnet, and a scale. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area.

Step 1: Crush your ore. If it's hard rock, crush it with a hammer until most particles pass a 2 mm sieve. If it's alluvial, break up clay lumps. Weigh your crushed ore and record the weight.

Step 2: Classify. Use your sieves to separate the ore into three size fractions: coarse (2-1 mm), medium (1-0.5 mm), and fine (less than 0.5 mm). Weigh each fraction and record.

Step 3: Pan each fraction separately. Start with the medium fraction. Fill your pan halfway with material, add water, and pan as described in Lesson 3. Collect the gold concentrate in a snuffer bottle. Repeat for the coarse and fine fractions. For the fine fraction, add a drop of dish soap to the water to prevent floating gold.

Step 4: Clean and weigh. Combine all concentrates. Use a magnet to remove black sand. Weigh your final concentrate. Calculate your recovery: (weight of concentrate / weight of original ore) x 100. For example, if you started with 20 kg and got 2 g of concentrate, your recovery is 0.01% (which is low; good ore might give 0.1% or more).

Step 5: Analyze and improve. If your recovery is low, check for mistakes: Did you classify properly? Was water flow too fast? Did you lose fine gold? Re-process your tailings to see if you missed gold. Repeat the exercise with adjustments. Aim to improve your recovery by 10% each time.

Record your results in a notebook. Note the ore type, weights, and recovery. This data helps you plan for smelting. After this exercise, you will be ready to smelt your concentrate. Remember, practice makes perfect. The more you pan, the better you get.

Practical Reference: Gravity Concentration Before Smelting

Gravity concentration is the bridge between raw gold-bearing ore and a clean, high-grade smelt feed. For small and medium-scale African operators, this step determines whether you pour a profitable button of gold or burn money on flux, fuel, and lost metal. Done well, gravity concentration upgrades low-grade ore into a concentrate that smelts faster, cleaner, and at lower cost. â  SAFETY WARNING â Mercury and Dust

Never add mercury to your gravity concentrate to "catch" fine gold. Mercury is a neurotoxin that contaminates your soil, water, and lungs, and it destroys the market value of your gold. Gravity concentration is a mercury-free method â keep it that way. Also, always wear a P2/N95 respirator when handling dry concentrate or crushing ore, and work in a ventilated area. Silica dust causes silicosis, an irreversible lung disease common among African small-scale miners. Wet processing reduces dust dramatically.

Step-by-Step Practical Method

  1. Crush and grind to liberation size. Gold must be physically freed from the host rock. For most African hard-rock ores, grind to 0.5â2 mm for sluice work, or finer (0.1â0.5 mm) for tables and centrifuges. Test by panning a sample â if you see locked gold in rock fragments, grind finer.
  2. Classify the feed. Screen out fines and coarse material separately. Wide size ranges kill gravity efficiency because fine gold washes away while coarse gold sits in the riffles.
  3. Set water flow correctly. Too fast and gold washes out; too slow and the bed clogs with light material. Aim for a steady, even flow that moves gangue but lets gold settle.
  4. Run a controlled feed rate. Overloading is the number-one cause of gold loss. Feed slowly and consistently â it is better to process less ore well than more ore badly.
  5. Clean up regularly. Stop and clean the concentrate every 30â60 minutes, or after every 200â500 kg of feed. Do not let concentrate build up and get re-washed.
  6. Pan the concentrate to a final heavy fraction. This is your smelt feed. Dry it, weigh it, and record the weight against the ore tonnage processed.
  7. Assay or estimate grade. If you have access to a lab, assay the concentrate. If not, use a fire assay on a small sample or a proven local benchmark to estimate recovery.
  8. Store and label. Keep concentrate in sealed, labelled containers away from moisture and contamination before smelting.

Common Mistakes and Fixes

  • Feeding too fast: Gold rides over the riffles. Fix: reduce feed rate and increase residence time.
  • Too much water: Fine gold flushes out. Fix: slow the flow, add a settling pond, and re-run tailings.
  • Wrong riffle spacing: Gold passes through. Fix: match riffle size to your gold particle size; use finer riffles for finer gold.
  • Not classifying feed: Mixed sizes reduce recovery. Fix: screen into at least two size fractions.
  • Skipping concentrate cleanup: Gold gets re-suspended. Fix: clean up on a strict schedule.
  • Using mercury: Health, legal, and market disaster. Fix: never use it â improve gravity instead.
