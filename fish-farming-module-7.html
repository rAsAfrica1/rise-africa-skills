1. Daily Routine Tasks and Record Keeping

Start your day at the pond just after sunrise. Walk the perimeter and look for dead fish, broken dykes, or leaks. Count any dead fish and remove them immediately to prevent disease spread. Check water clarity using a Secchi disk (a simple white disk on a string). In a healthy pond, you should see it down to 3040 cm. If it disappears before 20 cm, the water is too muddy or too rich in algae. Test water temperature with a simple thermometer: tilapia feed best between 25C and 30C. If it's below 20C, skip feeding that day. Test dissolved oxygen with a test kit (available at most agrovet shops) if it's below 3 mg/L early morning, fish may gasp at the surface. That means you need to aerate or exchange water.

Feed your fish at the same time each morning and afternoon. Use floating pellets so you can see if they eat. Feed only what they finish in 15 minutes. Overfeeding wastes money and fouls the water. For a 100 m pond stocked with 200 tilapia, start with 1 kg of feed per day, split into two meals. Adjust based on how much they eat. Record everything in a notebook or on a simple chart: date, time, water temp, DO, feed amount, dead fish count, and any unusual behaviour. This record helps you spot trends like a drop in feeding that signals disease.

After feeding, check the inlet and outlet screens for clogging. Remove debris, leaves, or mud. Walk the dykes and look for cracks, crab holes, or seepage. Repair small leaks immediately with clay soil. If you have a pump or aerator, start it and listen for unusual noise. Check oil levels and fuel. Clean the pump intake screen.

In the afternoon, repeat the feeding and observation. Remove any dead fish. If you see fish swimming erratically or rubbing against the pond bottom, they may have parasites. Note it in your record. At sunset, do a final check: ensure the pump is off if not needed overnight, and secure any equipment. Lock your feed store to prevent theft or spoilage. A daily routine takes 3045 minutes but saves you from disasters. Keep records for at least one year they are your best tool for improving yields and proving your farm's performance to buyers or lenders.

2. Pond Maintenance and Repair

A well-maintained pond holds water, keeps fish healthy, and lasts for years. Inspect your pond walls (dykes) every week. Look for cracks, holes from crabs or rodents, and areas where water seeps out. A small leak can become a major breach in heavy rain. To repair a leak, drain the pond partially if possible. Dig out the soft, wet soil around the leak until you reach firm ground. Fill the hole with good clay soil, compact it in layers of 15 cm, and water it lightly to help it seal. If the leak is in the dyke slope, pack clay and cover with a layer of topsoil and grass to prevent erosion.

Erosion is a common problem, especially on dykes with bare soil. Plant grass like Napier or local creeping grasses on the dykes. Their roots hold the soil. Do not allow livestock to graze on the dykes their hooves damage the structure. If you have a concrete pond, check for cracks. Clean the cracks, apply a concrete patch mix (1 part cement, 2 parts sand, 3 parts gravel), and let it cure for 3 days before refilling.

Pond bottom maintenance: after each harvest, drain the pond completely and let the bottom dry for 12 weeks until it cracks. This kills parasites and disease organisms. Remove the top 5 cm of sludge (fish waste, uneaten feed) and use it as fertiliser in your garden. If you cannot drain fully, use a pump to remove as much water as possible, then spread lime (calcium carbonate) at 200300 kg per hectare to disinfect. Wait one week before refilling.

Inlet and outlet structures: clean screens weekly. Repair broken pipes or channels with PVC cement or clay. Ensure the overflow pipe is clear so excess rainwater can escape without washing away fish. If you have a monk or standpipe, check that the boards are tight and can be adjusted to control water level. For earthen ponds, keep the water level at least 30 cm below the dyke top to prevent overflow during rains.

Finally, control vegetation. Remove excess aquatic plants like water hyacinth manually or by introducing grass carp (if allowed). Too many plants reduce oxygen at night. Keep the pond area free of tall bushes that harbour predators like snakes and birds. A monthly maintenance day with your family or workers will keep your pond in top shape and save you money on major repairs.

3. Equipment Maintenance: Pumps, Aerators, Nets

Your equipment is your investment maintain it or replace it often. Start with pumps. Most small farms use a 13 HP centrifugal pump. After every use, flush the pump with clean water to remove mud and debris. Check the impeller for wear or blockage. Remove the pump cover and clean the impeller with a brush. Check the seals and O-rings if they leak, replace them. Lubricate the bearings every 50 hours of operation with grease recommended by the manufacturer. For petrol or diesel pumps, change the oil every 100 hours, clean the air filter every 25 hours, and replace the spark plug yearly. Store the pump in a dry, shaded place. Before storage, drain all fuel and run the engine until it stops to prevent gumming.

Aerators: Paddlewheel or diffused air aerators are critical for high-density ponds. Check the motor and gearbox oil levels weekly. Clean the paddles or air diffusers of algae and debris. For diffused systems, check the air lines for leaks soapy water bubbles show leaks. Replace worn air stones every 6 months. If the aerator makes a grinding noise, stop it immediately and check bearings. Keep a spare aerator or a backup pump if possible, because a power outage at night can kill fish within hours. For solar aerators, clean the solar panels weekly and check battery water levels (for lead-acid) monthly.

Nets: Seine nets, cast nets, and scoop nets need care. After each use, rinse nets in clean water to remove mud and fish slime. Dry them in the shade never in direct sun, which weakens the fibres. Inspect for holes and tears. Repair small holes with a net needle and twine. For large tears, replace the panel. Store nets hanging on hooks in a dry, rodent-free shed. Never leave nets on the ground where they can be chewed by rats or damaged by termites. For cast nets, check the lead line and swivels. Replace worn ropes.

Other tools: Keep buckets, scales, and water test kits clean. Rinse test tubes after each use. Store chemicals like lime and salt in sealed containers away from moisture. Sharpen knives and machetes used for harvesting. Create a maintenance log for each piece of equipment: date, task, and next service date. A simple wall chart works. Spending 15 minutes a week on maintenance prevents 3-hour breakdowns during harvest. Train one worker to be responsible for equipment care and give them a small bonus for zero breakdowns.

4. Harvesting Techniques and Equipment

Harvest at the right time for the best price and least stress. For tilapia, harvest when they reach 250500 g, usually 57 months after stocking. For catfish, 800 g1 kg at 68 months. Check your records if feed conversion has worsened and growth slowed, it's time. Harvest early morning when temperatures are cool and oxygen is higher. Stop feeding 24 hours before harvest to empty their guts, which keeps the water cleaner and fish fresher.

There are three main methods: complete draining, seining, and hook-and-line. Complete draining is best for total harvest. Use a pump or open the outlet to lower water slowly. As water drops, fish concentrate in a small area. Use a seine net to pull them to one corner, then scoop with a dip net into buckets or crates. For partial harvest, use a seine net. Two people pull the net slowly from one end to the other. Keep the bottom of the net on the pond floor to avoid escaping fish. Pull at a steady pace too fast and you'll catch mud and debris. For catfish, use a larger mesh net to avoid gill entanglement. After seining, return undersized fish to the pond for further growth.

Equipment: You need a seine net (mesh size 25 cm depending on fish size), a dip net, plastic crates or buckets (2030 L), a weighing scale (050 kg), and a tarpaulin or clean mat. For catfish, wear gloves their spines can sting. Use a stunning method: place fish in water with a few drops of clove oil (0.5 ml per 10 L) for 5 minutes to sedate them. This reduces stress and makes handling easier. Never throw fish place them gently.

After harvest, sort fish by size. Keep large ones for market, small ones for restocking or further growth. Weigh and record total harvest and average weight. Clean the pond bottom and repair dykes before restocking. If you sell live fish, keep them in aerated tanks. For fresh fish, kill humanely by a quick blow to the head or by chilling in ice water. Bleed and gut immediately if selling whole fish. Harvesting is hard work organise a team of 34 people and start early. A well-planned harvest takes 23 hours for a 100 m pond and minimises losses.

5. Post-Harvest Handling and Transport

How you handle fish after harvest determines your price. Fish spoil within hours in African heat. Immediately after harvest, sort fish and place them in clean, chilled water or ice. If you don't have ice, use a cool box with wet sand or keep fish in the shade under a wet cloth. For live fish transport, use a tank with aeration. A 100 L tank can hold 2030 kg of tilapia for up to 4 hours if aerated. Add a few drops of stress coat or salt (3 g per L) to reduce stress. Do not feed fish during transport.

For fresh fish, kill humanely. A quick blow to the head with a wooden club stuns them. Then bleed by cutting the gills and placing in ice water for 15 minutes. This removes blood and extends shelf life. Gut the fish: make a cut from the vent to the gills, remove intestines, and rinse the cavity with clean water. Do not use pond water use clean, chlorinated water or well water. If you sell whole fish, leave the head and scales on. For fillets, scale, fillet, and skin the fish, then pack in ice.

Ice is your best friend. Use a ratio of 1 kg ice to 1 kg fish. Crush the ice and layer fish and ice in a cooler box. Never store fish directly on ice use a plastic sheet or bag to prevent ice burn. If you have no ice, use a charcoal cooler: a double-walled box with wet sand between the walls. Evaporation cools the inner chamber. Keep fish in the shade and sell within 4 hours.

Transport: Use a vehicle with a covered bed. For live fish, use a tank with oxygen. For fresh fish, use insulated boxes. Pack fish in single layers to avoid crushing. Do not stack heavy items on top. Transport during the coolest part of the day early morning or evening. Avoid bumpy roads if possible; vibration stresses fish. If you sell at a market, arrive early to get the best spot. Keep fish covered and sprinkle ice as needed.

Hygiene: Wash hands, knives, and surfaces with soap and clean water. Never use dirty containers. A clean product attracts repeat buyers. Record the time of harvest, time of sale, and weight sold. This helps you calculate losses and improve. If you process fish (smoking, drying), do it in a clean, screened area away from flies. Proper post-harvest handling can double your income by reducing spoilage and meeting buyer standards.

6. Farm Safety and Emergency Procedures

Fish farming has real hazards: drowning, electric shock, cuts, and disease. Always work with a partner, especially when near water. Never work alone at the pond, especially if you cannot swim. Wear a life jacket when using a boat or working in deep water. Keep a long pole or rope near the pond for rescue. Teach children to stay away from ponds a child can drown in 30 cm of water.

Electrical safety: Pumps and aerators run on electricity. Use an RCD (residual current device) or earth leakage breaker on all circuits. Check cables for cracks and exposed wires. Never touch electrical equipment with wet hands. Keep electrical panels elevated and dry. If you get a shock, turn off the main switch before touching the person. For petrol pumps, store fuel in a locked, ventilated shed away from the pond. Never refuel a hot engine. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder) near the pump house.

Chemical safety: Lime, salt, and formalin can burn skin and eyes. Wear gloves, goggles, and a mask when handling. Store chemicals in original containers, locked away from children and feed. Never mix chemicals in food containers. If you get lime in your eye, rinse with clean water for 15 minutes and seek medical help. For formalin, use in a well-ventilated area.

Cuts and injuries: Nets, knives, and catfish spines can cause cuts. Wear gloves when handling catfish. Clean any cut immediately with soap and water, apply antiseptic, and cover with a waterproof plaster. If a cut becomes red, swollen, or pus-filled, see a health worker pond bacteria can cause serious infections. Keep a first aid kit at the farm: bandages, antiseptic, painkillers, and a whistle to call for help.

Emergency procedures: If a fish die-off occurs (many fish gasping or dead), act fast. Turn on all aerators. If you have no aerator, use a pump to spray water back into the pond, or exchange 20% of water with fresh water. Stop feeding. If you suspect disease, isolate the pond and consult an extension officer. For fire, turn off fuel sources and use the extinguisher. For a snake bite, keep the victim calm, immobilise the limb, and get to a clinic immediately. For drowning, pull the person out using a pole, check breathing, and start CPR if trained. Call for help. Always have a phone with emergency numbers saved. Review safety rules with your workers every month. A safe farm is a productive farm.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Fish Farm Operations and Maintenance

This practical reference consolidates the daily, weekly, and seasonal tasks that keep a fish farm productive, profitable, and compliant. It is written for farm managers and owner-operators in Africa who need to protect stock, reduce losses, and maintain cash flow. Every operation described here has a direct cost or revenue implication: a blocked drain means lost fish; a worn net means escapees and lost revenue; a failed pump means a missed market window. Use this module as a checklist and a training aid for farm staff.

Before starting, confirm the farm's production system (earthen ponds, concrete tanks, hapa nets, cages, or recirculating systems) and the species being cultured (tilapia, catfish, or other). Operations differ by system, but the principles of observation, record keeping, and preventive maintenance are universal.

1. Daily Routine Tasks and Record Keeping

Start each day with a walk-around at dawn, when dissolved oxygen is lowest and fish behaviour is most telling. Record water temperature, dissolved oxygen (DO), pH, and fish behaviour. Feed according to the feeding chart, but adjust for weather and observed appetite. Remove dead fish immediately and record mortality. Check inlet and outlet screens, embankments, and predators. At the end of the day, reconcile feed used, stock numbers, and any sales.

Records are a business tool, not paperwork. They allow you to calculate feed conversion ratio (FCR), survival rate, and cost per kilogram. Without records, you cannot price fish correctly or prove losses to insurers or lenders. Use a simple notebook or a mobile app; the key is consistency and daily entry.

2. Pond Maintenance and Repair

Inspect embankments weekly for erosion, burrows, and seepage. Repair immediately with compacted soil; a small leak can become a breach. Keep grass short to deter pests and allow inspection. Remove excess silt and organic matter annually to prevent anaerobic conditions. Check inlet and outlet structures for cracks and blockages. In Africa, termites and rodents are common causes of embankment failure; control them with approved methods and regular inspection.

Water quality management is part of maintenance. Test alkalinity and hardness monthly; add agricultural lime if needed. Maintain a healthy phytoplankton bloom (green water) but avoid excessive algae that can crash oxygen at night.

3. Equipment Maintenance: Pumps, Aerators, Nets

Pumps and aerators are the heart of intensive systems. Follow the manufacturer's service schedule: clean impellers, check seals, lubricate bearings, and inspect electrical connections. Keep a spare pump or aerator on site; downtime during a hot afternoon can kill stock within hours. Nets must be inspected after every use for holes and repaired immediately. Store nets dry, away from sunlight and rodents.

Fuel and power costs are significant in Africa. Maintain generators and solar systems to reduce reliance on unreliable grid power. Keep a log of running hours and fuel consumption to detect inefficiencies.

4. Harvesting Techniques and Equipment

Harvest when fish reach market size or when cash flow requires. Partial harvesting extends production and improves cash flow. Use seine nets for ponds, and ensure the mesh size is appropriate to avoid gilling small fish. For catfish, harvest at night or early morning to reduce stress. Always have a backup net and extra hands. Weigh and record every harvest; this data feeds into your FCR and profitability calculations.

In cages and hapas, harvest by lifting and transferring fish to holding tanks with clean, oxygenated water. Avoid rough handling; bruises reduce market value and increase mortality during transport.

5. Post-Harvest Handling and Transport

Fish quality determines price. After harvest, keep fish in clean, cool water or on ice. For live transport, use oxygenated tanks with clean water and avoid overcrowding. For fresh fish, chill immediately to near 0C using ice in insulated boxes. In many African markets, live fish fetch a premium; invest in proper tanks and aeration for live transport. Record weight, destination, and buyer for every consignment.

Transport time and distance matter. Plan routes to avoid delays and extreme heat. Use covered vehicles and check water temperature and oxygen during stops.

6. Farm Safety and Emergency Procedures

Fish farms present risks: drowning, electrocution, cuts, and zoonotic diseases. Never work alone around deep water. Use life jackets on cages and boats. Keep electrical equipment properly grounded and protected from water. Store chemicals and fuels securely. Train staff in first aid and emergency contacts. Have a written emergency plan for power failure, flood, disease outbreak, and fire.

SAFETY WARNING: Never enter a pond or tank alone, and never work near water during electrical storms. Electrocution from faulty aerators or pumps is a leading cause of accidental death on fish farms. Ensure all electrical installations are protected by residual current devices (RCDs) and inspected regularly. In case of power failure, do not attempt to repair live equipment; switch off at the main breaker first. Keep a first-aid kit and emergency numbers posted at the farm office.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Walk the farm at dawn; observe fish behaviour and check for dead fish.
  • Record water temperature, DO, pH, and mortality daily.
  • Feed according to chart; adjust for weather and appetite.
  • Inspect embankments, screens, and nets for damage; repair immediately.
  • Service pumps and aerators weekly; keep spare parts and fuel.
  • Harvest at market size; use correct mesh size and handle fish gently.
  • Chill or oxygenate fish immediately after harvest; record weights and buyers.
  • Follow safety rules: no lone work near water, RCDs on all electricals, first-aid kit ready.
