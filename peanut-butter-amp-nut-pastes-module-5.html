1. Classic Smooth Peanut Butter Recipe

To make classic smooth peanut butter, start with 1 kg of raw shelled peanuts. In Africa, groundnuts are common; use freshly shelled ones. Spread them on a baking tray and roast at 160C for 2025 minutes, stirring every 5 minutes, until golden brown and fragrant. If you don't have an oven, roast in a dry heavy pot over medium heat, stirring constantly for 1015 minutes. Let cool for 10 minutes.

Next, remove skins. Rub peanuts between your palms or in a cloth; discard skins. You should have about 900 g after skinning. Put peanuts into a food processor or heavy-duty blender. Blend for 1 minute, scrape down sides, then blend again. Continue blending in 1-minute bursts until smooth about 57 minutes total. The peanuts will first become crumbs, then a ball, then liquid. Add 1 teaspoon of salt and 12 tablespoons of peanut oil (or any neutral oil) to help the process. For a smoother texture, add up to 4 tablespoons of oil. Blend until completely smooth, about 2 more minutes.

If you want a pourable consistency, add another tablespoon of oil. Taste and adjust salt. For sweetness, add 12 teaspoons of sugar or honey, but classic smooth peanut butter often has no sweetener. Transfer to clean, dry glass jars. Press down to remove air bubbles. Seal tightly. Store in a cool, dark place for up to 3 months, or in the fridge for 6 months. Makes about 800 g.

For a local twist, use groundnut oil instead of neutral oil. In West Africa, many use a pinch of ground ginger or a small dried chili for subtle heat, but that's optional. Always sterilize jars by boiling for 10 minutes and drying. Label with date. This recipe is your base for all variations.

2. Crunchy Peanut Butter with Added Peanut Pieces

Crunchy peanut butter is made exactly like smooth, but you reserve some chopped peanuts to add at the end. Start with 1 kg raw shelled peanuts. Roast at 160C for 2025 minutes, or in a dry pot for 1015 minutes, stirring often. Cool, then skin. Set aside 200 g of the roasted peanuts. Chop these coarsely with a knife or pulse in a blender for 23 seconds you want pieces about the size of rice grains. Do not over-blend into powder.

Take the remaining 800 g peanuts and make smooth peanut butter following the classic recipe: blend in a food processor for 57 minutes, adding 1 teaspoon salt and 24 tablespoons peanut oil until smooth. Once smooth, add the chopped peanuts and pulse 34 times just to mix. Do not blend long, or you'll lose the crunch. Taste and add salt if needed. You can also add 1 teaspoon of honey or sugar if you like a touch of sweetness, but it's optional.

Transfer to jars. Press down gently. The crunch will stay for months if sealed well. Makes about 800 g. For variation, use 150 g chopped peanuts for lighter crunch, or 250 g for extra crunch. In East Africa, some add roasted sesame seeds (50 g) for a different crunch. Store in a cool dark place for 3 months, or fridge for 6 months. Always label with date and 'crunchy'.

Note: If your blender struggles, do it in batches. Never add water it causes mold. Use oil only. For a local flair, add a pinch of chili flakes or ground cinnamon to the chopped peanuts before mixing.

3. Spicy Peanut Butter with Chili and Local Spices

Spicy peanut butter is a popular variation in many African markets. Start with 1 kg raw shelled peanuts. Roast and skin as in the classic recipe. In a dry pan, toast 24 dried red chilies (like bird's eye or cayenne) for 1 minute until fragrant, then remove seeds and crush. You can also use 12 teaspoons of chili powder. For local spices, gather 1 teaspoon ground ginger, 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon, and 1/4 teaspoon ground cloves or nutmeg. Some regions use ground dried shrimp or fish powder for umami, but that's optional.

Blend the 900 g skinned peanuts in a food processor for 57 minutes until smooth, adding 1 teaspoon salt and 24 tablespoons peanut oil. Once smooth, add the crushed chilies and spices. Blend for another 12 minutes to mix well. Taste it should be spicy but balanced. Adjust chili to your preference. For a milder version, use 1 chili; for hot, use 4. You can also add 1 teaspoon of honey or sugar to round out the heat.

Transfer to jars. Store in a cool dark place for 3 months, or fridge for 6 months. Makes about 800 g. In West Africa, this is often called 'groundnut pepper soup' base. In Southern Africa, some add a pinch of smoked paprika. Always label with heat level. For a smoky flavor, roast the chilies with the peanuts for the last 5 minutes.

Safety: Wear gloves when handling chilies, and avoid touching your eyes. If you have sensitive skin, use powder instead. This spicy version is excellent on bread, in stews, or as a dip. Experiment with local spices like grains of paradise or African nutmeg.

4. Chocolate Peanut Butter: Using Cocoa Powder

Chocolate peanut butter is a rich, popular product. Start with 1 kg raw shelled peanuts. Roast at 160C for 2025 minutes, or in a pot for 1015 minutes, then cool and skin. You should have about 900 g. In a bowl, mix 4 tablespoons of unsweetened cocoa powder (not drinking chocolate) with 4 tablespoons of powdered sugar or honey. If using honey, warm it slightly for easier mixing. Set aside.

Blend the peanuts in a food processor for 57 minutes until smooth, adding 1 teaspoon salt and 24 tablespoons peanut oil. Once smooth, add the cocoa-sugar mixture. Blend for 23 minutes until fully incorporated. Taste add more cocoa for darker chocolate, or more sugar for sweeter. For a milk chocolate style, add 2 tablespoons of powdered milk. For a darker, bitter version, reduce sugar to 2 tablespoons. You can also add a pinch of vanilla extract or ground vanilla bean.

Transfer to jars. Press down to remove air. Store in a cool dark place for 2 months (cocoa can go stale faster), or fridge for 4 months. Makes about 800 g. In Africa, local cocoa powder from Ghana or Cte d'Ivoire is excellent. For a unique twist, add 1 teaspoon of ground coffee or a pinch of chili for a Mexican-style chocolate peanut butter.

Note: Cocoa powder absorbs oil, so you may need an extra tablespoon of oil. If the mixture is too thick, add oil slowly. Always label with date. This is great for gifts or value-added products. Experiment with local sweeteners like date syrup or jaggery.

5. Honey Peanut Butter: Using Local Honey

Honey peanut butter uses local honey for natural sweetness and flavor. Start with 1 kg raw shelled peanuts. Roast and skin as usual. You should have about 900 g. In a small bowl, measure 46 tablespoons of raw local honey. If honey is thick, warm it gently in a bowl over hot water do not boil, as it kills beneficial enzymes.

Blend the peanuts in a food processor for 57 minutes until smooth, adding 1 teaspoon salt and 23 tablespoons peanut oil. Once smooth, add the honey. Blend for 12 minutes until fully mixed. Taste add more honey if you like it sweeter. For a runnier consistency, add another tablespoon of oil. For a thicker, spreadable butter, use less oil. You can also add 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon or a pinch of ground ginger for warmth.

Transfer to jars. Store in a cool dark place for 2 months, or fridge for 4 months. Honey can crystallize over time; if it does, place the jar in warm water to liquefy. Makes about 800 g. In Africa, honey from acacia, baobab, or miombo trees gives unique flavors. Always use pure honey, not syrup. Label with date and honey type.

Note: Honey peanut butter is more prone to separation; stir before use. For a vegan version, honey is not vegan, so use date syrup or maple syrup instead. This variation is excellent on pancakes, in smoothies, or as a dip for fruit. Experiment with local honey varieties for signature flavors.

6. Developing Your Own Signature Flavor

Now that you've mastered the basics, it's time to create your own signature flavor. Start with a base of 1 kg roasted, skinned peanuts (about 900 g after skinning). Make smooth peanut butter using 1 teaspoon salt and 24 tablespoons peanut oil. This is your canvas. Think about local ingredients that tell your story: dried fruits like mango, banana, or baobab powder; spices like cloves, cardamom, or grains of paradise; or savory additions like dried tomato, onion powder, or ground dried fish.

Choose one main flavor and one accent. For example, 2 tablespoons of baobab powder for tang, plus 1 teaspoon of ground ginger. Or 3 tablespoons of dried mango powder, plus 1/2 teaspoon of chili. Add these after the peanut butter is smooth, then blend for 12 minutes. Taste and adjust. Keep notes: record exact amounts, roasting time, and oil used. This is your recipe development log. Make small batches first 250 g peanuts to avoid waste.

Consider texture: add 100 g chopped nuts, seeds, or dried fruit for crunch. Consider sweetness: honey, date syrup, or jaggery. Consider heat: chili, black pepper, or ginger. Consider umami: dried shrimp powder, soy sauce powder, or fermented locust bean (dawadawa). Balance is key start with small amounts, taste, and add more. A signature flavor should be unique but not overpowering.

Once you have a winner, scale up to 1 kg batches. Test shelf life: store in a cool dark place and check after 1 week, 1 month, 3 months. If it separates, stir. If it molds, reduce moisture (avoid fresh ingredients). Package in clean jars with a label that includes your brand name, date, and ingredients. In Africa, unique flavors like moringa, hibiscus, or tamarind can set you apart. Sell at local markets, and ask for feedback. Adjust and refine. Your signature flavor is your business identity.

Practical Reference: Product Development and Flavour Variations for Peanut Butter & Nut Pastes

This practical reference is written for African agribusinesses from smallholder cooperatives in Malawi and Senegal to urban food processors in Kenya, Ghana and Nigeria that want to move beyond a single commodity paste into a portfolio of differentiated, higher-margin products. Flavour variation is not a gimmick: it is a pricing strategy. A plain 250 g jar of peanut butter may sell for the equivalent of US$1.502.50, while a branded chocolate or honey variant in attractive packaging can command 3080% more in supermarkets and export markets. This module gives you the technical parameters, safety boundaries and commercial logic to develop six core products and then design your own signature flavour.

SAFETY WARNING Aflatoxin and Allergen Control

Groundnuts are highly susceptible to Aspergillus flavus and A. parasiticus moulds, which produce aflatoxins carcinogenic compounds that are not destroyed by normal roasting or grinding. Never use discoloured, mouldy, shrivelled or insect-damaged nuts. Reject any lot that tests above 10 g/kg total aflatoxin (4 g/kg for export to the EU). Store raw nuts below 10% moisture and at < 25 C with good ventilation. Roasting at 150180 C reduces but does not eliminate aflatoxin prevention at farm and storage level is the only reliable control. Separately, peanut is a major allergen: declare "contains peanuts" on every label, use dedicated equipment, and never repack a non-peanut product on the same line without full clean-down. Failure on either point can cause illness, death or market exclusion.

4. Developing Your Own Signature Flavour

A signature flavour is the fastest route from commodity supplier to brand. The discipline is to work in small, documented batches and to test with real buyers before investing in packaging. A practical sequence:

  1. Choose a local, story-rich ingredient. Baobab (Malawi, Senegal, Zimbabwe), moringa (Kenya, Ghana), coconut (Mozambique, Tanzania), hibiscus (Nigeria, Sudan), coffee (Ethiopia, Uganda, Rwanda), vanilla (Madagascar, Uganda) or local chili varieties all carry origin value that imported flavours cannot.
  2. Fix the base first. Get a smooth, stable 90% peanut base that does not separate. Only then add the flavour component at 210%.
  3. Run 35 pilot batches at 2 kg each, varying the flavour level. Record temperature, mixing time and particle size.
  4. Blind taste test with 2030 target consumers not staff. Score for taste, texture, aroma and purchase intent at a stated price.
  5. Check regulatory fit. Confirm the ingredient is permitted, that you can declare it correctly, and that any health claim you make is allowed by your national standards authority.
  6. Cost and price it. Target a gross margin of at least 3545% on premium SKUs. If the flavour ingredient pushes cost above that, reduce the level or raise the pack price.
  7. Protect it. Register the brand name and, where possible, the recipe trade secret. A signature flavour is intellectual property.

5. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Raw nuts screened for mould, discolouration and insect damage; aflatoxin tested before processing.
  • Roasting profile recorded (temperature and time) and repeated batch to batch for consistent flavour.
  • Base paste ground to a stable, non-separating consistency before any flavour is added.
  • Flavour ingredients measured by weight, not volume, and pre-blended for even dispersion.
  • Moisture and water activity checked; honey and cocoa variants adjusted for added water.
  • Shelf-life trial run for at least 8 weeks at ambient temperature before setting a sell-by date.
  • Allergen declaration ("contains peanuts") and ingredient list on every label, in the local language.
  • Unit cost, pack price and gross margin calculated per SKU before launch; break-even volume known.
