1. Routine Field Scouting and Record Keeping

Walk your sweet potato field at least twice a week, early in the morning when pests are active and dew helps you spot disease. Start scouting one week after planting. Use a simple notebook or a mobile app to record what you see. Divide your field into sectionssay, 10 rows by 10 plantsand check 10 random plants per section. Look for yellowing leaves, holes, wilting, or insects. Common pests in Africa include sweet potato weevils, caterpillars, and aphids. Diseases like sweet potato virus disease cause yellow veins and stunted growth. Record the date, section, problem, and action taken. For example, if you find 5 out of 10 plants with aphids, write 'Aphids on 5 plants, sprayed neem oil 5ml/L.' Keep a monthly summary to track patterns. If you see more than 10% of plants affected, act immediately. Use a hoe to remove severely diseased plants and burn them away from the field. Also note weatherrain, drought, or strong windsbecause it affects pest outbreaks. A simple rain gauge made from a tin can help measure rainfall. At the end of each week, review your notes and plan next steps. This habit prevents small problems from becoming total crop loss. Many farmers lose 30-50% of yield due to late detection. With consistent scouting, you can catch issues early and protect your harvest.

2. Pruning and Vine Management

Sweet potato vines grow fast and can smother each other if left unchecked. Pruning means cutting back excess vines to direct energy into roots. Start when vines are about 30 cm long, usually 4-6 weeks after planting. Use a sharp knife or secateurs. Remove weak, diseased, or overcrowded vines. Leave 3-4 main vines per plant. Cut them back to 20-30 cm from the base. Do not prune more than 30% of the vine at once, or you will stress the plant. In dry areas, prune less; in wet areas, prune more to prevent rot. Vine management also includes lifting vines that have rooted at nodesthese 'daughter plants' compete for nutrients. Gently lift vines every two weeks and turn them back onto the ridge. This is called 'vine turning' and prevents rooting. In Africa, many farmers practice vine cutting for livestock feed. You can cut up to 20% of vines for fodder without reducing yield, as long as you leave the main stems. After pruning, apply a light dose of fertilizerone matchbox of NPK 15-15-15 per plantto help recovery. Keep records of pruning dates and amounts. Pruning improves air flow, reduces disease, and increases marketable roots by 15-25%. Do not prune after the plants start flowering, as this can reduce root bulking. Always prune in the morning when it's cool, and avoid pruning during flowering or drought stress.

3. Hilling and Ridge Maintenance

Ridges are the backbone of sweet potato farming. They keep roots from rotting and allow easy harvesting. Over time, rain and foot traffic flatten ridges. You must rebuild them every 3-4 weeks. Use a hoe to pull soil up from the furrows onto the ridge. Aim for ridges 30-40 cm high and 60-80 cm wide. This is called 'hilling' or 'earthing up'. Do it when the soil is moist but not waterlogged. Hilling covers exposed roots and prevents greening, which makes roots bitter. It also controls weeds. In sandy soils, ridges collapse faster, so check after every heavy rain. In clay soils, ridges hold shape longer but can waterlogmake furrows deeper. For large fields, use a tractor-drawn ridger, but most smallholders use hand hoes. A good ridge has a flat top, not pointed, so vines can spread. If you see cracks in the ridge, fill them with soil to stop weevil entry. Weevils lay eggs in cracks. Also, repair ridges after harvesting any early roots. In Africa, some farmers use 'flat planting' instead of ridges, but ridges give 20-30% higher yield and better root shape. Hilling is hard workdo it in the cool of the day. One person can hill about 0.1 hectare per day. After hilling, apply mulch like dry grass to conserve moisture and suppress weeds. Keep records of hilling dates and ridge condition. Well-maintained ridges mean bigger, straighter roots.

4. Detecting Nutrient Deficiencies Early

Nutrient deficiencies show up in leaves before they affect roots. Learn to read the signs. Nitrogen deficiency: older leaves turn yellow, starting from the tip and moving along the vein. Plants look stunted. Fix it by applying 50 kg/ha of urea or 2 handfuls of poultry manure per plant. Potassium deficiency: leaf edges turn brown and dry, especially on older leaves. Roots become small and poorly shaped. Apply 100 kg/ha of muriate of potash or wood ash (2 cups per plant). Phosphorus deficiency: leaves turn dark green with purple tints, and roots are stubby. Apply 50 kg/ha of triple superphosphate or bone meal. Magnesium deficiency: yellowing between veins on older leaves. Spray 2% Epsom salt solution (20 g per litre) every two weeks. Iron deficiency: young leaves turn yellow while veins stay green. Common in alkaline soils. Spray 0.5% ferrous sulphate. Zinc deficiency: small leaves with yellow spots. Spray 0.2% zinc sulphate. Always check soil pH firstsweet potatoes prefer pH 5.5-6.5. If pH is wrong, nutrients get locked up. Use a simple soil test kit from an agro-dealer. Test every season. Also, look for patterns: deficiencies often appear in patches, not whole fields. If you see symptoms on more than 10% of plants, act immediately. Over-fertilizing can also cause problemstoo much nitrogen gives lots of leaves but few roots. Keep a notebook with dates, symptoms, and treatments. Early detection can save 30% of your yield.

5. Managing Growth to Prevent Cracking and Oversizing

Cracked or oversized sweet potatoes fetch low prices. Cracking happens when roots grow too fast after a dry spell followed by heavy rain or irrigation. To prevent this, keep soil moisture steady. Water every 7-10 days if no rain, giving about 25 mm each time. Use drip irrigation or a watering can. Mulch with dry grass to hold moisture. Oversizing occurs when plants stay in the ground too long. Harvest at the right time: most varieties are ready 3-5 months after planting. Check by digging one or two rootsif they are 15-20 cm long and 5-8 cm wide, they are ready. Do not leave them longer than 6 months. For market, aim for roots 150-300 g each. To slow growth, reduce nitrogen fertilizer after 2 months. If you see roots cracking, harvest immediately and sell quickly. Also, avoid heavy tillage near roots. In Africa, many farmers lose 20-40% of roots to cracking. Another cause is uneven hillingif ridges are too shallow, roots push out and crack. Keep ridges 30 cm high. If you want large roots for a specific market, space plants wider40 cm between plants instead of 30 cmand harvest at 5 months. But for most markets, medium size is best. Record harvest dates and root sizes. If cracking is a recurring problem, switch to a variety like 'Tanzania' or 'Ejumula' that resists cracking. Always test-dig before full harvest.

6. Using Technology for Crop Monitoring

You don't need expensive tools to use technology. A basic mobile phone can help. Use free apps like 'Plantix' or 'Crop Diagnosis' to identify pests and diseases from photos. Take clear pictures of affected leaves and uploadthe app suggests treatments. For record keeping, use 'Google Sheets' or a notebook app. Record planting date, variety, fertilizer, and harvest. Set reminders for scouting, hilling, and harvesting. If you have internet, join WhatsApp groups for sweet potato farmers to share alerts. For soil moisture, buy a cheap moisture meter (about $10) or use the 'feel method': squeeze soil in your handif it crumbles, it's dry; if it holds shape, it's moist. For larger farms, consider a drone or satellite service. In Kenya and Nigeria, companies like 'Ujuzi Kilimo' provide soil testing and SMS advice for a small fee. You can also use a rain gauge made from a plastic bottle to measure rainfall. Keep a simple weather log. If you have a smartphone, use 'AccuWeather' to track rain. For pest traps, use yellow sticky traps (buy or make with yellow plastic and grease) to monitor aphids and whiteflies. Count insects weekly. If numbers rise, spray neem oil. Technology is a tool, not a replacement for walking your field. Use it to save time and spot trends. Share your data with extension officers for better advice. Start smallone app, one notebookand build from there.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Crop Maintenance and Growth Monitoring for Sweet Potato

Sweet potato is one of Africa's most reliable food and income crops, but yield gaps remain wide. Average smallholder yields in sub-Saharan Africa sit between 4 and 8 t/ha, while well-managed fields with improved varieties, proper vine management, and timely nutrient correction routinely reach 2030 t/ha. This lesson is a field-ready reference for the six maintenance and monitoring topics in this module. Treat it as a working document: print it, laminate it, and carry it to the field. Every recommendation here is designed to protect the market value of your harvest, not just the tonnage.

Business first: a farmer who scouts weekly, keeps records, and manages growth will consistently out-earn a neighbour who does not. Record keeping turns farming from guesswork into a repeatable, financeable business. Buyers, processors, and lenders all want evidence of consistent quality and volume.

1. Routine Field Scouting and Record Keeping

Scout on a fixed schedule: twice weekly from 3 weeks after planting (WAT) to 16 WAT, then weekly until harvest. Walk a W-pattern through each block and inspect 10 plants at 5 stops. Record: date, block, variety, pest or disease seen, severity (15 scale), action taken, and cost. A simple exercise book with ruled columns is enough; a smartphone with a free app such as Plantix or FAO's eLocust/Kobo Toolbox speeds it up.

Why it matters commercially: buyers for processing (crisps, flour, puree) require traceability. A farmer who can show scouting records and input logs is far more likely to win a supply contract than one who cannot. Records also expose hidden losses a 10% weevil infestation caught in week 2 costs a fraction of one caught in week 8.

2. Pruning and Vine Management

Sweet potato vines grow aggressively. Unchecked, they root at nodes, produce many small storage roots, and divert energy from the main roots. Key practices:

  • Lift vines at 8 WAT and again at 12 WAT gently lift and turn them back toward the ridge centre without breaking. This prevents nodal rooting.
  • Prune runners longer than 60 cm. Cut cleanly with a sharp knife or secateurs; leave 34 nodes per runner.
  • Do not over-prune. Removing more than 30% of leaf area reduces photosynthesis and lowers yield. Prune for structure, not volume.
  • Use prunings as feed or planting material a second income stream for livestock keepers and a source of free vines for the next season.

3. Hilling and Ridge Maintenance

Ridges should be 2530 cm high and 6080 cm apart at planting. Erosion, rain, and root expansion flatten them over time. Re-hill at 4 WAT and after every heavy rain. Use a hand hoe or, for larger plots, an animal-drawn ridger. Well-maintained ridges improve drainage (critical in the humid forest zones of West and Central Africa) and make harvesting easier and faster, reducing labour cost per tonne.

5. Managing Growth to Prevent Cracking and Oversizing

Cracked or oversized roots are rejected by processors and fetch low farmgate prices. Cracking is caused by uneven moisture a dry spell followed by heavy irrigation or rain causes rapid expansion and splits the skin. Oversizing results from leaving roots in the ground too long or excessive nitrogen late in the season.

SAFETY WARNING PESTICIDE AND FERTILISER HANDLING

Always wear gloves, a long-sleeved shirt, trousers, closed shoes, and a face mask when handling or applying pesticides and concentrated fertilisers. Never eat, drink, or smoke during application. Store chemicals in original labelled containers, locked away from children and food. Observe the pre-harvest interval (PHI) on every label harvesting before the PHI leaves illegal residues that can cause buyers to reject your entire crop and may harm consumers. If you feel dizzy, nauseous, or have blurred vision after spraying, stop immediately, wash with soap and water, and seek medical help. In case of poisoning, contact your nearest poison control centre or health facility and bring the product label with you.

6. Using Technology for Crop Monitoring

Technology does not have to be expensive. Practical, Africa-ready options:

  • Smartphone apps: Plantix and FAO's Fall Armyworm app (adaptable) for pest ID; Kobo Toolbox (free) for digital scouting forms.
  • Soil testing: Use national soil labs or the Africa Soil Information Service (AfSIS) for baseline data; a single test every 3 years guides fertiliser spend.
  • Simple sensors: Low-cost soil moisture meters (US$1530) help schedule irrigation and prevent cracking.
  • Drone and satellite imagery: Now offered as a service in Kenya, Ghana, Nigeria, and South Africa. NDVI maps show stress zones before the eye can see them useful for larger commercial plots (>5 ha).
  • WhatsApp groups: Farmer groups sharing photos and prices in real time is the single highest-ROI "technology" for most smallholders.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Scout twice weekly from 316 WAT; record date, block, pest/disease, severity, action, cost.
  2. Lift vines at 8 and 12 WAT; prune runners over 60 cm, never remove more than 30% leaf area.
  3. Check ridge height (2530 cm) after every heavy rain; re-hill at 4 WAT and as needed.
  4. Inspect older leaves first for N, P, K, Mg; correct deficiencies within 7 days.
  5. Maintain even moisture from 10 WAT onward to prevent root cracking.
  6. Sample 3 roots per scouting point from 13 WAT; harvest at 200400 g per root.
  7. Wear full PPE when handling agrochemicals; respect the pre-harvest interval on every label.
  8. Review records weekly to compare blocks, plan inputs, and forecast harvest volumes for buyers.
