1. Understanding the Egg Laying Cycle

A hen's egg laying cycle is a daily rhythm that starts when she reaches sexual maturity, usually around 18 to 22 weeks of age, depending on breed and management. In Africa, many smallholder farmers use indigenous or improved breeds like Isa Brown, Lohmann Brown, or Kuroiler. The cycle begins with the hen ovulating a yolk into the oviduct. Over the next 24 to 26 hours, the egg white (albumen), shell membranes, and shell are added. The egg is then laid, and the process repeats. Most hens lay in the morning, with peak production between 7 am and 11 am. A healthy hen can lay 5 to 6 eggs per week during her peak, but this drops with age. The first year of lay is the most productive; after 12 months, production declines by about 10-15% per year. To manage this, farmers often keep hens for one or two laying cycles, then cull or molt. In Africa, where feed costs are high, many farmers use forced molting to extend lay, but this requires careful nutrition and lighting. Understanding the cycle helps you time feed, lighting, and nest box management. For example, provide 16 hours of light daily during peak lay to stimulate ovulation. Keep stress low: sudden noise, predators, or moving hens can delay or stop laying for days. Track your flock's lay percentage: if you have 100 hens, expect 80-90 eggs per day at peak. If it drops below 60, investigate immediately. Record daily egg counts to spot trends early.

2. Factors Affecting Egg Production and Persistency

Egg production and persistencyhow long a hen keeps laying welldepend on several factors you can control. First, nutrition: a laying hen needs 16-18% protein, 3.5-4.5% calcium, and 0.4-0.5% available phosphorus. In Africa, many farmers mix their own feed using maize, soybean meal, fishmeal, and limestone. A common mistake is insufficient calcium, leading to thin shells and fewer eggs. Provide oyster shell or limestone grit free-choice. Second, lighting: hens need 14-16 hours of light daily to maintain lay. If you rely on natural daylight, expect lower production in winter or rainy seasons. Use a bulb (e.g., 40-watt LED) per 20 square meters to extend light. Third, temperature: heat stress above 30C reduces feed intake and egg numbers. Provide shade, ventilation, and cool water. Fourth, water: a hen drinks up to 250 ml daily; lack of clean water for even a few hours can drop production. Fifth, disease: Newcastle, infectious bronchitis, and worms reduce lay. Vaccinate and deworm regularly. Sixth, age: persistency is highest in the first 20 weeks of lay, then declines. Replace hens after 80 weeks. Seventh, stress: moving, overcrowding, or predators cause drops. Keep 4-5 hens per square meter in deep litter. Finally, breed: hybrid layers like Isa Brown outlay indigenous breeds. With good management, you can achieve 85% lay persistency for 12 months. Record daily egg numbers and feed intake to spot problems early.

3. Egg Collection, Handling, and Storage

Collect eggs at least twice dailymorning and afternoonto prevent breakage, dirt, and heat damage. In Africa, where temperatures often exceed 30C, eggs left in nests for hours lose quality fast. Use clean, dry hands or a soft basket. Never stack eggs more than 5 high. After collection, sort out cracked, dirty, or abnormal eggs. Do not wash eggs unless necessary; washing removes the protective bloom and invites bacteria. If eggs are dirty, wipe with a dry cloth or use fine sandpaper. For storage, keep eggs at 15-18C and 70-80% humidity. In rural areas without refrigerators, store in a cool, dark room, e.g., a ventilated mud-brick store or a clay pot with a lid. You can also use evaporative cooling: place eggs in a porous pot surrounded by wet sand. Never store eggs near onions, kerosene, or chemicalsthey absorb odours. For sale, keep eggs in clean trays or cartons. If you sell at local markets, transport in padded crates to avoid cracks. For longer storage, use refrigeration at 4-7C, but only if eggs are clean and dry. Remember: eggs stored at room temperature lose quality faster; after 7 days, the air cell grows and albumen thins. For best quality, sell within 3 days. If you keep eggs for hatching, store at 15-18C and turn daily for up to 7 days. Always label collection dates. Good handling reduces losses by up to 20% and earns better prices.

4. Egg Grading, Sizing, and Packaging

Grading eggs by size and quality helps you get better prices. In Africa, markets often pay more for large, clean, uncracked eggs. Grade by weight: small (under 53g), medium (53-63g), large (63-73g), and extra-large (over 73g). Use a simple egg scale or a kitchen scale. For quality, check shell cleanliness, shape, and texture. Reject eggs with cracks, rough shells, or abnormal shapes. Also check internal quality by candling: in a dark room, hold the egg against a bright light (torch or candle). A fresh egg has a small air cell (less than 5mm) and a clear, firm yolk. If the air cell is large or the yolk moves freely, the egg is old. For packaging, use clean, dry trays or cartons. In many African countries, 30-egg trays are standard. Label with grade, date, and your farm name. For retail, consider 6- or 12-egg cartons. Packaging protects eggs during transport and shows professionalism. If you sell to hotels or supermarkets, they may require specific grading and packaging. Invest in a simple egg candler and scalethey cost little and improve your marketability. Also, train workers to handle eggs gently. Remember: graded eggs sell faster and at higher prices. Even in local markets, customers prefer uniform, clean eggs. So sort, grade, and package neatly.

5. Quality Control: Shell Integrity and Internal Quality

Quality control starts with the shell. A strong shell protects the egg from bacteria and breakage. Shell strength depends on calcium, phosphorus, and vitamin D3 in the diet. Provide limestone or oyster shell free-choice, and ensure hens get sunlight or vitamin D3 supplement. Avoid stressing hensstress reduces shell quality. Collect eggs gently and check for cracks by tapping two eggs together; a dull sound means a crack. Discard cracked eggs for sale; use them immediately in your home or sell for processing. For internal quality, candling is key. A fresh egg has a small air cell, a firm yolk that stays centered, and thick albumen. As eggs age, the air cell grows, the yolk flattens, and albumen thins. To check freshness without candling, place the egg in a bowl of water: fresh eggs sink, old eggs float. For selling, aim for eggs less than 7 days old. Store at cool temperatures and high humidity. Also, keep nest boxes clean and change bedding regularly to prevent dirty eggs. Dirty eggs carry bacteria like Salmonella. If you must clean, use a dry method or sanitize with a warm, mild bleach solution (1 teaspoon per liter) and dry immediately. Never soak eggs. For internal quality, avoid feeding hens excessive cottonseed meal or other toxins that cause off-flavours. Regularly test a few eggs by breaking them open: look for blood spots, meat spots, or abnormal colours. These are usually harmless but reduce consumer acceptance. Maintain records of quality checks and train workers. Good quality control reduces waste and builds customer trust.

6. Dealing with Abnormal Eggs and Production Drops

Abnormal eggs and sudden drops in production are common challenges. Abnormal eggs include: soft-shelled, shell-less, double-yolked, small, misshapen, or eggs with rough shells. Soft-shelled eggs often result from calcium deficiency, stress, or disease like infectious bronchitis. Double-yolked eggs occur when two yolks are released too close together; they are safe to eat but not for hatching. Small eggs may come from young hens or stress. To fix, adjust diet: add limestone grit, ensure 4% calcium in feed, and provide vitamin D3. Also, reduce stress by keeping consistent lighting and avoiding sudden changes. Production drops can be caused by many factors: disease (Newcastle, avian influenza, worms), poor nutrition, heat stress, lack of water, predators, or aging. If your flock's lay drops by more than 10% in a week, act fast. Check water and feed quality, look for signs of disease (e.g., green droppings, coughing), and review lighting. Deworm if needed. In Africa, heat stress is a major cause; provide shade, increase ventilation, and offer cool water. Also, check for external parasites like mites and licethey suck blood and reduce lay. Treat with appropriate acaricides. If hens are older than 80 weeks, consider culling or forced molting. Keep daily records of egg numbers, feed intake, and mortality. This helps you pinpoint the cause. For abnormal eggs, collect and dispose of them properly; do not sell cracked or soft-shelled eggs. If you see many abnormal eggs, consult a vet or extension officer. Early action saves your flock and income.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Egg Production and Quality in African Layer Farming

Layer farming in Africa is a business of margins. A flock that peaks at 92% and holds above 80% for 40 weeks will out-earn a flock that peaks at 88% and collapses to 60% within 12 weeks even if both flocks cost the same to feed. The difference is rarely genetics alone. It is management of the egg laying cycle, collection discipline, grading accuracy, and rapid response to production drops. This practical reference consolidates the operational standards that separate profitable layer units from break-even operations across Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia, and South Africa.

Use this module alongside the six core lessons. The tables, checklist, and diagrams below are designed to be printed, laminated, and posted in the egg room or farm office. They are not theory they are the daily operating parameters that determine your egg income per bird housed.

Practical Operating Guidance

Lighting. Layers respond to light. From 18 weeks, provide 1416 hours of light daily, including natural daylight, and never decrease light during lay. In equatorial Africa, natural day length is roughly 12 hours year-round, so supplementary lighting is essential to reach peak and maintain persistency. Use a timer, keep bulbs clean, and provide at least 10 lux at bird eye level.

Feed and water. A layer eats 110120 g/day at peak. Calcium must be 3.84.2% with particle size large enough to stay in the gizzard overnight. Water intake is roughly twice feed intake by weight; a bird drinking less than 200 ml/day at 25C is under stress. Test water quality salinity above 2,000 ppm total dissolved solids reduces intake and shell quality.

Heat stress. Above 30C, layers reduce feed intake, drop egg size, and produce thin shells. In the Sahel, coastal West Africa, and lowland East Africa, provide shade, ventilation, cool water, and consider feeding during cooler hours. Heat stress can cut production by 1020% within days.

Collection frequency. Collect eggs at least 34 times daily, more in hot weather. Eggs left in nests for hours become dirty, cracked, and hot all reduce grade and value. Roll-away nests reduce floor eggs and contamination.

Storage. After collection, cool eggs to 1015C where possible. In off-grid farms, evaporative cooling (wet sand, charcoal cooler, or Zeer pot) can hold eggs at 1822C. Never store eggs in direct sun or near ammonia. Sell within 710 days for best quality and price.

SAFETY WARNING Salmonella and Food Safety

Eggs are a high-risk food safety product. Never wash eggs in dirty water or reuse egg trays contaminated with manure. Washing with contaminated water pushes bacteria through the shell pores and can cause Salmonella outbreaks, which will destroy your market reputation and may trigger regulatory closure. Use dry cleaning (sandpaper or dry cloth) for slightly soiled eggs; heavily soiled eggs should be discarded or sold only for industrial processing with full disclosure. Always wash hands before handling eggs, keep rodents and wild birds out of the egg room, and store eggs away from raw meat and chemicals. In Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa, egg-borne Salmonella cases have led to product recalls protect your brand.

Quick Reference Checklist Daily and Weekly Egg Operations

  1. Collect eggs 34 times daily early morning, midday, afternoon, and evening. Never leave eggs in nests overnight.
  2. Record production by house and by flock age hen-day percentage, total eggs, floor eggs, cracked eggs, dirty eggs, and mortality. Compare against Table 1 benchmarks.
  3. Grade and size eggs immediately after collection separate jumbo, large, medium, small, cracked, and dirty. Do not mix grades in one tray.
  4. Store at 1015C with 7080% relative humidity use evaporative cooling if no refrigeration. Keep eggs pointed end down.
  5. Inspect shell and internal quality weekly break 1020 eggs per house and check Haugh unit, yolk colour, and air cell. Record findings.
  6. Monitor feed, water, and light daily check intake, water flow, and lighting hours. Any sudden change is an early warning of a production drop.
  7. Clean and disinfect egg trays, nests, and the egg room weekly use approved disinfectants and allow trays to dry fully before reuse.
  8. Review production curve every 2 weeks if production drops more than 35% below expected for the flock age, investigate within 48 hours (feed, water, disease, heat, lighting, or predators).
