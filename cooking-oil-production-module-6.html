1. Role of Bleaching in Oil Processing

Bleaching is the step where you remove colour, off-flavours, and impurities from crude oil. In Africa, many small-scale processors skip bleaching, but it is critical for making oil that is clear, stable, and appealing. The main job is to adsorb pigments like chlorophyll and carotenoids, plus trace metals (iron, copper), soaps, and oxidation products. Without bleaching, your oil may look dark, taste bitter, and go rancid quickly.

In practice, bleaching uses an adsorbentâusually bleaching earthâmixed with oil at high temperature under vacuum. For a 100 kg batch of crude palm oil, you might add 1â2 kg of bleaching earth (1â2% by weight). Heat the oil to 90â110Â°C, stir for 20â30 minutes, then filter out the earth. The earth traps pigments and impurities. You need a vacuum to prevent oxidation and remove moisture. In rural setups, a simple vacuum pump and a stainless steel tank work.

Why is this important in Africa? Many local oils, like groundnut, sunflower, and palm, have strong colours and flavours. Bleaching makes them neutral, so they can be used in cooking, cosmetics, and export. It also improves shelf life. For example, unbleached palm oil may turn rancid in 3 months, while bleached oil lasts 6â12 months if stored properly. Also, bleaching removes soap residues from earlier refining steps, which can cause foaming during frying.

You don't need expensive equipment. A used 200-litre drum, a gas burner, a stirrer, and a hand pump can do the job. But keep temperatures steadyâtoo hot (above 120Â°C) can damage the oil. Always test your oil after bleaching: it should be lighter in colour and have a mild taste. Remember, bleaching is not just about colour; it's about quality and safety. For local markets, customers judge oil by clarity. So investing in proper bleaching can increase your selling price by 20â30%.

2. Types of Bleaching Earths and Adsorbents

Bleaching earths are the most common adsorbents for edible oil. They come in two main types: natural (fuller's earth) and activated (bentonite). Natural earths are mined and dried; they work but need more dosage (2â5% of oil weight). Activated earths are treated with acid, which increases their surface area and adsorption powerâuse only 0.5â2%. In Africa, activated earth is imported, but you can find local deposits in countries like Nigeria, South Africa, and Kenya. Always buy from a reliable supplier and check the pHâit should be neutral to slightly acidic (pH 6â7).

Besides bleaching earth, other adsorbents include activated carbon and silica gel. Activated carbon is excellent for removing polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and off-odours, but it's expensive. Use it in small amounts (0.1â0.5%) mixed with bleaching earth. Silica gel is good for removing soaps and phospholipids, but it's less common in small-scale operations. In practice, a blend of 90% bleaching earth and 10% activated carbon works well for heavily coloured oils like crude palm oil.

How to choose? Test each type with a small sample. Take 1 litre of oil, heat to 100Â°C, add 1% earth, stir 30 minutes, filter, and compare colour and taste. The best earth will give the lightest colour with the least oil loss. Oil loss occurs because earth absorbs some oilâtypically 20â40% of the earth's weight. So if you use 2 kg earth, you lose 0.4â0.8 kg oil. That's money lost. So use the minimum effective dose.

In Africa, storage matters. Bleaching earth absorbs moisture from air, which reduces its power. Keep it in sealed bags in a dry place. If it gets damp, dry it in an oven at 105Â°C for 2 hours before use. Also, avoid inhaling dustâwear a mask. For small processors, buying 25 kg bags of activated earth is cost-effective. You can also regenerate used earth by washing and heating, but it's rarely economical at small scale. Stick to fresh earth for best results.

3. Batch and Continuous Bleaching Processes

Batch bleaching is the simplest method for small-scale African processors. You mix oil and bleaching earth in a tank, heat, stir, and then filter. It's flexibleâyou can do 50 kg or 500 kg. Typical batch: Heat 200 kg oil to 100Â°C in a stainless steel tank with a stirrer. Add 2 kg activated earth (1%). Maintain vacuum (if available) to prevent oxidation. Stir for 30 minutes. Then pump the slurry through a filter press. The whole cycle takes 1â2 hours. Batch is labour-intensive but requires low capital. Many West African palm oil processors use this method.

Continuous bleaching is for larger operations, above 5 tonnes per day. Oil flows continuously through a series of tanks: first heated to 90â110Â°C, then mixed with earth in a slurry tank, then held in a retention vessel for 20â30 minutes, and finally filtered. The advantage is consistent quality and lower labour. But it needs precise control and expensive equipment like metering pumps and continuous filters. In Africa, continuous plants are found in countries like Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa, often for export-grade oil.

Which to choose? If you produce less than 1 tonne per day, batch is better. You can start with a 200-litre drum, a gas burner, and a manual filter press. Cost: $500â$1,500. For 1â5 tonnes per day, consider a semi-continuous setup: two batch tanks alternating. This keeps oil flowing to filtration. For above 5 tonnes, invest in continuous. But remember, continuous requires steady electricity and skilled operators. Many African processors start batch and upgrade as they grow.

Key parameters for both: temperature (90â110Â°C), time (20â30 minutes), earth dosage (0.5â2%), and vacuum (if possible). Without vacuum, use nitrogen blanketing or keep the tank covered. After bleaching, filter immediatelyâdon't let the slurry sit, as oil can re-absorb pigments. Also, pre-coat the filter with a layer of earth to prevent fines passing through. In batch, you can pre-coat by mixing a small amount of earth with oil and pumping through the filter before the main batch. This improves clarity.

4. Filtration Techniques: Plate, Frame, and Pressure Leaf

After bleaching, you must separate the oil from the spent earth. The most common filter for small-scale African processors is the plate-and-frame filter press. It consists of alternating plates and frames covered with filter cloth. The oil-earth slurry is pumped into the frames; oil passes through the cloth, while earth builds up as a cake. When the cake is full, you open the press and scrape it out. A 20-plate press can handle 200â500 kg per batch. Cost: $800â$2,000. It's manual but reliable.

Pressure leaf filters are another option. They use vertical leaves (metal screens) coated with filter aid. The slurry is pumped in, and oil passes through the screens while earth deposits on the leaves. Cleaning is done by backwashing or opening the filter. Pressure leaf filters are compact and good for continuous operation, but they cost more ($3,000â$10,000) and need more maintenance. In Africa, they are found in medium-scale refineries.

For very small operations, you can use a simple bag filter or a vacuum filter. A bag filter is a cloth bag inside a drum; you pour the slurry in and let it drain. It's cheap ($50â$200) but slow and labour-intensive. Vacuum filters use a vacuum pump to pull oil through a cloth, leaving earth behind. They work well but need a vacuum source.

No matter the type, pre-coating is key. Before filtering, mix a small amount of diatomaceous earth (filter aid) with clean oil and pump it through the filter. This coats the cloth and prevents fine earth particles from passing. Also, keep the oil hot (80â90Â°C) during filtrationâcold oil is thick and slow. After filtration, test the oil for clarity: hold a sample against light. If you see haze, filter again. Spent earth cake should be dry and crumbly; if it's wet, you're losing oil. You can recover oil from the cake by steaming or solvent extraction, but that's advanced. For most, just dispose of the cake properly.

5. Optimizing Bleaching Conditions

Optimizing bleaching means getting the best colour and quality with the least earth and oil loss. The main variables are temperature, time, earth dosage, and vacuum. Temperature: 90â110Â°C is ideal. Below 90Â°C, adsorption is slow; above 110Â°C, oil may oxidize and earth may lose activity. Use a thermometerâdon't guess. Time: 20â30 minutes after adding earth. Shorter time means incomplete adsorption; longer time can cause oxidation and free fatty acid (FFA) increase. Stir continuously at 100â200 rpm.

Earth dosage: start with 1% of oil weight. For dark oils like crude palm, use up to 2%. For lighter oils like sunflower, 0.5% may suffice. Test small samples to find the minimum. Remember, each 1% earth causes about 0.3â0.5% oil loss. So if you use 2% earth, you lose 0.6â1% oil. That's significant. Vacuum: if you have a vacuum pump, apply 50â100 mbar. This removes moisture and prevents oxidation. Without vacuum, keep the tank covered and use nitrogen if available.

Other tips: Pre-heat the oil before adding earth to avoid clumping. Add earth slowly with stirring. After bleaching, cool the oil to 80Â°C before filtration to protect the filter cloth. Use a filter aid like diatomaceous earth (0.5â1% of oil weight) to improve filtration and reduce oil in the cake. Monitor FFA and peroxide value before and after bleachingâthey should not increase. If FFA rises, your temperature is too high or time too long.

In Africa, where electricity can be unreliable, use a gas burner for heating and a manual stirrer. Keep records: for each batch, note oil type, weight, earth type and amount, temperature, time, and colour results. After a few batches, you'll find the sweet spot. For example, a processor in Kenya found that 1.2% activated earth at 100Â°C for 25 minutes gave the best colour for sunflower oil, with only 0.4% oil loss. That's optimization.

6. Spent Bleach Earth Handling and Disposal

Spent bleaching earth (SBE) is the used earth after filtration. It contains 20â40% oil, pigments, and impurities. In Africa, improper disposal is commonâdumping it in fields or rivers. That's harmful: SBE can spontaneously combust if piled wet, and it can pollute soil and water. You must handle it safely. First, let the cake cool and dry. If it's wet, spread it in a thin layer under the sun for 1â2 days. Dry SBE is less likely to catch fire.

Disposal options: The best is to send it to a licensed landfill or incinerator. But in many African countries, these are scarce. Alternative: use it as a fuel source. SBE has a calorific value of 10â15 MJ/kg, similar to low-grade coal. You can burn it in a kiln or boiler to generate heat for your process. But burning releases smoke and odoursâdo it in a controlled incinerator with a scrubber. Another option: mix it with agricultural waste and compost it. The oil and minerals can enrich soil, but only if the SBE is from food-grade oil and not contaminated with solvents. Test with a small plot first.

If you must dispose on land, bury it deep (at least 1 metre) away from water sources. Never dump in rivers or wetlands. Also, check local regulationsâsome countries classify SBE as hazardous waste. In Nigeria, for example, the National Environmental Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency (NESREA) requires proper disposal. For small processors, the simplest safe method is to store SBE in sealed drums and transport it to a cement kiln, which can use it as fuel. Cement plants in South Africa, Nigeria, and Kenya often accept SBE.

Reduce SBE generation by optimizing bleachingâuse less earth. Also, you can recover oil from SBE by solvent extraction or pressing, but that's for larger operations. For most, just dry, store, and dispose responsibly. Remember, SBE is not garbage; it's a waste that needs management. Proper handling protects your health, your community, and your business reputation.

Practical Reference: Bleaching and Filtration in African Cooking Oil Production

Bleaching is the step where crude or degummed oil is treated with an adsorbent â usually bleaching earth â to strip out pigments (carotenoids, chlorophyll), residual gums, soaps, trace metals (iron, copper), and oxidation products. In the African context, this is where many small and medium-scale processors either make or lose money. Done well, bleaching improves colour, flavour stability, and shelf life, and protects the downstream deodoriser from fouling. Done badly, it burns oil, wastes expensive earth, and produces oil that fails buyer specs or goes rancid within weeks.

This practical reference is written for plant managers, quality officers, and operations staff running batch or continuous bleaching lines in Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, Tanzania, Uganda, and similar markets. It focuses on the decisions that affect cost per tonne, oil yield, and compliance with buyer and regulatory requirements.

1. Why bleaching matters commercially

Buyers of refined cooking oil â supermarkets, food processors, hotels, and export traders â judge oil on colour, clarity, and stability. Crude palm oil, sunflower, soybean, and cottonseed oils carry pigments and impurities that must be reduced before deodorisation. Bleaching typically targets a Lovibond red colour below 2.0 (for many refined oils) and reduces phosphorus to below 5 ppm and soap to below 50 ppm. If these targets are missed, deodorisation becomes harder, oil losses rise, and the final product darkens during storage.

For an African processor buying bleaching earth at USD 400â900 per tonne (depending on grade and import duties), earth cost can represent 1â3% of total refining cost. Over-dosing by 20% on a 20 t/day line can waste tens of thousands of dollars annually. Under-dosing, on the other hand, leads to rejected batches and customer complaints. Getting the dose, temperature, time, and filtration right is therefore a direct profit lever.

2. Types of bleaching earths and how to choose

Two families dominate: natural (fullerâs) earth and acid-activated bleaching earth. Natural earths are cheaper but have lower adsorptive capacity. Acid-activated earths have higher surface area and remove chlorophyll and soap more effectively, but cost more and generate more acidic spent earth. In Africa, imported acid-activated earths are common, though locally mined bentonite and fullerâs earth are increasingly available in Nigeria, South Africa, and Kenya.

Synthetic silica adsorbents are sometimes used in combination with earth to reduce earth dose and improve filtration. They are especially useful for oils with high soap or phospholipid content. The choice should be based on oil type, impurity load, and total cost per tonne of oil treated â not just price per bag.

3. Batch vs continuous bleaching

Batch bleaching is the norm for plants up to about 50 t/day. Oil is charged to a vessel, heated under vacuum, earth is added, the slurry is agitated for a set time, then filtered. It is flexible and easier to run with variable crude quality, but cycle times are long and energy use per tonne is higher.

Continuous bleaching suits larger plants (above 50â100 t/day). Oil and earth are dosed continuously into a series of vessels, then filtered. It gives consistent quality, lower labour, and better steam economy, but requires stable crude quality and skilled operators. In Africa, many mid-sized refiners run semi-continuous systems â batch dosing with continuous filtration â as a pragmatic compromise.

Whichever route is used, the key operating variables are the same: temperature, vacuum, contact time, agitation, earth dose, and filtration pressure. Vacuum is critical â it removes moisture and air, reduces oxidation, and helps colour removal. Running bleaching without vacuum is a common cause of dark, off-flavour oil.

4. Filtration techniques

Three filtration technologies dominate: plate-and-frame presses, pressure leaf filters, and (less commonly in Africa) candle filters. Plate-and-frame presses are robust, cheap to maintain, and widely used in batch plants. They require manual labour to change plates and cloths, and oil losses in spent earth can be significant if not blown or steamed properly. Pressure leaf filters use vertical leaves coated with earth; they are better for continuous operation and give clearer filtrate, but have higher capital cost and need careful pre-coating. Candle filters are used in some large refineries but are less common in the African SME sector.

Filtration performance is measured by flow rate, filtrate clarity, and residual oil in spent earth. A well-run filter press should leave less than 25â30% oil in spent earth; poorly run presses can leave 40% or more, which is a direct yield loss. Blowing the filter with steam or nitrogen before opening, and controlling earth dose to avoid over-thick cakes, are the main levers.

5. Optimising bleaching conditions

The goal is to achieve the required colour and impurity removal at the lowest earth dose and lowest oil loss. Practical steps:

  • Start with good degumming. If phosphorus and soap are high, earth dose rises sharply. Target < 10 ppm phosphorus and < 50 ppm soap before bleaching.
  • Control moisture. Oil should be dry (< 0.05% moisture). Water competes with pigments for adsorption sites.
  • Use vacuum. 50â100 mbar helps remove air and moisture and protects oil quality.
  • Optimise temperature. Too low slows adsorption; too high causes oxidation and colour fixation. 90â110 Â°C is the usual window.
  • Do a dose-response trial. Run lab or plant trials at 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5%, and 2.0% earth and measure colour, phosphorus, and soap. Choose the lowest dose that meets spec.
  • Minimise contact time after filtration. Hot bleached oil should go to deodorisation quickly to avoid oxidation.

Spent bleaching earth can self-heat and ignite spontaneously when exposed to air, especially when it contains residual oil. In African plants, fires in spent earth storage areas are a known hazard. Never store spent earth in open piles or in contact with combustible materials. Keep it in closed, ventilated metal containers, monitor temperature, and dispose of it promptly through approved channels. Also wear dust masks and gloves when handling dry earth â respirable silica and acid-activated earth dust can damage lungs and skin.

6. Spent bleach earth handling and disposal

Spent earth is both a waste and a resource. It contains 15â40% oil, which can be recovered by solvent extraction or by pressing, though this is rarely economic at small scale. More commonly, spent earth is used as a fuel in cement kilns, brick kilns, or as a soil amendment after testing. In many African countries, disposal to landfill is regulated, and dumping spent earth illegally can attract fines and community complaints.

Best practice is to:

  • Recover as much oil as possible before disposal (blow or steam the filter).
  • Store in closed containers away from heat and ignition sources.
  • Test for heavy metals and acidity before land application.
  • Partner with cement or brick manufacturers who can co-process it as fuel.
  • Keep records for environmental compliance and buyer audits.
