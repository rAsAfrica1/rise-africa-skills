1. Choosing the Right Field for Potato Planting

Potatoes need loose, well-drained soil and at least 6 hours of sunlight daily. Avoid fields where you grew potatoes, tomatoes, peppers, or eggplants in the last 3 yearsâthese crops share diseases like bacterial wilt and nematodes. In Africa, rotate with cereals like maize, sorghum, or millet. A good field has deep soil (at least 30 cm) and is not prone to flooding. If your area gets heavy rains, choose a gentle slope (2-5%) so water drains away. Test the soil texture: take a handful of moist soil, squeeze it. If it forms a ball that crumbles easily, it's loamâideal for potatoes. If it sticks together like clay, add organic matter. If it feels gritty like sand, it drains too fast and needs compost. Also check previous crop history: if the field had cotton, ensure no herbicide residues remain, as potatoes are sensitive. In East Africa, fields near wetlands may have cool temperatures but risk waterlogging. In West Africa, avoid lowlands that flood during the rainy season. Clear the field of rocks, stumps, and perennial weeds like couch grass before planting. Potatoes also need good air circulation, so avoid fields surrounded by tall trees or buildings. Finally, consider access: you'll need to transport seed, fertilizer, and harvested tubers. A field near a road or path saves labour. If you must use a sloped field, plant across the slope, not up and down, to reduce erosion. Remember, potatoes are heavy feeders; a field that grew a legume like beans or groundnuts last season will have extra nitrogen, reducing fertilizer costs. Always walk the field and dig a few holes to check for hardpan (a compacted layer) at 20-30 cm depth. If you hit a hard layer, break it with a fork or chisel plough before planting. Choosing the right field is the first step to a healthy crop.

2. Soil Testing and pH Adjustment

Potatoes grow best in soil pH between 5.0 and 6.0. This slightly acidic range prevents potato scab, a common disease that makes tubers unsightly. Testing your soil is cheap and saves money on fertilizer. You can buy a simple pH test kit from agricultural supply shops for a few dollars, or send a sample to a government soil lab. To take a sample, walk in a zigzag across the field, dig 10-15 spots to 15 cm depth, mix all soil in a clean bucket, and take about 500 g for testing. If pH is below 5.0, add agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) at 2-4 tonnes per hectare, depending on how low it is. Lime takes 2-3 months to change pH, so apply it before the rainy season. If pH is above 6.5, add elemental sulfur at 200-500 kg per hectare, or use ammonium sulfate fertilizer, which acidifies soil. In Africa, many soils are naturally acidic, so liming is common. But don't over-limeâpH above 6.0 encourages scab. Also test for phosphorus and potassium. A simple NPK soil test costs more but tells you exactly what to add. If you can't afford a lab test, observe indicator weeds: sorrel and plantain indicate acidic soil; chamomile indicates alkaline. For a quick home test, mix soil with vinegarâif it fizzes, it's alkaline; mix with baking soda and waterâif it fizzes, it's acidic. After adjusting pH, wait at least 3 weeks before planting. Remember, organic matter helps buffer pH changes. Adding compost or manure (well-rotted) at 5-10 tonnes per hectare improves soil structure and nutrient holding. Never apply fresh manureâit burns roots and carries disease. Adjusting pH is not a one-time job; test every 2-3 years. In sandy soils, pH changes faster, so use smaller lime doses and retest. In clay soils, lime moves slowly; incorporate it into the top 15 cm. Correct pH means healthier plants and higher yields.

3. Tillage Practices for Potato Production

Tillage loosens soil so potato tubers can expand easily. Start with primary tillage: plough to 20-30 cm depth when soil is moist but not wet. If soil sticks to your boots, wait. In small plots, use a hand hoe or fork. For larger fields, a tractor-drawn mouldboard plough or disc plough works. After ploughing, do secondary tillage with a harrow or rake to break clods and level the field. This creates a fine seedbed. Avoid over-tillingâit destroys soil structure and causes hardpan. In sandy soils, one pass is enough. In clay soils, you may need two passes, but let soil dry between passes. Potatoes need deep, loose soil, so consider subsoiling if you have a compacted layer at 30 cm. A subsoiler or chisel plough breaks hardpan without inverting soil. In Africa, many farmers use hand tillage; that's fine if you dig to 20 cm and remove rocks. Tillage should be done 2-4 weeks before planting. This allows weeds to germinate; then you can remove them with a light harrow. If you practice conservation agriculture, you can plant potatoes with minimum tillage using a dibber or hoe, but yields may be lower unless you add extra compost. Raised beds are excellent for potatoes in wet areas. Make beds 20-30 cm high and 75-90 cm wide, with 30 cm paths. This improves drainage and warms soil. In dry areas, flat planting conserves moisture. After tillage, do not walk on the soilâit compacts. Use planks or paths. Tillage also controls weeds and incorporates crop residues. But leave some residue on the surface to reduce erosion. If you use a tractor, avoid working wet soilâit creates clods that are hard to break. Test by squeezing a handful: if it forms a ribbon, it's too wet. Proper tillage is the foundation for good tuber shape and easy harvest.

4. Incorporating Organic Matter and Cover Crops

Organic matter is the key to healthy potato soil. It improves water holding, drainage, and nutrient supply. Apply well-rotted manure or compost at 5-10 tonnes per hectare (about 2-4 kg per square meter) before planting. Spread it evenly and incorporate into the top 15 cm with a hoe or plough. Fresh manure must be composted for at least 3 months to kill weed seeds and pathogens. In Africa, good sources include cattle, goat, and poultry manure, as well as crop residues like maize stover and bean haulms. You can also make compost from kitchen waste, grass clippings, and leaves. Cover crops are plants grown to improve soil, not for harvest. Before potatoes, plant a legume like cowpea, lablab, or groundnuts. These fix nitrogen from the air, adding 40-100 kg N per hectare. Sow cover crops at the start of the rains and let them grow for 6-8 weeks. Then cut them down and incorporate into the soil 2-3 weeks before planting potatoes. This adds organic matter and nutrients. In dry areas, use drought-tolerant cover crops like pigeon pea or sorghum-sudan grass. Avoid cover crops that host potato diseases, like tomatoes or peppers. Also, consider green manures like sunhemp or mucuna, which produce lots of biomass. If you can't grow a cover crop, add crop residues from other fields. But don't use residues from potato fieldsâthey may carry disease. Organic matter also feeds soil microbes that help potatoes take up nutrients. Over time, regular additions reduce the need for chemical fertilizers. In sandy soils, organic matter is especially important because it holds water and nutrients. In clay soils, it improves drainage and prevents crusting. Apply organic matter every season, even if only a little. A simple practice: after harvest, plant a cover crop instead of leaving soil bare. This protects soil from erosion and adds organic matter for the next potato crop. Remember, organic matter takes time to build, so be patient and consistent.

5. Soil Drainage and Erosion Control

Potatoes rot if water sits around tubers for more than a day. Good drainage is critical. If your field has a hardpan or clay layer, water pools after rain. Test drainage by digging a 30 cm deep hole, filling it with water, and seeing how long it takes to drain. If it takes more than 2 hours, you have a problem. Solutions include raised beds, drainage furrows, or subsurface drains. Raised beds are easiest: make beds 30 cm high, 75 cm wide, with 30 cm furrows between. In flat fields, dig shallow furrows every 5-10 meters to channel water away. For large fields, use a plough to make ridges and furrows. In Africa, many farmers use ridges (earth mounds) for potatoes. This raises the tubers above the water table. If your field is on a slope, erosion is a risk. Plant across the slope, not up and down. Use contour ridges or grass strips every 10-15 meters. Keep soil covered with mulch or cover crops. After planting, avoid leaving soil bare. Erosion removes the fertile topsoil and exposes tubers. To control erosion, also reduce tillageâleave some crop residue on the surface. In steep areas (over 10% slope), consider terracing or planting on contour bunds. Another method is to plant potatoes in trenches (furrows) that catch water, but only if drainage is good. In dry areas, you want to conserve water, so use flat planting and mulch. But in wet areas, drainage is priority. Also, avoid walking on wet soilâit compacts and reduces drainage. If you have a drainage problem, add organic matter to improve soil structure. Gypsum can help break up clay. For severe cases, install French drains (gravel-filled trenches) or plastic pipes. Remember, potatoes are sensitive to waterlogging; even 2 days of standing water can kill plants. So plan drainage before planting. Also, keep field edges clear of weeds and debris to allow water flow. After heavy rain, check for pooling and fix it immediately. Good drainage means healthy tubers and less disease.

6. Preparing Seedbeds for Planting

A good seedbed is fine, level, and free of clods. After primary tillage, break clods with a rake or harrow until soil particles are no bigger than a marble. The seedbed should be 20-30 cm deep. If you use raised beds, make them 20-30 cm high and 75-90 cm wide. For flat planting, level the field so water doesn't pool. Remove rocks, roots, and old crop residues. Incorporate fertilizer and organic matter into the top 15 cm. A common recommendation is to apply NPK 17-17-17 at 300-500 kg per hectare (about 30-50 g per square meter) before planting. If you use manure, apply 2-4 kg per square meter. Mix well. Then mark rows. For potatoes, rows should be 75-90 cm apart. Within rows, space seed potatoes 20-30 cm apart. If you plant in furrows, open a trench 10-15 cm deep. Place seed potatoes with the sprout facing up, then cover with 5-8 cm of soil. Don't plant too deepâdeep planting delays emergence. After planting, ridge soil around plants when they are 15-20 cm tall. This is called hilling. It prevents tubers from turning green and improves drainage. In Africa, many farmers plant on flat ground and hill later. But raised beds are better for wet areas. If you use a tractor, a ridger can form beds and plant at the same time. For small plots, use a hoe. The seedbed should be ready just before the rains start, so potatoes get moisture. If you plant in dry soil, irrigate lightly after planting. But avoid overwateringâit causes rot. Also, ensure the seedbed is free of weeds. Weeds compete for nutrients and water. You can pre-irrigate to germinate weeds, then remove them before planting. A fine seedbed also helps tuber skin quality. Lumpy soil causes misshapen tubers. So take time to prepare properly. After planting, mulch with straw or grass to conserve moisture and suppress weeds. But don't mulch heavily in wet areasâit can keep soil too wet. A well-prepared seedbed gives potatoes a strong start.

Practical Reference: Soil Preparation and Site Selection for Potato Farming in Africa

Potato (Solanum tuberosum) is one of Africa's fastest-growing food and cash crops. From the highlands of Kenya, Ethiopia, and Rwanda to the cool escarpments of Cameroon and the Mediterranean-influenced zones of North Africa, potatoes reward farmers who invest in the soil before the first seed tuber touches the ground. This Practical Reference module consolidates the six core lessons of Soil Preparation and Site Selection into an actionable, business-first guide. It is designed for commercial smallholders, aggregators, and agripreneurs who treat potato farming as a business â not a subsistence activity.

Poor site selection and inadequate soil preparation account for a significant share of yield losses across African potato value chains. A farmer who skips soil testing, plants on a waterlogged slope, or tills too shallowly will lose money twice: once on inputs and again on a depressed harvest. The reference below gives you the decision tools, benchmarks, and checklists to avoid those losses.

Why Site Selection Is a Business Decision

Potatoes demand loose, well-drained, deep soils with good organic matter. In Africa, the best potato zones are typically 1,800â3,000 metres above sea level in East Africa, or cool, high-altitude areas in the tropics and subtropics. But altitude alone is not enough. You must evaluate:

  • Previous crop history â avoid fields that hosted tomatoes, peppers, or eggplants in the last 3â4 years (shared diseases such as bacterial wilt and late blight).
  • Soil texture â sandy loams and loams are ideal; heavy clays need organic matter and drainage work.
  • Slope and aspect â gentle slopes (2â8%) with good air drainage reduce frost and disease pressure.
  • Water access â irrigation potential for dry spells, but no risk of flooding.
  • Market proximity and road access â potatoes are bulky and perishable; transport economics matter.

Soil Testing and pH Adjustment

Potatoes tolerate a slightly acidic soil pH of 5.2â6.5. This range suppresses common scab (Streptomyces scabies) and improves nutrient availability. In African highlands, soils are often acidic (pH 4.5â5.5), which can lock up phosphorus and molybdenum. Liming with agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) or dolomitic lime raises pH, but must be based on a soil test â over-liming increases scab risk.

Soil testing should be done 2â3 months before planting. In Kenya, KALRO and the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organization offer subsidised testing; in Rwanda, RAB (Rwanda Agriculture Board) provides similar services. Where laboratory access is limited, portable pH meters and NPK test kits are a practical alternative for smallholders.

Tillage Practices for Potato Production

Potatoes need a deep, loose seedbed. Primary tillage should break compacted layers to 30â40 cm, allowing tubers to bulk without restriction. In African smallholder systems, hand hoeing and ox-drawn ploughs are common; where tractors are available, a chisel plough followed by a rotavator gives excellent results. Avoid excessive tillage â it destroys soil structure, accelerates organic matter loss, and increases erosion on sloping fields.

Key tillage principles:

  • Primary tillage: 30â40 cm depth, 2â4 weeks before planting, to allow soil to settle.
  • Secondary tillage: break clods, level the field, and incorporate organic matter.
  • Ridging: form ridges 60â75 cm apart for planting; ridges improve drainage and tuber development.
  • Conservation tillage: on steep slopes, minimum tillage with cover crops reduces erosion.

Incorporating Organic Matter and Cover Crops

Organic matter is the backbone of potato soil health. It improves water-holding capacity, buffers pH, feeds soil biology, and reduces erosion. In Africa, well-rotted cattle manure, poultry litter, and compost are the most accessible sources. Apply 10â20 t/ha and incorporate 4â6 weeks before planting.

Cover crops such as lablab, mucuna, and cereal rye can be grown in rotation or as green manure. They fix nitrogen, suppress weeds, and add biomass. A rotation of potato â legume cover crop â cereal â potato is a proven model in Kenyan and Rwandan highlands.

Soil Drainage and Erosion Control

Potatoes are highly sensitive to waterlogging. Even 24â48 hours of saturated soil can cause tuber rot and yield loss. On sloping land, contour ridges, cut-off drains, and grass strips are essential. On flat, heavy soils, raised beds or furrow drainage may be necessary. Erosion control is not just agronomic â it protects your investment. A single heavy rain on a bare, sloping potato field can wash away topsoil, nutrients, and seed tubers.

Preparing Seedbeds for Planting

A well-prepared seedbed is firm, level, and free of large clods. Final preparation should happen 1â2 weeks before planting. Ridges should be 60â75 cm apart, with planting depth of 8â12 cm. Seed tubers should be 30â40 g, disease-free, and properly sprouted. In dry areas, plant on flat beds and ridge later; in wet areas, plant on ridges from the start.
