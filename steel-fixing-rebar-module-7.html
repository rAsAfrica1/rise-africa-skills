1. Module 7 Lesson 1: Reading Bar Bending Schedules and Setting Out on African Sites

Welcome to Module 7 of your Steel Fixing training. In this first lesson we focus on the document that controls almost everything a steel fixer does: the bar bending schedule, often called the BBS. On many African sites, from Lagos to Nairobi to Accra to Johannesburg, the BBS is the single most important piece of paper in the rebar yard. If you can read it correctly, you save steel, save time, and avoid costly rework. If you cannot read it, you will cut wrong, bend wrong, and place wrong, and the engineer will reject your work. This lesson teaches you how to read a BBS, how to set out a grid on site, and how to organise your team so that the right bars reach the right place at the right time. First, understand what a BBS contains. A standard bar bending schedule lists bar mark numbers, bar diameter in millimetres, shape code, number of bars required, cutting length, bending dimensions, and the total weight. Some schedules also show the member the bar belongs to, such as footing F1, column C2, or beam B3. On smaller African projects, the BBS may be handwritten by the engineer or site agent. On larger projects, it is printed from CAD or BIM software. Either way, your job is to translate those numbers into steel on the ground. Start by checking the revision number and date. Rebar drawings get revised often, and using an old schedule is one of the most common and expensive mistakes on site. Confirm with your foreman or engineer that you are working from the latest revision. Next, check the shape codes. Shape codes describe how a bar is bent. Common codes include straight bars, L-bars, U-bars, stirrups, and links. In many African countries, the shape codes follow British Standard BS 8666 or a local adaptation. Learn the codes used on your project and keep a laminated copy in your site office. When you read a BBS, always cross-check the cutting length against the shape. A bar with a 90-degree bend needs a deduction for the bend, and a bar with a 135-degree hook needs a longer allowance. If the cutting length looks too short for the shape, stop and ask. Do not guess. Now let us talk about setting out. Setting out means marking the positions of footings, columns, beams, and slabs on the ground or on formwork before you place steel. On most African sites, setting out is done with a tape, string line, chalk, and sometimes a theodolite or total station. As a steel fixer, you need to know the grid lines. Grid lines are usually labelled A, B, C on one axis and 1, 2, 3 on the other. Column C2 sits at the intersection of grid line C and grid line 2. When you set out, always work from the grid lines, not from the edge of the excavation, because excavations can be over-dug or under-dug. Use a plumb bob or a laser level to transfer grid lines down into the excavation. Mark the centre of each footing with a peg and a nail. Then mark the extent of the footing with chalk or string. For columns, mark the column faces on the formwork or on the starter bars. For beams and slabs, mark the beam centre lines and the slab edges. A good steel fixer keeps a notebook with all grid dimensions and checks them against the drawing before placing any steel. Organisation is where African steel fixing teams win or lose money. In the yard, sort bars by bar mark and diameter. Use timber dunnage to keep bars off the ground so they do not rust or get covered in mud. Label each bundle with a tag showing the bar mark, diameter, and member. When you move bars to the work face, move them in the order they will be placed. Do not dump a mixed bundle at the bottom of an excavation and expect the team to sort it out in the heat. Use a small crane, a telehandler, or a team of labourers with a rope and a bar basket. On sites without machinery, use a ramp and a controlled slide, but never throw bars from height. Safety is part of the job. Rebar is heavy, and a bundle of 32 mm bars can weigh several tonnes. Always use gloves, boots, and eye protection. When cutting with a hacksaw or a bar cutter, keep your hands clear. When bending with a bar bender, stand to the side. In hot African climates, start early, take shade breaks, and drink water. Dehydration causes mistakes. Finally, remember the business side. A steel fixer who reads the BBS correctly and sets out accurately becomes a foreman or a subcontractor. A steel fixer who guesses stays a labourer. Every hour you spend learning to read drawings is an hour that increases your daily rate. In the next lesson, we will cover cutting and bending, including how to use bar cutters, bar benders, and how to check your work with a tape and a try square.

2. Module 7 Lesson 2: Cutting, Bending, and Fabricating Rebar Accurately

In this lesson we move from reading the bar bending schedule to actually cutting and bending steel. This is the heart of steel fixing. On African sites, cutting and bending is done in three main ways: manually with a hacksaw and a bending jig, with a manual bar cutter and bar bender, or with an electric or diesel rebar cutting and bending machine. Your job is to choose the right method for the bar diameter, the quantity, and the site conditions, and then to produce bars that match the schedule exactly. Start with cutting. The cutting length on the BBS is the length of the bar before bending, measured along the centre line. For a straight bar, the cutting length is simply the required length plus any laps. For a bent bar, the cutting length is the sum of the straight lengths plus the bend allowances minus the bend deductions. Most African projects use the standard deductions from BS 8666 or a local code. For a 90-degree bend, the deduction depends on the bar diameter and the bend radius. For a 135-degree hook, the allowance is longer. If you are not sure, ask the engineer for the bend deduction table. Never cut by eye. Measure twice and cut once. When cutting with a hacksaw, use a fine-tooth blade for small bars and a coarse-tooth blade for large bars. Clamp the bar in a vice or a bar stand so it does not move. Use cutting fluid or water to keep the blade cool. When cutting with a manual bar cutter, place the bar between the jaws, align the cutting edge with your mark, and pull the handle firmly. Do not extend the handle with a pipe unless the manufacturer allows it, because you can break the cutter. When cutting with an electric or diesel machine, follow the manufacturer instructions, keep the machine on level ground, and never put your hands near the cutting blade. After cutting, deburr the ends with a file or a grinder so that the bars do not cut the hands of the team. Now bending. Bending can be done cold or hot. Cold bending is standard for rebar up to about 32 mm. Hot bending is used for larger bars or when the bar is very stiff, but it must be done carefully because overheating can weaken the steel. For cold bending, use a bending jig or a bar bender. A bending jig is a simple steel frame with pins that you can set to the required radius. A bar bender is a machine with a bending head and a control lever. On many African sites, the bar bender is manual, with a long handle for leverage. When bending, always mark the start and end of the bend on the bar. Place the bar in the bender so that the mark aligns with the centre of the bending pin. Pull the handle smoothly and steadily. Do not jerk the handle, because you can crack the bar or damage the machine. For stirrups and links, use a stirrup bending jig. This jig has pins set to the required dimensions, and you can produce many identical stirrups quickly. Check the first stirrup against the drawing before you produce the rest. For large quantities, a stirrup bending machine is faster and more accurate. After bending, check the bar against the BBS. Measure the overall length, the leg lengths, and the angles. Use a tape and a try square. For 90-degree bends, the try square should sit flush against both legs. For 135-degree hooks, use a template or a protractor. If a bar is out of tolerance, do not force it into place. Reject it and bend a new one. Forcing a wrong bar into position can crack the concrete or leave insufficient cover. Now let us talk about fabrication on site. Fabrication means assembling bars into cages or mats before placing them. On African sites, fabrication is often done in a shaded area near the yard. You need a flat, clean surface, such as a concrete slab or a timber platform. For column cages, assemble the vertical bars and the links on the ground, then lift the cage into position. For beam cages, assemble the top bars, bottom bars, and stirrups, then lower the cage into the formwork. For slab mats, assemble the bottom mat, then the top mat, and connect them with chairs or spacers. When assembling, use tying wire, usually 1.6 mm or 2.0 mm annealed wire. Cut the wire into lengths of about 300 mm. Use a tying hook or a pair of pliers. The standard tie is the double tie, which is strong and does not slip. For mats, tie every intersection at the edges and every second or third intersection in the middle. Do not overtighten the wire, because you can cut into the bar or break the wire. Do not leave sharp ends sticking out, because they can injure workers and reduce cover. Quality control is essential. Keep a sample of each bent bar shape near the yard as a reference. Check the first bar of every batch. Record the bar mark, diameter, and quantity in a fabrication log. If the engineer or clerk of works visits, show them the log and the samples. This builds trust and reduces rejections. Safety in the fabrication yard is critical. Wear gloves, boots, and eye protection. Keep the yard tidy. Store cutting and bending machines on level ground. Use lockout procedures when maintaining machines. In hot climates, provide shade and water. Finally, think about productivity. A well-organised fabrication yard can produce two to three times more bars per day than a disorganised one. Sort your bars by mark, set up your jigs and machines in a logical sequence, and assign clear roles: one person cuts, one person bends, one person assembles, one person checks. With practice, your team will become faster and more accurate, and you will win more work.

3. Module 7 Lesson 3: Placing and Fixing Rebar in Footings, Columns, and Beams

Now that you can read the bar bending schedule and fabricate bars, it is time to place and fix them in the actual structure. This lesson covers footings, columns, and beams, which are the three most common reinforced concrete elements on African construction sites. We will discuss setting out, cover, laps, tying, and the checks you must do before the concrete pour. Start with footings. Footings spread the load of the building into the ground. They can be pad footings, strip footings, or raft footings. On most African sites, pad footings are square or rectangular, and they have a bottom mat of bars in both directions. Some footings also have top bars, especially if the footing is deep or if the ground is weak. Before placing steel, check the excavation. The bottom must be level and compacted. If there is loose soil, remove it and replace it with compacted hardcore or lean concrete. Then place the bottom mat. The bars should be spaced according to the BBS, usually 100 mm, 150 mm, or 200 mm centre to centre. Use chalk or a marking stick to mark the spacing on the formwork or on a timber batten. Place the bars in one direction first, then the other direction on top. Tie every intersection at the edges and every second intersection in the middle. Use concrete cover blocks or plastic spacers to lift the mat off the ground. The cover is usually 50 mm for footings in contact with earth. Without cover blocks, the steel will sit on the ground, rust, and have no protection. For columns, the vertical bars transfer load from the beams to the footings. The links hold the vertical bars in position and resist buckling. Start by checking the starter bars. Starter bars are the bars that project from the footing or the slab below. They must be in the correct position and at the correct height. If they are bent or misplaced, you must straighten them carefully or, if the engineer allows, drill and grout new bars. Do not simply bend them back and forth, because you can crack the steel. Next, place the vertical bars. They should be cut to the correct length, with a lap at the top and bottom. Laps are usually 40 to 50 times the bar diameter. For a 16 mm bar, a lap is about 640 to 800 mm. Stagger the laps so that they do not all occur at the same height. Then place the links. Links are usually rectangular or square, with 135-degree hooks. The hooks should alternate around the cage. Space the links according to the BBS, usually 100 mm, 150 mm, or 200 mm. Tie the links to the vertical bars with double ties. Use a plumb bob or a laser level to check that the cage is vertical. Use timber spacers or cover blocks to maintain the cover, usually 40 mm for columns. For beams, the reinforcement resists bending and shear. A typical beam has top bars, bottom bars, and stirrups. The bottom bars resist tension at mid-span, and the top bars resist tension at the supports. The stirrups resist shear and hold the bars in position. Start by placing the bottom bars on cover blocks or chairs. Then place the stirrups. The stirrups should be spaced according to the BBS, with closer spacing near the supports. Then place the top bars. Tie the stirrups to the top and bottom bars. For deep beams, you may need side bars and cross ties. Check the lap lengths and the anchorage lengths at the supports. Anchorage is the length of bar that extends into the support to develop the force. It is usually 40 to 50 times the bar diameter, but it can be longer for large bars or for seismic zones. Before the concrete pour, you must do a pre-pour check. This is a formal inspection with the engineer, the clerk of works, and the concrete team. Check the following: the bar diameter and spacing match the BBS; the laps are in the correct position and length; the cover blocks are in place and the cover is correct; the cages are stable and will not move during the pour; the formwork is clean and oiled; and the starter bars for the next element are in place. Take photographs and record the inspection in your site diary. If the engineer asks for a change, get it in writing. Never allow a change without a written instruction, because it can affect your payment. Safety during placing and fixing is important. Use gloves when handling bars. Use a bar bending machine or a bar bender for any bends on site. Do not cut bars with an angle grinder unless you have eye protection and a fire watch. When working at height, use a harness and a scaffold. When working in an excavation, check for collapsing sides and use shoring if necessary. Finally, think about productivity and quality. A good steel fixing team places steel in a logical sequence: footings first, then columns, then beams, then slabs. They keep the work face clean and the bars organised. They check their work before the engineer arrives. This reduces rework and keeps the project on schedule. In the next lesson, we will cover slabs, walls, and stairs, and then we will finish with quality control and business skills.

4. Module 7 Lesson 4: Fixing Rebar in Slabs, Walls, and Stairs

In this lesson we continue with placing and fixing, but now we focus on slabs, walls, and stairs. These elements are common in African residential, commercial, and industrial projects. They require careful setting out, correct cover, and strong tying. We will also discuss how to work with mesh reinforcement, which is increasingly used in slabs and walls. Start with slabs. A slab is a flat horizontal element that carries floor or roof loads. Slabs can be one-way or two-way, solid or ribbed, and they can be reinforced with bars or with mesh. On most African sites, solid slabs are reinforced with a bottom mat and a top mat. The bottom mat resists tension at mid-span, and the top mat resists tension at the supports. The mats are connected with chairs or spacers. Before placing steel, check the formwork. It must be clean, oiled, and at the correct level. Check the props and the bearers. Then mark the spacing on the formwork. Place the bottom mat first. The bars in the shorter direction usually go at the bottom, and the bars in the longer direction go on top. Tie every intersection at the edges and every second or third intersection in the middle. Use cover blocks or plastic spacers to maintain the cover, usually 20 mm to 25 mm for slabs. Then place the top mat. Use chairs to support the top mat at the correct height. The chairs should be strong enough to support the weight of the bars and the workers. For mesh reinforcement, unroll the mesh and cut it to size. Overlap the mesh by at least one square plus 50 mm. Tie the overlaps with wire. Mesh is faster to place than bars, but it must be supported on chairs or spacers to maintain the cover. For walls, the reinforcement resists horizontal and vertical forces. A typical wall has vertical bars on both faces and horizontal bars on both faces. The bars are connected with links or cross ties. Start by setting out the wall centre line and the wall faces. Place the vertical bars first, then the horizontal bars. Tie the intersections. Use cover blocks to maintain the cover, usually 25 mm to 40 mm. For walls that will be backfilled, such as retaining walls, the cover on the earth face should be 50 mm. Check the lap lengths and the anchorage at the top and bottom of the wall. For stairs, the reinforcement follows the slope of the stair. A typical stair has a waist slab with bottom bars and top bars, and it may have a landing slab. Start by setting out the stair on the formwork. Mark the positions of the risers and the treads. Place the bottom bars along the slope, then the top bars. Tie the bars to the transverse bars. Use cover blocks to maintain the cover. Check the anchorage at the top and bottom of the stair. For all these elements, the same quality control rules apply. Check the bar diameter, spacing, laps, and cover. Check that the cages are stable. Check that the starter bars for the next element are in place. Take photographs and record the inspection. Now let us talk about working with mesh. Mesh is supplied in sheets or rolls. It is usually made of 6 mm to 12 mm bars welded in a grid. Mesh is common in slabs, walls, and pavements. When placing mesh, wear gloves, because the cut ends are sharp. Use a mesh cutter or a grinder to cut the mesh. Do not use a hacksaw, because it is too slow. Overlap the mesh by at least one square plus 50 mm, and tie the overlaps with wire. Support the mesh on chairs or spacers. Do not allow the mesh to sit on the formwork, because it will have no cover. For large areas, consider using a mesh laying machine or a team of workers with a mesh roller. Productivity on slabs, walls, and stairs depends on planning. Before you start, make sure you have enough bars, mesh, chairs, cover blocks, and tying wire. Set up a small fabrication area near the work face. Assign clear roles: one team places the bottom mat, one team places the top mat, one team ties, and one team checks. Keep the work face clean. Remove offcuts and empty wire coils. At the end of the day, cover the steel with a tarpaulin if rain is expected. Safety is critical. Slabs and stairs are often at height. Use edge protection, such as guardrails and toe boards. Use a harness when working near an edge. Do not walk on the top mat without a walkway, because you can bend the bars and reduce the cover. In hot climates, provide shade and water. Finally, think about the business. Slabs, walls, and stairs are high-volume elements. If you can place them quickly and correctly, you can earn more per day. If you make mistakes, you will spend time cutting and re-tying, and you may lose money. Learn to read the drawings, plan your work, and check your work. In the next lesson, we will cover quality control, testing, and how to grow your steel fixing business in Africa.

5. Module 7 Lesson 5: Quality Control, Testing, and Growing Your Steel Fixing Business in Africa

In this final lesson of Module 7, we bring everything together. You have learned to read the bar bending schedule, cut and bend bars, and place and fix them in footings, columns, beams, slabs, walls, and stairs. Now we focus on quality control, testing, and the business skills that will help you grow from a steel fixer to a foreman, a subcontractor, or a business owner. Quality control starts with the steel itself. When steel arrives on site, check the delivery note against the order. Check the bar diameter, the length, and the quantity. Check for rust, cracks, and bends. Rust is normal on the surface, but deep pitting is not acceptable. If the steel is heavily rusted, the engineer may require it to be cleaned or rejected. Check the mill certificates. These certificates show the chemical composition and the mechanical properties of the steel. They are important for structural steel. Keep them in your site file. Next, check the fabrication. Use a tape and a try square to check the bent bars. Check the cutting length, the leg lengths, and the angles. Check the first bar of every batch. Keep a sample of each shape. Record the results in a fabrication log. If a bar is out of tolerance, reject it and bend a new one. Do not force a wrong bar into place. Then check the placement. Before the concrete pour, do a pre-pour inspection. Check the bar diameter and spacing against the BBS. Check the laps and the anchorage. Check the cover blocks and the cover. Check that the cages are stable. Check that the starter bars for the next element are in place. Take photographs and record the inspection in your site diary. If the engineer asks for a change, get it in writing. Testing is part of quality control. On most African projects, the engineer will require concrete cube tests and, sometimes, steel tensile tests. Steel tensile tests are done in a laboratory. A sample of steel is pulled until it breaks. The test measures the yield strength, the ultimate strength, and the elongation. The results must meet the project specification. If you are a subcontractor, you may be asked to provide test certificates. Keep copies of all test results. If you are responsible for ordering steel, buy from a reputable supplier. In many African countries, there are local steel mills and importers. Local steel is often cheaper, but you must check the quality. Imported steel may be more expensive, but it may have better certificates. Do not buy steel from a roadside vendor unless you can verify the quality. Now let us talk about business skills. Steel fixing is a skilled trade, and skilled trades are in demand across Africa. To grow your business, you need to do three things: deliver quality, manage your time, and manage your money. Quality means doing the job right the first time. Time means planning your work so that you do not delay the concrete pour. Money means knowing your costs and your prices. Start by calculating your costs. Your costs include labour, tools, transport, and consumables such as tying wire, cover blocks, and cutting blades. Add a margin for profit and risk. Then quote your price. Do not quote too low, because you will lose money. Do not quote too high, because you will lose the job. Learn to estimate. A simple way to estimate is to use a rate per tonne of steel fixed. On many African sites, the rate for fixing steel is between 80 and 200 US dollars per tonne, depending on the complexity and the location. For simple footings and slabs, the rate is lower. For complex columns and stairs, the rate is higher. Use your past projects to build your own rate. Next, manage your team. A good steel fixing team has a foreman, a few skilled fixers, and a few labourers. The foreman reads the drawings, sets out the work, and checks the quality. The skilled fixers cut, bend, and tie. The labourers move bars and clean the work face. Pay your team on time. Provide safety equipment. Train your team. A trained team is faster and safer. Then manage your relationships. Build good relationships with engineers, clerks of works, main contractors, and suppliers. Deliver on time. Communicate clearly. If there is a problem, raise it early. Do not hide mistakes. Engineers respect honesty. Finally, think about the future. Africa is urbanising fast. There is a huge demand for housing, roads, bridges, schools, and hospitals. Steel fixing is at the centre of all of this. If you can read drawings, fix steel accurately, and run a team, you will always have work. Consider registering your business, getting a tax number, and opening a business bank account. Consider buying your own tools: a bar cutter, a bar bender, a grinder, and a set of hand tools. Consider taking further training in rebar detailing, BIM, or project management. The steel fixing trade can take you from a labourer to a business owner. It starts with the skills you have learned in this module. Practise them, teach them to others, and keep learning. Thank you for completing Module 7. We wish you success on your next project.

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fdecea; padding:14px; margin:20px 0; font-family:Arial;">
<strong style="color:#b00020; font-size:15px;"> SAFETY WARNING REBAR HANDLING AND CUTTING</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; font-size:13px; color:#333;">Rebar is a hazardous material on site. Follow these mandatory precautions:</p>
<ul style="font-size:13px; color:#333; margin:8px 0 0 18px;">
<li><strong>Never</strong> cut rebar with an angle grinder without a full face shield, leather gloves, and hearing protection. Sparks and metal fragments cause eye injuries and burns a leading cause of site accidents in African construction.</li>
<li><strong>Cap all protruding bar ends</strong> at ground level with plastic safety caps or timber. Impalement injuries from uncapped starter bars are a documented cause of fatalities on sites in Kenya, South Africa, and Nigeria.</li>
<li><strong>Do not weld</strong> high-yield (Y) rebar unless the engineer has approved a welding procedure. Welding changes the metallurgy and can cause brittle failure.</li>
<li><strong>Bend bars cold.</strong> Never heat rebar to bend it heating destroys the yield strength and is a common shortcut on informal sites.</li>
<li><strong>Stack bars safely.</strong> A falling bundle of Y25 bars weighs over a tonne. Use timber bearers and never stack higher than 1.5 m.</li>
<li><strong>Site first aid:</strong> keep a stocked first aid kit and eye wash station within 30 seconds of the cutting area. Report all cuts and eye injuries immediately.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist Before You Fix Any Bar</h3>

<ol style="font-family:Arial; font-size:14px; line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Check the bar bending schedule (BBS) against the drawing.</strong> Confirm bar mark, diameter, shape code, and quantity. If they don't match, stop and ask the engineer do not guess.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm cover spacers are on site.</strong> Plastic or concrete spacers must be the correct size for the exposure condition (see Table 1). Never use broken bricks or timber offcuts as cover.</li>
<li><strong>Verify lap lengths with a tape.</strong> Measure the actual lap, not the assumed lap. Under-length laps are the most common structural defect on African sites.</li>
<li><strong>Check bar spacing before tying.</strong> Use a spacing gauge or a cut timber template. Uneven spacing causes concrete honeycombing and weak sections.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect bends and hooks.</strong> Minimum bend radius = 2d for stirrups, 3.5d for main bars. Hook length = 10d minimum (or as specified).</li>
<li><strong>Tie securely but not excessively.</strong> Use 1.6 mm black annealed wire. Cross-tie at corners and every 4th intersection. Over-tying wastes wire and time.</li>
<li><strong>Check the cage is square and plumb.</strong> Use a spirit level and diagonal measurements. A twisted column cage cannot be corrected after the pour.</li>
<li><strong>Photograph the completed cage before the pour.</strong> This is your evidence for payment, your defence against blame, and your portfolio for the next tender.</li>
</ol>
