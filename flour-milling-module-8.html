1. Bleaching and Maturing Agents

Freshly milled wheat flour is not ready for baking. It produces dense, sticky dough and small, pale loaves. In Africa, where most flour is sold within weeks of milling, bleaching and maturing agents fix this fast. Bleaching agents like benzoyl peroxide (up to 50 ppm) whiten flour by oxidizing carotenoid pigments. Maturing agents like potassium bromate (now banned in many countries) or azodicarbonamide (ADA, up to 45 ppm) strengthen gluten by forming disulfide bonds. In Nigeria, Ghana, and Kenya, the standard maturing agent is ADA, often combined with ascorbic acid. A small mill in Kampala can add 20 ppm ADA using a micro-doser on the flour line. Without a doser, pre-blend 1 gram of ADA with 1 kg of flour, then add that premix at 45 g per 100 kg of flour. Mix for 10 minutes in a ribbon blender. Let the treated flour rest for 24â48 hours before packing. This rest allows the maturing agent to work and any residual gas to escape. If you skip maturing, your bread will have poor volume and a tight crumb. For bleaching only, benzoyl peroxide is fast but does not improve baking quality. Many African bakers prefer unbleached flour for whole wheat, but white bread sells better when bright. Always store treated flour in dry, cool conditions. Test each batch by baking a small loaf: if it rises well and has a fine crumb, your treatment is correct. If the loaf is flat, increase ADA slightly but never exceed 45 ppm. Remember: bromate is illegal in most of Africa; use ADA or ascorbic acid instead.

2. Oxidizing and Reducing Agents

Oxidizing agents make dough stronger and more elastic. Reducing agents do the opposite: they make dough softer and more extensible. In African bakeries, you use both depending on the product. For bread, you want oxidation. For biscuits, cakes, and pastries, you often want reduction. Common oxidizing agents: ascorbic acid (vitamin C), potassium iodate, and calcium peroxide. Ascorbic acid is the safest and most popular in Africa. At 50â200 ppm, it improves dough strength and gas retention. It works best in dough with some yeast activity, because yeast produces an enzyme that converts ascorbic acid to dehydroascorbic acid, the active oxidizer. Add ascorbic acid at 100 ppm for pan bread; for baguettes, use 150 ppm. Reducing agents include L-cysteine (up to 90 ppm) and sodium metabisulfite. L-cysteine breaks disulfide bonds, making dough easier to roll and shape. Use 30â50 ppm for biscuit dough to prevent shrinkage. In a small Kenyan bakery, add 20 g of L-cysteine to 100 kg of flour for soft dough. Always pre-blend with a little flour before adding to the mixer. Never mix oxidizing and reducing agents in the same premix; they cancel each other. Store ascorbic acid in a sealed container away from light and heat. For reducing agents, keep them dry; moisture makes them release sulfur dioxide, which smells bad and weakens gluten too much. Test with a farinograph or extensograph if you have one. If not, do a simple dough stretch test: a well-oxidized dough stretches without tearing; an over-reduced dough is sticky and weak. Adjust by 10 ppm at a time.

3. Enzymes in Flour Treatment

Enzymes are proteins that speed up reactions. In flour treatment, they improve dough handling, bread volume, and shelf life. The main enzymes used in Africa are alpha-amylase, xylanase, lipase, and glucose oxidase. Alpha-amylase breaks down starch into sugars, feeding yeast. Fungal alpha-amylase is added at 10â50 ppm. Use it when your flour has low falling number (sprout damage). For normal flour, add 20 ppm to get better crust color and volume. Xylanase breaks down hemicellulose, making dough more extensible and easier to machine. Add 20â40 ppm for bread and chapati. Lipase improves dough stability and crumb softness, especially for long shelf life. Use 10â30 ppm. Glucose oxidase strengthens gluten by producing hydrogen peroxide. It is a clean-label alternative to ADA. Add 5â15 ppm. In a Tanzanian mill, a common blend is 30 ppm fungal alpha-amylase + 20 ppm xylanase + 10 ppm lipase. Mix these into a premix with 1 kg of flour, then add to 100 kg of flour in the blender. Enzymes are sensitive: store them in a cool, dry place (below 25Â°C). Never add enzymes directly to hot water; heat above 60Â°C destroys them. Add enzymes to the flour before the dough mixer, not after. For small bakeries, buy a ready-made enzyme blend from a supplier like Novozymes or DSM. Test each new batch by baking: if the dough is too slack, reduce xylanase; if the bread is pale and flat, increase alpha-amylase. Always follow the supplierâs dosage sheet.

4. Vitamin and Mineral Fortification

Fortification adds essential vitamins and minerals to flour to fight malnutrition. In Africa, many countries mandate fortification of wheat and maize flour. Common nutrients: iron (as ferrous fumarate or electrolytic iron), zinc oxide, folic acid, vitamin A (as retinyl palmitate), and B vitamins (thiamine, riboflavin, niacin). The standard premix for wheat flour in Nigeria includes: 30 ppm iron, 20 ppm zinc, 1.5 ppm folic acid, 2 ppm vitamin A, and B vitamins. You add this premix at 0.5â1.0 kg per metric ton of flour. Use a micro-doser or a pre-blend with 10 kg of flour, then mix for 15 minutes. Fortification does not change baking quality if done correctly. But too much iron can cause rancidity; too much vitamin A can make dough orange. Store premix in a cool, dark place; use within 6 months. In Kenya, the Ministry of Health requires fortification of all wheat flour for human consumption. A small mill in Nakuru uses a volumetric feeder to add 0.8 kg of premix per ton. They test every batch with a rapid iron test kit (e.g., from BioAnalyt). If you sell flour in a country with mandatory fortification, you must comply or face fines. Even without a mandate, fortification adds value: you can label your flour as âfortifiedâ and charge 5â10% more. Train your workers to wear masks when handling premix; inhaling vitamin A powder is harmful. Always record the batch number and amount added. For export to the EU, check maximum levels: vitamin A is limited to 3 ppm in some markets.

5. Emulsifiers and Dough Conditioners

Emulsifiers and dough conditioners make dough stronger, softer, and longer-lasting. In Africa, the most common are DATEM (diacetyl tartaric acid esters of monoglycerides), SSL (sodium stearoyl lactylate), and lecithin. DATEM strengthens gluten and improves volume. Use 0.3â0.5% of flour weight (3â5 g per kg). SSL improves crumb softness and shelf life; use 0.2â0.5%. Lecithin (from soy or sunflower) is a natural emulsifier; use 0.5â1.0% for better mixing tolerance. For a 50 kg bag of flour, add 150â250 g of DATEM. Pre-blend with 1 kg of flour, then add to the mixer. Dough conditioners also include ascorbic acid, enzymes, and reducing agents (see other lessons). A typical African bread improver contains: 100 ppm ascorbic acid, 20 ppm fungal alpha-amylase, 0.3% DATEM, and 0.2% SSL. This blend costs about $2â3 per kg and treats 100 kg of flour. In a Ghanaian bakery, using this blend increased loaf volume by 20% and extended shelf life from 3 to 7 days. For chapati and flatbreads, skip DATEM and use lecithin only; too much emulsifier makes the dough tough. Store emulsifiers in sealed bags; they absorb moisture and clump. Test by baking: if bread is gummy, reduce SSL; if volume is low, increase DATEM. Always follow local regulations: some countries limit DATEM to 0.5% and SSL to 0.5%. Never exceed these limits; they can cause off-flavors and legal problems.

6. Regulatory Aspects of Flour Additives

Every flour additive must be approved by your countryâs food safety authority. In Africa, key regulators are NAFDAC (Nigeria), KEBS (Kenya), FDA Ghana, and the South African Bureau of Standards. They set maximum levels for each additive. For example, NAFDAC allows potassium bromate? Noâbromate is banned in Nigeria. ADA is allowed up to 45 ppm. Ascorbic acid is allowed at good manufacturing practice (GMP) levels. Enzymes are generally recognized as safe (GRAS) if from approved sources. Fortification is mandatory in Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, and several others. You must register your flour product and list all additives on the label. Labels must show: additive name, purpose (e.g., âdough conditionerâ), and amount added. If you use a premix, keep the supplierâs certificate of analysis. Inspectors can visit your mill without notice. They will check your batch records, additive storage, and final product. Fines for non-compliance can be high: in Kenya, up to KES 1 million or prison. In Nigeria, NAFDAC can shut down your mill. To stay compliant: (1) Buy additives from reputable suppliers with certificates. (2) Keep a logbook of every addition: date, batch, additive, amount. (3) Train staff on safe handling. (4) Test finished flour for additive levels if possible. For export to the EU or US, you must meet their rules: EU bans ADA and bromate; US allows ADA up to 45 ppm but bans bromate. So if you export, use only ascorbic acid and enzymes. Many African mills now switch to clean-label additives to access premium markets. Always check the latest regulations online or ask your local standards office. Never guess.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Flour Treatment and Additives for African Millers

This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in the Flour Treatment and Additives module into a working document for mill managers, quality controllers, and production supervisors. It is written for the African milling context, where flour is often sold into informal and formal markets, where regulatory capacity varies by country, and where cost per tonne and shelf life determine whether a mill wins or loses a customer. The focus is on decisions that affect margins, compliance, and product performance.

Flour treatment is not an end in itself. Every additive must earn its place by solving a specific problem: poor dough strength in low-protein wheat, variable starch damage after milling, vitamin deficiency in the population, or bread that stales before it reaches the consumer. The miller who understands the function of each class of additive can negotiate with suppliers, defend prices, and avoid regulatory penalties.

Why Treatment Matters in African Milling

African mills typically process wheat with variable protein quality, often imported from different origins. A single mill may receive wheat from the Black Sea, East Africa, or South America within the same quarter. This variability forces millers to use treatment agents to standardise dough performance. The business case is simple: a consistent loaf volume and crumb structure protects bakery contracts, reduces customer complaints, and allows the mill to sell at a premium over commodity flour.

At the same time, fortification is increasingly mandatory. Nigeria, South Africa, Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, and Ghana have national fortification standards for wheat flour, usually requiring iron, zinc, folic acid, and B-vitamins. Compliance is not optional for large mills selling into formal retail. The cost of premix is typically 0.5â2% of flour price, but non-compliance can result in product seizure, fines, or delisting from supermarket chains.

Bleaching and Maturing Agents

Bleaching agents remove yellow pigments, while maturing agents oxidise gluten sulfhydryl groups to form stronger disulphide bonds. In Africa, where consumers often equate whiteness with quality, bleaching can command a price premium. However, regulatory divergence is a real risk. A mill exporting to the EU cannot use benzoyl peroxide or ADA. A mill selling only domestically may use them if permitted. The practical rule is to verify the destination market before purchasing premix.

Ascorbic acid is the safest maturing agent for cross-border trade. It is permitted in most jurisdictions, has no residual toxicity concern, and works well in high-speed bread lines. For mills without dosing equipment, ascorbic acid can be included in a dry improver blend.

Oxidizing and Reducing Agents

Oxidizing agents strengthen dough, which is essential when wheat protein is weak or when the mill is blending low-protein wheat with higher-protein stock. Reducing agents do the opposite: they relax gluten, which is useful for biscuits, crackers, and some flatbreads. In West Africa, where biscuit and cracker production is growing, L-cysteine and sodium metabisulphite are used to control dough extensibility.

The business decision is straightforward. If the mill sells bread flour, oxidizing agents protect loaf volume and reduce returns. If the mill sells biscuit flour, reducing agents improve machinability and reduce breakage. Using the wrong class wastes money and damages product quality.

Enzymes in Flour Treatment

Enzymes are proteins that catalyse specific reactions. They are attractive because they are used at very low doses, are generally considered processing aids rather than additives in many jurisdictions, and can replace chemical oxidants. Alpha-amylase converts damaged starch into fermentable sugars, which feeds yeast and improves crust colour. Xylanase breaks down arabinoxylans, improving dough extensibility and oven spring. Lipase and glucose oxidase are also used in some improver systems.

For African mills, the key advantage is dose control. A 5â10% variation in enzyme dose can be corrected at the mixer, whereas a chemical oxidant overdose can ruin a whole batch. Enzymes also support clean-label marketing, which matters for urban middle-class consumers and export customers.

Vitamin and Mineral Fortification

Fortification is the highest-impact public health intervention available to a mill. It also carries commercial risk: premix is expensive, and incorrect dosing can cause off-flavours or colour changes. The practical approach is to use a premix designed for wheat flour, with a carrier that disperses evenly, and to install a volumetric or gravimetric feeder with a lockout to prevent accidental over-dosing.

In Nigeria, the mandatory fortification standard requires vitamin A, B1, B2, B3, B6, B12, folic acid, iron, and zinc. In South Africa, fortification is regulated under R. 504 of 2003. Kenya and Tanzania have similar standards. Compliance is verified by laboratory analysis, and mills should retain samples for at least the shelf life of the flour.

Emulsifiers and Dough Conditioners

Emulsifiers improve dough handling, crumb softness, and shelf life. DATEM strengthens dough and improves volume, while SSL and CSL soften crumb and slow staling. In Africa, where bread may travel long distances in hot conditions, staling resistance is a major commercial advantage. A bun that stays soft for five days instead of three can win a supermarket contract.

Dough conditioners are usually blends of emulsifiers, enzymes, oxidants, and carriers. They simplify dosing but increase cost. Mills should compare the cost per tonne of flour treated against the price premium or waste reduction achieved.

Regulatory Aspects of Flour Additives

Regulation varies widely across Africa. South Africa, Nigeria, Kenya, and Egypt have relatively developed frameworks. Other countries may rely on Codex standards or imported regulations. The miller's obligation is to know the national standard, the permitted list, maximum levels, and labelling requirements. Codex CXS 192-1995 is the international reference, but national law prevails.

Documentation is critical. Keep certificates of analysis for every additive, record batch numbers, and retain production records. In an inspection, the mill that can produce a complete traceability file is far less likely to face penalties.

Never exceed the maximum permitted dose for any additive. Over-dosing oxidizing agents such as ADA or benzoyl peroxide can produce toxic residues and violate food safety law. Fortification premix contains concentrated vitamins and minerals; inhaling dust or prolonged skin contact can cause irritation or toxicity. Always use calibrated dosing equipment, wear dust masks and gloves, and store additives away from food contact surfaces. Keep safety data sheets (SDS) on file and train all staff on emergency procedures. If a dosing error occurs, quarantine the affected flour and do not release it until laboratory clearance is obtained.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Verify the destination market's permitted additive list before purchasing any bleaching, maturing, or oxidizing agent. Codex is a guide, not a substitute for national law.
  2. Calibrate dosing equipment weekly and record the calibration. A 10% dosing error on premix can cost thousands of dollars per month.
  3. Retain a sample of every treated flour batch for at least the declared shelf life. This is your defence in a regulatory inspection or customer complaint.
  4. Train staff on additive handling and SDS requirements. Dust masks, gloves, and locked storage are minimum controls.
  5. Test base flour before treatment. Farinograph, extensograph, and test bake results determine which treatment agents are actually needed.
  6. Compare cost per tonne of treatment against the price premium or waste reduction. If the additive does not pay for itself, remove it.
  7. Keep certificates of analysis for every additive batch and file them with production records for traceability.
  8. Review national fortification standards annually. Requirements change, and non-compliance can result in delisting from formal retail.
