1. The Aluminium Family of Alloys

Aluminium is rarely used pure. It is mixed with other metals to make alloys that are stronger, easier to weld, or more resistant to corrosion. The main alloying elements are copper, magnesium, manganese, silicon, and zinc. In Africa, you will commonly find alloys from the 1000, 3000, 5000, and 6000 series. The 1000 series is almost pure aluminium (99% or more). It is soft, very weldable, and used for electrical bus bars and cooking pots. The 3000 series contains manganese. It is stronger than 1000 series and used for roofing sheets and heat exchangers. The 5000 series contains magnesium. It is strong, corrosion-resistant, and used in boat hulls, truck bodies, and fuel tanks. The 6000 series contains magnesium and silicon. It is heat-treatable and used for window frames, ladders, and structural beams.

When you pick up a piece of aluminium, you cannot tell its alloy by looking. It might have a stamp like '6061-T6' or '5083-H321'. The first four digits tell you the alloy family. The letter and numbers after the dash tell you the temper, or how it was strengthened. If there is no stamp, you must be careful. Welding a mystery alloy can lead to cracks or weak joints.

In many African workshops, you will work with recycled aluminium from car rims, engine blocks, or old window frames. These are often 6000 or 7000 series, but they may be mixed. To identify them, use a spark test: touch the metal to a grinding wheel. 5000 series gives a short, dull spark. 6000 series gives a longer, brighter spark. 7000 series gives a very bright spark with many forks. This is not exact, but it helps. For critical jobs, buy certified new stock from a supplier. Common suppliers include Hulamin in South Africa, AluAfrica in Nigeria, and Egyptalum in Egypt. Always ask for the mill certificate.

As a welder, your job is to match the filler wire to the base alloy. For 5000 series, use 5356 or 5183 filler. For 6000 series, use 4043 or 5356. For 1000 and 3000 series, use 4043. Never use 4043 on 5000 series if the joint will be exposed to salt water, because it can corrode. Understanding the alloy family is the first step to a strong weld.

2. Heat-Treatable vs Non-Heat-Treatable

Aluminium alloys are divided into two big groups: heat-treatable and non-heat-treatable. This difference decides how you weld them, how you strengthen them after welding, and what jobs they are good for. Non-heat-treatable alloys get their strength from working the metalârolling, hammering, or stretching it. You cannot make them stronger by heating and cooling. The 1000, 3000, 4000, and 5000 series are non-heat-treatable. The most common in Africa are 1100, 3003, and 5052. They are easy to weld and form. They do not lose much strength in the weld zone, so you can weld them and use them without extra heat treatment. However, if you weld a heavily cold-worked 5052-H34, the heat will soften the area near the weld. You cannot restore that strength without re-rolling, which is impossible on a finished job.

Heat-treatable alloys get their strength from a process called precipitation hardening. You heat the metal to about 500Â°C, quench it in water, then let it age at room temperature or in an oven. This traps alloying elements in the metal and makes it hard. The 2000, 6000, and 7000 series are heat-treatable. Common ones are 6061, 6063, 2024, and 7075. When you weld these, the heat from welding destroys the heat treatment in the weld zone. The area becomes soft and weak. For 6061-T6, the weld zone can lose up to 50% of its strength. You can recover some strength by re-heat-treating the whole part after welding, but this is expensive and often not practical in a small workshop. Instead, you design the joint so the weld is not carrying the full load, or you use a thicker piece.

In practice, many African welders work with 6061 because it is common in window frames and truck parts. If you must weld 6061, use 4043 or 5356 filler. After welding, you can do a local heat treatment with a torch, but it is tricky. Better to accept the loss and over-engineer the joint. For non-heat-treatable alloys like 5052, you can weld without worry about losing strength, but you must use the right filler. 5052 is often used for boat hulls and chemical tanks because it is strong and corrosion-resistant. Remember: heat-treatable alloys are stronger but more difficult to weld. Non-heat-treatable are weaker but more forgiving. Choose based on the job.

3. The Oxide Layer Problem

Aluminium reacts with oxygen in the air to form a thin layer of aluminium oxide on its surface. This layer is only about 0.005 to 0.01 microns thickâthinner than a soap bubbleâbut it is very tough. It melts at 2072Â°C, while the aluminium underneath melts at 660Â°C. That means when you heat aluminium to weld it, the oxide layer stays solid and blocks the weld. If you do not remove it, your filler wire will ball up and not stick, or you will get a cold weld with no penetration. The oxide also traps dirt, oil, and moisture, which cause porosity and weak joints.

To weld aluminium, you must break up the oxide layer and keep it from forming during welding. The most common method is to use alternating current (AC) with a tungsten inert gas (TIG) welder. The AC cycle has two parts: the positive half cleans the oxide, and the negative half penetrates and melts the metal. The cleaning action is called 'arc cleaning' or 'etching'. You will see a white, frosted zone around the weld. That is normal. For thicker metal, you can use direct current electrode negative (DCEN) with helium shielding gas, but you must clean the oxide mechanically first. With MIG welding, you use direct current electrode positive (DCEP), which also provides some cleaning, but you still need to clean before welding.

Mechanical cleaning is essential. Use a stainless steel wire brush that is dedicated to aluminium only. Never use a brush that has touched steel, because steel particles will embed in the aluminium and cause rust. Brush in one direction, not back and forth, to avoid pushing oxide into the metal. Then wipe with acetone or isopropyl alcohol to remove oil and grease. Do not use chlorinated solvents like brake cleanerâthey create toxic fumes when heated. Clean the joint and at least 25 mm on each side just before welding. If you wait more than a few hours, the oxide will grow back. In humid African coastal areas, it can regrow in minutes. So clean, then weld immediately.

Another trick is to use a stainless steel wire brush on a drill or grinder for large areas. But be careful not to overheat the metal. Also, you can use a chemical etch, but that is messy and not common in small shops. The best practice is: brush, wipe, weld. Keep your brushes clean and stored in a plastic bag. If you see a dark, dull surface, that is oxide. If you see a bright, silvery surface, you are ready to weld. Remember, the oxide layer is your enemy. Defeat it with mechanical cleaning and the right welding current.

4. Thermal Conductivity and Distortion

Aluminium conducts heat about five times faster than steel. That means when you weld, the heat does not stay in one spotâit races away into the rest of the metal. This causes two big problems: you need more heat to start the weld, and the surrounding metal expands and then contracts, causing distortion. In steel, you can weld a small area and the heat stays local. In aluminium, you must use higher amperage and sometimes preheat the whole part to 100-150Â°C to help the weld fuse. But preheating also increases distortion. It is a balancing act.

Distortion happens because aluminium expands and contracts more than steel when heated and cooled. For a 1-meter length, aluminium expands about 23 microns per degree Celsius, while steel expands about 12. So if you weld a long seam, the metal around the weld expands, then cools and shrinks, pulling the part out of shape. You will see bending, twisting, or buckling. Thin sheets (1-3 mm) are the worst. They can warp like a potato chip. To control distortion, use these methods: First, tack weld frequentlyâevery 50-75 mmâbefore welding the full seam. Second, use a back-step welding technique: weld short segments in the opposite direction of the overall travel. For example, weld from B to A, then D to C, and so on. Third, use heat sinks like copper or aluminium blocks clamped to the workpiece to draw heat away. Fourth, use the lowest amperage that gives good fusion. Fifth, if possible, weld both sides alternately to balance the stress.

In Africa, many workshops do not have fancy fixtures. You can use heavy steel plates or even wet sandbags as heat sinks. Clamp the work to a strong table. For long seams, you can also use a technique called 'stitch welding' where you weld 25 mm, skip 75 mm, then come back and fill the gaps. This gives the metal time to cool. Another trick is to pre-set the parts with a slight opposite bend, so when they distort, they end up straight. This takes experience.

Remember, distortion is not just about looksâit can make a part unusable. A warped window frame will not fit. A distorted boat hull can leak. So plan your welds. Use as few passes as possible. Use a skip technique. And if you are welding thin sheets, practice on scrap first. The heat is your enemy, but with the right technique, you can control it.

5. Hot Cracking and Why It Happens

Hot cracking, also called hot shortness or solidification cracking, happens when the weld metal is cooling and is still weak. It occurs at temperatures just below the melting point, when the metal is mushy. If the stresses from shrinking are too high, the weld tears apart along the centerline or in the heat-affected zone. You will see a crack that looks like a jagged line, often right down the middle of the weld. It can happen immediately after welding or hours later. Hot cracking is a serious defect because it weakens the joint and can lead to failure.

The main cause is the wrong filler alloy or too much dilution from the base metal. For example, if you weld 6061 with 4043 filler, the silicon in the filler helps, but if you use 5356 filler on 6061, you might get cracking because 5356 contains magnesium, which can form brittle phases with copper in the base metal. The most crack-sensitive alloys are the 6000 series, especially 6061 and 6063. The 7000 series is also prone. The 5000 series is generally resistant to hot cracking. Another cause is a weld bead that is too wide or too deep. A deep, narrow bead cools faster and has more stress. A wide, shallow bead is better. Also, if you have a lot of restraintâlike a thick plate clamped tightlyâthe weld cannot shrink, so it cracks.

To prevent hot cracking, use the right filler. For 6061, use 4043 (ER4043) or 5356 (ER5356). 4043 is more crack-resistant but weaker. 5356 is stronger but more crack-sensitive. Many welders prefer 4043 for 6061. For 7000 series, use 4043 or 5356, but 4043 is often better. For 5000 series, use 5356 or 5183. Never use 4043 on 5000 series if the joint will be anodized, because it turns black. Also, control your heat input. Use a higher travel speed and lower amperage to reduce the size of the mushy zone. Use a backstep technique to reduce stress. Preheat is not recommended for crack-sensitive alloys because it increases the mushy zone time. Instead, keep the part cool between passes. If you are welding thick sections, use a U-groove instead of a V-groove to reduce the amount of weld metal.

In Africa, hot cracking is common when welders use recycled 6061 without knowing it. They might use a filler that is not matched. Always ask for the alloy or test it. If you cannot identify it, use 4043 fillerâit is the most forgiving. Also, make sure your fit-up is good. A gap that is too wide requires more filler and increases cracking risk. A gap of 1-2 mm is good for TIG. Finally, if you see a crack, grind it out completely and reweld. Do not try to cover it up. Hot cracking is a sign of a bad weld. Fix it properly.

6. Choosing the Right Alloy for the Job

Choosing the right aluminium alloy for a job is about matching the metal's properties to the demands of the application. You need to consider strength, corrosion resistance, weldability, formability, and cost. In Africa, availability is also a big factor. You might want 5083 for a boat hull, but if only 6061 is available, you have to adapt. Here is a practical guide for common jobs.

For structural frames, ladders, and window frames, use 6061 or 6063. These are heat-treatable, strong, and take paint well. 6061 is stronger and used for truck bodies and bicycle frames. 6063 is easier to extrude and used for window and door frames. They are both weldable, but you must use 4043 or 5356 filler and accept some strength loss in the weld zone. For boat hulls, fuel tanks, and marine applications, use 5052 or 5083. These are non-heat-treatable, highly corrosion-resistant, and very weldable. 5083 is stronger and used for hulls of larger boats. 5052 is good for small boats and canoes. Use 5356 filler for both. For chemical tanks and food processing equipment, use 5052 or 3003. They resist corrosion and are easy to clean. 3003 is softer and used for low-pressure tanks. For electrical bus bars and heat exchangers, use 1100 or 3003. They have high thermal and electrical conductivity. They are very weldable with 4043 filler. For high-strength aircraft parts, use 7075 or 2024. These are very strong but difficult to weld. They are rarely welded in small workshops because they crack easily. If you must weld them, use 4043 filler and expect reduced strength.

Cost is always a factor. 6061 is more expensive than 3003 or 1100. 5083 is expensive and may need to be imported. In many African countries, recycled aluminium is cheaper but of unknown alloy. If you use recycled, test it with a spark test or ask for the source. For example, car rims are often 6061 or 356 (a casting alloy). Window frames are usually 6063. Engine blocks are often 319 or 356. These casting alloys can be welded but may be porous. Use 4043 filler for castings. Always clean castings well because they hold oil and dirt.

Finally, think about the service environment. If the part will be near salt water, avoid 2000 and 7000 series because they corrode. Use 5000 series. If it will be anodized, avoid 4043 filler because it turns black. Use 5356. If it will be painted, any alloy works if prepped properly. If it will be bent or formed after welding, use a non-heat-treatable alloy like 3003 or 5052 because they bend without cracking. If it will be machined, 6061 is best. So before you start, ask: What is the job? What loads will it carry? What environment? What alloys can I get? Then choose. When in doubt, 6061 with 4043 filler is a good all-round choice for many jobs.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Aluminium Metallurgy for the Welder's Bench

This lesson is the working reference for the module. It pulls together the six lessons into a single bench-side document you can print, laminate and keep in the fabrication shop. It is written for the African context: imported alloys, variable power supply, high ambient temperatures, and jobs where a failed weld means a lost contract. Read it once for the theory, then keep it for the numbers.

The core business point is simple. Aluminium welding is not difficult because the metal is exotic; it is difficult because aluminium gives you very little warning before it fails. Steel turns red, then orange, then flows. Aluminium stays silver and then collapses. Your margin for error is measured in seconds and in amperes. Everything below exists to widen that margin.

Why the Alloy Family Matters More Than the Welding Machine

In most African fabrication workshops the welder does not choose the alloy. The customer, the architect, or the imported stock sheet chooses it. A tank builder in Nairobi may be handed 5083 plate; a window fabricator in Lagos may be handed 6063 extrusion; a truck body shop in Johannesburg may be handed 6082. Each of these behaves differently under the arc. Knowing the family is the difference between a first-time pass and three hours of grinding.

The 1xxx, 3xxx and 5xxx families are non-heat-treatable. They gain strength through work hardening (cold rolling, stretching) and through solid-solution alloying, not through heat treatment. Welding softens the heat-affected zone (HAZ) because the welding heat anneals the worked structure. This is why a 5083 boat hull is strong in the plate and weaker beside the weld. Designers compensate by thickening the weld zone or by using 5xxx filler that matches the parent chemistry.

The 2xxx, 6xxx and 7xxx families are heat-treatable. They gain strength through a deliberate sequence of solution treatment, quench and artificial ageing, which precipitates fine particles inside the grains. Welding destroys that precipitation in the HAZ. The 6xxx family (6061, 6063, 6082) is the workhorse of African architectural and transport fabrication because it extrudes easily and welds reasonably. The 7xxx family (7075 especially) is strong but notoriously crack-sensitive and is generally avoided for arc welding. The 2xxx family (2024) is common in aerospace scrap and is also difficult to weld without cracking.

Heat-Treatable versus Non-Heat-Treatable: The Practical Split

On the shop floor, the practical question is not "what is the metallurgy?" but "will this joint be as strong as the plate after welding?" For non-heat-treatable alloys the answer is usually yes, provided you use the correct filler and control heat input. For heat-treatable alloys the answer is usually no, because the HAZ loses its precipitation hardening and cannot be restored without a full re-heat-treatment that most workshops cannot perform.

This has a direct commercial consequence. If you are quoting a job in 6061-T6, either design the joint so the weld is not the critical section, or specify that the assembly will be re-aged, or accept a design strength reduction of roughly 40â50% in the HAZ. Quoting as if the weld equals the parent metal is how fabricators lose money on warranty claims.

Thermal Conductivity, Distortion and Hot Cracking

Aluminium conducts heat roughly five to six times faster than steel. A 6 mm aluminium plate pulls heat away from the weld pool so quickly that you must use higher current and a faster travel speed than steel of the same thickness, or you will get lack of fusion. The same conductivity means the whole workpiece gets hot, which causes expansion and, on cooling, contraction and distortion. Long straight welds in thin sheet will bow unless you sequence the welds, use back-step technique, or clamp to a rigid jig.

Hot cracking is the other failure mode. It happens when the weld metal or the partially melted zone has a freezing range that allows low-melting eutectics to remain liquid between solidifying grains. The grains pull apart as they contract. Alloys with a wide freezing range (notably 6061 and 7075) are more crack-sensitive than 5xxx alloys, which have a narrow freezing range. Filler selection is the main control: 4043 (Al-5% Si) is more crack-resistant than 5356 (Al-5% Mg) on 6xxx parents, but 5356 gives higher strength and better colour match after anodising.

Aluminium welding produces ozone and fine oxide fume. In the high ambient temperatures common across Africa, natural ventilation is often insufficient. Use forced fume extraction at the arc, and wear a supplied-air or P3 respirator when welding inside tanks, truck bodies or enclosed workshops.

Aluminium has no colour change before melting. Never judge temperature by eye. Use a temperature crayon or infrared thermometer when preheating 6xxx and 2xxx alloys â overheating above 150 Â°C damages the parent metal.

Never weld onto degreased surfaces that still carry solvent residue. Solvents containing chlorinated hydrocarbons break down in the arc to phosgene, a lethal gas. Clean with acetone or a dedicated aluminium cleaner and allow to dry fully before striking the arc.

Choosing the Right Alloy for the Job

Selection is a business decision as much as a technical one. Ask four questions in order:

  1. Is the service corrosive? Marine, coastal or chemical exposure pushes you to 5083 or 5052 with 5183/5356 filler. Inland dry service allows 6xxx.
  2. Is the joint strength-critical? If yes, prefer non-heat-treatable 5xxx, or design the weld as a non-critical connection.
  3. Will it be anodised? Anodising colour match between weld and parent is better with 5356 on 5xxx and 6xxx parents than with 4043.
  4. What is actually available? In many African markets, 6061 and 6063 extrusion and 5083 plate are the realistic stock. Design around what you can buy reliably, not around what is theoretically ideal.

  • Confirm the alloy grade from the mill certificate or stamp before quoting â never assume from appearance.
  • Match filler to parent: 5356/5183 for 5xxx, 4043 for 6xxx where crack resistance matters, 5356 where strength or anodising matters.
  • Remove the oxide mechanically (stainless brush reserved for aluminium only) and degrease with acetone immediately before welding.
  • Set AC with balanced or high cleaning, or DCEN for thicker 5xxx; never use DCEP for the root.
  • Use a push angle of 10â15Â° and travel faster than you would on steel to outrun the heat sink.
