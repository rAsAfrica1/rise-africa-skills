1. Cutting and sizing metal to meet buyer specifications

Before you sell any scrap metal, you must cut it to the size your buyer wants. Most dealers and foundries have specific size requirements because their furnaces or processing equipment can only handle certain dimensions. For example, a typical steel mill might want pieces no longer than 1.5 metres and no heavier than 50 kg. If you bring in a whole car body, they will either reject it or pay you a lower price. So your first job is to cut everything down to size.

For cutting, you need the right tools. An angle grinder with a cutting disc is good for thin metal like car panels and exhaust pipes. For thicker steel like chassis rails, use a reciprocating saw with a metal blade or an oxy-acetylene torch. In many African scrapyards, a petrol-powered cut-off saw (like a Stihl TS 420) is common because it's portable and doesn't need electricity. Always wear safety glasses, gloves, and ear protection. Sparks fly and metal edges are sharp.

Start by sorting metal by type: light iron (sheet metal, car bodies), heavy iron (engine blocks, axles), and non-ferrous (aluminium, copper, brass). Each has different size specs. Light iron is often baled, but if you're selling loose, cut it into pieces that fit in a wheelbarrow or sack. Heavy iron can be cut into chunks that one person can lift. Non-ferrous metals like copper wire should be stripped and cut into lengths of 1 metre or less.

A good rule: if you can't lift it safely by yourself, it's too big. Buyers pay by weight, but they also pay for convenience. Clean, uniformly sized pieces are worth more because they save the buyer time and processing costs. So take the extra time to cut properly. A few hours with a grinder can increase your price by 10-20%.

2. Using a hydraulic press or shear to bale light iron

Light ironâcar panels, roofing sheets, washing machines, and other thin steelâtakes up a lot of space. If you sell it loose, you'll need many trips to the dealer, and you'll get a lower price because it's bulky. The solution is to bale it. A bale is a compressed block of metal, usually about 60 cm x 60 cm x 60 cm, weighing 100â200 kg. Baling saves transport costs and increases the value because buyers can load it easily.

You have two main options: a hydraulic press or a shear. A hydraulic press uses a ram to crush metal into a bale. A shear uses a large blade to cut and compress. For light iron, a press is more common. In many African scrapyards, you'll find manual or semi-automatic balers. A small manual baler can cost around $2,000â$5,000, but you can also hire time on a neighbour's machine. If you don't have a baler, you can sometimes sell loose to a dealer who will bale it themselves, but you'll get less money.

To bale properly, first sort out any non-ferrous metals or thick pieces that won't compress. Then feed the light iron into the baler. Don't overload itâfollow the machine's capacity. For a typical 100-ton press, you can bale about 100â150 kg of light iron per cycle. The cycle takes 1â2 minutes. After baling, use wire or strapping to tie the bale so it doesn't spring open. Most balers come with wire slots. Use heavy-duty galvanised wire (like 4 mm) and tie at least four bands around each bale.

Safety is critical. Never put your hands inside the compression chamber while the machine is running. Keep clear of the ram. Wear steel-toe boots and gloves. If you're using a shear, be careful of flying pieces. A well-made bale should be tight and uniform. Stack bales on a pallet or on the ground in a dry area. Buyers pay a premium for dense, well-tied bales. So invest in a baler or partner with someone who has one.

3. Cleaning and preparing metals to remove attachments and debris

Dirty scrap is worth less. Buyers deduct for attachments like plastic, rubber, wood, and dirt because these contaminate the melt. For example, a car engine block with the alternator, wiring, and rubber hoses still attached will be downgraded to lower-grade iron. So your job is to clean and prepare every piece of metal before you sell it. This step can increase your price by 15â30%.

Start by removing all non-metal attachments. Use a screwdriver, pliers, and a hammer. For car parts, take off plastic bumpers, rubber hoses, wiring looms, and any glass. For white goods (fridges, washing machines), remove the plastic liners, rubber seals, and motors (which contain copper). For copper wire, strip off the plastic insulation using a wire stripper or a sharp knife. A 1 kg piece of clean copper wire can sell for $6â$8, while insulated wire might only get $2â$3 per kg. So stripping is worth it.

Next, remove dirt, oil, and rust. Use a wire brush or a grinder with a wire cup brush. For oily parts, wipe them down with a rag or wash with soapy water. Rust is not a big problem for steel, but heavy rust can reduce weight and quality. If you have a sandblaster, that's ideal, but most small yards just use manual brushing. For aluminium, remove any steel bolts or screws because they contaminate the melt. Aluminium with steel attachments is downgraded.

Sort metals into clean categories: clean heavy iron, clean light iron, clean aluminium, clean copper, brass, and stainless steel. Keep them separate. Even a small amount of copper mixed with steel can ruin a whole load. Use separate bins or piles. Once cleaned, store metals under cover to prevent re-rusting or contamination. A clean, well-sorted load is what buyers want. They will pay top price and may even offer you a contract for regular supply.

4. Weighing and calculating the value of your scrap

You need to know the weight of your scrap to know what it's worth. Never guess. Buyers use calibrated scales, and if you don't know your weight, you can't negotiate. So invest in a good scale. A platform scale that can weigh up to 500 kg is ideal. In many African markets, you can buy a 300 kg digital scale for around $100â$200. For smaller amounts, a hanging scale (up to 100 kg) costs $20â$50. Always check that your scale is accurate by testing with a known weight, like a 20 kg bag of cement.

Weigh each metal type separately. For example, put all clean copper in one bin, weigh it, and record the weight. Do the same for aluminium, brass, heavy iron, and light iron. Use a notebook or a phone app to keep records. If you have a lot of scrap, weigh in batches and add up. For bales, weigh each bale individually. A typical bale of light iron weighs 100â150 kg. A car engine block might weigh 150â200 kg. A car body (without engine) might weigh 300â500 kg.

Once you have weights, calculate the value using current market prices. Prices fluctuate, so check with local dealers or online sources. For example, in many African countries, clean heavy iron might sell for $0.20â$0.30 per kg, light iron $0.10â$0.15 per kg, aluminium $1.00â$1.50 per kg, copper $6.00â$8.00 per kg, and brass $3.00â$4.00 per kg. These are rough guides; actual prices depend on location and global markets. Multiply weight by price per kg to get total value.

Keep a record of your costs: fuel, tools, labour, transport. Subtract costs from total value to know your profit. For example, if you have 500 kg of heavy iron at $0.25/kg, that's $125. If transport costs $30, your net is $95. Knowing your numbers gives you power in negotiations. Don't accept a price without knowing what your scrap is worth. Always weigh, calculate, and then negotiate.

5. Packaging and loading scrap for transport to a dealer

How you package and load your scrap affects how much you can transport and how much you get paid. Loose scrap takes up space and can fall off a truck, which is dangerous and illegal. Proper packaging means baling, bagging, or stacking securely. For light iron, bales are best. For non-ferrous metals, use strong sacks or drums. For heavy iron, stack pieces neatly on a pallet or in a crate. Always secure your load with ropes or straps.

If you're using a pickup truck or a small lorry, plan your load to maximise weight without exceeding the vehicle's capacity. For example, a 1-ton pickup can carry about 1,000 kg of scrap, but you must include the weight of the driver and fuel. Overloading is dangerous and can damage your vehicle. Distribute weight evenly. Put heavy items at the bottom and light items on top. Use wooden planks or metal sheets to create layers. For bales, stack them two or three high and tie them down.

For non-ferrous metals, use 50 kg rice bags or 200-litre drums. Fill bags with clean, sorted metal. Tie them closed. Drums should have lids secured. Label each bag or drum with the metal type and weight. This makes it easy for the buyer to verify and speeds up unloading. If you're transporting copper wire, keep it in a locked box or bag to prevent theft. Never leave your load unattended.

Before you leave, check that everything is secure. Use ratchet straps or ropes. Drive carefully, especially on rough roads. When you arrive at the dealer, they will weigh your load. If your packaging is neat, they may trust your weights more and offer a better price. Also, consider the cost of transport. If the dealer is far, you might need to hire a truck. A 7-ton truck can cost $100â$200 per trip. So only transport when you have enough scrap to make it worthwhile. Aim for at least 1 ton per trip.

6. Negotiating prices with scrap metal dealers and foundries

Negotiation is where you turn your hard work into money. Never accept the first price. Dealers expect you to negotiate, and they often start low. Your job is to know the market price, know your weight, and be prepared to walk away if the offer is too low. Before you go, call several dealers and ask for their current prices per kg for each metal type. Write them down. This gives you a baseline.

When you arrive, be polite but firm. Show the dealer your clean, sorted, and weighed scrap. If you have bales, point out how tight and uniform they are. If you have clean copper, show that it's stripped. Let them inspect. They will make an offer. If it's below your baseline, counter with a higher price. For example, if they offer $0.20/kg for heavy iron and you know the market is $0.25/kg, say, "I was expecting $0.25. I have 500 kg here, all clean. Can you do $0.24?" Often they will meet you halfway. If they won't budge, thank them and say you'll think about it. Sometimes they'll call you back with a better offer.

For large loads (over 1 ton), you have more power. You can ask for a premium for volume. You can also negotiate for them to cover transport or to pay cash immediately. Foundries often pay more than scrap dealers because they use the metal directly. So if you have a foundry nearby, try them first. But foundries may have stricter quality requirements. Make sure your metal meets their specs.

Build relationships. If you bring clean, consistent scrap regularly, dealers will value you as a reliable supplier. They may offer you a contract with fixed prices. That's the goal. Always get a receipt or written record of the weight and price. Count your money before you leave. And never be afraid to negotiateâit's your livelihood.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Processing Scrap Metal for Sale

In the car-breaking and metal scrapyard business, the difference between a profitable load and a wasted trip to the dealer often comes down to processing. A stripped car body, a pile of mixed light iron, or a tangle of copper wire all sell for more â sometimes 30â60% more â once they are cut to size, cleaned, separated, weighed, and presented to the buyer in the form he actually wants. This reference lesson walks you through the practical steps that turn "junk metal" into a graded, weighed, saleable commodity, with the African market realities of informal dealers, foundries, and export consolidators in mind.

Treat every kilogram as cash. Your job in the yard is to increase the value per kilogram and reduce the cost per kilogram of getting it to the buyer. Cutting, baling, cleaning, weighing, and negotiating are not separate chores â they are the value chain of your business.

1. Cutting and sizing metal to meet buyer specifications

Dealers and foundries buy to a size spec because their furnaces, shears, and containers have physical limits. Common African market specs:

  • Heavy melting scrap (HMS 1/2): plate and structural steel, minimum 6 mm thick, maximum length 1.5 m, width 0.5 m. No sealed containers, no cast iron mixed in.
  • Light iron / shredded feed: sheet metal, car body panels, roofing sheets â usually baled or sheared to under 1.5 m.
  • Cast iron: engine blocks, brake drums, water pumps â kept separate because it pays a different price and contaminates steel melts.
  • Aluminium: sorted into extrusion, casting, and sheet; copper into bright bare, No. 1, and No. 2.

In practice, a yard with an oxy-acetylene torch or a petrol cut-off saw and a trained cutter can process a car body into HMS-grade pieces in under an hour. If you have a hydraulic shear or guillotine, you can cut faster and more consistently â consistency is what gets you the top price band rather than the "mixed" rate.

2. Using a hydraulic press or shear to bale light iron

Light iron is bulky and low-density. A car body uncompressed occupies 8â10 mÂ³ and weighs perhaps 1 tonne; baled, the same body takes 1â1.5 mÂ³. That matters because transport in most African markets is charged per trip or per tonne-km. Baling or shearing light iron:

  • Cuts transport cost per tonne by 40â70%.
  • Increases the load per truck from 3â4 tonnes to 15â20 tonnes.
  • Meets the density spec many foundries and export consolidators require (typically 1,000â1,500 kg/mÂ³).

A small yard can start with a manual or motorised baling press (5â20 tonne force). Larger yards run a hydraulic shear-baler that both cuts and compresses. Keep blades sharp and hydraulic oil clean â downtime on a baler is the single biggest cause of missed collection deadlines.

3. Cleaning and preparing metals to remove attachments and debris

Contamination is the fastest way to lose money. A load of copper with plastic insulation, or steel with rubber bushes and oil filters, will be downgraded or rejected. Preparation checklist:

  • Strip plastic, rubber, wood, and glass from car bodies before baling.
  • Drain engine oil, fuel, brake fluid, and coolant into labelled containers for safe disposal or resale.
  • Remove batteries (lead-acid) and store on a sealed, bunded pallet â they are a separate, higher-value stream.
  • Remove catalytic converters intact; they contain platinum-group metals and sell separately.
  • Strip copper wire of insulation where the labour cost is justified by the price gap (often 20â40% higher for bare bright).
  • Remove aluminium wheels, radiators, and alternators before the hulk is baled.

4. Weighing and calculating the value of your scrap

Price is quoted per tonne (or per kilogram) by grade. Your job is to know the weight of each grade before you arrive at the dealer, so you can check his scale and challenge a short weight. Use a certified platform or axle scale where possible; a 500 kg hanging scale is adequate for non-ferrous. Record every load in a simple ledger.

Presentation affects price. A tidy, labelled, correctly baled load signals a professional supplier and supports a better negotiation. Practical steps:

  • Bale light iron; band heavy scrap into bundles of similar size.

  • Load heaviest material at the bottom, light bales on top; secure with chains or straps.
  • Weigh the loaded truck at a certified weighbridge and keep the ticket â it is your proof of weight.
  • Carry a simple delivery note listing grade, weight, and agreed price.

6. Negotiating prices with scrap metal dealers and foundries

Negotiation is a business skill, not a shouting match. Prepare before you arrive:

  • Know your grade and weight. Arrive with a weighbridge ticket and a grade sheet. Dealers respect suppliers who know their numbers.
  • Get at least two quotes. Foundries often pay more than traders because they skip the middleman, but they may require larger minimum loads and stricter specs.
  • Ask about deductions. Moisture, contamination, and "handling" deductions are common. Agree them in writing before unloading.
  • Trade up the value chain. If you can strip copper, separate aluminium, and bale light iron, you can sell directly to a foundry or export consolidator at a higher band.
  • Build a relationship. Consistent volume, on-time delivery, and clean material earn better prices and priority off-take than one-off loads.
  • Watch the market. LME copper and aluminium prices move daily; a load held a week can gain or lose 5â10%. Sell into strength, not panic.
