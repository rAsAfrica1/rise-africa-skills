1. Shipping Marks and Handling Instructions

Shipping marks are the letters, numbers, and symbols you paint or print on the outside of your export cartons. They tell everyone in the supply chainâfrom the truck driver in Nairobi to the crane operator in Rotterdamâwhat is inside, where it is going, and how to handle it. Without them, your goods get lost, damaged, or delayed. The most common standard is ISO 780, which sets universal symbols like a broken glass for fragile, an umbrella for keep dry, and arrows for this way up. You must use these symbols because they are understood in every country, even if the worker cannot read English or French.

Every carton needs four key marks: the consignee mark (who receives the goods), the port of discharge (e.g., Mombasa, Lagos, or Durban), the package number (e.g., 1 of 10), and the country of origin (e.g., Product of Kenya). For example, if you export 200 cartons of roasted macadamia nuts from Malawi to Hamburg, each carton should have: âConsignee: Hamburg Nut Traders, Port: Hamburg, Carton 1 of 200, Made in Malawi.â Use waterproof ink or stencil. Do not use paper labels that peel off in humidity.

Handling instructions go beyond symbols. If your product is temperature-sensitive, like shea butter, write âStore below 25Â°Câ in English and the destination language. If it is fragile, add âHandle with Careâ and the broken glass symbol. For dangerous goods, you need UN numbers and hazard diamondsâfor example, UN 1263 for paint. In Africa, many exporters lose money because they skip these marks. A 2022 study by the African Export-Import Bank found that 30% of rejected shipments from Africa were due to incorrect or missing shipping marks.

Practical steps: Buy a stencil set and waterproof paint from a local hardware store. Print marks in black on a light background or white on a dark background. Make them at least 5 cm tall. Place them on two adjacent sides of the carton. Before sealing, take a photo of each marked carton. Use a checklist: consignee, port, package number, country of origin, weight, handling symbols. Train your team to check every carton. For air freight, IATA rules require similar marks. For sea freight, follow the carrierâs instructions. Always confirm with your buyerâs freight forwarder. Good shipping marks are cheap insurance against loss and rejection.

2. Barcoding and RFID for Export Tracking

Barcodes and RFID tags are the digital eyes of your export shipment. A barcode is a series of black lines that a scanner reads to identify a product. RFID (Radio Frequency Identification) uses a small chip and antenna that communicates wirelessly with a reader. For export, barcodes are mandatory for most retail buyers, while RFID is growing for high-value or high-volume shipments. The global standard is GS1. You need a GS1 company prefix, which you can get from GS1 Kenya, GS1 Nigeria, or other national member organisations. In Kenya, a GS1 Kenya membership costs about KES 20,000 per year for up to 10 products. That gives you a unique number to create barcodes.

The most common barcode for export is the EAN-13 (13 digits) for retail units and the ITF-14 for cartons. For example, a 500g bag of Ethiopian coffee might have an EAN-13 like 1234567890123. The outer carton of 12 bags gets an ITF-14 like 12345678901231. You print these using a thermal barcode printer (e.g., Zebra GK420d, about $300) and labels that meet GS1 specifications. The barcode must be at least 80% of the label height and have a quiet zone (white space) on each side. Test with a verifierâa simple scanner from Amazon costs $50. If the barcode does not scan, your shipment may be rejected at the warehouse.

RFID goes further. A passive RFID tag (e.g., Alien Higgs-4) costs $0.10â$0.50 each. You attach it to pallets or cartons. A handheld reader (e.g., Zebra MC3330R, about $1,500) can scan 100 tags per second without line of sight. This is useful for tracking pallets in a warehouse or container. For export to the EU, some retailers like Carrefour require RFID on pallets. In South Africa, Woolworths uses RFID for apparel. For African exporters, RFID is not yet mandatory for most products, but it reduces labour costs and errors. Start with barcodes, then pilot RFID on high-value shipments like vanilla or cocoa.

Practical steps: Join GS1 in your country. Get a company prefix. Assign GTINs (Global Trade Item Numbers) to each product and carton. Buy a barcode printer and labels. Print and verify. For RFID, buy tags and a reader, and test read rates. Keep records in a spreadsheet or ERP system. Remember: a barcode is only as good as the data behind it. Always double-check that the number on the label matches your invoice and packing list.

3. Country of Origin and Customs Labels

Country of origin (COO) labels tell customs authorities where your product was made. This is not just a courtesyâit determines tariffs, trade agreements, and even whether your goods can enter. For example, under the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), goods made in Ghana can enter Kenya duty-free if they meet rules of origin. But if your label says âMade in Chinaâ when the product is actually from Ghana, you lose that benefit and may face penalties. Most countries require a permanent mark like âMade in Kenyaâ or âProduct of Nigeriaâ on the product or its immediate packaging. The mark must be legible, in English or the destination language, and not easily removed.

Customs labels also include the Harmonized System (HS) code. This is a 6-digit number that classifies your product. For example, 0901.21 is roasted coffee, not decaffeinated. You need the correct HS code on your commercial invoice, packing list, and sometimes on the carton itself. A wrong HS code can lead to delays, fines, or seizure. In 2021, a Kenyan exporter of macadamia nuts lost $50,000 because they used the wrong HS code (0802.31 instead of 0802.32) and the shipment was held for three weeks. You can find HS codes on the World Customs Organization website or ask your freight forwarder.

For preferential trade agreements, you may need a certificate of origin, such as the AfCFTA Certificate of Origin or the EUâs EUR.1. These are separate documents, but the information must match your labels. For example, if your label says âMade in Rwandaâ but your certificate says âMade in Uganda,â customs will reject the shipment. Also, some countries require additional marks: Saudi Arabia requires âMade inâ and a production/expiry date in Arabic; the EU requires the address of the manufacturer for certain products. Always check the destination countryâs labeling rules on the embassy website or with a customs broker.

Practical steps: Determine the correct HS code for each product. Print âMade in [Country]â in a font size at least 2 mm high. Use waterproof ink. Keep a copy of your certificate of origin with the shipment. Train your team to verify that all labels match the commercial documents. For AfCFTA, keep records of raw materials to prove origin. A simple mistake like a missing country of origin can cost you the entire shipment.

4. Language and Symbol Requirements

Language and symbols on your export labels must speak to the people who will handle, sell, or use your product. If you only use English, you may fail in markets like France, Egypt, or Brazil. Many countries have mandatory language laws. For example, Canada requires English and French on all consumer labels. The EU requires the official language(s) of the destination countryâso for Germany, German; for Spain, Spanish. Saudi Arabia requires Arabic, and Israel requires Hebrew. In Africa, Nigeria uses English, but Senegal uses French, and Egypt uses Arabic. You do not need to translate everything, but the key informationâproduct name, ingredients, net weight, country of origin, and safety warningsâmust be in the required language.

Symbols are universal and often replace words. The most important are the ISO 780 handling symbols: fragile (broken glass), keep dry (umbrella), this way up (arrows), and temperature limits (thermometer). For food, you need the âbest beforeâ date symbol (an hourglass) and the âopen hereâ symbol. For hazardous goods, use the UN hazard diamonds: red for flammable, yellow for oxidizer, white for toxic. These are required by IATA for air freight and IMDG for sea freight. If you export cosmetics to the EU, you need the âperiod after openingâ symbol (an open jar with a number like 12M). For electronics, the WEEE symbol (crossed-out wheelie bin) is mandatory in the EU.

In Africa, many exporters fail because they use symbols that are not recognized. For example, a Kenyan exporter of honey sent jars to Saudi Arabia with only English and a generic âkeep coolâ symbol. The shipment was rejected because Arabic was missing and the symbol was not the standard thermometer. Another common mistake: using the recycling symbol (three arrows) when the product is not recyclable in the destination country. That can lead to fines under greenwashing laws. Always check the destinationâs mandatory symbols. The International Trade Centre has a free online tool called âExporting toâ¦â that lists requirements by country.

Practical steps: Identify the required languages for your target market. Translate only the mandatory text using a professional translator (cost: $0.10â$0.20 per word). Use standard symbols from ISO 780 and UN. Print them clearly, at least 10 mm tall. Do not use symbols that are not relevant. For multi-language labels, use a table or separate panels. Test with a native speaker. Update your labels when regulations change. A small investment in language and symbols prevents big losses at the border.

5. Electronic Documentation and E-Labels

Electronic documentation is replacing paper in export. Instead of printing and mailing documents, you send them digitally through platforms like TradeNet, ASYCUDA, or the Single Window system. In Africa, many countries have adopted electronic single windows: Kenyaâs TradeNet, Ghanaâs ICUMS, Nigeriaâs NICIS, and South Africaâs SARS eFiling. These systems let you submit customs declarations, certificates of origin, and permits online. The benefit is speed: a shipment that took 5 days to clear with paper can clear in 24 hours with electronic documents. You need a computer, internet, and a digital signature or token. The cost is usually a small fee per declaration (e.g., KES 1,000 in Kenya).

E-labels are digital versions of physical labels. They can be a QR code on the carton that links to a webpage with all the information: product details, certificates, handling instructions, and even videos. For example, a Ghanaian cocoa exporter can put a QR code on each bag. When a buyer in the Netherlands scans it, they see the farm location, harvest date, and organic certification. This builds trust and reduces the need for paper inserts. E-labels are especially useful for products with lots of information, like pharmaceuticals or electronics. The EUâs new Digital Product Passport, coming for textiles and electronics, will require a QR code or RFID that gives access to data. African exporters should prepare now.

To use electronic documentation, you must have a reliable internet connection and a scanner. Many freight forwarders offer this service for a fee. But you can do it yourself. Start by registering on your countryâs single window portal. For example, in Kenya, go to tradeNet.go.ke. You will need your KRA PIN, business registration, and product HS codes. Upload your commercial invoice, packing list, and certificate of origin as PDFs. The system will validate and send to customs. For e-labels, use a free QR code generator like QR Code Monkey. Print the QR code at least 2 cm x 2 cm on the carton. Test it with a smartphone. Make sure the linked page is mobile-friendly and in the destination language.

Practical steps: Register for your national single window. Get a digital signature. Train one staff member on the system. Use a cloud folder (Google Drive or Dropbox) to store all documents. For e-labels, create a simple webpage or use a service like Bitly to shorten the URL. Always keep a paper backup of critical documents. Check that your e-label complies with destination lawsâsome countries still require physical labels. Electronic documentation saves time and money, but only if you have a backup plan for power or internet failures.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Labeling, Marking & Documentation for African Exporters

For an African exporter, a label is not decoration â it is a legal declaration, a customs trigger, and a quality signal all at once. A shipment of Ghanaian shea butter, Kenyan cut flowers, or Nigerian processed cashew can clear a European or Gulf port in hours, or sit for weeks, depending on what is printed, where it is printed, and what documents travel with it. This lesson is a working reference: the marks, symbols, codes, and documents you must get right before your container leaves Mombasa, Tema, or Durban.

1. Shipping Marks and Handling Instructions

Shipping marks are the human-readable identifiers on the outer carton that allow a consignment to be traced from stuffing to unpacking. Under ISO 780 and IMO/ILO conventions, handling symbols are standardised pictograms â no translation required. On African corridors where cargo is often transhipped (e.g., Nairobi â Jebel Ali â Rotterdam), consistent marks reduce mis-routing and pilferage.

Minimum shipping mark set for export cartons:

  • Consignee mark â buyer name or code, plus port of discharge.
  • Reference â PO number, invoice number, or shipment ID.
  • Carton count â "Carton 3 of 240" so a partial delivery is visible immediately.
  • Gross/net weight and dimensions in metric units.
  • Country of origin â "Produce of Kenya" or "Made in Nigeria".
  • Handling pictograms â this side up, keep dry, temperature limits, fragile.

3. Country of Origin and Customs Labels

Origin marking is a customs and trade-preference issue, not marketing. Under AfCFTA's Rules of Origin, goods must carry evidence of originating status to claim preferential tariffs. In practice this means:

  • A Certificate of Origin (e.g., AfCFTA CoO, EU Form A under GSP, or COMESA/EAC certificates).
  • A physical origin mark on the product or carton where the destination customs authority requires it.
  • Correct HS code on the commercial invoice and packing list.

For the EU, non-preferential origin rules apply to goods not claiming preference; for the US, Customs and Border Protection requires country of origin marking on most imports under 19 U.S.C. Â§1304. Getting origin wrong can mean loss of preference (a real cost â often 5â15% of landed value) or seizure.

4. Language and Symbol Requirements

Language is a hard compliance gate. Food and cosmetic labels in the EU must be in the official language(s) of the member state of sale; Gulf markets require Arabic; Francophone Africa requires French; Lusophone markets require Portuguese. English-only labels fail at the border.

Symbols travel better than words. Mandatory and near-mandatory symbols for African exporters include: recycling (Mobius loop), food-safe (fork and glass), CE marking for EU-regulated categories, the Green Dot where packaging recovery schemes apply, and allergen pictograms. For organic exports, the EU organic logo or USDA organic seal requires certification â a label claim you cannot self-declare.

5. Electronic Documentation and E-Labels

African exporters are moving from paper to digital faster than many expect. The Kenya TradeNet system, Ghana's ICUMS, Nigeria's NICIS II, and South Africa's SARS e-filing all accept electronic submission of export declarations. On the buyer side, ASN (Advance Shipping Notice) via EDI or e-mail with SSCC-linked data is now standard for retail supply chains.

E-labels â digital product information accessed by QR code â are growing for consumer goods in the EU, where the Digital Product Passport is being phased in. Keep the URL permanent; broken QR codes damage trust faster than a missing symbol.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm the buyer's label specification sheet in writing, including language and symbol requirements for the destination market.
  2. Verify your GS1 prefix is valid and your GTIN/SSCC are correctly allocated (check digit included).
  3. Print shipping marks on at least two adjacent carton faces, with carton count and weights.
  4. Apply ISO 780 handling pictograms â this side up, keep dry, fragile, temperature limits.
  5. State country of origin clearly and match it to the Certificate of Origin and HS code.
  6. Include all mandatory allergens, nutrition, and safety information in the destination language.
  7. Attach a QR code linking to a permanent, hosted document set (spec sheet, CoO, SDS).
  8. Sign off a printed proof against the checklist before plate-making or mass printing.
