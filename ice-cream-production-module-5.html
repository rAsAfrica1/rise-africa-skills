1. Understanding Mix Composition: Fat, MSNF, Sugar, Stabilizers

Ice cream mix is a balance of four key components: fat, milk solids-not-fat (MSNF), sugar, and stabilizers. In Africa, where fresh cream can be expensive, many producers use locally available ingredients like whole milk, skimmed milk powder, and vegetable fats such as palm oil or coconut oil. Fat gives creaminess and carries flavour. A standard mix has 8â12% fat. MSNF, mainly proteins and lactose, adds body and helps prevent ice crystals. Aim for 9â12% MSNF. Sugar sweetens and lowers the freezing point; typical level is 12â16% sucrose. Stabilizers like gelatin, guar gum, or locust bean gum (common in Nigeria and Ghana) prevent melting and improve texture; use 0.2â0.5%.

To calculate percentages, you need the composition of your ingredients. For example, whole milk (3.5% fat, 8.5% MSNF), skimmed milk powder (0.8% fat, 96% MSNF), and sugar (100% sucrose). If you use 10 kg whole milk, you get 0.35 kg fat and 0.85 kg MSNF. A simple recipe for 10 kg mix: 6 kg whole milk, 1 kg skimmed milk powder, 2 kg sugar, 0.03 kg stabilizer, and 0.97 kg water. This gives roughly 2.1% fat from milk plus 0.8% from powder? Actually, calculate: 6 kg milk gives 0.21 kg fat, 1 kg powder gives 0.008 kg fat, total 0.218 kg fat in 10 kg = 2.18% fat â too low. So you need to add cream or vegetable fat. If you add 0.8 kg coconut oil (100% fat), total fat becomes 1.018 kg = 10.18% fat. Good.

Always weigh ingredients with a kitchen scale accurate to 1 g. In many African markets, a 5 kg scale costs about $20. Use a notebook to record each batch. If you change ingredients, recalculate. For MSNF: 6 kg milk gives 0.51 kg MSNF, 1 kg powder gives 0.96 kg MSNF, total 1.47 kg = 14.7% MSNF â slightly high; reduce powder to 0.5 kg to get 10.7% MSNF. Sugar: 2 kg = 20% â too sweet; reduce to 1.2 kg = 12%. Stabilizer: 0.03 kg = 0.3%. This iterative balancing is the heart of mix formulation.

Keep a simple spreadsheet or notebook with columns for ingredient, weight, fat%, MSNF%, sugar%, stabilizer%. Multiply weight by percentage to get kg of each component. Sum and divide by total mix weight to get final percentages. Adjust until within target ranges. This method works whether you produce 10 litres or 1000 litres. Start small, taste, and adjust.

2. Balancing Mix for Desired Overrun and Texture

Overrun is the amount of air whipped into ice cream, expressed as a percentage of the mix volume. If 1 litre of mix becomes 1.5 litres of ice cream, overrun is 50%. In Africa, where customers often prefer dense, creamy ice cream, aim for 30â50% overrun. High overrun (80â100%) gives more profit but poorer texture. To control overrun, you must balance fat, MSNF, sugar, and stabilizers. Fat destabilizes air bubbles; more fat means lower overrun. MSNF and stabilizers stabilize air cells, allowing higher overrun without collapse.

A mix for 50% overrun might have 10% fat, 11% MSNF, 14% sugar, and 0.3% stabilizer. If you want 30% overrun (denser), increase fat to 12% and reduce stabilizer to 0.2%. If you want 70% overrun (fluffier, cheaper), reduce fat to 8%, increase MSNF to 12%, and use 0.5% stabilizer. Sugar also affects overrun: too much sugar (above 16%) makes the mix soft and reduces overrun because it lowers freezing point. Keep sugar between 12% and 15%.

Texture is also influenced by the freezing process. In a manual or small electric churn, you can only achieve about 30% overrun because of slow freezing. A continuous freezer can reach 100%. For African producers using a batch freezer (e.g., a 10-litre Carpigiani), typical overrun is 50â70%. To calculate the final volume: mix volume Ã (1 + overrun/100). For 10 litres of mix at 50% overrun, you get 15 litres of ice cream. If you sell 100 ml scoops, that's 150 scoops. Knowing this helps you price and plan.

To adjust texture, also consider the type of stabilizer. Guar gum gives a slightly slimy texture if overused; locust bean gum gives a smoother, creamier texture. In West Africa, many use cassava starch (0.5â1%) as a cheap stabilizer, but it can make the ice cream gummy. A blend of 0.2% guar and 0.1% locust bean works well. Always dissolve stabilizers in warm water (60Â°C) before adding to the mix to avoid lumps.

Finally, test your mix by freezing a small sample. If it's too icy, increase MSNF or stabilizer. If it's too soft, reduce sugar or increase fat. Record your results. Over time, you'll develop a standard recipe that gives consistent overrun and texture for your market.

3. Pearson Square and Algebraic Methods for Mix Calculation

The Pearson Square is a simple tool to calculate how much of two ingredients to blend to achieve a target fat percentage. It works for any component, but fat is most common. Draw a square. Put the target fat percentage in the centre. On the left corners, write the fat percentages of two ingredients: one higher, one lower. For example, you have cream with 40% fat and whole milk with 3.5% fat. You want a mix with 10% fat. Subtract diagonally: 40 - 10 = 30 parts of milk; 10 - 3.5 = 6.5 parts of cream. So for every 6.5 kg cream, use 30 kg milk. Total 36.5 kg gives 10% fat. Check: (6.5Ã0.40 + 30Ã0.035) / 36.5 = (2.6 + 1.05)/36.5 = 3.65/36.5 = 10%. Correct.

But ice cream mix has more than fat. You also need to hit MSNF, sugar, and stabilizer targets. Use the Pearson Square for fat first, then add skimmed milk powder to adjust MSNF, sugar to taste, and stabilizer. For example, after blending cream and milk, you have 36.5 kg with 10% fat and about 8.5% MSNF (from milk) plus 0.8% from cream? Actually, cream has little MSNF. Whole milk has 8.5% MSNF, so 30 kg milk gives 2.55 kg MSNF. Cream (40% fat) has about 5% MSNF, so 6.5 kg gives 0.325 kg MSNF. Total MSNF = 2.875 kg in 36.5 kg = 7.9%. You need 11% MSNF, so add skimmed milk powder (96% MSNF). Required additional MSNF = (0.11Ã36.5) - 2.875 = 4.015 - 2.875 = 1.14 kg. Since powder is 96% MSNF, add 1.14/0.96 = 1.19 kg powder. Now total weight becomes 37.69 kg. Recalculate fat: still 10%? Added powder has 0.8% fat, so extra fat = 1.19Ã0.008 = 0.0095 kg, negligible. MSNF now 4.015 kg = 10.65% â close enough. Add sugar: target 14% of final mix. Final mix weight after sugar = 37.69 + sugar. Let sugar = S. S/(37.69+S) = 0.14 â S = 0.14Ã37.69 + 0.14S â 0.86S = 5.2766 â S = 6.14 kg. Add stabilizer 0.3%: 0.003Ã(37.69+6.14) = 0.131 kg. Final mix weight = 37.69+6.14+0.131 = 43.96 kg.

Algebraic methods are more precise. Set up equations: let x = kg cream, y = kg milk, z = kg powder, s = kg sugar, t = kg stabilizer. Total weight W = x+y+z+s+t. Fat: 0.40x + 0.035y + 0.008z = 0.10W. MSNF: 0.05x + 0.085y + 0.96z = 0.11W. Sugar: s = 0.14W. Stabilizer: t = 0.003W. Also x+y+z+s+t = W. Solve by substitution. For small-scale producers, the Pearson Square plus stepwise addition is easier. Use a calculator or a simple spreadsheet. In Africa, many phones have spreadsheet apps. Practice with your local ingredients: fresh cream, whole milk, skimmed milk powder, and sugar. Always verify with a fat test if possible (e.g., Gerber test).

4. Adjusting Mix for Different Product Categories

Different ice cream products need different mix compositions. In Africa, popular categories include hard scoop ice cream, soft-serve, gelato, sorbet, and frozen yoghurt. Each has target ranges. Hard scoop: 10â12% fat, 10â11% MSNF, 14â16% sugar, 0.3% stabilizer. Soft-serve: 4â6% fat, 10â12% MSNF, 12â14% sugar, 0.4% stabilizer (often with emulsifier). Gelato: 4â8% fat, 8â10% MSNF, 16â20% sugar (lower fat, denser). Sorbet: 0% fat, 0% MSNF, 25â30% sugar, 0.5% stabilizer (fruit puree base). Frozen yoghurt: 2â6% fat, 10â12% MSNF, 12â15% sugar, 0.3% stabilizer, with yoghurt culture.

To adjust from a standard hard scoop mix to soft-serve, reduce fat by using more milk and less cream. For example, a 10 kg mix with 10% fat uses 1 kg fat. For 5% fat, use 0.5 kg fat. Replace 0.5 kg fat with 0.5 kg skimmed milk (or water plus powder) to keep total weight. But reducing fat increases MSNF if you use milk; so also adjust powder. Use Pearson Square again: target 5% fat from cream (40%) and milk (3.5%). 40-5=35 parts milk; 5-3.5=1.5 parts cream. So for 1.5 kg cream, use 35 kg milk â that's 36.5 kg total with 5% fat. Then add MSNF to reach 11%: milk gives 35Ã0.085=2.975 kg MSNF, cream gives 1.5Ã0.05=0.075 kg, total 3.05 kg in 36.5 kg = 8.36%. Need 11% = 4.015 kg, so add 0.965/0.96=1.005 kg powder. Then sugar to 13%: S/(36.5+1.005+S)=0.13 â S=0.13Ã37.505+0.13S â 0.87S=4.87565 â S=5.60 kg. Stabilizer 0.4%: 0.004Ã(37.505+5.60)=0.172 kg. Final weight ~43.28 kg.

For gelato, increase sugar to 18% and reduce fat to 6%. For sorbet, use fruit puree (e.g., mango) with 25% sugar and no dairy. For frozen yoghurt, replace some milk with yoghurt (which has 3% fat, 10% MSNF). Adjust stabilizer to 0.3% and add 0.1% emulsifier (e.g., lecithin) for smoothness.

In Africa, consider local preferences: in Nigeria, people like sweeter, creamier ice cream; in Kenya, fruit sorbets are popular. Test small batches. Keep records of each category's recipe. Use a notebook or phone app. Adjust for cost: soft-serve is cheaper because less fat. Sorbet is cheapest if fruit is in season. Always label your products clearly.

5. Computer Software for Mix Formulation

You don't need expensive software to formulate ice cream mix. A simple spreadsheet like Microsoft Excel or Google Sheets can do all calculations. In Africa, many phones have Google Sheets or WPS Office. Start by creating a table with columns: Ingredient, Weight (kg), Fat %, MSNF %, Sugar %, Stabilizer %, and then columns for kg of each component. Use formulas: =B2*C2 for fat kg, etc. At the bottom, sum each component column and divide by total weight to get percentages. Then use 'Goal Seek' or 'Solver' to adjust ingredient weights to hit targets. For example, set fat % to 10 by changing cream weight. This is powerful and free.

There are also dedicated ice cream formulation apps. 'Ice Cream Calculator' (available on Google Play) is a simple app for Android that lets you input ingredients and targets. 'Dairy Mix' is another. But many are in English and may not have African ingredients. You can create your own custom spreadsheet with local ingredients: whole milk, skimmed milk powder, sugar, palm oil, guar gum, etc. Save it as a template. Each time you produce, just change the batch size.

For larger producers, software like 'Formulator' or 'SAP' is overkill. Instead, use Excel with macros if you know how. But even without macros, you can do iterative calculations. For example, to find how much sugar to add for 14% in a 50 kg mix: target sugar kg = 0.14 * total weight. But total weight includes sugar. So sugar = 0.14*(other ingredients + sugar) â sugar = 0.14*other/(1-0.14). In Excel, put other weight in A1, then formula =0.14*A1/0.86. This avoids trial and error.

Another useful tool is a free online calculator like 'Ice Cream Mix Calculator' from Dairy Science websites. But internet may be unreliable. So download a spreadsheet template. You can also use a simple calculator and the algebraic method from Lesson 3. The key is to have a reliable, repeatable method. Train one staff member to use the spreadsheet. Print a recipe card for each product. In Africa, where power can be intermittent, keep a paper backup. Always double-check calculations manually for the first few batches. As you grow, consider investing in a small laptop (e.g., HP Stream) for $200. It will pay for itself by reducing errors and waste.

6. Costing and Scaling Up Mix Recipes

To cost your ice cream, you must know the cost of each ingredient per kilogram. In Africa, prices vary by season and location. For example, in Nairobi, whole milk might cost $0.60/litre (1.03 kg), skimmed milk powder $4/kg, sugar $0.90/kg, cream $3/kg, stabilizer $15/kg. For a 10 kg mix with 10% fat, 11% MSNF, 14% sugar, 0.3% stabilizer, you need: cream (40% fat) 2.1 kg? Let's calculate properly. Using Pearson Square for 10% fat from cream (40%) and milk (3.5%): 40-10=30 parts milk, 10-3.5=6.5 parts cream. So for 36.5 kg mix, 6.5 kg cream, 30 kg milk. Scale to 10 kg: multiply by 10/36.5=0.274. Cream: 6.5*0.274=1.78 kg, milk: 30*0.274=8.22 kg. Then add MSNF: milk gives 8.22*0.085=0.699 kg, cream gives 1.78*0.05=0.089 kg, total 0.788 kg. Need 11% of 10 kg = 1.1 kg, so add 0.312/0.96=0.325 kg powder. Sugar: 14% of final weight. Let final weight W = 1.78+8.22+0.325+sugar+stabilizer. Sugar = 0.14W, stabilizer=0.003W. So W = 10.325 + 0.143W â 0.857W=10.325 â W=12.05 kg. Sugar=1.687 kg, stabilizer=0.036 kg. Check total: 1.78+8.22+0.325+1.687+0.036=12.048 kg. So for 12.05 kg mix, cost: cream 1.78*$3=$5.34, milk 8.22*$0.60/1.03? Wait, milk price per kg: $0.60/litre, density ~1.03 kg/litre, so $0.583/kg. Milk cost: 8.22*0.583=$4.79. Powder: 0.325*$4=$1.30. Sugar: 1.687*$0.90=$1.52. Stabilizer: 0.036*$15=$0.54. Total cost = $13.49 for 12.05 kg mix. That's $1.12/kg mix. With 50% overrun, 1 kg mix gives 1.5 kg ice cream. So cost per kg ice cream = $1.12/1.5 = $0.75. Add packaging, labour, electricity, overheads (say 50% of ingredient cost), total cost ~$1.12/kg. Sell at $2.50/kg for profit.

Scaling up: If you want to produce 100 kg mix, multiply all ingredient weights by 100/12.05 = 8.3. So cream 14.8 kg, milk 68.2 kg, powder 2.7 kg, sugar 14.0 kg, stabilizer 0.3 kg. Always scale by weight, not volume, because densities differ. Use a large scale (100 kg capacity) and a notebook. For very large batches, consider a mixing tank with agitator. But start small, then double. Keep a record of costs each month. Adjust prices as they change. In Africa, buying in bulk (e.g., 50 kg sugar bags) reduces cost. Join a cooperative to get better prices on milk powder. Finally, calculate your break-even point: if fixed costs are $500/month, and profit per kg is $1.38, you need to sell 363 kg/month to break even.

Mix formulation is where your ice cream business either makes money or loses it. Every kilogram of mix you prepare has a cost, a fat content, a total solids level, and a freezing behaviour that determines your yield, your texture, and your shelf life. In the African context â where milk powder, butter, cream, and stabilizers are imported and priced in foreign currency â getting the formulation right is not an academic exercise. 2. Pearson Square: Worked Example with Local Ingredients

The Pearson Square is the fastest way to calculate how much of two ingredients to blend to hit a target fat or MSNF level. It is used daily in small dairies across Africa because it needs no software â just a pen, a calculator, and the composition of your ingredients.

Problem: You want 100 kg of mix at 10% fat. You have fresh milk at 3.5% fat and fresh cream at 40% fat. How much of each?

  1. Write the target (10) in the centre.
  2. Write cream fat (40) top-left and milk fat (3.5) bottom-left.
  3. Subtract diagonally: 10 â 3.5 = 6.5 parts cream; 40 â 10 = 30 parts milk.
  4. Total parts = 36.5.
  5. Cream needed = (6.5 / 36.5) Ã 100 = 17.8 kg.
  6. Milk needed = (30 / 36.5) Ã 100 = 82.2 kg.

Check: (17.8 Ã 0.40) + (82.2 Ã 0.035) = 7.12 + 2.88 = 10.0 kg fat. Correct.

For multi-component mixes (fat, MSNF, sugar, stabilizer), the Pearson Square is used sequentially â first balance fat, then MSNF, then sugar â or you switch to algebraic methods. The algebraic approach sets up simultaneous equations: if x is kg of ingredient A and y is kg of ingredient B, then x + y = total mix and (fat_A Â· x) + (fat_B Â· y) = target fat Â· total. Solving gives the same answer but scales cleanly to three or more ingredients.

3. Costing and Scaling Up

Formulation and costing must be done together. A mix that hits 10% fat using fresh cream may cost three times more than one using vegetable fat and SMP, depending on your local market. The practical approach is to build a simple spreadsheet with four columns: ingredient, kg per 100 kg mix, unit cost (per kg), and cost per 100 kg. Then divide by your yield to get cost per litre of finished ice cream.

Worked costing example (indicative, Kenya/Uganda market, USD):

  • Fresh milk (82 kg @ $0.55/kg) = $45.10
  • Fresh cream 40% (17.8 kg @ $2.80/kg) = $49.84
  • Sucrose (14 kg @ $0.90/kg) = $12.60
  • Stabilizer blend (0.4 kg @ $8.00/kg) = $3.20
  • Packaging, energy, labour, overheads = $18.00
  • Total per 100 kg mix â $128.74

At 80% overrun, 100 kg mix yields approximately 180 litres of ice cream. Cost per litre â $0.72. If you sell at $2.50 per litre retail, gross margin is strong â but if you sell wholesale at $1.00 per litre to a distributor, your margin is thin and every percentage point of overrun and every cent of ingredient cost matters. This is why overrun control and formulation discipline are business decisions, not just technical ones.

When scaling up, remember that stabilizer, emulsifier, and sugar quantities scale linearly, but freezing time, ageing time, and overrun control do not. A recipe that works at 20 litres may need adjustment at 200 litres. Always run a small trial batch before committing a full production run.
