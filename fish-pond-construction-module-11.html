Lessons

1. Filling and Testing the Pond

Start filling your pond only after the walls and bottom are fully cured. For concrete ponds, cure for 7 days by keeping the surface wet. For lined ponds, check that the liner has no wrinkles or sharp objects underneath. Fill with clean water from a borehole, well, or stream. If using stream water, filter it through a fine mesh to keep out wild fish and insects. Fill the pond to about 10 cm below the top of the dyke or the overflow pipe. This takes 1-2 days for a 10m x 10m pond that is 1.5m deep, which holds about 150,000 litres. Use a water pump if you have one, or a hose from a tap. While filling, watch for leaks. Mark the water level with a stick. After 24 hours, check if the level has dropped. A drop of more than 2 cm means you have a leak. For concrete ponds, leaks are often at joints or cracks. Drain and repair with cement mortar. For lined ponds, check for punctures. Also test the overflow pipe: pour a bucket of water near it and see if it drains freely. Test the inlet: open the tap and see if water flows without splashing. After filling, let the water sit for 2-3 days. This allows chlorine to evaporate if you used tap water. If you used borehole water, still wait 24 hours. Then test the pH with a simple pH test kit (available at agro-vet shops). The pH should be between 6.5 and 8.5. If it is too low (acidic), add agricultural lime at 100g per square metre. If too high (alkaline), add gypsum at 50g per square metre. Also test for hardness if you can. Finally, check water temperature. It should be between 25C and 30C for tilapia and catfish. If it is too cold, wait for a warmer day or add a shade net to reduce evaporation but not temperature. Record all test results in a notebook. This baseline helps you later if problems arise.

2. Conditioning Water for Fish Stocking

Conditioning water means making it safe and productive for fish. After filling and testing, you need to build a natural food base. The easiest way is to add organic matter. For a 10m x 10m pond, add 20 kg of dry chicken manure or 30 kg of cow dung. Put it in a sack and hang it in the water near the inlet. This releases nutrients slowly. Alternatively, add 5 kg of NPK fertiliser (20-10-10) dissolved in a bucket of water and spread over the pond. Wait 3-5 days. The water should turn a light green colour. This means plankton (tiny plants and animals) are growing. If it stays clear, add another 10 kg of manure. Do not over-fertilise: dark green water with a bad smell means too many nutrients, which can kill fish. Use a Secchi disk (a white plate on a stick) to check clarity. Lower it into the water until it disappears. The depth should be 30-40 cm. If less than 20 cm, the water is too cloudy with plankton; stop feeding. If more than 50 cm, add more manure. Also check dissolved oxygen. You can buy a simple test kit. Oxygen should be above 5 mg/l. If it is low, increase water movement by splashing water or using a small pump. For catfish, you can add salt at 1 kg per 1000 litres to reduce stress and prevent fungus. For tilapia, salt is not needed. Adjust pH again after fertilising. If pH drops below 6.5, add lime. If above 8.5, add gypsum. Also check ammonia and nitrite if you have test kits. They should be near zero. High ammonia means too much waste; do a partial water change (20%) and stop feeding. Conditioning takes 7-10 days. During this time, run a pump or aerator for at least 6 hours a day if you have one. If not, manually splash the water with a bucket twice a day. By the end, the water should be green, warm, and smell earthy, not rotten. Then you are ready to stock.

3. Acclimating and Releasing Fish

Never dump fish straight into the pond. They will die from shock. First, prepare the pond: stop feeding for 24 hours before stocking. This reduces waste. Bring the fish in plastic bags or buckets from the hatchery. The water in the bag has a different temperature and pH. Float the sealed bags on the pond surface for 15-20 minutes. This equalises temperature. Then open the bags and add a cup of pond water every 5 minutes for 20 minutes. This mixes the waters slowly. After that, gently net the fish out of the bag and release them into the pond. Do not pour the bag water into the pond; it may contain waste or diseases. For tilapia, stock at 3 fish per square metre if you have no aeration. For a 10m x 10m pond, that is 300 fish. With aeration, you can stock 5-6 per square metre. For catfish, stock at 5-10 per square metre without aeration, and up to 20 with aeration. Buy fingerlings that are at least 5-10 grams each. Smaller ones are cheaper but have lower survival. Release fish in the early morning or late evening when it is cool. Do not release in the heat of the day. Spread them around the pond, not all in one spot. Watch them swim away. If some float or swim sideways, they are stressed. If many die within 24 hours, check water quality. Also, do not mix species unless you know they are compatible. Tilapia and catfish can be mixed, but catfish may eat small tilapia. If you mix, stock catfish after tilapia have grown to at least 20g. After release, do not feed for 24 hours. Let them settle. Then start with small amounts. Record the number and size of fish stocked. This helps you calculate feed later. Always buy from a reputable hatchery. Ask for a health certificate if possible.

4. Initial Feeding and Health Checks

After stocking, wait 24 hours before feeding. Start with a high-quality floating pellet feed, 2mm size for fingerlings. Feed them twice a day, morning and evening. Give only what they can eat in 15 minutes. For 300 tilapia fingerlings, start with 100g per day total. For catfish, start with 150g per day. Broadcast the feed evenly across the pond. Watch the fish come to eat. If they do not eat, check water quality. Overfeeding wastes money and pollutes water. Remove any uneaten feed after 30 minutes with a fine net. Increase feed gradually as fish grow. After one week, increase to 200g per day for tilapia and 300g for catfish. Always check fish health daily. Look for signs of disease: white spots on skin, frayed fins, red sores, or fish gasping at the surface. If you see these, isolate affected fish if possible. Common problems are fungus and bacteria. For fungus, add salt at 3g per litre as a bath for 5 minutes, or treat the whole pond with 1kg salt per 1000 litres. For bacteria, use oxytetracycline at 50mg per kg of fish, mixed with feed for 5 days. But prevention is better: keep water clean, do not overstock, and avoid handling fish roughly. Also check for predators like birds, snakes, and frogs. Cover the pond with netting if needed. Keep a daily log: date, feed amount, water temperature, fish behaviour, and any deaths. Remove dead fish immediately; they spread disease. If many fish die, test pH and ammonia. Do a 30% water change and stop feeding for a day. After two weeks, you can start sampling fish to check growth. Use a cast net to catch a few, weigh them, and return them. If they are growing well, adjust feed. Healthy fish are active and have shiny skin.

5. Monitoring Post-Stocking Behavior

After stocking, spend at least 30 minutes each morning and evening watching your fish. Normal behaviour: fish swim calmly, come to the surface at feeding time, and scatter when you approach. Abnormal behaviour tells you problems. If fish gasp at the surface in early morning, oxygen is low. Increase aeration by splashing water or running a pump. If they stay at the bottom and do not eat, check temperature and pH. If they rub against the pond walls, they may have parasites. If they swim erratically or spin, it could be a toxin or disease. Also watch for grouping. If all fish crowd in one corner, there may be a predator or low oxygen in other areas. Check water temperature daily with a simple thermometer. It should be 25-30C. If it drops below 20C, fish stop eating. If it rises above 35C, they stress. Use shade nets or increase water depth to buffer temperature. Check pH every 3 days. Keep a record. If pH changes by more than 0.5 in a day, test again and adjust slowly. Also check for algae blooms. Green water is good, but if it turns pea-soup thick and smells bad, it can crash at night, causing oxygen loss. If that happens, do a 20% water change and reduce feeding. Look for dead fish every morning. Remove them immediately. A few deaths in the first week are normal (stress), but more than 1% per day is a problem. Also check for insects or frogs. Dragonfly larvae eat small fish. Net them out. Birds like herons can eat many fish. Put a net over the pond. If you see fish with white spots or red patches, treat as in Lesson 4. Keep a notebook with daily observations. After one week, you will know your pond's rhythm. Then you can relax but still check twice a day.

6. Troubleshooting Common Start-Up Issues

Even with good preparation, problems happen. Here are common start-up issues and fixes. 1) Fish dying within 24 hours: likely temperature or pH shock. Next time, acclimate longer. Check pH now; adjust if needed. Remove dead fish. 2) Fish gasping at surface: low oxygen. Add a pump or splash water. Stop feeding for 24 hours. 3) Water turns milky white: bacterial bloom. Do a 30% water change. Add salt at 1kg per 1000 litres. 4) Water turns dark green and smells rotten: too much fertiliser. Stop feeding, do 50% water change, add fresh water. 5) Fish not eating: check temperature (below 20C or above 35C), pH, or ammonia. Fix accordingly. 6) Fish have white spots: fungus. Treat with salt bath as in Lesson 4. 7) Fish have red sores: bacteria. Use oxytetracycline in feed. 8) Many fish hiding: predator. Check for birds, snakes, or frogs. Net the pond. 9) Leaking pond: water level drops. Find leak and repair. For concrete, use cement. For liner, use patch kit. 10) Algae too thick: use a rake to remove some, or add tilapia that eat algae. 11) Ammonia high: do 50% water change, stop feeding for 2 days. 12) pH too low: add lime at 100g per square metre. 13) pH too high: add gypsum at 50g per square metre. 14) Fish jumping out: stress or poor water. Check water quality. 15) No growth after 2 weeks: feed more or check feed quality. Always keep records. If you cannot solve a problem, ask your local fisheries officer or an experienced farmer. Do not guess. Many start-up issues are caused by overstocking, overfeeding, or poor water quality. Test your water weekly. A simple test kit costs little and saves fish. Also, buy healthy fingerlings from a trusted source. If you follow the steps in this module, you will have a successful stocking. Remember, patience is key. Do not rush to stock before the water is ready.

<h2>Practical Reference: Pond Commissioning and Stocking</h2>

<p>Commissioning a fish pond is the single most important make-or-break step in the production cycle. For African fish farmers whether operating a 200 m homestead pond in Kisumu, a 1,000 m commercial pond in Ogun State, or a 5,000 m intensive operation in Luapula Province the first 14 days after filling determine whether you harvest profit or lose your entire stock investment. This practical reference covers the full commissioning sequence: filling and leak-testing, water conditioning, fish acclimation, initial feeding, post-stocking monitoring, and troubleshooting. Every step is framed around one business question: <strong>how do I get fish to market faster with lower mortality?</strong></p>

<h3>Step 1: Filling and Testing the Pond</h3>
<p>Fill your pond to 80% capacity and mark the water level. Wait 24 hours. If the level drops more than 2 cm, you have a leak fix it before proceeding. In sandy soils common across the Sahel and coastal West Africa, compact the bottom with clay or install a HDPE liner (0.5 mm minimum). Test water source quality before filling: borehole water may have high iron; river water may carry wild fish eggs and pathogens. If using river water, filter through a 1 mm mesh screen and treat with agricultural lime at 100200 kg/ha. For rain-fed ponds in East Africa, fill during the long rains (MarchMay) to capture free water and reduce pumping costs.</p>

<h3>Step 2: Conditioning Water for Fish Stocking</h3>
<p>Freshly filled water is sterile fish will starve and stress. You must build a plankton bloom before stocking. Apply organic fertilizer (poultry manure at 5001,000 kg/ha, or compost at 2,000 kg/ha) in a sack suspended at four points around the pond. Within 35 days, the water should turn green this is your natural food base. For commercial operations, supplement with inorganic fertilizer (urea at 50 kg/ha + triple superphosphate at 100 kg/ha). Test pH and DO daily. If the bloom is too dense (Secchi &lt; 25 cm), stop fertilizing; if too thin (Secchi &gt; 60 cm), add more. In Africa, where commercial fish feed costs $0.80$1.20/kg, a healthy plankton bloom can cut feed costs by 3040% in the first month.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Acclimating and Releasing Fish</h3>
<p>Fingerlings transported in plastic bags from a hatchery arrive stressed. Float the sealed bags on the pond surface for 1520 minutes to equalize temperature. Then open the bags and gradually add pond water (about 1 cup every 5 minutes) over 2030 minutes. This equalizes pH and temperature. Finally, release the fish gently at the pond edge never dump them from a height. For catfish, release at dusk to reduce stress. Stock at recommended densities: Nile tilapia at 35 fish/m in semi-intensive ponds, African catfish at 23 fish/m. Overstocking is the most common cause of stunted growth and disease outbreaks.</p>

<h3>Step 4: Initial Feeding and Health Checks</h3>
<p>Do not feed on stocking day. Start feeding 24 hours after stocking with a fine crumble or powder feed at 58% of body weight per day, split into 34 feeds. Observe feeding response: healthy fish rise to the surface within 2 minutes. If they ignore feed, check DO and temperature immediately. Inspect a sample of 1020 fish daily for the first week for signs of disease: frayed fins, white spots, red ulcers, or erratic swimming. In Africa, common early problems include <em>Streptococcus</em> in tilapia (treated with oxytetracycline in feed at 50 mg/kg for 7 days) and <em>Aeromonas</em> in catfish (treated with florfenicol). Always consult a veterinarian or extension officer before using antibiotics misuse creates resistance and market rejection.</p>

<h4 style="color:#c62828; margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING: Water Quality and Chemical Handling</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;">Never enter a pond deeper than 1 meter alone. Pond bottoms can be soft and trap you. Always wear a life vest when working near deep water, especially during netting or sampling. When applying agricultural lime, wear goggles, gloves, and a dust mask lime causes severe eye and skin burns. Store all chemicals (lime, fertilizers, antibiotics) away from children and livestock. If you feel dizzy, nauseous, or develop a skin rash after handling pond water, seek medical attention immediately some pond-borne bacteria (e.g., <em>Aeromonas hydrophila</em>) can infect human wounds. In malaria-endemic areas, use mosquito nets and repellent when working at dawn and dusk.</p>
</div>

<h3>Step 5: Monitoring Post-Stocking Behavior</h3>
<p>For the first 7 days, spend 15 minutes at dawn and dusk observing your fish. Healthy fish swim actively, feed aggressively, and stay in the water column. Warning signs: fish gasping at the surface (low DO), crowding at the inlet (poor water quality), or hiding at the bottom (stress or disease). Keep a daily log: water temperature, DO, pH, feeding response, mortality count, and weather. In Africa, where extension services may be distant, your logbook is your best diagnostic tool. If mortality exceeds 1% per day, act immediately partial water exchange, increased aeration, or emergency harvest of affected fish.</p>

<h3>Step 6: Troubleshooting Common Start-Up Issues</h3>
<p><strong>Problem: Water stays clear after fertilizing.</strong> Cause: low alkalinity or cold water. Solution: add lime and wait 2 more days. <strong>Problem: Fish die within 24 hours of stocking.</strong> Cause: temperature or pH shock. Solution: always acclimate for 30 minutes; test transport water. <strong>Problem: Fish gasp at surface at dawn.</strong> Cause: low DO from plankton respiration. Solution: reduce fertilizer, add aeration, or flush 20% of water. <strong>Problem: Sudden green water turns brown.</strong> Cause: plankton crash. Solution: flush and re-fertilize at half dose. <strong>Problem: Fish refuse feed.</strong> Cause: stress, disease, or poor water quality. Solution: test water, inspect fish, and consult an extension officer.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Fill pond to 80%, mark level, and check for leaks after 24 hours.</li>
<li>Test pH, DO, ammonia, nitrite, and alkalinity before stocking.</li>
<li>Apply organic or inorganic fertilizer to build a green plankton bloom (Secchi 3045 cm).</li>
<li>Acclimate fingerlings for 30 minutes by floating bags and gradually adding pond water.</li>
<li>Stock at recommended density (tilapia 35/m; catfish 23/m semi-intensive).</li>
<li>Start feeding 24 hours after stocking at 58% body weight, split into 34 feeds.</li>
<li>Observe fish behavior daily at dawn and dusk; record all readings in a logbook.</li>
