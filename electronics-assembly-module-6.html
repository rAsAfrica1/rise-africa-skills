1. PCB Types and Materials

Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs) come in three main types: single-sided, double-sided, and multilayer. Single-sided PCBs have components on one side and copper traces on the other. They are cheap and used in simple devices like power supplies. Double-sided PCBs have copper on both sides and are common in consumer electronics. Multilayer PCBs stack multiple copper layers with insulating material between them; they are used in smartphones and computers. In Africa, most small workshops start with single-sided or double-sided boards because they are easier to repair and cost less.

The base material is usually FR-4, a fiberglass-epoxy composite. FR-4 is strong, flame-resistant, and handles heat well. Cheaper boards may use CEM-1 or paper-based materials, but they warp easily. The copper thickness is measured in ounces per square foot. 1 oz copper is about 35 micrometers thick; 2 oz copper handles more current for power circuits. The solder mask is the green (or other color) coating that protects copper traces and prevents solder bridges. The silkscreen is white text that shows component locations and polarity.

When you buy PCBs locally, check for warping, scratches, or exposed copper. A good board lies flat on a table. In hot, humid African climates, store PCBs in sealed bags with silica gel to prevent oxidation. Oxidized copper looks dull brown and will not solder well; clean it with fine steel wool or a pencil eraser before assembly.

For prototyping, you can make your own single-sided PCB using the toner transfer method: print the layout on glossy paper, iron it onto a clean copper board, then etch in ferric chloride. This is messy but works. Alternatively, use perfboard or stripboard for simple circuits. Always wear gloves and eye protection when etching.

Understanding PCB types helps you choose the right board for the job and handle it correctly. A well-chosen board saves time and prevents assembly problems.

2. ESD-Safe PCB Handling

Electrostatic discharge (ESD) is a sudden flow of electricity between two objects at different potentials. You feel a shock when you touch a doorknob after walking on a carpet. That same spark, too small to feel, can destroy sensitive electronic components like microchips. A human body can carry up to 25,000 volts in dry conditions. A CMOS IC can be damaged by as little as 10 volts. In many African workshops, dry harmattan winds or air-conditioned rooms increase ESD risk.

To prevent ESD damage, always work at an ESD-safe workstation. Use a wrist strap connected to a grounded mat. The wrist strap has a 1 megaohm resistor to protect you from electric shock. If you don't have a mat, touch a grounded metal object (like a water pipe) before handling boards. Never wear synthetic clothing (polyester, nylon) when working on PCBs; cotton is safer. Avoid plastic chairs and carpeted floors.

Store PCBs and components in anti-static bags (usually silver or pink). Do not use ordinary plastic bags. Handle boards by the edges; never touch the gold fingers or component leads. When shipping, use anti-static foam or pink bubble wrap. In humid climates, ESD is less of a problem, but still take precautions.

If you don't have a wrist strap, you can make a simple one: connect a 1M resistor to a wire, attach one end to your wrist with a band, and ground the other end. Test it with a multimeter to ensure the resistor is working. Always check your wrist strap daily.

ESD damage may not cause immediate failure; it can weaken components so they fail later. This is called latent damage. By following ESD-safe practices, you protect your work and your reputation. Teach everyone in your workshop to do the same.

3. Component Placement Guidelines

Placing components correctly on a PCB is critical for the circuit to work and for easy soldering. Start by organizing your components: resistors, capacitors, ICs, connectors, etc. Use the silkscreen markings and your schematic or layout diagram to identify each location. Work from shortest to tallest components: first resistors and diodes, then capacitors, then transistors, then ICs, and finally connectors and tall parts. This order makes soldering easier because shorter parts don't get in the way.

For through-hole components, bend the leads to fit the hole spacing. Insert the component so it sits flat against the board. For resistors, the color code should be readable from left to right or top to bottom. For capacitors, check polarity: electrolytic capacitors have a stripe marking the negative lead. For diodes, the band marks the cathode. For ICs, the notch or dot indicates pin 1. Align pin 1 with the square pad or silkscreen mark.

For surface-mount components (SMD), use tweezers to place them. Apply solder paste to the pads first, then place the component, then reflow with a hot air station or reflow oven. Ensure the component is centered on the pads. For fine-pitch ICs, use a magnifying lamp or microscope. In Africa, many workshops use manual pick-and-place with vacuum pens.

Keep components close to the board to avoid vibration damage. For heavy components like transformers, use adhesive or mounting screws. Leave space between components for cooling and for soldering iron access. Do not place heat-sensitive components near power resistors or voltage regulators. Route wires neatly; use cable ties or heat shrink.

After placement, double-check all polarities and orientations. A single reversed electrolytic capacitor can explode. A reversed IC can burn out instantly. Take your time and verify each component before soldering. Good placement saves rework and ensures reliability.

4. Polarity and Orientation Checks

Polarity and orientation mistakes are the most common assembly errors. They can destroy components, damage boards, and waste time. Always check polarity before soldering. For electrolytic capacitors, the longer lead is positive; the stripe on the body marks negative. On the PCB, the positive pad may be square while the negative is round, or there may be a '+' mark. If you install it backwards, it can bulge, leak, or explode when power is applied.

Diodes have a band indicating the cathode (negative). The PCB silkscreen shows a band or arrow. For LEDs, the flat side of the rim and the shorter lead indicate the cathode. For Zener diodes, the band is also the cathode. For bridge rectifiers, the '+' and '-' symbols are marked on the case and board.

Integrated circuits (ICs) have a notch or dot at pin 1. The PCB has a matching mark, often a square pad or a dot. For DIP ICs, pin 1 is at the top-left when the notch is up. For SMD ICs, pin 1 is marked with a dot or chamfer. If you insert an IC backwards, it will likely overheat and fail immediately.

Transistors have three leads: base, collector, emitter. The flat side usually faces a certain direction. Check the datasheet and the PCB silkscreen. For TO-92 packages, the flat side faces you when reading the part number. For power transistors, the metal tab is often the collector. Always verify with a multimeter if unsure.

Connectors and plugs may have keying or polarity marks. For example, barrel jacks have center positive or center negative. Check before soldering. For battery holders, the '+' and '-' are marked. Use a multimeter to confirm continuity to the correct pads.

After soldering, do a visual inspection with a magnifying glass. Use a checklist: all electrolytic capacitors correct? All diodes correct? All ICs oriented correctly? All connectors correct? If you find a mistake, desolder carefully with a solder sucker and wick. Do not power up until you are sure. A few minutes of checking saves hours of troubleshooting.

5. Adhesive and Staking Compounds

Adhesives and staking compounds secure components to the PCB, especially for surface-mount parts, heavy components, and wires. They prevent movement during soldering, vibration, and thermal cycling. In Africa, where transportation and handling can be rough, proper staking is essential for reliability.

For surface-mount components, use solder paste to hold them in place during reflow. For through-hole components that might shift, use a small dot of cyanoacrylate (super glue) or epoxy. But be careful: some adhesives are conductive or corrosive. Use only electronics-grade adhesives, such as Loctite 3609 or Henkel Hysol. For temporary holding, use double-sided tape or Blu Tack, but remove it after soldering.

Staking compounds, also called conformal coatings or potting compounds, protect against moisture, dust, and vibration. Silicone-based compounds like Dow Corning 3140 are flexible and easy to rework. Epoxy-based compounds like Hysol EA 9394 are rigid and provide strong mechanical support. Polyurethane compounds offer good chemical resistance. Choose based on your environment: in humid coastal areas, silicone is better; in high-vibration applications, epoxy is better.

Apply staking compound with a syringe or toothpick. For large components like electrolytic capacitors, apply a bead along the base. For wires, use a dot at the exit point. Avoid covering test points, connectors, or adjustable parts. Cure according to the datasheet: some cure at room temperature in 24 hours, others need heat (e.g., 80Â°C for 1 hour).

If you need to rework, use a solvent like isopropyl alcohol or acetone to soften the adhesive. For silicone, you can cut it with a knife. Always work in a ventilated area and wear gloves. Store adhesives in a cool, dry place; many have a shelf life of 6-12 months.

Proper use of adhesives and staking compounds makes your products last longer, especially in Africa's challenging environments. It's a small step that makes a big difference.

6. PCB Cleaning and Contamination Control

After soldering, PCBs often have flux residue, dust, and other contaminants. Flux can be corrosive, conductive, and can cause short circuits or poor adhesion of conformal coating. Cleaning is essential for reliability, especially in humid African climates where moisture combines with flux to cause corrosion.

There are three main flux types: rosin (RA), water-soluble (WS), and no-clean (NC). Rosin flux leaves a sticky residue that must be cleaned with isopropyl alcohol (IPA) or a commercial flux remover. Water-soluble flux must be cleaned with deionized water, often with a brush, within a few hours. No-clean flux can be left on, but it's still best to remove it for high-reliability applications.

For manual cleaning, use a soft-bristle brush (like an anti-static brush) and 99% isopropyl alcohol. Pour IPA into a small container, dip the brush, and scrub the board gently. Work in a well-ventilated area away from sparks. For stubborn residue, use a flux remover spray like Chemtronics Flux-Off. After scrubbing, rinse with fresh IPA and blow dry with compressed air or a lint-free cloth. Do not use cotton swabs; they leave fibers.

For water-soluble flux, use warm deionized water (about 60Â°C) and a brush. Rinse thoroughly and dry immediately with compressed air. Do not let water sit on the board. For no-clean flux, you can often skip cleaning, but if you need to remove it, use IPA.

Ultrasonic cleaning is effective but can damage certain components (crystals, relays). Use only if your components are compatible. In Africa, many workshops use simple manual cleaning. Always wear gloves and eye protection.

After cleaning, inspect the board under a magnifying glass for remaining residue, solder splashes, or loose metal. Remove any splashes with tweezers. If you plan to apply conformal coating, clean first and let the board dry completely. Contamination control also means storing boards in anti-static bags with desiccant. Clean boards solder better and last longer.

Practical Reference: PCB Handling and Component Placement

This practical reference gives electronics assemblers, workshop supervisors, and small-batch manufacturers in Africa a working guide to handling printed circuit boards (PCBs) and placing components reliably. It is written for real production floors â from a two-person workshop in Kumasi assembling inverter boards to a contract manufacturer in Nairobi running SMT lines for solar charge controllers. The emphasis is on decisions that protect yield, reduce rework, and keep your business profitable.

Poor PCB handling and placement are among the most common causes of field failures in locally assembled electronics. Contamination, electrostatic discharge (ESD), and reversed polarity account for a large share of warranty returns. Each of these is preventable with disciplined process control, and each prevention step costs far less than a returned unit.

In African assembly operations, PCB defects rarely surface immediately. A board with a partially contaminated pad may pass a quick functional test but fail after three months in a solar inverter or a vehicle tracker. ESD-Safe PCB Handling

Static electricity is invisible but destructive. In dry African highland climates (for example, Addis Ababa and Nairobi during the dry season), relative humidity can fall below 30%, dramatically increasing ESD risk. A single discharge from a technician's finger can exceed 3,000 V and puncture a MOSFET gate oxide without leaving visible damage.

Minimum controls for any assembly bench:

  • Grounded ESD wrist strap, tested daily (target resistance 1 MÎ©).
  • Dissipative work surface mat bonded to the same earth point.
  • PCB storage in pink or black conductive bags, never bare on plastic trays.
  • Ionisers where humidity is consistently below 40%.
  • No synthetic clothing or plastic chairs at the assembly bench.

Never handle bare PCBs or moisture-sensitive components (MSDs) without a verified ESD ground. Do not touch board contacts with bare fingers â skin oils and salts cause corrosion and leakage currents. When using leaded solder, work under fume extraction; lead exposure is cumulative and hazardous. Treat all boards as live until proven otherwise, and never place a populated board on a conductive surface during testing.

Component Placement Guidelines

Placement quality determines solder joint reliability, thermal performance, and assembly speed. Follow a consistent sequence: place low-profile passives first, then ICs, then connectors and tall components last. This prevents taller parts from interfering with the placement nozzle or hand tool.

Key placement rules:

  • Keep 0.5 mm minimum clearance between component bodies for hand soldering; 0.2 mm for reflow.
  • Orient all polarised components in the same direction where the layout allows (reduces inspection errors).
  • Place decoupling capacitors within 2 mm of the IC power pin, with the shortest possible trace to ground.
  • Keep heat-sensitive parts away from power resistors and regulators.
  • Respect courtyard boundaries defined in the CAD library â do not overlap.

Polarity and Orientation Checks

Reversed electrolytic capacitors, LEDs, diodes, and ICs are the single most common assembly defect in small workshops. Build a verification habit: mark the polarity on your assembly drawing, then verify with a multimeter in diode mode before power-up. For ICs, confirm pin 1 orientation against the silkscreen dot or chamfer. For LEDs, confirm anode/cathode against the flat side of the package.

Adhesive and Staking Compounds

Adhesives hold components during wave soldering, damp vibration, and protect large parts (electrolytics, inductors, connectors) from mechanical shock. Use red-dot epoxy for bottom-side SMT parts before wave soldering, and silicone or polyurethane staking for tall through-hole parts. Cure schedules matter: most epoxies require 30â60 minutes at 120â150 Â°C, while RTV silicones cure at room temperature over 24 hours. In humid African conditions, ensure full cure before conformal coating or enclosure sealing.

PCB Cleaning and Contamination Control

Flux residues, dust, and finger oils cause leakage currents, corrosion, and electrochemical migration. For no-clean fluxes, cleaning is optional if the process window is respected â but in high-humidity environments, cleaning is strongly recommended. Use isopropyl alcohol (IPA â¥ 99%) with lint-free wipes for manual cleaning, or a water-based saponifier system for volume production. After cleaning, dry boards thoroughly (bake at 60 Â°C for 30 minutes) before coating or enclosure.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. ESD wrist strap tested and connected before touching any board.
  2. Boards stored in conductive bags; MSD parts logged with open-date.
  3. Placement drawing shows polarity and pin-1 orientation for every polarised part.
  4. Decoupling capacitors within 2 mm of IC power pin.
  5. Clearances verified against IPC-7351B courtyard rules.
  6. Adhesive applied and fully cured before wave soldering or enclosure.
  7. Boards cleaned with IPA or saponifier; dried before coating.
  8. Final visual and electrical inspection signed off by a second technician.
