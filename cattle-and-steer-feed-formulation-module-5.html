1. Cereal grains and by-products: corn, barley, wheat, DDGS

Cereal grains are the main energy source in cattle diets. In Africa, maize (corn) is the most common, followed by barley, wheat, and by-products like DDGS (distillers dried grains with solubles). Maize has about 3.3 Mcal/kg of digestible energy and 9% crude protein. It is highly digestible but must be cracked or ground to improve utilization. Barley has slightly less energy (3.0 Mcal/kg) and more fiber, making it safer for rumen health. Wheat is high in energy (3.4 Mcal/kg) but ferments quickly, risking acidosis; limit to 30-40% of the grain mix. DDGS is a by-product of ethanol production, with 25-30% crude protein and high energy (3.2 Mcal/kg). It is an excellent protein and energy source, especially for growing steers.

When feeding grains, introduce them gradually over 7-10 days to avoid digestive upsets. A typical starter diet for feedlot steers might include 60% cracked maize, 20% DDGS, 15% roughage, and 5% mineral-vitamin premix. In Africa, small-scale feedlots often use locally available maize bran or wheat bran as cheaper energy sources. These have lower energy (2.5-2.8 Mcal/kg) but are cost-effective. Always test grain quality for molds (aflatoxins) which are common in humid regions. Store grains in dry, ventilated areas.

For a 300 kg steer gaining 1 kg/day, you need about 7.5 kg of dry matter (DM) per day, with 60-70% from grains. If using maize, that's about 4.5-5 kg of maize. If replacing with DDGS, use 2-3 kg DDGS plus 2-3 kg maize. Barley can replace maize at 1:1 but may reduce gain slightly. Wheat should not exceed 2 kg per day. Always provide free-choice mineral mix with calcium and salt. In East Africa, maize silage plus DDGS is a popular combination. In West Africa, sorghum and millet grains are used similarly. Remember, grain is energy, but too much causes acidosis. Balance with roughage and monitor manure consistency.

2. Oilseed meals: soybean, cottonseed, canola meals

Oilseed meals are the primary protein supplements in cattle diets. Soybean meal (SBM) is the gold standard, with 44-48% crude protein and a good amino acid profile. It is highly digestible and palatable. In Africa, full-fat soybeans can be roasted and ground on-farm to save costs. Cottonseed meal (CSM) has 36-41% protein but contains gossypol, a toxin that can affect fertility and growth. Limit CSM to 15-20% of the diet for growing cattle. Canola meal has 34-38% protein and is low in toxins, but it can reduce feed intake if included above 20%. Sunflower meal is another option, with 28-32% protein but high fiber.

For a 250 kg growing steer, you might feed 1-1.5 kg of SBM per day to meet protein needs for 1 kg daily gain. If using CSM, feed 1.5-2 kg, but ensure gossypol levels are below 0.1% of diet. Canola meal can replace SBM at 1.2:1 ratio. In West Africa, groundnut meal is common, with 45-50% protein, but it can be contaminated with aflatoxins. Always source from reputable suppliers and test for toxins. In East Africa, cottonseed cake is widely available and affordable, but it should be balanced with energy sources like maize.

When mixing rations, oilseed meals are usually 10-20% of the total diet. For a 300 kg steer eating 8 kg DM, that's 0.8-1.6 kg of oilseed meal. Combine with cereals and roughages. For example, a ration of 3 kg maize, 1 kg SBM, 3 kg hay, and 1 kg silage provides balanced energy and protein. Always provide clean water and minerals. In intensive systems, oilseed meals are essential for high gains. In extensive grazing, they are used as supplements during dry season. Remember, protein is expensive, so use the cheapest source that meets requirements without toxins.

3. Roughage sources: hay, silage, crop residues

Roughages are fibrous feeds that maintain rumen health and provide energy. Hay is dried forage, with 8-12% protein and 2.0-2.5 Mcal/kg energy. Legume hays like alfalfa have 15-20% protein. In Africa, common hays include Rhodes grass, Napier grass, and maize stover. Silage is fermented forage, usually maize or sorghum, with 8-10% protein and 2.5-3.0 Mcal/kg energy. It is high in moisture (60-70%) and must be stored anaerobically. Crop residues like maize stover, wheat straw, and rice straw are low in protein (3-5%) and energy (1.5-2.0 Mcal/kg) but are abundant and cheap. They need supplementation with protein and energy.

For a 300 kg steer, roughage should be 30-50% of dry matter intake. If feeding maize silage, 3-4 kg DM per day is typical. For hay, 4-5 kg. Crop residues can be 2-3 kg, but must be supplemented with 1 kg of oilseed meal and 1 kg of maize. In Africa, urea treatment of straw can increase digestibility and protein content. Mix 5 kg urea in 100 liters of water, spray on 100 kg straw, cover for 3 weeks. This raises protein from 3% to 8% and improves intake.

Silage making is practical: chop maize at 30-35% dry matter, pack into a pit or silo, and cover tightly. Add salt or molasses to improve fermentation. In dry areas, hay is made from grasses cut at early flowering. Store in dry, shaded areas. Crop residues are collected after harvest and stored under cover. Always provide roughage to prevent acidosis from high-grain diets. In feedlots, roughage is 10-15% of diet. In cow-calf operations, it's 60-80%. For growing steers, aim for 30-40% roughage. Remember, roughage is not just filler; it supports rumen microbes and provides energy. Balance with grains and protein meals for optimal growth.

4. Urea and non-protein nitrogen utilization

Urea is a non-protein nitrogen (NPN) source that rumen microbes convert into microbial protein. It contains 46% nitrogen, equivalent to 287% crude protein. It is the cheapest nitrogen source, but must be used carefully. In Africa, urea is widely available as fertilizer, but feed-grade urea is safer. Urea is only effective when the rumen has enough energy (from grains or molasses) and minerals (sulfur, phosphorus) for microbial growth. Without energy, urea can cause ammonia toxicity, leading to death. The rule: never feed urea to starving cattle or on empty rumen. Introduce gradually over 2-3 weeks.

For a 300 kg steer, the maximum urea in the diet is 1% of dry matter, or about 30-40 grams per day. A typical urea-based supplement: 1 kg urea, 10 kg maize meal, 1 kg salt, 1 kg mineral mix. Feed 0.5-1 kg per day. Urea can replace part of the protein from oilseed meals. For example, 100 g urea plus 1 kg maize can replace 0.5 kg soybean meal in terms of nitrogen, but not amino acids. In Ethiopia, urea-treated straw is common: 5 kg urea in 100 L water for 100 kg straw, covered for 21 days. This increases digestibility and protein. In Kenya, urea-molasses blocks are used for dry season supplementation.

Safety: mix urea thoroughly with other feeds. Never feed urea alone. Provide plenty of water. Symptoms of toxicity: bloat, staggering, rapid breathing. Treat with 2-5 liters of vinegar or 5% acetic acid. In practice, use urea only when energy is available. For growing steers, urea can supply 20-30% of protein needs. For high-producing animals, rely more on true protein. Always consult a nutritionist. Urea is a tool, not a complete feed. In Africa, it is a cost-effective way to improve low-quality roughages. But remember, it is toxic if misused. Store in a dry place, away from children and animals.

5. Rumen degradable and undegradable protein sources

Protein in cattle diets is classified by how it is broken down in the rumen. Rumen degradable protein (RDP) is fermented by microbes to ammonia, which is used to make microbial protein. Rumen undegradable protein (RUP), also called bypass protein, escapes rumen fermentation and is digested in the small intestine. Both are important. RDP supports microbial growth, while RUP provides amino acids directly to the animal. In Africa, common RDP sources include urea, soybean meal, and fresh forage. RUP sources include fish meal, blood meal, meat meal, and heat-treated soybean meal.

For a growing steer gaining 1 kg/day, you need about 12-14% crude protein in the diet. Of that, 60-70% can be RDP and 30-40% RUP. For example, a 300 kg steer eating 8 kg DM needs 1 kg protein. If using urea (RDP) and fish meal (RUP), you might feed 50 g urea (provides 140 g protein equivalent) plus 200 g fish meal (provides 120 g RUP). The rest comes from grains and roughages. In practice, most diets meet RDP needs with oilseed meals and urea. RUP is critical for high-producing animals. In East Africa, fish meal is expensive, so blood meal from abattoirs is a good alternative, with 80% protein and high RUP. Heat treatment of soybeans (roasting) increases RUP by 20-30%.

To balance, use the Cornell Net Carbohydrate and Protein System (CNCPS) or simple software like Feedsoft. In Africa, many extension services provide ration balancing charts. For a simple approach: ensure 1 kg of oilseed meal per 100 kg body weight for growing cattle. If using urea, limit to 1% of DM. For high gains, add 100-200 g of bypass protein. Remember, excess RDP is wasted as urea in urine, costing money and polluting the environment. Excess RUP is also wasted. So balance is key. In dry season, RDP is often limiting, so urea or poultry litter can help. In wet season, RUP may be limiting for high growth. Test your feeds for protein content if possible. Use local sources: brewers grains, cottonseed cake, sunflower cake. Adjust for stage of growth and body condition.

6. Balancing energy-to-protein ratios

Balancing energy and protein is the most critical part of cattle feed formulation. The ratio of energy to protein affects growth, feed efficiency, and cost. Energy is measured in megacalories (Mcal) of digestible energy (DE) or total digestible nutrients (TDN). Protein is crude protein (CP). For growing steers, the ideal ratio is about 5-6 Mcal DE per kg of CP, or 12-14% CP in the diet. For finishing cattle, energy is higher (70-80% TDN) and protein lower (10-12% CP). For dry cows, energy is low (50-55% TDN) and protein 8-10% CP.

To balance, first determine the animal's requirements. A 300 kg steer gaining 1 kg/day needs 7.5 kg DM, 65% TDN, and 13% CP. That means 4.9 kg TDN and 0.98 kg CP. If your feed has 60% TDN and 10% CP, you need to supplement. Add 1 kg maize (80% TDN, 9% CP) and 0.5 kg soybean meal (80% TDN, 44% CP). Calculate: total TDN = (5.5 kg base x 0.60) + (1 kg maize x 0.80) + (0.5 kg SBM x 0.80) = 3.3 + 0.8 + 0.4 = 4.5 kg TDN. Still short, so add 0.5 kg maize. Total CP = (5.5 x 0.10) + (1.5 x 0.09) + (0.5 x 0.44) = 0.55 + 0.135 + 0.22 = 0.905 kg CP. Close to 0.98. Adjust with 0.2 kg SBM. In practice, use a Pearson Square or software. For example, to make a 14% CP mix from maize (9% CP) and SBM (44% CP): parts maize = 44-14=30, parts SBM = 14-9=5. So 30:5 = 6:1, meaning 6 kg maize and 1 kg SBM gives 14% CP.

In Africa, energy is often cheaper than protein, so maximize energy from grains and roughages, then add protein. But too much energy causes acidosis; too little slows growth. Monitor body condition score (BCS) monthly. If BCS drops, increase energy. If manure is loose, reduce energy. Use local feed tables from ILRI or FAO. For example, in Kenya, a common ration for growing steers: 3 kg maize silage, 2 kg Napier hay, 1 kg cottonseed cake, 0.5 kg maize bran, and minerals. This gives about 12% CP and 60% TDN. Adjust based on weight gain. Weigh cattle monthly to track progress. Remember, the cheapest ration is not always the most profitable; balance for optimal gain.

7. Practical Reference

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Energy and Protein Sources in Cattle Diets</h2>

<p>Feed cost represents 6075% of total operating costs in African beef and dairy enterprises. Within that feed cost, energy and protein sources typically account for 8090% of the ration budget. For commercial cattle feeders in Kenya, South Africa, Nigeria, Ethiopia, and Tanzania, the difference between profit and loss often comes down to how intelligently you combine locally available energy sources (cereal grains and by-products) with protein sources (oilseed meals, urea, and rumen-undegradable protein) to meet animal requirements at the lowest cost per kilogram of gain or litre of milk.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference lesson provides the working data, ratios, and decision rules you need to formulate cost-effective cattle diets using ingredients available in African markets. It is designed as a field companion print it, laminate it, and keep it in the feed store.</p>

<h3>Energy Sources: Cereal Grains and By-Products</h3>

<p>Cereal grains are the backbone of intensive cattle feeding. In Africa, the most commercially relevant are maize (corn), barley, wheat, and sorghum, plus by-products such as wheat bran, maize bran, and dried distillers grains with solubles (DDGS). Energy density is measured in Total Digestible Nutrients (TDN) or Megajoules of Metabolisable Energy (MJ ME/kg DM).</p>

<p>The critical business consideration is starch availability and processing cost. Whole or coarsely cracked grains are cheaper to process but less digestible. Steam-flaking or fine grinding increases starch digestibility by 515% but adds processing cost. In most African feedlots, a hammer mill producing a 24 mm particle size offers the best economic compromise.</p>

<p>Protein is the most expensive component of cattle diets. In African markets, soybean meal (SBM) is the gold standard but often priced at 23 times the cost of energy sources. Cottonseed meal, canola meal, sunflower meal, and urea offer cheaper alternatives but each has constraints.</p>

<h3>Roughage Sources: Hay, Silage, and Crop Residues</h3>

<p>Roughage is not just filler it maintains rumen health, stimulates chewing, and buffers acid production. In African systems, the most available roughages are maize stover, sorghum stover, wheat straw, rice straw, natural pasture hay, and legume hays (lucerne/alfalfa, groundnut haulms, cowpea hay).</p>

<p>Quality varies enormously. Maize stover harvested immediately after grain harvest may test 57% CP and 5055% TDN, while weathered stover can drop to 3% CP and 40% TDN. Treating crop residues with urea (45 kg urea per 100 kg dry residue) can increase digestibility by 1015 percentage points and CP by 46 percentage points a highly cost-effective intervention where labour is cheap.</p>

<p>Silage making (maize, sorghum, or Napier grass) preserves energy and protein better than hay, especially in high-rainfall areas. The business case for silage is strongest where land is available and mechanisation is accessible. A 20-tonne silage pit costs roughly USD 8001,200 to establish (plastic, labour, chopping) and can feed 30 head for 90 days.</p>

<h3>Balancing Energy-to-Protein Ratios</h3>

<p>The single most important formulation decision is the energy-to-protein ratio. Too little protein and rumen microbes cannot digest fibre efficiently; too much protein and you waste money on nitrogen excreted in urine. The practical target for most beef cattle is a dietary CP of 1214% for growing/finishing and 1618% for lactating dairy cows, with a TDN:CP ratio of approximately 5:1 to 6:1.</p>

<p>In African conditions, where roughage quality is often poor, the first step is to correct the energy deficit with molasses or maize bran, then add protein. A simple rule of thumb: for every 10 percentage points increase in dietary TDN, you need approximately 2 percentage points more CP to maintain microbial efficiency.</p>

<h3>Practical Formulation Rules for African Feedlots</h3>

<p><strong>Rule 1: Start with the roughage.</strong> Determine how much roughage you have and its quality. If TDN is below 50%, treat with urea or supplement with molasses before adding expensive protein.</p>

<p><strong>Rule 2: Fix energy first, then protein.</strong> Energy is the primary driver of gain. A ration with adequate energy but marginal protein will outperform a high-protein, low-energy ration every time.</p>

<p><strong>Rule 3: Use urea strategically.</strong> Urea is best used in roughage-based diets (up to 1% of DM) to stimulate fibre digestion. In high-grain finishing diets, reduce urea and increase RUP sources.</p>

<p><strong>Rule 4: Match protein degradability to rumen needs.</strong> High-roughage diets need more RDP (6070% of CP). High-grain diets need more RUP (3545% of CP).</p>

<p><strong>Rule 5: Monitor animal performance monthly.</strong> If average daily gain drops below target, adjust the energy-to-protein ratio before adding more of either nutrient. Weigh feed ingredients weekly to ensure the ration matches the formulation.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>1. Know your roughage quality.</strong> Test TDN and CP before formulating. If TDN &lt; 50%, treat with urea or supplement with molasses.</li>
<li><strong>2. Target 5:1 to 6:1 TDN:CP ratio.</strong> Beef cattle: 1214% CP. Dairy: 1618% CP. Adjust based on stage of production.</li>
<li><strong>3. Limit urea to 1% of DM.</strong> Introduce over 710 days. Never feed to starving animals. Mix thoroughly with energy sources.</li>
<li><strong>4. Balance RDP and RUP.</strong> High-roughage diets: 6070% RDP. High-grain diets: 3545% RUP. Use fish meal or blood meal for RUP.</li>
<li><strong>5. Cap cottonseed meal at 15% DM.</strong> Gossypol toxicity risk, especially in young bulls. Canola meal max 15% DM due to glucosinolates.</li>
<li><strong>6. Use molasses at 510% DM.</strong> Cheaper energy but laxative and high in sulphur. Never exceed 15% DM.</li>
<li><strong>7. Calculate cost per kg TDN and cost per kg CP.</strong> Buy the cheapest source of each nutrient, not the cheapest ingredient.</li>
<li><strong>8. Weigh ingredients weekly.</strong> A ration that looks good on paper fails if the loader operator guesses the scoop weight.</li>
</ul>
