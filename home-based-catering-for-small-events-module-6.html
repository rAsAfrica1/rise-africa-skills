1. Choosing Serveware and Platters

When you cater from home, your serveware is part of your brand. Start with what you have: white ceramic plates and platters are the safest base because they make food colours pop. If you don't have enough matching plates, borrow from family or buy plain white melamine platters from local markets. In Nairobi, a 30 cm white melamine platter costs about KES 300; in Lagos, a similar one is around â¦2,500. Avoid dark or patterned plates unless you are confident they won't clash with the food.

For buffet-style events, use platters that are at least 30 cm by 40 cm so you don't have to refill constantly. Rectangular platters fit more food and are easier to arrange. For small bites like samosas or puff-puff, use shallow baskets lined with banana leaves or clean cloth. In West Africa, calabash bowls are beautiful for serving jollof rice or stews, but they must be lined with food-safe plastic or foil if they are not sealed.

Think about height. Flat platters are fine, but you can create visual interest by placing a small bowl upside down under a platter to elevate it. This is called a riser. Use a clean tin can or a small wooden block. Just make sure it's stable. For cold foods like salads, use glass or ceramic bowls that have been chilled in the fridge for at least 30 minutes before serving.

Always have a plan for transporting serveware. Use sturdy plastic crates lined with towels to prevent breakage. Label each platter with the dish name using sticky notes or small cards. At the event, arrange platters so that similar colours are separated. For example, don't put a red tomato stew next to a red pepper sauce. Alternate with green or white dishes. This makes the buffet look balanced and professional.

Finally, invest in a few good serving utensils: tongs, slotted spoons, and ladles. In many African markets, you can find stainless steel utensils at affordable prices. Avoid using the same spoon for different dishes to prevent flavour mixing. Keep a small bowl of hot soapy water near the buffet to swap utensils if needed. Cleanliness and organisation are as important as the food itself.

2. Garnishing and Visual Appeal

Garnishing is not just about looks; it shows care. A simple garnish can turn a plain plate of rice into something that looks restaurant-quality. The rule is: edible, relevant, and minimal. Never put something on the plate that you wouldn't eat. For African dishes, use fresh herbs like coriander (dhania), parsley, or basil. A sprig of coriander on top of a stew adds colour and aroma. In Nigeria, sliced red onions and green peppers are common garnishes for jollof rice.

Use contrasting colours. If your dish is brown (like a meat stew), garnish with something green or red. If it's white (like fufu or rice), use a dark sauce or a sprinkle of paprika. For fried foods like plantain or fish, a wedge of lime or lemon on the side adds a pop of yellow and a fresh scent. Always wash and dry herbs before using them; wet herbs wilt quickly and look sad.

Tools matter. A pair of tweezers (or clean chopsticks) helps place small garnishes precisely. A small squeeze bottle (you can recycle a clean ketchup bottle) is great for drizzling sauces in patterns. For example, drizzle a thin line of pepper sauce over a white plate before placing the food. This is called a 'swoosh' and it looks very professional. You can also use a small sieve to dust paprika or powdered pepper over a dish.

Keep garnishes simple. One or two elements are enough. A common mistake is over-garnishing, which makes the plate look messy. For a plate of rice and chicken, a sprig of parsley and a few slices of tomato are plenty. For a dessert like fruit salad, a mint leaf and a dusting of cinnamon are perfect. If you're serving soup, a swirl of cream or yoghurt and a sprinkle of chopped chives work well.

Timing is critical. Garnish just before serving. If you garnish too early, herbs wilt and sauces dry out. For buffet-style, you can pre-garnish some items but keep them covered with cling film in the fridge. At the event, do a final touch-up. Also, consider the climate: in hot weather, avoid garnishes that wilt quickly like lettuce. Use hardy options like carved carrots or cucumber slices. A simple carved carrot flower takes 2 minutes and impresses guests.

3. Buffet Setup and Flow

A buffet should flow like a river: guests should move smoothly from start to finish without bumping into each other. The golden rule is to set up the buffet in the order that food should be eaten. Start with plates and cutlery at the beginning, then cold dishes (salads, cold meats), then hot main dishes, then sides, then sauces, and finally desserts. This prevents guests from having to backtrack. In many African events, the buffet is a central part of the celebration, so make it easy for elders and people with disabilities to access.

Use tables that are at least 75 cm high. If you don't have enough tables, use sturdy boxes covered with clean tablecloths. Tablecloths should be long enough to touch the floor or at least cover the legs. White or cream cloths are best because they make food stand out. If you're using a patterned cloth, keep it subtle. Place serving spoons in each dish, with the handle facing the guest. For rice and stews, use a large spoon; for salads, use tongs.

Spacing is important. Leave at least 30 cm between dishes so guests can serve themselves without knocking things over. If you have many dishes, use two tables in a U-shape or L-shape. This creates more serving space and reduces congestion. Put drinks on a separate table to avoid spills on the food. If you're serving hot food, use chafing dishes or insulated containers. In a home setting, you can use large pots with lids and keep them on low heat if electricity is available. Otherwise, serve hot food first and keep the rest covered.

Label each dish. Use small cards with the name of the dish and any allergens (e.g., 'Contains peanuts'). This is especially important for guests with dietary restrictions. You can write on plain index cards with a marker. Place the card in a small photo frame or fold it like a tent. For a professional touch, print labels if you have access to a computer and printer.

Finally, assign someone to monitor the buffet. This person can refill dishes, wipe spills, and help guests. If you're catering alone, check the buffet every 15 minutes. Keep extra serving spoons and napkins nearby. After the event, pack up leftovers quickly and safely. Buffet flow is not just about logistics; it's about making guests feel welcome and cared for.

4. Plated Service Techniques

Plated service means you serve each guest an individual plate, rather than letting them serve themselves. This is common at sit-down dinners, weddings, and formal events. The key is consistency: every plate should look the same. Use a ladle or spoon to portion food evenly. For rice, use a small bowl or cup to mould a perfect dome, then turn it onto the plate. For stews, use a ladle to pour sauce over the rice, leaving a border of rice visible. This is called 'framing'.

Start with the plate. Hold the plate in your left hand (if you're right-handed) and serve with your right. Place the starch (rice, fufu, ugali) at the centre or slightly off-centre. Then place the protein (meat, fish, chicken) next to it. Finally, add the vegetables or sauce. Leave about 2 cm of empty plate around the edge. This is called the 'margin' and it makes the plate look clean. Don't overcrowd. A good rule: the food should cover no more than two-thirds of the plate.

Use tools to help. A ring mould (you can make one from a clean tin can with both ends removed) is great for shaping rice or mashed potatoes. A squeeze bottle for sauce allows you to draw patterns. For example, zigzag a line of pepper sauce over the protein. A pair of tongs helps place vegetables neatly. Always wipe the edge of the plate with a clean cloth or paper towel before serving. Any drips or fingerprints ruin the presentation.

Temperature matters. Hot food should be served on warm plates. You can warm plates in a low oven (50Â°C) for 10 minutes or run them under hot water and dry them. Cold food, like salads, should be served on chilled plates. If you're serving multiple courses, clear the plates between courses. In African settings, plated service is often used for special guests or at the head table. It shows respect and attention to detail.

Practice portion control. A standard portion of rice is about 150-200g (one cup cooked). A portion of meat is 100-150g. Vegetables should be about 100g. Use a kitchen scale for the first few events to train your eye. Serve sauces on the side if guests have different preferences. Finally, serve from the right side of the guest, and clear from the right as well. If you're serving a large group, consider hiring a helper to assist with plating and serving.

5. Food Photography for Marketing

Good photos can get you more catering jobs. You don't need an expensive camera; a modern smartphone works fine. The key is lighting. Natural daylight is best. Take photos near a window but avoid direct sunlight, which creates harsh shadows. If you must shoot at night, use a lamp with a white bulb and place a white cloth or paper over it to diffuse the light. Never use flash; it makes food look greasy and flat.

Set up a simple background. A clean white or light wooden table works well. You can also use a plain wall or a large piece of cardboard painted white. Avoid busy patterns. Place the food on a nice plate or platter. Add props like a fork, a napkin, or a few fresh ingredients (e.g., a tomato, a sprig of parsley) to add context. But keep it simple. The food is the star.

Angle matters. For most dishes, a 45-degree angle (slightly above) looks best. For tall items like burgers or layered cakes, shoot straight on. For flat items like pizza or platters, shoot from directly above (flat lay). Take multiple shots: close-up, medium, and wide. Use the grid feature on your phone to keep lines straight. Tap on the food to focus, then adjust exposure by sliding your finger up or down.

Edit your photos. Free apps like Snapseed or Lightroom Mobile can brighten, increase contrast, and sharpen. Don't over-edit; the food should look natural. Crop to remove distractions. For social media, use a square or 4:5 ratio. Post at times when your audience is active, like lunchtime or early evening. Use hashtags like #AfricanCatering #HomeCatering #FoodieAfrica.

Consistency is key. Develop a style: maybe you always use a white plate and a wooden background. This makes your brand recognisable. Take photos of your dishes before every event, even if you're busy. Build a portfolio. You can also take short videos of you plating or cooking. These perform well on Instagram Reels and TikTok. Remember, people eat with their eyes first. A good photo can be the difference between a booking and a scroll-past.

6. Theme and Table Design

A theme ties everything together: the food, the decor, and the atmosphere. For small events, choose a theme that is easy to execute and fits your budget. Popular themes in Africa include 'African Heritage' (using traditional fabrics like kente or ankara as table runners), 'Garden Party' (fresh flowers and greenery), and 'Modern Minimalist' (clean lines, white and gold). Pick one theme and stick to it. Don't mix too many ideas.

Start with a colour palette. Choose two or three colours. For example, for an African Heritage theme, use earthy tones like brown, orange, and green. For a Garden Party, use pastel colours like pink, mint, and white. Use these colours in your tablecloth, napkins, and centrepieces. If you don't have coloured tablecloths, use white and add colour with runners or placemats. In many African markets, you can find beautiful local fabrics at affordable prices. A simple strip of ankara fabric as a table runner instantly adds character.

Centrepieces should be low so guests can see each other. Use small vases with a single flower, or a bowl of fruit that matches the theme. For an African theme, use small calabashes or wooden carvings. For a Garden Party, use mason jars with wildflowers. Candles add warmth but use unscented ones so they don't compete with the food. If you're using candles, place them in glass holders to prevent wax drips. For daytime events, fresh flowers are enough.

Table settings: each guest should have a plate, cutlery, a napkin, and a glass. For a formal look, fold napkins into a simple fan or roll and tie with a ribbon. Place cutlery in the order of use: fork on the left, knife and spoon on the right. If you're serving multiple courses, you may need more cutlery. For buffet, you can set the table with just plates and cutlery at the start.

Lighting sets the mood. For evening events, use warm white string lights or candles. Avoid bright overhead lights. If you're outdoors, use tiki torches or lanterns. Music should be soft background music, not too loud. Finally, personalise the table with place cards if it's a seated dinner. This makes guests feel special. A theme doesn't have to be expensive; it just needs to be consistent and thoughtful.

Plated service is where a home caterer competes with restaurants. The sequence: hot plate (warm in the oven at 60Â°C), starch placed at the 6 o'clock position, protein at the centre, vegetable at 10 o'clock, sauce drizzled last, garnish on top. Serve from the guest's left with the right hand, clear from the right. For small events, two servers can handle 40 guests comfortably if plates are pre-portioned on trays.
