1. Understanding the car breaking and metal scrapyard value chain in Africa

The car breaking and metal scrapyard business in Africa is a cashflow industry built on recovering value from old vehicles and scrap metal. The value chain starts with sourcing: you buy endoflife cars from individuals, insurance companies, taxi fleets, or government auctions. In many African cities, a nonrunning sedan might cost $200$500, while a small truck could be $800$1,500. You then tow or drive it to your yard.

Next comes dismantling. You remove usable parts: alternators, starters, radiators, batteries, tyres, and engines. These are cleaned, tested, and sold to mechanics or roadside parts dealers. A used alternator for a Toyota Corolla might sell for $25$40, while a good engine can fetch $300$800. Parts that dont sell are sorted by metal type: steel, aluminium, copper, and brass. Copper wiring, for example, sells for $2$4 per kilogram to local foundries or exporters.

The final stage is processing and selling scrap metal. You cut large pieces with a hacksaw or angle grinder, then sell to larger scrap dealers or directly to steel mills. In countries like Nigeria, Kenya, or South Africa, a tonne of mixed steel scrap might earn $150$300. Aluminium cans and castings can bring $800$1,200 per tonne. The key is to separate metals carefullycontaminated loads get lower prices.

This business is labourintensive but lowtech. You can start with a hand truck, a set of spanners, and a hacksaw. The value chain works because Africa has a growing vehicle population, limited recycling infrastructure, and high demand for affordable spare parts. By understanding each stepsourcing, dismantling, sorting, and sellingyou can turn a dead car into a steady income stream.

2. Legal requirements and permits for starting a scrapyard in your country

Before you open a scrapyard, you must comply with local laws. In most African countries, you need a business registration certificate from the companies commission. For a sole proprietorship, this costs about $20$50 in Kenya, Nigeria, or Ghana. You also need a tax identification number (TIN) and a local government trade licence, which can cost $50$200 per year depending on your municipality.

Environmental permits are critical. Scrapyards can leak oil, battery acid, and coolant. In South Africa, you must register with the Department of Environmental Affairs and get a waste management licence. In Kenya, the National Environment Management Authority (NEMA) requires an environmental impact assessment (EIA) for yards above a certain size. The EIA can cost $300$1,000. Even for small yards, you must have a plan for used oil, tyres, and batteries. Many countries ban open burning of cables to recover copperyou can be fined heavily.

You also need a scrap metal dealers licence. In Nigeria, the Scrap Metal Dealers Association requires registration, and some states demand a police clearance certificate to prove you are not dealing in stolen vehicles. In Uganda, you must get a permit from the Ministry of Trade. Always keep receipts for every car you buy, including the sellers ID copy and vehicle registration papers. This protects you if police question the origin of a vehicle.

Finally, check zoning laws. You cannot operate a noisy, dusty scrapyard in a residential area. Look for land zoned for industrial or commercial use. If you rent, get a written lease. Visit your local council, environmental office, and police station to ask exactly what is needed. Budget $200$800 for all permits in the first year. Cutting corners leads to closures, fines, or confiscation of your stock.

3. Choosing a strategic location: near roads, towns, or industrial areas

Location can make or break your scrapyard. You need a site that is easy to reach for both sellers and buyers. The best spots are on the outskirts of a town, along a major road or highway. Why? Because tow trucks and customers bringing scrap dont want to drive deep into traffic. A yard 510 km from the city centre on a tarmac road is ideal. In Accra, for example, yards along the Nsawam Road do well because they are visible and accessible.

You also need spaceat least half an acre (2,000 square metres) to start. The ground should be flat, welldrained, and preferably with a hard surface like compacted gravel or concrete. Muddy yards lose metal in the mud and are hard to work in during rains. If you can, choose a site near an industrial area where factories generate scrap metal and also buy your sorted materials. Being close to a steel mill or foundry saves transport costs.

Consider neighbours. You will make noise with hammers, grinders, and trucks. You will have dust and occasional oil spills. So keep away from schools, hospitals, and dense housing. A site near a mechanic village or auto repair cluster is excellent because those businesses both supply old cars and buy spare parts. In Lagos, the Ladipo market area is a hub for used partsa yard nearby has ready customers.

Check access to utilities: water for cleaning parts, electricity for tools (or a generator), and phone network for calls. Security is also keyscrap metal is valuable and can be stolen. A fenced yard with a gate and a night watchman is worth the extra cost. Finally, verify land ownership or lease terms. A verbal agreement can lead to eviction. Get a written lease of at least 35 years so you can build your business without moving.

4. Essential tools and equipment for a beginner car breaker

You dont need a fortune to start breaking cars. Begin with a basic set of hand tools: a socket set (8mm24mm), combination spanners, screwdrivers (flat and Phillips), pliers, a hammer, and a chisel. A good socket set costs $30$60. You also need a hacksaw with spare blades for cutting metal, and a heavyduty pry bar to separate panels. A wheel brace and jack are essential for removing wheels and tyres.

For dismantling engines and transmissions, youll need a torque wrench (to avoid overtightening bolts), a breaker bar, and an engine hoist or a strong chain block. A used engine hoist can cost $150$300. If you cant afford one, use a sturdy tree branch or a steel Aframe with a chain blockjust make sure its safe. You also need drain pans for oil, coolant, and fuel. Never drain these onto the ground.

Power tools speed up work. An angle grinder with cutting and grinding discs ($40$80) is the most versatile tool for cutting exhausts, chassis, and seized bolts. A reciprocating saw ($60$120) is great for cutting through car bodies. A cordless drill/driver ($50$100) helps with removing panels. If you have electricity, a small air compressor ($100$200) and impact wrench make removing rusty bolts much easier. A generator ($200$400) is useful if your yard has no power.

For moving heavy parts, a hand truck (trolley) and a wheelbarrow are cheap and essential. A magnetic pickup tool saves dropped nuts. For safety, you need a fire extinguisher, a firstaid kit, and a spill kit (sand or sawdust for oil). Finally, a small welding machine ($150$300) lets you cut and repair metal for resale. Start with the basics, then reinvest profits into better tools.

5. Safety gear and basic first aid for scrapyard work

Scrapyard work is dangerous: sharp metal, heavy parts, toxic fluids, and fire risks. You must wear personal protective equipment (PPE) every day. Start with steeltoe safety boots ($20$40). They protect your feet from falling parts and nails. Never wear sandals or open shoes. Next, heavyduty work gloves ($5$15) to prevent cuts and burns. Keep a spare pair. Safety glasses ($3$10) are essential when grinding, cutting, or hammeringflying metal can blind you.

Hearing protection (earplugs or earmuffs, $5$20) is needed when using grinders or hammers. A dust mask or respirator ($10$30) protects you from brake dust, paint, and rust particles. When draining fuel or working with batteries, wear chemicalresistant gloves and a face shield. Always have a fire extinguisher (dry powder, $30$60) and a bucket of sand near your work area. Never smoke or use open flames near fuel tanks or batteries.

Basic first aid can save a life. Keep a firstaid kit ($20$40) stocked with bandages, antiseptic cream, gauze, adhesive tape, scissors, and painkillers. For cuts, clean the wound with clean water, apply antiseptic, and cover with a sterile bandage. If bleeding is heavy, apply pressure and seek medical help. For burns, cool the area with running water for 10 minutes, then cover loosely. For eye injuries, flush with clean water and go to a clinic immediately.

You must also handle hazardous fluids safely. Drain oil, coolant, and brake fluid into labelled containers. Never mix them. Used oil can be sold to recyclers. Battery acid is corrosivewear goggles and gloves, and neutralise spills with baking soda. Keep a written emergency plan: phone numbers for a clinic, fire service, and a trusted neighbour. Train anyone working with you on these rules. A safe yard is a productive yard.

6. Setting up a simple record-keeping system for purchases and sales

Record keeping is the difference between a hobby and a real business. You need to know what you bought, what you sold, and what you still have. Start with a simple cash booka notebook with columns for date, description, money in, and money out. Every time you buy a car, write the date, sellers name, vehicle make and model, registration number, and amount paid. Keep a copy of the sellers ID and the vehicle papers. This protects you from stolengoods accusations.

For sales, record the date, item sold (e.g., alternator for Toyota Hilux), buyers name, and price. If you sell scrap metal by weight, note the weight and price per kilogram. At the end of each week, total your income and expenses. This tells you if you are making a profit. A simple exercise book costs $1$2. You can also use a free spreadsheet app on your phone, like Google Sheets, if you have a smartphone.

Inventory is critical. Make a list of all major parts you have in stock: engines, gearboxes, alternators, starters, radiators, batteries, tyres. Write the date you removed them and a condition note (e.g., tested, good). Update the list when you sell. This prevents theft and helps you answer customers quickly. For scrap metal, keep separate piles for steel, aluminium, copper, and brass. Weigh each pile monthly and record the weights.

Finally, keep receipts for all expenses: tools, fuel, transport, permits, and wages. At tax time, these reduce your taxable profit. If you have employees, keep a simple wage book. Review your records every month. Are you spending too much on transport? Are certain parts not selling? Adjust your buying. Good records turn guesswork into decisions. Start today with a notebook and a penno excuses.

<h3>3. Choosing a Strategic Location</h3>

<p>Location determines your transport cost, your customer base, and your regulatory risk. The best sites balance three factors:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Proximity to supply:</strong> Near major roads, taxi ranks, industrial areas, or insurance salvage yards. Every kilometre you tow an ELV costs money.</li>
<li><strong>Proximity to buyers:</strong> Near mechanics, panel beaters, foundries, or metal dealers who will buy your sorted output.</li>
<li><strong>Zoning and neighbours:</strong> Industrial or commercial zoning. Residential areas will generate complaints about noise, dust, and traffic and complaints trigger inspections.</li>
</ul>

<p>A site on the edge of a town, on a feeder road to an industrial zone, with space for a weighbridge and a hard standing area, is close to ideal. Avoid flood-prone land contaminated runoff is an environmental liability and a health risk to your workers.</p>

<h3>5. Safety Gear and Basic First Aid</h3>

<p>Scrapyard work is dangerous. Cuts, crush injuries, burns, and chemical exposure are common. The minimum safety kit for every worker:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Safety boots</strong> (steel toe, puncture-resistant sole)</li>
<li><strong>Heavy-duty gloves</strong> (cut-resistant for handling metal; chemical-resistant for fluids)</li>
<li><strong>Safety glasses / face shield</strong> (grinding, cutting, battery work)</li>
<li><strong>Ear protection</strong> (grinders, hammering)</li>
<li><strong>High-visibility vest</strong> (vehicle movement on site)</li>
<li><strong>First aid kit</strong> burns, cuts, eye wash, and a written emergency contact list</li>
</ul>

<p>Train every worker on <strong>safe lifting</strong>, <strong>fuel handling</strong>, and <strong>battery acid first aid</strong>. A 30-minute induction prevents most injuries. Keep a first aid kit in a fixed, marked location and check it monthly.</p>

<h3>6. Simple Record-Keeping for Purchases and Sales</h3>

<p>You cannot grow a business you cannot measure. A simple record system even a notebook and a phone will transform your margins. Record every transaction with:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Date</strong> and <strong>vehicle/description</strong></li>
<li><strong>Source</strong> (seller name, ID, vehicle registration)</li>
<li><strong>Purchase price</strong> and <strong>transport cost</strong></li>
<li><strong>Parts removed</strong> and their <strong>sale price</strong></li>
<li><strong>Metal sold</strong> (kg, grade, price per kg, buyer)</li>
<li><strong>Daily cash balance</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>A simple spreadsheet or a free app like <em>Wave</em> or <em>Kobo Collect</em> works well. The key is <strong>consistency</strong> record the same day, every day. After one month you will know which vehicles are profitable and which are not.</p>
