1. Irrigation System Design and Layout for African Smallholder Farms

Welcome to Module 6, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on the practical design and layout of irrigation systems for smallholder and commercial farms across Africa. The goal is simple: move water from its source to the crop root zone efficiently, affordably, and in a way the farmer can maintain with locally available tools and spare parts. Before any pipe is laid or pump is bought, you must answer five questions. First, what is the water source and how reliable is it through the dry season? Second, what is the peak crop water requirement in millimetres per day? Third, what is the field size, shape, and slope? Fourth, what is the energy source: diesel, petrol, solar, grid electricity, or gravity? Fifth, what is the farmer budget and labour availability? A design that ignores any of these will fail in the field. Start with a simple sketch on paper. Mark the water source, the pump position, the mainline route, sub-main lines, and lateral positions. For a one hectare vegetable plot in Kenya or Tanzania, a typical layout uses a 32 mm or 40 mm HDPE mainline buried 30 to 40 cm deep along the highest edge of the field, with 16 mm or 20 mm drip laterals running down the slope. Burying the mainline protects it from sun damage, theft, and foot traffic. Always design for gravity where possible. If the source is a river or a dam, a simple gravity-fed system using a raised tank can eliminate fuel costs entirely. A tank placed 10 metres above the field gives roughly 1 bar of pressure, which is enough for drip but not always enough for sprinklers. Calculate pressure needs carefully. Drip systems typically need 1 to 1.5 bar at the lateral inlet. Sprinklers may need 2 to 3 bar. Add friction losses in pipes, which increase with length, flow rate, and smaller diameters. A common mistake is using a long 16 mm lateral on a large plot. Keep lateral runs under 50 metres for 16 mm drip tape and under 100 metres for 20 mm. Use a manifold or sub-main to split the field into blocks. Block sizes should match the pump output so that only one block runs at a time. This is called rotational or block irrigation and it saves water and energy. For a 1 hectare plot with a 2 cubic metre per hour pump, you might design four blocks of 2500 square metres each. Each block runs for one to two hours per day depending on crop demand. Slope matters. On slopes above 2 percent, place drip laterals along the contour, not down the slope, to avoid pressure differences between the top and bottom of the line. Pressure-compensating drippers solve this problem but cost more. On flat land, any layout works but keep runs short to reduce friction. For sprinkler systems on slopes, use smaller sprinkler blocks and adjust nozzle sizes. Water quality is critical. If the source has sand, silt, or algae, you must include a filtration system. Sand filters for open water, screen or disc filters for clean water. A simple settling tank or a gravel and sand filter built from a 200 litre drum can work for small farms. Always include a flush valve at the end of each lateral. Without flushing, sediment builds up and drippers clog within weeks. Design for maintenance access. Leave space around the pump, filters, and valves. Label every valve and block. Train the farmer to flush weekly and clean filters daily if using river water. Consider future expansion. A farmer who starts with half a hectare often wants to double in two years. Size the mainline and pump for the future field, not just the current one. The extra cost now is far less than replacing everything later. Finally, document the design. Draw a simple as-built diagram showing pipe sizes, valve positions, block boundaries, and pump specifications. Laminate it and keep it in the pump house. This single document saves hours of troubleshooting and prevents accidental damage during ploughing. In summary, good design is about matching water source, crop demand, field shape, and farmer capacity. Keep it simple, use locally available materials, design for gravity where possible, and always include filtration and flushing. A well-designed system pays for itself in one or two seasons through higher yields and lower labour.

2. Pump Selection, Installation, and Maintenance in African Conditions

Welcome to Lesson 2. The pump is the heart of any pressurised irrigation system. In Africa, pump failures are one of the top three reasons irrigation projects fail, alongside clogged emitters and poor water source management. This lesson gives you a practical framework for selecting, installing, and maintaining pumps that work in real field conditions. First, understand the two main pump types used in irrigation: centrifugal and submersible. Centrifugal pumps sit above the water surface and suck water up through a suction pipe. They are cheap, easy to repair, and widely available in markets from Nairobi to Accra. However, they can only lift water about 6 to 7 metres vertically because of atmospheric pressure limits. If your water source is deeper than 7 metres, you need a submersible pump or a jet pump. Submersible pumps sit inside the water, usually in a borehole or a deep well. They are more efficient and can lift water from 50 metres or more. They cost more and require a qualified technician to install and repair. For most smallholder surface irrigation from rivers, dams, or shallow wells, a centrifugal pump is the right choice. For boreholes, use a submersible. Second, match the pump to the system. You need two numbers: flow rate in cubic metres per hour and total head in metres. Total head is the vertical lift plus friction losses plus pressure required at the emitters. If your tank is 5 metres above the field and you need 1 bar at the drip lateral, that is 10 metres of pressure head. Add 5 metres lift and 3 metres friction loss, and your total head is 18 metres. Now look at the pump curve. Every pump has a curve showing flow at different heads. Choose a pump that delivers your required flow at your total head, not at zero head. A common mistake is buying a pump rated at 10 cubic metres per hour at zero head, then finding it only delivers 3 cubic metres per hour at 20 metres head. Always read the curve. Third, power source. Diesel and petrol pumps are common but fuel costs eat profits. A 5 horsepower diesel pump running 4 hours per day can cost 200 to 400 US dollars per month in fuel in many African countries. Solar pumps have high upfront cost but zero fuel cost and low maintenance. A 1 kilowatt solar array with a 0.75 kilowatt pump can deliver 3 to 5 cubic metres per hour at 20 metres head, enough for half a hectare of drip. Payback is typically 18 to 36 months compared to diesel. Grid electricity is cheapest per unit where available, but outages are common. Always have a backup or a tank for gravity feeding during outages. Fourth, installation. Place the pump on a concrete pad, level and secure. Use a foot valve with a strainer on the suction pipe to prevent debris and loss of prime. Keep the suction pipe as short and straight as possible. Use a larger diameter suction pipe than the discharge pipe to reduce friction. Install a check valve on the discharge to prevent backflow. Install a pressure gauge before and after the filter so you can monitor clogging. Wire the pump with proper earthing and a circuit breaker. For solar pumps, use a controller with dry-run protection. Dry running destroys pumps in minutes. Fifth, maintenance. Daily: check for leaks, unusual noise, and vibration. Weekly: clean the suction strainer and check the foot valve. Monthly: check impeller wear, replace worn seals, and test the pressure gauge. Annually: service the engine, replace bearings if noisy, and flush the pump housing. Keep a logbook. Record hours run, fuel used, and any repairs. This log helps you predict failures and budget for replacements. In Africa, spare parts availability is a real constraint. Before buying any pump, check that seals, impellers, and bearings are available in your local market or can be ordered within two weeks. A pump that works perfectly but cannot be repaired is useless. Standardise on one or two pump models across your farm or your clients farms. This reduces spare parts inventory and simplifies training. Finally, train the operator. A pump operator should know how to prime the pump, check the oil, clean the filter, read the pressure gauge, and shut down safely. They should know the signs of cavitation, which sounds like gravel moving through the pump. Cavitation destroys impellers. It happens when the pump is starved of water or the suction lift is too high. Fix it immediately by checking the water level, the strainer, and the suction pipe. In summary, choose the right pump type for your water source, match the pump curve to your system head and flow, consider solar for long-term savings, install correctly with strainers and valves, and maintain daily, weekly, monthly, and annually. A well-maintained pump lasts 5 to 10 years. A neglected pump lasts one season.

3. Drip Irrigation Troubleshooting and Emitter Clogging Solutions

Welcome to Lesson 3. Drip irrigation is the most efficient way to deliver water and nutrients to crops, but it is also the most sensitive to clogging. In African conditions, where water often carries sand, silt, algae, and dissolved minerals, emitter clogging is the number one cause of drip system failure. This lesson teaches you how to prevent, diagnose, and fix clogging problems in the field. First, understand the three types of clogging: physical, chemical, and biological. Physical clogging comes from sand, silt, and clay particles. Chemical clogging comes from calcium carbonate, iron, and manganese precipitates. Biological clogging comes from algae, bacteria, and roots. Most real-world clogging is a combination. Prevention is far cheaper than cure. Start with filtration. For open water sources like rivers, dams, and canals, use a sand filter followed by a screen or disc filter. Sand filters remove organic matter and fine particles. Screen filters catch the rest. For clean borehole water, a screen or disc filter is usually enough. Never run drip without a filter. The filter mesh size should be 120 to 200 mesh for drip tape and 100 to 150 mesh for drip lines with turbulent flow emitters. Clean filters daily if using river water, weekly if using borehole water. A clogged filter shows a pressure difference of more than 0.5 bar between the inlet and outlet gauges. Install gauges before and after the filter. Second, flush laterals regularly. Every drip lateral should have a flush valve at the end. Open the flush valve for 30 to 60 seconds once a week, or more often if water is dirty. Flushing removes accumulated sediment before it reaches the emitters. Flush one block at a time at full pressure. If you have many blocks, create a flushing schedule and stick to it. Third, chemical treatment. If your water is hard, meaning it has high calcium and magnesium, you may need acid injection. Muriatic acid or citric acid can dissolve calcium carbonate. Use food-grade citric acid at 1 to 2 percent concentration for 30 to 60 minutes, then flush. For iron bacteria, which form slimy orange deposits, use chlorine. Inject chlorine at 1 to 2 parts per million continuously, or 10 to 20 parts per million for 30 minutes weekly, then flush. Be careful with chlorine and acid. Never mix them. Always add acid to water, never water to acid. Wear gloves and goggles. Fourth, biological control. Algae grow in open water and in drip lines exposed to sunlight. Cover your tank and filter with a lid or shade net. Use dark-coloured drip tape or bury laterals slightly. Chlorination also controls algae. If roots are entering emitters, which happens when crops are stressed for water, check your irrigation schedule. Roots grow toward water. If you are underwatering, roots will seek moisture at the emitters. Increase run time or frequency. Some farmers use root inhibitors, but these are expensive and not always available. Fifth, diagnosis. If a block is not performing well, walk the laterals. Look for dry spots, wet spots, and visible leaks. Check the pressure gauge at the block inlet. Low pressure means a clogged filter, a closed valve, a leak upstream, or a pump problem. High pressure means a closed valve downstream or too many emitters clogged. Open the flush valve and observe the flow. Weak flow means upstream clogging. Strong flow means the problem is in the laterals or emitters. Remove one emitter and inspect it. Cut open a section of drip tape and look for sediment, slime, or scale. A simple test is to collect water from several emitters in a measuring cup for one minute. Compare the volumes. If some emitters give 50 percent less than others, they are partially clogged. Sixth, rehabilitation. If a lateral is heavily clogged, you can try to rehabilitate it. Remove the end cap and flush at high pressure. If that fails, soak the lateral in a chlorine solution or acid solution for several hours. If that fails, replace the lateral. Drip tape is cheap. Do not waste labour on a lateral that is beyond saving. For drip lines with individual emitters, you can remove and clean emitters or replace them. Seventh, design to reduce clogging. Use larger diameter laterals and shorter runs to maintain higher flow velocity. Higher velocity scours the lines. Use pressure-compensating emitters if your water is dirty, because they have wider flow paths. Avoid using surface water without settling. Build a settling pond or tank before the pump. Let water sit for 24 hours so sand and silt settle. Pump from the top, not the bottom. Finally, train the farmer. A drip system is not maintenance-free. It requires daily filter cleaning, weekly flushing, and monthly inspection. Give the farmer a simple checklist and a logbook. Record filter cleaning, flushing, and any clogging events. This record helps you identify patterns and fix root causes. In summary, drip clogging is preventable. Filter properly, flush weekly, treat chemically when needed, control algae, diagnose systematically, and rehabilitate or replace failed laterals. A well-maintained drip system can last 5 to 10 years and deliver 30 to 50 percent water savings compared to furrow irrigation. That is money in the farmer pocket.

4. Solar-Powered Irrigation Systems: Design, Economics, and Maintenance

Welcome to Lesson 4. Solar-powered irrigation is transforming agriculture across Africa. With falling panel prices and rising diesel costs, solar pumps are now economically viable for smallholder and commercial farms. This lesson covers the design, economics, and maintenance of solar irrigation systems in African conditions. First, understand the components. A solar irrigation system has four main parts: the solar array, the controller or inverter, the pump, and the water distribution system. The solar array converts sunlight to electricity. The controller manages the power and protects the pump from dry running and voltage fluctuations. The pump moves water. The distribution system delivers water to crops. For smallholder systems, the most common configuration is a DC submersible pump powered directly by solar panels through a controller. This is called a direct-coupled system. It has no batteries, which reduces cost and maintenance. Water is pumped during sunny hours and stored in a tank for use later. For larger systems, an AC pump with a solar inverter is used. This allows grid backup or diesel backup. Second, sizing the system. You need to know three things: daily water requirement, total head, and solar radiation in your location. Daily water requirement depends on crop, stage, and climate. A rough figure for vegetables in East Africa is 4 to 6 millimetres per day, which is 40 to 60 cubic metres per hectare per day. For a half hectare, that is 20 to 30 cubic metres per day. Total head is the vertical lift plus friction loss plus pressure at the emitters. If you pump from a borehole 20 metres deep to a tank 5 metres above ground, and the tank is 100 metres from the borehole, your total head might be 20 plus 5 plus 3 metres friction, equal to 28 metres. Solar radiation is measured in peak sun hours. Most of Africa receives 4 to 6 peak sun hours per day. To find pump power, use this formula: power in watts equals flow in cubic metres per hour times total head in metres times 2.725 divided by pump efficiency. For 3 cubic metres per hour at 28 metres head with 40 percent efficiency, power equals 3 times 28 times 2.725 divided by 0.4, which is about 572 watts. Add 20 percent for losses, and you need a 700 watt pump. Solar array size is pump power divided by peak sun hours, then multiplied by a safety factor of 1.2. For 700 watts and 5 peak sun hours, array size is 700 divided by 5 times 1.2, equal to 168 watts. But in practice, you need more because of temperature derating and dust. Use 200 to 250 watts of panels for a 700 watt pump. Third, economics. A solar system costs more upfront than a diesel system. A 1 kilowatt solar pump system might cost 1500 to 3000 US dollars, while a comparable diesel pump costs 500 to 1000 US dollars. But diesel costs 200 to 400 US dollars per month in fuel and maintenance. Solar has near-zero fuel cost and low maintenance. Payback is typically 12 to 36 months. After payback, solar is almost free. For a farmer growing high-value crops like tomatoes, peppers, or onions, solar irrigation increases profit significantly because it allows reliable irrigation through the dry season. Fourth, installation. Mount panels facing the equator, which means south in the northern hemisphere and north in the southern hemisphere. Tilt angle should equal your latitude. In Kenya, latitude is near zero, so panels are nearly flat. In South Africa, latitude is 30 degrees, so tilt panels 30 degrees. Keep panels clean. Dust reduces output by 10 to 30 percent. Use a sturdy mounting structure that withstands wind. Secure panels against theft. In some areas, panels are stolen for resale. Use tamper-proof bolts and consider fencing. Install the controller in a shaded, dry place. Use proper cable sizing to avoid voltage drop. Undersized cables waste power and can overheat. Fifth, maintenance. Daily: check that the pump is running and water is flowing. Weekly: clean panels with water and a soft cloth. Monthly: check all electrical connections for corrosion and tightness. Check the pump for unusual noise. Check the tank and distribution system for leaks. Annually: test the panels output, inspect the controller, and service the pump. Keep a logbook. Record daily water output, panel cleaning, and any faults. In Africa, dust and birds are common problems. Bird droppings on panels can reduce output. Clean them off promptly. Sixth, common failures. Dry running is the most common cause of pump failure. The controller should have dry-run protection. If not, install a float switch or a level sensor in the water source. Voltage spikes from lightning can destroy controllers. Install surge protection. Theft of panels is a major risk. Use security bolts and consider community-based security. Finally, financing. Solar systems are eligible for many green financing and agricultural loan programs in Africa. Organizations like the African Development Bank, GIZ, and various microfinance institutions offer loans for solar irrigation. Payback periods of 12 to 24 months make a strong case for lending. In summary, solar irrigation is reliable, clean, and increasingly affordable. Size the system correctly, install it properly, maintain it regularly, and protect it from theft and lightning. A well-designed solar system can irrigate half a hectare of vegetables year-round and pay for itself in two seasons.

5. Irrigation Scheduling and Water Management for African Crops

Welcome to Lesson 5. Irrigation scheduling is the decision of when to irrigate and how much water to apply. It is the single most important factor in crop yield, water use efficiency, and energy cost. In Africa, where water is often scarce and fuel is expensive, good scheduling can mean the difference between profit and loss. This lesson gives you practical, field-ready methods for scheduling irrigation on African farms. First, understand crop water requirement. Crops lose water through transpiration and evaporation from the soil, collectively called evapotranspiration or ET. ET depends on crop type, growth stage, climate, and soil. A simple way to estimate ET is to use a pan evaporimeter. A Class A pan placed near the field measures evaporation. Crop water use is roughly 0.7 to 1.0 times pan evaporation, depending on crop and stage. For example, if the pan shows 6 millimetres of evaporation per day, a mature vegetable crop might use 5 to 6 millimetres per day. That is 50 to 60 cubic metres per hectare per day. For a half hectare, that is 25 to 30 cubic metres per day. Second, know your soil. Soil type determines how much water it can hold and how often you need to irrigate. Sandy soils hold little water and need frequent, small applications. Clay soils hold more water but drain slowly and can become waterlogged. Loam soils are ideal. A simple field test: dig a hole 30 centimetres deep, fill it with water, and see how long it takes to drain. If it drains in less than 30 minutes, you have sandy soil. If it takes more than 4 hours, you have clay. For sandy soil, irrigate every 1 to 2 days. For loam, every 2 to 3 days. For clay, every 3 to 5 days. Third, use the feel method. This is the simplest scheduling tool and requires no equipment. Take a handful of soil from the root zone, 15 to 20 centimetres deep. Squeeze it in your hand. If it forms a ball and leaves moisture on your palm, it is too wet. Do not irrigate. If it forms a ball but breaks apart when you poke it, it is at field capacity. Irrigate soon. If it crumbles and does not form a ball, it is dry. Irrigate now. Train farmers to use this method daily. It costs nothing and works surprisingly well. Fourth, use tensiometers. A tensiometer measures soil moisture tension. It is a tube with a ceramic tip and a gauge. Install them at 20 and 40 centimetres depth. When the gauge reads 30 to 50 centibars, it is time to irrigate for most vegetables. For fruit trees, irrigate at 50 to 70 centibars. Tensiometers cost 50 to 100 US dollars each and require maintenance, but they are accurate and reliable. They are best for commercial farms or extension demonstrations. Fifth, use crop growth stage. Young seedlings need little water. Vegetative growth needs moderate water. Flowering and fruiting need the most water. For maize, the critical period is tasselling and silking. For tomatoes, it is flowering and fruit set. For onions, it is bulb formation. Never stress crops during these critical periods. A common mistake is to irrigate heavily during early growth and then reduce during flowering. This is backwards. Reduce irrigation during early growth to encourage deep roots, then increase during flowering and fruiting. Sixth, use simple scheduling tables. For a drip-irrigated tomato crop in Kenya, a typical schedule might be: week 1 to 2, 2 litres per plant per day. Week 3 to 4, 3 litres per plant per day. Week 5 to 8, 4 to 5 litres per plant per day. Week 9 to 12, 5 to 6 litres per plant per day. Adjust based on weather. On hot, windy days, increase by 20 percent. On cool, cloudy days, decrease by 20 percent. Seventh, manage water quality. Salinity is a growing problem in many African irrigation schemes. If water has high salt content, you must leach the soil periodically. Apply extra water to push salts below the root zone. This requires good drainage. Without drainage, leaching will waterlog the field. Test your water at least once a year. Electrical conductivity above 2 decisiemens per metre requires careful management. Above 4 decisiemens per metre, only salt-tolerant crops like barley, cotton, or date palm will grow. Eighth, measure and record. Install a simple water meter on the pump or mainline. Record daily water use. Compare it to crop requirement. If you are using more water than the crop needs, you are wasting money and leaching nutrients. If you are using less, you are reducing yield. A water meter costs 50 to 150 US dollars and pays for itself in one season. Ninth, use mulch. Mulch reduces evaporation from the soil surface by 50 to 70 percent. In Africa, maize stover, rice straw, and dry grass are readily available. Apply a 5 to 10 centimetre layer around plants. Mulch also suppresses weeds and improves soil health. It is one of the cheapest and most effective water-saving tools. Tenth, consider deficit irrigation. For some crops, you can apply less water than full requirement without significant yield loss. This is called regulated deficit irrigation. It works well for fruit trees, grapes, and some vegetables. For example, you might reduce water by 20 percent during vegetative growth and then return to full irrigation during flowering. This saves water and can improve fruit quality. However, deficit irrigation requires careful monitoring. Do not try it without good soil moisture data. Finally, train and empower the farmer. Give them a simple rain gauge, a soil auger, and a notebook. Teach them to record rainfall, irrigation dates, and crop observations. Review the records monthly. Adjust the schedule based on what the field tells you. In summary, irrigation scheduling is about matching water application to crop demand. Use the feel method, tensiometers, growth stages, and simple tables. Measure water use. Mulch. Manage salinity. And always record and review. Good scheduling saves water, saves energy, increases yield, and increases profit. That is the bottom line.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Irrigation Water Quality, Filtration, and Chemigation Field Guide

In African smallholder and emerging commercial irrigation, the single most common cause of premature system failure is not pump breakdown or poor design it is dirty, chemically unsuitable, or biologically contaminated water. A drip system that clogs in month three destroys the business case for the entire investment. This practical reference gives irrigation technicians a field-ready framework for assessing water quality, sizing filtration correctly, and applying chemigation (fertilizer and chemical injection) safely and profitably. It is written for the technician who is accountable to a farm owner's cash flow, not just to a specification sheet.

Why Water Quality Is a Business Issue First

Emitter clogging reduces application uniformity, which reduces yield, which reduces revenue. In a 1-hectare drip-irrigated tomato enterprise in Kenya, a 20% drop in uniformity can translate to a 1015% yield loss often more than the annual cost of a proper filtration package. Sediment, algae, iron bacteria, and carbonate scale are the four dominant clogging agents across African irrigation schemes. Each requires a different, and often combined, treatment approach. Treating the wrong problem wastes money and time; treating all four blindly is expensive. Diagnosis must come first.

Step 1: Diagnose the Water Source Before You Buy Anything

Test the source at least twice once in the dry season and once after the first heavy rains, because quality shifts dramatically. The minimum lab panel for irrigation water includes: pH, electrical conductivity (EC), total suspended solids (TSS), iron, manganese, calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate, carbonate, and a biological indicator (algae or iron bacteria presence). For surface water in open canals or rivers, assume biological loading is high until proven otherwise.

Step 2: Size Filtration Correctly

Filtration is not one filter it is a train. The correct sequence for surface water is: coarse screen or settling basin sand/media filter screen or disc filter drip laterals. For borehole water with low TSS, a screen or disc filter alone may suffice. The most common sizing error is undersizing the sand filter relative to pump flow. A sand filter must be sized so that the linear velocity through the bed does not exceed the manufacturer's maximum (typically 4060 m/h for irrigation media). If you exceed that, media fluidizes, fines pass through, and emitters clog downstream.

SAFETY WARNING CHEMIGATION AND WATER TREATMENT CHEMICALS

Chlorine, acids (hydrochloric, sulfuric, or phosphoric), and injected fertilizers are hazardous. Never mix acid and chlorine this releases toxic chlorine gas. Always inject chemicals upstream of filters but downstream of the pump, and install a backflow prevention valve to protect the water source and drinking supplies. Wear chemical splash goggles, nitrile gloves, and an apron. Store acids in a locked, ventilated area away from fertilizers. If acid contacts skin or eyes, flush with clean water for at least 15 minutes and seek medical attention immediately. Follow the product Safety Data Sheet (SDS) and national regulations (e.g., Kenya's Occupational Safety and Health Act; South Africa's OHSA). Do not allow untrained workers to handle treatment chemicals.

Step 3: Chemigation Profit Tool, Not an Afterthought

Fertigation through drip is one of the highest-return interventions available to African irrigators. It places nutrients in the root zone, cuts leaching losses, and reduces labour. But it only works if the injection system is correctly installed and the flush sequence is followed. The standard field sequence is: (1) irrigate with clean water to wet the soil, (2) inject fertilizer solution for the calculated duration, (3) flush the entire lateral network with clean water for at least 1520 minutes or until the flush water runs clear at the farthest emitter. Skipping the flush is the number one cause of fertilizer precipitation and emitter blockage.

Injection rate calculation: if you want to apply 5 kg of N per hectare through a system applying 20 m/ha, and your stock solution is 100 g N per litre, you need 50 litres of stock solution injected over the irrigation event. Set the injection pump to deliver that volume at a steady rate across the event never a single slug. For a 1-hectare block irrigated over 4 hours, that is 12.5 L/hour of stock solution.

Step 4: Routine Monitoring The Technician's Weekly Routine

Once installed, the system must be monitored with the same discipline as a pump. The weekly routine should include: pressure gauge readings at the filter inlet and outlet (a rise in differential pressure signals clogging), visual inspection of the farthest emitters, a flush of all lateral ends, and a check of the injection pump calibration. In African conditions with high biological load, chlorination may be needed weekly during warm months at 12 mg/L free chlorine at the end of the lateral but never exceed crop tolerance, and always check chlorine compatibility with the fertilizers and pesticides in use.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test the water source at least twice a year dry season and post-rain for pH, EC, TSS, iron, manganese, bicarbonate, and biological load.
  2. Size the sand filter so bed velocity stays within manufacturer limits (typically 4060 m/h); undersizing passes fines to emitters.
  3. Install the filter train in the correct order: settling/coarse screen sand media screen/disc laterals.
  4. Fit pressure gauges before and after the filter; a rising differential means it is time to backwash.
  5. Install a backflow prevention valve between the injection point and the water source before any chemigation.
  6. Flush laterals weekly and after every fertigation event 1520 minutes minimum, or until water runs clear.
  7. Calibrate the injection pump monthly and record the stock solution volume used per block.
  8. Keep a logbook of EC, pH, pressure readings, flush dates, and chemical applications this is the farm's early-warning system.
