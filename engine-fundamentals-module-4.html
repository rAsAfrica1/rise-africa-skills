1. L1

Before you buy or mount any pump, you must know two numbers: how much water you need per minute, and how high you need to push it. For a small plot of 1 hectare of maize, you need about 30,000 litres per day during peak season. If you run your pump 8 hours, that's 3,750 litres per hour, or about 62 litres per minute. For a 2-hectare vegetable garden, double that to 125 L/min. The height is the total head: the vertical lift from water surface to the highest point of your irrigation pipe, plus friction losses in pipes and fittings. A rough rule: add 1 metre of head for every 10 metres of straight pipe, and 0.5 metres for each elbow or valve. For example, if you lift water 5 metres from a river and push it through 50 metres of pipe to a tank 3 metres high, total head = 5 + 3 + (50/10) = 13 metres. Write these two numbers down: flow rate (L/min) and total head (m). Without them, you are guessing. Use a bucket and stopwatch to measure flow from your source if unsure. For head, use a tape measure and a string level. In Africa, many farmers use a 20-litre bucket: if it fills in 10 seconds, that's 120 L/min. That's your starting point.

2. L2

Pumps have a performance curve that shows flow rate against head. A centrifugal pump, common for car engine conversions, will give high flow at low head, and low flow at high head. You need to match your required flow and head to the pump's curve. For example, a typical 2-inch centrifugal pump might give 200 L/min at 10 m head, but only 50 L/min at 30 m head. If your requirement is 100 L/min at 20 m, that pump works. But if you need 150 L/min at 25 m, it won't. Check the pump's datasheet or test it yourself. To test: set up a temporary tank, run the pump at a known engine speed (say 1500 RPM), and measure flow at different discharge heights. Record the numbers. Also consider the engine's power. A rule of thumb: 1 horsepower (HP) can pump about 150 L/min at 10 m head. A small car engine (e.g., 1.0 litre) might produce 30 HP at 2000 RPM, so it can drive a pump requiring up to 20 HP, leaving margin for losses. But if you run the engine too slow, the pump won't reach its curve. In Africa, many conversions use a pulley system to step up RPM. For a pump needing 1500 RPM and engine running at 800 RPM, you need a pulley ratio of 1500/800 = 1.875. Use a larger pulley on the engine and smaller on the pump. Always align pulleys with a straight edge to avoid belt wear.

3. L3

Suction lift is the vertical distance from the water surface to the pump impeller. Most centrifugal pumps can only suck water up to about 7 metres at sea level, and less at higher altitudes. In Africa, many farms are above 1000 metres elevation, so maximum suction lift drops to about 6 metres. If your water source is deeper, you need a different pump type, like a submersible or a self-priming pump with a foot valve. For car engine conversions, you usually mount the pump close to the water. Keep the suction pipe as short and straight as possible. Use a pipe diameter at least as large as the pump inlet. For a 2-inch pump, use 2-inch suction pipe. Never use a smaller pipe on the suction side. Install a strainer at the end to keep out debris, and a foot valve to keep water in the pipe for priming. Priming is filling the pump casing and suction pipe with water before starting. Without priming, a centrifugal pump won't suck. On a car engine conversion, you can prime by pouring water into the pump outlet or using a small primer pump. Check for air leaks in the suction line: any loose joint will break the vacuum. Use thread seal tape on all connections. In sandy areas, place the strainer in a bucket with holes to avoid clogging. Test suction lift by running the pump and measuring the vacuum at the inlet with a gauge. If vacuum is too high, reduce lift or increase pipe size.

4. L4

Discharge head is the vertical distance from the pump outlet to the highest point of water delivery, plus friction losses in the discharge pipe. For irrigation, you often pump to a storage tank or directly to sprinklers. If you pump to a tank 10 metres above the pump, that's 10 metres of static head. Add friction: for a 2-inch pipe, friction loss is about 1 metre per 30 metres of pipe at 100 L/min. So 60 metres of pipe adds 2 metres. Total discharge head = 10 + 2 = 12 metres. Then add any pressure needed at the sprinkler: a typical sprinkler needs 2 bar (about 20 metres of head). So total head = 12 + 20 = 32 metres. Now you can select a pump that gives your required flow at 32 metres. Remember, the pump's total head is suction lift + discharge head + friction + pressure. Keep discharge pipe size large enough to minimise friction. For 200 L/min, use 2.5-inch or 3-inch pipe. Use smooth bends instead of sharp elbows. In Africa, many farmers use lay-flat hose for temporary lines; it has higher friction, so add 50% more for friction loss. Also consider the engine speed: if you reduce engine RPM, pump flow drops. So run the engine at the speed that matches the pump's rated curve. Use a tachometer or a simple optical RPM gauge to check. If you don't have one, mark the pulley and use a stopwatch to count revolutions per minute.

5. L5

The pump must be matched to the engine's power and speed. Power required by the pump depends on flow, head, and efficiency. For example, 2 L/s (120 L/min) at 20 m head with 60% efficiency: Power = (2 Ã 20 Ã 9.81) / (1000 Ã 0.6) = 0.654 kW, or about 0.88 HP. So a 1 HP engine is enough, but you need margin for belt losses and altitude. Add 20% margin: 1.1 HP. A car engine of 30 HP is way oversized, but you can run it at low RPM to save fuel. However, running an engine at very low RPM may not produce enough torque. Better to match pulley sizes so the engine runs at its torque peak, often around 2000-2500 RPM for small petrol engines. For diesel engines, torque peak is lower, around 1500-1800 RPM. Use a pulley ratio to adjust. If pump needs 1500 RPM and engine torque peak is 2000 RPM, ratio = 2000/1500 = 1.33. So engine pulley diameter = 1.33 Ã pump pulley diameter. For example, if pump pulley is 100 mm, engine pulley should be 133 mm. Use V-belts or flat belts. Check belt tension: should deflect about 10 mm under thumb pressure. In Africa, many conversions use old car alternator pulleys. Make sure the pump shaft can handle the side load from the belt. If not, use a separate bearing pillow block. Also, ensure the engine has a governor to maintain speed under varying load. If not, you may need to adjust throttle manually.

6. L6

After selecting and matching, you must install the pump correctly. Mount both engine and pump on a rigid frame, such as a steel channel or heavy wooden beams. Align the pulleys using a straight edge or laser. Misalignment causes belt wear and vibration. Bolt everything down to prevent movement. Connect the suction pipe with a strainer and foot valve. Prime the pump. Start the engine at low idle, then gradually increase to the speed that gives the required pump RPM. Check the flow rate using a bucket and stopwatch. If flow is lower than expected, check for air leaks, clogged strainer, or wrong engine speed. Measure the suction vacuum and discharge pressure with gauges. If vacuum is too high, reduce suction lift or increase pipe size. If discharge pressure is too high, check for closed valves or kinks in the pipe. For irrigation, you may need to adjust the engine speed to match the water demand. For example, if you only need 50 L/min for a small nursery, run the engine slower. But never run the engine so slow that the pump stalls or overheats. Keep a log of engine RPM, flow, and pressure for different settings. This helps you find the most fuel-efficient point. In Africa, fuel is expensive, so efficiency matters. Also, protect the pump from debris: use a screen box around the suction strainer. After each use, drain the pump if freezing is possible (rare in most of Africa, but highlands can get cold). Regularly grease bearings and check belt tension. With proper matching and maintenance, your car engine conversion can run for years.
