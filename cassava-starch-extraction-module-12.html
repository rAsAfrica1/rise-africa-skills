1. Utilization of Cassava Peels and Pulp

After extracting starch from cassava, you are left with two main solid by-products: peels (from the outer skin) and pulp (the fibrous residue after grating and washing). For every tonne of fresh cassava roots, you get about 200300 kg of peels and 250350 kg of wet pulp. Instead of throwing these away, you can turn them into valuable products.

First, peels can be dried and milled into cassava peel flour, which is used as animal feed, especially for pigs and poultry. Sun-dry peels on a concrete slab for 23 days until brittle, then grind in a hammer mill. A 5 HP hammer mill can process 500 kg per hour. Sell the flour in 50 kg bags to local livestock farmers for $0.15$0.25 per kg. Alternatively, you can compost peels with other organic waste to make organic fertilizer. Build a heap 1.5 m high, turn it every two weeks, and in 810 weeks you have rich compost.

Second, wet pulp can be used fresh as animal feed, but it spoils quickly. To preserve it, press out excess water using a screw press or manually with a hydraulic jack, then dry it in the sun. Dried pulp can be mixed with molasses and urea to make silage for cattle. A simple silage pit: dig a trench 1 m deep, line with plastic, layer pulp with 5% molasses and 1% urea, compact well, and cover with plastic and soil. After 3 weeks, it's ready to feed.

Third, pulp can be used as a substrate for mushroom farming. Oyster mushrooms grow well on pasteurized cassava pulp mixed with rice bran. Pasteurize by soaking in hot water (80C) for 1 hour, drain, and pack into bags. In 34 weeks, you harvest mushrooms that sell for $2$4 per kg. This is a high-value use for a waste product.

Finally, if you have a biogas digester (see Lesson 3), both peels and pulp can be fed into it to produce gas for cooking or electricity. A 10 m digester can handle 50 kg of peels and pulp per day, producing enough gas for a family of six. By utilizing these by-products, you reduce waste, create extra income, and protect the environment.

2. Wastewater Treatment and Disposal

Cassava starch processing uses a lot of waterabout 510 litres per kg of starch. The wastewater contains high organic matter, cyanide, and suspended solids. If discharged untreated into rivers or on land, it kills fish, pollutes groundwater, and causes bad smells. You must treat it before disposal.

Start with simple screening and sedimentation. Build a screening channel with a mesh (12 mm) to catch pieces of peel and pulp. Then let the water flow into a sedimentation tank (at least 2 m deep) where heavy particles settle. This tank should have a retention time of 46 hours. Remove the sludge every week and add it to your compost heap.

Next, do biological treatment. The most affordable method for small-scale processors is a series of anaerobic ponds followed by aerobic ponds. Anaerobic ponds are deep (23 m) and covered to exclude oxygen; bacteria break down organic matter and produce biogas (which you can collect). Retention time: 1020 days. Then aerobic ponds are shallow (0.51 m) and exposed to air, where algae and bacteria further clean the water. Total retention time: 510 days. After this, the water can be used for irrigation or discharged.

If you have limited space, use a constructed wetland. Plant reeds, cattails, or water hyacinth in a shallow bed with gravel and sand. Wastewater flows slowly through the roots, which absorb nutrients and break down pollutants. A wetland of 10 m can treat 1 m of wastewater per day. This is low-cost and low-maintenance.

For cyanide removal, add a small amount of chlorine (bleach) or lime to the wastewater. About 100 g of lime per m of water raises pH and breaks down cyanide. Test with simple cyanide test strips (available from agricultural suppliers).

Finally, never discharge untreated wastewater into streams. Check local regulationsmany countries require a permit. Reuse treated water for washing roots or irrigation, reducing your water bill. Proper treatment protects your community and keeps your business legal.

3. Biogas and Biofertilizer from Waste

Cassava waste is a goldmine for biogas and biofertilizer. Biogas is a clean fuel made when bacteria digest organic matter without oxygen. You can build a simple biogas digester using locally available materials: a plastic tank, bricks, or even a pit lined with polythene. For a small starch business, a 1020 m digester is enough.

How to build a fixed-dome digester: Dig a pit 2 m deep and 3 m wide. Construct a dome-shaped brick structure with an inlet for waste and an outlet for slurry. The gas collects at the top and is piped to a stove or generator. Feed the digester daily with a mixture of cassava peels, pulp, and water (1:1 ratio). The bacteria will produce gas within 24 weeks. A 10 m digester can produce 23 m of biogas per dayenough to cook three meals for a family of six or run a 1 kW generator for 2 hours.

Biogas saves money on firewood or LPG. In many African countries, a 12 kg LPG cylinder costs $15$20. Biogas from your waste is free after the initial investment of $300$500 for a small digester. You can also use the gas for lighting or to power a water pump.

The slurry that comes out of the digester is a powerful biofertilizer. It is rich in nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (NPK) and kills weed seeds and pathogens. Apply it directly to crops or mix with compost. One tonne of cassava waste produces about 0.5 tonnes of biofertilizer. Farmers pay $0.10$0.20 per kg for organic fertilizer. You can sell it in 50 kg bags or use it on your own farm to grow more cassava, reducing your raw material costs.

To start, begin with a small digester (5 m) and scale up as you learn. Train one worker to manage feeding and maintenance. Biogas and biofertilizer turn waste disposal costs into income and energy savings.

4. Costing and Profitability Analysis

To run a successful cassava starch business, you must know your costs and profits. Start by calculating the cost of producing 1 tonne of starch. Assume you process 4 tonnes of fresh cassava roots to get 1 tonne of starch (25% yield). In many African countries, fresh cassava costs $50$100 per tonne. So raw material: $200$400.

Labour: You need 46 workers for a small plant. If each earns $5 per day and you produce 1 tonne in 2 days, labour cost is $40$60. Water and electricity: $20$30. Packaging: 20 bags of 50 kg at $0.50 each = $10. Transportation: $30$50. Equipment maintenance and depreciation: $20$30. Total production cost: roughly $320$580 per tonne.

Now, revenue. Starch sells for $500$900 per tonne depending on quality and market. If you sell at $700, your profit is $120$380 per tonne. But you also have by-products: peels and pulp for feed or biogas, and wastewater for fertilizer. Selling 200 kg of dried peel flour at $0.20/kg gives $40. Selling 500 kg of biofertilizer at $0.15/kg gives $75. These add to your profit.

To improve profitability, focus on three things: reduce raw material cost by growing your own cassava or buying in bulk during harvest season when prices are low. Increase yield by using better extraction methodsa good grater and press can raise yield from 20% to 30%. And find premium markets: sell to food companies, bakeries, or exporters who pay more for consistent quality.

Keep records daily: record cassava bought, starch produced, hours worked, and sales. Use a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone. After one month, calculate your actual cost per tonne and profit margin. Adjust prices or costs accordingly. Break-even point: if your fixed costs are $1000 per month and you make $200 profit per tonne, you need to sell 5 tonnes to break even. Aim for 10 tonnes to make a good living.

5. Marketing Cassava Starch

You have produced high-quality cassava starchnow you need to sell it. Start by identifying your customers. In Africa, starch is used in food (e.g., tapioca, baking), textiles, paper, adhesives, and pharmaceuticals. Local bakeries, hotels, and food processors are easy to reach. Also, consider export markets in Europe and Asia, but they require strict quality standards.

Packaging matters. For local sales, pack starch in 50 kg polypropylene bags with a clear label: your business name, weight, production date, and contact. For premium buyers, use 25 kg paper bags with a liner. Always keep starch drymoisture above 14% causes spoilage. Store on pallets in a dry room.

Price competitively. Research local prices: in Nigeria, starch sells for $600$800 per tonne; in Kenya, $700$900. Offer a discount for bulk orders (e.g., 5% off for 10 tonnes). But do not undercut too muchyou need profit to reinvest.

Promote your business. Use WhatsApp groups for farmers and traders. Create a simple Facebook page with photos of your starch and production process. Attend local agricultural shows and trade fairs. Give free samples to potential buyersa 1 kg sample can win a 10-tonne order. Word of mouth is powerful: ask satisfied customers to refer others.

Build relationships. Visit bakeries and factories in person. Ask about their needs: do they want organic starch? Gluten-free? Custom particle size? If you can meet specific requirements, you can charge more. For export, work with an export agent or join a cooperative to meet volume requirements. Consider certification like ISO or HACCP, but start with basic food safety practices.

Finally, diversify your product line. Sell starch in different grades: food-grade, industrial-grade, and cosmetic-grade. Also sell by-products like peel flour and biofertilizer. The more products you offer, the more revenue streams you have. Marketing is ongoingspend at least one day a week on sales and customer follow-up.

6. Scaling Up and Automation Options

Once your small cassava starch business is profitable, you may want to scale up. Scaling means producing more starch with less manual labour, increasing efficiency and income. Start by analyzing your bottlenecks: is it grating, pressing, drying, or packaging? Then invest in equipment step by step.

For grating, a manual grater processes 50 kg per hour. A motorized grater (5 HP) does 500 kg per hour. Cost: $800$1500. For pressing, a manual screw press takes 30 minutes per batch; a hydraulic press does it in 5 minutes. Cost: $2000$4000. For drying, sun drying is free but slow and weather-dependent. A flash dryer (capacity 100 kg per hour) costs $5000$10000 but dries in minutes. For packaging, a semi-automatic filling machine ($1000$2000) speeds up bagging.

Automation doesn't have to be all-or-nothing. You can automate one step at a time. For example, buy a motorized grater first, then a hydraulic press, then a flash dryer. This spreads capital costs and lets you learn as you grow. Many equipment suppliers in China, India, and South Africa offer small-scale machines suitable for African conditions. Look for diesel-powered options if electricity is unreliable.

Financing scale-up: Use your profits, or apply for a loan from a microfinance bank or agricultural development bank. Some NGOs and government programs offer grants for agro-processing. Prepare a business plan showing your current production, costs, and projected profits after scaling. A typical loan of $10,000 can double your capacity.

When scaling, also consider location and layout. A bigger site with good drainage, water access, and road connectivity is essential. Hire and train more workersassign specific tasks like grating, pressing, drying, and quality control. Implement simple quality management: test starch for moisture and purity regularly.

Finally, think about vertical integration. You could start growing your own cassava, producing your own biogas for energy, and selling by-products. This reduces costs and increases control. Scaling up is a journeytake it one step at a time, and always reinvest a portion of your profits.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: By-Products, Waste Management, and Business for Cassava Starch Extraction

In African cassava starch enterprises, profitability is rarely determined by starch alone. It is determined by how well the processor converts peels, pulp, wastewater, and residual biomass into value or safely disposes of them. A well-managed by-product stream can reduce waste handling costs, generate biogas for heat or electricity, produce biofertilizer for outgrower schemes, and create additional revenue from livestock feed. A poorly managed one can trigger environmental fines, community conflict, and shutdowns. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the numbers, diagrams, and checklists to make by-product management a business advantage.

1. Utilization of Cassava Peels and Pulp

Fresh cassava roots typically yield 2025% starch, leaving 1525% as peels and pulp on a wet basis. In many African plants, this material is dumped, burned, or left to rot, creating odour, fly breeding, and methane emissions. Yet peels and pulp have real economic uses:

  • Livestock feed: Dried cassava peel meal contains 24% crude protein and 6070% nitrogen-free extract. It can replace 2040% of maize in poultry and pig rations after drying and, where necessary, detoxification (sun-drying or ensiling reduces cyanogenic glucosides).
  • Pulp for ethanol or mushroom substrate: Starch-rich pulp can be hydrolysed and fermented, or used as a substrate for oyster mushroom production.
  • Compost: Mixed with animal manure and ash, peels compost in 46 weeks to a usable soil amendment.

For a 2-tonne/day starch plant, peel and pulp output is roughly 0.40.6 tonnes/day. Drying this to 1214% moisture yields 0.150.25 tonnes of feed, worth USD 80150 per tonne in many West and East African markets. That is USD 1238/day of additional revenue often enough to cover a significant share of labour costs.

2. Wastewater Treatment and Disposal

Cassava starch wastewater is the single biggest environmental risk. It carries high organic load: COD of 3,00012,000 mg/L and BOD of 1,5006,000 mg/L, plus cyanide and low pH. Untreated discharge kills aquatic life, contaminates groundwater, and violates national environmental regulations (e.g., Nigeria's NESREA, Kenya's NEMA, Ghana's EPA).

A practical, low-cost treatment train for small and medium plants:

  1. Screen and settle: Remove grit and suspended solids in a settling basin.
  2. Anaerobic digestion: A covered lagoon or biodigester reduces COD by 7090% and produces biogas.
  3. Aerobic polishing: A shallow pond or constructed wetland reduces remaining BOD.
  4. Land application: Treated effluent can irrigate and fertilise nearby fields, provided salinity and cyanide levels are monitored.
SAFETY WARNING Hydrogen Sulphide and Cyanide Exposure

Cassava wastewater and digesters can release hydrogen sulphide (HS), a colourless gas that causes loss of smell and unconsciousness at high concentrations. Never enter a pit, lagoon, or biodigester without forced ventilation, an HS monitor, and a standby observer with a harness. Fresh cassava wastewater and peels contain cyanogenic glucosides; avoid prolonged skin contact, do not drink untreated effluent, and keep children and livestock away from open wastewater channels. Always wear gloves, boots, and eye protection when handling peels and effluent.

3. Biogas and Biofertilizer from Waste

Anaerobic digestion converts cassava wastewater and pulp into two revenue streams: biogas (6070% methane) and digestate (a nutrient-rich biofertilizer). A 2-tonne/day starch plant can generate roughly 3060 m of biogas per day from wastewater and pulp, equivalent to 1530 kg of LPG or 2040 litres of diesel. This can power a boiler, run a generator, or supply cooking fuel. Digestate contains 1.52.5% nitrogen, 0.51.5% phosphorus, and 12% potassium, and can be sold or used on outgrower farms.

5. Marketing Cassava Starch

Starch marketing in Africa is relationship-driven and quality-sensitive. Key buyers include food processors (noodles, biscuits, sauces), textile and paper mills, pharmaceutical companies, and breweries. To win contracts you need consistent moisture (12%), whiteness, low fibre, and reliable volume. Packaging in 25 kg or 50 kg food-grade bags with clear labelling, batch numbers, and a certificate of analysis is essential. Price varies from USD 350700 per tonne depending on grade and country. Value addition pre-gelatinised starch, modified starch, or glucose syrup can double or triple margins but requires capital and technical capacity.

6. Scaling Up and Automation Options

Scaling from 2 t/day to 1020 t/day requires mechanisation of washing, peeling, rasping, and dewatering, plus continuous rather than batch processing. Automation options include:

  • Automated root washers and peelers (reduces labour by 4060%).
  • Hydrocyclone and centrifuge systems for starch separation and refining.
  • PLC-controlled drying and moisture monitoring.
  • SCADA or simple IoT sensors for wastewater pH, COD, and biogas flow.

Automation raises capital expenditure but lowers unit cost, improves consistency, and is often required to meet export or multinational buyer standards. Start with the bottleneck usually dewatering or drying and automate incrementally.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Quantify daily peel, pulp, and wastewater volumes before designing any treatment or revenue plan.
  • Dry and bag peels/pulp within 24 hours to prevent spoilage and cyanide retention.
  • Install a covered anaerobic digester or lagoon; never discharge untreated wastewater.
  • Monitor biogas for HS and use forced ventilation before any confined-space entry.
  • Test digestate and effluent for pH, salinity, and cyanide before land application.
  • Keep records of by-product sales, treatment costs, and compliance to prove profitability.
  • Secure at least one off-take agreement for feed or biofertilizer before scaling.
  • Review national environmental regulations (NESREA, NEMA, EPA) annually.
