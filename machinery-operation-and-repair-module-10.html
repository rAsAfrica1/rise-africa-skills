1. Preventive Maintenance Schedules for African Conditions

Welcome to Module 10, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on building and running a preventive maintenance schedule that actually works in African operating environments. Whether you run a maize mill in Kenya, a quarry in Ghana, a generator fleet in Nigeria, or a tractor hire business in Tanzania, the principle is the same: breakdowns cost far more than maintenance. A single failed bearing can stop production for days, and in many rural areas spare parts must travel hundreds of kilometres. Preventive maintenance is your cheapest insurance. Start by understanding that African conditions are not the same as the conditions in the manuals written in Europe or Asia. Dust, heat, humidity, uneven roads, long supply chains, and variable fuel quality all accelerate wear. Your schedule must reflect this reality. The first step is to create an asset register. List every machine you own, its make, model, serial number, year, and current hours or kilometres. Include small equipment too, because a failed water pump can stop a whole site. For each asset, write down the manufacturer service intervals, then adjust them for local conditions. As a rule of thumb, reduce service intervals by twenty to thirty percent when operating in dusty or high-temperature environments. If the manual says change engine oil every 250 hours, plan for 180 to 200 hours. If it says grease every 50 hours, do it every 35 to 40 hours. Next, build a schedule calendar. Use a simple wall chart, a notebook, or a free spreadsheet on a phone or laptop. Divide maintenance into daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, and annual tasks. Daily tasks include checking oil levels, coolant, fuel, tyre pressure, and looking for leaks, loose bolts, or unusual noises. Weekly tasks include greasing points, cleaning air filters, checking battery terminals, and inspecting belts and hoses. Monthly tasks include changing oil and filters, checking brake and clutch adjustments, and testing safety devices. Quarterly tasks include hydraulic system checks, valve clearances, and electrical inspections. Annual tasks include major overhauls, replacing worn components, and calibrating instruments. Assign responsibility. Every task must have a name and a deadline. In many African workshops, the biggest failure is not lack of knowledge but lack of accountability. Write the name of the operator or technician next to each task. Use a sign-off sheet or a simple app. Keep records. A maintenance log book for each machine is essential. Record the date, hours, task performed, parts used, and the person who did the work. These records help you spot recurring problems, prove to buyers that the machine was cared for, and plan budgets. They also help when you sell the machine, because a well-documented service history adds value. Now let us talk about condition monitoring. Not all maintenance must be scheduled by time. You can also monitor the condition of the machine. Check oil for metal particles by using a magnet or sending samples to a lab if available. Listen to bearings with a screwdriver or stethoscope. Feel for excessive heat on gearboxes and motors. Watch for changes in exhaust colour, fuel consumption, and vibration. When you notice a change, investigate immediately. This is called predictive maintenance, and it can save you from unexpected breakdowns. In Africa, where transport is expensive, condition monitoring is especially valuable because it lets you order parts before a failure, often at a lower price and with less downtime. Another key point is spare parts management. Keep a minimum stock of fast-moving items: filters, belts, hoses, bearings, seals, fuses, and lubricants. But do not overstock, because cash tied up in parts is cash not available for fuel or wages. Use the maintenance records to calculate how many of each part you use per month, then keep one to two months of stock. Build relationships with reliable suppliers. In many countries, counterfeit parts are a serious problem. Buy from authorised dealers or trusted importers. A cheap filter can destroy an engine. A fake bearing can fail in days. Train your operators. The person who runs the machine is your first line of maintenance. Teach them how to do daily checks, how to spot problems, and how to report them. Give them a simple checklist and a way to communicate with the workshop. In many African businesses, operators are not told to care for the machine, so they just run it until it stops. Change that culture. Reward operators who keep their machines clean and report issues early. Finally, budget for maintenance. Set aside a percentage of your monthly revenue, typically three to five percent, for maintenance and repairs. If you wait until a breakdown, you will pay more and lose income. A preventive maintenance schedule is not a cost; it is an investment that keeps your business running. In the next lesson, we will look at troubleshooting common diesel engine problems. Before you move on, take a moment to write a simple maintenance schedule for one machine you own or operate. Start with daily checks and work up to annual tasks. Then put it on the wall where everyone can see it. That single action can save you thousands of dollars and many days of lost work.

2. Troubleshooting Diesel Engines in the Field

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 10. Diesel engines power most of the machinery used in African agriculture, transport, mining, and construction. They are chosen for their fuel efficiency, torque, and durability. But they can fail, and when they do, you need to diagnose the problem quickly and correctly. In this lesson we will cover a practical troubleshooting method for diesel engines, focusing on the most common faults and how to fix them in the field with limited tools. The first rule of troubleshooting is to start with the simple things. Many engine problems are caused by fuel, air, or electrical issues, not by major mechanical failure. Before you open the engine, check the basics. Is there fuel in the tank? Is the fuel valve open? Is the battery charged? Are the battery terminals clean and tight? Is the air filter blocked? Is the fuel filter clogged? These simple checks solve a large percentage of no-start and low-power complaints. Let us begin with a no-start condition. If the engine cranks but does not start, first check the fuel system. Diesel engines need clean fuel at the right pressure and timing. Check the fuel filter and water separator. In many African countries, fuel quality varies, and water or dirt in the fuel is common. Drain the water separator daily. Replace the fuel filter at the recommended interval, and more often if you buy fuel from informal sellers. If the filter is clogged, the engine will not get enough fuel. Next, check the fuel lift pump and injection pump. If the lift pump is weak, it may not deliver fuel to the injection pump. You can test it by loosening the fuel line and cranking the engine; you should see strong pulses of fuel. If not, replace the lift pump. If the injection pump is faulty, it usually requires a specialist, but you can check for obvious issues like a broken drive belt or a disconnected wire. Bleed the fuel system. Air in the fuel lines is a very common cause of no-start after running out of fuel or changing filters. Locate the bleed screws on the fuel filter housing and injection pump. Open them and pump the hand primer until bubble-free fuel comes out. Then tighten the screws and try to start. If the engine still does not start, check the glow plugs or intake heater, especially in cold weather. In high-altitude areas of Ethiopia, Kenya, or South Africa, cold mornings can make starting difficult. Test glow plugs with a multimeter or by connecting them directly to a battery; they should glow red within a few seconds. If not, replace them. Now consider low power or black smoke. Black smoke usually means incomplete combustion, often due to too much fuel or not enough air. Check the air filter first. A clogged air filter is the most common cause. Clean or replace it. Check the turbocharger if fitted. Listen for a whining noise, check for oil leaks, and inspect the compressor wheel for damage. A failed turbocharger will cause low power and black smoke. Check the fuel injectors. Dirty or worn injectors can spray too much fuel or produce a poor spray pattern. In the field, you can remove injectors and have them tested at a workshop. If you see white smoke, that usually indicates unburned fuel, often from a faulty injector or low compression. Blue smoke indicates burning oil, often from worn piston rings or valve guides. These are more serious and may require an overhaul. Overheating is another common problem. Diesel engines run hot, and in African conditions, cooling systems are under stress. Check the coolant level, radiator cap, fan belt, and thermostat. A loose or broken fan belt will cause rapid overheating. A stuck thermostat will either keep the engine cold or cause overheating. Test the thermostat by boiling it in water; it should open at the specified temperature. Check the radiator for blockages. In dusty areas, the radiator fins can clog with dust and chaff. Clean them with compressed air or a soft brush. Check the water pump for leaks and play. If the engine overheats, stop immediately. Continuing to run an overheated engine can crack the cylinder head or seize the pistons. Let it cool, then diagnose. Strange noises are another category. A knocking sound from the bottom of the engine may indicate worn bearings or a loose flywheel. A ticking sound from the top may indicate a valve clearance problem or a worn camshaft. A squealing sound may be a loose belt or a failing bearing. Use a long screwdriver or a stethoscope to locate the noise. If you hear a heavy knock, stop the engine and do not run it until it is inspected. It may be cheaper to repair now than to replace the engine later. Electrical problems are also common. Diesel engines need electricity for the starter, fuel solenoid, and sensors. If the engine cranks slowly, check the battery, starter, and cables. Clean and tighten connections. Test the battery with a load tester. If the engine does not crank at all, check the starter solenoid, ignition switch, and safety switches. Many machines have safety switches on the clutch, seat, or gear lever. If one is faulty, the engine will not start. Bypass them only for testing, never for permanent use. Fuel contamination is a major issue in Africa. Water, dirt, and bacteria can grow in diesel tanks. Keep your storage tanks clean and dry. Use a water separator and drain it regularly. Add a biocide if you store fuel for long periods. Buy fuel from reputable suppliers when possible. If you suspect contaminated fuel, drain the tank, flush the lines, and replace the filters. Finally, keep a basic tool kit and spares on site. A good diesel mechanic in a remote area needs wrenches, screwdrivers, pliers, a hammer, a multimeter, a compression tester, and a fuel pressure gauge. Keep spare filters, belts, hoses, and a fuel primer. Learn to bleed the fuel system and change injectors. These skills will save you days of downtime. In the next lesson, we will cover hydraulic systems and how to maintain them. Before you move on, practice the troubleshooting steps on a machine you have access to. Start with the simple checks and work through the system. The more you practice, the faster you will diagnose problems.

3. Hydraulic System Maintenance and Repair

Welcome to Lesson 3. Hydraulic systems are used in almost every type of machinery in Africa, from tractors and excavators to truck cranes and workshop presses. They transmit force through fluid under pressure, and they are powerful, compact, and reliable when maintained properly. But hydraulic systems are also sensitive to contamination, heat, and poor maintenance. In this lesson we will cover the basics of hydraulic system maintenance and repair, with a focus on practical field techniques. The first principle of hydraulic maintenance is cleanliness. Dirt is the number one enemy of hydraulic systems. Particles smaller than a grain of sand can scratch cylinders, jam valves, and destroy pumps. In African workshops, dust is everywhere, so you must take extra care. Always clean the area around a hydraulic component before opening it. Use a pressure washer or a brush and rags. Cap open lines and ports immediately. Never use a rag that sheds lint inside a hydraulic system. Use clean, lint-free cloths or plastic caps. Store hydraulic oil in clean, sealed containers. Filter new oil before adding it if possible. The second principle is using the correct oil. Hydraulic oil is not just any oil. It must have the right viscosity, anti-wear additives, and compatibility with your system. Check the manufacturer specification. In hot African climates, you may need a higher viscosity oil, such as ISO VG 46 or 68, depending on the machine. In cold highland areas, a lower viscosity may be needed. Do not mix different brands or types of oil unless they are compatible. Mixing can cause foaming, sludge, and seal damage. The third principle is keeping the system cool. Hydraulic oil overheats when the system is overloaded, when the cooler is blocked, or when the relief valve is set too low. Overheating thins the oil, reduces lubrication, and damages seals. Check the hydraulic oil temperature with a gauge or infrared thermometer. If it is too hot, find the cause. Clean the oil cooler. Check the cooling fan. Ensure the relief valve is set correctly. Do not run the system at high pressure for long periods without a break. Now let us look at common hydraulic problems. The most common is low power or slow operation. This can be caused by low oil level, a clogged filter, a worn pump, a leaking cylinder, or a faulty relief valve. Start by checking the oil level. If it is low, top up with the correct oil and find the leak. Check the filter indicator or replace the filter. If the pump is worn, it will make a whining noise and the oil will be hot. Test the pump pressure with a gauge. If the pressure is low, the pump may need repair or replacement. A leaking cylinder is another common issue. You may see oil on the cylinder rod or around the seals. A cylinder can leak internally, which means oil bypasses the piston, causing slow or weak operation. To test, extend the cylinder and disconnect the return line. If oil flows out of the return port, the piston seal is leaking. Repair kits are available for most cylinders. Replacing seals is a job that can be done in a workshop with the right tools. You will need a seal pick, a clean workbench, and a torque wrench. Always replace all seals in the cylinder, not just the leaking one. Check the cylinder rod for scratches or dents. If the rod is damaged, it will destroy new seals. You may need to replace the rod or have it re-chromed. Valves are another area of concern. Hydraulic valves control direction, pressure, and flow. They can stick, leak, or fail electrically. If a valve is stuck, it may be due to contamination or a broken spring. Clean the valve with compressed air and hydraulic oil. Do not use metal tools that can scratch the spool. If the valve is electrically controlled, check the solenoid, wiring, and connectors. A bad ground can cause intermittent operation. Hoses and fittings are a common source of leaks. Inspect hoses for cracks, bulges, and abrasion. Replace any hose that shows damage. When replacing a hose, use the correct pressure rating and size. Do not use a hose clamp on a high-pressure line. Use proper crimped fittings or reusable fittings rated for the pressure. Tighten fittings to the correct torque. Over-tightening can crack the fitting or damage the seal. Under-tightening will leak. Use a torque wrench and follow the manufacturer specification. Filtration is critical. Most hydraulic systems have a suction filter, a return filter, and sometimes a pressure filter. Check and replace filters at the recommended intervals. In dusty conditions, replace them more often. If you see metal particles in the filter, it is a sign of internal wear. Cut the filter open and inspect the pleats. If you find brass or bronze particles, the pump or motor may be failing. If you find steel particles, a cylinder or valve may be worn. Keep a log of filter changes and oil analysis results. Oil analysis is a powerful tool. If you can send oil samples to a lab, do it every 500 to 1000 hours. The lab will tell you the condition of the oil and the wear metals present. This helps you plan repairs before a failure. In remote areas, you can use a simple patch test or a magnet to check for particles. Now let us talk about safety. Hydraulic systems store energy. Before working on a hydraulic system, relieve the pressure. Lower the implement to the ground, shut off the engine, and operate the controls to release trapped pressure. Never use your hands to check for leaks. Use a piece of cardboard or paper. High-pressure oil can inject into your skin and cause serious injury. If that happens, seek medical attention immediately. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. Finally, train your operators to check hydraulic systems daily. They should look for leaks, listen for unusual noises, and check the oil level. A small leak can become a major failure if ignored. Keep a spill kit on site to contain oil leaks and protect the environment. In the next lesson, we will cover electrical systems and diagnostics. Before you move on, inspect one hydraulic machine and identify all the filters, hoses, and cylinders. Write down their condition and plan any repairs.

4. Electrical Systems and Diagnostics for Machinery

Welcome to Lesson 4. Electrical systems are the nervous system of modern machinery. They control starting, charging, lighting, safety, and increasingly, engine management and hydraulics. In Africa, electrical problems are very common because of dust, vibration, heat, moisture, and poor-quality components. In this lesson we will cover the basics of electrical diagnostics for machinery, focusing on practical skills you can use in the field. The first tool you need is a digital multimeter. It does not have to be expensive, but it must be reliable. Learn to measure voltage, resistance, and continuity. A test light is also useful for checking power and ground. A clamp meter is helpful for measuring current without disconnecting wires. Keep spare fuses, relays, and wire in your toolbox. The first rule of electrical troubleshooting is to check the battery. A weak battery causes many problems. Measure the voltage with the engine off. A fully charged 12-volt battery should read about 12.6 volts. If it reads 12.0 or less, it is partially discharged. If it reads below 11.8, it may be faulty. Check the battery terminals for corrosion. Clean them with a wire brush and apply petroleum jelly. Check the battery hold-down; a loose battery can crack or short. Test the battery under load. A load tester will tell you if the battery can deliver enough current. If the battery is good, check the charging system. Start the engine and measure the voltage at the battery. It should read between 13.8 and 14.4 volts for a 12-volt system. If it is lower, the alternator or generator is not charging. Check the belt tension and condition. Check the wiring from the alternator to the battery. If the voltage is higher than 14.8, the regulator may be faulty, which can boil the battery and damage electronics. Next, check the starting system. If the engine does not crank, check the battery, starter, and ignition switch. Use a multimeter to check for voltage at the starter solenoid when the key is turned. If there is no voltage, check the ignition switch, neutral safety switch, and wiring. If there is voltage but the starter does not turn, the starter may be faulty. You can test the starter by connecting it directly to a battery with jumper cables. If it spins, the problem is in the wiring or switch. If it does not, the starter needs repair or replacement. Now let us talk about fuses and relays. A blown fuse is easy to spot, but sometimes a fuse looks good and is still faulty. Test it with a multimeter for continuity. Replace fuses with the same rating. Never use a higher-rated fuse or a piece of wire, as this can cause a fire. Relays are switches that use a small current to control a large current. If a relay fails, the circuit it controls will not work. You can test a relay by listening for a click when power is applied, or by testing continuity across the contacts. Keep spare relays for critical circuits. Wiring problems are common in African machinery. Vibration, heat, and rodents can damage wires. Look for chafed insulation, broken wires, and loose connectors. Use a wiring diagram to trace circuits. If you do not have a diagram, trace the wire physically. Check grounds carefully. A bad ground can cause many strange problems, such as dim lights, slow cranking, and erratic sensor readings. Clean and tighten all ground connections. Add a star washer to ensure good contact. Sensors and actuators are used on modern engines. Common sensors include coolant temperature, oil pressure, intake air temperature, and crankshaft position. If a sensor fails, the engine may not start or may run poorly. A diagnostic scanner can read fault codes, but many African workshops do not have one. You can still test sensors with a multimeter. For example, a coolant temperature sensor changes resistance with temperature. Check the manufacturer specification and compare. Actuators include fuel injectors, solenoids, and stepper motors. Test them for resistance and listen for them to operate. Lighting systems are also important for safety and productivity. Check headlights, work lights, and warning lights. Replace burnt bulbs. Check the alternator output if lights are dim. Clean the lenses and reflectors. Use LED lights where possible, as they use less power and last longer. In dusty conditions, keep lights clean for visibility. Batteries need special care in Africa. Heat shortens battery life. Keep the battery in a cool, shaded place if possible. Check the electrolyte level in serviceable batteries and top up with distilled water. Do not overfill. Keep the terminals clean and tight. If you store a battery, charge it every month. When replacing a battery, choose the correct size and type. A larger battery may not fit or may overload the charging system. Now let us discuss safety. Electricity can kill. Always disconnect the battery before working on electrical systems. Use insulated tools. Wear safety glasses. Do not work on electrical systems in wet conditions. If you are unsure, ask a qualified electrician. Do not bypass safety devices. They are there to protect you and the machine. Finally, keep records. Write down electrical problems and solutions. Note the date, machine, symptom, and repair. This helps you diagnose similar problems faster in the future. Train your operators to report electrical issues immediately. A flickering light or a slow crank is an early warning. In the next lesson, we will cover workshop management and spare parts planning. Before you move on, practice using a multimeter on a battery and a simple circuit. Measure voltage, resistance, and continuity. These skills are the foundation of electrical diagnostics.

5. Workshop Management and Spare Parts Planning

Welcome to Lesson 5, the final lesson of Module 10. In this lesson we focus on the business side of machinery operation and repair: managing a workshop and planning spare parts. In Africa, many workshops fail not because of poor technical skills, but because of poor management. Tools go missing, parts are not available, jobs are not scheduled, and customers are not billed properly. Good management turns a struggling workshop into a profitable business. Let us start with the workshop layout. A good workshop is organised, clean, and safe. Divide the space into zones: a reception area for customers, a diagnosis area, a repair bay, a parts store, and a wash area. Keep walkways clear. Store tools on racks or in cabinets. Label everything. A place for everything and everything in its place saves time and reduces frustration. Invest in good lighting. In many African workshops, poor lighting leads to mistakes and accidents. Use LED lights, which are cheap to run and bright. Ensure good ventilation, especially when running engines indoors. Install a fire extinguisher and a first aid kit. Keep a spill kit for oil and fuel. Now let us talk about tools. A workshop needs a basic set of hand tools, power tools, and measuring instruments. Hand tools include spanners, sockets, screwdrivers, pliers, hammers, and chisels. Power tools include a grinder, drill, impact wrench, and compressor. Measuring instruments include a multimeter, vernier caliper, micrometer, and torque wrench. Buy the best quality you can afford. Cheap tools break and cause injuries. Keep a tool register. Every tool has a number and a place. Check tools in and out. If a tool is lost or broken, record it and replace it. This may seem strict, but it saves money. Train your technicians to use tools correctly and to clean them after use. Next, spare parts planning. This is one of the biggest challenges in African workshops. Supply chains are long, and parts may take weeks to arrive. You need to balance having enough stock with not tying up too much cash. Start by identifying fast-moving parts for the machines you service. These include filters, belts, hoses, bearings, seals, fuses, relays, and lubricants. Keep a minimum stock of these items. Use a simple bin card or spreadsheet to track stock levels. When stock reaches the reorder point, order more. Calculate your reorder point based on lead time and usage. For example, if you use 10 oil filters per month and it takes 4 weeks to get more, keep at least 15 in stock. Build relationships with suppliers. Visit them, negotiate prices, and ask for credit terms if possible. In some countries, you can join a buying group or cooperative to get better prices. Avoid counterfeit parts. Buy from authorised dealers or trusted importers. A cheap part can destroy an expensive machine and ruin your reputation. Now let us discuss job scheduling. A workshop runs on time. When a customer brings a machine, write a job card. Record the customer name, machine details, complaint, and date. Inspect the machine and provide an estimate. Get the customer approval before starting work. Schedule the job based on priority and parts availability. Keep the customer informed of progress. When the job is done, test the machine and clean it. Write a detailed invoice with parts and labour. Follow up with the customer to ensure satisfaction. Good communication builds trust and repeat business. Pricing is another key issue. You must charge enough to cover your costs and make a profit. Calculate your costs: rent, wages, utilities, tools, parts, and transport. Add a profit margin. Do not undercut yourself just to win a job. Compete on quality and reliability, not just price. Offer value-added services such as preventive maintenance contracts, machine inspections, and operator training. These bring steady income and build long-term relationships. Now let us talk about safety and environment. A workshop must be a safe place. Train your staff on safety procedures. Use personal protective equipment: gloves, safety glasses, boots, and ear protection. Handle chemicals and oils properly. Dispose of waste oil and filters responsibly. Do not pour oil on the ground or into rivers. In many African countries, environmental laws are becoming stricter. Being responsible protects your business and your community. Finally, let us discuss record keeping and continuous improvement. Keep records of every job, every part used, and every payment. Use a simple accounting system, either paper or software. Review your records monthly. Look for patterns: which machines break most often, which parts you use most, which jobs are most profitable. Use this information to improve your business. Train your staff regularly. Send them to courses or bring in a trainer. Share knowledge within the team. Encourage them to suggest improvements. A workshop that learns is a workshop that grows. As we close Module 10, remember that machinery operation and repair is not just about turning wrenches. It is about running a business that serves customers, employs people, and contributes to the economy. Whether you are in a small town or a big city, the principles are the same: maintain your machines, diagnose problems correctly, manage your workshop, and plan your parts. Do these things well, and you will succeed. Thank you for completing this module. In the next module, we will look at advanced diagnostics and machine-specific systems. Before you move on, take a walk through your workshop or the workshop you work in. Identify one improvement you can make this week, whether it is organising tools, creating a job card system, or ordering spare parts. Write it down and do it. Small steps lead to big results.

<h3>Why Records Beat Memory</h3>

<p>Most workshop failures in African agribusinesses are not mechanical they are informational. The operator who "knows" the tractor was serviced last month cannot tell you the engine hours, the oil brand, or whether the fuel filter was changed. When a diesel injector fails 40 hours after a rebuild, memory cannot prove whether the cause was contaminated fuel or a worn pump. A written log can.</p>

<p>Records also unlock finance. Banks and leasing companies in Kenya, Ghana and Nigeria increasingly require three to six months of maintenance records before approving machinery loans or hire-purchase agreements. A well-kept logbook is a credit document as much as a technical one. For a small machinery-hire business, the logbook is the difference between a 15% interest rate and a 28% one.</p>

<p>Fuel quality is the single largest uncontrolled variable in African machinery operations. Diesel bought from informal roadside vendors often carries water, sulphur and particulate contamination well above manufacturer specifications. <h3>Building the Maintenance Log That Actually Gets Used</h3>

<p>A log that is too complex will not be filled in. The most effective logs in African agribusinesses use a single A5 sheet per machine per month, with four columns: date, engine hours, action taken, and operator initials. Anything more elaborate tends to collapse within two weeks.</p>

<p>Three practical rules make logs survive:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Tie the log to the fuel purchase.</strong> Every time fuel is bought, the operator records litres and engine hours. This creates a fuel-consumption baseline that reveals injector or filter problems before failure.</li>
<li><strong>Photograph the dashboard.</strong> A smartphone photo of the hour meter at each service is faster than writing and creates an audit trail for buyers or lenders.</li>
<li><strong>Review weekly, not monthly.</strong> A 10-minute Friday review with the workshop manager catches small issues before they become weekend breakdowns.</li>
</ol>

<p>For a 5-tractor hire fleet, this system typically reduces unplanned downtime by 3045% within one season and extends engine life by 1,5002,500 hours. At an average revenue of USD 25 per tractor-hour, that is USD 37,50062,500 of protected revenue per tractor over its life far more than the cost of the logbook and the filters.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li>Record engine hours at every refuelling this is your master data point.</li>
<li>Check water separator and drain daily in the rainy season; weekly in dry season.</li>
<li>Replace fuel filters every 250 hours or 40% sooner if using informal fuel.</li>
<li>Verify every bearing and seal by ISO/DIN number before substitution.</li>
<li>Never substitute injectors, injection pumps or hydraulic valves with unbranded parts.</li>
<li>Keep a 10-metre clear zone around fuel storage; no smoking, no open flame.</li>
<li>Photograph the hour meter at each service and store with the logbook.</li>
<li>Review the log every Friday with the workshop manager and flag anomalies.</li>
</ul>
