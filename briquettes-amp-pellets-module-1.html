1. What Are Briquettes and Pellets? A Simple Explanation

Briquettes and pellets are solid fuels made by compressing loose biomass materials like sawdust, agricultural residues, or waste paper. Think of them as dense, uniform blocks or small cylinders that burn hot and long. Briquettes are typically largeroften the size of a brick or a large eggwhile pellets are small, uniform cylinders about the size of a pencil eraser. Both are made using a machine called a press, which applies high pressure to the raw material, sometimes with a binder like clay or cassava starch to hold it together.

In Africa, briquettes are often produced manually or with small motorized presses. A common manual press is the 'screw extruder,' which you turn by hand to push the mixture through a die. Motorized versions use a 5 to 10 horsepower electric motor or a diesel engine. Pellets are made with a pellet mill, which forces the material through a die with small holes. The friction heats the material, causing natural binders like lignin to glue the pellets together. A small pellet mill can produce 100 to 300 kilograms per hour.

The key difference between briquettes and pellets is size and production method. Briquettes are more forgivingyou can make them with simple tools and a lot of muscle. Pellets require more precise equipment and consistent raw material. But both offer the same benefits: they burn cleaner than raw wood or charcoal, produce less smoke, and can be made from waste that would otherwise rot or be burned in the open.

For a beginner, briquettes are the easier entry point. You can start with a manual press, a mixing area, and a drying rack. Pellets are better for larger operations that can afford a pellet mill and a hammer mill to grind the material finely. In either case, the final product is a renewable fuel that helps reduce deforestation and saves money. In Kenya, a 50 kg sack of briquettes sells for about 1,500 to 2,000 shillings, while charcoal costs 2,500 to 3,000 shillings for the same weight. That price difference is why briquettes are gaining popularity in homes and small businesses across Africa.

2. Why Africa Needs Alternative Fuels: Charcoal, Firewood, and Deforestation

Across Africa, over 80% of households rely on wood or charcoal for cooking. In countries like Malawi, Tanzania, and the Democratic Republic of Congo, that figure is above 90%. This dependence comes at a huge cost. To make one tonne of charcoal, you need about six tonnes of wood. That means millions of trees are cut every year just to feed cooking fires. In Tanzania alone, charcoal production destroys over 100,000 hectares of forest annuallyan area larger than the city of Dar es Salaam.

The result is severe deforestation. As forests disappear, soil erodes, rainfall patterns change, and rivers dry up. Women and children spend hours collecting firewood, often walking 5 to 10 kilometers. Indoor air pollution from cooking with wood and charcoal causes respiratory diseases that kill over 600,000 Africans each year, according to the World Health Organization. Charcoal also releases methane and carbon dioxide, contributing to climate change.

Alternative fuels like briquettes and pellets directly address these problems. They are made from wastesawdust from sawmills, rice husks from rice mills, maize cobs, sugarcane bagasse, and even waste paper. Instead of cutting trees, you collect residues that would otherwise be dumped or burned in the open. Briquettes burn cleaner, producing less smoke and soot. They also burn longer: one kilogram of briquettes can cook a meal that would require 1.5 kilograms of charcoal.

Switching to briquettes saves money. In Uganda, a family that spends 80,000 shillings per month on charcoal can spend 50,000 shillings on briquettes for the same cooking needs. For small businesses like restaurants, bakeries, and poultry farms, the savings are even bigger. A bakery in Nigeria that uses 10 bags of charcoal per week can cut costs by 40% by switching to briquettes. The raw materials are often free or very cheapsawmills will pay you to take away their sawdust.

But adoption faces challenges. Many people are unfamiliar with briquettes and worry about smell or performance. Education and demonstrations are key. Also, briquettes require a dry storage area and a slightly different cooking techniquethey need good airflow to start. Once users learn, they rarely go back. In Ghana, a women's cooperative that switched to briquettes reported saving 30% on fuel costs and spending less time collecting wood. The environmental impact is just as important: every tonne of briquettes used saves about six tonnes of wood, preserving forests for future generations.

3. Common Raw Materials in Africa: Sawdust, Agricultural Residues, and Waste

The best raw materials for briquettes and pellets are dry, carbon-rich, and locally available. In Africa, you have plenty of options. Sawdust from sawmills is the most common. A medium-sized sawmill produces 5 to 10 cubic meters of sawdust per day. It is often freesawmills pay to dispose of it. You need to sieve it to remove stones and large chips. Sawdust from hardwood like mahogany or teak burns hotter than softwood like pine.

Agricultural residues are abundant after harvest. Rice husks are available in rice-growing regions like Nigeria, Tanzania, and Senegal. They have high silica content, which makes them abrasive, so you should mix them with other materials. Maize cobs and stalks are found everywhere. Groundnut shells, coconut shells, and coffee husks are also excellent. In Ethiopia, coffee husks are used to make briquettes that burn with a pleasant aroma. Sugarcane bagassethe fibrous waste after juice extractionis available at sugar factories. It is wet, so you must dry it first.

Waste paper and cardboard are another source. Offices, schools, and shops throw away tons of paper. You can shred it and soak it in water to make a pulp, then mix with sawdust. This acts as a binder. In South Africa, some entrepreneurs use waste paper from printing presses. You can also use agricultural waste like banana peels and cassava peels, but they must be dried and ground.

For briquettes, you want a mix of materials. A good recipe is 60% sawdust, 30% agricultural residues, and 10% binder like clay or cassava starch. The binder helps hold the briquette together. Cassava starch is made by mixing cassava flour with water and heating it until it becomes a gel. Clay is cheaper but adds ash. For pellets, you need a consistent material like sawdust or rice husk, ground to a fine powder (less than 6 mm).

Collecting raw materials requires planning. You can set up collection points at sawmills, markets, and farms. Pay a small fee or offer to clear the waste. In Kenya, briquette makers often partner with sawmills to get sawdust for free. In Nigeria, they buy rice husks from rice mills for 500 naira per bag. Drying is critical: materials should have less than 15% moisture. You can dry them on a concrete floor in the sun for 2 to 3 days, or use a solar dryer. Always store materials under a roof to keep them dry.

4. How Briquettes and Pellets Are Used in Homes and Small Businesses

In homes, briquettes and pellets are used in improved cookstoves. These stoves are designed with a grate and airflow control to burn solid fuels efficiently. A common stove is the 'rocket stove,' which uses a insulated combustion chamber and a chimney. You can buy one for 2,000 to 5,000 shillings in Kenya, or make one from a metal bucket and clay. For pellets, you need a specialized pellet stove, which has a hopper and an auger to feed pellets automatically. These stoves cost more50,000 to 150,000 shillingsbut they are clean and convenient.

To start a briquette fire, place a small amount of kindling (dry twigs or paper) at the bottom of the stove. Add a few briquettes on top, leaving space for air. Light the kindling and let it catch. Once the briquettes are glowing red, you can add more. Control the air vents to adjust the heat. Briquettes burn for 1 to 2 hours per kilogram, depending on the density. Pellets burn hotter and faster, so you need to feed them regularly if using a manual stove.

Small businesses use briquettes and pellets in many ways. Restaurants and food vendors use them for grilling, frying, and boiling. A typical street food vendor in Uganda uses 5 kg of briquettes per day, costing 3,000 shillings, compared to 6,000 shillings for charcoal. Bakeries use briquettes to fire ovens. A bakery in Tanzania reported that switching to briquettes cut their fuel costs by 35% and reduced smoke in the bakery. Poultry farmers use briquettes for broodingkeeping chicks warm. A farmer in Nigeria uses 20 kg of briquettes per day for 500 chicks, saving 40% compared to charcoal.

Other uses include heating water for laundry, drying crops like tea and tobacco, and even firing clay bricks. In Ghana, a brick maker uses rice husk briquettes to fire his kiln, saving 50% on fuel. Schools and institutions use briquettes for cooking meals. A boarding school in Kenya switched to briquettes and saved 200,000 shillings per term.

The key to successful use is proper training. Users must learn to not overload the stove, to keep the briquettes dry, and to clean the stove regularly. Ash removal is importantbriquettes produce ash, which can be used in gardens as fertilizer. With practice, briquettes and pellets become as easy to use as charcoal, but cheaper and cleaner.

5. Success Stories: African Entrepreneurs Making Briquettes

Across Africa, entrepreneurs are building successful briquette businesses. In Kenya, a company called 'Chardust' started in 2001 by making briquettes from charcoal dust. They collected waste charcoal dust from markets and mixed it with clay. Today, they produce 10 tonnes per day and employ over 50 people. They sell to hotels, schools, and households. Their briquettes are sold in 50 kg bags for 1,800 shillings. The founder, Elkanah Odembo, says the key was consistent quality and good distribution.

In Uganda, 'Briquette Africa' is a women-led enterprise. They use sawdust and agricultural waste to make briquettes. They started with a manual press and now have three motorized presses. They produce 2 tonnes per day and supply 500 households. They also train other women to start their own briquette businesses. One of their trainees, Sarah, now runs a briquette shop in Kampala and earns 1.5 million shillings per month.

In Nigeria, 'Green Energy' makes briquettes from rice husks and cassava peels. They have a partnership with rice mills to collect husks for free. They sell briquettes to restaurants and bakeries in Lagos. Their biggest challenge was drying the briquettes during the rainy season. They built a solar dryer with a capacity of 1 tonne per day. Now they produce 5 tonnes per day and have 20 employees.

In Tanzania, 'Mkaa Bora' makes pellets from sawdust. They use a pellet mill imported from China for $5,000. They produce 500 kg per hour and sell to institutions. They also sell pellet stoves. The founder, Joseph, says the biggest lesson was to start small and reinvest profits. He started with a manual press and saved for two years to buy the pellet mill.

In Ghana, 'EcoFuel' makes briquettes from coconut shells and sawdust. They have a unique recipe that includes a small amount of beeswax as a binder. Their briquettes are popular for grilling because they impart a smoky flavor. They sell 1 kg for 2 cedis, and they have a waiting list of customers. They employ 15 people and are expanding to other regions.

These stories show that briquette businesses can succeed with determination, local materials, and a focus on quality. Common success factors include: starting small, keeping costs low, finding reliable raw material sources, training staff, and marketing to institutions. Many entrepreneurs also diversifyselling stoves, providing training, or offering delivery services. The market for briquettes in Africa is growing as more people seek cheaper, cleaner cooking fuels.

6. Overview of the Production Process from Start to Finish

Making briquettes or pellets involves six main steps: collection, drying, grinding, mixing, pressing, and drying again. Each step is critical for a quality product. Let's walk through the process for briquettes, which is simpler for beginners.

Step 1: Collection. Gather raw materials like sawdust, rice husks, or maize cobs. You can collect from sawmills, farms, or markets. Aim for 100 kg of dry material to start. Step 2: Drying. Spread the material on a concrete floor or tarp in the sun. Turn it every few hours. It should be dry to the touchmoisture content below 15%. This takes 2 to 3 days. If it rains, move it under a shed.

Step 3: Grinding. If you use maize cobs or stalks, you need to grind them into small pieces (less than 5 mm). You can use a hammer mill, which costs about $500 for a small one, or a manual grinder. For sawdust, you may skip this step if it's already fine. Step 4: Mixing. Combine your materials in a large basin or on a tarp. A good mix is 70% sawdust, 20% rice husks, and 10% binder. For binder, use cassava starch gel: mix 1 kg of cassava flour with 10 liters of water, heat until it thickens. Add to the dry mix and stir until it holds together when squeezed.

Step 5: Pressing. Use a manual briquette press (cost: $50 to $200) or a motorized press ($500 to $2,000). For manual, fill the mold with the mixture, then pull the lever or turn the screw to compress. For motorized, feed the mixture into the hopper. The press will extrude briquettes in a continuous log, which you cut to length. Aim for a density of 0.6 to 0.8 g/cm. Step 6: Drying. Place the briquettes on a drying rack in the sun for 3 to 5 days. Turn them daily. They are ready when they are hard and dark. You can also use a solar dryer to speed up drying and protect from rain.

For pellets, the process is similar but uses a pellet mill. You need to grind material finer (less than 6 mm) and control moisture (12-15%). The pellet mill presses the material through a die, and the friction heats it, causing lignin to bind. Pellets come out hot and need cooling. Then they are bagged. A small pellet mill (100-300 kg/hr) costs $3,000 to $8,000.

Quality control: Test your briquettes by burning a few. They should light easily, burn with a steady flame, and produce little smoke. If they crumble, add more binder. If they burn too fast, increase density by pressing harder. Store in a dry place. With practice, you can produce 50 to 100 kg per day with a manual press, or 500 kg per day with a motorized press. The entire process can be set up in a small workshop or backyard.

<p>Briquettes and pellets are solid fuels made by compressing biomass residuessawdust, rice husks, maize cobs, sugarcane bagasse, coconut shells, coffee husks, and similar materialsinto dense, uniform shapes. The difference is mainly size and production method:</p>
<ul>

<h3>Why Africa Needs Alternative Fuels</h3>
<p>Across sub-Saharan Africa, more than 80% of households rely on wood fuel and charcoal for cooking. The consequences are severe: deforestation, land degradation, indoor air pollution, and rising fuel costs. The charcoal trade alone is worth billions of dollars annually, yet most of it is informal, unsustainable, and inefficient.</p>
<p>Briquettes and pellets offer a practical alternative. They can be made from waste materials that are otherwise burned or dumped. They burn cleaner than charcoal, produce less smoke, and can be produced locallycreating jobs and keeping money in the community. For entrepreneurs, the opportunity is clear: a growing urban population needs affordable cooking fuel, and traditional charcoal is becoming scarcer and more expensive.</p>

<h3>Common Raw Materials in Africa</h3>
<p>The best feedstock is whatever is abundant, cheap, and close to your production site. Common options include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sawdust and wood shavings</strong> from sawmills and carpentry workshops</li>
<li><strong>Rice husks</strong> from rice millsespecially in Nigeria, Tanzania, and Madagascar</li>
<li><strong>Maize cobs and stalks</strong> from farms and agro-processors</li>
<li><strong>Sugarcane bagasse</strong> from sugar factories</li>
<li><strong>Coconut shells and palm kernel shells</strong> from oil processing</li>
<li><strong>Coffee husks</strong> from coffee-producing regions in Ethiopia, Kenya, and Uganda</li>
<li><strong>Paper waste, cardboard, and municipal organic waste</strong> in urban areas</li>
</ul>
<p>Feedstock selection affects everything: calorific value, ash content, binder requirements, and production cost. A good rule is to start with one or two reliable local feedstocks and master them before experimenting with blends.</p>

<h3>How Briquettes and Pellets Are Used</h3>
<p><strong>Homes:</strong> Briquettes are used in improved cookstoves, charcoal stoves (with slight modification), and purpose-built briquette stoves. Pellets are used in pellet stoves and gasifiers. Both reduce smoke and save money compared to charcoal.</p>
<p><strong>Small businesses:</strong> Restaurants, bakeries, poultry farms, tea shops, and food processors use briquettes and pellets for cooking, heating, and boiling. Schools, hospitals, and prisons are also potential bulk buyers. In industrial settings, pellets feed boilers and kilns for tea processing, tobacco curing, and brick firing.</p>
<p><strong>Institutions:</strong> Schools and hospitals that cook for hundreds of people daily are ideal customers because they buy in bulk and need consistent quality.</p>

<h3>Success Stories: African Entrepreneurs</h3>
<p>Across the continent, entrepreneurs are proving the model works:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Kenya:</strong> Companies like Chardust Ltd. and Briquette Kenya produce briquettes from sawdust and charcoal dust for urban households and institutions. They have created jobs and reduced waste.</li>
<li><strong>Uganda:</strong> Green Bio Energy produces briquettes from agricultural waste and sells them through a network of local distributors, focusing on affordability and community impact.</li>
<li><strong>Nigeria:</strong> Several enterprises in Lagos and Abuja convert sawdust and rice husks into briquettes for cooking and small-scale industrial use.</li>
<li><strong>Ghana:</strong> Briquette producers are targeting the growing urban market in Accra and Kumasi, where charcoal prices continue to rise.</li>
<li><strong>Tanzania:</strong> Entrepreneurs are using coffee husks and rice husks to produce briquettes for tea factories and households.</li>
</ul>
<p>These businesses share common traits: they secure reliable feedstock, invest in quality control, build distribution networks, and educate customers on how to use their products.</p>

<h3>Overview of the Production Process</h3>
<p>The production process has six main stages:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Feedstock collection and storage:</strong> Secure a steady supply. Store under cover to prevent moisture and contamination.</li>
<li><strong>Drying:</strong> Reduce moisture content to 1015%. Sun drying is cheapest; solar dryers are more reliable during rainy seasons.</li>
<li><strong>Size reduction:</strong> Grind or crush feedstock to a consistent particle size. A hammer mill is standard for larger operations.</li>
<li><strong>Mixing:</strong> Add binder (cassava starch, clay, molasses, or paper pulp) and water. The right mix ratio determines durability and burn quality.</li>
<li><strong>Densification:</strong> Press into briquettes or pellets. Manual presses work for micro-enterprises; motorized presses and pellet mills increase output.</li>
<li><strong>Drying and packaging:</strong> Dry finished products to remove excess moisture, then package in bags or sacks for sale.</li>
</ol>

<h3 style="color:#c62828;margin-top:0;"> Safety Warning: Dust, Heat, and Machinery Hazards</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:8px;"><strong>Biomass dust is explosive and harmful.</strong> Fine sawdust and husk particles can ignite or explode in enclosed spaces. Always:</p>
<ul style="margin-top:0;">
<li>Wear N95 dust masks, safety goggles, and gloves when grinding, mixing, and pressing.</li>
<li>Ensure proper ventilation in all production areas. Never operate hammer mills or presses in sealed rooms.</li>
<li>Keep ignition sources away from dust clouds. Use explosion-proof electrical fittings where required.</li>
<li>Guard all moving partsbelts, pulleys, augers, and press dies. Never reach into a running machine.</li>
<li>Store finished briquettes and pellets away from moisture and direct sunlight to prevent spontaneous heating.</li>
<li>Train all workers on emergency shutdown procedures and first aid for burns and dust inhalation.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;"><strong>Carbon monoxide risk:</strong> Never burn briquettes or pellets in unventilated rooms. Always use a chimney or proper ventilation.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist for Startups</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Secure feedstock:</strong> Identify at least two reliable local sources and negotiate long-term supply.</li>
<li><strong>Test your product:</strong> Measure moisture, ash, and burn time before selling. Get customer feedback early.</li>
<li><strong>Choose the right equipment:</strong> Start manual or semi-automated; upgrade as demand grows.</li>
<li><strong>Control quality:</strong> Consistent size, density, and moisture are what keep customers coming back.</li>
<li><strong>Price competitively:</strong> Compare with charcoal and firewood prices in your area. Offer bulk discounts.</li>
<li><strong>Build distribution:</strong> Use local shops, agents, and direct delivery to schools, restaurants, and institutions.</li>
<li><strong>Train your team:</strong> Safety, machine operation, and quality control are non-negotiable.</li>
<li><strong>Track your numbers:</strong> Monitor feedstock cost, labor, output, and profit margin every month.</li>
</ul>
