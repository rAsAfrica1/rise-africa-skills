1. Personal Hygiene for Caterers

As a home-based caterer, your body is the most direct route for germs to enter food. Start with a daily routine: shower before cooking, wear clean clothes, and tie back long hair. Keep fingernails short and unpolishedâdirt hides under nails. Remove all jewellery (rings, bracelets, watches) because they trap bacteria. If you have a cut or sore on your hand, cover it with a waterproof plaster and wear a glove over it. Never cook if you have diarrhoea, vomiting, or feverâyou can pass germs to food even if you feel slightly better.

Handwashing is your number one defence. Wash hands for at least 20 seconds with soap and warm running water. Scrub palms, back of hands, between fingers, and under nails. Rinse and dry with a clean cloth or paper towel. Wash hands before starting cooking, after using the toilet, after handling raw meat or eggs, after touching your face or phone, after taking out rubbish, and after cleaning. Keep a bucket with a tap or a large jug of water and soap near your cooking area if you donât have a sink.

Wear an apron that you only use for cooking. Wash it regularly, especially after handling raw meat. Avoid touching your face, hair, or phone while cooking. If you must sneeze or cough, turn away and cover with your elbow, then wash hands. Donât taste food with your finger or a spoon you then reuseâuse a clean spoon each time. If youâre sick, ask a healthy helper to cook, or reschedule the event. Your reputation depends on safe food.

Also, keep your workspace clean: wipe surfaces before and after cooking. Donât let pets or children near the cooking area. In many African homes, cooking is communal, but for catering, limit the number of people handling food. Train any helper in these same rules. A simple sign at the handwashing station reminds everyone. Remember, customers trust you with their healthâpersonal hygiene is non-negotiable.

2. Safe Food Handling Temperatures

Bacteria that cause food poisoning multiply fastest between 5Â°C and 60Â°Câthe danger zone. In hot African climates, food can spoil in under two hours if left out. 3. Cross-Contamination Prevention

Cross-contamination happens when harmful germs from raw food, dirty surfaces, or hands get into ready-to-eat food. In a home kitchen, the biggest culprits are raw meat, poultry, eggs, and unwashed produce. The solution: separate everything. Use separate chopping boardsâone for raw meat (red), one for vegetables and cooked food (green or white). If you only have one board, wash it with hot soapy water between uses. Never cut vegetables on a board that just held raw chicken without washing it first.

Keep raw meat and eggs away from other foods in the fridge. Store raw meat on the bottom shelf in a sealed container so juices donât drip onto food below. Donât store eggs in the doorâtemperature fluctuates. Use separate utensils for raw and cooked foods. Never taste with the same spoon youâre cooking with; use a clean spoon each time. When grilling, donât put cooked meat back on the plate that held raw meat. Wash plates and utensils with hot soapy water after contact with raw meat.

Clean as you go. Wipe spills immediately, especially raw meat juices. Use paper towels or a cloth that you wash after each use. Sanitize surfaces with a bleach solution: 1 teaspoon (5 ml) of household bleach per 1 litre of water. Spray or wipe, leave for 1 minute, then air dry or wipe with clean water. For dishes, wash in hot soapy water, rinse, then soak in the bleach solution for 1 minute, then air dry. Donât dry with a dirty towelâair drying is safest.

Also, be careful with reusable shopping bags. Wash them regularly, especially if they carried raw meat. In many African markets, meat is wrapped in plastic or paperâdouble-bag it. When you get home, immediately put meat in the fridge or cook it. If youâre catering an event, pack raw and cooked foods in separate coolers. Label containers. Train helpers to follow these rules. Cross-contamination is invisible, but the resultsâfood poisoningâare very visible. Stay vigilant.

4. Cleaning and Sanitizing Procedures

Cleaning removes dirt and grease; sanitizing kills germs. You need both. Start by scraping food scraps into the bin. Then wash with hot water, soap, and a scrub brush. For dishes, use three basins if possible: one with hot soapy water for washing, one with clean hot water for rinsing, and one with a sanitizing solution (1 teaspoon bleach per 1 litre water) for soaking. If you only have one basin, wash, rinse, then sanitize in a separate bucket. Air dry on a clean rackâdo not towel dry, as towels can recontaminate.

Surfaces: After each use, wipe counters with hot soapy water, then sanitize with bleach solution. Let it sit for 1 minute before wiping with a clean cloth or letting it air dry. For wooden cutting boards, scrub with salt and lemon to remove odours, then wash and sanitize. Plastic boards can go in the dishwasher if you have one. Replace boards that have deep grooves where bacteria hide. Clean your fridge weekly: remove shelves, wash with hot soapy water, rinse, and sanitize. Donât forget door handles, taps, and light switchesâthese are high-touch areas.

Equipment: Clean your thermometer probe with alcohol wipes before and after each use. Clean your cooler boxes with soap and water, then sanitize, and leave open to dry. Wash dishcloths and towels frequentlyâsoak in bleach solution, then wash with hot water. Use separate cloths for dishes and surfaces. Sponges harbour bacteria; replace them weekly or boil them for 5 minutes. If you use a dish rack, clean it regularly.

Schedules: At the end of each cooking day, do a deep clean: sweep and mop floors, wipe all surfaces, empty bins, and sanitize. For events, clean as you go. Have a bin for waste near your station, and take it out frequently. In Africa, water may be scarce; use a spray bottle with bleach solution to sanitize without rinsing. Always wash hands after cleaning. A clean kitchen is a safe kitchenâmake it a habit, not a chore.

5. Allergen Management

Food allergies can be life-threatening. As a caterer, you must know the common allergens and prevent them from contaminating other foods. The big eight: peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, soy, and wheat. In Africa, also watch for sesame, mustard, and sulphites. Even a tiny amount can trigger a reaction. Symptoms include hives, swelling, difficulty breathing, and vomiting. If a guest has a severe reaction, call for medical help immediately.

When a client books you, ask about allergies. Keep a written record. If you cannot guarantee an allergen-free dish, be honestâdonât risk it. For each event, plan a separate area for preparing allergen-free food. Use clean utensils, boards, and pans that have been washed and sanitized. If youâre cooking multiple dishes, prepare the allergen-free one first, or use separate equipment. Never use the same oil that fried fish for chips if a guest is allergic to fish. Read labels on packaged foodsâmany sauces and spice mixes contain hidden allergens like wheat or soy.

Cross-contact is the biggest risk. For example, if you make a peanut sauce and then use the same spoon to stir a stew, you can transfer peanut protein. Always wash hands and change gloves after handling allergens. Use separate serving utensils for each dish. Label dishes clearly: âContains nutsâ or âGluten-free.â At a buffet, place allergen-free dishes separately and use a clean serving spoon for each. If a guest informs you of an allergy at the event, take it seriouslyâdonât just pick out the allergen; the whole dish may be contaminated.

Have an action plan: keep antihistamines on hand? No, thatâs for the guest. Your job is to prevent exposure. If a reaction occurs, call emergency services, and if the guest has an epinephrine auto-injector, help them use it. Document everything. Training your helpers on allergens is crucial. In many African cultures, food is shared, but for catering, you must respect individual needs. A reputation for safety will win you repeat business.

6. HACCP Basics for Home Kitchens

HACCP stands for Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points. Itâs a system to prevent food safety problems before they happen. For a home caterer, you donât need a formal certification, but you can apply the same principles. Start by identifying hazards: biological (bacteria, viruses), chemical (cleaning agents), and physical (hair, glass, bones). For each step in your cooking process, ask: what could go wrong? Then set controls.

The seven principles: 1) Conduct a hazard analysis. List all steps from shopping to serving. 2) Determine critical control points (CCPs)âsteps where you can prevent or eliminate a hazard. In a home kitchen, focus on key CCPs: receiving (check that meat is cold and fresh), storing (fridge below 5Â°C), cooking (proper temperatures), cooling (within 2 hours), reheating (74Â°C), and holding (above 60Â°C or below 5Â°C). Also, personal hygiene and cleaning are CCPs. Create a simple log sheet: date, food item, temperature, action taken. Keep it for at least 3 months. If you donât have a thermometer, get oneâitâs your most important tool.

Start small: pick one dish you cook often and map out the steps. Identify where things could go wrong. For example, making chicken stew: buy chicken (check cold), store in fridge, wash hands, cut on separate board, cook to 74Â°C, cool quickly, refrigerate. At each step, note what you do to keep it safe. Over time, this becomes second nature. HACCP is not just for big factoriesâitâs for anyone who wants to serve safe food. In home-based catering across Africa, your kitchen is your factory floor, your reputation, and your legal exposure all at once. A single case of food poisoning traced to your jollof, chapati, or wedding buffet can end a business that took years to build. 3. Personal Hygiene: The Invisible Ingredient

Your hands, hair, breath, and clothing are the most direct route for pathogens into food. For home caterers working without a commercial kitchen, personal hygiene is non-negotiable and must be visible to clients.

  • Handwashing: Wash with soap and running water for at least 20 seconds before starting, after handling raw meat or eggs, after using the toilet, after touching your phone or money, and after coughing or sneezing. Keep a dedicated handwash station (jerrycan with tap, soap, and paper towels) separate from the food prep area.
  • Gloves: Gloves are not a substitute for handwashing. Change them between raw and cooked foods, and never wear the same pair for more than 30 minutes.
  • Hair and nails: Tie back or cover hair. Keep nails short, unpainted, and clean. Nail polish flakes are a physical contaminant.
  • Illness: If you have diarrhoea, vomiting, jaundice, or an infected cut, do not cook for clients. Have a backup plan. This is a business decision, not just a health one.
  • Clothing: Wear a clean apron and, where possible, a head covering. Change aprons between raw and cooked food prep.

4. Cross-Contamination Prevention

Cross-contamination is the transfer of harmful bacteria from raw food (especially poultry, eggs, and meat) to ready-to-eat food. In small kitchens, this happens through shared chopping boards, knives, cloths, and hands.

  • Colour-code your boards: Red for raw meat, green for vegetables, blue for fish, yellow for cooked food. If you cannot afford a set, use one board and wash it with hot soapy water between every task.
  • Separate storage: Store raw meat on the bottom shelf of the fridge, covered, so juices cannot drip onto ready-to-eat food.
  • Cloths: Use disposable paper towels or change cloths frequently. A damp cloth left on the counter is a bacterial sponge.
  • Utensils: Never use the same spoon to taste and stir. Never reuse a marinade that has touched raw meat unless it is boiled first.
  • Market bags: Raw meat and vegetables bought at the market should be transported in separate bags. Reusable bags must be washed after every use.

5. Cleaning and Sanitizing

Cleaning removes visible dirt; sanitizing reduces invisible pathogens. You need both. For home caterers, a two-step process is sufficient: wash with hot soapy water, then sanitize with a diluted bleach solution (1 teaspoon of 5% bleach per litre of water) or a food-safe commercial sanitizer. Let surfaces air-dry â do not towel-dry with a cloth.

Focus on high-touch surfaces: countertops, fridge handles, taps, light switches, and your phone. Clean as you go â do not leave a mountain of dirty pots until the end, because that is when cross-contamination happens.

6. Allergen Management

Common allergens in African catering include groundnuts (peanuts), tree nuts (cashews, almonds), fish, shellfish, eggs, milk, wheat, and soy. Groundnut is the most dangerous in many West and East African contexts because it is used in soups, stews, and snacks, and reactions can be severe.

  • Ask every client, in writing, about allergies before the event.
  • Keep a separate small pot and utensils for allergen-free dishes.
  • Label all dishes clearly at the buffet, especially sauces and garnishes.
  • Never assume "a little bit won't hurt" â for a severely allergic guest, trace amounts can be fatal.
  • Keep an antihistamine in your kit and know the nearest clinic.

7. HACCP Basics for the Home Kitchen

HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) sounds corporate, but the logic is simple: identify where things can go wrong, set a limit, monitor it, and act if it fails. For a home caterer, your critical control points are usually:

  1. Receiving: Check meat, fish, and dairy for temperature and smell at purchase.
  2. Cooking: Verify internal temperatures with a probe thermometer.
  3. Cooling: Follow the two-stage cooling rule.
  4. Holding: Keep hot food above 60Â°C and cold food below 5Â°C.
  5. Service: Limit buffet time to 2 hours; discard leftovers.

Write these five steps on a single page, note the date and time each time you check, and keep the log. When a corporate client or NGO asks for your food safety documentation, this log is your proof â and often your competitive advantage over other home caterers.
