1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Metal furniture making is a profitable trade across Africa. With basic tools and a small workspace, you can build beds, chairs, tables, and gates that sell for 50,000 to 300,000 Naira depending on size and finish. The demand is high because metal furniture lasts longer than wood, resists termites, and suits both homes and offices. In cities like Lagos, Nairobi, and Accra, welders earn steady income by supplying local markets, schools, and hotels.

Starting small is possible. You need a welding machine (inverter type, 200-250 amps, costs about 150,000 Naira), an angle grinder, a hammer, a measuring tape, and safety gear. Raw materials like square pipes (1 inch by 1 inch, 1.2mm thickness) cost around 4,500 Naira per 6-meter length. A simple coffee table uses 2 lengths, so material cost is about 9,000 Naira plus paint and electrodes. You can sell it for 25,000 Naira, making a good profit.

The market is not just individuals. Schools order 50 desks at a time, restaurants need chairs, and event planners rent or buy metal frames. You can also make custom designs for wealthy clients who pay premium. Word of mouth and showing your work on WhatsApp or Facebook brings orders. Many successful welders started with one machine in a small shed.

This module will teach you the market, tools, step-by-step methods, common mistakes, safety, and give you a practical exercise. By the end, you will know how to identify what sells, price your work, and avoid costly errors. No formal training neededâjust focus and practice. Remember, every expert was once a beginner. Start where you are, use what you have, and build your skills one weld at a time.

2. Tools and Materials

To make metal furniture, you need the right tools and materials. The essential tool is a welding machine. For beginners, an inverter welding machine (200 amps) is best because it works on low voltage and is portable. It costs between 120,000 and 200,000 Naira. You also need an angle grinder (about 25,000 Naira) with cutting and grinding discs. A hammer, measuring tape (5 meters), square, and wire brush are cheap but vital. For safety, buy welding helmet, gloves, apron, and bootsâtotal around 30,000 Naira.

Materials: Square pipes are the backbone. Common sizes: 1 inch by 1 inch (1.2mm thick) for frames, and 2 inch by 2 inch for heavy-duty tables. A 6-meter length of 1x1 costs about 4,500 Naira; 2x2 costs about 8,000 Naira. You also need flat bars, round bars, and metal sheets for seats or tops. Welding electrodes (2.5mm or 3.2mm) cost about 1,500 Naira per packet. Paint (red oxide primer and gloss) and thinner are needed for finishing. Sandpaper or grinding discs smooth the welds.

Where to buy: In Nigeria, check markets like Ladipo in Lagos or Kano Street in Abuja. In Kenya, go to Industrial Area in Nairobi. In Ghana, Suame Magazine in Kumasi. Buy from reputable dealers to avoid weak pipes. Ask for 'full gauge'âthinner pipes are cheaper but break easily. For a start, budget 250,000 to 300,000 Naira for tools and initial materials. You can also rent a workshop or work under a tree.

Always keep a notebook to record prices and suppliers. Build a relationship with your metal dealer for discounts. As you grow, add a bench vice, files, and a spray gun. But do not wait for everythingâstart with the basics and upgrade as you earn.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to build a simple metal coffee table. First, design and measure. Draw a sketch with dimensions: top 60cm x 60cm, height 45cm. You need four legs (45cm each), four top rails (60cm each), and four bottom rails (40cm each). Use 1x1 inch square pipes. Cut with an angle grinder: measure twice, cut once. Wear goggles.

Second, clean and prepare. Use a grinder to remove rust and burrs from cut ends. This ensures strong welds. Set up a flat work surfaceâa concrete floor or a metal table. Use a square to check 90-degree angles. Clamp pieces if you have clamps, or use heavy objects to hold them.

Third, tack weld. A tack weld is a small weld at corners to hold the frame. Weld one corner at a time, checking squareness. Once the frame is square, weld all joints fully. Use 2.5mm electrodes at 70-90 amps. Move quickly to avoid burning through thin pipes. For strong joints, use a 'C' clamp to hold pieces. After welding, grind the welds smooth with a grinding disc.

Fourth, assemble the table. Weld the legs to the top frame, then the bottom rails. Check that all legs are levelâplace on flat ground and adjust. Weld a metal sheet or wooden top to the frame. If using sheet, cut to size and weld or screw it. Finally, clean with a wire brush and apply red oxide primer. Let dry, then paint with gloss enamel. Two coats give a professional finish.

Practice this table three times. Each time, you will be faster and neater. Then move to chairs, beds, and gates. Always measure, cut, weld, and finish.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Weak welds. Beginners often use too low amperage or move too fast, causing poor penetration. Fix: Set your welder to 80-100 amps for 2.5mm electrodes. Practice on scrap metal until the weld looks like a stack of coins. If a weld breaks, grind it out and re-weld with higher amps.

Mistake 2: Warping. Welding heats metal, causing it to bend. Fix: Tack weld first, then weld small sections at a time, alternating sides. Let the metal cool between welds. You can also clamp pieces to a heavy steel table to keep them straight.

Mistake 3: Wrong measurements. Cutting pipes too short or long wastes material. Fix: Measure twice, mark with a soapstone or marker, and cut with a steady hand. Use a square to check angles. Always add 2mm for weld gaps.

Mistake 4: Poor finishing. Rough welds and no paint make furniture look cheap. Fix: Grind all welds smooth, then sand with 80-grit paper. Apply primer and two coats of paint. Let each coat dry completely.

Mistake 5: Ignoring safety. Not wearing a helmet leads to eye damage. Fix: Always wear welding helmet, gloves, and apron. Keep a fire extinguisher or bucket of sand nearby.

Mistake 6: Underpricing. Many new welders charge too little. Fix: Calculate material cost, labor (at least 30% of material), and profit (20%). For a coffee table, material 10,000 Naira, labor 5,000, profit 5,000, total 20,000 Naira. Check competitors but do not undervalue your work.

Mistake 7: Not practicing. Skipping practice leads to bad work. Fix: Spend 30 minutes daily welding scrap. Build small items like key holders or stools to improve.

Avoid these mistakes and your furniture will sell fast.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional. Welding produces intense light, sparks, and fumes. Always wear a welding helmet with a dark lens (shade 10 or 11). Never look at the arc with naked eyesâit causes 'arc eye', which feels like sand in your eyes. Wear leather gloves, a leather apron, and steel-toe boots. Cover your arms and legsâno shorts or sandals.

Work in a well-ventilated area. Welding fumes contain harmful gases. If indoors, open windows and use a fan to blow fumes away. Do not weld near flammable materials like petrol, paint, or wood. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder) or a bucket of sand within reach. Sparks can travel far, so clear the area.

Handle tools safely. Angle grinders spin at high speedâalways use the guard and wear goggles. Unplug when changing discs. Keep your work area clean and dry. Store gas cylinders upright and secure. If using oxy-acetylene, check hoses for leaks.

Best practices: Inspect your equipment before use. Check cables for cuts, and ensure the earth clamp is connected properly. Use the correct electrode for the metal thickness. For thin pipes, use 2.5mm electrodes at lower amps. For thick metal, use 3.2mm at higher amps. Keep your welding area organizedâtools within reach, no clutter.

After welding, let the metal cool before touching. Use tongs or pliers. Clean up scraps and metal dust. Wash your hands before eating. If you feel dizzy or have eye irritation, stop and rest. Seek medical help if needed.

Remember, a safe welder is a productive welder. Accidents cost time and money. Follow these rules and you will work for years without injury.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is time to practice. Your task is to build a simple metal stool. This exercise uses the skills from all previous lessons. You will need: 1x1 inch square pipe (2 lengths of 6 meters), welding machine, angle grinder, measuring tape, square, hammer, and safety gear. Budget about 12,000 Naira for materials.

Step 1: Design. A stool with a square top 30cm x 30cm, height 45cm. You need four legs (45cm each), four top rails (30cm each), and four bottom rails (25cm each). Cut all pieces with a grinder. Clean ends.

Step 2: Weld the top frame. Lay the four top rails on a flat surface to form a square. Tack weld corners, check squareness with a square, then weld fully. Grind smooth.

Step 3: Attach legs. Stand the frame on its side. Weld one leg to each corner, ensuring it is perpendicular. Use a square to check 90 degrees. Tack weld first, then weld fully. Repeat for all legs.

Step 4: Add bottom rails. Weld the four bottom rails between the legs, about 10cm from the ground. This adds strength. Grind all welds.

Step 5: Finish. Clean with wire brush. Apply red oxide primer, let dry, then paint with gloss enamel (any color). Let dry.

Step 6: Test. Sit on the stool. It should not wobble. If it does, check legs for levelness and adjust.

Take a photo of your stool. Show it to friends and family. Ask for feedback. If you can, sell it for 8,000 to 10,000 Naira. Then build another, improving each time. This exercise builds confidence and skill. Once you master the stool, you can build chairs, tables, and beds. Keep practicing!

â  SAFETY WARNING â METAL FURNITURE WORKSHOP

Welding, cutting and grinding produce arc flash, metal fumes, sparks and noise that can permanently injure eyes, lungs and hearing. Before any fabrication work: wear a proper welding helmet with correct shade lens, leather gloves, apron, safety boots and ear protection; ensure adequate ventilation or fume extraction; keep a dry-powder or COâ fire extinguisher within reach; never weld near paint thinners, petrol or LPG cylinders; and check all electrical leads and earth clamps for damage before switching on. In many African workshops, tetanus and burn injuries are common â keep a well-stocked first-aid kit and ensure your tetanus vaccination is current. Do not allow untrained apprentices to operate angle grinders or welding machines unsupervised.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Know your market segment: decide whether you are selling to institutions (volume, tenders, tight specs) or households/retail (margin, design, finish).
  2. Cost every product before quoting: materials + labour + overheads + transport + tax + margin. Never quote from memory.
  3. Buy steel strategically: compare mill agents, importers and local stockists; buy in bulk where cash flow allows.
  4. Standardise 3â5 core designs and build jigs so that quality is repeatable and labour time falls.
  5. Never skip surface preparation: degrease, remove mill scale, apply anti-rust primer, then topcoat â this is your cheapest quality signal.
  6. Document every job: quote, order, delivery note, invoice, warranty terms. This builds trust with institutional buyers.
  7. Comply with safety and standards: PPE, ventilation, fire safety and, where required, national standards (e.g. KEBS, SON, SABS, Ghana Standards Authority).
  8. Reinvest in finishing and transport: the two areas that most often lose artisan workshops repeat institutional contracts.
