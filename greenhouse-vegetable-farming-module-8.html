1. Module 8 Lesson 1: Selecting Greenhouse Crops for African Markets

Welcome to Module 8, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on one of the most important business decisions you will make as a greenhouse vegetable farmer: choosing which crops to grow. A greenhouse is a significant investment, and the crop you select determines your costs, your labour needs, your market access, and ultimately your profit. In Africa, greenhouse farming has grown rapidly in countries such as Kenya, South Africa, Nigeria, Ethiopia, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, and Ghana. The most common greenhouse crops are tomatoes, capsicum, cucumbers, leafy greens, and herbs. Each has different temperature, humidity, and nutrition requirements. Your first task is to match the crop to your local climate and your target market. Tomatoes remain the most popular greenhouse crop in Africa because demand is consistent, prices are relatively stable, and many farmers already understand the crop. However, tomatoes are also the most competitive. If you are near a city with many greenhouse tomato producers, you may struggle to get a premium price. Capsicum, especially coloured bell peppers, often earns higher prices in urban supermarkets and hotels, but it requires more careful management of temperature and pollination. Cucumbers are fast growing and can produce income within eight to ten weeks, which is attractive if you need quick cash flow. Leafy greens such as spinach, kale, and lettuce have short cycles and strong demand in urban areas, but they are perishable and need a reliable cold chain. Herbs like basil, mint, coriander, and parsley serve niche markets including restaurants, hotels, and health food shops. They can be very profitable per square metre but require consistent supply and often special packaging. Before you plant, conduct a simple market survey. Visit local markets, supermarkets, hotels, restaurants, and roadside vendors. Ask what they buy, in what quantities, at what price, and how often. Identify gaps. For example, during the dry season, leafy greens become scarce and prices rise. A greenhouse can supply that gap. Also consider export markets. Kenya and Ethiopia export green beans, snow peas, and cut flowers, but vegetables for export must meet strict quality and phytosanitary standards. If you are a smallholder, focus first on domestic markets and build a reputation for quality and reliability. Crop selection also depends on your greenhouse type. A low-cost wooden or metal frame covered with UV-treated polythene is common in East Africa. These structures are affordable but have limited height and ventilation. Taller greenhouses with insect nets and proper vents allow better airflow and reduce disease. If you grow tall crops like tomatoes and cucumbers, you need enough height for trellising. If you grow leafy greens, you can use multi-tier benches or hydroponic systems to maximise space. Water availability is another critical factor. Tomatoes and cucumbers need consistent irrigation, often twice a day in hot weather. Leafy greens need less water but still require daily attention. If your water source is unreliable, choose drought-tolerant crops or invest in rainwater harvesting and storage tanks. Labour is also a major cost. Tomatoes require pruning, staking, and pest scouting. Capsicum requires similar care. Leafy greens are less labour-intensive but need careful harvesting and packaging. Herbs require frequent harvesting and handling. Be realistic about how much labour you and your family can provide, and whether you can hire additional workers during peak periods. Finally, think about crop rotation and diversification. Growing the same crop year after year in the same soil leads to pest and disease buildup. Rotate tomatoes with leafy greens or herbs. Diversification also spreads your market risk. If tomato prices fall, you still have income from capsicum or herbs. In summary, select crops based on market demand, climate suitability, greenhouse design, water availability, labour capacity, and your own experience. Start with one or two crops, master them, and then expand. In the next lesson we will look at greenhouse climate control and how to manage temperature, humidity, and ventilation for healthy crops. Before you move on, write down three potential crops for your greenhouse and list the pros and cons of each. This simple exercise will save you money and time.

2. Module 8 Lesson 2: Climate Control and Ventilation in African Greenhouses

Welcome to Module 8, Lesson 2. In this lesson we examine climate control and ventilation, which are the heart of successful greenhouse vegetable farming. A greenhouse creates a protected environment, but without proper management it can become too hot, too humid, or too stagnant. In Africa, where daytime temperatures often exceed 30 degrees Celsius, overheating is a major risk. At the same time, cold nights in highland areas such as Kenya, Ethiopia, and Rwanda can slow growth. Your goal is to keep temperature, humidity, and air movement within the optimal range for your chosen crops. Tomatoes grow best between 18 and 27 degrees Celsius during the day and 15 to 20 degrees at night. Capsicum prefers slightly warmer conditions, 20 to 28 degrees during the day. Cucumbers like 20 to 30 degrees. Leafy greens such as lettuce and spinach prefer cooler conditions, 15 to 22 degrees. Herbs vary, but most prefer 18 to 28 degrees. When temperatures rise above 30 degrees, pollen becomes sterile in tomatoes and capsicum, leading to poor fruit set. When temperatures drop below 10 degrees, growth slows and fruit may be deformed. Ventilation is your primary tool for temperature control. A greenhouse must have openings at both the bottom and the top to allow hot air to escape and cool air to enter. This is called stack ventilation. Roof vents, roll-up sides, and ridge vents are common. In many African greenhouses, roll-up sides covered with insect netting are the most practical and affordable option. Insect netting prevents whiteflies, aphids, thrips, and other pests from entering while allowing airflow. The mesh size should be fine enough to exclude pests but not so fine that it blocks too much air. A 40 to 50 mesh is often recommended. If your greenhouse has no vents, you can cut openings and cover them with netting. Make sure the openings can be closed during cold nights or heavy rain. Shade nets are another important tool. In hot, sunny regions, a 30 to 50 percent shade net over the greenhouse roof can reduce temperature by 5 to 10 degrees. Shade nets also reduce sunburn on fruits and leaves. However, too much shade reduces photosynthesis and yields. Use shade nets only during the hottest part of the day or during peak summer months. Some farmers use whitewash or lime on the greenhouse cover to reflect sunlight. This is cheap but permanent until washed off. Humidity control is closely linked to ventilation. High humidity encourages fungal diseases such as powdery mildew, downy mildew, and botrytis. It also reduces transpiration, which slows nutrient uptake. Low humidity causes wilting and flower drop. The ideal relative humidity for most greenhouse vegetables is 60 to 80 percent. To reduce humidity, increase ventilation, especially in the morning after the greenhouse has been closed all night. Avoid overhead watering in the evening. Use drip irrigation, which delivers water directly to the roots and keeps foliage dry. Mulching with plastic or organic material also reduces evaporation and keeps humidity lower. In hot, dry areas, you may need to increase humidity. You can do this by wetting the floor, using misting systems, or placing shallow pans of water near the air inlet. However, be careful not to create disease-friendly conditions. Air movement inside the greenhouse is also important. Even with vents, pockets of stagnant air can form. Small fans, including solar-powered fans, can help circulate air. This strengthens plant stems, reduces fungal growth, and improves pollination. For tomatoes and capsicum, gentle shaking of the plants or the use of bumblebees can improve pollination. In many parts of Africa, bumblebees are not readily available, so manual shaking or electric toothbrushes are used. Temperature monitoring is essential. Buy a simple maximum-minimum thermometer and a hygrometer. Record readings at least twice a day, morning and afternoon. Keep a logbook. This helps you identify patterns and make adjustments. If you cannot afford instruments, observe your plants. Wilting in the afternoon may indicate heat stress. Leaf curling, flower drop, and small fruit are also signs. In highland areas, cold nights can be a problem. Close vents in the late afternoon to trap warmth. Use thermal screens or double-layer polythene if available. In extreme cases, small heaters or hot water pipes can be used, but these are expensive. Solar-powered heating is an emerging option. Finally, consider the orientation of your greenhouse. In most of Africa, orient the greenhouse east to west so that the long side faces the sun. This reduces the surface area exposed to direct midday sun. In very hot areas, orient north to south to allow better airflow. In summary, climate control is about balance. You must manage temperature, humidity, and air movement together. Ventilation, shade, drip irrigation, and monitoring are your main tools. Practice daily observation and record keeping. In the next lesson we will cover irrigation and fertigation, where we will build on what you have learned about climate to deliver water and nutrients efficiently.

3. Module 8 Lesson 3: Irrigation and Fertigation for Greenhouse Vegetables

Welcome to Module 8, Lesson 3. In this lesson we focus on irrigation and fertigation, which are the lifelines of your greenhouse crops. Water and nutrients must be delivered precisely because greenhouse plants cannot send deep roots in search of water, and rainfall does not reach them. In Africa, water scarcity is a common challenge, so efficiency is not just good practice, it is essential for profit. The most recommended irrigation method for greenhouse vegetables is drip irrigation. Drip systems deliver water directly to the root zone, reducing evaporation and keeping foliage dry. This lowers disease pressure and saves water. A typical drip system includes a water source, a pump, a filter, main lines, sub-main lines, and drip lines with emitters. Emitters are spaced according to crop and soil type, usually 20 to 30 centimetres apart. For tomatoes and cucumbers, one or two drip lines per row are common. For leafy greens, you may use drip lines or overhead micro-sprinklers, but drip is still preferred for disease control. Before installing drip, test your water quality. High salinity, high iron, or high bicarbonate can clog emitters and harm plants. A simple electrical conductivity meter and pH meter are useful. Most greenhouse vegetables prefer water with a pH of 5.5 to 6.5 and electrical conductivity below 2.0 decisiemens per metre, though this varies by crop. If your water is saline, you may need to leach the soil periodically with extra water. Filtration is critical. Sand filters, screen filters, and disc filters remove particles that clog emitters. Clean filters regularly. If you use organic fertilisers through the drip system, you need a more robust filtration system. Fertigation is the practice of injecting soluble fertilisers into the irrigation water. It saves labour, ensures uniform application, and allows you to adjust nutrients to crop stage. You can use a simple venturi injector, a dosing pump, or a gravity-fed tank. For smallholder greenhouses, a tank with a tap and a hose connected to the drip line is the cheapest option. You mix fertiliser in the tank and let it flow into the system. However, you must monitor electrical conductivity and pH to avoid burning roots. A common fertigation schedule for tomatoes starts with a high-nitrogen formula during vegetative growth, then shifts to a balanced formula with more potassium during flowering and fruiting. Calcium and magnesium are also important to prevent blossom end rot and leaf yellowing. Micronutrients such as iron, zinc, boron, and manganese are needed in small amounts. Many African farmers use compound fertilisers like NPK 17:17:17, calcium nitrate, and potassium sulphate. These are widely available. Organic options include compost tea, vermicompost leachate, and liquid seaweed. Organic fertigation requires careful filtration and may not provide exact nutrient ratios, but it improves soil health and can earn premium prices. Water scheduling depends on crop, stage, soil, and climate. In hot weather, tomatoes may need 4 to 6 litres per plant per day, split into two or three applications. Cucumbers need similar amounts. Leafy greens need less, about 2 to 3 litres per plant per day, but they have shallow roots so frequent small doses are best. Herbs need moderate water. The best way to decide is to check soil moisture. You can use a simple tensiometer or just dig with your hand. If the soil is dry 10 centimetres down, it is time to irrigate. Avoid overwatering. Waterlogged soil causes root rot, yellowing, and fungal diseases. It also wastes water and leaches nutrients. In heavy clay soils, add organic matter and consider raised beds. In sandy soils, add compost and mulch to improve water holding capacity. Mulching is one of the cheapest and most effective ways to conserve water. Plastic mulch, straw, or dry grass reduces evaporation and suppresses weeds. Drip irrigation under plastic mulch is a standard practice in commercial greenhouses worldwide and works well in Africa. Maintenance is essential. Flush drip lines regularly to remove sediment. Use acid injection occasionally to dissolve mineral buildup. Check emitters for clogging. Repair leaks immediately. Keep a log of water use and fertiliser applications. This helps you calculate costs and improve efficiency. Water harvesting is also important. Roof catchment from the greenhouse can fill tanks. A 100 square metre greenhouse roof can collect 100 litres of water from just 1 millimetre of rain. In areas with erratic rainfall, this can be a lifesaver. Finally, think about labour. Drip irrigation reduces the need for manual watering, but someone must monitor the system daily. Train a worker to check filters, emitters, and tank levels. A well-managed irrigation system can increase yields by 30 to 50 percent compared to manual watering. In summary, invest in drip irrigation, learn fertigation, monitor water and nutrients, and keep records. In the next lesson we will discuss pest and disease management, where we will build on the clean, dry environment that proper irrigation creates.

4. Module 8 Lesson 4: Integrated Pest and Disease Management in Greenhouses

Welcome to Module 8, Lesson 4. In this lesson we tackle integrated pest and disease management, often called IPM. Greenhouses create a protected environment, but they also create ideal conditions for pests and diseases if not managed well. Warm temperatures, high humidity, and dense plant spacing can lead to rapid outbreaks. In Africa, common greenhouse pests include whiteflies, aphids, thrips, spider mites, leafminers, and fruit flies. Common diseases include powdery mildew, downy mildew, early blight, late blight, bacterial wilt, fusarium wilt, and root rot. The first principle of IPM is prevention. A clean greenhouse is a healthy greenhouse. Start with sanitation. Remove weeds, crop residues, and old plant material from inside and around the greenhouse. Weeds host pests and diseases. Use clean tools and disinfect them between uses. If you visit other farms, change clothes and shoes before entering your greenhouse. Quarantine new seedlings before planting them. Buy certified seed and disease-resistant varieties whenever possible. Resistant varieties are available for tomatoes, capsicum, and cucumbers. They may cost more, but they reduce pesticide costs and crop losses. The second principle is monitoring. Scout your crops at least twice a week. Look at the underside of leaves, growing tips, flowers, and fruits. Use yellow sticky traps to monitor whiteflies, aphids, and thrips. Blue sticky traps attract thrips. Place traps at crop height and replace them regularly. Keep a notebook. Record what pests you see, where, and how many. This helps you decide when to act. The third principle is cultural control. Adjust your climate management to make life difficult for pests and diseases. Ventilate well to reduce humidity. Avoid overhead watering. Space plants properly to allow airflow. Prune lower leaves of tomatoes and cucumbers to improve ventilation and reduce soil splash. Remove and destroy diseased plants immediately. Do not compost them unless you have a hot composting system. Use mulches to prevent soil-borne diseases from splashing onto leaves. The fourth principle is biological control. Encourage natural enemies. Ladybirds, lacewings, predatory mites, and parasitic wasps attack pests. You can buy some of these from suppliers in Kenya, South Africa, and other countries, but they are expensive for smallholders. You can also plant companion crops such as marigolds, basil, and coriander to attract beneficial insects. Avoid broad-spectrum pesticides that kill natural enemies. The fifth principle is chemical control, used as a last resort. If you must spray, choose the least toxic product and follow the label exactly. Use protective clothing, including gloves, mask, and goggles. Never spray during the hottest part of the day. Spray in the early morning or late afternoon. Rotate chemicals to prevent resistance. Do not spray close to harvest. Observe the pre-harvest interval. In Africa, many farmers use pesticides such as abamectin, imidacloprid, and mancozeb. These can be effective but must be used responsibly. Consider botanical pesticides such as neem oil, garlic extract, chili extract, and soap solutions. These are cheap and safer for the environment, but they may need more frequent application. For fungal diseases, improve ventilation, reduce leaf wetness, and remove infected leaves. Copper-based fungicides and potassium bicarbonate can help. For bacterial wilt, there is no cure. Remove infected plants and rotate crops. Solarisation of soil can reduce soil-borne pathogens. Use grafted tomatoes if available. Grafting onto resistant rootstocks is common in commercial greenhouses and can protect against fusarium and bacterial wilt. Record keeping is essential for IPM. Keep a log of pest sightings, weather conditions, treatments, and results. This helps you learn what works on your farm. It also helps you calculate the cost of pest control and compare it to the value of lost yield. If a pest outbreak costs you more than the treatment, you need a better prevention strategy. Finally, train your workers. Everyone who enters the greenhouse should know the signs of pests and diseases. They should know how to report problems. A well-trained team can spot an outbreak early, when it is still cheap to control. In summary, IPM is a combination of sanitation, monitoring, cultural practices, biological control, and careful chemical use. It is not about eliminating all pests, but about keeping them below the level that causes economic damage. 5. Module 8 Lesson 5: Harvesting, Post-Harvest Handling, and Marketing

Welcome to Module 8, Lesson 5. In this final lesson we focus on harvesting, post-harvest handling, and marketing. You have invested in your greenhouse, managed the climate, irrigated and fertilised, and controlled pests. Now you must harvest at the right time and get your produce to market in good condition. In Africa, post-harvest losses for vegetables can be 30 to 50 percent. Reducing these losses is one of the fastest ways to increase profit. Harvesting at the right stage is critical. Tomatoes for local market are usually harvested at the breaker stage, when they are starting to turn colour. This allows them to ripen during transport. Tomatoes for distant markets or supermarkets may be harvested at mature green stage. Capsicum is harvested when it reaches full size and colour. Green capsicum is harvested before it turns red, yellow, or orange. Cucumbers are harvested young, before seeds harden. Leafy greens are harvested when leaves are tender and before they bolt. Herbs are harvested before flowering for best flavour. Harvest in the coolest part of the day, usually early morning. Use clean, sharp tools. Do not pull fruits or leaves roughly, as this damages plants and creates entry points for disease. Use clean crates or baskets. Do not throw produce into bags or pile it too high. Bruised produce rots quickly. After harvest, move produce to a cool, shaded area immediately. Field heat is the enemy. The faster you remove field heat, the longer the shelf life. In many African farms, a simple shade structure with wet sacks or a fan can help. If you have electricity, a cold room is ideal, but it is expensive. For smallholders, a cool box or a well-ventilated shed works. Wash produce only if necessary. For leafy greens, washing can remove soil but also increases moisture and disease risk. If you wash, use clean water and dry gently. Sort and grade your produce. Remove damaged, diseased, or undersized items. Grade by size, colour, and quality. Uniform produce attracts better prices. Packaging is important. Use clean, ventilated crates for transport. For supermarkets and hotels, you may need branded punnets or bags. For local markets, simple crates are fine. Label your produce with your farm name and contact details. This builds trust and repeat business. Marketing starts before harvest. Identify your buyers early. Options include local markets, roadside stalls, supermarkets, hotels, restaurants, schools, hospitals, and export companies. Each has different requirements. Supermarkets want consistent quality, volume, and packaging. Hotels and restaurants want fresh, high-quality produce and may pay a premium. Local markets are flexible but prices fluctuate. Export markets have strict standards and require certification. Consider forming a cooperative or farmer group. Together you can meet larger orders, share transport costs, and negotiate better prices. Many African farmers have succeeded by aggregating their produce. Use digital tools. Mobile phones and platforms like WhatsApp, Facebook, and local agricultural apps can connect you to buyers. Some farmers use SMS-based market information systems to check prices. Keep records of your sales, costs, and profits. Calculate your cost per kilogram or per crate. This tells you whether you are making a profit. If not, adjust your prices, reduce costs, or change crops. Value addition can increase income. For example, you can dry herbs, make tomato sauce, or package mixed salad greens. However, value addition requires investment and food safety compliance. Start small and test the market. Finally, build relationships. Deliver on time. Be honest about quality. If you have a problem, communicate early. A reliable supplier is worth more than a one-time high price. In summary, harvest at the right time, handle gently, cool quickly, sort and grade, package well, and market strategically. This lesson completes Module 8. You now have the knowledge to select crops, control climate, irrigate and fertilise, manage pests and diseases, and bring your produce to market. Put these lessons into practice, keep records, and continue learning. Greenhouse vegetable farming is a business, and with careful management, it can be profitable and sustainable in Africa.

Greenhouse environments concentrate chemical vapours and reduce ventilation. Always wear full personal protective equipment (PPE) â gloves, goggles, respirator, coveralls, and boots â when handling, mixing, or applying pesticides or concentrated fertilizer salts. Never enter a greenhouse within the restricted-entry interval (REI) stated on the product label. Store all agrochemicals in a locked, ventilated, clearly labelled cabinet away from food, feed, and water sources. Observe the pre-harvest interval (PHI) strictly to prevent chemical residues in produce destined for local and export markets. In case of poisoning, contact your nearest poison control centre or hospital immediately and bring the product label. The WHO recommends that all agricultural workers receive training on the safe use of pesticides (WHO, 2020, Chemical Safety in Agriculture).

Quick Reference Checklist: Daily Greenhouse Operations

  1. Climate check (twice daily): Record temperature and relative humidity at 08:00 and 14:00. Adjust vents, shade nets, or fans to stay within crop-specific ranges (see Table 2).
  2. Irrigation and fertigation: Verify EC and pH of the nutrient solution before each fertigation event. Flush drip lines weekly to prevent emitter clogging â a common failure point in African greenhouses with high-sediment water.
  3. Scouting for pests and diseases: Walk the crop in a W-pattern and inspect at least 10 plants per bed. Check undersides of leaves, growing tips, and flowers. Record findings in a scouting log.
  4. IPM action threshold: Only apply chemical control when pest populations exceed established thresholds. Prioritise biological control agents, sticky traps, and hygiene. Rotate chemical classes to prevent resistance.
  5. Harvest hygiene: Harvest during the coolest part of the day. Use clean, sanitised crates and knives. Never place harvested produce directly on the ground.
  6. Post-harvest cooling: Move produce to shade or a cool room within 30 minutes of harvest. Pre-cooling extends shelf life by 2â5 days for leafy greens and up to 7 days for tomatoes.
  7. Record keeping: Log all inputs (seed, fertilizer, pesticide, water), labour, and harvest volumes. Accurate records are essential for traceability, certification, and accessing premium markets.
  8. Market intelligence: Check current market prices weekly. Adjust your next planting schedule based on demand trends and price seasonality to avoid gluts.

Business-First Notes for African Greenhouse Operators

Greenhouse vegetable farming in Africa is a business, not just a farming activity. The capital expenditure for a 500 mÂ² greenhouse in Kenya ranges from USD 3,000 to 8,000 depending on the structure (wooden vs. galvanised steel), covering material (UV-treated polythene vs. polycarbonate), and irrigation system. A well-managed tomato crop can generate USD 3,600â9,000 per cycle at 500 mÂ², with two cycles per year, yielding a gross revenue of USD 7,200â18,000 annually. After deducting input costs (seed, fertilizer, crop protection, labour, water, and energy), net margins of 30â50% are achievable for producers who consistently meet quality standards and have reliable market linkages.

Key business risks include price volatility, pest outbreaks, power interruptions (for pump-fed irrigation), and climate extremes. Mitigate these through diversified crop portfolios, staggered planting, rainwater harvesting, solar-powered irrigation, and formal contracts with supermarkets, hotels, or export aggregators. The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) opens new regional market opportunities for greenhouse produce, but only for producers who can meet phytosanitary and quality standards. Invest in training, record-keeping, and post-harvest infrastructure â these are the differentiators between subsistence and commercial success.
