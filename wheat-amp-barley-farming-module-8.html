Lessons

1. Signs of Maturity: Color, Hardness, and Moisture

Knowing when to harvest is critical. If you harvest too early, the grain is green and shriveled, leading to low yield and poor quality. If you wait too long, the grain shatters and falls to the ground, and birds and rodents eat it. For wheat and barley, the best time to harvest is when the grain is physiologically mature but still hard. The straw (stems and leaves) turns from green to yellow or golden. The heads (spikes) droop slightly and the grain inside is hard. A simple test: bite a grain. If it is doughy or soft, it is not ready. If it is hard and makes a cracking sound when bitten, it is ready. Another sign: the grain is difficult to separate from the chaff by hand. Moisture content should be around 20-25% for harvest to avoid shattering, but for safe storage it must be dried to 12-13%. In Africa, many smallholders harvest by hand, so timing is even more critical. Watch the crop daily as it approaches maturity. In hot, dry areas, maturity can happen quicklywithin a week. In cooler highlands, it may take longer. Also, check for black point or fusarium damage; if you see black tips on grains, harvest immediately and dry well. Do not delay once the crop is ready. Use a moisture meter if available, but if not, use the bite test and visual signs. Remember, barley matures earlier than wheat. For barley, the awns (bristles) become brittle and the grain hard. For wheat, the glumes (husks) open slightly and grain is exposed. Plan your harvest labor and equipment ahead of time so you can act fast when the crop is ready. A delay of even a few days can mean significant loss.

2. Harvesting with Sickles, Knives, and Small Machines

Most smallholder farmers in Africa harvest wheat and barley by hand using a sickle or knife. A sickle is a curved blade with a short handle, used to cut the stems near the ground. A sharp knife or machete can also work, but a sickle is faster. To harvest, grab a handful of stems with one hand and cut with the other, about 10-15 cm above the ground. Leave the stubble; it helps protect the soil. Cut in the early morning or late afternoon when it's cooler and grain is less likely to shatter. Avoid cutting when wet with dew, as grain may mold. After cutting, lay the bundles in small heaps or tie them into sheaves. Sheaves should be left to dry in the field for a few days, turning them occasionally. If you have access to a small machine, like a reaper or a combine harvester, it can save time and labor. But these machines are expensive and may not be suitable for small, rocky, or terraced fields. For small machines, ensure they are properly adjusted: the reel speed, cutter bar, and concave clearance. A combine harvester both cuts and threshes, but it requires a large field and a lot of fuel. For most smallholders, manual harvesting is the norm. Organize labor groups or family members to work together. A person can harvest about 0.1 hectare per day by hand. That means for a one-hectare field, you need 10 person-days. Plan ahead. After cutting, gather sheaves and transport them to the threshing floor. Use a tarpaulin or clean floor to avoid contamination. Do not throw sheaves; handle gently to prevent grain loss. If using a machine, check for grain loss behind the machineadjust settings if too much grain is blown out. Also, keep safety in mind: wear gloves, long sleeves, and closed shoes to avoid cuts. Harvesting is hard work, but with the right tools and timing, you can maximize your yield.

3. Threshing and Winnowing: Traditional and Improved Methods

Threshing is the process of separating grain from the stalks and husks. Winnowing removes the chaff. Traditionally, farmers thresh by beating sheaves with sticks or by trampling with animals. This is labor-intensive and can damage grain. An improved method is to use a threshing machine, either manual or motorized. A manual thresher costs less and can be shared among farmers. Motorized threshers are faster but need fuel. For small quantities, you can thresh by rubbing the heads between your hands or using a wooden mallet. After threshing, you have a mix of grain, chaff, and broken straw. Winnowing is done by tossing the mixture into the air with a winnowing basket or fork, allowing the wind to blow away the lighter chaff. Do this on a windy day, preferably in the morning or evening. If there is no wind, use a fan or a mechanical winnower. An improved method is to use a tarpaulin and a fan. Spread the threshed grain on a tarpaulin, then use a standing fan to blow chaff away while you pour grain slowly from a height. This is more controlled and reduces grain loss. Another method: use a sieve to separate larger pieces. For best results, thresh when grain moisture is about 15-18%; if too dry, grain cracks; if too wet, it smears. After winnowing, you should have clean grain. But even then, small stones and dirt may remain. You can further clean by washing? No, never wash grain for storageit introduces moisture. Instead, use a sieve or hand-pick. Threshing and winnowing should be done on a clean, dry surface, not on bare ground, to avoid soil contamination. In many parts of Africa, farmers use a 'threshing floor' made of cow dung and mud, which is smooth and clean. But a tarpaulin is better. After winnowing, bag the grain and move to drying. Remember, proper threshing and winnowing reduce losses and improve market quality. A good thresher can recover over 95% of grain, while traditional methods may lose 10-20%. Invest in a tarpaulin and a fan; they are cheap and effective.

4. Drying Grain to Safe Moisture Levels

After threshing and winnowing, grain must be dried to a safe moisture content for storage. For wheat and barley, safe moisture is 12-13%. If grain is stored wet, it will heat up, mold, and become toxic. To dry, spread grain in a thin layer (5-10 cm) on a clean, dry surface like a tarpaulin, concrete floor, or raised platform. Avoid drying on bare ground; it picks up moisture and dirt. Dry in the sun, stirring the grain every hour with a rake or shovel. This ensures even drying. In hot, dry weather, grain may dry in 2-3 days. In humid conditions, it may take longer. If you have a moisture meter, test daily. If not, use the salt test: put a handful of grain in a dry bottle with a spoonful of salt, shake for a minute, and if salt sticks to the sides, grain is still too wet. Another test: bite a grain; if it shatters and is hard, it is dry. You can also use a glass jar: put grain in a jar, seal, and leave in the sun for an hour; if moisture forms on the glass, it's too wet. For large quantities, consider a mechanical dryer, but these are expensive. Solar dryers are a good intermediate option. They use a black-lined box with a glass or plastic cover and vents. They dry faster and protect from rain and dust. Never dry grain on the road or where animals roam. Also, do not dry on metal roofs as it can cook the grain. Once dry, allow grain to cool before storage. Store in a cool, dry place. If grain is too dry (below 10%), it can crack during handling, so aim for 12-13%. For barley, ideal moisture is 12%; for wheat, 13%. Proper drying prevents aflatoxin and insect infestation. It also improves milling quality and market price. Take drying seriously; it is the most important step after harvest.

5. Cleaning and Grading for Better Market Prices

Clean grain fetches a higher price. After drying, remove all foreign matter: stones, dirt, broken grains, weed seeds, and chaff. Use a sieve or a cleaning machine. A simple method is to use a set of sieves with different mesh sizes. First, a coarse sieve removes large debris like straw and stones. Then a finer sieve removes smaller seeds and sand. You can also use a fan or winnowing to blow away light chaff. For small quantities, hand-picking is possible but tedious. Grading means separating grain by size, color, and quality. Buyers pay more for uniform, bold grain. For wheat, grade by test weight (hectoliter weight) and protein content. For barley, grade by plumpness and color. In Africa, many markets have grades: Grade 1, Grade 2, etc. Grade 1 is clean, bold, and uniform. To grade, use a sieve with round holes (e.g., 2.5 mm for wheat). Grain that stays on top is larger and higher grade. Broken grains and shriveled ones fall through. You can also use a gravity separator or a spiral separator, but these are for large operations. For smallholders, simple sieving and hand sorting work. After cleaning and grading, package grain in clean bags. Do not mix different grades. Label the bags with grade and date. Store in a clean, dry place. Clean grain also stores better because insects and molds are less likely to attack. When selling, present samples to buyers. A clean, well-graded sample can increase price by 10-20%. Also, remove any discolored or moldy grains; they can lower the entire lot's value. If you have black point or fusarium, remove affected grains. For barley, keep the color uniform; discolored grain is rejected for malting. Remember, cleaning and grading take time but pay off. Invest in a set of sieves and a tarpaulin. Work on a clean surface. Wear a mask to avoid dust. After grading, store in hermetic bags or silos to maintain quality. Good presentation opens markets.

6. Storage: Hermetic Bags, Silos, and Pest Control

Proper storage protects grain from insects, rodents, moisture, and mold. The key is to keep grain dry and airtight. Hermetic bags are a great option for smallholders. These are airtight plastic bags that seal out oxygen. Insects die due to lack of oxygen. Brands like GrainPro and PICS are available in Africa. To use, fill the bag with dry grain (12-13% moisture), squeeze out air, and seal tightly. Store bags on pallets, not directly on the floor. Keep in a cool, dark room. Hermetic bags can store grain for 1-2 years without chemicals. Silos are another option. Metal silos are popular in East and Southern Africa. They come in various sizes (e.g., 500 kg, 1 ton, 5 tons). A silo keeps grain airtight and protects from pests. But they are more expensive. For community storage, a large silo can be shared. If using a silo, ensure grain is dry and clean before filling. Also, check for leaks. Traditional storage methods like mud silos or gourds are used but are less effective. For pest control, avoid chemical pesticides if possible. Instead, use hermetic storage, diatomaceous earth (DE), or botanicals like neem leaves. DE is a powder that kills insects by drying them out; mix 1 kg per 100 kg grain. Neem leaves can be layered in bags. For rodents, keep storage area clean and use traps. Cats can help. For larger infestations, you may need phosphine tablets, but these are dangerous; use only if trained. Always follow safety instructions. Inspect stored grain monthly for signs of insects, moisture, or mold. If you see weevils or moths, act quickly. Sun-dry the grain again if needed. Never store grain on the ground; use pallets. Also, keep storage area well-ventilated but not humid. In humid areas, use hermetic bags. For long-term storage, keep grain cool. Remember, a good storage system reduces post-harvest losses, which can be 20-30% in Africa. With proper storage, you can sell when prices are high. Invest in hermetic bags or a silo; it pays off.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Harvesting & Post-Harvest Handling of Wheat and Barley in Africa

For African smallholder and emerging commercial grain farmers, the difference between a profitable wheat or barley season and a break-even one is rarely decided in the field alone. It is decided in the seven to fourteen days after the crop reaches physiological maturity. Post-harvest losses for cereals in sub-Saharan Africa commonly run between 10% and 20%, and in poorly managed storage can exceed 30% (FAO, 2019). Every percentage point recovered is direct margin. This practical reference condenses the key decision points maturity, harvesting, threshing, drying, cleaning, grading, and storage into an operator's guide you can print, laminate, and keep in the store.

1. Signs of Maturity: Color, Hardness, and Moisture

Wheat and barley mature in stages: milk, dough, and hard dough. Harvest at hard dough, when the grain resists a thumbnail, the peduncle turns yellow-brown, and grain moisture falls to 1420%. Barley matures 510 days earlier than wheat and shatters more readily. Delaying harvest past 18% moisture increases shattering losses; harvesting above 20% forces expensive artificial drying. A cheap hand-held moisture meter (or the salt-and-bottle method) is the single highest-return tool a smallholder can own.

2. Harvesting with Sickles, Knives, and Small Machines

For 0.55 hectare plots, the traditional sickle remains the standard, cutting 1015 cm above ground to leave straw for livestock. Reaping hooks and serrated knives reduce grain shatter versus tearing. For 550 hectares, walk-behind reapers and small combine harvesters (e.g., Chinese 4LZ series, Indian mini-combines) cut labour from 40 person-days/ha to under 3. Contract hire is often more profitable than ownership model the numbers before buying.

3. Threshing and Winnowing: Traditional and Improved Methods

Traditional threshing beating with sticks or trampling by animals damages grain and mixes in soil. Improved options include pedal threshers (approx. 150300 kg/hr), motorised multi-crop threshers (5001,500 kg/hr), and axial-flow machines that reduce broken grain to under 3%. Winnowing by wind or with a fan separator removes chaff before drying, which shortens drying time and improves airflow.

4. Drying Grain to Safe Moisture Levels

Safe storage moisture is 13% for wheat and 12% for barley (FAO/INPhO). Sun drying on clean tarpaulins never bare ground is the default. Spread grain 35 cm deep and rake every 30 minutes. A simple moisture test: place grain in a sealed jar with dry salt; if salt sticks to the jar walls after 15 minutes, the grain is too wet. For humid zones, consider solar bubble dryers or small mechanical dryers.

5. Cleaning and Grading for Better Market Prices

Buyers pay premiums for clean, uniform grain. Remove stones, chaff, shrivelled kernels, and foreign matter to below 2% for Grade 1. Use sieves (2.02.5 mm for wheat), aspirators, or gravity separators. Grading by test weight (kg/hL) directly correlates with milling yield the key price driver for commercial millers.

6. Storage: Hermetic Bags, Silos, and Pest Control

Hermetic bags (e.g., GrainPro, PICS) create oxygen-depleted atmospheres that kill weevils and grain borers without chemicals a 100 kg bag costs roughly US$24 and protects grain for 1224 months. Metal silos (5002,000 kg) suit cooperatives. Always store on pallets, 10 cm off the floor and 50 cm from walls. Inspect monthly.

SAFETY WARNING Aflatoxin and Grain Dust

Never store or sell grain with visible mould, musty smell, or discoloured kernels. Mouldy wheat and barley can carry aflatoxins and mycotoxins that cause liver cancer and are not destroyed by cooking. Wear a dust mask and goggles during threshing and cleaning grain dust can cause chronic respiratory disease. Do not allow children or pregnant women to handle mouldy grain. If in doubt, discard or have grain tested at a national agricultural laboratory before sale.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm hard dough stage: thumbnail-resistant grain, yellow-brown peduncle.
  2. Measure moisture before harvest aim 1418% for threshing, 13% before storage.
  3. Cut 1015 cm above ground with a sharp sickle; avoid tearing heads.
  4. Thresh on a clean tarpaulin never on bare soil.
  5. Winnow and clean to below 2% foreign matter before drying.
  6. Dry on raised tarpaulins to 13% (wheat) or 12% (barley); test with salt-jar method.
  7. Grade by size and test weight; bag separately for premium buyers.
  8. Store in hermetic bags or metal silos on pallets; inspect monthly for insects and moisture.

Business bottom line: A 5-hectare wheat farmer producing 3 t/ha has 15 tonnes at harvest. Reducing losses from a typical 15% to 5% recovers 1.5 tonnes at US$300/tonne, that is US$450 of pure margin, often more than the cost of a moisture meter, tarpaulins, and hermetic bags combined. Treat post-harvest as a profit centre, not a chore.
