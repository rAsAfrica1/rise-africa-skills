Lesson 1.1~11 min

Energy Metabolism in Poultry

In this lesson
  • Explain what metabolisable energy means and why it drives feed intake
  • Calculate the metabolisable energy of a mixed ration from ingredient values
  • Compare ingredients on cost per unit of energy rather than cost per kilogram

Energy is not a nutrient you can hold in your hand. It is a property of the feed, released when the bird burns carbohydrate, fat and surplus protein. But it is the single most important number in your mix sheet, because a chicken eats mainly to meet its energy need. Raise the energy density of the feed and the bird eats less of it. Lower the energy density and the bird eats more, up to the physical limit of its crop and gizzard. Every other nutrient must be set relative to energy, or the bird will get the wrong amount of it.

The measure used in poultry is metabolisable energy, written ME, in kilocalories per kilogram of feed. It is the gross energy of the feed minus what is lost in the droppings. Some guides use megajoules; one megajoule is about 239 kilocalories. Aviagen's Ross 2022 specifications set broiler starter at 2,975 kcal/kg (12.4 MJ), grower at 3,050 kcal/kg (12.8 MJ) and finisher at 3,100 kcal/kg (13.0 MJ). Notice that energy rises as the bird ages, while protein falls. The older bird is laying down fat and finishing muscle, not building frame. NRC 1994 took a different approach and held all broiler phases at 3,200 kcal AMEn/kg, letting the protein-to-energy ratio shift instead. Either approach works; what fails is mixing targets from two systems without thinking.

Layers are set by intake rather than by phase. A white-egg layer eating 100 g a day needs a diet of roughly 2,800 kcal ME/kg. If heat or poor palatability drops her to 80 g a day, the diet has to carry about 3,000 kcal/kg to deliver the same daily energy. This is the whole logic of hot-climate formulation: when intake falls, concentration must rise.

Ingredients differ enormously. Published AMEn values are yellow maize about 3,350 kcal/kg, wheat about 2,800, soybean meal (48% CP) about 2,150, fish meal about 2,700-2,900, meat and bone meal 2,200-2,400, barley 2,600-2,700, oats 2,400-2,500, and canola meal 1,900-2,000. Maize is the cheapest reliable energy on most African markets and that is why it dominates.

Work an example. Take a grower mix of 60% maize, 30% soybean meal, 6% wheat, 2% fish meal and 2% limestone, salt and premix. Multiply each inclusion by its energy:

  1. Maize: 0.60 x 3,350 = 2,010 kcal
  2. Soybean meal: 0.30 x 2,150 = 645 kcal
  3. Wheat: 0.06 x 2,800 = 168 kcal
  4. Fish meal: 0.02 x 2,800 = 56 kcal
  5. Minerals and premix: 0.02 x 0 = 0 kcal

Total: 2,010 + 645 + 168 + 56 = 2,879 kcal/kg. Against a Ross grower target of 3,050 kcal/kg you are 171 kcal short. Swapping 5% of the soybean meal for maize gains 0.05 x (3,350 - 2,150) = 60 kcal, but costs you protein. In practice the gap is closed with added fat or oil, which carries far more energy per kilogram than any grain - use the figure your own supplier states, as oil energy is not fixed by the tables above.

Now compare ingredients on energy cost, not bag cost. Maize at $0.35/kg gives 3.35 Mcal/kg, so $0.35 / 3.35 = $0.104 per Mcal. Wheat at $0.40/kg gives 2.80 Mcal/kg, so $0.40 / 2.80 = $0.143 per Mcal. Wheat looks only 14% dearer per bag but is 37% dearer per unit of energy. That is the calculation most small producers never do.

A producer in Ibadan, Nigeria running 1,000 broilers switched part of his maize to cheaper sorghum during a maize price spike. Bag price fell, but because he did not adjust for the lower energy of the substitute grain, his birds ate more feed to reach the same weight and his cost per live bird rose. Energy density, not bag price, decides what a bird costs to grow. Module 10 deals with keeping that energy intact in storage - warm, damp grain loses value fast.

Protein and Amino Acid Requirements

In this lesson
  • Distinguish crude protein from digestible amino acid supply
  • State the Ross 2022 lysine and methionine targets by broiler phase
  • Calculate whether a mix meets its lysine target and cost the correction

Crude protein is the number printed on every feed bag, and it is the number that misleads most producers. Crude protein is simply measured nitrogen multiplied by 6.25. It tells you how much nitrogen is present, not whether the bird can use it. A bird does not need protein; it needs about twenty individual amino acids, and it needs them in the right proportions. Feed a diet with plenty of nitrogen but too little methionine and the bird will grow like a low-protein diet while you pay for high protein.

The standard reference points are worth memorising. NRC 1994 sets broiler crude protein at 23.0% for 0-3 weeks, 20.0% for 3-6 weeks and 18.0% from 6 weeks. Ross 2022 works instead on digestible amino acids - standardised ileal digestibility, or SID. Its lysine targets are 1.32% starter (0-10 d), 1.18% grower (11-24 d) and 1.08% finisher (25 d onward). Methionine is 0.55, 0.51 and 0.48%; methionine plus cysteine is 1.00, 0.92 and 0.86%; threonine is 0.88, 0.79 and 0.72%; arginine is 1.40, 1.27 and 1.17%.

For laying hens, NRC 1994 gives requirements as daily intakes. At a 100 g daily feed intake that is 15,000 mg crude protein (15% of the diet), 690 mg lysine (0.69%), 300 mg methionine (0.30%), 580 mg methionine plus cysteine (0.58%), 470 mg threonine and 160 mg tryptophan. Hy-Line Brown at peak, on 90 g a day, is set higher: 19.83% crude protein, 0.92% SID lysine, 0.83% SID methionine plus cystine. As intake rises and production falls, the concentration comes down - Hy-Line's Layer 5 phase at 110 g a day runs 16.23% protein, 0.75% lysine, 0.68% met+cys.

Lysine is the reference amino acid. Once you know your lysine level, the others are set as ratios to it, which is the ideal protein concept: match the profile the bird needs and any nitrogen above that is wasted money and extra ammonia in the house.

Work through a check. Soybean meal (48% CP) carries roughly 3.0% lysine and maize roughly 0.26%. A mix of 62% maize and 30% soybean meal gives:

  1. Maize lysine: 0.62 x 0.26 = 0.161%
  2. Soybean meal lysine: 0.30 x 3.0 = 0.900%
  3. Total from these two: 1.061% total lysine

Soybean meal lysine is roughly 85-95% digestible and maize lysine 70-80%, so digestible lysine is meaningfully lower than total - take the mid-points and you are near 0.90-0.95% SID. Against a Ross starter target of 1.32% you are far short; against a finisher target of 1.08% you are still short. Synthetic L-lysine HCl is the cheap fix. At about $2.20/kg and 78% lysine content, adding 0.15% of the mix supplies 0.15 x 0.78 = 0.117 percentage points of lysine for 0.0015 x $2,200 per tonne = $3.30 per tonne of feed. That is roughly $0.0033/kg of feed - trivial against the cost of feeding an extra week.

Age changes the arithmetic. Published work on broilers shows maintenance takes about 6% of total daily digestible amino acids at 10-21 days but around 22% at 32-43 days, with utilisation efficiency falling from roughly 71% to 62%. The older bird converts protein into meat far less efficiently, which is one reason finisher diets are cheaper and one reason long grow-outs cost you.

A cooperative in Kumasi, Ghana buying groundnut cake as its main protein found birds underperforming despite a 22% crude protein label. Groundnut cake is low in lysine and methionine relative to soybean. Adding 0.2% synthetic methionine and 0.15% lysine at a cost of about $6 per tonne closed most of the gap without changing the cake inclusion. The lesson repeats everywhere: buy amino acids, not nitrogen.

Vitamin Requirements and Functions

In this lesson
  • List the fat-soluble vitamins and the job each does in the bird
  • Compare NRC 1994 minimums with Ross 2022 performance levels
  • Cost a premix correction per kilogram of finished feed

Vitamins are the smallest line in your feed budget and the fastest way to wreck a flock. They are added as a premix at typically 2.5-5 kg per tonne, cost a few dollars per tonne of finished feed, and no ingredient you buy locally will supply them reliably. Skipping or stretching the premix is the most expensive saving in poultry.

Start with the fat-soluble group. Vitamin A maintains vision, the epithelial linings of gut and airway, and immune function. Ross 2022 specifies 13,000 IU/kg in starter, 11,000 in grower and 10,000 in finisher. NRC 1994 gives a range of only 1,500-5,000 IU/kg. That gap is not an error: NRC describes the level below which visible deficiency appears, while breeder specifications describe the level at which a modern high-yield bird performs and defends itself. For commercial birds, formulate to the breeder figure.

Vitamin D3 governs calcium and phosphorus absorption and bone mineralisation. Ross 2022 sets 5,000 IU/kg starter, 4,500 grower, 4,000 finisher, against an NRC 1994 range of 200-1,100 IU/kg. The metabolite 25-OH D3 is absorbed more efficiently than plain D3, especially in young birds, because it bypasses the liver hydroxylation step and raises circulating levels faster. On a weight basis it is roughly four to five times more biologically active, and recommended dosing when it partly or fully replaces D3 is 2,760-5,529 IU/kg. Across twelve published trials, broilers on 25-OH D3 showed an average 0.75% improvement in breast meat yield versus D3.

Vitamin E is the main fat-soluble antioxidant. Ross 2022 gives 80 IU/kg starter, 65 grower, 55 finisher; NRC 1994 lists just 5-25 IU/kg. Vitamin E and selenium work together in the glutathione peroxidase system, and the published pairing guidance is 10-20 IU of vitamin E per 0.3 mg of selenium. Combined supplementation beats either alone for antioxidant defence in heat stress, immune response, gut integrity and microbiota stability - which matters enormously in African houses running above 30C. Vitamin K, needed for blood clotting, bone mineralisation and epithelial integrity, is required at 0.5-1.75 mg/kg per NRC 1994.

The B group are water-soluble and act as cofactors. NRC 1994 ranges are riboflavin 1.8-4.0 mg/kg, niacin 10-70, pantothenic acid 2-16, biotin 0.04-0.15, pyridoxine 2.0-3.0, cobalamin 0.002-0.004 and folate 0.5-1.0 mg/kg. Choline, which supports lipid metabolism, runs 500-1,600 mg/kg in layers depending on phase and on how much methionine and betaine are available as alternative methyl donors.

Cost it out. A broiler premix at $2.50/kg included at 3 kg per tonne costs 3 x $2.50 = $7.50 per tonne, which is $0.0075 per kilogram of feed. On a feed costing $0.55/kg, that is 1.4% of your feed bill. A 500-bird broiler batch eating 3.8 kg each consumes 1,900 kg of feed, so the entire vitamin-mineral premix bill for the batch is 1.9 x $7.50 = $14.25. Losing ten birds to a preventable deficiency at $4.00 each is $40. The premix is never the place to economise.

Storage destroys vitamins faster than most producers expect. Vitamins A, D3 and E are degraded by heat, light, moisture and contact with trace mineral salts. A premix left in an open sack in a tin-roofed store at 35C is losing potency every week. Buy in quantities you will use within about a month, keep the bag sealed inside a lidded container, and store it off the floor and out of sunlight. Module 10 covers feed storage and quality control in detail.

A layer producer in Arusha, Tanzania with 400 hens saw pale yolks, ragged feathering and a slow drift down in production. Her premix supplier could not state the vitamin A level on the label. Moving to a supplier who printed the vitamin A, D3, E and selenium figures, and storing the bag in a sealed bucket rather than an open sack near the roof, restored feather cover within a month at a premix cost difference of under $0.01 per kilogram of feed.

Mineral Requirements and Balance

In this lesson
  • State calcium and available phosphorus targets by bird class
  • Explain why the calcium to phosphorus ratio matters more than either alone
  • Calculate the dietary electrolyte balance of a ration and adjust it for heat

Minerals fall into two groups: the macro minerals you weigh in kilograms per tonne - calcium, phosphorus, sodium, chloride, potassium - and the trace minerals you weigh in grams. Both groups fail in the same way. It is rarely a shortage of one mineral that causes trouble; it is the balance between them.

Calcium and phosphorus are the pair to get right first. Ross 2022 gives broiler total calcium of 0.95% starter, 0.75% grower and 0.65% finisher, with available phosphorus of 0.50, 0.42 and 0.36% - ratios of 1.9:1, 1.8:1 and 1.8:1. The optimal ratio for growing poultry is about 2:1, and ratios below 1.5:1 or above 2.5:1 increase skeletal problems and nutrient malabsorption. The first ten days are the danger zone. Calcium above 1.0% in starter interferes with phosphorus metabolism and predisposes birds to leg problems including tibial dyschondroplasia and angular limb deformity, so starter calcium should sit at 0.85-0.95% and rise only gradually. Producers who tip extra limestone into a broiler starter 'to build strong bones' are causing the very lameness they fear.

Layers are a different animal entirely. Hy-Line Brown at peak needs about 4.0 g of calcium per bird per day, giving a dietary concentration of 3.8-4.1% depending on feed intake, with a calcium to phosphorus ratio of 8.4-11.0:1. In the decline phase from about week 77, daily calcium rises to 4.7 g with a concentration of 3.92-4.70%. Lohmann Brown-Classic follows the same shape: 0.90-1.00% calcium in the 1-3 week starter, 0.90% in the 9-17 week developer, 2.00-2.50% in the pre-layer phase, then 3.8-4.2% in layer phase 1. Particle size matters as much as amount, and Lesson 5 of Module 3 works through it.

Sodium and chloride are small numbers with big consequences. Ross 2022 holds both at 0.18-0.23% across all phases; NRC 1994 gives sodium about 0.15% and chlorine 0.12-0.15%. Above roughly 0.5% of either, feed intake and growth fall, water intake climbs, litter goes wet and eggshells suffer.

This is where dietary electrolyte balance, or DEB, becomes the most useful formula in a hot country. DEB in mEq/kg = [Na% x 435] + [K% x 256] - [Cl% x 282]. Target 200-250 mEq/kg in normal or cool conditions, 250-300 under mild heat stress at 28-32C, 300-350 under moderate stress at 32-35C, and 350-400 when it is above 35C.

Work one. A diet with 0.20% Na, 0.85% K and 0.25% Cl gives:

  1. Sodium: 0.20 x 435 = 87
  2. Potassium: 0.85 x 256 = 217.6
  3. Chloride: 0.25 x 282 = 70.5
  4. DEB: 87 + 217.6 - 70.5 = 234.1 mEq/kg

That is fine for a cool house but low for a 33C afternoon. Replacing part of the salt with sodium bicarbonate to reach 0.24% Na while dropping chloride to 0.18% gives (0.24 x 435) + 217.6 - (0.18 x 282) = 104.4 + 217.6 - 50.8 = 271.2 mEq/kg, moving you into the mild-to-moderate heat stress band. The mechanism is straightforward: excess chloride pushes blood towards acidosis, birds pant harder to compensate, growth falls and mortality rises. Raising DEB restores alkalinity, makes panting effective, holds feed intake and improves litter.

Trace minerals from Ross 2022 are zinc 120 mg/kg, copper 16, manganese 120 and selenium 0.30 mg/kg, with iodine around 0.30 and iron typically 40-60. NRC 1994 minimums are far lower - zinc 35-45, copper 4-8, manganese 20-60, selenium 0.06-0.2. Interactions matter: zinc above 150 mg/kg antagonises copper, iron and manganese absorption, and monensin may suppress copper absorption. Phytase at 500-1,500 FTU/kg improves calcium and phosphorus digestibility markedly, most strongly in birds of 2-14 days.

A broiler unit outside Lusaka, Zambia had 6% of birds lame by day 30. Their starter carried 1.3% calcium from over-generous limestone against 0.45% available phosphorus - a ratio of 2.9:1. Cutting limestone to bring calcium to 0.95% cost nothing and lameness fell within two cycles.

Water Requirements and Quality

In this lesson
  • State the published limits for the main drinking water quality parameters
  • Diagnose the common water faults on a small African poultry unit
  • Cost a water quality correction against the production it protects

Water is the cheapest input on your farm and the one most often responsible for poor performance. Birds drink far more than they eat, and every gram of feed intake depends on adequate water. If water is short, dirty, hot or badly distributed, no amount of feed formulation will rescue the flock. Yet almost no small producer ever tests it.

The published standards for broiler drinking water are specific. Total dissolved solids should be under 1,500 ppm and become a concern above 3,000 ppm. pH should sit between 6.5 and 7.5, with concern below 5.5 or above 8.5. Hardness as calcium carbonate should be 50-200 ppm and is a concern above 400 ppm. Chloride should be under 250 ppm, concern above 1,000. Iron should be under 0.3 ppm, concern above 1.0 ppm. Sulfates should be under 250 ppm, concern above 1,000. Microbial load should be under 1,000 CFU/mL and is a serious concern above 10,000.

Each of these fails in a recognisable way. High iron leaves orange-brown staining in drinker lines and troughs, feeds bacterial slime and blocks nipples. High sulfates loosen droppings and give wet litter that then drives hock burn and coccidiosis pressure. Very hard water scales up nipple drinkers and reacts with any medication or vitamin you try to dose through the line, which is why a dosed vitamin can simply fail to reach the bird. A pH outside 6.5-7.5 reduces the effectiveness of chlorination and of organic acid dosing. High microbial counts usually mean an open tank, a dirty header, or biofilm inside the line that has never been flushed.

Water and salt are linked. As Lesson 4 explained, sodium or chloride above about 0.5% of the diet drives water consumption up and litter quality down. If your litter is wet, check salt inclusion before you blame the birds. In heat, the picture reverses: birds drink much more and eat much less, which is exactly why hot-weather diets must be more concentrated and why dietary electrolyte balance is raised into the 300-400 mEq/kg range.

Drinker space is the fault nobody measures. A common practical shortfall on small farms is too few drinkers for the bird number, so subordinate birds simply do not get their turn, and flock uniformity collapses. Count your drinkers, count your birds, and watch at the hottest hour of the day - if there is a queue, you have found a limit on performance that no feed change will fix.

Cost the correction. Suppose you keep 500 broilers and your water sits at pH 8.6 with visible iron staining. A simple filter and a small dosing pump might cost $120 to $180 second-hand on many African markets, spread over perhaps ten cycles - roughly $15 per cycle, or $0.03 per bird. If poor water is costing you even 3% mortality, that is 15 birds at $4.00 each, or $60 per cycle in lost sales alone. The filter pays for itself in one to two cycles.

A cheaper first step costs nothing: flush the lines. Take the end cap off the drinker line, open the flow fully and run it until the water comes out clear. Do this weekly. Between flocks, clean the header tank and scrub the biofilm out - it is the slippery film you can feel with a finger, and it shelters bacteria from any disinfectant you add afterwards.

A producer in Kisumu, Kenya drawing water from a shallow borehole had chronic wet litter and repeated coccidiosis outbreaks despite an in-feed coccidiostat. pH strips from a pool supply shop read 8.7, the drinkers were coated orange, and the header tank had no lid. Fitting a lid, scrubbing the tank, flushing the lines weekly and adding a simple sediment filter cut mortality and stopped the recurring litter problem without any feed change at all. Module 10 covers water sanitation as part of the broader biosecurity and feed hygiene picture; here the point is narrower - measure your water before you re-formulate your feed.

Feed Ingredient Characteristics

In this lesson
  • Rank locally available ingredients by energy and protein contribution
  • Apply the published safe inclusion ceilings for cottonseed and rapeseed meals
  • Calculate the cost of a substitution before making it

Every feed you will ever mix is assembled from three functional groups: energy sources, protein sources, and the small additions that supply minerals, vitamins and enzymes. Knowing what each ingredient actually contributes - and what it costs you in hidden problems - is what separates a producer who survives a maize price spike from one who does not.

On energy, the published AMEn values are the starting point. Yellow maize sits at about 3,350 kcal/kg with low variability, around 88% digestible, and is the reference against which everything else is judged. Wheat comes in near 2,800 kcal/kg with moderate variability of about plus or minus 150, and winter wheat at 2,750-2,850 is lower than spring wheat because arabinoxylans reduce available energy. Barley is 2,600-2,700 kcal/kg at about 73% digestible, held back by beta-glucans. Oats are 2,400-2,500 at 67% digestible with high variability, being high in fibre.

On protein, soybean meal at 48% crude protein carries about 2,150 kcal/kg and roughly 75% digestible protein, with the best amino acid profile of the plant proteins - lysine standardised ileal digestibility of 85-95%, methionine plus cysteine 80-90%, threonine 75-85%. Fish meal at 60% crude protein gives 2,700-2,900 kcal/kg with 75-80% digestibility and the highest amino acid digestibility of all - lysine 90-98%. Meat and bone meal runs 2,200-2,400 kcal/kg at about 70% digestible but is highly variable, plus or minus 200, because processing and fat content differ between batches. Canola meal at 1,900-2,000 kcal/kg is 65-70% digestible and traditional rapeseed meal is lower still at 1,800-1,900.

Two ingredients carry hard ceilings you must respect. Cottonseed meal contains gossypol, a polyphenol that is reproductively toxic, enlarges the liver, suppresses immunity and depresses growth. Keep finished broiler diets below 0.1% gossypol, which in practice means limiting cottonseed meal to 5-10% of the diet depending on the gossypol content of the particular meal. Broilers are more sensitive than layers, and chicks more than older birds. Rapeseed and canola meals contain glucosinolates, which are goitrogenic - they enlarge the thyroid, reduce palatability and depress growth. Modern low-glucosinolate canola meal can go in at 5-15% of the diet; traditional high-glucosinolate rapeseed meal is limited to 2-5%. Ask which one you are actually buying.

Phytic acid is the invisible tax on plant ingredients. It binds calcium, phosphorus, zinc, iron, copper and manganese, cutting phosphorus bioavailability by 20-50%, zinc by 30-60%, and calcium, iron and copper by 15-40%. Maize carries about 0.7-1.0% phytic acid, soybean meal 1.0-1.3%, barley 0.8-1.2%, while fish meal has under 0.1%. Phytase at 500-1,500 FTU/kg degrades it and is most effective in birds of 2-14 days. Moderate heat treatment cuts phytic acid by about 20-30%, but excessive heat damages amino acids.

Work a substitution before you make it. Suppose maize jumps from $0.35 to $0.50/kg and sorghum is offered at $0.38/kg. Do not compare bag prices. Compare energy: at $0.50/kg, maize costs $0.50 / 3.35 = $0.149 per Mcal. To beat that, sorghum must deliver its energy for less - and unless your supplier can state the energy value for that batch, you are guessing. If you cut maize from 60% to 45% and add 15% of a lower-energy grain at, say, 3,000 kcal/kg, your mix loses 0.15 x (3,350 - 3,000) = 52.5 kcal/kg. Birds compensate by eating more, so your saving on the bag is partly cancelled by higher intake. Always run that arithmetic.

A maize miller turned feed producer in Ndola, Zambia built his least-cost mix around locally available sunflower cake because it was $0.22/kg against soybean meal at $0.72/kg. Sunflower cake is high in fibre and lower in lysine, and at 25% inclusion his birds slowed noticeably. Cutting it back to 12% and adding 0.15% synthetic lysine restored growth while still saving money against a full soybean mix. Local ingredients are an opportunity, but only inside a ceiling. Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A chicken given a lower-energy feed will usually do what?

Birds eat largely to satisfy energy demand. Diluting energy raises intake until the bird runs out of gut capacity, which is why very low-energy diets cap growth.

2. Ross 2022 broiler finisher metabolisable energy is:

Ross 2022 sets 2,975 for starter (0-10 d), 3,050 for grower (11-24 d) and 3,100 kcal/kg for finisher (25 d onward).

3. Maize at $0.35/kg (3,350 kcal/kg) versus wheat at $0.40/kg (2,800 kcal/kg): which is cheaper energy?

$0.35 divided by 3.35 Mcal equals $0.104 per Mcal for maize; $0.40 divided by 2.80 Mcal equals $0.143 for wheat. Maize is markedly cheaper per unit of usable energy.

4. Why does a layer eating only 80 g per day need a more concentrated diet?

Requirements are daily, not per kilogram of feed. When intake falls, the concentration of energy and every other nutrient must rise to deliver the same daily amount.

5. What does metabolisable energy subtract from gross energy?

ME is gross energy minus the energy excreted. It is the practical figure used in poultry formulation because it reflects what the bird can actually use.

6. Crude protein on a feed label is calculated from:

Crude protein is a nitrogen proxy. It says nothing about which amino acids are present or whether the bird can digest them.

7. Ross 2022 digestible lysine for broiler grower (11-24 days) is:

Ross 2022 SID lysine runs 1.32% starter, 1.18% grower and 1.08% finisher.

8. Maintenance takes what share of a broiler's daily amino acid intake at 32-43 days?

Published data give roughly 6% at 10-21 days rising to about 22% at 32-43 days, with utilisation efficiency falling from around 71% to 62%.

9. The 'ideal protein' concept means:

Formulating to the ideal profile means nitrogen above the amino acid requirement is economic loss and adds to nitrogen excretion.

10. Why is groundnut cake often a weaker protein source than soybean meal for poultry?

Its amino acid profile is less balanced for poultry, so a matching crude protein figure does not deliver matching performance without synthetic amino acid top-up.

11. Why is Ross 2022 vitamin A far above the NRC 1994 range?

The two documents answer different questions. NRC 1994 defines the point below which deficiency signs appear; breeder specifications define what a high-yield modern bird needs to perform.

12. 25-OH vitamin D3 is absorbed more efficiently than D3 because it:

It skips hepatic 25-hydroxylation, so circulating 25-OH D3 rises faster - an advantage that is largest in very young birds.

13. Ross 2022 vitamin E for broiler starter is:

Ross 2022 gives 80 IU/kg starter, 65 grower and 55 finisher, well above the NRC 1994 range of 5-25 IU/kg.

14. A premix costing $2.50/kg included at 3 kg per tonne costs how much per tonne of feed?

3 kg x $2.50 = $7.50 per tonne, or $0.0075 per kilogram of feed - a small fraction of total feed cost.

15. What most rapidly destroys vitamin potency in a stored premix?

Fat-soluble vitamins in particular degrade with heat, sunlight, moisture and mineral contact, so buy in quantities used within about a month and keep them sealed and shaded.

16. The optimal calcium to available phosphorus ratio for growing poultry is about:

About 2:1 is optimal for growing birds. Ratios below 1.5:1 or above 2.5:1 increase skeletal problems and malabsorption.

17. Adding extra limestone to a broiler starter typically causes:

Calcium above 1.0% in the 0-10 day phase disrupts phosphorus metabolism and predisposes birds to tibial dyschondroplasia and angular limb deformity.

18. A diet with 0.20% Na, 0.85% K and 0.25% Cl has a DEB of:

(0.20 x 435) + (0.85 x 256) - (0.25 x 282) = 87 + 217.6 - 70.5 = 234.1 mEq/kg, which is adequate in cool conditions but low above 32C.

19. Why does excess dietary chloride hurt birds in heat?

High chloride lowers blood pH. Birds then pant excessively to compensate, which costs growth and raises mortality; raising DEB restores alkalinity and effective heat loss.

20. Hy-Line Brown layers at peak need roughly how much calcium per bird per day?

About 4.0 g/bird/day at peak, rising to about 4.7 g/day from week 77 as shell deposition efficiency falls.

21. The recommended pH range for broiler drinking water is:

6.5-7.5 is recommended, with concern below 5.5 or above 8.5. Outside that range chlorination and acid dosing lose effectiveness.

22. Orange-brown staining in drinker lines most likely indicates:

Iron above about 0.3 ppm stains lines, feeds bacterial slime and blocks nipples. Above 1.0 ppm it is a clear problem.

23. Persistent wet litter with no disease signs should first prompt you to check:

Sodium or chloride above roughly 0.5% of the diet raises water intake and wets litter, and high sulfates loosen droppings. Both are cheap to check.

24. Microbial load in drinking water becomes a serious concern above:

Under 1,000 CFU/mL is the recommendation and above 10,000 CFU/mL is the concern level, usually caused by biofilm or an open tank.

25. What is the zero-cost first step for a farm with suspect water?

Flushing lines until the water runs clear and covering and scrubbing the tank removes biofilm and sediment, and costs nothing but time.

26. Cottonseed meal is limited in poultry diets because of:

Gossypol should stay below 0.1% of the finished diet, which usually means 5-10% cottonseed meal inclusion depending on the meal's gossypol content.

27. Low-glucosinolate canola meal can generally be included at:

Modern low-glucosinolate canola meal is usable at 5-15%, while traditional high-glucosinolate rapeseed meal must be held to 2-5% because of goitrogenic effects.

28. Phytic acid reduces phosphorus bioavailability by roughly:

Phytate chelates phosphorus and cuts availability by 20-50%, with zinc hit hardest at 30-60%. Phytase supplementation releases much of it.

29. Which ingredient has the lowest phytic acid content?

Fish meal contains under 0.1% phytic acid, compared with 0.7-1.0% in maize, 1.0-1.3% in soybean meal and 0.8-1.2% in barley.

30. Replacing 15% maize (3,350 kcal/kg) with a grain at 3,000 kcal/kg changes mix energy by:

0.15 x (3,350 - 3,000) = 52.5 kcal/kg lost. Birds eat more to compensate, cancelling part of the apparent saving on the bag price.
