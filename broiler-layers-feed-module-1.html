1. Overview of the Poultry Feed Industry

The poultry feed industry in Africa is growing fast. In Nigeria alone, over 180 million birds are raised each year, and feed accounts for 60-70% of the total cost of production. Most small-scale farmers mix their own feed using maize, soybean meal, and fishmeal, but commercial feed mills produce pelleted feeds with consistent quality. Key players include companies like Chikun, Vital, and Amo Byng in Nigeria, and similar feed manufacturers in Kenya, South Africa, and Ghana. The industry faces challenges: high cost of imported ingredients like soybean meal and vitamins, poor storage leading to aflatoxin contamination, and lack of quality control. For a new entrepreneur, starting a small feed mixing business can be profitable if you source local ingredients like maize, cassava, and groundnut cake. You need a hammer mill, a mixer, and a weighing scale. A typical 50 kg bag of broiler starter feed sells for about 18,000 to 22,000 Naira (2024 prices). To compete, focus on consistent quality and sell directly to farmers. The demand for poultry products is rising due to population growth and urbanisation, so feed production is a viable business. However, you must understand nutrient requirements, ingredient quality, and regulations. This module will equip you with the basics to formulate feed for broilers and layers, whether for your own farm or as a business.

2. Basic Anatomy and Digestive Physiology of Broilers and Layers

Birds have a unique digestive system. Unlike mammals, they have a beak for pecking, a crop for storing feed, a proventriculus (glandular stomach) and a gizzard (muscular stomach) that grinds feed with grit. The small intestine absorbs nutrients, and the ceca ferment fibre. The cloaca expels waste. Broilers and layers have similar anatomy, but layers have a longer digestive tract to handle more fibre. Feed passes through the gut in about 2-4 hours. To optimise digestion, feed particles should be 0.5-1.5 mm for mash, or pelleted for better intake. Grit helps grind grains. Enzymes like phytase break down phytic acid in maize and soybean, releasing phosphorus. Without phytase, birds need inorganic phosphorus, which is expensive. In Africa, many farmers use grit from crushed granite. The pH of the gut is acidic in the proventriculus (pH 2-3) and neutral in the intestines. Understanding this helps you choose ingredients: for example, high-fibre ingredients like rice bran are better for layers than broilers. Also, birds lack teeth, so feed texture matters. A good feed should be palatable and free of mould. If you see undigested feed in droppings, adjust particle size or add enzymes. This knowledge helps you avoid waste and improve feed conversion ratio (FCR). A broiler should convert 1.6-1.8 kg of feed into 1 kg of meat, while layers need 1.8-2.2 kg feed per kg eggs. Poor digestion increases FCR, raising costs.

3. Nutrient Requirements: Energy, Protein, Amino Acids, Vitamins, Minerals

Poultry need six nutrient classes: water, carbohydrates, fats, proteins, vitamins, and minerals. Energy is measured in metabolisable energy (ME) broilers need 3000-3200 kcal/kg, layers 2700-2900 kcal/kg. Maize provides about 3350 kcal/kg. Protein is needed for muscle and egg production. Broiler starter needs 22-24% crude protein, finisher 18-20%; layers need 16-18%. But protein quality matters: amino acids like lysine and methionine are critical. Maize is low in lysine, so you must add soybean meal or fishmeal. A typical broiler starter may need 1.2% lysine and 0.5% methionine. Vitamins and minerals are needed in small amounts. Calcium for layers: 3.5-4.0% for strong eggshells; broilers need 0.9-1.0%. Phosphorus: 0.45% available phosphorus. Salt (sodium chloride) at 0.25-0.5%. Trace minerals like zinc, iron, copper, and manganese are added via premixes. In Africa, many farmers use limestone or oyster shells for calcium. Vitamin premixes are imported but essential. Without them, birds suffer deficiency diseases like rickets or curled toe paralysis. You can use local ingredients: maize, sorghum, millet for energy; soybean, groundnut cake, sunflower cake for protein; fishmeal for amino acids; and palm oil for energy. Always test ingredients for nutrient content because local varieties vary. For example, maize from different regions may have 8-10% protein. Use a feed formulation software like WinFeed or Excel to balance nutrients. This ensures you meet requirements without overfeeding, which wastes money.

4. Feed Ingredients: Classification and Nutritional Characteristics

Feed ingredients are grouped into energy sources, protein sources, minerals, vitamins, and additives. Energy sources include maize (3350 kcal/kg), sorghum (3200 kcal/kg), cassava chips (3000 kcal/kg), and wheat bran (1300 kcal/kg). Maize is king but costly; you can replace up to 30% with cassava in broiler diets if you add methionine. Protein sources: soybean meal (44-48% CP), groundnut cake (40-45% CP), fishmeal (55-65% CP), and cottonseed cake (32-38% CP) but cottonseed has gossypol, toxic to layers. Use only 5-10% in broiler feed. Animal by-products like blood meal (80% CP) and meat meal (50% CP) are good but check for salmonella. Minerals: limestone (38% calcium), oyster shell (36% calcium), dicalcium phosphate (18% phosphorus), and bone meal (12% phosphorus). Salt provides sodium and chloride. Vitamins and trace minerals come as premixes. Additives include enzymes (phytase, xylanase), antioxidants, and coccidiostats. In Africa, locally available ingredients include palm kernel cake (14-18% CP), brewers dried grains (20-25% CP), and moringa leaves (25-30% CP). However, high fibre limits their use in broiler feed. Always analyse ingredients for moisture (should be below 12%), protein, and aflatoxin. Aflatoxin above 20 ppb is dangerous. Store ingredients in dry, ventilated rooms on pallets. Buy from reputable suppliers and keep records. A simple rule: energy sources are grains, protein sources are cakes and meals, and minerals are rocks and shells. Balance them to meet nutrient needs.

5. Feed Formulation Concepts and Objectives

Feed formulation is the process of mixing ingredients to meet the nutrient requirements of birds at the lowest cost. The main objectives are: 1) meet nutrient needs for health and productivity, 2) minimise cost, 3) use locally available ingredients, and 4) ensure feed is palatable and safe. You start with a nutrient specification table: for broiler starter, ME 3000 kcal/kg, CP 22%, lysine 1.2%, methionine 0.5%, calcium 1.0%, available phosphorus 0.45%. Then list available ingredients with their nutrient composition and cost. Use the Pearson Square method for simple two-ingredient mixes, or linear programming software for complex mixes. For example, to make a 100 kg broiler starter with maize and soybean meal, you can use Pearson Square to find the ratio of maize (8.5% CP) and soybean meal (44% CP) to get 22% CP. But you also need to add minerals, vitamins, and amino acids. A typical formula: maize 55%, soybean meal 30%, fishmeal 5%, wheat bran 5%, limestone 1%, dicalcium phosphate 1.5%, premix 0.5%, salt 0.3%, methionine 0.2%, lysine 0.1%, and oil 1.4%. Always check that the final mix meets all nutrient requirements. Use a computer or smartphone app like Feed Formulation (available on Google Play) to save time. The objective is not just least cost but also least cost per kg of meat or eggs. For layers, you might use more limestone and less protein. Remember, feed formulation is dynamic: ingredient prices change, so adjust formulas regularly. Keep records of your formulas and results. Test your feed on a small batch of birds before scaling up. This reduces risk and ensures you learn from mistakes.

6. Regulatory and Safety Considerations in Feed Production

Feed safety is critical. In Africa, many countries have feed regulations: Nigeria's NAFDAC and SON, Kenya's KEBS, and South Africa's NRCS. They set standards for maximum levels of toxins, heavy metals, and pathogens. Aflatoxin is the biggest threat: it causes liver damage and reduces egg production. Maize and groundnut cake are often contaminated. Test with aflatoxin test kits (e.g., ELISA) or send samples to labs. Safe level is below 20 ppb for poultry. Salmonella and E. coli can be controlled by proper storage and heat treatment (pelleting kills many pathogens). Avoid using mouldy ingredients. Also, some ingredients are banned: e.g., meat and bone meal from ruminants to prevent BSE. Antibiotics as growth promoters are banned in many countries; use alternatives like probiotics and organic acids. Label your feed with nutrient content, date, and batch number. Keep records for traceability. For a small business, register with the relevant authority if you sell feed commercially. Even for home mixing, follow good manufacturing practices: clean equipment, use gloves and masks, and store feed in dry, cool places. Train workers on hygiene. In Kenya, the Feed Act requires that feed be free from harmful substances. In Nigeria, NAFDAC registration is needed for commercial feed. Non-compliance can lead to fines or closure. Also, consider environmental safety: dust from mixing can cause respiratory problems. Use a dust mask. Dispose of waste properly. By following regulations, you protect your birds, your customers, and your business reputation. Safe feed leads to healthy birds and better profits.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Formulating Broiler and Layer Diets in African Feed Mills



This practical reference lesson consolidates the six lessons in the module and translates them into a working reference for feed millers, farm managers, and agripreneurs in Africa. The emphasis is on decisions that affect cost per kilogram of live weight or per egg, because feed represents 6575% of total poultry production cost in most African markets (FAO, 2013). A formulator who understands anatomy, nutrient requirements, ingredient behaviour, formulation logic, and regulation can reduce cost without sacrificing performance.





1. From Anatomy to Formulation Logic


The chicken has no teeth and a very short digestive tract relative to body size. Feed passes quickly, so ingredients must be highly digestible and ground to an appropriate particle size. The gizzard works best with coarse particles, while the small intestine absorbs most amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals. This is why maize and soybean meal dominate African poultry diets: they are energy-dense and protein-rich, and their nutrients are readily available to the bird.


Practical implication for a formulator in Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, or South Africa: if you substitute a fibrous by-product such as wheat bran or palm kernel cake, you must compensate for lower metabolisable energy (ME) and reduced amino acid digestibility. Otherwise, feed intake rises, feed conversion ratio (FCR) worsens, and cost per kilogram of meat or per egg increases.

3. Ingredient Classification and African Sourcing


Feed ingredients fall into energy sources, protein sources, mineral sources, vitamin premixes, and additives. In Africa, maize and soybean meal are the default energy and protein sources, but price volatility and local availability often force substitution. Cassava, sorghum, millet, and wheat bran can replace part of the maize; groundnut cake, sunflower cake, cottonseed cake, fishmeal, and palm kernel cake can replace part of the soybean meal. 4. Formulation Concepts in Practice


Formulation is a least-cost optimisation problem. The objective is to meet nutrient requirements at the lowest cost per kilogram of feed, subject to ingredient availability, regulatory limits, and physical constraints. A practical African formulation workflow looks like this:



  1. Define the target: broiler starter, broiler finisher, layer grower, layer layer, or breeder.

  2. Set nutrient constraints: ME, crude protein, digestible lysine, methionine + cystine, calcium, available phosphorus, and minimum/maximum inclusion levels.

  3. Select ingredients: energy sources, protein sources, minerals, vitamins, and additives.

  4. Run least-cost software or a spreadsheet solver: linear programming finds the cheapest mix that meets all constraints.

  5. Check physical quality: pelletability, flowability, mixing uniformity, and feed form.

  6. Validate on farm: measure feed intake, FCR, egg production, and mortality.


Business-first insight: a 1% reduction in feed cost per tonne can lift net margin by 35% on a broiler farm, because feed is the largest variable cost. In layer operations, a 1% improvement in FCR or a 2% increase in lay persistency can be the difference between profit and loss.



5. Regulatory and Safety Considerations


Feed safety is both a legal and commercial issue. In many African countries, feed is regulated under national standards or veterinary drug residue rules. Aflatoxin contamination in maize and groundnut cake is the most common risk. Salmonella and other pathogens can enter through animal protein by-products. Drug residues can arise from medicated feed if withdrawal periods are ignored. Feed mills that implement HACCP-based controls, ingredient testing, and traceability are better positioned to supply commercial farms and export markets.




SAFETY WARNING AFLATOXIN AND FEED CONTAMINATION

Never use mouldy, discoloured, or damp maize, groundnut cake, or other ingredients in poultry feed. Aflatoxin B1 is a potent liver carcinogen and causes reduced growth, immunosuppression, and mortality in broilers and layers. In tropical African storage conditions, aflatoxin can exceed 20 ppb (the common regulatory limit for poultry feed) within days. Test every incoming batch, store ingredients below 12% moisture, use mould inhibitors where appropriate, and reject any batch above the legal limit. Do not dilute contaminated ingredients into feed this spreads the risk and may be illegal.




6. Quick Reference Checklist



  • Confirm the bird type, age, and production stage before selecting a diet specification.

  • Test incoming maize and oilseed cakes for moisture, aflatoxin, and protein content.

  • Use digestible amino acid values, not just crude protein, when formulating.

  • Check calcium and available phosphorus ratios, especially for layer diets.

  • Match feed form to age: mash or crumble for chicks, pellets for growers and layers.

  • Record feed intake, FCR, egg production, and mortality weekly to validate formulation.

  • Keep a batch record of ingredient sources, formulations, and mixing times for traceability.

  • Review least-cost formulations monthly as ingredient prices and availability change.
