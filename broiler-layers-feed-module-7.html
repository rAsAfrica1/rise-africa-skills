Lesson 7.1~12 min

Temperature Management and Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Calculate dietary electrolyte balance and adjust it for the heat band your house reaches
  • Explain why panting efficiency depends on blood pH and dietary chloride
  • Reformulate a hot-weather ration to protect intake without over-diluting nutrients

In most of Africa the limiting factor on poultry performance is not feed quality. It is the afternoon. A bird that stops eating between noon and four o'clock is a bird that will not hit target weight, whatever is in the bag. Feeding for heat is therefore a housing subject as much as a nutrition subject, and the two must be handled together.

Start with the physiology. A chicken cannot sweat. It loses heat by panting, and panting blows off carbon dioxide, which shifts blood chemistry. Dietary chloride makes this worse: excess chloride raises blood acidity and lowers pH, and a bird already fighting metabolic acidosis pants harder and less effectively, grows more slowly and dies more often. Raise the blood's alkaline reserve and panting works properly again.

That is what dietary electrolyte balance measures. The formula is DEB in mEq/kg = [Na% x 435] + [K% x 256] - [Cl% x 282]. Work an example. A ration with 0.20% sodium, 0.65% potassium and 0.22% chloride gives (0.20 x 435) + (0.65 x 256) - (0.22 x 282) = 87 + 166.4 - 62.0 = 191 mEq/kg. That is below the 200-250 mEq/kg band recommended for normal or cool conditions, and far below what a hot house needs.

The published targets are banded by temperature, and you should treat them as an operating instruction:

  1. Normal or cool environment: DEB 200-250 mEq/kg, standard formulation.
  2. Mild heat stress, 28-32 degrees: DEB 250-300 mEq/kg. Increase sodium, reduce chloride.
  3. Moderate heat stress, 32-35 degrees: DEB 300-350 mEq/kg. Further sodium increase, further chloride reduction.
  4. Severe heat stress, above 35 degrees: DEB 350-400 mEq/kg. Maximise sodium and potassium, minimise chloride.

How do you move it? Not by adding more common salt, because sodium chloride raises sodium and chloride together and barely changes DEB. The published guidance is explicit: sodium bicarbonate with reduced dietary chloride gives the maximum DEB increase; potassium chloride as a partial potassium source gives a balanced elevation; sodium chloride alone gives minimal change. In practice, replace part of your salt with sodium bicarbonate. Take the example ration above and swap 0.15 kg of the 0.35 kg of salt per 100 kg for sodium bicarbonate. Sodium stays similar, chloride falls by roughly 0.09%, and DEB rises by about 0.09 x 282 = 25 mEq/kg. Do it again with potassium and you are inside the mild heat-stress band.

The results are practical, not theoretical: better regulated water consumption, reduced wet litter, maintained feed intake under heat stress, and improved feed conversion and growth in difficult environments. Note the apparent contradiction - a correctly raised DEB with the right sodium-to-potassium ratio can actually lower water consumption while improving heat tolerance, because the bird is no longer drinking to compensate for acidosis.

Nutrient concentration is the second lever. When a bird eats 15% less feed in a heat wave, it receives 15% less of everything. The correct response is to raise the concentration of the limiting nutrients rather than to feed the same diet and hope. Published guidance notes that feed intake and amino acid requirements vary significantly with temperature, humidity and stocking density, and that adjusting dietary amino acid concentration compensates. So in a hot season, hold digestible lysine and Met+Cys at or slightly above the Ross figures of 1.18% and 0.92% for grower even though total intake has fallen, and raise energy density with oil rather than starch, because fat produces less heat during digestion than fibre does.

Antioxidant support belongs here too. Vitamin E and selenium work together in the glutathione peroxidase system, and combined supplementation is more effective than either alone for antioxidant defence during heat stress, immune response, gut integrity and microbiota stability. Ross sets vitamin E at 80 IU/kg in starter and selenium at 0.30 mg/kg, with the pairing guidance of 10-20 IU vitamin E per 0.3 mg selenium.

Finally, feed at the right time. Move the main feeding to early morning and late evening and remove feed during the hottest hours. Digestion itself generates heat, so a full crop at two in the afternoon adds to a load the bird cannot shed.

A Senegalese example: a producer outside Thies with 700 broilers in a low-roofed block house measured 37 degrees at bird level in the afternoon. He shifted feeding to 05:30 and 18:30, replaced 40% of his salt with sodium bicarbonate, and added an electrolyte pack to the water on days above 35. Afternoon mortality in the last week of the cycle dropped from 11 birds to 3, and his final weight rose without any change to the formulation.

Ventilation and Air Quality

In this lesson
  • Design natural ventilation openings that move air across birds without draughts on chicks
  • Link wet litter and ammonia back to specific feed and water faults
  • Correct an air quality problem using changes you can afford this month

Ventilation does four jobs at once: it removes heat, removes moisture, removes ammonia and other gases, and brings in oxygen. On a small African farm you are almost always doing this with openings, roof height and wind rather than with fans, so the design of the building is the ventilation system.

The basic natural-ventilation layout is well established. Run the long axis of the house across the prevailing wind so air enters one long side and leaves the other. Keep the walls open above about knee height, with wire mesh and a curtain that can be lowered from the top for young birds and raised progressively as they grow. Extend the roof overhang far enough that rain does not drive in, generally at least a metre. Put an opening at the ridge, because hot air and moisture rise, and a closed ridge traps both. If you can afford one improvement to an existing block house, a ridge vent and a raised roof usually beat everything else.

Moisture is the enemy you can smell. Ammonia is produced when bacteria break down uric acid in wet litter. Dry litter produces very little; wet litter produces a great deal. Ammonia damages the epithelial lining of the airway - the same tissue that vitamin A maintains, which is why Ross sets 13,000 IU/kg in starter - and a damaged airway lining lets in respiratory disease. If your eyes sting when you enter the house in the morning, the birds have been breathing that all night at floor level, where it is worse.

The important insight for a nutrition course is that most wet litter is a feed or water problem, not a ventilation problem, and ventilating harder will not fix the cause. Work through the list.

  1. Salt. Above roughly 0.5% sodium or chloride, birds drink far more and litter goes wet. Check your salt weighing and check whether your fish meal is salted or adulterated. Ross asks for only 0.18-0.23% sodium and chloride.
  2. Dietary electrolyte balance. A correctly raised DEB improves water consumption regulation and reduces wet litter incidence. A ration heavy in chloride does the opposite.
  3. Gut health. Birds with damaged intestines pass wet droppings. Coccidiosis, necrotic enteritis and mycotoxin damage all show up first as litter that will not dry. Remember that fumonisins at 18.6 ppm, below the 20 ppm broiler limit, altered gut microflora and predisposed birds to necrotic enteritis.
  4. Leaking drinkers and drinker height. A nipple line set too low, or a bell drinker overfilled, puts water straight into the litter.
  5. Mineral excess. High potassium or magnesium from certain ingredient combinations increases water excretion.

So the sequence when litter goes wet is: check the mix sheet, check the water lines, check the birds' droppings, and only then look at the openings.

Dust is the other side of air quality and it is often ignored. Finely ground mash, dry litter turned too aggressively and high stocking density all raise airborne dust, which carries bacteria and irritates airways. A medium grind of maize - visible particles, no fine flour - reduces dust while also stopping birds selectively picking through the ration.

Check ventilation with your senses and your birds, since few small producers have gas meters. Walk in first thing in the morning before opening anything up. If the air is heavy, if your eyes sting, if condensation sits on the underside of the roof, ventilation is failing. Watch the birds: panting with wings held out means heat; huddling away from a wall means a draught; birds crowded at the open ends of a house mean stagnant air in the middle. Kneel down. Everything you need to judge is at bird level, not at your head height.

Costed fixes, in the order they usually pay back. Opening the eave gap along both long walls and fitting mesh: cheap, often under $60 for a 10 by 6 metre house. Cutting and capping a ridge vent: moderate, perhaps $80-150 depending on roof type. Painting a bare iron roof white or fitting an insulating underlay: high value in hot zones, roughly $100-200. A single circulation fan on a suspended mount, if you have power: $70-120 and it moves air across birds even when there is no wind.

A Rwandan example: a Bugesera producer with 400 layers had chronically damp litter and persistent respiratory rattling. Her ventilation was adequate. The problem was a drinker line she had never adjusted as the birds grew, spilling water down one side of the house, combined with a salt weighing error that had crept in when she changed suppliers of fish meal. Fixing both dried the litter within ten days without touching the building.

Light Programs and Production

In this lesson
  • Describe how daylength drives feed intake, growth rate and onset of lay
  • Time calcium delivery to the hours when the shell is actually being formed
  • Set a lighting routine that suits your climate, your birds and your electricity supply

Light is a feeding tool. Birds eat when they can see, so daylength sets the number of hours available for feed intake, and in layers it also drives the reproductive cycle. Specific hour-by-hour lighting schedules vary between breeder guides and between countries, so treat any particular programme you are given as approximate and follow the guide supplied with your own strain. What does not vary is the underlying logic, and that is what this lesson covers.

For broilers, light controls intake and growth rate. Long light periods let the bird eat more, which grows it faster, but continuous light removes the rest and dark-period activity that supports skeletal development and leads to more leg problems in fast-growing strains. Standard commercial practice provides a period of darkness each night once the chick is past the first few days, giving the bird a rest phase. On a smallholder farm without controlled lighting, the practical decisions are simpler: give bright light in the brooding period so chicks find feed and water, use natural daylight thereafter, and if you add artificial light in the evening, add it to extend eating time in the cool hours rather than to run all night.

For layers, light does two things. It triggers the onset of lay, and it fixes the timing of the daily egg cycle. Increasing daylength brings pullets into production; decreasing daylength stops it. That is why a pullet reared under a naturally shortening daylength and then moved into a house with a lengthening one behaves differently from one reared the other way round. Never reduce daylength during production - a hen given less light than she had yesterday will reduce or stop lay.

The nutritional consequence is the part most producers miss. Shell formation happens largely overnight, in the dark, when the hen is not eating. The calcium used comes from two places: the gizzard, where coarse limestone particles have been sitting and dissolving slowly, and the skeleton, where medullary bone reserves are drawn down. This is exactly why the published limestone particle guidance exists. At peak production use 40% fine and 60% coarse limestone; from around week 77 shift to 25% fine and 75% coarse, because a hen with more coarse particles in the gizzard has more calcium available through the night. Each egg takes 2.2-2.5 g of calcium out of the hen, and Hy-Line puts peak daily calcium intake at 4.0 g per bird per day, rising to 4.7 g from week 77 as shell quality declines.

The practical management move follows directly. Encourage a good feed intake in the last two hours of light. Many producers run the feeders empty in the afternoon so birds clean up, then top up again before dark; this puts fresh coarse limestone into the gizzard at the moment it is most needed. Adding coarse limestone or oyster shell to the feeder in the late afternoon, rather than mixing it all through the morning ration, achieves the same thing at almost no cost.

Light intensity matters as well as duration. Bright light in the brooding area helps chicks find feed. Intensity is normally reduced afterwards, partly to reduce pecking and aggression. If you keep birds in an open-sided house with strong tropical daylight, uniformity of light matters more than absolute level - a house where one end is dark will have birds crowding into the lit end and competing for feeder space.

Electricity is the real constraint for most African producers, and the arithmetic is friendly. A house lit by six 9-watt LED lamps for four extra hours a day uses 6 x 9 x 4 = 216 watt-hours, about 0.22 kWh. At $0.15 per kWh that is roughly $0.03 a day, or under $1 a month. Compare that with the value of even a 2% increase in lay from 500 hens: at 500 birds x 0.02 x 30 days, that is 300 extra eggs a month. Lighting is one of the cheapest interventions available. A small solar panel with a battery running LED lamps is a common and workable solution where grid power is unreliable.

A Kenyan example: a producer in Kisii with 600 layers on grid power that failed most evenings saw production wobble whenever the lights failed, because effective daylength changed from one day to the next. Installing a 100 W solar panel, a small battery and four LED lamps for about $180 stabilised her lighting hours. Lay steadied within three weeks, and the panel paid for itself in under four months on the recovered eggs alone.

Space Allocation and Stocking Density

In this lesson
  • Calculate stocking density for your own house and compare it against your climate
  • Explain how density changes feed intake, amino acid requirement and mortality
  • Model the profit effect of reducing bird numbers per square metre

Stocking density is the decision that most tempts a producer with limited capital and most often destroys the margin he was chasing. More birds in the same house spreads your fixed costs over more units, which is the correct instinct. Past a point, though, every extra bird costs the whole flock feed conversion, uniformity and lives.

Start with the arithmetic on your own building. Measure the internal floor area in square metres. A house 10 m by 6 m gives 60 square metres. Divide bird numbers by that area to get birds per square metre, and multiply by expected final weight to get live weight per square metre, which is the figure that actually matters. Six hundred broilers finishing at 2.0 kg in that house is 10 birds per square metre and 20 kg per square metre.

Published density recommendations vary between countries, welfare schemes and buyer specifications, and they are always lower for hot, naturally ventilated houses than for climate-controlled ones. Treat any figure you are quoted as approximate and check your buyer's own specification. What is not approximate is the direction of the effect: in an open-sided African house in the hot season, the same density that works comfortably in a cool month will kill birds in a hot one, because a crowded bird cannot lose heat to its neighbours' bodies.

The nutritional consequence is documented. Published guidance states that feed intake and amino acid requirements vary significantly with temperature, humidity and stocking density in layers, and that adjusting dietary amino acid concentration compensates. In other words, if you push density up you must accept that intake per bird falls and the diet must become more concentrated to deliver the same daily grams. Density is a formulation input, not just a housing one.

Work the money, because the arithmetic is not intuitive. Take a 60 square metre house, chicks at $0.80, feed at $0.52/kg, other costs of $0.35 per bird, and a sale price of $4.20 per bird.

Scenario A, 600 birds at 10 per square metre. Mortality 5%, so 570 sold. FCR 1.78 at 2.0 kg means 3.56 kg of feed per bird, $1.85 of feed. Cost per bird placed = 0.80 + 1.85 + 0.35 = $3.00, so $1,800 total. Revenue = 570 x 4.20 = $2,394. Margin $594.

Scenario B, 780 birds at 13 per square metre. Mortality rises to 9%, so 710 sold. FCR worsens to 1.92, so 3.84 kg of feed per bird at $2.00. Cost per bird placed = 0.80 + 2.00 + 0.35 = $3.15, so $2,457 total. Revenue = 710 x 4.20 = $2,982. Margin $525.

Thirty per cent more birds, more feed bought, more work, more risk - and $69 less profit. That is the trap. The extra birds do not fail to sell; they simply each cost more to produce and more of them die.

The same logic applies to layers, where crowding shows up as lower lay percentage, more floor eggs, more feather pecking and higher culling. Because a layer occupies your house for a year or more, a density mistake in a layer house compounds for twelve months rather than six weeks.

Space is not only floor space. Feeder space and drinker space per bird are separate constraints, covered in the next lesson, and a house at a comfortable floor density can still be functionally overcrowded if there is one drinker for eighty birds. Watch behaviour: birds queuing at feeders, birds that never reach the trough, and large weight variation within the flock all say space, not feed.

Measure uniformity as your early warning. Weigh 20 birds at random at three weeks and calculate the spread. If the heaviest is more than about a third heavier than the lightest, competition is occurring somewhere - and in a crowded house it usually is.

A Zambian example: a Kabwe producer running 900 broilers in a 65 square metre house through the hot October season lost 14% of the flock and finished at 1.78 kg average. The following cycle he placed 650 in the same house, kept everything else identical, lost 5%, finished at 2.05 kg, and made more money on fewer birds. He now stocks by season - lighter in the hot months, heavier in the cool ones - which is the correct answer for a naturally ventilated house.

Feeder and Drinker Design

In this lesson
  • Calculate feeder and drinker space so every bird can eat and drink without queuing
  • Set feeder and drinker height and fill level to cut feed and water wastage
  • Test your drinking water against published quality standards

Feed you spill is feed you paid for. Water your birds cannot reach is growth you will never see. Feeders and drinkers are the cheapest equipment on the farm and the place where most small producers lose several per cent of their margin without noticing.

Start with wastage, because it is measurable. A tube feeder filled to the brim spills; the same feeder filled one third full does not. The standard rule is simple and worth more than any additive you can buy: never fill a feeder more than about a third to a half of its depth. Producers who go from brim-filling to third-filling commonly cut feed wastage by several per cent. On 1,900 kg of feed per 500-bird cycle at $0.52/kg, saving 4% is $39.50 per cycle for no cost at all.

Height is the second control. Set the lip of the feeder level with the birds' backs, and raise it as they grow - weekly at least. A feeder at chick height when the birds are three weeks old is a feeder they stand in, foul and scratch out of. The same rule applies to drinkers: a bell drinker rim at back height, or a nipple line just above head height so the bird reaches up slightly, keeps water in the bird rather than in the litter. Wet litter caused by badly set drinkers is one of the most common and most avoidable problems on small farms.

Space per bird is the third. Published figures vary by equipment type and country, so treat specific numbers as approximate and use behaviour as your check. What you are looking for is that all birds can eat at once during the peak feeding period, and that no bird is more than a few metres from water. Practically, that means spreading many small feeders and drinkers through the house rather than concentrating a few large ones at one end. Birds queuing, birds pushing, and a wide weight spread at three weeks all mean too little space. Distribute drinkers so a bird never has to cross the whole house to drink, because in hot weather a bird that must walk far will drink less, and a bird that drinks less eats less.

Water quality is the fourth and the most neglected. The published standards for broiler drinking water are specific and testable.

  1. Total dissolved solids below 1,500 ppm, with above 3,000 ppm a serious concern.
  2. pH between 6.5 and 7.5, with below 5.5 or above 8.5 a concern.
  3. Hardness as calcium carbonate of 50-200 ppm, with above 400 ppm a concern.
  4. Chloride below 250 ppm, with above 1,000 ppm a concern.
  5. Iron below 0.3 ppm, with above 1.0 ppm a concern - and iron above that level also feeds the biofilm that blocks nipple lines.
  6. Sulfates below 250 ppm, with above 1,000 ppm a concern.
  7. Microbial count below 1,000 CFU/mL, with above 10,000 CFU/mL a concern.

You can test pH with strips from a pharmacy or a swimming pool shop for a few dollars. Iron shows itself as orange staining on tank walls and drinker cups. Biofilm shows itself as slime you can feel with a finger inside a pipe or a drinker. Note the interaction with the feed: chloride in the water counts towards the bird's total chloride load, which matters when you are trying to raise dietary electrolyte balance in hot weather. Water at 900 ppm chloride will fight every formulation change you make.

Cleaning is the fifth item, and it is a routine, not an event. Flush lines between flocks, scrub bell drinkers daily, and keep the header tank covered and shaded. A tank in full sun grows algae and warms the water; birds drink less warm water precisely when they need it most.

Cost the improvements. Extra bell drinkers cost a few dollars each. A shade cover for a header tank costs almost nothing. A second feeder line spread through the house costs perhaps $30-60 for a small flock. All of these repay within one or two cycles from reduced wastage and better uniformity alone.

A Ghanaian example: a producer near Kumasi with 500 broilers was losing feed steadily and could not find the cause. Weighing the spillage swept up from under the feeders for three days showed roughly 1.9 kg a day - about 5% of daily intake. He was brim-filling six tube feeders twice a day. Filling them a third full four times a day, and raising them weekly, cut the sweepings to under 0.4 kg a day and saved roughly $38 across the cycle, which is more than his entire premix bill.

Biosecurity Through Housing

In this lesson
  • Site and lay out a poultry house so disease has fewer routes in
  • Build a working footbath and perimeter barrier from local materials
  • Run an all-in all-out cycle with an effective downtime between flocks

Biosecurity is usually taught as a set of rules about people and vehicles. Half of it is actually built into the building itself - where it sits, which way it faces, what it is made of and what can get into it. This lesson deals with that structural half. Feed and ingredient biosecurity, feed store construction, rodent-proof storage and mycotoxin control in stored grain are covered in Module 10; do not treat this lesson as a substitute for that one.

Siting comes first because it cannot be changed later. Four rules matter.

  1. Distance. Put as much distance as your land allows between your house and the nearest other poultry, including your neighbours' free-range birds and any live-bird market. Distance is the cheapest barrier there is.
  2. Wind direction. Site the house upwind of any older flock, manure heap or slaughter point, not downwind.
  3. Drainage. Build on the high point, not the low one. Water running towards a poultry house carries whatever is in it. A house that floods once a year will have wet litter and coccidiosis every year.
  4. Access. Design so that deliveries and sales happen at the perimeter, not at the house door. Every vehicle that reaches the door is a risk you did not need to take.

The perimeter is the second structure. A simple fence that stops people walking straight through, with one gate and one path, does most of the work. What matters is not the strength of the fence but that there is a single, obvious way in, so a visitor cannot arrive at the birds without passing your control point.

The footbath is the third, and most farm footbaths do nothing. A working footbath needs four things: a shallow tray wide enough that a person cannot step over it; disinfectant made up at the labelled dilution; a brush to knock mud off boots first, because disinfectant does not work through mud; and a schedule for changing the solution, typically every day or two and immediately after rain. A footbath full of brown water is a container of contaminated liquid, not a barrier. Place it at the single entrance, with a step-over barrier so people cannot bypass it. Better still, keep a pair of boots that never leave the poultry area.

Exclusion of wild birds and rodents is the fourth, and this is where the building fabric does the work. Wild birds carry disease directly into open-sided houses, and they also eat and foul feed. Mesh every opening with wire of a gauge small enough that sparrows and weavers cannot pass, check it monthly for holes, and repair immediately. Do not leave feed in open troughs overnight where wild birds can reach it at dawn. For rodents, seal the base of the walls, maintain a cleared strip of at least a metre around the house with no vegetation, rubbish, timber piles or long grass, and keep spilled feed swept up. Rats travel along edges and dislike open ground; a clean, bare margin is a physical deterrent that costs nothing but labour.

All-in all-out is the fifth and most powerful practice available to a small producer. One age of bird in one house at a time, all placed together, all removed together, followed by a full clean and a genuine empty period. Mixing ages in one house means the older birds continuously seed the younger ones with whatever they carry, and there is no point in the year at which you can break the cycle. The cleaning sequence is: remove all birds, remove all litter and take it well away from the house, dry-clean by brushing down every surface, wash with detergent and water, disinfect at the labelled dilution, then leave the house dry and empty. A commonly recommended downtime is around two weeks, and the value of it is that pathogens die without a host.

Separate your equipment too. Feed scoops, buckets, brooms and boots should belong to one house and stay there. A single shared scoop moving between an old flock and a new one undoes a great deal of careful work.

Cost this honestly. Mesh for a 10 by 6 metre open-sided house might be $50-90. A footbath tray, brush and a month of disinfectant, perhaps $25. Clearing and maintaining a one-metre margin costs labour only. Two weeks of downtime costs you roughly a sixth of a broiler cycle in lost throughput - the one genuinely expensive item on the list, and the one that pays back most. If downtime prevents even one 15% mortality event in a 500-bird flock, that is 75 birds at $4.20, or $315.

A Ugandan example: a producer near Masaka ran two houses with overlapping batches for three years, with mortality drifting between 9% and 16%. He rebuilt his schedule so each house held one batch at a time, meshed both houses properly, cleared a bare margin around them and enforced a 14-day downtime. Over the following four cycles mortality settled between 4% and 6%. He built nothing new; he changed how the buildings were used.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Why does excess dietary chloride worsen heat stress?

Excess chloride pushes the bird towards metabolic acidosis. A bird with lower blood pH pants excessively to compensate, which reduces growth and raises mortality.

2. A ration has 0.20% Na, 0.65% K and 0.22% Cl. What is its DEB?

(0.20 x 435) + (0.65 x 256) - (0.22 x 282) = 87 + 166.4 - 62.0 = 191 mEq/kg, below even the cool-weather band of 200-250.

3. Which change raises DEB the most?

Sodium chloride raises sodium and chloride together, so DEB barely moves. Sodium bicarbonate raises sodium without chloride, which is exactly what the formula rewards.

4. When feed intake falls 15% in a heat wave, what is the correct nutritional response?

Requirements are daily intakes. If the bird eats less, the diet must be more concentrated, and published guidance supports adjusting dietary amino acid concentration to compensate.

5. Why is oil preferred to fibre for raising energy in hot weather?

The heat increment of digestion adds to the bird's thermal load. Fat gives more energy per gram with less metabolic heat than fibrous ingredients.

6. Ammonia in a poultry house is produced by what?

Ammonia comes from microbial breakdown of nitrogenous waste in damp litter. Dry litter produces very little, which is why moisture control is the primary defence.

7. The first thing to check when litter goes wet is what?

Most wet litter is a feed or water problem. Excess sodium or chloride drives water intake, and misadjusted drinkers put water straight into the litter.

8. Why should a house have a ridge opening?

Warm, moist air rises. Without an outlet at the ridge it collects under the roof, condenses and returns to the litter.

9. Birds crowded at the open ends of a long house indicate what?

Bird distribution is a ventilation map. Crowding at the ends means air is not moving through the centre, which is where ammonia and heat will be worst.

10. Why does a medium rather than a fine grind of maize improve air quality?

Finely ground mash produces dust that stays airborne, carrying bacteria and irritating the respiratory tract, and it also separates in the bag.

11. Why must daylength never be reduced during a laying period?

Increasing daylength brings hens into production and decreasing daylength takes them out of it. A shortened day during production is read by the hen as the end of the season.

12. When is eggshell mostly formed?

Shell formation happens largely at night, which is why calcium must come from slowly dissolving coarse limestone in the gizzard and from skeletal reserves rather than from feed being eaten at that moment.

13. What is the cheapest practical way to support overnight shell formation?

Coarse 2-4 mm limestone eaten late in the day sits in the gizzard and dissolves through the night, exactly when the shell gland needs calcium.

14. Why is continuous 24-hour light avoided for broilers?

A dark rest period supports skeletal development and normal behaviour. Continuous light maximises intake but at the cost of leg health in modern fast-growing birds.

15. In an open-sided tropical house, what matters most about light?

Strong tropical daylight is rarely too dim overall. Uneven light causes uneven bird distribution, which creates local crowding at feeders and drinkers.

16. Why can placing 30% more birds reduce total profit?

In the worked example, 780 birds at 13/m2 gave 9% mortality and FCR 1.92 against 600 birds at 10/m2 with 5% and 1.78 - $525 of margin against $594 despite far more inputs bought.

17. Why is stocking density also a formulation decision?

Published guidance notes that feed intake and amino acid requirements vary significantly with temperature, humidity and stocking density, and that dietary amino acid concentration should be adjusted to compensate.

18. Why should stocking be lighter in the hot season in an open-sided house?

A naturally ventilated house has no way to remove the extra heat load of more bodies. The same density that is comfortable in a cool month becomes lethal in a hot one.

19. Which measurement is more meaningful than birds per square metre?

A bird's space requirement grows with its size. Live weight per square metre at finishing captures the real load on ventilation, litter and floor space.

20. A house at comfortable floor density can still be overcrowded because of what?

Floor space, feeder space and drinker space are three separate constraints. One drinker for eighty birds creates queuing and weight variation regardless of floor area.

21. What is the simplest way to cut feed wastage from tube feeders?

Brim-filled feeders spill. Filling to a third and raising the lip to back height as birds grow commonly cuts wastage by several per cent for no cost.

22. The published upper limit for iron in broiler drinking water is what?

Iron should stay below 0.3 ppm, with above 1.0 ppm a concern. High iron also stains equipment and feeds biofilm growth in water lines.

23. Why does water chloride matter to your feed formulation?

DEB subtracts chloride. Water at high chloride will undermine every ration change you make to raise DEB for heat stress.

24. Why should drinkers be spread through the house rather than grouped at one end?

Distance to water suppresses drinking, and drinking drives feed intake. Distribution matters as much as total drinker number.

25. What is the correct height for a feeder lip?

A feeder at back height lets birds eat without standing in it. Left at chick height as the flock grows, it gets fouled and scratched out.

26. Which siting decision cannot be corrected later?

Distance from other flocks, position relative to prevailing wind and drainage are fixed once you build. Everything else on the biosecurity list can be changed.

27. Why is a footbath full of brown water worse than useless?

Organic matter inactivates most disinfectants. A working footbath needs a brush to remove mud first and a fresh solution changed every day or two.

28. What is the main problem with keeping mixed ages of bird in one house?

All-in all-out exists to create a point at which every bird leaves and the house can be cleaned and rested. Overlapping batches remove that point entirely.

29. Why does a bare one-metre margin around the house deter rats?

Rodents move along walls, vegetation and clutter. Removing cover forces them into the open, which they avoid, and it makes any activity visible to you.

30. Which biosecurity item is genuinely expensive and also repays best?

Downtime costs real throughput - roughly a sixth of a broiler cycle. But preventing one 15% mortality event in a 500-bird flock is worth around $315, far more than the lost time.

Module 7 capstone

Audit your own house and rebuild one thing. Week 1: draw your poultry house to scale on paper, marking dimensions, every opening, the position of every feeder and drinker, the roof material and which way the prevailing wind blows. Calculate your floor area, your current bird numbers and your stocking density in birds per square metre. Week 2: record house temperature at bird level four times a day for seven days - dawn, mid-morning, mid-afternoon and after dark - along with feed intake and water intake per 100 birds, and mark every day the afternoon reading passed 32 degrees. Week 3: measure feeder space and drinker space per bird, test water at the drinker furthest from the tank for pH and visible iron or slime, and photograph the wettest and driest litter patches in the house. Week 4: choose the single change with the best return - eave opening, ridge vent, roof insulation, extra drinker line, a raised feeder, a proper footbath and perimeter, or reduced stocking - cost it in USD from a real supplier, and state the mortality or feed conversion improvement needed to repay it within two batches. Present the drawing, the temperature and intake records, and the costed decision.
