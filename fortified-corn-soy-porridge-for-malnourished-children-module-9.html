1. Identifying local health centers and NGOs that help malnourished children

Start by making a list of every place in your area that deals with child nutrition. Go to your local government health center, mission hospital, or clinic. Ask the nurse or nutritionist if they see malnourished children. Many do, especially in rural areas. Also look for NGOs: check with the district health office, social welfare office, or community development office. They often know which NGOs work on nutrition. Big NGOs like World Vision, Save the Children, UNICEF, and Concern Worldwide may have local offices or partners. Also ask at churches, mosques, and community centersthey sometimes run feeding programs.

Once you have names, visit each place. Bring a notebook and pen. Introduce yourself as a local producer of fortified corn-soy porridge. Ask: How many malnourished children do you see per month? What do you currently give them? Do you buy food or receive donations? Who decides what to buy? Get the name and phone number of the person in charge of nutrition or procurement. For health centers, that might be the nutritionist or the officer in charge. For NGOs, it might be a project officer or logistics officer.

Not all places will buy from you. Some get free ready-to-use therapeutic foods (RUTF) like Plumpy'Nut from donors. But many health centers need supplementary porridge for children who are moderately malnourished or for follow-up after discharge. That is your market. Also, some NGOs run local feeding programs and prefer to buy from local producers to support the economy. Ask if they have a budget for local purchase. If they say no, ask when their next budget cycle starts and who to contact then.

Keep a simple record: name of organization, contact person, phone number, number of malnourished children they see, what they currently use, and their interest level (hot, warm, cold). Follow up every two weeks with a polite call or visit. Building these relationships takes time, but one health center can order 50 to 200 kg per month. That is steady income for you and better nutrition for children.

2. Preparing a sample and proposal for health workers

Health workers are busy and skeptical. They need proof that your porridge is safe, nutritious, and affordable. Start with a clean, professional sample. Package 500g or 1kg in a well-sealed plastic bag or container. Label it clearly: product name, ingredients (corn, soy, sugar, salt, vitamins/minerals if added), net weight, production date, best before date, and your phone number. Include cooking instructions: mix 1 part porridge to 4 parts boiling water, stir, simmer 5 minutes. If you can, get a simple nutritional analysis from a local university or food lab. Even a basic proximate analysis (protein, fat, moisture, energy) costs about $50-$100 and adds huge credibility.

Your proposal should be one or two pages, not a long report. Use simple language. Start with the problem: 'Many children in [your area] are malnourished. They need affordable, local, nutritious food.' Then describe your product: 'Fortified corn-soy porridge, made from locally grown corn and soy, fortified with vitamins and minerals. 100g provides 400 kcal and 15g protein.' Next, show how it helps: 'One serving (50g dry) gives a child 200 kcal and 7.5g proteinperfect for supplementary feeding.' Then give your price: 'We sell at $1.50 per kg, which is cheaper than imported RUTF at $3 per kg.' Finally, offer a free sample and a trial: 'We will provide 5kg free for you to test with 10 children for two weeks. If you like it, we can supply regularly.'

When you meet the health worker, be respectful and brief. Hand over the sample and proposal. Explain that you are a local producer and you want to work with them. Ask for feedback: 'What do you think? Would this work for your patients?' Listen more than you talk. If they have concerns about hygiene or quality, address them. Show them your production area if possible. Offer to adjust the formula or packaging. If they say yes to a trial, agree on a date to deliver and a date to follow up. After the trial, ask for a written testimonial or a simple letter of recommendation. That letter will open doors to other health centers.

3. Getting testimonials from mothers who use your porridge

Mothers are your best salespeople. When a mother says her child gained weight or stopped being sick, that story is more powerful than any advertisement. To get testimonials, start by giving free or discounted porridge to 10 mothers with malnourished children. Work with a local health center to identify them. Ask the mothers to use the porridge every day for two weeks. Check in with them weekly: weigh the children, ask about appetite, energy, and any problems. Take photos (with permission) of the children looking healthy and happy. Also take a short video of the mother speaking in her own language. Keep it simple: 'My child was thin and weak. After two weeks of this porridge, he is eating well and playing again.'

Collect the testimonials in writing, audio, or video. If written, ask the mother to sign or thumbprint. If audio or video, get verbal permission to use it. Store these files on your phone or a small notebook. When you visit a health center or NGO, show them the testimonials. A photo of a healthy child with a mother's quote is very convincing. Also, ask the mothers if they would be willing to talk to other mothers or health workers. Sometimes they will come with you to a meeting. That is powerful.

Use the testimonials in your marketing. Print small posters with a photo and quote. Play the audio on your phone when you visit clinics. Share the video on WhatsApp groups for health workers. But always be honest. Do not exaggerate. If a child gained 500g in two weeks, say that. Health workers will trust you more if your claims are realistic. Also, keep records of the children's weights before and after. A simple table with name, age, weight before, weight after, and number of days is enough. This data, combined with mother testimonials, makes a strong case. Over time, you can build a booklet of success stories. That booklet becomes your best marketing tool.

4. Participating in community health days to demonstrate porridge

Community health days are events where health workers, NGOs, and local leaders gather to provide services like immunizations, growth monitoring, and nutrition education. These days are perfect for demonstrating your porridge. Find out when the next health day is happening. Ask your local health center or district health office. Often they are held monthly or quarterly. Register as a vendor or exhibitor if possible. If not, ask if you can set up a small table near the entrance. Bring a cooking pot, a stove or firewood, water, and your porridge flour. Also bring small cups or spoons for tasting. Cook a batch of porridge on site. The smell will attract people. Offer free tastes to mothers, health workers, and anyone interested.

While cooking, talk to the crowd. Explain how to make the porridge: mix flour with cold water first to avoid lumps, then add to boiling water, stir for 5 minutes. Mention the benefits: energy, protein, vitamins. Show the packaging and the price. Hand out small flyers with your phone number and the price per kg. If you have testimonials, show them on your phone. If a mother likes the taste, ask her to sign up for a trial. Collect names and phone numbers of interested mothers and health workers. You can also ask the health workers to recommend your porridge to mothers who need supplementary feeding.

After the event, follow up with everyone you met. Call the health workers within two days. Deliver samples to those who asked. For mothers, send a WhatsApp message thanking them and reminding them where to buy. If you can, attend every health day in your area. Consistency builds trust. Also, offer to give a short talk on nutrition at the next health day. That positions you as a knowledgeable local producer. Over time, health workers will start referring mothers to you directly. One health day can generate 20-50 new customers and 2-3 health center contacts. That is worth the effort.

5. Using radio and WhatsApp to reach more customers

Radio is still the most powerful media in rural Africa. Many people listen to local FM stations, especially in the morning and evening. To use radio, first identify the community radio station that covers your area. Call them and ask for their advertising rates. A 30-second spot might cost $5-$20, depending on the station and time. If you cannot afford that, offer to be a guest on a health or farming program. Call the station and say you are a local producer of fortified porridge for malnourished children. Offer to share your knowledge for free. They often welcome local experts. During the interview, mention your product, where to buy it, and your phone number. Speak clearly and slowly. Repeat your phone number at least twice.

WhatsApp is free and very effective. Create a WhatsApp Business account. Set your profile photo as your product or your logo. Write a short description: 'Fortified corn-soy porridge for malnourished children. 1kg = $1.50. Order via WhatsApp.' Save the numbers of all health workers, NGO staff, and mothers you meet. Create broadcast lists for different groups: health workers, mothers, and shops. Send a message once a week with a tip, a testimonial, or a special offer. For example: 'Tip: Add 1 spoon of oil to porridge for extra energy. We have 50kg ready. Order now.' Do not spam. Only send useful messages. Also, join local WhatsApp groups for mothers, farmers, or health volunteers. Share your tips and let people ask questions. If someone asks where to buy, reply publicly so others see.

Use photos and short videos. A 15-second video of you cooking porridge or a mother giving a testimonial gets more attention than text. You can also use WhatsApp status to post daily updates. When you get a new order, ask the customer to refer a friend. Offer a small discount for referrals. Combine radio and WhatsApp: mention your WhatsApp number on radio, and use WhatsApp to follow up with listeners. This two-step approach multiplies your reach at low cost.

6. Building trust with nutritionists and community leaders

Nutritionists and community leaders are gatekeepers. If they trust you, they will recommend your porridge to health centers, NGOs, and mothers. If they don't, they can block you. So invest time in building relationships. Start with the nutritionist at your local health center. Visit them regularly, even when you don't need anything. Ask about their work, their challenges, and how you can help. Offer to provide free porridge for their most severe cases. Ask for their advice on improving your product. If they suggest adding a vitamin or changing the packaging, do it and tell them. That shows you listen. Also, invite them to visit your production site. Show them your hygiene practices, your ingredients, and your records. Transparency builds trust.

Community leaders include village chiefs, religious leaders, and local government officials. They influence what people buy and which businesses get support. Meet them respectfully. Explain that your porridge helps malnourished children in their community. Ask for their blessing and their help in spreading the word. Offer to give a free sample to their family or to a needy child they know. If they like it, they will tell others. You can also ask to speak at a community meeting or after a church service. Keep your talk short: 2 minutes about the problem, your product, and where to buy. Bring a small sample for people to taste.

Another key group is community health volunteers (CHVs). They visit homes and know which children are malnourished. Train them on how to use your porridge. Give them a small commission for every mother they refer who buys 5kg or more. For example, pay them $0.50 per referral. That motivates them. Also, give them a free 1kg sample to show mothers. When CHVs recommend your porridge, mothers trust it because they know the volunteer. Over time, these relationships create a network of trusted advocates. They will defend your product if rumors arise and will connect you to new opportunities. Remember: trust is built slowly, one honest interaction at a time.

<h2>Practical Reference: Selling Fortified Corn-Soy Porridge to Health Centers and NGOs</h2>

<p>This module is for small-scale producers of fortified corn-soy porridge who want to sell to health centers, NGOs, and community nutrition programs. The market is real: across sub-Saharan Africa, millions of children under five suffer from acute malnutrition, and health facilities and NGOs constantly need reliable, affordable, locally produced fortified foods. Your porridge can be part of that supply chain but only if you approach it as a business, not a charity. This Practical Reference gives you the tools, templates, and checklists to move from production to consistent sales.</p>

<h3>Lesson 1: Identifying Local Health Centers and NGOs That Help Malnourished Children</h3>
<p>Start with a mapping exercise. In most African districts, the following actors work on child nutrition:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Government health centers and district hospitals</strong> they run outpatient therapeutic programs (OTP) for severe acute malnutrition.</li>
<li><strong>Community health worker networks</strong> they screen children and refer cases.</li>
<li><strong>International NGOs</strong> e.g., Concern Worldwide, World Vision, Action Against Hunger, Save the Children.</li>
<li><strong>Local CBOs and faith-based organizations</strong> often run feeding programs.</li>
<li><strong>District nutrition officers</strong> they coordinate all nutrition activities and control budgets.</li>
</ul>
<p>Visit the district health office and ask for the nutrition focal person. Introduce yourself as a local producer of fortified porridge. Ask: What products do you currently use? Where do you buy them? What are your gaps? This conversation is your market research.</p>

<h3>Lesson 2: Preparing a Sample and Proposal for Health Workers</h3>
<p>Health workers are busy and skeptical. Your sample must be clean, well-labeled, and accompanied by a one-page proposal. The proposal should include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Product name and ingredients (corn, soy, micronutrient premix).</li>
<li>Nutritional content per 100g (energy, protein, fat, vitamins, minerals).</li>
<li>Preparation instructions for caregivers.</li>
<li>Price per kg and minimum order.</li>
<li>Your contact details and location.</li>
<li>Any certifications (e.g., KEBS, Ghana Standards Authority, NAFDAC).</li>
</ul>
<p>Deliver the sample in person. Ask the nutritionist to try it with a small group of mothers. Offer to attend a health education session to demonstrate preparation. Follow up within one week.</p>

<h3>Lesson 3: Getting Testimonials from Mothers Who Use Your Porridge</h3>
<p>Testimonials are powerful social proof. Ask mothers who have used your porridge for permission to record a short video or written statement. Focus on:</p>
<ul>
<li>How the child's appetite or weight changed.</li>
<li>How easy the porridge is to prepare.</li>
<li>How affordable it is compared to other options.</li>
</ul>
<p>Use these testimonials in your proposal to NGOs and in WhatsApp marketing. Always get written consent and never exaggerate health claims.</p>

<h3>Lesson 4: Participating in Community Health Days to Demonstrate Porridge</h3>
<p>Community health days (e.g., Child Health Days, Vitamin A campaigns) attract hundreds of mothers. Set up a table with a cooking demonstration. Prepare small cups of porridge for tasting. Hand out flyers with your contact and price. Collect names and phone numbers of interested mothers and health workers. Follow up via WhatsApp within 48 hours.</p>

<h3>Lesson 5: Using Radio and WhatsApp to Reach More Customers</h3>
<p>Radio remains the most trusted mass medium in rural Africa. Buy a 30-second spot on a local station during the morning health program. Script: "Is your child not growing well? Try [Brand] fortified corn-soy porridge available at [health center] and [shop]. Call [number]."</p>
<p>WhatsApp is your direct sales channel. Create a broadcast list of health workers, NGO staff, and mothers. Send weekly messages: new stock, price, testimonials, and health tips. Use WhatsApp Business catalog to show your product.</p>

<h3>Lesson 6: Building Trust with Nutritionists and Community Leaders</h3>
<p>Trust is your most valuable asset. To build it:</p>
<ul>
<li>Never make medical claims. Say "supports good nutrition," not "cures malnutrition."</li>
<li>Invite nutritionists to visit your production site.</li>
<li>Share your quality control records.</li>
<li>Attend district nutrition coordination meetings.</li>
<li>Partner with community leaders for endorsements.</li>
</ul>

<strong style="color:#c62828;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#b71c1c;">Fortified corn-soy porridge is a <strong>complementary food</strong>, not a treatment for severe acute malnutrition (SAM). Children with SAM require therapeutic foods (RUTF) and medical care under trained health workers. Never claim your porridge can treat or cure malnutrition. Incorrect preparation or contaminated ingredients can cause illness. Always follow national food safety standards and label clearly: "Not for children under 6 months. Use as part of a balanced diet."</p>
