1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Foundry economics is about making money from your metal casting skills. Many small foundries fail not because they can't melt metal, but because they don't understand costs and pricing. In Africa, where access to capital is tough, you must know exactly how much each casting costs and what price gives you a profit. This module will teach you to calculate costs, set prices, and manage your foundry like a business.

Consider a simple example: You make a aluminium cooking pot. You buy scrap aluminium at 1,500 Naira per kg. The pot weighs 2 kg, so metal costs 3,000 Naira. But that's not all. You spend 500 Naira on fuel (charcoal or gas), 200 Naira on flux, and 300 Naira on labour (your time or a helper). Total cost = 4,000 Naira. If you sell for 4,500 Naira, your profit is only 500 Naira. But if you sell for 6,000 Naira, profit is 2,000 Naira. Without knowing your costs, you might sell at a loss.

Why does this matter? Because in many African markets, competition is fierce, and customers bargain hard. If you don't know your break-even point, you could be persuaded to sell below cost. Also, understanding economics helps you decide which products are most profitable. Maybe making grinding mills for maize is more profitable than making pots. You can then focus on high-margin items.

Furthermore, foundry economics includes managing waste. Scrap metal, sand, and fuel are expensive. Reusing sand, recycling sprues, and buying in bulk reduce costs. You also need to account for equipment maintenance and replacement. A broken furnace means no income, so setting aside money for repairs is part of economics.

In this module, you'll learn practical steps: how to calculate unit cost, how to price for profit, how to track expenses, and how to grow your foundry. We'll use real African examples, from Kenya to Nigeria to South Africa. By the end, you'll be able to run a foundry that not only produces quality castings but also makes a steady profit.

2. Tools and Materials

To manage foundry economics, you need a few simple tools. You don't need a computer or expensive software. A notebook and pen are your best friends. Use a hardcover exercise book to record every expense and income. Divide it into sections: date, item, cost, and notes. Also, keep a calculator (even a phone calculator works) to do quick sums. A simple weighing scale for metal and a measuring tape for fuel are essential. For tracking fuel, a bucket or container of known volume helps.

Materials you need include a price list from your local scrap dealer. Visit them regularly to know current prices for aluminium, brass, bronze, and cast iron. Prices change, so update your list weekly. Also, collect receipts for everything: fuel, sand, binder, flux, and any paid labour. If you buy from a market without receipts, write the amount in your notebook immediately. Another useful material is a sample cost sheet. Create a form with columns: material cost, fuel cost, labour cost, overhead cost, total cost, selling price, profit. Fill one for each product you make.

For pricing, you need to know your market. Visit local markets and shops that sell similar products. Note their prices. Talk to potential customers to see what they are willing to pay. A simple survey of 10 people can give you a good idea. Also, keep a file of competitor prices. If you don't have a file, use your notebook.

For safety and efficiency, you need personal protective equipment (PPE) like gloves, goggles, and apron. These are also a cost, so include them in your overhead. Finally, a simple first aid kit is necessary; accidents cost money. Remember, these tools and materials are not optional if you want to run a profitable foundry. They help you make informed decisions and avoid losses.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Calculate the cost of one casting. Start by weighing the finished product. Add the weight of the gating system (sprues, runners, risers) that will be cut off and remelted. For example, if a pot weighs 2 kg and the gating weighs 0.5 kg, you need 2.5 kg of metal. But you also lose some metal as dross and spillage. Assume 10% loss. So total metal = 2.5 kg / 0.9 = 2.78 kg. Round to 2.8 kg. Multiply by the price per kg of scrap. If scrap is 1,500 Naira/kg, metal cost = 4,200 Naira.

Step 2: Calculate fuel cost. If you use charcoal, find out how much charcoal it takes to melt 2.8 kg of aluminium. Typically, it takes about 1 kg of charcoal to melt 1 kg of aluminium in a small furnace. So you need 2.8 kg of charcoal. If charcoal is 500 Naira/kg, fuel cost = 1,400 Naira. If you use gas, calculate based on gas consumption per kg of metal. For a small furnace, a 12.5 kg gas cylinder might melt 20 kg of aluminium, so gas cost per kg = (price of refill) / 20. If refill is 10,000 Naira, cost per kg = 500 Naira, so for 2.8 kg, fuel cost = 1,400 Naira.

Step 3: Add flux and other materials. Flux costs about 200 Naira per melt. Sand for moulding: if you use green sand, you can reuse it many times, but you need to add new sand and clay occasionally. Estimate 100 Naira per casting for sand upkeep. Also, add labour. If you pay a helper 2,000 Naira per day and you make 5 castings per day, labour per casting = 400 Naira. If you do it yourself, put a value on your time; say 1,000 Naira per casting.

Step 4: Calculate overhead. Overhead includes rent, electricity, depreciation of tools, and maintenance. If your monthly overhead is 50,000 Naira and you produce 200 castings per month, overhead per casting = 250 Naira. Add all costs: metal 4,200 + fuel 1,400 + flux 200 + sand 100 + labour 400 + overhead 250 = 6,550 Naira. That is your break-even cost.

Step 5: Set your selling price. Add a profit margin. In competitive markets, 20-30% is common. So price = 6,550 * 1.25 = 8,187 Naira. Round to 8,200 Naira. Check market prices: if similar pots sell for 7,000 Naira, you are too expensive. Then you must reduce costs or find a niche. If they sell for 10,000 Naira, you have a good margin. Record this calculation for every product. Review monthly as prices change.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Forgetting to include your own labour. Many beginners think their time is free. But if you spend 8 hours making a casting, that time could be used for other income. Fix: Pay yourself a wage. Decide on an hourly rate, say 500 Naira per hour. If a casting takes 2 hours, add 1,000 Naira to cost. This ensures you don't underprice and work for nothing.

Mistake 2: Not accounting for metal loss. You buy 3 kg of scrap but only 2 kg ends up in the casting. The rest is dross or spillage. If you only cost 2 kg, you underprice. Fix: Always add 10-15% for loss. Weigh your dross and sprues to know your actual loss rate. Adjust your calculations accordingly.

Mistake 3: Ignoring fuel price changes. Charcoal and gas prices fluctuate. If you set prices based on old fuel costs, you lose money when prices rise. Fix: Update your cost sheet weekly. Keep a buffer in your selling price to absorb small increases. If fuel rises sharply, adjust prices immediately.

Mistake 4: Overestimating production volume. You might think you can make 10 castings per day, but breakdowns, power cuts, or market days reduce output. If you spread overhead over 10 castings but only make 5, your overhead per casting doubles. Fix: Be realistic. Track actual production for a month. Use that number for overhead allocation.

Mistake 5: Not tracking small expenses. Items like sand, clay, binding wire, and PPE add up. If you ignore them, your profit is overstated. Fix: Keep a petty cash box. Every small purchase goes in the notebook. At month end, total these and divide by number of castings.

Mistake 6: Pricing based on competitors only. If a competitor sells cheap because they use child labour or substandard materials, you can't match them. Fix: Compete on quality and service. Explain to customers why your product lasts longer. Also, find a niche where customers value quality over price. For example, custom parts for machinery can fetch higher prices.

Mistake 7: Not saving for equipment replacement. Your furnace will wear out. If you don't save, you'll be out of business. Fix: Include depreciation in overhead. If a furnace costs 100,000 Naira and lasts 2 years (24 months), monthly depreciation = 4,167 Naira. Add this to overhead.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in foundry work is not just about avoiding burns; it's also about financial safety. A serious accident can cost you medical bills, lost production, and even your life. So, best practices in safety directly affect your bottom line. Always wear PPE: leather gloves, face shield, apron, and safety boots. These cost money, but they are cheaper than a hospital bill. For example, a good face shield costs 5,000 Naira, while treating an eye injury can cost 50,000 Naira or more. Include PPE in your overhead costs.

Maintain your equipment. A well-maintained furnace uses less fuel and lasts longer. Check for cracks, replace worn crucibles, and clean burners regularly. A cracked crucible can cause a metal spill, leading to burns and wasted metal. Set a maintenance schedule: daily check, weekly cleaning, monthly inspection. The cost of maintenance is small compared to the cost of a breakdown during a big order.

Keep your workspace clean and organised. Scrap metal, sand, and tools should have designated places. This prevents accidents and improves efficiency. A clean foundry also impresses customers, which can lead to more sales. Use signs to mark hot areas and emergency exits. Have a fire extinguisher or sand bucket nearby. In Africa, where fire services may be far, a bucket of dry sand is a cheap and effective fire extinguisher for metal fires.

Train your workers. If you employ helpers, teach them safe practices. Untrained workers are more likely to have accidents, and you are responsible. Also, provide clean drinking water and shade to prevent heat exhaustion. A hydrated worker is more productive. Consider providing lunch; it reduces downtime and builds loyalty.

Best practice also includes ethical business. Don't cut corners on safety to save money. Don't underpay workers. Don't cheat customers. A good reputation is worth more than short-term profit. Keep records of safety incidents and near-misses. Review them monthly to prevent repeats. Finally, insure your workshop if possible. In some African countries, micro-insurance for small businesses is available. It covers fire, theft, and accidents. The premium is small compared to potential losses.

6. Practical Exercise

For this exercise, you will calculate the cost and set a price for a simple casting: a 1 kg aluminium cooking pot. You will need: a notebook, pen, calculator, and current prices from your local market. If you don't have a foundry yet, use estimated prices. Follow these steps:

1. Weigh a similar pot or estimate its weight. Assume 1 kg. Add 20% for gating and loss: 1.2 kg total metal. Find the price of scrap aluminium per kg. Let's say 1,500 Naira. Metal cost = 1,800 Naira.

2. Estimate fuel. For 1.2 kg aluminium, you need about 1.2 kg charcoal. If charcoal is 500 Naira/kg, fuel cost = 600 Naira. If using gas, calculate gas cost per kg and multiply by 1.2.

3. Add flux: 100 Naira. Sand: 50 Naira. Labour: If you pay yourself 500 Naira per hour and it takes 1 hour, labour = 500 Naira. Overhead: If monthly overhead is 30,000 Naira and you produce 100 pots, overhead per pot = 300 Naira. Total cost = 1,800 + 600 + 100 + 50 + 500 + 300 = 3,350 Naira.

4. Decide profit margin. Aim for 25%. Selling price = 3,350 * 1.25 = 4,187.5 Naira. Round to 4,200 Naira.

5. Check market. Visit three shops selling pots. If they sell for 3,500 Naira, your price is too high. You need to reduce costs. Can you get cheaper scrap? Use less fuel? If they sell for 5,000 Naira, you have a good margin.

6. Record everything in your notebook. Create a table with columns: Item, Cost. Do this for three different products: a pot, a hoe blade, and a grinding mill part. Compare profits. Which gives the best profit per hour of work? Focus on that product.

7. Finally, write a short plan for one month. How many castings will you make? What are your total costs? What is your expected income? What profit do you hope to make? Set a savings target for equipment. This exercise will give you a real feel for foundry economics. Do it with actual numbers from your area, not just the examples here.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Foundry Economics Field Manual

This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the numbers, tools and decision rules that determine whether a small metal casting operation in Africa makes money or quietly bleeds cash. It is written for the artisan-entrepreneur, the workshop owner and the technical trainer who must turn molten metal into margin. Every figure here can be checked, recalculated and adapted to your own furnace, your own scrap prices and your own market. Treat it as a working document: print it, pin it above the moulding bench and update the local prices every quarter.

The core economic reality of African foundries is simple. Metal is expensive, fuel is expensive, and downtime is the most expensive cost of all because it is invisible. A foundry that casts 40 kg of good aluminium per day at a 60% yield is not "almost as good" as one casting 40 kg at 85% yield it is paying roughly 40% more for every saleable kilogram. This lesson gives you the reference tables, the diagram and the checklist to close that gap.

SAFETY WARNING ECONOMIC PRESSURE KILLS

The most dangerous moment in a foundry is when an order is late and the owner pushes the melt. Never:

  • pour wet, damp or rusty scrap into a hot crucible to save drying time steam explosion risk;
  • skip fluxing or degassing to save consumable cost porosity creates rejects, and rejects destroy margin;
  • reuse cracked crucibles or patch a furnace lining to avoid a replacement purchase;
  • allow untrained casual labour to pour metal to meet a deadline;
  • operate without leather aprons, face shields, spats and dry footwear, even for "one small pour".

A single serious burn costs more than a year of correct PPE. Safety is a cost-control measure, not an overhead.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Weigh everything. Charge weight, poured weight and good-casting weight, every heat. Unweighed metal is unmanaged cost.
  2. Calculate yield weekly. Target 80%+ for aluminium, 65%+ for cast iron. Investigate any drop of more than 5 percentage points.
  3. Keep a fuel log. Record litres or kWh per melt. A sudden rise means a damaged lining, a dirty burner or a leaking crucible.
  4. Segregate scrap by alloy. Mixed scrap sells cheap and casts badly. Clean, sorted aluminium scrap commands a better price and a better casting.
  5. Price from the cost card, not from the competitor. Know your total cost per good casting before you quote.
  6. Track rejects by cause. Porosity, misrun, sand inclusion, shrinkage. Fix the top cause each month; a 5% reject reduction is often a 5% profit increase.
  7. Plan furnace campaigns. Melt in batches, not one-off heats. Every extra heat costs a full warm-up in fuel and time.
  8. Reinvest a fixed percentage. Allocate at least 510% of revenue to crucibles, PPE, patterns and sand. Deferred maintenance is deferred bankruptcy.
