Lesson 4.1~12 min

Obligate Carnivore Protein and Amino Acid Needs

In this lesson
  • Calculate the protein gap between a dog formula and a cat formula on the same ingredients
  • Explain why arginine is the amino acid that kills fastest when it is short
  • Price the cost per kilogram of crude protein across your local protein sources

A cat is not a small dog with a different shape. The word obligate in obligate carnivore is doing real work: it means the cat has lost metabolic machinery that the dog still has, and the missing pieces have to arrive in the food, every day, already made.

Start with the number that decides your ingredient budget. Cat protein minima sit about 8 percentage points above dog minima at both life stages. For adult maintenance the AAFCO figure is 25.00 g of protein per 100 g of dry matter - 25 percent - against 18 percent for an adult dog. For growth the cat figure rises to 28-30 g per 100 g DM.

That 8-point gap is not a rounding difference. It changes which ingredients you can afford to use and how much of the cheap carbohydrate filler you can get away with.

Work it through on one tonne of feed:

  1. A dog diet at 18 percent protein needs 180 kg of crude protein per tonne.
  2. A cat diet at 25 percent needs 250 kg.
  3. The difference is 70 kg of crude protein per tonne.
  4. If your marginal protein source is fish meal at 60 percent crude protein, you need 70 / 0.60 = 117 kg more fish meal per tonne.
  5. At USD 1.20 per kg for fish meal - an illustrative local price you must replace with your own - that is about USD 140 more per tonne, or USD 0.14 per kg of finished feed.

That USD 0.14 is the floor under your cat product. Anyone selling cat food at the same price as their dog food is either using the same formula for both, or lying about one of them. Both are worth knowing when you study a competitor's shelf.

Now the amino acid that will catch you out. Arginine.

  • Cat minimum: 1.24 percent DM for growth, 1.04 percent for adult maintenance.
  • Dog minimum: 1.00 percent for growth, 0.51 percent for adult.

The adult cat needs roughly double the adult dog. Cats have minimal intestinal ornithine and citrulline synthesis, which means they cannot manufacture arginine when the diet runs short. Arginine drives the urea cycle, and the urea cycle is how the body clears the ammonia produced by digesting protein. A high-protein diet with insufficient arginine is a trap: you have loaded the animal with ammonia and removed its means of disposal.

Classic feline nutrition teaching holds that a single arginine-free meal can precipitate acute hyperammonaemia. The reference flags that specific single-meal claim as unverified against a primary source, so treat the speed as uncertain but the mechanism as real. What is not uncertain is the requirement figure, and that is what you formulate to.

Practically: animal proteins are arginine-rich, so a formula built on fish meal, poultry meal or meat and bone meal will usually clear 1.04 percent without help. A formula that leans on maize gluten or a large fraction of plant protein may not. If you cut animal protein to save money, arginine is one of the first things you break, and you will not see it in a proximate analysis.

A producer in Nairobi selling into a vet clinic ran into this from the other direction. Her cat formula met 25 percent protein comfortably because she was using a lot of maize gluten meal, which is cheap and tests high for crude protein. Crude protein is nitrogen multiplied by 6.25 - it counts nitrogen, not usefulness. Her amino acid balance was poor even though her protein number looked fine. The fix was to swap part of the gluten for fish meal, which cost more per tonne but produced a formula that actually met the profile.

Before you formulate anything, price your protein sources on the basis that matters. Not cost per kilogram of ingredient - cost per kilogram of crude protein. Fish meal at USD 1.20/kg and 60 percent protein costs USD 2.00 per kg of protein. Soya cake at USD 0.65/kg and 44 percent costs USD 1.48. That ranking is the one that runs your business, and it changes every season.

Lesson 4.2~11 min

Taurine and Arachidonic Acid Requirements

In this lesson
  • State the taurine minimum for dry and for wet cat food and explain why they differ
  • Calculate the synthetic taurine addition and its cost for a one-tonne batch
  • Identify which fats supply arachidonic acid and which cannot

Two nutrients separate a competent cat food from one that slowly damages the animal eating it. Neither shows up in a proximate analysis. Both must be added deliberately.

Taurine first. The AAFCO and FEDIAF minima are 0.10 percent of dry matter for extruded dry food and 0.20 percent for canned or wet food. The wet figure is double. That is not a safety margin - it is a correction. Moist processing and higher gut losses reduce how much of the taurine in a wet food actually reaches the cat, so you have to put more in to deliver the same amount.

If you make both a dry and a wet product, they need different taurine inclusion rates. Using the dry figure in a wet formula produces a food that meets its label and starves the cat anyway.

Taurine is a dietary essential for cats because they conjugate bile acids with taurine obligately, so losses through the gut are continuous and must be continuously replaced. Deficiency causes central retinal degeneration, dilated cardiomyopathy and reproductive failure. Those three outcomes are established. The reference flags the specific enzyme name behind the mechanism as unverified, so do not quote enzyme names to customers - quote the requirement and the consequences.

Dogs synthesise their own taurine. It does not appear on the AAFCO dog profile at all. This is the single clearest reason you cannot sell one product for both species.

The arithmetic for a one-tonne dry batch:

  1. Target 0.10 percent of dry matter. Assume your finished feed is 10 percent moisture, so 1000 kg of feed contains 900 kg of dry matter.
  2. 900 kg x 0.001 = 0.9 kg of taurine.
  3. Add a margin for processing loss. Extrusion heat degrades some taurine, so many producers formulate to 0.12-0.15 percent to land above 0.10 percent in the finished bag. Decide your margin, write it down, and be consistent.
  4. At 0.15 percent: 900 x 0.0015 = 1.35 kg per tonne.
  5. Synthetic taurine typically runs USD 15-25 per kg as an illustrative import price. At USD 20, that is USD 27 per tonne, or USD 0.027 per kilogram of feed.

Under three cents a kilogram to prevent blindness and heart failure. There is no formulation decision in this whole course with a better return, and it is the one most often skipped because nothing visible happens for months.

Arachidonic acid is the second. The cat minimum is 0.02 percent of dry matter at both life stages. Cats have negligible delta-6-desaturase activity, so they cannot make enough arachidonic acid from linoleic acid the way dogs can. It must arrive pre-formed, from animal fat.

Vegetable oils contain none. Not a little - none. A cat formula whose only added fat is sunflower, soya or palm oil cannot meet this requirement no matter how much you add. Chicken fat, fish oil, poultry offal and animal tallow carry it; plant oils do not.

Dogs have no AAFCO arachidonic acid minimum at all, which is another reason the two formulas diverge.

A producer in Kampala making a "premium plant-forward" cat food learnt this the hard way. The marketing was good, the protein number was met with soya and maize gluten, and the fat came entirely from sunflower oil. The formula was arachidonic-acid-free and taurine-free. Cats fed it exclusively for several months developed coat and reproductive problems. The product was reformulated with poultry fat and synthetic taurine, and it worked - but the reputational cost took two years to clear.

If a customer asks you for a vegetarian cat food, the honest answer is that a cat is not built for one, and that meeting the profile without animal ingredients requires synthetic supplementation you probably cannot source or verify. Say so. Sell them the dog product if they have a dog.

Lesson 4.3~11 min

Urinary Health and Mineral Balance

In this lesson
  • State the calcium and phosphorus minima for adult cats and calculate the ratio in your formula
  • Explain how water intake differs between dry-fed and wet-fed cats and why it matters
  • List the minerals you cannot responsibly formulate without obtaining the current AAFCO Official Publication

This lesson has to begin with something the course does not know, because pretending otherwise would be worse than useless.

Magnesium is central to feline urinary health. It is one of the minerals most discussed in relation to struvite crystal formation. The nutrient reference underpinning this course explicitly did not retrieve the AAFCO magnesium figure for cats - it is listed as not retrieved, alongside linoleic acid, vitamin K and biotin. This course will not invent a magnesium number for you. Anyone who quotes you one without a source has either checked the current AAFCO Official Publication or is guessing, and you cannot tell which from the outside.

So the instruction is direct: before you formulate a cat product for sale, obtain the current AAFCO Official Publication and read the cat nutrient profile in full. It is a purchased document. Treat it as a cost of doing business, the same as a scale or a mixer. Formulating a cat food from a partial nutrient table is how urinary disease gets manufactured at scale.

What this course can give you with confidence is calcium and phosphorus, both verified:

  • Calcium: 1.0 percent DM for growth and reproduction, 0.6 percent for adult maintenance.
  • Phosphorus: 0.8 percent DM for growth and reproduction, 0.5 percent for adult maintenance.

Notice both stages give a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of 1.25 to 1. That ratio matters as much as either number alone. Work yours out every time:

  1. Your adult formula calculates to 0.72 percent calcium and 0.61 percent phosphorus.
  2. Both clear their minima - 0.72 above 0.6, and 0.61 above 0.5.
  3. Ratio: 0.72 / 0.61 = 1.18 to 1. Acceptable, close to the profile's own 1.25.
  4. Now suppose you add bone meal to cut costs and calcium rises to 1.4 percent while phosphorus stays at 0.61. Ratio becomes 2.3 to 1. Both minima are still met. The balance is wrong anyway.

Meeting minima is not the same as being balanced. A formula can pass every individual line and still be a bad diet.

The other lever you control is water, and it is bigger than most producers realise. A cat eating dry food takes in very little water with its meal. A cat eating wet food takes in a great deal. Total daily water intake tends to be higher on wet food, which means more dilute urine passing through the bladder more often. That is protective, and it costs you nothing to explain to a customer.

This gives you an honest selling line that does not require a health claim: a cat on dry food needs water available at all times, in more than one place, in a wide shallow bowl kept away from the food. Cats drink poorly from deep narrow bowls and dislike drinking beside their food. Telling customers this builds the kind of trust that sells the next bag.

A shop owner in Lagos who stocked cat food started keeping a simple record of which customers came back. The ones who returned were disproportionately the ones she had spent two minutes talking to about water bowls. She was not making a medical claim. She was giving away something true and useful, and it worked as marketing precisely because it was not a pitch.

One more constraint worth respecting. Do not attempt to formulate a urinary-specific therapeutic product on the strength of this lesson. Diets that manipulate urine pH or restrict specific minerals to manage diagnosed crystal disease belong with a veterinarian, and Module 9 covers therapeutic formulation properly. What you are building here is a sound everyday adult diet - which, done well, is the thing that keeps most cats out of that territory in the first place.

Lesson 4.4~11 min

Fat as Energy Source in Cats

In this lesson
  • State the cat fat minimum and explain why it does not fall for adult maintenance as the dog figure does
  • Calculate the metabolisable energy of a cat formulation using the modified Atwater route
  • Balance fat inclusion against rancidity risk in a hot climate

Here is a detail that catches out producers who learnt on dog food: the cat fat minimum does not drop for adult maintenance. It stays at 9.00 g per 100 g of dry matter - 9 percent - for both growth and adult maintenance. Dogs get a reduction as they mature. Cats do not.

Cats run on fat and protein. Fat is their preferred energy substrate, it carries the fat-soluble vitamins, it supplies the arachidonic acid from the previous lesson, and it is the single biggest driver of whether a cat will eat your food twice. Palatability in cats is closely tied to fat, and a cat that refuses food is a customer you have lost permanently - cats are far less forgiving than dogs about a diet they dislike.

To know what you are actually feeding, calculate metabolisable energy rather than guessing. The modified Atwater route for cats:

  1. Gross energy, kcal per 100 g = (5.7 x protein) + (9.4 x fat) + (4.1 x [NFE + crude fibre])
  2. Energy digestibility for cats, percent = 87.9 minus [(0.88 x crude fibre x 100) / (100 minus percent moisture)]
  3. Digestible energy = gross energy x digestibility
  4. Metabolisable energy for cats = digestible energy minus (0.77 x protein), in kcal per 100 g

Note that step 2 and step 4 both use cat-specific coefficients. The dog equations use different ones - 91.2 and 1.43 for digestibility, and 1.04 for the protein subtraction. Running a cat food through the dog equations gives you a wrong answer that looks perfectly plausible.

Worked example on a formula testing at 27 percent protein, 12 percent fat, 3 percent crude fibre, 48 percent NFE, 8 percent moisture:

  1. GE = (5.7 x 27) + (9.4 x 12) + (4.1 x 51) = 153.9 + 112.8 + 209.1 = 475.8 kcal/100 g
  2. Digestibility = 87.9 - [(0.88 x 3 x 100) / (100 - 8)] = 87.9 - (264 / 92) = 87.9 - 2.87 = 85.03 percent
  3. DE = 475.8 x 0.8503 = 404.5 kcal/100 g
  4. ME = 404.5 - (0.77 x 27) = 404.5 - 20.8 = 383.7 kcal/100 g, which is 3837 kcal/kg

That sits close to the 3800 kcal/kg figure the reference uses as a mid-range dry food midpoint - and note the reference marks that 3800 as a derived midpoint, not a published standard. Your own number, calculated from your own analysis, always beats a borrowed one.

Now the constraint that bites in African conditions. Fat goes rancid, and it goes rancid faster when it is hot. A high-fat cat food in an uncooled store in Accra or Mombasa has a materially shorter usable life than the same formula in a temperate warehouse. Rancid fat smells wrong to a cat long before it smells wrong to you, and the cat simply stops eating.

Three practical responses:

  • Use an antioxidant in the fat and record which one and at what rate. Do not treat this as optional in a hot climate.
  • Size your batches to your actual sales rate. A tonne of cat food that takes four months to sell is a tonne that spends four months oxidising.
  • Package in a way that limits air and light, and tell your retailers to keep stock off concrete floors and out of direct sun.

A producer in Accra found her repeat purchase rate on cat food was much lower than on her dog line, and assumed the formula was wrong. It was not. Her cat product moved slowly, sat longer in the shop, and by the time the second bag was opened the fat had gone off. She cut the batch size, added an antioxidant and asked her shops to rotate stock. The formula never changed and the complaints stopped.

Lesson 4.5~11 min

Feeding Strategies and Portion Control

In this lesson
  • Calculate a cat's daily energy requirement using RER and the correct life-stage factor
  • Convert a daily energy figure into grams of your feed and a monthly cost for the customer
  • Explain why the neutered indoor cat is the highest obesity risk you will sell to

A feeding guide printed on your bag is a promise. If it is wrong, the cat gets fat or thin and the owner blames your food. Getting it right takes one equation and one table.

Resting energy requirement, valid at any weight:

RER (kcal/day) = 70 x body weight in kg to the power 0.75

Then multiply by a life-stage factor. For cats:

  • Kitten, weaning to adult: 2.5 x RER
  • Adult, intact: 1.4 to 1.6 x RER
  • Adult, neutered: 1.2 x RER
  • Obesity-prone: 1.0 x RER

Note how narrow the adult range is compared with dogs, and how low the neutered figure sits. Neutering reduces energy requirement substantially while appetite often stays the same. The neutered indoor cat is the single most obesity-prone customer you will ever formulate for.

Worked example, a 4 kg neutered adult:

  1. RER = 70 x 4^0.75. Four to the power 0.75 is 2.83, so RER = 70 x 2.83 = 198 kcal/day.
  2. Neutered adult factor 1.2: MER = 198 x 1.2 = 238 kcal/day.
  3. Your feed calculates to 3837 kcal/kg, which is 3.837 kcal per gram.
  4. Daily ration = 238 / 3.837 = 62 g/day.
  5. Monthly = 62 x 30 = 1.86 kg.
  6. At USD 6.00/kg retail - use your own price - that is USD 11.16 per month to feed this cat.

That last number is the one the customer actually cares about, and almost no producer prints it. Putting a realistic monthly cost on your marketing material is disarming and it filters out the customers who were going to complain about price anyway.

There is a second, incompatible system you should know exists so you do not mix them. FEDIAF calculates maintenance energy for cats directly as 75 kcal x BW^0.67 or 100 kcal x BW^0.67, using an exponent of 0.67 rather than 0.75. This gives different answers from the RER-times-factor approach. Both are legitimate. Mixing them is not. Pick one system, state on your documentation which one you used, and stay with it.

On feeding pattern: cats evolved eating many small meals. Free-feeding dry food suits that pattern but removes all portion control, which is exactly the wrong trade for a neutered indoor cat. Measured meals two or three times a day give you control while staying closer to natural behaviour than one large meal.

Tell customers to measure with a scale, not a cup. Cup volumes vary with kibble size and density, and a cup of your feed is not a cup of the last brand they used. A cheap kitchen scale is the most effective obesity intervention available, and it costs less than one bag of food.

A producer in Dar es Salaam started printing a small table on every bag: body weight down one side, grams per day across the other, split for neutered and intact. She also printed the monthly cost at her own retail price. Two things happened. Customers stopped over-feeding, which slightly reduced her sales per cat. And her repeat purchase rate climbed, because their cats stayed the right weight and they trusted her. The second effect was much larger than the first.

Print the table. Include the neutered column. It is the honest thing to do and it happens to be good business.

Lesson 4.6~11 min

Disease Prevention Through Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Explain why the vitamin A maximum matters more in cats than the minimum
  • State the cat niacin requirement and why tryptophan cannot substitute for it
  • Distinguish everyday preventive formulation from therapeutic diets that require a veterinarian

Most feline nutritional disease is not caused by exotic deficiencies. It is caused by ordinary formulas built by people who assumed a cat was a dog. This lesson covers the three that a competent formulator prevents for free.

Vitamin A, and specifically its maximum. Cats need preformed vitamin A - 6668 IU/kg DM for growth and reproduction, 3332 IU/kg for adult maintenance. The reason is that cats lack the intestinal dioxygenase enzyme needed to cleave beta-carotene, so they cannot convert it to retinol. Beta-carotene in a cat food is a colourant or an antioxidant. It is not a vitamin A source. Dogs can make the conversion; their profile lists 5000 IU/kg for both life stages.

But the number that causes real harm in practice is the maximum: 333,300 IU/kg. That ceiling exists because vitamin A toxicity in cats is a genuine clinical problem, and the classic route to it is liver.

Liver is cheap, palatable and extremely high in preformed vitamin A. A producer making a "natural, liver-rich" cat food, or an owner supplementing daily with liver, can cross that ceiling without much effort. Chronic hypervitaminosis A causes painful bony changes, particularly around the neck and forelimbs. It develops slowly and it is largely irreversible by the time it is visible.

So the practical rule: cap your liver inclusion, write the cap on the formulation sheet, and treat any request for a liver-heavy cat product as a formulation problem rather than a marketing opportunity. If you do not know your vitamin A total, you do not know whether you are near the ceiling.

Niacin second. The cat minimum is 60 mg/kg DM - more than four times the dog minimum of 13.6 mg/kg. Cats cannot synthesise meaningful amounts of niacin from tryptophan. The reference notes the accepted mechanism involves high picolinate carboxylase activity shunting the pathway away from niacin, and flags that specific enzyme detail as unverified, while confirming the inability to substitute.

The consequence is what matters commercially: adding tryptophan does not fix a low-niacin cat formula. Niacin must be added as niacin. If you buy a premix, check that it is a cat premix. A dog premix used in a cat food will under-deliver niacin by a factor of four while looking, on the invoice, like the same product.

Third, the whole-diet principle. The single most preventive thing you can do is make one complete, balanced food and persuade the customer to feed it as the whole diet. Cats fed a good commercial food plus daily table scraps, plus liver, plus milk, end up with a diet no one has calculated. Dilution of a balanced formula with unbalanced extras is a common route to trouble, and it is invisible until it is not.

A veterinary practice in Harare that stocked one producer's food asked her to print a single line on the bag: "This food is complete. Adding meat, liver or milk unbalances it." Complaints about coat and stool quality dropped noticeably over the following months. Nothing about the formula changed.

Finally, the boundary. Everything in this lesson is prevention through an ordinary well-built diet. It is not the management of diagnosed disease. Chronic kidney disease, diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, urinary crystal disease and food allergy all require a veterinary diagnosis first and a therapeutic formulation second - and Module 9 covers that properly, including the point at which the decision stops being yours.

Know where that line sits and say so out loud to customers. A producer who says "that needs your vet, and I can make what they prescribe" is more trusted, not less. It is also the position that keeps you out of trouble when a sick cat does not get better.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. How far above the adult dog minimum does the adult cat protein minimum sit?

Cat protein minima sit about 8 percentage points above dog minima at both life stages - 25 percent DM for adult cats against 18 percent for adult dogs.

2. Why is arginine deficiency dangerous in cats specifically?

Cats have minimal intestinal ornithine and citrulline synthesis. Without dietary arginine the urea cycle stalls and ammonia from protein digestion accumulates.

3. A formula tests at 25 percent crude protein but uses mostly maize gluten meal. What is the risk?

Crude protein is nitrogen multiplied by 6.25. A high number can hide a poor amino acid profile, which no proximate analysis will reveal.

4. Fish meal costs USD 1.20/kg at 60 percent protein. What is the cost per kg of crude protein?

1.20 divided by 0.60 equals 2.00. Cost per kilogram of protein, not per kilogram of ingredient, is the figure that should drive your ingredient choices.

5. What does 'obligate' mean in obligate carnivore?

Cats have lost specific synthetic capabilities dogs retain. Nutrients such as taurine, arachidonic acid and preformed vitamin A must arrive already made in the food.

6. Why is the taurine minimum doubled for canned cat food?

The wet figure of 0.20 percent corrects for reduced bioavailability, not for portion size. Using the dry figure in a wet formula under-delivers taurine.

7. Which fat source supplies arachidonic acid?

Arachidonic acid comes pre-formed from animal fat. Vegetable oils contain none, and cats have negligible delta-6-desaturase activity to make their own.

8. A one-tonne dry batch at 10 percent moisture, formulated to 0.15 percent taurine on a dry matter basis. How much taurine?

1000 kg of feed at 10 percent moisture is 900 kg dry matter. 900 x 0.0015 = 1.35 kg.

9. Why does taurine not appear on the AAFCO dog profile?

Dogs synthesise taurine adequately, so it carries no dietary minimum for them. Cats cannot, which is why it is essential in cat diets.

10. Which three outcomes does taurine deficiency cause in cats?

These three are the established deficiency syndromes. They develop over months, which is why a taurine-free formula can sell well before the damage becomes visible.

11. What does this course say about the AAFCO magnesium figure for cats?

The source reference lists magnesium as not retrieved. Inventing a figure would be worse than admitting the gap, and urinary health is exactly where a guessed number does damage.

12. A formula has 1.4 percent calcium and 0.61 percent phosphorus. What is wrong?

Both minima are met but the balance is wrong. Meeting each line individually does not make a formula balanced.

13. Why does wet food tend to support urinary health?

Cats on wet food take in substantially more water with their meal, which dilutes urine. This is a physical effect of the food's moisture, not an additive.

14. Where does a therapeutic urinary diet belong?

Diets that manipulate urine pH or restrict minerals for diagnosed disease require veterinary involvement. Module 9 covers therapeutic formulation; this module builds the sound everyday diet.

15. What is the adult cat calcium minimum?

Adult maintenance is 0.6 percent DM, rising to 1.0 percent for growth and reproduction.

16. What happens to the cat fat minimum at adult maintenance?

The cat fat minimum does not drop for adult maintenance. Dogs get a reduction as they mature; cats do not.

17. Which coefficient does the cat metabolisable energy equation subtract per unit of protein?

ME for cats is DE minus 0.77 x protein. The dog equation uses 1.04, so running a cat food through the dog equation gives a wrong but plausible-looking answer.

18. A cat formula in a hot uncooled store loses repeat sales. What is the most likely cause?

Fat oxidises faster in heat. Cats detect rancidity long before humans do and simply stop eating, which reads as a formula problem but is a shelf-life problem.

19. Why is palatability a bigger commercial risk in cats than in dogs?

A cat that refuses food is a permanently lost customer. Dogs will usually eat a mediocre diet; cats often will not.

20. In the worked example, what was the calculated metabolisable energy?

ME came to 383.7 kcal per 100 g, which is 3837 kcal/kg. The 4750 figure is gross energy and 4045 is digestible energy - both before the relevant deductions.

21. What is the RER of a 4 kg cat?

RER = 70 x 4^0.75. Four to the power 0.75 is 2.83, giving 198 kcal per day before any life-stage factor.

22. Which cat is at highest obesity risk?

Neutering cuts energy requirement to about 1.2 x RER while appetite often stays the same. Combined with indoor living, this is the highest-risk group you will formulate for.

23. Why should customers measure food with a scale rather than a cup?

A cup of your feed is not a cup of the previous brand. Weight is the only measure that transfers between products.

24. What is wrong with mixing the RER-times-factor system and the FEDIAF BW^0.67 system?

FEDIAF uses an exponent of 0.67 for cats rather than 0.75, giving different results. Both are legitimate; state which one you used and stay consistent.

25. A 4 kg neutered cat needs 238 kcal/day and the feed is 3837 kcal/kg. What is the daily ration?

3837 kcal/kg is 3.837 kcal per gram. 238 divided by 3.837 gives about 62 g per day.

26. Why can beta-carotene not supply a cat's vitamin A?

Cats cannot perform the conversion, so beta-carotene functions only as a colourant or antioxidant in cat food. Vitamin A must be supplied preformed from animal sources.

27. What is the usual route to vitamin A toxicity in cats?

Liver is cheap, palatable and extremely high in preformed vitamin A. Chronic excess causes painful and largely irreversible bony changes.

28. How does the cat niacin requirement compare with the dog's?

Cats require 60 mg/kg DM against 13.6 mg/kg for dogs, because they cannot synthesise meaningful niacin from tryptophan.

29. A customer feeds your complete food plus daily liver and milk. What is the risk?

Extras that nobody has calculated unbalance a balanced diet, and liver specifically pushes vitamin A toward its ceiling.

30. A customer's cat has been diagnosed with kidney disease. What is the right response?

Diagnosed disease requires veterinary direction and therapeutic formulation, covered in Module 9. Saying so builds trust and keeps you out of trouble when a sick animal does not improve.

Formulate a diet that meets 25 g protein and 9 g fat per 100 g dry matter, then add synthetic taurine at 0.10 percent and show the arithmetic.
