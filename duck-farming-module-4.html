1. Common Duck Diseases and Symptoms

Ducks are hardy but can fall sick quickly. Learn to spot the early signs: a duck that separates from the flock, sits hunched, refuses feed, or has watery eyes and a dirty vent. Common diseases in Africa include Duck Viral Hepatitis (DVH), Duck Plague (Duck Viral Enteritis), Avian Influenza (Bird Flu), Botulism, and Aspergillosis. DVH kills young ducklings fast, often within 24 hours, with symptoms like spasms and head retraction. Duck Plague causes sudden death, bloody diarrhea, and swollen eyelids. Avian Influenza brings high fever, swollen head, and sudden death; it is a zoonotic disease, so report it to authorities immediately. Botulism comes from eating spoiled food or dead animals in stagnant water; ducks become paralyzed, starting with the legs, then neck (limberneck). Aspergillosis is a fungal infection from moldy feed or bedding; symptoms include gasping, sneezing, and weight loss.

To diagnose, observe your flock twice daily. Look for changes in behavior, appetite, and droppings. A healthy duck has bright eyes, clean feathers, and stands upright. Sick ducks may have greenish or bloody droppings. For bacterial diseases like fowl cholera, you may see swollen joints and difficulty breathing. Viral diseases often have no cure, so prevention is key. If you suspect a disease, isolate the sick duck immediately in a separate pen with clean water and feed. Wear gloves and wash hands after handling. Do not sell or eat sick birds. Call a vet or extension officer for confirmation. In many African countries, you can contact the local veterinary department for free or low-cost diagnosis. Early action saves the flock.

Keep records: note the date, symptoms, number affected, and deaths. This helps the vet identify patterns. For example, if ducklings die suddenly with opisthotonus (head pulled back), suspect DVH. If adult ducks die after swimming in a stagnant pond, suspect botulism. Remember, many diseases spread through contaminated water, feed, and equipment. Clean and disinfect drinkers and feeders daily. Avoid mixing ducks with other poultry, especially chickens, as they can carry diseases like avian influenza without showing symptoms. Vaccinate where possible (see Lesson 2). Your quick response can mean the difference between losing a few birds and losing your entire flock.

2. Vaccination and Deworming Protocols

Vaccination is your best defense against killer diseases. For ducks, the core vaccines are Duck Viral Hepatitis (DVH) and Duck Plague (Duck Viral Enteritis). In many African countries, these vaccines are available from veterinary supply stores or government extension offices. DVH vaccine is given to ducklings at 1-2 weeks of age, often as a shot under the wing (subcutaneous) or in the breast muscle. A booster is given 2-4 weeks later. For Duck Plague, vaccinate at 3-4 weeks old, then annually. If you keep breeders, vaccinate them 2-4 weeks before laying so chicks get maternal immunity. Note: Avian Influenza vaccine is not commonly used in ducks in Africa due to trade restrictions; instead, focus on biosecurity. Always buy vaccines from a reputable source and check the expiry date. Keep vaccines cold (2-8C) using a cooler with ice packs during transport. Use a new needle for each bird or disinfect between birds with 70% alcohol to avoid spreading disease.

Deworming: Ducks get internal parasites like roundworms, tapeworms, and gizzard worms. Deworm every 3-4 months with a poultry dewormer such as piperazine, levamisole, or albendazole. Dosage: piperazine is often given in water at 100 mg per kg body weight for one day, repeated after 2 weeks. Levamisole: 20-30 mg/kg orally. Albendazole: 10-20 mg/kg orally for 3 days. For external parasites like lice and mites, use ivermectin at 0.2 mg/kg injected under the skin, repeated after 10 days. Alternatively, dust with diatomaceous earth or use permethrin powder. Always follow label instructions and withdraw eggs for the recommended period (e.g., 7 days for most dewormers).

Create a health calendar: Month 1: DVH vaccine at week 1 and 3. Month 2: Duck Plague vaccine at week 4. Month 3: first deworming. Then repeat deworming every 3 months. Keep a notebook with dates, products used, and batch numbers. If you see tapeworm segments in droppings (white, flat, moving), deworm immediately. For coccidiosis (bloody droppings), use amprolium in water for 5 days. Remember, vaccines only work if ducks are healthy; do not vaccinate sick birds. Also, avoid vaccinating during extreme heat stress. If you are unsure, consult a vet or experienced duck farmer in your area. Many African farmers share vaccines to reduce costs, but never share needles without sterilizing.

3. Biosecurity Measures on the Farm

Biosecurity means keeping germs out and stopping them from spreading. On a duck farm, start with a fence to keep wild birds, rodents, and strangers out. Wild ducks and waterfowl carry avian influenza and duck plague. If your ducks free-range near a pond, they can catch diseases from wild birds. Ideally, keep your ducks in a covered run with a roof and wire mesh sides. Use a footbath at the entrance of each pen: a shallow tray with disinfectant (e.g., 2% sodium hypochlorite or quaternary ammonium). Change the disinfectant daily. Wear dedicated boots and overalls only inside the farm. Do not let visitors enter pens unless necessary; if they must, give them clean boots and a lab coat.

Clean water is critical. Ducks foul water quickly. Use nipple drinkers or troughs that are easy to empty and scrub. Empty and refill drinkers at least twice a day. Once a week, scrub with a brush and disinfectant, then rinse well. Feeders should be raised off the ground to prevent contamination with droppings. Store feed in sealed containers away from rodents. Never feed moldy or spoiled feedit causes aspergillosis and botulism. Remove dead ducks immediately; bury them deep (1 meter) with lime or burn them. Do not throw carcasses into the bush or water.

All-in, all-out management works best: raise one batch of ducks at a time, then clean and disinfect the pen thoroughly before bringing new birds. If you must add new ducks, quarantine them for 30 days in a separate area. Watch for symptoms. After handling sick birds, change clothes and wash hands with soap. Keep a separate set of tools for sick pens. Control flies and mosquitoes with screens and traps; they spread fowl pox and other diseases. Finally, keep records of all visitors, new birds, and deaths. This helps trace disease outbreaks. In Africa, many farmers share equipment; avoid borrowing or lending nets, feeders, or boots without disinfecting. A simple biosecurity routine can cut disease by 80%.

4. Parasite Control: Internal and External

Parasites steal nutrients, reduce egg production, and can kill young ducks. Internal parasites include roundworms (Ascaridia), tapeworms (Raillietina), gizzard worms (Amidostomum), and coccidia (Eimeria). Symptoms: weight loss, pale comb, diarrhea, and seeing worms in droppings. Deworm regularly: every 3 months for adults, every 6 weeks for growing ducks. Use piperazine (100 mg/kg in water for 1 day), levamisole (20-30 mg/kg orally), or albendazole (10-20 mg/kg orally for 3 days). For coccidiosis, use amprolium (1 g per 2 liters of water for 5 days). Rotate dewormers to prevent resistance. After deworming, collect droppings and burn them; do not use as fertilizer for 2 weeks.

External parasites: lice, mites, fleas, and ticks. Lice are pale and move slowly on feathers; mites are tiny and cause crusty skin, especially around the vent and under wings. Ducks infested with mites become restless, lose weight, and may stop laying. Treat with ivermectin injection (0.2 mg/kg under the skin, repeat after 10 days). Alternatively, dust ducks with permethrin powder or diatomaceous earth, focusing on the vent, under wings, and neck. For severe lice, use a 0.5% malathion dust. Treat the pen too: spray with permethrin or sprinkle diatomaceous earth on bedding. Clean nest boxes and replace bedding weekly. Sun-drying bedding kills mites.

Prevention: Keep the pen dry. Ducks love water but wet litter breeds parasites. Provide a shallow water bath for swimming, but change it daily and keep the area around it drained. Avoid overcrowding: allow at least 1 square meter per duck in the pen. Rotate grazing areas if you free-range. Plant herbs like garlic, neem, and wormwood around the pen; some farmers feed crushed neem leaves (1 teaspoon per duck per week) as a natural dewormer, but this is not a substitute for proper medication. Quarantine new birds for 30 days and treat them for parasites before mixing. Check your ducks weekly: part feathers and look at the skin. If you see moving specks or scabs, treat immediately. Remember, parasites spread fast, so act on the first sign.

5. First Aid and Treating Injuries

Ducks get injured from predators, sharp objects, or fighting. Keep a first aid kit: clean cloth, antiseptic (povidone-iodine), antibiotic ointment, gauze, vet wrap, scissors, tweezers, and saline solution. For a cut or scrape, wash with saline or clean water, apply antiseptic, then cover with gauze and vet wrap. Change daily. If bleeding heavily, apply pressure with a clean cloth for 5 minutes. For a broken leg, splint with a small stick and vet wrap, keeping the leg straight. Isolate the duck in a small, quiet pen with soft bedding. Limit movement for 2 weeks. For a broken wing, wrap the wing against the body with vet wrap, but do not wrap too tight. Check circulation. Most wing fractures heal in 3-4 weeks.

Bumblefoot is a common foot infection: a swollen, black scab on the foot pad. Soak the foot in warm salt water (1 teaspoon per liter) for 10 minutes twice daily. Apply antibiotic ointment and bandage. If not improved in 3 days, see a vet for antibiotics. For eye injuries, flush with saline and apply terramycin eye ointment. For poisoning (e.g., botulism or lead), give activated charcoal (1 g per kg) mixed in water, and keep the duck warm and quiet. There is no antidote for botulism; supportive care may save mild cases. For heat stress, move ducks to shade, provide cool water, and dip their feet in cool water. For frostbite (rare in Africa but possible in highlands), warm the area gradually with lukewarm waternever hot.

After any injury, keep the duck separate but within sight of the flock to reduce stress. Provide easy access to feed and water. Use a shallow dish to prevent drowning if the duck is weak. If the duck is not eating, try offering mashed boiled egg or soft porridge. Administer electrolytes in water (e.g., 1 teaspoon salt, 1 teaspoon sugar per liter). Always wash your hands before and after treating. If an injury is severe (deep wound, exposed bone, or maggots), call a vet. Maggots can be removed with tweezers and the wound flushed with hydrogen peroxide diluted 1:1 with water. Do not use human painkillers like ibuprofen or paracetamolthey are toxic to ducks. With prompt care, most ducks recover quickly.

6. Working with a Veterinarian

A good veterinarian is your partner in keeping ducks healthy. In Africa, find a vet who treats poultry or has experience with waterfowl. Contact your local veterinary department, agricultural extension office, or a nearby veterinary college. Some vets specialize in poultry; ask if they have worked with ducks. Establish a relationship before an emergency. Invite the vet to your farm for a routine visit once or twice a year. They can check overall health, advise on vaccination schedules, and inspect biosecurity. A farm visit may cost $20-50, but it can save you hundreds in losses. If you cannot afford a vet, look for community animal health workers or experienced duck farmers who can mentor you.

When a disease outbreak occurs, call the vet immediately. Describe symptoms: number sick, number dead, age, and any recent changes (new feed, new birds, weather). If the vet cannot come, they may ask you to bring a sick duck or a dead one for post-mortem. For post-mortem, wrap the dead duck in a plastic bag and keep it cool (not frozen) until delivery. Never open a dead duck yourself unless trainedyou could spread disease. The vet may take samples (blood, swabs, organs) and send them to a lab. In many countries, government labs test for avian influenza and Newcastle disease for free. Results may take 1-2 weeks. Meanwhile, isolate sick birds and disinfect.

Follow the vet's instructions exactly. If they prescribe antibiotics, complete the full course even if ducks look better. Withdrawal periods for meat and eggs: for example, oxytetracycline has a 5-day meat withdrawal and 7-day egg withdrawal. Record all treatments. If the vet recommends culling, do it humanely: use a sharp knife to sever the jugular vein or use a captive bolt. Bury deep with lime. Do not sell sick ducks. After an outbreak, work with the vet to review biosecurity and vaccination. Ask for a written health plan. Keep the vet's phone number handy. In remote areas, you can use mobile phones to send photos of symptoms to a vet for advice. Many African vets now offer teleconsultation. Remember, a vet is not just for emergenciesregular check-ups keep your flock productive and profitable.

<h2>Practical Reference: Duck Health Field Manual for African Farms</h2>

<p>Ducks are among the hardiest poultry species on African farms, but "hardy" does not mean "maintenance-free." A duck flock that goes unmonitored for two weeks can lose 3050% of its value through mortality, stunted growth, and condemned carcasses. This practical reference consolidates the diagnostic, treatment, and record-keeping knowledge you need to protect revenue. It is written for the farm manager, not the laboratory scientist: every action here has a direct cost or profit consequence.</p>

<p>In African duck enterprises whether you run 50 Muscovy in a backyard or 2,000 Pekin under contract three disease categories drive most losses: viral infections (duck plague, duck viral hepatitis), bacterial infections (pasteurellosis, colibacillosis, botulism), and parasites (internal worms, external mites and lice). Vaccination, deworming, and biosecurity are your three profit-protection pillars. Everything else in this module supports them.</p>

<h3>Biosecurity: The Cheapest Insurance on Your Farm</h3>

<p>Biosecurity is not a cost it is avoided loss. A single outbreak of duck plague can wipe out a year's profit. Implement three zones: <strong>clean</strong> (feed store, brooder), <strong>transition</strong> (footbath, changing area), and <strong>dirty</strong> (market crates, visitor vehicles). Never let a visitor walk from the market crate directly into your brooder. Use a footbath with disinfectant (e.g., 2% sodium hypochlorite or quaternary ammonium) at every entry. Keep different age groups separated ducklings are far more vulnerable than adults. Source stock only from hatcheries with vaccination records. Quarantine new birds for 14 days before mixing.</p>

<h3>Parasite Control: Internal and External</h3>

<p>Internal parasites (roundworms, tapeworms, gapeworms) rob feed conversion silently. You will notice it as "the ducks eat well but don't gain." Deworm every 812 weeks with a vet-approved product, and rotate between drug classes (benzimidazoles, imidazothiazoles) to slow resistance. External parasites mites, lice, ticks thrive in damp litter and crowded housing. Provide dry dust-bathing areas (sand + wood ash), inspect under wings and around the vent weekly, and treat housing with approved acaricides. In free-range systems, rotate grazing areas and avoid stagnant water where snails (intermediate hosts) breed.</p>

<h3>First Aid and Treating Injuries</h3>

<p>Ducks injure themselves on wire, sharp feeders, and during transport. Keep a farm first-aid kit: antiseptic (povidone-iodine), clean gauze, vet wrap, tweezers, and a small isolation cage. For cuts: clean with saline, apply antiseptic, bandage lightly ducks heal fast if the wound is clean. For bumblefoot (swollen foot pad): soak in warm salt water, remove the kernel if trained, apply antibiotic ointment, and change bedding to soft, dry material. For fractures: immobilize and call a vet; do not attempt surgery. For heat stress: provide shade and cool water immediately ducks pant and can die within hours in 35C+ heat.</p>

<h3>Working with a Veterinarian</h3>

<p>Find a vet <em>before</em> you need one. In most African countries, district veterinary officers provide subsidised or free advisory services; private poultry vets charge per visit but save far more than they cost. Build a relationship: invite the vet for a quarterly flock inspection, keep written records of mortality, feed consumption, and treatments, and ask for a written diagnosis whenever medication is prescribed. A vet who knows your farm will diagnose faster by phone when an outbreak starts. Never self-prescribe antibiotics misuse creates resistance and leaves residues that can disqualify your birds from premium markets.</p>

<ul>
<li> Walk the flock twice daily count, observe droppings, listen for coughing or sneezing.</li>
<li> Footbath at every house entry, refreshed with disinfectant every 23 days.</li>
<li> Vaccination records for every batch date, vaccine, batch number, route.</li>
<li> Deworm every 812 weeks; rotate drug class; respect withdrawal periods.</li>
<li> Clean waterers daily; sanitise drinkers with chlorine or iodine solution.</li>
<li> Quarantine all new birds for 14 days in a separate pen.</li>
<li> Isolate sick birds immediately; call the vet within 24 hours.</li>
<li> Dispose of dead birds by deep burial or incineration never in water.</li>
</ul>
