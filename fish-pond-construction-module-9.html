Lessons

1. Testing Water Parameters: pH, DO, Ammonia

Water quality is the foundation of fish health. You must test regularly. For pH, use a liquid test kit or electronic meter. Ideal range for tilapia and catfish is 6.5 to 8.5. Test at least twice a week, early morning. If pH is below 6.5, add agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) at 100-200 kg per hectare. If above 8.5, add gypsum or partially exchange water. Dissolved oxygen (DO) is critical. Fish need at least 4 mg/L; below 2 mg/L they gasp at surface. Use a DO meter or chemical test kit. Test at dawn when DO is lowest. If low, increase aeration by splashing water or using a paddle wheel aerator. Ammonia is toxic. Unionized ammonia (NH3) is deadly above 0.1 mg/L. Test with a kit. Ammonia comes from fish waste and uneaten feed. To reduce, stop feeding for a day, increase aeration, and do a 30% water exchange. In Africa, many farmers use simple tools: a secchi disk for clarity, a thermometer, and locally made test kits from agricultural suppliers. Record all readings in a notebook. Always test before and after adding any treatment. Remember, consistent monitoring prevents fish kills. Start with a basic kit: pH, ammonia, and DO. These three tests will save your fish.

2. Water Treatment: Chlorination and Dechlorination

Chlorine is used to disinfect water, killing bacteria and viruses. But chlorine is also toxic to fish. You must dechlorinate before adding water to your pond. For chlorination, use calcium hypochlorite (bleaching powder) at 5-10 mg/L for empty ponds. For water supply, if using municipal water, it may already contain chlorine. Test with a chlorine test kit. To dechlorinate, you can use sodium thiosulfate. Dose: 1 gram per 100 liters of water to neutralize 1 mg/L chlorine. Alternatively, let water sit in an open tank for 24-48 hours in sunlight; chlorine evaporates. In rural Africa, many farmers use rainwater or well water, which may not need chlorination. But if you must treat, always dechlorinate. For emergency fish health, potassium permanganate at 2-4 mg/L can treat external parasites, but it also kills beneficial bacteria. Use only in isolated tanks. Never chlorinate a pond with fish. If you accidentally add chlorine, immediately add sodium thiosulfate or do a large water exchange. Store chemicals in a dry, dark place. Wear gloves and goggles. Measure carefully; overdose can kill everything. After dechlorination, test again to confirm zero chlorine. Then add water to pond. Always have a backup water source. In Africa, sun-drying water in black tanks is a cheap dechlorination method. Remember: chlorine kills fish, so treat with respect.

3. Biological Filtration and Nitrification

Biological filtration uses beneficial bacteria to convert toxic ammonia into less harmful nitrate. This process is called nitrification. Two types of bacteria do the work: Nitrosomonas convert ammonia to nitrite, and Nitrobacter convert nitrite to nitrate. Nitrate is safe up to 200 mg/L for most fish. To grow these bacteria, you need a biofilter with high surface area. Common materials: plastic bioballs, lava rock, or even cut plastic bottles. Water must flow over the media. In Africa, many farmers build trickle filters using buckets filled with gravel and sand. The bacteria colonize within 4-6 weeks. Start by adding a source of ammonia (fish waste or ammonium chloride) to feed them. Keep pH between 7 and 8, temperature 25-30C. Avoid chlorinated water; it kills bacteria. If you use chlorinated water, dechlorinate first. Test for ammonia and nitrite daily during startup. When both read zero, your filter is cycled. Then you can add fish gradually. For maintenance, rinse filter media in pond water, never tap water. If ammonia spikes, do a 50% water exchange and add a bacterial starter culture. Commercial starters are available, but you can also use mud from a healthy pond. In a well-cycled pond, nitrification happens naturally in the pond bottom and on walls. But a dedicated biofilter allows higher stocking densities. Always have backup aeration. Nitrification consumes oxygen, so DO must be above 4 mg/L. Monitor nitrite; it is toxic above 0.5 mg/L. With proper filtration, your fish will thrive.

4. Managing Algae and Aquatic Plants

Algae are microscopic plants that turn water green. A little algae is goodit produces oxygen and feeds fish. But too much causes problems: low oxygen at night, clogged filters, and off-flavor in fish. To control algae, reduce nutrients. Don't overfeed; uneaten feed fuels algae. Maintain a healthy plant cover: floating plants like water hyacinth or water lettuce shade the pond and absorb nutrients. But be carefulwater hyacinth can become invasive. Use 10-20% coverage. For string algae, manually remove with a rake. For planktonic algae (green water), use barley straw: place 50 g per square meter in a mesh bag. As it decomposes, it releases compounds that inhibit algae. In Africa, many farmers use tilapia to control algaethey graze on it. Stock 2-3 tilapia per square meter. For serious blooms, use copper sulfate at 0.5 mg/L, but this is toxic to fish at higher doses and kills invertebrates. Better to use aeration and water exchange. Aquatic plants like water lilies provide shade and habitat, but they can cover the pond. Limit to 25% coverage. Remove dead plants to prevent oxygen depletion. If you see blue-green algae (cyanobacteria), it's toxic. Do not let fish eat it. Treat with a 30% water exchange and add aeration. Test for ammonia and nitritehigh nutrients cause blooms. In summary, balance is key: some algae and plants are beneficial, but too much is harmful. Monitor weekly and act early.

5. Emergency Water Quality Interventions

Emergencies happen: sudden fish kills, gasping, or erratic swimming. Act fast. First, identify the problem. Test pH, ammonia, nitrite, and DO immediately. If fish gasp at surface, DO is low. Turn on all aerators, splash water, or add fresh water. If ammonia is high, do a 50% water exchange with dechlorinated water. Stop feeding for 24-48 hours. Add zeolite or activated carbon to absorb ammonia. If pH is crash (below 5), add agricultural lime at 200 kg/ha. If pH is too high (above 9), add gypsum or do a water exchange. For chlorine emergency, add sodium thiosulfate at 1 g per 100 L. For nitrite spike, add salt (sodium chloride) at 0.1% (1 kg per 1000 L) to block nitrite toxicity. Always have emergency supplies: dechlorinator, lime, salt, and a backup pump. In Africa, power cuts are common. Use a generator or manual paddle wheel. If you have no electricity, use a bucket to splash water for hours. For fish kills, remove dead fish immediately to prevent ammonia spike. Do not feed until water quality is stable. After the crisis, review your monitoring records to prevent recurrence. Keep a emergency contact for local extension officer. Remember, quick action saves fish. Never add chemicals without testing first. In an emergency, water exchange is your best friend. Have a reserve tank of dechlorinated water ready. Train your workers on emergency procedures. A calm, systematic response is key.

6. Record Keeping and Monitoring Systems

Record keeping is not paperworkit's your early warning system. Keep a logbook for each pond. Record daily: water temperature (morning and evening), pH, DO, ammonia, nitrite, and fish behavior. Also record feeding amounts, fish deaths, and treatments. Use a simple notebook or a whiteboard near the pond. In Africa, many farmers use a calendar to mark testing days. Test at least twice a week for pH and ammonia, daily for DO in intensive systems. For DO, test at dawn. For pH, test at 6 AM and 2 PM. Write down everything. After a month, you'll see patterns: when ammonia rises, when algae blooms. This helps you predict problems. For example, if pH drops every morning, you know to add lime. If ammonia spikes after feeding, reduce feed. Share records with your extension officer. Use a smartphone to take photos of test results. Create a simple graph: plot ammonia over time. If it trends up, act. For monitoring systems, you can install a floating thermometer and a secchi disk. For DO, a battery-powered meter is best. In remote areas, use a chemical test kit. Always calibrate meters. Store kits in a cool, dry place. At the end of each month, review your records. Calculate average pH and ammonia. Set targets: pH 7-8, ammonia <0.1 mg/L. If off, adjust. Keep records for at least one year. This data helps you improve. Remember, what gets measured gets managed. Good records lead to healthy fish and higher profits.

<h2>Practical Reference: Water Quality &amp; Treatment for African Fish Ponds</h2>

<p>Water quality is the single most important factor determining whether a fish pond makes money or loses money. In African small- and medium-scale aquaculture tilapia, catfish (<em>Clarias</em>), and mixed systems poor water management typically accounts for 6080% of preventable stock losses. This Practical Reference gives you the numbers, the tests, and the treatment protocols you need to run a profitable pond. Keep it printed in the farm office and refer to it daily.</p>

<p><strong>Business-first principle:</strong> every naira, shilling, cedi, or rand spent on a water test kit or a bag of lime should be justified by reduced mortality, faster growth, or lower feed conversion ratio (FCR). A basic test kit costing the equivalent of 23 kg of feed can prevent a total stock loss worth 50200 kg of feed. The return on investment is not theoretical it is the difference between a viable farm and a hobby.</p>

<h3>1. Testing Water Parameters: pH, DO, Ammonia</h3>

<p>Test at least twice daily early morning (05:0007:00) and late afternoon (15:0017:00). Morning readings capture the dissolved oxygen (DO) low point; afternoon readings capture the pH high point. Record every reading. Without records, you are guessing.</p>

<p><strong>pH:</strong> Low pH (acidic) reduces fish growth and makes ammonia more toxic. High pH (alkaline) makes ammonia far more toxic. In many African ponds particularly in the humid tropics and areas with acid sulphate soils liming is essential. Apply agricultural lime (CaCO) at 200500 kg/ha to raise alkalinity and buffer pH swings. Do not use quicklime (CaO) directly into a stocked pond.</p>

<p><strong>Dissolved Oxygen:</strong> DO is the most common cause of fish kills in African ponds. DO is produced by phytoplankton during daylight and consumed by all organisms (including phytoplankton) at night. The critical period is just before dawn. If morning DO drops below 3 mg/L, reduce or stop feeding for the day and prepare to aerate. A simple splash aerator, paddle wheel, or even a pump recirculating water can save a crop. In the dry season, when water levels drop and temperatures rise, DO problems intensify.</p>

<p><strong>Ammonia:</strong> Ammonia is the primary nitrogenous waste from fish and the end product of protein breakdown. Total ammonia exists in two forms: ionised (NH), which is relatively harmless, and unionised (NH), which is highly toxic. The proportion of toxic NH increases with higher pH and temperature. At pH 8.5 and 28 C, roughly 15% of total ammonia is in the toxic form. Keep total ammonia below 1 mg/L and unionised ammonia below 0.05 mg/L. Ammonia spikes are common after overfeeding, after a phytoplankton crash, or when the pond has no biological filter.</p>

<h3>2. Water Treatment: Chlorination and Dechlorination</h3>

<p>Chlorination is used to disinfect water before it enters the pond particularly when filling from municipal supplies, boreholes, or surface water that may carry pathogens. Chlorine kills bacteria, viruses, and many parasites. However, chlorine is also toxic to fish at very low concentrations (0.020.05 mg/L residual chlorine can kill fry). Therefore, <strong>dechlorination is mandatory</strong> before any chlorinated water reaches a stocked pond.</p>

<h3>3. Biological Filtration and Nitrification</h3>

<p>Biological filtration is the engine of water quality in intensive and semi-intensive ponds. Nitrifying bacteria <em>Nitrosomonas</em> (ammonia nitrite) and <em>Nitrobacter</em> (nitrite nitrate) colonise surfaces: pond walls, substrate, plant roots, and dedicated biofilter media. These bacteria are slow-growing (especially <em>Nitrobacter</em>) and sensitive to pH, temperature, and chlorine. In new ponds, it takes 48 weeks to establish a full nitrifying population. This is why "new pond syndrome" ammonia and nitrite spikes kills fish in newly stocked ponds.</p>

<p><strong>Practical biofilter options for African ponds:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Submerged media beds:</strong> use locally available materials gravel, crushed brick, bamboo sections, or plastic bio-balls in a flow-through chamber. Minimum surface area: 200500 m per m of media.</li>
<li><strong>Moving bed biofilm reactors (MBBR):</strong> plastic media suspended in aerated water. High efficiency, but requires reliable aeration.</li>
<li><strong>Constructed wetlands:</strong> plant <em>Typha</em> (bulrush), <em>Phragmites</em> (reed), or <em>Eichhornia</em> (water hyacinth manage carefully, it is invasive). These take up nitrate and provide surface area for bacteria.</li>
<li><strong>Pond-bottom substrate:</strong> in earthen ponds, the mud-water interface supports nitrification. Do not over-clean or lime excessively you will destroy the biofilm.</li>
</ul>

<p>To boost nitrification: maintain pH 78, alkalinity above 50 mg/L, DO above 4 mg/L, and temperature above 20 C. Add a commercial nitrifying bacteria starter if available, or seed from an established pond's filter media. Never chlorinate a biofilter you will kill the bacteria and crash your system.</p>

<h3>4. Managing Algae and Aquatic Plants</h3>

<p>Phytoplankton (microscopic algae) is the foundation of pond productivity. A healthy "green water" pond visibility 3045 cm (Secchi disk) produces oxygen, consumes ammonia, and provides natural food. But too much algae causes nighttime oxygen crashes and pH swings. Too little means ammonia accumulates.</p>

<p><strong>Management strategies:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Colour and visibility:</strong> aim for a green colour with 3045 cm Secchi depth. Dark green or blue-green (cyanobacteria) indicates problems reduce feeding, increase water exchange.</li>
<li><strong>Floating plants:</strong> water hyacinth, water lettuce, and <em>Azolla</em> can cover 1020% of pond surface to shade out excess algae and take up nutrients. Do not let them cover more than 30% they block light and oxygen.</li>
<li><strong>Submerged plants:</strong> <em>Ceratophyllum</em> and <em>Hydrilla</em> provide habitat but can become invasive. Control by manual removal or grass carp (where legal).</li>
<li><strong>Barley straw:</strong> a traditional, low-cost method 50 g/m of barley straw in mesh bags releases compounds that inhibit algae. Replace every 34 months.</li>
<li><strong>Chemical control:</strong> use only approved algicides (e.g., copper sulphate at 0.51.0 mg/L) with extreme caution. Copper is toxic to fish at higher doses and accumulates in sediments. Never treat more than 1/3 of the pond at once decaying algae consumes oxygen.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Emergency Water Quality Interventions</h3>

<p>When fish are gasping at the surface, lying on their sides, or dying, act immediately:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low DO:</strong> turn on all aerators, add fresh water, reduce or stop feeding, and if possible, flush part of the pond. Emergency aeration with a pump or even manual splashing can save fish.</li>
<li><strong>Ammonia spike:</strong> stop feeding immediately, increase aeration, add fresh water, and if available, add a zeolite or ammonia-removing filter media. Do not add lime it raises pH and makes ammonia more toxic.</li>
<li><strong>pH crash (acidic):</strong> add agricultural lime (CaCO) at 100200 kg/ha, or sodium bicarbonate at 1020 g/m. Do not use quicklime in a stocked pond.</li>
<li><strong>pH spike (alkaline):</strong> add gypsum (calcium sulphate) at 100200 kg/ha, or partially exchange water. Avoid adding lime.</li>
<li><strong>Chlorine contamination:</strong> add sodium thiosulphate immediately (see Table 2), increase aeration, and stop water inflow.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Record Keeping and Monitoring Systems</h3>

<p>You cannot manage what you do not measure. A simple notebook or spreadsheet with daily entries is the most valuable tool on the farm. Record: date, time, temperature, pH, DO, ammonia (if tested), feeding amount, fish behaviour, mortality count, and any treatments applied. Review weekly to spot trends. A drop in morning DO over three consecutive days is an early warning act before fish die.</p>

<p><strong>Minimum monitoring schedule:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Daily:</strong> temperature (2), pH (2), DO (2), fish behaviour, mortality count, feed consumed.</li>
<li><strong>Weekly:</strong> ammonia, nitrite, alkalinity, Secchi depth, water level.</li>
<li><strong>Monthly:</strong> full water analysis (if possible), weight sampling, FCR calculation.</li>
<li><strong>Seasonally:</strong> pond bottom condition, liming, biofilter maintenance.</li>
</ul>

<p>Use a simple paper form or a mobile app (e.g., AquaManager, Farmnote) if available. In areas with unreliable electricity, a waterproof notebook and pencil work perfectly. Photograph your records monthly as a backup.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Test pH and DO twice daily</strong> morning and afternoon. Record every reading.</li>
<li><strong>Keep DO above 5 mg/L.</strong> If morning DO falls below 3 mg/L, stop feeding and aerate immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Keep unionised ammonia below 0.05 mg/L.</strong> Test weekly; if high, stop feeding and increase aeration.</li>
<li><strong>Never add chlorinated water to a stocked pond.</strong> Always dechlorinate with sodium thiosulphate and test residual chlorine before stocking.</li>

<li><strong>Manage algae.</strong> Aim for 3045 cm Secchi depth. Never treat more than 1/3 of the pond with algicide at once.</li>
<li><strong>Keep daily records.</strong> Review weekly. A written record is your best insurance against stock loss.</li>
</ol>
