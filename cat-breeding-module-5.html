1. Breeding Soundness Examination of the Stallion

A breeding soundness examination (BSE) checks if a stallion can breed successfully. In Africa, where veterinary services may be limited, you can do a basic BSE yourself before calling a vet. First, observe the stallion's general health: body condition score (1-9 scale, aim for 5-6), bright eyes, shiny coat. Check the reproductive organs by palpation. The testes should be firm, oval, and freely movable within the scrotum. Each testis in a mature stallion measures about 8-12 cm long and 5-7 cm wide. Small, hard, or uneven testes indicate problems. The penis should be free of scars, tumors (like squamous cell carcinoma), or discharge. Smegma (dirty black substance) is normal but should not have a foul smell. Next, assess libido. A healthy stallion shows interest in a mare in heat within 1-2 minutes. If he ignores her, he may have low testosterone or pain. Collect semen using a condom or artificial vagina (see Lesson 2) and evaluate sperm motility and morphology. Normal motility is >60% progressively motile sperm. Normal morphology is >70% normal sperm. If below these, the stallion may still be fertile but needs more testing. In Africa, common issues include malnutrition (especially low protein and minerals like zinc and selenium), tick-borne diseases (e.g., equine piroplasmosis) causing fever and temporary infertility, and injuries from poor handling. Always test for equine infectious anemia (EIA) and contagious equine metritis (CEM) if you plan to sell semen or move the stallion. A BSE should be repeated 2-3 times over 6-8 weeks for accuracy. Record all findings: testis size, libido score, semen volume, concentration, motility, and morphology. This baseline helps you detect changes later. If any parameter is abnormal, consult a vet. Remember, a stallion can be subfertile but still father foals; the BSE tells you how many mares he can cover per season. In Africa, where pastures may be poor, supplement with 1-2 kg of concentrate per day plus free-choice mineral licks to support sperm production.

2. Semen Collection Techniques and Equipment

Collecting semen from a stallion requires a mare in heat (or a phantom mare), an artificial vagina (AV), and a jump mare or dummy. In Africa, many breeders use a jump mare: a mare in standing heat restrained in stocks. The stallion mounts, and you guide his penis into the AV. The AV is a cylinder with a rubber liner filled with warm water (45-50Â°C) and lubricated inside. A collection bottle attaches to the end. The temperature and pressure must be right: too hot burns the penis, too cold prevents ejaculation. Use a non-spermicidal lubricant like K-Y Jelly. Have a handler hold the mare's head and another control the stallion's lead. Approach from the side, not behind, to avoid kicks. As the stallion thrusts, hold the AV at a 45-degree angle to his body. He will ejaculate in 3-5 jets over 10-30 seconds. Immediately after collection, remove the AV and keep the bottle warm (37Â°C) by wrapping it in a towel or placing it in a thermos. If you don't have an AV, you can use a condom (e.g., EquiPak) fitted over the penis after washing. But AV gives cleaner samples. For a phantom mare, build a sturdy dummy with a padded top and a hole for the AV. Train the stallion to mount it using a mare in heat first. Always wash the stallion's penis with warm water and mild soap before collection to reduce bacteria. Collect in a clean, quiet area away from other horses. In Africa, where electricity may be unreliable, use a battery-powered microscope and a portable incubator. If you cannot collect fresh, you can use a condom and immediately dilute with extender (see Lesson 3). Never use a dirty AV: bacteria cause endometritis in mares. After collection, evaluate the semen within 10 minutes. If you need to transport, keep it at 37Â°C for up to 1 hour, then cool slowly. Record volume (usually 50-120 ml), color (milky white is good; yellow or bloody is bad), and consistency. Gel fraction (the thick part) should be filtered out using a gauze or a special filter. A good stallion can be collected every other day, up to 3 times per week, without losing fertility. Overuse leads to low sperm count. Always have a vet check the AV and equipment regularly.

3. Semen Evaluation and Processing

After collecting semen, evaluate it within 10 minutes. You need a microscope (100x and 400x magnification), a warm stage (37Â°C), slides, cover slips, and a counting chamber (hemocytometer). First, look at the raw sample: volume (measure in a graduated cylinder), color (opaque milky white is ideal; clear or watery means low sperm), and gel (remove with gauze). Next, check motility. Place a drop on a warm slide, add a cover slip, and view at 100x. Count 10 fields. Progressive motility means sperm swim forward in a straight line. Normal is >60%. If less, the sample may have been too cold or old. Then, assess concentration using a hemocytometer or a densimeter. A normal stallion ejaculate has 100-300 million sperm per ml. Total sperm per ejaculate = volume x concentration. A good ejaculate has 2-8 billion sperm. For fertility, you need at least 500 million progressively motile sperm per breeding dose. Morphology: mix a drop of semen with a stain (e.g., eosin-nigrosin), smear, and view at 400x. Count 100 sperm. Normal >70% have normal heads and tails. Defects like bent tails, double heads, or cytoplasmic droplets reduce fertility. If you don't have a microscope, you can do a rough check: a drop of semen on a slide should look like milk, not water. For processing, extend the semen with a commercial extender (e.g., INRA96, Kenney's) at a 1:1 to 1:4 ratio. Warm the extender to 37Â°C. Add it slowly, mixing gently. This provides nutrients and buffers. For cooled transport, use a skim milk-glucose extender. For freezing, use a lactose-egg yolk-glycerol extender. In Africa, where heat is a challenge, process in an air-conditioned room or early morning. If no extender, you can use pasteurized skim milk (heated to 92Â°C for 10 minutes, then cooled) as a short-term extender. After extending, package in sterile bags or tubes. Label with stallion name, date, and sperm count. Keep at 37Â°C for immediate use, or cool to 5Â°C for storage up to 48 hours. Never freeze without proper cryoprotectant. Record all data in a breeding log. Poor semen quality may improve with better nutrition (add 100 g of sunflower seeds daily for vitamin E) or rest. If quality remains low, consult a vet for possible infection or hormonal issues.

4. Cooled and Frozen Semen Handling

Cooled semen (also called fresh-cooled) is used within 24-48 hours. After collection and evaluation, extend the semen with a cold-shock protectant extender (e.g., skim milk-glucose). The extender must be at 37Â°C when added. Then, cool slowly: place the extended semen in a styrofoam box with ice packs, or use a commercial cooling unit. The goal is to reach 5Â°C over 1-2 hours. Rapid cooling kills sperm. For transport, use a shipping container with a coolant (e.g., gel packs) that maintains 5Â°C. In Africa, where roads may be rough, use a sturdy insulated box. Label clearly. On arrival, warm the semen to 37Â°C in a water bath before insemination. Check motility again; it should be >30% after cooling. If lower, the semen may have been damaged. For frozen semen, the process is more complex. Use a freezing extender with 2-5% glycerol as cryoprotectant. Equilibrate at 5Â°C for 1-2 hours. Then, package in 0.5 ml straws. Freeze using a programmable freezer or a nitrogen vapor method: place straws on a rack 5 cm above liquid nitrogen for 10 minutes, then plunge into liquid nitrogen. Store in a liquid nitrogen tank at -196Â°C. In Africa, liquid nitrogen may be hard to get; plan ahead. Frozen semen can last years. To thaw, remove straw from nitrogen, hold in air for 10 seconds, then plunge into 37Â°C water for 30 seconds. Wipe dry and load into an insemination pipette. Thawed semen must be used within 15 minutes. Motility after thaw should be >30% for acceptable fertility. Frozen semen often has lower fertility than fresh, so inseminate 2-3 times per cycle. Always keep records: date frozen, stallion, sperm count, motility before and after. Handle liquid nitrogen with care: wear gloves and face shield. Never store frozen semen in a frost-free freezer. For cooled semen, if you cannot ship, you can inseminate on-farm within 12 hours. In Africa, many breeders use cooled semen to share stallions across farms. Test your cooling and freezing protocol with a trial before breeding valuable mares. If you lack equipment, collaborate with a vet or a stud farm that has a lab.

5. Stallion Behavior and Handling for Breeding

Stallions are powerful and can be dangerous. Good handling keeps you and the horse safe. Always use a sturdy halter and a lead rope with a chain. Never wrap the rope around your hand. Approach the stallion from the side, speak calmly, and watch his ears and tail. A pinned ear or swishing tail means he is angry. For breeding, use a breeding halter or a twitch on the nose for control. If the stallion is aggressive, use a lip chain or a stud chain over the nose. In Africa, many stallions are kept in small paddocks and may be frustrated. Daily exercise and turnout reduce aggression. Before breeding, let the stallion see the mare but not mount. This teases him and increases arousal. Use a jump mare in heat. If the mare is not in heat, she may kick and injure the stallion. Check her by teasing with a stallion across a fence: she will squat and urinate if in heat. During mounting, have one handler hold the mare's head and another hold the stallion's lead. Stand to the side, not behind. If the stallion is nervous, use a blindfold or ear plugs. After breeding, wash the stallion's penis with warm water and mild soap to prevent infection. Allow him to rest for 30 minutes. Some stallions need to be collected with an artificial vagina rather than natural cover to avoid injury. For natural cover, wrap the mare's tail and use a breeding roll (a padded roll) to prevent the stallion from penetrating too deep. If the stallion is a kicker, use a muzzle. Never punish a stallion during breeding; it causes lasting behavioral problems. Instead, reward calm behavior with a pat or a treat. In Africa, where many stallions are kept for work as well as breeding, separate breeding from work. A stallion used for plowing may be too tired to breed. Train him to a routine: tease, mount, ejaculate, dismount. Consistency reduces stress. If a stallion refuses to breed, check for pain (back, legs, testicles) or poor nutrition. A vet can test testosterone levels. For hand breeding, use a phantom mare if the stallion is aggressive to live mares. Always have a first-aid kit nearby: iodine, bandages, and a phone to call a vet. Remember, a calm handler makes a calm stallion.

6. Managing the Breeding Stallion's Health and Nutrition

A breeding stallion needs extra care to produce good semen. Feed a balanced diet: good-quality hay (e.g., alfalfa or Rhodes grass) at 1.5-2% of body weight per day. For a 500 kg stallion, that's 7.5-10 kg of hay. Add 2-4 kg of concentrate (e.g., oats, barley, or commercial stud mix) during breeding season. Protein should be 12-14% of total diet. Add 100-200 g of sunflower seeds or linseed for omega-3 fats, which improve sperm motility. Minerals: zinc (100 mg/day) and selenium (1-2 mg/day) are critical. In Africa, many soils are deficient in selenium; use a mineral lick or supplement. Vitamin E (1000 IU/day) protects sperm. Fresh water always available. A stallion can drink 40-60 liters per day in heat. Exercise daily: 30-60 minutes of trotting or turnout in a paddock. Obesity lowers fertility; keep body condition score 5-6. Health care: deworm every 8 weeks with ivermectin or praziquantel. Vaccinate against tetanus, influenza, and African horse sickness (where endemic). Check teeth every 6 months; sharp points cause weight loss. Hooves trimmed every 6-8 weeks. Common African diseases: trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness) causes fever and infertility; control tsetse flies. Equine piroplasmosis (tick-borne) causes anemia and jaundice; use tick control. Strangles and EVA (equine viral arteritis) can cause temporary infertility. Test for EVA before breeding. If the stallion has a fever, stop breeding. Stress from heat: provide shade and fans. In hot climates, breed early morning or evening. Rest the stallion one day between collections. Overuse leads to low sperm count and poor libido. A stallion can breed 2-3 times per week for a full season (e.g., 20-30 mares). Keep records of each breeding: date, mare, semen quality. If fertility drops, check nutrition, then health, then semen. A vet can do a blood test for testosterone and thyroid. In Africa, where vet services may be far, learn to do a basic physical exam: temperature (37.5-38.5Â°C), heart rate (28-40 beats/min), and respiration (8-16 breaths/min). Any abnormality means rest and call a vet. Finally, handle the stallion gently; stress lowers sperm quality.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Reproductive Management of the Stallion



For the African horse breeder, the stallion is both the genetic engine and the single largest recurring cost centre of the operation. A stallion that is sub-fertile, injured during collection, or sidelined by preventable disease does not simply lose one breeding season â it erodes the value of every mare booked to him, delays foal crops, and damages the reputation that underpins stud fees. This module is written business-first: every technique below exists to protect revenue, reduce veterinary spend, and raise the number of live foals per mare bred.



Across Africa, horse breeding is concentrated in South Africa (Thoroughbred, Warmblood, Arab), Morocco, Tunisia, Egypt, Kenya, Nigeria, Ethiopia and Zimbabwe, with growing sport-horse and endurance markets. The practical constraint is rarely the stallion's libido â it is access to reliable electricity for freezers, consistent cold-chain for cooled semen, trained technicians, and affordable veterinary laboratory time. The reference below is therefore built around what can be done reliably on-farm with basic equipment, with clear thresholds for when to call a veterinarian.





1. Breeding Soundness Examination (BSE): The Commercial Gate



A BSE is not a formality â it is due diligence. Buyers of stud services, insurers, and syndicate partners increasingly demand a BSE within 30 days of the season. The exam has four commercial outputs: (a) can this stallion breed safely, (b) can he produce adequate sperm numbers, (c) is his semen shippable, and (d) what is his realistic book size this season.



Minimum BSE components for an African stud farm: full history (previous fertility, illness, medications, injuries), general physical exam, examination of testes/epididymides/penis/prepuce, two semen collections at least one hour apart, and laboratory semen analysis. Testicular volume should be measured â a 500 kg Thoroughbred stallion typically carries 150â300 cmÂ³ total testicular volume; below 100 cmÂ³ is a red flag for sub-fertility.



2. Semen Collection Techniques and Equipment



The artificial vagina (AV) remains the standard. For on-farm African conditions, the Missouri-model AV with a disposable liner is the most practical: it is robust, easy to clean, and can be operated with a hot-water jacket if electricity is intermittent. A breeding phantom (dummy) is strongly recommended â it eliminates the risk of the stallion injuring a mare or the handler, and it reduces collection time.



Equipment checklist for a basic collection kit:



  • Missouri-model AV, 60â65 Â°C water jacket, lubricated disposable liner

  • Collection bottle insulated at 37 Â°C (use a thermos or heated sleeve)

  • Portable microscope + pre-warmed slides + Makler or Neubauer counting chamber

  • Cooled-semen shipping container (Equitainer or equivalent) with ice packs



For a stallion with penile or behavioural issues, a condom (internal collection) can be used, but semen quality is generally lower. Never collect more than once per day during the breeding season unless directed by a veterinarian; twice daily is acceptable for a short period in a high-value stallion under supervision.

5. Stallion Behaviour and Handling for Breeding



Behaviour is a commercial variable. A stallion that cannot be collected safely costs the farm money in staff time, veterinary treatment and lost bookings. Train young stallions to the phantom before their first breeding season, using positive reinforcement and short, calm sessions. Key rules:



  • Never punish a stallion for normal sexual behaviour; redirect instead.

  • Use a nose twitch only as a last resort and never during collection.

  • Keep the collection area quiet, with consistent handlers and routine.

  • Wash the penis with warm water and non-spermicidal soap before and after collection.

  • Limit collection to one ejaculate per day in the breeding season unless under veterinary advice.

  • Monitor for "phantom" behaviour â a stallion that mounts but does not ejaculate may have musculoskeletal pain.



6. Managing the Breeding Stallion's Health and Nutrition



A breeding stallion in work needs 20â30% more digestible energy than a maintenance horse, plus high-quality protein (lysine â¥ 0.5% of dry matter). In African conditions, where good-quality forage may be scarce in the dry season, supplement with a balanced concentrate and ensure free access to clean water and salt. Body condition score should be 5â6 on the Henneke 1â9 scale â neither fat nor thin.



Core health programme: annual influenza, tetanus, rabies (where endemic), African horse sickness (AHS) vaccination in endemic zones, and regular deworming based on faecal egg counts. In AHS-endemic areas of South Africa and parts of East Africa, vaccination is essential and movement is regulated â check with the national veterinary authority before shipping semen or moving the stallion. Also screen for equine arteritis virus (EAV) â carrier stallions shed the virus in semen and can infect mares; testing is mandatory for many stud books and for international semen export.



Teeth should be checked every 6 months; a stallion in pain will not eat or breed well. Hooves should be trimmed every 6â8 weeks. Lameness is a common cause of poor collection performance and must be investigated early.
