1. What is First Aid and Why It Matters

First aid is the immediate help you give to someone who is injured or suddenly ill, using simple skills and everyday items. In Africa, where ambulances can take 30 minutes or more to arrive in rural areas, your actions in the first 10 minutes often determine whether a person lives or dies. For example, severe bleeding from a machete cut can kill in 5 minutes. Applying direct pressure with a clean cloth can stop it. That is first aid.

In a business settinga shop, farm, or workshopfirst aid matters because accidents happen: a worker cuts a hand, a customer faints from heatstroke, a child swallows a coin. Without basic training, people panic or do nothing. With training, you can save a life, reduce pain, and prevent small injuries from becoming permanent disabilities. The key aims are: preserve life, prevent worsening, and promote recovery.

You do not need expensive equipment. A basic first aid kit can be made from a plastic box with: sterile gauze (4x4 inches), adhesive bandages, roller bandages (2 and 4 inches), medical tape, scissors, disposable gloves, a CPR face shield, antiseptic wipes, and a thermal blanket. In many African markets, these items cost less than 500 KES or 50 NGN total. Keep one at your business and one in your car.

Remember the three Ps: Preserve life, Prevent injury, Promote recovery. Also remember your limits: you are not a doctor. Your job is to stabilize and get help. If someone is not breathing, start CPR. If they are bleeding, apply pressure. If they are burned, cool with clean water. Simple, fast action saves lives. This module will teach you exactly how to do that, step by step, with no prior medical training needed.

2. Legal and Ethical Considerations: Good Samaritan Laws and Consent

Many African countries have Good Samaritan laws that protect you from being sued if you help in an emergency. For example, South Africa's Protection of Persons and Property Act, Nigeria's Civil Liability Act, and Kenya's Health Act all provide some protection as long as you act in good faith, without gross negligence, and within your training. This means you should not fear helping. However, you must still act reasonably: do not perform surgery, do not give injections, and do not move someone with a suspected neck injury unless they are in immediate danger.

Consent is simple: before touching someone, ask, 'Can I help you?' If they say yes, you have consent. If they are unconscious, the law assumes they would consent to life-saving help. For children, get consent from a parent or guardian if present. If not, treat as an emergency. For a person who is confused or intoxicated, still try to get verbal consent, but if they refuse, do not force help unless their life is in danger. Document what you did and when.

Ethically, you must respect privacy and dignity. Do not share photos or details on social media. Do not discuss the person's condition with others. Keep their information confidential. Also, do not abandon someone after starting carestay until help arrives or you are too exhausted to continue.

In a business, you should have a written emergency action plan that includes consent procedures and contact numbers. Train your staff annually. Keep a log of incidents. If you follow these steps, you protect yourself and your business. Remember: the goal is to help, not to be a hero. Act within your skills, get consent when possible, and always call for professional help.

3. The Chain of Survival and Emergency Medical Services

The Chain of Survival is a sequence of actions that gives a person the best chance of surviving a cardiac arrest or major emergency. In Africa, the chain has five links: 1) Early recognition and calling for help, 2) Early CPR, 3) Early defibrillation (if available), 4) Advanced medical care, and 5) Post-resuscitation care. Your role is in the first two links: recognize the emergency and start CPR. In many African communities, the weakest link is the firstpeople wait too long to call for help.

Emergency Medical Services (EMS) vary widely. In cities like Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg, you can dial 112 or 999 (depending on country) and get an ambulance in 1020 minutes. In rural areas, you may need to use a private car, motorcycle, or bicycle to transport the patient. Know your local numbers: Kenya 999 or 112, Nigeria 112, South Africa 10177, Ghana 193, Uganda 999. Save these in your phone now.

When you call, speak clearly and give: your exact location (use landmarks like 'near the Shell station on Mombasa Road'), what happened, how many people are injured, and what help you need. Do not hang up until the operator tells you to. If you are far from a hospital, start first aid immediately and arrange transport. Do not wait for an ambulance that may never come.

For businesses, designate a 'first aid champion' who is trained and responsible for calling EMS. Keep a list of nearby clinics, hospitals, and taxi services. Practice your emergency plan every 6 months. Remember: the chain is only as strong as its weakest link. Your quick call and immediate CPR can double or triple survival chances.

4. Personal Safety and Scene Assessment

Before you help anyone, you must ensure the scene is safe for you. If you become a victim, you cannot help others. Look for dangers: traffic, fire, smoke, live electrical wires, gas leaks, unstable buildings, or violent people. In African markets, be aware of crowds, animals, and criminal activity. If the scene is unsafe, do not enter. Call for help and wait. For example, if a car has crashed into a power pole with live wires down, stay at least 10 meters away.

Once safe, assess the scene quickly. Use your senses: What do you see? (blood, vomit, broken glass) What do you hear? (moaning, silence) What do you smell? (gas, smoke, alcohol) Count the number of victims. Look for bystanders who can help. Put on disposable gloves if available. If no gloves, use plastic bags over your hands. Avoid direct contact with blood or body fluids.

If the person is in immediate danger (e.g., fire, oncoming traffic), move them only if you can do so safely. Otherwise, do not move them. For a fallen person, tap their shoulders and shout, 'Are you okay?' If they respond, keep them still and comfortable. If not, check breathing. Remember: your safety first, then the victim's safety, then bystander safety.

After the emergency, wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water. If you suspect exposure to blood, report it to a clinic within 2 hours for possible HIV or hepatitis prophylaxis. In a business, conduct a scene safety check as part of your emergency plan. Train staff to identify hazards. Never rush in. A dead rescuer helps no one.

5. Initial Assessment: ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation)

The ABCs are the foundation of first aid. A stands for Airway, B for Breathing, C for Circulation. Check them in order. First, ensure the airway is open. If someone is unconscious, tilt their head back gently and lift the chin. Look inside the mouth for vomit, blood, or objects. If you see something, sweep it out with a finger only if it is loose and visible. Do not push it deeper. For a choking conscious person, give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades, then 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver).

Next, check breathing. Look for chest rise, listen for air movement, and feel for breath on your cheek for 10 seconds. If they are not breathing, start CPR immediately. For adults, give 30 chest compressions (push hard and fast at least 5 cm deep, 100120 per minute) then 2 rescue breaths. If you are untrained or unwilling, do hands-only CPR (compressions only). For children, use one hand, and for infants, two fingers. Continue until help arrives or the person breathes.

Finally, check circulation. Look for severe bleeding. If blood is spurting or pooling, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth or gauze. Do not remove the cloth if it soaks throughadd more on top. If bleeding is from an arm or leg, elevate it above the heart. Use a tourniquet only if pressure fails and bleeding is life-threatening. For shock, lay the person flat, raise legs 30 cm, and keep them warm with a blanket.

Remember: ABCs are done in order. If the airway is blocked, breathing cannot happen. If breathing stops, circulation fails. Recheck every few minutes. In Africa, where help may be far, your ABC skills are the difference between life and death. Practice on a mannequin or a pillow.

6. Activating Emergency Response and Communicating with Dispatch

When an emergency happens, the first step is to activate the emergency response system. Shout for help. If you are alone, call emergency services on your phone. If you are with others, point to one person and say, 'You, call 112 now. Come back and tell me what they said.' Do not say 'someone call'everyone will assume someone else did. In Africa, many people do not know their local emergency number. Learn yours today: 112 works in many countries, but also know 999 (Kenya, Uganda), 10177 (South Africa), 193 (Ghana), 199 (Nigeria).

When you speak to dispatch, stay calm. Give your exact location first. Use landmarks: 'We are at the Kibera Market, near the main gate, next to the blue kiosk.' Then say what happened: 'A man fell from a ladder and is not breathing.' Give the number of victims and their condition. Do not hang up until the operator says to. If you are not sure, ask: 'What should I do while we wait?' Follow their instructions.

If you cannot call, send someone else. If no phone, use a vehicle horn, whistle, or shout to attract attention. In a business, have a designated person to call EMS and another to guide the ambulance. Keep a written list of emergency numbers, your address, and GPS coordinates if possible. Practice a mock call with your staff every 3 months.

After the call, clear a path for the ambulance. Send someone to the road to wave them down. Lock up pets. If the patient is conscious, keep them calm and still. Do not give them food or water. Remember: the faster you activate help, the better the outcome. Your calm, clear communication can save precious minutes.

Practical Reference: First Aid and Emergency Response for African Businesses and Communities

This practical reference consolidates the core skills from the module Introduction to First Aid and Emergency Response. It is written for business owners, managers, staff, and community responders operating in African contextswhere formal emergency medical services (EMS) may be delayed, roads may be impassable, and the nearest clinic may be hours away. The goal is simple: equip you with the knowledge to act confidently, legally, and safely when every minute counts.

In sub-Saharan Africa, road traffic injuries are a leading cause of death, and workplace accidents in agriculture, construction, and manufacturing remain common. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), timely first aid can reduce preventable deaths by up to 60% in low-resource settings. For businesses, this translates directly into reduced downtime, lower insurance costs, and a safer, more productive workforce.

1. What First Aid Is and Why It Matters in Africa

First aid is the immediate, temporary care given to a sick or injured person before professional medical help arrives. It is not a substitute for medical treatmentit is a bridge. In African business and community settings, that bridge is often long. The WHO estimates that over 50% of deaths from injuries in low- and middle-income countries occur before the patient reaches a health facility. For a farm, factory, or market, having trained first aiders on site is not just complianceit is operational resilience.

Consider a real scenario: a worker on a commercial farm in Kenya sustains a deep cut from a machete. Without first aid, blood loss can become life-threatening within 1015 minutes. With proper bleeding controldirect pressure, elevation, and a clean dressingthe worker survives the transport to a clinic 45 minutes away. That is the difference first aid makes.

2. Legal and Ethical Considerations: Good Samaritan Laws and Consent

Across most African jurisdictions, Good Samaritan laws protect bystanders who provide reasonable assistance in good faith without expectation of reward. However, these laws vary by country. In South Africa, the Good Samaritan principle is embedded in common law and the Constitution; in Nigeria, the National Health Act (2014) provides some protection; in Kenya, the Health Act (2017) encourages emergency care but does not explicitly codify Good Samaritan protection. Businesses should verify their local legal framework and include first aid provision in their health and safety policies.

Consent is both a legal and ethical requirement. For a conscious adult, always ask: "May I help you?" If they refuse, respect that decision unless they are a minor or lack decision-making capacity. For unconscious persons, the law presumes implied consentyou may act to save a life. For minors, seek parental consent if present, but do not delay life-saving care.

3. Personal Safety and Scene Assessment

Before you touch anyone, assess the scene. Your safety comes firstyou cannot help if you become a second casualty. Use the DRABC approach: Danger, Response, Airway, Breathing, Circulation. In African contexts, hazards may include traffic on poorly lit roads, wild animals, chemical spills, electrical wires, or unstable structures. If the scene is unsafe, do not enter. Call for help and wait for trained responders.

4. Initial Assessment: The ABCs

The ABCs remain the foundation of first aid assessment:

  • Airway: Is it open? If not, tilt the head back and lift the chin. Remove visible obstructions only if you can do so safely.
  • Breathing: Look, listen, and feel for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. If absent, begin CPR.
  • Circulation: Check for severe bleeding. Apply direct pressure with a clean cloth or gloved hand. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing, start chest compressions at 100120 per minute.

5. Activating Emergency Response and Communicating with Dispatch

In many African countries, emergency numbers vary: 112 (Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa), 999 (Kenya), 10111 (South Africa police), 767 (Ghana), 114 (Rwanda). Know your local numbers. When calling, speak clearly and give: your location (with landmarks), the nature of the emergency, the number of casualties, and your callback number. Do not hang up until the dispatcher tells you to. If EMS is unavailable, arrange transport to the nearest clinic or hospital immediatelydo not wait.

SAFETY WARNING:

Never move a person with a suspected spinal injury unless they are in immediate danger (fire, traffic, collapsing structure). Moving them can cause permanent paralysis. If you must move them, keep the head, neck, and spine aligned. In African road traffic incidents, spinal injuries are commonwait for trained responders if the scene is safe.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Assess the scene for danger to yourself and others before approaching.
  2. Check response tap and shout. If unresponsive, call for help immediately.
  3. Open the airway head tilt, chin lift. Remove visible obstructions only.
  4. Check breathing for no more than 10 seconds. If absent, start CPR.
  5. Control severe bleeding with direct pressure and a clean dressing.
  6. Call emergency services or arrange transport. Give clear location and details.
  7. Stay with the person until help arrives. Monitor breathing and consciousness.
  8. Record what happened time, injury, care given for medical staff and insurance.
