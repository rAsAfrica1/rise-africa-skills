1. Poor Growth Rates: Causes and Solutions

If your steers are not gaining at least 1.2 kg per day, you are losing money. First, weigh your animals monthly using a weigh tape or scale. Poor growth often starts with feed quality. Test your feed for protein and energy. A good fattening ration should have 12-14% crude protein and 70-75% total digestible nutrients. If you are using maize stover or poor hay, the steers cannot grow. Supplement with cottonseed cake, molasses, and urea-treated straw. For example, treat 100 kg of chopped straw with 4 kg urea dissolved in 40 liters of water, cover for 21 days, then feed. Second, check for internal parasites. Deworm every 3 months with albendazole or ivermectin. A heavy worm load can reduce growth by 30%. Third, ensure fresh water is always available. A steer drinks 30-50 liters per day. If water is dirty or too far, intake drops. Fourth, look at stocking density. Give each steer at least 10 square meters of pen space. Overcrowding causes stress and reduces feed intake. Fifth, check teeth and feet. Broken teeth or foot rot make eating painful. Fix these issues quickly. Finally, consider breed. Local zebu crosses gain slower than exotic crosses like Boran or Sahiwal. If you want faster growth, upgrade your genetics gradually. Track your feed conversion: it takes about 6-8 kg of feed to produce 1 kg of gain. If it is higher, adjust your ration. Keep records of weight, feed used, and costs. Without records, you cannot know what works. Act on the worst problem first: usually feed quality or parasites.

2. Digestive Upsets: Bloat and Acidosis

Bloat and acidosis are killers. They happen when you change feed too fast or feed too much grain. Bloat is a build-up of gas in the rumen. The left side of the belly swells up like a balloon. The steer stops eating, kicks at its belly, and breathes fast. If you do not act, it dies in hours. Immediate treatment: drench with 500 ml of vegetable oil or mineral oil to break the foam. Then walk the steer for 10-15 minutes. If that fails, a vet can insert a trocar into the rumen to release gas. To prevent bloat, introduce lush green feeds like lucerne or clover gradually over 7-10 days. Never turn hungry steers onto wet, frosty pasture. Acidosis happens when steers eat too much starch (maize, sorghum) at once. The rumen becomes acidic (pH below 5.5). Symptoms: loose dung, no cud chewing, off-feed, and staggering. Treat with 200 g of baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) in 5 liters of water as a drench. Then feed only hay for 24 hours. To prevent acidosis, always feed roughage first, then grain. Limit grain to 1% of body weight per day, split into two meals. For a 300 kg steer, that is 3 kg of grain per day. Increase grain slowly over 3 weeks. Add 1% bicarbonate to the grain mix if you feed high grain. Also, use ionophores like monensin (Rumensin) at 200 mg per head per day to reduce acidosis risk. Always have clean water available. If you see bloat or acidosis, act immediately. Delay costs you the animal.

3. Lameness and Foot Problems

A lame steer cannot walk to feed or water, so it loses weight fast. The most common causes are foot rot, foot abscess, and laminitis. Foot rot starts with swelling between the toes, bad smell, and pain. It happens when steers stand in wet, muddy pens. Treat foot rot by cleaning the hoof with water and antiseptic (like iodine), then inject long-acting oxytetracycline (20 mg per kg body weight) for 3-5 days. Also, trim the hoof to drain pus. For an abscess, you may need to open it with a clean knife and flush with hydrogen peroxide. Laminitis is inflammation of the hoof wall, often from too much grain. The hoof becomes hot and the steer walks on its heels. Treat by removing all grain for 3 days and feeding only hay. Give 200 g of baking soda in water. To prevent lameness, keep pens dry. Use concrete or gravel in high-traffic areas. Scrape manure daily. Provide a footbath with 5% copper sulfate solution at the entrance to the pen. Trim hooves every 6 months using hoof nippers and a rasp. Check hooves weekly for cuts or stones. If you see a steer limping, separate it and examine the foot. Do not ignore it. A simple foot wrap with gauze and iodine can prevent infection. Also, ensure bedding is dry. In wet season, raise the pen floor with sand or wood chips. If many steers go lame, check your feed for excess grain or mycotoxins. A balanced ration with 0.5% calcium and 0.3% phosphorus keeps hooves strong. Remember, a lame steer is a dead loss. Act early.

4. Heat Stress and Cold Stress Management

Steers perform best between 15C and 25C. Above 30C, they eat less and gain less. Below 5C, they burn energy to stay warm. In hot African climates, heat stress is the bigger problem. Signs: panting, drooling, standing in water, and reduced feed intake. To cool steers, provide shade. A simple roof of corrugated iron or thatch covering 3-4 square meters per steer works. Use sprinklers or misters to wet the coat. Fans help if air is moving. Feed during cool hours: early morning and evening. Increase energy density of the ration with 5% added fat (like palm oil or tallow). Add 1% potassium bicarbonate to the feed to replace lost electrolytes. Ensure unlimited cool water. A steer can drink 60-80 liters on a hot day. If water is warm, they drink less. Bury pipes or use insulated tanks. For cold stress, in highland areas or winter, provide windbreaks. Use straw bedding 30 cm deep. Increase feed by 10-20% because steers burn more calories. Feed warm gruel (cooked maize meal) in the evening. Check for frostbite on ears and tail. If a steer shivers, bring it inside or give a blanket. Also, avoid wet bedding. Cold and wet together is dangerous. In both cases, watch for pneumonia: coughing, nasal discharge, and fever. Treat with antibiotics. Keep stress low by handling steers calmly. Move them slowly. Use flags instead of sticks. A stressed steer eats less. So manage the environment, and your steers will grow.

5. Feed Refusals and Sorting

When steers refuse feed or sort through it, you are losing money. Sorting means they eat the tasty bits (grain) and leave the hay or supplements. This causes acidosis and poor growth. First, check feed quality. Moldy or spoiled feed smells bad. Steers will refuse it. Never feed moldy silage or hay. If feed is too dry or dusty, sprinkle water to reduce dust. Mix the ration thoroughly. Use a feed mixer or shovel to blend grain, protein, minerals, and roughage. A total mixed ration (TMR) prevents sorting. If you cannot mix, feed roughage first, then grain. Second, check for health issues. A sick steer with a fever or acidosis will refuse feed. Take its temperature. Normal is 38-39C. If fever, treat with antibiotics. Check for mouth sores or injured tongue. Third, ensure feed is fresh. Steers dislike stale or fermented feed. Feed twice daily and remove leftovers after 2 hours. Fourth, check water. If water is dirty or unavailable, feed intake drops. Clean troughs daily. Fifth, check bunk space. Each steer needs 60-75 cm of bunk space. If crowded, shy steers cannot eat. Sixth, change feed gradually. A sudden change causes refusals. Mix old and new feed over 7 days. Seventh, add molasses or flavoring to improve palatability. A 5% molasses inclusion often solves refusal. Finally, watch for bullying. Dominant steers chase others away. Use tie stalls or divide pens. If sorting continues, chop roughage finer (2-5 cm) so it cannot be separated. Monitor feed refusals: if more than 5% is left, adjust the ration. Keep records. A steer that refuses feed is telling you something. Listen.

6. Addressing Aggression and Behavioral Issues

Aggressive steers injure others, damage pens, and stress the whole group. Common behaviors: mounting, head-butting, and bullying. Mounting is often a sign of mixing unfamiliar animals or too much energy in the diet. It can also be a dominance display. To reduce aggression, first, keep group size small (10-15 steers). Large groups create more competition. Second, provide enough space: 10-15 square meters per steer. Third, feed at the same time each day. Hungry steers become aggressive. Use enough trough space so all can eat. Fourth, mix new animals gradually. Put new steers in a separate pen next to the group for 3 days, then combine. Fifth, use visual barriers like solid fences or hay bales to break sight lines. Sixth, castrate males if not already done. Bulls are more aggressive. Seventh, check for pain. A steer with foot rot or arthritis may attack when approached. Treat the pain. Eighth, consider environmental enrichment. Hang a rope or tire for them to play with. This redirects energy. Ninth, if one steer is a chronic bully, remove it. Sell it or put it in a separate pen. Do not use electric prods or sticks. They make steers fearful and more aggressive. Instead, use flags or plastic bags on a stick to move them. Speak calmly. Move slowly. Tenth, if mounting is constant, check for high grain diet. Reduce grain and increase hay. Also, check for external parasites like ticks or lice. Irritation causes restlessness. Finally, if aggression is due to heat stress, cool the pen. Steers are herd animals. They need a stable social order. Provide that, and aggression drops.

<h2>Practical Reference: Troubleshooting Common Problems in Steer Fattening</h2>

<p>In African steer fattening enterprises, profitability hinges on consistent daily gain, feed conversion efficiency, and low mortality. When problems arise poor growth, bloat, lameness, heat stress, feed refusals, or aggression each day of inaction erodes margins. This practical reference equips feedlot managers, extension officers, and emerging commercial cattle finishers with field-tested diagnostics and corrective actions. It draws on research and extension guidance relevant to tropical and subtropical African production systems, where heat, variable feed quality, and limited veterinary access are common constraints.</p>

<h3>1. Poor Growth Rates: Causes and Solutions</h3>
<p>Poor growth is rarely caused by a single factor. Common causes include inadequate energy or protein density in the ration, poor feed intake due to palatability or spoilage, internal parasites, subclinical acidosis, water restriction, and chronic heat stress. In African feedlots where maize stover, agro-industrial by-products (brewers' grains, molasses, cottonseed cake), and locally mixed rations dominate, nutrient imbalances are frequent. A systematic check of feed analysis, water availability, faecal consistency, and body condition scoring (BCS) usually identifies the bottleneck.</p>

<p><strong>Solutions:</strong> Conduct a feed analysis at least every three months; adjust energy density with molasses or maize meal; ensure crude protein meets 1214% for growing steers; deworm strategically using faecal egg counts; and ensure clean water within 5 metres of feeding troughs. Weigh a representative sample of steers monthly to calculate average daily gain (ADG). If ADG falls below 0.8 kg/day in a 250350 kg steer, investigate immediately.</p>

<h3>2. Digestive Upsets: Bloat and Acidosis</h3>
<p>Bloat (ruminal tympany) and acidosis are the two most economically damaging digestive disorders in intensive steer fattening. Bloat occurs when gas trapped in the rumen cannot be eructated, often triggered by lush legumes (e.g., lucerne, clover) or high-grain diets with insufficient effective fibre. Acidosis results from rapid fermentation of highly digestible carbohydrates, dropping rumen pH below 5.5, causing rumenitis, laminitis, and reduced intake.</p>

<p><strong>Prevention and treatment:</strong> Introduce grain gradually over 23 weeks; maintain at least 40% effective fibre (e.g., maize stover, hay) in the total mixed ration (TMR); use ionophores (e.g., monensin) where legally available; provide sodium bicarbonate buffers at 0.51% of dry matter. For bloat, administer anti-foaming agents (e.g., vegetable oil drench) and move affected animals. For acidosis, remove grain, provide hay and water, and consult a veterinarian for severe cases.</p>

<h3>3. Lameness and Foot Problems</h3>
<p>Lameness reduces feed intake, weight gain, and carcass value. In African feedlots, common causes include laminitis secondary to acidosis, foot rot (interdigital necrobacillosis), digital dermatitis, and injuries from rough or muddy pens. Wet, manure-heavy pens during rainy seasons exacerbate foot lesions. A lameness prevalence above 5% signals a systemic issue.</p>

<p><strong>Management:</strong> Maintain dry, well-drained pens; use footbaths with copper sulphate or zinc sulphate where practical; trim hooves during dry season; and treat foot rot early with systemic antibiotics (e.g., oxytetracycline) under veterinary guidance. Prevent acidosis to reduce laminitis incidence. Cull chronically lame animals to protect margins.</p>

<h3>4. Heat Stress and Cold Stress Management</h3>
<p>African feedlots often face extreme heat, especially in Sahelian and savannah zones. Heat stress reduces dry matter intake by up to 30%, lowers ADG, and increases mortality. Cold stress, though less common, affects highland areas (e.g., Ethiopian highlands, Kenyan highlands) where night temperatures drop sharply.</p>

<p><strong>Heat stress:</strong> Provide shade (trees or shade cloth), ensure unlimited cool water, feed during cooler hours (early morning, evening), increase dietary energy density, and use sprinklers or fans where feasible. <strong>Cold stress:</strong> Provide windbreaks, dry bedding, and increase energy in the ration. Monitor temperature-humidity index (THI); when THI exceeds 78, implement cooling strategies.</p>

<h3>5. Feed Refusals and Sorting</h3>
<p>Feed refusal and sorting are early warning signs of palatability issues, spoilage, mycotoxins, or rumen discomfort. In mixed rations, steers may sort out long fibre or fine concentrates, leading to inconsistent nutrient intake and acidosis risk. In African contexts, feed refusals often stem from mouldy silage, high-moisture grains, or sudden ration changes.</p>

<p><strong>Corrective actions:</strong> Test feed for mycotoxins (especially aflatoxin); ensure proper silage fermentation and storage; introduce ration changes gradually over 57 days; process feed to reduce sorting (e.g., grinding, adding molasses); and clean troughs daily. Monitor refusals a consistent 25% refusal is acceptable, but above 10% requires investigation.</p>

<h3>6. Addressing Aggression and Behavioral Issues</h3>
<p>Aggression in feedlot steers increases injury risk, reduces feed intake in subordinate animals, and raises labour costs. Common causes include overcrowding, mixing unfamiliar groups, competition for feed and water, and heat stress. In African feedlots with limited space, aggression can escalate quickly.</p>

<p><strong>Strategies:</strong> Provide adequate space (at least 1520 m per steer in pens); ensure sufficient trough space (6075 cm per animal); avoid mixing groups after establishment; use visual barriers or shade to reduce competition; and cull persistently aggressive animals. Consider using low-stress handling techniques to reduce fear and reactivity.</p>

<ul>
<li>Weigh a representative sample of steers monthly and calculate ADG; investigate if below 0.8 kg/day.</li>
<li>Test feed for mycotoxins and nutrient composition every 3 months.</li>
<li>Maintain at least 40% effective fibre in the TMR to prevent acidosis and bloat.</li>
<li>Check water availability, cleanliness, and flow rate daily steers need 3050 litres/day.</li>
<li>Monitor THI; implement cooling when THI exceeds 78.</li>
<li>Inspect pens weekly for mud, manure build-up, and foot lesion risks.</li>
<li>Keep anti-foaming agents, buffers, and emergency veterinary contacts accessible.</li>
<li>Record all health events, treatments, and feed refusals for trend analysis.</li>
</ul>
