1. Asking for Directions: Where is...?

In this lesson, you will learn how to ask for directions in Mandarin. The most useful phrase is "...?" (... z i nl?) which means "Where is ...?" For example, "?" (xshujin z i nl?) means "Where is the restroom?" You can replace with any place: (ynhng, bank), (shchng, market), (gngjiozh n, bus stop), etc. To be polite, start with "" (qngwn, excuse me). So: "?" (Qngwn, ynhng z i nl?) "Excuse me, where is the bank?"

When you ask, listen for key words in the answer: (zu, left), (yu, right), (qin, front), (hu, back), (yzh zu, go straight), (gui, turn). For example, "" (yzh zu, rnhu zu gui) means "Go straight, then turn left." If you don't understand, say "" (w tng b dng, I don't understand) or "" (qng shu m n ydin, please speak slower). In many African cities, locals may not speak Mandarin, but in Chinese-run shops or with Chinese colleagues, these phrases are gold.

Practice with real locations. In Nairobi, you might ask a Chinese shopkeeper: "?" (Qngwn, Zhnggu chosh z i nl?) "Excuse me, where is the Chinese supermarket?" In Lagos, if you work with Chinese engineers, ask: "?" (Qngwn, gngd z i nl?) "Where is the construction site?" Always repeat the place name clearly. Use hand gestures if needed, but try to use the Mandarin words.

Numbers and landmarks help. Learn: (zubin, left side), (yubin, right side), (dumi n, opposite), (pngbin, next to). For example, "" (Ynhng z i chosh pngbin) "The bank is next to the supermarket." If you get lost, show the address on your phone and ask: "?" (Qngwn, zhge dfng z i nl?) "Excuse me, where is this place?"

Your task: Write down 5 places you visit often (market, bank, bus stop, friend's house, church/mosque). Practice asking for each in Mandarin. Record yourself. Then, next time you meet a Chinese speaker, ask one of these questions. You'll be surprised how far a smile and "" will take you.

2. Giving Directions: Left, Right, Straight

Now that you can ask, you need to give directions. Start with the key words: (zu) = left, (yu) = right, (zh) or (yzh) = straight, (gui) = turn, (zu) = walk/go. Combine them: (zu gui) = turn left, (yu gui) = turn right, (yzh zu) = go straight. Add distance: 100 (zu ybi m) = walk 100 meters, (gu ling g lku) = pass two intersections. For example: "" (Yzh zu, gu ling g lku, rnhu yu gui) "Go straight, pass two intersections, then turn right."

When giving directions, use landmarks. In African cities, landmarks like (jiyuzh n, petrol station), (hngldng, traffic light), (shchng, market), (ji otng, church), (qngzhns, mosque) are common. For example: "" (K nd o hngldng, zu gui) "When you see the traffic light, turn left." Or "" (Z i shchng dumi n, yu gui) "Opposite the market, turn right." Use (z i) for location: (z i zubin) = on the left, (z i yubin) = on the right.

Be clear and slow. If someone doesn't understand, repeat and use hand gestures. In Mandarin, you can say: "......" (xin... rnhu...) = first... then... Example: "" (Xin yzh zu, rnhu zu gui, z i yu gui) "First go straight, then turn left, then turn right." The word (z i) means "then" for a subsequent action.

Practice by giving directions to a friend in your neighborhood. Use Google Maps to check distances. For instance, from your house to the nearest bus stop: "5020" (Chmn yu gui, yzh zu wsh m, zu gui, z i zu rsh m, ji d o le) "Exit and turn right, go straight 50 meters, turn left, then walk 20 meters, and you arrive." The phrase (ji d o le) means "you've arrived."

Remember: giving directions is a life skill. In business, you might direct a Chinese visitor to your office. In daily life, you might help a Chinese tourist. Practice with these phrases until they become automatic. Write a short script for directing someone from your home to the nearest shop. Say it aloud five times.

3. Taking a Taxi: Telling the Driver

Taking a taxi in a Chinese-speaking context requires specific phrases. First, tell the driver where you want to go: "..." (Qng d o...) = "Please go to..." For example: "" (Qng d o huchzh n) "Please go to the train station." "" (Qng d o jchng) "Please go to the airport." "" (Qng d o zhge dzh) "Please go to this address." Show the address on your phone if pronunciation is hard. In many African cities, taxis may not use meters, so agree on a price first: "?" (Dusho qin?) "How much?" Or "...?" (D o... dusho qin?) "How much to...?"

If you need to give directions during the ride, use: (zu gui) = turn left, (yu gui) = turn right, (yzh zu) = go straight, (tng) = stop, (z i zhl tng) = stop here, (qinmi n tng) = stop ahead. For example: "" (Qinmi n yu gui) "Turn right ahead." "" (Qng z i zhl tng) "Please stop here." If you want the driver to slow down: "" (M n ydin) "Slower." If you're in a hurry: "" (Ku i ydin) "Faster." But be polite; use (qng) = please.

Payment: Ask "?" (Dusho qin?) at the end. If you need a receipt: "" (fpi o) "receipt." In some countries, you might use mobile money, but in Chinese-speaking areas, cash or WeChat Pay/Alipay are common. Learn: "?" (W ky yng shuj zhf ma?) "Can I pay by phone?" If not, "" (xi njn) = cash.

Safety phrases: "" (Qng d bio) "Please use the meter." If the driver refuses, you can say: "" (W y o xi ch) "I want to get off." Also, "" (Qng ki m n din) "Please drive slower." And "" (Qng xioxn) "Please be careful."

Practice role-playing with a friend. One person is the driver, the other the passenger. Use a map of your city. Practice saying the destination, giving directions, and asking for the price. In Africa, taxi drivers may speak some Mandarin in tourist areas; these phrases will help you communicate. Write down 5 common destinations (market, airport, bus station, hotel, friend's house) and practice saying "..." for each.

4. Using Public Transport: Bus and Subway

Public transport in Mandarin: (gngjioch) = bus, (dti) = subway/metro, (chzch) = taxi. To ask about bus routes: "...?" (N l gngjioch d o...?) "Which bus goes to...?" For example: "?" (N l gngjioch d o shzhngxn?) "Which bus goes to the city center?" The answer might be: "1" (Zu y l) "Take bus number 1." Or "2" (Zu dti r h o xi n) "Take subway line 2." The word (l) means "route" for buses, and (h o xi n) means "line number" for subway.

At the bus stop or station, look for signs: (gngjiozh n) = bus stop, (dtizh n) = subway station. To ask where to buy a ticket: "?" (Z i nl mi pi o?) "Where to buy a ticket?" For buses, you often pay the driver: "?" (Dusho qin?) "How much?" For subway, use a ticket machine or counter: " " (W y o y zhng pi o) "I want one ticket." Or "..." (W y o d o...) "I want to go to..." Then show the station name.

On the bus, you might hear: (xi y zh n) = next stop, (zhngdinzh n) = terminal, (qng xi ch) = please get off. To ask the driver to stop: "" (Qng tng ch) "Please stop." Or "" (Yu xi ) "Someone getting off." In some African cities, buses are minibuses (matatus, tro-tros). You can use the same phrases: "...?" (D o... dusho qin?) "How much to...?" And "" (Qng tng yxi ) "Please stop."

For subway, learn: (rku) = entrance, (chku) = exit, (hu nchng) = transfer. Example: "2" (W y o hu nchng r h o xi n) "I need to transfer to line 2." If you get lost, ask station staff: "...?" (Qngwn, znme q...?) "Excuse me, how to go to...?"

Practice: Find a bus route in your city. Learn the number and destination in Mandarin. Practice saying "......" (W zu... l gngjioch q...) "I take bus number... to go to..." For subway, if your city has one, practice asking for a ticket to a specific station. If not, use the phrases in a role-play. Write a short dialogue between you and a bus conductor.

5. Buying Tickets and Reading Signs

Buying tickets in Mandarin: (mi pi o) = buy a ticket, (shupi och) = ticket office, (zdng shupi oj) = ticket machine. To ask for a ticket: " ..." (W y o y zhng d o... de pi o) "I want one ticket to..." For example: " " (W y o y zhng d o Bijng de pi o) "I want one ticket to Beijing." If you need a round trip: "" (wngfn pi o) "round-trip ticket." One-way: "" (dnchng pi o). For two tickets: " " (ling zhng). The measure word for tickets is (zhng).

At the ticket office, you may hear: ? (N y o q nl?) "Where do you want to go?" ? (J zhng?) "How many?" ? (Chf shjin?) "Departure time?" Answer with: ... (W y o q...) "I want to go to..." (y zhng) "one," (ling zhng) "two." For time: (mngtin) = tomorrow, (jntin) = today, (sh ngw) = morning, (xi w) = afternoon. Example: "" (Mngtin sh ngw) "tomorrow morning."

Reading signs: Common signs include (rku) = entrance, (chku) = exit, (shupi och) = ticket office, (huchsh) = waiting room, (zh nti) = platform, (xshujin) = restroom, (wnxnch) = information desk. On trains: (chxing) = carriage, (zuwi) = seat, (w zu) = no seat. In subways: (hu nchng) = transfer, (xi nlt) = route map.

6. Role-Play: Getting to the Train Station

In this final lesson, you will combine everything to role-play getting to the train station. Scenario: You are in a Chinese-speaking city (or with Chinese speakers in Africa) and need to get to the train station (, huchzh n). You start by asking a passerby: "?" (Qngwn, huchzh n znme zu?) "Excuse me, how do I get to the train station?" The person might answer: "200" (Yzh zu, rnhu zu gui, z i zu rbi m ji d o le) "Go straight, then turn left, then walk 200 meters and you'll arrive." If you don't understand, say: "" (Qng shu m n ydin) "Please speak slower." Or "" (W tng b dng) "I don't understand."

Next, you decide to take a taxi. You flag one and say: "" (Qng d o huchzh n) "Please go to the train station." The driver asks: "?" (Nge huchzh n?) "Which train station?" You reply: "" (Shzhngxn n ge) "The one in the city center." Or give the full name. During the ride, you might say: "" (Qinmi n yu gui) "Turn right ahead." When you arrive: "?" (Dusho qin?) "How much?" You pay and say: "" (Xixi) "Thank you."

At the station, you need to buy a ticket. Go to the ticket office (, shupi och) and say: " ..." (W y o y zhng d o... de pi o) "I want one ticket to..." For example, if you're in Nairobi and going to Mombasa: " " (W y o y zhng d o Mngbs de pi o). The clerk asks: " ?" (J zhng?) "How many?" You say: " " (Y zhng). Then ask: "?" (Dusho qin?) "How much?" And " ?" (J din chf?) "What time does it depart?"

After buying the ticket, find the platform (, zh nti). Ask: "?" (Qngwn, zh nti z i nl?) "Excuse me, where is the platform?" Look for signs: (rku) = entrance, (chku) = exit, (huchsh) = waiting room. If you need help, go to the information desk (, wnxnch). Say: "?" (Qngwn, q Mngbs de huch z i nge zh nti?) "Excuse me, which platform is the train to Mombasa?"

Practice this entire role-play with a friend. One person plays you, the other plays different characters: passerby, taxi driver, ticket clerk, station staff. Use a map of your city and pretend the train station is a real place. Do it three times, each time faster. This will build your confidence. After practicing, you'll be ready to navigate transportation in Mandarin-speaking environments, whether in Africa or abroad.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Directions & Transportation in Mandarin

For African agribusiness professionals operating in Chinese-speaking markets, the ability to navigate directions and transportation independently is a commercial necessity, not a tourist convenience. Whether you are a Kenyan avocado exporter meeting a buyer in Guangzhou, a Ghanaian cocoa trader transiting through Shanghai Pudong, or a Nigerian agro-inputs dealer sourcing equipment in Yiwu, the first thirty minutes after arrival often determine whether a deal proceeds smoothly. This reference module consolidates the essential Mandarin vocabulary, sentence patterns, and practical scripts you need to move confidently through Chinese cities while protecting your schedule, your samples, and your margins.

This lesson is designed for business travellers who need functional fluency, not academic perfection. Every phrase here is chosen because it appears in real transactions: taxi queues at trade fair venues, subway signage near agricultural wholesale markets, and ticket counters at high-speed rail stations. Where tonal accuracy matters most, we flag it. Where a simple written note can substitute for pronunciation, we recommend it.

Core Sentence Patterns for Business Travel

Three patterns cover most situations. First, the location question: ( z i nl?) "Where is ?" Second, the direction instruction: // (wng zu / wng yu / yzh zu) "Go left / right / straight." Third, the destination statement: (w y o q) "I want to go to" These three structures, combined with a place name, will handle taxi drivers, subway staff, and street vendors alike.

For taxi and ride-hailing, the critical phrase is (qng d i w q zhge dfng) "Please take me to this place," while showing a written address or map pin. This bypasses pronunciation risk entirely and is the single most commercially valuable phrase in this module. Chinese drivers are accustomed to this method; do not feel it is impolite.

SAFETY WARNING Unlicensed Taxis and Overcharging

At major Chinese airports and train stations, unlicensed drivers ( hich) approach foreign travellers, particularly those arriving from African routes, offering "fixed price" rides. These fares can be 35 times the metered rate, and there is no (official receipt) for reimbursement. Always use the official taxi queue (), the Didi app, or the airport shuttle. If a driver refuses to use the meter ( dbio), exit the vehicle before it moves. Keep your passport and samples with you at all times never in the trunk.

Role-Play Script: Getting to the Train Station

Scenario: You are in Guangzhou, departing for a supplier meeting in Foshan. You need to reach Guangzhou South Railway Station () by 08:00 with two boxes of cocoa samples.

You (to hotel front desk):
Qngwn, Gungzhu Nn Zh n znme zu?
"Excuse me, how do I get to Guangzhou South Station?"

Staff:
N ky zu dti, y ky dch.
"You can take the subway, or you can take a taxi."

You:
Dch d g i dusho qin?
"About how much is a taxi?"

Staff:
D g i bsh ku i, snsh fnzhng.
"About 80 yuan, thirty minutes."

You (to taxi driver, showing phone map):
Shfu, qng d i w q zhge dfng. W y o fpi o.
"Driver, please take me to this place. I need a receipt."

This script is deliberately short. In practice, showing the map pin and saying (fpi o) accomplishes 90% of the transaction. The politeness marker (shfu) literally "master" is the standard respectful address for drivers and is appreciated.

Reading Signs and Buying Tickets

Chinese transport signage follows a consistent colour and character system. Subway exits are marked with letters (A, B, C, D) and the character (chku). Ticket machines at high-speed rail stations require passport scanning for foreign travellers; the English interface is available by tapping (Yngwn) in the corner. At bus stops, the route number appears in large digits, with the terminus written in characters learn to recognise your destination's final two characters rather than the full name.

For ticket purchase, the phrase (w y o y zhng q de pi o) "I want one ticket to" is sufficient. At rail stations, add (goti) for high-speed or (ptng huch) for standard. Always confirm the departure time with (j din?) "What time?" and repeat it back to avoid mishearing.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Save your destination in Chinese characters on your phone before leaving the hotel screenshots work without data.
  2. Learn (fpi o) without it, you cannot claim taxi or transport expenses.
  3. Download Didi and WeChat Pay before arrival; both require a bank card or international top-up.
  4. Carry your passport required for high-speed rail tickets and hotel check-in.
  5. Avoid unlicensed taxis at airports; use the official queue or app.
  6. Memorise (zu) and (yu) the two most frequently misheard direction words.
  7. Note your subway exit letter (A/B/C/D) exits can be 500 metres apart.
  8. Keep samples in the cabin, never checked luggage or taxi trunk, especially perishable agro-products.
