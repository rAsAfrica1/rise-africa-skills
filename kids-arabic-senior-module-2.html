1. Common Greetings: Hello, Goodbye, Good Morning

In Arabic, greetings change depending on the time of day and formality. The most common hello is 'As-salaam alaykum' (peace be upon you), replied with 'Wa alaykum as-salaam'. For a simple hi, use 'Marhaba' or 'Ahlan'. Good morning is 'Sabah al-khayr' (morning of goodness), replied with 'Sabah an-nur' (morning of light). Good evening is 'Masa al-khayr', replied with 'Masa an-nur'. Goodbye is 'Ma'a as-salaama' (go with safety), replied with 'Allah yisallimak' or simply 'Ma'a as-salaama'. In Africa, especially in countries like Egypt, Sudan, and Morocco, you'll hear these daily. For example, in Cairo, a shopkeeper might greet you with 'Ahlan wa sahlan' (welcome). In Zanzibar, Swahili greetings mix with Arabic: 'Habari' is common, but Arabic 'Marhaba' is understood. Practice with a partner: greet at different times of day. Record yourself on your phone and compare to native audio on YouTube. Use apps like Duolingo or Memrise for repetition. Write each greeting on a flashcard with the English on the back. Review 5 minutes daily. In 3 days, you'll know 6 greetings. In 1 week, you can greet anyone in Arabic. Remember: in Africa, greetings are long and friendly. Don't rush. Ask about family, health, and work. 'As-salaam alaykum' opens doors. Try it with a neighbor from Sudan or Somalia. They'll smile and reply. That's your first real conversation.

2. Introducing Yourself: Name, Origin, and Nationality

To introduce yourself in Arabic, start with 'Ismi' (my name is) followed by your name. For example, 'Ismi Amina' means 'My name is Amina'. To ask someone's name, say 'Ma ismuka?' (to a male) or 'Ma ismuki?' (to a female). To say where you are from, use 'Ana min' (I am from) plus your country. 'Ana min Nigeria' means 'I am from Nigeria'. For nationality, Arabic uses a suffix: 'Nigeriyy' (Nigerian male), 'Nigeriyya' (Nigerian female). Similarly, 'Misri' (Egyptian male), 'Misriyya' (Egyptian female). In Africa, you might say 'Ana min Kenya' (I am from Kenya) or 'Ana Sudani' (I am Sudanese). To ask 'Where are you from?' say 'Min ayna anta?' (male) or 'Min ayna anti?' (female). Practice with a friend: swap names, countries, and nationalities. Use a mirror to watch your mouth movements. Record a 30-second introduction on your phone. Listen for mistakes. Use online resources like ArabicPod101 or Forvo for pronunciation. Write your introduction in Arabic script and transliteration. For example: 'As-salaam alaykum. Ismi John. Ana min Ghana. Ana Ghanawi.' (Hello. My name is John. I am from Ghana. I am Ghanaian.) Repeat until fluent. In Africa, knowing how to introduce yourself in Arabic helps in business, travel, and making friends. In 2 days, you can introduce yourself confidently. Try it with a Arabic speaker in your community. They'll appreciate the effort.

3. Asking and Answering 'How Are You?'

The most common way to ask 'How are you?' in Arabic is 'Kayfa haluk?' (to a male) or 'Kayfa haluki?' (to a female). In Egyptian Arabic, you'll hear 'Izzayyak?' (male) and 'Izzayyik?' (female). In Levantine Arabic, it's 'Kifak?' (male) and 'Kifik?' (female). To answer, say 'Ana bi-khayr' (I am fine) or 'Al-hamdulillah' (Praise God). If you're not fine, say 'Ana mish bi-khayr' (I am not fine) or 'Ta'ban' (tired). In Africa, especially in Egypt, Sudan, and Morocco, these phrases are used daily. For example, in Cairo, a taxi driver might ask 'Izzayyak?' You reply 'Al-hamdulillah'. To ask 'How is your family?' say 'Kayfa hal al-usra?' or 'Izzayy al-ahil?' (Egyptian). Practice with a language partner on WhatsApp or Tandem. Set a goal: learn 3 ways to ask and 3 ways to answer. Use flashcards with audio. Listen to Arabic songs or news for intonation. Record yourself asking and answering. Compare to native speakers on YouTube. In 1 week, you can ask and answer 'How are you?' naturally. In Africa, this greeting shows respect and care. Don't just say 'fine' and walk away. Ask back: 'Wa anta?' (And you?). This builds rapport. Try it with a Sudanese friend. They'll be impressed.

4. Polite Expressions: Please, Thank You, Excuse Me

In Arabic, 'please' is 'Min fadlak' (to a male) or 'Min fadlik' (to a female). 'Thank you' is 'Shukran'. The response is 'Afwan' (you're welcome). 'Excuse me' is 'Afwan' as well, but for getting attention, say 'Law samaht' (if you please, male) or 'Law samahti' (female). For 'I'm sorry', say 'Asif' (male) or 'Asifa' (female). In Africa, these phrases are essential in markets, taxis, and offices. For example, in Morocco, when you want to pass, say 'Law samaht'. In Egypt, 'Shukran' is used constantly. To say 'Thank you very much', say 'Shukran jazeelan'. To say 'No, thank you', say 'La, shukran'. Practice with role-plays: buying bread, asking for directions, apologizing for being late. Use apps like HelloTalk to chat with native speakers. Write each phrase on a sticky note and place it where you'll see it. Repeat aloud 10 times. Record yourself. In 3 days, you'll use these naturally. In Africa, politeness opens doors. A simple 'Shukran' to a vendor can lead to a discount. 'Afwan' to a stranger who helps you. Try it today: say 'Shukran' to a Arabic-speaking colleague. They'll smile. Then say 'Min fadlak' when you ask for something. You'll be treated with respect.

5. Numbers 1-10 and Counting Basics

Arabic numbers 1-10 are: wahid (1), ithnan (2), thalatha (3), arba'a (4), khamsa (5), sitta (6), sab'a (7), thamaniya (8), tis'a (9), ashara (10). In Africa, these are used in Egypt, Sudan, Morocco, and other Arabic-speaking countries. To count objects, use the number plus the noun. For example, 'khamsa kutub' (five books). For 11-20, the pattern changes: ahada ashar (11), ithna ashar (12), etc. Practice counting from 1 to 10 aloud. Use your fingers. Write the numbers in Arabic script and transliteration. Use flashcards with the numeral on one side and the Arabic word on the other. Test yourself: say the number in Arabic when you see a digit. Use apps like Anki for spaced repetition. In 2 days, you'll know 1-10. In 1 week, you can count to 20. In daily life, use numbers for prices, phone numbers, and quantities. For example, in a Cairo market, ask 'Bikam?' (How much?) and the vendor says 'Khamsa junayh' (five pounds). You reply 'Shukran'. Practice with a friend: one says a number in English, the other in Arabic. Do this for 5 minutes daily. In Africa, bargaining is common. Knowing numbers helps you get fair prices. Try it at a local shop. Say 'Wahid, ithnan, thalatha' as you count items. The shopkeeper will notice.

6. Days of the Week and Months

In Arabic, days of the week are: al-ithnayn (Monday), ath-thulatha (Tuesday), al-arbi'a (Wednesday), al-khamis (Thursday), al-jum'a (Friday), as-sabt (Saturday), al-ahad (Sunday). Note that Friday is the holy day, and Sunday is 'the first'. Months in Arabic follow two systems: Gregorian and Islamic. Gregorian months are: Yanayir (January), Fibrayir (February), Maris (March), Abril (April), Mayu (May), Yunyu (June), Yulyu (July), Aghustus (August), Sibtambar (September), Oktubar (October), Nufambar (November), Disambar (December). Islamic months are: Muharram, Safar, Rabi' al-awwal, Rabi' al-thani, Jumada al-ula, Jumada al-akhira, Rajab, Sha'ban, Ramadan, Shawwal, Dhu al-Qi'dah, Dhu al-Hijjah. In Africa, both are used. For example, in Egypt, Ramadan is a key month. In Morocco, Gregorian months are common in business. Practice saying the days in order. Use a calendar and point to each day. Write the Arabic name next to the English. Use songs or rhymes to memorize. For months, learn the Gregorian first, then Islamic. In 3 days, you'll know the days. In 1 week, the months. Use them in sentences: 'Al-yawm al-ithnayn' (Today is Monday). 'Shahru Ramadan' (the month of Ramadan). In Africa, knowing days and months helps with appointments, holidays, and business. Try scheduling a meeting in Arabic: 'Al-jum'a, min fadlak' (Friday, please).

Practical Reference: Arabic for African Agribusiness Professionals



Arabic is spoken by over 420 million people across more than 25 countries, and it is an official language of the African Union. For agribusiness professionals operating across North Africa, the Sahel, and the Horn of Africa from Morocco and Egypt to Sudan, Chad, and Somalia even basic Arabic greetings can open doors to traders, cooperatives, transporters, and government extension officers. This reference module consolidates the essentials from the seven lessons in this course into a single practical toolkit for field and trade contexts.



Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) is used in formal business, media, and written contracts. However, in markets, farms, and border towns, you will hear regional dialects such as Egyptian Arabic, Moroccan Darija, Sudanese Arabic, or Chadian Arabic. The greetings below are MSA and are widely understood, but learning a few local dialect phrases will strengthen relationships. Always greet before discussing business in most Arabic-speaking cultures, a warm greeting is a prerequisite for a productive negotiation.



Core Greetings and Their Use



The most universal greeting is As-salamu alaykum (peace be upon you), answered with Wa alaykum as-salam (and peace be upon you). This works at any time of day and in any setting, from a grain market in Khartoum to a citrus packing house in Morocco. For a simple hello, Marhaban or Ahlan is friendly and informal. Good morning is Sabah al-khayr, answered with Sabah an-nur. Good evening is Masa al-khayr, answered with Masa an-nur. Goodbye is Ma'a as-salama (go with safety), and a common response is Allah yisallimak (may God keep you safe).



When introducing yourself, the standard pattern is Ismi [name] (my name is...). To state origin: Ana min [country] (I am from...). To state nationality: Ana [nationality], e.g., Ana Sudani (I am Sudanese) or Ana Misri (I am Egyptian). Asking someone's name: Ma ismuka? (to a man) or Ma ismuki? (to a woman). Asking where they are from: Min ayna anta? (to a man) or Min ayna anti? (to a woman).



To ask how someone is: Kayfa haluka? (to a man) or Kayfa haluki? (to a woman). A common reply is Ana bikhayr, shukran (I am fine, thank you). Polite expressions include Min fadlik (please, to a man), Shukran (thank you), Afwan (you're welcome / excuse me), and Al-ma'dhira (excuse me / apologies).

SAFETY WARNING: Cultural and Dialect Sensitivity


Arabic dialects vary significantly across Africa. A phrase that is polite in Egyptian Arabic may be misunderstood in Moroccan Darija or Sudanese Arabic. In some conservative rural areas, direct eye contact with a woman you are not related to, or offering to shake hands with a woman, may be considered inappropriate. Always observe local customs first. In conflict-affected areas of the Sahel and Horn of Africa, avoid discussing politics, religion, or tribal affiliations during initial business greetings. If you are unsure, use the universal As-salamu alaykum and wait for your counterpart to set the tone. Never assume that MSA will be perfectly understood in remote markets consider hiring a local interpreter for contract negotiations.




Quick Reference Checklist



  • Greet first, always. Start every interaction with As-salamu alaykum before mentioning business.

  • Use the correct gender form. Kayfa haluka? to a man, Kayfa haluki? to a woman.

  • Introduce yourself in order: name origin nationality purpose of visit.

  • Learn numbers 110 before negotiating quantities, prices, or delivery dates.

  • Know the days and months to confirm schedules without relying on translators.

  • Use polite expressions (Min fadlik, Shukran, Afwan) generously they build trust.

  • Adapt to local dialect and observe cultural norms, especially in rural and conservative areas.

  • Confirm in writing any verbal agreement, as spoken Arabic numbers and dates can be misheard in noisy market settings.
