1. Choosing the Right Greenhouse Structure for African Conditions

A greenhouse is a capital investment that will shape your farm for the next ten to twenty years. In Africa, the choice of structure is not simply about copying what works in the Netherlands or Israel. Our sunlight is intense, our temperatures can exceed forty degrees Celsius, our winds can be violent, and our access to spare parts and skilled labour varies enormously. This lesson walks you through the practical decisions that determine whether your greenhouse becomes a profitable asset or an expensive white elephant. The first decision is the type of structure. There are three broad categories you will encounter: the low-cost wooden or bamboo frame with plastic cover, the mid-range galvanised steel frame with UV-treated polythene, and the high-end polycarbonate or glass structure. For most commercial vegetable growers in Africa, the mid-range galvanised steel frame with a well-fitted polythene cover is the sweet spot. Wooden structures are cheap to build but rot quickly in humid conditions, need frequent repairs, and cannot support insect netting properly. Glass is excellent for light transmission but is expensive, fragile, and requires a level of technical maintenance that few rural areas can support. Galvanised steel gives you a ten to fifteen year lifespan if properly installed, and replacement covers are available in most capital cities. The second decision is the shape. A gable roof, also called a pitched roof, sheds rain effectively and allows hot air to rise and escape through roof vents. A tunnel or hoop structure is cheaper to build and works well in drier areas, but it can overheat because hot air has nowhere to go. A sawtooth or multi-span design is best for large operations because it combines good ventilation with efficient use of space. If you are farming in a high-rainfall area like the coastal belts of West Africa or the highlands of East Africa, choose a gable or sawtooth design with gutters that direct water away from the structure. If you are in a semi-arid zone like northern Nigeria, Senegal, or parts of Kenya, a tunnel with roll-up sides may be sufficient and cheaper. The third decision is the covering material. Polythene film is the most common and most affordable. Look for a film that is at least 150 microns thick and treated against ultraviolet radiation. Untreated film will become brittle and tear within one or two seasons. In Africa, the sun is unforgiving, so UV stabilisation is not optional. A good quality UV-treated film should last three to five years. Polycarbonate sheets last ten to fifteen years but cost three to five times more. Insect netting, usually 40 to 50 mesh, is used on the sides and sometimes the roof to exclude pests. Netting reduces ventilation, so you must design larger openings or add fans. The fourth decision is orientation. In most of Africa, which lies in the northern or southern tropics, the sun passes overhead or slightly to the north or south depending on the season. The ideal orientation is east to west, so that the long axis of the greenhouse runs east to west. This minimises the surface area exposed to the midday sun and reduces overheating. If your site slopes, orient the greenhouse so that the ridge runs along the contour to avoid erosion. The fifth decision is site selection. Choose a site with good drainage, because waterlogged soil will destroy your foundation and create disease problems. Avoid low-lying areas where cold air and water collect. Ensure you have access to water, electricity, and a road that a truck can use. If you are selling to supermarkets or hotels, your site should be within two hours of your main buyer. The sixth decision is size. Start small. A greenhouse of 8 metres by 20 metres, which is 160 square metres, is a manageable size for a beginner. It can produce enough tomatoes, peppers, or cucumbers to generate income while you learn the skills. Do not build a half-hectare greenhouse on your first attempt. The learning curve is steep, and mistakes are expensive. The seventh decision is the foundation. In Africa, termites and soil movement are real problems. Use concrete footings or treated timber posts set in concrete. Do not simply push metal posts into the ground, because they will shift and the structure will twist. The eighth decision is ventilation. A greenhouse without ventilation becomes an oven. You need roof vents, side vents, or roll-up sides. In hot climates, you may need both. The total vent area should be at least fifteen to twenty percent of the floor area. If you cannot achieve that with passive vents, plan for exhaust fans. The ninth decision is water and electricity. Drip irrigation is standard in greenhouses because it saves water and reduces humidity. You will need a water tank, a pump, and a filtration system. Electricity is needed for pumps, fans, and lighting if you are growing in winter. If the grid is unreliable, budget for a solar pump and battery system. The tenth decision is cost. A basic 8 by 20 metre galvanised steel greenhouse with UV polythene, insect netting, drip irrigation, and a small water tank will cost between three thousand and eight thousand US dollars in most African countries, depending on local material prices and labour. That is a significant investment. Before you build, write a simple business plan. Estimate your yields, your selling price, your costs, and your break-even point. If you cannot repay the investment within three years, reconsider the design or the crop. Finally, remember that a greenhouse is a tool, not a guarantee. It protects your crops from rain, pests, and some diseases, but it also creates a microclimate that you must manage. The best structure in the world will fail if you do not monitor temperature, humidity, and soil moisture every day. Start with a design that suits your climate, your budget, and your market, and you will have a solid foundation for profitable greenhouse vegetable farming.

2. Soil, Growing Media, and Fertility Management in Greenhouses

In a greenhouse, you are not simply growing plants in soil. You are managing a controlled environment where the growing medium, water, and nutrients must be balanced carefully. In Africa, many growers make the mistake of treating greenhouse soil the same way they treat open-field soil. That approach leads to poor yields, disease outbreaks, and wasted money. This lesson explains how to prepare and manage your growing medium for maximum productivity. The first question is whether to grow in soil or in a soilless medium. Soil-based greenhouse production is cheaper and more familiar, but it has serious drawbacks. Soil in a greenhouse is protected from rain, so salts from fertiliser accumulate. It is also difficult to sterilise, so soil-borne diseases like fusarium wilt and bacterial wilt build up over time. Soilless culture, using coco peat, perlite, vermiculite, or rockwool, gives you more control but requires more technical knowledge and a steady supply of inputs. For most small and medium-scale African growers, a hybrid approach works best: grow in raised beds filled with a mixture of local soil, compost, and sand, and rotate crops regularly. If you choose soil, you must test it before planting. A basic soil test costs very little at a government agricultural office or a private lab. You need to know the pH, the organic matter content, and the levels of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. Most vegetables grow best at a pH between 6.0 and 6.8. If your soil is too acidic, add agricultural lime. If it is too alkaline, add gypsum or elemental sulphur. Do not guess. A pH problem will lock up nutrients even if you apply them. The second issue is soil structure. Greenhouse soil becomes compacted because you walk on it and because irrigation water pushes fine particles down. Compacted soil has poor drainage and poor root growth. To prevent this, build permanent beds and never walk on them. Use paths between beds. Add organic matter every season. Compost, well-rotted manure, and crop residues improve structure, water holding capacity, and nutrient availability. In Africa, we have abundant sources of organic matter: cattle manure, poultry litter, goat droppings, and crop residues like maize stalks and bean haulms. Compost them properly before use to kill weed seeds and pathogens. The third issue is salinity. In greenhouses, salts accumulate because there is no rain to leach them away. You may see a white crust on the soil surface. This is a sign of high salinity, which stunts plants and reduces yields. To manage salinity, apply more water than the crop needs occasionally to leach salts below the root zone. This is called leaching. You must have good drainage for leaching to work. If your soil is heavy clay, install drainage tiles or grow in raised beds with a gravel layer at the bottom. The fourth issue is fertilisation. Greenhouse vegetables are heavy feeders. A crop of tomatoes can remove more than two hundred kilograms of nitrogen per hectare. You cannot rely on soil alone. You need a fertiliser programme that matches the crop stage. At planting, apply a balanced basal fertiliser. During vegetative growth, apply nitrogen. During flowering and fruiting, apply potassium and phosphorus. Calcium and magnesium are also important, especially for tomatoes and peppers, because deficiency causes blossom end rot. The best approach is fertigation, which means injecting soluble fertilisers into the irrigation water. This delivers nutrients directly to the root zone in the right concentration. You will need a fertigation tank, a dosing pump, and soluble fertiliser blends. If fertigation is too expensive, you can apply granular fertiliser and water it in, but you will have less control. The fifth issue is organic matter and compost. Compost is not just a source of nutrients. It also feeds soil microbes, improves water retention, and suppresses some diseases. Make compost from a mixture of green materials, like fresh grass and vegetable waste, and brown materials, like dry leaves and straw. Turn the pile every two weeks. It is ready when it is dark, crumbly, and smells earthy. Apply ten to twenty tonnes per hectare per year, or about one to two kilograms per square metre. The sixth issue is mulching. A layer of mulch on the soil surface reduces evaporation, suppresses weeds, and keeps the soil temperature stable. In Africa, you can use dry grass, straw, or black plastic. Black plastic also warms the soil in cooler highland areas. The seventh issue is crop rotation. Do not grow the same crop in the same bed season after season. Rotate between leaf crops, fruit crops, and root crops. This breaks disease cycles and balances nutrient use. For example, follow tomatoes with lettuce, then with carrots, then back to tomatoes. The eighth issue is soil solarisation. If you have a disease problem, you can solarise the soil by covering it with clear plastic for four to six weeks during the hottest part of the year. The heat kills many pathogens and weed seeds. This is a cheap and effective technique in Africa, where solar radiation is strong. The ninth issue is growing media for soilless culture. Coco peat is the most popular medium in Africa because it is available, affordable, and has good water holding capacity. It is usually mixed with perlite or rice husks to improve drainage. Rockwool is excellent but expensive and not widely available. Vermiculite and perlite are also imported in many countries. If you use soilless media, you must use a complete nutrient solution. You cannot rely on the medium to provide nutrients. You will need to measure electrical conductivity and pH regularly. The tenth issue is record keeping. Write down what you apply, when you apply it, and what the crop looks like. This helps you learn from your mistakes and repeat your successes. A simple notebook is enough. Record the date, the crop, the fertiliser type, the amount, and any observations. Over time, you will develop a fertility programme that works for your farm. In summary, greenhouse fertility management is about balance. You must balance pH, salinity, nutrients, organic matter, and water. Test your soil, build your organic matter, use fertigation if you can, rotate your crops, and keep records. Do these things, and your greenhouse will produce healthy, high-value vegetables season after season.

3. Climate Control and Irrigation in African Greenhouses

A greenhouse in Africa is not the same as a greenhouse in Europe. In Europe, the main challenge is keeping the greenhouse warm. In Africa, the main challenge is keeping it cool. If you do not manage temperature and humidity, your plants will suffer heat stress, flowers will drop, and diseases will thrive. This lesson covers the practical techniques for climate control and irrigation that work in African conditions. Start with temperature. The ideal temperature for most greenhouse vegetables, such as tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers, is between twenty and twenty-eight degrees Celsius during the day and fifteen to eighteen degrees at night. In many parts of Africa, midday temperatures inside a closed greenhouse can reach forty-five degrees or more. At that temperature, pollen becomes sterile, fruit set fails, and plants wilt. You must have a strategy to remove heat. The first tool is ventilation. Passive ventilation uses vents and openings to allow hot air to escape and cooler air to enter. Hot air rises, so roof vents are the most effective. Side vents or roll-up sides allow air to move horizontally. For passive ventilation to work, the total vent area should be at least fifteen to twenty percent of the floor area. If your greenhouse has only small vents, you will not get enough air exchange. The second tool is shading. A shade net over the greenhouse, or shade paint on the covering, reduces the amount of solar radiation entering. In Africa, a shade factor of thirty to fifty percent is often appropriate during the hottest months. You can use a removable shade net so that you can take it off during cooler seasons. Shade paint is cheaper but permanent until it washes off. The third tool is evaporative cooling. This works by passing air through a wet pad. The water evaporates and cools the air. Evaporative cooling is very effective in hot, dry climates like the Sahel, but it does not work well in humid areas like the coast. You can build a simple fan and pad system using local materials, but it requires a reliable water supply and electricity. The fourth tool is fogging or misting. Fine nozzles spray water into the air, which evaporates and cools the greenhouse. Fogging also increases humidity, which can be good in dry climates but bad in humid ones. Use fogging carefully and monitor humidity. The fifth tool is thermal mass. Materials like water barrels, concrete walls, or stone floors absorb heat during the day and release it at night. This moderates temperature swings. In highland areas where nights are cold, thermal mass can prevent chilling injury. Now consider humidity. Most greenhouse vegetables prefer a relative humidity between sixty and eighty percent. If humidity is too low, plants lose water too quickly and stomata close, reducing growth. If humidity is too high, fungal diseases like powdery mildew, downy mildew, and botrytis thrive. High humidity also reduces transpiration, which reduces calcium uptake and causes disorders like blossom end rot. To manage humidity, you need ventilation and heating. Ventilation removes moist air. Heating raises the air temperature, which lowers relative humidity. In Africa, heating is rarely needed except in highland areas. The main tool is ventilation. Open vents in the morning to let out humid air. Avoid watering late in the day, because the water will not evaporate and humidity will stay high overnight. Use drip irrigation instead of overhead irrigation, because drip irrigation wets only the root zone and leaves the air drier. Now consider irrigation. Water is the most critical input in a greenhouse. You cannot rely on rain. You must have a reliable water source, a storage tank, and a distribution system. The best system for greenhouse vegetables is drip irrigation. Drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root zone, saves water, reduces weed growth, and keeps the foliage dry. A drip system consists of a water source, a pump, a filter, a main line, sub-main lines, and drip lines with emitters. You also need a fertigation unit if you want to inject fertiliser. The amount of water you need depends on the crop, the stage of growth, and the weather. A mature tomato crop in a hot climate may use six to eight litres of water per plant per day. A lettuce crop may use one to two litres per plant per day. You must monitor soil moisture. You can do this by hand, by squeezing the soil, or with a tensiometer or moisture meter. Do not guess. Overwatering causes root diseases and nutrient leaching. Underwatering causes wilting and yield loss. The best practice is to irrigate little and often. For example, instead of one long irrigation, apply several short irrigations per day. This keeps the root zone at a constant moisture level. In sandy soils, you may need to irrigate four to six times per day. In clay soils, two to three times per day is enough. You should also adjust irrigation for the weather. On cloudy days, plants use less water. On hot, windy days, they use more. If you have a timer, adjust it. If you do not, check the soil every morning. Water quality is also important. Greenhouse vegetables are sensitive to salinity. If your water has high electrical conductivity, you may need to leach the soil more often or use a different water source. Test your water before you use it. You can also collect rainwater from the greenhouse roof and store it in a tank. This is a good practice in Africa, where rain is often abundant in the wet season. Finally, consider automation. A simple timer for irrigation is affordable and saves labour. A thermostat for fans is also affordable. You do not need a fully automated system to be profitable. Start with manual control, learn your crop, and then add automation where it makes sense. In summary, climate control and irrigation are the daily management tasks that determine your success. Keep the greenhouse cool with ventilation, shading, and evaporative cooling. Keep humidity in the right range with ventilation and drip irrigation. Water carefully, monitor soil moisture, and adjust for the weather. Do these things, and your plants will reward you with high yields and quality fruit.

4. Integrated Pest and Disease Management for Greenhouse Vegetables

Pests and diseases are the biggest threat to greenhouse vegetable production. In a greenhouse, the environment is warm, humid, and crowded. These are ideal conditions for pests and pathogens. Unlike open-field farming, you cannot rely on rain, wind, and natural predators to keep problems in check. You must manage pests and diseases proactively. This lesson explains integrated pest management, or IPM, for African greenhouses. The first principle of IPM is prevention. It is easier and cheaper to prevent a pest outbreak than to control one. Start with clean planting material. Buy certified seeds or seedlings from a reputable supplier. Do not use seeds saved from infected plants. If you raise your own seedlings, use sterilised trays and clean media. The second principle is sanitation. Remove weeds from inside and around the greenhouse. Weeds host pests like aphids, whiteflies, and thrips. Remove crop residues after harvest. Do not leave old plants in or near the greenhouse. Clean your tools and boots before entering. If you visit other farms, change your clothes and shoes before entering your greenhouse. The third principle is exclusion. Keep pests out. Use insect netting on vents and doors. A forty to fifty mesh netting will exclude most insects. Make sure there are no gaps. Use a double door system, where you enter one door, close it, and then enter the second door. This prevents pests from flying in. The fourth principle is monitoring. You cannot manage what you do not measure. Walk through your greenhouse at least twice a week. Look at the undersides of leaves, the growing tips, and the flowers. Use yellow sticky traps to monitor flying insects like whiteflies, aphids, and thrips. Use blue sticky traps for thrips. Check the traps weekly and record the numbers. If you see a sudden increase, act immediately. The fifth principle is cultural control. This means using farming practices to reduce pest and disease pressure. Rotate crops. Do not grow the same crop in the same bed season after season. Use resistant varieties if available. Space plants properly to allow air movement. Prune lower leaves to improve airflow and reduce humidity. Avoid overhead watering. Remove and destroy infected plants immediately. Do not compost diseased plants unless you are sure your compost reaches high temperatures. The sixth principle is biological control. This means using natural enemies to control pests. In Africa, several biological control agents are available. For whiteflies, you can use Encarsia formosa, a parasitic wasp. For aphids, you can use ladybirds, lacewings, or Aphidius colemani. For spider mites, you can use Phytoseiulus persimilis. For thrips, you can use Amblyseius cucumeris. These agents are living organisms, so you must handle them carefully. Order them from a reputable supplier and release them according to the instructions. Biological control works best when pest populations are low. Do not wait until you have a severe infestation. The seventh principle is chemical control. This is the last resort. If you must use pesticides, choose the least toxic option that will solve the problem. Use selective pesticides that do not kill beneficial insects. Always read the label and follow the instructions. Wear protective clothing. Do not spray on windy days. Do not spray just before harvest. Observe the pre-harvest interval. Rotate chemicals with different modes of action to prevent resistance. Never mix more than two chemicals unless the label says it is safe. Never use a chemical that is banned in your country. The eighth principle is record keeping. Write down every pest and disease you see, every treatment you apply, and the result. This helps you identify patterns and make better decisions. Now let us look at the most common problems in African greenhouses. Whiteflies are small, white, flying insects that suck sap and transmit viruses. They are a major problem in tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers. Control them with yellow sticky traps, insect netting, and biological control. If you must spray, use neem oil or insecticidal soap. Aphids are small, soft-bodied insects that cluster on new growth. They also transmit viruses. Control them with ladybirds, lacewings, and neem oil. Thrips are tiny, slender insects that cause silvery streaks on leaves and damage flowers. Control them with blue sticky traps, predatory mites, and spinosad. Spider mites are tiny, eight-legged pests that cause yellow stippling and fine webbing. Control them with predatory mites, neem oil, and sulphur. Fungal diseases are also common. Powdery mildew appears as white powdery spots on leaves. It thrives in humid conditions with poor airflow. Control it with ventilation, resistant varieties, and potassium bicarbonate or sulphur. Downy mildew appears as yellow patches on the upper leaf surface and grey mould on the underside. It thrives in cool, wet conditions. Control it with ventilation, drip irrigation, and copper-based fungicides. Botrytis, also called grey mould, appears as grey fuzzy growth on stems, leaves, and fruit. It thrives in high humidity. Control it with ventilation, pruning, and removing infected tissue. Bacterial wilt causes sudden wilting and death. It is soil-borne and difficult to control. Prevent it by rotating crops, using clean media, and removing infected plants. Viral diseases cause mosaic patterns, leaf curling, and stunted growth. They are transmitted by insects, especially whiteflies and aphids. There is no cure for viral diseases. Prevent them by controlling insects, using resistant varieties, and removing infected plants. In summary, IPM is a system, not a single technique. It combines prevention, sanitation, exclusion, monitoring, cultural control, biological control, and chemical control. Start with prevention. Monitor regularly. Act early. Keep records. If you do these things, you will reduce your losses and increase your profits. Remember that a healthy plant is a resistant plant. Give your crops the right water, nutrients, and climate, and they will be better able to withstand pests and diseases.

5. Harvesting, Post-Harvest Handling, and Marketing Greenhouse Vegetables

You have invested in a greenhouse, managed the climate, fed the soil, and protected your crops from pests. Now comes the moment of truth: harvesting and selling. In Africa, many greenhouse growers produce excellent vegetables but lose money because of poor post-harvest handling and weak marketing. This lesson covers the practical steps to harvest, handle, and sell your vegetables at a premium price. Start with harvesting. The timing of harvest depends on the crop and the market. For tomatoes, harvest at the breaker stage, when the fruit is mature green to pink, if you are shipping long distances. Harvest at the red ripe stage if you are selling locally or to hotels. For peppers, harvest when the fruit is full size and firm. For cucumbers, harvest when the fruit is young and tender, before the seeds harden. For lettuce, harvest in the cool of the morning or late afternoon. Never harvest in the heat of the day. Heat causes wilting and reduces shelf life. Use clean, sharp tools. Do not pull fruit off the plant, because that damages the plant and the fruit. Use a knife or scissors. Handle fruit gently. Do not drop or throw produce into crates. Place it carefully. Use clean crates. Do not use crates that have held chemicals or manure. Line crates with paper or leaves to prevent bruising. Do not overfill crates, because the weight will crush the produce at the bottom. Now consider post-harvest handling. The goal is to slow down respiration and prevent water loss. The first step is to remove field heat. Field heat is the heat that the produce has absorbed from the sun. If you do not remove it, the produce will continue to ripen and spoil quickly. The best way to remove field heat is to cool the produce. In Africa, you may not have a cold room, but you can use evaporative cooling. A simple evaporative cooler is a box with wet sand or charcoal around it. The water evaporates and cools the air inside. You can also use a shaded, well-ventilated area. Do not put produce in the sun. The second step is sorting and grading. Remove damaged, diseased, or unripe produce. Sort by size, colour, and quality. Uniform produce sells for a higher price. Buyers want consistency. The third step is cleaning. Remove soil and dust with a soft brush or a clean cloth. Do not wash produce unless the buyer requires it. Washing can introduce disease and reduce shelf life. If you must wash, use clean water and dry the produce thoroughly. The fourth step is packaging. Use packaging that protects the produce and allows air circulation. For tomatoes and peppers, use shallow crates or boxes. For leafy greens, use bunches tied with string or bags with holes. Do not use sealed plastic bags, because they trap moisture and cause rot. Label your packages with your farm name, the crop, the grade, and the date. The fifth step is storage. If you have a cold room, store produce at the correct temperature. Tomatoes store best at twelve to fifteen degrees Celsius. Peppers store at seven to ten degrees. Leafy greens store at zero to two degrees. If you do not have a cold room, store in a cool, dark, well-ventilated place. Do not store produce with ethylene-producing fruits like bananas or apples, because they will cause your vegetables to ripen too fast. Now consider marketing. This is where many growers fail. They produce a crop and then look for a buyer. That is backwards. You should identify your market before you plant. There are several market channels in Africa. The first is the local market. This is the easiest to access but often pays the lowest price. You sell directly to consumers or to small traders. The second is the hotel and restaurant market. This pays a premium for high-quality, consistent produce. You need to build relationships with chefs and purchasing managers. You must be reliable. If you promise to deliver every Tuesday, you must deliver every Tuesday. The third is the supermarket market. Supermarkets like Shoprite, Carrefour, and local chains buy in large volumes but have strict quality standards. They may require you to have a barcode, a food safety certificate, and a consistent supply. The fourth is the export market. This is the most demanding. You need to meet international standards, including GlobalGAP. You need a cold chain, proper packaging, and documentation. Export is not for beginners. The fifth is processing. You can add value by making tomato sauce, dried tomatoes, or pickles. This requires additional investment but can increase your income. To succeed in marketing, you need to differentiate yourself. Do not compete on price alone. Compete on quality, consistency, and service. Deliver on time. Communicate clearly. Be honest about your capacity. If you cannot supply a certain volume, say so. Build trust with your buyers. Use simple marketing tools. A WhatsApp group with your customers is a powerful tool in Africa. Send photos of your produce. Announce when it is available. Take orders. Deliver. Use social media like Facebook and Instagram to showcase your farm and your produce. Tell your story. Customers like to buy from farmers they know. Keep records of your sales. Record the date, the buyer, the crop, the quantity, the price, and the payment status. This helps you understand your profitability and plan for the future. Calculate your cost of production per kilogram. This includes seeds, fertiliser, water, labour, packaging, and transport. If your selling price is below your cost, you are losing money. You must either reduce costs or increase prices. Finally, consider value addition. If you have a surplus, do not let it rot. Dry it, process it, or preserve it. Dried tomatoes, peppers, and herbs have a long shelf life and can be sold in urban markets. This reduces waste and increases income. In summary, harvesting and marketing are not afterthoughts. They are core business activities. Harvest at the right time. Handle produce gently. Cool it quickly. Sort and grade it. Package it well. Identify your market before you plant. Build relationships. Deliver consistently. Keep records. Do these things, and you will turn your greenhouse vegetables into a profitable business that supports your family and your community.

<h2>Practical Reference: Greenhouse Record-Keeping, Input Sourcing, and Compliance for African Vegetable Growers</h2>

<p>Greenhouse vegetable farming in Africa is a business before it is a technology. The growers who survive the first three seasons are rarely the ones with the most expensive structures; they are the ones who know their numbers, buy inputs at the right price, and can prove to buyers, regulators, and lenders that their produce is safe and traceable. This practical reference lesson pulls together the record-keeping, input-sourcing, and compliance practices that turn a greenhouse from a hobby into a bankable enterprise. Use it alongside the earlier lessons on structures, growing media, climate control, IPM, and post-harvest handling. Everything here is designed to be photocopied, pinned to the packhouse wall, and used daily.</p>

<h3>1. Why Records Are a Financing Tool, Not a Chore</h3>
<p>In most African markets, greenhouse vegetables tomato, capsicum, cucumber, leafy greens are sold into one of four channels: open markets, supermarkets and hotels, export/regional trade, or processing. Each channel demands a different level of documentation. A hotel buyer may only want consistent supply; a supermarket or exporter will ask for spray records, harvest dates, and increasingly a GlobalG.A.P. or local food-safety certificate. Development finance institutions and banks such as the African Development Bank and many national agricultural banks now assess loan applications on cash-flow records, not collateral alone. A simple, honest record book is therefore a credit file.</p>

<h3>2. The Five Records That Matter Most</h3>
<p>You do not need a computer to run a professional greenhouse. You need five records, kept daily and reviewed weekly:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Input purchase register</strong> date, supplier, product, batch/lot number, quantity, unit price, receipt number. This is your defence if a chemical residue issue arises.</li>
<li><strong>Spray and IPM log</strong> date, pest/disease observed, product used, active ingredient, dose, pre-harvest interval (PHI), operator name, weather at application.</li>
<li><strong>Harvest and sales register</strong> date, crop, grade, weight (kg), buyer, unit price, payment status. This drives your gross margin per m.</li>
<li><strong>Labour and cash book</strong> daily wages, casual vs permanent, other cash in/out. Without this, profit is a guess.</li>
<li><strong>Traceability sheet per batch</strong> links a harvest date to the block, the inputs used, and the buyer. Supermarkets and exporters increasingly demand this.</li>
</ol>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;font-family:Arial;font-weight:bold;color:#b71c1c;font-size:15px"> SAFETY WARNING PESTICIDES, RESIDUES AND WORKER HEALTH</p>
<p style="margin:0;font-family:Arial;font-size:13px;color:#3e0000">
Greenhouse spraying is more dangerous than open-field spraying because air movement is restricted and chemical concentrations build up. Always: (1) use only pesticides registered in your country for the specific crop and pest; (2) wear the full PPE stated on the label gloves, boots, long sleeves, eye protection, and a respirator where required; (3) observe the pre-harvest interval (PHI) strictly never harvest inside the PHI window, even for a high-paying buyer; (4) never mix or store pesticides in food or drink containers; (5) keep a locked chemical store away from children and animals; (6) after spraying, ventilate the greenhouse and keep workers out for the label-stated re-entry interval; (7) if a worker shows dizziness, blurred vision, sweating, or vomiting after handling chemicals, stop work, move them to fresh air, and seek medical help immediately bring the product label to the clinic. Non-compliance can cause poisoning, cancer, and rejection of your produce. Sources: FAO/WHO <em>International Code of Conduct on Pesticide Management</em>; WHO pesticide exposure guidance; national plant protection regulations.
</p>

<h3>4. Input Sourcing: Buy Right, Buy Once</h3>
<p>Input cost is the largest single variable in a greenhouse budget. In many African markets, counterfeit or expired seed and agrochemicals are common. Protect your business by:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Buying from registered agro-dealers</strong> who can show a licence and give a receipt with batch numbers. Avoid open-market repackaged seed.</li>
<li><strong>Testing seed germination</strong> on a small sample before planting the whole house. A 10-day germination test on a damp cloth saves a season.</li>
<li><strong>Grouping purchases</strong> with neighbouring growers or a cooperative to access wholesale prices and formal invoices this also strengthens your credit file.</li>
<li><strong>Storing inputs correctly:</strong> seed in a cool, dry, dark place; fertiliser off the floor on pallets; chemicals locked and separate from food and feed.</li>
<li><strong>Recording every purchase</strong> in the input register. If a product fails, you have evidence for a claim and for your own planning.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Compliance Without Fear</h3>
<p>Compliance sounds bureaucratic, but in practice it is three habits: keep records, follow label instructions, and be traceable. Start with the requirements of your main buyer. If you sell to a supermarket, ask for their supplier checklist and work backwards. If you hope to export, look at GlobalG.A.P. or the relevant national food-safety standard, but do not try to implement everything at once. Most African greenhouse businesses become compliant over two to three seasons, adding one requirement at a time. The record book is the foundation everything else is built on it.</p>

<h3>6. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<div style="border:2px solid #558b2f;background:#f1f8e9;padding:14px;border-radius:6px;font-family:Arial;font-size:13px">
<ul style="margin:0;padding-left:20px">
<li> Input purchase register updated after every purchase, with batch numbers and receipts filed.</li>
<li> Spray/IPM log completed the same day, including active ingredient, dose, PHI, and operator.</li>
<li> Harvest and sales register completed daily date, grade, kg, buyer, price, payment status.</li>
<li> Labour and cash book balanced weekly; know your cost per kg produced.</li>
<li> Traceability batch sheet links each harvest to block, inputs, and buyer.</li>
<li> All chemicals stored locked, labelled, and away from food; full PPE available and used.</li>
<li> Seed germination tested before full planting; expired inputs removed and disposed of safely.</li>
<li> Records reviewed monthly with an agronomist, accountant, or trusted peer; actions noted.</li>
</ul>
</div>
