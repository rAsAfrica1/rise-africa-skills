1. Alkyd, Polyurethane, and Epoxy Resin Systems

Alkyd resins are the workhorse of solvent-based paints in Africa. They are made by reacting vegetable oils like soya or linseed with acids and alcohols. For a basic alkyd, you need a reaction kettle with a thermometer, stirrer, and condenser. Start by heating 100 kg of soya oil to 240C, then add 50 kg of pentaerythritol and 70 kg of phthalic anhydride. Hold at 240C for 3 hours until the acid value drops below 10 mg KOH/g. Cool to 180C and thin with 50 kg of mineral spirits. This gives a medium-oil alkyd for brushable enamel.

Polyurethane resins come in two parts: a polyol and an isocyanate. For a moisture-cure polyurethane, you mix a polyol with toluene diisocyanate (TDI) at 60C for 2 hours. The ratio is critical: 1 mole of polyol to 2 moles of TDI. Use a nitrogen blanket to prevent moisture from reacting. Polyurethane gives tough, chemical-resistant coatings for floors and metal. In Africa, it's popular for concrete floors in factories because it withstands heavy foot traffic and cleaning chemicals.

Epoxy resins are also two-part: resin and hardener. The most common is bisphenol A diglycidyl ether (BADGE) with a polyamine hardener. Mix 100 parts resin with 50 parts hardener by weight. Epoxy cures at room temperature but can be accelerated to 60C for faster set. It has excellent adhesion to metal and concrete, making it ideal for water tanks and boat hulls. However, epoxy is not UV stableit yellows and chalks in sunlight, so use it only indoors or topcoat with polyurethane.

In your workshop, always check resin quality: alkyd should be clear and free of gel particles; polyurethane should not have a skin; epoxy should be uniform. Store resins in sealed drums away from moisture. When scaling up, keep records of temperature, time, and acid value. A simple acid-base titration kit costs about $50 and is essential for alkyd production.

2. Solvent Selection and Blending

Solvents dissolve the resin and control drying. The key properties are evaporation rate, solvency power, and cost. In Africa, common solvents are mineral spirits (white spirit), xylene, toluene, acetone, and butyl acetate. Mineral spirits are cheap and slow-evaporating, good for brush application. Xylene and toluene are faster and stronger but more toxic and flammable. Acetone is very fast but weak for alkyds. Butyl acetate is a medium evaporator for polyurethanes.

Use the 'solubility parameter' concept without complex math: if the resin and solvent have similar polarity, they mix. Alkyds are non-polar, so use mineral spirits or xylene. Polyurethanes need esters like butyl acetate. Epoxies often use ketones like methyl ethyl ketone (MEK) or acetone. A simple test: put 10 g of resin in a jar, add 20 ml of solvent, stir, and see if it dissolves clear. If cloudy, try another solvent.

Blending solvents gives better control. For a brushable enamel, blend 70% mineral spirits with 30% xylene. This gives a drying time of 4-6 hours. For spray application, use 50% xylene, 30% toluene, 20% butyl acetate for a 30-minute flash-off. Always blend by volume in a clean drum with a stirrer. Never mix solvents in a plastic containeruse metal or glass. Measure with a graduated cylinder or flow meter.

Safety and cost: xylene and toluene are hazardous; use gloves and respirator. In many African countries, mineral spirits cost $2-3 per liter, xylene $3-4, butyl acetate $5-6. Buy in 200-liter drums to save 30%. Store solvents in a cool, ventilated shed away from sparks. Test each batch for evaporation rate by putting 10 ml in a dish and timing how long to dry. Record results. A good blend will give a smooth film without orange peel or blushing.

3. Formulation of Enamels and Varnishes

Enamel is a pigmented solvent-based paint that dries to a hard, glossy film. A basic white enamel formula for 100 kg: 40 kg alkyd resin (60% solids), 25 kg titanium dioxide, 20 kg mineral spirits, 10 kg xylene, 3 kg calcium carbonate (filler), 1 kg driers (cobalt and zirconium), and 1 kg anti-skinning agent. Mix in a high-speed disperser: first add resin and solvents, then slowly add pigments. Disperse for 30 minutes at 1500 rpm. Then add driers and anti-skin. Check viscosity with a Ford cup #4should be 60-80 seconds. If too thick, add solvent; if too thin, add more resin.

Varnish is a clear coating without pigment. For a polyurethane varnish, mix 50 kg polyurethane resin (50% solids), 30 kg butyl acetate, 15 kg xylene, 3 kg UV absorber, and 2 kg flow agent. Stir gently to avoid bubbles. Varnish is used on wood to show grain. In Africa, it's popular for furniture and doors. Apply with brush or spray. Drying time is 2-4 hours for touch, 24 hours for full cure.

For epoxy enamel, use 50 kg epoxy resin, 20 kg titanium dioxide, 20 kg xylene, 5 kg butyl acetate, and 5 kg hardener (added just before use). Pot life is 4-6 hours at 25C. Mix only what you can use in that time. Epoxy enamel is excellent for metal railings and machinery.

Quality checks: test adhesion with cross-hatch tape test; test gloss with a gloss meter (60 reading should be >80 for enamel); test hardness with a pencil (2H or harder). Store enamels in lined cans to prevent reaction with metal. Label with batch number and date. In a small African workshop, you can produce 200 liters per day with a 500-liter disperser and a few drums.

4. Reaction Kettles and Process Control

A reaction kettle is a heated vessel with a stirrer, thermometer, condenser, and nitrogen inlet. For alkyd production, you need a stainless steel kettle of at least 500 liters. In Africa, many small factories use mild steel kettles with a glass-lined interior to resist acids. The kettle sits on a gas or electric heater. A simple setup costs $5,000-$10,000. You also need a condenser to recover solvents and a receiver flask.

Process control means monitoring temperature, time, and acid value. For alkyd, charge oil and polyol, heat to 240C with stirring at 200 rpm. Hold for 1 hour. Then add acid and heat to 240C again. Take a sample every 30 minutes: dissolve 1 g resin in 20 ml ethanol, add 2 drops phenolphthalein, and titrate with 0.1N KOH until pink. Acid value = (ml KOH 5.6)/g sample. Stop when acid value is below 10. Cool to 180C, add solvent, and stir for 30 minutes.

For polyurethane, control is simpler: mix polyol and isocyanate at 60C for 2 hours under nitrogen. Check NCO content by titration: react with dibutylamine, then titrate with HCl. NCO% should be 3-5%. For epoxy, mix resin and hardener at room temperature; no heating needed. But control the mix ratio preciselyuse a digital scale.

Safety in the kettle area: never leave a hot kettle unattended. Keep a fire extinguisher (CO2 or dry powder) nearby. Use a fume hood or exhaust fan. In Africa, power cuts are commonhave a backup generator for the stirrer and heater. Clean the kettle after each batch with solvent and scrape. Record all data in a logbook: batch number, temperatures, times, acid values, and yields. This helps you repeat good batches and troubleshoot bad ones.

5. Safety Measures for Solvent Handling

Solvents are flammable and toxic. In Africa, many workshops lack proper ventilation, so safety is critical. Always store solvents in a cool, dry, locked shed away from sunlight and ignition sources. Use metal drums with tight bungs. Keep only one day's supply in the work area. Never smoke or use open flames near solvents. Vapors can travel 10 meters and ignite. Use explosion-proof electrical fittings if possible; otherwise, keep motors and switches outside the solvent zone.

Personal protective equipment (PPE): wear nitrile gloves (not latexsolvents pass through latex), safety goggles, and a respirator with organic vapor cartridges. A half-mask respirator costs $30-$50 and cartridges last 40 hours. Wear cotton overalls, not synthetic, because synthetics can generate static sparks. Have a eyewash station and a safety shower nearby. If solvent splashes in eyes, rinse for 15 minutes with clean water.

Ventilation: use a fan to push air from the work area to outside. A simple exhaust fan of 0.5 kW can move 3000 m/hour. Open windows and doors. Never work in a closed room. If you feel dizzy, headache, or nausea, leave immediately and get fresh air. Solvent poisoning can cause liver and kidney damage over time.

Fire safety: keep a CO2 or dry powder extinguisher (5 kg) at each exit. Never use water on solvent firesit spreads them. Train everyone to use the extinguisher: pull pin, aim at base, squeeze, sweep. Have a fire blanket. In case of spill, absorb with sand or sawdust, then sweep into a metal container. Do not wash down drains. Dispose of solvent waste through a licensed handler. In many African cities, you can contact local environmental authorities for disposal. Keep a first aid kit and a list of emergency numbers.

6. Troubleshooting Solvent-Based Paint Issues

Common problems in solvent-based paints include slow drying, orange peel, blushing, sagging, and poor adhesion. Slow drying: usually caused by too much slow solvent (mineral spirits) or insufficient driers. Fix by adding 0.1% cobalt drier or replacing 10% of solvent with xylene. In humid African coastal areas, blushing (white haze) occurs when moisture condenses in the film. Prevent by adding 2% butyl cellosolve or drying in a warm room above 25C.

Orange peel (bumpy surface) happens when solvent evaporates too fast or spray gun is too far. Use a slower solvent blend (more mineral spirits) and spray at 20-25 cm distance with 40 psi pressure. For brush marks, thin the paint by 5% with xylene and brush in one direction. Sagging (runs) means too much paint or too thin. Apply thinner coats, wait 10 minutes between coats. If sagging occurs, sand and recoat.

Poor adhesion: often due to oily or dirty substrate. Clean metal with acetone or xylene, then sand with 120-grit. For concrete, etch with 10% muriatic acid, rinse, and dry. If paint peels, check for moisture in the substrateuse a moisture meter (should be <5%). For epoxy on metal, prime with a wash primer first. In Africa, dust is a problempaint in a clean area or use a tack cloth.

Other issues: skinning in the can (add 0.2% anti-skin), settling of pigments (add 0.5% anti-settling agent like fumed silica), and yellowing of alkyds in dark (use UV absorber). Keep a troubleshooting log: record problem, cause, and fix. Test small batches before full production. A simple test panel: apply paint on a 10x10 cm metal plate, dry, then scratch with a coin. If it flakes, adhesion is poor. Adjust formula and retest.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Solvent-Based Paint Manufacturing in Africa

This practical reference supports the RISE AFRICA Skills module on Solvent-Based Paint Manufacturing. It is written for the African entrepreneur, plant supervisor, or technical manager who needs to make fast, evidence-based decisions on the factory floor. Whether you are blending alkyd enamels in Nairobi, running a small epoxy floor-coating operation in Lagos, or supplying polyurethane varnishes to the furniture sector in Abidjan, the principles below apply. The focus is on throughput, cost control, product consistency, and above all safety.

Solvent-based paints remain commercially dominant across much of sub-Saharan Africa because they cure fast, adhere well to metal and wood, and tolerate the high humidity and temperature swings common in coastal and tropical markets. However, they carry real hazards: flammable solvents, reactive resins, and toxic isocyanates. This lesson gives you a single practical reference to manage those trade-offs profitably.

1. Resin Systems: Choosing the Right Chemistry

Alkyds dominate the African decorative and industrial enamel market because raw materials (phthalic anhydride, pentaerythritol, soya or linseed oil) are widely available and the reaction is forgiving. Polyurethanes give superior gloss and chemical resistance for wood and automotive refinish, but require moisture control and careful isocyanate handling. Epoxies deliver the best adhesion and chemical resistance for floors and tank linings, but they are two-pack systems with limited pot life and poor UV stability.

2. Solvent Selection and Blending

Solvent choice is a profit lever. In Africa, xylene and toluene are common but increasingly regulated; mineral turpentine (white spirit) is the workhorse for alkyds; methyl ethyl ketone (MEK) and butyl acetate are used for polyurethanes; and glycol ethers (e.g., butyl glycol) improve flow and levelling in epoxies. Blending is governed by the Hansen solubility parameter (HSP) model and by evaporation-rate profiles. A poorly balanced blend causes orange-peel, blushing, or slow drying all of which cost you rework and reputation.

3. Formulation of Enamels and Varnishes

A typical solvent-based alkyd enamel contains 4055% resin solids, 2030% pigment, 1525% solvent, and 13% additives (driers, anti-skinning agents, flow modifiers). Varnishes are simpler: resin, solvent, and additives only. In African conditions, increase slow-evaporating solvent (white spirit) to prevent dry-spray in 35 C heat, and add anti-skinning agent (e.g., methyl ethyl ketoxime) to extend can stability. Always run a small-batch trial before scaling; pigment-oil absorption varies by supplier and can shift viscosity by 2030%.

4. Reaction Kettles and Process Control

Alkyd and some epoxy-ester resins are made in heated reaction kettles with agitation, nitrogen blanket, and reflux condenser. Control acid value, viscosity, and temperature (typically 200240 C for alkyd esterification). In polyurethane and epoxy two-pack production, the kettle is used for blending rather than reaction temperature control below 40 C is critical to avoid premature curing. Install calibrated thermocouples, record every batch, and keep a logbook. Inconsistent process control is the number-one cause of batch rejection in African paint plants.

SAFETY WARNING FLAMMABLE SOLVENTS AND ISOCYANATES

Solvent vapours can ignite at temperatures as low as 9 C (MEK). Never use open flames, non-flameproof motors, or ungrounded drums in the blending area. Bond and earth all metal containers during transfer. Isocyanates used in polyurethane systems can cause asthma and permanent sensitisation even at low concentrations. Provide local exhaust ventilation, air-fed or cartridge respirators with appropriate filters, and medical surveillance for exposed workers. Store solvents in a cool, ventilated, bunded area away from oxidisers. In many African jurisdictions, failure to comply with flammable-liquid storage rules can void insurance and trigger regulatory shutdown.

5. Safety Measures for Solvent Handling

Build a safety culture, not just a safety poster. Practical steps: (1) designate a flameproof zone with signage; (2) install local exhaust ventilation at every mixing and filling point; (3) provide nitrile gloves, splash goggles, and organic-vapour respirators; (4) keep spill kits and dry powder or foam extinguishers within 10 metres of solvent handling; (5) train every operator on the SDS and emergency shutdown; (6) conduct monthly air monitoring for VOCs and isocyanates; (7) never decant solvents into unlabelled containers; (8) maintain a solvent inventory with daily reconciliation.

6. Troubleshooting Solvent-Based Paint Issues

Common defects and their business impact:

  • Orange peel / poor flow: solvent too fast; add slow solvent or flow additive. Cost: rework and customer complaints.
  • Blushing (white haze): moisture condensation during drying; reduce fast solvent, control humidity, or add anti-blush agent. Common in coastal African cities.
  • Slow drying / tacky film: insufficient drier or excess slow solvent; check drier balance and batch temperature.
  • Sagging: over-thinning or excessive film thickness; adjust viscosity and application technique.
  • Poor adhesion: contaminated substrate or wrong primer; improve surface prep.
  • Skinning in can: add anti-skinning agent; check can headspace and storage temperature.
  • Yellowing of epoxy: UV exposure; specify aliphatic polyurethane topcoat instead.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Verify resin, solvent, and pigment batch numbers and COAs before charging the kettle.
  2. Confirm flameproof electricals, bonding, and earthing are in place before any solvent transfer.
  3. Record kettle temperature, agitation speed, and reaction time for every batch.
  4. Check acid value or viscosity at the specified hold point before cooling.
  5. Run a drawdown and viscosity test before filling; retain a sample for 12 months.
  6. Confirm PPE gloves, goggles, respirator is worn by all operators in the blending zone.
  7. Inspect spill kits, extinguishers, and ventilation filters weekly.
  8. Reconcile solvent inventory daily and investigate any unexplained loss.
