1. Water Supply Systems in African Cities and Towns

This lesson opens Module 6 by grounding you in how water actually reaches the taps, tanks, and yards where you will work. In most African cities and towns, water supply is a layered system: a utility or municipal source, a bulk storage tank on the property, a booster pump where pressure is weak, and a distribution network of pipes to kitchens, bathrooms, and outdoor taps. Your job as a plumber is not just to connect pipes but to understand the whole chain so you can diagnose problems and advise customers honestly. Start with the source. In many areas, customers receive water from a public mains connection that may be intermittent. Others rely on boreholes, shallow wells, rainwater harvesting, or water vendors. Each source has different quality and pressure characteristics. A borehole may deliver water with sand, iron, or high hardness. Rainwater is soft but can be acidic and carries debris from roofs. Vendor water may be clean but expensive and stored in tanks that need cleaning. When you quote a job, ask where the water comes from, how often it is available, and whether the customer stores it. The answers shape the pipe sizes, tank capacity, pump choice, and filtration you recommend. Next, understand pressure and flow. A gravity system from a rooftop tank gives roughly 0.1 bar for every meter of height. A tank 10 meters above a tap gives about 1 bar, which is enough for a shower but not for a long horizontal run. If the tank is only 2 meters above the tap, expect a weak trickle. In many African homes, the tank sits on a concrete stand or a metal frame beside the house, often only 3 to 5 meters high. That is why booster pumps are common. When you install a pump, size it to the demand: count the fixtures, estimate simultaneous use, and add a margin. A pump that is too small will run continuously and burn out. A pump that is too large will cycle rapidly and damage the pressure switch. Use a pressure tank and a pressure switch set correctly, and always install a non-return valve to protect the pump from backflow. Pipe sizing matters more than many trainees expect. In a typical African home, 20 mm or 25 mm pipes are common for mains and sub-mains, with 15 mm or 20 mm branches to fixtures. If you undersize, the customer complains of low pressure when two taps run at once. If you oversize, you waste money and water sits in the pipes longer, which can affect quality. Learn to use simple flow tables and the rule that velocity should stay below about 1.5 to 2 meters per second in small pipes to avoid noise and erosion. For long runs, increase the diameter rather than the pump size. Storage is the next pillar. In areas with intermittent supply, a storage tank is not a luxury; it is the heart of the system. Recommend a tank sized for at least one day of household demand, often 1000 to 5000 liters for a family. Place it where it can be filled by gravity or a pump, and where it can be drained and cleaned. Install a float valve or a level switch to prevent overflow. Fit a first-flush diverter for rainwater and a screen or filter before the tank. Make sure the tank has an overflow pipe that discharges safely away from the foundation, and a washout valve at the lowest point. A tank that cannot be cleaned becomes a breeding ground for sediment and bacteria. Water quality is part of your professional duty. You are not a chemist, but you can spot problems: cloudy water, a metallic taste, a rotten-egg smell, blue-green stains, or white scale on fixtures. These suggest sediment, iron, sulfur, copper corrosion, or hardness. Recommend appropriate treatment: sediment filters for particles, activated carbon for taste and odor, iron filters for iron, water softeners for hardness, and UV or chlorination for disinfection where needed. In many African markets, customers are suspicious of chemicals, so explain that treatment is about safety and equipment life, not just taste. Finally, think about the business side. A plumber who understands supply systems can sell maintenance contracts: tank cleaning twice a year, pump servicing, filter replacement, and pressure checks. These recurring jobs smooth your income between big installations. Keep a simple record for each customer: source, tank size, pump model, pipe sizes, and service dates. When a problem occurs, you can diagnose faster and charge fairly. In this module, you will practice sketching a supply system from source to fixture, calculating tank size and pump head, and writing a one-page recommendation for a customer. That document is often what wins the job. Remember that in Africa, water is not just a utility; it is a daily negotiation with time, distance, and money. Your skill is to make that negotiation easier for the customer, and to build a reputation for systems that work when the mains do not.

2. Drainage, Waste, and Vent Systems for African Buildings

This lesson covers the other half of plumbing: taking waste away safely and keeping smells out of the building. In many African homes and small commercial buildings, drainage is where the most serious health and structural problems occur. A blocked or badly sloped drain can flood a kitchen, undermine a foundation, or spread disease. Your work here protects the customer and the community. Start with the basic principle: waste flows downhill by gravity, and air must follow it. Every fixture that discharges waste needs a trap to hold a water seal, and every trap needs a vent to equalize pressure. Without a vent, the water seal is sucked out when a nearby fixture drains, and sewer gas enters the building. That gas smells bad and can carry pathogens. In practice, many African buildings have poor or missing venting. Your job is to improve it without tearing down walls when possible. Learn the three main drainage categories: soil and waste, which carry human waste and greywater; stormwater, which carries rain; and vent pipes, which carry air. Never connect stormwater to the foul drain unless the local authority allows it, because heavy rain will overload the sewer and back up into the house. Keep them separate. For soil and waste, use the correct pipe material. uPVC is common and affordable, but it must be supported and protected from sunlight if exposed. Cast iron is quieter but expensive. HDPE is flexible and good for underground runs, but it needs proper joints. In many African markets, uPVC is the practical choice. Use solvent cement correctly: clean the joint, apply cement to both surfaces, push and twist a quarter turn, and hold for a few seconds. A bad joint will leak and attract insects. Slope is critical. A common rule is 1 in 40 to 1 in 60 for 100 mm pipe, and 1 in 20 to 1 in 30 for smaller branches. Too little slope causes solids to settle. Too much slope causes water to run away from solids, leaving them behind. Use a spirit level and a string line, and check the fall over the whole run, not just one section. For long runs, install access points or rodding eyes so you can clear blockages without digging. Traps come in several types: P-traps, S-traps, bottle traps, and drum traps. In modern practice, use P-traps with a vent, or bottle traps under sinks where space is tight. Avoid S-traps because they can siphon. The water seal should be at least 50 mm deep. If a trap dries out because the fixture is unused, pour a little oil or water down it, or install a trap primer. In hot, dry African climates, floor drains in bathrooms and kitchens often dry out and let smells in. Recommend a self-priming trap or a regular maintenance visit. Venting can be done in several ways. The best is a full vent stack that runs up through the roof. Where that is not possible, use an air admittance valve, which is a one-way valve that lets air in but not out. These are affordable and easy to install under sinks or in cabinets. They are not a substitute for a proper vent in every case, but they solve many smell problems in existing buildings. For groups of fixtures, use a common vent or a wet vent, but follow local code and keep the vent size adequate. Inspection chambers, also called manholes or inspection eyes, are essential. Place them at changes of direction, at junctions, and every 15 to 20 meters on straight runs. In many African compounds, the drain runs under a concrete slab or a paved yard, so an inspection chamber is the only way to clear a blockage. Build them with a removable cover, a benched channel, and a smooth invert. A poorly built chamber becomes a sump for solids. For septic tanks and soakaways, understand the basics. A septic tank separates solids and lets liquid flow to a soakaway or drain field. It needs to be pumped out every few years, and it must not receive stormwater or chemical waste. In dense urban areas, connection to a public sewer is preferred, but where that is not available, a well-designed septic system is the norm. Size it for the number of users, and keep the soakaway away from wells and foundations. Finally, think about maintenance and business. Drainage problems are urgent, so customers pay quickly. Offer a call-out service for blockages, and use the visit to inspect traps, vents, and inspection chambers. Sell a yearly drain check that includes flushing, rodding, and checking slopes. Keep a drain map for each property you service. In this module, you will practice designing a simple drainage layout for a two-bedroom house, calculating slope, choosing pipe sizes, and writing a maintenance plan. A plumber who can stop smells and blockages is trusted with bigger jobs, including new builds and renovations. That trust is the foundation of a profitable plumbing business in Africa.

3. Plumbing Materials, Tools, and Sourcing in African Markets

This lesson is about the practical side of buying, using, and caring for the materials and tools that make your work profitable. In many African markets, the quality of plumbing materials varies widely. A pipe that looks the same as another may have thinner walls, recycled plastic, or poor fittings. Your reputation depends on choosing materials that last and on knowing where to source them reliably. Start with pipes. The most common materials are uPVC for drainage and cold water, cPVC or PPR for hot water, and PEX or HDPE for flexible runs. In many African countries, PPR is popular for hot and cold water because it is heat-fused and leak-resistant. uPVC is common for cold water and drainage but should not be used for hot water. Galvanized steel is still found in older buildings; it corrodes and restricts flow, so replace it when possible. Copper is excellent but expensive and often stolen, so it is less common in some areas. When you buy, check the wall thickness, the manufacturer mark, and the pressure rating. Ask for a sample and bend it slightly; good uPVC will flex without cracking, while poor quality will snap. For fittings, match the brand and material. A cheap elbow can crack under pressure. Buy from a supplier who will replace faulty goods, and keep receipts. In many markets, counterfeit fittings are common, so build a relationship with one or two trusted shops rather than chasing the lowest price. Valves and taps are another quality decision. Gate valves are for isolation, not for throttling. Ball valves are better for quick shutoff. Check valves prevent backflow. Pressure reducing valves protect fixtures from high pressure. In areas with intermittent supply, a non-return valve on the main prevents stored water from flowing back into the mains and protects the pump. Buy brass or good quality plastic valves; cheap ones seize within a year. For taps, ceramic cartridge taps last longer than rubber washer taps, but they need clean water. If the water has sand, install a filter before the tap. Tools are your livelihood. A basic plumbing kit should include pipe wrenches, adjustable wrenches, a hacksaw, a deburring tool, a pipe cutter, a threading kit for steel, a heat gun or fusion tool for PPR, a solvent cement applicator, a spirit level, a measuring tape, a plumb bob, a drain snake, a plunger, and a pressure test pump. For drainage, add a rodding tool and a set of drain rods. For hot water, add a tube bender and a flaring tool if you work with copper. Keep your tools clean and oiled. In many African workshops, rust and dust destroy tools quickly. Store them in a dry box, and wipe them after use. A dull hacksaw blade or a worn wrench costs you time and makes the job look unprofessional. Safety gear is not optional. Wear gloves, eye protection, and a dust mask when cutting or mixing cement. Use ear protection when using a hammer drill. Keep a first aid kit in your vehicle. In hot climates, drink water and take breaks. A plumber who works safely is a plumber who works tomorrow. Sourcing is a business skill. Build a list of suppliers for pipes, fittings, tanks, pumps, and tools. Compare prices but also compare stock reliability and return policies. In many cities, prices change with fuel and import costs, so quote with a validity period, such as 14 days. Buy in bulk for common items like 15 mm elbows, 20 mm tees, and solvent cement, but do not overstock items that may become obsolete. For special orders, ask for a deposit and give the customer a written estimate. Transport matters. If you do not have a vehicle, plan how to move long pipes and heavy tanks. Many suppliers deliver for a fee, which you can pass on to the customer. For remote jobs, carry extra fittings and a small generator or battery pump for testing. Learn to improvise safely. In some African markets, you may not find the exact fitting you need. You can sometimes combine fittings or use a threaded adapter, but never force a joint or use tape on a pressure fitting as a permanent fix. If you must improvise, tell the customer it is temporary and schedule the proper repair. Finally, think about pricing and profit. Calculate the cost of materials, transport, labor, and a margin for warranty. Do not compete only on price; compete on reliability. A customer who has had three bad plumbers will pay more for one who answers the phone and finishes the job. Keep a simple inventory sheet and a job record. In this module, you will practice costing a small installation, writing a materials list, and negotiating with a supplier. These skills turn a trade into a business. In Africa, where supply chains can be unpredictable, the plumber who plans ahead and buys wisely is the one who stays in work.

4. Pumps, Tanks, and Pressure Systems for Homes and Small Businesses

This lesson focuses on the equipment that keeps water moving when gravity and the mains are not enough. In many African homes, small hotels, schools, and clinics, a pump and tank system is the difference between a working tap and a dry one. Your ability to specify, install, and maintain these systems is a high-value skill. Start with the types of pumps. The most common are centrifugal pumps, jet pumps, submersible pumps, and booster pumps. A centrifugal pump is simple and good for moving water from a tank to a house, but it cannot lift water from a deep well. A jet pump can lift from a shallow well, usually up to about 8 meters, and is common in older installations. A submersible pump sits inside a borehole or well and pushes water up; it is efficient and quiet but needs a control box and protection from dry running. A booster pump increases pressure in a line, often after a tank or where the mains pressure is low. For most urban African homes with a rooftop or ground tank, a centrifugal booster pump is the practical choice. Sizing a pump is about flow and head. Flow is the amount of water in liters per minute that the system must deliver. Head is the total height the pump must push, plus friction losses in pipes and fittings. To estimate head, measure the vertical lift from the water level to the highest outlet, then add about 10 percent for every 10 meters of pipe, plus allowances for elbows and valves. A pump that is too small will not reach the top floor. A pump that is too large will cycle on and off, wear out the pressure switch, and waste electricity. Use the pump curve from the manufacturer and choose a model that delivers the required flow at the required head, not just the maximum head. Tanks come in plastic, steel, and concrete. Plastic tanks are affordable, easy to install, and resistant to corrosion, but they can become brittle in strong sunlight. Steel tanks are strong but need painting and can rust. Concrete tanks are durable but expensive and must be properly sealed. In many African settings, a black or dark plastic tank on a stand is common. Place the tank on a stable base, preferably concrete, and allow space for cleaning and inspection. Fit an inlet with a float valve, an outlet with a filter, an overflow that discharges away from the foundation, and a washout at the bottom. If the tank is underground, make sure it is watertight and protected from contamination. Pressure systems need controls. A pressure switch turns the pump on when pressure drops and off when it reaches the set point. A pressure tank, also called an accumulator, stores a small amount of water under pressure and reduces pump cycling. Set the pressure switch according to the system: for a typical house, 1.5 bar on and 3 bar off is common, but check the fixtures and the pump rating. Install a pressure gauge so you and the customer can see what is happening. Fit a non-return valve on the suction or inlet side to keep water from flowing back. For submersible pumps, install a dry-run protection device or a low-level cut-off switch in the tank or well. Electrical safety is critical. Pumps use motors that can be damaged by low voltage, which is common in some African areas. Use a surge protector and a properly sized circuit breaker. Earth the pump and the tank if it is metal. Never connect a pump with bare wires or tape. If you are not qualified to do electrical work, work with a licensed electrician. Water and electricity are a dangerous combination. Maintenance is where you build recurring income. A pump system needs regular checks: clean the tank, check the float valve, test the pressure switch, inspect the pressure tank charge, clean the pump strainer, and check for leaks. In dusty or sandy areas, clean or replace filters often. In areas with hard water, descale the pump and pipes. Keep a service log for each customer and remind them when service is due. A pump that is serviced lasts years longer and uses less electricity. Troubleshooting is a core skill. If the pump runs but no water comes, check for a closed valve, a blocked strainer, a lost prime, or a broken pipe. If the pump does not start, check the power, the pressure switch, and the capacitor. If the pump cycles rapidly, check the pressure tank charge and for leaks. If the water is dirty, check the tank and filters. Teach the customer simple checks, such as looking at the pressure gauge and listening for the pump, so they can call you with useful information. Finally, think about the business case. A pump and tank installation is a significant sale, but the maintenance contract is the steady income. Offer a package: installation, a one-year warranty, and a service plan. For small businesses like guesthouses, a reliable water system is essential, so they will pay for quality. In this module, you will practice sizing a pump for a two-story house, designing a tank stand, and writing a service checklist. These skills make you the go-to plumber for water systems in your area.

5. Sanitary Ware Installation, Maintenance, and Customer Care

This final lesson in Module 6 brings together the fixtures that customers see and use every day: toilets, sinks, showers, baths, and urinals. In African homes, hotels, schools, and offices, sanitary ware is where plumbing meets hospitality. A leaking toilet or a stained sink affects the customer experience and your reputation. Learn to install, maintain, and upgrade these fixtures with care. Start with toilets. The most common types are close-coupled, back-to-wall, and squat pans. In many African countries, squat pans are still preferred in some communities, while close-coupled toilets are common in urban homes. When installing a toilet, set the pan level and secure it to the floor with the correct bolts and a wax or rubber seal. The cistern must be level and connected with a flexible or rigid supply. The flush pipe must align with the pan inlet. Check the water level in the cistern; too high wastes water, too low gives a weak flush. Adjust the float valve and the flush valve. For dual-flush toilets, make sure both buttons work and that the seal seats properly. A running toilet is one of the most common and wasteful problems. It is usually caused by a worn flapper, a faulty float valve, or a misaligned flush valve. Fix it on the first visit. Carry common spares: flappers, float valves, washers, and supply hoses. Sinks and basins come in many styles: wall-hung, pedestal, countertop, and under-mount. The key to a good installation is a solid support and a proper trap. Wall-hung basins need a sturdy bracket or a frame, especially in buildings with weak walls. The waste pipe must slope to the drain, and the trap must be accessible for cleaning. Use silicone sealant at the wall and the counter, but do not rely on sealant for support. For countertop basins, cut the hole accurately and seal the edge. For under-mount basins, the counter must be sealed to prevent water from getting under the rim. Showers and baths are high-use fixtures. A shower needs a stable base, a waterproof surround, and a drain that flows fast. In many African homes, a simple shower tray or a tiled floor with a channel drain is common. Make sure the floor slopes to the drain and that the waterproofing is intact. A leaking shower can damage the ceiling below and cause mold. For baths, set the tub level and support it along the base. Connect the waste and overflow correctly. A bath that drains slowly is often a blocked trap or a poorly sloped waste. Urinals are common in schools, offices, and public buildings. They need a flush valve or a cistern, a trap, and a regular cleaning schedule. Waterless urinals are becoming popular because they save water, but they need proper maintenance and a suitable drain. If you install them, train the cleaning staff. Taps and mixers are the most touched fixtures. A dripping tap wastes water and annoys the customer. Fix it with a new washer or cartridge. For mixers, check the cartridge and the seals. In areas with hard water, scale builds up quickly, so recommend a water softener or regular descaling. For outdoor taps, use a lockable valve to prevent theft and waste. Customer care is part of the job. When you enter a home, protect the floors, keep your tools tidy, and explain what you are doing. Ask before you turn off the water. When you finish, test everything with the customer present. Show them how to use the fixtures and how to shut off the water in an emergency. Leave the work area clean. A small courtesy, like wiping the sink, makes a big impression. Maintenance contracts are valuable here. Offer a quarterly or biannual service: check for leaks, descale taps, adjust toilet mechanisms, clean traps, and inspect seals. For hotels and schools, this prevents emergencies and keeps guests and students comfortable. Keep a checklist and a log for each building. Train the cleaning staff to report problems early. A dripping tap reported today is a cheap fix; a flooded floor tomorrow is expensive. Finally, think about upgrades and sales. Many customers want to improve their bathrooms. You can offer to replace old toilets with dual-flush models, install a new shower, or add a hand bidet spray, which is common and appreciated in many African communities. Learn the local preferences and stock accordingly. In this module, you will practice installing a close-coupled toilet, a basin with a bottle trap, and a shower mixer. You will also write a customer care script and a maintenance checklist. These skills turn a technical trade into a trusted service. In Africa, where word of mouth travels fast, a plumber who respects the customer and the fixtures will always have work.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Water Quality, Testing, and Safe Plumbing Practice for African Buildings

This Practical Reference lesson supports Module 6 of the Rise Africa Skills Plumbing course. It is written for plumbers, plumbing contractors, facility managers, and small water-service businesses working in African cities, towns, and peri-urban settlements. The goal is simple: help you deliver water that is safe to drink, protect your reputation, and win repeat business from households, landlords, schools, clinics, hotels, and small factories.

In many African markets, plumbing work is judged by whether water flows. But the bigger commercial opportunity is whether water flows safely. A plumber who can test water, interpret results, recommend treatment, and document the work can charge more, reduce callbacks, and win maintenance contracts. This lesson gives you the practical reference data you need on site.

Why Water Quality Is a Business Issue

Customers do not buy pipes. They buy confidence that the water in their building is safe. In African cities, water quality problems are common: intermittent supply, aging municipal networks, boreholes with high iron or fluoride, rooftop tanks that are rarely cleaned, and cross-connections between plumbing and drainage. A plumber who can identify these problems and fix them is not a repairman he is a water-safety contractor.

WHO estimates that unsafe drinking-water causes hundreds of thousands of diarrhoeal deaths each year, with the greatest burden in low- and middle-income countries. The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme reports that in sub-Saharan Africa a large share of the population still lacks safely managed drinking-water services. That gap is your market. Every school, clinic, hotel, and apartment block needs someone who can test, treat, and maintain water systems.

SAFETY WARNING: Chlorine, Disinfection, and Confined Spaces

Chlorine chemicals can kill. Calcium hypochlorite and sodium hypochlorite are corrosive and can release toxic chlorine gas when mixed with acids, ammonia, or organic matter. Never mix chlorine with any other chemical. Always add chlorine to water, never water to chlorine.

Confined space entry: Water tanks, pits, and manholes are confined spaces. They can be oxygen-deficient or contain hydrogen sulphide and methane. Never enter without gas testing, forced ventilation, a harness, a rescue line, and a standby person outside. Many plumbers in Africa have died in tanks and pits that looked safe.

Personal protective equipment: Wear chemical-resistant gloves, eye protection, and a respirator when handling chlorine. Have clean water available for eye and skin flushing. Store chlorine in a cool, dry, locked place away from acids, fuels, and fertilisers.

If someone is exposed: Move them to fresh air immediately, flush eyes and skin with clean water for at least 15 minutes, and seek medical help. Do not induce vomiting if chlorine was swallowed.

How to Run a Simple Field Water Test

You do not need a laboratory to give your customer useful information. A basic field kit chlorine test tablets or DPD powder, a turbidity tube or portable meter, pH strips or a pocket meter, and a bacteriological test (HS vial or lab sample) costs little and pays for itself on the first job.

Start at the source, then test at the tank, then at the furthest tap. Record the results. If chlorine residual is zero at the tap but present at the tank, you have a problem in the distribution system: biofilm, dead legs, or contamination. If turbidity is high, clean the tank before you chlorinate, because chlorine is wasted on sediment. If E. coli is present, stop the customer from drinking the water and treat the system immediately.

For boreholes, test at least once a year for microbial contamination, nitrate, fluoride, iron, and manganese. For municipal supply, test chlorine residual and turbidity weekly in commercial buildings. For rainwater, test after the first major rain and after any tank cleaning.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm the water source and ask the customer about supply interruptions, taste, odour, and recent illness.
  2. Test free chlorine residual, turbidity, and pH at the source, tank, and furthest tap; record all results.
  3. Check for cross-connections between drinking-water pipes and drainage, irrigation, or chemical lines.
  4. Inspect tanks for cracks, missing covers, screens, sediment, biofilm, and mosquito larvae.
  5. Clean and disinfect tanks every 36 months using the correct chlorine dose, then flush and retest.
  6. Never enter a tank, pit, or manhole without confined-space controls: gas test, ventilation, harness, standby person.
  7. Label all tanks and pipes clearly: drinking water, non-drinking water, and chemical lines.
  8. Give the customer a written test record and a maintenance schedule; this is your proof of professional service.

Business-First Tips for African Plumbers

Sell water safety as a service, not a one-off repair. Offer a quarterly package: tank inspection, chlorine residual test, turbidity test, filter check, and a written report. Price it as a contract. Schools, clinics, guesthouses, and food businesses need this for their own compliance and reputation.

Partner with a local laboratory for bacteriological and chemical tests you cannot do in the field. Add a markup for sampling and interpretation. Train one staff member as your water-quality technician. Keep a simple logbook for every client. When a customer asks why their water tastes bad or why their children are sick, you will have data and data wins trust.

Use the WHO drinking-water guidelines as your technical reference and the WHO/UNICEF JMP data as your market argument. In African cities where municipal supply is unreliable, the plumber who can guarantee safe water at the tap will always have work.
