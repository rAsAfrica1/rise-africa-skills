1. Understanding Drain, Waste, and Vent (DWV) Systems

A DWV system has three jobs: drain wastewater away, carry solid waste to a sewer or septic tank, and vent sewer gases safely outside. In a typical African home, PVC pipes are used because they are cheap, smooth inside (so waste flows easily), and resist corrosion. The drain pipes slope downward so gravity does the work. Waste pipes carry toilets, sinks, and showers. Vent pipes go up through the roof and let air in, so water flows smoothly and gases escape. Without a vent, a toilet flush can suck the water out of a nearby sink trap, letting sewer smell into the house.

Start by mapping your system. Use a pencil and paper to sketch where the toilet, sinks, showers, and outside tank or sewer line are. Mark the main stack (the big vertical pipe) and where it exits the building. In most homes, the main stack is 110 mm PVC for the toilet, and branch lines are 50 mm or 40 mm for sinks and showers. Every fixture needs a trap (P-trap or bottle trap) to hold water and block gas. The vent pipe is usually 50 mm and must go above the roof by at least 300 mm. If you cannot vent through the roof, an air admittance valve (AAV) can work, but check local rules.

When installing, cut PVC with a fine-tooth saw or pipe cutter. Deburr the cut with a file or sandpaper. Dry-fit everything first. Use PVC solvent cement: apply primer to both pipe and fitting, then cement, push together with a quarter turn, and hold for 30 seconds. Slope horizontal drain pipes at least 1:60 (about 1 cm per 60 cm) and no more than 1:40 (1.5 cm per 40 cm). Too little slope causes clogs; too much slope lets water run away and leave solids behind. Support pipes every 1.2 m with plastic clips or straps. Test by pouring 20 litres of water down each fixture and checking for leaks. Vent pipes must be clear and open to air. Never connect a vent to a chimney or use a vent as a drain. Keep it simple, slope it right, and vent it well.

2. Selecting the Right Pipe Diameter for Toilets, Sinks, and Showers

Using the wrong pipe diameter is a top cause of clogs and slow drains. In PVC DWV systems, the size is measured by the outside diameter (OD) but named by nominal bore: 40 mm, 50 mm, 75 mm, 110 mm. For a toilet, you need 110 mm (4-inch) pipe. A toilet discharges about 69 litres per flush with solid waste. A smaller pipe will block. The toilet's waste outlet is usually 100 mm or 110 mm, so the pipe must match. Never reduce below 110 mm for a toilet branch.

For sinks (kitchen and bathroom), use 40 mm (1.5-inch) pipe. That handles soapy water and small food particles. If the sink is far from the main stack (more than 3 metres), use 50 mm to reduce clogging. For showers and bathtubs, use 40 mm or 50 mm. A shower drain sees hair and soap scum; 50 mm is safer if multiple fixtures share the line. For floor drains (common in wet rooms), use 50 mm. For a washing machine, use 50 mm because it pumps water fast.

When combining fixtures, the branch pipe must be large enough for the total flow. A bathroom group (toilet, sink, shower) often uses a 110 mm branch for the toilet and 50 mm branches for sink and shower, all connecting to a 110 mm main stack. The main stack should be 110 mm for a single-storey house. For two storeys, keep 110 mm if only one toilet per stack; otherwise use 110 mm and add a second stack. The pipe from the house to the septic tank or sewer (the drain line) should be 110 mm minimum. If the run is longer than 15 metres or has multiple toilets, use 160 mm. Always check local plumbing codes. In many African countries, 110 mm PVC is standard for main drains. Use fittings (bends, tees, wyes) of the same diameter. Avoid reducing diameter in the direction of flow. A 110 mm toilet branch must never connect to a 50 mm line. If you are unsure, go one size largerit costs a little more but prevents blockages.

3. Proper Slope for Drainage Pipes (1:60 to 1:40)

Slope, or fall, is the angle at which a drain pipe drops. If the pipe is too flat, water moves slowly and solids settle, causing clogs. If it is too steep, water runs away too fast, leaving solids behind. The correct slope for PVC drainage pipes is between 1:60 and 1:40. That means for every 60 units of horizontal run, the pipe drops 1 unit (1:60), up to 1 unit per 40 units (1:40). In practical terms: 1 cm drop per 60 cm length (minimum) to 1.5 cm drop per 40 cm length (maximum). For a 5-metre run, the total drop should be between 8.3 cm (at 1:60) and 12.5 cm (at 1:40).

To set the slope, use a spirit level and a measuring tape. Mark the start and end heights. For example, if the drain starts at the toilet and runs 5 metres to the septic tank, the end should be 812 cm lower. You can use a line level or a string line with a line level. A simpler method: use a 1-metre level. For 1:60, the bubble should show a drop of about 1.7 cm per metre (since 100 cm / 60 = 1.67 cm). For 1:40, drop 2.5 cm per metre. Mark the pipe supports accordingly. Support the pipe every 1.2 m so it does not sag. Sagging creates a belly where water and waste collect.

In Africa, many homes have sandy or clay soils. Dig the trench with a slight slope, then compact the bottom. Lay a 50 mm bed of sand or fine gravel to protect the pipe. After installing, check slope with a level before gluing. If the pipe is already glued, you can adjust supports. Never slope a drain pipe backwards. For vent pipes, slope does not matter; they go up. For horizontal vent pipes (if any), slope back to the drain at 1:60. Test by pouring a bucket of water at the high end; it should flow smoothly out the low end without gurgling. If it pools, adjust slope. Remember: 1:60 is minimum, 1:40 is maximum. Aim for 1:50 as a good middle. Write the numbers on your hand: 1 cm per 60 cm, 1.5 cm per 40 cm.

4. Installing P-Traps and Vent Pipes to Prevent Sewer Gas

Sewer gas smells like rotten eggs and can be dangerous. It comes from decomposing waste in the drain, septic tank, or sewer. To keep it out of your home, every fixture needs a trap, and the system needs a vent. A P-trap is a U-shaped pipe that holds water. That water seal blocks gas. The trap must have a water depth of 50100 mm. Too shallow, and it evaporates quickly; too deep, and it slows flow. For sinks and showers, use a 40 mm or 50 mm P-trap. For toilets, the toilet itself has a built-in trap. Never install a trap with a flexible corrugated pipeit catches debris and smells. Use smooth PVC.

Install the P-trap directly under the fixture. The trap arm (the pipe from the trap to the drain line) must slope at 1:60 to 1:40 and be no more than 1.5 m long for 40 mm pipe, or 1.8 m for 50 mm. If the trap arm is too long, the trap can siphon dry. After the trap, connect to a vent pipe. The vent must rise vertically (or at 45 degrees) to at least 300 mm above the roof. It should be at least 50 mm diameter. If you cannot go through the roof, use an air admittance valve (AAV) on the trap arm, but AAVs can fail; check local rules. In many African homes, a single 50 mm vent stack for the whole house works if it is correctly sized.

Vent pipes must be clear. Do not stuff them with insulation or let birds nest in them. Cover the top with a vent cap or mesh. For a bathroom group, you can use a common vent: a 50 mm pipe that connects the sink and shower traps and goes up. The toilet is vented through the main stack. When gluing, use PVC primer and cement. Support the vent pipe every 1.2 m. Test the system: run water in all fixtures, then smell near the traps. If you smell gas, check for a dry trap or a blocked vent. Pour a cup of water into floor drains every week to keep the trap seal. In dry season, traps can evaporate; add cooking oil to slow evaporation. Never connect a vent to a chimney or use a drain as a vent. Keep vents separate and open to air.

5. Connecting to Existing Septic Tanks or Municipal Sewers

Before you connect, know where your waste goes. In many African cities, you connect to a municipal sewer line in the street. In rural or peri-urban areas, you connect to a septic tank and soakaway. Never connect stormwater to a septic tank or sewerit overloads the system. First, locate the existing pipe or tank. For a septic tank, the inlet pipe is usually 110 mm PVC. For a sewer, the connection point is a Y-junction or a manhole. If you are unsure, ask neighbours or the local council. You need permission to dig up a public road or connect to a municipal sewer. In some areas, you must hire a licensed plumber.

For a septic tank, run a 110 mm PVC pipe from the house to the tank inlet. Slope at 1:60 to 1:40. The pipe should be laid on a 50 mm sand bed, with sand around it. Do not use 90-degree elbows; use two 45-degree bends or a long-sweep bend to reduce clogging. At the tank, the inlet should be at least 50 mm above the water level. Seal the pipe into the tank wall with mortar or a rubber grommet. If the tank is far (over 15 m), use 160 mm pipe. For a municipal sewer, connect to the existing line via a Y-junction or a saddle. The connection must be watertight. Use a rubber coupling or a PVC adaptor. Never reduce pipe size at the connection. The pipe from the house to the sewer should be 110 mm minimum. If the sewer is deeper than 1.2 m, you may need a drop manhole to avoid a steep slope.

After connecting, test by flushing toilets and running water. Check for leaks at joints. If you connect to a sewer, install a backwater valve if the sewer can flood. In many African cities, sewers overflow during heavy rain. A backwater valve prevents sewage from entering your home. For septic tanks, pump out the tank every 23 years. Never pour chemicals that kill bacteriathey stop the tank from working. If you connect to a sewer, do not dump cooking oil or sanitary pads; they cause blockages. Keep a cleanout every 15 m on long runs. A cleanout is a Y-fitting with a screw cap that lets you rod the line. Label your pipes: 'SEWER' or 'SEPTIC'. If you sell the house, the next owner will thank you.

6. Common Drainage Problems in African Homes and How to Fix Them

Slow drains are the most common problem. Usually, it is a clog of hair, soap, food, or toilet paper. First, try a plunger. If that fails, use a drain snake (manual or drill-powered). For sinks, remove the P-trap and clean it. For toilets, use a toilet auger. Never use caustic soda on PVCit can soften the pipe. Instead, use a mixture of baking soda and vinegar, then flush with hot water. If the clog is in the main line, open the cleanout and rod it. In many African homes, tree roots enter pipes through joints. If you suspect roots, call a plumber with a camera. You may need to replace that section with new PVC and seal joints well.

Bad smells mean a dry trap or a blocked vent. Pour a litre of water into floor drains and unused sinks. If the smell persists, check the vent on the roof. Birds, leaves, or a dead rat can block it. Clear the vent with a plumber's snake. If you have an air admittance valve, replace it. Another cause: a broken pipe under the floor. Look for wet patches or extra green grass. Fix by cutting out the damaged section and coupling a new piece with rubber couplings or PVC cement. Gurgling sounds mean the vent is partially blocked or the pipe is undersized. Check that the vent is at least 50 mm and goes above the roof. If you have a septic tank, gurgling can mean the tank is full. Pump it out.

Frequent clogs in toilets often mean the pipe is too small or slope is wrong. Toilets need 110 mm pipe. If you have 50 mm, replace it. Slope must be 1:60 to 1:40. If the pipe is too flat, water runs slow. If too steep, solids stay behind. Fix by re-laying the pipe with correct slope. Another problem: roots and soil settlement. In sandy soil, pipes can shift. Use flexible couplings or add supports. In clay soil, pipes can crack. Replace cracked pipes. For floor drains that overflow, the trap may be clogged. Remove the grate and clean. If you have a septic tank, do not use too much bleach or antibacterial soapit kills the bacteria. Add a bucket of water to the tank monthly to keep it active. For municipal sewers, if your neighbours have backups, the main sewer is blocked. Report to the council. Never open a manhole yourself; it is dangerous. Keep a basic toolkit: plunger, drain snake, bucket, gloves, and a torch. Most problems are preventable with correct installation and monthly maintenance.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Drainage & Wastewater Piping with PVC

This practical reference consolidates the seven lessons in the Drainage and Wastewater Piping with PVC module into a single job-site companion. It is written for the African plumber, hardware retailer, small contractor, and property developer who needs to install, price, and troubleshoot PVC drainage systems that work the first time in Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, Kampala, Dar es Salaam, or Johannesburg. Every specification below is tied to a recognised standard so you can quote it to a client, a building inspector, or a supplier and defend your price.

Why this matters commercially: drainage failures are the single most expensive plumbing defect in African residential construction. A wrongly sloped 110 mm line can cost a landlord a tenant, a contractor a retention payment, and a retailer a warranty claim. Getting slope, diameter, and venting right on day one protects your margin.

1. Drain, Waste and Vent (DWV) the three jobs in one system

A DWV system does three things at once, and every design decision serves all three:

  • Drain carry soiled water away from the fixture by gravity.
  • Waste transport solids and greywater through the pipe without blocking.
  • Vent admit air behind the flow so traps are not siphoned dry, and release sewer gas above the roof.

In African low-rise housing, the most common mistake is treating the vent as optional. Without a vent, a WC flush pulls the water out of a nearby basin P-trap, and the bathroom smells of sewer gas within weeks. A vent stack costs very little at construction stage and is extremely expensive to retrofit.

2. Selecting the right pipe diameter

Undersized pipe blocks; oversized pipe deposits solids because flow velocity drops. Use the standard fixture-unit (FU) method and match it to locally available PVC sizes (40, 50, 75, 110, 160 mm).

3. Proper slope: 1:60 to 1:40

Slope (fall) is the single biggest determinant of whether a drain self-cleanses or silts up. For 110 mm PVC soil and waste pipes, the practical working range is 1:60 to 1:40 that is, 16.7 mm to 25 mm of fall per metre of pipe.

  • Too flat (<1:60): velocity is too low, solids settle, and you get recurring blockages and smells.
  • Too steep (>1:40 on long runs): liquids outrun solids, leaving the solids behind, and traps can self-siphon.
  • Sweet spot: aim for 1:50 (20 mm/m) as a default target, which sits comfortably inside the 1:601:40 band and is easy to set with a spirit level and a 20 mm packer.

4. P-traps and vent pipes: stopping sewer gas

The P-trap holds a water seal (typically 5075 mm deep) that blocks sewer gas. The vent protects that seal from being siphoned away when a large volume usually a WC flush passes down the stack. Three rules that save callbacks:

  1. One trap per fixture. Never double-trap; it causes slow drainage and gurgling.
  2. Vent within 1.5 m of the trap (or as close as local code allows) so the branch is protected.
  3. Vent stack must terminate in open air, at least 150 mm above the roof and away from windows and air intakes critical in dense African compounds where buildings sit close together.

SAFETY WARNING SEWER GAS AND CONFINED SPACES

Sewer gas contains hydrogen sulphide (HS), methane and, in some systems, carbon monoxide. It is toxic, explosive in confined spaces and can cause loss of consciousness within minutes at high concentrations. Never enter a septic tank, soakaway, manhole or inspection chamber without forced ventilation, a calibrated multi-gas detector, a harness and a standby person at the surface. Never use an open flame to test for gas leaks. If you smell HS (rotten eggs) indoors, ventilate immediately, isolate the affected trap, and find the dry trap or broken seal before re-occupying the space. In many African jurisdictions, confined-space entry is a statutory permit-to-work activity check your national OSH authority (e.g. DOSHS in Kenya, NECA in Nigeria) before entering.

5. Connecting to septic tanks or municipal sewers

Confirm the receiving point before you order pipe this is where budgets blow out. Ask four questions:

  • Is there a municipal sewer? If yes, get the invert level and connection point from the utility (e.g. Nairobi Water, Lagos Water Corporation, GWCL). Municipal connections may require an inspection chamber and a paid permit.
  • If septic: size the tank for at least 2 days' retention; provide a separate soakaway or drainage field sized to local soil percolation.
  • Invert levels: the drain must fall continuously to the tank/sewer. If the sewer is higher than your site, you need a pumped chamber price this into the quote early.
  • Access: install an inspection chamber or rodding eye at every change of direction greater than 45 and at the property boundary.

6. Common drainage problems in African homes and how to fix them

  • Recurring WC blockages: usually a flat or back-falling 110 mm run, or a builder's rubble/debris left in the pipe. Fix: re-grade to 1:50, rod and camera the line, and flush.
  • Sewer smell in bathrooms: dry P-trap from an unvented or siphoned branch, or a missing gully trap. Fix: add a vent and refill/repair the trap seal.
  • Slow shower and basin drainage: hair and soap in the trap, or undersized 40 mm branch on a long run. Fix: clean the trap; upgrade the branch to 50 mm.
  • Septic tank overflowing after heavy rain: soakaway undersized or groundwater ingress. Fix: increase soakaway area, and check that surface water is not draining into the tank.
  • Joint leaks at couplings: solvent cement applied to wet pipe, or a mismatched rubber ring. Fix: cut out and re-make the joint dry, with the correct cement and cleaner.
  • Roots in the drain: cracked pipe at a joint. Fix: replace the section and re-bed with compacted sand surround.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm the receiving point (sewer invert level or septic tank position) before ordering pipe.
  2. Size each branch to the fixture table: WC 110 mm, shower/basin/sink 50 mm minimum.
  3. Set the main drain at 1:50 (within the 1:601:40 band) and check with a level every 5 m.
  4. Fit one P-trap per fixture with a 5075 mm water seal; never double-trap.
  5. Vent within 1.5 m of each trap; terminate the stack 150 mm above the roof, clear of windows.
  6. Install an inspection chamber or rodding eye at every bend over 45 and at the boundary.
  7. Solvent-weld only dry, clean pipe ends; allow full cure before pressure/flow testing.
  8. Pressure/water-test the line before backfilling, and photograph the layout for the client file.
