6. Processing Soybean Safely

This is the most safety-critical module in the course. Raw soybean is toxic — genuinely toxic. Fed raw, it will cause vomiting, diarrhoea and gut damage in the very children you are trying to help. Processed correctly, it is one of the most nutritious foods on earth. The difference between the two is entirely in how you handle it.

Module 6 of 12 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · three processing methods with exact times and temperatures · off-grid techniques · video demonstrations · quiz at the end
🌍 Off-Grid Processing

No electricity needed. All three methods (roasting, boiling, fermenting) work on firewood, charcoal or three-stone fire.

No special equipment. You need a pot, a pan, a grinding method and time.

No refrigeration. Processed soy flour stores 4–6 months in airtight containers at ambient temperature.

Solar drying works. A solar dryer turns a 3-day sun-drying job into 1 day.

Lesson 6.1 — Why raw soybean causes vomiting and diarrhoea — the trypsin inhibitor problem

If you remember nothing else from this module, remember this: never, under any circumstances, feed a child raw or undercooked soybean. Not as flour. Not as porridge. Not "just a little to test." Not ever.

Here is why, in plain language.

The three anti-nutrients in raw soybean

Raw soybean contains three compounds that interfere with digestion and damage the gut:

CompoundWhat it doesAmount in raw soy
Trypsin inhibitorBlocks the enzyme trypsin, which your body uses to digest protein. Protein passes through undigested, causing cramping and diarrhoea.High
Lectin (phytohemagglutinin)A protein that binds to cells in the gut lining. Causes vomiting, nausea and gut damage within hours.Very high
Phytic acidBinds to iron, zinc and calcium, preventing their absorptionHigh

These three are not "toxins" in the poison sense. They are natural defense compounds that the soybean plant evolved to stop insects and animals eating its seed. They work exactly as designed on any animal — including humans — that eats the bean raw.

What happens when a child eats raw soy

Within 3–12 hours:

  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Abdominal cramping
  • Watery diarrhoea
  • Loss of appetite
  • Lethargy

In a well-nourished adult, this passes in 24–48 hours. In a malnourished child, the damage is much worse:

  • The gut is already damaged from malnutrition and repeated infections. Raw soy compounds hit harder on a compromised gut.
  • Dehydration sets in fast. A malnourished child has less fluid reserve and cannot afford to lose water.
  • Nutrient loss deepens malnutrition. The child loses the very nutrients you were trying to deliver.
  • The cycle worsens. Damage to gut lining makes the next feeding less effective. This can spiral into severe acute malnutrition within days.
A malnourished child fed raw soy can die. Not from the soy itself, but from dehydration and the worsening of malnutrition caused by the vomiting and diarrhoea. This is why so many home porridge attempts fail silently in rural communities — nobody connects the child's sickness to the raw soy in the porridge.

The good news — heat destroys them

All three anti-nutrients are destroyed by heat:

CompoundDestroyed byTime
Trypsin inhibitorMoist heat (boiling) or dry heat (roasting)15–30 minutes
LectinMoist heat (boiling) — dry heat is less effective10–15 minutes of boiling
Phytic acidSoaking, fermenting, germination12–24 hours

This is why the correct processing method matters. Every soybean you use in this porridge must be processed correctly — not "heated a bit," but processed to destroy these compounds completely.

Lesson 6.2 — The three safe processes: roasting, boiling, fermentation

There are exactly three safe ways to process soybean for human food. Every one of them is centuries old, works off-grid, and produces soy flour you can store for months.

Process 1 — Dry roasting

The most common method in Africa. Soybeans are roasted in a dry pan, over firewood or charcoal, until the beans darken slightly and crack open. This destroys trypsin inhibitor and lectin, and gives the flour a pleasant nutty flavour.

Best for: Full-fat soy flour, small-batch home production, communities with easy access to soybeans but no reliable water for boiling.

Weakness: Less effective against lectin than boiling. Roasting must be done thoroughly — pale-roasted soy still has active anti-nutrients.

Process 2 — Boiling (wet heat)

Soybeans are soaked overnight, then boiled in plenty of water for 20–30 minutes. This is the most effective method for destroying all three anti-nutrients. The beans are then dried and ground.

Best for: Commercial production, feeding programs where safety is critical, anywhere you have reliable water.

Weakness: Takes longer. Requires drying after boiling, which needs sun or a solar dryer.

Process 3 — Fermentation

Soybeans are soaked, cooked and fermented with beneficial bacteria (usually naturally occurring) for 2–4 days. This is how traditional African foods like dawadawa, iru, ogiri and soy sauce are made.

Best for: Reducing phytates (improves mineral absorption), adding flavour, long-term preservation without refrigeration.

Weakness: Changes the flavour significantly — fermented soy has a strong, savoury, "umami" taste that does not blend well into sweet porridge. Fermented soy is better used in soups and stews, not in this recipe.

Which method for this porridge?

Roasting or boiling are the two that work for fortified porridge. Fermentation changes the taste too much for a child's sweet porridge.

MethodTimeKills anti-nutrients?Changes taste?Off-grid OK?
Dry roasting15–25 minYes (mostly)Nutty, mild✅ Yes
Boiling + drying12+ hr (with drying)Yes (fully)Neutral✅ Yes
Fermentation2–4 daysYes + reduces phytatesStrong, savoury⚠️ Yes but different product

My recommendation for the porridge: use the boiling method when water and time allow, because it fully destroys lectin. Use roasting when time is short — but roast thoroughly. Never combine under-processed soy with raw soy.

Safety margin. When in doubt, boil AND roast. Boil first to destroy anti-nutrients in water, then dry-roast briefly to develop flavour and remove residual moisture. This "double-processed" soy is the safest possible.

Lesson 6.3 — Roasting method: temperature, time, and how to tell it's done

Roasting is the most common small-scale method in Africa. Here is the exact procedure.

Equipment needed

  • A wide, flat pan (cast iron is best, aluminium works)
  • A wooden spoon or paddle
  • A firewood, charcoal or gas heat source
  • A tray or mat for cooling
  • A grinding mill or mortar and pestle

The step-by-step roasting procedure

  1. Clean the soybeans. Spread them on a tray and pick out stones, dirt, mouldy beans and any foreign matter. This takes 5 minutes per kilogram and is not optional — one mouldy bean can contaminate the whole batch with aflatoxin.
  2. Heat a DRY pan over medium heat. Do not add oil. Do not add water. The pan should be hot enough that a drop of water sizzles away instantly when flicked on it. On firewood, this is a small, focused fire — not a blazing one.
  3. Add soybeans in a single layer. Do not pile them deep — they will not roast evenly. For a typical home pan, 500 g of raw soybeans is one batch.
  4. Stir constantly with a wooden spoon. Do not walk away. Soybeans burn in seconds. Stir continuously for the entire roasting time.
  5. Roast for 15–20 minutes. The beans will change colour from pale cream to a golden brown, will crack and pop slightly, and will give off a distinct roasted, nutty smell.
  6. Watch for these signals that roasting is complete:
SignalWhat you see / hear / smell
ColourPale cream → golden brown, evenly on all beans. No pale patches remain.
SoundBeans begin to pop and crackle — like dry rice in a hot pan
SmellDistinct nutty, toasty aroma replaces the raw beany smell
TextureBean splits easily when pressed between two spoons — dry, brittle
TasteNo bitterness. Slightly sweet, nutty. If still bitter, keep roasting.
  1. Do not over-roast. If beans turn dark brown or black, or if smoke pours off them, they are burning. Burnt soy is bitter and loses nutrition. Pull them off the heat immediately.
  2. Spread on a tray to cool. Do not pile them — they will continue cooking from residual heat and can scorch. Cool for 20–30 minutes until completely cool to the touch.
  3. Roast the next batch. Do not try to roast 5 kg at once — you will get uneven roasting. Roast in 500 g batches.

Roasting on different heat sources

Heat sourceBatch sizeTimeNotes
Firewood (three-stone)300–500 g18–25 minBuild a small fire. Large fires burn the pan unevenly.
Charcoal500–800 g15–20 minBest control. Even heat. Wait until coals are red with grey ash.
Gas stove500 g12–18 minFastest, most consistent
Open fire300 g20–30 minHarder to control temperature — most likely to burn
Do not walk away from a roasting pan. Soybeans go from perfectly roasted to burnt in 30 seconds. Someone must stir continuously for the entire process — no exceptions. If you need to leave, take the pan off the heat first.

How to know you have roasted enough

The most reliable home test: the water drop test.

  1. Take 10 roasted beans and drop them in a glass of water.
  2. Properly roasted beans sink immediately and stay at the bottom.
  3. Undercooked beans float or swim.

This works because roasting reduces the density of the bean and removes moisture — roasted beans are heavier. If your beans float, roast longer.

Lesson 6.4 — Boiling method: water ratio, cooking time, and drying afterwards

Boiling is the gold standard for safety. It destroys 100% of the trypsin inhibitor and lectin — no guessing, no "close enough." If you can boil water, you can process soy safely.

Equipment needed

  • A large pot (at least 3× the volume of dry beans)
  • Clean water (for soaking and boiling)
  • A colander or basket with small holes
  • Drying trays or a solar dryer
  • A grinding mill or mortar

The full boiling procedure

  1. Clean the soybeans. Same as roasting — remove stones, dirt and mouldy beans. Freshness matters most here.
  2. Soak overnight. Cover the beans with 3 parts water to 1 part beans (by volume). Soak for 12 hours (overnight is easiest). The beans will double in size. Discard the soaking water — it contains some anti-nutrients and phytates already removed.
  3. Rinse thoroughly. Rinse the soaked beans in clean water 2–3 times.
  4. Boil in fresh water. Put the beans in a pot. Add 3 parts fresh water to 1 part beans. Bring to a rolling boil.
  5. Boil for 20–30 minutes. Keep a rolling boil the whole time. Do not reduce to a simmer — the rolling action and high heat together destroy the anti-nutrients.
  6. Add water if needed. The beans will absorb water. Keep them covered throughout.
  7. Test for doneness. A properly boiled bean is soft, breaks easily with a spoon, and has no bitterness when tasted.
  8. Drain. Pour beans into a colander. Let them drain fully — 5 minutes.
  9. Dry. Spread drained beans on clean drying trays or mats. See drying options below.
  10. Grind when fully dry. Beans must be bone-dry before grinding — any moisture means the flour will clump and spoil.

Drying methods (no electricity needed)

MethodTimeNotes
Open sun drying2–3 daysSpread thin on mat. Bring indoors at night or during rain.
Solar dryer1 day (6–8 hrs)Enclosed box with glass or plastic top. Faster, cleaner, no flies.
Warm room / near fire2 daysHang in a warm, well-ventilated room — never directly over fire (smoke affects taste).
Hot rocks / hot sand1 dayTraditional method — heating clean sand in a pan and laying beans on it. Fast, but hard to control.

Test for dryness: Press a bean between your thumb and forefinger. It should crack — not squash. If it dents, it is still wet. If it crumbles into powder, it is definitely dry.

Water ratio table — boiling

Dry beansSoaking waterBoiling waterFinal yield (boiled)
500 g1.5 L1.5 L~1 kg soaked, then ~350 g dried
1 kg3 L3 L~2 kg soaked, then ~700 g dried
5 kg15 L15 L~10 kg soaked, then ~3.5 kg dried
10 kg30 L30 L~20 kg soaked, then ~7 kg dried

Notice the yield: 1 kg of dry soybeans becomes about 700 g of processed dry flour. About 30% is lost to soaking, boiling and drying. Plan your purchases accordingly.

Advantages of boiling over roasting

  • Safety: Destroys 100% of lectin — the most dangerous anti-nutrient
  • Consistency: Every batch is identical if you follow the time
  • Less burn risk: You cannot scorch water the way you can scorch dry beans
  • Softer bean: Easier grinding, finer flour

Disadvantages

  • Takes 2–3 days total (soaking + boiling + drying)
  • Requires a lot of water
  • Requires sun or a solar dryer
  • More fuel (firewood/charcoal) than roasting
The 20-minute rule. Twenty minutes of rolling boil is the minimum to destroy trypsin inhibitor and lectin fully. Ten minutes is not enough. Thirty minutes is safe but wastes fuel. Set a timer — do not estimate.

Lesson 6.5 — Grinding: how fine, what mill, and how to avoid contamination

After roasting or boiling and drying, the soybeans must be ground into flour. The quality of your grinding affects everything: texture, mixability, and shelf life.

How fine should the flour be?

Your soy flour should be about as fine as the maize flour you are mixing it with. If the soy is coarser, the two will separate in the bag. If the soy is finer, it disappears into the mix — which is good.

Grind levelParticle sizeResult in porridge
Coarse (like rice)2–3 mm❌ Gritty texture, children refuse
Medium (like semolina)1 mm⚠️ Slightly gritty, still edible
Fine (like maize flour)0.3–0.5 mm✅ Best — smooth porridge
Very fine (like wheat flour)0.1 mm✅ Excellent if you can achieve it

For porridge, fine is best. Coarse soy flour makes a gritty porridge that children — especially malnourished children with sensitive guts — will refuse.

Grinding options (off-grid)

MethodOutput per hourCostQuality
Mortar and pestle1–2 kg/hrFree (if you have one)Coarse — not ideal for porridge
Hand-crank grain mill3–5 kg/hr$30–$80Fine — good for home use
Village grinder (diesel mill)100+ kg/hr~$0.10/kg feeExcellent — but shared with other customers
Electric hammer mill50–100 kg/hr$200–$500Best — needs electricity or generator

For home use, a hand-crank grain mill is the best investment. For anything over 20 kg per week, use a village grinder or invest in a mill.

The contamination risk

If you grind your soybean at a village mill, the same mill is used for maize, groundnuts, chili, dried fish and who-knows-what-else. Contamination risks:

  • Cross-flavour: Chili or dried fish residue makes your soy flour taste of chili and fish — destroys the porridge.
  • Aflatoxin: If a previous customer ground mouldy maize or groundnuts, residue in the mill can contaminate your flour.
  • Allergens: If the mill grinds groundnuts, peanut allergen residue can trigger reactions in sensitised children.

How to mitigate mill contamination

  1. Bring your own cleaning material — a small bag of dry maize (rice or cassava) to grind first, which cleans the mill of previous residue. Discard that first flour.
  2. Ask when the mill was last cleaned. If it was used for chili or fish, ask them to clean it before your turn.
  3. Go early in the day — the first customer of the day gets the cleanest mill.
  4. Bring your own container — do not let the miller pour into a shared basin. Bring a clean, dry bucket or sack.
  5. Grind your own if you can. A hand-crank mill eliminates the risk entirely.
Aflatoxin is the real risk. If the previous customer ground mouldy produce, mould spores and aflatoxin remain in the mill. Always run a "cleaning grind" first — throw away the first few hundred grams of your own flour, and start collecting from the second grind onwards.

After grinding

  1. Let the flour cool. Grinding generates heat. Fresh-ground flour is warm. Spread it thin and let it reach room temperature before packaging — warm flour creates condensation inside the bag.
  2. Sift if needed. A fine mesh sieve removes any coarse pieces. Return the coarse pieces to the mill or grind them again.
  3. Pack immediately. Do not leave soy flour open for hours — it absorbs moisture and odours fast.

Lesson 6.6 — Storage of processed soy: airtight, cool, away from light — and how long it lasts

Your processed soy flour is a valuable commodity. Stored correctly, it lasts 4–6 months and retains most of its nutrition. Stored badly, it goes rancid, absorbs moisture, grows mould, and can become dangerous.

Shelf life of processed soy

FormShelf life (proper storage)What degrades first
Roasted whole soybeans6 monthsOils go rancid after 6 months — beans develop a "paint-like" smell
Roasted soy flour (full-fat)4 monthsOils oxidise faster when ground — flour surface area is huge
Boiled + dried whole soybeans8 monthsMoisture absorption, then mould
Boiled + dried soy flour4–6 monthsSame as roasted flour
Defatted soy flour12+ monthsVery stable — but not suitable for this recipe

Notice that ground flour lasts shorter than whole beans. Grinding exposes the fats to oxygen, which is why whole beans are better for long-term storage. If you can, store whole processed beans and grind fresh weekly.

The four enemies of soy flour

  1. Moisture. The #1 cause of spoilage. Moisture = mould = aflatoxin. Flour must be bone-dry before packaging.
  2. Heat. Every 10°C above room temperature roughly doubles the rate of oil oxidation. A flour that lasts 4 months at 20°C lasts only 2 months at 30°C.
  3. Light. Ultraviolet light destroys riboflavin (vitamin B2) and degrades oils. Store in opaque containers.
  4. Air. Oxygen makes oils rancid. Limit air exposure — smaller containers that get emptied sooner are better than one big container opened repeatedly.

Storage container comparison

ContainerProtectionBest forCost
Open paper bag❌ NoneImmediate use only—
Thin plastic bag⚠️ Some moisture barrier1 month maxLow
Heavy plastic bag (vacuum or zip)✅ Good moisture + air barrier2–3 monthsLow
Glass jar with lid✅ Excellent — opaque if brown glass4–6 monthsMedium
Food-grade metal tin✅ Excellent — blocks light6–9 monthsMedium
Food-grade plastic bucket with lid✅ Good — if dark coloured4–6 monthsLow
Vacuum-sealed Mylar bag✅✅ Best possible9–12 monthsHigher

Storage location rules

  • Coolest room in the house. Away from kitchen heat, sun-facing walls, and cooking fires.
  • Dark. A cupboard or covered shelf — never on a sunny windowsill.
  • Elevated. On a shelf or wooden pallet — not directly on concrete floor.
  • Sealed. Airtight containers opened only when needed, then resealed immediately.
  • Labeled. Date, product, weight. You will forget what is in that bucket in 3 weeks.

The three quick quality checks before using stored flour

  1. Smell test. Open the container. It should smell nutty and clean. If it smells like old paint, crayons, or "off" — the oils have gone rancid. Discard.
  2. Visual test. Flour should be uniform. Any clumping, colour patches, or fuzzy growth means moisture has entered. Discard.
  3. Touch test. Squeeze a handful. It should flow freely. If it clumps or feels damp, moisture is present. Use immediately — do not store.
🌍 Off-Grid Storage Cheat Sheet — Processed Soy
  • Whole roasted soybeans: 6 months in airtight container, cool and dark
  • Whole boiled + dried soybeans: 8 months in airtight container, cool and dark
  • Ground soy flour: 4–6 months in airtight container, cool and dark
  • Rancid signal: paint-like smell = discard
  • Mould signal: clumping, colour patches, fuzzy growth = discard immediately (aflatoxin risk)
  • Best practice: store whole, grind weekly, use what you grind within 30 days

What to do with the final soy flour

Once you have properly processed and stored soy flour, it goes into your porridge mix at 25% by weight. It is now shelf-stable for months and safe for children. From this point forward, everything you make with it is nutritionally sound.
