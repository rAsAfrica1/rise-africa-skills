1. Days of the Week and Months

In Swahili, the days of the week are based on a seven-day cycle, but the week starts on Saturday. The days are: Jumamosi (Saturday), Jumapili (Sunday), Jumatatu (Monday), Jumanne (Tuesday), Jumatano (Wednesday), Alhamisi (Thursday), and Ijumaa (Friday). Notice that most days start with 'Juma' which means 'week'. Jumamosi means 'first day of the week', Jumapili means 'second day', and so on. Alhamisi and Ijumaa are borrowed from Arabic. To say 'today is Monday', you say 'Leo ni Jumatatu'. 'Tomorrow is Tuesday' is 'Kesho ni Jumanne'. 'Yesterday was Sunday' is 'Jana ilikuwa Jumapili'. Practice these with a friend: ask 'Leo ni siku gani?' (What day is today?) and answer with the day.

Months in Swahili are also borrowed from Arabic and are similar to English: Januari, Februari, Machi, Aprili, Mei, Juni, Julai, Agosti, Septemba, Oktoba, Novemba, Desemba. To say 'in January', you say 'mwezi wa Januari' or simply 'Januari'. For example, 'I was born in March' is 'Nilizaliwa Machi'. 'The meeting is in August' is 'Mkutano ni Agosti'. Note that Swahili months do not have the same seasonal associations as in English because East Africa is near the equator. However, you can talk about rainy seasons: 'mvua' (rain) and 'jua' (sun). For example, 'In April, there is a lot of rain' is 'Mwezi wa Aprili, kuna mvua nyingi'.

When making appointments or planning events, use these words. For example, 'Let's meet on Wednesday' is 'Tukutane Jumatano'. 'The shop opens on Saturday' is 'Duka linafungua Jumamosi'. To ask 'What day is it today?' say 'Leo ni siku gani?' To ask 'What month is it?' say 'Ni mwezi gani?' Practice with a local calendar or your phone. Write down important dates in Swahili: birthday is 'siku ya kuzaliwa', holiday is 'likizo'. For example, 'My birthday is in June' is 'Siku yangu ya kuzaliwa ni Juni'. Use these in daily conversation to remember them.

2. Telling Time

Telling time in Swahili is different from English because the Swahili day starts at 6:00 AM, which is considered 12:00 (saa kumi na mbili). So, 7:00 AM is 'saa moja' (1:00), 8:00 AM is 'saa mbili' (2:00), and so on until 6:00 PM, which is 'saa kumi na mbili' again. Then 7:00 PM is 'saa moja' (1:00) of the night. To avoid confusion, you can add 'asubuhi' (morning), 'mchana' (afternoon), 'jioni' (evening), or 'usiku' (night). For example, 7:00 AM is 'saa moja asubuhi', and 7:00 PM is 'saa moja jioni'.

To ask 'What time is it?' say 'Ni saa ngapi?' The answer uses 'ni saa...' plus the number. For example, 'Ni saa mbili' means 'It is 8:00 AM' (or 2:00 PM in Swahili time? Wait, careful: 8:00 AM is saa mbili asubuhi. 2:00 PM is saa nane mchana). Actually, 2:00 PM is saa nane? No: 6:00 AM is saa kumi na mbili, 7:00 AM saa moja, 8:00 AM saa mbili, 9:00 AM saa tatu, 10:00 AM saa nne, 11:00 AM saa tano, 12:00 PM saa sita, 1:00 PM saa saba, 2:00 PM saa nane, 3:00 PM saa tisa, 4:00 PM saa kumi, 5:00 PM saa kumi na moja, 6:00 PM saa kumi na mbili. So 2:00 PM is 'saa nane mchana'. To say 'half past', use 'na nusu'. For example, 7:30 AM is 'saa moja na nusu asubuhi'. For 'quarter past', use 'na robo'. For 'quarter to', use 'kasoro robo'. For example, 7:45 AM is 'saa mbili kasoro robo asubuhi' (literally 'two less a quarter').

When making appointments, use these phrases: 'Tukutane saa nne' (Let's meet at 10:00 AM). 'Nitakuwa hapa saa mbili' (I will be here at 8:00 AM). 'Treni inaondoka saa tano' (The train leaves at 11:00 AM). Practice by looking at a clock and saying the time in Swahili. Ask a friend 'Ni saa ngapi?' and answer. Use a local clock or your phone to check. Remember that in East Africa, many people use both Swahili and English time, so clarify if needed: 'Ni saa ngapi kwa Kiswahili?' (What time is it in Swahili?). With practice, it becomes natural.

3. Asking for Directions

When you are lost in a Swahili-speaking area, you need to ask for directions. Start with a polite greeting: 'Samahani' (Excuse me) or 'Naomba radhi' (I beg your pardon). Then ask 'Ninaweza kukuuliza?' (Can I ask you?). The most common question is 'Njia ya kwenda ... iko wapi?' (Where is the way to ...?). For example, 'Njia ya kwenda sokoni iko wapi?' (Where is the way to the market?). Or simply 'Soko liko wapi?' (Where is the market?). For a specific place like a bank: 'Benki iko wapi?' (Where is the bank?).

When giving directions, Swahili uses these words: 'kushoto' (left), 'kulia' (right), 'moja kwa moja' (straight ahead), 'nyuma' (behind), 'mbele' (in front), 'karibu na' (near), 'mbali na' (far from). For example, 'Geuka kushoto' (Turn left), 'Geuka kulia' (Turn right), 'Endelea moja kwa moja' (Continue straight), 'Simama hapa' (Stop here). To say 'It is near the post office', say 'Iko karibu na posta'. To say 'It is far from here', say 'Iko mbali na hapa'. If you need to ask 'Is it far?' say 'Ni mbali?' The answer might be 'Hapana, ni karibu' (No, it is near) or 'Ndiyo, ni mbali' (Yes, it is far).

If you don't understand, say 'Samahani, sijaelewa' (Sorry, I didn't understand). Ask them to repeat: 'Tafadhali rudia' (Please repeat). Or ask them to speak slowly: 'Tafadhali sema polepole' (Please speak slowly). To confirm, say 'Unamaanisha ...?' (Do you mean ...?). For example, 'Unamaanisha ni geuke kushoto?' (Do you mean I turn left?). It's also helpful to ask for landmarks: 'Kuna alama gani?' (What landmarks are there?). Common landmarks: 'kanisa' (church), 'shule' (school), 'hospitali' (hospital), 'soko' (market), 'benki' (bank), 'posta' (post office). Practice with a map of your town. Ask a friend for directions in Swahili and follow them. This will build your confidence.

4. Common Questions: Who, What, Where

In Swahili, the main question words are: 'nani' (who), 'nini' (what), 'wapi' (where), 'lini' (when), 'kwa nini' (why), and 'vipi' (how). These are essential for everyday conversation. To ask 'Who is that?' say 'Yule ni nani?' To ask 'What is this?' say 'Hii ni nini?' To ask 'Where is the toilet?' say 'Choo kiko wapi?' To ask 'When is the party?' say 'Sherehe ni lini?' To ask 'Why are you late?' say 'Kwa nini umechelewa?' To ask 'How are you?' say 'Habari yako?' or 'U hali gani?'

When using these question words, the word order is often the same as in statements, but with a question word. For example, 'Unakwenda wapi?' (Where are you going?) literally 'You go where?' 'Unataka nini?' (What do you want?) 'Nani anakuja?' (Who is coming?) 'Lini utarudi?' (When will you return?) 'Kwa nini unalia?' (Why are you crying?) 'Vipi unajisikia?' (How do you feel?). Notice that the verb comes before the question word in many cases. Practice forming questions with these patterns.

To answer these questions, use appropriate responses. For 'Who is that?' you might say 'Yule ni mama yangu' (That is my mother). For 'What is this?' say 'Hii ni kitabu' (This is a book). For 'Where is the market?' say 'Soko liko mjini' (The market is in town). For 'When is the meeting?' say 'Mkutano ni kesho' (The meeting is tomorrow). For 'Why are you late?' say 'Kwa sababu nilikwama kwenye trafiki' (Because I was stuck in traffic). For 'How are you?' say 'Nzuri, asante' (Fine, thank you). Use these in daily interactions. Ask a friend questions and answer them. For example, 'Nani rafiki yako?' (Who is your friend?), 'Unapenda nini?' (What do you like?), 'Unaishi wapi?' (Where do you live?). This will help you become fluent.

5. Yes/No Questions and Answers

In Swahili, yes/no questions are formed by changing the intonation or by adding 'je' at the beginning. For example, 'Unasema Kiingereza?' (Do you speak English?) with a rising tone. Or 'Je, unasema Kiingereza?' The word 'je' is like 'do' in English questions. To answer yes, say 'Ndiyo' or 'Ndio' (Yes). To answer no, say 'Hapana' or 'La' (No). For example, 'Ndiyo, ninasema Kiingereza' (Yes, I speak English). 'Hapana, sisemi Kiingereza' (No, I don't speak English).

When answering negatively, the verb changes. For example, 'Unakwenda sokoni?' (Are you going to the market?) Positive answer: 'Ndiyo, ninakwenda sokoni.' Negative answer: 'Hapana, siendi sokoni.' Notice 'ninakwenda' becomes 'siendi'. Similarly, 'Unataka chai?' (Do you want tea?) Positive: 'Ndiyo, ninataka chai.' Negative: 'Hapana, sitaki chai.' 'Unaweza kusaidia?' (Can you help?) Positive: 'Ndiyo, ninaweza.' Negative: 'Hapana, siwezi.' The negative prefix 'si-' is used for I, and other subjects have different prefixes: 'hu-' for you (singular), 'ha-' for he/she, 'hatu-' for we, 'ham-' for you (plural), 'hawa-' for they. For example, 'Hujui?' (Don't you know?) 'Hapana, sijui' (No, I don't know).

Practice with common yes/no questions: 'Una njaa?' (Are you hungry?) 'Ndiyo, nina njaa' or 'Hapana, sina njaa.' 'Una kiu?' (Are you thirsty?) 'Ndiyo, nina kiu.' 'Umechoka?' (Are you tired?) 'Ndiyo, nimechoka.' 'Unajua Kiswahili?' (Do you know Swahili?) 'Ndiyo, ninajua kidogo.' (Yes, I know a little.) 'Una pesa?' (Do you have money?) 'Hapana, sina pesa.' (No, I don't have money.) Use these in everyday situations. Ask a friend yes/no questions and answer them. This will help you respond quickly.

6. Polite Requests and Responses

Politeness is very important in Swahili culture. To make a polite request, use 'tafadhali' (please) or 'naomba' (I beg/request). For example, 'Tafadhali nisaidie' (Please help me). 'Naomba maji' (I request water). 'Tafadhali nipe chakula' (Please give me food). Another way is to use 'naomba' with a verb: 'Naomba unisaidie' (I request that you help me). 'Naomba unipe chai' (I request that you give me tea). The word 'tafadhali' can be placed at the beginning or end: 'Nisaidie tafadhali' (Help me please).

When responding to a request, you can say 'Ndiyo, sawa' (Yes, okay) or 'Hakuna shida' (No problem). If you cannot help, say 'Samahani, siwezi' (Sorry, I can't) or 'Samahani, sina' (Sorry, I don't have). For example, if someone asks 'Tafadhali nipe kalamu' (Please give me a pen), you can say 'Hii hapa' (Here it is) while handing it over. Or 'Samahani, sina kalamu' (Sorry, I don't have a pen). To thank someone, say 'Asante' (Thank you) or 'Asante sana' (Thank you very much). They may reply 'Karibu' (You're welcome).

Other polite phrases: 'Samahani' (Excuse me/Sorry), 'Naomba radhi' (I apologize), 'Tafadhali subiri' (Please wait), 'Tafadhali ingia' (Please come in), 'Tafadhali keti' (Please sit). When asking for permission, say 'Naweza kuingia?' (May I come in?) or 'Naweza kukaa hapa?' (May I sit here?). The response might be 'Ndiyo, karibu' (Yes, welcome) or 'Hapana, samahani' (No, sorry). In markets, bargaining politely: 'Naomba punguza bei' (Please reduce the price). 'Bei ni ngapi?' (How much is it?) Then 'Nipe kwa shilingi elfu tano' (Give it to me for 5000 shillings). Always use 'tafadhali' and 'asante' to show respect. Practice these with friends and in shops. Politeness opens doors in Swahili-speaking communities.

<h2>Practical Reference: Swahili Everyday Essentials for African Agribusiness</h2>

<p>In East African agribusiness, Swahili is not just a courtesy languageit is a commercial operating language. From the produce markets of Kampala and Nairobi to the tea auctions of Mombasa and the coffee cooperatives of Moshi, Swahili enables traders, extension officers, transporters, and buyers to negotiate, schedule, and resolve problems in real time. This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the six Everyday Essentials topicsdays, months, time, directions, question words, yes/no answers, and polite requestsinto a single field-ready toolkit. Each section is designed for business use: confirming delivery dates, asking a boda-boda rider for directions to a farm, negotiating a price politely, or clarifying a purchase order. The focus is on phrases you can use immediately, with cultural and safety notes that reflect real African trading environments.</p>

<p>Before diving into the tables, note a core principle: in Swahili business conversations, politeness and clarity are inseparable. A request framed with <em>tafadhali</em> (please) and closed with <em>asante</em> (thank you) is more likely to secure a favourable response than a blunt command. Similarly, confirming dates and times verballyrather than assumingprevents costly misunderstandings in perishable goods supply chains. Use this module as a reference card, not a textbook.</p>

<p>Swahili days of the week are built on the root <em>-juma</em> (week). Note that the Swahili week traditionally begins on Saturday, which matters when scheduling market days and delivery windows. Months are borrowed from Arabic and are widely understood across Tanzania, Kenya, Uganda, and the DRC. In business, always confirm the day and date verbally, especially when coordinating with smallholder farmers who may use both Gregorian and local calendars.</p>

<h2>2. Telling Time</h2>
<p>Swahili time is famously offset by six hours from Western clock time: 7:00 a.m. Western is <em>saa moja asubuhi</em> (first hour of the morning) in Swahili. This is because the Swahili day traditionally begins at sunrise (around 6:00 a.m.). For agribusiness, this matters when scheduling early-morning market deliveries or coordinating with farmers who start at dawn. Always clarify using <em>asubuhi</em> (morning), <em>mchana</em> (afternoon), <em>jioni</em> (evening), or <em>usiku</em> (night).</p>

<h2>3. Asking for Directions</h2>
<p>In rural agribusiness, giving and receiving directions is a daily necessity. Whether locating a coffee washing station in Kilimanjaro or a maize aggregation point in Nakuru, the vocabulary below will keep you moving. Combine directions with landmarks: <em>karibu na soko</em> (near the market), <em>baada ya daraja</em> (after the bridge).</p>

<p>Key phrases: <em>"Njia ya kwenda soko ni wapi?"</em> Where is the road to the market? <em>"Geuka kushoto/kulia"</em> Turn left/right. <em>"Endelea mbele"</em> Continue straight. <em>"Ni mbali?"</em> Is it far? <em>"Ni karibu?"</em> Is it near? Directions are often given using relative terms: <em>kaskazini</em> (north), <em>kusini</em> (south), <em>mashariki</em> (east), <em>magharibi</em> (west).</p>

<p><strong>Business usage:</strong> <em>"Ghala liko wapi?"</em> Where is the warehouse? <em>"Boda boda inaweza kufika shambani?"</em> Can a motorcycle taxi reach the farm? Confirm with <em>"Sawa"</em> (okay) or <em>"Nimeelewa"</em> (I understand).</p>

<h2>4. Common Questions: Who, What, Where</h2>
<p>Question words are the backbone of negotiation and verification. Master these six: <em>nani</em> (who), <em>nini</em> (what), <em>wapi</em> (where), <em>lini</em> (when), <em>kwa nini</em> (why), and <em>vipi</em> (how). In agribusiness, they help you verify supplier identity, product quality, delivery location, timing, and price justification.</p>

<p>Examples: <em>"Nani anauza mahindi?"</em> Who is selling maize? <em>"Nini bei ya kilo?"</em> What is the price per kilogram? <em>"Wapi tunakutana?"</em> Where do we meet? <em>"Lini mzigo utafika?"</em> When will the consignment arrive? <em>"Kwa nini bei ni juu?"</em> Why is the price high? <em>"Vipi tutalipa?"</em> How shall we pay?</p>

<h2>5. Yes/No Questions and Answers</h2>
<p>Swahili yes/no questions are often formed by intonation or by adding <em>je</em> at the beginning. Answers are straightforward: <em>ndiyo</em> (yes), <em>hapana</em> (no), <em>sawa</em> (okay/agreed), <em>labda</em> (maybe). For business, avoid ambiguity. If a supplier says <em>"labda"</em>, follow up with a specific question to get a firm commitment.</p>

<p>Examples: <em>"Je, una mahindi?"</em> Do you have maize? <em>"Ndiyo, ninayo."</em> Yes, I have it. <em>"Je, bei ni elfu moja?"</em> Is the price one thousand? <em>"Hapana, ni elfu mbili."</em> No, it is two thousand. <em>"Je, unaweza kufikisha kesho?"</em> Can you deliver tomorrow? <em>"Sawa, nitaifikisha."</em> Okay, I will deliver it.</p>

<h2>6. Polite Requests and Responses</h2>
<p>Politeness opens doors in African trading culture. Use <em>tafadhali</em> (please), <em>samahani</em> (excuse me/sorry), <em>asante</em> (thank you), and <em>karibu</em> (welcome). When making a request, soften it with <em>naomba</em> (I request) or <em>tafadhali nisaidie</em> (please help me). Responses should acknowledge the request before agreeing or declining: <em>"Sawa, nitakusaidia"</em> Okay, I will help you. <em>"Samahani, siwezi"</em> Sorry, I cannot.</p>

<p><strong>Business usage:</strong> <em>"Tafadhali niletee sampuli"</em> Please bring me a sample. <em>"Naomba unipatie bei nafuu"</em> I request you give me a better price. <em>"Asante kwa biashara"</em> Thank you for the business. <em>"Karibu tena"</em> Welcome again.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c62828;background:#ffebee;padding:14px;margin:20px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING Verbal Contracts and Payment Risks</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#333;">In cross-border and rural agribusiness, verbal agreements in Swahili are common but carry legal and financial risk. Always confirm quantities, prices, delivery dates, and payment terms in writingeven a handwritten note or SMSbefore releasing goods or funds. Be cautious of phrases like <em>"nitakulipa baadaye"</em> (I will pay you later) without a specific date. In Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, mobile money confirmations (M-Pesa, Tigo Pesa, Airtel Money) should be verified on your own device before releasing produce. Never hand over goods based solely on a phone call. For high-value transactions, use a licensed broker or cooperative officer as a witness. Report suspected fraud to local authorities and your cooperative board immediately.</p>
</div>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li> Greet before business: <em>"Habari"</em> or <em>"Jambo"</em> it builds trust.</li>
<li> Confirm the day and date: <em>"Jumatatu, tarehe ngapi?"</em> avoid scheduling errors.</li>
<li> Clarify Swahili time: <em>"Saa ngapi?"</em> remember the six-hour offset.</li>
<li> Repeat directions back: <em>"Kushoto, kisha kulia"</em> confirm before departing.</li>
<li> Ask who, what, where, when, why, how before agreeing to any deal.</li>
<li> Use <em>tafadhali</em> and <em>asante</em> in every negotiation.</li>
<li> Get written confirmation of price, quantity, and delivery date.</li>
<li> Verify mobile money payment on your own device before releasing goods.</li>
</ul>
