Module 1

The Business of Steer Fattening

Steer fattening is a short-cycle trading and feeding business: you buy a lean animal, add weight and condition over roughly 90 to 120 days, and sell it into a better market. This module shows you how the money moves, how much working capital one batch really needs, how to size your first batch so a mistake does not end your business, and the four records that tell you whether you made money or only felt busy.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • You will be able to explain the difference between a fattening business and a breeding herd, and say which one your capital suits.
  • You will be able to draw your own cash cycle from purchase day to sale day and identify the weeks when you have no incoming money.
  • You will be able to build a working capital estimate for one batch using your own phoned-in local prices.
  • You will be able to choose a first batch size that matches your cash, feed supply, pen space and labour.
  • You will be able to calculate margin per head and margin per day, and tell the difference between them.
  • You will be able to keep the four core records - animal, feed, money, health - in a simple book from day one.

Lesson 1.114 min

What Fattening Actually Is, and Why It Suits Small Capital

In this lesson
  • Describe the fattening business in one sentence
  • Contrast fattening with breeding on cash cycle and risk
  • Decide whether your situation suits fattening

Fattening, also called finishing, means buying a store animal - a lean, growing steer that has frame but no fat cover - feeding it a planned ration for a defined period, and selling it heavier and better conditioned than you bought it. You are not producing calves. You are buying weight cheaply, adding weight at a controlled cost, and selling the whole animal at a higher value per kilogram than you paid.

The one-sentence version

You buy kilograms at a low price per kilogram, you manufacture more kilograms at your feed cost per kilogram of gain, and you sell all the kilograms at a higher price per kilogram. If any of those three legs breaks, you lose money.

Fattening versus breeding

A breeding herd is a long, slow asset. A cow must be bought or reared, bred, carried through gestation, and her calf reared before anything can be sold. From investment to first sale is commonly two to three years, and drought, abortion or calf death can wipe out a season with nothing to show. Breeding rewards land, patience and deep capital.

Fattening is short. A finishing period for store cattle typically runs about 90 to 120 days, sometimes up to 150 days if the animals came in very lean or the ration is modest. That means your money turns over two to three times a year instead of once every two or three years. For a person with limited capital, turnover is everything: a small margin earned three times beats a larger margin earned once.

Where the profit actually comes from

There are three separate profit sources and you should always know which one you are relying on.

  1. Weight gain. You add kilograms. On a well balanced finishing ration, average daily gain commonly runs about 1.0 to 1.5 kg per day, and lower on poor rations, poor genetics or heat and disease stress.
  2. Condition improvement. A fatter, better finished animal usually dresses out at a higher percentage and attracts a better price grade. Dressing percentage commonly runs 50 to 58 percent depending on condition, breed and gut fill.
  3. Market timing. Buying when store cattle are cheap - often at the end of the dry season when everyone is destocking - and selling when demand is high.

Beginners should plan to make money on weight gain and condition only. Treat market timing as a bonus. If your batch only works because you assume prices will rise, you are speculating, not fattening.

Who fattening does not suit

  • People with no reliable feed source. Feed is the dominant cost in fattening, and no amount of good buying rescues a batch you cannot feed.
  • People whose only money is also their household money. A batch locks up your cash for months.
  • People with no water security. Water needs are substantial and rise with temperature and dry feed intake. A dry borehole ends a batch.

If you have a small secure pen, access to crop residues and a concentrate supply, some cash you can lock away for four months, and a market within reach, fattening fits you better than almost any other cattle enterprise.

Lesson 1.215 min

The Cash Cycle: Where Your Money Sits and When It Comes Back

In this lesson
  • Map the money-out and money-in points of one batch
  • Identify the weeks of maximum cash exposure
  • Plan for the gap between batches

The single most common way small fatteners fail is not disease and not bad prices. It is running out of cash in month three, being forced to sell animals before they are finished, and taking a store price for a nearly finished animal. To avoid that, you must know your cash cycle before you buy anything.

Draw the cycle

Take a page. Draw a line for the batch, marked in weeks from week 0 to week 18. Mark every point where money leaves and every point where money comes in.

  1. Week minus 2 to 0: setup. Pen repairs, troughs, water connection, first feed purchase, any deposit on transport. Money out, nothing in.
  2. Week 0: purchase day. The largest single outflow. Animal purchase price, market levies or commission, movement permit fees, transport. Money out, nothing in.
  3. Week 0 to 3: arrival and adaptation. Vaccines, dewormer, any vet treatment, hay or roughage, and the start of the concentrate step-up. Money out, nothing in. Gains in this window are usually modest because the animals are recovering from transport and adapting.
  4. Week 3 to 14 or 16: the feeding grind. Steady weekly feed purchases, water, labour, occasional treatment. Money out every single week, nothing in. This is the long dry stretch.
  5. Week 14 to 18: sale. The animals go. Money in, all at once.

The exposure point

Your maximum cash exposure is the day before you sell. On that day you have spent the purchase price, all the feed, all the health cost, all the labour, and you have received nothing. Everything is standing in the pen as living inventory. That number - total cash committed before any return - is the number you must be able to survive.

Write it plainly for yourself: I must be able to spend X and receive nothing for four months. If you cannot say that sentence honestly, your batch is too big.

The gap between batches

Cattle do not sell the moment you decide. Between selling one batch and buying the next there is usually a gap of one to four weeks: waiting for buyer payment, waiting for a market day, waiting for suitable store cattle to appear. Payment terms are the hidden killer here. Some abattoirs and butchers pay on the day, others pay after grading, others pay in two or three weeks. Ask before you sell, not after.

A practical rule: assume you receive full payment two weeks later than promised. If your plan still works, proceed.

Never spend the whole pot

Split your capital into three parts before you buy a single animal:

  • Stock money. Buys the animals.
  • Feed and running money. Covers feed, water, health and labour to the end of the batch. Feed is the dominant cost in fattening, so this pot is often as large as the stock pot or larger over a full cycle.
  • Reserve. A buffer for a sick animal, a feed price jump, or a delayed payment. If you have no reserve at all, one bad week forces an early sale.

Selling early is expensive

An animal sold at day 60 has eaten most of the adaptation feed - the least productive feed of the whole batch - and has not yet laid down the finish that earns the better price. The last weeks of a batch are usually where the condition premium is won. Plan your cash so that the decision to sell is made by the animal's condition and the market, never by an empty pocket.

Lesson 1.316 min

Working Out Your Capital Requirement

In this lesson
  • List every cost line in a fattening batch
  • Build a per-head capital estimate from phoned-in prices
  • Test the plan against a bad-case price

You cannot copy a budget from a book, from a neighbour, or from this course. Prices for cattle, maize bran, cottonseed cake, molasses, salt licks, transport and slaughter fees differ enormously between Bulawayo and Lusaka, between Nakuru and Kano, and between the wet and dry season in the same town. What you can copy is the method.

The rule: three phone calls per line

For every cost line below, phone three suppliers and write down three prices with the date and the supplier name. Use the middle price for planning and the highest price for your stress test. Never plan on the lowest price you were quoted, because that supplier will be out of stock the week you need him.

The cost lines

  1. Animal purchase. Price per head or per kilogram liveweight. Ask how it is quoted - per head, per kg live, or per kg cold dressed - because the three are not comparable.
  2. Market and legal costs. Auction commission, market levy, movement permit, brand or ownership inspection, ear tag.
  3. Transport in. Per truckload or per head per kilometre. Ask the minimum load charge.
  4. Arrival health. Dewormer, tick control, vaccinations relevant to your area, vitamin or mineral injection if your vet advises it.
  5. Roughage. Hay, maize or sorghum stover, silage, sugarcane tops. Price per bale or per tonne, and be sure of the bale weight.
  6. Concentrate. Maize bran, brewers grain, cottonseed cake or meal, sunflower cake, molasses, urea if used, and a mineral and salt source. Price per bag and per tonne, and always confirm bag weight.
  7. Water. Pumping fuel or electricity, or purchased water.
  8. Labour. Even if it is your own labour, cost it. If you do not, you will not notice when the business is paying you less than a job would.
  9. Selling costs. Transport out, commission, slaughter or grading fees, weighbridge fee.
  10. Reserve. A contingency line for the unexpected.

Estimating feed quantity, not feed price

Feed quantity is the part you can estimate from science rather than guessing. Two anchors do most of the work.

  • Dry matter intake in finishing cattle commonly runs roughly 2.0 to 2.5 percent of liveweight per day, and can approach 3 percent on high roughage rations.
  • Feed conversion in finishing cattle commonly runs roughly 6 to 10 kg of dry matter per kg of liveweight gain, with better rations and younger animals at the better end.

So for a target gain of, say, 120 kg over the batch, at a feed conversion of 8 kg dry matter per kg gain, you need in the order of 960 kg of dry matter for that gain, plus the feed the animal needs simply to maintain itself. Work in dry matter, then convert to the as-fed weight you actually buy, because wet by-products like brewers grain contain a lot of water.

Lesson 1.414 min

Choosing Your First Batch Size

In this lesson
  • Apply the four limits that cap batch size
  • Choose a defensible starting number
  • Plan a growth path across batches

New fatteners almost always start too big. The pen looks empty with four animals in it, the trader offers a discount for ten, and the arithmetic of profit per head is seductive. Then feed prices move, one animal gets sick, payment is delayed, and the whole thing unravels. Batch size is a risk decision before it is a profit decision.

The four limits

Your batch size is the smallest of four separate limits. Not the average. The smallest.

  1. Cash limit. How many head can you buy and feed to the end of the batch, using only money you can afford to lock away for four to five months, while keeping a reserve? Calculate it from your per-head capital figure in Lesson 3.
  2. Feed limit. How many head can you feed continuously to the last day? Not the first month - the last day. Count what you have stored, what you can reliably buy weekly, and what happens in the dry season when everyone else wants the same bran.
  3. Space limit. How many head fit in your pen with proper space at the feed trough and water? Overcrowding cuts intake, raises injury and spreads disease. Space standards are covered in Module 3, and they are not optional.
  4. Labour and attention limit. How many animals can you or your worker feed, water, observe and treat every single day without shortcuts? Fattening is a daily observation business. If nobody looks at every animal every day, you will find problems late.

A sensible first batch

For a first-time smallholder fattener, a batch of about 3 to 5 head is usually the right learning size. It is enough to see real patterns - different animals gaining at different rates, feed running out faster than expected - and small enough that one death or one bad price does not end you. Larger operators moving from experience can step to 10 to 20 and beyond, but the same four limits apply.

Why small first is genuinely cheaper

Your first batch buys you information: how your local store cattle really gain, how much feed they really eat, what your local buyer really pays, how long payment really takes. Buying that information with 4 animals costs a fraction of buying it with 20. Treat batch one as paid training with a chance of profit.

Uniformity matters more than number

Five animals of similar age, frame and condition will finish together, eat similarly and sell as a lot. Five mismatched animals finish at different times, which means either selling some too early or feeding some too long. Where you have a choice, buy a uniform group rather than the cheapest available mixture.

Growing across batches

A disciplined growth path:

  • Batch 1: Small. Record everything. Aim to learn, not to maximise.
  • Batch 2: Same size or slightly larger, applying the lessons. Confirm your figures repeat.
  • Batch 3 onward: Grow only if two conditions hold - your recorded margin was positive on real numbers, and your feed supply for the larger number is contracted or stored, not hoped for.

The overlap trap

Once you are experienced, running two batches that overlap - one finishing while another starts - smooths cash flow considerably. But do not do it in your first year. Overlapping doubles your feed demand and your attention demand at exactly the moment you are least sure of your figures, and mixing new arrivals with settled animals raises disease risk unless you have a separate arrival pen.

The one-line test

Before confirming any batch size, say this out loud: If every one of these animals gained only half what I expect, and I sold them for ten percent less than I hope, would my household still be fine? If the answer is no, buy fewer.

Margin Per Head and Margin Per Day

In this lesson
  • Calculate margin per head from real cost lines
  • Convert margin per head into margin per day
  • Use cost of gain to decide when to stop feeding

Two fatteners can both make the same margin per head, and one of them is running a much better business. The difference is time. This lesson gives you the three numbers that actually run a fattening enterprise: margin per head, margin per head per day, and cost of gain.

1. Margin per head

Sale value minus every cost attributable to that animal.

Margin per head = sale price - (purchase price + purchase costs + feed cost + health cost + water and labour + selling costs)

Two disciplines make this honest. First, include your own labour at what you would pay someone else. Second, spread the cost of any animal that died or was sold at a loss across the surviving animals in the batch, because that loss was a real cost of running the batch.

2. Margin per head per day

Divide margin per head by the number of days the animal was on your feed, from arrival to sale.

Margin per day = margin per head divided by days on feed

This is the number that lets you compare options fairly. An animal earning a given margin over 90 days is doing far better work for your capital than one earning the same margin over 150 days, because you can start the next batch sooner. Margin per day is also how you compare fattening against any other use of the same money.

3. Cost of gain

This is the most useful management number of the three, and the one almost nobody calculates.

Cost of gain per kg = total feed and running cost for a period divided by kilograms of liveweight gained in that period

Compare cost of gain against what the market pays per kilogram of liveweight. While your cost of adding a kilogram is comfortably below the value of that kilogram, keep feeding. When the two approach each other, you are feeding for no reason. This crossover is the honest answer to the question every fattener asks: how long do I keep him?

Why cost of gain rises near the end

Gain is not linear. Early in the batch a lean animal shows compensatory growth - it lays down frame and lean tissue relatively efficiently. Later, as the animal finishes, more of the gain is fat, and fat costs more energy per kilogram to deposit than lean tissue does. So feed conversion worsens as the animal approaches finish, moving toward the poorer end of the roughly 6 to 10 kg dry matter per kg gain range. Meanwhile daily maintenance feed keeps rising because the animal is heavier.

The consequence: there is a point where the extra kilogram costs more than it earns. Your target market decides where that point sits. If your buyer pays a genuine premium for a well finished carcass, feeding longer can pay even at poor conversion. If your buyer pays a flat live price with no condition premium, stop earlier.

Lesson 1.614 min

The Four Records That Decide Whether You Made Money

In this lesson
  • Set up the four record books
  • Know what to write and when
  • Use records to make the next batch better

Most small fatteners can tell you what they sold an animal for. Very few can tell you what that animal cost them. Without records, you cannot separate the animal that made money from the animal that lost it, and so you repeat the same mistake next batch. You need four records. They fit in one exercise book with four sections, or four cheap notebooks. Paper is fine. Consistency beats sophistication.

Record 1: The animal record

One line per animal, opened on arrival day and closed on sale day.

  • Animal number - a numbered ear tag, or paint mark. Every animal must have an identity or nothing else works.
  • Date bought, seller name, place bought.
  • Purchase price and purchase costs.
  • Arrival weight, or a chest girth measurement plus a description if you have no scale.
  • Estimated age from dentition, breed or cross, body condition score, frame size.
  • Weights taken during the batch, with dates.
  • Date sold, buyer, sale weight, sale price, and dressing percentage if the abattoir reports it.

If you have no scale, a weigh band or a girth tape used the same way every time still shows you the direction of travel. Weigh at the same time of day, ideally before the morning feed, because gut fill can swing apparent weight substantially.

Record 2: The feed record

The single most valuable record, because feed is the dominant cost.

  • Every purchase: date, supplier, feed type, quantity, bag weight, total price, price per kilogram.
  • Every day fed: date, feed type, quantity fed to the pen.
  • Refusals: how much was left in the trough. Rising refusals are an early warning of sickness, heat stress or a ration problem.

At the end of the batch, total kilograms of dry matter fed divided by total kilograms gained gives you your real feed conversion. Compare it against the usual 6 to 10 kg dry matter per kg gain range. If you are worse than that, the fault is in the ration, the adaptation, the water, or the health of the animals, and now you know to look.

Record 3: The money record

A simple cash book with two columns, money out and money in, and a column naming the batch.

  • Never mix household money with batch money in the same book. If you take money from the batch for household use, write it as a withdrawal so it is visible.
  • Record every small cost. Rope, salt, a bag of lime, a bicycle trip to the market. Small costs are invisible individually and heavy in total.
  • Keep receipts where you can, especially for stock purchase and movement permits, because they also prove ownership.

Record 4: The health record

  • Date, animal number, what you observed, what you did, what product and dose, and who advised it.
  • Withdrawal periods. Any medicine has a withdrawal period before slaughter. Write the earliest safe slaughter date next to every treatment. Selling an animal inside its withdrawal period is a food safety failure and can get your stock rejected.
  • Deaths, with the date and your best account of the cause. If you lose an animal and cannot say why, call a vet - a post mortem on the first death can save the rest of the batch.

The weekly ten minutes

Set one fixed time each week - say Sunday evening - to bring the four records up to date and to write a two-line note: what went well, what worried you. At the end of the batch, read those notes before you buy the next one. That habit, more than any other single practice, is what turns a first batch into a business.

Knowledge check

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. What is the typical finishing period for store cattle in a fattening operation?

Fattening is a short cycle business. A typical finishing period runs about 90 to 120 days, and up to 150 days if animals arrive very lean or the ration is modest.

2. Your batch size under each limit is: cash 8 head, feed 12 head, pen space 6 head, labour 10 head. How many should you buy?

Batch size is the smallest of the four limits, not the average. Pen space caps you at 6. Overcrowding cuts intake and spreads disease, so the extra animals would cost you money on all of them.

3. Why is margin per head per day more useful than margin per head alone?

The same margin earned in 90 days is better business than the same margin over 150 days, because your capital is freed sooner for the next batch.

4. Which statement about local prices in this course is correct?

Prices vary enormously by country, town and season. The course teaches the costing method; you supply real quotes, dated, from at least three suppliers per line.

5. Why does cost of gain per kilogram usually rise toward the end of a batch?

Feed conversion worsens as the animal finishes because fat deposition is more energy costly than lean gain, and a heavier animal needs more feed just for maintenance.
