1. Newborn calf care: colostrum management and navel dipping

The first hours of a calf's life determine whether it will thrive or struggle. As soon as a calf is born, ensure it breathes and clear mucus from its nose and mouth. The single most important step is feeding colostrumthe first milk from the motherwithin the first 2 hours of birth. A newborn calf's gut can only absorb antibodies for about 6 hours, so timing is critical. Give 2 to 3 litres of clean, high-quality colostrum using a clean bottle and teat or an oesophageal feeder. If the dam cannot be milked, use frozen colostrum from a healthy cow, thawed in warm water (never boiling). Test colostrum quality with a colostrometer; good colostrum has a reading above 50 mg/ml IgG. If you don't have a colostrometer, thick, creamy yellow colostrum is usually good. Feed colostrum again 12 hours later, another 2 litres.

Navel dipping prevents infection. Mix 7% iodine or chlorhexidine solution in a small cup. Dip the entire navel stump for 5 seconds, ensuring full coverage. Do this immediately after birth and again 6 hours later. If the navel is bleeding, tie it with clean thread. Keep the calf in a clean, dry, well-bedded pen away from adult cows to reduce disease risk.

In many African settings, calves are left with the dam, but this risks poor colostrum intake if the dam rejects the calf or if the calf is weak. Instead, restrain the cow and assist the calf to suckle or milk the cow by hand. If the calf is weak, use a stomach tube. Always record the calf's birth date, dam, and colostrum feeding times.

After the first 24 hours, continue feeding transition milk (the milk from the next 2-3 milkings) as it still contains some antibodies and nutrients. Provide fresh, clean water at all times. By day 2, the calf should be active and alert. If it is dull, scouring, or not suckling, consult a vet immediately. Good newborn care reduces calf mortality from 20% to under 5%.

2. Feeding programs for calves: milk replacer and starter feed

From day 2 to weaning, calves need a consistent feeding program. If the dam is milked for sale, use milk replacer. Mix 125 grams of milk replacer powder per litre of clean warm water (about 40C). Feed 4 to 6 litres per day, split into two feedings (morning and evening). Use a clean bucket or bottle with a teat. Never feed more than 2.5 litres at one time to avoid digestive upset. If using whole milk, feed 10% of the calf's body weight dailya 40 kg calf gets 4 litres.

Start offering calf starter feed from day 3. Use a shallow trough and place a small handful of fresh, coarse starter (18-20% protein) in it. Calves will nibble at it. By 2 weeks, they may eat 100-200 grams per day. By 8 weeks, they should eat 1-1.5 kg per day. Starter feed should be palatable: mix in a little molasses or ground maize if needed. Always provide fresh, clean watercalves drink water even when on milk.

Introduce good-quality hay from week 2. Fine-stemmed hay or chopped forage helps rumen development. Avoid dusty or mouldy hay. In Africa, use locally available feeds like lucerne, desmodium, or sweet potato vines, but ensure they are wilted, not wet.

Feeding schedule: Week 1-2: colostrum then milk replacer or whole milk, 4-6 L/day. Week 3-4: same milk, plus starter and hay. Week 5-6: reduce milk to 2-3 L/day, increase starter to 1 kg. Week 7-8: milk once a day, starter 1.5 kg. By week 8-10, wean if the calf eats 1.5 kg starter daily.

Always wash feeding equipment with hot water and disinfectant after each use. Dirty equipment causes scours. Keep records of milk and feed intake. If a calf refuses milk, check for fever or scours. Adjust feed graduallysudden changes cause diarrhoea. In hot weather, feed early morning and late evening to reduce stress.

3. Housing and environment for calves

Calves need a dry, draught-free, well-ventilated space. In Africa, many farmers use individual pens or hutches. A calf hutch made from local materialswood, thatch, or corrugated ironshould be at least 1.2 m wide, 1.5 m deep, and 1.2 m high. Place it on a slight slope so water drains away. The floor should be earth or concrete, covered with 10-15 cm of dry straw or wood shavings. Never use wet or muddy beddingit causes navel infections and pneumonia.

Ventilation is critical but avoid direct draughts. In hot climates, orient the hutch so the opening faces away from prevailing winds but allows cross-flow of air. In cold or rainy seasons, use a removable canvas or plastic sheet to block wind and rain. The temperature inside should not exceed 30C; if it does, provide shade with a tree or shade cloth.

Keep calves separated from adult cows. A distance of at least 5 metres prevents disease transmission. If group housing, limit to 4-6 calves of similar age, with 2.5 square metres per calf. Provide a clean, dry lying area and a separate feeding area.

Clean the pen daily: remove wet bedding and manure. Disinfect weekly with a 2% caustic soda solution or commercial disinfectant. Allow the pen to dry in the sun. Provide fresh water in a clean bucket, and feed from a trough that calves cannot step in.

In free-range systems, ensure calves have access to a shaded, dry resting area. Rotate pens to prevent build-up of parasites. Always check for predators (dogs, wild animals) and secure the area with a fence. A well-designed calf house reduces respiratory disease and scours, the two biggest killers of young stock.

4. Weaning strategies and transition to group housing

Weaning is the most stressful period in a calf's life. The goal is to move from liquid milk to solid feed without weight loss or disease. Start planning at 6 weeks: reduce milk gradually. For example, if feeding 4 L/day, drop to 3 L for 3 days, then 2 L for 3 days, then 1 L for 3 days, then stop. Meanwhile, increase starter feed to 1.5-2 kg per day. By 8-10 weeks, a calf eating 1.5 kg of starter and 0.5 kg of hay daily is ready to wean.

Wean based on feed intake, not age. A calf should weigh at least 70-80 kg (double birth weight) and have a rumen developed enough to digest forage. If a calf is not eating enough starter, delay weaning by one week.

After weaning, move calves to group housing. Groups of 4-8 calves of similar size and age work best. Provide 2.5-3 square metres per calf in a well-bedded pen with access to a dry outdoor area. Introduce calves to the group early in the morning when they are hungry, so they focus on feed rather than fighting. Monitor for bullyingsmaller calves may not get enough feed. Use multiple feeding spaces (one trough space per calf) and water points.

Transition diet: continue the same starter feed for 2 weeks after weaning. Then gradually introduce a grower ration (16% protein) and good-quality forage. Increase forage slowly to avoid bloat.

Keep stress low: avoid dehorning, castration, or vaccination during the first week after weaning. Provide clean water and shade. Weigh calves monthly to track growth. If a calf loses weight, isolate it and check for parasites or disease. Successful weaning sets the stage for healthy heifer growth.

5. Heifer growth and development targets

A dairy heifer must reach key weight and height targets to calve at 24 months and produce milk profitably. Weigh your heifers monthly using a weigh tape or scale. Record weights in a notebook. The targets: birth weight 30-40 kg; weaning (8-10 weeks) 70-80 kg; 6 months 140-160 kg; 12 months 220-250 kg; 15 months (breeding age) 280-320 kg; 24 months (calving) 400-450 kg.

Height at withers is also important. At 12 months, a heifer should be 110-115 cm tall; at 15 months, 120-125 cm. Use a measuring stick or a marked wall. If a heifer is below target, increase feed quality and quantity. If above, reduce energy to avoid fat deposition in the udder.

Feed to target: from 3 to 6 months, feed 2-3 kg of grower ration (16% protein) plus free-choice forage. From 6 to 12 months, feed 1-2 kg concentrate plus good pasture or hay. From 12 to 24 months, feed 1-2 kg concentrate and forage, adjusting based on body condition. Provide mineral licks.

In Africa, use locally available feeds: maize silage, Napier grass, lucerne, cottonseed cake, molasses. For protein, use sunflower cake or fish meal if available. Always provide clean watera heifer drinks 20-40 litres per day.

Monitor body condition score (BCS) monthly: aim for 3.0-3.5 (on a 1-5 scale) at breeding and 3.5 at calving. Too thin (BCS <2.5) delays puberty; too fat (BCS >4) causes calving problems.

Health checks: deworm every 3 months, vaccinate against brucellosis and anthrax as per local schedule. Hoof trimming twice a year. If a heifer fails to reach 280 kg by 15 months, consult an extension officer. Proper heifer rearing reduces age at first calving and increases lifetime milk yield.

6. Health management for young stock

Young stock health is about prevention, not just treatment. The main diseases are scours (diarrhoea), pneumonia, and parasites. Scours kills quicklywithin 24 hours. Causes include dirty feeding equipment, overfeeding, or E. coli. Signs: watery faeces, sunken eyes, weakness. Treat with oral rehydration solution (1 litre water + 1 teaspoon salt + 6 teaspoons sugar) and continue milk feeding. If severe, use antibiotics prescribed by a vet.

Pneumonia causes coughing, rapid breathing, and fever. It spreads in poorly ventilated, damp pens. Prevent by good ventilation, dry bedding, and avoiding overcrowding. Vaccinate against pneumonia if available. Treat early with antibioticswaiting reduces lung function permanently.

Parasites: internal worms cause weight loss and diarrhoea. Deworm calves at 2 months, then every 3 months. Use albendazole or ivermectin as per vet advice. External parasites (ticks, lice) cause anaemia. Spray or dip calves with acaricide every 2 weeks in tick-prone areas.

Vaccination schedule: at 2 weeks, vaccinate against blackleg and malignant oedema (if in endemic area). At 1 month, vaccinate against anthrax and botulism. At 3 months, vaccinate against brucellosis (heifers only). At 6 months, booster. Always follow local veterinary recommendations.

Daily observation: check each calf for appetite, alertness, and manure consistency. A healthy calf is curious, eats well, and has firm droppings. Isolate sick calves immediately. Keep a health record for each calf: date, symptoms, treatment, and outcome.

Biosecurity: limit visitors, disinfect boots and equipment, quarantine new animals for 2 weeks. Provide clean water and feed. In Africa, many diseases are preventable with simple hygiene and timely vaccination. If you see blood in manure or sudden death, call a vet immediatelyit could be coccidiosis or anthrax.

<h2>Practical Reference: Calf and Heifer Rearing for African Dairy Enterprises</h2>

<p>In African dairy systems, the calf and heifer enterprise is the factory that builds tomorrow's milking herd. Every shilling, naira, cedi, or birr invested in a well-reared heifer returns through earlier first calving, higher lifetime yield, and lower replacement costs. Conversely, poor early rearing is the single largest hidden cost in smallholder and commercial dairy operations: delayed puberty, stunted frame, and high pre-weaning mortality quietly destroy profitability. This Practical Reference consolidates the operational benchmarks, feeding schedules, housing standards, and health protocols you need to rear replacement stock that calves at 2224 months and enters the milking herd at 500550 kg live weight (for Holstein-Friesian crosses under African conditions).</p>

<h2>1. Newborn Calf Care: The First 24 Hours Decide Everything</h2>

<p>Colostrum is not optional. A calf is born with no immunity and a gut that can only absorb antibodies for the first 612 hours. Feed 10% of body weight (34 litres for a 35 kg calf) of clean, high-quality colostrum within 2 hours of birth, and another 2 litres within 12 hours. Use a clean oesophageal feeder if the calf will not suckle. Test colostrum quality with a colostrometer or Brix refractometer; discard watery, bloody, or mastitic milk. For African smallholders without a refractometer, a practical rule: thick, creamy, yellow colostrum from a healthy second-or-later lactation cow is usually acceptable.</p>

<p>Navel dipping with 7% iodine (or 2% chlorhexidine) immediately after birth and again 12 hours later prevents navel ill and joint infections. In humid coastal or high-rainfall zones, dip twice daily for three days.</p>

<h2>2. Feeding Programs: Milk Replacer and Starter Feed</h2>

<p>Whole milk is the gold standard where surplus is available, but in many African dairy enterprises every litre sold as milk competes with household income. Milk replacer (2022% crude protein, 1520% fat) is economically viable when priced below the farm-gate milk price per litre of reconstituted feed. Feed calves 1012% of body weight in milk or replacer daily, split into two feeds, from week 1 to week 8. Introduce a calf starter (1820% CP, highly palatable, coarse-textured) from day 37, and fresh clean water at all times.</p>

<h2>3. Housing and Environment</h2>

<p>Calves need dry, draught-free, well-ventilated housing. In African conditions, the biggest threats are heat stress, mud, and ammonia build-up. Individual calf pens (1.2 m 1.5 m) for the first 24 weeks reduce disease transmission; thereafter group pens of 46 calves with 34 m per calf. Use raised slatted floors or deep dry bedding (sawdust, wood shavings, or dry grass). Provide shade a simple iron-sheet roof at 3 m height with open sides works well. In highland areas, protect against cold winds; in lowland and coastal areas, maximise airflow and orient pens eastwest to reduce solar load.</p>

<h2>4. Weaning Strategies and Transition to Group Housing</h2>

<p>Weaning is the most stressful event in a calf's life. The rule is simple: do not wean until the calf eats at least 1 kg of starter concentrate per day for three consecutive days, and is at least 8 weeks old and 2.22.5 times birth weight. Reduce milk feeding gradually over 710 days rather than stopping abruptly. Move weaned calves into small groups (46) of similar age and weight to reduce competition. Monitor for 2 weeks after weaning; any calf that loses weight or shows diarrhoea should be returned to individual care.</p>

<h2>5. Heifer Growth and Development Targets</h2>

<p>For Holstein-Friesian crosses in African systems, target average daily gain (ADG) of 0.70.8 kg from birth to 12 months, and 0.80.9 kg from 12 to 24 months. Key benchmarks: 3 months 90100 kg; 6 months 160180 kg; 12 months 250280 kg; service weight 340360 kg at 1516 months; first calving 2224 months at 500550 kg post-calving. Use a weighband monthly and record in a simple notebook or mobile app. Heifers below target need a ration review: increase energy density, check for parasites, and ensure adequate water.</p>

<h2>6. Health Management for Young Stock</h2>

<p>Pre-weaning mortality should stay below 5%; above 10% signals a management crisis. Vaccinate according to local veterinary advice (anthrax, blackquarter, brucellosis where endemic, and FMD in outbreak zones). Deworm at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, then every 6 months. Treat diarrhoea with oral rehydration salts immediately do not wait. Pneumonia is the second biggest killer; ensure ventilation and avoid overcrowding. Keep a simple health record: date, symptom, treatment, cost. This data drives your culling and breeding decisions.</p>
