Watermelon Processing

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Cucurbits

1 Watermelon varieties

Watermelons (Citrullus lanatus) are 92% water — this makes them ideal for juice but challenging for drying.

Seeded varieties: Traditional watermelons with black seeds. Sweet, high sugar (10–12 brix). Seeds can be dried and roasted. Best for juice.

Seedless varieties: No seeds, higher consumer appeal for fresh sale. Lower juice yield because flesh is looser. Not ideal for processing.

Yellow flesh: Higher beta-carotene. Used in premium juice blends. Lower consumer recognition.

Sugar baby: Small, very sweet (12–14 brix). Best for premium juice.

Test brix with a refractometer before buying — below 8 brix makes bland juice.

2 Washing and cutting

Watermelon rind often carries soil and bacteria because it grows on the ground.

  1. Wash the whole melon in clean water
  2. Sanitise with 50 ppm chlorine solution for 1 minute
  3. Rinse again in clean water
  4. Cut in half lengthwise
  5. Cut into wedges
  6. Remove seeds (save for roasting)
  7. Cube or chunk flesh

Chill the flesh before juicing (4°C) — cold fruit gives higher juice yield and better flavour.

⚠ Never skip sanitising step. Watermelon rind carries Listeria and Salmonella risk in unpasteurised products.

3 Watermelon juice

Watermelon juice is a refreshing summer product with strong seasonal demand.

  1. Cube chilled flesh
  2. Blend or press (no need to peel)
  3. Strain through fine mesh
  4. Add 0.1% citric acid and 0.05% ascorbic acid (stabilisers)
  5. Add lemon juice to taste
  6. Strain again through 100 micron
  7. Pasteurise at 85°C for 15 seconds
  8. Hot-fill into bottles or PET

Yield: 100 kg watermelon → 65–75 litres juice (watermelon is high juice content).

Shelf life: 6–9 months pasteurised, sealed. Refrigerate after opening.

4 Rind pickling

Watermelon rind is often thrown away, but pickled rind is a traditional product in the US South and Asia. It uses what would be waste — high margin.

  1. Peel green outer skin, keep white rind
  2. Cut rind into 2 cm cubes
  3. Boil in water for 10 minutes to soften
  4. Drain and cool
  5. Cover with brine: 2 parts vinegar, 1 part water, sugar, salt, spices (cloves, cinnamon, ginger)
  6. Refrigerate 1 week before eating

Yield: 1 watermelon → 500 g pickled rind. Retail $6–$12 per jar.

5 Seed roasting and salting

Watermelon seeds are a popular snack in Africa, the Middle East and Asia.

  1. Wash seeds thoroughly
  2. Soak in salted water 2 hours
  3. Drain and spread on trays
  4. Roast at 150°C for 25 minutes, stirring every 8 minutes
  5. Or sun-dry 3 days, then roast 10 minutes
  6. Cool and pack

Yield: 1 watermelon (5 kg) → 100 g raw seeds → 80 g roasted. Retail $10–$20 per kg.

Shelled seeds (kernels) sell for 2× unshelled.

6 Watermelon concentrate

Concentrate is used as a base for drinks, cocktails, and ice cream.

  1. Juice fresh watermelon
  2. Heat gently to 60°C
  3. Pass through vacuum evaporator (or simmer carefully)
  4. Reduce to 1/3 volume
  5. Add citric acid to taste
  6. Bottle in sterilised glass

Yield: 100 L juice → 30–35 L concentrate.

Sells to drink manufacturers at $4–$8 per litre.

7 Jam and syrup

Watermelon jam: Cook watermelon juice with sugar and pectin. Watermelon is low in pectin, so add commercial pectin (1–2%). Simmer until 65 brix. Jar hot.

Watermelon syrup: Reduce juice with sugar to 65 brix. Add citric acid. Bottle. Used for cocktails and desserts.

Retail: $6–$12 per 350 g jar of jam; $8–$15 per 500 ml syrup.

8 Cold chain and shelf life

Watermelon products are all sensitive to temperature.

  • Fresh juice (unpasteurised): 4°C, 3–5 days
  • Pasteurised juice: room temp sealed 6–9 months; refrigerate after opening
  • Concentrate: room temp sealed 12 months; refrigerate after opening
  • Jam/syrup: cool dark storage 18 months
  • Roasted seeds: airtight, dry storage 6 months
  • Pickled rind: refrigerated 3 months
💰 Watermelon is very cheap in season ($0.10–$0.30 per kg in Africa). Processed into juice at $2–$4 per litre, the value-add is 10× the raw cost.
