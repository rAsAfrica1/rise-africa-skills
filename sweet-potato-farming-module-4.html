1. Selecting Varieties for Climate and Market

Choosing the right sweet potato variety is your first step to a profitable harvest. In Africa, you need varieties that match your climate and what buyers want. Start by checking rainfall and temperature. Sweet potatoes grow best between 20Â°C and 30Â°C. If you farm in a dry area like northern Nigeria or Senegal, pick drought-tolerant types such as 'Tanzania' or 'Zambezi'. In wetter areas like Uganda or Kenya highlands, choose varieties resistant to diseases like sweet potato virus disease (SPVD).

Market demand matters. Visit your local market and ask traders what sells. In many cities, orange-fleshed varieties like Beauregard and Jewel are popular because they are rich in vitamin A and fetch higher prices. White-fleshed types like 'Tanzania' are preferred for making flour and chips. If you plan to sell to processors, ask them which varieties they buy. For example, a chip factory may want varieties with high dry matter and low sugar.

Also consider maturity time. Early-maturing varieties (3-4 months) like 'Kabode' give quick returns, while late ones (5-6 months) like 'Naspot 1' yield more but need longer. If you have limited land, go for high-yielding varieties. Always test a new variety on a small plot before planting the whole farm. Talk to extension officers or research stations like IITA or CIP for advice on varieties suited to your area. Remember, a variety that works for your neighbour may not work for you if your soil or market is different.

2. Characteristics of Popular Varieties (e.g., Beauregard, Jewel)

Beauregard and Jewel are two of the most widely grown sweet potato varieties in Africa and around the world. Knowing their traits helps you decide which to plant. Beauregard is an orange-fleshed variety developed in the USA. It matures in 90-110 days, yields 15-25 tonnes per hectare under good management, and has good resistance to some soil insects. Its vines are vigorous and can be used for cuttings. However, Beauregard is susceptible to sweet potato virus disease, so you must source clean planting material. It stores well for 4-6 months if cured properly.

Jewel is another orange-fleshed variety, slightly later maturing (120-135 days). It produces uniform, cylindrical roots that are excellent for baking and processing. Yield potential is 20-30 tonnes per hectare. Jewel has moderate resistance to root-knot nematodes but is also vulnerable to viruses. Its roots have high sugar content, making them sweet and popular in fresh markets.

Other important varieties in Africa include 'Tanzania' (white-fleshed, drought-tolerant, high dry matter, good for flour), 'Kabode' (orange-fleshed, early maturing, rich in vitamin A), and 'Naspot 1' (white-fleshed, high yielding, resistant to weevils). When choosing, match the variety to your purpose: fresh eating, processing, or animal feed. For fresh market, Beauregard and Jewel are good. For flour, Tanzania is better. Always check with local agricultural offices for varieties recommended in your region. Keep records of how each variety performs on your farm.

3. Producing Healthy Seed Potatoes

Healthy seed potatoes are the foundation of a good sweet potato crop. Unlike Irish potatoes, sweet potatoes are usually grown from vine cuttings or slips, not from storage roots. However, you can produce your own clean planting material by following a few steps. Start with disease-free vines from a certified source. Plant them in a nursery bed away from your main field to avoid contamination. The bed should be in full sun, with well-drained soil. Add compost or manure to give nutrients.

Space the vines 30 cm apart in rows 60 cm apart. Water regularly but do not overwater, as sweet potatoes rot in waterlogged soil. Inspect the plants weekly for signs of disease like yellowing leaves, stunting, or mosaic patterns. Remove and burn any sick plants immediately. After 6-8 weeks, the vines will be long enough to cut. Use a sharp, clean knife to cut 20-30 cm lengths, each with at least 3-4 nodes. These are your planting material.

To produce seed potatoes from storage roots, select small, healthy roots from a disease-free crop. Cure them for 7-10 days in a warm, humid place. Then plant them in a nursery bed 5 cm deep, 30 cm apart. They will sprout and produce slips in 4-6 weeks. You can then cut these slips and transplant them. Always keep your nursery tools clean with a 10% bleach solution to prevent spreading viruses. Never take cuttings from a field with virus symptoms. If you cannot produce your own, buy certified vines from reputable suppliers like KEPHIS in Kenya or NARO in Uganda.

4. Vine Cuttings vs. Slips: Advantages and Disadvantages

When planting sweet potatoes, you can use either vine cuttings or slips. Vine cuttings are pieces of mature vines, usually 20-30 cm long, cut from a growing plant. Slips are young shoots that grow from a storage root or from a nursery bed. Each has pros and cons.

Vine cuttings are quick and easy to get if you already have a crop. They root fast and can be planted directly. You can take many cuttings from one plant, making them cheap. However, cuttings from an infected plant carry viruses. They also dry out quickly if not planted immediately. You must keep them shaded and moist. Vine cuttings may also carry pests like weevils.

Slips are generally cleaner because they come from sprouts of roots that you can select for health. They are less likely to carry viruses if you start with clean roots. Slips also have a higher survival rate when transplanted carefully. But producing slips takes time and space. You need a nursery bed or a sprouting box. Slips are more delicate and can be damaged during transport. They also cost more if you buy them.

In Africa, many farmers use vine cuttings because they are free and readily available. But if you want to avoid disease build-up, invest in slips from certified roots. A good compromise: produce your own slips from healthy roots every two years to refresh your planting material. Whichever you choose, plant them immediately after cutting. Water well for the first week. Space vines 30 cm apart in rows 1 m apart. With good care, both methods give good yields.

5. Sourcing and Certifying Disease-Free Planting Material

Disease-free planting material is critical for sweet potato farming. Viruses like SPVD can reduce yields by 50-90%. In Africa, many farmers unknowingly plant infected vines, leading to poor harvests. To source clean material, start with certified suppliers. In Kenya, contact KEPHIS (Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service). In Uganda, NARO (National Agricultural Research Organisation) provides clean vines. In Nigeria, IITA (International Institute of Tropical Agriculture) has clean planting material. These organisations test for viruses and other pathogens.

When buying, ask for a certificate or label. It should state the variety, date of production, and that it is disease-free. Check the vines yourself: they should be green, vigorous, and free of yellowing, mosaic patterns, or insect damage. Avoid buying from roadside vendors unless you trust their source. If you cannot access certified material, you can produce your own clean vines using a technique called 'positive selection'. Walk through your field and mark the healthiest plants with no symptoms. Take cuttings only from these plants. Do this every season to gradually improve your stock.

Another method is thermotherapy: treat vines in hot water at 50Â°C for 30 minutes to kill viruses, then grow them in a clean nursery. This is advanced but can be done with training. Always isolate your clean nursery from your main field by at least 50 metres. Use clean tools and wash hands before entering. If you see disease, remove and burn affected plants. By using certified material and good practices, you can keep your farm productive for years.

6. Handling and Storage of Planting Material

How you handle and store planting material determines whether it survives until planting. Vine cuttings and slips are living plants and can easily dry out or rot. After cutting vines, keep them in a cool, shaded place. Do not pile them in the sun. If you must store them for a few days, wrap them in moist cloth or banana leaves. You can also stand the cut ends in a bucket with 5 cm of clean water. Change the water daily. This keeps them fresh for up to 3 days. For slips, keep them in a nursery bed until you are ready to plant. Water them lightly every morning.

For longer storage, you can store vine cuttings in a cool room at 15-20Â°C with high humidity (80-90%). Spread them in a single layer on a damp sack. Do not stack them, as they will heat up and rot. Check daily and remove any that show mould or black spots. If you have a refrigerator, you can store cuttings at 10-15Â°C for up to one week, but never freeze them.

Storage roots used for producing slips should be cured first. Cure by keeping roots at 30-32Â°C and 85-90% humidity for 7-10 days. Then store them in a dark, well-ventilated place at 15-20Â°C. Lay them in single layers on dry straw or sand. Do not wash them before storage. Check regularly for rot. With proper handling, you can keep planting material viable for weeks, ensuring you plant at the right time.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Sweet Potato Varieties and Planting Material

In African sweet potato production, the difference between a profitable season and a loss often comes down to two decisions made before a single vine touches the soil: which variety you plant and where you source your planting material. This practical reference consolidates the key technical and commercial knowledge from this module into a single working document for farmers, extension officers, and agripreneurs operating in African conditions.

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) is vegetatively propagated, which means the planting material you buy or save carries the genetic identity and the disease history of its parent. Unlike seed crops, there is no cheap, clean, annual reset. This makes variety selection and planting material management the highest-leverage decisions in the value chain.

1. Selecting Varieties for Climate and Market

Variety selection must satisfy two masters simultaneously: the agro-ecology of your farm and the buyer who will purchase your harvest. In Africa, sweet potato is grown from sea level to over 2,000 metres, in rainfall regimes from 400 mm to 2,000 mm per year. A variety that yields 25 t/ha in the Lake Victoria basin may fail entirely in the Sahel.

Key climate matching criteria:

  • Maturity period: 90â120 days for early varieties (important for food security and double-cropping); 150â180 days for late varieties (higher dry matter, better storage).
  • Drought tolerance: Critical in semi-arid zones (e.g., northern Nigeria, Senegal, Ethiopia lowlands).
  • Altitude adaptation: Highland varieties (e.g., NASPOT series in Uganda) differ from lowland types.
  • Vine growth habit: Spreading vs. erect â affects weed competition and labour.

Market matching criteria are equally important. Orange-fleshed sweet potato (OFSP) commands premium prices in urban health-conscious markets and is the focus of biofortification programmes. White-fleshed varieties dominate traditional markets in West Africa where they are processed into elubo (flour) or consumed boiled. Purple-fleshed varieties are increasingly demanded for anthocyanin-rich products.

3. Producing Healthy Seed Potatoes

In sweet potato, "seed" refers to roots or vines used for propagation, not botanical seed. Healthy seed production is a specialised activity that can become a profitable enterprise in itself. The process involves:

  1. Selection: Choose roots from disease-free plants that show true-to-type characteristics (skin colour, flesh colour, root shape).
  2. Isolation: Grow seed multiplication plots at least 100 m from other sweet potato fields to reduce aphid-transmitted virus pressure.
  3. Roguing: Remove and destroy any plant showing virus symptoms (mottled leaves, stunting, vein clearing) immediately.
  4. Harvest timing: Harvest seed roots at 90â120 days, before they become over-mature and prone to weevil damage.
  5. Curing: Cure roots at 28â30Â°C and 85â90% humidity for 5â7 days to heal wounds and extend storage life.

For commercial seed producers, a formal certification pathway through national seed authorities (e.g., KEPHIS in Kenya, NASC in Nigeria, TOSCI in Tanzania) adds significant market value. Certified seed potatoes can fetch 2â3Ã the price of farmer-saved material.

5. Sourcing and Certifying Disease-Free Planting Material

Disease-free planting material is the foundation of profitable sweet potato farming. The main diseases of concern in Africa are:

  • Sweet potato virus disease (SPVD): A complex of viruses transmitted by whiteflies and aphids. Causes stunting, leaf distortion, and yield losses of 50â90%.
  • Sweet potato weevil (Cylas spp.): Larvae tunnel into roots, making them unmarketable.
  • Fusarium wilt and root rot: Soil-borne, persists in fields for years.

Sourcing options, in order of reliability:

  1. National certified seed programmes: Contact your national agricultural research organisation (NARO, KALRO, NARS) or seed certification authority for a list of certified seed producers.
  2. CIP-supported decentralised seed enterprises: CIP and partners have established community-based seed producers in Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania, Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Ghana.
  3. Farmer groups with clean seed protocols: Some cooperatives maintain isolated multiplication plots and roguing schedules.
  4. Farmer-saved material: Acceptable only if you have maintained strict roguing and have not observed virus symptoms for at least two seasons.

When purchasing, always ask for: variety name, source plot, date of harvest, and any certification documentation. Keep records â traceability protects your investment.

6. Handling and Storage of Planting Material

Planting material is perishable. Poor handling between source and field can destroy viability within hours.

Vine cuttings: Cut in early morning or late evening. Remove leaves from the lower two-thirds of the cutting. Bundle in groups of 50â100. Keep bundles upright in a shaded, humid place. Plant within 2â5 days. If transport is required, wrap bundles in moist banana leaves or damp cloth and keep out of direct sun.

Slips: Lift slips carefully from nursery beds when 20â30 cm tall. Keep roots attached. Place in shallow crates or baskets lined with moist cloth. Store in shade. Plant within 3â7 days.

Seed roots: Cure for 5â7 days after harvest. Store in a cool, dark, ventilated space at 12â15Â°C if possible. In tropical conditions without cold storage, use raised platforms in a shaded, well-ventilated store. Inspect weekly and remove any rotting roots.
