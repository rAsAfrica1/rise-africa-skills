Lessons

1. Presenting Your Business Plan to Investors

When pitching to Spanish-speaking investors, structure your presentation with a clear problem, solution, market size, traction, team, and financials. In Latin America, investors often expect a 10-15 minute pitch followed by Q&A. Use formal address (usted) unless invited to use t. Start with a strong hook: "Nuestro negocio resuelve un problema crtico en el sector agrcola en Nigeria." Emphasize local impact and scalability. For example, if you run a cassava processing plant in Ghana, highlight how you supply 200 farmers and generate $50,000 monthly revenue. Use tools like Canva or PowerPoint with Spanish slides. Practice pronunciation of key terms: "inversin" (investment), "retorno" (return), "escalabilidad" (scalability). Rehearse with a native speaker via italki or Tandem. In Mexico, investors may ask about your "modelo de negocio" and "proyecciones financieras". Be ready to justify your valuation. A common mistake is translating directly from English; instead, use business Spanish phrases like "estamos buscando una ronda de inversin de $200,000" (we are seeking an investment round of $200,000). Also, mention your competitive advantage: "Nuestra ventaja es la logstica local y el conocimiento del mercado." After the pitch, follow up with a thank-you email in Spanish. Remember that building trust is key: in many African and Latin American contexts, personal relationships matter as much as numbers. So, start with a brief personal introduction and show genuine interest in the investor's portfolio. Finally, be prepared to answer questions about risks and mitigation. Use conditional tenses: "Si obtenemos la inversin, expandiramos a tres pases ms." This shows forward thinking. Practice your pitch aloud, record yourself, and refine.

2. Negotiating Large Orders and Partnerships

Negotiating in Spanish requires both language skills and cultural awareness. In many Spanish-speaking countries, negotiations are relationship-driven and may take multiple meetings. Start with small talk about family or football before getting to business. Use polite forms: "Le parece si discutimos los trminos?" (Would you like to discuss the terms?). When negotiating price, avoid direct refusals. Instead, say "Entiendo su posicin, pero necesitamos un mejor precio para cubrir nuestros costos." For large orders, ask about payment terms: "Cul es el plazo de pago?" (What is the payment term?). In Africa, if you're exporting to Spain, you might negotiate a 30% deposit and 70% on delivery. Use Incoterms like FOB or CIF; learn their Spanish equivalents: FOB (Franco a bordo), CIF (Costo, seguro y flete). For partnerships, propose a trial period: "Propongo un pedido inicial de 500 unidades para probar el mercado." Be clear about quality standards: "Nuestro producto cumple con las normas ISO." Use numbers and data to support your position. If the other party says "Es muy caro," respond with value: "Pero nuestra calidad reduce sus costos a largo plazo." In some cultures, silence is used as a tactic; don't fill it immediately. Also, be aware of indirect communication: a "maybe" might mean no. Learn to read context. Tools like WhatsApp are common for quick follow-ups. Always confirm agreements in writing: "Le enviar un correo con los detalles." Practice role-plays with a partner. Common phrases: "Podemos llegar a un acuerdo?" (Can we reach an agreement?), "Qu tal si dividimos la diferencia?" (How about we split the difference?). Remember that building trust over time can lead to better terms than aggressive haggling.

3. Discussing Import and Export Procedures

To discuss import/export in Spanish, you need vocabulary for documents, tariffs, and logistics. Key terms: "aduana" (customs), "arancel" (tariff), "factura comercial" (commercial invoice), "conocimiento de embarque" (bill of lading), "certificado de origen" (certificate of origin). For African businesses exporting to Spanish-speaking markets, say: "Necesitamos un certificado de origen para exportar a Mxico." In conversation, use phrases like "Qu documentos se requieren para importar?" (What documents are required to import?). Practice describing your product: "Exportamos caf de Etiopa a Espaa." Discuss procedures: "El proceso de despacho de aduana puede tardar cinco das." Learn about trade agreements: "El acuerdo comercial entre la Unin Europea y frica facilita los aranceles." For imports, ask about restrictions: "Hay cuotas de importacin?" (Are there import quotas?). Use numbers: "El arancel es del 10% sobre el valor CIF." When talking about shipping, mention modes: "Envo martimo" (sea freight), "envo areo" (air freight). Discuss costs: "El flete martimo es ms econmico pero ms lento." For payment, use letters of credit: "Usamos carta de crdito para garantizar el pago." Practice dialogues with a customs broker. Tools like the International Trade Centre's Market Access Map can help you find tariffs. In Spanish, say: "Consult el mapa de acceso a mercados." Also, discuss packaging and labeling: "El etiquetado debe estar en espaol." For compliance, mention: "Cumplimos con las regulaciones sanitarias." Role-play a conversation with a customs agent: "Buenos das, necesito declarar estas mercancas." Learn to ask for help: "Puede explicarme el procedimiento?" Finally, keep a glossary of terms and practice with real documents. This will make you confident in import/export discussions.

4. Expanding to New Spanish-Speaking Markets

Expanding to a new Spanish-speaking market requires research and adaptation. Start by identifying target countries: Mexico, Colombia, Spain, Argentina. Each has different consumer preferences. For example, in Mexico, price sensitivity is high; in Spain, quality and sustainability matter. Use Spanish to conduct market research: "Cul es el tamao del mercado para nuestro producto?" (What is the market size for our product?). Tools: Google Trends, Statista, and local chambers of commerce. For African businesses, consider cultural fit: "Nuestro producto se alinea con la cultura local." Learn about local regulations: "Necesitamos registrar la marca en el pas." (We need to register the trademark in the country). Adapt your marketing: "Traduciremos nuestro sitio web al espaol." But translation alone isn't enough; localization is key. For instance, in Argentina, use "vos" instead of "t". In Colombia, use "usted" more formally. Hire local talent: "Contrataremos un gerente de ventas local." Set up distribution: "Buscaremos distribuidores locales." Use phrases like "Queremos establecer una oficina en Bogot." Financial considerations: "Necesitamos una cuenta bancaria local." Also, understand payment methods: "En Mxico, se usa mucho OXXO para pagos." For digital marketing, use local social media: "En Espaa, LinkedIn es clave para B2B." Test the market with a pilot: "Lanzaremos una prueba piloto en Chile." Learn from competitors: "Analizaremos a la competencia." Attend trade shows: "Participaremos en la feria internacional." Network: "Conectaremos con empresarios locales." Finally, be patient: expansion takes time. Use Spanish to build relationships: "Estamos comprometidos con el mercado latinoamericano." Practice pitching your expansion plan in Spanish to potential partners.

5. Using Formal and Informal Speech Appropriately

In Spanish, choosing between formal (usted) and informal (t) is crucial in business. Use "usted" with new contacts, older people, and in hierarchical situations. Use "t" with peers, colleagues of similar age, and after invitation. In Latin America, "usted" is more common in business than in Spain. For example, when meeting a potential client in Colombia, say: "Cmo est usted?" not "Cmo ests?" As you build trust, they may say "tutame" (use t with me). In Spain, "t" is more common in business, but still use "usted" for formal presentations. In Argentina, "vos" is used instead of "t", with its own conjugations: "vos tens" instead of "t tienes". Learn these variations. For email, start with "Estimado/a" (formal) or "Hola" (informal). In negotiations, formal speech shows respect. Use phrases: "Le agradezco su tiempo" (I appreciate your time). For informal: "Gracias por tu ayuda." When in doubt, use formal until invited otherwise. Also, note that in some contexts, using "t" too soon can seem disrespectful. Practice role-plays: introduce yourself formally, then switch to informal. Listen to how natives address each other. Tools: watch Spanish business TV shows or podcasts. For written communication, formal letters use "usted" and informal use "t". In group settings, use "ustedes" for plural you, but in Spain, "vosotros" is informal plural. In Latin America, "ustedes" is both formal and informal plural. Be mindful of regional differences. For example, in Mexico, "Mande?" is a polite way to say "Pardon?" instead of "Qu?" which can be rude. Adapt your speech to the local norm. This shows cultural intelligence and builds rapport. Practice with a language partner, focusing on verb conjugations: "Puede usted...?" vs "Puedes t...?" Mastery of formality will enhance your business relationships.

6. Practice: Deliver a Business Pitch in Spanish

Now it's time to put everything together and deliver a full business pitch in Spanish. Follow this structure: 1) Introduction: Greet formally, state your name and company. "Buenos das, me llamo [Name] y soy el fundador de [Company]." 2) Problem: Describe the problem your business solves. "En frica, muchos agricultores pierden cosechas por falta de almacenamiento." 3) Solution: Explain your product or service. "Ofrecemos soluciones de almacenamiento asequibles." 4) Market: State your target market and size. "Nuestro mercado son 10,000 agricultores en Ghana." 5) Traction: Share achievements. "Hemos aumentado los ingresos en un 200% este ao." 6) Team: Introduce key members. "Nuestro equipo tiene experiencia en agricultura y logstica." 7) Financials: Present key numbers. "Buscamos $500,000 para expandirnos." 8) Ask: State what you need. "Inversin para comprar equipos y contratar personal." 9) Closing: Thank the audience and invite questions. "Gracias por su atencin. Preguntas?" Practice with a timer: aim for 5-7 minutes. Record yourself and check pronunciation. Use visual aids with Spanish text. Rehearse with a native speaker via iTalki. Common mistakes: speaking too fast, using English words, forgetting formal address. To improve, listen to TED Talks in Spanish and mimic. Also, prepare for Q&A: anticipate questions about costs, competition, and risks. Practice answers: "Nuestro principal competidor es X, pero nos diferenciamos por Y." Use phrases like "Permtanme explicar" (Allow me to explain). For the pitch, use storytelling: "Conoc a una agricultora que duplic su produccin gracias a nuestro producto." This creates emotional connection. Finally, be confident and passionate. Even with imperfect grammar, enthusiasm and clarity matter. After the pitch, ask for feedback. Repeat the exercise until you feel natural. This practice will prepare you for real investor meetings in Spanish-speaking markets.

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Never confirm a price, volume or delivery date in Spanish without written confirmation of the Incoterm, currency, payment method and applicable quality standard. <h3>Register: Formal vs. Informal in Practice</h3>
<p>Spanish has a clear distinction between <em>usted</em> (formal) and <em>t</em> (informal), and in business the default is formal until the counterpart signals otherwise. In Equatorial Guinea, Spain and most of Latin America, using <em>usted</em> with a new buyer, ministry official or investor is expected. In Argentina and parts of Central America, <em>vos</em> may appear in informal settings, but it is not appropriate for first contact. Watch for cues: if a buyer writes <em>te adjunto</em> rather than <em>le adjunto</em>, they are inviting informality. Until then, keep verbs in the third-person formal form and use titles such as <em>seor</em>, <em>seora</em>, <em>licenciado</em> or <em>ingeniero</em> where relevant.</p>

<p>In negotiations, register also affects how firm you sound. <em>No podemos aceptar eso</em> is blunt; <em>Nos resultara difcil aceptar esa condicin</em> is firm but diplomatic. For African exporters negotiating with Spanish-speaking buyers, the second form preserves the relationship while protecting margin. When discussing large orders, use conditional and polite forms to probe without committing: <em>Sera posible ampliar el pedido a 500 toneladas?</em>.</p>

<h3>Delivering a Business Pitch in Spanish</h3>
<p>A pitch in Spanish follows a predictable structure: greeting and introduction, problem and opportunity, your solution and capacity, market evidence, financials, team, and a clear ask. Keep sentences short. Use numbers. Replace adjectives with data: not <em>calidad excelente</em> but <em>humedad por debajo del 8 %, certificacin orgnica y trazabilidad por lote</em>. For an African agribusiness, lead with supply reliability and certification, because that is what Spanish-speaking importers and investors screen for first.</p>

<p>A workable opening: <em>Buenos das. Gracias por su tiempo. Somos una empresa exportadora con sede en [pas], especializada en [producto]. Exportamos [volumen] toneladas anuales y buscamos un socio para [objetivo].</em> Then move to the ask: <em>Buscamos una inversin de [monto] para [uso especfico], con un retorno estimado de [plazo].</em> Close with a next step: <em>Podemos agendar una reunin tcnica la prxima semana para revisar los detalles?</em></p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Know your register:</strong> default to <em>usted</em> with buyers, officials and investors; switch to <em>t</em> only when invited.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare numbers in Spanish:</strong> volumes in tonnes, prices in the agreed currency, dates in dd/mm/yyyy format.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm Incoterms exactly:</strong> state the 2020 code (FOB, CIF, DAP) and who pays freight, insurance and duties.</li>
<li><strong>List required documents:</strong> factura comercial, certificado de origen, conocimiento de embarque, lista de empaque, certificado fitosanitario.</li>
<li><strong>Check destination rules:</strong> labelling, food safety, tariffs and import licences before quoting a final price.</li>
<li><strong>Use diplomatic firmness:</strong> conditional forms for objections; avoid blunt refusals that damage the relationship.</li>
<li><strong>Close with a concrete next step:</strong> a date, a document to send, or a meeting to schedule.</li>
<li><strong>Follow up in writing in Spanish:</strong> repeat the agreed terms to avoid misunderstandings across time zones.</li>
</ul>
