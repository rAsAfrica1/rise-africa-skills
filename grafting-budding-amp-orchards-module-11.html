1. Knowing when to harvest mango, avocado, and citrus

Harvesting at the right time is the difference between fruit that sells for a premium and fruit that rots before it reaches the market. For mangoes, start checking 90 to 120 days after flowering. The skin changes from dark green to light green or yellow, and the shoulders (the area around the stem) fill out. A ripe mango will snap off easily when you lift it. For export, harvest when the fruit is mature but still firm, usually at 70-80% maturity. In Kenya, the Apple mango is ready from November to January, while Ngowe comes later. Test by cutting a fruit: the flesh should be yellow-orange and the seed hard.

Avocados are trickier because they don't ripen on the tree. Harvest when the fruit reaches a certain dry matter content: 24% for Hass, 20% for Fuerte. You can test by weighing a fruit, drying it in the sun, and reweighing. If the dry matter is too low, the fruit will shrivel and never ripen properly. The skin also turns from green to dark purple or black in Hass, but color is not reliable. In Tanzania, Hass is harvested from March to September. Pick one fruit and let it ripen indoors; if it softens in 5-7 days, the tree is ready.

Citrus: Oranges, lemons, and mandarins are ready when they change color and taste sweet. For oranges, the sugar-to-acid ratio should be at least 8:1. Use a refractometer to check Brix. A simple taste test works too. In South Africa, Valencia oranges are harvested from June to September. Lemons are picked when they turn yellow but still firm. Mandarins are ready when the skin loosens easily. Don't leave citrus on the tree too long after color change, or it becomes puffy and dry.

Always harvest in the cool of the day, early morning or late afternoon. Fruit picked in the heat will respire faster and spoil sooner. Use a sharp pair of secateurs or a harvesting pole with a clipper. Leave a short stem (2-3 mm) on mangoes and avocados to prevent sap burn. For citrus, cut close to the fruit. Never pull fruit off the tree; that damages the stem end and invites rot. After harvest, keep fruit in the shade and move it to a cool store as soon as possible.

2. Harvesting tools and techniques to avoid bruising

Bruising is the silent killer of profit. A bruised fruit may look fine but will rot within days. To avoid bruising, you need the right tools and careful handling. For low trees, use a sharp pair of bypass secateurs or a small harvesting knife. For tall trees, use a harvesting pole with a clipper head and a catching bag. The bag should be padded with foam or cloth so fruit doesn't hit hard plastic. In Kenya, many farmers use a simple pole with a hook and a net, but a proper pole with a clipper is better because it cuts the stem cleanly.

When harvesting, never drop fruit into a bucket or basket from a height. Place it gently. Wear soft cotton gloves to protect the fruit from your fingernails and rough skin. For mangoes and avocados, leave a short stem (2-3 mm) to prevent sap from burning the skin. For citrus, cut the stem flush with the fruit. Use a clean, sharp tool; a dull blade will crush the stem and cause rot. Sanitize your tools with alcohol or bleach solution between trees to prevent spreading disease.

Collect fruit in shallow crates or buckets lined with soft material. Never use deep sacks or gunny bags because the weight of the fruit on top will bruise the ones below. Plastic crates are best because they stack and allow air circulation. In many African markets, you can buy 20-litre plastic crates for a few dollars. If you use baskets, line them with banana leaves or newspaper. Do not overfill; leave a 5 cm gap at the top.

After harvest, move fruit to a shaded area immediately. Do not leave it in the sun. Field heat causes rapid deterioration. If you can, use a simple evaporative cooler (a box with wet sand and a fan) to pre-cool fruit. For avocado and mango, aim to bring fruit temperature down to 10-15Â°C within 2 hours. Never pile fruit more than 3 layers deep in a crate. Handle each fruit as if it were an egg. One bruised fruit can release ethylene gas that ripens others prematurely. Sort out any damaged fruit before storage or transport.

3. Sorting, grading, and packing for local markets

Sorting and grading turn a pile of fruit into a product that buyers trust. Start by removing any fruit that is damaged, diseased, or unripe. One rotten fruit can spoil the whole crate. For mangoes, grade into three sizes: large (over 400g), medium (250-400g), and small (under 250g). For avocados, grade by count per 4 kg box: 12, 14, 16, 18, or 20. For citrus, grade by diameter: e.g., oranges 65-75 mm, 75-85 mm, etc. Use a simple sizing ring or a caliper. In local markets, uniform size and color fetch a better price.

Grading also means checking for blemishes. Supermarkets reject fruit with spots, scars, or sunburn. For local markets, you can sell blemished fruit at a lower price, but keep them separate. Never mix grades in one crate. Buyers want consistency. Use a clean, shaded area for sorting. Work on a table covered with a soft cloth. Wear gloves to avoid scratches. Have a bucket of clean water and a cloth to wipe off dirt or sap. Do not wash fruit unless you have a drying system, as wet fruit grows mould.

Packing is both art and science. Use the right container: for local markets, a 20 kg plastic crate or a wooden box. For supermarkets, use a 4 kg cardboard box with ventilation holes. Line the box with paper or a soft liner. Arrange fruit in layers, stem down for mangoes and avocados to prevent sap dripping. Do not pack more than 2 layers for large fruit. For citrus, you can pack in a single layer in a shallow tray. Label the box with your farm name, grade, count, and date. This builds trust and makes it easy for buyers to reorder.

For transport, keep crates out of direct sun. If using a pickup, cover the load with a white tarpaulin. Do not stack crates more than 4 high, or the bottom ones will crush. For distant markets, use a refrigerated truck if possible, but many smallholders use ordinary trucks at night when it's cooler. Aim to deliver within 24 hours of harvest. Always keep a record of how many crates you sent and what price you got. That data helps you plan the next harvest.

4. Simple value addition: drying, juice, and jam

Value addition means turning fresh fruit into products that last longer and sell for more. Drying is the simplest. For mango, choose ripe but firm fruit. Wash, peel, and cut into 5 mm slices. Remove the seed. Soak slices in a solution of 1 teaspoon of ascorbic acid (or lemon juice) per litre of water for 10 minutes to prevent browning. Drain and arrange on drying trays. Use a solar dryer or an oven at 60Â°C. Dry for 6-10 hours until leathery but not brittle. Store in airtight jars or vacuum-sealed bags. Dried mango sells for 3-5 times the price of fresh fruit in urban markets.

Juice: For citrus, juice is a quick win. Wash and cut oranges or lemons. Use a manual or electric juicer. Strain out seeds and pulp. Pasteurize by heating to 80Â°C for 2 minutes, then cool quickly. Bottle in clean glass bottles. Add preservative (sodium benzoate, 0.1%) if you want a 6-month shelf life. For mango juice, blend pulp with water (1:1) and sugar to taste. Heat to 85Â°C for 3 minutes, cool, and bottle. Label with production and expiry dates. In many African countries, you can sell fresh juice to local shops, schools, and hotels. A 500 ml bottle can sell for $1-2.

Jam: Mango and citrus jam are easy. For mango jam: 1 kg mashed mango pulp, 800 g sugar, 2 tablespoons lemon juice. Cook in a heavy pot, stirring constantly, until it thickens (about 30 minutes). Test by putting a drop on a cold plate; if it wrinkles, it's ready. Pour into sterilized jars, seal, and process in a hot water bath for 10 minutes. For citrus marmalade, use sliced peel and pulp. Jam lasts up to a year. Sell in 250 ml jars to supermarkets, gift shops, and online.

Other value-added products: fruit leather, fruit powder, and chutney. Always follow hygiene rules: wash hands, sterilize equipment, and use clean water. Start small and test the market. Many African farmers double their income by drying just 20% of their harvest. You can dry on a solar dryer made from a wooden frame and clear plastic sheet. The key is to control temperature and airflow. Don't dry fruit on the ground; use raised trays. Package attractively with a label that tells your story.

5. Selling to supermarkets, hotels, and export markets

Selling to supermarkets, hotels, and export markets requires a different mindset than selling at the local market. These buyers want consistency, volume, and quality. Start by researching what they need. A supermarket may want 100 crates of grade A mangoes per week. A hotel may want 20 kg of avocados twice a week. Exporters want specific varieties, sizes, and packaging. Visit the buyers, ask for their specifications, and get a contract. In Kenya, supermarkets like Naivas and Carrefour buy from local farmers. In Tanzania, hotels in Arusha and Zanzibar source from nearby farms.

To get a contract, you need to meet their standards. That means sorting, grading, and packing exactly as they specify. You also need a reliable supply. If you can't supply 100 crates, partner with other farmers to form a cooperative. Many African supermarkets prefer to buy from groups because it reduces their transaction costs. Register your group and get a business permit. You will also need a food safety certificate, such as HACCP or GlobalG.A.P., for export. That takes time and money, but it opens doors.

Pricing is key. Supermarkets often pay 20-30% more than local markets, but they also demand credit terms (payment after 30 days). You need cash flow to survive. Hotels pay faster, often on delivery. Exporters pay in dollars but require you to cover transport to the port. Always calculate your cost of production: labor, inputs, packaging, transport. Add a 30% profit margin. Don't undercut yourself. Negotiate a price that covers your costs and allows you to reinvest.

For export, you must meet phytosanitary requirements. That means no live insects, no disease, and proper documentation. You will need a phytosanitary certificate from your country's agriculture department. For avocado exports to Europe, fruit must be from a registered orchard and packed in a certified packhouse. The process is complex, but the rewards are high. Start by selling to local supermarkets and hotels to build your track record. Then, approach exporters. Many exporters will help you get certified if you can supply consistent quality. Always deliver on time. One late delivery can lose a contract. Keep records of every sale and buyer feedback.

6. Hands-on: Harvesting and packing a crate of fruit for sale

This lesson is practical. You will harvest and pack a crate of fruit ready for sale. Choose a tree with mature fruit. For this exercise, use mango, avocado, or citrus. You will need: a harvesting pole or secateurs, a 20 kg plastic crate, soft cloth or newspaper, a clean bucket, and gloves. Work in the early morning. Wear closed shoes and a hat. Before you start, check that your tools are clean and sharp. Wash your hands.

Step 1: Harvest. Use the pole to reach high fruit. Place the clipper around the stem and cut, letting the fruit fall into the catching bag. For low fruit, hold the fruit gently and cut the stem with secateurs. Leave a 2-3 mm stem on mangoes and avocados. For citrus, cut flush. Never pull. Place each fruit gently into the bucket. Do not drop. Fill the bucket only halfway to avoid bruising.

Step 2: Sort and grade. Set up a table in the shade. Cover with a soft cloth. Empty the bucket onto the table. Wipe each fruit with a dry cloth to remove dirt or sap. Check for blemishes. Remove any damaged fruit. Grade by size: for mangoes, use a sizing ring or weigh. Large: >400g, medium: 250-400g, small: <250g. For avocados, count per 4 kg: 12, 14, 16, 18, 20. For citrus, measure diameter. Keep grades separate.

Step 3: Pack. Line your crate with newspaper or a soft liner. Arrange fruit in layers, stem down. Do not pack more than 2 layers for large fruit. For mangoes, you can fit about 20-25 medium fruits in a 20 kg crate. For avocados, about 50-60 fruits. For citrus, about 80-100. Leave a small gap at the top. Close the crate. Label with your name, grade, count, and date.

Step 4: Store and transport. Keep the crate in a cool, shaded place. If you have a cooler, set it to 10-15Â°C. If not, use a dark room with a fan. Transport to the market or buyer as soon as possible. Record the weight and number of crates. After sale, note the price and any feedback. Repeat this process every harvest. With practice, you will reduce bruising and increase your income. Remember: one bruised fruit can spoil the crate, so handle every fruit with care.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Harvesting, Post-Harvest & Value Addition for Mango, Avocado & Citrus

In African fruit value chains, the money is not made in the orchard â it is made at the point of sale. A mango that leaves the farm bruised, ungraded, or at the wrong maturity loses 30â60% of its market value before it reaches a buyer. This lesson gives you the practical operating rules for harvesting, handling, packing, and adding value to mango, avocado, and citrus so you can sell to supermarkets, hotels, and export buyers at premium prices.

Treat post-harvest handling as a business process, not a chore. Every bruise is a discount. Every rejected crate is lost revenue. Every day of delay is a day closer to spoilage. The farmers who win contracts with formal buyers are the ones who can deliver consistent quality, consistent volume, and consistent timing.

1. Knowing When to Harvest Mango, Avocado, and Citrus

Harvest maturity is different from eating ripeness. For distant markets, you harvest mature but firm fruit; it ripens during transport and on the buyer's shelf. For local markets and processing, you can harvest closer to eating ripeness. Getting this wrong is the single biggest cause of rejected consignments.

Mango: Harvest when the shoulders fill out (the fruit widens at the stem end), the skin develops a slight colour break, and the fruit sinks in water (specific gravity test). For export, target 70â80% maturity. A mature mango ripens properly; an immature one shrivels and never develops full flavour.

Avocado: Avocado does not ripen on the tree â it ripens after harvest. Harvest when dry matter content reaches the minimum for the variety (typically 21â24% for Hass). Use the dry matter test or count days from full bloom. Never harvest immature avocado; it will never ripen properly and buyers will reject it.

Citrus: Harvest when the juice-to-acid ratio meets the buyer's specification. For oranges, a Brix-to-acid ratio of 8:1 to 12:1 is typical. Use a refractometer and titratable acidity test. Colour is not a reliable indicator â some green oranges are fully mature.

2. Harvesting Tools and Techniques to Avoid Bruising

Bruising is the enemy of premium pricing. A bruised mango or avocado may look fine at harvest but develops dark spots and soft patches within 48 hours, making it unsellable to formal buyers. The tools and techniques below are non-negotiable for anyone targeting supermarkets, hotels, or export.

Tools: Use sharp harvesting clippers or secateurs for citrus and avocado; use a pole harvester with a catching bag for tall mango trees. Never shake trees or throw fruit into buckets. Line all collection buckets and crates with soft material (foam, cloth, or banana leaves). Use plastic field crates, not woven sacks, which cause friction damage.

Technique: Harvest in the cool of the morning (before 10am) to reduce field heat. Leave a short stem (2â5mm) on mango and avocado to prevent latex or sap burn. For citrus, clip the stem flush to avoid puncture damage to neighbouring fruit. Place fruit gently â never drop. Carry crates on your head or shoulder, not dragged on the ground.

3. Sorting, Grading, and Packing for Local Markets

Sorting and grading are where you convert a commodity into a branded product. Buyers pay for consistency. A crate of uniform, clean, correctly sized fruit commands 20â40% more than a mixed crate of the same volume.

Sorting: Remove all damaged, diseased, undersized, or overripe fruit at the field edge. Do not send rejects to the packhouse â they contaminate good fruit and waste transport cost.

Grading: Grade by size (count per crate), colour, and freedom from defects. Use sizing rings or a simple grading table. Keep grades consistent: Grade 1 for premium buyers, Grade 2 for local markets, Grade 3 for processing.

Packing: Use clean, ventilated crates. Line with paper or soft material. Pack fruit in a single layer or in trays to prevent compression. Label the crate with your farm name, grade, count, and harvest date. This is your brand â treat it as a business asset.

4. Simple Value Addition: Drying, Juice, and Jam

Value addition is your hedge against price crashes and rejects. When fresh fruit prices fall or a buyer rejects a consignment, processing converts a loss into a product with a longer shelf life and a different market.

Drying: Mango and citrus peel can be dried for snacks, tea, or spice. Use a solar dryer or dehydrator at 55â60Â°C for 6â12 hours. Dried mango slices sell to local and export health-food markets. Dried citrus peel is sold to tea blenders and cosmetic makers.

Juice: Mango and citrus juice can be sold fresh, pasteurised, or frozen. A small press and pasteuriser can turn Grade 2 fruit into a value-added product. Bottle in clean, food-grade containers and label with production and expiry dates. Target hotels, schools, and local retailers.

Jam: Mango and citrus jam uses fruit, sugar, and pectin. It has a 12â18 month shelf life and can be sold in local markets, gift shops, and to hotels. Start with simple recipes and scale as demand grows.

Business tip: Value addition is not a fallback â it is a parallel business. Keep separate records for fresh sales and processed sales. Many African agribusinesses fail because they mix the two and cannot see which is profitable.

5. Selling to Supermarkets, Hotels, and Export Markets

Formal buyers pay more but demand more. Before you approach a supermarket, hotel, or exporter, prepare the following:

  • Consistency: Can you supply the same grade, same count, same quality every week?
  • Volume: Can you meet the minimum order quantity? If not, join a farmer group or cooperative.
  • Compliance: Do you have a food safety certificate, traceability records, and a registered business?
  • Packaging: Can you deliver in the buyer's preferred crate size and labelling format?
  • Pricing: Do you know your cost of production per crate? Never negotiate without this number.

Export markets add phytosanitary requirements, cold chain, and documentation. Start with local supermarkets and hotels, build a track record, then approach exporters. Many African exporters prefer to buy from farmers who already supply formal local buyers.

6. Hands-On: Harvesting and Packing a Crate of Fruit for Sale

This is your practical assessment. Complete this task with a real crate of fruit and record the results.

  1. Select 20 fruit of one crop (mango, avocado, or citrus) at correct harvest maturity.
  2. Harvest using correct tools and technique â no bruising, no dropping, short stem left on.
  3. Sort at the field edge: remove all damaged, diseased, and undersized fruit.
  4. Grade into Grade 1 and Grade 2 using size and colour.
  5. Pack Grade 1 fruit into a clean, ventilated crate with soft lining.
  6. Label the crate: farm name, crop, grade, count, harvest date.
  7. Weigh the crate and record the weight.
  8. Store in the coolest available place and record temperature.
  9. After 48 hours, inspect the crate and count bruised or spoiled fruit.
  10. Calculate your loss percentage and compare to the 5% target.

If your loss is above 5%, review your harvesting technique, crate lining, and storage. Repeat until you consistently hit the target. This is the skill that wins supermarket and hotel contracts.
