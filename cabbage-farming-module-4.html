1. Optimal Planting Dates and Spacing

In Africa, cabbage can be grown year-round in cooler highland areas, but timing is critical in warmer regions. Aim to plant at the start of the rains or when temperatures are between 15C and 20C. In Kenya's highlands, plant in March-April and September-October. In Nigeria's Jos Plateau, plant from April to June and September to November. Avoid planting in extreme heat (above 30C) as heads become loose and bitter. If you have irrigation, you can plant in dry seasons but expect more pest pressure.

Spacing determines head size and yield. For most varieties, use 60 cm between rows and 45 cm between plants. This gives about 37,000 plants per hectare. For smaller heads, tighten to 45 cm x 30 cm (about 74,000 plants/ha). For larger heads, widen to 75 cm x 60 cm (about 22,000 plants/ha). In raised beds, plant in double rows 60 cm apart, with 45 cm between plants in the row, and 90 cm between bed centers.

Always plant on raised beds or ridges, especially in heavy rain areas. Beds should be 20-30 cm high and 1 m wide. This prevents waterlogging and root diseases. If you are in a dry area, plant in furrows or flat beds to conserve moisture. Mark your spacing with a measuring stick or string to keep uniform. Uniform spacing makes weeding, spraying, and harvesting easier.

Before planting, ensure your soil is well-prepared with compost or manure. Apply 2-3 handfuls of well-rotted manure per planting hole. Mix it well with soil. Water the holes before planting. Plant seedlings in the evening or on a cloudy day to reduce transplant shock. After planting, water immediately. If you plant too close, you get small heads and more disease. Too far apart, you waste land and get sunburn on heads. So stick to recommended spacing for your variety and market.

2. Planting Depth and Seedling Handling

Cabbage seedlings are delicate. The depth you plant them at can make or break your crop. The rule is simple: plant the seedling so that the first true leaves are just above the soil surface. The root ball should be covered, but the growing point (where new leaves emerge) must not be buried. If you bury the growing point, the plant will rot or produce a deformed head. If you plant too shallow, roots dry out and the seedling falls over.

When transplanting, handle seedlings by the leaves, not the stem. The stem is easily crushed, and a damaged stem means a dead plant. Use a small trowel or a stick to make a hole deep enough to take the entire root ball. Place the seedling in the hole, then firm the soil around it gently with your hands. Do not press too hardyou want the soil to be firm but not compacted. Water immediately after planting to settle the soil around the roots.

Seedlings should be 4-6 weeks old when transplanted. They should have 4-5 true leaves and be about 10-15 cm tall. If you buy seedlings from a nursery, check that they are healthy, stocky, and free of pests. Avoid seedlings with yellow leaves or thin, stretched stems. Before transplanting, water the nursery bed thoroughly so the soil sticks to the roots. This reduces root damage when you lift the seedlings.

If you are planting in hot weather, transplant in the late afternoon or evening. If it is very sunny, provide shade for the first 2-3 days using palm fronds or shade nets. In the first week after transplanting, water twice dailymorning and eveningunless it rains. After one week, reduce to once daily. This helps the seedlings establish quickly. A well-planted seedling will start growing within 3-4 days. If you see wilting after 5 days, check for root damage or pests.

3. Row Arrangement and Plant Density

How you arrange your cabbage plants in the field affects airflow, sunlight, and disease pressure. The most common arrangement is single rows on raised beds. Rows should run in the direction of the prevailing wind to improve air movement and reduce fungal diseases. In flat beds, use a north-south orientation if possible, so both sides of the row get sunlight. In Africa, many farmers use ridges or beds, so rows follow the bed direction.

Plant density is the number of plants per unit area. For cabbage, the recommended density varies with variety and market. For standard varieties like Gloria F1 or Copenhagen Market, aim for 35,000 to 40,000 plants per hectare. This means spacing of 60 cm between rows and 45 cm between plants. For compact varieties like Green Boy, you can go up to 50,000 plants per hectare with 50 cm x 40 cm spacing. For large heads for restaurants, reduce to 25,000 plants per hectare with 75 cm x 60 cm.

Higher density gives more heads per hectare but smaller heads and more disease because of poor airflow. Lower density gives larger heads but fewer of them, and you may get sunburn on exposed heads. The key is to match density to your market. If you sell to supermarkets that want 1-1.5 kg heads, use medium density. If you sell to local markets that accept smaller heads, you can plant denser.

In double-row beds, plant two rows 45 cm apart on a 1 m wide bed, with 45 cm between plants in the row, and 75 cm between bed centers. This gives about 40,000 plants per hectare. Always leave paths every 10-15 meters for access. After planting, draw a simple map of your field showing rows and beds. This helps you plan weeding, spraying, and harvesting. Remember, overcrowding stresses plants and invites pests like aphids and diamondback moth.

4. Mulching Techniques for Cabbage

Mulching is covering the soil around your cabbage plants with a layer of material. It conserves moisture, suppresses weeds, and keeps the soil cool. In Africa, you can use organic mulches like dry grass, straw, banana leaves, or maize stover. Spread a layer 5-10 cm thick around the plants, but keep it 5 cm away from the stem to prevent rot. You can also use plastic mulch, which is more expensive but lasts longer and warms the soil in cool highlands.

Organic mulches are cheap and easy to find. After planting, wait until the seedlings are established (about 2 weeks), then spread mulch. Dry grass is excellentmake sure it has no seeds, or you will introduce weeds. Banana leaves are good but break down quickly. Maize stover is durable but may harbor pests like stem borers; chop it finely. In dry areas, a thick mulch of 10 cm can reduce watering by half. In wet areas, a thinner mulch of 5 cm is enough to suppress weeds.

Plastic mulch comes in rolls, usually black or silver. Black plastic warms the soil and controls weeds completely. Silver plastic reflects sunlight and repels aphids and thrips. To use plastic mulch, lay it on the bed before planting, secure the edges with soil, and cut holes for your seedlings. It costs more but saves labor on weeding and can increase yields by 20-30%. In South Africa and Kenya, many commercial cabbage farmers use plastic mulch.

Mulching also prevents soil splash on leaves, which reduces soil-borne diseases like black rot. It keeps heads clean, so you get a better market price. If you use organic mulch, add more as it decomposes. Do not use fresh manure as mulchit burns plants and attracts flies. After harvest, you can plow organic mulch into the soil to improve organic matter. For plastic mulch, remove and dispose of it properly, or reuse it for the next season if intact. Mulching is one of the cheapest ways to improve cabbage production.

5. Irrigation Setup After Planting

Cabbage needs consistent moisture, especially in the first 3 weeks after transplanting. A good irrigation setup ensures you can water evenly and efficiently. The simplest method is a watering can, but for more than 0.5 hectare, you need a better system. Drip irrigation is the best for cabbage because it saves water, reduces weeds, and keeps leaves dry, which prevents fungal diseases. A drip kit for 1 hectare costs about $500-$1000, but you can start with a smaller kit for 0.2 hectare.

If you cannot afford drip, use furrow irrigation. Make furrows between your beds, and let water flow gently. Do not flood the bedswater should reach the root zone, not the leaves. In sandy soil, water more often but less amount. In clay soil, water less often but more amount. A general rule: cabbage needs 25-30 mm of water per week. In hot weather, it may need 40 mm. Check soil moisture by digging 10 cm deepif it feels dry, irrigate.

For overhead irrigation, use sprinklers but water early in the morning so leaves dry before evening. Wet leaves at night invite downy mildew and black rot. If you use a hose, water at the base of the plant. After planting, water immediately, then every 2-3 days for the first week. After 2 weeks, water twice a week if no rain. During head formation, do not let the soil dry out, or heads will crack. Mulching helps retain moisture, so you can water less.

Set up your irrigation system before planting so you can water immediately after transplanting. For drip, lay lines along the rows, with emitters 30 cm apart. Connect to a water source and a filter. Test the system for even flow. For furrow, ensure a gentle slope so water reaches the end without pooling. Always have a backup plana tank or drum for emergency watering. In dry seasons, irrigation is the difference between a good harvest and crop failure. Keep your system clean and repair leaks promptly.

6. Protecting Seedlings from Pests and Weather

Young cabbage seedlings are vulnerable to pests and harsh weather. The most common pests are cutworms, aphids, diamondback moth, and flea beetles. Cutworms cut the stem at ground level. To control them, put a collar of cardboard or plastic around the stem, or sprinkle wood ash around the base. Aphids and flea beetles suck sap and transmit diseases. Spray with neem oil (5 ml per liter of water) every 7 days, or use soapy water (1 tablespoon liquid soap per liter). For diamondback moth, handpick caterpillars or spray Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), a natural bacteria that kills only caterpillars.

Weather extremes can also kill seedlings. Heavy rain can wash away seeds or bury small plants. If you expect heavy rain, plant on raised beds and make drainage channels. Strong sun can scorch leaves. Use shade nets (50% shade) for the first week, or plant in the shade of taller crops like maize. In cold highlands, frost can kill seedlings. Cover plants with dry grass or plastic tunnels at night. In hot lowlands, plant early in the season or use mulch to keep soil cool.

You can also use physical barriers. A row cover (agro-net) over seedlings keeps out insects and birds. It is cheap and reusable. For birds, scarecrows or reflective tape work. For snails and slugs, sprinkle ash or crushed eggshells around the bed. Check your crop every morning for signs of damage. Early detection is key. If you see holes in leaves, look for caterpillars. If leaves are curling, look for aphids underneath.

Always keep the area around your seedbed clean. Remove weeds and crop debris, as they harbor pests. Rotate cropsdo not plant cabbage where you grew cabbage or other brassicas last season. If you use chemicals, choose those recommended for cabbage and follow the label. But start with natural methods first. A healthy seedling that is well-watered and mulched can withstand some pest damage. Your goal is to get the plants past the first 3 weeks, after which they become stronger and more resistant.

<h2>Practical Reference: Planting and Crop Establishment for Cabbage in African Smallholder and Commercial Systems</h2>

<p>Cabbage (<em>Brassica oleracea</em> var. <em>capitata</em>) is one of the highest-value brassica crops for African smallholders and emerging commercial growers. It performs well in cool highland zones (Kenya, Ethiopia, Rwanda, Tanzania, Zimbabwe, South Africa) and in winter production windows in warmer lowland areas. Establishment is the single most profit-sensitive stage: a poorly planted crop never recovers full yield potential, and replanting costs money, labour, and market timing.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in the <strong>Planting and Crop Establishment</strong> module into one operational guide you can use in the field. Treat it as a checklist you return to before every planting block.</p>

<h3>1. Optimal Planting Dates and Spacing</h3>
<p>Cabbage is a cool-season crop. In Africa, the highest-quality heads come from plantings timed so that head formation occurs at 1520 C. In highland areas (1,5002,500 m), this means year-round production with peaks in the two rainy seasons. In lowland areas, planting must be shifted to the coolest months (typically MayJuly in East Africa, AprilJune in southern Africa).</p>

<p>Spacing drives head size, market grade, and total yield. Close spacing produces more, smaller heads suited to fresh-market bundles; wider spacing produces larger heads suited to supermarkets and processors.</p>

<h3>2. Planting Depth and Seedling Handling</h3>
<p>Whether you transplant or direct-seed, depth and root handling determine survival. Cabbage seedlings should be transplanted when they have 46 true leaves (about 34 weeks after sowing in the nursery). Set the seedling so the root ball sits 12 cm below the soil surface the growing point (crown) must remain above the soil line. Deeper planting causes crown rot; shallower planting causes the seedling to dry out.</p>

<p>Handle seedlings by the leaf, never by the stem. A bruised stem rarely recovers. Water the nursery thoroughly 23 hours before lifting so the root ball holds together. Discard any seedling with a curled, twisted, or bent root it will produce a stunted head.</p>

<h3>3. Row Arrangement and Plant Density</h3>
<p>Row arrangement affects airflow, light interception, spray coverage, and harvest labour. For most African open-field systems, single rows on raised beds with 60 cm between rows are standard. Double-row beds (two rows 30 cm apart, 70 cm between beds) improve land use in high-value irrigated blocks but require careful airflow management to reduce black rot and downy mildew pressure.</p>

<h3>4. Mulching Techniques for Cabbage</h3>
<p>Mulch is one of the cheapest yield-protecting investments. In African conditions, it does four jobs: conserves moisture during dry spells, suppresses weeds that compete for nutrients, moderates soil temperature, and reduces splash-borne disease (notably black rot and Alternaria).</p>

<p>Organic options: dry grass (avoid seeding grass), maize stover, banana pseudostem chopped, coffee husk (excellent in East African highlands). Apply 58 cm thick, leaving 5 cm clear around the stem to prevent collar rot. Plastic mulch (silver/black) is used in commercial blocks silver reflects aphids, black suppresses weeds but costs 35 more and requires disposal planning.</p>

<h3>5. Irrigation Setup After Planting</h3>
<p>Establishment irrigation must be reliable for the first 14 days. The goal is to keep the top 15 cm of soil moist without waterlogging the crown. Drip irrigation is the highest-efficiency option (6080% water saving vs furrow) and is increasingly affordable through African suppliers and donor programmes. Furrow irrigation remains common but requires careful levelling to avoid ponding at the lower end.</p>

<p>Practical schedule after transplanting:</p>
<ul>
<li>Day 0 (transplant day): irrigate immediately to settle soil around roots.</li>
<li>Days 17: light irrigation every 12 days; keep soil moist but not saturated.</li>
<li>Days 821: irrigate every 23 days depending on rainfall and soil type.</li>
<li>From week 4: shift to a regular schedule based on crop water requirement (cabbage needs roughly 350500 mm total).</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Protecting Seedlings from Pests and Weather</h3>
<p>Young cabbage seedlings are the most vulnerable stage. In Africa, the main establishment threats are diamondback moth (DBM), aphids, cutworms, flea beetles, and in highland areas hail and heavy rain. Practical protection measures include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Physical barriers:</strong> floating row covers or insect netting (0.40.8 mm mesh) for the first 3 weeks reduce DBM and aphid pressure.</li>
<li><strong>Collars:</strong> a 5 cm cardboard or plastic collar around the stem base prevents cutworm damage.</li>
<li><strong>Windbreaks:</strong> a single row of maize or napier grass on the windward side reduces seedling desiccation and hail damage.</li>
<li><strong>Scouting:</strong> walk the block twice weekly; treat only when thresholds are reached (e.g. 1 DBM larva per plant).</li>
<li><strong>Integrated pest management:</strong> rotate with non-brassica crops, use <em>Bacillus thuringiensis</em> (Bt) for DBM, and conserve natural enemies such as <em>Cotesia</em> wasps.</li>
</ul>
