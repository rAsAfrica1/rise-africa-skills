1. Feed Per Stage: Nursery to Finisher

Pigs eat different amounts and types of feed as they grow. Knowing how much to feed at each stage saves money and keeps your pigs healthy. A pig's life is divided into stages: nursery (weaning to 10 weeks), grower (10 to 16 weeks), and finisher (16 weeks to market). Each stage has specific feed needs.

In the nursery stage, piglets are small and their stomachs are sensitive. They need highly digestible feed, often called creep feed or nursery pellets. A piglet will eat about 0.5 kg per day. Over 4 weeks, that's 14 kg per pig. For 20 piglets, you need 280 kg of nursery feed. This feed is expensive, so buy only what you need.

During the grower stage, pigs grow fast and eat more. They need grower feed with about 16% protein. Each pig eats around 1.5 kg per day. Over 6 weeks (42 days), that's 63 kg per pig. For 20 pigs, that's 1,260 kg. You can mix your own grower feed using maize, soya, and minerals to cut costs.

In the finisher stage, pigs are almost market weight. They eat about 2.5 kg per day. Over 8 weeks (56 days), that's 140 kg per pig. For 20 pigs, that's 2,800 kg. Finisher feed has less protein (14%) and more energy. You can use cheaper ingredients like cassava or maize bran.

Total feed for one pig from nursery to market: 14 + 63 + 140 = 217 kg. For 20 pigs, that's 4,340 kg. At 20,000 Naira per 50 kg bag, that's 1,736,000 Naira. Knowing these numbers helps you plan your budget and avoid running out of feed. Always buy feed in bulk to get discounts. Store feed in a dry place to prevent mold.

2. Calculating Feed Conversion Ratio

Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) is a simple number that tells you how well your pigs turn feed into meat. It is the total feed eaten divided by the total weight gained. A low FCR means your pigs are efficient; a high FCR means they are wasting feed. Calculating FCR helps you spot problems early and make more profit.

To calculate FCR, you need two numbers: total feed consumed and total weight gained. For example, if you have 10 pigs and they eat 1,000 kg of feed over a period, and their total weight increases by 400 kg, then FCR = 1,000 400 = 2.5. This means it takes 2.5 kg of feed to produce 1 kg of pig.

You must weigh your pigs regularly. Use a livestock scale or a weigh tape. Weigh them at the start of the period and at the end. Also, keep track of how much feed you give them. Use a notebook or a simple phone app to record daily feed. Subtract any feed left in the trough.

If your FCR is higher than 3.0, something is wrong. Maybe the feed is poor quality, the pigs are sick, or you are overfeeding. Check for feed wastage: holes in troughs, rodents, or birds. Also, make sure pigs have clean water. Without water, they eat less and grow slower.

Good FCR saves money. If your FCR is 2.5 instead of 3.0, you save 0.5 kg of feed per kg of gain. For a 100 kg pig, that's 50 kg of feed saved. At 20,000 Naira per 50 kg, that's 20,000 Naira saved per pig. With 20 pigs, you save 400,000 Naira. So, calculate FCR every month and aim to keep it below 2.8.

3. Target FCR of 2.5

A target Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) of 2.5 is the gold standard for pig farmers. It means your pigs gain 1 kg of weight for every 2.5 kg of feed they eat. Achieving this target requires good genetics, proper nutrition, and excellent management. It is not easy, but it is possible and very profitable.

To hit FCR 2.5, start with healthy piglets from a good breed. Crossbreeds like Large White and Landrace grow faster and convert feed better than local breeds. Buy piglets from a reputable breeder. Also, feed them a balanced diet. Use a feed formula that matches their stage. For nursery pigs, feed should have 18-20% protein. For growers, 16% protein. For finishers, 14% protein. Add vitamins and minerals.

Water is critical. Pigs need clean water at all times. A lactating sow drinks up to 20 liters a day. A growing pig drinks 5-10 liters. If water is limited, feed intake drops and FCR worsens. Use nipple drinkers or clean troughs. Check water flow daily.

Health management also affects FCR. Sick pigs eat less and grow slowly. Vaccinate against common diseases like swine fever and pneumonia. Deworm regularly. Keep pens clean and dry. Avoid overcrowding. A pig needs at least 1 square meter of space in the grower stage.

Monitor FCR monthly. If it is above 2.8, find the cause. Weigh feed and pigs accurately. If you achieve 2.5, your cost per kg of pig is lower. For example, if feed is 20,000 Naira per 50 kg (400 Naira per kg), then feed cost per kg gain is 2.5 x 400 = 1,000 Naira. If FCR is 3.0, it is 1,200 Naira. That 200 Naira difference per kg adds up to 20,000 Naira for a 100 kg pig. So, aim for 2.5.

4. Buying Feed in Bulk

Buying feed in bulk is one of the easiest ways to cut costs on your pig farm. When you buy in large quantities, you get discounts, save on transport, and ensure you never run out of feed. But you must store it properly to avoid spoilage.

First, calculate your total feed need for a batch. For 20 pigs from nursery to market, you need about 4,340 kg (as calculated earlier). That is roughly 87 bags of 50 kg. If you buy one bag at a time, you might pay 22,000 Naira per bag. But if you buy 50 bags or more, you can negotiate down to 20,000 Naira per bag. That saves 2,000 Naira per bag, or 174,000 Naira for the batch.

Buy from a reputable feed mill or supplier. Check the feed quality: it should smell fresh, not moldy. Look at the production date. Feed older than 3 months loses vitamins. Buy from a supplier who delivers to your farm to save transport costs. Some mills offer credit if you are a regular customer.

Storage is key. Keep feed in a dry, cool place. Use pallets to raise bags off the floor. Stack bags no more than 10 high to prevent crushing. Keep away from walls to allow air circulation. Use airtight containers for opened bags. Add a desiccant like silica gel to prevent moisture. Check for rodents and insects. Use traps or cats.

If you mix your own feed, buy ingredients in bulk: maize, soya meal, fish meal, and premix. Maize can be stored in silos or airtight bags. Buy during harvest season when prices are low. For example, maize is cheaper in November after harvest. Store it properly and use it over 3-4 months. Bulk buying requires planning but pays off.

5. Mixing Your Own Feed

Mixing your own pig feed can save you 20-30% on feed costs. It gives you control over ingredients and quality. But you must get the formula right, or your pigs will grow slowly. Here is a simple guide to mixing feed for different stages.

You need a feed mixer or a clean concrete floor and a shovel. Weigh ingredients accurately using a scale. For nursery pigs (weaning to 10 weeks), mix: 50% maize, 25% soya meal, 15% fish meal, 5% milk powder, 3% vegetable oil, 1% minerals and vitamins, 1% salt. This gives about 20% protein. Mix well and feed fresh.

For grower pigs (10-16 weeks), use: 60% maize, 20% soya meal, 10% fish meal, 5% wheat bran, 3% minerals and vitamins, 1% salt, 1% limestone. This gives about 16% protein. For finisher pigs (16 weeks to market), use: 70% maize, 15% soya meal, 5% fish meal, 5% wheat bran, 3% minerals and vitamins, 1% salt, 1% limestone. This gives about 14% protein.

You can substitute some ingredients. Cassava can replace up to 30% of maize if dried and ground. Palm kernel cake can replace some soya but not more than 10%. Always add a premix that contains vitamins and minerals. Without it, pigs get sick.

Mix in small batches to ensure uniformity. Use a shovel to turn the pile at least three times. Store in clean bags. Make only enough for 2 weeks to prevent mold. If you see clumps or smell mold, throw it away. Moldy feed can kill pigs. Start by mixing for a few pigs, then scale up as you gain confidence.

6. The Cost of Bad Feed

Bad feed is the most expensive mistake a pig farmer can make. It can kill your pigs, slow their growth, and waste your money. Bad feed includes moldy, expired, or nutritionally deficient feed. Understanding the cost helps you avoid it.

Moldy feed contains aflatoxins, poisons produced by mold. Aflatoxins damage the liver and immune system. Pigs may eat less, grow slowly, and die. If you feed moldy maize, you might lose 10-20% of your pigs. For 20 pigs, that's 2-4 dead pigs. At 50,000 Naira per pig, that's 100,000-200,000 Naira lost. Plus, you wasted the feed money. Always check feed for mold. If it smells musty or has green spots, do not use it.

Expired feed loses vitamins and energy. Feed older than 3 months has less nutritional value. Pigs eat more but gain less. Your FCR goes up. For example, if FCR rises from 2.5 to 3.5, you need 1 extra kg of feed per kg gain. For a 100 kg pig, that's 100 kg extra feed, costing 40,000 Naira. With 20 pigs, that's 800,000 Naira wasted. Always check production dates and use feed within 3 months.

Nutritionally deficient feed lacks protein, vitamins, or minerals. This happens when you mix your own feed without a proper formula. Pigs grow slowly and get sick. You might think you are saving money, but you are losing more. For example, if you skip the premix, pigs may get rickets or anemia. Treatment costs money and time. Always use a balanced formula and add premix.

To avoid bad feed, buy from trusted suppliers, store properly, and mix accurately. Test your feed occasionally at a lab. The cost of testing is small compared to the loss from bad feed. Remember, cheap feed is often expensive in the long run.

<h2>Practical Reference: Feed and the Feed Budget</h2>

<p>
Feed is the single largest cost line in any pig enterprise typically <strong>6075% of total production cost</strong> in African small- and medium-scale operations. For a batch farrowing system selling weaners, porkers or finished pigs daily, feed discipline is the difference between a business that pays weekly bills and one that quietly bleeds cash. This practical reference gives you the numbers, the tools and the habits that turn feed from a vague expense into a controlled, budgeted input.
</p>

<p>
The goal is simple: <strong>get pigs to market weight on the least kilograms of feed, at the lowest cost per kilogram of gain, without compromising health or growth</strong>. Everything below serves that goal.
</p>

<h3>Calculating Feed Conversion Ratio</h3>

<p>
Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) is the kilogrammes of feed required to produce one kilogramme of live weight gain. It is the single most important efficiency number in your business.
</p>

<p style="background:#eef4e8;padding:12px;border-left:4px solid #4a7c2f;">
<strong>FCR = Total feed consumed (kg) Total weight gain (kg)</strong>
</p>

<p>
Example: a batch of 20 pigs consumes 4,600 kg of feed and gains 2,000 kg of live weight. FCR = 4,600 2,000 = <strong>2.3</strong>. If the same batch had consumed 5,000 kg for the same gain, FCR = 2.5 and every 0.1 of FCR on a 20-pig batch costs roughly one extra 50 kg bag of feed for nothing.
</p>

<h3>Target FCR of 2.5</h3>

<p>
A target FCR of <strong>2.5</strong> is realistic and commercially sound for smallholder and emerging commercial pig units in Africa using good-quality commercial or well-mixed home rations. Top commercial units achieve 2.22.4; poorly managed units sit at 3.03.5 or worse.
</p>

<h3>Buying Feed in Bulk</h3>

<p>
Bulk buying is the fastest way to cut your feed cost per kilogram. In most African markets, a 50 kg bag bought singly costs 1025% more than the same feed bought in 20-bag (1 tonne) lots. If you batch-farrow, you already have predictable volumes use that predictability to negotiate.
</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Group with neighbours.</strong> Form a buying group of 35 farmers and order by the tonne to unlock wholesale prices.</li>
<li><strong>Buy raw materials, not just finished feed.</strong> Maize, soya, sunflower cake and fishmeal bought at harvest are far cheaper than finished pellets.</li>
<li><strong>Store properly.</strong> Airtight drums, pallets off the floor, dry ventilated store. Weevils, rats and mould destroy 510% of stored feed in poor conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Never buy more than 46 weeks of feed</strong> unless storage is excellent vitamin potency and fat quality decline.</li>
</ul>

<h3>The Cost of Bad Feed</h3>

<p>
Bad feed is not just feed that is spoiled. It is also feed that is wrong for the stage, poorly balanced, or inconsistent. The hidden costs are brutal:
</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Higher FCR.</strong> A ration 10% short on protein can push FCR from 2.5 to 3.2 that is 70 extra kg of feed per pig.</li>
<li><strong>Slower growth.</strong> Pigs take 2030 extra days to reach market, tying up pens and delaying your next batch.</li>
<li><strong>Disease and mortality.</strong> Mycotoxins and nutrient deficiencies open the door to diarrhoea, respiratory disease and sudden death.</li>
<li><strong>Poor carcass quality.</strong> Buyers discount soft, pale or low-yielding carcasses.</li>
</ul>

<p>
On a 20-pig batch, an FCR blowout from 2.5 to 3.0 can wipe out your entire margin. Feed quality is not a place to save money it is a place to <em>invest</em> money wisely.
</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Weigh feed, don't guess.</strong> Use a hanging scale or calibrated scoop for every feed. Eyeballing overfeeds by 1015%.</li>
<li><strong>Track FCR every batch.</strong> Record total feed in and total live weight out. Review against the 2.5 target.</li>
<li><strong>Match feed to stage.</strong> Nursery, grower and finisher rations are not interchangeable.</li>
<li><strong>Buy in bulk or group-buy.</strong> Aim for tonne-level purchases to cut unit cost 1025%.</li>
<li><strong>Store dry, cool and off the floor.</strong> Use pallets, airtight containers, and first-in-first-out stock rotation.</li>
<li><strong>Test raw materials for moisture and mould</strong> before mixing. Reject anything above 13% moisture.</li>
<li><strong>Mix accurately.</strong> Weigh premix and minerals never approximate. Poor mixing causes uneven growth.</li>
<li><strong>Watch water.</strong> Pigs drink 23 litres per kg of feed. Clean, cool water is part of the feed budget.</li>
</ol>
