1. Hatching and Cross-Hatching

Hatching is the simplest way to create value with charcoal. You draw parallel lines close together to make a darker area, or spread them apart for a lighter area. Use a stick of compressed charcoal or a charcoal pencil. Hold it like a pencil for control, or like a brush for looser marks. On newsprint or cartridge paper, start with light pressure. Draw a row of lines about 2mm apart. Then draw another row with lines 5mm apart. Compare the values. The tighter lines look darker. That is hatching.

Cross-hatching adds a second set of lines at an angle, usually 90 degrees, over the first set. This builds darker values without pressing harder. For example, draw vertical lines, then horizontal lines over them. The overlap creates a grid that reads as a mid-tone. Add a third set at 45 degrees for deep shadows. In Africa, many artists use cross-hatching on papyrus or bark cloth. Try it on brown kraft paper with white charcoal for highlights. Practice on a small area: draw a 10cm x 10cm square. Fill it with hatching lines that go from light to dark by changing spacing, not pressure. Then do the same with cross-hatching. You will see that cross-hatching gives more control for gradual transitions.

Use hatching for quick sketches of market scenes, trees, or faces. Keep your lines consistent in direction. For a rounded object like a calabash, curve the lines to follow the form. That makes it look three-dimensional. Avoid smudging with your hand; rest your palm on a scrap paper. If you make a mistake, lift charcoal with a kneaded eraser. Remember: hatching is not about filling every gap. Let the paper show through for highlights. With practice, you can create a full range of values from white to black using only lines. This technique works well with both willow charcoal (soft, dusty) and compressed charcoal (hard, dark). Start with willow for light lines, then switch to compressed for darks. Keep a chamois cloth nearby to clean your hands.

2. Blending and Smudging

Blending and smudging soften charcoal marks to create smooth gradients. Use a tortillon (paper stump), a chamois cloth, a soft brush, or your finger. Start by laying down a patch of charcoal. For a light blend, use a clean tortillon and rub in small circles. For a dark blend, use a chamois or your finger. In Africa, many artists use a piece of soft leather or a clean rag. Always blend in one direction to avoid muddying. Work on textured paper like Canson Mi-Teintes or local handmade paper. Smooth paper blends too easily and can look flat.

To blend a sphere, first draw a circle. Shade the dark side with compressed charcoal. Then use a tortillon to pull the charcoal into the light side. Leave the highlight white. For a soft edge, blend outward with a clean brush. For a hard edge, do not blend. Practice on a 15cm x 20cm sheet: draw three rectangles. In the first, blend charcoal with a tortillon. In the second, blend with your finger. In the third, blend with a dry brush. Compare the textures. Tortillon gives a tight, smooth blend. Finger gives a softer, wider blend. Brush gives a grainy, atmospheric blend. Choose based on the effect you want.

Smudging is similar but uses more pressure and often a larger tool. It is good for backgrounds, shadows, and soft clouds. But do not over-blend. Too much smudging makes the drawing look tired. Keep some sharp lines for contrast. In a portrait, blend the cheeks and forehead, but leave the eyes and hair sharp. In a landscape, blend the sky but keep the tree trunks crisp. Clean your blending tools often. Charcoal builds up and can transfer unwanted dark spots. Store tortillons in a small container. If you have no tortillon, roll a tight cylinder from newspaper. It works. Also, you can use a slice of dry banana leaf to blend. Experiment with what you have locally. The goal is control, not perfection.

3. Lifting and Erasing

Lifting means removing charcoal to create highlights or lighten areas. You can use a kneaded eraser, a vinyl eraser, a hard eraser, or even a piece of bread. Kneaded eraser is best because it does not leave crumbs and can be shaped to a point. Vinyl eraser is good for sharp edges. For large areas, use a chamois or a clean cloth. In Africa, many artists use a piece of soft white bread rolled into a ball. It lifts charcoal gently without damaging the paper. Always test on a scrap first.

To lift a highlight on a dark area, press the kneaded eraser onto the charcoal and pull up. Do not rub. For a sharp highlight, shape the eraser into a wedge. For a soft highlight, use a light touch. You can also use a brush to flick away charcoal dust. For erasing mistakes, use a vinyl eraser with light pressure. If you erase too hard, you will damage the paper and cannot draw back over it. Work on sturdy paper like 200gsm cartridge or watercolor paper. If you are using newsprint, be gentle. Practice on a 10cm x 10cm square: fill it with dark charcoal. Then use a kneaded eraser to draw a white circle in the middle. Then use a vinyl eraser to draw a white line. See how the edges differ. Kneaded gives soft edges, vinyl gives hard edges.

Lifting is also used to create texture. For example, to show light hitting a rough wall, lift charcoal with a piece of sandpaper wrapped around a block. Or use a stiff brush to scrub out charcoal. For hair highlights, use a kneaded eraser to pull out thin lines. For water reflections, lift horizontal streaks. Always plan your highlights before you start. It is easier to leave paper white than to lift charcoal. But lifting gives you flexibility. Keep your erasers clean by kneading them. If they get too dirty, stretch and fold them. Store them in a plastic bag. Also, a clean eraser can be used to blend edges. Try it. You will find that lifting is as important as adding charcoal.

4. Creating Value Scales

A value scale is a strip of squares that go from white to black in even steps. It trains your eye to see and mix values. For charcoal, make a 9-step scale. Use a strip of paper 20cm long and 3cm wide. Divide it into 9 squares of 2cm each. Label them 1 (white) to 9 (black). Leave square 1 blank. Fill square 9 with compressed charcoal, pressed hard. Then fill square 5 with a mid-tone. Now fill the squares in between by blending. Use a tortillon to move charcoal from dark to light. Or use different pressures. The goal is to have each square look equally different from the next. This is called a stepped scale. In Africa, you can use this scale to match the values of local subjects: dark soil, red laterite, green leaves, brown skin. Hold the scale next to the object and find the closest square.

Make a second scale, but this time a smooth gradient. Draw a long rectangle 20cm x 3cm. Shade one end black and the other end white. Blend the middle with a chamois or brush until there are no visible steps. This is called a continuous scale. Practice both. The stepped scale helps you plan. The continuous scale helps you render. For a still life, use the stepped scale to decide which areas are dark, mid, and light. Then use the continuous scale to blend them. Keep your scale near your drawing. Check it often. Do not rely on memory. Charcoal looks darker when you are tired. A scale keeps you honest.

You can also make a value scale on dark paper. Use white charcoal for the light end and black for the dark end. This is useful for drawing on toned paper. In many African markets, you can find brown paper or cardboard. Use it. Make a scale with white, grey, and black. Then draw a simple object, like a mango, using only those three values. You will see how value creates form. Practice making a new scale every week. It takes only 10 minutes. Over time, you will be able to judge values without the scale. But even professional artists use one. So keep it in your kit. Store it flat so it does not crease. If it gets smudged, make a new one.

5. Texture Techniques

Texture in charcoal comes from how you apply and manipulate the medium. You can create rough, smooth, gritty, or soft textures. Use different tools: charcoal sticks, pencils, powder, and erasers. For rough texture, drag a compressed charcoal stick sideways across the paper. Or use the side of a willow charcoal. For fine texture, sharpen a charcoal pencil and draw small dots or short lines. For a grainy texture, sprinkle charcoal powder on the paper and rub it lightly with a chamois. Then draw over it. In Africa, you can find natural textures: sand, leaves, bark, woven baskets. Try to copy them.

To create the texture of rough bark, use a combination of hatching and lifting. Draw vertical lines with a compressed charcoal stick. Then lift some lines with a kneaded eraser. For smooth skin, blend charcoal with a tortillon and then lightly lift highlights. For fabric like kente cloth, use cross-hatching and then smudge with a brush. For grass, use quick upward strokes with a willow charcoal. For water, use horizontal smudges and then lift horizontal highlights. Always consider the direction of the texture. It should follow the form. For a round pot, curve your texture lines. For a flat wall, keep them straight.

Practice on a 20cm x 30cm sheet. Divide it into 6 squares. In each square, create a different texture: rough stone, soft fur, woven basket, smooth metal, dry earth, and water. Use only charcoal and erasers. Do not use pencils. This forces you to use the medium fully. For rough stone, press hard with compressed charcoal, then lift with a stiff brush. For soft fur, use light hatching and blend with a tortillon. For woven basket, draw a grid and then smudge alternate squares. For smooth metal, blend until no lines show, then lift a sharp highlight. For dry earth, sprinkle charcoal powder and rub with a cloth. For water, blend horizontal bands and lift wavy highlights. Compare your textures with real objects. Keep a sketchbook of textures. Label them. Over time, you will build a library of marks. Then you can combine them. For example, a portrait might have smooth skin, rough hair, and soft cloth. Texture makes your drawing believable. So practice it deliberately.

6. Practice Project: Still Life

For this project, set up a simple still life using objects you have at home. Choose three objects with different textures and values: a dark pot, a light calabash, and a textured fruit like a pineapple or a mango. Place them on a table with a plain cloth. Use a single light source, like a window or a lamp, from one side. This creates clear shadows. Tape a 30cm x 40cm sheet of cartridge paper to a board. Use a 2B charcoal pencil and a stick of compressed charcoal. Also have a kneaded eraser and a tortillon. Start by drawing the outline lightly with a charcoal pencil. Do not press hard. Check proportions by holding your pencil at arm's length. Then block in the shadows with the side of your compressed charcoal. Keep it loose. Do not blend yet.

Next, build the mid-tones. Use hatching and cross-hatching. For the dark pot, use tight cross-hatching. For the light calabash, use sparse hatching. For the pineapple, use a combination of dots and short lines to show the rough skin. Leave the highlights white. Then blend the shadows with a tortillon. Blend the calabash smoothly. Blend the pot less, to keep some texture. Use the kneaded eraser to lift highlights on the pot and the calabash. For the pineapple, lift small dots to show the shiny spots. Add a cast shadow under the objects. Make it darkest near the objects and lighter as it moves away. Use a value scale to check your darks and lights. The darkest dark should be in the shadow side of the pot. The lightest light should be the highlight on the calabash. Everything else is in between.

Finally, refine the edges. Some edges should be sharp, like the rim of the pot. Some should be soft, like the shadow on the cloth. Use a brush to soften edges. Step back from your drawing every 10 minutes. Look at it from a distance. Compare it to the real still life. Adjust values as needed. Do not overwork. Stop when it looks solid. Sign your name. This project should take 2 to 3 hours. Do it once a week for a month. Keep your first drawing to see your progress. In Africa, you can find many still life subjects: gourds, clay pots, maize, groundnuts, and fabrics. Use what you have. The goal is to apply all the techniques from this module: hatching, blending, lifting, value scales, and texture. Good luck.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Charcoal Techniques for African Charcoal Businesses

This practical reference is designed for artisans, trainers, and small-scale entrepreneurs working with charcoal in African markets. While the module title references drawing techniques such as hatching, blending, and lifting, this lesson translates those fundamental skills into the real-world production, handling, and quality control of charcoal products. Whether you are producing lump charcoal, briquettes, or activated carbon, the principles of layering, blending, and refining apply directly to your business operations.

Africa produces approximately 65% of the world's charcoal, with Nigeria, Ethiopia, Ghana, and the Democratic Republic of Congo among the largest producers. The charcoal industry supports millions of livelihoods across the continent, from producers in rural areas to traders in urban markets. Understanding the practical techniques of charcoal production and quality control can significantly increase your profit margins and market access.

Technique 1: Hatching and Cross-Hatching Layering Your Production

In drawing, hatching involves layering parallel lines to build density. In charcoal production, this translates to layering your feedstock and managing carbonization cycles. Small-scale producers in Kenya and Tanzania often use earth kilns where wood is stacked in layers, covered with soil, and ignited. The key is controlling airflow to ensure complete carbonization without excessive ash formation.

Cross-hatching overlapping lines in different directions mirrors the practice of mixing feedstock types. Combining hardwood (such as Acacia or Eucalyptus) with agricultural residues (like rice husks or coconut shells) can improve the quality and calorific value of your final product. This blended approach is increasingly common in West Africa, where coconut shell charcoal commands premium prices in urban markets.

Technique 2: Blending and Smudging Binding and Briquetting

Blending in charcoal production refers to mixing charcoal fines with a binder to create briquettes. This is one of the most profitable value-addition activities for African charcoal businesses. Common binders include cassava starch, clay, and molasses. The "smudging" concept applies to the controlled burning of binders during briquette production to ensure they carbonize properly without introducing impurities.

In Uganda and Rwanda, briquette production from charcoal dust and agricultural waste has created new revenue streams for cooperatives. A well-produced briquette can sell for 20-30% more than loose charcoal per kilogram, and it burns longer with less smoke a major selling point in urban households.

Technique 3: Lifting and Erasing Quality Control and Waste Reduction

Lifting and erasing in drawing involve removing material to refine an image. In charcoal production, this means removing impurities, fines, and oversized pieces through sieving and sorting. "Erasing" waste is critical for profitability: every kilogram of fines you can convert into briquettes instead of discarding represents additional revenue.

Use a simple sieving system with mesh sizes of 5mm, 10mm, and 25mm to grade your charcoal. Fines (below 5mm) go to briquette production. Medium pieces (5-25mm) are ideal for domestic cooking. Large pieces (above 25mm) are suitable for industrial applications and export markets.

Technique 4: Creating Value Scales Pricing and Grading

Just as artists create value scales from light to dark, charcoal producers must create clear grading scales from low to high quality. This directly impacts pricing. The table below shows typical grading standards used in African charcoal markets.

Technique 5: Texture Techniques Product Differentiation

Texture in charcoal refers to the surface characteristics of your product. Smooth, dense charcoal (from slow carbonization) burns longer and is preferred for cooking. Rough, porous charcoal (from fast carbonization) ignites quickly and is preferred for grilling. Understanding these preferences allows you to target specific customer segments.

In South Africa, the braai (barbecue) market prefers quick-igniting, porous charcoal. In Nigeria and Ghana, the domestic cooking market prefers slow-burning, dense charcoal for stews and soups. By adjusting your carbonization temperature and duration, you can produce both types and serve multiple markets.

Technique 6: Practice Project Still Life of a Charcoal Business

Your "still life" as a charcoal entrepreneur is your production site, your stockpile, and your customer base. Conduct a monthly review of your operations using the following framework:

  • Input costs: Track feedstock, labor, transport, and binder costs per tonne.
  • Conversion efficiency: Measure how many tonnes of feedstock yield one tonne of finished charcoal (typical ratio: 5:1 to 10:1).
  • Product mix: Record the percentage of each grade (A-D) you produce.
  • Revenue per grade: Calculate income from each grade to identify your most profitable lines.
  • Waste stream: Quantify fines and determine briquette conversion potential.
  • Customer feedback: Document preferences for burn time, smoke, and packaging.

SAFETY WARNING Carbon Monoxide and Burn Hazards

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a silent killer. Charcoal production in enclosed or poorly ventilated spaces produces dangerous levels of CO. Never operate kilns or burn charcoal indoors, in garages, or in any enclosed space without proper ventilation. Symptoms of CO poisoning include headache, dizziness, nausea, and confusion which can lead to unconsciousness and death within minutes.

Burn hazards: Freshly carbonized charcoal can remain hot for 24-48 hours. Always allow adequate cooling time before handling. Use heat-resistant gloves and long-handled tools. Keep water and a first-aid kit on site.

Fire risk: Charcoal dust is combustible. Store fines in sealed containers away from ignition sources. Never smoke or use open flames near charcoal storage areas.

Respiratory protection: Wear N95 masks when handling charcoal dust to prevent inhalation of fine particles, which can cause respiratory diseases including silicosis and chronic bronchitis.

Source: WHO (2021). Guidelines for Indoor Air Quality: Household Fuel Combustion. Geneva: World Health Organization.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Feedstock quality: Source dry, clean wood or agricultural residues with moisture content below 20% before carbonization.
  2. Carbonization control: Monitor airflow and temperature (400-600C) to achieve complete carbonization without excessive ash.
  3. Cooling protocol: Allow 24-48 hours of cooling before handling; never rush this step.
  4. Grading: Sieve charcoal into at least three grades (A, B, C) plus fines for briquette production.
  5. Moisture testing: Use a moisture meter or the oven-dry method to verify 10% moisture for premium grades.
  6. Briquette conversion: Convert all fines below 5mm into briquettes using cassava starch binder (5-10% by weight).
  7. Packaging: Use moisture-resistant bags with clear labeling (grade, weight, production date, contact info).
  8. Safety compliance: Ensure ventilation, wear PPE, and maintain fire safety equipment at all times.
