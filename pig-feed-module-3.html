Module 3

🐷 Growing Pig Nutrition (8-25 kg)

The phase where feed conversion is at its lifetime best and mistakes are cheapest to fix. Covers realistic growth and intake expectations, balancing protein against energy, working down the amino acid ratio list after lysine, minerals for frame and leg strength, whether enzymes and probiotics earn their cost in a home mix, and the weighing routine that turns guesswork into decisions.

  • State the calcium, phosphorus and trace mineral targets for 12 to 26 kg pigs

Lesson 3.1~12 min

Growth Rate and Feed Intake

In this lesson
  • Calculate average daily gain and feed conversion from your own pen records
  • Match diet specification to bodyweight rather than to age
  • Identify the four practical causes of low feed intake in growing pigs

Between about 8 and 25 kg a pig converts feed into bodyweight better than it ever will again. Every kilogram of gain here costs less feed than the same kilogram in the finishing house, which means a mistake in this phase is doubly expensive: you pay for it now, and you pay again because the pig reaches market later.

Three numbers describe performance and you can measure all of them with a scale and a notebook.

Average daily gain is end weight minus start weight, divided by days. Feed conversion ratio is total feed eaten divided by total weight gained. Average daily feed intake is total feed divided by pigs divided by days. That is the whole toolkit.

Match the diet to weight, not age. The published specifications for this stage are:

  1. Late nursery, 11.5 to 23 kg, typically lasting 2 to 4 weeks: SID lysine 1.08 to 1.15 percent, energy about 3,200 kcal ME/kg, with lactose minimal and specialty proteins reduced
  2. Grower, 12 to 26 kg: SID lysine 1.08 to 1.25 percent declining with bodyweight, energy 3,200 to 3,350 kcal ME/kg

Age is a poor proxy because two pens weaned on the same day can differ by 5 kg within a month. Weigh, then feed.

Feed intake is the limiting factor at this stage and it fails for four practical reasons, in the order you should check them:

  • Water. A blocked or slow nipple caps intake immediately. Module 1 lesson 6 gives you the sixty-second bucket test.
  • Feeder space and adjustment. Pigs that must queue eat less, and a flooded pan wastes feed into the dung.
  • Feed quality and freshness. Stale, rancid or mouldy feed is refused. Deoxynivalenol reduces voluntary intake from 1 to 3 ppm, well below the 5 ppm swine maximum.
  • Temperature and stocking. Pigs that are too hot eat less; pigs that are too cold eat more but convert worse because energy goes to keeping warm.

Worked example. A producer in Mutare, Zimbabwe, has a pen of 24 pigs. On 1 March he weighs them: total 216 kg, so an average of 9.0 kg. On 29 March, 28 days later, total 528 kg, average 22.0 kg. He recorded 21 bags of 50 kg going into the pen, which is 1,050 kg, with 30 kg swept out as waste and spoilage, so 1,020 kg eaten.

  • Total gain: 528 - 216 = 312 kg
  • Average daily gain: 312 kg / 24 pigs / 28 days = 0.464 kg per pig per day, that is 464 g
  • Feed conversion: 1,020 / 312 = 3.27
  • Average daily feed intake: 1,020 / 24 / 28 = 1.52 kg per pig per day

Now the diagnosis. A feed conversion of 3.27 in the 9 to 22 kg range is poor; pigs this size should be converting far better than pigs at 100 kg, not worse. He checks the four causes. Water: two of six nipples deliver a trickle. Feeder: the pan is flooded and he is sweeping 30 kg a month into the drain, which is 3 percent of everything he bought. Feed: his ration tests at 0.92 percent SID lysine against a 1.08 to 1.25 percent requirement.

He costs the fixes. Two nipples at an illustrative 2.50 USD each is 5 USD. Adjusting feeders is free. Closing the lysine gap of 0.16 percentage points needs 1.6 kg of SID lysine per tonne, which at 78 percent purity is 2.05 kg of synthetic L-lysine per tonne, costing 2.05 x 3.20 = 6.56 USD per tonne, about 0.007 USD per kg of feed.

If those three changes take his feed conversion from 3.27 to 2.70, then moving a pig from 9 to 22 kg needs 13 x 2.70 = 35.1 kg of feed instead of 13 x 3.27 = 42.5 kg. That is 7.4 kg saved per pig. At an illustrative 0.48 USD/kg, 3.55 USD per pig. Across 24 pigs per batch and 6 batches a year, 511 USD. His total outlay was 5 USD of hardware and about 7 USD per tonne of lysine. Prices are illustrative; the arithmetic is what you copy.

Protein and Energy for Growth

In this lesson
  • Calculate the protein to energy ratio of a ration in grams of SID lysine per Mcal ME
  • Explain what happens when protein and energy are out of balance in either direction
  • Adjust a home-mixed grower ration to bring its ratio into the published window

Protein and energy are not two separate decisions. They are one decision, because the pig eats to meet its energy need, and whatever amino acids arrive alongside that energy are what it has to build muscle with. Balance them and you get lean growth. Get them out of step and you either waste protein or waste growth.

The industry tool is the protein to energy ratio, defined as standardised ileal digestible lysine as a percentage of the diet, divided by energy density in Mcal ME per kg. The result is expressed as grams of SID lysine per Mcal ME. The published optimum sits between 3.0 and 3.5, varying with lean growth potential and bodyweight.

Work it through for a grower diet. Take a ration at 1.15 percent SID lysine and 3,300 kcal ME/kg.

  • Convert lysine to grams per kg: 1.15 percent of 1,000 g is 11.5 g per kg
  • Convert energy to Mcal: 3,300 kcal is 3.30 Mcal per kg
  • Ratio: 11.5 / 3.30 = 3.48 g SID lysine per Mcal ME

That sits at the top of the 3.0 to 3.5 window, which is right for a young growing pig with high lean deposition potential still ahead of it.

Now consider the two ways to get it wrong.

Too little protein for the energy supplied. The pig meets its energy target quickly, stops eating, and has insufficient lysine to build lean tissue. The surplus energy goes to fat. You pay for feed and get backfat instead of meat, and on any grading system that pays for lean you are paid less.

Too much protein for the energy supplied. The pig cannot store surplus amino acids. It strips off the nitrogen, excretes it as urea, and burns the carbon skeleton for energy. That is an expensive way to buy energy, since protein sources cost far more per unit of energy than cereals. It also raises the heat load on the animal, which matters in hot climates, and increases the nitrogen going onto your land or into your effluent pit.

Practical consequences for African home mixers:

  1. If you must cut cost, cut it from energy sources first and protect lysine. A slightly less dense diet fed to appetite loses less than an amino-acid-deficient diet.
  2. If you add fat or oil to lift energy, you must lift lysine in step or the ratio falls and the pig fattens.
  3. Cassava, maize bran and similar cheap energy sources dilute both protein and energy. Adding them without recalculating pulls the ratio in unpredictable directions.
  4. Recalculate the ratio every time an ingredient changes, not every time the season changes.

Worked example. A producer in Livingstone, Zambia, mixes a grower ration for 12 to 26 kg pigs. Maize became expensive so he replaced 15 percent of it with cassava meal, which is cheaper but lower in both protein and lysine. He recalculates.

  • Before: 1.12 percent SID lysine, 3,280 kcal ME/kg. Ratio = 11.2 / 3.28 = 3.41
  • After the cassava substitution his supplier's figures put the mix at 0.98 percent SID lysine and 3,190 kcal ME/kg. Ratio = 9.8 / 3.19 = 3.07

He is still just inside the 3.0 to 3.5 window, but his lysine at 0.98 percent is now below the 1.08 to 1.25 percent grower requirement in absolute terms, which the ratio alone would not have told him. This is the trap: the ratio is a check, not a substitute for the requirement table. He needs both.

To restore lysine to 1.10 percent he needs 0.12 percentage points, which is 1.2 kg of SID lysine per tonne, or 1.2 / 0.78 = 1.54 kg of synthetic L-lysine at 78 percent purity. At an illustrative 3.20 USD/kg that is 4.93 USD per tonne. His cassava substitution saved, illustratively, 22 USD per tonne. He keeps 17 USD of the saving and keeps his pigs growing lean. That is what a two-minute calculation is worth.

Amino Acid Ratios

In this lesson
  • Convert lysine requirements into targets for methionine, threonine and tryptophan
  • Identify which amino acid becomes limiting once lysine is corrected
  • Cost a synthetic amino acid addition per tonne of home-mixed feed

Once lysine is right, the next amino acid in line becomes the brake on growth. The ideal protein system handles this by expressing every other essential amino acid as a fixed percentage of lysine, so that a single lysine target sets the whole pattern. The ratios are remarkably stable across weight classes, which is what makes the system usable on a farm without a laboratory.

The published ratios for growing pigs, as a percentage of SID lysine:

  • Methionine: 28 to 32 percent
  • Methionine plus cysteine: 56 to 58 percent for growers
  • Threonine: 62 to 64 percent for growers
  • Tryptophan: 18 to 19 percent for growers
  • Valine: 65 to 75 percent
  • Isoleucine: 60 to 70 percent
  • Leucine: 85 to 100 percent
  • Phenylalanine: 50 to 60 percent
  • Phenylalanine plus tyrosine: 95 to 110 percent
  • Histidine: 30 to 35 percent

Younger pigs on creep and nursery specifications sit slightly wider on some of these, with methionine plus cysteine at 56 to 62 percent, threonine at 62 to 65 percent and tryptophan at 18 to 20 percent.

Converting a ratio into a working number is straightforward arithmetic. Take a grower target of 1.15 percent SID lysine, which is 11.5 g per kg of feed:

  1. Methionine plus cysteine at 57 percent: 11.5 x 0.57 = 6.56 g per kg, that is 0.656 percent of the diet
  2. Threonine at 63 percent: 11.5 x 0.63 = 7.25 g per kg, that is 0.725 percent
  3. Tryptophan at 18.5 percent: 11.5 x 0.185 = 2.13 g per kg, that is 0.213 percent
  4. Valine at 70 percent: 11.5 x 0.70 = 8.05 g per kg, that is 0.805 percent

Write those four numbers on your ration sheet next to lysine and you have covered the amino acids that limit growth in practice. In cereal and soya diets the usual order of limitation after lysine is methionine plus cysteine, then threonine, then tryptophan. In diets leaning heavily on maize and cassava with limited soybean meal, tryptophan often becomes limiting sooner than producers expect, because maize protein is notably poor in it.

Two cautions. First, synthetic amino acids are sold at different purities: L-lysine HCl is about 78 percent lysine, while DL-methionine and L-threonine products are usually declared closer to pure. Always divide your requirement by the declared purity before ordering. Second, adding synthetic amino acids allows you to cut crude protein, which saves money and reduces heat load, but only down to the point where a non-supplemented amino acid becomes limiting. Cutting protein without checking the whole ratio list is how producers turn a saving into a growth loss.

Worked example. A producer in Abeokuta, Nigeria, mixes one tonne of grower feed. His formulation software is a notebook. Analysis of his mix gives 1.15 percent SID lysine after adding synthetic lysine, but methionine plus cysteine at 0.55 percent against a target of 0.656 percent.

  • Shortfall: 0.656 - 0.55 = 0.106 percentage points, which is 1.06 g per kg, so 1.06 kg per tonne
  • DL-methionine declared at 99 percent: 1.06 / 0.99 = 1.07 kg per tonne
  • At an illustrative local price of 4.60 USD/kg: 1.07 x 4.60 = 4.92 USD per tonne
  • That is 0.005 USD per kg of feed, or on a pig eating 45 kg through the grower phase, 0.22 USD per pig

Set that against the cost of the growth it protects. If methionine deficiency was holding average daily gain back by even 25 g a day over a 30-day phase, that is 0.75 kg per pig of lost gain. At an illustrative 2.30 USD per kg liveweight, 1.73 USD per pig. The methionine costs 0.22 and protects 1.73. Ratios are not a laboratory nicety; they are the cheapest correction available to a home mixer.

Mineral Nutrition for Skeletal Development

In this lesson
  • State the calcium, phosphorus and trace mineral targets for 12 to 26 kg pigs
  • Explain how an incorrect calcium to phosphorus ratio wastes both minerals
  • Calculate the return from adding phytase to a grower ration

A growing pig lays down skeleton faster than at any later stage, and the frame it builds now has to carry 100 kg or more later. Leg weakness in finishers is often a mineral failure that happened months earlier. Minerals are also where home mixers make their most expensive unforced errors, because limestone is cheap and it is tempting to add more of it.

The published grower targets for 12 to 26 kg pigs:

  1. Total calcium: 0.70 percent of the diet
  2. Total phosphorus: 0.60 percent
  3. STTD phosphorus, the digestible fraction: 0.35 percent
  4. Calcium to phosphorus ratio: hold between 1:1 and 1.5:1, because wide ratios impair absorption

Trace minerals for this class: zinc 80 to 100 ppm, copper 5 to 6 ppm as a nutritional baseline with 15 ppm on some commercial specifications, iron 40 to 100 mg/kg, manganese 2 to 4 mg/kg, selenium 0.15 to 0.30 mg/kg, and iodine 0.14 to 0.20 mg/kg. Vitamin D3 at 150 to 220 IU/kg completes the bone-building package, because without it calcium absorption falls regardless of how much limestone is in the bag.

Two interactions decide whether the minerals you buy actually reach the bone.

The first is the calcium to phosphorus ratio. Above about 1.5:1 in growing pigs, excess calcium interferes with phosphorus absorption. So adding limestone to a diet that is already short of phosphorus does not strengthen bone; it weakens it, by locking up the phosphorus that was there. The second interaction is calcium against zinc: dietary calcium above 1.0 percent reduces zinc absorption, and zinc deficiency shows as skin problems and poor growth that look like disease.

The third piece is availability. Around 70 to 80 percent of the phosphorus in plant ingredients is bound in phytate and unavailable, which is why formulation uses STTD phosphorus. Microbial phytase at 500 to 2,500 FTU per kg releases some of it, improving available phosphorus by 0.10 to 0.20 percentage points depending on dose and substrate.

Worked example. A producer in Eldoret, Kenya, mixes 4 tonnes of grower feed a month. His current formula meets 0.35 percent STTD phosphorus using dicalcium phosphate, and he wants to test phytase.

  • Phytase at 1,000 FTU/kg, taking a conservative release of 0.12 percentage points of available phosphorus
  • 0.12 percentage points of a tonne: 0.12 / 100 x 1,000 kg = 1.2 kg of available phosphorus per tonne
  • His dicalcium phosphate supplies about 18 percent phosphorus, so he can remove 1.2 / 0.18 = 6.7 kg per tonne
  • At an illustrative 0.95 USD/kg, that saves 6.7 x 0.95 = 6.37 USD per tonne
  • Phytase at 1,000 FTU/kg costs, illustratively, 2.60 USD per tonne
  • Net saving: 6.37 - 2.60 = 3.77 USD per tonne, which over 48 tonnes a year is 181 USD

But the calculation is not finished. Removing 6.7 kg of dicalcium phosphate also removes calcium, since that product carries roughly 22 to 24 percent calcium. He must add back limestone to hold total calcium at 0.70 percent, and then recheck that the calcium to STTD phosphorus relationship has not drifted outside the 1:1 to 1.5:1 window. Producers who skip this step get the phosphorus saving and a calcium problem at the same time.

A last practical note on sources. Bone meal and rock phosphate are widely available across Africa and vary enormously in composition and contamination. If you use them, ask for an analysis, and treat any product with an unknown source as a risk to both mineral balance and food safety. A cheap mineral source with no analysis is not cheap; it is unmeasured.

Enzyme and Probiotic Supplementation

In this lesson
  • Match each enzyme class to the ingredient problem it solves
  • State realistic performance responses for probiotics and prebiotics
  • Decide whether an additive pays at your own feed price and herd performance

Additives are sold hard and evaluated poorly. The honest position from the published evidence is that most of them deliver small, real improvements, that the improvements are largest where management or ingredients are weak, and that the only way to know whether one pays on your farm is to run the arithmetic before you buy.

Enzymes first, because they have the clearest mechanism. The pig does not produce enzymes for certain plant components, so you supply them:

  1. Phytase releases phytate-bound phosphorus, improving available phosphorus by 0.10 to 0.20 percentage points at 500 to 2,500 FTU per kg. This is the one enzyme that pays almost universally in plant-based diets, because it directly replaces purchased inorganic phosphate.
  2. Non-starch polysaccharide enzymes, mainly xylanase and cellulase, break down fibre fractions in wheat bran, rice bran, brewers' grain and other fibrous by-products.
  3. Protease improves protein digestibility, most usefully in diets carrying lower-grade protein sources.

The published response for enzymes overall is a 2 to 5 percent improvement in feed conversion, and it is explicitly ingredient-dependent. An enzyme aimed at fibre in a clean maize and soybean meal diet has little substrate to work on and will do nothing. The same enzyme in a diet carrying 20 percent brewers' spent grain has plenty.

Probiotics, meaning live cultures fed directly:

  • Bacillus subtilis at 1 to 5 x 10^9 CFU per kg. Spore-forming, so it survives pelleting, improves protein and amino acid digestibility, and suppresses pathogenic bacteria.
  • Lactobacillus plantarum at 1 to 10 x 10^9 CFU per kg. Produces lactic acid and antimicrobial peptides and competes with E. coli.
  • Saccharomyces cerevisiae yeast at 0.1 to 0.5 percent of the diet. Beta-glucans in the cell wall stimulate immune response and it promotes Lactobacillus growth.

Published efficacy is a 5 to 15 percent improvement in average daily gain in challenged environments, and variable results in clean conditions. Read that carefully: the dirtier and more stressed your system, the more a probiotic is worth, which is the opposite of how they are usually sold.

Prebiotics feed the good bacteria rather than adding them. Mannan oligosaccharides at 0.1 to 0.2 percent bind pathogenic bacteria such as E. coli and Salmonella and stop them attaching to the gut wall. Fructooligosaccharides at 0.5 to 1.0 percent selectively ferment to support Lactobacillus. Published response is a modest 3 to 8 percent gain improvement, best when combined with other measures. Module 9 lesson 4 sets out how to assemble these into a full gut health package; here the question is narrower, namely whether a given product pays in the 8 to 25 kg phase.

Worked example. A producer in Blantyre, Malawi, feeds 200 growers a year through the 10 to 25 kg phase, using 30 kg of feed per pig, so 6 tonnes a year. A probiotic is offered at an illustrative 5.50 USD per kg, included at 0.5 kg per tonne.

  • Additive cost: 0.5 x 5.50 = 2.75 USD per tonne, so 16.50 USD a year
  • His current feed conversion in this phase is 2.1, so 15 kg of gain takes 31.5 kg of feed
  • Take the conservative end of the published range, a 5 percent gain improvement translating to roughly a 4 percent feed conversion improvement: FCR 2.1 becomes 2.02
  • Feed for 15 kg of gain becomes 30.3 kg, a saving of 1.2 kg per pig
  • At an illustrative 0.48 USD/kg that is 0.58 USD per pig, or 116 USD across 200 pigs

Cost 16.50, return 116. On those assumptions it pays comfortably. But note where the assumption sits: his herd is under moderate challenge with a feed conversion of 2.1, which is where the published response is real. A producer already converting at 1.7 in a clean, well-managed nursery should expect much less, and should test on half his pigs before committing the whole herd. Run the same arithmetic with your own price quote and your own measured feed conversion, and be willing to conclude that an additive is not for you.

Lesson 3.6~12 min

Monitoring Growth and Adjusting Feed

In this lesson
  • Set up a weighing and recording routine that takes under thirty minutes a month
  • Interpret a widening weight spread within a pen and act on it
  • Convert performance figures into a cost per kilogram of gain you can track over time

Everything in this module depends on measurement. A producer who does not weigh is guessing, and a guess cannot be improved. The good news is that useful monitoring needs one scale, one notebook and about half an hour a month.

Build the routine around four events:

  1. Weigh a fixed sample at the start of each phase. Ten pigs, individually, marked so you can find the same ten again. If you can weigh all of them, better, but a marked sample is far better than nothing.
  2. Record every kilogram of feed entering the pen. Count bags, and record the day the phase feed budget runs out.
  3. Weigh the same sample at the end of the phase, on the same scale, at the same time of day, ideally before feeding.
  4. Calculate three figures: average daily gain, feed conversion, and cost per kilogram of gain.

The third figure is the one that runs your business. Cost per kilogram of gain is feed conversion multiplied by feed cost per kilogram. A ration at 0.48 USD/kg with a feed conversion of 2.2 gives 0.48 x 2.2 = 1.06 USD per kilogram of gain. A cheaper ration at 0.42 USD/kg that pushes feed conversion to 2.6 gives 0.42 x 2.6 = 1.09 USD. The cheaper feed is the more expensive feed. This single calculation settles more arguments than any other in pig farming.

Pay attention to spread, not just average. Within any pen there is a heaviest pig and a lightest pig, and the gap between them tells you about competition and health. A widening spread usually means one of four things: not enough feeder space so dominant pigs eat first, not enough drinker capacity, sub-clinical disease in the tail-end pigs, or a group that was too variable when mixed. Sorting pigs by size when you form the pen removes much of this at no cost.

Decide in advance what you will do at each result. Written trigger rules stop you drifting:

  • If average daily gain falls more than 15 percent below your own previous batch, check water, feeder adjustment and feed batch before changing the formula.
  • If feed conversion worsens while daily gain holds, suspect feed wastage, spillage, rodents or spoiled feed rather than nutrition.
  • If both fall together, suspect the ration specification or a health problem, and check the feed analysis against the published requirement windows.
  • If the weight spread widens by more than about a quarter across a phase, split the pen and feed the tail-end pigs the previous, richer phase diet.

Worked example. A producer in Kitale, Kenya, runs two pens of 20 pigs each through the 10 to 25 kg phase and uses them to compare a formula change. Pen A keeps the existing ration; Pen B gets the same ration with synthetic lysine added to lift SID lysine from 0.98 to 1.12 percent, at an extra 5.75 USD per tonne.

Pen A, 35 days: start average 10.2 kg, end average 22.5 kg, feed used 700 kg.

  • Gain per pig: 12.3 kg. Total gain: 246 kg. ADG: 12.3 / 35 = 0.351 kg
  • FCR: 700 / 246 = 2.85
  • Feed cost 0.46 USD/kg. Cost per kg gain: 0.46 x 2.85 = 1.31 USD

Pen B, 35 days: start average 10.4 kg, end average 24.1 kg, feed used 712 kg.

  • Gain per pig: 13.7 kg. Total gain: 274 kg. ADG: 13.7 / 35 = 0.391 kg
  • FCR: 712 / 274 = 2.60
  • Feed cost 0.46 + 0.006 = 0.466 USD/kg. Cost per kg gain: 0.466 x 2.60 = 1.21 USD

Pen B produced 28 kg more gain from 12 kg more feed and cut cost per kilogram of gain by 0.10 USD. Across 400 pigs a year each gaining 15 kg in this phase, that is 400 x 15 x 0.10 = 600 USD. The trial cost him nothing but the discipline of weighing. That is the whole argument for monitoring: it converts an opinion about lysine into 600 USD you can bank, or it tells you honestly that the change did not work and saves you from repeating it.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A pen of 24 pigs gains 312 kg in 28 days. What is the average daily gain per pig?

312 kg divided by 24 pigs divided by 28 days equals 0.464 kg, or 464 g per pig per day.

2. Why should diet phase be chosen by bodyweight rather than by age?

Published requirements are set against bodyweight ranges. Groups diverge in weight rapidly, so feeding by age misfeeds both the fast and the slow pigs.

3. Which should you check first when growing pigs eat less than expected?

Water restriction cuts feed intake immediately and is the cheapest fault to find and repair, so it comes before any ration change.

4. A pen eats 1,020 kg of feed and gains 312 kg. What is the feed conversion ratio?

Feed conversion is feed divided by gain: 1,020 divided by 312 equals 3.27, which is poor for pigs in the 9 to 22 kg range.

5. Why is a feeding error at 8 to 25 kg more expensive than the same error at 90 kg?

Gain is cheapest to buy in this phase, so lost gain here is lost at the best exchange rate on the farm, and the pig also occupies the pen longer.

6. How is the protein to energy ratio calculated?

The ratio is grams of SID lysine per Mcal of metabolisable energy, and the published optimum is 3.0 to 3.5.

7. A diet contains 1.15 percent SID lysine and 3,300 kcal ME/kg. What is its protein to energy ratio?

11.5 g of lysine per kg divided by 3.30 Mcal per kg gives 3.48 g SID lysine per Mcal ME.

8. What happens when a diet supplies plenty of energy but too little lysine?

Without enough lysine the pig cannot build lean tissue, so surplus energy is deposited as fat, reducing carcass value on any lean-based grading system.

9. Why is excess dietary protein an expensive way to supply energy?

Surplus amino acids are deaminated and burned, wasting the price premium paid for protein and increasing nitrogen excretion and heat load.

10. After substituting cheap cassava for maize, why must a producer check both the ratio and the absolute lysine level?

Diluting both protein and energy can leave the ratio inside the window while lysine drops below the 1.08 percent grower floor, which the ratio alone would hide.

11. A grower diet targets 1.15 percent SID lysine. What is the threonine target at 63 percent of lysine?

11.5 g of lysine per kg multiplied by 0.63 gives 7.25 g per kg, which is 0.725 percent of the diet.

12. Why is every amino acid expressed as a percentage of lysine?

The ideal protein system fixes the pattern relative to lysine, so once lysine is set the other targets follow by simple arithmetic.

13. In maize and cassava heavy diets with limited soybean meal, which amino acid often becomes limiting sooner than producers expect?

Maize protein is notably poor in tryptophan, so diets built heavily on maize can run short of it once lysine has been corrected.

14. L-lysine HCl is about 78 percent lysine. To supply 2.5 kg of lysine per tonne, how much product is needed?

2.5 divided by 0.78 gives 3.2 kg of product per tonne. Ignoring purity under-doses by roughly a fifth.

15. What is the risk of cutting crude protein while adding synthetic amino acids?

Protein reduction only works down to the point where the next unsupplemented amino acid runs short, which is why the whole ratio list must be checked.

16. What is the published STTD phosphorus requirement for a 12 to 26 kg grower?

STTD phosphorus is 0.35 percent for growers, while total phosphorus is 0.60 percent. The two are not interchangeable.

17. What happens when the calcium to phosphorus ratio goes above about 1.5 to 1 in growing pigs?

Wide ratios interfere with phosphorus absorption, so adding limestone to a phosphorus-short diet makes the bone problem worse, not better.

18. Dietary calcium above 1.0 percent reduces the absorption of which trace mineral?

High calcium depresses zinc absorption, producing skin and growth problems that can be mistaken for disease.

19. Removing dicalcium phosphate after adding phytase creates which secondary problem?

Dicalcium phosphate carries roughly 22 to 24 percent calcium, so limestone must be added back and the ratio rechecked after a phytase change.

20. What is the published manganese requirement for growing pigs?

Growing pigs absorb manganese efficiently and need only 2 to 4 mg/kg, unlike sows and boars which require 25 mg/kg.

21. Which enzyme pays most reliably in plant-based pig diets?

Phytase directly replaces purchased inorganic phosphate by releasing phytate-bound phosphorus, so its return is easy to calculate and usually positive.

22. When are non-starch polysaccharide enzymes such as xylanase least likely to help?

Enzyme response is ingredient-dependent. With little fibre substrate present there is nothing for the enzyme to act on.

23. Why does Bacillus subtilis survive feed pelleting better than many other probiotics?

Spore-forming organisms tolerate the heat and pressure of pelleting, which is why Bacillus species are common in pelleted feeds.

24. The published probiotic response of 5 to 15 percent improvement in daily gain applies mainly to which conditions?

Results are strongest under challenge and variable in clean conditions, so a well-managed unit should expect less and test before committing.

25. What do mannan oligosaccharides do in the gut?

MOS at 0.1 to 0.2 percent binds pathogens and prevents adherence, an approach described as competitive colonisation prevention.

26. How is cost per kilogram of gain calculated?

Multiplying feed conversion by feed price per kilogram converts both variables into one comparable figure, which is what actually drives profit.

27. Ration A costs 0.48 USD/kg at FCR 2.2. Ration B costs 0.42 USD/kg at FCR 2.6. Which is cheaper per kilogram of gain?

A gives 0.48 x 2.2 = 1.06 USD and B gives 0.42 x 2.6 = 1.09 USD, so the more expensive feed is the cheaper feed per kilogram of gain.

28. Feed conversion worsens while daily gain stays the same. What is the most likely cause?

If pigs are still gaining normally but more feed is disappearing, the feed is going somewhere other than into pigs.

29. What does a rapidly widening weight spread within a pen usually indicate?

Spread reflects unequal access and unequal health. Sorting pigs by size at mixing and adding feeder space removes much of it at low cost.

30. Why should the same marked pigs be weighed at both the start and end of a phase?

Comparing the same individuals removes sampling error, so the change you measure is real growth rather than a different selection of pigs.

Module 3 capstone

Run a 28-day Growth Trial on your own growing pigs and turn it into a feeding decision. Step 1: pick one pen of at least 10 pigs between 8 and 25 kg, weigh every pig individually on day 0, and record each weight with an ear tag or paint mark so you can match pigs at the end. Step 2: weigh every kilogram of feed that enters that pen for 28 days and record any feed removed or wasted. Step 3: weigh all pigs again on day 28 and calculate average daily gain per pig, group feed conversion, and the spread between your heaviest and lightest pig. Step 4: write out your ration with local ingredient prices, calculate cost per kg of feed and cost per kg of gain, and compare your ration's SID lysine and energy against the published grower windows in this module. Step 5: price two specific changes, such as closing a lysine gap with synthetic lysine or adding phytase, using quotes from your own agro-dealer. Step 6: state which change you will make, the expected effect on cost per kilogram of gain, and the date you will repeat the trial to check whether it worked.
