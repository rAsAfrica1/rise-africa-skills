1. Classification of Burns: First, Second, Third Degree

Burns are classified by how deep they damage the skin. First-degree burns affect only the outer layer (epidermis). The skin is red, painful, and dry, like a mild sunburn. Second-degree burns go deeper into the dermis, causing blisters, severe pain, and a wet, shiny appearance. Third-degree burns destroy all skin layers and may damage nerves, fat, muscle, or bone. The skin may be white, brown, charred, or leathery, and the area may be painless because nerves are destroyed. In Africa, common causes include open fires for cooking, hot water, paraffin stoves, and workplace accidents. To estimate burn size, use the palm of the victim's hand (including fingers) as about 1% of their total body surface area (TBSA). For example, if a burn covers 5 palms, it is roughly 5% TBSA. Seek emergency care if a burn is larger than 3% TBSA (about 3 palms) in children, or larger than 10% in adults, or if it involves the face, hands, feet, genitals, or major joints. Also get help for any third-degree burn, electrical burn, chemical burn, or if the victim has trouble breathing. Remember: never apply butter, oil, toothpaste, or mud to a burnthese trap heat and cause infection. Cool the burn with clean, cool running water for at least 20 minutes as soon as possible. Remove jewelry or tight clothing before swelling starts. Cover the burn loosely with a clean, dry cloth or sterile non-stick dressing. Do not break blisters. For third-degree burns, do not cool with water if the person is shivering or if the burn is largekeep them warm and get urgent help. In rural areas, use boiled and cooled water if clean tap water is not available. Always wash your hands before touching a burn.

2. Cooling and Dressing Burns

Cooling a burn quickly reduces pain, stops the burning process, and limits damage. Use clean, cool (not ice-cold) running water for at least 20 minutes. If you have no running water, use a clean container to pour water gently over the burn. Do not use ice, iced water, or freezing compressesthey can cause further injury. In many African communities, people may use traditional remedies like honey, aloe vera, or papaya. While honey has some antibacterial properties, it is not a substitute for proper cooling. Only apply honey or aloe after cooling if you have no other dressing and the burn is minor (first-degree or small second-degree). For dressing, use sterile non-stick gauze or a clean, lint-free cloth. If the burn is oozing, apply a sterile dressing and change it daily. Do not wrap tightlyswelling can cut off blood flow. For second-degree burns with blisters, leave blisters intact. If a blister breaks, clean gently with mild soap and water, apply an antibiotic ointment if available, and cover with a sterile dressing. For pain, give paracetamol (acetaminophen) at 15 mg per kg of body weight every 6 hours for children, or 500-1000 mg every 6 hours for adults. Avoid aspirin in children. Keep the person hydratedgive small sips of water or oral rehydration solution (ORS) if they are alert. Watch for signs of infection: increased pain, redness, swelling, pus, or fever. If these occur, seek medical help. In remote areas, train community health workers to recognize serious burns and arrange transport to a clinic. Always document the time of burn, cause, and size. For chemical burns, brush off dry chemicals first, then flush with water for 20 minutes. For electrical burns, ensure the power source is off before touching the victim.

3. Chemical and Electrical Burns

Chemical burns happen when strong acids or bases touch the skin, eyes, or are swallowed. Common sources in Africa include battery acid, bleach, cement, and agricultural chemicals. First, protect yourselfwear gloves and avoid touching the chemical. If the chemical is dry, brush it off with a clean cloth before flushing. Then flush the area with large amounts of clean, cool water for at least 20 minutes. For eye burns, flush from the inner corner outward, holding the eyelid open. Remove contact lenses if present. Do not use neutralizing agentsthey can cause heat and more damage. For electrical burns, the danger is not just the burn site but the effect on the heart and breathing. Never touch the victim until the power source is turned off. If you cannot turn off the power, use a dry, non-conductive object (like a wooden broom handle) to move the wire away. Once safe, check for breathing and pulse. If not breathing, start CPR. Electrical burns often have entry and exit woundslook for both. The visible burn may be small, but internal damage can be severe. Seek emergency care for all electrical burns, even if the person feels fine, because heart rhythm problems can develop hours later. For chemical burns, note the chemical name if possible and bring it to the clinic. If the chemical is in the eyes, flush continuously during transport. Do not apply ointments to chemical burns before flushing. After flushing, cover with a clean, dry dressing. Give pain relief as needed. In workplaces, ensure chemicals are stored safely and workers wear protective gear. For electrical safety, use circuit breakers and avoid overloaded sockets. Teach children not to play with wires or electrical appliances.

4. Fractures: Immobilization and Splinting

A fracture is a broken bone. It can be closed (skin intact) or open (bone breaks through skin). Signs include severe pain, swelling, deformity, inability to move the limb, and sometimes a grating sound. For any suspected fracture, do not try to straighten the bone. Instead, immobilize the area in the position found. Use a splint made from rigid materials like wood, cardboard, or rolled-up newspaper. Pad the splint with cloth to prevent pressure sores. Splint from the joint above the fracture to the joint below. For example, a forearm fracture needs a splint from the elbow to the wrist. Secure the splint with bandages, cloth strips, or tapenot too tight. Check circulation every 10 minutes: press a fingernail or toenail until it turns white, then release; it should turn pink within 2 seconds. If it stays white or blue, loosen the bandage. For an open fracture, cover the wound with a clean, sterile dressing before splinting. Do not push the bone back in. Control bleeding with direct pressure around the wound, not on the bone. For a leg fracture, you can tie the injured leg to the good leg using cloth or a belt as a natural splint. For a arm fracture, use a sling made from a triangular bandage or a piece of cloth to support the arm against the chest. Give pain relief (paracetamol or ibuprofen) if the person is alert and not allergic. Do not give food or drink in case surgery is needed. In Africa, motorcycle accidents and falls from trees are common causes. Always seek medical help for any fracture. If transport is delayed, keep the person warm and monitor breathing. For suspected spine fracture, do not move the person unless there is immediate danger. If you must move them, keep the head, neck, and back aligned and use a rigid board or door as a stretcher.

5. Sprains, Strains, and Dislocations

A sprain is a stretched or torn ligament (connects bone to bone). A strain is a stretched or torn muscle or tendon (connects muscle to bone). A dislocation is when a bone is forced out of its joint. Common causes include sports injuries, falls, and lifting heavy objects. Signs of a sprain or strain: pain, swelling, bruising, and limited movement. For a dislocation, the joint looks deformed, and the person cannot move it. For sprains and strains, use the R.I.C.E. method: Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation. Rest the injured areado not use it for 24-48 hours. Apply ice wrapped in a cloth for 15-20 minutes every 2-3 hours for the first 48 hours. If no ice, use a cold pack or cold water. Compress with an elastic bandage, but not too tight. Elevate the injured limb above heart level if possible. After 48 hours, switch to gentle warmth and movement. For pain, give paracetamol or ibuprofen. Do not give aspirin to children. For dislocations, do not try to put the bone back in placeyou can damage nerves and blood vessels. Immobilize the joint in the position found and seek medical help. If a dislocation is in the shoulder, you can support the arm with a sling. For finger dislocations, do not pull. In Africa, traditional bone setters may be consulted, but improper manipulation can cause permanent damage. Encourage people to go to a clinic or hospital for X-rays. For minor sprains, teach home care: rest, ice, compression, elevation, and gradual return to activity. Use a walking stick or crutches if needed. Watch for signs of poor circulation: cold, pale, or blue skin, numbness, or tingling. If these occur, loosen the bandage and seek help. Prevention: warm up before exercise, wear proper footwear, and use safe lifting techniques.

6. Head, Neck, and Spinal Injury Precautions

Head, neck, and spinal injuries can be life-threatening. They often result from falls, road traffic accidents, diving into shallow water, or violence. Signs include loss of consciousness, confusion, severe headache, vomiting, seizures, clear fluid or blood from the nose or ears, weakness or numbness in limbs, and neck or back pain. If you suspect a spinal injury, do not move the person unless they are in immediate danger (e.g., fire, traffic). Keep the head, neck, and spine aligned. If you must move them, use the log-roll technique: with at least 2-3 helpers, roll the person onto a rigid board (like a door or wooden plank) while keeping the head, neck, and back straight. Do not twist or bend the neck. For a head injury, check for breathing and bleeding. Control bleeding with direct pressure using a clean cloth, but do not press on a depressed skull fracture. If the person is unconscious but breathing, place them in the recovery position (on their side) only if you do not suspect spinal injury. If spinal injury is suspected, keep them flat and still. Monitor breathing every 5 minutes. If breathing stops, start CPR. Do not give food or drink. Do not remove a helmet unless you need to clear the airway and have been trained. In Africa, motorcycle helmets are often not worn, increasing risk. Always call for emergency help. If transport is delayed, keep the person warm and calm. For minor head bumps without loss of consciousness, observe for 24 hours for any new symptoms. For any suspected spinal injury, avoid moving the person. Use a stiff collar or rolled towels on both sides of the head to prevent movement. Teach community members: never lift a suspected spinal injury victim by the arms and legs. Always support the head and neck.

<h2>Practical Reference: Burns, Fractures, and Soft Tissue Injuries</h2>

<p>In African marketplaces, agro-processing units, transport depots, construction sites, and community halls, injuries happen fast. A scalded hand at a roadside food stall, a fractured ankle on a farm, a chemical splash at a small manufacturing workshop, or a spinal injury after a motorcycle collision each demands immediate, correct action. For a business owner or community responder, the first ten minutes can determine whether an employee returns to work in days or faces permanent disability. This practical reference consolidates the essential actions for burns, fractures, and soft tissue injuries in a business-first, Africa-relevant format.</p>

<p>Burns are classified by depth and extent. First-degree burns affect only the epidermis red, dry, painful, like sunburn. Second-degree burns extend into the dermis blistered, wet, intensely painful. Third-degree burns destroy all skin layers and may involve fat, muscle, or bone the skin appears white, charred, or leathery, and paradoxically may be painless because nerve endings are destroyed. In African business settings, the most common causes are scalds from cooking oil or water, flame burns from open fires or gas explosions, chemical burns from agro-chemicals or cleaning agents, and electrical burns from faulty wiring or generators.</p>

<h3>Cooling and Dressing Burns</h3>

<p>Cooling is the single most effective first aid action for thermal burns. Use clean, cool running water for 20 minutes not ice, not butter, not toothpaste, not engine oil. These traditional remedies trap heat, introduce infection, and complicate assessment. In many African communities, applying oil or mud to burns is common; this practice must be actively discouraged in workplace first aid training. After cooling, cover the burn loosely with sterile non-adherent dressing or clean plastic wrap. Do not burst blisters. Do not remove clothing stuck to the burn.</p>

<h3>Chemical and Electrical Burns</h3>

<p>Chemical burns require immediate and prolonged irrigation. Dry chemicals should be brushed off the skin before flushing to avoid activating the chemical with water. Alkalis (e.g., cement, lime, caustic soda) penetrate deeper than acids and require longer flushing at least 20 minutes, often 3060 minutes. In African agro-processing, cement burns are common among construction workers; alkali burns among soap-makers and food processors. Always remove contaminated clothing and jewellery while flushing. Electrical burns are deceptive: the visible wound may be small, but current travels through the body, causing cardiac arrhythmias, muscle damage, and kidney injury. Never touch a casualty still in contact with a live electrical source. Switch off the power first, or use a dry non-conductive object to separate them.</p>

<h3>Fractures: Immobilization and Splinting</h3>

<p>A fracture is a break in bone continuity. In African business and community settings, fractures commonly result from falls, motorcycle collisions (boda boda), agricultural machinery, and workplace accidents. The first aid priority is immobilization preventing movement of the broken ends to reduce pain, bleeding, and nerve/vessel damage. Do not attempt to straighten a deformed limb. Do not push protruding bone back. Splint in the position found. Use whatever is available: wooden planks, cardboard, rolled newspaper, or a broom handle. Pad the splint with cloth to prevent pressure sores. Immobilize the joint above and below the fracture. Check circulation (colour, warmth, pulse, capillary refill) before and after splinting.</p>

<h3>Sprains, Strains, and Dislocations</h3>

<p>Sprains (ligament injury) and strains (muscle/tendon injury) are common in manual labour, farming, and sports. The RICE protocol Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation remains the standard for the first 48 hours. Ice should be wrapped in cloth, never applied directly. Compression with an elastic bandage should be snug but not restrict circulation. Dislocations where a bone is displaced from its joint should never be reduced by a first aider. Immobilize in the position found, apply ice, and refer urgently. Attempting to "pop" a shoulder or finger back into place can cause permanent nerve and vessel damage. In African contexts where referral may be delayed by distance or transport cost, proper immobilization and pain control become even more critical.</p>

<h3>Head, Neck, and Spinal Injury Precautions</h3>

<p>Any injury involving a fall from height, motorcycle collision, or heavy object impact should be treated as a potential spinal injury until proven otherwise. The golden rule: do not move the casualty unless there is immediate danger (fire, traffic, structural collapse) or the airway is compromised. If movement is essential, use a log-roll technique with at least three trained helpers, keeping the head, neck, and spine aligned. Support the head in a neutral position with hands or rolled clothing on both sides. Monitor breathing continuously. For head injuries, watch for deteriorating consciousness, unequal pupils, repeated vomiting, or clear fluid from nose/ears all signs requiring urgent referral.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Scene safety first:</strong> Switch off power for electrical burns; move away from traffic, fire, or chemical spill before approaching.</li>
<li><strong>Cool thermal burns:</strong> 20 minutes of clean running water no ice, oil, butter, toothpaste, or mud.</li>
<li><strong>Chemical burns:</strong> Brush off dry chemical, flush 20+ minutes, remove contaminated clothing, bring MSDS/label to facility.</li>
<li><strong>Fractures:</strong> Immobilize in position found, splint joint above and below, check circulation before and after.</li>
<li><strong>Sprains/strains:</strong> RICE Rest, Ice (wrapped), Compression, Elevation for 48 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Dislocations:</strong> Never reduce yourself immobilize and refer urgently.</li>
<li><strong>Spinal precautions:</strong> Do not move unless in danger; log-roll with helpers; support head in neutral.</li>
<li><strong>Referral:</strong> Third-degree burns, open fractures, dislocations, electrical burns, and any head/neck/spinal injury require emergency referral.</li>
</ol>
