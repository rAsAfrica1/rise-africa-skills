Lessons

1. Press Brake Operations

A press brake is a machine that bends metal sheets by pressing them between a punch and a die. In Africa, many small workshops use manual press brakes, but CNC press brakes are becoming more common. Before you start, check the machine's tonnage. For example, bending a 2 mm mild steel sheet 1 meter long requires about 20 tons. Always wear gloves and safety glasses. Set the back gauge to the bend line. The back gauge is a stop that positions the sheet. For a 90-degree bend, use a 88-degree punch and die set to allow for springback. The die opening should be 8 times the sheet thickness. So for 2 mm sheet, use a 16 mm die. Clamp the sheet firmly. Step on the pedal to bring the punch down. The bend happens quickly. Release the pedal and remove the part. For multiple bends, use a stop or a fixture to keep consistency. In hot climates, metal can expand; work in the morning when it's cooler. Always check the first piece with a square and protractor. Adjust the ram if needed. Common mistakes include using the wrong die, not accounting for springback, and bending against the grain. Practice on scrap metal first. Keep your hands clear of the punch area. Never reach into the machine while it's running. Regular maintenance includes checking hydraulic fluid and lubricating the guides. With practice, you can bend complex shapes like boxes and channels. Remember, safety first: never operate a press brake alone, and always have a foot pedal guard.

2. Roll Bending and Plate Rolling

Roll bending, also called plate rolling, shapes metal into curves using a machine with three or four rolls. This is how tanks, pipes, and curved roofs are made. In Africa, many workshops use manual slip rolls for small jobs and hydraulic rolls for thicker plates. The process: feed the plate between the rolls, adjust the top roll down to apply pressure, and turn the rolls to bend the plate. For a cylinder, you need to roll the plate in steps. Start with a slight bend, then increase pressure gradually. Otherwise, the plate may slip or crack. The minimum bending radius depends on thickness. For mild steel, the inside radius should be at least 1.5 times the thickness. So a 6 mm plate needs a minimum radius of 9 mm. But for practical rolling, you often need a larger radius. Use a template to check the curve. For a full cylinder, roll the plate until the ends meet, then weld the seam. If the plate is thick, you may need to pre-bend the edges with a press brake to avoid flat spots. Always keep the rolls clean and lubricated. In dusty environments, cover the machine when not in use. Common defects include ovality, where the cylinder is not perfectly round, and cone shape, where one end is larger. To fix, adjust the roll pressure and re-roll. For large tanks, you may need a plate rolling machine with a capacity of 10 mm or more. Safety: never put your hands near the rolls while they are turning. Use a remote control or foot pedal. Wear tight-fitting clothes. After rolling, stress-relieve the plate if it will be used in critical applications. Practice with thin sheets first.

3. Pipe and Tube Bending

Pipe and tube bending is used for handrails, exhaust systems, and structural frames. There are two main methods: hot bending and cold bending. Cold bending is done with a pipe bender or a mandrel bender. For small pipes up to 2 inches, a manual pipe bender works. You place the pipe in the bender, align the mark, and pull the handle. The bender has a die that determines the radius. For a 1-inch pipe, a typical bend radius is 4 inches. Always use a pipe that is free of rust and cracks. Measure and mark the bend line with a soapstone or marker. For accurate bends, use a protractor or angle finder. Hot bending involves heating the pipe with a torch until it is cherry red, then bending it around a form. This is common in rural areas where electricity is limited. Heat the area evenly, then bend slowly. Do not overheat, as it can weaken the metal. After bending, let it cool slowly. For thin-walled tubes, use a mandrel inside to prevent collapsing. In Africa, many workshops use sand to fill the pipe before bending to prevent kinking. Pack dry sand tightly, plug the ends, then bend. This is an old but effective method. Always wear gloves and face shield when hot bending. Common defects include flattening, wrinkling, and cracking. To avoid, use the correct die and bend slowly. For large pipes, use a hydraulic pipe bender. After bending, check the angle with a square. If the bend is not correct, you can heat and straighten, but this weakens the pipe. Practice on scrap pipes first. Keep your work area clear.

4. Hot and Cold Forming

Hot forming and cold forming are two ways to shape steel. Cold forming is done at room temperature. It includes bending, rolling, and pressing. It is faster and gives a better surface finish, but it requires more force and can cause cracking in thick or brittle steel. Hot forming involves heating the steel to a high temperature, usually between 800C and 1200C, making it easier to shape. In Africa, blacksmiths have used hot forming for centuries. For example, to make a hoe, heat a piece of mild steel in a forge until it is orange-yellow, then hammer it into shape on an anvil. The steel becomes soft and easy to bend. After shaping, cool it slowly in air or ash to prevent hardening. Cold forming is used in modern workshops with press brakes and roll benders. For cold forming, the steel must be ductile. Mild steel is good; high-carbon steel may crack. If you need to cold bend a thick plate, you may need to anneal it first by heating and slow cooling. Hot forming allows you to bend thicker sections with less force. But it causes scale to form on the surface, which must be removed by grinding or pickling. Also, hot forming can change the metal's properties, so you may need to heat-treat afterwards. In rural areas, charcoal or wood fires are used for heating. Use tongs and a hammer. Always wear leather gloves and a face shield. For cold forming, use lubricant on the dies to reduce friction. Common defects in hot forming include burning the steel (if overheated) and cracking if cooled too fast. In cold forming, springback and cracking are common. Choose the method based on the material, thickness, and available tools. Practice both to understand how steel behaves.

5. Springback and Compensation

Springback is the tendency of metal to return partially to its original shape after bending. This happens because steel is elastic. When you bend a sheet to 90 degrees, it may spring back to 92 or 93 degrees. To get an accurate bend, you must compensate for springback. The amount of springback depends on the material, thickness, and bend radius. For mild steel, springback is about 1 to 2 degrees. For stainless steel, it can be 3 to 5 degrees or more. To compensate, you can overbend. For example, if you need a 90-degree bend and springback is 2 degrees, set the press brake to bend to 88 degrees. The metal will spring back to 90. Another method is to use a bottoming die, where the punch forces the metal into the die, reducing springback. For roll bending, springback means the rolled cylinder will open up slightly. To compensate, roll the plate a little tighter than needed. For pipe bending, springback causes the bend angle to be less than desired. You can overbend by a few degrees. A simple way to test springback is to bend a sample piece and measure the angle after release. Do this for each material and thickness. In Africa, where material may vary, always test. Use a protractor or angle gauge. For critical bends, you can use a hydraulic press with a gauge to control the depth. Remember, springback increases with higher strength steel. If you are bending aluminum, springback is less. Practice on scrap. Keep a record of springback for common jobs. This saves time and material. Never assume; always measure. With experience, you can predict springback and adjust your machine settings. This is a key skill for any fabricator.

6. Forming Defects and Remedies

Forming defects can ruin your work and waste material. Common defects include cracking, wrinkling, springback, and distortion. Cracking happens when the metal is bent too sharply or when it is brittle. To fix, increase the bend radius or anneal the metal first. For example, if a 6 mm mild steel plate cracks at a 1 mm radius, try a 6 mm radius. Wrinkling occurs in pipe bending when the inner curve compresses. To prevent, use a mandrel or fill the pipe with sand. For plate rolling, wrinkling can happen if the rolls are not parallel. Adjust the rolls and re-roll. Springback we discussed earlier; compensate by overbending. Distortion is when the part twists or warps after bending. This can happen due to uneven heating or cooling in hot forming, or due to stress in cold forming. To remedy, stress-relieve the part by heating it to 600C and cooling slowly. Another defect is orange peel, where the surface becomes rough like an orange. This happens when bending a coarse-grained material. Use a finer-grained material or anneal. In Africa, where materials may be recycled or of unknown quality, defects are common. Always test a sample before production. Use the right tool for the job. Keep your dies and rolls clean and lubricated. If a defect occurs, don't hide it; analyze the cause. Check the material, the machine settings, and the technique. Sometimes, a simple adjustment like slowing down the bend speed can help. For cracking, you can also grind a small radius on the bend line. Remember, prevention is better than cure. Train your workers to recognize early signs of defects. Maintain your machines regularly. With practice, you can minimize defects and produce quality work.

<h2>Practical Reference: Forming and Bending</h2>

<p>In the African steel fabrication sector, forming and bending are where raw plate, pipe, and structural sections become sellable products: water tanks, gates, truck bodies, agro-processing frames, trusses, and pressure vessels. For a small or growing workshop in Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, or Johannesburg, the difference between profit and loss often sits in a few millimetres of bend allowance, a worn die, or a stretched pipe wall. This practical reference condenses the field knowledge you need to quote, set up, and deliver formed steelwork that passes inspection and pays the bills.</p>

<p>Treat this as a shop-floor companion to the other lessons in this module. It assumes you already understand the basic principles of press brake operations, roll bending, pipe and tube bending, hot and cold forming, springback, and defect control. Here we focus on the numbers, tables, and checks that make those principles usable under real African site conditions intermittent power, variable material supply, and clients who pay on delivery.</p>

<h3>Why the Numbers Matter in an African Workshop</h3>
<p>Most forming failures in the region are not caused by exotic metallurgy. They come from three things: using the wrong die opening, ignoring springback, and buying steel of inconsistent thickness or yield. When you buy from a local merchant, the plate may be under-thickness by 510% and the yield strength may be higher than the nominal grade because it was re-rolled from scrap. Both push your bend radius and springback outside the drawing tolerance. A business-first approach means you build allowances into your quote and your setup, not into your rework budget.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Hot forming above 400 C releases fumes and creates severe burn risk. In many African workshops, ventilation is poor and power is intermittent, so operators may be tempted to work with inadequate extraction or to reach into the press brake to reposition hot plate. Never do this. Use tongs, heat-resistant gloves, and a face shield. Isolate the press brake before any die change or hand entry. Ensure the light curtain or two-hand control is functional. If you are heating with a torch or furnace, keep a fire extinguisher within 3 metres and never heat galvanised or coated steel without forced ventilation zinc and paint fumes are toxic.</p>

<h3>Roll Bending and Plate Rolling</h3>
<p>For cylindrical work water tanks, silos, ducting, and pipe shells roll bending is the workhorse. The key variables are roll diameter, plate thickness, and the number of passes. A common mistake in small African shops is trying to roll a full cylinder in one pass. This overloads the machine and produces flat spots at the leading and trailing edges. Instead, pre-bend the edges on a press brake or with a edge-bending attachment, then roll in multiple passes with decreasing radius. For mild steel, a good rule is: first pass at 1.5 final radius, second at 1.2, third at final radius, then a final pass with no adjustment to relax springback.</p>

<p>Plate rolling capacity is usually quoted as a maximum thickness for a given roll diameter and width. If you buy a used machine from a dealer in Mombasa or Durban, insist on a test roll with your own plate. The nameplate may say 6 mm 2000 mm, but worn rolls and a tired hydraulic system can reduce that by 30%.</p>

<h3>Pipe and Tube Bending</h3>
<p>Pipe bending is a different animal from plate rolling because wall thinning and ovality become critical. For structural pipe (e.g., handrails, gates, trusses), a rotary draw bender with a mandrel is preferred. Without a mandrel, the inside of the bend wrinkles and the outside thins. For a 90 bend, keep the centreline radius (CLR) at least 3 the outside diameter (OD) for mild steel, and 5 OD for stainless. For exhaust and fluid lines, follow the manufacturer's minimum CLR.</p>

<p>In many African markets, pipe is supplied to nominal dimensions with wall thickness tolerance of 10%. If you are bending for a pressure application, measure the actual wall thickness and calculate the thinning using the standard formula: t_final = t_initial (1 OD/(2CLR)). If thinning exceeds 10%, consider hot bending or a thicker starting pipe.</p>

<h3>Springback and Compensation</h3>
<p>Springback is the elastic recovery of the material after the load is removed. It is the reason your 90 bend comes out at 92. For mild steel, typical springback is 13; for stainless and high-strength steel, it can be 510. You compensate by over-bending: set the machine to 93 to get 90. For roll bending, springback shows up as a larger final radius, so you roll tighter and let it relax.</p>

<p>Springback increases with:</p>
<ul>
<li>Higher yield strength (S355 vs S235)</li>
<li>Larger die opening relative to thickness</li>
<li>Larger bend radius</li>
<li>Thinner material</li>
</ul>
<p>In practice, the fastest way to dial in springback is to bend a test piece from the same heat/lot, measure the angle with a digital protractor, and adjust. Keep a logbook per material supplier. In African markets where steel quality varies, this logbook becomes a commercial asset it reduces scrap and speeds up quoting.</p>

<h3>Forming Defects and Remedies</h3>
<p>Common defects in African fabrication shops include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cracking on the outside of the bend:</strong> radius too tight, material too hard, or bending too fast. Remedy: increase radius, anneal, or hot form.</li>
<li><strong>Wrinkling on the inside:</strong> insufficient support or mandrel. Remedy: use a mandrel or increase CLR.</li>
<li><strong>Springback out of tolerance:</strong> over-bend and verify with a protractor.</li>
<li><strong>Orange peel / rough surface:</strong> coarse grain or excessive deformation. Remedy: anneal or use finer grain material.</li>
<li><strong>Die marks:</strong> dirty or worn dies. Remedy: clean and polish, use urethane film.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Confirm material grade, actual thickness, and yield strength before setting the machine.</li>
<li>Select die opening V 8t for mild steel; never exceed machine tonnage.</li>
<li>Calculate bend allowance and springback for the specific lot; run a test piece.</li>
<li>For pipe, check CLR 3 OD (mild steel) and verify wall thinning 10%.</li>
<li>Pre-bend plate edges before rolling to avoid flat spots.</li>
<li>Inspect for cracks, wrinkles, and die marks after the first article; adjust before batch production.</li>
<li>Record setup, springback, and material source in a logbook for repeat jobs.</li>
<li>Isolate power and use PPE before any die change, hot work, or hand entry.</li>
</ol>
