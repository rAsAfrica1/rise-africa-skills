Lessons

1. Installing Front Fairing and Windscreen

Start by laying out the front fairing, windscreen, and all mounting hardware on a clean cloth. Check the fairing for cracks or scratches, especially around the mounting holes. If any holes are cracked, reinforce them with a small piece of fibreglass mat and resin, then let it cure for at least 2 hours before installation. You will need a 10mm socket and ratchet, a Phillips screwdriver, and a torque wrench that reads up to 10 Nm. Most EV motorbike fairings use M6 bolts with rubber grommets to reduce vibration.

Position the fairing on the bike, aligning the top mounting tabs with the brackets on the steering head. Start all bolts by hand first to avoid cross-threading. Once all bolts are in, tighten them in a crisscross pattern to 8 Nm. Do not overtightenplastic cracks easily. If the fairing uses quick-release fasteners, push them in until they click. For the windscreen, apply a thin bead of silicone sealant around the mounting edge to prevent rattles. Use M5 screws with plastic washers, and tighten to 3 Nm. If the windscreen is polycarbonate, clean it with a soft cloth and soapy water only; never use petrol or alcohol as it clouds the plastic.

In many African workshops, you will not have a torque wrench. A good rule: tighten until the rubber grommet just starts to compress, then stop. Check alignment by sitting on the bike and looking through the windscreenit should be centred and not block your view of the front wheel. If the fairing touches the handlebars or brake lines, adjust the brackets by bending them slightly with an adjustable spanner. Test ride at low speed over bumps to listen for rattles. Re-tighten after the first 50 km.

2. Mounting Side Panels and Tank Cover

Side panels and tank covers hide the battery pack and wiring, so they must fit snugly but still allow quick access for maintenance. Gather the panels, M5 bolts, nylon washers, and a 4mm Allen key. Before mounting, check that all rubber grommets and metal inserts are in placemissing grommets cause panels to crack. If you are missing a grommet, cut a 5mm length of fuel hose as a substitute.

Start with the tank cover. It usually slides over the top of the battery box and locks into place with two bolts at the front and two at the rear. Insert the front bolts first, then push the cover down gently until the rear holes align. Tighten to 4 Nm. Do not force the coverif it does not sit flat, check that no wires are trapped underneath. For side panels, hook the top tab into the slot on the frame, then swing the bottom into place. Secure with one bolt at the bottom and one at the rear. Use nylon washers to protect the paint. Tighten to 4 Nm.

In dusty conditions, add a strip of foam tape along the panel edges to keep out dust and water. If the panels vibrate, stick a small piece of rubber inner tube on the inside where they touch the frame. Test the fit by pressing on each panelthere should be no movement. Remove and refit the panels a few times to make sure you can do it quickly with the tools you carry on the bike. A common mistake is overtightening, which cracks the plastic around the bolt holes. If a hole cracks, stop-drill a 2mm hole at the end of the crack and reinforce with a washer.

3. Attaching Rear Fender and Tail Section

The rear fender and tail section protect you from mud and water and also hold the rear light and number plate. You will need a 10mm spanner, a 5mm Allen key, and a drill with a 6mm bit if you need to make new holes. Start by removing the rear wheel or propping the bike so the rear is off the groundthis gives you room to work. Check the fender for cracks, especially around the mounting points. If cracked, repair with fibreglass before installing.

Mount the rear fender to the swingarm or frame using the original bolts. Most fenders use four M6 bolts. Tighten them to 8 Nm. Make sure the fender is centred over the tyremeasure the gap on both sides; it should be equal. If not, adjust by loosening the bolts and shifting the fender. For the tail section, slide it over the rear frame and align the holes with the seat subframe. Bolt it at the front first, then the rear. Use blue threadlocker on these bolts because they vibrate loose. Tighten to 8 Nm.

Connect the rear light wiring before final tightening. The tail light usually has three wires: earth (black), brake (red), and tail (brown or yellow). Use a multimeter to confirm which is which. Solder and heat-shrink all connectionsdo not use twist connectors. In Africa, rain and dust demand waterproofing. Wrap the connectors with self-amalgamating tape. Test the lights: press the brake lever, the brake light should glow brighter than the tail light. Finally, check that the number plate is visible from behind and not covered by the fender. If you carry a passenger, make sure the tail section does not rub on the tyre when the suspension compresses.

4. Securing Seat and Cushions

A secure seat is critical for safety and comfort. Most EV motorbike seats are bolted to the frame with two M8 bolts at the rear and a tongue that slots under the tank cover at the front. You will need a 12mm socket, a 6mm Allen key, and a torque wrench. Start by checking the seat pan for cracks. If the pan is plastic and cracked, reinforce it with a metal plate and rivets. For cushions, if the foam is torn or compressed, replace it with high-density foam (30-40 kg/m). You can cut foam with an electric knife or a hacksaw blade.

Install the seat by sliding the front tongue under the tank cover, then lower the rear onto the mounting points. Insert the bolts and tighten to 15 Nm. Do not overtightenyou can crush the seat pan. If the seat wobbles, add rubber washers between the seat and frame. For cushion attachment, use stainless steel staples (8mm) with a manual staple gun. Pull the cover tight and staple from the centre outwards. If you do not have staples, use contact adhesive and clamp for 24 hours.

In hot, humid climates, choose a marine-grade vinyl cover to resist mould. Add a layer of plastic sheeting between the foam and cover to stop water soaking in. Test the seat by sitting on it and bouncingthere should be no movement. Check that the seat does not rub on the battery box or wiring. If you carry a passenger, make sure the seat is long enough and the rear cushion is secure. After 100 km, re-tighten the seat bolts.

5. Installing Foot Pegs and Guards

Foot pegs and guards keep your feet secure and protect the bike in a fall. You will need a 14mm spanner, a 6mm Allen key, and a torque wrench. Start by checking the mounting brackets on the framethey must be straight and free of cracks. If the brackets are bent, straighten them with a hammer and anvil, or replace them. Foot pegs usually mount with a single M10 bolt and a return spring. Apply grease to the pivot pin before assembly so the peg folds smoothly.

Install the foot peg bracket to the frame using two M8 bolts. Tighten to 20 Nm. Then slide the peg into the bracket, insert the pivot pin, and secure with a split pin or circlip. Test that the peg springs back when you kick it. For passenger pegs, repeat on the rear brackets. Guardsusually made of plastic or aluminiumbolt to the frame or engine mounts. Use M6 bolts with rubber washers to reduce vibration. Tighten to 8 Nm. Make sure the guard does not interfere with the brake pedal or gear lever.

In Africa, foot pegs often take a beating on rough roads. Check them weekly for looseness. If the peg is worn, replace the rubber sleeve or the whole peg. For off-road use, consider wider pegs for better grip. Test ride standing on the pegsthey should not flex. If they do, the bracket is weak; reinforce it with a gusset. Always use threadlocker on foot peg bolts. After installation, check that your boots do not slip off when wet. Clean the pegs with soap and water, not oil.

6. Final Bodywork Alignment

Final alignment ensures all panels fit together with even gaps and no rattles. You will need a tape measure, a 4mm and 5mm Allen key, a 10mm spanner, and a friend to help. Start by loosening all bodywork bolts slightlyabout half a turn. This allows the panels to shift into their natural position. Stand back and look at the bike from the front, side, and rear. Check the gaps between the fairing and tank cover, side panels and seat, and tail section and rear fender. Aim for a uniform gap of 3-5 mm.

Adjust one panel at a time. Start with the front fairing: push it up or down until the gap to the tank cover is even, then tighten its bolts to 8 Nm. Next, align the side panels so their top edges are parallel to the seat. Tighten to 4 Nm. For the tail section, make sure it is centred over the rear wheel and the gap to the seat is even. Tighten to 8 Nm. Finally, check the seatit should sit flush with the tank cover and side panels. If not, adjust the seat mounting bolts or add shims.

Once aligned, tighten all bolts in a diagonal pattern to the specified torque. Do not overtighten. Go for a slow ride over bumps and listen for rattles. If you hear a rattle, locate it by pressing on panels while riding (have a helper do this). Common causes are missing grommets, loose bolts, or wires tapping. Fix by adding foam tape or tightening. After 100 km, re-check all bolts. In dusty conditions, alignment may shift; check monthly. A well-aligned bodywork not only looks good but also reduces vibration and extends the life of your panels.

<h2>Practical Reference: Bodywork and Fairings for EV Motorbike Assembly</h2>

<p>In the African e-mobility market, bodywork and fairings do more than make an electric motorbike look good. They protect the battery pack, controller, wiring harness, and rider from dust, rain, and road debris. They also determine how the bike is perceived by customers and perception drives sales in cities like Kigali, Nairobi, Lagos, and Accra, where riders increasingly judge an e-bike by how solid and professional it looks. For assemblers and workshop owners, mastering bodywork assembly is a commercial advantage: fewer warranty claims, faster build times, and higher resale value.</p>

<p>This practical reference condenses the six lessons in this module installing the front fairing and windscreen, mounting side panels and tank cover, attaching the rear fender and tail section, securing seat and cushions, installing foot pegs and guards, and final bodywork alignment into a single field-ready guide. Use it as a bench reference during assembly and as a quality-control checklist before a bike leaves your workshop.</p>

<h3>1. Front Fairing and Windscreen Installation</h3>
<p>Start with the front fairing because it sets the reference point for the rest of the bodywork. On most EV motorbike kits sold in Africa including those from Ampersand, Roam, and Spiro the front fairing carries the headlight housing, turn signals, and often the display cluster. Align the fairing mounting brackets with the frame head tube before tightening anything. A fairing installed even 5 mm off-centre will make the windscreen lean and cause wind noise that customers notice immediately on a test ride.</p>
<p>Use rubber grommets and nylon washers at every mounting point. These absorb vibration from African road surfaces murram, potholes, and unpaved feeder roads and prevent the fairing from cracking at bolt holes. Torque fasteners to the manufacturer's specification, typically 812 Nm for M6 bolts on plastic bodywork. Over-tightening is the single most common cause of cracked fairings in workshops.</p>

<h3>2. Side Panels and Tank Cover</h3>
<p>Side panels protect the battery compartment and controller from dust and water ingress. In dusty markets like northern Nigeria or the Sahel, this is a functional requirement, not cosmetic. Fit the tank cover first it usually locks into the frame's top tube then slide the side panels into their clips. Check that panel edges do not contact the battery box; contact points cause rattling and eventual abrasion of the battery casing.</p>

<h3>3. Rear Fender and Tail Section</h3>
<p>The rear fender must clear the tyre by at least 15 mm under full suspension compression. Load a rider or equivalent weight onto the seat before final tightening to confirm clearance. The tail section houses the rear light cluster and often the number plate holder. Route the rear wiring harness through the grommet in the tail section before bolting it down retrofitting wiring afterwards risks pinching the harness.</p>

<h3>4. Seat and Cushions</h3>
<p>Seat mounting is a safety-critical step. A seat that shifts under braking can cause a rider to lose control. Use the manufacturer's seat latch and confirm it engages with an audible click. For passenger seats, check that the grab rail and footrests are rated for the load in many African markets, passenger carrying is a primary use case, and regulatory inspections (such as those by the National Transport and Safety Authority in Kenya) will check seat security.</p>

<h3>5. Foot Pegs and Guards</h3>
<p>Foot pegs must be installed with the correct left/right orientation and secured with the supplied split pins or thread-locking compound. Guards protect the rider's legs and the bike's lower bodywork. On e-bikes, guards also shield the motor and controller from debris kicked up by the front wheel. Verify that guards do not interfere with the side stand or centre stand travel.</p>

<h3>6. Final Bodywork Alignment</h3>
<p>Final alignment is a quality gate. Walk around the bike at eye level and check panel gaps. Gaps should be uniform within 2 mm. Check that no panel touches another under static load, that all fasteners are present, and that the windscreen is clean and free of distortion. Take photographs of the finished bodywork for your build record this protects you in warranty disputes and helps train new assemblers.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:14px 18px; margin:20px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong> Never install bodywork while the battery pack is connected. Metal tools contacting live terminals can cause arc flash, burns, or fire. Disconnect the main battery isolator, wait for the controller capacitors to discharge (minimum 5 minutes), and use insulated tools. In workshops without proper ventilation, lithium battery off-gassing during a fault can be toxic always assemble bodywork in a well-ventilated area away from open flame. Follow your national electrical safety code and the battery manufacturer's lockout/tagout procedure.
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Battery isolated</strong> main isolator off, capacitors discharged, insulated tools in use.</li>
<li><strong>Front fairing aligned</strong> centred on head tube, grommets fitted, torque 810 Nm.</li>
<li><strong>Windscreen secured</strong> no distortion, fasteners snug but not over-compressed.</li>
<li><strong>Side panels and tank cover</strong> clips fully engaged, 5 mm clearance from battery box.</li>
<li><strong>Rear fender clearance</strong> minimum 15 mm from tyre under loaded suspension.</li>
<li><strong>Seat latch</strong> audible click, no movement under braking force, passenger grab rail secure.</li>
<li><strong>Foot pegs and guards</strong> correct orientation, thread-locking compound applied, no stand interference.</li>
<li><strong>Final walk-around</strong> uniform panel gaps (2 mm), all fasteners present, photographs taken for build record.</li>
</ol>
