1. Cane Preparation: Washing, Chopping, and Shredding

Before you can extract juice, you must prepare the cane properly. In Africa, cane often arrives with mud, stones, and trash. Start by washing the cane on a sloped concrete pad with a water hose or high-pressure spray. Use a simple screen to catch debris. Washing removes dirt that would wear out your mills and contaminate juice. After washing, chop the cane into 20â30 cm billets using a mechanical chopper or a sharp cane knife if manual. Chopping increases the surface area for extraction.

Next, shred the billets into fine fibers using a shredder or a hammer mill. A good shredder reduces cane to pieces smaller than 5 mm. This step is critical: shredded cane releases up to 95% of its juice in the first mill, compared to only 60â70% for whole stalks. In small-scale operations, you can use a motorized shredder powered by a 10â15 HP diesel engine. Feed cane evenly to avoid jams. Wear gloves and safety glassesâshredders are dangerous.

For manual operations, a heavy wooden mallet or a hand-cranked shredder can work, but output is low (about 50 kg per hour). If you process 1 tonne of cane per day, invest in a motorized shredder. Keep the blades sharp; dull blades tear rather than cut, reducing efficiency. After shredding, move the cane to the mill within 30 minutes. Delays cause sugar loss due to microbial action. In African conditions, where temperatures often exceed 30Â°C, work quickly.

A simple test for shred quality: squeeze a handful of shredded cane; it should feel like wet sawdust, not chunks. If you find whole pieces, adjust the shredder clearance. Proper preparation can increase juice extraction by 10â15%, meaning more sugar from the same cane. Train your workers to check for stones and metal that can damage equipment. A small investment in preparation saves big money in repairs and lost juice.

2. Milling Process: Crushers and Mills

Milling is the heart of juice extraction. A typical small-scale mill in Africa consists of three rollers: two on top and one at the bottom, or a set of three rollers in a triangle. The cane passes between the rollers, which crush it and squeeze out juice. Rollers are made of cast iron or steel and are grooved to grip the cane. The gap between rollers is adjustableâstart with 6 mm for the first mill and reduce to 3 mm for later mills.

You need a series of mills (usually 3 to 5) to extract maximum juice. After each mill, the bagasse (crushed cane) is sprayed with water (imbibition) to dilute remaining sugar, then passed to the next mill. A single mill extracts about 60% of juice; three mills in series can extract 90â95%. For a 5-tonne-per-day operation, use a 3-roller mill powered by a 20â30 HP engine. Run the mill at 10â15 RPM. Too fast and you lose juice; too slow and you reduce capacity.

In many African countries, local artisans fabricate mills from recycled steel. Ensure the rollers are balanced and the bearings are well-lubricated. Use food-grade grease. Juice flows from the mill into a collection trough, then to a screen or tank. Keep the mill area cleanâspilled juice attracts ants and flies. Check the bagasse after the last mill: it should be dry and crumbly, not wet. If it's wet, your mill gaps are too wide or you need more mills.

Safety: never put your hand near the rollers. Use a wooden pusher to feed cane. Install a guard over the gears. A well-maintained mill can last 10 years. In countries like Kenya, Uganda, and Nigeria, small mills are common. For example, a 3-roller mill from a local workshop costs about $2,000â$5,000. It pays back in one or two seasons. Learn to adjust the rollers and replace worn parts.

3. Imbibition and Juice Extraction Efficiency

Imbibition is the process of spraying water on bagasse between mills to dissolve and extract more sugar. Without imbibition, you leave 30â40% of sugar in the bagasse. With proper imbibition, you can recover over 95%. The rule: use 20â30% water by weight of cane. For 1 tonne of cane, add 200â300 liters of clean water. Spray the water on the bagasse as it enters the last mill, and sometimes on the second-to-last mill.

Use a simple perforated pipe or a spray nozzle to distribute water evenly. The water should be at room temperature. Hot water (60â70Â°C) can improve extraction but may require heating. In Africa, where firewood or solar heating is available, warm water can increase efficiency by 5%. However, cold water works fine if you have enough mills. The key is to ensure the bagasse is soaked for at least 30 seconds before pressing.

Measure extraction efficiency by weighing cane in and juice out. A simple formula: extraction % = (juice weight Ã Brix of juice) / (cane weight Ã Brix of cane) Ã 100. Brix is measured with a refractometer or a hydrometer. For small-scale, aim for 85â90% extraction. If below 80%, check: mill gaps, imbibition water amount, and number of mills. Also, ensure cane is shredded finely. Over-imbibition (more than 40% water) dilutes juice and increases evaporation costs. Under-imbibition leaves sugar in bagasse.

In practice, many African processors use a 3-mill tandem with imbibition on the second and third mills. For example, in Tanzania, a 5-tonne mill uses 250 liters of water per tonne of cane. They achieve 92% extraction. Test your bagasse: take a sample, squeeze it, and taste the juice. If sweet, you're losing sugar. Adjust water flow and mill pressure. Keep records daily to track performance.

4. Bagasse Handling and Utilization

Bagasse is the fibrous residue left after juice extraction. For every tonne of cane, you get about 250â300 kg of bagasse (50% moisture). In Africa, bagasse is often wasted or burned inefficiently. But it's a valuable resource. First, handle it properly: convey it from the last mill to a storage pile or directly to a boiler. Use a simple belt conveyor or a wheelbarrow. Do not let it pile up wetâit will ferment and smell. Spread it to dry in the sun for 2â3 days to reduce moisture to 15â20%.

Dry bagasse is an excellent fuel. It has a calorific value of 17â19 MJ/kg, similar to wood. Use it to fire the boiler that generates steam for evaporation and crystallization. A 5-tonne mill produces enough bagasse to power the whole operation, with surplus. In many African sugar factories, bagasse is the primary fuel. For small-scale, you can burn bagasse in a simple furnace to heat pans. Alternatively, use it for cooking or brick making.

Other uses: bagasse can be composted with animal manure to make organic fertilizer. It can be mixed with clay to make building blocks (bagasse bricks are lightweight and insulating). In some countries, it's used to make paper or particle board, but that requires capital. For a small business, the best uses are fuel and compost. If you have excess, sell it to local brick makers or farmers. In Kenya, a tonne of dry bagasse sells for about $20â$30.

Store bagasse under a roof or tarp to keep it dry. Wet bagasse rots and loses fuel value. Also, bagasse dust can cause respiratory problemsâwear masks when handling. A well-managed bagasse pile reduces waste and cuts energy costs. Many African processors save 30â40% on fuel by using their own bagasse. So, don't throw it away; treat it as a by-product that adds profit.

5. Juice Screening and Clarification Basics

After extraction, raw juice contains dirt, bagasse particles, and other impurities. Screening removes large solids. Use a vibrating screen or a simple mesh (1â2 mm openings) to strain the juice. In small operations, a static screen with a 1 mm stainless steel mesh works. Pour juice through the screen into a holding tank. Clean the screen every 30 minutes to prevent clogging. Screening removes about 60% of suspended solids.

Next, clarification removes fine particles and some color. The traditional method is heating juice to 70â80Â°C, then adding lime (calcium hydroxide) to raise pH to 7.0â7.5. Lime helps settle impurities. Add lime as a milk of lime (1 part lime to 5 parts water). Use 1â2 kg of lime per tonne of cane. Stir well, then heat to 100â105Â°C for 5â10 minutes. This coagulates proteins and other impurities. Then let it settle in a tank for 1â2 hours. The clear juice is decanted, and the mud is filtered or discarded.

For small-scale, you can use a simple clarification setup: a large pot, a heat source (firewood or gas), and a settling tank. In Africa, many processors use a triple-tank system: one for heating, one for settling, and one for storing clear juice. If you don't clarify, the juice will ferment quickly and produce dark sugar. Clarification improves sugar quality and shelf life. Test pH with pH strips or a meter. If pH is too low, add more lime; if too high, add juice.

After clarification, the juice is ready for evaporation. Keep everything cleanâuse food-grade equipment and sanitize with hot water. In countries like Malawi and Zambia, smallholders use this method to make jaggery or brown sugar. Clarification is not optional if you want a good product. It takes 1â2 hours per batch, but it's worth it. You can also use natural clarifiers like okra powder or moringa seeds, but lime is cheapest and most effective.

6. Equipment Maintenance and Troubleshooting

Equipment breakdowns cost time and money. In African sugar processing, the most common issues are worn mill rollers, clogged screens, and engine problems. Start with a daily maintenance routine: before operation, check oil levels in the engine and gearbox. Lubricate bearings with food-grade grease. Inspect belts and chains for wear. Tighten loose bolts. After operation, clean all juice contact surfaces with hot water. Remove bagasse from mills and conveyors. A clean machine lasts longer.

Weekly maintenance: check mill roller gaps. Use a feeler gauge to measure. If gaps are uneven, adjust the shims. Inspect roller groovesâif worn smooth, they won't grip cane. Replace or re-groove rollers. Check the shredder blades; sharpen or replace if dull. Clean or replace screen mesh. For engines, change oil every 100 hours. Replace air and fuel filters. In dusty African conditions, clean filters more often.

Troubleshooting: if juice extraction drops, first check cane preparationâis it shredded finely? Then check mill gaps and roller condition. If juice is dark, check clarification pH and lime dose. If the mill jams, stop immediately, turn off power, and remove the blockage with a wooden stick. Never use metal tools near rollers. If the engine overheats, check coolant and radiator. If the mill vibrates, check for loose bolts or unbalanced rollers. Keep a spare set of bearings, belts, and a spare screen.

Train one worker as a mechanic. In many African villages, local welders can repair mill parts. Keep a logbook of maintenance and repairs. This helps predict failures. For example, if you note that bearings last 6 months, replace them before they fail. A simple toolkit: wrenches, screwdrivers, feeler gauge, grease gun, and a hammer. With regular care, your mill can run for years. Remember, prevention is cheaper than repair. A $50 bearing replaced on time saves a $500 breakdown.

Imbibition: The Cheapest Efficiency Gain

Imbibition is the addition of water (or dilute juice) to bagasse between mills to displace remaining sucrose. It is the single most cost-effective lever in extraction. The principle: macerate the bagasse with water, then press it out. Repeat. Compound imbibition across four mills can push extraction from 90% to 97%.

Rule of thumb: Imbibition water should be 2.0â2.5 times the fibre content of cane. For cane at 13% fibre, that's roughly 26â32% on cane â but most African mills operate at 18â25% on cane due to evaporation constraints. The trade-off: more water = better extraction but higher steam consumption in the evaporators. Find your economic optimum.

Water quality matters. Use condensate or clarified juice, not raw river water, to avoid introducing mud, microbes, and minerals. In factories using river water, ensure it is screened and dosed with biocide.

Mill rollers exert crushing forces exceeding 1,000 tonnes. Never attempt to clear a cane choke, remove bagasse, or adjust a mill while the tandem is running. Isolate and lock out all drives before any intervention. Bagasse piles can self-heat and combust spontaneously â store in ventilated areas away from ignition sources. Ensure all emergency stop cables and pull-cords are tested weekly. In African mills where casual labour is common, enforce a strict no-loose-clothing, no-jewellery rule and provide mandatory induction before floor access. Amputations in sugar mills are almost always preventable and almost always involve untrained or rushed workers.

Juice Screening and Clarification Basics

Mixed juice from the mills carries bagasse fines, sand, and soil. Screening removes coarse particles (typically through DSM screens or rotary screens with 0.5â1.5 mm apertures). Clarification then removes colloidal and dissolved impurities using heat, lime, and flocculant.

Practical sequence: Heat juice to 70Â°C â add lime to pH 7.0â7.2 â heat to 102â105Â°C â flash to remove air â add flocculant â settle in clarifier â filter mud for recovery. In African mills with variable cane quality (burnt, stale, or muddy cane), clarification is often the bottleneck. Keep lime dosing calibrated daily and never let pH drift below 6.8 or above 7.5.

Equipment Maintenance and Troubleshooting

Mill stoppages are expensive. A 2,500 TCD mill loses roughly 100 tonnes of cane per hour of downtime. At USD 40/tonne cane, that's USD 4,000/hour. Maintenance is not a cost centre â it is revenue protection.

Common faults and fixes:

  • Low extraction: Check imbibition flow, shredder hammers, mill roll wear, and juice drainage grooves. Worn rolls (diameter reduction >5%) must be replaced.
  • High bagasse moisture: Check hydraulic pressure, roll settings, and bagasse carrier. Often a sign of worn or misaligned rolls.
  • Mill choke: Reduce feed rate, check cane preparation quality (too coarse = poor feeding), inspect donnelly chutes.
  • Juice screen blockage: Increase cleaning frequency during burnt cane season; burnt cane produces more fines.
  • Excessive vibration: Check bearings, coupling alignment, and foundation bolts. Vibration destroys gearboxes.
  • Steam shortage: Usually bagasse moisture or boiler fouling â not a mill problem, but the mill is blamed.

Preventive maintenance schedule: Daily â lubrication, roll gap check, screen cleaning. Weekly â hydraulic system inspection, bearing temperature logging. Monthly â roll wear measurement, gearbox oil analysis. Annual â full tandem overhaul before harvest season.
