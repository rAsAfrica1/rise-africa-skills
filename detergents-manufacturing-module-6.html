1. Types of mixers: batch, continuous, inline

When you start making detergents, you need to choose how you will mix your ingredients. The three main types are batch, continuous, and inline mixers. Each has its place in a small African workshop.

Batch mixing is the simplest. You measure all your ingredients, put them in a tank, and mix until uniform. Then you stop and empty the tank. For example, to make 200 litres of liquid dishwash, you fill a 250-litre plastic drum with 150 litres of water, add 20 kg of sulphonic acid slowly while stirring, then add caustic soda solution until the pH is 7-8, then add salt and fragrance. You mix for 30-45 minutes with a paddle or electric drill with a mixing attachment. Batch mixing is good for small-scale production, say up to 500 litres per day. It is easy to control quality because you can test each batch.

Continuous mixing means ingredients flow into one end of a mixer and product flows out the other end non-stop. This is used in big factories making thousands of litres per hour. You need pumps, flow meters, and automatic controls. For a beginner, continuous mixing is too expensive and complicated. But you can see it in large soap and detergent plants.

Inline mixing is when you mix as the product moves through a pipe. For example, you can use a static mixerâa pipe with twisted blades insideâto blend a liquid detergent with a fragrance just before filling bottles. Inline mixers are cheap and have no moving parts. They work well when you already have a liquid stream and want to add a small amount of another liquid. You can buy a static mixer for under $100.

For most small African businesses, batch mixing is the best starting point. You can use a 200-litre drum, a heavy-duty drill with a paint mixer attachment, and a weighing scale. As you grow, you might add an inline mixer for adding perfume. Continuous mixing only makes sense when you produce more than 5,000 litres per day. So start with batch, master it, and then consider other types when your sales demand it.

2. Order of ingredient addition

The order in which you add ingredients can make the difference between a perfect batch and a ruined one. In detergent making, always follow this rule: start with the hardest-to-dissolve ingredients and end with the easiest. Also, never add concentrated acid to concentrated causticâthat can cause a violent reaction.

For a typical liquid detergent like dishwash, begin with water. Fill your mixing tank about 70% full with clean water. If you use borehole water, let it settle or filter it first. Then add the acid, such as sulphonic acid or LABSA. Pour it slowly while stirring. The acid is thick and needs time to dissolve. Stir for 10-15 minutes until no lumps remain. Next, add the caustic soda solution (sodium hydroxide dissolved in water) very slowly. This neutralises the acid and creates the detergent. Watch the temperatureâit will rise. If it gets above 60Â°C, stop adding caustic and wait for it to cool. Stir for another 15 minutes.

After neutralisation, add builders like sodium carbonate or sodium silicate. These help soften water and improve cleaning. Add them slowly to avoid clumping. Then add foam boosters like coco diethanolamide (CDEA) or lauryl sulphate. These are thick and sticky, so mix well. Next, add salt (sodium chloride) to thicken the mixture. Add a small amount at a timeâtoo much salt will thin it instead. Finally, add fragrance, colour, and preservative. These are sensitive to heat, so add them when the mixture is below 40Â°C.

For powdered detergents, the order is different: first mix dry powders like soda ash, STPP, and sodium sulphate. Then add surfactants that are already in powder form (like sodium lauryl sulphate powder). Finally, spray or mix in liquid ingredients like fragrance and nonionic surfactants. Always add liquids last to avoid caking.

Remember: never add water to acid; always add acid to water. And always add caustic to water, not water to caustic. Following the correct order prevents accidents and ensures a smooth, stable product.

3. Mixing parameters: speed, time, temperature

To get consistent quality, you must control three key mixing parameters: speed, time, and temperature. If you ignore any of these, your detergent may be lumpy, too thin, too thick, or unstable.

Speed: The speed of your mixer depends on what you are mixing. For dissolving powders in water, use a medium speedâabout 300 to 600 RPM (revolutions per minute). A standard electric drill with a paddle mixer runs at about 500-800 RPM. If you mix too fast, you create foam and air bubbles that are hard to remove. If you mix too slow, powders settle at the bottom. For emulsification (mixing oil and water), you need higher speed, around 1,500 to 3,000 RPM. You can use a high-shear mixer or a blender for small batches. For thickening with salt, use low speed (100-200 RPM) to avoid thinning the mixture.

Time: Each step has a minimum mixing time. For acid dissolution, mix for at least 15 minutes. For neutralisation, mix for 20 minutes after adding caustic. For salt thickening, mix for 10 minutes, then check viscosity. Over-mixing can also be a problemâit can break down foam or cause excessive aeration. A good rule: mix until the mixture is uniform, then mix 5 more minutes. Use a timer.

Temperature: Most liquid detergents are made at room temperature (25-30Â°C). But neutralisation generates heat. Keep the temperature below 60Â°C. If it gets too hot, the fragrance may evaporate and the colour may fade. For powdered detergents, you may need to dry the mixture at 60-80Â°C, but that is after mixing. For emulsification, warming the oil phase to 70Â°C and the water phase to 70Â°C helps form a stable emulsion. Then cool slowly while stirring.

In Africa, ambient temperatures can be high, so you may need to cool your mixing tank with a water bath or add ice to the water jacket. Always use a thermometerâa simple digital one costs less than $10. Record the speed, time, and temperature for every batch so you can repeat good results.

4. Homogenization and emulsification

Homogenization and emulsification are two processes that make your detergent look smooth and stay mixed. Without them, your product may separate into layersâoil on top, water on bottomâwhich customers will not buy.

Emulsification is mixing two liquids that don't normally mix, like oil and water. In detergents, you often need to emulsify fragrance oils, silicone antifoams, or plant oils. To make a stable emulsion, you need an emulsifierâa chemical that bridges oil and water. Common emulsifiers in detergents are nonionic surfactants like alcohol ethoxylates or fatty acid ethoxylates. The process: heat the oil phase to 70Â°C, heat the water phase to 70Â°C, then slowly pour the oil into the water while stirring at high speed (1,500-3,000 RPM). Stir for 15-20 minutes, then cool slowly while stirring. If you cool too fast, the emulsion may crack. A simple hand blender can work for 5-10 litre batches. For larger batches, use a high-shear mixer.

Homogenization is forcing the mixture through a narrow gap at high pressure to break oil droplets into tiny sizes. This makes the emulsion more stable and gives a smoother feel. A homogenizer is expensive, but you can achieve similar results with a high-speed mixer or a colloid mill. For small-scale production, a colloid mill (costing around $500-$1,000) can homogenize 100-500 litres per hour. You can also use a high-pressure pump if available.

In Africa, many small producers skip homogenization because of cost. But if you make liquid handwash or body wash, homogenization makes a big difference. You can start by using a good emulsifier and a high-speed mixer. Test your emulsion by leaving a sample in a bottle for 7 days. If it separates, you need more emulsifier or better mixing. Also, avoid adding salt before emulsificationâsalt can break the emulsion. Add salt after the emulsion is formed and cooled.

Remember: stable emulsions need the right emulsifier, the right temperature, and the right mixing speed. Practice with small batches until you get a product that stays mixed for months.

5. Monitoring and control of mixing

Monitoring and control mean checking your mixing process while it is happening and making adjustments to keep the product on track. Without monitoring, you are guessing, and guessing leads to wasted batches.

What to monitor: First, check the appearance. Is the mixture uniform? Are there lumps or streaks? Use a glass rod or a clean stick to take a sample. Second, measure pH. For most liquid detergents, pH should be between 7 and 9. Use pH paper or a digital pH meter (costing $20-$50). Third, check viscosity. You can use a simple flow cup or a viscometer. A cheap method: time how long it takes for a fixed volume to flow through a funnel. Record the time and compare to your standard. Fourth, check temperature with a thermometer. Fifth, check foam height if your product is for handwashingâtoo little foam means poor quality.

How to control: If pH is too high, add a little more acid. If too low, add a little caustic. Add in small amounts and mix for 5 minutes before testing again. If viscosity is too low, add salt solution (25% salt in water) slowly. If too high, add water. If temperature is too high, reduce mixing speed or cool the tank. If you see lumps, increase mixing speed or mix longer.

Tools for monitoring: In a small African workshop, you need a thermometer, pH paper or meter, a weighing scale, a measuring jug, and a timer. A notebook to record every batch is essential. Write down the date, batch number, ingredients and amounts, mixing speed, time, temperature, pH, and viscosity. This helps you trace problems and repeat successes.

Automation: If you grow, you can add simple controls like a variable speed drive for your mixer, a temperature controller, and a pH controller. But start manually. Train your workers to check and record. A good practice is to have a checklist for each step: Did you add acid slowly? Did you check pH after neutralisation? Did you add fragrance after cooling? Sign off each step. This simple system prevents most mistakes.

6. Cleaning and changeover procedures

Cleaning between batches is critical. Leftover detergent from a previous batch can contaminate the next one, causing wrong colour, wrong smell, or even chemical reactions. Changeover means switching from making one product to another, like from dishwash to handwash. Proper cleaning and changeover save you money and protect your reputation.

Cleaning steps: First, drain all leftover product from the tank, pipes, and mixer. You can save this as 'first rinse' and use it for the next batch of the same product if it is still good. Second, rinse with water. Use a hose or buckets. For sticky residues, use warm water (40-50Â°C). Third, scrub with a brush or sponge. Pay attention to corners, the mixer shaft, and the bottom of the tank. Fourth, use a cleaning solution. For detergent residues, a 2% caustic soda solution works well. For oily residues, use a detergent solution. Let it sit for 10 minutes, then scrub again. Fifth, rinse with clean water until the water runs clear and has no foam. Sixth, sanitise if needed. For products like handwash, use a dilute bleach solution (100 ppm chlorine) and rinse again. Finally, dry the equipment or let it air dry.

Changeover procedure: When switching from one product to another, follow a written procedure. For example, from dishwash to handwash: drain, rinse, clean with caustic, rinse, then rinse with a weak acid (like vinegar) to neutralise any caustic. Then rinse again. Check the last rinse water with pH paperâit should be neutral (pH 7). Then you can start the new batch. If the two products have very different colours or fragrances, you may need to disassemble the mixer and clean it manually.

In Africa, water can be scarce, so use water wisely. Use a high-pressure sprayer to reduce water use. Collect rinse water and reuse it for the first rinse of the next cleaning. Keep a cleaning log: date, product, who cleaned, and how long it took. Train your workers to clean immediately after each batchâdried detergent is harder to remove. A clean plant is a safe plant. Also, cleaning prevents pests and bacteria that can spoil your product. Make cleaning a habit, not an afterthought.

You cannot control what you do not measure. At minimum, monitor and record the following for every batch:

  • Temperature â check at start, during addition, and at end.

6. Cleaning and Changeover Procedures

Changeover between products â for example, from a bleach-based cleaner to a dishwash â must be thorough. Residues cause cross-contamination, off-odours, and quality failures. Follow this sequence:

  1. Drain and recover usable product residue where possible.
  2. Rinse with warm water (40â50 Â°C) to remove bulk product.
  3. Wash with detergent solution and brush all contact surfaces.
  4. Rinse with clean water until no foam remains.
  5. For bleach or caustic products, use a dilute acid rinse (e.g., citric acid) to neutralize residues.
  6. Inspect visually and by smell. If in doubt, clean again.
  7. Record the changeover in the cleaning log with date, time, operator, and products involved.

In hot, humid African conditions, wet surfaces grow bacteria and mould quickly. Dry equipment after cleaning where possible, and do not leave standing water in tanks overnight.
