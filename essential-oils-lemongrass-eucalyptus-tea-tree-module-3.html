Lessons

1. Steam Distillation Using a Cooking Pot and Local Materials

You can build a working steam distillation setup with a large cooking pot (at least 20 litres), a lid, a length of copper or stainless steel tubing (about 1 metre of 68 mm diameter), a heat source (firewood, charcoal, or gas), and a bucket of cold water. First, place your plant materialchopped lemongrass, eucalyptus leaves, or tea tree brancheson a rack or sieve inside the pot so it sits above the water. Add water until it is just below the rack. Put the lid on upside down so the handle acts as a collection point, or drill a hole in the lid and insert the tubing. Seal gaps with dough made from flour and water. As the water boils, steam passes through the plant material, vaporising the essential oil. The steam and oil vapour travel through the tubing, which you run through a bucket of cold water (the condenser). The cold water causes the vapour to condense back into liquid, which drips into a receiving jar. For lemongrass, use 1 kg of fresh chopped leaves per 5 litres of water. For eucalyptus, 1 kg of leaves per 4 litres. For tea tree, 1.5 kg of leaves per 5 litres. Distillation takes 12 hours. Collect about 500 ml of distillate; the oil will float or sink depending on density. Siphon off the oil layer with a small syringe or separate it using a separatory funnel made from a clear plastic bottle. Expect yields of 0.51% for lemongrass, 0.81.5% for eucalyptus, and 0.61.2% for tea tree. Keep the fire steadytoo hot and you may burn the plant material; too cool and distillation stalls. This method works in rural areas with no electricity.

2. Cold Pressing for Lemongrass: A Step-by-Step Guide

Cold pressing is best for lemongrass because heat can damage the delicate citrus-like oils. You need fresh lemongrass stalks (not dried), a strong knife or machete, a clean stone or wooden pounding block, a manual press (or a heavy stone), a clean cloth (muslin or cheesecloth), and a glass jar. Harvest lemongrass early in the morning when oil content is highest. Cut the stalks about 10 cm above the ground. Remove the outer dry leaves and roots. Chop the stalks into 510 cm pieces. You will need about 2 kg of fresh stalks to get 1020 ml of oil. Pound the pieces with a stone or wooden mallet until they are bruised and fibrousthis breaks the oil cells. Do not reduce to a paste; just crush them well. Place the crushed lemongrass in a clean cloth and twist it tightly to squeeze out the juice. Collect the greenish liquid in a jar. Let it stand for 24 hours. The essential oil will separate and float on top of the water. Use a small syringe or a spoon to skim off the oil layer. You can also pour the liquid into a narrow-necked bottle and use a pipette to remove the top oil. For better yield, repeat the pressing with the same pulp after adding a little clean water, then separate again. Yields are lowabout 0.51 ml of oil per kg of fresh stalksso cold pressing is best for small batches or for making fresh lemongrass oil for immediate use. Store the oil in a dark glass bottle in a cool place. Cold pressing preserves the fresh, lemony aroma that steam distillation can sometimes alter.

3. Solar Distillation: Using the African Sun to Extract Oils

Solar distillation uses the suns heat to evaporate water and essential oils from plant material, then condenses them. You need a clear plastic sheet (UV-resistant, 23 metres), a large black pot or basin, a smaller collection container, a stone or weight, and a sunny spot. Dig a hole in the ground about 1 metre wide and 50 cm deep. Place the black pot in the centre filled with water and plant materialuse 500 g of chopped lemongrass or eucalyptus leaves per 2 litres of water. Put the collection container in the middle of the pot, raised so it doesnt touch the water. Cover the hole with the plastic sheet, sealing the edges with soil or stones. Place a small stone in the centre of the plastic so it dips slightly over the collection container. The sun heats the pot, water evaporates, and oil vapours rise. They condense on the cooler plastic and run down into the collection container. This works best in hot, dry climates with full sun. Start early in the morning and collect in the late afternoon. Expect 100200 ml of distillate per day, containing a small amount of oil. For better yield, use a solar still with a slanted glass cover and a separate condenser. You can also use a black hose coiled in a box with a glass lidthe sun heats the hose, and steam travels through it, condensing at the end. Solar distillation is slow and yields less oil than steam distillation, but it requires no fuel and works in remote areas. Use it for lemongrass and eucalyptus; tea tree may need higher temperatures. Separate the oil layer with a syringe after letting the distillate stand.

4. Solvent Extraction with Cheap Local Solvents

Solvent extraction uses a chemical to pull oil from plant material. For small-scale, use food-grade ethanol (available as strong drinking alcohol or from chemical suppliers) or hexane (from hardware stores as a cleaning solvent). Avoid methanol or petrolthey are toxic. You need 1 kg of dried, ground plant material (lemongrass, eucalyptus, or tea tree), 23 litres of solvent, a large glass jar with a lid, a cloth for filtering, and a heat source for evaporating the solvent. Place the plant material in the jar and cover with solvent. Seal and shake well. Let it soak for 2448 hours, shaking occasionally. Strain through a cloth into another jar. Repeat the soaking with fresh solvent on the same plant material two more times to get all the oil. Combine all the solvent extracts. Now you must remove the solvent. For ethanol, you can evaporate it by placing the jar in a warm water bath (5060C) outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Ethanol evaporates first, leaving the essential oil and some plant waxes. For hexane, you need a still or careful heatinghexane is flammable, so do this outside, away from flames. After evaporation, you will have a thick, dark oil called a concrete or absolute. It contains essential oil plus waxes and pigments. To purify, mix the concrete with ethanol, chill it, and filter out the waxes, then evaporate the ethanol again. Yields are higher than steam distillation: 13% for lemongrass, 24% for eucalyptus. But solvent extraction is more dangerous and requires careful ventilation. Only use this method if you cannot get a steam distillation setup.

5. Filtering and Purifying Your Oil Without Expensive Equipment

After extraction, your oil may contain water, plant particles, waxes, or other impurities. Filtering and purifying improve quality and shelf life. Start with a simple gravity filter: place a funnel lined with a coffee filter, clean cotton cloth, or blotting paper. Pour your oil through it. For faster filtering, use a clean plastic bottle with the bottom cut off, packed with clean sand and a cloth at the neck. Pour the oil through the sand; it traps particles. If your oil contains water, let it stand in a narrow-necked bottle for 24 hours. The oil will separate and float (or sink). Use a syringe or pipette to remove the oil layer. For lemongrass and eucalyptus, oil floats; for tea tree, it may sink. To remove waxes, chill the oil in a refrigerator or in a bucket of ice water for 23 hours. Waxes solidify and can be scraped off or filtered out. For deeper purification, mix the oil with an equal volume of warm water (60C), shake well, and let separate. Repeat 23 times. This removes water-soluble impurities. Then add a small amount of anhydrous sodium sulfate (available from chemical suppliers) or dry sand to absorb remaining water. Stir and filter again. If you want to remove colour, pass the oil through activated charcoal (from water filters) packed in a cloth. But note: charcoal may also remove some aroma. For most small-scale producers, simple filtering and water separation are enough. Store the purified oil in a dark glass bottle with a tight lid. Proper filtering prevents spoilage and makes your oil more marketable.

6. Storing Your Oil to Prevent Spoilage in Hot Climates

Essential oils can spoil if exposed to heat, light, and oxygen. In hot African climates, you must store them carefully. Use dark glass bottles (amber or cobalt blue) with tight-fitting caps. Plastic bottles can absorb oil and degrade; avoid them for long-term storage. Fill bottles almost to the top to reduce air spaceoxygen causes oxidation. Store in a cool, dark place: a cellar, a shaded cupboard, or even a hole in the ground lined with a box. Ideal temperature is below 25C; if your area is hotter, consider a small solar-powered fridge or a clay pot cooler (zeer pot). Keep away from sunlightUV light breaks down oils. For lemongrass and tea tree, which are prone to oxidation, add a few drops of vitamin E oil (if available) as an antioxidant. You can also purge the bottle with nitrogen or use a vacuum sealer, but these are optional. Label each bottle with the oil name, date, and method of extraction. Use within 12 years for best quality; citrus oils like lemongrass last 1 year, eucalyptus 23 years, tea tree 12 years. Check for spoilage: a rancid, sour, or thick smell means discard. To extend shelf life, store oils in smaller bottles and only open the one you are using. For transport to market, wrap bottles in cloth and keep them in a cool box. Never store oils near strong-smelling items like onions or petrolthey absorb odours. Following these steps will keep your oil fresh and sellable even in the hottest months.

Practical Reference: Simple Oil Extraction for Small-Scale African Producers

This practical reference is the working handbook for the module Simple Oil Extraction Methods for Small-Scale Production. It consolidates the seven lessons steam distillation in a cooking pot, cold pressing lemongrass, solar distillation, solvent extraction with cheap local solvents, filtering and purifying without expensive equipment, and storing oil in hot climates into one business-first reference you can print, laminate, and pin to the wall of your processing shed.

The goal is not laboratory perfection. The goal is sellable oil, repeatable batches, and a margin that survives the rainy season. Every technique below uses materials available in most African towns: cooking pots, jerrycans, PVC pipe, mabati (corrugated iron) roofing, charcoal, cloth, and sunshine. Where a method needs a chemical or a heat source, the cost and safety implications are stated plainly.

Choosing your method: business decision table

Before buying anything, match your crop, capital, and market to a method. Cold pressing preserves the delicate lemongrass top notes but only works on fresh, high-oil material. Steam distillation gives the highest recovery and works for eucalyptus and tea tree. Solar distillation is slow but has near-zero fuel cost ideal for remote sites with strong sun. Solvent extraction is the cheapest per kilogram of plant material but carries the highest safety and market-acceptance risk (residual solvent concerns).

Process notes by lesson

1. Steam distillation using a cooking pot and local materials

Build the unit in Figure 1. Charge the pot with water to just below the perforated rack, load chopped plant material (25 cm pieces) onto the rack, seal the lid with a dough or clay gasket, and run the vapour pipe into a coil submerged in a bucket of cold water. Replace the condenser water every 2030 minutes. Distil for 60120 minutes for eucalyptus, 90150 minutes for tea tree, and 4590 minutes for lemongrass. Collect the distillate in a narrow-necked glass jar so the oil layer is easy to separate.

2. Cold pressing lemongrass

Harvest early morning when oil content peaks. Wash, chop, and pound fresh stalks into a paste. Wrap the paste in clean cotton cloth and press between two wooden boards using a car jack or lever. Collect the liquid, refrigerate overnight, and skim the oil that solidifies or floats. Cold pressing yields less oil but commands a premium for fresh, "green" top notes in the soap and cosmetics market.

3. Solar distillation

Use a parabolic solar concentrator or an insulated solar box to heat a sealed pot. The same condenser logic applies, but cooling water must be shaded and replenished from a jerrycan. Best in the 10:0015:00 window. Expect 3050% of the yield of a fire-heated still per day, but at zero fuel cost a strong selling point for organic certification and carbon-conscious buyers.

4. Solvent extraction with cheap local solvents

Use food-grade ethanol (available from local distillers) rather than hexane wherever possible. Soak chopped plant material in ethanol at a 1:3 ratio for 2448 hours, filter, then evaporate the solvent in a shallow tray in a well-ventilated, shaded area never over an open flame. The residue is a "concrete" or "absolute" that industrial buyers can use. This method is not suitable for aromatherapy-grade retail oil unless residual solvent testing is done.

5. Filtering and purifying without expensive equipment

Let the distillate stand in a separating funnel made from a clear plastic bottle with a tap, or use a syringe with a fine needle. Filter through clean cotton cloth, then through a coffee filter or compressed charcoal bed to remove waxes and water. For small volumes, a simple glass pipette or turkey baster separates the oil layer from the hydrosol. Store the hydrosol separately it sells as a by-product for linen sprays and cosmetics.

6. Storing oil to prevent spoilage in hot climates

Essential oils oxidise with heat, light and air. Use amber or dark green glass bottles with tight caps, fill to the neck to minimise headspace, and store in a cool, dark place ideally a clay pot or a pit 1 metre deep where temperature is stable. Never store in plastic for more than a few days; citrus and tea tree oils dissolve plasticisers. Label every bottle with crop, date, batch number and method.

SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE EVERY BATCH

  • Never evaporate solvent over an open flame. Ethanol and hexane vapours are explosive. Evaporate outdoors, in shade, with wind at your back, and keep a bucket of sand and a dry-powder fire extinguisher within reach.
  • Steam scalds. Open the still lid only after the pot has cooled and pressure has released. Wear long sleeves, closed shoes and eye protection when handling hot condensate.
  • Never apply undiluted essential oil to skin. Tea tree and eucalyptus oils cause sensitisation. Dilute to 13% in carrier oil before any skin contact, and patch-test on the forearm.
  • Keep children and livestock away from the distillation and solvent areas. Store solvents in clearly labelled, locked containers never in drinking-water bottles.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Harvest at peak oil: early morning, before the sun drives volatile compounds off the leaf.
  2. Chop to 25 cm to expose oil glands without creating mush that blocks steam flow.
  3. Check your seal: a leaking lid wastes 2040% of your yield test with a mirror for escaping steam.
  4. Control condenser water: keep it cold; replace every 2030 minutes or the vapour will not condense.
  5. Separate oil from hydrosol within 24 hours using a narrow-neck jar, syringe or separating bottle.
  6. Filter twice: cloth first, then fine filter or charcoal bed to remove waxes and water.
  7. Bottle in amber glass, fill to the neck, cap tight, and label with crop, date, batch and method.
  8. Store cool and dark: a clay pot or 1 m pit keeps temperature stable in hot climates. Never store in plastic long-term.
  9. Record every batch: input weight, oil volume, time, temperature and buyer feedback this is your business intelligence.
