1. Seed Selection and Rootstock Varieties

When you grow citrus from seed, the tree may not be the same as the parent, and it will take many years to fruit. That is why farmers use rootstocks. A rootstock is the bottom part of the tree that provides the roots. The top part, called the scion, is the variety you want fruit from. Choosing the right rootstock is key. In Africa, common rootstocks include Rough Lemon, which is vigorous and drought-tolerant but susceptible to some diseases; Sour Orange, which is hardy and tolerates calcareous soils but is sensitive to tristeza virus; and Cleopatra Mandarin, which is resistant to tristeza and produces good quality fruit. For sandy soils, Rough Lemon is often used. For heavy soils, Sour Orange may be better. Always buy rootstock seeds from a certified supplier to avoid diseases. Sow seeds in a nursery bed with well-drained soil, rich in organic matter. Plant seeds 1-2 cm deep, spaced 5 cm apart. Water regularly. Seeds germinate in 2-4 weeks. When seedlings are 15-20 cm tall, they are ready for grafting. Remember, rootstock choice affects tree size, fruit quality, and pest resistance. Talk to extension officers or other farmers to know what works in your area. Always keep records of your rootstock and scion sources.

2. Grafting Techniques for Citrus

Grafting joins a scion (the fruiting variety) to a rootstock. The most common method for citrus is the cleft graft, done when the rootstock is 1-2 cm thick. First, cut the rootstock horizontally about 20-30 cm above the soil. Make a vertical slit about 3 cm deep in the center of the cut surface. Prepare the scion: take a healthy shoot of the desired variety, about 10-15 cm long, with 2-3 buds. Cut the bottom of the scion into a wedge shape, about 3 cm long. Insert the wedge into the slit so the cambium layers (the green layer under the bark) of scion and rootstock meet on at least one side. Wrap the union tightly with grafting tape or polythene strip to hold it and prevent drying. Cover the scion and union with a plastic bag to keep humidity high. Place the grafted plant in a shaded area. After 2-3 weeks, check for new growth. Remove the bag gradually. Grafting is best done during active growth, usually at the start of the rainy season. Tools needed: sharp knife, grafting tape, plastic bags, and pruning shears. Keep tools clean with alcohol to avoid disease. Success rate can be 70-90% if done properly. Grafting allows you to grow many trees with the same good fruit quality and disease resistance.

3. Budding Methods and Timing

Budding is another way to propagate citrus. It involves inserting a single bud from the desired variety into the rootstock. The most common method is T-budding or shield budding. Do this when the rootstock is about 1 cm thick, usually 6-12 months after sowing. The best time is when the bark slips easily, which is during active growth, often in the rainy season. To T-bud, make a T-shaped cut on the rootstock about 15-20 cm above the ground. Cut a bud from the scion with a piece of bark (the shield) about 2-3 cm long. Insert the shield under the bark flaps of the T-cut. Wrap with budding tape, leaving the bud exposed. After 2-3 weeks, check if the bud is green and plump. If it is, cut the rootstock about 10 cm above the bud to force the bud to grow. Remove any shoots from the rootstock below the bud. Budding is faster and uses less scion wood than grafting. It also allows you to bud many rootstocks quickly. Keep the nursery free of weeds and pests. Water regularly but avoid waterlogging. With good care, the bud will grow into a new shoot, which will become the main trunk. Budding is a skill that requires practice, but it is very rewarding.

4. Nursery Setup and Care

A citrus nursery should be in a sunny spot with good drainage. Prepare beds 1 meter wide and 10-20 meters long, raised 15-20 cm above ground. Mix soil with compost or well-rotted manure at a ratio of 3:1. Sterilize the soil by solarization: cover the bed with clear plastic for 4-6 weeks to kill pests and diseases. Alternatively, use fumigants if available. Sow seeds or place potted rootstocks. If using pots, use black polythene bags of 20-30 cm diameter filled with the soil mix. Space pots 30 cm apart. Water daily, preferably in the morning. Fertilize with a balanced NPK (e.g., 17-17-17) every 4-6 weeks, about 1 teaspoon per plant. Control weeds by hand weeding. Watch for pests like aphids, leaf miners, and mites. Use neem oil or recommended pesticides. For diseases like citrus canker, remove affected leaves and apply copper-based fungicide. Shade young seedlings with a shade net (50% shade) for the first 2-3 months. Harden them by gradually removing shade. Keep records of sowing, grafting, and treatments. A well-managed nursery gives healthy, vigorous seedlings ready for transplanting in 12-18 months.

5. Transplanting Seedlings to Field

Transplant citrus seedlings when they are 12-18 months old, 60-80 cm tall, and have a stem diameter of at least 1 cm. Choose a site with full sun and well-drained soil, pH 5.5-6.5. Prepare the field by clearing weeds and ploughing. Dig planting holes 40 cm wide and 40 cm deep, spaced 4-6 meters apart for standard trees, or 2-3 meters for dwarf varieties. Mix topsoil with 1-2 buckets of compost or manure and 100 g of phosphate fertilizer. Place the seedling in the hole, ensuring the graft union is 15-20 cm above the soil. Backfill and firm the soil. Water immediately with 10-20 liters. Mulch around the base with dry grass to conserve moisture. Stake the tree if needed. Transplant at the start of the rainy season to ensure adequate moisture. After planting, water every 2-3 days for the first month, then reduce to weekly. Protect from livestock and pests. Apply fertilizer after 2-3 months. Keep the area weed-free. With proper care, trees will start bearing fruit in 3-5 years. Transplanting is stressful for the plant, so handle roots carefully and avoid damaging the graft union.

6. Certification and Disease-Free Planting Material

Diseases like citrus greening (HLB), tristeza, and canker can destroy your orchard. The best way to avoid them is to plant certified disease-free seedlings. Certification means the nursery follows strict rules to produce clean plants. In many African countries, the government or authorized agencies inspect nurseries and issue certificates. Buy seedlings only from certified nurseries. Look for a label or certificate that shows the variety, rootstock, and date. Avoid buying from roadside vendors unless you know their source. To produce disease-free material, nurseries must use clean rootstock seeds, sterilized soil, and control insects that spread diseases, like psyllids for HLB. They also test mother trees for viruses. If you want to grow your own seedlings, start with certified seeds and budwood. Keep records of all treatments and inspections. Certification also ensures the variety is true to type. It may cost a little more, but it saves you from losing your entire investment. Work with your local extension officer to find certified nurseries. Remember, healthy planting material is the foundation of a profitable citrus farm.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Citrus Propagation and Nursery Management in Africa

This practical reference consolidates the core operational knowledge from the seven lessons in this module. It is written for African agribusiness operators nursery managers, commercial citrus growers, extension officers and investors who need to move from theory to a functioning, profitable nursery. Every recommendation here is framed around the business case: certified planting material commands a price premium, reduces orchard replacement costs, and protects the long-term asset value of a citrus enterprise.

Across Africa, citrus is grown from Morocco to South Africa and from Senegal to Kenya. The dominant commercial rootstocks, the pest and disease pressures, and the certification regimes differ by region, but the propagation principles are universal. This reference brings them together in one place.

1. Seed Selection and Rootstock Varieties

Rootstock choice is the single most consequential decision in citrus propagation. It determines tree vigour, fruit quality, tolerance to soil-borne pathogens, and adaptability to local soils and climate. In Africa, the most widely used commercial rootstocks are:

Seed selection for rootstock production must come from certified mother trees that are free of CTV, Citrus greening (HLB), and other graft-transmissible pathogens. In Africa, where HLB is present in East Africa (Tanzania, Kenya, Ethiopia) and CTV is widespread, sourcing seed from a recognised certification scheme such as the Citrus Foundation Block in South Africa or the Citrus Certification Programme in Morocco is not optional. It is the foundation of orchard viability.

Business note: A certified rootstock seed can cost 510 times more than uncertified seed, but the resulting tree can produce 2040% more marketable fruit over its lifetime and avoids the risk of whole-orchard removal. The return on investment is measured in years of production, not seed cost.

2. Grafting Techniques for Citrus

Grafting is the union of a scion (the fruiting variety) with a rootstock. In Africa, the most common methods are:

  • Whip and tongue grafting: Used for rootstocks 610 mm diameter. High success rate when scion and rootstock are of similar size. Best done in late winter or early spring when sap is rising.
  • Cleft grafting: Used for larger rootstocks or top-working mature trees. Suitable for changing variety in an existing orchard.
  • Side grafting: Used when rootstock is larger than scion. Common in nursery production where rootstock is grown in containers.
  • Approach grafting: Used for difficult-to-graft combinations or when a high-value scion is scarce. Labour-intensive but reliable.

Grafting success depends on: cambium contact, temperature (2028C is optimal), humidity, and hygiene. In African nurseries, where power outages and water scarcity are common, grafting should be scheduled during the cooler, wetter months to reduce desiccation risk. Sterilise tools with 70% alcohol or a 10% bleach solution between trees to prevent pathogen spread.

3. Budding Methods and Timing

Budding is preferred over grafting for large-scale commercial nursery production because it is faster, requires less scion material, and produces a cleaner union. The two main methods in Africa are:

  • T-budding (shield budding): The most common method. A single bud is inserted into a T-shaped incision in the rootstock bark. Best done when the bark slips easily typically spring and early summer.
  • Chip budding: Used when bark is not slipping or when rootstock is small. A chip of wood with a bud is removed and fitted into a matching cut.

Timing is critical. In Southern Africa, budding is best from March to May (autumn) and September to November (spring). In East Africa, the long rains (MarchMay) and short rains (OctoberDecember) define the windows. Budding during extreme heat or drought stress reduces take rates dramatically.

Business note: A skilled budder can complete 300500 buds per day. At a nursery producing 50,000 trees per year, labour for budding represents one of the largest variable costs. Training and retaining skilled budders is a competitive advantage.

4. Nursery Setup and Care

A citrus nursery must be designed to exclude pathogens and pests. Key requirements:

  • Site: Well-drained, level land, away from old citrus orchards. Minimum 100 m isolation from any citrus tree.
  • Water: Clean, pathogen-free water. Borehole or municipal water is preferred over river water.
  • Media: Sterilised potting mix (e.g., pine bark, vermiculite, sand). Never use field soil without sterilisation.
  • Containers: 46 litre bags or pots, raised on benches 3050 cm above ground to prevent soil splash.
  • Shade: 3050% shade cloth for young grafts; full sun after hardening.
  • Hygiene: Footbaths, tool sterilisation, restricted access, and regular scouting for pests and diseases.

SAFETY WARNING Pesticide and Pathogen Handling

Citrus nurseries in Africa frequently use copper-based fungicides, insecticides (e.g., imidacloprid) and sterilants (e.g., bleach, alcohol). These chemicals are hazardous if mishandled. Always wear gloves, eye protection and a respirator when mixing or applying. Never eat, drink or smoke during application. Store chemicals in a locked, ventilated area away from children and livestock. In areas where HLB is present, strict hygiene is a biosecurity requirement not a suggestion. A single infected bud can destroy an entire nursery's certification status and market access.

5. Transplanting Seedlings to Field

Transplanting is the point at which nursery investment converts into orchard asset. Key steps:

  • Site preparation: Clear land, rip to 6080 cm depth, and mark planting positions at 46 m spacing (depending on rootstock and variety).
  • Planting hole: 40 40 40 cm. Mix topsoil with compost and a phosphate fertiliser (e.g., 200 g superphosphate per hole).
  • Timing: Plant at the onset of rains. In irrigated systems, plant during cooler months to reduce transplant shock.
  • Handling: Remove container without disturbing roots. Set the tree at the same depth as in the nursery. Do not bury the graft union.
  • Aftercare: Water immediately; mulch; stake if windy; protect from livestock and weeds.

Business note: Transplant shock can cost 1020% of trees in the first year. Proper hardening, timing and irrigation reduce losses to under 5%. At a tree cost of US$38, that is a direct saving of thousands of dollars per hectare.

6. Certification and Disease-Free Planting Material

Certification is the mechanism that guarantees a tree is true-to-type and free from graft-transmissible pathogens. In Africa, certification schemes vary by country:

  • South Africa: Citrus Foundation Block (CFB) and the Citrus Improvement Scheme (CIS).
  • Morocco: INRA certification programme.
  • Kenya: Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service (KEPHIS) and the Citrus Certification Programme.
  • Tanzania: Tanzania Official Seed Certification Institute (TOSCI).
  • Nigeria: National Agricultural Seeds Council (NASC).

Certified trees carry a premium price but open access to formal markets, export chains and bank financing. For African agribusinesses, certification is not a cost it is an investment in market access and asset protection.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Source rootstock seed and budwood only from certified, pathogen-tested mother trees.
  2. Match rootstock to soil type, climate and target market (fresh vs. processing).
  3. Sterilise all grafting and budding tools between trees with 70% alcohol or 10% bleach.
  4. Bud or graft when bark slips and temperatures are 2028C; avoid extreme heat or drought stress.
  5. Maintain nursery hygiene: footbaths, restricted access, raised benches, sterile media.
  6. Harden seedlings for 24 weeks before transplanting; plant at the onset of rains.
  7. Never bury the graft union; keep it 1015 cm above soil level.
  8. Keep certification records for every batch traceability is a market requirement.
