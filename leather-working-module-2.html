1. Selection of Raw Hides

Selecting the right raw hide or skin is the first step to good leather. In Africa, you will mostly work with goat, sheep, cow, or camel hides. Goat skins are thin and flexible, good for gloves and bookbinding. Cow hides are thick and strong, used for belts and shoes. Sheep skins are soft but delicate, best for garments. Inspect the hide for holes, cuts, scars, or insect damage. Reject any hide with green or black patches, which mean bacterial rot. A fresh hide should smell slightly meaty, not foul. If you buy from a slaughterhouse, ask for the hide immediately after slaughter. If you buy from a market, check that the hide has been kept cool or salted. In hot African climates, a hide can rot within 6 hours if left in the sun. So salt it or start soaking within 2 hours. For small-scale tanning, goat skins are easiest for beginners because they are thin and require less chemicals. Cow hides need more time and stronger solutions. Weigh the hide: a fresh goat skin weighs about 1.5â2 kg, a cow hide 20â30 kg. You will need this weight to calculate chemical amounts later. Also check the grain side (the hair side) for tick bites or scars. These will show in the final leather. A good hide has uniform thickness, no bad odour, and no visible decay. If you are not tanning immediately, salt the hide with coarse salt (about 1 kg salt per 1 kg hide) and roll it up. Store in a cool, dry place. Never use a hide that has been lying in water for daysâit will be weak and may already be rotting. Remember: poor selection means poor leather, no matter how good your tanning is.

2. Soaking and Rehydration

Soaking brings a dried or salted hide back to its original softness. If you skip this, the hide will be stiff and chemicals won't penetrate. For a dried goat skin, use a clean plastic drum or basin. Add 20 litres of clean water per skin. If the skin is very dry, add 1 gram of salt per litre to help rehydrate gently. Soak for 12 to 24 hours at room temperature (25â30Â°C). In hot weather, change the water every 6 hours to prevent bacteria. For salted hides, first rinse off the salt with cold water, then soak in fresh water for 8 to 12 hours. Cow hides need longer: 24 to 48 hours, with water changes every 8 hours. Check softness by pinching the hideâif it feels like a wet cloth, it's ready. If it still has hard spots, soak longer. You can add 0.5% of a wetting agent (like mild soap) to speed up soaking, but never use bleach or detergent. After soaking, the hide should be pliable and slightly swollen. If it smells bad, add 1 gram of sodium carbonate (washing soda) per litre and soak for another 2 hours, then rinse. For rehydration of salted hides, you must also remove dirt and blood. Use a soft brush to scrub the flesh side. Do not use hot waterâit can cook the hide. Always use clean water; dirty water introduces bacteria. In rural areas, rainwater is best because it's soft. After soaking, drain the water and proceed to fleshing immediately. If you leave a soaked hide sitting for more than 2 hours, it will start to rot. So plan your time. For a small workshop, soak 5 goat skins in a 100-litre drum. Weigh them down with a clean stone to keep them submerged. Never soak in metal containers that rustâuse plastic or concrete. Proper soaking makes the next steps easier and gives you better leather.

3. Fleshing and Trimming

Fleshing removes the leftover meat, fat, and membrane from the flesh side of the hide. If you leave this on, it will rot and stop chemicals from working. You need a fleshing beam or a smooth log. A simple beam is a wooden plank (2 metres long, 30 cm wide) mounted at an angle. Or use a clean, smooth tree trunk. Lay the hide flesh-side up over the beam. Use a fleshing knifeâa dull, curved blade with two handles. In Africa, many tanners use a traditional knife called a 'mbege' or a dull machete. Hold the knife at a 30-degree angle and push away from you, scraping off the fat and meat. Work from the neck down to the tail. Do not cut into the hide. For a goat skin, use light pressure; for a cow hide, use firmer strokes. Take short strokes, 10â15 cm long. After every few strokes, wipe the knife clean. If the hide is slippery, sprinkle a little wood ash or sand on it for grip. For small skins, you can also flesh on a flat table with a dull knife. Trimming means cutting away the edgesâlegs, head, and any torn partsâwith a sharp pair of shears or a knife. Trim about 2 cm from the edges to remove thin, weak areas. Also cut off any remaining tail or ears. After fleshing, the hide should be uniform in thickness and free of fat. You can check by holding it up to lightâno dark patches. If you see fat, scrape again. Fleshing is hard work; a goat skin takes 20â30 minutes, a cow hide 1â2 hours. Do it within 2 hours of soaking. After fleshing, rinse the hide in clean water to remove scraps. Then proceed to unhairing. A well-fleshed hide absorbs chemicals evenly and gives soft, strong leather. A poorly fleshed hide will have hard spots and may rot. So take your time and keep your knife sharp but not too sharp.

4. Unhairing and Liming

Unhairing removes the hair and epidermis (outer skin) and swells the hide. The traditional method uses lime (calcium hydroxide) and water. For 10 kg of hides, use 1 kg of lime in 30 litres of water. Mix in a plastic drum. Add 0.5 kg of sodium sulfide (if available) to speed up hair removal, but this is dangerousâwear gloves and goggles. Without sulfide, it takes 3â5 days; with it, 1â2 days. Soak the fleshed hides in this lime bath. Stir twice a day with a wooden stick. The hide will swell and become slippery. Check after 24 hours: try pulling hair from the back. If it comes off easily, it's ready. If not, leave longer. For goat skins, 2 days is usually enough. For cow hides, 4â5 days. After unhairing, remove the hide and scrape off the loosened hair and epidermis with a dull knife over a beam. Rinse in clean water. Then put the hide back into a fresh lime bath (1 kg lime per 20 litres water) for another 12â24 hours to complete swelling. This is called 'liming'. The hide becomes plump and firm. Then remove and rinse. You must now 'delime' to remove the lime. But first, trim any remaining hair or flesh. Liming also opens up the fibre structure. In Africa, some tanners use wood ash instead of lime. Mix 2 kg ash with 10 litres water, soak for 3 days. But lime is more reliable. Always add lime to water, not water to limeâit can splash. Keep children and animals away. After liming, the hide is ready for deliming and bating. If you leave it in lime too long (more than 7 days), the hide will weaken. So plan your schedule. A well-limed hide is white, swollen, and has no hair. This step is critical for soft leather.

5. Deliming and Bating

After liming, the hide is swollen and alkaline (pH about 12). Deliming lowers the pH to about 8â9 and removes lime. Bating further softens the hide and removes non-structural proteins. For deliming, use ammonium sulfate or ammonium chloride. For 10 kg of hides, dissolve 0.5 kg of ammonium sulfate in 20 litres of water. Soak the limed hides for 1â2 hours, stirring every 30 minutes. Check the pH with litmus paperâit should be around 8.5. If not, add more ammonium sulfate. You can also use vinegar (acetic acid) but it's weaker: 1 litre vinegar per 10 litres water, soak for 2 hours. After deliming, rinse the hides in clean water. Then bate. Bating uses enzymes, traditionally from animal dung (like chicken or pigeon droppings). Mix 1 kg of fresh dung in 10 litres of water, strain, and soak the hides for 12â24 hours at 30â35Â°C. The enzymes break down elastin and make the hide soft. In modern tanneries, use commercial bating powder (0.2% of hide weight) for 1 hour. For small-scale, dung is effective but smelly. After bating, the hide should be soft and floppy, like a wet cloth. If it still feels rubbery, bate longer. Then rinse well. Now the hide is ready for pickling. Deliming and bating must be done carefully: if you skip deliming, the pickle acid will be neutralized and won't work. If you over-bate, the hide can become too loose. Test by pressing a finger into the hideâit should leave a mark that slowly disappears. For goat skins, 12 hours bating is enough; cow hides need 24 hours. Always use clean water. After bating, drain and proceed immediately to pickling. Do not let the hide dry out. This step gives you soft, pliable leather.

6. Pickling and Depickling

Pickling acidifies the hide to pH 2â3 and preserves it before tanning. It also allows tanning agents to penetrate. For 10 kg of hides, use 1 kg of salt and 0.5 litre of sulfuric acid (or 1 litre of formic acid) in 20 litres of water. Always add acid to water, never water to acidâit can boil and splash. Wear gloves and goggles. Soak the bated hides in this pickle solution for 12â24 hours. Stir every 2 hours. The hide will become white and firm. Check pH with litmus paperâit should be around 2.5. If higher, add more acid. After pickling, the hide can be stored for weeks if kept in the pickle solution. But for tanning, you must depickle. Depickling means raising the pH to about 4â5 before tanning. To depickle, add 0.5 kg of sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) or 1 kg of sodium acetate to the pickle bath slowly. Stir well. Add a little at a timeâit will fizz. Check pH every 30 minutes until it reaches 4.5. Then soak for another 2 hours. For vegetable tanning, you can depickle to pH 4.5; for chrome tanning, pH 3.5â4. After depickling, the hide is ready for the tanning process. If you skip depickling, the tanning agent won't work properly and the leather will be uneven. For small-scale tanners, pickling and depickling can be done in a plastic drum. Always test pH with litmus paper or a cheap pH meter. In Africa, many tanners use formic acid instead of sulfuric because it's safer. Use 1 litre formic acid per 20 litres water. After pickling, the hide should be plump and white. After depickling, it should be soft and slightly swollen. Now you can proceed to tanning. Store pickled hides in a cool place away from sunlight. Never mix pickled hides with limed hides. This step is critical for good leather.

Practical Reference: Hide and Skin Preparation



In African leather value chains, the difference between a profitable tannery and a loss-making one is decided long before the first drum turns. Raw material accounts for roughly 60â70% of the cost of finished leather, so every handling decision during preparation either protects or destroys margin. This practical reference consolidates the operational benchmarks, water and chemical quantities, and quality thresholds you need to run a hide and skin preparation line â from the abattoir gate to the pickled pelt â with the African market in mind.



Across the continent, tanneries process a wide range of raw stocks: cattle hides from Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, Tanzania, and South Africa; goatskins from Somalia, Sudan, and Mali; sheepskins from Egypt, Morocco, and South Africa. Each species and each origin carries its own handling quirks, but the preparation sequence â selection, soaking, fleshing, unhairing, deliming, bating, pickling â is universal. Master it, and you build a platform for consistent crust and finished leather. Neglect it, and no amount of finishing can rescue the batch.



Why Preparation Is a Business Decision



Preparation is where you decide your yield. A hide that is properly selected, soaked without over-hydration, and correctly limed will yield more usable area per kilogram of raw material. A hide that is over-soaked loses substance; one that is under-fleshed carries fat that blocks chemical penetration; one that is badly limed produces loose grain and rejects. In African markets where buyers increasingly demand consistent thickness, grain clarity, and low chemical residue, preparation is your quality gate.



Water and chemical costs dominate the preparation budget. A typical beamhouse consumes 20â40 litres of water per kilogram of hide and significant quantities of lime, sodium sulphide, ammonium salts, and acid. Managing these inputs is both an environmental and a financial imperative, particularly in water-scarce regions such as North Africa and the Sahel.

Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Grade before you soak. Record flay damage, holes, scars, and cure quality. Reject or downgrade defective hides at intake â not after liming.

  2. Weigh and label every batch. All chemical percentages depend on accurate soaked weight. Guessing is the fastest route to inconsistent leather.

  3. Control soak water temperature. 25â30 Â°C for cattle, 25â28 Â°C for goats and sheep. Above 35 Â°C, bacteria multiply and grain slips.

  4. Flesh within 24 hours of soak. Delayed fleshing allows fat to oxidise and blocks subsequent chemical penetration.

  5. Verify lime strength. Titrate lime liquor daily; a drop in alkalinity means incomplete unhairing and loose grain.

  6. Delime to pH 8.5â9.0 before bating. Residual lime kills bating enzymes and produces harsh, papery leather.

  7. Bate with the right enzyme for the species. Goatskins need gentler bating than cattle hides; over-bating causes loose grain.

  8. Pickle to pH 2.5â3.0 and hold. Check pH before and after the drum; drift means uneven chrome uptake and patchy dyeing.
