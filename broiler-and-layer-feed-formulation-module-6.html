1. Nutrient Requirements for Pullet Development (0-18 weeks)

Pullets are young female chickens from day-old to point of lay. Their nutrition from 0 to 18 weeks determines how well they will lay as adults. In Africa, many farmers use local feedstuffs like maize, soybean meal, fishmeal, and sunflower cake. The key nutrients are energy, protein, amino acids, vitamins, and minerals. Energy is measured in metabolizable energy (ME) and should be around 2800-2900 kcal/kg for starter (0-6 weeks), 2700-2800 kcal/kg for grower (7-12 weeks), and 2600-2700 kcal/kg for developer (13-18 weeks). Protein: starter 20-22%, grower 16-18%, developer 15-16%. But protein quality matters: lysine, methionine, and tryptophan are critical. For example, starter needs 1.1% lysine, 0.45% methionine. Use tools like the Pearson Square to balance energy and protein with local ingredients. A typical starter diet: 55% maize, 30% soybean meal, 8% fishmeal, 4% wheat bran, 3% premix (vitamins, minerals, salt, limestone). Always add 0.3% salt and 1% limestone for calcium. For growers, reduce fishmeal and increase bran to lower cost. Clean water is essential. In hot African climates, add electrolytes. Weigh feed daily and keep records. Poor pullet development leads to small eggs and low production. Vaccinate and deworm regularly. By 18 weeks, pullets should weigh 1.3-1.5 kg for light breeds or 1.5-1.8 kg for heavy breeds. Use a hanging scale to check. Adjust feed if underweight. This phase is an investment; skimping now reduces future egg income.

2. Pre-Lay and Early Lay Diets (18-22 weeks)

Between 18 and 22 weeks, pullets transition to laying. This is the pre-lay and early lay phase. The bird's body is still growing while starting to produce eggs. Nutrient needs change: calcium must increase from 1% to 2-2.5% to build medullary bone for shell formation. But too much calcium too early can damage kidneys. A pre-lay diet (18-20 weeks) should have 2% calcium, 16% protein, and 2750 kcal/kg ME. At 20-22 weeks, switch to early lay diet with 2.5-3% calcium, 16-17% protein, and 2700-2750 kcal/kg ME. Use limestone or oyster shell as calcium sources; particle size matterslarger particles stay in gizzard longer. In Africa, oyster shell may be expensive; alternative is ground limestone or even crushed eggshells (sterilized). Phosphorus should be 0.4-0.45% available. A typical early lay diet: 60% maize, 20% soybean meal, 5% fishmeal, 5% wheat bran, 7% limestone, 0.3% salt, 0.2% methionine, 2.5% premix. Mix well. Provide feed ad libitum but monitor body weight. At 22 weeks, expect 5-10% egg production. If birds are underweight, delay switching to layer diet. Use a simple feed formulation app like FeedPro or Excel with local prices. Record daily egg numbers. In hot weather, feed early morning and evening. Ensure constant water. This phase sets the stage for peak lay. Mistakes here cause small eggs or prolapse. Work with a vet or extension officer.

3. Peak Lay and Post-Peak Layer Diets

Peak lay occurs around 28-32 weeks when hens produce 90-95% eggs. Nutrient demand is highest. Peak layer diet should have 17-18% protein, 2800-2850 kcal/kg ME, 3.5-4% calcium, and 0.45% available phosphorus. Amino acids: lysine 0.8%, methionine 0.38%. After peak (32-60 weeks), production slowly declines. Post-peak diet can have 16-17% protein, 2750-2800 kcal/kg ME, 4-4.2% calcium, and 0.4% phosphorus. The main difference is calcium level increases as shell quality becomes critical. Use a phase-feeding approach: change diet every 6-8 weeks. In Africa, maize is the main energy source, but consider cassava chips or sorghum if cheaper. Protein from soybean meal, fishmeal, groundnut cake, or sunflower cake. Always check for aflatoxins in maize and groundnut. Use a toxin binder. For calcium, mix fine and coarse limestone (50:50) for better absorption. Add 0.1% methionine and 0.05% lysine if using alternative proteins. Provide oyster shell free-choice from 40 weeks. Monitor feed intake: hens eat 110-120 g/day. If intake drops, increase nutrient density. Use a feed mill or mix on-farm with a horizontal mixer. Weigh ingredients accurately. Keep records of egg weight, shell strength, and mortality. In heat stress, add vitamin C and electrolytes. Cull non-layers. This phase determines profitability.

4. Calcium and Phosphorus Management for Shell Quality

Eggshell is 95% calcium carbonate. A hen needs 4-4.5 g calcium per egg. Blood calcium is low; she mobilizes from medullary bone. If diet lacks calcium, bones weaken and shells become thin. Calcium sources: limestone (38% Ca), oyster shell (38% Ca), dicalcium phosphate (22% Ca, 18% P). In Africa, limestone is common; oyster shell may be imported. Use particle size: fine (0-1 mm) for immediate absorption, coarse (2-5 mm) for slow release. A 50:50 mix is ideal. Phosphorus is critical for bone and shell. Available phosphorus should be 0.4-0.45% in layer diets. Total phosphorus 0.6-0.7%. Too much phosphorus binds calcium and reduces shell quality. Use phytase enzyme to release plant phosphorus, reducing inorganic phosphate cost. For example, add 500 FTU/kg phytase to save 0.1% dicalcium phosphate. Calcium to phosphorus ratio should be 10:1 to 12:1. In hot climates, calcium absorption decreases; increase calcium by 0.2% or add vitamin D3. Provide oyster shell free-choice from 40 weeks. Check shell thickness with a micrometer; target 0.35-0.40 mm. Cracked eggs indicate calcium or phosphorus imbalance. Use a simple feed formulation software like WinFeed or Excel. Test limestone purity; some local sources have low calcium. Mix thoroughly to avoid segregation. Store feed in dry place. This management ensures strong shells and high market value.

5. Formulating Layer Diets for Egg Production and Eggshell Strength

Formulating a layer diet requires balancing energy, protein, amino acids, calcium, and phosphorus at least cost. Start with a nutrient specification table: ME 2750 kcal/kg, CP 17%, lysine 0.8%, methionine 0.38%, calcium 4%, available P 0.45%. Then list available ingredients with their nutrient composition and prices. Common African ingredients: maize (ME 3350 kcal/kg, CP 9%), soybean meal (ME 2440, CP 44%), fishmeal (ME 2800, CP 60%), wheat bran (ME 1300, CP 15%), limestone (Ca 38%), dicalcium phosphate (Ca 22%, P 18%), salt, premix. Use the Pearson Square or a linear programming tool like FeedPro, WinFeed, or Excel Solver. For example, to make 100 kg: 60 kg maize, 20 kg soybean meal, 5 kg fishmeal, 5 kg wheat bran, 7 kg limestone, 1 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.3 kg salt, 0.2 kg methionine, 1.5 kg premix. This gives approximately 17% CP, 4% Ca, 0.45% available P. Adjust based on actual analysis. For eggshell strength, ensure calcium particle size and add 0.1% vitamin C in heat stress. Use a mixer to blend for 5-10 minutes. Test feed for uniformity. Provide feed ad libitum. Monitor egg production, egg weight, shell thickness. If shells are weak, increase coarse limestone. If production drops, check protein and energy. Keep records. In Africa, use local ingredients to reduce cost but ensure no toxins. This formulation can achieve 85-90% lay and strong shells.

6. Case Study: Formulating a Phase-Feeding Program for Layers

A small farm in Kenya with 500 layers wants a phase-feeding program. They have maize, soybean meal, fishmeal, wheat bran, limestone, dicalcium phosphate, salt, premix, methionine, and lysine. They want to maximize profit. We design four phases: 0-6 weeks starter, 7-18 weeks grower, 19-22 weeks pre-lay, 23-32 weeks peak, 33-60 weeks post-peak. But module focuses on layer phases. For pre-lay (19-22 weeks): 62% maize, 18% soybean meal, 5% fishmeal, 5% wheat bran, 7% limestone, 1% dicalcium phosphate, 0.3% salt, 0.2% methionine, 1.5% premix. This gives 16% CP, 2.5% Ca, 0.45% P. For peak (23-32 weeks): 60% maize, 20% soybean meal, 5% fishmeal, 4% wheat bran, 8% limestone, 1% dicalcium phosphate, 0.3% salt, 0.2% methionine, 1.5% premix. Gives 17.5% CP, 3.8% Ca. For post-peak (33-60 weeks): 62% maize, 18% soybean meal, 4% fishmeal, 4% wheat bran, 9% limestone, 0.8% dicalcium phosphate, 0.3% salt, 0.2% methionine, 1.7% premix. Gives 16.5% CP, 4.2% Ca. They mix on-farm with a horizontal mixer. They record daily feed intake, egg production, and shell quality. After 6 months, they achieve 88% peak lay, 92% eggshell strength, and profit of $0.05 per egg. They adjust if maize price rises by using cassava chips. This case shows phase-feeding works in Africa.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Layer Feed Formulation for Pullet and Laying Phases

In African egg production, feed represents 6575% of total cost of production. A well-managed layer flock should generate a return on feed investment through higher hen-housed egg numbers, better eggshell quality, and lower mortality. This practical reference distils the nutrient specifications, phase-feeding logic, and calciumphosphorus management required to build a profitable layer enterprise from day-old pullet to end of lay. It is written for farm managers, feed millers, and agripreneurs who need numbers they can act on today.

Why Phase Feeding Matters in Africa

Many small and medium layer farms in Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Zambia, and South Africa still feed a single "layer mash" from point-of-lay to culling. This is expensive and inefficient. A pullet at 10 weeks needs 1516% crude protein and ~1% calcium to build skeletal frame; a hen at peak lay needs 1718% CP and 3.84.2% calcium to sustain 90%+ production and shell quality. Feeding the same diet to both wastes protein in the pullet phase and under-supplies calcium at peak the two most common causes of poor persistency and cracked eggs on African farms.

Phase feeding typically delivers a 612% reduction in feed cost per dozen eggs, mainly through lower protein and energy wastage outside the phase where they are needed. For a 5,000-bird flock, that can mean USD 3,0006,000 saved per cycle.

Practical Calcium and Phosphorus Management

Calcium is the single most important mineral in layer nutrition. A hen producing a 60 g egg deposits roughly 2.02.3 g of calcium into the shell and she can only absorb about 5060% of dietary calcium. This is why layer diets must contain 3.84.2% Ca at peak, with a portion supplied as coarse limestone (24 mm particles) that dissolves slowly through the night when shell formation is most active.

Phosphorus must be managed carefully. Excess available phosphorus (aP) binds calcium, reduces shell quality, and increases phosphorus excretion into the environment. Modern layer recommendations target 0.300.40% available P during lay, with higher levels (0.400.45%) in the pullet phase for bone development. Phytase enzymes (5001,000 FTU/kg) are now standard in African commercial mills because they release plant-bound phosphorus and reduce reliance on imported monocalcium phosphate a major cost and forex issue.

SAFETY WARNING Calcium and Phosphorus Imbalance

Never feed layer diets containing more than 4.5% calcium to pullets under 16 weeks excess calcium causes urolithiasis (kidney stones), permanent kidney damage, and high mortality. Conversely, do not feed a pullet or grower diet (1.2% Ca) to hens in lay: acute calcium deficiency causes cage layer fatigue, brittle bones, and shell-less eggs within 710 days. Always verify the Ca:P ratio (target 10:1 to 12:1 in layer diets) with a laboratory analysis before mixing bulk batches. Store limestone and phosphate separately and clearly labelled to prevent cross-contamination at the mill.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Split the flock cycle into at least 5 phases: starter, grower, pre-lay, peak, post-peak.
  • Target 3.84.2% Ca at peak lay, with 5065% as coarse limestone (24 mm).
  • Keep available P at 0.300.40% during lay; use phytase to cut DCP cost.
  • Test maize for aflatoxin every delivery never mix visibly mouldy grain.
  • Weigh 100 eggs monthly to monitor egg weight and shell quality trends.
  • Record daily feed intake per bird; a drop signals heat stress, disease, or diet fault.
  • Provide cool, clean water ad libitum a layer drinks 2 her feed weight daily.
  • Re-evaluate the diet every 6 weeks with your nutritionist or feed miller.
