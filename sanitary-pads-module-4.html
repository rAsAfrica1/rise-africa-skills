1. Raw Material Preparation

Before you start making pads, you need to gather and prepare your raw materials. For disposable pads, the core materials are: absorbent fluff pulp (wood pulp), super absorbent polymer (SAP), non-woven fabric for the top sheet, polyethylene film for the back sheet, and adhesive glue. In Africa, you can source fluff pulp from local paper mills or import from suppliers in South Africa or Egypt. SAP is imported from countries like China or India. Non-woven fabric can be purchased from textile factories in Nigeria or Kenya. You will also need release paper for the adhesive, and wrapping film for packaging.

Once you have your materials, you must prepare them. Fluff pulp comes in large rolls or bales. You need to break it down into fluffy fibers using a hammer mill or a grinder. This increases its absorbency. SAP comes as a powder; you must store it in a dry place because it absorbs moisture from the air. Non-woven fabric and polyethylene film need to be cut to the correct width for your pad size. For a standard pad, the top sheet and back sheet are about 7 cm wide for a panty liner, or 10-12 cm for a regular pad.

You also need to prepare your workspace. Set up a clean, dry area with tables for laying out materials. Use gloves and masks to avoid contamination. Measure and cut materials in batches to save time. For example, cut 100 top sheets and 100 back sheets at once. Keep SAP in an airtight container. Weigh the SAP you need per pad: typically 0.5 to 1 gram for a regular pad. Fluff pulp per pad: about 3 to 5 grams. Prepare adhesive by checking its viscosity; if too thick, warm it slightly. Always keep a record of batch numbers and dates for quality control. Proper preparation reduces waste and ensures smooth production.

2. Forming and Layering

Forming and layering is where you build the pad's absorbent core and assemble the layers. The core is made of fluff pulp mixed with SAP. First, fluff the pulp using a hammer mill or a grinder until it's soft and fluffy. Then, mix in SAP powder. A typical ratio is 1 part SAP to 5 parts fluff pulp by weight. For a regular pad, use about 4 grams of fluff and 0.8 grams of SAP. Mix them thoroughly in a mixing drum or by hand with gloves. This mixture is your absorbent core.

Next, you need to form the core into a rectangular shape. Use a mold or a simple wooden frame to shape the core. The core should be about 6-8 cm wide and 20-25 cm long for a regular pad. Press it lightly to hold its shape. If you don't have a mold, you can spread the mixture evenly on a flat surface and cut it to size later. But for consistency, a mold is better.

Now, layer the pad. Start with the back sheet: polyethylene film, cut to the same size as the core plus 1 cm margin all around. Place the core on top of the back sheet. Then cover with the top sheet: non-woven fabric, also cut to size. The top sheet should be slightly larger than the core to allow for sealing. If you are making a winged pad, the wings are extensions of the top sheet and back sheet on the sides. You will attach them later. For now, focus on the basic three layers: back sheet, core, top sheet.

After layering, you need to bond the layers together. You can use a heat sealer or a simple iron with a cloth over the pad to melt the non-woven and polyethylene slightly, creating a seal. Alternatively, use adhesive glue along the edges. Make sure the layers are aligned and flat. Any wrinkles will cause leaks. Work on a clean table, and do one pad at a time. For small-scale production, you can layer 10-20 pads at once and then bond them. Check each pad for even distribution of the core. A well-formed pad should be smooth and uniform.

3. Cutting and Shaping

After forming and layering, you need to cut and shape the pads to their final size and contour. Disposable pads come in various shapes: rectangular, contoured (hourglass), and with wings. For a basic rectangular pad, you simply cut the layered stack to the desired length and width. Use a sharp rotary cutter or a die-cut press for accuracy. For a regular pad, the final dimensions are typically 22-25 cm long and 7-8 cm wide. For a panty liner, 15-18 cm long and 5-6 cm wide. If you don't have a die-cut machine, you can use a template made of wood or cardboard and cut with a sharp utility knife on a cutting mat. Always cut on a clean, hard surface.

For contoured pads, you need to shape the sides to fit the body. The contour is narrower at the center and wider at the ends. You can create a template with the desired shape, then trace it onto the layered pads and cut. Alternatively, use a die-cut machine with a custom die. The contoured shape reduces bulk and improves comfort. For winged pads, you cut the basic shape first, then cut wings on the sides. Wings are typically 3-4 cm long and 2-3 cm wide, extending from the sides of the pad. They are used to wrap around the underwear. Cut wings as part of the top sheet and back sheet, ensuring they are sealed together.

After cutting, you need to seal the edges to prevent the core from leaking out. Use a heat sealer or a hot iron with a protective cloth to seal the edges of the pad. You can also use adhesive glue along the edges. For a clean finish, trim any excess material. Check each pad for sharp edges or loose fibers. A good cut and shape ensures the pad fits well and performs its function. For small-scale production, you can cut 50-100 pads per hour with a rotary cutter. Always wear gloves to avoid cuts and keep the cutting area clean. Store cut pads in a dust-free container until the next step.

4. Adhesive Application and Wing Attachment

Adhesive application is critical for keeping the pad in place. You need two types of adhesive: one for the back of the pad to stick to the underwear, and one for the wings if they are present. Use a hot melt adhesive glue, which is applied in a thin line or pattern. For the back sheet, apply a strip of adhesive about 1-2 cm wide along the center of the pad's back. You can use a glue gun or a glue applicator machine. For small-scale production, a glue gun works fine. Apply the glue in a zigzag or dot pattern to allow flexibility. The adhesive should be pressure-sensitive, meaning it sticks when pressed. After applying, cover the adhesive with release paper (silicone-coated paper) to prevent it from sticking to other surfaces before use.

For winged pads, you need to attach wings to the sides. Wings are usually extensions of the top sheet and back sheet, but if they are separate, you can attach them by heat sealing or gluing. Cut wing pieces from non-woven fabric and polyethylene film. Place them on the sides of the pad, aligning with the edges. Seal them using a heat sealer or glue. Ensure the wings are securely attached and can fold around underwear. The wings also need adhesive on their back side to stick to the underwear. Apply a small strip of adhesive on each wing and cover with release paper.

After applying adhesive, test a few pads to ensure they stick well but don't tear the underwear. The adhesive should not be too strong or too weak. Store pads in a cool, dry place because heat can melt the adhesive. For quality control, check that the release paper is easy to peel off and that the adhesive remains on the pad, not on the paper. If you are using a glue gun, keep the nozzle clean to avoid clogs. Work in a well-ventilated area. For medium-scale production, consider a hot melt glue applicator with a nozzle that applies a consistent pattern. This speeds up production and ensures uniformity. Always wear gloves and avoid skin contact with hot glue.

5. Packaging and Sterilization

Packaging protects the pads from contamination and makes them easy to carry and store. For disposable pads, each pad is usually wrapped individually in a thin plastic film or paper wrapper. You can use a flow wrap machine or manually wrap each pad. For manual wrapping, cut a piece of wrapper about 12 cm x 20 cm. Place the pad in the center, fold the wrapper over, and seal the edges with a heat sealer or tape. Ensure the wrapper is sealed on all sides to keep out dust and moisture. For bulk packaging, place 8-10 individually wrapped pads in a larger bag or box. Label the package with the brand name, pad type, quantity, and manufacturing date. Use a printing stamp or sticker.

Sterilization is not always required for disposable pads, but if you want to ensure they are free from bacteria, you can use gamma irradiation or ethylene oxide (EtO) sterilization. These are industrial processes and may not be available for small-scale producers. Alternatively, you can use UV light sterilization, but it is less effective. For most small-scale producers, the key is to maintain a clean production environment. Use gloves, masks, and clean equipment. Avoid touching the absorbent core with bare hands. If you cannot sterilize, focus on hygienic packaging: wrap pads immediately after production, and store in a clean, dry place.

For sterilization, if you have access to a gamma irradiation facility, send your packaged pads for treatment. The typical dose is 25 kGy. This kills bacteria and viruses. EtO sterilization uses ethylene oxide gas, but it requires aeration to remove residues. UV sterilization can be done with a UV lamp, but it only sterilizes surfaces. For small-scale, you can use a simple sterilization method: place wrapped pads under a UV lamp for 30 minutes on each side. However, this is not a substitute for proper industrial sterilization. Always test for sterility by sending samples to a lab. For packaging, use food-grade plastic or paper. Avoid recycled materials that may contain contaminants. Proper packaging and sterilization ensure the pads are safe for use.

6. Quality Control in Manufacturing

Quality control (QC) is essential to ensure every pad meets standards for absorbency, comfort, and safety. Start by inspecting raw materials: check fluff pulp for lumps, SAP for clumping, and non-woven for holes. Reject any material that is contaminated or damaged. During production, check each step. After forming, weigh the core to ensure it has the right amount of fluff and SAP. For a regular pad, the core should weigh 4-5 grams. After cutting, check dimensions: length and width within 0.5 cm tolerance. After adhesive application, test the stickiness by pressing a pad onto a piece of fabric and pulling gently. It should stick but not tear the fabric.

After packaging, perform final QC on a sample of pads. Test absorbency by pouring 5 ml of saline solution (or water with a little salt) onto the center of the pad. It should absorb within 10 seconds and not leak. Test the top sheet for dryness: press a tissue on the pad after absorption; the tissue should remain dry. Check the wings for secure attachment. Inspect the wrapper for seals and tears. If any pad fails, identify the cause and correct the process. Keep records of tests and defects. For small-scale production, test at least 5 pads per batch. For larger batches, test 10% or more.

Maintain a clean workspace. Sterilize equipment regularly. Train workers on hygiene and QC procedures. Use a checklist for each production run. Common defects include uneven core, weak adhesive, and poor sealing. Address these by adjusting the mixing, adhesive amount, or heat sealing temperature. For example, if the core is uneven, mix fluff and SAP more thoroughly. If adhesive is weak, increase the amount or change the glue. If seals are poor, increase heat or pressure. Document everything. This helps you trace problems and improve. Quality control is not just about rejecting bad pads; it's about building a reputation for reliable products. In Africa, where access to affordable pads is limited, quality is key to gaining trust and growing your business.

<h2>Practical Reference: Manufacturing Processes for Disposable Pads</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a single shop-floor guide for African sanitary pad producers. Whether you are setting up a semi-automated line in Nairobi, a manual press in Kumasi, or scaling a cottage operation in Lagos, the principles are the same: control your raw materials, control your process, control your quality. In this market, your competitive edge is not brand alone it is consistent absorbency, reliable supply, and a cost per pad that works for low-income consumers. This reference gives you the numbers, standards, and checklists to get there.</p>

<h3>1. Raw Material Preparation</h3>
<p>Disposable pads are built from four functional layers: a topsheet (hydrophilic nonwoven), an acquisition/distribution layer (ADL), an absorbent core (fluffed pulp + superabsorbent polymer, SAP), and a backsheet (breathable polyethylene or biodegradable film). Wings use the same backsheet and a pressure-sensitive adhesive. In Africa, sourcing is your biggest risk: nonwoven and SAP are largely imported from Asia, while fluff pulp can sometimes be sourced regionally (e.g., South Africa, Egypt).</p>

<p>Key preparation steps:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Incoming inspection:</strong> Check GSM (grams per square metre), width, roll tension, and moisture content. Reject damp pulp moisture causes SAP clumping and core failure.</li>
<li><strong>Conditioning:</strong> Store rolls at 1825C and 4565% relative humidity for at least 24 hours before use. In humid coastal cities (Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Accra), dehumidification is not optional.</li>
<li><strong>Pulp defibration:</strong> Hammer mills convert pulp board into fluff at 600900 kg/m bulk density. Worn hammers produce fines that reduce absorbency.</li>
<li><strong>SAP handling:</strong> Keep sealed, use within 6 months, and blend at 2540% of core weight depending on target capacity.</li>
</ul>

<h3>2. Forming and Layering</h3>
<p>Forming is where pad performance is won or lost. The core must be uniform in density and SAP distribution, or you will get leaks, gel blocking, and customer complaints. On a semi-automated line, the sequence is: pulp defibration SAP dosing core forming drum or vacuum box compaction calendar layer unwinding thermal or ultrasonic bonding.</p>

<p>Practical targets for a standard regular pad (240 mm):</p>
<ul>
<li>Core weight: 3.55.0 g</li>
<li>SAP load: 0.81.5 g</li>
<li>Core density: 0.080.15 g/cm</li>
<li>Line speed: 80200 m/min (semi-automated), 300600 m/min (full line)</li>
</ul>

<p>In Africa, many producers run at lower speeds to manage humidity and power fluctuations. That is fine but keep tension constant. Variable tension is the number one cause of wrinkled cores and rejected pads.</p>

<h3>3. Cutting and Shaping</h3>
<p>Rotary die cutting shapes the pad and creates the wings. Die wear is a hidden cost: a worn die produces ragged edges, incomplete wing cuts, and dust that contaminates the core. Inspect dies weekly and replace or resharpen after 200,000500,000 cuts depending on material.</p>

<p>Common shapes in African markets: straight (economy), contoured (standard), and ultra-thin with ADL (premium urban). Keep your SKU count low at start two shapes maximum to reduce die changeover downtime.</p>

<h3>4. Adhesive Application and Wing Attachment</h3>
<p>Three adhesive zones matter: wing adhesive (attaches to underwear), panty-positioning adhesive (the strip on the backsheet), and, on some designs, a topsheet-to-core bonding adhesive. Use hot-melt pressure-sensitive adhesive (PSA) applied at 140170C with a slot-die or spray system.</p>

<p>Wing attachment is a frequent failure point. Weak wings detach during use; over-strong adhesive tears the backsheet. Test peel strength on a standard cotton panty fabric at 23C and 50% RH. Target 0.30.8 N/25 mm for wings.</p>

<h3>5. Packaging and Sterilization</h3>
<p>Disposable pads are not sterile products and do not require sterilization for normal use. They are, however, hygiene-critical: contamination during packaging causes odour, irritation, and infection risk. The industry standard is controlled-environment packaging with wrapped individual pads and multi-pack outer bags.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Individual wrap:</strong> 2540 GSM film or tissue, printed with brand and instructions.</li>
<li><strong>Multi-pack:</strong> 8, 10, or 16 pads per pack; PE or BOPP film with easy-tear feature.</li>
<li><strong>Carton:</strong> 2448 packs per carton for distribution.</li>
<li><strong>Hygiene:</strong> UV or ozone treatment of packaging area; positive air pressure; operator PPE.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:2px solid #c00;background:#fff5f5;padding:14px;margin:18px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#c00;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong> Do NOT claim your pads are "sterile" unless you operate a validated sterilization process (e.g., ethylene oxide or gamma irradiation) with documented dosimetry and bioburden testing. In most African markets, regulators (e.g., KEBS, NAFDAC, SAHPRA) will require evidence. False sterility claims are a legal and reputational risk. Label accurately: "hygienically manufactured," not "sterile," unless certified.
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li> Verify incoming nonwoven GSM, width, and pulp moisture before releasing to production.</li>
<li> Confirm SAP dose weight every 30 minutes and log it.</li>
<li> Check core density and uniformity visually every hour; reject wrinkled or thin cores.</li>
<li> Inspect cutting dies weekly; replace or resharpen on schedule.</li>
<li> Test wing peel strength daily on cotton fabric; adjust adhesive temp if out of range.</li>
<li> Maintain packaging area positive pressure and operator hygiene (PPE, hand wash).</li>
<li> Run absorbency and rewet tests on every batch; retain samples for 12 months.</li>
<li> Label accurately never claim "sterile" without validated sterilization evidence.</li>
</ol>
