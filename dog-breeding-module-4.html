Lessons

1. Estrus Detection and Teasing Techniques

Estrus detection is the foundation of any successful breeding program. In Africa, where many mares run on pasture or in group housing, you must observe them carefully twice daily, ideally at dawn and dusk when temperatures are cooler and horses are active. A mare in heat (estrus) will show specific signs when exposed to a stallion or a teaser. The most reliable sign is 'winking' the opening and closing of the vulva often accompanied by squatting, urinating frequently, and tail raising. She may also stand still for the stallion, whereas a mare not in heat will kick, squeal, and move away.

Use a teaser stallion that is calm and easy to handle. If you don't have a stallion, a vigorous gelding or even a pony can work, but stallions are more effective. Tease the mare over a sturdy fence or in a small pen. Keep sessions short 5 to 10 minutes per mare to avoid fatigue and injury. Record everything: date, time, intensity of signs (score 1 to 5), and any changes. Many African breeders use a simple notebook or a smartphone app to track cycles.

For mares that show weak or silent heats, try teasing them with a stallion across a barrier for 15 minutes daily. Some mares need to be teased in the presence of other mares to stimulate signs. If a mare still shows nothing after 21 days, she may be in anestrus (not cycling) due to poor nutrition, heavy parasite load, or the season. In tropical Africa, mares often cycle year-round, but in cooler highland areas, they may be seasonal (spring to autumn).

Practical tips: Wear gloves and boots when handling mares near a stallion. Avoid teasing during the hottest part of the day (11am3pm). Keep a calendar the estrous cycle averages 21 days (range 1824), with estrus lasting 57 days. If you breed on the first day of standing heat, you are often too early. The best time is 2448 hours after she first stands for the teaser, or every other day during heat. Always confirm with a veterinarian using ultrasound if possible.

2. Ultrasound Examination of the Reproductive Tract

Ultrasound is the most powerful tool for managing mare reproduction, but many African breeders lack access. If you can afford it, a portable ultrasound machine (linear probe, 57.5 MHz) costs from $3,000 to $8,000. Alternatively, you can hire a veterinarian who owns one. The exam is quick (510 minutes) and safe. You will need a chute or stocks to restrain the mare, plus a tail wrap and a clean sleeve.

The vet will insert a gloved, lubricated arm into the rectum and hold the ultrasound probe against the reproductive tract. On the screen, you will see the uterus and ovaries. Key things to identify: follicles (fluid-filled black circles) on the ovaries, the corpus luteum (a solid grey area after ovulation), and any fluid or abnormalities in the uterus. A follicle of 35 mm or larger usually means the mare will ovulate within 2448 hours. This is the best time to breed or inseminate.

For pregnancy diagnosis, ultrasound can detect an embryonic vesicle as early as 14 days after ovulation. In Africa, where transport to a clinic may be difficult, learning to do basic ultrasound yourself can save money and time. Many agricultural colleges offer short courses. You can also use a technique called 'rectal palpation' without ultrasound feeling the uterus and ovaries with your hand but it is less accurate and requires experience.

Practical use: Scan mares every 23 days during heat to track follicle growth. If you are using artificial insemination (AI) with chilled or frozen semen, ultrasound helps you time insemination precisely. After breeding, scan at 1416 days for pregnancy, then again at 2830 days to confirm a heartbeat. If the mare is not pregnant, you can restart the cycle sooner. In areas with high tick-borne diseases, scan for uterine cysts or infections that can cause infertility. Always clean the probe and wear new gloves between mares to prevent disease spread.

3. Manipulation of the Estrous Cycle

Manipulating the mare's cycle lets you breed at a convenient time, synchronize mares for a stallion, or treat problem breeders. The most common method in Africa is using hormones. Prostaglandin F2 (e.g., dinoprost or cloprostenol) is used to shorten the luteal phase. If a mare has a corpus luteum (days 514 after ovulation), a single injection of 5 mg dinoprost will bring her into heat in 24 days. This is useful if you missed a breeding or want to breed several mares at once.

For mares that are not cycling (anestrus), you can use a progesterone-releasing device (like a CIDR or PRID) inserted into the vagina for 714 days, often combined with estradiol. When removed, most mares come into heat within 25 days. In Africa, these devices may be expensive or unavailable, so some breeders use a simpler method: daily injections of progesterone in oil for 1014 days, then stop. The mare will ovulate 57 days after the last injection.

Another technique is 'short cycling': give prostaglandin, then breed on the next heat. This works well for mares with normal cycles. For mares with prolonged heat or anovulatory follicles, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) 15002500 IU can be given when a follicle reaches 35 mm to induce ovulation within 3648 hours. This is especially helpful with frozen semen, which requires precise timing.

Practical cautions: Hormones must be stored correctly (refrigerated, protected from light). Use sterile needles and syringes. Never use prostaglandin on a pregnant mare it will cause abortion. If you are unsure of pregnancy status, scan first. In hot climates, work early morning to reduce stress. Record all treatments and observe for signs of heat daily. If a mare does not respond, check for nutritional deficiencies (especially energy and protein) or uterine infection. Always consult a veterinarian before starting a synchronization program, as incorrect use can cause cysts or persistent heat.

4. Breeding Soundness Examination of the Mare

A breeding soundness examination (BSE) tells you if a mare is likely to conceive and carry a foal. It is essential before buying a mare or starting a breeding season. In Africa, where veterinary services may be limited, you can do a basic exam yourself, but a full BSE requires a vet with ultrasound and a speculum. The exam has four parts: history, physical exam, reproductive exam, and sometimes laboratory tests.

History: Ask about previous foals, heat cycles, any abortions, retained placenta, or difficult births. Note her age mares over 15 years may have reduced fertility. Physical exam: check body condition score (BCS). A mare should be 57 on a 9-point scale. Thin mares (BCS <4) often don't cycle; obese mares (BCS >8) may have trouble conceiving. Look at the udder, vulva, and perineum. A 'sunken' vulva or poor perineal conformation can allow air and bacteria into the vagina, causing infection.

Reproductive exam: The vet will use a speculum to look at the cervix and vagina for discharge, tears, or tumors. Then, rectal palpation and ultrasound to assess the uterus (size, tone, fluid) and ovaries (follicles, cysts). A uterine culture and cytology can identify bacteria like Streptococcus zooepidemicus or fungi. In Africa, common problems include endometritis (uterine infection) from poor hygiene during breeding, and ovarian cysts. A biopsy may be taken to check for fibrosis (scarring) a common cause of infertility in older mares.

Practical interpretation: A mare with a clean culture, normal uterus, and regular cycles is sound. If abnormalities are found, treatment may include uterine lavage with saline, antibiotics, or hormones. For mares with poor perineal conformation, a Caslick's suture (stitching the top of the vulva) can prevent contamination. Always do a BSE at least 30 days before breeding. If you are buying a mare, ask for a written report. In many African countries, a BSE costs $50$150, which is small compared to the cost of feeding a barren mare for a year.

5. Managing the Problem Mare

A 'problem mare' is one that fails to conceive after three breeding cycles, or has repeated early pregnancy loss. In Africa, common causes include poor nutrition, uterine infection, hormonal imbalances, and anatomical defects. Managing these mares requires patience and a systematic approach. First, review her history: when was she last bred? Did she conceive? Was there any discharge? Then, do a full breeding soundness exam.

Uterine infection (endometritis) is the most common problem. Signs include a cloudy or bloody discharge, short cycles, and failure to conceive. Treatment: uterine lavage with 12 liters of warm saline, followed by infusion of antibiotics (e.g., gentamicin or ceftiofur) for 35 days. In Africa, where antibiotics may be scarce, you can use a dilute povidone-iodine solution (0.2%) for lavage, but never use strong chemicals. Repeat the lavage daily until the fluid is clear. Also, ensure the mare is teased and bred with clean technique use a sterile sleeve and lubricant for AI.

Hormonal problems: Some mares have persistent follicles that don't ovulate (anovulatory follicles). Give hCG (15002500 IU) when the follicle is >35 mm. If she has a corpus luteum that persists (prolonged diestrus), prostaglandin will bring her back into heat. For mares with low progesterone after breeding, supplement with altrenogest (Regumate) orally for 14 days. This is expensive but can save a pregnancy.

Anatomical defects: A 'sunken' anus or poor vulvar seal can cause pneumovagina (air in the vagina). A Caslick's suture is a simple surgical fix the vet stitches the top of the vulva, leaving a small opening for urine. This prevents fecal contamination and air. For mares with cervical tears, surgery may be needed. In all cases, improve nutrition: feed a balanced diet with 1214% protein, good quality forage, and minerals (especially copper and selenium). Provide shade and fresh water. If a mare still fails after 6 months of treatment, consider embryo transfer or retiring her as a broodmare. Always consult a veterinarian before using hormones or antibiotics.

6. Nutritional Management for Reproductive Health

Nutrition is the single most important factor affecting a mare's ability to conceive and carry a foal. In Africa, where pastures vary from lush to dry, you must adjust feeding to her reproductive stage. A mare at maintenance (not pregnant, not lactating) needs about 1.52% of her body weight in dry matter per day. For a 500 kg mare, that is 7.510 kg of hay or pasture. If she is thin (body condition score <4), she will not cycle. Increase energy with grains like oats, maize, or barley up to 0.5 kg per 100 kg body weight. But avoid overfeeding: obese mares (BCS >8) have more difficulty conceiving and foaling.

During early pregnancy (first 5 months), the foal grows slowly. Feed a maintenance diet plus a vitamin-mineral supplement. From month 6 onward, the foal grows rapidly. Increase energy by 1020% and protein to 1214%. Good sources of protein in Africa include lucerne (alfalfa) hay, cottonseed meal, and sunflower meal. Provide free-choice minerals, especially calcium, phosphorus, copper, zinc, and selenium. In many African soils, selenium is deficient, so use a supplement containing 12 mg selenium per day. Iodine is also important for foal thyroid development.

After foaling, a lactating mare needs 23 times more energy and protein than a dry mare. She may lose weight, so feed 23 kg of concentrate per day plus good hay. Always provide clean water a lactating mare drinks 5080 liters daily. In hot climates, add electrolytes to water if she sweats heavily. Monitor body condition weekly. If she drops below BCS 4, increase feed immediately. For mares that are hard keepers, add oil (e.g., 250 ml vegetable oil per day) for extra energy. Avoid sudden feed changes introduce new feeds over 710 days. Also, ensure she has access to salt licks. Finally, keep her free of internal parasites (deworm every 812 weeks) and external parasites (ticks, flies) that can transmit diseases. A well-fed mare is your best guarantee for a healthy foal.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Reproductive Management of the Mare



In African horse breeding enterprises from Thoroughbred studs in the Western Cape and Kenyan highlands to endurance Arabians in North Africa and working-horse operations across the Sahel reproductive efficiency is the single largest driver of profitability. A mare that fails to conceive, or conceives late in the season, costs the same to feed as a productive one but returns nothing. This practical reference condenses the core operational knowledge from the seven lessons of this module into a working document for stud managers, veterinary technicians, and farm owners.



The commercial logic is straightforward: every 21-day cycle missed adds roughly three weeks of feed, labour, and opportunity cost. On a 40-mare stud, a 15% improvement in first-cycle conception rate can translate into several additional foals per year often the difference between a viable and a marginal operation. The techniques below are presented with that business lens in mind.



1. Estrus Detection and Teasing Techniques



Estrus detection is the foundation of any breeding programme. Mares are seasonally polyestrous, cycling in response to increasing day length. In Southern Hemisphere operations (South Africa, Namibia, Zimbabwe), the natural breeding season runs roughly September to February; in Northern Hemisphere Africa (Egypt, Morocco, Tunisia), it runs March to August. In equatorial regions, mares may cycle year-round but with reduced fertility during the hottest months.



Teasing should be performed daily, ideally with a robust teaser stallion or a mare-in-estrus-suppressed gelding. A standard teasing session involves bringing the mare to a teasing rail or chute and presenting the stallion at a distance of 12 metres. Signs of estrus include:




  • Winking of the vulva (rhythmic eversion of the clitoris)

  • Urination in small quantities, often repeatedly

  • Squatting and tail-raising

  • Vulval relaxation and hyperaemia

  • Vocalisation and receptivity to the stallion

  • Standing firmly when the stallion approaches



Silent or "dumb" heats are common, particularly in maiden mares and during the transition period. These require ultrasound confirmation. In African conditions, heat stress can suppress overt estrus behaviour, so teasing should be done in the cool of early morning.





2. Ultrasound Examination



Transrectal ultrasound is the single most valuable diagnostic tool in mare reproduction. A 57.5 MHz linear array probe is used. Key applications:




  • Confirming estrus by follicle size and uterine oedema pattern

  • Timing ovulation (follicles 35 mm)

  • Detecting ovulation failure or haemorrhagic anovulatory follicles

  • Early pregnancy diagnosis from Day 1416

  • Detecting twins (critical mares rarely carry twins to term)

  • Monitoring uterine fluid, cysts, or pathology



In resource-limited African settings, a portable battery-operated scanner is essential. Where ultrasound is unavailable, teasing combined with serial progesterone assays can substitute, though with reduced accuracy.

SAFETY WARNING Prostaglandin Handling

Prostaglandin F2 analogues (cloprostenol, dinoprost) are hazardous to humans. They can cause bronchospasm, uterine contraction, and miscarriage. Pregnant women, asthmatics, and anyone with respiratory conditions must not handle these drugs. Use gloves, avoid needle-stick injury, and dispose of sharps and drug vials in sealed containers. In field conditions across Africa, improper disposal of hormone vials is a real environmental and public health risk follow national veterinary drug regulations.

5. Managing the Problem Mare



Problem mares fall into several categories: those with persistent endometritis, those with anatomical defects (caslick, cervical tears), those with ovarian dysfunction, and older maiden mares. Management priorities:




  • Endometritis: Uterine lavage with sterile saline, oxytocin to clear fluid, and targeted antibiotics based on culture.

  • Anatomical: Caslick's vulvoplasty for pneumovagina; surgical repair of cervical tears.

  • Ovarian: Prostaglandin for persistent corpus luteum; hCG for ovulation failure.

  • Older mares: Consider embryo transfer to a recipient mare increasingly available in South Africa and Kenya.



In African conditions, malnutrition and parasite burden are frequent contributors to poor reproductive performance. A problem mare should always be assessed for body condition, faecal egg count, and trace mineral status before expensive interventions.



6. Nutritional Management for Reproductive Health



Nutrition is the most cost-effective reproductive intervention available. A mare in poor body condition (BCS <4/9) will often fail to cycle or will cycle irregularly. Conversely, obesity (BCS >8/9) is associated with reduced fertility and increased risk of laminitis.



Key nutrients for reproductive function:




  • Energy: Maintain BCS 57/9. In late gestation, energy requirements rise by 2030%.

  • Protein: 1012% crude protein for maintenance; 14% in late gestation and lactation.

  • Minerals: Calcium, phosphorus (2:1 ratio), copper, zinc, selenium, iodine. Selenium deficiency is common in parts of South Africa and East Africa supplement carefully.

  • Vitamins: Vitamin E and A for immune and reproductive function.

  • Water: Clean, ad libitum. A lactating mare may drink 6080 litres/day in hot climates.



In extensive African systems, natural pasture may be sufficient in good rainfall years but deficient in dry years. Supplementary feeding during the breeding season is a sound investment. Avoid sudden dietary changes; introduce concentrates gradually over 710 days.



Quick Reference Checklist




  1. Tease mares daily during the breeding season early morning in hot climates.

  2. Confirm estrus and ovulation timing by transrectal ultrasound (follicle 35 mm).

  3. Perform a full BSE on every mare before the season history, palpation, ultrasound, culture if indicated.

  4. Use prostaglandin to shorten diestrus; hCG or deslorelin to time ovulation.

  5. Maintain body condition score 57/9 throughout gestation and lactation.

  6. Supplement selenium, copper, and zinc where soil deficiency is documented.

  7. Isolate and investigate any mare not conceiving after two cycles.

  8. Keep accurate records teasing dates, ultrasound findings, breeding dates, treatments.
