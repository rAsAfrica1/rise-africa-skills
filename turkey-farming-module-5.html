1. Selecting Breeding Stock

Selecting the right breeding stock is the foundation of a profitable turkey farm. Start by choosing birds that are healthy, vigorous, and free from deformities. In Africa, local breeds like the indigenous turkey are hardy and disease-resistant, but they may grow slower. For meat production, consider crossbreeds such as the Norfolk Black or Bronze. Look for broad-breasted birds with strong legs, as they must support heavy weight during mating. Select hens that lay regularly and have a calm temperament; aggressive hens may injure chicks. For toms (males), choose those with a large, upright fan, bright caruncles, and active strutting behavior. Avoid birds with crooked keels, slipped wings, or pasty vents. A good rule: select from parents that have a proven record of high hatchability and fast growth. In Africa, where feed costs are high, prioritize feed efficiency. Weigh birds at 16-20 weeks; keep those above average weight. Keep a ratio of 1 tom to 8-10 hens to ensure fertile eggs without overworking males. Buy stock from reputable hatcheries or established farmers, not from live bird markets where disease risk is high. Quarantine new birds for 3 weeks before mixing. Record the source, age, and performance of each bird. Cull any bird that shows signs of illness, poor growth, or low egg production. Remember, good genetics plus good management equals success. Start with 10-20 hens and 2-3 toms for a small-scale farm. Over time, replace older birds (after 2-3 years) with their best offspring to maintain vigor.

2. Mating Systems

Mating systems determine how you pair toms and hens to produce the next generation. The simplest is pen mating: keep 1 tom with 8-10 hens in a confined area. This works well for small farms but can lead to inbreeding if you keep related birds. To avoid this, rotate toms every 2 years or bring in new blood. Another system is flock mating, where multiple toms run with a larger flock. This mimics natural mating but you cannot control which tom fathers which poults. For genetic improvement, use artificial insemination (AI). AI allows you to use one superior tom to fertilize many hens, and it prevents injuries from heavy toms mounting hens. In Africa, AI is gaining popularity because it reduces the number of males needed. To collect semen, train the tom to mount a dummy or a hen, then massage the vent. Collect 0.2-0.5 ml per ejaculation, dilute with saline, and inseminate hens within 30 minutes. Inseminate each hen every 7-10 days. For natural mating, ensure the pen floor is not slippery; provide deep litter (wood shavings or sand) to prevent leg injuries. Watch for over-mating: hens with bare backs or wounds need rest. Separate them for a week. Rotate breeding groups every 6 months to maintain fertility. If you keep a tom for more than 2 years, fertility drops. Replace him with a young, vigorous tom. Always keep records of which tom mated which hens so you can trace parentage. In hot climates, mate in early morning or evening to avoid heat stress. Provide fresh water and shade. A good mating system balances genetic diversity, fertility, and bird welfare.

3. Egg Production and Management

A healthy turkey hen lays 60-100 eggs per year, mostly between 30 and 54 weeks of age. In Africa, egg production can be lower due to heat and poor nutrition. To maximize egg numbers, provide a laying diet with 16-18% protein and 3-4% calcium. Use crushed oyster shells or limestone as calcium sources. Hens need 14-16 hours of light daily; if natural daylight is short, use a bulb (40 watts per 20 square meters) to extend light. Collect eggs at least 3 times a day to prevent breakage and dirty eggs. Nest boxes should be dark, clean, and lined with soft bedding like dry grass. One nest box per 4-5 hens. Eggs for hatching should be stored at 15-18C and 75% humidity for no more than 7 days. Turn them twice daily if stored longer. Do not wash hatching eggs; instead, gently wipe with a dry cloth. If eggs are dirty, use a fine sandpaper. In hot climates, store eggs in a cool room or evaporative cooler. Never store eggs near strong smells like kerosene or onions, as they absorb odors. Keep a record of lay: date, number of eggs, and any abnormalities. Soft-shelled or thin-shelled eggs indicate calcium deficiency or disease. If a hen lays outside the nest, confine her to the nest box for a few days. Broody hens stop laying; if you want eggs, break broodiness by moving the hen to a wire-bottom cage for 3-5 days. For hatching, select eggs that are well-shaped, average size, and free of cracks. Discard very small or double-yolked eggs. With good management, you can get 70-80% fertile eggs and 60-70% hatchability.

4. Incubation and Hatching

Turkey eggs take 28 days to hatch. You can use a natural hen (a broody turkey or chicken) or an incubator. For small flocks, a broody hen is cheap and effective. Set 8-12 eggs under a broody hen at night. She will turn them and keep them warm. Provide her with food and water nearby. For larger scale, use a forced-air incubator. Set temperature at 37.5C (99.5F) and humidity at 55-60% for the first 25 days. Then increase humidity to 70-80% for the last 3 days (lockdown). Turn eggs at least 5 times a day, or use an automatic turner. Stop turning on day 25. Candle eggs on day 7 and day 14 to remove infertile or dead embryos. Infertile eggs look clear; dead embryos show a blood ring. Remove them to prevent contamination. In Africa, power outages are common. Use a backup generator or a solar-powered incubator. If power fails, wrap the incubator in blankets and do not open it for up to 2 hours. After hatching, leave poults in the incubator until they are dry and fluffy (12-24 hours). Then move them to a brooder with a heat source (infrared lamp or charcoal stove) at 35C for the first week, reducing by 3C each week. Provide clean water with vitamins and a starter feed of 28% protein. Dip beaks in water to teach drinking. Keep bedding dry. Hatch rate depends on egg quality, incubation hygiene, and temperature control. Sanitize incubator before and after each hatch. With practice, you can achieve 80% hatch of fertile eggs.

5. Genetic Improvement

Genetic improvement means selecting the best birds to breed so that each generation is better than the last. Start by defining your goal: do you want faster growth, more eggs, or better disease resistance? In Africa, disease resistance and heat tolerance are often more important than maximum growth. Choose a selection method. Individual selection: pick birds based on their own performance (e.g., weight at 20 weeks). Family selection: pick birds whose parents and siblings also perform well. This is better for traits like egg production. For growth, weigh birds at 8, 16, and 24 weeks. Keep the heaviest 20% as breeders. For egg production, count eggs per hen from 30 to 45 weeks. Keep hens that lay above average. Use a breeding card for each bird: record ID, hatch date, weight, eggs laid, and health. Avoid inbreeding by not mating close relatives (father-daughter, brother-sister). Introduce new blood every 3-4 years from a different farm or hatchery. Crossbreeding can give hybrid vigor: cross two different breeds (e.g., local hen with Broad Breasted White tom) to get faster-growing offspring. But keep pure lines if you want to sell breeding stock. In hot climates, select for smaller body size and good feed conversion. Cull aggressively: any bird with deformities, poor growth, or chronic illness should not breed. Over time, you can improve growth by 5-10% per generation. Keep records for at least 3 generations to see trends. Join a farmer group to exchange breeding stock and ideas. Remember, genetic improvement is slow but permanent. Good nutrition and health unlock genetic potential.

6. Record Keeping for Breeding

Record keeping is the backbone of a successful breeding program. Without records, you cannot know which birds are performing well. Start with a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on a phone. For each bird, assign a unique ID (e.g., leg band or wing tag). Record: hatch date, sex, parent IDs, weight at 8, 16, and 24 weeks, date of first egg, number of eggs laid per week, egg weight, fertility, hatchability, and health treatments. For mating, record which tom was with which hens and dates. For incubation, record set date, number of eggs set, candling results, hatch date, and number of poults hatched. Use a calendar to track dates. In Africa, where many farmers operate without electricity, use a chalkboard or a waterproof notebook. Keep records in a safe, dry place. At the end of each season, calculate key figures: average eggs per hen, fertility rate (fertile eggs / eggs set), hatch rate (poults hatched / fertile eggs), and growth rate (weight gain per day). Compare these to previous seasons. Identify your best 10% of birds and use them as breeders next year. Cull the bottom 20%. Share records with buyers to prove quality. For example, a buyer will pay more for poults from a hen that lays 90 eggs per year than from one that lays 50. Records also help you spot problems early: a drop in fertility may mean a tom is too old or overheated. A drop in egg production may mean disease or poor feed. Keep records for at least 3 years to see genetic trends. Train your workers to record daily. Use simple codes: 'E' for egg, 'H' for hatched, 'D' for dead. Good records turn a hobby into a business.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Turkey Breeding and Genetics

Turkey production in Africa is a growing business, driven by rising demand for lean meat, festive season sales, and the need for diversified poultry enterprises. Unlike broilers, turkeys are not yet dominated by a handful of global genetics companies on the continent, which means African farmers who master breeding and genetics can build a real competitive advantage. This Practical Reference module distils the essential knowledge from the seven lessons in this module into a single, business-focused resource. It is designed for farm managers, extension officers, and agripreneurs who want to move from simply keeping turkeys to running a profitable, genetically sound breeding operation.

Whether you are selecting your first breeding stock or planning a multi-generation improvement programme, the principles here will help you make decisions that pay. Remember: genetics is a long-term investment. A good breeding decision today affects your flock's performance for years. A poor one can set you back several seasons and thousands of dollars.

Why Breeding and Genetics Matter for African Turkey Businesses

Across Africa, local turkey ecotypessuch as the Nigerian Beladi, the Ugandan local, and the South African indigenous turkeyare well adapted to local conditions, disease challenges, and low-input management. However, they often grow slowly and lay fewer eggs than commercial hybrids. The business opportunity lies in structured crossbreeding and selection: using local adaptability combined with improved growth and egg production from exotic or improved lines.

Key economic drivers in turkey breeding include:

  • Egg production per hen: A hen that lays 80 eggs per year instead of 40 doubles your poult supply without increasing feed costs proportionally.
  • Hatchability: Poor fertility and hatchability can destroy margins. A 10% improvement in hatch rate can mean hundreds of extra poults per cycle.
  • Growth rate and feed conversion: Faster-growing poults reach market weight earlier, reducing feed and labour costs per kilogram of meat.
  • Survivability: Poults that survive to market age are the ultimate measure of breeding success. Local genetics often excel here.

Your breeding programme should always be tied to your market. Are you selling day-old poults, live birds for festivals, or processed meat? Each market demands different genetic priorities.

Selecting Breeding Stock

Selection is the foundation of genetic improvement. In turkeys, you should select for a balance of traits: body weight, breast width, egg production, fertility, hatchability, and livability. Never select on a single trait alonethis leads to unintended consequences such as reduced fertility in heavy toms.

Practical selection steps for African farms:

  1. Tag and identify: Use wing bands or leg tags. Without individual identification, selection is guesswork.
  2. Record performance: Track body weight at 8, 16, and 24 weeks; age at first egg; number of eggs laid; and poult survival.
  3. Select from the best families: Choose both male and female lines from families with proven performance.
  4. Cull ruthlessly: Remove birds with physical defects, poor growth, or chronic illness. A smaller, high-quality breeding flock outperforms a large, mediocre one.
  5. Maintain genetic diversity: Avoid mating close relatives. Introduce new blood every 34 generations.

For smallholder and medium-scale farms, a practical rule is to select the top 2030% of hens and the top 1015% of toms as breeders. This selection intensity is achievable without sophisticated equipment.

Mating Systems

Turkeys can be bred using natural mating or artificial insemination (AI). In Africa, natural mating is most common, but AI is increasingly used in commercial operations because heavy toms often cannot mate effectively.

Common mating systems:

  • Pen mating: One tom to 812 hens. Simple and effective for small flocks.
  • Flock mating: Multiple toms with a larger hen group. Requires careful monitoring to avoid fighting and injury.
  • Artificial insemination: Collect semen from selected toms and inseminate hens. Allows maximum use of superior sires and reduces injury. Requires training and equipment.
  • Line crossing: Maintain two or more distinct lines and cross them to produce hybrid vigour (heterosis). This is the basis of commercial turkey breeding.

For most African farms, a practical approach is to maintain two lines: a dam line selected for egg production and a sire line selected for growth and conformation. Cross them to produce market poults. This captures heterosis and simplifies management.

Egg Production and Management

Turkey hens typically begin laying at 2832 weeks of age, depending on breed and management. A well-managed hen can produce 80100 eggs per year in a controlled breeding programme, while local hens may produce 4060. Egg production is influenced by genetics, nutrition, lighting, and stress.

Key management practices:

  • Lighting: Provide 1416 hours of light per day to stimulate laying. In many African regions, natural daylight is sufficient, but supplemental lighting may be needed during short-day seasons.
  • Nutrition: Breeder hens need a diet with 1618% protein, adequate calcium (34%), and balanced vitamins and minerals. Do not feed broiler dietsthey are too high in energy and can cause obesity and laying problems.
  • Nesting: Provide one nest box for every 45 hens. Keep nests clean and dark to reduce egg breakage and brooding behaviour.
  • Egg collection: Collect eggs at least 46 times daily. Store at 1518C and 7580% humidity for no more than 7 days before incubation.
  • Health: Vaccinate breeders against Newcastle disease, fowl pox, and other locally prevalent diseases. Healthy hens lay more and transmit maternal immunity to poults.

Incubation and Hatching

Turkey eggs require 28 days of incubation. Successful hatching depends on precise temperature, humidity, and turning. In Africa, both natural incubation (by hens or broody chickens) and artificial incubation are used. Artificial incubation gives better control and higher hatch rates when managed correctly.

Critical incubation parameters:

  • Temperature: 37.537.8C (99.5100F) for forced-air incubators.
  • Humidity: 5055% relative humidity during days 125; increase to 6570% during the last 3 days (lockdown).
  • Turning: Turn eggs at least 46 times daily for the first 25 days. Stop turning at lockdown.
  • Ventilation: Ensure adequate oxygen exchange, especially during hatching.
  • Sanitation: Disinfect incubators before each set. Poor sanitation is a major cause of poult mortality.

Hatchability of fertile eggs should be 8085% in well-managed operations. If it is lower, check fertility, egg storage, and incubator calibration.

Genetic Improvement

Genetic improvement is a cycle: select the best, mate them, evaluate offspring, and repeat. In turkeys, generation intervals are long (1218 months), so progress is slower than in broilers or layers. However, consistent selection can yield 25% improvement per generation in growth and egg production.

Practical genetic improvement strategies for African farms:

  • Define your breeding objective: Write down the traits you want to improve and their relative importance.
  • Use performance records: Without records, you are guessing. Even simple notebooks or mobile apps can work.
  • Select across families: Avoid selecting only from one or two favourite hens. Use family averages.
  • Introduce new genetics: Every 34 years, bring in unrelated breeding stock from a reputable source to avoid inbreeding depression.
  • Test cross: Evaluate crossbred offspring against pure lines to confirm heterosis.

Record Keeping for Breeding

Record keeping is the backbone of any breeding programme. Without records, you cannot measure progress, identify problems, or make informed decisions. In Africa, where labour and technology may be limited, simple, consistent records are better than complex systems that are never used.

Essential records for turkey breeding:

  • Individual bird records: ID, hatch date, sex, parentage, body weights, health events.
  • Egg production records: Daily egg count per hen or per pen, broken eggs, double yolks.
  • Incubation records: Set date, number of eggs set, fertility, hatch date, number hatched, poult quality.
  • Mating records: Which tom mated which hens, mating dates, AI records if applicable.
  • Performance summaries: Monthly and annual averages for key traits.

Use a simple spreadsheet or a farm record book. Train workers to record accurately and review records weekly. In the digital age, affordable mobile apps for poultry record keeping are available in many African countries.

SAFETY WARNING: Biosecurity and Handling

Biosecurity is non-negotiable. Turkey breeding flocks are highly susceptible to disease. Always:

  • Quarantine new birds for at least 30 days before introducing them to your breeding flock.
  • Disinfect incubators, egg trays, and equipment between every batch.
  • Wear clean protective clothing and footwear when entering breeding pens.
  • Do not allow visitors into breeding areas without strict biosecurity measures.
  • Handle toms carefully during mating or AI collectionthey can inflict serious scratches and pecks. Use proper restraint and protective gloves.
  • Dispose of dead birds and broken eggs immediately and properly to prevent disease spread.

Remember: A disease outbreak in your breeding flock can wipe out months of genetic progress and cause significant financial loss. Prevention is always cheaper than treatment.

  2. Select the top 2030% of hens and top 1015% of toms based on recorded performance, not appearance alone.
  3. Maintain at least two separate lines (sire and dam) and cross them to capture hybrid vigour.
  4. Provide 1416 hours of light and a balanced breeder diet to sustain egg production.
  5. Collect eggs 46 times daily and store them properly before incubation.
  6. Calibrate your incubator before every set and follow the temperature and humidity table above.
  7. Keep simple, consistent records for every bird, egg, and poultreview them weekly.
