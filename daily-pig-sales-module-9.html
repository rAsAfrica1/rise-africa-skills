1. All-In-All-Out Cleaning

All-in-all-out (AIAO) cleaning means you empty the whole pig house, clean it completely, and only then bring in new pigs. This breaks disease cycles. In a batch farrowing system, you wean a whole batch of piglets at once, move them out, and clean the farrowing house before the next batch comes in. This is the most important biosecurity step you can take.

Start by removing all pigs, feed, and manure. Scrape off solid waste with a shovel and wheelbarrow. Then soak the floors, walls, and pens with water and detergent. Use a high-pressure sprayer if you have one. Scrub every surface, including feeders, drinkers, and corners. Pay attention to cracks and crevices where dirt hides.

Next, disinfect. Use a disinfectant that works against viruses and bacteria, like Virkon S or a 2% caustic soda solution. Mix according to the label. Apply with a knapsack sprayer or brush. Let it sit for at least 30 minutes. Rinse with clean water. Then let the house dry completely. Sunlight helps kill germs. If possible, leave the house empty for 37 days. This is called downtime.

After cleaning, do not let anyone walk in with dirty boots or clothes. Keep a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance. Use dedicated boots and overalls for that house. If you have multiple houses, clean one at a time and work from youngest to oldest pigs to avoid carrying disease back.

In Africa, many farms have limited water or electricity. You can still do AIAO with buckets and a broom. The key is to remove all pigs, wash thoroughly, disinfect, and rest the house. Even a small farm can do this. If you cannot empty the whole house, at least clean and disinfect each pen between batches. But full AIAO gives the best results. It reduces pneumonia, scours, and parasites. Your pigs grow faster and you spend less on medicine. Plan your batches so you have time for proper cleaning. A clean house is a healthy house.

2. Vaccination Schedule

Vaccines protect your pigs from deadly diseases. In a batch farrowing system, you vaccinate the sow and the piglets on a schedule. This keeps the whole herd healthy. You need a fridge to store vaccines at 28C. Do not freeze them. Use a cooler box with ice packs when you go to the pen.

Start with the sow. Before breeding, vaccinate against parvovirus, leptospirosis, and erysipelas. Give two doses, 34 weeks apart. Then boost every 6 months. Two weeks before farrowing, vaccinate against E. coli and Clostridium perfringens. This gives the piglets protection through colostrum. Also vaccinate against porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome (PRRS) if it is common in your area. Check with your vet for local diseases.

For piglets, vaccinate at weaning (usually 34 weeks old). Give vaccines for circovirus, mycoplasma, and PRRS if needed. Some farms also vaccinate against foot-and-mouth disease and African swine fever but there is no effective vaccine for ASF yet. Always follow the label. Use a new needle for each pig. Clean the injection site with alcohol. Give the vaccine under the skin (subcutaneous) or into the muscle (intramuscular) as directed.

Keep records. Write down the date, vaccine name, batch number, and which pigs got it. If you miss a booster, the protection drops. In hot climates, handle vaccines carefully. Do not leave them in the sun. If you have many pigs, work in teams to move fast. A 50-dose vial should be used within 2 hours after opening. Do not mix vaccines unless the label says so.

If you cannot afford all vaccines, prioritise the ones for diseases that are common in your area. Talk to other pig farmers or your vet. A simple schedule: sows get parvovirus and erysipelas before breeding; piglets get circovirus at 3 weeks. This alone saves many pigs. Remember, vaccines are not a cure. They prevent disease. Good nutrition and clean housing make them work better.

3. Common Diseases in Batch Systems

In a batch farrowing system, pigs of the same age are together, so diseases spread fast. You need to know the common ones. Scours (diarrhoea) is the biggest killer of piglets. It is caused by E. coli, rotavirus, or coccidia. Piglets get watery yellow or white stool. They dehydrate and die within days. Treat with oral rehydration and antibiotics if bacterial. Prevent by vaccinating sows and keeping pens dry.

Pneumonia is common in weaners and growers. It is caused by Mycoplasma, Pasteurella, or PRRS virus. Pigs cough, breathe fast, and lose weight. Use antibiotics in water or feed. Improve ventilation. Avoid overcrowding. In Africa, dusty and hot houses make it worse. Open windows and use fans if possible.

African swine fever (ASF) is the most feared. There is no vaccine. It kills almost all infected pigs. Signs: high fever, red skin, vomiting, bloody diarrhoea. It spreads by contact, ticks, and contaminated pork. If you suspect ASF, call the vet immediately. Do not sell or move pigs. Cull and bury deep. Disinfect everything. Never feed raw kitchen waste. Always cook swill.

Parasites like roundworms and mange cause poor growth. Deworm every 3 months with ivermectin or levamisole. Mange makes pigs scratch and get crusty skin. Treat with ivermectin injection or spray. Erysipelas causes diamond-shaped skin patches and sudden death. Vaccinate sows and pigs. Treat with penicillin.

Other diseases: leptospirosis causes abortion in sows. Vaccinate. Foot-and-mouth disease causes blisters on the mouth and feet. Report to authorities. Coccidiosis causes bloody scours in piglets. Use coccidiostats in feed. Keep a notebook. Write down any sickness, what you did, and what worked. This helps you spot patterns. In batch systems, one sick pig can infect the whole batch. Isolate sick pigs immediately. Treat early. If many pigs get sick, call the vet. Do not guess. A vet can test and give the right medicine. Prevention is cheaper than cure.

4. Quarantine for New Stock

When you bring new pigs onto your farm, they can carry diseases. Quarantine means keeping them separate for at least 30 days. This protects your existing herd. In a batch farrowing system, you often buy boars or replacement gilts. Never mix them directly with your pigs. Set up a quarantine pen at least 100 metres away, downwind. If you cannot do 100 metres, use a separate building with its own boots and tools.

The quarantine pen must have its own feeders, drinkers, and cleaning tools. Do not share them. Feed and water the quarantine pigs last. Wear dedicated boots and overalls. Wash your hands after. Keep a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance. The pen should be clean, dry, and have shade. Provide good feed and water. Observe the pigs daily for signs of disease: coughing, diarrhoea, skin spots, or not eating.

During quarantine, test for common diseases. Your vet can take blood samples. Test for brucellosis, leptospirosis, PRRS, and ASF if in an outbreak area. If any pig tests positive, do not bring it into the main herd. Either treat or cull. If you cannot test, at least deworm and vaccinate. Give the new pigs a booster of the vaccines your herd uses. Wait 2 weeks after vaccination before mixing.

After 30 days, if the pigs are healthy, you can introduce them. But do it slowly. Put one of your own pigs next to the quarantine pen for a few days. Then mix them in a neutral pen. Watch for fighting. If all is well, move them to the main herd. Always keep records: date of arrival, source, tests done, and date of mixing.

In Africa, many small farms skip quarantine because of space. But one disease outbreak can wipe out your whole batch. A simple quarantine pen made of local poles and wire mesh is cheap. Even a small pen 20 metres away helps. If you buy from a market, assume the pigs are sick. Quarantine them. Do not borrow a neighbour's boar without quarantine. It is not worth the risk. Quarantine is your first line of defence.

5. Record Keeping for Health

Good records help you spot health problems early and make better decisions. In a batch farrowing system, you must record every health event. Use a simple notebook, a chalkboard, or a phone app. Write in pencil or pen that does not smudge. Keep records in a dry place. For each batch, start a new page. Write the batch number and date.

Record daily observations. For each pen, note any pig that is coughing, limping, off feed, or has diarrhoea. Write the date, pen number, and what you saw. If you treat a pig, record the drug name, dose, and route (injection, water, feed). Also record the withdrawal period the time you must wait before selling meat. For example, if you use oxytetracycline, withdrawal is 28 days. Write the date the withdrawal ends. This protects consumers and keeps you legal.

Keep a vaccination log. For each vaccine, write the date, vaccine name, batch number, and which pigs got it. Note the next due date. If you use a vet, record the vet's name and advice. Also record deaths. For each dead pig, write the date, age, and suspected cause. If you send a pig for post-mortem, keep the report. This helps you understand patterns. For example, if many piglets die at 10 days old, you might have a scours problem.

At the end of each batch, review your records. Count how many pigs got sick, how many died, and what treatments you used. Compare with the previous batch. If sickness is increasing, change something cleaning, ventilation, or vaccination. Records also help when you sell. Buyers like to see health records. They may pay more for pigs from a farm with good records. In Africa, many farmers keep records in their head. But memory fails. Write it down. A small notebook costs little. It can save you a lot of money. If you have a smartphone, use a simple note app. Take photos of sick pigs and treatments. This is also a record. Start today. Even one line per day is better than nothing.

6. When to Call the Vet

You cannot treat every problem yourself. Some diseases need a vet. Calling a vet early can save your herd. But vets can be expensive or far away. So know when it is urgent. In a batch farrowing system, you have many pigs of the same age. A disease can spread fast. If you see any of these signs, call the vet immediately: sudden death of more than one pig in a day, bloody diarrhoea, high fever (over 40C), blisters on the mouth or feet, or many pigs not eating. These could be African swine fever, foot-and-mouth disease, or classical swine fever. These are reportable diseases. Do not wait.

Also call the vet if you have a sow that aborts or gives birth to dead piglets. This could be leptospirosis, brucellosis, or PRRS. If you see pigs with severe breathing problems that do not respond to antibiotics within 2 days, call the vet. If you have a disease outbreak that you cannot identify, call. A vet can take samples and send to a lab. Do not guess with antibiotics. Using the wrong drug wastes money and creates resistance.

Before you call, prepare information. Write down: number of sick pigs, age, signs, when it started, what you have already tried. Have your records ready. If the vet must come, make sure the farm is easy to find. If you cannot get a vet, call an agricultural extension officer or a experienced pig farmer. In many African countries, there are government veterinary services. They may be free or cheap. Keep their number in your phone.

For routine problems like mild scours or a single pig with a limp, you can treat yourself. But if it spreads or gets worse, call. Also call the vet for vaccination advice. Your vet knows which diseases are common in your area. They can help you design a vaccination schedule. Do not buy vaccines without advice. Some vaccines are not effective against local strains. A vet can also help with biosecurity. They can visit and spot problems you miss. Build a relationship with your vet. Call them before an emergency. Ask questions. A good vet is your partner in keeping pigs healthy.

<h2>Practical Reference: Health and Biosecurity in a Batch Herd</h2>

<p>In a batch farrowing system, pigs move through the farm in groups all-in, all-out. That single management decision is your biggest biosecurity asset. When one batch leaves and the house is emptied, cleaned and rested before the next batch enters, you break the disease cycle that otherwise quietly eats your margins. For an African pig enterprise whether you run 20 sows in Kiambu or 200 in Ogun State health failures rarely kill the whole herd at once. They show up as poor feed conversion, uneven weaning weights, extra antibiotic bills and dead piglets that never made it to market weight. This lesson gives you the practical routines: cleaning protocol, vaccination timing, disease watch-list, quarantine rules, records and the point at which you must call a vet rather than guess.</p>

<h3>Why Batch Health Management Pays</h3>
<p>Continuous-flow farms keep pathogens circulating permanently. There is always a susceptible pig somewhere. Batch systems create windows: a clean, dry, rested house is a hostile environment for most viruses and bacteria. The return on this discipline is measurable lower mortality, lower medication cost, more uniform batches reaching 90100 kg at the same time, and therefore better bargaining power with buyers and abattoirs.</p>

<h3>All-In-All-Out Cleaning: The Sequence That Works</h3>
<p>Cleaning fails when farmers disinfect over dirt. Organic matter neutralises most disinfectants. Follow the order strictly:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Remove all pigs</strong> no stragglers, no "hospital pen" left inside the same airspace.</li>
<li><strong>Dry clean</strong> scrape and shovel out dung, leftover feed, bedding and cobwebs. Feed residues grow mould and attract rodents.</li>
<li><strong>Soak and wash</strong> detergent, warm water where possible, brush floors, walls to 1.5 m, pen rails, feeders and drinkers. Pay attention to corners, behind troughs and under slats.</li>
<li><strong>Rinse</strong> remove all soap and loosened dirt.</li>
<li><strong>Disinfect</strong> apply an approved product at the label dilution. Common choices: glutaraldehyde, quaternary ammonium, sodium hypochlorite (freshly made), or lime wash on walls and floors. Rotate actives to avoid resistance.</li>
<li><strong>Dry and rest</strong> 3 to 7 days empty. Dryness kills more pathogens than most farmers believe. Never re-stock a wet house.</li>
<li><strong>Verify</strong> walk the house. If you can smell pigs, it is not clean.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Vaccination Schedule for a Batch Herd</h3>
<p>Vaccination must be timed to the batch cycle, not to convenience. In most African production systems the priority vaccines are: <strong>FMD</strong> (where endemic and where vaccine is available), <strong>CSF (classical swine fever / hog cholera)</strong>, <strong>ASF</strong> (no vaccine exists biosecurity only), <strong>Erysipelas</strong>, <strong>Parvovirus/Leptospira</strong> for breeding stock, and <strong>PCV2</strong> for weaners where available. Confirm what is legally permitted and available in your country with your veterinarian.</p>

<h3>Common Diseases in Batch Systems</h3>
<p>Batch systems reduce but do not eliminate disease. The main threats in African pig units are ASF, CSF, FMD, porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome (PRRS), Erysipelas, piglet scours (E. coli, rotavirus), and internal parasites.</p>

<h3>Quarantine for New Stock</h3>
<p>New pigs are the single highest-risk entry point on any farm. Whether you buy gilts, boars or weaners, isolate them for a minimum of <strong>30 days</strong> in a separate pen at least 10 metres from the main herd, with its own boots, tools and feeding equipment. Test where possible (ASF, CSF, brucellosis, PRRS). Observe daily. Vaccinate and deworm during quarantine never after mixing. Introduce animals only when they are healthy, eating well and free of discharge or lameness. If you cannot quarantine, do not buy.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING AFRICAN SWINE FEVER (ASF)</p>
<p style="margin:0;color:#7f1d1d;">There is <strong>no vaccine and no cure</strong> for ASF. If you suspect it sudden deaths, high fever, reddened skin, abortions do <strong>not</strong> sell, move, slaughter or consume the pigs. Do <strong>not</strong> feed kitchen waste or swill. Notify your veterinary authority immediately. Moving infected pigs or pork spreads the virus across farms, districts and borders. Practise strict farm-gate biosecurity: no visitors into pens, no shared vehicles, no borrowed boots, no unapproved meat products on site.</p>

<h3>Record Keeping for Health</h3>
<p>Records are not paperwork they are your early-warning system. Without them you cannot tell whether mortality is normal, whether a vaccine worked, or where an outbreak started. Keep, at minimum:</p>
<ul>
<li>Daily mortality log per batch (date, pen, age, cause if known)</li>
<li>Treatment register (animal ID, drug, dose, withdrawal period)</li>
<li>Vaccination register (batch, vaccine, batch number, date, person)</li>
<li>Feed intake per pen a drop in intake is the earliest sign of disease</li>
<li>Cleaning and disinfection log (date, product, dilution, person)</li>
<li>Visitors and vehicle log at the gate</li>
<li>Quarantine and post-mortem notes</li>
</ul>
<p>Review records weekly. If mortality in a batch rises above 35% in weaners or 10% in piglets, investigate immediately.</p>

<h3>When to Call the Vet</h3>
<p>Call a vet <strong>today</strong>, not next week, if you see: sudden deaths of two or more pigs in 24 hours; abortion in more than one sow; blisters on snouts or hooves; widespread diarrhoea in piglets; or a batch-wide drop in feed intake. For ASF and CSF suspicion, call the veterinary authority first, before any treatment or movement. A vet who knows your batch cycle and records can make a diagnosis faster and cheaper than guesswork and broad-spectrum antibiotics.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li> All pigs out before cleaning begins no stragglers left behind.</li>
<li> Dry clean first, then wash, then disinfect never disinfect over dirt.</li>
<li> House rested and fully dry for 37 days before re-stocking.</li>
<li> Batch vaccination done on schedule; register signed and dated.</li>
<li> New stock quarantined 30 days, separate boots and tools.</li>
<li> Footbath and gate log in use for every visitor and vehicle.</li>
<li> Daily mortality and feed intake recorded for every pen.</li>
<li> Vet called immediately for sudden deaths, abortions or blisters.</li>
</ol>
