1. Structuring Cross-Border Trade Deals in African Markets

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 1 of Foreign Trade Negotiation. This lesson focuses on the architecture of cross-border trade deals across African markets. By the end of this session you should be able to map the key structural elements of a trade agreement, identify the points where African market conditions change the negotiation dynamic, and draft a term sheet that protects your commercial interests while remaining attractive to your counterpart. Africa is not a single market. It is 54 countries, multiple currencies, overlapping regional economic communities, and a wide spectrum of legal traditions. A deal that works in Nairobi may collapse in Lagos or Casablanca if you assume the same rules apply. The first structural decision is the legal vehicle. Will you trade through a direct sales contract, a distribution agreement, a joint venture, or a special purpose vehicle registered in a free zone? Each choice carries different tax, currency, and dispute resolution consequences. For example, a Kenyan exporter selling to a buyer in the Democratic Republic of Congo may prefer a direct contract governed by the CISG, while a Moroccan manufacturer entering Senegal might use a joint venture with a local partner to access public procurement. The second structural decision is the Incoterm. In African trade, logistics risk is often the largest cost driver. Port congestion in Durban, Mombasa, Abidjan, and Douala can add weeks to delivery schedules. If you accept CIF or DAP terms without pricing demurrage and detention into your offer, you transfer risk to yourself. A practical rule is to match the Incoterm to your operational capability. If you do not have a reliable customs broker in the destination country, use FOB or FCA and let the buyer manage import clearance. If you do, DAP can be a competitive advantage. The third structural decision is payment security. Letters of credit remain common in African trade, but not all banks in all countries are equally reliable. Confirm the issuing bank is acceptable to your bank, and consider confirmation by a prime bank. For smaller transactions, mobile money and fintech escrow services are increasingly viable, especially in East and West Africa. For larger deals, a standby letter of credit or a documentary collection through a correspondent bank may balance cost and risk. Always negotiate the payment mechanism before price, because payment risk changes the true value of the contract. The fourth structural decision is currency. Many African currencies are volatile against the dollar and euro. If you invoice in a hard currency, you shift exchange risk to the buyer, who may demand a discount. If you invoice in local currency, you absorb the risk. A common compromise is to price in a hard currency but allow settlement in local currency at a pre-agreed rate band, with a sharing clause for movements beyond the band. This is especially relevant in Nigeria, Egypt, Ghana, and Zambia, where foreign exchange availability can delay payments even when the buyer is willing. The fifth structural decision is dispute resolution. Litigation in African courts can be slow and unpredictable. Arbitration is often preferred. The New York Convention has broad ratification across Africa, and institutions such as the Cairo Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration, the Lagos Chamber of Commerce and Industry, and the Arbitration Foundation of Southern Africa offer credible forums. When drafting an arbitration clause, specify the seat, the language, the number of arbitrators, and the governing law. Avoid vague clauses that say disputes will be resolved amicably, because they are unenforceable. The sixth structural decision is compliance and documentation. African trade is subject to rules of origin, sanitary and phytosanitary measures, and increasingly to environmental and social standards. Under the African Continental Free Trade Area, tariff preferences depend on meeting rules of origin. If your product includes inputs from outside Africa, you may need to prove substantial transformation. Build a documentation checklist into your negotiation: certificate of origin, phytosanitary certificate, commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, and any sector-specific permits. Missing documents at the border can cost more than the tariff you tried to avoid. The seventh structural decision is local content and partnership. Many African governments require local equity, local employment, or technology transfer in sectors such as mining, oil and gas, telecommunications, and agriculture. Even where not legally required, a local partner can unlock permits, networks, and political goodwill. When negotiating with a local partner, define governance, profit sharing, exit rights, and dispute resolution clearly. Do not rely on trust alone. Put it in writing. Now let us apply this to a practical scenario. Imagine you are a Ghanaian processor of shea butter negotiating a supply contract with a French cosmetics company. Your structural choices might be: a direct sales contract governed by English law, FOB Tema, payment by irrevocable letter of credit confirmed by a European bank, pricing in euros with a currency adjustment clause, arbitration in London or Accra, and a documentation package including organic certification and AfCFTA certificate of origin. Your counterpart may push for DAP Lyon, open account payment, and French law. Your job is to trade these points against price, volume, and duration. If they want open account, you ask for a higher price or a bank guarantee. If they want DAP, you ask for a longer contract or a minimum volume commitment. This is the essence of structural negotiation: every risk you accept must be paid for somewhere else. Before you move to the next lesson, draft a one-page term sheet for a real or imagined African trade deal. Include the parties, the product, the Incoterm, the payment mechanism, the currency, the governing law, the dispute resolution forum, and the duration. Then identify three points you would trade and three points you would not. This discipline will serve you in every negotiation. 2. Cultural Intelligence and Communication in African Trade Negotiations

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 2. This lesson addresses cultural intelligence and communication in African trade negotiations. Africa is home to thousands of ethnic groups, hundreds of languages, and multiple business cultures. Treating the continent as one cultural bloc is a strategic error. At the same time, there are patterns that experienced negotiators recognize across many African markets. Your task is to combine general awareness with specific preparation for each counterpart. Start with the concept of relationship before transaction. In many African business contexts, trust is built through personal connection, shared meals, and demonstrated respect before contracts are discussed. This does not mean business is secondary. It means the business conversation is embedded in a social one. If you arrive with a rigid agenda and insist on signing in the first meeting, you may be seen as transactional and unreliable. A better approach is to invest the first meeting in listening, asking about the counterpart's history, their family, their community, and their vision. Then propose a second meeting for commercial details. This is not wasted time. It is due diligence on character. The second pattern is indirect communication. In many African cultures, direct refusal is considered rude. A counterpart may say we will see, it is difficult, or we shall try, when they mean no. Your job is to read the context, tone, and body language, and to ask clarifying questions that allow the counterpart to save face. For example, instead of asking will you accept this price, ask what price would make this work for your side. Instead of asking do you have authority to sign, ask who else needs to be comfortable with this decision. This approach surfaces constraints without forcing a public no. The third pattern is hierarchy and respect for elders. In many African business settings, age and title carry weight. Address senior people first, use appropriate titles, and avoid challenging a senior person in front of their juniors. If you need to disagree, do it privately or through a trusted intermediary. In some cultures, a younger manager may have technical knowledge but will not contradict an older director in a meeting. If you need a technical answer, ask for a separate working session with the technical team. The fourth pattern is time. Punctuality norms vary. In some cities, meetings start on time. In others, a 10 a.m. meeting may begin at 10:45. This is not necessarily disrespect. It may reflect traffic, public transport, or a different relationship to scheduling. Your response should be professional: arrive on time, bring work to do while waiting, and do not show irritation. At the same time, set deadlines clearly and confirm them in writing. If a counterpart is chronically late with deliverables, escalate through the relationship, not through public criticism. The fifth pattern is negotiation style. Many African negotiators are comfortable with long discussions, repeated revisiting of points, and the use of proverbs, stories, and analogies. This can frustrate counterparts who prefer linear, time-bound agendas. The practical response is to build extra time into your negotiation calendar, to summarize agreements at the end of each session, and to send a written recap within 24 hours. The recap becomes the anchor for the next meeting. If there is a disagreement about what was agreed, the recap allows you to correct it early. The sixth pattern is language. English, French, Portuguese, Arabic, and Swahili are widely used in different regions. But the working language of a negotiation may not be the mother tongue of your counterpart. Speak clearly, avoid idioms, and confirm understanding by asking the counterpart to restate key points. If you are negotiating in French with a West African counterpart, remember that African French has its own expressions. If you are negotiating in English with an East African counterpart, remember that British and American business terms differ. Use a glossary of key terms and agree on definitions before discussing numbers. The seventh pattern is religion and calendar. In Muslim-majority countries such as Senegal, Mali, Niger, and Egypt, Friday prayers and Ramadan affect scheduling. In Christian-majority countries, Sunday and religious holidays matter. In Ethiopia, the calendar and fasting periods follow Orthodox traditions. In Israel, the Jewish calendar applies, though this is outside Africa. In India, Diwali and other festivals matter for Indian-African trade. Check the calendar before proposing dates. Avoid scheduling major decisions during Ramadan if your counterpart is fasting, or during the last week of December when many offices close. The eighth pattern is gender. African business culture is not monolithic on gender. In some contexts, women hold senior commercial roles and are treated with authority. In others, gender bias persists. If you are a woman negotiating in a male-dominated environment, prepare thoroughly, use data, and build alliances with senior sponsors. If you are a man negotiating with a woman counterpart, treat her with the same professional respect you would give a male peer. Do not address questions to her male colleague unless she directs you to. The ninth pattern is diaspora and return migration. Many African negotiators have studied or worked abroad and are comfortable with Western business norms. Others have built careers locally and expect local norms. Do not assume that a counterpart who speaks fluent English or French shares your cultural reference points. Ask about their background, their company history, and their preferred way of working. The tenth pattern is conflict and reconciliation. In some African contexts, preserving harmony is more important than winning a point. If a disagreement arises, allow a cooling-off period, use a neutral intermediary, and frame concessions as mutual gains rather than defeats. This is especially important in family-owned businesses, which are common across the continent. A public confrontation can damage the relationship permanently. Now let us practice. Choose a country where you currently do or plan to do business. Research its business culture using at least three sources: a country commercial guide, a cultural intelligence database, and an interview with someone who has negotiated there. Write a one-page brief covering greetings, meeting etiquette, decision-making style, communication preferences, and holiday calendar. Then role-play a negotiation with a colleague who plays the counterpart. Record the role-play and review it for moments where you talked too much, missed an indirect signal, or failed to confirm understanding. This exercise will build the muscle memory you need for real negotiations. In the next lesson we will focus on pricing, costing, and value creation in African trade deals, including how to build a defensible price model.

3. Pricing, Costing, and Value Creation in African Trade

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 3. This lesson is about pricing, costing, and value creation in African trade negotiations. Price is not a number. It is the outcome of a cost model, a value proposition, and a negotiation strategy. If you cannot explain your price, you cannot defend it. If you cannot quantify your value, you will compete only on cost. We will build a practical framework you can use in any African market. Start with the cost floor. Your absolute minimum price must cover all incremental costs of the deal plus a contribution to fixed costs and profit. Incremental costs include raw materials, direct labor, packaging, inland transport, export documentation, customs clearance, port charges, freight, insurance, and financing costs. Fixed costs include factory overhead, management salaries, and depreciation. If you price below the sum of incremental and allocated fixed costs, you are subsidizing the buyer. In African trade, hidden costs often appear at the border. These include demurrage, detention, storage, unexpected inspections, and informal payments. Build a contingency line into your cost model, typically 3 to 7 percent of landed cost, depending on the corridor. Next, map the value chain from your factory to the end user. Identify every actor who touches the product: freight forwarder, customs broker, transporter, wholesaler, retailer, and sometimes a local agent. Each actor takes a margin. Your price must leave enough margin for the next actor to sell. If you sell to an importer at a price that leaves them no margin, they will not push your product. A useful exercise is to calculate the landed cost in the destination market and compare it to the retail price. If the retail price is 100 and your ex-works price is 20, there is 80 of value to distribute across the chain. Your negotiation is about how much of that 80 you can capture. Then build your value proposition. African buyers, like buyers everywhere, care about more than price. They care about reliability, quality consistency, lead time, after-sales support, and access to finance. If you can offer shorter lead times, you can charge a premium. If you can offer credit terms, you can charge a financing margin. If you can offer technical support, you can differentiate from competitors who only sell product. Write down three things you do better than your competitors and quantify them. For example: we deliver in 21 days versus the industry average of 35 days, which reduces your inventory holding cost by 40 percent. That is a value argument, not a price argument. Now consider currency. If you invoice in dollars or euros, you bear less currency risk but you may be less competitive in local currency terms. If you invoice in local currency, you bear more risk but you may win more business. A practical approach is to price in hard currency and offer a local currency option at a rate that includes a risk premium. For example, if the spot rate is 1 dollar equals 600 local units, you might offer 1 dollar equals 620 local units to cover expected depreciation. If the buyer accepts, you are protected. If they do not, you keep the hard currency price. Another approach is to include a currency adjustment clause that triggers if the rate moves beyond a band, say plus or minus 5 percent. Both parties share the risk beyond the band. This is common in long-term supply contracts in Nigeria, Egypt, and Angola. Next, consider payment terms. A letter of credit costs money. The issuing bank charges a fee, the confirming bank charges a fee, and the document preparation takes time. If you offer open account terms, you save those fees but you take credit risk. Price the difference. If a letter of credit costs 2 percent of contract value, and you are asked to move to open account, you should ask for a price increase of at least 2 percent, or a bank guarantee, or credit insurance. Do not give away payment security for free. Then consider volume and duration. Buyers always ask for a discount for large volumes. But large volumes may require you to invest in new capacity, hire workers, or buy additional raw materials. Calculate the incremental cost of the additional volume and the risk of holding that capacity if the buyer reduces orders. A volume discount should be tied to a minimum purchase commitment, a take-or-pay clause, or a longer contract term. Otherwise you are giving a discount for a promise that may not materialize. Now consider the negotiation itself. Never open with your best price. Open with a price that is defensible based on your value proposition and leaves room to concede. If your target price is 100, open at 115. If the buyer opens at 70, do not counter at 100. Counter at 110 and ask what they can offer in return. Trade concessions, do not give them away. If they want a lower price, ask for a longer contract, a larger volume, faster payment, or a reference customer. Every concession should have a price. Also, be prepared to walk away. The most powerful position in a negotiation is the ability to say no. If the buyer's price is below your cost floor, decline politely and explain why. Sometimes the buyer will come back with a better offer. Sometimes they will not. Either way, you have protected your business. Let us work through a case. A Tanzanian coffee exporter is negotiating with a German roaster. The exporter's cost floor is 3.20 dollars per kilogram FOB Dar es Salaam. The roaster offers 3.00 dollars. The exporter calculates that the roaster's retail price for the roasted coffee is 25 dollars per kilogram. The exporter also knows that the roaster values traceability and direct trade relationships. The exporter proposes 3.40 dollars per kilogram, with a three-year contract, a minimum volume of two containers per month, and payment by irrevocable letter of credit. In return, the exporter will provide farm-level traceability data, participate in the roaster's marketing campaign, and host an annual buyer visit. The roaster counters at 3.25 dollars with a two-year contract. The exporter accepts 3.30 dollars with a three-year contract and a price review clause tied to the New York C price. Both parties gain. The exporter has a higher price and a longer commitment. The roaster has traceability and a reliable supply. This is value creation, not just price splitting. Before the next lesson, build a cost model for one of your products or services. Include all incremental costs, a contingency, and a contribution to fixed costs. Then write three value arguments that justify a price above your cost floor. Practice saying them out loud in 30 seconds each. 4. Risk Management and Contract Structuring in African Trade

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 4. This lesson covers risk management and contract structuring in African trade. Every cross-border deal carries risk. Your job is not to eliminate risk but to identify it, price it, and allocate it to the party best able to manage it. We will examine six categories of risk and the contractual tools you can use to address them. The first category is political and sovereign risk. This includes expropriation, currency inconvertibility, war, civil unrest, and changes in law. In Africa, political risk varies widely. Some countries have stable institutions and predictable policy. Others experience sudden changes in leadership, trade policy, or foreign exchange rules. Tools to manage political risk include political risk insurance from providers such as MIGA, the African Trade Insurance Agency, or private insurers. You can also structure the deal through an offshore special purpose vehicle, use a multilateral or bilateral investment treaty, and include a stabilization clause that freezes certain terms for the duration of the contract. If you are exporting, you can require payment confirmation from a bank in a stable jurisdiction. The second category is currency risk. This includes devaluation, inconvertibility, and transfer delays. In countries such as Nigeria, Angola, Zimbabwe, and Ethiopia, access to foreign currency has been a major constraint. Tools include invoicing in hard currency, using a currency adjustment clause, requiring payment through an offshore account, using a letter of credit confirmed by a prime bank, and hedging with forward contracts or options where available. For long-term contracts, consider a pricing formula that shares currency risk between buyer and seller. For example, if the local currency depreciates by more than 10 percent, the price is adjusted by half the depreciation. The third category is counterparty risk. This is the risk that the buyer does not pay or the seller does not deliver. Tools include letters of credit, standby letters of credit, bank guarantees, credit insurance, documentary collections, and escrow accounts. For smaller transactions, advance payment or partial advance payment is common. For larger transactions, a combination of advance payment, progress payments, and retention is common. Always conduct due diligence on your counterpart. Check their registration, their tax status, their litigation history, and their reputation in the market. Ask for trade references and call them. A few hours of due diligence can save months of litigation. The fourth category is performance risk. This is the risk that the goods do not meet specifications, arrive late, or are damaged. Tools include clear technical specifications, pre-shipment inspection, third-party quality certification, Incoterms that allocate risk clearly, insurance, and liquidated damages clauses. If you are the buyer, insist on pre-shipment inspection by a reputable firm such as SGS, Bureau Veritas, or Intertek. If you are the seller, document your quality control process and share it with the buyer. The fifth category is legal and regulatory risk. This includes changes in tariffs, rules of origin, sanitary and phytosanitary measures, and environmental regulations. Tools include a force majeure clause that covers changes in law, a change in law clause that allocates the cost of compliance, and a requirement that the buyer obtains import permits. Under the African Continental Free Trade Area, rules of origin are critical. If your product does not meet the origin criteria, you may lose tariff preferences. Build a compliance file with bills of materials, supplier declarations, and production records. The sixth category is logistics and infrastructure risk. This includes port congestion, road conditions, border delays, and theft. Tools include choosing reliable freight forwarders, using GPS tracking, insuring cargo, building buffer stock, and selecting routes with better infrastructure. In landlocked countries such as Uganda, Rwanda, Zambia, and Mali, transit times can vary significantly. Build flexibility into your delivery schedule and communicate proactively with the buyer. Now let us discuss contract structuring. A well-structured contract does not prevent disputes, but it makes them easier to resolve. Key clauses include: definitions, scope of supply, price and payment, delivery and Incoterms, quality and inspection, title and risk, force majeure, change in law, currency adjustment, confidentiality, intellectual property, warranties, indemnities, limitation of liability, termination, dispute resolution, and governing law. Each clause should be negotiated with the risk allocation in mind. For example, if you accept a broad force majeure clause, you may be excused from performance for events beyond your control. If you accept a narrow clause, you may be liable for delays caused by port congestion. If you are the seller, push for a broad clause that includes supplier failure, transport disruption, and government action. If you are the buyer, push for a narrow clause and require notice and mitigation. Another important clause is limitation of liability. Many African legal systems allow parties to limit liability, but some do not. Specify a cap, such as the contract value or a multiple of the contract value, and exclude indirect and consequential damages. This protects you from catastrophic claims. Also, specify the dispute resolution mechanism. Arbitration is often preferred over litigation. Choose a seat that is neutral and has a good track record. Choose arbitrators with expertise in your sector. Specify the language and the rules. If you choose institutional arbitration, name the institution. If you choose ad hoc arbitration, specify the appointing authority. Consider mediation as a first step. Many African cultures value reconciliation, and mediation can preserve the relationship. Now let us apply this to a scenario. A South African engineering firm is bidding for a water treatment project in Kenya. The project is funded by a development bank. Risks include political risk, currency risk, counterparty risk, performance risk, legal risk, and logistics risk. The firm structures the deal as follows: a special purpose vehicle registered in Mauritius, a political risk insurance policy from MIGA, a contract governed by English law, arbitration in London, payment in dollars through a letter of credit confirmed by a European bank, a currency adjustment clause, a performance bond, a parent company guarantee, and a comprehensive insurance package. The firm also conducts due diligence on the Kenyan partner and includes a local content plan. This structure is more complex than a simple export contract, but it matches the risk profile of the project. Before the next lesson, identify the top five risks in one of your current or planned deals. For each risk, write down the likelihood, the impact, and the mitigation tool you will use. Then draft the relevant contract clause or negotiate it with your counterpart. 5. Closing, Implementation, and Relationship Management

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 5. This lesson covers closing, implementation, and relationship management in African trade negotiations. A signed contract is not the end of the negotiation. It is the beginning of a relationship that will determine whether the deal creates value or destroys it. Many international deals fail not because the terms were bad but because implementation was poor. Your job is to close well and then manage the relationship with the same discipline you applied to the negotiation. Start with closing. Closing is the process of moving from agreement in principle to a signed, enforceable contract. It requires careful planning. Before the final session, prepare a closing checklist. This should include: the final text of the contract, all annexes and schedules, the agreed payment instruments, the required permits and licenses, the shipping and logistics plan, the quality inspection protocol, the insurance certificates, and the contact list for implementation. Assign a responsible person for each item and a deadline. During the closing session, review the contract clause by clause. Do not assume that a clause is agreed because it was discussed. Read it aloud and confirm understanding. If there is a language difference, use a certified translator. If there is a legal difference, consult local counsel. Do not sign a contract you do not understand. Also, confirm the authority of the signatories. Ask for a board resolution or a power of attorney if the signatory is not the CEO or the owner. This is not a sign of distrust. It is standard practice in cross-border transactions. After signing, hold a kick-off meeting within two weeks. The purpose is to align the implementation teams. Invite the commercial, operational, finance, and logistics people from both sides. Review the contract summary, the delivery schedule, the payment schedule, the quality standards, and the communication protocol. Agree on a single point of contact for each side and a regular reporting cadence. For most African trade deals, a weekly or biweekly call is appropriate during the first three months, moving to monthly once the relationship stabilizes. Now consider implementation. The first shipment or first payment is the moment of truth. If it goes well, trust grows. If it goes badly, trust collapses. Prepare meticulously. Confirm the production schedule, the inspection date, the booking with the freight forwarder, the customs documentation, and the payment instructions. Send a pre-shipment sample if required. Confirm the letter of credit terms with your bank and the buyer's bank. If there is a discrepancy, resolve it before shipment, not after. Communicate proactively. If you anticipate a delay, tell the buyer before they discover it. A proactive call preserves trust. A surprise delay destroys it. During implementation, document everything. Keep a file with all correspondence, invoices, bills of lading, inspection certificates, and payment receipts. If a dispute arises, this file is your evidence. Also, track key performance indicators. These might include on-time delivery, quality acceptance rate, payment days, and customer satisfaction. Review them monthly with the buyer. If performance is below target, discuss corrective action. If performance is above target, discuss expansion. Now consider relationship management. In African trade, relationships are assets. They take time to build and can be lost quickly. Invest in the relationship beyond the transaction. Visit your counterpart in their country at least once a year. Attend industry events together. Share market intelligence. Introduce them to useful contacts. Celebrate their successes. If there is a problem, address it directly and privately. Do not escalate in public. Do not copy senior people on a dispute before you have tried to resolve it at the working level. If you need to escalate, do it respectfully and with a proposed solution. Also, manage expectations. African markets can change quickly. A new regulation, a currency devaluation, or a political transition can affect the deal. If circumstances change, renegotiate in good faith. Do not insist on the letter of the contract if the spirit of the deal is at risk. A flexible partner is a valued partner. Now let us discuss dispute resolution in practice. Even with good relationships, disputes happen. The first step is to talk. Many disputes are caused by misunderstanding, not bad faith. Clarify the facts, listen to the other side, and look for a solution that preserves the relationship. If direct talks fail, consider mediation. A neutral mediator can help both sides find a solution without the cost and delay of arbitration. If mediation fails, proceed to arbitration or litigation as provided in the contract. Do not delay. In some jurisdictions, delay can weaken your position. Also, enforce your rights. If the buyer does not pay, use the letter of credit or the bank guarantee. If the seller does not deliver, claim under the performance bond. Do not let a dispute drag on for years. It consumes management time and damages your reputation. Finally, consider exit. Every relationship has a lifecycle. At some point, you may need to exit the deal. Plan for it. Include termination provisions in the contract. Specify notice periods, outstanding obligations, and post-termination rights. If you are in a joint venture, include a buyout formula and a dispute resolution mechanism for valuation. If you are in a distribution agreement, include performance targets and termination rights. A clean exit preserves your reputation and allows you to redeploy your resources. Let us work through a case. A Nigerian agro-exporter signs a contract with a Dutch importer for 12 containers of sesame seeds over 12 months. The first shipment is delayed by two weeks due to port congestion in Lagos. The importer is frustrated. The exporter calls the importer before the delay becomes a crisis, explains the cause, provides a revised schedule, and offers a small discount on the next shipment. The importer accepts. The exporter also invites the importer to visit the farm and the processing facility. The visit builds trust. By the sixth shipment, the importer increases the volume. By the twelfth shipment, the two companies sign a three-year agreement. This is relationship management in action. Before you complete this module, write a closing and implementation plan for one of your deals. Include a closing checklist, a kick-off agenda, a reporting cadence, a risk monitoring plan, and a relationship management plan. Share it with your team and assign responsibilities. Then review it after 90 days and adjust. This discipline will turn a signed contract into a profitable, long-term partnership. This concludes Module 7 of Foreign Trade Negotiation. You have learned how to structure cross-border deals, read cultural dynamics, price for value, manage risk, and close and implement agreements. Practice these skills in every negotiation, and you will build a reputation as a reliable, effective partner in African trade.

<strong style="color:#b00020;">SAFETY WARNING Food, Feed, and Chemical Compliance</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7a0016;">If your transaction involves food, animal feed, live animals, veterinary products, or agricultural chemicals, do not sign or ship until you have verified maximum residue limits (MRLs), withholding periods, and product registration in the destination market. Non-compliant pesticide residues, veterinary drug residues, or aflatoxin levels can trigger border rejection, destruction of consignments, and legal liability. Confirm requirements with the destination competent authority and the relevant Codex Alimentarius, WHO, or WOAH (formerly OIE) standard. When in doubt, hold the shipment and obtain written clearance.</p>

<h3>Negotiation instruments you should have on the table</h3>

<p><strong>1. Incoterms 2020.</strong> Incoterms define where risk and cost transfer. For African cross-border deals, FCA, FOB, CIF, and DAP are the most common. Agree the Incoterm before price. A CIF price and a DAP price are not comparable. For inland African corridors, DAP and DDP require careful treatment of border delays and clearing costs.</p>

<p><strong>2. Payment instruments.</strong> Letters of credit (L/C), documentary collections, advance payment, and open account each shift risk. In many African markets, confirmed L/Cs from a reputable bank remain the safest instrument for first-time transactions. For repeat relationships, open account with credit insurance may be more competitive. Negotiate the payment instrument alongside the price, not after.</p>

<p><strong>3. Rules of origin.</strong> Preferential access under AfCFTA, ECOWAS, EAC, or an EPA depends on meeting rules of origin. If your product is only traded, not substantially transformed, it may not qualify. Confirm the product-specific rule and the documentary evidence required before you promise a duty-free landed price.</p>

<p><strong>4. SPS and quality certificates.</strong> Phytosanitary certificates, health certificates, certificates of origin, and private scheme certificates (GlobalG.A.P., organic, Fairtrade) are negotiation assets. They reduce buyer risk and can justify a price premium. Build the cost of certification into your costing.</p>

<p><strong>5. Dispute resolution.</strong> Specify the governing law, the seat of arbitration, and the language. For intra-African deals, consider the OHADA regime in West and Central Africa, or arbitration under a recognised centre such as the Cairo Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration or the Arbitration Foundation of Southern Africa. A clear clause is cheaper than a cross-border lawsuit.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Confirm the tariff line and preference.</strong> Verify the HS code and whether your product qualifies for AfCFTA, ECOWAS, EAC, EPA, or AGOA treatment.</li>
<li><strong>Verify SPS and food safety requirements.</strong> Obtain the destination market's MRLs, aflatoxin limits, and certification requirements in writing.</li>
<li><strong>Agree the Incoterm before the price.</strong> State the edition (Incoterms 2020) and the named place.</li>
<li><strong>Match the payment instrument to the risk.</strong> Use confirmed L/C, documentary collection, or insured open account as appropriate.</li>
<li><strong>Document rules of origin.</strong> Keep supplier declarations, production records, and certificates of origin.</li>
<li><strong>Check currency and transfer risk.</strong> Confirm convertibility, repatriation rules, and hedging options.</li>
<li><strong>Include a dispute resolution clause.</strong> Specify governing law, seat, language, and number of arbitrators.</li>
<li><strong>Plan logistics and inspection.</strong> Agree inspection timing, sampling, and who bears delay costs at the border.</li>
</ul>
