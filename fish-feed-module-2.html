Lesson 2.1~11 min

Yolk Sac Absorption and First Feeding

In this lesson
  • Recognise the end of yolk sac absorption and the window when first feeding must begin
  • Set the protein, lipid and energy specification for a first-feeding diet
  • Estimate the cost of a missed first feeding in surviving fingerlings

A newly hatched larva carries its own packed lunch. The yolk sac supplies everything it needs for the first days of life, and while that sac lasts the larva does not need you. The moment it empties, the larva must find food within a short window or it passes what hatchery workers call the point of no return, where it is too weak to feed even if food arrives.

How long the sac lasts depends on temperature, because the larva is cold-blooded. Warmer water burns the yolk faster. For Nile tilapia at 27-30C and African catfish at 25-28C the sac is typically gone within a few days of hatching, and the larva begins swimming up in the water column looking for food. The practical signal is behavioural, not a calendar date: when the belly bulge disappears and the fry start moving horizontally rather than lying on the bottom, food must already be in the water. Do not wait for them to look hungry. Have food present before the sac is finished.

The specification for that first food is the most demanding of the whole production cycle. Nile tilapia fry need 50-56 percent crude protein; African catfish, channel catfish and common carp fry need 45-50 percent. Total lipid runs 8-12 percent for tilapia fry and fingerlings and 10-12 percent for channel catfish fry, with 0.8-1.5 percent EPA plus DHA for tilapia fry and 0.5-1.0 percent for the catfishes. Digestible energy runs 3,300-3,500 kcal/kg with a protein-to-energy ratio of 6-7 kcal per gram of protein, tighter and richer than the 8-10 used at grow-out. Vitamin C sits at 100-200 mg/kg and, since fry store none, must be a stabilised form.

Amino acid density matters more than total protein at this stage. Tilapia fry need 1.8-2.0 percent lysine, 0.9-1.0 percent methionine plus cystine and 1.5-1.7 percent arginine in the dry diet. Growth stops at the first one that runs out. This is why a 50 percent protein feed built from cheap plant meal often performs worse than a 45 percent feed built around fish meal or black soldier fly larvae meal.

Particle size is the other hard constraint. A fry under two weeks old has a mouth less than 1 mm wide, so the feed must be 0.8-1.5 mm, in practice a fine powder or microdiet. A pellet the fry cannot physically take is not feed, it is pollution.

Worked example. Amina runs a small tilapia hatchery near Ibadan, Nigeria. She stocks 20,000 swim-up fry. Good hatchery practice and the broodstock conditioning covered in Module 11 should put her survival from yolk-sac to free-feeding well above 80 percent. Suppose a delayed first feeding drops it to 60 percent. She ends with 12,000 fry instead of 16,000, a loss of 4,000. Local hatcheries sell fingerlings at USD 0.03-0.10 each; take USD 0.06. That is 4,000 x 0.06 = USD 240 lost from one batch, against a first-feed cost that is trivial: a fry microdiet at even USD 2.00/kg, fed at a few hundred grams over the first week, is under USD 1. Nothing else on the farm has that ratio.

The reference is clear that broodstock feeding drives this directly. Larval survival from yolk-sac to free feeding improves 15-25 percent when the mother was fed above 15 percent lipid with EPA plus DHA above 1.5 percent, and deformity rates fall from 5-10 percent to 1-3 percent with balanced vitamin supplementation. If your first feeding keeps failing, look upstream at the broodstock diet before you blame the fry feed.

Practical routine for the first week: keep food continuously available rather than in two large meals, feed little and often through daylight hours, keep the water warm and stable, and siphon uneaten material daily because at these feed rates a fouled tank kills faster than hunger.

Lesson 2.2~11 min

Live Food Culture (Rotifers, Brine Shrimp, Copepods)

In this lesson
  • Decide when live food is worth the labour for a small African hatchery
  • Set up a simple green-water and zooplankton system using pond fertilisation
  • Compare the cost of live food against an imported microdiet per thousand fry

Live food solves the problem that a very small larva often will not recognise a dead particle as food. A moving prey item triggers the strike reflex, is soft enough to digest with an immature gut, and carries its own digestive enzymes into the larva. For African catfish in particular, hatchery operators across the continent rely on live or fresh moving feed for the first days before any dry diet is accepted.

There are three practical categories. Rotifers are the smallest, suitable for the tiniest larvae. Brine shrimp, Artemia, hatched from cysts, are the industry standard because the newly hatched nauplius is a convenient size and the cysts store dry for long periods. Copepods and pond zooplankton such as Daphnia and Moina are the cheapest option and often the only realistic one for a farm without imported inputs. Chironomid larvae, the red worms in pond mud, are also used, and the reference lists live Artemia and chironomids as spawning-trigger and conditioning feeds for broodstock.

The honest position for a small African hatchery is this. Imported Artemia cysts are expensive and often hard to source, and prices vary far too much between countries for any single figure to be reliable, so treat any price you are quoted as your own local number and work from it. Green water and pond-cultured zooplankton cost you fertiliser, a tank or a small pond, and labour, and nothing else. Most small operations should build the green-water route first and buy Artemia only for the species and the days where nothing else works.

The green-water method in practice. Fill a shallow tank or small nursery pond with filtered water. Fertilise it to grow phytoplankton, which then feeds zooplankton. The reference warns that the nitrogen to phosphorus ratio matters: keep it around 3:1 to 5:1, because excess nitrogen relative to phosphorus pushes the bloom towards cyanobacteria, which produce microcystin and anatoxin and can kill fish or accumulate in their organs. Organic fertilisation with manure or inorganic urea and phosphate both work. Allow several days for the bloom to establish and the zooplankton to follow, then stock the larvae into it or harvest the zooplankton with a fine net and carry it to the larval tanks.

Good zooplankton is nutritionally strong on protein and generally weak on the long-chain fatty acids larvae need. This is why hatcheries enrich live food, usually by feeding the live food an oil emulsion for some hours before harvest. For a smallholder the practical version is simpler: do not rely on live food alone for more than the first days, and get the fry onto a properly formulated microdiet, carrying 50-56 percent protein for tilapia and 0.8-1.5 percent EPA plus DHA, as early as they will accept it.

Worked example. Peter runs a catfish hatchery near Kisumu, Kenya and stocks 50,000 larvae. He builds two 4 m by 2 m green-water tanks. Costs: pond liner and frame USD 120 for the pair, urea and single superphosphate for a season USD 25, a fine plankton net USD 15, and roughly one hour of labour a day. Call it USD 160 of capital and USD 25 of consumables in the first season. If that live food lifts his day-30 survival from 40 percent to 55 percent, he gains 7,500 fingerlings. At USD 0.06 each that is USD 450 from one batch. The system pays for itself in a single cycle, and the capital is reusable.

A caution on hygiene. Live food cultures crash, and a crashed culture is a bacterial soup. Keep two cultures running so one failure does not stop feeding, harvest with a clean net, rinse the harvest before feeding, and never carry water from a sick larval tank back into the culture. Module 10 covers the wider disease picture; the rule here is simply that live food is a feed and a disease route at the same time.

Lesson 2.3~11 min

Weaning to Formulated Feeds

In this lesson
  • Plan a co-feeding overlap between live food and dry microdiet
  • Match particle size to fry mouth width through the weaning period
  • Calculate the feed cost saving from weaning one week earlier

Weaning is the transfer from live or moist food to a dry formulated diet. Done well it is invisible: growth continues in a straight line. Done badly it produces a week of stalled growth, a spread of sizes that never closes, and a wave of mortality that farmers usually blame on disease.

The governing principle is overlap, not switching. Never take live food away on a Monday and offer pellets on a Tuesday. Introduce the dry microdiet while live food is still being given, at first as a small share of the daily ration, and increase the dry share day by day while reducing the live share. A common pattern is to run the overlap over roughly a week to ten days, though the right length depends on species and temperature and you should let fry behaviour, not the calendar, decide when to finish. Watch the fry: if dry particles are being taken in the water column rather than settling, the transition is working.

Particle size drives the schedule as much as time does. Fry from zero to two weeks have a mouth under 1 mm and take 0.8-1.5 mm particles. Early fingerlings from two to eight weeks have a mouth of 1-2 mm and take 1.5-3 mm crumble; the reference notes that crumble sinks inconsistently, which is a nuisance you manage by feeding smaller amounts more often. From eight to twelve weeks the mouth is 2-3 mm and the fish takes 2-4 mm, often as a 50:50 mix of crumble and pellet. Every step up in size should be tested on a small group before you move the whole batch.

The dry diet must not be a diluted grow-out feed. Through weaning, tilapia fry still need 50-56 percent protein falling to 40-45 percent as fingerlings, African catfish 45-50 percent falling to 40-45 percent, with 8-12 percent lipid and 3,300-3,500 kcal/kg digestible energy. Water stability matters more here than anywhere else, because tiny particles leach fast: a formulation holding only 2-4 hours loses 20-30 percent of its protein and energy within two hours, and at fry particle size it is worse. Gelatinised starch or a proper binder is not optional.

Worked example. Fatou weans catfish fry at a small hatchery near Kafue, Zambia, working with 30,000 fry. Her live-food operation costs her about USD 4 a day in fertiliser, net replacement and labour. Her microdiet costs USD 2.50/kg and the fry batch eats about 1.2 kg a day at this stage, USD 3.00. Full live food is therefore USD 4.00 a day and full dry feed USD 3.00 a day. Weaning one week earlier saves 7 x USD 1.00 = USD 7 in direct cost, which is small. The real prize is elsewhere: dry feed is available on demand, does not crash, and lets her stock the next larval batch into the green-water tanks a week sooner, adding an extra production run over a season. Against that, weaning too early costs survival, and at USD 0.06 a fingerling a 5 percent survival loss on 30,000 fry is 1,500 fish, USD 90, which wipes out thirteen weeks of that USD 1 a day saving. Wean as early as the fry allow and not one day earlier than that.

Two practical checks. First, sample and weigh 30 fry before weaning starts and again a week after it ends; if average weight has not increased, the transition failed and you should step back to a higher live-food share. Second, watch size spread. Weaning is the point where uniform batches turn ragged, and Module 3 of this course deals with grading; the cheapest grading is a weaning that does not create the spread in the first place.

Lesson 2.4~11 min

Larval Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Build a fry diet that meets protein, lipid, energy and amino acid targets together
  • Select ingredients that give high digestibility at fry particle size
  • Price a 50 kg farm-made fry diet against an imported commercial microdiet

A fry diet is not a grow-out diet ground finer. Everything about it is tighter: higher protein, higher energy, higher amino acid density, better ingredient digestibility, and a much smaller tolerance for anti-nutritional factors, because a fry gut is short, immature and easily overwhelmed.

The specification to hit for tilapia fry is 50-56 percent crude protein, 8-12 percent lipid, 3,300-3,500 kcal/kg digestible energy giving a 6-7 kcal per gram protein-to-energy ratio, 0.8-1.5 percent EPA plus DHA, 1.8-2.0 percent lysine, 0.9-1.0 percent methionine plus cystine, 1.5-1.7 percent arginine, 0.6-0.8 percent phosphorus, 0.8-1.2 percent calcium, vitamin C at 100-200 mg/kg in stabilised form and vitamin D3 at 1,000-2,000 IU/kg. For African catfish fry drop protein to 45-50 percent, lipid to 7-10 percent and EPA plus DHA to 0.5-1.0 percent.

Ingredient choice follows from digestibility. Fish meal at 60-65 percent protein digests at 90-95 percent with 2.2-2.4 percent lysine and 0.9-1.1 percent methionine, and costs USD 1.50-3.00/kg. Black soldier fly larvae meal is the outstanding local alternative: 40-45 percent protein, 25-35 percent lipid, 85-90 percent protein digestibility, no phytate, and USD 1.00-1.50/kg, with the one caveat that it is slightly low in methionine and needs more than 0.3 percent supplemental methionine. Trials put tilapia fingerling growth on BSFL meal at 90-100 percent of a fish meal control, and near equal when methionine is supplemented. Soybean meal is cheap at USD 0.30-0.50/kg but digests at only 75-85 percent and carries phytate, trypsin inhibitors and oligosaccharides. Keep soy modest in a fry diet, and if you use it, ferment it: 48-72 hours with a Lactobacillus starter from yoghurt cuts phytate 30-50 percent and lifts methionine bioavailability 10-15 percent for USD 0.05-0.08/kg.

Worked example, a 50 kg farm-made tilapia fry diet near Bagamoyo, Tanzania. Take local artisanal fish meal 40 percent of the mix (20 kg at USD 2.00 = USD 40), black soldier fly larvae meal 20 percent (10 kg at USD 1.25 = USD 12.50), fermented soybean meal 20 percent (10 kg at USD 0.45 plus USD 0.06 fermentation = USD 5.10), gelatinised maize 8 percent (4 kg at USD 0.22 = USD 0.88), soybean oil 6 percent (3 kg at USD 1.00 = USD 3.00), vitamin premix 2.5 percent (1.25 kg, contributing about USD 0.05/kg of finished feed = USD 2.50), mineral premix 2.5 percent (USD 2.50), and DL-methionine 0.5 percent (0.25 kg at USD 5.00 = USD 1.25) plus L-lysine 0.5 percent (0.25 kg at USD 3.00 = USD 0.75). Total USD 68.48 for 50 kg, which is USD 1.37/kg. Protein contribution is roughly 20 x 0.62 plus 10 x 0.42 plus 10 x 0.46, which is 12.4 plus 4.2 plus 4.6, giving 21.2 kg of protein in 50 kg, about 42 percent. That is short of the 50-56 percent target, so she must lift fish meal and BSFL and cut the maize and soy. Redoing it at 50 percent fish meal, 25 percent BSFL and 10 percent fermented soy gives 15.5 plus 5.25 plus 2.3, which is 23.05 kg, about 46 percent, closer but still needing either better fish meal or a small share of a concentrated protein source. That arithmetic is the whole exercise: you cannot reach fry protein levels with cheap ingredients, and pretending otherwise costs you the batch.

Compare that USD 1.37-1.60/kg with an imported commercial microdiet. Fry eat very little in absolute terms, so at fry stage the price per kg matters far less than the result. Spend on the fry diet and save on the grow-out diet, never the other way round.

One last constraint: mycotoxins. Tilapia are the most aflatoxin-sensitive of the species here and their feed should stay under 5 ppb AFB1 against under 10 ppb for catfish. Maize and groundnut meal are the usual carriers in humid African conditions. In a fry diet, where the fish is small and the exposure per gram of body weight high, use only clean, dry, well-stored grain, and limit maize.

Lesson 2.5~11 min

Growth Rate and Survival Optimization

In this lesson
  • Measure fry growth and survival with a repeatable sampling method
  • Identify the three feeding faults that most often cause fry losses
  • Convert a survival improvement into USD for your own hatchery

At fry stage you are not really selling growth, you are selling survivors. A batch that grows fast and loses half its numbers is worth less than a batch that grows moderately and keeps 85 percent. So measure both, and measure them the same way every time.

A sampling method you can repeat. Take three scoops with the same container from three different parts of the tank or hapa, count every fry in each scoop, and average. Weigh 30 fry together on a kitchen scale and divide by 30 for average weight. Do this on a fixed day of the week, always at the same time relative to feeding, and write it in a book with the date, water temperature and the previous day's feed weight. Two months of that book is worth more than any advice you will be given.

Three feeding faults account for most fry losses. The first is particle size wrong for mouth width: anything the fry cannot take is uneaten feed rotting in the tank. The second is feeding too much at once: fry have tiny stomachs and continuous demand, so a day's ration in two meals means half is wasted and the water fouls, while the same ration in six or eight small meals is largely eaten. The third is a diet that is nominally high in protein but short of lysine or methionine, so growth stops at the missing amino acid while you pay for protein you cannot use.

Water and feed interact hard at this stage. Tilapia fry want dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L and become critical at 1.5 mg/L, with poor growth and high mortality below 2 mg/L. Channel catfish fry want above 6 mg/L, critical at 2.5 mg/L, with only about 50 percent survival at 2 mg/L. Ammonia above 0.05 mg/L already irritates gills and costs 10-15 percent of growth, and fry sit in a small water volume where ammonia climbs quickly. Feeding to satiation raises oxygen consumption 30-40 percent, and a fry diet above 40 percent protein raises post-feeding oxygen demand 50-100 percent, so the very feed that grows them fastest is the feed that stresses the water hardest. That is the trade you are managing.

Worked example. Moses runs tilapia hapas at Fort Portal, Uganda, stocking 25,000 swim-up fry per cycle and running four cycles a year. His current day-30 survival is 55 percent, giving 13,750 fingerlings per cycle and 55,000 a year. He changes three things: he splits the daily ration from three meals into seven, he moves to a stabilised vitamin C microdiet, and he siphons tank bottoms every morning. Survival rises to 70 percent. That is 17,500 per cycle, 70,000 a year, an extra 15,000 fingerlings. At USD 0.06 each that is USD 900 a year. His added cost is roughly USD 0.05/kg for stabilised vitamin C on perhaps 400 kg of fry feed a year, USD 20, plus his own labour. Almost everything he changed was a habit, not a purchase.

Grading and uniformity are dealt with at fingerling stage in the next module, but the seed of a ragged batch is sown here. Feed frequently enough that the small fish get a turn, spread the feed over the whole water surface rather than one corner, and remember that in tilapia and African catfish a large fry will readily eat a smaller one. Uniform feeding is cannibalism control.

Finally, keep the mortality record honest. Survival below 90 percent artificially worsens apparent FCR, because you are counting feed given to fish that died. If your fry FCR looks bad, check your counts before you change your feed.

Water Quality Management During Larval Rearing

In this lesson
  • Link the daily fry ration to the ammonia load in a small rearing volume
  • Set feed reduction rules specific to larval tanks and hapas
  • Judge when to reduce feed rather than exchange water

A larval tank is a small volume holding a large number of very sensitive animals eating the highest-protein feed on the farm. That combination makes water quality a feeding problem before it is an engineering problem. Module 9 covers filtration, aeration equipment and monitoring routine; what follows is only about how the ration interacts with the water in a nursery.

The load arithmetic is unforgiving. Around 66-72 percent of fed protein leaves the fish as nitrogen. In a grow-out pond that nitrogen disperses into a large volume with algae to absorb it. In a 500 litre fry tank there is nowhere for it to go. Tilapia and African catfish need unionised ammonia below 0.05 mg/L; at 0.05 mg/L fry already lose 10-15 percent of growth and at 0.1 mg/L the loss is 25-30 percent with reduced feed intake. Nitrite should stay below 0.5 mg/L for tilapia and carp and below 1 mg/L for channel catfish, and salt at 1-2 g/L roughly halves nitrite toxicity by competing at the gill, which in a fry tank is cheap insurance well worth taking.

Oxygen is the other half. Tilapia fry want above 6 mg/L against a critical 1.5 mg/L; channel catfish fry want above 6 mg/L with only about 50 percent survival at 2 mg/L. African catfish fry are the most forgiving because they can breathe air, but even they grow poorly below 4 mg/L. Feeding to satiation raises oxygen use 30-40 percent, and fry feeds above 40 percent protein raise the post-feeding demand a further 50-100 percent. So the peak oxygen demand in a nursery comes shortly after the biggest meal, and if you feed heavily late in the day that peak lands as the pond or tank is already drawing down towards its dawn minimum.

Uneaten feed is the largest single source of ammonia in a nursery, and fry feed is fine, expensive and easy to overfeed. Fine particles also leach: a diet with only 2-4 hours of stability loses 20-30 percent of its protein and energy to the water within two hours, and that leached protein becomes ammonia without ever entering a fish. Two conclusions follow. Feed small amounts often, so almost nothing is left. And buy or make a well-bound diet, because binding is a water quality measure as much as a nutrition one.

The rules to apply in a nursery, written down and followed:

  1. Siphon the tank bottom every morning before the first feed, and judge the ration by how much waste came out.
  2. If ammonia is above 0.05 mg/L or dissolved oxygen is below 5 mg/L, cut the ration 20-30 percent that day.
  3. If water temperature drops below 20C, halve the ration; fry appetite falls sharply and uneaten feed is pure pollution.
  4. If fry show any disease signs, cut the ration 30-50 percent while you diagnose.
  5. Feed the largest share in the middle of the day, not at dawn or dusk.

When do you cut feed rather than exchange water? Exchange first if you have clean water available at the right temperature; a 20-30 percent exchange is fast and does not cost growth. But a large sudden exchange with colder or chemically different water shocks fry, and many small farms simply do not have spare water in the dry season. Cutting the ration always works, costs a little growth, and never kills anything.

Worked example. Selam runs tilapia nursery tanks near Ziway, Ethiopia: eight tanks of 500 litres, 3,000 fry each, on a 52 percent protein microdiet. She feeds about 90 g per tank per day, and 24,000 fry across the system eat 720 g a day at USD 1.60/kg, USD 1.15. One tank reads ammonia at 0.08 mg/L on a Tuesday. She has two options. Exchange 30 percent of the tank, 150 litres, which she can do in ten minutes from her header tank at no cash cost. Or cut the ration 25 percent, from 90 g to 67.5 g, saving USD 0.04 that day but slowing growth slightly. The right answer is to do both: exchange the water and hold the ration down for two days until the reading is back under 0.05 mg/L. The cash at stake is pennies. What is at stake is 3,000 fry worth USD 180 at USD 0.06 each.

Do this today: siphon the bottom of one fry tank into a clear bucket before the morning feed and look at how much uneaten feed comes out. If you can see pellets, cut today's ration by a quarter.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Crude protein for a Nile tilapia first-feeding diet should be:

Tilapia fry need 50-56 percent crude protein, the highest requirement of any life stage.

2. The protein-to-energy ratio for fry diets is:

Fry need 6-7 kcal DE per gram of protein, a richer ratio than the 8-10 used for grow-out.

3. Maximum particle size for fry in their first two weeks is about:

Mouth width is under 1 mm, so feed must be 0.8-1.5 mm, usually a microdiet powder.

4. Good broodstock lipid and EPA+DHA improve yolk-sac to free-feeding survival by roughly:

Survival improves 15-25 percent with maternal feed above 15 percent lipid and 1.5 percent EPA plus DHA.

5. Losing 4,000 fingerlings worth USD 0.06 each costs:

4,000 x USD 0.06 = USD 240, against a first-feed cost of about USD 1 for that week.

6. Why do very small larvae often need live food?

A moving prey item triggers the strike reflex and is easier for an immature gut to digest.

7. The safe nitrogen to phosphorus ratio when fertilising a green-water pond is:

Around 3:1 to 5:1. Excess nitrogen relative to phosphorus favours cyanobacteria, which produce microcystin and anatoxin.

8. Pond-cultured zooplankton is usually deficient in:

Zooplankton is protein-rich but usually short of EPA and DHA, which fry need at 0.8-1.5 percent of diet.

9. Which cyanotoxin causes liver necrosis and accumulates in fish organs?

Microcystin from Microcystis blooms causes liver necrosis and bioaccumulates, and is prevented by avoiding over-fertilisation.

10. Gaining 7,500 extra fingerlings worth USD 0.06 each is worth:

7,500 x USD 0.06 = USD 450, which pays for a simple green-water system in one cycle.

11. The safest way to move fry from live food to dry feed is:

Co-feeding with a gradual increase in the dry share avoids the stall, size spread and mortality that an abrupt switch causes.

12. Fry aged two to eight weeks should receive particles of about:

Mouth width is 1-2 mm at this age, so 1.5-3 mm crumble is correct.

13. Protein for a tilapia weaning diet should be:

Tilapia move from 50-56 percent as fry down to 40-45 percent as fingerlings; a weaning diet stays in that band.

14. Why is water stability especially critical in weaning diets?

At fry particle size, leaching is fast; a poorly bound diet loses 20-30 percent of nutrients within two hours.

15. Losing 5 percent survival on 30,000 fry worth USD 0.06 each costs:

1,500 fry x USD 0.06 = USD 90, far more than the small daily saving from weaning early.

16. Which locally producible protein comes closest to fish meal for a fry diet?

BSFL meal is 40-45 percent protein at 85-90 percent digestibility with no phytate, and needs only methionine supplementation.

17. Maximum aflatoxin B1 in a tilapia feed should be:

Tilapia are the most aflatoxin-sensitive species covered here, so feeds should stay under 5 ppb AFB1.

18. Fermenting soybean meal for 48-72 hours reduces phytate by roughly:

Lactobacillus fermentation cuts phytate 30-50 percent and lifts methionine bioavailability 10-15 percent for USD 0.05-0.08/kg.

19. A mix of 20 kg fish meal at 62 percent protein, 10 kg BSFL at 42 percent and 10 kg soy at 46 percent, in 50 kg total, gives about:

12.4 + 4.2 + 4.6 = 21.2 kg of protein in 50 kg, which is about 42 percent, short of the fry target.

20. Digestible energy in a fry diet should be about:

Fry diets run 3,300-3,500 kcal DE/kg to support a 6-7 kcal per gram protein-to-energy ratio.

21. Dissolved oxygen for tilapia fry should be kept above:

Fry want above 6 mg/L; the critical level is 1.5 mg/L and growth is already poor below 2 mg/L.

22. Splitting the daily fry ration into more meals mainly helps because:

Small stomachs mean a large single meal is largely wasted and fouls the water; six to eight small meals are eaten.

23. Survival below 90 percent affects your FCR figure by:

Feed eaten by fish that later died still counts against surviving weight, so apparent FCR worsens artificially.

24. Raising day-30 survival from 55 to 70 percent on 25,000 fry gains you:

70 percent of 25,000 is 17,500 against 13,750 at 55 percent, a gain of 3,750 per cycle.

25. Which is the most common feeding fault causing fry loss?

A particle a fry cannot physically take is not feed but pollution, and it is the commonest single error.

26. In a small fry tank, the largest single source of ammonia is usually:

Fine fry feed is easy to overfeed and leaches fast; that protein becomes ammonia without ever entering a fish.

27. When ammonia in a fry tank reaches 0.08 mg/L, the correct first response is:

Exchange if clean water is available and hold the ration down until the reading is back under 0.05 mg/L.

28. Adding salt at 1-2 g/L to a fry tank protects mainly against:

Chloride competes with nitrite at the gill and roughly halves its toxicity.

29. A fry feed above 40 percent protein raises post-feeding oxygen demand by roughly:

High-protein feeds raise post-feeding oxygen demand 50-100 percent, on top of the 30-40 percent rise from satiation feeding.

30. If water temperature in a nursery falls below 20C you should:

Appetite falls sharply below 20C, so the ration is halved; uneaten feed at low temperature is pure pollution.

Module 2 capstone

Run one complete fry batch from swim-up to 5 g and cost it honestly. Record the source and price of your eggs or swim-up fry, the day yolk sac absorption finished, what you fed at first feeding and at what particle size, and the day you began weaning to dry feed. Count survivors at day 7, day 14 and day 30 by taking three scoop samples of at least 50 fry each and averaging, and weigh 30 fry at each count to get average weight. Measure water temperature twice daily, dissolved oxygen at dawn at least three times a week, and ammonia twice a week, writing feed weight beside every reading. At the end produce one page giving: survival percentage at day 30, average weight, feed used in kg, feed cost in USD, cost per surviving fingerling, and the price a local hatchery charges for the same fingerling. State whether producing your own fry beats buying them, and name the two changes that would most improve your survival next batch.
