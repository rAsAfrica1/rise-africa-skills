Module 6

Comb Guides and Fixing Interior Structure

A masonry hive does not have frames. The bees build comb from top bars or from comb guides built into the walls. This module is how to give the bees the guidance they need without creating a structure that blocks harvest.

Module 6 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Choose between top bars, comb guides and fixed rails
  • Design top bars that fit the masonry opening correctly
  • Cut comb guide grooves into the internal face if used
  • Provide for the harvest — how comb is removed and the structure is cleaned
  • Avoid the four mistakes that produce crooked or unusable comb
  • Complete the comb guidance system for your brick hive

Lesson 6.1

Three comb guidance systems

SystemDescriptionBest for
Top barsRemovable bars spanning the cavity at 32–35 mm centresMost masonry hives; the standard approach
Comb guidesGrooves or ridges cast or cut into the underside of the roof or a fixed ceilingHives where top bars are not practical
Fixed railsThin wooden rails mortared into the walls to hold comb in fixed positionRare; not recommended

For a small African masonry hive, top bars are the correct choice. They let you inspect the colony, harvest comb cleanly, and manage swarm control. Fixed comb guides work only when harvest is by cutting entire comb blocks.

Lesson 6.2

Top bars for a masonry hive

Dimensions

DimensionValue
LengthCavity width + 80 mm (40 mm overhang each side)
Width32 mm
Thickness20 mm
Spacing35 mm centre-to-centre
Comb guideSaw kerf, 3 mm wide × 3 mm deep, along bottom centre

For a cavity 450 mm wide, bars should be 530 mm long, so that 40 mm overhangs the wall on each side.

Notching the wall top

The top course of the wall must have a notch where each bar sits. The notch dimensions:

  • Width: 32 mm (matches bar)
  • Depth: 15 mm
  • Spacing: 35 mm centre-to-centre

Cut the notches into the top brick using an angle grinder with a masonry blade. The first notch is 30 mm in from the end. Every subsequent notch is 35 mm further.

The bar rest

Bars sit in the notches and are supported along their full length. The underside of the bar must be at least 15 mm above the highest point of the wall, so bees can pass under the ends.

Lesson 6.3

Comb guides if used

If you choose fixed comb guides instead of top bars, they are cast or cut into the underside of the roof.

Design

  • Ridge guides: narrow wooden or metal ridges fixed to the roof underside at 100 mm spacing. Bees build comb down between the ridges.
  • Groove guides: grooves 5 mm wide × 5 mm deep cut into the roof underside at 100 mm spacing.
  • Starter strip guides: strips of wax or wood 20 mm wide fixed at 100 mm spacing.

Spacing

Fixed comb guides must be spaced wider (100 mm) than top bars (35 mm) because comb built from fixed guides cannot be removed individually. You are building a single fixed comb mass per gap. This makes harvest harder and inspection impossible.

Fixed comb guides are a poor choice for a working hive. They work for wild colonies in wall cavities where the beekeeper rarely opens the hive. If you want to inspect and manage the colony, use top bars.

Lesson 6.4

Harvest access and structure

The comb guidance system must support harvest. In a masonry hive with top bars, harvest works as follows:

The harvest sequence

  1. Open the roof. Set aside with two people.
  2. Identify a comb that is ready for harvest (capped honey, no brood).
  3. Lift the top bar gently. If the comb is attached to the wall, cut it free with a long knife.
  4. Slide the bar out with comb attached.
  5. Cut the comb off the bar, leaving a 20 mm strip attached.
  6. Return the bar to the hive. The bees will draw new comb from the strip.

Preventing comb attachment to walls

  • Sloped internal walls (the KTBH slope) reduce attachment.
  • Smooth rendered interior makes attachment harder.
  • Regular inspection — bees attach comb gradually. Opening the hive every 2–3 weeks breaks the attachment early.
  • Slightly warm interior — bees attach comb more in cool conditions when the wax is stiff.

Managing cross-comb

Bees sometimes build comb perpendicular to the bars, joining two bars. This is cross-comb. Prevention:

  • Correct bar spacing (35 mm).
  • Comb guide on every bar (saw kerf).
  • Inspect within the first 2 weeks to break any cross-comb while it is small.
  • Do not leave too many empty bars — put a follower board in place so the bees do not have empty space to fill chaotically.

Lesson 6.5

The four mistakes

1. Wrong bar spacing

Bars spaced wider than 40 mm allow bees to build burr comb between them. Too narrow (under 30 mm) and they propolise. The correct range is 32–35 mm.

2. No comb guide

Bars without a guide (kerf, wax strip, chamfer) leave bees to choose. In practice, they build cross-comb 20–40% of the time. Always provide a guide.

3. Rough internal walls

Unrendered brick or block gives bees a rough surface to attach comb to. Render smooth with cement and a food-safe paint. This one change reduces wall attachment by 60–80%.

4. Too many bars

Bars should fill the hive with a small bar of gap at the follower end. Too many empty bars and bees fill them chaotically. Too few and the colony outgrows the hive and swarms. Provide 24–28 bars for a 900 mm hive, and use a follower board to restrict access to the rest.

The comb guide rule: 32–35 mm bars, saw kerf on every one, smooth rendered interior, follower board in place. Four details and the masonry hive works. Miss one and comb is crooked or unusable.

Knowledge check — Module 6

1. What is the correct bar spacing for a masonry hive?

Correct: 32–35 mm. Bee space for top bars. Wrong spacing breaks management.

2. What is a comb guide?

Correct: starting line for comb. Simple but essential. Without it, cross-comb is common.

3. Why render the internal walls smooth?

Correct: less comb attachment. Smooth walls mean comb lifts out cleanly at harvest.

4. What is cross-comb and how is it prevented?

Correct: perpendicular to bars. Broken up early while small. Once established, hard to fix.

5. Why use a follower board in a masonry hive?

Correct: confine the colony. Empty bars without a follower lead to cross-comb and chaotic building.

Practical assignment — Module 6

Complete the comb guidance system for your masonry hive.

  1. Bars cut and assembled — photographs with a tape measure.
  2. Comb guides cut into every bar — photographs showing the kerf.
  3. The bar notches cut into the wall — photographs showing spacing and depth.
  4. Bars laid in the notches — photographs showing correct spacing and fit.
  5. The follower board in position, 4 bars in from the entrance.
  6. Interior rendering complete, with before and after photographs.
  7. Your harvest procedure written out for the staff.
  8. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest comb-management risk in your hive and how you will reduce it.

Marks are weighted toward points 2 and 8. Comb guides are the single most useful detail; the paragraph is where you commit to a decision.
