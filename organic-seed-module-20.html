Record Keeping for Certification - What Inspectors Actually Look For

~13 min read25 min gameIntermediate

🧠 Brain Opener

An inspector from a certification body walks onto your farm with one question: can you prove everything you claim? Your records are the answer.

  • What are the three types of records every certified farm needs?
  • How long do you keep them?
  • What happens if records are missing during an inspection?
🎁 Reveal a strong answer

The three types are input records (what you used and when), process records (what you did and when), and output records (what you sold and to whom). Keep them for at least 3 years. Missing records during an inspection can lead to denial or suspension of certification.

📖 Farai Prepares for the Inspection

Farai had been certified for two years. He had a comfortable routine. Records were okay - not perfect, but enough.

Then the certification body changed. His old certifier merged with a larger European body. The new body had stricter standards.

The inspection was scheduled for a Tuesday morning. Farai spent the weekend before in a panic.

He had input records - sort of. Some were in a notebook, some were on old phone messages, some were in his head.

He had process records - but only for the last four months. The earlier ones had been eaten by a mouse, or lost, or never written.

He had output records - but the buyer names were incomplete. Some were just first names. Some were phone numbers with no name.

The inspector arrived. She was polite, patient, and thorough. She asked four questions.

"Show me your compost records from two years ago."

Farai could not.

"Show me your roguing records from the last growing season."

Farai had some.

"Show me the buyer list and traceability for the last twelve months."

Farai had a partial list.

"Show me your seed quality test results for every batch you sold."

Farai had some, not all.

The inspector wrote her notes. At the end of the visit, she was honest.

"You are farming well. The quality of your seed is good. But your records are not complete enough for certification renewal. I am going to recommend a conditional renewal - you have 90 days to submit the missing records."

Farai asked what would happen if he could not find them.

"Then we suspend the certification. You cannot sell certified organic for one year."

Farai spent the next 90 days reconstructing what he could and admitting what he could not. He submitted what he had. He received a warning.

The next season, he built a proper record system. Notebook, dividers, dated entries, kept in a locked cabinet. He now keeps records for 5 years instead of 3.

He has not had a single warning since.

💡 The Big Idea

Records are your certification proof

A certification body is not there to judge your farming. They are there to verify your claims. If you say the seed is organic, they need to see the records that prove it. Missing records do not mean you farmed wrong. They just mean you cannot prove you farmed right. And that is enough to lose certification.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

The three record types

Input records. Every input that comes onto your farm: seed (variety, supplier, batch), compost (materials, date made, quantity), manure (source, date), botanical sprays (ingredients, date made, applied where), water source. Every entry needs a date.

Process records. Every action you take on the farm: planting date, weeding dates, roguing dates and counts, pest observations, harvest date and quantity, drying days, threshing, sieving, testing results. Every entry needs a date and the plot code.

Output records. Every sale: buyer name, contact, batch number, weight, price, date, payment received. Every entry needs the batch number.

2

How to keep records that survive inspection

Write daily, not weekly. Memory fades in three days. Write the same day.

One notebook, not three. Use a single bound notebook. Loose sheets get lost. Multiple notebooks get mixed.

Number the pages. Inspectors want to see a complete book. Missing pages look suspicious.

Date every entry. No undated entries. Ever.

Keep it dry and safe. A locked cabinet. Away from mice, rain, and children.

Keep for 5 years. Not 3. Five is safer. Inspectors may ask for a longer history than the legal minimum.

3

What to do during an inspection

Prepare in advance. Two weeks before, review all your records. Fill gaps. Sort by date. Check the buyer list is complete.

Be honest. If a record is missing, say so. Do not invent entries. Inspectors spot inconsistencies.

Show the actual books. Do not type up a summary. The inspector wants the raw log with dates and details.

Have your certificate and any correspondence ready. Previous inspection reports, certificates, buyer complaints, corrective actions - all in one folder.

Walk them around. Show them the plot, the compost pile, the storage. Records match reality. That is the goal.

🛠 Hands-On - Reconstruct Six Months of Records

You need

  • Your existing notes, phone messages, memory
  • A new bound notebook
  • A pen
  1. Take a new bound notebook.
  2. Number the pages from 1 to 200.
  3. Write "Input records" on the first 60 pages.
  4. Write "Process records" on pages 61 to 140.
  5. Write "Output records" on pages 141 to 200.
  6. Starting today, write every entry in the right section.
  7. At the end of the week, review. What did you forget to write?
  8. Adjust the system until it captures everything.
Why it works: A single bound notebook is the simplest and most reliable record system. Six months of consistent recording makes every future inspection easier.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Uganda coffee inspection

A Ugandan coffee cooperative failed its organic inspection in 2019 because the buyer list was incomplete. They lost certification for one year and about 80,000 dollars in premium sales. They now keep five years of records in a fireproof cabinet.

Ghana cocoa certification

Ghanaian cocoa farmers certified under Rainforest Alliance keep daily records of every farming activity. Inspections pass above 95 percent. The key is a simple bound notebook used every single day.

🏆 Project Challenge

Set Up a Five-Year Record System

Build a record system that will pass inspection next year and the year after.

  1. Buy a bound notebook with numbered pages.
  2. Divide into three sections: input, process, output.
  3. Print or draw a header at the top of each page: date, plot, activity, quantity, notes.
  4. Write one entry per activity, every day.
  5. Store the notebook in a locked, dry cabinet.
  6. Photograph every page at the end of each month.
  7. Keep both the paper and the digital photo for 5 years.
