Lessons

1. DC Motors and Motor Drivers

A DC motor is the workhorse of robotics. In Africa, you'll find them in cheap toys, car wipers, and even washing machines. A typical small DC motor runs on 312V and draws 0.52A. For example, the yellow TT gear motor (used in many Arduino kits) runs at 6V, 200mA no-load, and has a stall current of 1.2A. You can salvage motors from old printers, DVD players, or remote-controlled cars at places like Kenya's Ngara market or Nigeria's Alaba International. To control a DC motor with a microcontroller, you need a motor driver because the Arduino can only supply 20mA per pin. Common drivers: L293D (1A per channel), L298N (2A per channel), and TB6612FNG (1.2A continuous). The L298N is popular in Africa because it's cheap (around $2) and available in most electronics shops. Wiring: connect the motor to OUT1 and OUT2, battery to 12V and GND, and control pins to Arduino. For a single motor, you need two control pins (IN1, IN2) to set direction, and an enable pin (ENA) for speed via PWM. If you want to drive two motors, you need two sets of control pins. Always use a separate battery for motorsnever power them from the Arduino's 5V pin. A 6V AA battery pack (4 AA batteries) works for small motors. Add a 100F capacitor across the motor terminals to reduce noise. Test with a simple sketch: set IN1 HIGH, IN2 LOW, and analogWrite ENA to 200 (out of 255) for half speed. If the motor doesn't spin, check your battery voltage and wiring. In many African workshops, learners use recycled 18650 lithium cells from laptop batteries to power motorsjust be careful with charging and use a protection circuit.

2. Stepper Motors and Servo Motors

Stepper motors move in precise steps, making them ideal for 3D printers, CNC machines, and robot arms. A common stepper is the NEMA 17, which has 200 steps per revolution (1.8 per step). In Africa, you can salvage steppers from old printers and scannersthey often have 1.8 or 7.5 step angles. To drive a stepper, you need a driver like the A4988 or DRV8825 (around $12 each). These take step and direction signals from an Arduino. For example, to rotate a NEMA 17 one full revolution, you send 200 pulses to the STEP pin. Microstepping (e.g., 1/16) gives smoother motion but reduces torque. Power them with 12V at 11.5A. Servo motors, on the other hand, are for angular positioning. The SG90 micro servo (plastic gears, 1.8 kgcm torque) costs about $2 and runs on 4.86V. The MG996R (metal gears, 11 kgcm) is stronger and costs $58. Servos have three wires: brown/black (GND), red (5V), and orange/yellow (signal). They expect a PWM pulse every 20ms, with pulse width from 1ms (0) to 2ms (180). In Arduino, use the Servo library: `myservo.write(90)` sets the shaft to 90. For African makers, servos are great for robotic grippers and pan-tilt cameras. But beware: cheap servos can jitter if your power supply is weak. Use a separate 5V 2A regulator for multiple servos. Also, steppers are better for continuous rotation and holding torque. A typical project: a solar panel cleaner that uses a stepper to rotate a brush and a servo to adjust the angle. Salvage steppers from printers at local e-waste dumpsthey often work fine.

3. Pneumatic and Hydraulic Actuators

Pneumatic and hydraulic actuators use air or fluid pressure to create linear or rotary motion. In Africa, pneumatics are more common in small workshops because air is free and clean. A basic pneumatic system needs a compressor, air filter, regulator, solenoid valves, and a cylinder. For example, a double-acting cylinder (bore 32mm, stroke 100mm) at 6 bar (87 psi) can push about 480N (48 kg) of force. That's enough to lift a small robot arm or clamp a workpiece. You can buy pneumatic kits from online stores like AliExpress or local industrial suppliers in South Africa (e.g., RS Components) or Kenya (e.g., Birmingham). But for low-cost robotics, consider using a bicycle pump and a 2/2 solenoid valve (12V DC, $5) to control a single-acting cylinder. Hydraulics use incompressible fluid (oil) and can generate much higher forcesup to 200 bar in car jacks. However, hydraulic systems are messy, expensive, and require safety precautions. For African robotics, hydraulics are rare except in large-scale mining or agricultural equipment. Instead, focus on pneumatics for pick-and-place robots. A simple project: a pneumatic gripper that opens and closes using a 5/2 solenoid valve controlled by an Arduino through a relay. Use 6mm PU tubing and push-to-connect fittings. Always install a water trap and lubricator to protect valves. In dusty environments, use filtered air. Safety: never exceed the cylinder's rated pressure, and always secure tubing with clamps. Pneumatics can be louduse a silencer on exhaust ports. For a low-budget start, salvage a car tyre inflator as a compressor (but it's not continuous duty). Better: use a fridge compressor (quiet, but needs oil). Many African makers repurpose refrigerator compressors for vacuum or low-pressure air.

4. Gearing, Belts, and Pulleys

Gears, belts, and pulleys let you trade speed for torque or change direction. In robotics, you often need more torque than a motor provides. A gear ratio is the number of teeth on the driven gear divided by the driver gear. For example, if a 10-tooth gear drives a 50-tooth gear, the ratio is 5:1output speed is 1/5 of input, but torque is 5 times higher (minus friction). In African workshops, you can salvage gears from old printers, photocopiers, and car alternators. Plastic gears are common but wear out; metal gears (brass or steel) last longer. For belts, timing belts (e.g., GT2) are best for precisionthey don't slip. A 20-tooth pulley driving a 60-tooth pulley gives a 3:1 reduction. Use a belt tensioner to avoid skipping. V-belts are for high torque but slip; they're used in car engines but not ideal for robots. Pulleys and belts are great for transferring motion over distancelike a robot arm with a shoulder and elbow. For a differential drive robot, you might use a pulley to connect two wheels on the same axle. In Africa, bicycle chains and sprockets are a cheap source of robust gearing. A bicycle chain with a 14-tooth sprocket driving a 28-tooth sprocket gives 2:1 reduction. You can also use a worm gear for high reduction (20:1 to 100:1) and self-locking, but efficiency is low (3050%). When designing, calculate the required torque: torque = force radius. For a wheel of radius 3cm to climb a 10 slope with a 1kg robot, you need about 0.5 Nm. Then choose a motor and gear ratio accordingly. Remember: more reduction means slower speed but more pushing power. Test with a multimeter and a tachometer (or use a phone app) to measure actual RPM.

5. Motor Control: PWM and PID

PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) is how you control motor speed. The Arduino's analogWrite() outputs a PWM signal at about 490Hz (or 980Hz on pins 5 and 6). The duty cycle (0255) sets the average voltage. For example, analogWrite(motorPin, 127) gives 50% duty cycle, so a 12V motor sees about 6V average. But PWM alone doesn't hold speed under loadthe motor slows down when you add weight. That's where PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative) control comes in. PID reads the actual speed (using an encoder) and adjusts PWM to match a target. A simple PID for a DC motor: error = targetRPM - actualRPM; output = Kp*error + Ki*integral + Kd*derivative. You tune Kp, Ki, Kd experimentally. Start with Kp=1, Ki=0, Kd=0, then increase Kp until oscillation, then add Kd to damp. In Africa, many learners use the Arduino PID library. For a line-following robot, PID keeps the robot centered on the line. For a balancing robot, PID keeps it upright. You need an encodera slotted optical sensor or a magnetic encoder (e.g., AS5600) on the motor shaft. A cheap way: use a recycled mouse encoder (quadrature) and count pulses. For example, a 20-slot encoder disc gives 20 pulses per revolution. With gear ratio 30:1, you get 600 pulses per output revolution. Then calculate RPM: RPM = (pulses per second 60) / pulses per revolution. PID runs in a loop every 1050ms. Use a hardware interrupt for encoder counting to avoid missing pulses. In resource-limited settings, you can skip PID and use a simple proportional controller for many tasks. But for precision like 3D printing, PID is essential. Test with a serial plotter to see the response. Always clamp the output to 0255. A well-tuned PID makes a robot move smoothly and accurately.

6. Lab: Controlling a DC Motor with Arduino

In this lab, you'll control a DC motor's speed and direction using an Arduino, an L298N motor driver, and a 6V battery pack. You need: Arduino Uno, L298N module, DC motor (612V, 1A max), 4 AA batteries (6V), jumper wires, and a small switch. First, wire the L298N: connect battery + to 12V terminal and battery to GND. Connect motor to OUT1 and OUT2. Connect Arduino pin 9 to ENA (enable A), pin 8 to IN1, pin 7 to IN2. Also connect Arduino GND to L298N GND. Now write the code. In setup(), set pins 8, 7, and 9 as OUTPUT. In loop(), first test direction: digitalWrite(IN1, HIGH); digitalWrite(IN2, LOW); then analogWrite(ENA, 200); delay(2000); then stop: analogWrite(ENA, 0); delay(1000); then reverse: digitalWrite(IN1, LOW); digitalWrite(IN2, HIGH); analogWrite(ENA, 200); delay(2000); stop. Upload and observe. If the motor doesn't spin, check battery voltage (should be >5V) and wiring. If it spins but not smoothly, adjust the PWM valuetry 100, 150, 255. For a speed sweep, use a for loop: for (int speed=0; speed<=255; speed+=5) { analogWrite(ENA, speed); delay(50); } then reverse. To control with a potentiometer, connect its middle pin to A0, and map the value (01023) to PWM (0255). This gives you a manual speed knob. For African makers, this lab uses cheap, available parts. You can salvage the motor from a toy car and the L298N from an old printer. Always use a separate battery for motorsnever the Arduino's 5V. Add a 100nF capacitor across the motor terminals to reduce noise. If you hear a high-pitched whine, that's normal PWM. For a challenge, add a second motor and make a small robot car. Remember to common the grounds. This lab is the foundation for all motorized robots.

<h2>Practical Reference: Actuators and Drive Systems for African Agribusiness Robotics</h2>

<p>In African agribusiness, robotics is not a laboratory curiosityit is a practical response to labour shortages, rising input costs, and the need for precision in planting, spraying, harvesting, and post-harvest handling. Whether you are building a solar-powered weeding robot for a smallholder cooperative in Ghana, an automated irrigation boom for a commercial farm in Kenya, or a sorting arm for a maize mill in Zambia, the actuator and drive system you choose determines whether your machine works reliably in dusty, hot, and humid conditionsand whether it pays for itself within a season.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the key engineering decisions across DC motors, stepper and servo motors, pneumatic and hydraulic actuators, mechanical power transmission, and closed-loop control. It is written for the African context: intermittent power, high ambient temperatures, dust, vibration, and the need for locally available spare parts. Every recommendation is framed around business outcomesthroughput, downtime, maintenance cost, and return on investment.</p>

<h3>1. DC Motors and Motor Drivers</h3>
<p>DC brushed motors remain the workhorse of African agribusiness robotics because they are cheap, widely available in informal markets, and easy to drive with simple H-bridge modules. For solar-powered systems, choose motors rated at 12V or 24V to match battery banks. A 24V system halves current for the same power, reducing cable thickness and voltage dropcritical when your robot operates 50 metres from the power source.</p>

<p>Motor drivers must be sized for stall current, not running current. A motor that draws 2A running may pull 10A when stalled against a rock or dense weed. Use MOSFET-based drivers (e.g., BTS7960, VNH2SP30) for currents above 5A. In dusty environments, enclose drivers in IP54-rated boxes with conformal coating on PCBs to prevent corrosion from humidity and fertiliser dust.</p>

<h3>2. Stepper Motors and Servo Motors</h3>
<p>Stepper motors excel where precise positioning matters without feedbackseed metering, chemical dosing, and camera pan-tilt systems. They are open-loop, so they can lose steps under sudden load, which is common when a planter hits a clod of dry soil. Use steppers with a torque margin of at least 2 the calculated load, and always pair them with a current-limiting driver (e.g., A4988, TMC2209) to prevent overheating in 35C+ ambient temperatures.</p>

<p>Servo motors (both hobby and industrial) are closed-loop by design and are ideal for steering, arm joints, and valve control. For agricultural robots, choose IP-rated servos or retrofit seals. In West Africa, where Harmattan dust penetrates everything, a standard hobby servo will fail within weeks unless protected. Industrial servos with IP65 rating cost more but reduce downtimea critical factor when harvest windows are short.</p>

<h3>3. Pneumatic and Hydraulic Actuators</h3>
<p>Pneumatics are fast, clean, and forgiving of overloadperfect for pick-and-place fruit sorting or actuating seed singulation gates. However, they require a compressor, which adds cost and maintenance. In off-grid areas, a small 12V compressor charged by solar can work for low-duty-cycle applications.</p>

<p>Hydraulics deliver high force in a compact packageessential for lifting heavy loads like cassava roots or bales of hay. But hydraulic systems demand clean oil, leak-free seals, and skilled maintenance. In African agribusiness, hydraulic robots are best suited to large commercial operations with existing tractor hydraulics. For smallholders, electric actuators are usually more practical.</p>

<h3>4. Gearing, Belts, and Pulleys</h3>
<p>Motors are fast and weak; gears make them slow and strong. A 10:1 gearbox multiplies torque by ten and divides speed by ten. For African agribusiness robots, use metal gears (not plastic) in high-load applications. Planetary gearboxes offer high torque density but are expensive; worm gearboxes are cheap and self-locking but less efficient.</p>

<p>Belts and pulleys are ideal for transmitting power over distancee.g., driving a conveyor from a motor mounted away from dust and heat. Use timing belts (not V-belts) for precise speed control. In humid environments, choose neoprene or polyurethane belts over natural rubber to resist rot.</p>

<h3>5. Motor Control: PWM and PID</h3>
<p>Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) is the standard method for controlling motor speed. By switching the motor on and off rapidly (typically 120 kHz), you control average voltage without resistive losses. For agricultural robots, use PWM frequencies above 15 kHz to avoid audible whining that stresses livestock.</p>

<p>PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative) control closes the loop. The proportional term corrects current error; the integral term eliminates steady-state error; the derivative term dampens oscillation. In practice, tune PID empirically: start with P only, add I to eliminate drift, add D to reduce overshoot. For a wheeled robot on uneven soil, a well-tuned PID keeps speed constant regardless of loadcritical for uniform seed spacing.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c62828; background:#ffebee; padding:16px; border-radius:8px; margin:20px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c62828;"> SAFETY WARNING: Electrical and Mechanical Hazards</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#b71c1c;">DC motors and drivers can deliver high currents that cause burns, fires, or battery explosions. Always fuse the positive battery line within 20 cm of the battery terminal. Never disconnect a motor while the driver is poweredinductive kickback can destroy the driver and injure you. Hydraulic systems operate at pressures exceeding 200 bar; a pinhole leak can inject oil into your skin, causing serious injury. Depressurise before maintenance. Pneumatic actuators can move unexpectedlyalways lock out air supply before working on the mechanism. In African field conditions, ensure all enclosures are sealed against dust and water; a short circuit in a dusty environment can ignite crop residue.</p>

<h3>6. Lab: Controlling a DC Motor with Arduino</h3>
<p>This lab uses an Arduino Uno, an L298N driver, a 12V DC motor, and a potentiometer. Wire the motor to the driver, the driver to the Arduino, and the potentiometer to analog input A0. The Arduino reads the potentiometer and outputs a PWM signal to the driver's enable pin. This is the foundation for speed control in seeders, sprayers, and conveyors.</p>

<p>For African agribusiness, extend the lab by adding an encoder and implementing PID. Use a slotted optical sensor or a hall-effect encoder salvaged from a printer. The Arduino PID Library (Brett Beauregard) is free and well-documented. Test on a bench, then on a mock conveyor with variable load (e.g., bags of maize) to simulate field conditions.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Size drivers for stall current, not running current.</strong> Add 30% margin for dust and heat.</li>
<li><strong>Use 24V systems where possible.</strong> Lower current means thinner cables and less voltage drop over long runs.</li>
<li><strong>Protect against dust and moisture.</strong> IP54 enclosures, conformal coating, and sealed connectors are non-negotiable.</li>
<li><strong>Choose metal gears for high-torque applications.</strong> Plastic gears fail in heat and under shock loads.</li>
<li><strong>Tune PID empirically.</strong> Start with P, add I, then D. Test under real load, not just on the bench.</li>
<li><strong>Fuse every battery line.</strong> A 20A fuse costs less than a burnt-out robotor a burnt-down barn.</li>
<li><strong>Keep spare drivers and motors on hand.</strong> In rural areas, replacement parts may take weeks to arrive.</li>
<li><strong>Document your wiring and settings.</strong> A simple notebook can save hours of troubleshooting when you scale to multiple units.</li>
</ul>
