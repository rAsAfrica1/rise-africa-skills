5. Grain Spawn Production

Grain spawn is the product you actually sell. This module covers grain selection, preparation, sterilisation, inoculation, incubation, and the moment when grain becomes spawn. By the end, you will know how to produce 50 kg of high-quality spawn per month.

Module 5 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 5.1 — Choosing grain — wheat, sorghum, millet, rye

Any whole grain can be used for spawn, but some work better than others. The grain must be:

  • Whole — cracked grain clumps and sticks together
  • Clean — no dust, no pesticide residue
  • Fresh — old grain has less nutrition and higher contamination
  • Locally available — you cannot sustain production on imported grain
GrainProsConsCost
WheatWidely available, high nutrition, best colonisationCan be expensive, must be whole grainMedium
Sorghum (milo)Cheap, drought-tolerant, works well for oysterSlightly slower colonisationLow
MilletCheapest, small grains = more colonisation pointsRequires careful moisture controlVery low
RyeThe traditional spawn grain, excellent colonisationRare in Africa, importedHigh
Brown riceWorks, widely availableCan be sticky, more expensiveMedium

For most African producers, sorghum or millet is the practical choice. Both are cheap, local, and produce excellent spawn. Wheat is the "professional" grain but costs more. Rye is the best but almost impossible to source in Africa at reasonable prices.

Whatever grain you choose, buy a large bag once (25–50 kg). Store it in a cool, dry place, protected from rodents. Grain lasts 6–12 months if kept dry.

Practical exercise 5.1: Price 3 grains in your area: wheat, sorghum, millet. Buy 5 kg of the cheapest that is whole (not cracked). Test a small batch — you will know within 2 weeks which grain you prefer.

Lesson 5.2 — Hydration, cooking, and preparing grain

The single biggest factor in grain spawn quality is moisture. Too dry and the mycelium grows slowly. Too wet and bacteria flourish, and the grain rots.

The target: 45–55% moisture content in the cooked grain. In practice, this means the grain is soft enough to bite through but not mushy, and no free water remains in the jar after cooking.

Preparation steps:

  1. Rinse and soak. Wash the grain, then soak in clean water for 12–24 hours. Sorghum and millet need 24 hours; wheat needs 12–18.
  2. Drain and rinse. Pour off the soaking water. Rinse once with clean water.
  3. Boil or steam. Boil the grain for 15–25 minutes, OR steam for 30–40 minutes. The goal: hydrate the grain all the way through without bursting it.
  4. Drain thoroughly. Spread the grain on a clean surface to steam-dry for 30–60 minutes. Surface must be dry to the touch.
  5. Check moisture. Bite a grain — should be soft, not mushy, no crunch. Squeeze a handful — no drops of water should emerge.
  6. Fill containers. Fill jars or bags to 50–60% of volume. Mycelium needs air space to breathe.
GrainSoak timeBoil/steam time
Wheat12–18 h15–20 min boil
Sorghum20–24 h20 min boil
Millet20–24 h15 min boil or 30 min steam
Rye12–16 h15–20 min boil
The squeeze test: Before filling jars, squeeze a handful of prepared grain in your fist. No water should drip out. If it does, spread and dry longer. Too-wet grain is the #1 cause of failed spawn batches.
Practical exercise 5.2: Prepare 3 kg of grain using the process above. Do the squeeze test. If it passes, you are ready for module 5.3.

Lesson 5.3 — Filling containers — jars, bags, bottles

You need a container for grain spawn that:

  • Withstands pressure-cooker temperatures (121°C)
  • Allows gas exchange but blocks contaminants
  • Can be sealed for shipping
  • Is affordable and reusable
ContainerCostProsCons
Glass jars with metal lids (500 ml)$0.30–$1.00 eachReusable, cheap, widely availableHeavy, breakable
Glass jars with filter patch lids$1.50–$3.00 eachProfessional, fast colonisationExpensive
Polypropylene bags with filter patch$0.30–$0.50 eachLight, cheap, good gas exchangeSingle use
Bottles (milk, soda)FreeFree, widely availableHard to modify, uneven sterilisation

For startup, glass jars with simple metal lids are the best choice. You drill a small hole in each lid, cover with a piece of filter material (polyfill, micropore tape, or a coffee filter), and secure with glue or a metal washer. This is called a "filter lid" and it allows gas exchange while keeping contaminants out.

How to make filter lids:

  1. Drill a 6–8 mm hole in the centre of each lid.
  2. Cut a small square of filter material (polyfill works well).
  3. Place filter over the hole from the inside.
  4. Secure with silicone glue or a metal washer pushed onto the lid.
  5. Cover the hole with tape during sterilisation. Remove tape after sterilisation to allow gas exchange.
Practical exercise 5.3: Make 20 filter lids this week. If you cannot find polyfill, use micropore tape (medical tape) stuck over the hole. Both work. Fill the jars 60% with prepared grain, leaving 40% air space.

Lesson 5.4 — Sterilisation in the pressure cooker or autoclave

This is the step that ensures no contamination. Undercooked, you lose the whole batch. Overcooked, the grain becomes mush. Get it right.

Standard cycle for 500 ml grain jars:

  1. Place jars in pressure cooker, not touching each other too tightly. Leave space for steam.
  2. Add water to the cooker — 3–4 cm at the bottom. Do not submerge jars.
  3. Lock the lid. Bring to 15 PSI pressure.
  4. When 15 PSI is reached, start timing. Cook for 45–60 minutes for 500 ml jars.
  5. Turn off heat. Let the cooker cool naturally — do not release pressure quickly. Rapid pressure change can crack jars.
  6. Once pressure is gone, open the lid. Remove jars with heat-proof gloves.
  7. Let jars cool to room temperature before inoculating. Too-hot jars kill the culture.
Container sizeTime at 15 PSI
500 ml jars45–60 min
1 L jars60–90 min
2 kg grain bags90–120 min
Larger substrate bags2–3 hours
Never open the pressure cooker while pressurised. Wait for full pressure release. Opening early can cause serious burns from steam, and can crack the jars inside. Cool down is part of the process, not a delay.
Practical exercise 5.4: Sterilise 10 jars in your pressure cooker. Note the total cycle time (heat up, pressurise, cook, cool down). This is your per-batch time. Plan production around it.

Lesson 5.5 — Inoculation — the clean-room moment

Once the grain jars are sterilised and cooled, you introduce mycelium. This is the most critical step in the process.

Step-by-step inoculation:

  1. Prepare the still-air box. Wipe with 70% alcohol. Light the alcohol lamp.
  2. Gather materials: sterilised grain jars, healthy agar plate with pure mycelium, sterile scalpel, alcohol wipe.
  3. Clean the jars. Wipe the outsides with alcohol. Bring them into the still-air box.
  4. Sterilise the scalpel by flaming until red-hot. Let cool inside the box.
  5. Open the agar plate. Cut a 2×2 cm square of mycelium (or 4–5 smaller pieces).
  6. Open one grain jar. Quickly drop the mycelium piece inside. Close immediately.
  7. Shake gently to distribute the mycelium across the grain surface (this speeds colonisation).
  8. Repeat for each jar. You can inoculate 8–10 jars from one agar plate.
  9. Remove tape from filter lids (if you covered with tape during sterilisation). This allows gas exchange.
  10. Label with species, date, and batch number.
  11. Incubate at 24–28°C for 7–14 days.
Work fast and clean. Every second a jar is open is a window for contamination. Open, drop, close — 3 seconds per jar. If you fumble, set that jar aside and continue with the next.
Practical exercise 5.5: Inoculate 10 jars from one agar plate. Do not open any of them for 5 days. On day 5, check colonisation. On day 7, check again. Log the growth pattern of each jar.

Lesson 5.6 — Incubation and colonisation — the waiting game

During incubation, the mycelium spreads through the grain. This is a quiet period — but it is where failures show up.

What to expect:

  • Days 1–3: No visible change. Mycelium is settling in and beginning to spread.
  • Days 4–7: First white patches appear near the inoculation points. Growth accelerates.
  • Days 8–14: Full colonisation. The grain turns completely white with mycelium.
  • Days 14+: If not fully colonised, check for contamination or slow growth.

Daily checks:

  • Look for green, black, pink, or orange patches — contamination. Remove immediately.
  • Look for wet, glossy areas — bacterial contamination. Remove.
  • Look for grey fuzz at the top of the grain — typically mould. Remove.
  • If one jar contaminates, isolate it. Do not let it sit with healthy jars.

Colonised spawn is ready when:

  • The whole jar is covered in white mycelium (no exposed grain)
  • No signs of contamination
  • Smells mushroomy, not sour or alcoholic
  • Mycelium "pulls back" from the glass slightly (sign of vigour)
Use spawn within 1–2 weeks of full colonisation. Spawn keeps for 3–4 weeks in the fridge, but after that it starts to weaken or even fruit inside the jar. Fresh is best.
Practical exercise 5.6: Log every jar's colonisation date. Calculate your average colonisation time. Track contamination rate. Aim for under 10% contamination in the first 3 months, under 5% by month 6.
