1. International Standards for Sanitary Pads

When you make sanitary pads, you need to meet certain international standards so your product is safe and effective. The most common standard is ISO 9001, which is about quality management. It means you have a system to consistently produce good pads. Another key standard is ISO 13485, specifically for medical devices. In many countries, sanitary pads are considered medical devices because they touch the body and manage blood. ISO 13485 requires strict control of materials, production, and traceability. For absorbency, there is ISO 9073-6, which tests how much liquid a pad can hold. Leakage resistance is covered by ISO 9073-8. These tests use a standard solution that mimics menstrual fluid. You don't need a big lab to start. You can use simple tools like a graduated cylinder, a stopwatch, and a kitchen scale. For example, to test absorbency, cut a pad, place it in a funnel, and pour 10 ml of saline solution (0.9% salt water) slowly. Wait 5 minutes, then weigh the wet pad. The difference in weight gives you the absorbency in grams. International standards also require that materials are non-toxic and don't cause irritation. The WHO and UNICEF have guidelines for menstrual products in low-resource settings. They recommend that pads be free from harmful chemicals and have good absorbency (at least 10 ml for regular pads). In Africa, many countries adopt these standards through their national bureaus, like KEBS in Kenya or SON in Nigeria. Meeting these standards can open doors to bigger markets, including NGOs and government tenders. Start by documenting your process, testing each batch, and keeping records. Even small workshops can follow these basic principles. Remember, quality is not just for exportit protects your local customers and builds trust.

2. Testing for Absorbency and Leakage

Absorbency and leakage are the two most important performance tests for sanitary pads. If a pad leaks, it's useless. You can test these at your workshop with simple tools. For absorbency, you need a way to measure how much liquid a pad can hold. Use a syringe or graduated cylinder to measure 10 ml of saline solution (9 grams of salt in 1 litre of water). Place the pad on a flat surface, then slowly pour the solution onto the center. Wait 5 minutes, then place a pre-weighed dry paper towel under the pad. If the paper towel gets wet, the pad leaked. To measure absorbency, weigh the pad before and after. The difference in grams is the amount absorbed. A regular pad should absorb at least 10 ml; a heavy pad should absorb 15-20 ml. For leakage, you can do a simple test: put the pad on a piece of blotting paper, pour 10 ml of saline, and press with a 1 kg weight for 1 minute. Check if liquid seeps to the blotting paper. Another method is to wear the pad with a coloured liquid and see if it leaks through the sides or back. In Africa, many producers use locally available materials like cotton, wood pulp, and SAP (super absorbent polymer). Test each batch of raw materials because absorbency can vary. For example, if your SAP is old or exposed to humidity, it won't absorb as well. Keep a logbook with dates, batch numbers, and test results. If a batch fails, don't sell itrework it or discard it. Also test the backsheet (the waterproof layer). A good backsheet should not let any liquid pass. You can test it by filling a pad with water and pressing. If it leaks, your backsheet is poor quality. Remember, consistent testing builds a reputation for reliability. Many African brands have grown by word-of-mouth because their pads don't leak.

3. Skin Irritation and Biocompatibility Testing

Sanitary pads touch sensitive skin, so they must not cause irritation or allergic reactions. Biocompatibility means the pad is compatible with the human body. The main concerns are the top sheet (the layer that touches the skin) and any chemicals or fragrances used. In international standards, biocompatibility is tested using ISO 10993. This involves tests like skin irritation and sensitization. You can do a simple patch test with volunteers. Cut a small piece of the top sheet, moisten it with saline, and tape it to the inner forearm of 10-20 people. Leave it for 24 hours. Check for redness, itching, or swelling. If more than one person reacts, your material may be irritating. Another test is the HRIPT (Human Repeated Insult Patch Test), but that's expensive. For small producers, a basic patch test is enough. Also, avoid using recycled paper or cloth that may have residues of bleach, dyes, or chemicals. Use virgin wood pulp or cotton that is washed and dried. If you use SAP, make sure it's cosmetic or medical grade. In Africa, many women have sensitive skin due to heat and friction. So your pad should be soft and breathable. The backsheet should be breathable too, to prevent sweat and rash. You can test breathability by placing a piece of the backsheet over a cup of hot water and seeing if steam passes through. If it does, it's breathable. Also, avoid perfumes and lotions unless they are dermatologically tested. Some brands add aloe vera or chamomile, but these can cause allergies in some people. Always label ingredients clearly. If you can't afford lab tests, do your own user trials. Give free samples to 50 women for one month and ask for feedback. Ask specifically about itching, rash, or discomfort. Keep records. If you get complaints, change your materials. Remember, a safe pad is a pad that women can use without worry.

4. Chemical Safety: Phthalates, Dioxins, and Pesticides

Chemicals in sanitary pads can be harmful. Three common concerns are phthalates, dioxins, and pesticides. Phthalates are used to make plastics soft, but they can disrupt hormones. They are often found in fragrances and some plastic backsheets. To avoid them, don't use recycled plastic or fragranced materials. Choose polyethylene or polypropylene backsheets that are food-grade or medical-grade. Dioxins are highly toxic and can be created when chlorine bleaching is used for wood pulp or cotton. Always buy unbleached or chlorine-free bleached pulp. Look for suppliers who say "TCF" (totally chlorine free) or "ECF" (elemental chlorine free). Pesticides can be in non-organic cotton. If you use cotton, source from organic or at least pesticide-free farms. In Africa, cotton is often sprayed with pesticides, so washing and processing is important. You can test for pesticide residues with simple test kits, but they are not cheap. A better approach is to buy from trusted suppliers and ask for certificates of analysis. Also, avoid using recycled paper because it may contain inks and chemicals. For SAP, ensure it's free from heavy metals. You can ask your supplier for a safety data sheet (SDS). In many African countries, there are no strict regulations on chemicals in sanitary pads, but you should still protect your customers. Do a simple water extraction test: soak a pad in distilled water for 24 hours, then send the water to a lab for testing if you can. If not, rely on supplier documentation. Also, never use pesticides or insecticides in your production area. Keep chemicals away from raw materials. Store materials in a dry, clean place. If you use glue, choose a hot-melt adhesive that is food-grade. Remember, chemical safety is about prevention. It's easier to buy safe materials than to fix a contaminated product.

5. Regulatory Bodies and Certifications

To sell sanitary pads legally, you need to meet the regulations of your country. In Africa, each country has a national standards body. For example, Kenya has KEBS (Kenya Bureau of Standards), Nigeria has SON (Standards Organisation of Nigeria), South Africa has SABS (South African Bureau of Standards), and Ghana has GSA (Ghana Standards Authority). These bodies set the standards for sanitary pads, which are often based on international standards like ISO. To get certified, you must submit your product for testing. They will check absorbency, pH, microbial contamination, and chemical safety. The process usually involves: 1) Applying for a permit, 2) Submitting samples, 3) Paying fees, 4) Passing tests, and 5) Getting a certificate. The certificate allows you to use the standard mark on your packaging. This mark builds trust with customers and is required for many retail stores. In addition to national bodies, there are international certifications. ISO 9001 is for quality management, and ISO 13485 is for medical devices. You can get these through certification bodies like SGS or Bureau Veritas. They audit your factory and processes. It can be expensive, but it opens export markets. Another important certification is from the FDA in the USA, but that's only needed if you export there. For Africa, the African Organisation for Standardisation (ARSO) harmonizes standards. You can also get Halal or Kosher certification if you target those markets. For small producers, start with your national standard. Many countries have simplified procedures for SMEs. For example, KEBS has a "SMES" program. Also, join a local manufacturers associationthey can guide you. Keep records of all tests and certifications. Renew them before they expire. If you don't comply, your product can be banned or seized. So, budget for certification as part of your production costs. It's an investment in credibility.

6. Compliance and Labeling Requirements

Once your pads meet quality standards, you must label them correctly. Labeling is not just about looksit's a legal requirement. Most African countries require the following on the package: product name, brand, net quantity (e.g., 10 pads), absorbency level (regular, super, etc.), ingredients (especially if fragranced), manufacturer's name and address, country of origin, batch number, and expiry date. The expiry date is usually 3 years from production if stored properly. You also need a barcode for retail. If you have certification, include the standard mark (e.g., KEBS diamond mark). For export, you may need additional information like importer details. The label must be in the official language(s) of the country. In many African countries, English or French is used, but some require local languages. For example, in Tanzania, Swahili may be needed. Also, include instructions for use and disposal. For disposal, say "wrap and dispose in a bin"do not flush. This is important for environmental health. If you make organic or eco-friendly claims, you must prove them. False claims can lead to fines. In terms of compliance, you must also follow good manufacturing practices (GMP). This means your production area is clean, workers wear gloves and hairnets, and there is no cross-contamination. Keep a log of cleaning and sanitation. If you use a contract manufacturer, ensure they are certified. For labeling, use waterproof ink so it doesn't smudge. Test your labels by rubbing with a wet cloth. Also, ensure the packaging is sealed properly to prevent contamination. If you sell in bulk to NGOs, they may have their own labeling requirements. Always ask. Finally, keep a copy of your label approval from the standards body. If you change your label, you may need re-approval. Compliance is ongoingnot a one-time event.

<h2>Practical Reference: Quality Standards &amp; Safety Compliance for Sanitary Pad Production in Africa</h2>

<p>For an African sanitary pad manufacturer, quality standards are not a bureaucratic hurdle they are the passport to contracts with NGOs, supermarkets, ministries of health, and export buyers. A single failed absorbency test or an unlabelled chemical residue can cost you a tender worth millions of shillings, naira, or rand. This practical reference consolidates the international standards, testing protocols, and regulatory pathways you need to move from artisanal production to compliant, scalable manufacturing. It is written for the business owner who must decide which certifications to pursue first, which tests to budget for, and how to label products that will pass customs and health authority scrutiny.</p>

<h3>Why Standards Drive Revenue, Not Just Compliance</h3>
<p>In most African markets, the buyers with reliable, repeatable orders are institutional: UN agencies, ministries of education (school pad programmes), NGOs, and supermarket chains. Each of these buyers demands documented evidence of safety and performance. ISO 9001 (quality management) and ISO 13485 (medical devices) are the two certifications most frequently requested in tender documents. ISO 13485 is particularly relevant because sanitary pads are classified as medical devices in many jurisdictions, including under the EU Medical Device Regulation and Kenya's Pharmacy and Poisons Board guidelines. Achieving ISO 13485 signals that your product is safe for intimate body contact a powerful differentiator against unregulated imports.</p>

<h3>Core International Standards You Must Know</h3>
<p>The primary performance standard for sanitary pads is <strong>ISO 11948-1:1996</strong> (Urine-absorbing aids Part 1: Whole-product testing), which, although originally written for incontinence products, is widely adapted for menstrual pads because no dedicated ISO menstrual pad standard exists. The <strong>EDANA/INDA NWSP 350.0</strong> series provides test methods for absorbency, rewet, and leakage. For chemical safety, <strong>ISO 10993</strong> series governs biocompatibility. For labelling, <strong>ISO 15223-1</strong> specifies symbols for medical devices. In the EU, pads fall under the General Product Safety Regulation (GPSR) and, if marketed with health claims, the Medical Device Regulation (MDR 2017/745). In the US, the FDA regulates pads as Class I medical devices under 21 CFR 880. In Africa, the <strong>African Organization for Standardization (ARSO)</strong> has developed harmonised standards (ARS 1350:2020 for sanitary pads) that many national bureaus of standards are adopting.</p>

<h3>Testing for Absorbency and Leakage</h3>
<p>Absorbency is the single most important performance parameter for consumer trust. The two key metrics are <strong>absorption capacity</strong> (total fluid held in grams) and <strong>rewet</strong> (fluid that escapes back to the surface under pressure, measured in grams). A pad that holds 10 ml but rewets 2 ml will feel wet and fail in the market. The standard test involves applying a measured volume of saline solution (0.9% NaCl) at a controlled rate, then applying a standard weight to simulate body pressure and measuring the fluid that transfers to filter paper.</p>

<p>Leakage testing simulates real-use conditions: the pad is placed on a curved form, fluid is introduced at intervals, and the edges are inspected for seepage. For African manufacturers, the challenge is often the SAP (superabsorbent polymer) quality. Cheap SAP with low gel strength will absorb quickly but release fluid under pressure a common cause of leaks in low-cost pads. Investing in higher-grade SAP from reputable suppliers (e.g., BASF, Evonik, Sumitomo) may increase unit cost by 1520% but reduces returns and builds brand loyalty.</p>

<h3>Skin Irritation and Biocompatibility Testing</h3>
<p>Sanitary pads contact mucosal and perineal skin for hours. Biocompatibility testing under <strong>ISO 10993</strong> is therefore non-negotiable for any product making health or medical claims. The relevant tests are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>ISO 10993-5:</strong> Cytotoxicity (in vitro) checks whether material extracts kill cells.</li>
<li><strong>ISO 10993-10:</strong> Sensitisation and irritation typically using reconstructed human epidermis (RhE) models such as EpiSkin or SkinEthic, which avoid animal testing and are accepted by EU regulators.</li>
<li><strong>ISO 10993-23:</strong> Irritation testing (updated 2021) provides in vitro alternatives.</li>
</ul>
<p>For African manufacturers targeting export markets, RhE testing is essential because the EU banned animal testing for cosmetic ingredients and increasingly expects in vitro data for medical devices. A single RhE irritation test costs approximately USD 1,5003,000 per sample a significant but necessary investment. Batch testing every 612 months is typical once a formulation is locked.</p>

<h3>Chemical Safety: Phthalates, Dioxins, and Pesticides</h3>
<p>Chemical residues are the hidden risk in sanitary pad manufacturing. They arise from three sources: raw materials (pulp, SAP, adhesives), processing (chlorine bleaching of pulp), and contamination (pesticides in cotton).</p>

<p><strong>Phthalates</strong> are plasticisers sometimes found in adhesives and printed films. They are endocrine disruptors linked to reproductive harm. The EU REACH regulation restricts DEHP, DBP, BBP, and DIBP to &lt;0.1% by weight in articles. Test method: GC-MS after solvent extraction.</p>

<p><strong>Dioxins and furans</strong> are unintentional by-products of chlorine bleaching of wood pulp. The WHO sets a tolerable intake of 14 pg TEQ/kg body weight per day. Elemental chlorine-free (ECF) or totally chlorine-free (TCF) pulp eliminates this risk. Test method: HRGC/HRMS (high-resolution gas chromatography/mass spectrometry).</p>

<p><strong>Pesticides</strong> are a concern if you use non-organic cotton. Organochlorine pesticides (e.g., DDT, lindane) persist in cotton and can transfer to the pad. The EU MRL for cotton is typically 0.010.5 mg/kg depending on the compound. Test method: GC-MS/MS or LC-MS/MS multi-residue screen.</p>

<p style="margin: 0; font-weight: bold; color: #b71c1c; font-size: 16px;"> SAFETY WARNING: Chlorine-Bleached Pulp and Dioxin Risk</p>
<p style="margin: 8px 0 0 0; color: #b71c1c;">Do not use chlorine-gas-bleached wood pulp in sanitary pad cores. Chlorine bleaching generates dioxins and furans, which are persistent environmental pollutants and known carcinogens. Even low-level dioxin exposure is associated with reproductive and immune system harm. If your supplier cannot provide a certificate of analysis confirming ECF (Elemental Chlorine-Free) or TCF (Totally Chlorine-Free) pulp, switch suppliers immediately. In several African markets, imported chlorine-bleached pulp is sold at a discount the short-term savings are not worth the regulatory, legal, and reputational risk. Always request batch-specific dioxin test results before accepting pulp deliveries.</p>

<h3>Regulatory Bodies and Certifications</h3>
<p>Navigating the regulatory landscape is easier when you know which bodies matter for your target market:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Kenya:</strong> Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) KS 2474:2017 for sanitary pads; Pharmacy and Poisons Board for medical device registration.</li>
<li><strong>Nigeria:</strong> Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON) NIS 547:2017; NAFDAC registration for medical devices.</li>
<li><strong>South Africa:</strong> South African Bureau of Standards (SABS) SANS 11948; SAHPRA for medical device licensing.</li>
<li><strong>Ghana:</strong> Ghana Standards Authority (GSA) GS 1119:2017; FDA Ghana for medical device registration.</li>
<li><strong>Regional:</strong> ARSO harmonised standard ARS 1350:2020; African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) mutual recognition discussions ongoing.</li>
<li><strong>International:</strong> ISO 9001, ISO 13485, CE marking (EU MDR), FDA 510(k) exemption (US Class I).</li>
</ul>

<h3>Compliance and Labeling Requirements</h3>
<p>Labelling is where many African manufacturers lose contracts. A compliant label must include: product name and type (e.g., "Ultra-Thin Regular"), net quantity, batch/lot number, manufacturing and expiry dates, manufacturer name and address, country of origin, material composition (top sheet, core, back sheet), disposal instructions, and any relevant certifications (ISO, KEBS, SON, etc.). For export to the EU, the label must also include the CE mark, a single European authorised representative, and instructions in the official language(s) of the destination country. For medical device claims (e.g., "hypoallergenic," "dermatologically tested"), you must hold the supporting test reports on file and make them available to regulators on request.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Confirm your target market's mandatory standard</strong> e.g., KS 2474 in Kenya, NIS 547 in Nigeria, SANS 11948 in South Africa.</li>
<li><strong>Test every batch for absorbency, rewet, and leakage</strong> using NWSP 350.0 methods; keep records for at least 5 years.</li>
<li><strong>Commission ISO 10993-5 and 10993-10/23 tests</strong> on final product before commercial launch.</li>
<li><strong>Require ECF/TCF pulp certificates</strong> from your supplier and test for dioxins annually.</li>
<li><strong>Screen raw materials for phthalates, pesticides, and heavy metals</strong> especially cotton and adhesives.</li>
<li><strong>Implement ISO 9001 first, then ISO 13485</strong> if pursuing medical device markets or institutional tenders.</li>
<li><strong>Design labels to meet the strictest market you serve</strong> this future-proofs your packaging for export.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain a compliance file</strong> with all test reports, certificates, and supplier declarations ready for buyer audits.</li>
</ol>
