1. Introduction and Why It Matters

In African ornamental ironwork, leaves, flowers, and natural forms are everywhereâfrom the acacia leaf gate in Nairobi to the flame lily scroll on a Durban balcony. Clients pay more for work that mimics nature because it feels alive and rooted in local culture. A plain bar gate might sell for 8,000 KES; add hand-forged leaves and the same gate can fetch 15,000 KES or more. This module teaches you to forge those organic shapes using simple tools and techniques.

Natural forms are not just decoration; they solve design problems. A leaf can hide a weld, a flower can cover a bolt, and a vine can tie two panels together. In West Africa, blacksmiths have forged stylised leaves from repurposed car springs for generations. In North Africa, palm and date motifs dominate. You will learn to look at a real leafâmango, baobab, or jacarandaâand break it down into tapers, curves, and a central spine. That spine is usually a piece of 6 mm round bar; the blade is flattened 3 mm sheet or flattened rod.

Start by collecting samples: pick fresh leaves and press them between newspaper for a day. Trace the outline on paper, then transfer to metal. For flowers, study a simple 5-petal shape like a hibiscus or frangipani. Each petal is a tapered flat bar bent with a scrolling fork. The centre can be a 10 mm ball bearing or a forged dome from 12 mm round bar.

Your first goal is not perfection but repetition. Forge 20 leaves of the same size before moving to flowers. Use a gas forge or a charcoal forge with a hand-crank blower. Mild steel is fine; 6 mm and 8 mm round bars, plus 2 mm and 3 mm sheet offcuts, are your main materials. A 1 kg hammer, a 150 mm anvil, and a pair of scrolling tongs are enough to begin. By the end of this module, you will have a small panel of leaves and a flower to show a client.

2. Tools and Materials

You do not need a fully equipped shop to forge natural forms. A basic setup includes: a forge (gas or solid fuel), a 5â10 kg anvil or a heavy piece of railway line, a 1 kg ball-peen hammer, a 1.5 kg cross-peen hammer, scrolling tongs, flat tongs, a hot cut hardy, and a wire brush. For leaves, a rounding hammer or a ball-peen hammer is essential to stretch the metal into a blade shape. For flowers, you need a scrolling fork (a two-pronged tool that fits in the hardy hole) and a bending fork. A vise with a 100 mm jaw is useful for holding stems.

Materials: mild steel is cheapest and easiest. Use 6 mm, 8 mm, and 10 mm round bars for stems and spines. For leaf blades, use 2 mm or 3 mm flat bar (20 mm or 25 mm wide) or flatten round bar yourself. For flower petals, 2 mm sheet metal cut into strips 15â20 mm wide works well. Avoid galvanised steelâthe fumes are toxic. If you use rebar, test it first; some rebar is brittle and cracks when bent cold. Old car springs (5160 steel) are excellent for small chisels and punches but too hard for leaves unless you anneal them.

You will also need a forge fire with a neutral flame (slightly reducing) to avoid burning the steel. For gas forges, set the regulator to 0.5â1 bar. For charcoal, use hardwood charcoal and a blower to reach orange-yellow heat (about 900â1000Â°C). Quench your tools in water or oil as needed. Keep a bucket of water and a first-aid kit nearby.

In Africa, source materials from local scrapyards, hardware stores, or construction sites. A 6 m length of 6 mm round bar costs about 500 KES (Kenya), 20 GHS (Ghana), or 50 ZAR (South Africa). Buy in bulk to save. Make your own scrolling fork from a piece of 20 mm square bar: heat one end, split it with a hacksaw, bend the two prongs apart, and fit it in the hardy hole. That tool alone will pay for itself in a week.

3. Step by Step Method

We will forge a simple leaf from 6 mm round bar and a 5-petal flower from flat bar. Work at a bright orange heat (about 900Â°C) and reheat as soon as the metal turns dark red.

Leaf: 1) Cut a 200 mm length of 6 mm round bar. 2) Heat one end to orange and hammer it into a taper about 40 mm long, then flatten the taper into a blade 3 mm thick and 20 mm wide using the ball-peen hammer. 3) Use the cross-peen to draw out the sides into a pointed oval, working from the centre outward. 4) Reheat and use a hot cut or chisel to mark a central spine (a shallow groove) down the middle. 5) Bend the leaf into a gentle curve by hammering over the anvilâs horn or a pipe. 6) For the stem, leave the remaining 160 mm as round bar; taper the end to a point. 7) Wire-brush and finish with a wire wheel or file. Make 20 leaves like this, varying sizes.

Flower: 1) Cut five strips of 2 mm sheet, each 80 mm long and 15 mm wide. 2) Round one end of each strip with a grinder or file. 3) Heat a strip and use a scrolling fork to bend it into a petal shapeâa shallow S-curve. 4) Repeat for all five. 5) For the centre, forge a 12 mm round bar into a dome (like a small bowl) or use a 10 mm ball bearing. 6) Weld or braze the petals around the centre, overlapping slightly. 7) Attach a 6 mm stem to the back with a forge weld or arc weld. 8) Clean and finish.

For a vine, twist two 6 mm round bars together while hot, then open the twists with a chisel to create a spiral. Attach leaves by welding or by wrapping thin 3 mm wire around the stem. Always test-fit before final welding. Practice each step slowly; speed comes with repetition.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Burning the steel. If you heat mild steel above 1200Â°C, it sparks and becomes brittle. Fix: watch the colourâbright yellow is too hot; orange is ideal. If you burn a piece, cut it off and start again.

Mistake 2: Hammering cold metal. Cold working 6 mm round bar causes cracks. Fix: always work at orange heat. If you must bend cold, use a bending fork and go slowly.

Mistake 3: Uneven leaf thickness. Beginners often make the tip too thin and the base too thick. Fix: draw out the tip first, then the middle, and leave the base slightly thicker for strength. Use calipers to check thickness (aim for 3 mm).

Mistake 4: Petals that look flat. A flower needs depth. Fix: after bending the petal, hammer a shallow dish into it using a ball-peen hammer over a softwood stump or a sandbag. This gives a 3D effect.

Mistake 5: Weak welds. Welding thin sheet to round bar can blow through. Fix: use a lower amperage (60â80 A for 2 mm sheet) and short tack welds. Alternatively, braze with a torch. Forge welding is traditional but needs clean metal and flux (borax).

Mistake 6: No plan for assembly. Leaves welded randomly look messy. Fix: draw a full-size layout on paper or plywood first. Mark where each leaf and flower goes. Use a jigâa piece of flat board with nailsâto hold parts in place while welding.

Mistake 7: Ignoring scale. A giant leaf on a small gate looks wrong. Fix: keep leaves 100â200 mm long and flowers 80â120 mm across for typical gates. For large commissions, scale up proportionally.

Mistake 8: Poor finish. Forgetting to remove scale and welds. Fix: wire-brush while hot, then use a flap disc on an angle grinder. Paint with red oxide primer and two coats of enamel. In coastal areas, use galvanised or stainless steel for longer life.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in ornamental ironwork is non-negotiable. You are working with 900Â°C metal, sharp edges, and power tools. Wear the right gear: leather apron, safety boots (steel toe), safety glasses, ear plugs when grinding, and leather gloves. Never wear synthetic clothingâit melts. Cotton or leather only. Tie back long hair and remove jewellery.

Forge safety: set up your forge on a fireproof surface (concrete or bare earth). Keep a bucket of water and a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO2) within 3 metres. Never leave a lit forge unattended. If using charcoal, ensure good ventilationâcarbon monoxide kills silently. Work in a well-ventilated area, ideally outdoors or with a chimney hood.

Hammer safety: check your hammer head regularly for cracks or looseness. A flying hammer head is lethal. Strike with the face, not the side. Keep your tongs in good conditionâworn jaws drop hot metal. Always hold the work securely. Never put hot metal on the floor where someone can step on it. Use a quench tank clearly marked.

Grinding and cutting: use a face shield over safety glasses. Check grinding discs for cracks and never exceed the rated RPM. Clamp your work. Sparks can ignite dust or paperâclear your bench.

Welding: use a welding helmet with at least shade 10. Protect others with a screen. Fumes from galvanised or painted metal are toxicâgrind off coatings first or use a respirator.

Best practice: keep your workspace tidy. Store metal vertically to avoid trips. Label your tools. After work, sweep up scale and metal offcutsâthey cause cuts. Wash hands before eating. In Africa, many workshops are informal; set your own standard. A clean shop is a safe shop. Also, stretch before and after workâblacksmithing is physical. Drink water. Take breaks. Your body is your main tool.

6. Practical Exercise

Your task: forge a decorative panel measuring 300 mm x 300 mm that includes at least three leaves, one flower, and a twisted vine stem. This exercise combines all skills from this module. You will need: 1 m of 6 mm round bar, 0.5 m of 3 mm x 20 mm flat bar, a 100 mm x 100 mm piece of 2 mm sheet, and a 300 mm x 300 mm backing frame made from 20 mm x 3 mm flat bar. Tools: forge, anvil, hammers, scrolling tongs, welder, angle grinder, wire brush.

Step 1: Make the frame. Cut four pieces of 20 mm x 3 mm flat bar to 300 mm. Miter the corners and weld them into a square. Grind the welds smooth.

Step 2: Forge the vine. Cut two 400 mm lengths of 6 mm round bar. Heat the middle 300 mm and twist them together tightly (about 6 twists per 100 mm). Use a vise and a twisting wrench. Leave 50 mm at each end straight for welding.

Step 3: Forge leaves. Make three leaves from 6 mm round bar as described in Lesson 3. Vary sizes: 80 mm, 120 mm, and 150 mm long. Curve them slightly.

Step 4: Forge the flower. Make a 5-petal flower from 2 mm sheet and a 12 mm round bar centre. Petals should be 60 mm long, 15 mm wide. Weld petals to centre.

Step 5: Assemble. Lay the frame flat. Position the vine diagonally across the frame. Weld the vine ends to the frame corners. Attach leaves along the vine by welding their stems to the vine. Place the flower at one end of the vine. Use a jig (nails in a board) to hold parts in place.

Step 6: Finish. Wire-brush all welds and scale. Grind any sharp edges. Paint with red oxide primer, then two coats of black enamel. Allow to dry.

Step 7: Present. Take a photo and show it to a local hardware store or potential client. Ask for feedback. If you can, make three panels and sell them as a set. Price: a panel like this can sell for 5,000â8,000 KES (Kenya), 200â300 GHS (Ghana), or 400â600 ZAR (South Africa). Repeat the exercise until you can complete it in under 4 hours.
