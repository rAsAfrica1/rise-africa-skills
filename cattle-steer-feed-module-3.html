Lesson 3.1~12 min

Energy Requirements for Growth

In this lesson
  • Separate maintenance energy from growth energy and calculate both
  • Convert a forage ADF value into net energy for gain
  • Decide a target daily gain on economic rather than habitual grounds

Every kilogram of feed an animal eats is spent in a fixed order. Maintenance is paid first - keeping the body warm, the heart beating, the gut moving. Only what is left over becomes growth. This is why a half-fed animal does not grow half as fast; it grows hardly at all, because the whole ration disappears into maintenance before any of it reaches the muscle.

Maintenance is calculated from metabolic body weight: NEm = 0.077 x BW to the power 0.75 Mcal per day for beef cattle, with BW in kilograms. The published worked values are about 3.5 Mcal NEm per day for a 500 lb steer and 6.0 to 7.0 Mcal per day for a 1,200 lb cow. Cold stress, walking distance and health challenges all raise it above the table figure.

Growth energy sits on top. Published figures give a 500 lb steer gaining 1.5 lb a day a requirement of 3.2 Mcal NEg per day, and a 1,200 lb finishing steer gaining 3 lb a day 6.8 to 7.2 Mcal NEg per day. Later-maturing frame types need more. In the TDN system, growing replacement heifers of 1,100 to 1,400 lb mature weight need 5.0 to 5.1 lb TDN daily at 0.5 lb of gain, rising to 6.8 to 7.0 lb TDN at 2.0 lb of gain. Notice the shape of that: quadrupling the gain requires only about 36 percent more feed, because maintenance is already paid. Faster gain is more feed-efficient, which is the whole economic argument for feeding a growing animal well rather than dragging it out.

Now connect this to the forage in your field. Convert ADF to energy in two steps. First, TDN% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%). Then NEg Mcal/kg = (0.0257 x TDN%) - 0.315. For dairy the parallel is NEL Mcal/kg = (0.0245 x TDN%) - 0.12.

Worked example. A 250 kg growing steer on Rhodes grass hay at 36 percent ADF and 62 percent NDF.

Maintenance: 250 to the power 0.75. Square root of 250 is 15.81; square root of 15.81 is 3.98. So 250 to the power 0.75 = 250 / 3.98 = 62.8. NEm = 0.077 x 62.8 = 4.84 Mcal per day. Forage energy: TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 36) = 88.9 - 28.0 = 60.9 percent. NEg = (0.0257 x 60.9) - 0.315 = 1.565 - 0.315 = 1.25 Mcal per kg. Intake: DMI = 120 / 62 = 1.94 percent of body weight, so 250 x 0.0194 = 4.84 kg dry matter per day. Total energy available for gain from the forage: 4.84 kg x 1.25 = 6.05 Mcal per day, less the 4.84 Mcal of maintenance, leaves 1.21 Mcal for growth.

That is a slow-growing animal. To lift growth you have two levers: raise the energy density of the diet, or raise intake. Add 1.5 kg of maize meal at roughly 1.9 Mcal NEg per kg and you add 2.85 Mcal, nearly tripling the energy available for gain. At a maize meal price of 0.30 USD per kg that is 0.45 USD per head per day. Whether that pays depends on your live weight price. If the extra energy delivers an additional 0.35 kg of daily gain and live weight sells at 2.20 USD per kg, the gain is worth 0.77 USD against 0.45 USD of feed - a margin of 0.32 USD per head per day, or roughly 29 USD over 90 days. Do this sum before you buy, not after.

A named African case. Communal and small commercial producers in Botswana running Tswana and Tswana-cross steers on veld have long accepted 24 to 36 months to market weight, which is consistent with published grass-fed finishing timelines of 24 to 36 months from birth. Producers who added a targeted dry-season energy and protein supplement rather than trying to buy their way through with hay alone brought that timeline down substantially, and the shorter cycle freed grazing for more animals. The gain was not from a better pasture; it was from stopping the annual reversal.

Two habits to build. First, always write maintenance down before you plan gain, because it tells you how much of the ration is already committed. Second, price the target gain before you commit to it. A high gain is not automatically right; it is right when the extra kilograms are worth more than the extra feed.

Protein and Amino Acid for Growing Cattle

In this lesson
  • Read the published crude protein requirement for a given weight and gain
  • Explain why microbial protein makes amino acid balancing less critical in ruminants
  • Cost a protein supplement against the exact shortfall rather than by habit

Protein requirement in a growing animal is not a single number. It moves with both body weight and rate of gain, and the published tables make the pattern clear.

For a 400 kg medium-frame steer or heifer, the requirement is 0.88 lb per day of crude protein at 1.0 lb of daily gain, which is 10.2 percent of diet dry matter; 1.33 lb per day at 1.5 lb of gain, which is 10.5 percent; and 1.60 lb per day at 2.0 lb of gain, which is 10.8 percent. For lighter 500 lb feeder calves, the range is 1.04 to 1.12 lb per day at 1 lb of gain and 1.87 to 1.94 lb per day at 3 lb of gain. By the time an animal reaches 600 kg and is finishing at 2.0 to 3.5 lb of daily gain, the diet requirement has fallen to 8.5 to 9.5 percent crude protein of dry matter. The percentage falls as the animal gets heavier because intake has grown faster than the protein requirement, and because a finishing animal is depositing fat rather than lean tissue.

Underneath everything sits the floor: 7.5 percent crude protein of diet dry matter, the lowest level used in NRC 2016 beef recommendations. Below that the rumen microbes are nitrogen-starved, fibre digestion stalls and intake collapses, which was covered in Module 1.

Now the amino acid question. In a pig or a chicken you must balance individual amino acids, because what goes in the mouth reaches the small intestine largely intact. In a ruminant, most dietary protein is dismantled in the rumen and rebuilt as microbial protein, which has a consistent and well-balanced amino acid profile regardless of what the animal ate. That is the great advantage of the rumen: it converts poor-quality protein into good-quality protein for free. The published standards behind this course do not give amino acid requirement figures for growing beef cattle, and for practical purposes on an African smallholder unit you should not try to formulate to individual amino acids. Focus instead on total crude protein and on the degradable and undegradable split.

That split is 60 to 70 percent rumen-degradable protein and 30 to 40 percent rumen-undegradable protein of total crude protein for growing and finishing beef. High-RDP feeds are fresh forage, legumes and soybean meal. High-RUP feeds are heat-treated meals, blood meal and fish meal at 70 to 90 percent RUP, with soybean meal at 35 to 40 percent. A growing steer on grass plus an oilseed cake is generally already in the target range without any special effort. Bypass protein products are worth paying a premium for in high-producing dairy cows, not in growing beef.

Worked example. A 400 kg steer eating 9 kg of dry matter a day, targeting 1.5 lb of gain, needs 10.5 percent crude protein, which is 9 x 0.105 = 0.945 kg per day. His basal forage is maize stover at 5 percent crude protein. If he eats 7 kg of stover that supplies 7 x 0.05 = 0.35 kg. The shortfall is 0.945 - 0.35 = 0.595 kg of crude protein from 2 kg of supplement, meaning the supplement must average 0.595 / 2 = 29.8 percent crude protein.

Compare options at that requirement. Groundnut cake at 45 percent crude protein and 0.48 USD/kg: 0.595 / 0.45 = 1.32 kg needed, costing 0.63 USD/day, or 1.07 USD per kg of protein delivered. Sunflower cake at 30 percent and 0.28 USD/kg: 1.98 kg needed, 0.55 USD/day, 0.93 USD per kg of protein. Cottonseed cake at 36 percent and 0.32 USD/kg: 1.65 kg needed, 0.53 USD/day, 0.89 USD per kg of protein. The cheapest per kg of protein here is cottonseed cake, but remember it should not be fed to breeding bulls within two to three months of the season and is limited to 3 to 5 lb per day for bulls, and whole cottonseed is capped at 25 percent of diet dry matter or 0.5 percent of body weight daily.

A named African case. In the groundnut basin of Senegal and across southern Mali, groundnut cake left after local oil pressing is the standard growing-cattle protein. It is excellent feed, but because it is a food-crop by-product its price swings sharply with the human oil market. Producers who recalculate cost per kg of crude protein each season, rather than defaulting to groundnut cake every year, regularly find cottonseed or sunflower cake cheaper for the same delivered protein in a given year. The right supplement is a price question with a seasonal answer.

Mineral Requirements for Skeletal Development

In this lesson
  • State calcium, phosphorus and magnesium targets specific to growing cattle
  • Calculate whether a mineral product delivers the requirement at your feeding rate
  • Recognise the deficiency signs that appear in growing frames before growth stops

A growing animal is building a skeleton, and the skeleton is mostly calcium and phosphorus. Get the minerals wrong at this stage and you produce an animal that never reaches its frame potential, no matter how much energy and protein you feed afterwards. Bone is laid down once.

The published targets for growing cattle, as a percentage of diet dry matter, are calcium 0.35 to 0.65 percent and phosphorus 0.20 to 0.30 percent for animals around 400 kg, falling to calcium 0.16 to 0.35 percent and phosphorus 0.14 to 0.20 percent by 600 kg as growth slows. Magnesium is 0.10 to 0.12 percent for growing cattle. Potassium is 0.60 percent, sodium 0.06 to 0.08 percent and sulphur 0.15 percent of diet dry matter. The published summary for a 500 lb steer gaining 1.5 lb a day is calcium 0.35 to 0.45 percent, phosphorus 0.20 to 0.25 percent and magnesium 0.10 percent.

The ratio is as important as the levels. Target calcium to phosphorus at 2.0 to 2.5 to 1. Diets built heavily on grain and bran are naturally high in phosphorus and low in calcium, which pushes the ratio the wrong way and causes secondary hyperparathyroidism - the bone weakens because the body is pulling calcium out of it to balance the blood. This is a common and entirely avoidable problem in African fattening units where maize bran and brewers grains form the bulk of the ration. Distillers dried grains carry the same warning: they have a high phosphorus to calcium ratio and published guidance says to mix them with a calcium source.

Trace minerals for skeletal work include manganese, required at 20 to 40 mg/kg of diet dry matter for beef and 40 mg/kg for dairy, with a maximum tolerable level of 2,000 mg/kg; manganese is specifically involved in bone development. Copper at 10 mg/kg, maximum 40, affects hoof quality as well as coat colour. Zinc at 30 mg/kg, maximum 500, affects hoof, skin and coat. Vitamin D at 125 IU per lb of diet dry matter is essential to calcium absorption, and deficiency produces rickets in young stock. Dried forages retain vitamin D better than fresh or ensiled forage, and animals with normal sun exposure make their own.

Worked example. A 300 kg growing steer eats 7 kg of dry matter a day, of which 5 kg is grass hay at about 0.30 percent calcium and 0.18 percent phosphorus, and 2 kg is maize bran at about 0.05 percent calcium and 1.10 percent phosphorus.

Calcium supplied: (5 x 0.0030) + (2 x 0.0005) = 0.0150 + 0.0010 = 0.0160 kg, which is 16.0 g. Phosphorus supplied: (5 x 0.0018) + (2 x 0.0110) = 0.0090 + 0.0220 = 0.0310 kg, which is 31.0 g. Ratio: 16.0 / 31.0 = 0.52 to 1. That is inverted, far from the 2.0-2.5 to 1 target, and it is a recipe for weak bone.

The fix is calcium, not less bran. Feeding 40 g a day of feed-grade limestone at roughly 38 percent calcium adds 40 x 0.38 = 15.2 g of calcium, bringing the total to 31.2 g against 31.0 g of phosphorus - a ratio of 1.0 to 1, better but still short. Eighty grams of limestone adds 30.4 g, giving 46.4 g calcium to 31.0 g phosphorus, a ratio of 1.5 to 1, and 120 g gives 61.6 g calcium, a ratio of 2.0 to 1 and inside target. Limestone at perhaps 0.12 USD per kg means 120 g costs 0.014 USD per head per day. That is one and a half US cents to protect the skeleton of an animal worth several hundred dollars.

A named African case. Smallholder fatteners in central Malawi commonly build rations around maize bran because it is cheap and locally milled. Extension work there has repeatedly identified inverted calcium to phosphorus ratios in those rations, showing up as lameness and poor frame development in young stock rather than as any dramatic disease. The correction was locally quarried limestone flour at a few cents a head a day, plus a trace mineral lick.

Check your own ration on paper before you assume a mineral lick is covering it.

Forage Utilization in Growing Diets

In this lesson
  • Build a growing ration around the cheapest forage that can still meet intake needs
  • Apply by-product inclusion limits when substituting cheap feeds into a forage base
  • Calculate cost per kg of gain from a forage-plus-supplement ration in USD

Forage is the cheapest feed on almost every African cattle unit, and it is also the feed most often wasted. Getting more out of what you already grow or buy is usually a larger opportunity than buying anything new.

Start with the two equations, applied to a real decision. You have two forages available for a group of 250 kg growing steers. Napier grass cut at 1.2 m tests roughly 40 percent ADF and 68 percent NDF. Maize stover tests roughly 47 percent ADF and 74 percent NDF.

Napier: DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 40) = 88.9 - 31.2 = 57.7 percent. DMI = 120 / 68 = 1.76 percent of body weight, so 250 x 0.0176 = 4.41 kg dry matter. Stover: DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 47) = 88.9 - 36.6 = 52.3 percent. DMI = 120 / 74 = 1.62 percent of body weight, so 250 x 0.0162 = 4.05 kg dry matter.

Digestible dry matter delivered per day: Napier 4.41 x 0.577 = 2.54 kg; stover 4.05 x 0.523 = 2.12 kg. The Napier delivers about 20 percent more usable feed from the same animal's stomach. If Napier costs 0.08 USD per kg dry matter and stover 0.04 USD, the stover still looks cheaper per kg of digestible dry matter - 0.077 USD versus 0.139 USD - but neither forage alone gets close to a decent growth rate, so what actually matters is the total cost of the ration that does.

That is the key insight for growing cattle: the forage is the platform, not the answer. Your job is to pick the cheapest platform that leaves the animal enough intake capacity to consume the supplement that will actually drive gain. A very high-NDF forage crowds out the supplement, because the bulk sits in the rumen.

By-products are usually the cheapest way to lift a forage ration, and each has a published ceiling. Distillers dried grains: 50 percent of diet dry matter maximum, above which sulphur toxicity becomes a risk, and the high phosphorus to calcium ratio must be corrected with calcium. Wet corn gluten feed: 25 to 30 percent of diet dry matter. Soybean hulls: 15 to 20 percent, low in protein but high in NDF and an excellent fibre source. Wet brewers grains: 30 to 40 percent, with storage losses around 15 percent and palatability problems above 30 percent. Beet pulp: 15 to 20 percent, palatable and high in moisture, good as a carrier. Whole cottonseed: 25 percent of diet dry matter and a maximum of 0.5 percent of body weight daily. As a general principle, by-products should not exceed 50 percent of the concentrate fraction, and forage quality should be kept up to buffer the variable nutrient content of by-product loads.

Worked ration and cost. Take the 250 kg steer on Napier at 4.41 kg dry matter. Add 1.5 kg dry matter of wet brewers grains and 0.7 kg of maize bran. Total intake is 6.61 kg dry matter. Brewers grains at 1.5 / 6.61 = 23 percent of diet dry matter is inside the 30 to 40 percent ceiling. Suppose Napier costs 0.08 USD per kg DM, brewers grains 0.10 and maize bran 0.34. Daily cost: (4.41 x 0.08) + (1.5 x 0.10) + (0.7 x 0.34) = 0.353 + 0.150 + 0.238 = 0.741 USD per head per day. If that ration delivers 0.65 kg of daily gain, feed cost per kg of gain is 0.741 / 0.65 = 1.14 USD. At a live weight price of 2.20 USD per kg, the margin over feed is 1.06 USD per kg of gain. That is the number to track, and it is the number to compare between ration options.

Account for storage losses in your budgeting: published allowances are about 3 percent for dry feed and 15 percent for high-moisture feeds. Buying 1,000 kg of wet brewers grains means feeding about 850 kg.

A named African case. In Zimbabwe's Midlands, fatteners treating maize stover with urea to lift its feeding value, and combining it with locally available brewers grains from small breweries, have built growing rations at a fraction of the cost of commercial feed. The discipline that separates the successful ones is measuring: they know their cost per kg of dry matter for every ingredient and they weigh cattle to know their cost per kg of gain. The details of interpreting laboratory forage reports are covered in Module 10.

Grazing Management for Growing Cattle

In this lesson
  • Match stocking and rotation to the growth stage of the pasture rather than the calendar
  • Plan a supplement strategy that covers the predictable dry-season gap
  • Identify the seasonal grazing hazards that cost growing cattle most

Grazing is the cheapest feeding system available and the hardest to do well, because the feed changes quality under your feet every week and you cannot see it happening.

The core biological fact is that forage quality falls as the plant matures. Published work characterises the decline well: ADF increases by roughly 2 to 4 percentage units per week during the growth cycle, though the exact timing of peak quality varies a great deal by region, species and weather. Run that through the digestibility equation and the consequences are stark. A pasture at 30 percent ADF gives DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 30) = 65.5 percent. Three weeks later at 39 percent ADF it gives 88.9 - (0.779 x 39) = 58.5 percent. The same paddock, the same rain, seven percentage points of digestibility gone. For a growing steer that difference is the gap between gaining and standing still.

Rotation exists to manage that. Move animals so that each paddock is grazed while it is vegetative and then rested long enough to regrow but not long enough to go to seed. In practice on smallholder land this means subdividing what you have, even with a single strand of wire or a herder, and moving on plant condition rather than on a fixed number of days. Rest periods must be longer in the dry season than in the wet, because regrowth is slower.

Stocking rate is the other half. Published comparisons put pasture-based systems at 2 to 5 acres per head depending on forage productivity, with grass-fed daily gains of 1.0 to 2.0 lb per day depending on pasture quality, and finishing timelines of 24 to 36 months from birth against 150 to 180 days for grain finishing from 600 to 1,200 lb. Feed conversion on forage alone runs 8:1 to 12:1 against 5.5:1 to 6.5:1 in a feedlot - roughly 50 percent worse. None of that makes grazing wrong. It makes grazing a low-cost, low-intensity system whose economics depend on keeping the input cost genuinely low and not fighting the seasons.

The dry season is the predictable enemy. Every year, in most African systems, growing cattle lose condition for two to four months and then spend the early wet season regaining what they lost. That annual reversal is the single largest source of lost growth in African cattle production, and it is entirely foreseeable. The answer is a planned supplement across the gap: a protein source to keep the rumen working on low-quality standing hay, which was covered in Module 1, plus energy if the gap is long. A worked comparison: 90 dry-season days at 0.25 kg gain a day is 22.5 kg. The same 90 days at a loss of 0.15 kg a day is minus 13.5 kg. The difference is 36 kg of live weight, worth 79 USD at 2.20 USD per kg. A supplement costing 0.20 USD a day is 18 USD over the period. The arithmetic is not marginal.

Seasonal hazards to plan around:

  1. Grass tetany on lush spring or early-wet-season growth. Lactating cattle need 0.20 to 0.25 percent dietary magnesium during high-risk grazing, or about 0.6 oz of magnesium oxide per head daily. Risk indicators are forage magnesium below 0.15 percent of dry matter, a potassium to magnesium ratio above 10:1, and a potassium to calcium-plus-magnesium ratio above 2.2. Published prevention includes delaying spring grazing until the sward is 6 inches or more, reducing nitrogen fertiliser, and adding legumes, which carry more magnesium than grasses.
  2. Prussic acid on sorghum, sudangrass and especially johnsongrass. Allow regrowth to reach 18 to 24 inches before grazing; young, frost-damaged and rapidly regrowing plants carry the most.
  3. Nitrate on drought-stressed or heavily fertilised pasture. Safe below 0.5 percent of dry matter; caution from 0.5 to 1.5 percent with at least 2 lb per head daily of energy grain; unsafe for grazing from 1.5 to 3.0 percent; never feed above 3.0 percent. Heavy nitrogen fertilisation above 100 lb per acre, and ponded water, are the classic risk combination.
  4. Vitamin A on dormant winter or dry-season pasture, where beta-carotene is largely gone.

A named African case. Ranchers in Laikipia, Kenya, running growing cattle on semi-arid rangeland use planned rotational grazing with long rests and bunched herds. The consistent finding reported from those systems is not a dramatic gain in wet-season growth, where forage was never limiting, but a much smaller dry-season loss - which is precisely where the money is.

Lesson 3.6~12 min

Monitoring Growth and Adjusting Rations

In this lesson
  • Set up a simple weighing and recording routine that survives a busy farm week
  • Calculate average daily gain, feed conversion and feed cost per kg of gain
  • Diagnose from records whether a shortfall is energy, protein, mineral or intake

A ration is a hypothesis. Weighing is the experiment. Most rations on most farms have never been tested, which is why so many producers can tell you exactly what they feed and not at all what it returns.

The routine is simple and it must be simple to survive. Weigh or heart-girth tape the same animals, on the same day of the fortnight, with the same person doing it, and write the date and weight in a book that lives in the same place every time. Add a body condition score on a 1 to 5 scale monthly. That is the whole system. A weigh-band is accurate enough to detect a stall if used consistently, and consistency matters more than absolute accuracy.

From those numbers, three calculations tell you everything.

Average daily gain. Take weight at the end minus weight at the start, divided by days. Worked example: a steer at 218 kg on 1 March and 254 kg on 30 April, 60 days. ADG = (254 - 218) / 60 = 36 / 60 = 0.60 kg per day.

Feed conversion. Total dry matter fed divided by total gain. If that steer ate 7.2 kg dry matter a day for 60 days, that is 432 kg of dry matter for 36 kg of gain, a conversion of 432 / 36 = 12:1. That is at the poor end of the published forage-only range of 8:1 to 12:1 and tells you there is room to improve.

Feed cost per kg of gain. If the ration cost 0.68 USD per head per day, total feed cost is 0.68 x 60 = 40.80 USD for 36 kg of gain, which is 40.80 / 36 = 1.13 USD per kg of gain. At a live weight price of 2.20 USD per kg, margin over feed is 1.07 USD per kg of gain, or 38.5 USD over the period. Now you have a number you can compare against any alternative ration, and against simply selling the animal today.

Diagnosis from records is the skill worth building. When gain is below target, work through the possibilities in order.

  1. Is intake short? Check the forage NDF and calculate DMI = 120 / NDF as a percent of body weight. If the animal cannot physically eat enough, no change in ration quality alone will fix it. Also check water: restricted or dirty water cuts intake immediately.
  2. Is protein short? Calculate the crude protein percentage of the whole diet. Below 7.5 percent, the rumen itself is failing and intake is falling with it. A growing animal targeting moderate gain wants roughly 10.2 to 10.8 percent of diet dry matter at 400 kg.
  3. Is energy short? Convert the forage ADF to TDN with 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF), then to NEg with (0.0257 x TDN) - 0.315, multiply by intake, and subtract maintenance from NEm = 0.077 x BW to the power 0.75. If there is little left over, the answer is energy density.
  4. Are minerals short or unbalanced? Check the Ca:P ratio against 2.0 to 2.5 to 1, and check for the interactions that cause deficiency despite supplementation - molybdenum above 5 mg/kg, dietary sulphur above 0.20 percent, or zinc above 500 mg/kg all block copper.
  5. Is it health, not nutrition? Parasites, lameness and chronic infection all raise maintenance requirement and cut intake. Records that show one animal falling behind while the group holds usually point here.

Then change one thing at a time, in steps of no more than 10 to 15 percent of diet dry matter, allow two to three weeks for the rumen to adapt, and weigh again. Changing three things at once teaches you nothing.

A named African case. Peri-urban fattening operations around Accra, Ghana, buying store cattle and finishing them for the local market, work on short holding periods where a wrong ration is expensive fast. The operators who consistently make money weigh animals at purchase and at intervals, and calculate cost per kg of gain against the day's live weight price. Those who buy on appearance and sell on appearance frequently discover at sale that they fed an animal for three months to add value worth less than the feed.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Why does a half-fed animal grow far less than half as fast?

Energy goes to maintenance before growth. On a restricted ration, most or all of the feed disappears into maintenance.

2. A forage at 36% ADF converts to what TDN?

TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 36) = 88.9 - 28.0 = 60.9%.

3. At 60.9% TDN, the NEg of that forage is about:

NEg = (0.0257 x 60.9) - 0.315 = 1.565 - 0.315 = 1.25 Mcal per kg.

4. Growing heifers need 5.0-5.1 lb TDN at 0.5 lb gain and 6.8-7.0 lb at 2.0 lb gain. This shows that:

Quadrupling gain requires only about 36% more total feed, so each additional kilogram of gain costs less feed than the first.

5. Maintenance requirement rises above the table figure because of:

Published guidance flags cold stress, exercise and health challenges as factors that increase NEm beyond the calculated baseline.

6. A 400 kg steer gaining 2.0 lb/day requires what percentage crude protein in diet dry matter?

Published requirement is 1.60 lb/day of crude protein at 2.0 lb gain, which is 10.8% of diet dry matter.

7. Why is individual amino acid balancing less critical in cattle than in pigs?

Most dietary protein is dismantled and rebuilt as microbial protein with a consistent amino acid profile, so total crude protein and the RDP/RUP split matter more.

8. By 600 kg and finishing, the crude protein requirement of the diet is:

NRC 2016 puts 600 kg finishing cattle gaining 2.0-3.5 lb/day at 8.5-9.5% crude protein of diet dry matter.

9. A steer needs 0.945 kg CP/day and his forage supplies 0.35 kg. The shortfall is:

0.945 - 0.35 = 0.595 kg of crude protein must come from supplement.

10. Whole cottonseed should be limited to:

Published guidance caps whole cottonseed at 25% of diet DM and a maximum of 0.5% of body weight daily because of gossypol.

11. A ration supplies 16 g calcium and 31 g phosphorus per day. The Ca:P ratio is:

16 / 31 = 0.52 to 1, an inverted ratio far below the 2.0-2.5:1 target.

12. Bran-heavy rations typically cause which mineral problem?

Grain and bran are high in phosphorus and low in calcium, inverting the ratio and driving secondary hyperparathyroidism.

13. Calcium requirement for a growing 400 kg animal is:

Published tables give 0.35-0.65% calcium of diet dry matter for growing cattle around 400 kg.

14. Vitamin D deficiency in young growing stock causes:

Vitamin D at 125 IU per lb of diet DM is required for calcium absorption; deficiency produces rickets in young stock.

15. Which trace mineral is specifically flagged for bone development?

Manganese, required at 20-40 mg/kg for beef, is noted as involved in bone development, with a tolerable ceiling of 2,000 mg/kg.

16. A forage at 47% ADF and 74% NDF gives a 250 kg steer how much digestible dry matter per day?

DMI = 120/74 = 1.62% of BW = 4.05 kg; DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 47) = 52.3%; 4.05 x 0.523 = 2.12 kg.

17. Why can a very high-NDF forage undermine a growing ration even when it is cheap?

NDF limits intake. A bulky forage fills the rumen and leaves no capacity for the supplement that supplies the energy for growth.

18. Published storage loss allowance for high-moisture feeds is:

Published guidance uses about 3% for dry feed and 15% for high-moisture feeds.

19. A ration costs 0.741 USD/day and delivers 0.65 kg of gain. Feed cost per kg of gain is:

0.741 / 0.65 = 1.14 USD per kg of gain.

20. Distillers dried grains above 50% of diet DM risk:

Published guidance caps DDG at 50% of diet DM because of sulphur toxicity, and flags the need to add calcium.

21. A pasture moves from 30% to 39% ADF over three weeks. Digestibility falls from:

DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 30) = 65.5%, and 88.9 - (0.779 x 39) = 58.5% - seven points lost on the same paddock.

22. Feed conversion on a forage-only diet runs about:

Published comparisons put forage-only conversion at 8:1 to 12:1, roughly 50% worse than a high-concentrate feedlot diet.

23. Which forage magnesium level indicates high grass tetany risk?

Forage magnesium below 0.15% of dry matter is listed as a high-risk indicator, along with K:Mg above 10:1.

24. The largest single source of lost growth in African grazing systems is:

Cattle lose condition for two to four months every dry season and then spend the wet season regaining it, a foreseeable and preventable reversal.

25. Published guidance says to delay spring grazing until the sward reaches:

Delaying grazing until the sward is 6 inches or more is listed among grass tetany prevention measures, alongside reducing nitrogen fertiliser and adding legumes.

26. A steer goes from 218 kg to 254 kg in 60 days. Average daily gain is:

(254 - 218) / 60 = 36 / 60 = 0.60 kg per day.

27. 432 kg of dry matter fed for 36 kg of gain is a feed conversion of:

432 / 36 = 12:1, which sits at the poor end of the published 8:1 to 12:1 forage-only range.

28. If total diet crude protein is below 7.5%, the first consequence is:

Below the 7.5% floor the microbial population stalls, fibre digestion slows, and intake drops - so the problem shows as an intake problem.

29. One animal falls behind while the rest of the group holds. This most likely indicates:

A ration fault affects the whole group. An individual falling behind points to health, which raises maintenance requirement and cuts intake.

30. Why change only one thing at a time in a ration?

Simultaneous changes make the result uninterpretable, and steps larger than 10-15% of diet DM outrun microbial adaptation.

Module 3 capstone

Growing Ration Plan and 90-Day Test on your own cattle. Step 1: select five growing animals, weigh or weigh-band each one, and record body condition score on a 1-5 scale. Step 2: calculate each animal's maintenance requirement using NEm = 0.077 x BW(kg) to the power 0.75, showing the arithmetic, and set a target average daily gain you can defend on market grounds. Step 3: analyse or estimate your main forage's ADF, NDF and crude protein, convert to energy with TDN% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%) then NEg Mcal/kg = (0.0257 x TDN%) - 0.315, and estimate intake with DMI% of body weight = 120 / NDF%. Step 4: compare the energy and protein your forage can deliver at that intake against requirement, and quantify the shortfall. Step 5: cost three supplement options in USD per kg of crude protein and per Mcal of energy delivered, and choose one. Step 6: run the ration for 90 days, weighing fortnightly and writing every weight in a book. Step 7: at day 90 calculate actual average daily gain, total feed cost in USD, feed cost per kg of gain, and the value of the gain at your local live weight price - then state in one paragraph whether the ration paid, and what you would change.
