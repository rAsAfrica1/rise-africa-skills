1. Screen Printing on Glass

Screen printing is the most common way to decorate glass in small African workshops. You need a wooden or aluminium frame, a mesh screen (usually 110â160 threads per inch for glass), a squeegee, and UV-curable or air-dry glass paint. For a simple one-colour logo on 500 ml bottles, start by stretching the mesh tightly over the frame and securing it with staples or glue. Coat the screen with photo emulsion in a dark room, let it dry, then expose it to sunlight with your design printed on a transparent film for 2â5 minutes. Wash out the unexposed emulsion with a gentle spray of water. Now you have a stencil. Place the screen on a clean glass surface, apply a bead of paint across the top, and pull the squeegee firmly at a 60â70Â° angle. For glass, use a hard squeegee (70 durometer). Cure the print: if using UV paint, pass under a UV lamp for 30â60 seconds; if air-dry, let it sit for 24 hours. In Africa, many workshops use locally available screen printing supplies from Kenya, Nigeria, or South Africa. A common problem is paint bleeding under the stencilâfix it by adding more tension to the screen or using a thicker emulsion. For multi-colour prints, you need one screen per colour and must register them carefully. Start with single-colour jobs like branding on drinking glasses or window decals. Always wear gloves and work in a well-ventilated area. With practice, you can print 100â200 pieces per hour. Clean your screen immediately with the recommended solvent, or the paint will harden and ruin the mesh.

2. Digital Printing and Decals

Digital printing and decals let you put full-colour, detailed images on glass without making a screen. The two main methods are direct UV printing and water-slide decals. For direct printing, you need a UV flatbed printer (costly, but many African print shops rent time on them). You design your artwork in CorelDRAW or Photoshop, then print directly onto the glass with UV-curable ink. The printer cures the ink instantly with UV light. This works best on flat glass like windows, mirrors, or tiles. For curved or small items, use water-slide decals. You print your design on special decal paper using a normal inkjet or laser printer. Then you spray it with a clear lacquer to protect the ink. Soak the decal in water for 30â60 seconds, slide it onto the clean glass, and smooth out air bubbles with a soft squeegee. Let it dry for 24 hours, then bake in an oven at 150Â°C for 30 minutes to make it dishwasher-safe. In Africa, decals are popular for personalised gifts like mugs, bottles, and awards. You can buy decal paper from online suppliers or local ceramic shops. A key tip: clean the glass with alcohol before applying any decal, or it will peel. For small-scale production, a heat press can help adhere decals evenly. If you don't have a printer, you can order custom decals from print shops in cities like Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg. Always test on a scrap piece first. Digital printing gives you photo-quality results, but it's slower and more expensive per piece than screen printing for simple designs. Use it for short runs, complex images, or personalised items.

3. Painting and Staining

Painting and staining glass by hand is a low-cost way to add colour and design. You need glass paints (solvent-based or water-based), fine brushes, masking tape, and a clean workspace. For staining, you can use glass stain that is transparent and lets light through, or opaque paint for solid colours. Start by cleaning the glass with methylated spirits to remove any grease. If you want sharp lines, apply masking tape to create borders or patterns. For a simple stained-glass effect, use lead-free outliner to draw the design, let it dry, then fill in with glass paint. Solvent-based paints dry in 24 hours and are more durable; water-based paints are easier to clean up but need a topcoat. In Africa, many artisans use locally made glass paints from South Africa or imported brands. A common technique is 'reverse painting' on the back of glassâyou paint the details first, then the background, so the front looks perfect. For bottles or vases, you can dip them in coloured glass stain or use a sponge for a textured look. Baking the painted glass at 160Â°C for 30 minutes makes it more resistant to washing. Always work in a dust-free area; dust ruins the finish. If you make a mistake, wipe it off immediately with a solvent before it dries. For large panels, work in sections. You can also mix paint with a medium to make it flow better. Practice on small pieces first. Hand-painted glass sells well for gifts, church windows, and decorative panels. With a steady hand and a few brushes, you can create unique pieces that stand out.

4. Lamination and Coloring

Lamination and coloring involve bonding coloured interlayers between two sheets of glass, usually under heat and pressure. This is common for safety glass, but also for decorative panels. The most basic method uses EVA (ethylene vinyl acetate) film, which you can buy in rolls. You cut the film to size, place it between two clean glass sheets, and put the sandwich in a vacuum bag. Then you heat it in an oven or kiln at 120â140Â°C for 30â60 minutes. The vacuum removes air bubbles, and the EVA melts and bonds the glass. After cooling, you have a coloured, laminated glass panel. For more vibrant colours, use coloured PVB (polyvinyl butyral) interlayers, which come in many shades. These require higher pressure (10â12 bar) and temperature (140â150Â°C) in an autoclaveâexpensive equipment, so many small workshops outsource this step. In Africa, EVA lamination is popular because it's affordable and can be done with a simple vacuum bag and a kitchen oven. You can also add fabrics, leaves, or paper between the glass layers for a unique look. Always work in a clean room; any dust becomes a permanent bubble. Cut the film 2â3 mm smaller than the glass to avoid overflow. After lamination, trim excess film with a sharp blade. For coloring without lamination, you can use coloured adhesive films (window tint) or back-painted glass. Laminated glass is saferâit holds together when broken. Use it for table tops, shower screens, and decorative walls. Test your oven's temperature accuracy first; too hot and the glass may crack. Start with small samples to perfect your timing.

5. Bending and Slumping

Bending and slumping shape glass using heat in a kiln. You need a kiln that reaches at least 700Â°C, plus moulds made from stainless steel, ceramic, or fibreboard. For slumping, you place glass over a mould and heat it until it softens and sags into the shape. For bending, you heat glass until it curves, often between two supports. The process: clean your glass, apply a kiln wash (like alumina hydrate) to the mould so the glass doesn't stick, then place the glass on the mould. Heat slowlyâabout 100Â°C per hourâto 600Â°C, then faster to 700â750Â°C. Watch through the peephole: the glass will start to bend. When it reaches the desired shape, hold for 10â30 minutes, then cool slowly to anneal. Annealing is critical: cool to 500Â°C, hold for 1 hour per 6 mm of thickness, then cool at 50Â°C per hour to room temperature. If you cool too fast, the glass cracks. In Africa, small kilns are available from suppliers in South Africa or imported from China. You can make simple moulds from fire bricks or ceramic fibre board. Common items: slumped bottles for cheese boards, bent glass for curved shelving, and fused bowls. Start with 3 mm glass; thicker glass takes longer. Always wear heat-resistant gloves and safety glasses. Ventilate the kiln areaâfumes from binders or dirt can be toxic. Practice with scrap glass. Bending and slumping turn flat glass into 3D art, and there's high demand for custom pieces like sinks, lampshades, and jewellery.

6. Fusing and Kiln Forming

Fusing is melting two or more pieces of glass together in a kiln. Kiln forming covers fusing, slumping, and casting. You need a kiln, glass that is compatible (same COEâcoefficient of expansion), and a kiln shelf with kiln wash. Start with two layers of 3 mm glass, cut to shape. Clean them with glass cleaner. Stack them on the shelf. Heat the kiln at 200Â°C per hour to 750â800Â°C. At this temperature, the glass fuses into one piece. Hold for 10â20 minutes, then cool to 500Â°C and anneal for 1 hour per 6 mm thickness. Then cool slowly. For a tack fuse (textured surface), stop at 730Â°C. For a full fuse (flat, smooth), go to 800Â°C. You can add frit (crushed glass), stringers, or copper foil for designs. In Africa, fusing is growing in studios in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa. You can buy compatible glass from suppliers like Bullseye or Spectrum, but it's expensive. Some artisans use recycled bottle glass, but it often cracks because COE varies. Test small pieces first. Always keep a log of your firing schedules. A digital controller makes it easier. After fusing, you can slump the piece into a mould for a bowl. Or cast glass by filling a mould with glass and heating to 850Â°C. Safety: never open the kiln above 200Â°C; thermal shock can crack glass and burn you. Wear a respirator when handling frit. Fused glass sells well as jewellery, coasters, and wall art. With practice, you can create complex, multi-layered pieces. Start simple, and always anneal properly.

4. Lamination and Coloring

Laminated glass sandwiches a coloured or printed interlayer (PVB, EVA, or SentryGlas) between two panes under heat and pressure. In Africa this is the highest-value finishing route for facades, balustrades and transport glazing because it adds safety, UV control and colour in one operation. Capital cost is real: autoclave or vacuum-bag systems, clean room conditions, and interlayer stock held in hard currency. Partner with an established laminator for your first contracts rather than buying equipment before you have orders.

The barrier to entry is a reliable electric kiln and a stable power supply; the barrier to profit is consistency. Keep a firing log for every load: date, programme, glass COE, result, rejects.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm the client's end use (indoor/outdoor, safety-critical, food-contact) before choosing a technique.
  2. Verify glass COE and frit compatibility against the manufacturer datasheet â never mix brands blindly.

  4. Log every kiln firing: programme, peak temperature, soak, result, rejects.
  5. Cost the job with consumables + labour + kiln time + reject allowance, not just materials.
