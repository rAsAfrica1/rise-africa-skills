Lessons

1. Seed Selection and Germination

To grow a strong avocado tree, start with the best seed. Choose seeds from healthy, high-yielding local trees or from known varieties like Hass or Fuerte. Avoid seeds from fruits that are diseased or misshapen. After eating the avocado, wash the seed thoroughly to remove any flesh, as this can cause rot. Soak the seed in water for 24 hours to soften the outer skin. Then, peel off the brown skin carefully; this helps the seed germinate faster. Next, you need to germinate the seed. There are two common methods: in water or in soil. For the water method, stick three toothpicks into the sides of the seed and suspend it over a glass of water, with the pointed end up and the flat end down, just touching the water. Place it in a warm, bright spot but out of direct sunlight. Change the water every week. Roots should appear in 2-4 weeks, and a shoot in 6-8 weeks. For the soil method, fill a pot with well-draining soil mix (like 1 part sand, 1 part compost, 1 part topsoil). Plant the seed with the pointed end up, leaving the top third exposed. Water regularly to keep the soil moist but not waterlogged. Germination takes 4-8 weeks. In Africa, many farmers use the soil method because it's simpler and avoids disturbing the roots later. Keep the pots in a shaded area or nursery. Once the seedling has 2-3 leaves, it's ready for potting on or grafting. Remember, seeds from hybrid avocados won't produce the same fruit, so for consistent quality, you'll need to graft. But seed selection and germination are the first steps to getting rootstocks for grafting.

2. Grafting Methods: Whip and Cleft

Grafting joins a scion (the desired variety) to a rootstock (the seedling) so they grow as one tree. The whip (or splice) graft is best for rootstocks and scions of similar thickness (about 1-2 cm). Use a sharp, clean knife or grafting tool. Make a smooth, diagonal cut about 3-5 cm long on both the rootstock and the scion. The cuts should match perfectly. Then, make a small vertical slit at the top of the rootstock cut and a corresponding tongue on the scion to interlock. Fit them together so the cambium layers (the green layer under the bark) align on at least one side. Wrap tightly with grafting tape or polythene strip, leaving the bud exposed. The cleft graft works when the rootstock is thicker than the scion. Cut the rootstock horizontally, then make a vertical split about 5 cm deep in the center. Sharpen the scion into a wedge shape and insert it into the split, ensuring the cambium matches on one side. Wrap and seal with tape. For both methods, cover the graft with a plastic bag to maintain humidity, but remove it after 2-3 weeks when the scion shows signs of growth. Keep the grafted plant in a shaded, humid place. Success rates are higher in warm weather (20-30C). In Africa, whip grafting is common for young seedlings, while cleft grafting is used for top-working older trees. Always sterilize tools with alcohol or bleach to prevent disease. After grafting, remove any shoots from the rootstock below the graft to direct energy to the scion. With practice, you can achieve 80-90% success.

3. Budding Techniques

Budding is a form of grafting where a single bud, rather than a whole scion, is joined to the rootstock. It's useful when scion wood is scarce. The most common method for avocados is patch budding or T-budding. For T-budding, select a healthy bud from a mature branch of the desired variety. Make a T-shaped cut in the rootstock bark about 15-20 cm above the soil. Gently lift the bark flaps. Cut a bud from the scion with a small piece of wood and leaf stalk attached. Slide the bud into the T-cut and press the bark flaps back over it. Wrap with budding tape, leaving the bud exposed. After 2-3 weeks, if the bud is green and plump, it has taken. Cut the rootstock about 5 cm above the bud to force the bud to grow. Patch budding is similar but uses a rectangular patch of bark with a bud. Budding is faster and less damaging than grafting, and you can bud multiple rootstocks from one scion stick. However, it requires skill and clean tools. In Africa, budding is often done during the rainy season when the bark slips easily. Keep the budded plants in a nursery with partial shade. Remove any shoots from the rootstock below the bud. Once the bud grows into a shoot, you can train it into a new tree. Budding success rates can be 70-90% with practice. Always use disease-free budwood.

4. Rootstock Selection

The rootstock is the foundation of your avocado tree. It determines the tree's size, disease resistance, and tolerance to soil conditions. In Africa, common rootstocks include seedlings of local varieties like 'Fuerte' or 'Hass', but these may be susceptible to root rot (Phytophthora). For better resistance, use clonal rootstocks like 'Dusa' or 'Bounty', which are propagated from cuttings. However, these may not be available everywhere. If you're using seeds, choose seeds from trees that are healthy and productive. Avoid seeds from trees with root rot. The rootstock should be grown in well-draining soil to prevent waterlogging. For areas with heavy clay, consider grafting onto rootstocks that tolerate wet feet, such as 'Duke 7'. Also, consider the soil pH: avocados prefer 5.5-6.5. If your soil is too alkaline, you may need to add organic matter. In Africa, many smallholders use seedling rootstocks because they are cheap and easy. But for commercial orchards, clonal rootstocks are worth the investment. When selecting rootstocks, also consider the size: dwarfing rootstocks like 'Dusa' can keep trees small for high-density planting. Always source rootstock seeds from reputable suppliers or your own best trees. Test for root rot by planting in a small trial area. Remember, the rootstock is the base; a good one ensures a long-lived, productive tree.

5. Care of Nursery Seedlings

Nursery seedlings need consistent care to grow strong and healthy. Water them regularly, but don't overwateravocado roots rot easily. In hot African climates, water once or twice daily in the morning and evening, but reduce if it rains. Use a watering can with a fine rose to avoid disturbing the soil. Fertilize with a balanced liquid fertilizer (like NPK 20-20-20) diluted to half strength every two weeks. Alternatively, use compost tea. Keep the nursery area free of weeds, as they compete for nutrients. Watch for pests like aphids, whiteflies, and thrips. Spray with neem oil or insecticidal soap if needed. For fungal diseases, ensure good air circulation and avoid overhead watering. If you see yellowing leaves, it could be a nutrient deficiency or root rot. Adjust care accordingly. Seedlings should be potted into larger bags as they grow, usually when they are 15-20 cm tall. Handle roots carefully to avoid damage. Provide shade for young seedlings (50% shade cloth) but gradually increase sunlight as they mature. In Africa, many nurseries use simple shade nets made from local materials. Keep records of planting dates and treatments. Healthy nursery seedlings are ready for grafting when they are pencil-thick (about 1 cm diameter), usually 6-12 months after sowing. Regular care ensures high grafting success.

6. Hardening Off and Transplanting

Hardening off prepares nursery seedlings for the harsh conditions of the field. Start 2-3 weeks before transplanting. Reduce watering gradually, but don't let them wilt. Increase sunlight exposure by removing shade cloth for a few hours each day, starting with 2 hours and increasing to full sun. This toughens the leaves and stems. Also, reduce fertilizer to slow growth and encourage root development. Stop watering a day before transplanting to make the root ball hold together. Choose a cloudy day or late afternoon for transplanting to reduce stress. Dig a hole twice the size of the root ball. Mix the soil with compost and a handful of rock phosphate. Gently remove the seedling from its bag, keeping the root ball intact. Place it in the hole so the graft union is 15-20 cm above the soil. Backfill and firm the soil, then water thoroughly. Create a basin around the tree to hold water. Mulch with dry grass or leaves to conserve moisture and suppress weeds. In Africa, transplanting is best at the start of the rainy season. Protect young trees from animals with a fence or thorny branches. Water every 2-3 days for the first month, then weekly. Watch for transplant shock: wilting, yellow leaves. If it occurs, provide shade and water. With proper hardening, survival rates can be over 90%.

<h2>Practical Reference: Avocado Propagation Techniques</h2>

<p>Avocado (<em>Persea americana</em>) is one of Africa's fastest-growing horticultural export crops. Kenya, Tanzania, Ethiopia, South Africa, Rwanda, and Uganda all now compete for the same European, Middle Eastern, and increasingly Asian markets. For an African agribusiness, the single biggest determinant of orchard profitability is <strong>the genetic quality and uniformity of planting material</strong>. A grafted Hass tree on a well-chosen rootstock will out-yield and out-earn a seedling tree by a factor of three to five over a 15-year orchard life. This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into one operational document you can print, laminate, and keep in the nursery shed.</p>

<p>Treat propagation as a business process, not a gardening hobby. Every failed graft is a lost sale; every mislabeled seedling is a reputational risk. The numbers below are drawn from African nursery economics and peer-reviewed horticultural data, and are designed to be adapted to your own cost structure.</p>

<h3>1. Seed Selection and Germination Commercial Reality</h3>
<p>In Africa, most nurseries use open-pollinated seed from local 'Fuerte' or 'Puebla' type trees because it is cheap and locally available. That is acceptable <em>only</em> if the seed is destined for rootstock, never for a fruit-bearing orchard. Mexican and Guatemalan race seedlings are the standard rootstocks in Kenya, Tanzania, and Rwanda because they tolerate <em>Phytophthora cinnamomi</em> root rot better than West Indian types.</p>

<p>Select heavy, well-filled seeds from mature fruit. Sow within 57 days of extraction; viability drops sharply after two weeks in ambient conditions. Plant the seed pointed end up, 23 cm deep in a free-draining medium (forest soil : sand : compost 2:1:1). Germination takes 36 weeks at 2028 C. A nursery producing 10,000 grafted trees per year needs roughly 14,000 seedlings to cover losses budget for a 30% attrition rate.</p>

<h3>2. Grafting Methods: Whip and Cleft</h3>
<p><strong>Whip-and-tongue grafting</strong> is the industry standard for avocado because scion and rootstock are matched in diameter (812 mm). Perform it when the rootstock is 610 months old and the scion wood is dormant, collected from the current season's growth of a proven mother tree. Success rates of 7590% are achievable in a clean nursery. The diagram above shows the cuts.</p>

<p><strong>Cleft grafting</strong> is used when the rootstock is significantly thicker than the scion (e.g., top-working a mature tree, or using a 2-year-old rootstock). Split the rootstock 35 cm, insert two wedge-cut scions, and seal with grafting wax. Cleft grafting is slower per tree but rescues valuable rootstock that is too large for whip grafting.</p>

<h3>3. Budding Techniques</h3>
<p>Patch budding and chip budding are used in commercial avocado nurseries in South Africa and Israel for high-volume propagation. A single bud from a certified mother tree is inserted under a T-cut or into a chip removed from the rootstock. Budding uses less scion wood than grafting a critical advantage when mother-tree material is scarce or expensive. Expect 6080% take under humid, shaded nursery conditions.</p>

<h3>5. Care of Nursery Seedlings</h3>
<p>A commercial nursery is a factory. Keep it clean, shaded, and consistent. Key parameters: 3050% shade cloth for the first 4 weeks after grafting, then harden to full sun; irrigation every 23 days to field capacity; weekly scouting for thrips, mites, and <em>Phytophthora</em>; fortnightly foliar feed (NPK 20-10-10 at 2 g/L). Label every batch with mother-tree ID, graft date, and rootstock traceability is a market requirement for export certification.</p>

<h3>6. Hardening Off and Transplanting</h3>
<p>Hardening off is the difference between a 95% and a 60% field survival rate. Reduce shade and irrigation frequency over 34 weeks before dispatch. The tree is ready when the graft union is fully callused, the scion has at least two hardened flushes, and the root ball holds together. Transplant at the start of the long rains, in the late afternoon or on an overcast day. Water immediately and mulch 10 cm from the trunk.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING Grafting Knife and Chemical Handling</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#333;">Grafting knives are razor-sharp and cause serious lacerations. Always cut <em>away</em> from your body, never toward your hand. Wear a cut-resistant glove on the holding hand. Keep a first-aid kit with sterile dressings and a tourniquet in the nursery. Disinfect blades between mother trees with 70% alcohol or 10% bleach to prevent the spread of <em>Phytophthora</em> and avocado sunblotch viroid. When mixing bleach, fungicides, or rooting hormones, wear nitrile gloves, eye protection, and a dust mask, and work in a ventilated area. Never store grafting tape, wax, or chemicals near food or drinking water.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Seed source verified:</strong> heavy, disease-free seed from identified mother trees; sown within 7 days.</li>
<li><strong>Rootstock matched to site:</strong> Duke 7 / Thomas for high-rainfall highlands; Nabal for irrigated semi-arid; West Indian only for hot lowlands.</li>
<li><strong>Scion wood certified:</strong> collected from a proven, high-yielding Hass or Fuerte mother tree; labelled by tree ID.</li>
<li><strong>Graft union clean:</strong> whip graft performed on 812 mm stock, tongues interlocked, taped and sealed within 5 minutes.</li>
<li><strong>Nursery hygiene:</strong> 3050% shade, weekly scouting, fortnightly foliar feed, blade disinfection between trees.</li>
<li><strong>Traceability records:</strong> batch number, mother-tree ID, graft date, rootstock type written on every label.</li>
<li><strong>Hardening off 34 weeks:</strong> gradual shade and water reduction; two hardened flushes before dispatch.</li>
<li><strong>Transplant timing:</strong> start of long rains, late afternoon, immediate watering, 10 cm mulch ring.</li>
</ol>
