Photo and Video for Real Clients - What People Actually Pay For

🧠 Brain Opener

A client does not pay for drone photos. They pay for the result the photos produce. A restaurant owner pays for photos that make people hungry. A farmer pays for photos that show which rows need water. Before you fly, know what the deliverable is for.

  • What is the difference between a drone photo and a drone deliverable?
  • What do clients actually pay for?
  • What are the three most common drone video jobs?
🎁 Reveal a good answer

A drone photo is a file. A deliverable is a finished thing: a 30-second video with music, a printed map, a set of twenty photos with captions. Clients pay for deliverables. The three most common jobs are real-estate tours, event coverage, and agricultural surveys.

📖 Farai Shoots the Wedding

Farai was seventeen and had a drone. His cousin was getting married in Mutare. The family asked if he could "take some photos from above."

Farai said yes immediately. Then he thought about it. He had never shot a wedding. He had never planned a shoot. He had only flown for fun.

He called a friend who worked for a production company in Harare. The friend gave him three pieces of advice.

First: know what the couple actually wants. Not "drone photos." What specific moments. The arrival. The vows. The first dance. The send-off. Maybe five key moments total.

Second: plan each shot. Altitude, angle, distance. For the arrival, fly high and pull back to show the whole venue. For the vows, fly lower and close for the faces. For the first dance, hover overhead and drift slowly.

Third: watch the battery. A wedding is long. You cannot fly for three hours. Choose the moments, fly each for two minutes, land, wait. Four flights, eight minutes total.

Farai did this. He wrote down five shots. He flew five flights. He took forty photos and thirty seconds of video.

He delivered one 45-second video and ten edited photos. The couple was delighted. They paid him fifty dollars. Two of their friends asked him for their weddings.

Farai learned the lesson. He had not become a better photographer. He had learned to plan the deliverable, not just fly the drone.

💡 The Big Idea

Plan the deliverable, not the flight

Every drone job starts with the deliverable. What specific output does the client need? What is it for? How will they use it? Only then do you plan the flights. The flight is the means. The deliverable is the end.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

The three main drone jobs in Africa

Real estate: photos and a 30 to 60 second walkthrough video of a property, land, or development. Typical price: 50 to 200 dollars per property.

Events: weddings, church services, sports, festivals. A 60 to 90 second highlight video plus a set of photos. Typical price: 100 to 400 dollars per event.

Agriculture: crop mapping, irrigation checking, livestock counting. A stitched map plus a report on findings. Typical price: 50 to 300 dollars per farm, depending on size.

2

How to plan a shoot

Write the shot list first. For real estate: wide establishing shot, then rooms from above, then the boundary line, then the surrounding area. For events: arrival, ceremony, key moments, send-off. For agriculture: grid lines across the field with 70 percent overlap.

For each shot, write four things: altitude, distance, angle, and duration. A five-shot list is enough for most jobs. Ten is plenty. Twenty is too many - you will miss the important ones.

3

Editing and delivery

Editing is where the difference between amateur and professional shows. Crop, straighten, adjust colour, and add music. For video, keep it short - 45 to 90 seconds - and cut on beats.

Deliver in a format the client can use. WhatsApp if small, WeTransfer if large, USB stick if the client is not online. Always include one high-resolution version for printing and one small version for WhatsApp and social media.

🛠 Hands-On - Plan and Shoot a Five-Shot Set

You need

  • A drone (or a phone as a stand-in)
  • A notebook
  • Five specific shots to capture
  1. Pick a subject - a house, a tree, a road, a person.
  2. Write down five specific shots you want.
  3. For each, write altitude, distance, angle, duration.
  4. Shoot them in order. Do not improvise.
  5. Review every shot before you leave the site.
  6. Reshoot any that did not work.
  7. Edit the best five into one coherent set.
  8. Show them to someone and ask: does this tell a story?
Why it works: Five well-planned shots beat twenty random ones. The discipline is the skill.

🎮 Game - Shot List Race

2 to 10 players - 20 min

The teacher gives the same brief: shoot a 30-second video tour of the school. Every player has 15 minutes to write a shot list of eight shots, each with altitude, distance, angle, duration. Then the class votes on the shot list most likely to produce a usable video. Winner explains why they chose their shots.

🤖 AI-Era Skill

AI can help plan a shot list. But you must know the subject first.

Try this prompt: "I am shooting a 30-second video tour of a small restaurant for a client. What are the eight most important shots, and for each one, what altitude, angle, and duration should I use?"

Check the answer against the deliverable. A good restaurant video shows: exterior, entrance, dining area from above, close-ups of food, the chef, happy customers, the bathroom (people care), the sign. If AI missed any, add them.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Cape Town property videos

Cape Town real estate agents pay 150 to 500 dollars for a drone video per property. The videos are 45 to 90 seconds, shot in the golden hour, and edited with a slow ambient soundtrack. The formula is standard, which is why it works.

Nairobi wedding videographers

A Nairobi drone videographer shoots 30 weddings a year at 300 dollars each. His shot list is the same for every wedding: arrival, ceremony, vows, first dance, send-off. The formula lets him shoot fast and deliver reliably.

🏆 Project Challenge

Shoot a Real Client Job

Shoot a five-shot set for a real person or business.

  1. Find a real client - a family member business, a neighbour house, a school event.
  2. Ask them what they actually need. A video? Photos? A map?
  3. Write a five-shot plan. Include altitude, distance, angle, duration.
  4. Shoot the plan. Do not improvise.
  5. Edit and deliver within three days.
  6. Ask for one piece of feedback. Adjust for next time.
  7. Write 3 sentences about what worked.
