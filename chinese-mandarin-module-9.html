Lessons

1. Body Parts and Common Ailments

Start by learning the core body parts in Mandarin: (tu) head, (ynjing) eyes, (rduo) ears, (bzi) nose, (zuba) mouth, (shu) hand, (jio) foot, (dzi) belly, (bi) back, (hulng) throat. Practice pointing to each part and saying the word aloud. In many African clinics, you may need to describe pain without a translator, so these words are your first tool.

Common ailments: (fsho) fever, (ksu) cough, (tutng) headache, (witng) stomachache, (fxi) diarrhea, (nj) malaria, (gnm o) cold/flu, (gumn) allergy. For malaria, say (w d le nj) I have malaria. In Africa, malaria is common, so this phrase is vital. Also learn (shushng) injured, (lixu) bleeding.

Useful phrases: (w tu tng) My head hurts. (w dzi tng) My stomach hurts. (w fsho le) I have a fever. (w ksu) I have a cough. To ask someone else: (n nl b shfu?) Where are you uncomfortable? Respond: (w zhl tng) It hurts here.

Practice with a partner: one points to a body part, the other says the Mandarin word and a sentence. For example, point to your head and say . Do this 10 minutes daily. Use flashcards with pictures. If you have a smartphone, use apps like Pleco or Anki to review. In an emergency, clear pronunciation matters more than perfect grammar. Speak slowly and repeat if needed. Remember: (tng) means pain, (tng) also means pain but is slightly more formal. Both are understood.

2. Describing Symptoms to a Doctor

When you see a doctor in a Chinese-speaking context, start with a polite greeting: (yshng, n ho) Doctor, hello. Then say (w b shfu) I am not feeling well. The doctor may ask (n znme le?) What's wrong? Or (nl b shfu?) Where are you uncomfortable?

Describe your main symptom first. Use + symptom + . For example: (w fsho le) I have a fever. (w ksu le) I have a cough. (w tu tng le) I have a headache. If you have multiple symptoms, use (hi yu) also have. Example: (w fsho, hi yu ksu) I have a fever and also a cough.

Mention duration: (ling tin le) for two days. (cng zutin kish) since yesterday. (yzh) continuously. Example: (w dzi tng, ling tin le) My stomach hurts for two days. If you have a pre-existing condition like diabetes or HIV, say (w yu tngni obng) I have diabetes. (w yu izbng) I have HIV/AIDS. This helps the doctor prescribe safely.

Be ready to answer: (n ch gu y o ma?) Have you taken medicine? Respond: (miyu) No, or (ch le) Yes, I took. (du shnme gumn ma?) Are you allergic to anything? Say (w du qngmis gumn) I am allergic to penicillin. If you don't know, say (b zhd o).

Finally, ask questions: (ynzhng ma?) Is it serious? (xy o dzhn ma?) Do I need an injection? (ch shnme y o?) What medicine should I take? Practice these phrases with a friend. In rural Africa, you might see a Chinese doctor at a clinic; these phrases will help you get proper care.

3. At the Pharmacy: Buying Medicine

In a Chinese pharmacy, called (y odi n), you can buy many medicines without a prescription. Start by saying (n ho, w y o mi y o) Hello, I want to buy medicine. The pharmacist may ask (n nl b shfu?) Where are you uncomfortable? Describe your symptoms as in Lesson 2.

Common medicines: (tusho y o) fever reducer, (zhk y o) cough medicine, (zhtng y o) painkiller, (k ngshngs) antibiotics, (k ngn y o) antimalarial, (wishngs) vitamins, (chungkti) band-aid, (xiodshu) disinfectant. For diarrhea: (zhxi y o). For allergies: (k nggumn y o).

Ask for specific medicine: (w y o y h tusho y o) I want a box of fever reducer. (y h) means one box. (y png) one bottle. (y bn) one blister pack. (dushao pi n?) How many tablets? The pharmacist might say (y tin sn c, y c y pi n) Three times a day, one tablet each time. (f n hu ch) Take after meals. (du h shu) Drink plenty of water.

If you need a prescription, say (w yu chfng) I have a prescription. Or (w xy o chfng y o) I need prescription medicine. Ask about price: (dushao qin?) How much? If too expensive, say (t i gu le) Too expensive. (yu pinyi de ma?) Do you have a cheaper one?

In Africa, counterfeit drugs are a risk. Buy from reputable pharmacies. Check expiry date: (yuxi oq). Ask (zhge guq le ma?) Is this expired? Always get a receipt: (shuj). Practice a dialogue: You: . Pharmacist: . You: Pharmacist: 20. You: . Do this until you are comfortable.

4. Emergency Phrases: Help, Police, Ambulance

In an emergency, every second counts. Learn these phrases cold. (jimng!) Help! (literally 'save life'). (bng bng w!) Help me! (ku i ji o jngch!) Call the police quickly! (ku i ji o jihch!) Call an ambulance quickly! (zhohu le!) Fire! (yu xiotu!) Thief! (w ml le) I am lost.

Emergency numbers in China: 110 for police, 120 for ambulance, 119 for fire, 122 for traffic accidents. In many African countries, numbers differ: in Kenya, 999 or 112; in Nigeria, 112; in South Africa, 10111 for police, 10177 for ambulance. But if you are in a Chinese-speaking area, use 110, 120, 119. Memorize them.

When calling, speak slowly and clearly. Say: (w xy o bngzh) I need help. [] (w z i [ddin]) I am at [location]. Learn to give your location: (w z i jchng l) I am on Airport Road. (w z i yyu n fjn) I am near the hospital. ... (w de dzh sh...) My address is...

If someone is injured: (t shushng le) He is injured. (t lixu le) She is bleeding. (t hnm le) He is unconscious. (t b nng hx) He cannot breathe. (t yu xnz ngbng) He has a heart condition.

Practice shouting these phrases. In a crisis, you may forget grammar, so repeat the key words: Also, carry a card with your emergency contacts and medical info in Chinese. If you are in Africa, know local emergency numbers and how to say 'help' in local languages too. But for Chinese speakers, these phrases are your lifeline. Role-play with a friend: one pretends to be hurt, the other calls for help. Do it until it's automatic.

5. Making a Doctor's Appointment

In many Chinese-speaking countries, you can book a doctor's appointment by phone or online. Start with: (n ho, w xing yyu k n yshng) Hello, I want to make an appointment to see a doctor. The receptionist may ask: (n nl b shfu?) What's wrong? (n xing k n nge k?) Which department do you want to see? Common departments: (nik) internal medicine, (w ik) surgery, (rk) pediatrics, (fk) gynecology, (yk) dentistry, (jzhn) emergency.

Give your name and phone number: ... (w ji o...) My name is... ... (w de di nhu sh...) My phone number is... Ask for a time: (mngtin sh ngw yu kng ma?) Are you free tomorrow morning? (xi w) afternoon. (j din?) What time? The receptionist may say: (sh ngw ji din) 9 AM. (xi w sn din) 3 PM. Confirm: (ho de, w hu zhnsh d o) Okay, I will arrive on time.

If you need to cancel or reschedule: (w xing qxio yyu) I want to cancel the appointment. (w xing gi shjin) I want to change the time. (ky gi d o hutin ma?) Can we change to the day after tomorrow?

In Africa, some private clinics allow walk-ins, but booking saves time. If you are in a Chinese-run clinic, use these phrases. Also learn to ask: (xy o d i shnme?) What should I bring? (y o d i shnfnzhng ma?) Should I bring ID? (y o d i boxin k ma?) Should I bring insurance card? Practice a phone call with a friend. Write down your appointment details: date, time, department, doctor's name. Arrive 10 minutes early. Say (n ho, w yu yyu) Hello, I have an appointment.

6. Role-Play: Visiting a Clinic

This lesson puts everything together. You will role-play a visit to a clinic in a Chinese-speaking setting. First, at reception: (n ho, w yu yyu) Hello, I have an appointment. If no appointment: (w xing k n yshng) I want to see a doctor. The receptionist asks: (n nl b shfu?) Where are you uncomfortable? You: (w fsho, hi yu ksu) I have a fever and a cough. (ling tin le) For two days.

They may ask you to fill a form. Learn: (xngmng) name, (ninlng) age, (xngbi) gender, (dzh) address, (di nhu ) phone. If you don't understand, say (qng shu m n ydin) Please speak slower. (w b dng) I don't understand. (qng xi xi li) Please write it down.

Then see the doctor. Doctor: You: (w tu tng, fsho, hi yu qunshn tng) I have a headache, fever, and body aches. Doctor: You: (miyu). Doctor: You: (w du qngmis gumn). Doctor: (n xy o dzhn) You need an injection. You: (ynzhng ma?) Is it serious? Doctor: (b ynzhng, d n xy o xixi) Not serious, but you need rest.

After seeing the doctor, go to the pharmacy: (w y o n y o) I want to get medicine. Pharmacist: (y tin sn c, y c y pi n, f n hu ch) Three times a day, one tablet, after meals. You: (dushao qin?) Pharmacist: 50. You: .

Practice this entire role-play with a friend. Take turns being the patient, receptionist, doctor, and pharmacist. Use props like a toy stethoscope or a notebook for prescriptions. Do it three times until you can do it without notes. In real life, stay calm, speak clearly, and don't be afraid to ask for repetition. This will help you get proper care in any Chinese-speaking clinic in Africa or beyond.

<h2>Practical Reference: Health and Emergencies in Mandarin for African Agribusiness Professionals</h2>

<p>For African agribusiness owners, farm managers, traders, and exporters working with Chinese partners, suppliers, and buyers, health-related Mandarin is not a "nice to have" it is a business continuity tool. A delayed shipment because a farm manager cannot explain a fever at a Guangzhou clinic, a supplier meeting missed because nobody could call an ambulance, or a phytosanitary inspection postponed because a team member could not describe a rash at a pharmacy: these are real commercial losses. This Practical Reference lesson equips you with the vocabulary, sentence patterns, and cultural protocols to handle health and emergencies in Mandarin while protecting your operations, your staff, and your margins.</p>

<p>The module covers six lessons: <strong>Body Parts and Common Ailments</strong> | <strong>Describing Symptoms to a Doctor</strong> | <strong>At the Pharmacy: Buying Medicine</strong> | <strong>Emergency Phrases: Help, Police, Ambulance</strong> | <strong>Making a Doctor's Appointment</strong> | <strong>Role-Play: Visiting a Clinic</strong>. This reference sheet consolidates the highest-value language for business travel and on-site management in China, as well as for managing Chinese-speaking staff or partners visiting your operations in Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania, or South Africa.</p>

<p>Start with the parts of the body most relevant to workplace injuries and common travel illnesses. In agribusiness, cuts from machinery, back strain from lifting, dehydration, and food-borne illness are the most frequent incidents.</p>

<h2>2. Describing Symptoms to a Doctor</h2>

<p>The fastest way to lose a day of supplier meetings is to arrive at a clinic unable to explain what is wrong. Use the pattern <strong> + [body part] + [symptom]</strong> (w + ... + ...). For example: <strong></strong> (w dzi tng) "My stomach hurts." Add duration with <strong>...</strong> (yjng... le): <strong></strong> (w yjng fsho ling tin le) "I have had a fever for two days."</p>

<p>Key questions a doctor will ask, and how to answer:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong> </strong> (n nl b shfu?) Where does it hurt? <strong></strong> (w tu tng) My head hurts.</li>
<li><strong></strong> (duji le?) How long? <strong></strong> (sn tin le) Three days.</li>
<li><strong></strong> (yu miyu gumn?) Any allergies? <strong> </strong> (w du qngmis gumn) I am allergic to penicillin.</li>
<li><strong></strong> (chgu y o ma?) Have you taken medicine? <strong></strong> (miyu) No.</li>
</ul>

<p>For business travelers, always carry a written list of your medications and allergies in Chinese. If you are managing Chinese-speaking staff on your farm, keep a translated first-aid card in the site office.</p>

<h2>3. At the Pharmacy: Buying Medicine</h2>

<p>Chinese pharmacies (<strong></strong>, y odi n) are common and efficient. Prescription drugs (<strong></strong>, chfngy o) require a doctor's note; over-the-counter drugs (<strong></strong>, fi chfngy o) do not. Useful phrases:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong></strong> (w y o mi gnm o y o) I want to buy cold medicine.</li>
<li><strong></strong> (zhge y o znme ch?) How do I take this medicine?</li>
<li><strong> </strong> (y tin j c?) How many times a day?</li>
<li><strong></strong> (yu fzuyng ma?) Are there side effects?</li>
<li><strong></strong> (ky ki fpi o ma?) Can I get a receipt/invoice? (critical for business expense claims)</li>
</ul>

<h2>4. Emergency Phrases: Help, Police, Ambulance</h2>

<p>In an emergency, seconds matter. Memorize these before you travel or host Chinese partners:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong></strong> (jimng!) Help! (life-threatening)</li>
<li><strong></strong> (qng bngbng w!) Please help me!</li>
<li><strong></strong> (ji o jihch!) Call an ambulance!</li>
<li><strong></strong> (ji o jngch!) Call the police!</li>
<li><strong></strong> (w shushng le.) I am injured.</li>
<li><strong></strong> (w dile hzh o.) I lost my passport.</li>
<li><strong></strong> (zujn de yyu n z i nl?) Where is the nearest hospital?</li>
</ul>

<p>Emergency numbers in China: <strong>120</strong> (ambulance), <strong>110</strong> (police), <strong>119</strong> (fire). In Kenya: <strong>999</strong> or <strong>112</strong>. In Nigeria: <strong>112</strong>. In South Africa: <strong>10177</strong> (ambulance). Save these in your phone and in your team's WhatsApp group.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;"><strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Emergency and Medical Protocol</strong></p>
<p style="margin:0;">Never self-diagnose or self-medicate for malaria, typhoid, or severe infections based on Mandarin phrases alone. For agribusiness sites, ensure at least two staff members are trained in basic first aid and can communicate emergency information in the local language and Mandarin if Chinese partners are on site.</p>

<h2>5. Making a Doctor's Appointment</h2>

<p>In China, appointments can be made via hospital apps (e.g., WeChat mini-programs), phone, or in person. Useful phrases:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong></strong> (w xing yyu k n yshng.) I would like to make an appointment.</li>
<li><strong></strong> (mngtin sh ngw yu kng ma?) Are you free tomorrow morning?</li>
<li><strong>/</strong> (w xy o k n nik/w ik.) I need internal medicine/surgery.</li>
<li><strong></strong> (ky gi shjin ma?) Can I reschedule?</li>
</ul>

<p>For business travelers, book appointments early in the morning to preserve the rest of the day for meetings. Many international clinics in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen offer English-speaking staff, but knowing these phrases ensures you are never stranded.</p>

<h2>6. Role-Play: Visiting a Clinic Business Scenario</h2>

<p><strong>Scenario:</strong> You are a Kenyan agribusiness exporter in Guangzhou for a trade fair. You wake up with fever and stomach pain. You visit a clinic.</p>

<p><strong>You:</strong> (Nho, w dzi tng, hi fsho.) Hello, my stomach hurts and I have a fever.<br>
<strong>Doctor:</strong> (Duji le?) How long?<br>
<strong>You:</strong> (Yjng ling tin le.) Two days.<br>
<strong>Doctor:</strong> (Chgu shnme?) What did you eat?<br>
<strong>You:</strong> (Zutin chle hixin.) I ate seafood yesterday.<br>
<strong>Doctor:</strong> (Knng sh shw zhngd. Xy o dzhn.) Possibly food poisoning. You need an injection.<br>
<strong>You:</strong> (Ho de, ky ki fpi o ma?) Okay, can I get a receipt?<br>
<strong>Doctor:</strong> (Ky.) Yes.</p>

<p>This scenario shows how language directly protects your business: you get treated faster, you obtain documentation for insurance, and you return to negotiations sooner.</p>
