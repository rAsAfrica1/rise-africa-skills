Lessons

1. Assessing Local Demand: What Do Farmers, Builders, and Processors Need?

Before you buy any machine, you must know who will pay to use it. Start by walking through your local area and talking to at least 20 potential customers. For farmers, ask what tasks take the most time and labour. In many African communities, maize shelling, cassava grinding, and groundnut oil pressing are top needs. A farmer with 2 hectares of maize may spend 10 days shelling by hand; a small maize sheller can do that in 2 hours. That saves 9 days of labour, which is worth about 50,000 to 100,000 shillings depending on local wages. For builders, demand often centres on block-making, concrete mixing, and cutting iron sheets. A manual block-making machine can produce 500 blocks per day, while a motorised one can do 1,500. Check how many building projects are within 20 km of your location. For processors, look at what raw materials are abundant. If there is a lot of cassava, a cassava grater and press are needed. If there are many oilseeds, an oil expeller is profitable. Visit local markets and ask traders what products they buy from outside the area. That tells you what processing is missing. Also check existing machines: if three people already own a maize mill, the market may be saturated. A simple demand survey tool is a notebook and pen. Record names, phone numbers, and specific needs. After 20 interviews, you will see a pattern. Choose the machine that solves the most common, most painful problem. That is your best bet for steady income.

2. The 10 Most Profitable Small Machines in Africa

Based on real earnings across Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, and Tanzania, these 10 small Chinese machines consistently make money. 1) Maize sheller: costs $200-$500, shells 1-2 tonnes per hour. Charge $5 per 50kg bag; 20 bags a day = $100. 2) Cassava grater: $300-$600, grates 500kg per hour. Custom grating earns $0.05 per kg; 2 tonnes daily = $100. 3) Groundnut oil expeller: $500-$1,200, presses 50kg per hour. Oil sells for $2-$3 per litre; 20 litres per day = $40-$60 profit. 4) Rice mill: $800-$2,000, mills 500kg per hour. Milling fee $0.03 per kg; 2 tonnes = $60. 5) Block-making machine: manual $400, motorised $1,500. Manual makes 500 blocks per day; each block sells for $0.30 = $150. 6) Concrete mixer: $300-$800, mixes 200 litres per batch. Rent for $20 per day. 7) Welding machine: $150-$400, charges $5-$10 per job. 8) Water pump: $200-$500, irrigates 1 acre per day. Rent for $10-$15 daily. 9) Poultry feed pellet machine: $400-$900, makes 100kg per hour. Sell pellets at $0.50 per kg = $50 per hour. 10) Cassava press: $300-$700, presses 200kg per hour. Charge $0.10 per kg = $20 per hour. These numbers assume you find customers. Always start with the machine that matches your local raw materials and skills. For example, if you are near a lake, a water pump for irrigation is gold. If you are in a maize belt, a sheller is safer. The key is not the machine alone but the demand you can serve.

3. Matching Machine Capacity to Your Market Size

Buying a machine that is too big wastes money; too small loses customers. First, estimate your daily demand. Count how many farmers, builders, or processors need your service within a 10 km radius. For example, if 50 farmers each have 1 hectare of maize, that is 50 hectares. A hectare yields about 2 tonnes of maize. Total 100 tonnes. Shelling season lasts 3 weeks (21 days). So you need to shell 100,000 kg / 21 days = 4,762 kg per day. A small sheller does 1,000 kg per hour. You need 5 hours of work per day. That is manageable. If you buy a machine that does 5,000 kg per hour, you will only work 1 hour per day and waste fuel and capital. Second, check your power source. Chinese machines often come with diesel or electric motors. A 5 HP diesel engine uses about 1 litre per hour. If you run 5 hours, that is 5 litres = $5. Electric motors are cheaper per hour but need reliable power. Third, consider transport. If your customers are far, you may need to move the machine. A small, portable machine (under 100 kg) is better than a heavy one. Fourth, think about growth. If you expect demand to double in 2 years, buy a machine that can be upgraded or run at higher speed. But do not overbuy. A good rule: buy a machine that can handle 120% of your current peak daily demand. That gives room to grow without sitting idle. Always ask the seller for the machine's capacity in kg per hour or blocks per day. If they cannot tell you, walk away. Real numbers matter.

4. New vs. Refurbished: Pros and Cons

New Chinese machines come with a warranty, usually 6 to 12 months. They are cleaner, more reliable, and often have better fuel efficiency. But they cost 30% to 50% more than refurbished ones. For example, a new maize sheller might cost $500, while a refurbished one is $300. Refurbished machines are used machines that have been repaired and repainted. They can work well if the repair was done properly. However, you must inspect them carefully. Check for cracks in the frame, worn bearings, and rust in the engine. Ask for a demonstration with actual maize or cassava. Listen for unusual noises. A good refurbished machine can last 2-3 years, while a new one can last 5-7 years. If you have limited capital and a reliable mechanic nearby, refurbished can be a good start. But if you need the machine for daily income and cannot afford downtime, buy new. Also consider spare parts: new machines often use standard parts that are easy to find. Refurbished machines may have modified parts that are hard to replace. One smart strategy: buy a new machine for your main income earner, and a refurbished one for backup or for less critical tasks. Another factor is resale value. New machines hold value better. If you plan to upgrade in 2 years, new is better. If you plan to use it until it dies, refurbished is fine. Always get a receipt and a written guarantee, even for refurbished. Test the machine under load for at least 30 minutes before paying. Never buy without seeing it run.

5. The Importance of Spare Parts Availability

A machine without spare parts is a pile of metal. Before you buy any Chinese machine, find out where you can buy parts. Ask the seller: Where is your nearest parts dealer? How long does it take to order a replacement? What are the most common parts that break? For maize shellers, the rubber rollers and sieves wear out. For oil expellers, the pressing screws and rings wear. For block-making machines, the moulds and bearings wear. If you cannot find these parts locally, you may wait weeks for a shipment from China. That means lost income. A simple rule: only buy a machine if you can find at least two suppliers of its spare parts within 100 km. If not, consider a different brand or model. Also, learn to do basic maintenance yourself. Change oil every 100 hours of use. Grease bearings every 50 hours. Keep the machine clean. A well-maintained machine breaks less often. But even with care, parts wear out. So budget 10% of the machine's cost per year for spare parts. For a $500 machine, set aside $50 per year. That is $4 per month. When a part breaks, you can fix it immediately. Another tip: buy a machine that uses common parts. For example, many Chinese diesel engines use standard oil filters that are also used in motorcycles. That makes them easy to find. Avoid machines with unique, proprietary parts. Ask other owners in your area what they use and where they buy parts. Their experience is your best guide.

6. When a Small Machine Beats a Large One

Bigger is not always better. In many African markets, a small machine outperforms a large one. Here is why. First, small machines cost less to buy and maintain. A small maize sheller at $300 can be paid off in 2 months. A large one at $3,000 might take 2 years. Second, small machines are easier to move. If your customers are scattered, you can carry a 50 kg machine on a motorcycle. A 500 kg machine needs a truck. Third, small machines use less fuel. A 3 HP engine uses 0.5 litres per hour; a 10 HP engine uses 2 litres per hour. If you only have 2 hours of work, the small machine is cheaper. Fourth, small machines are less risky. If demand drops, you can sell it easily or use it for other tasks. A large machine may sit idle. Fifth, small machines are easier to repair. You can learn to fix them yourself. Large machines need specialised mechanics. When does a large machine win? When you have a guaranteed, high-volume contract. For example, if a large farm hires you to shell 50 tonnes of maize in one week, a large machine is better. But most small businesses start with small machines and upgrade later. A good strategy: start with a small machine, serve your local market, save profits, then buy a second small machine or a larger one when demand is proven. Do not buy a large machine hoping to find customers. Buy a small machine that you know you can keep busy. That is the safest path to profit.

<h2>Practical Reference: Finding the Right Machine for Your Market</h2>

<p>Buying a small machine is a capital decision, not a shopping trip. In most African markets, a single machine can represent 618 months of an operator's disposable income, so the difference between a good and a bad purchase is often the difference between a business that compounds and one that stalls. This practical reference gives you a decision framework: assess real demand, compare machine economics, match capacity to market size, weigh new versus refurbished, and protect yourself with spare-parts planning. It is written for the operator who intends to earn money, not just own equipment.</p>

<h3>1. Start With Demand, Not With the Machine</h3>
<p>Farmers, builders, and processors buy <em>outputs</em>, not equipment. Before you choose a machine, identify who pays, how often, and at what price. A maize miller in a district with one harvest per year has a very different cash-flow profile from a miller near a trading town with year-round throughput. The same logic applies to block-making machines near a growing peri-urban corridor, or oil presses in a groundnut belt.</p>

<p>Practical demand signals to verify on the ground:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Volume:</strong> How many tonnes, blocks, or litres move through your catchment each month?</li>
<li><strong>Seasonality:</strong> Which months are peak, and how deep is the trough?</li>
<li><strong>Willingness to pay:</strong> What do customers currently pay for the same service (including manual labour)?</li>
<li><strong>Competition:</strong> How many existing machines serve the same catchment, and are they underutilised?</li>
<li><strong>Logistics:</strong> Can raw material reach you and finished product leave you at a cost that still leaves margin?</li>
</ul>

<p>Where possible, run a short trial: hire or borrow a machine for two to four weeks and record actual throughput and revenue. Trial data beats market gossip.</p>

<h3>3. Matching Capacity to Market Size</h3>
<p>Under-sizing frustrates customers; over-sizing destroys cash flow. The rule of thumb is to buy a machine whose rated capacity is 6070% of your realistic peak demand, so you have headroom without carrying idle capacity. If your peak demand is 3 tonnes/day and a mill is rated at 1 tonne/hour over an 8-hour shift, that is 8 tonnes/day over-specified unless you have storage and a second market.</p>

<h3>4. New vs Refurbished</h3>
<p>New Chinese machines are usually cheaper than equivalent Western or Indian units and increasingly reliable. Refurbished units can cut capital cost by 3050% but carry hidden risks: worn bearings, non-standard parts, and uncertain history. For a first machine, new is often safer because downtime is the biggest killer of small businesses. For a second machine in an established operation with in-house maintenance, refurbished can be a smart cash-flow play.</p>

<h3>5. Spare Parts: The Silent Decider</h3>
<p>A machine with no local spare-parts supply is a machine that stops. Before purchase, confirm: (a) which parts wear fastest (belts, bearings, screens, hammers, dies, seals); (b) whether those parts are stocked within 24 weeks of your site; (c) whether a local technician has serviced that model; and (d) whether the supplier will commit to a parts lead time in writing. If the answer to any of these is no, negotiate a spare-parts kit into the purchase price.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b23b3b; background:#fdecec; padding:14px 16px; border-radius:6px; margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#b23b3b;"> SAFETY WARNING Rotating and Belt-Driven Machinery</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Small mills, expellers, and block machines cause severe injuries when guards are removed or when operators wear loose clothing. In many African markets, second-hand machines arrive without guards, and local fabricators may not replace them. Before operating any machine: (1) ensure all belt, pulley, and feed-inlet guards are fitted; (2) install an emergency stop within reach; (3) train every operator on lock-out/tag-out before clearing jams; (4) never feed material by hand into a running mill use a pusher; and (5) keep a basic first-aid kit and fire extinguisher on site. Electrical installations must be earthed and protected by an RCD/earth-leakage breaker. Do not operate a machine that has exposed live wiring or a missing guard, regardless of deadline pressure.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. When a Small Machine Beats a Large One</h3>
<p>Bigger is not better when: demand is seasonal or fragmented; capital is scarce and interest rates are high; power supply is unreliable (small diesel units can run independently); you lack skilled maintenance staff; or you want to test a market before scaling. Two small machines also give redundancy if one breaks, you can still serve customers. In many African contexts, distributed small-scale capacity beats centralised large-scale capacity on resilience, even if it loses on unit cost at full utilisation.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Confirmed real paying demand (volume, seasonality, price) with at least 3 customers or a trial.</li>
<li>Machine rated capacity is 6070% of realistic peak demand, not nameplate maximum.</li>
<li>Total cost of ownership calculated: purchase + fuel/power + labour + maintenance + spare parts.</li>
<li>Spare-parts availability confirmed locally within 24 weeks, in writing if possible.</li>
<li>At least one local technician trained on the specific model.</li>
<li>Safety guards, emergency stop, and operator training in place before first run.</li>
<li>Financing plan stress-tested against a 30% demand shortfall in year one.</li>
<li>Exit or resale route identified (used-machine market, lease-to-own, or transfer to another site).</li>
</ol>
