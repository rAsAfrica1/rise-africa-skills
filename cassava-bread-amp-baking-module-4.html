1. Building a simple charcoal or firewood oven

To build a simple charcoal or firewood oven, you need a metal drum (like a 200-litre oil drum), sand, clay, bricks, and a metal grate. First, cut a door about 30 cm by 30 cm near the bottom of the drum. This is where you will load fuel and place bread. Inside, create a shelf using a metal grate or stones to hold the bread above the fire. Line the bottom with a layer of sand or clay to protect the metal from burning through. For the outer structure, stack bricks or stones around the drum, leaving the door open. Fill gaps with clay or mud to insulate. The chimney can be a metal pipe attached to a hole at the top of the drum, directing smoke away. To use it, burn charcoal or firewood in the bottom until the oven is hot (about 200-220C). You can test heat by sprinkling a little flour on the grate; it should brown in 30 seconds. Then place your cassava bread dough on the grate, close the door (use a metal sheet), and bake. A 500g loaf takes about 40-50 minutes. This oven costs little and uses local materials. Keep it dry and repair cracks with clay after each use. Always have a fire extinguisher or sand bucket nearby for safety.

2. Using a pot-on-pot method for baking bread

The pot-on-pot method is a simple way to bake cassava bread using two pots: a large pot with a lid (the oven) and a smaller pot or metal stand to hold the bread above the heat. You need a large cooking pot (at least 10 litres), a smaller pot or a metal trivet, and a lid that fits the large pot. First, place a layer of sand or small stones (about 2 cm deep) at the bottom of the large pot. This distributes heat evenly. Put the smaller pot or trivet on top of the sand. Preheat the large pot over a fire or charcoal stove for 10-15 minutes with the lid on. Meanwhile, shape your cassava dough into a round loaf about 5 cm thick. Once the pot is hot, carefully place the dough on the small pot or trivet. Cover with the lid. Place the whole setup over medium heat (charcoal or firewood). Bake for 30-40 minutes. To check, lift the lid after 30 minutes; the bread should be golden brown and sound hollow when tapped. If not, bake 10 more minutes. This method works because the sand insulates and the small pot raises the bread off direct heat, preventing burning. Its perfect for small-scale baking. Always use a thick cloth to handle hot pots. Clean the sand after each use and store the pots dry.

3. Baking cassava bread in a sandbox oven

A sandbox oven is a simple, effective way to bake cassava bread using a box, sand, and a heat source. You need a wooden or metal box (about 60 cm x 60 cm x 60 cm), clean sand, a metal tray or stone slab, and charcoal or firewood. First, fill the box with 10 cm of sand. Place a metal tray or flat stones on top of the sand to hold the bread. Build a fire using charcoal in a metal container (like an old pot) and place it at one end of the box, or use a firewood fire outside and shovel hot coals into the box. Cover the box with a lid or metal sheet to trap heat. Let it heat for 20-30 minutes until the sand is hot (you can feel warmth through the lid). Then place your cassava dough on the tray. Cover again. The sand will hold heat steadily. Bake for 35-45 minutes. Check after 30 minutes: the bread should be firm and browned. If you need more heat, add hot coals through a small opening. This oven is cheap and portable. Its ideal for areas with limited resources. After baking, let the fire die out and remove ashes. Store sand dry. Never use wet sand, as it creates steam and uneven baking.

4. Controlling heat and timing for even baking

Controlling heat and timing is critical for even baking of cassava bread. Cassava dough is dense and needs steady, moderate heat (180-200C) for 40-50 minutes. Too high heat burns the crust before the inside cooks; too low heat makes the bread dry and hard. To control heat in a charcoal or firewood oven, adjust the amount of fuel and the airflow. Start with a good bed of hot coals, then add small pieces of wood or charcoal as needed. Use a metal door or damper to control air. If the oven gets too hot, open the door slightly for 2-3 minutes. If too cool, add more fuel. For pot-on-pot, keep the fire medium; you can move the pot to a cooler part of the fire. For sandbox, add or remove hot coals. Timing: always preheat your oven for at least 20 minutes. Place the bread in the centre. For a 500g loaf, bake 45 minutes. For a 1kg loaf, 60-70 minutes. Rotate the bread halfway through if your oven has hot spots. Use a simple test: after 30 minutes, insert a thin stick into the centre; if it comes out clean, its done. If not, bake 10 more minutes. Keep a clock or timer. Write down what you did each time so you can repeat good results. With practice, you will learn your ovens behaviour.

5. Testing bread doneness without a thermometer

You dont need a thermometer to test if cassava bread is done. Use your senses and simple tools. First, the colour: the crust should be golden brown, not pale or dark. Second, the sound: tap the bottom of the loaf with your knuckles. If it sounds hollow, its likely done. Third, the stick test: insert a thin wooden stick or skewer into the centre. If it comes out clean or with dry crumbs, its done. If it has wet dough, bake longer. Fourth, the touch: press the top gently; it should spring back. If it leaves an indentation, it needs more time. Fifth, the smell: a done loaf smells nutty and toasted, not raw. For cassava bread, because its dense, also check the sides: they should pull away slightly from the pan or tray. If youre unsure, take the bread out, let it cool for 5 minutes, then cut a small slice from the centre. If its still doughy, return it to the oven for 10 minutes. Remember, overbaking makes it hard; underbaking makes it gummy. Practise with small loaves first. Keep a record of times and results. Soon youll judge doneness by look and feel alone.

6. Cooling and slicing cassava bread loaves

Cooling and slicing cassava bread properly is important for texture and taste. After baking, remove the loaf from the oven and place it on a wire rack or clean cloth. Do not slice immediatelyhot bread is fragile and steamy inside. Let it cool for at least 30 minutes for a small loaf, 1 hour for a large one. This allows the inside to set and the moisture to redistribute. If you cut too early, the bread will crumble and seem doughy. While cooling, cover loosely with a clean cloth to keep the crust soft, but dont wrap tightly or condensation will make it soggy. For slicing, use a sharp, serrated knife. A bread knife works best. Cut with a gentle sawing motion, not pressing down hard. For even slices, start at one end and cut 1-2 cm thick slices. If the bread is very dense, you can warm the knife slightly. Cassava bread is best eaten fresh, but you can store it in a paper bag for 2 days or freeze for up to 1 month. To reheat, wrap in foil and warm in a pot-on-pot oven for 10 minutes. Always cool completely before storing to prevent mould. With practice, youll get clean slices every time.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Baking Cassava Bread Without an Oven

Cassava bread whether the dense, fermented kpokpo garri style loaf of West Africa, the sweet pan de yuca rolls of Latin influence, or the enriched sandwich loaves now demanded by urban African supermarkets does not require a gas or electric oven to reach market. Across Ghana, Nigeria, Tanzania and Uganda, thousands of small bakeries profitably produce cassava loaves using charcoal drums, pot-on-pot steamers, sandbox ovens and open-fire hearths. This practical reference gives you the numbers, the methods and the quality checks to bake consistent, sellable cassava bread with the equipment you already own or can build for under US$80.

The commercial logic is simple: oven-less baking cuts capital expenditure by 7090% versus a deck oven, allows you to start at a roadside or market stall, and lets you scale by adding identical low-cost units rather than replacing one expensive machine. Your constraint is not equipment it is heat control, timing discipline and consistent product quality. Master those three and you have a business.

Method 1: Building a Simple Charcoal or Firewood Oven

The simplest commercial unit is a 200-litre steel drum laid on its side, cut with a door, lined with a clay-and-sand mortar, and fired from a separate combustion chamber beneath. This "drum oven" reaches 180220C in 4060 minutes and holds heat for 45 minutes enough for two to three baking batches per firing.

Construction steps:

  1. Cut a 45 cm 35 cm door in the drum side; weld or wire a hinged flap.
  2. Line the interior with a 5 cm layer of clay-sand mortar (3 parts clay : 1 part sand) to retain heat and prevent scorching.
  3. Build a firebox beneath using bricks or a second smaller drum; connect via a 10 cm flue.
  4. Fit a simple metal rack 10 cm above the oven floor so loaves bake on radiant heat, not direct flame.

For firewood, use dry hardwood it burns hotter and produces less smoke than softwood or green wood. Charcoal gives more stable temperature but costs more per batch in most African markets.

Method 2: Pot-on-Pot Baking

The pot-on-pot method a greased inner pot holding the loaf, placed inside a larger pot with sand or stones on the bottom creates a steam-and-radiant environment that bakes small cassava loaves in 3550 minutes. It is the lowest-cost entry point for a home-based bakery and works on any heat source: charcoal brazier, kerosene stove, or firewood hearth.

Fill the outer pot with 23 cm of clean sand or pebbles, preheat for 15 minutes over medium heat, place the covered inner pot inside, and bake. The sand buffers temperature swings and prevents the bottom of the loaf from burning before the centre sets.

Method 3: Sandbox Oven

A sandbox oven is a shallow metal box or drum half filled with dry sand, heated from below by a charcoal tray. Loaves are placed on a wire rack above the sand, and a metal lid traps heat. It is popular in northern Ghana and Nigeria for its even heat distribution and ability to bake 1015 small loaves per batch. Sandbox ovens are typically 6090 cm long, 40 cm wide, and reach 190210C.

Controlling Heat and Timing for Even Baking

Heat control is where most African cassava bakeries fail. Cassava dough has no gluten network to trap gas, so it depends on starch gelatinisation and steam expansion for rise. Too hot and the crust sets before the interior cooks; too cool and the loaf collapses into a dense, gummy centre.

Practical heat-control rules:

  • Preheat 3045 minutes before the first batch; test with a sprinkle of flour it should brown in 30 seconds at baking temperature.
  • Rotate loaves 180 halfway through baking to correct for hot spots.
  • Open the door only once, at the halfway mark; every opening loses 1525C.
  • Replenish fuel in small amounts; a sudden fire flare scorches the crust.

Cooling and Slicing Cassava Bread Loaves

Cooling is a commercial step, not a pause. Cassava loaves are fragile when hot; slicing too early crumbles the crumb and shortens shelf life by trapping steam. Cool on a raised wire rack for 4590 minutes until the loaf reaches room temperature (below 35C). Never cool on a solid surface the base sweats and goes mouldy within 24 hours.

Slice with a serrated knife using a gentle sawing motion, not downward pressure. For retail, cut 1.5 cm slices and wrap in food-grade paper or polypropylene within 2 hours of cooling. Shelf life at ambient African temperatures (2835C) is 23 days wrapped, 57 days refrigerated, and 34 weeks frozen.

SAFETY WARNING Fire, Burns and Fuel Storage

Charcoal and firewood ovens operate at 180220C and produce carbon monoxide. Always bake in a well-ventilated area outdoors or under an open-sided shelter. Never bake inside a closed room, even with a window open. Keep a bucket of sand and a bucket of water within 3 metres of every oven. Store charcoal and firewood at least 5 metres from the oven and never next to flour, packaging or cooking oil. Wear closed shoes, long trousers and heat-resistant gloves when loading and unloading loaves. Train every worker on burn first aid and keep a basic burns kit on site. In many African markets, fire is the single largest cause of bakery business closure.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Preheat fully: 3045 minutes before the first batch; confirm with the flour-browning test.
  2. Weigh dough: 500 g per loaf for consistent bake time across batches.
  3. Grease and dust: oil the rack or pot lightly and dust with cassava flour to prevent sticking.
  4. Load quickly: close the oven door within 60 seconds to preserve heat.
  5. Rotate at half-time: turn loaves 180 to even out hot spots.
  6. Test doneness: tap for a hollow sound and skewer the centre before removing.
  7. Cool on a rack: 4590 minutes; never stack hot loaves.
  8. Record every batch: fuel used, temperature feel, time, yield and customer feedback this is your business data.
