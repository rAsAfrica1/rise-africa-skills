1. Module 11 Lesson 1: Foundations of Business Translation in African Markets

Welcome to Module 11 of the Business Chinese, Arabic and Russian Translation course. This lesson establishes the practical foundations for translating business content across Chinese, Arabic and Russian into and out of African market contexts. Africa is not a single market. It is fifty-four countries, thousands of languages, multiple legal systems and dozens of business cultures. Your job as a business translator is not to produce beautiful literary prose. Your job is to produce accurate, usable, commercially safe text that lets a deal move forward. We begin with the core principle: context beats dictionary. A Chinese phrase like hezuo huoban can be translated as cooperative partner, but in a Lagos procurement meeting it may mean joint venture partner, while in a Nairobi fintech pitch it may simply mean strategic ally. The translator must ask: who is reading this, what decision are they making, and what risk exists if the meaning shifts? In Africa-focused business translation, the three source languages carry distinct commercial histories. Chinese business communication often uses indirect phrasing, relationship markers and collective framing. Arabic business writing may include religious formulas, honorifics and formal openings that carry social weight. Russian business correspondence tends to be direct, structured and document-heavy, with strong reliance on official titles and reference numbers. None of these patterns should be flattened into generic English. They should be rendered into a target style that an African business reader will recognize as professional and trustworthy. Consider a real scenario. A Chinese construction firm sends a tender document to a ministry in Accra. The Chinese text says the company has rich experience in tropical zones. A literal translation is fine, but a better translation says the company has delivered projects in similar climatic and regulatory environments in West Africa. That version answers the reader's unspoken question: can you work here? Similarly, an Arabic letter from a Gulf investor to a Kenyan bank may open with a long blessing. If you cut it entirely, you may seem disrespectful. If you keep it word for word, the Kenyan reader may find it irrelevant. The practical solution is a short, dignified opening that preserves respect without delaying the commercial point. Russian technical specifications often include GOST standards. When translating for a South African manufacturer, you must not simply replace GOST with SABS without verification. You note the standard, explain the equivalence if confirmed, and flag the difference if not. Accuracy protects the client from costly compliance errors. Your workflow should always include five steps. First, identify the document type: contract, tender, invoice, letter, presentation, product label or negotiation brief. Second, identify the audience: government, private buyer, bank, customs, factory or court. Third, identify the legal weight: is this binding, advisory or promotional? Fourth, identify terminology that must not drift, such as payment terms, incoterms, penalties and delivery dates. Fifth, identify cultural signals that affect trust, such as greetings, titles, references to God, hierarchy and time. In African business settings, trust is often built through consistent follow-up. Your translations should support that follow-up. Dates must be unambiguous. Use full month names, not numeric shortcuts, because 03/04/2025 means different things in different countries. Currency must include the currency code, not just a symbol. Payment terms should state the number of days and the starting event, for example thirty days after bill of lading date. Incoterms should be written in full the first time, such as Cost Insurance and Freight, followed by the acronym CIF. Names of people and companies must be checked against official registration documents. A misspelled company name on an invoice can delay customs clearance for weeks. Chinese names should be rendered consistently, preferably using the romanization already used by the client. Arabic names may include bin, bint, al and abu; these are not optional decorations, they are part of identity. Russian names have patronymics and gender endings; a woman named Ivanova should not become Ivanov in the target text. Numbers deserve special attention. Chinese uses wan for ten thousand and yi for hundred million. Russian uses tysyacha for thousand and million for million. Arabic uses alf for thousand and million for million. When a Chinese contract says 500 wan RMB, that is five million RMB, not five hundred. A translator who misses this can cause a ten-thousand-fold error. Always convert to a clearly written numeral and currency code, and add a parenthetical source-language figure if helpful. Punctuation and formatting also matter. Arabic is written right to left, so tables, numbered lists and mixed Arabic-English text need careful layout. Russian uses commas as decimal separators in some contexts and spaces for thousands. Chinese may use full-width punctuation. When you deliver a translation, preserve the source layout where possible, but adapt it so the target reader can scan it quickly. A customs officer in Mombasa or a bank officer in Casablanca should not have to decode your formatting. Finally, build a personal glossary for each client. Include company names, product names, job titles, payment terms and recurring legal phrases. Update it after every project. In Module 11, we will apply these foundations to contracts, negotiations, marketing, technical documents and correspondence. For now, remember the translator's commercial duty: reduce risk, preserve intent, and make the next business step easier. That is the standard by which your work will be judged in African markets.

2. Module 11 Lesson 2: Translating Contracts and Legal Documents for African Jurisdictions

This lesson focuses on contracts and legal documents, the highest-risk area of business translation. In Africa, legal systems vary widely. Some countries follow common law, some civil law, some mixed systems, and some incorporate customary or religious law. Your translation must respect the governing law clause. If a contract says the governing law is the laws of England and Wales, you translate that faithfully. If it says the laws of the Republic of Kenya, you do not substitute a different jurisdiction because it seems similar. The first rule of legal translation is: do not improve the law. Render it. Before translating, read the entire contract once without translating. Identify the parties, the recitals, the definitions, the obligations, the payment terms, the delivery terms, the liability clauses, the termination clauses, the dispute resolution clause and the signature blocks. In Chinese contracts, you will often see jiafang and yifang, which mean Party A and Party B. In Arabic contracts, you may see al-taraf al-awwal and al-taraf al-thani, First Party and Second Party. In Russian contracts, you will see Ispolnitel, Contractor or Executor, and Zakazchik, Customer or Client. These labels must be consistent throughout. If you translate Party A as the first party in clause one and the client in clause five, you create ambiguity that a court may interpret against your client. Definitions are the backbone of legal translation. A term defined in the definitions section controls the meaning everywhere else. If the Chinese text defines shangpin as goods, you must use goods consistently, not products, items or merchandise. If the Arabic text defines al-khidma as the service, you must not switch to the work or the assignment. Create a termbase before you translate the body. Use a simple spreadsheet with columns for source term, target term, definition, source clause and notes. Share it with the client if they have a legal reviewer. In African jurisdictions, several clauses deserve special care. The governing law clause tells you which legal system applies. The jurisdiction clause tells you where disputes will be heard. The arbitration clause may name a center such as the Lagos Court of Arbitration, the Cairo Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration, the Nairobi Centre for International Arbitration or the Arbitration Institute of the Stockholm Chamber of Commerce. Do not translate the name of an arbitration institution loosely. Use its official English name and verify it. The force majeure clause is critical in Africa because of real risks such as flooding, drought, civil unrest, border closures, currency controls and power outages. Chinese contracts may list bu ke kang li, force majeure, with specific events. Arabic contracts may invoke qadr or quwwa qahira, superior force. Russian contracts may refer to obstoyatelstva nepreodolimoy sily, circumstances of irresistible force. Translate the listed events accurately and do not add or remove events. Payment clauses require precision. Look for currency, amount, method, bank, account number, SWIFT code, payment trigger and late payment interest. A Chinese contract may say payment within thirty days after receipt of invoice. An Arabic contract may say payment after acceptance of the work. A Russian contract may say payment within five banking days after signing the act of acceptance. These are different triggers. Translate them exactly. If the source is ambiguous, do not guess. Flag it in a translator's note and ask the client. Confidentiality clauses are common in African business deals, especially in mining, oil and gas, telecoms and banking. Translate the scope of confidential information, the duration of the obligation, the permitted disclosures and the return or destruction of materials. Do not soften the duration. If the Chinese text says five years after termination, do not write five years after signing. Liability and indemnity clauses often use complex phrasing. Chinese may use peichang zeren, compensation liability, and mianze tiaokuan, exemption clause. Arabic may use al-mas'uliyya, liability, and al-ta'wid, compensation. Russian may use otvetstvennost, liability, and vozmeshchenie ubytkov, compensation of losses. These are not interchangeable. Liability is the legal responsibility. Compensation is the payment. Indemnity is the promise to cover loss. Keep them distinct. Termination clauses must state who can terminate, for what reason, with how much notice, and what happens to accrued rights. In some African countries, employment termination has statutory requirements that differ from the contract. You are not a lawyer, but you should recognize when a clause touches mandatory local law and flag it for legal review. Signature blocks require exact names, titles, company registration numbers and dates. In Arabic, the signature line may include the phrase bi-sifati, in my capacity as. In Russian, the signature block may include dolzhnost, position, and podpis, signature. In Chinese, it may include fa ren dai biao, legal representative. Translate these titles accurately. A legal representative is not the same as an authorized signatory, and an authorized signatory is not the same as a director. When translating for African courts or regulators, you may need a certified translation. Certification requirements differ by country. Some require a sworn translator, some require an affidavit, some require an apostille or legalization. Ask the client where the document will be filed. If it is going to a court in Ghana, Nigeria, South Africa, Kenya, Egypt, Morocco or the UAE, the formalities may differ. Build a checklist for each jurisdiction you serve. Legal translation also demands consistent formatting. Numbered clauses should keep their numbering. Cross-references such as clause 4.2 must point to the same clause in the translation. If you renumber, you break the document. Tables of payment schedules, delivery milestones and penalties must align exactly. Dates must be written in full. Amounts must include currency codes. Names must match official documents. Finally, always deliver a clean translation plus a query sheet. The query sheet lists ambiguities, missing information, inconsistent source terms and assumptions you made. This protects you and helps the client. In African business, a contract that moves smoothly through legal review is worth more than a poetic translation. Your value is precision, consistency and risk reduction.

3. Module 11 Lesson 3: Negotiation, Correspondence and Diplomatic Business Language

This lesson covers negotiation support, business correspondence and diplomatic language. In African markets, business deals often advance through relationships, meetings, phone calls and written follow-up. Your translations must support the tone of the relationship, not just the content of the message. A Chinese email may begin with hen gao xing ren shi ni, very pleased to meet you, and end with qi dai nin de hui fu, looking forward to your reply. An Arabic email may begin with al-salam alaykum, peace be upon you, and include insha'allah, if God wills, when referring to future plans. A Russian email may begin with uvazhaemyy, dear or respected, and end with s uvazheniem, with respect. These openings and closings are not empty. They signal respect, patience and good faith. In African business correspondence, similar signals exist. A letter in Nigeria may begin with Dear Sir and end with Yours faithfully. A letter in Kenya may use Dear Mr. Kamau and end with Yours sincerely. A letter in Egypt may use formal Arabic openings and closings. A letter in South Africa may be direct but polite. Your task is to match the target convention without losing the source intent. When translating negotiation documents, distinguish between positions and interests. A position is what a party says it wants. An interest is why they want it. Chinese negotiators may express positions indirectly, using phrases like women ren wei, we think, or ke neng you kun nan, there may be difficulties. Arabic negotiators may use phrases like na'mal, we hope, or bi-idhnillah, God willing, to keep options open. Russian negotiators may state positions firmly and expect a clear response. If you translate a Russian firm position into soft English, you may mislead your client. If you translate a Chinese indirect position into blunt English, you may damage the relationship. The practical approach is to preserve the level of commitment. A hope remains a hope. A requirement remains a requirement. A suggestion remains a suggestion. Do not upgrade or downgrade. Meeting interpretation requires different skills from document translation. In consecutive interpretation, you listen, take notes, and render the message in the target language. You must manage turn-taking, numbers, names and acronyms. In African settings, meetings may include multiple languages. A Chinese delegation may speak Mandarin, an Egyptian delegation may speak Arabic, a Russian delegation may speak Russian, and the host may speak English, French or Portuguese. You may be asked to relay through English as a pivot language. If so, confirm the relay arrangement before the meeting. Ask whether you should interpret directly or through a pivot. Ask for the agenda, the participant list and any presentation slides. Prepare a glossary of company names, project names, technical terms and titles. During the meeting, write down numbers immediately. Repeat them back if necessary. Use the correct honorifics. In Arabic, use Doctor for a PhD holder, Engineer for an engineer, and Sheikh for a religious or tribal leader when appropriate. In Chinese, use Mr. or Ms. with the family name, and use the full title for senior officials. In Russian, use the first name and patronymic in formal settings, or the title and family name in very formal settings. After the meeting, produce a summary in the target language. Include decisions, action points, responsible persons and deadlines. Send it to the client for confirmation. This summary becomes the record of the meeting and prevents disputes later. Business correspondence also includes letters of intent, memoranda of understanding, proposals, quotations and invoices. Each has a standard structure. A quotation should state the item, specification, quantity, unit price, total price, currency, validity period, delivery time, payment terms and incoterms. A letter of intent should state the parties, the proposed transaction, the indicative terms and the non-binding nature of the document. A memorandum of understanding should state the purpose, scope, responsibilities, duration and whether it is legally binding. Translate these structures faithfully. Do not merge sections or omit headings. In African procurement, a missing heading can cause a bid to be disqualified. Tone is especially important when writing to government officials. In many African countries, formal titles matter. Use His Excellency, Honorable Minister, Permanent Secretary, Director General, Managing Director or Chief Executive Officer as appropriate. In Arabic, use Ma'ali for His Excellency and Sa'adat for His Excellency or Mr. in some contexts. In Russian, use Gospodin or Gospozha for Mr. or Ms., and use the official title in full. In Chinese, use the surname plus title, such as Wang Zong, General Manager Wang, or Li Chu, Director Li. When in doubt, use the more formal option. It is easier to relax formality later than to recover from disrespect. Cultural notes for negotiation: In many African business cultures, time is relational. Meetings may start late, and relationship-building may take place before business. Do not translate this as inefficiency. In Chinese business culture, face and hierarchy matter. Avoid direct contradiction in front of senior people. In Arabic business culture, hospitality and personal connection matter. Accept tea or coffee if offered. In Russian business culture, punctuality and preparation matter. Bring printed documents and be ready to discuss details. Your translations should help your client navigate these differences. If a Chinese message says we will study the matter, it may mean we are not ready to decide. If an Arabic message says we will try, it may mean the outcome depends on factors outside the speaker's control. If a Russian message says we will consider it, it may mean the proposal is under review. Explain these nuances to your client in a separate note, not in the translation itself. Finally, keep a correspondence log. Record the date, sender, recipient, subject, key points and action taken. In long negotiations, this log is invaluable. It helps you maintain consistent terminology and helps the client track commitments. In African business, follow-up is a sign of seriousness. Your translations should make follow-up easy and accurate.

4. Module 11 Lesson 4: Technical, Industrial and Infrastructure Translation

This lesson addresses technical, industrial and infrastructure translation, a major growth area in Africa. Chinese, Arabic and Russian companies are active in African energy, mining, construction, telecommunications, railways, ports, water and manufacturing. Translators are needed for specifications, manuals, drawings, safety documents, training materials, inspection reports and maintenance schedules. Technical translation demands three things: terminology accuracy, safety awareness and usability. Start with terminology. Build a glossary for each project. Include equipment names, part numbers, units of measurement, materials, processes and safety terms. For Chinese, common terms include shebei, equipment, guandao, pipeline, dianlan, cable, fa dian ji, generator, and an quan, safety. For Arabic, common terms include ma'dat, equipment, anabib, pipes, kahraba, electricity, and salama, safety. For Russian, common terms include oborudovanie, equipment, truboprovod, pipeline, elektrooborudovanie, electrical equipment, and bezopasnost, safety. Do not rely on machine translation for technical terms. Verify each term against the manufacturer's documentation, the project glossary or a recognized standard. Units of measurement cause frequent errors. Africa uses the metric system in most countries, but some industries use imperial units. A Russian specification may use millimeters, meters, kilograms and degrees Celsius. A Chinese specification may use the same metric units, but may also use mu for land area or jin for weight in informal contexts. An Arabic specification may use metric units with Arabic names. Convert units carefully and show both the original and converted value when useful. For example, 100 mm, about 3.94 inches, if the target audience uses inches. Temperature, pressure, torque, voltage and flow rates must be exact. A wrong pressure rating can cause a pipeline failure. A wrong torque value can damage a turbine. A wrong voltage can destroy equipment. Safety documents are the highest priority. Translate warning labels, lockout-tagout procedures, emergency shutdown steps, personal protective equipment requirements and hazard classifications with absolute accuracy. Use standard signal words: Danger, Warning, Caution and Notice. In Arabic, use khatar for danger, tahdhir for warning and tanbih for caution. In Russian, use opasno for danger, preduprezhdenie for warning and vnimanie for caution. In Chinese, use wei xian for danger, jing gao for warning and zhu yi for caution. Do not soften a danger warning into a caution. Do not translate a mandatory action as a suggestion. Use imperative sentences for instructions. In English, write Close the valve before opening the drain. In Arabic, use the imperative form. In Russian, use the imperative form. In Chinese, use the imperative form. Avoid passive voice in instructions because it can hide who must act. Drawings and diagrams require special handling. You may need to translate labels, callouts, legends, dimensions and notes. Keep the label text short. Use consistent abbreviations. If a drawing is in Chinese, Arabic or Russian, you may need to create a bilingual version. Do not cover the drawing with long text. Use a numbered legend if space is limited. Ensure that translated labels point to the correct component. A mislabeled valve or switch can cause an accident. Training materials should be practical. African technicians may speak English, French, Portuguese, Swahili, Hausa, Amharic, Arabic or other languages. If you are translating training materials into English for a multilingual audience, use simple sentences, short paragraphs and clear visuals. Define technical terms the first time they appear. Use consistent terminology throughout the manual. Include a glossary at the end. If you are translating into Arabic or Russian for a specific audience, follow the same principles. Inspection reports and non-destructive testing reports require precise language. Translate test methods, acceptance criteria, results and conclusions accurately. Do not change pass to fail or acceptable to unacceptable. If the source report is ambiguous, flag it. In African infrastructure projects, inspection reports may be reviewed by lenders, insurers and regulators. Errors can delay payments or trigger penalties. Maintenance schedules should be translated with clear intervals. Daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, annual and after every 500 hours are different. Translate them exactly. Include the date format and the time zone if relevant. For remote sites, include the local time and the coordinated universal time if the source uses it. Spare parts lists should include part number, description, quantity, unit and remarks. Keep the part numbers unchanged. Translate the descriptions accurately. If a part is obsolete, note it. If a substitute is approved, note it. Procurement officers in Lagos, Nairobi, Cairo, Johannesburg or Casablanca will use these lists to order parts. A wrong part number wastes money and delays operations. Software and control systems add another layer. Human-machine interface text, alarm messages, parameter names and menu items must be short and consistent. Work with the software vendor if possible. Test the translated text in the interface if you can. Some languages expand or contract. Arabic may require more space. Russian may require more characters than English. Chinese may require fewer. Plan the layout accordingly. Finally, always ask for the project style guide. It may specify units, date formats, abbreviations, document numbering and approval workflow. Follow it. If there is no style guide, create one and get it approved. In African infrastructure projects, multiple contractors and consultants work together. A shared style guide reduces confusion. Your role is to make technical information usable by the people who build, operate and maintain the asset. Accuracy saves lives. Clarity saves money. Consistency saves time.

5. Module 11 Lesson 5: Marketing, Cultural Adaptation and Quality Assurance for African Audiences

This lesson covers marketing translation, cultural adaptation and quality assurance. Marketing is not literal translation. It is transcreation: adapting a message so it persuades the target audience while preserving the brand's intent. In Africa, audiences are diverse. A campaign that works in Cairo may not work in Cape Town. A slogan that works in Lagos may not work in Nairobi. A Chinese or Russian brand entering Africa must adapt to local languages, values, humor, religion, color symbolism and media habits. Start with the product name. Check whether the name means something unfortunate in the target language. Check whether it conflicts with an existing trademark. Check whether it is pronounceable. A Chinese brand name may use characters that sound fine in Mandarin but sound awkward in Swahili or Arabic. A Russian brand name may use Cyrillic that confuses English-speaking African consumers. A transliteration may be better than a translation. For example, a Chinese brand may keep its pinyin name and add a descriptive tagline in English, French, Arabic or Portuguese. Next, consider the value proposition. African consumers and businesses often care about reliability, affordability, after-sales service, spare parts availability, training and local partnership. A Chinese marketing text may emphasize scale and speed. An Arabic marketing text may emphasize trust and tradition. A Russian marketing text may emphasize engineering strength and durability. Adapt these emphases to the local market. If the product is a generator for Nigerian small businesses, emphasize fuel efficiency, easy maintenance and local service centers. If the product is a tractor for Kenyan farmers, emphasize durability, financing options and training. If the product is a smartphone for Egyptian youth, emphasize camera quality, battery life and price. If the product is a railway system for a North African government, emphasize safety, technology transfer and job creation. Cultural adaptation also involves images, colors and symbols. In many African countries, colors carry meanings. Green may signify agriculture, Islam or national identity depending on the context. Red may signify danger, passion or political movements. White may signify purity or mourning depending on the culture. Avoid images that reinforce stereotypes. Show real African business settings, professionals, technicians, farmers, students and families. Show diversity within countries. Do not use one image for all of Africa. Religion matters. In Muslim-majority areas such as North Africa and parts of West and East Africa, avoid images of alcohol, pork or inappropriate dress in general campaigns. In Christian-majority areas, be mindful of religious holidays and symbols. In mixed areas, use inclusive language. When translating Arabic marketing text, decide whether to use Modern Standard Arabic or a local dialect. Modern Standard Arabic is widely understood and formal. Egyptian Arabic, Moroccan Darija, Gulf Arabic and Levantine Arabic are more intimate but less universal. For a pan-African campaign, Modern Standard Arabic is usually safer. For a local campaign, dialect may be more effective. Russian marketing text for African audiences is usually translated into English, French or Portuguese, not Russian. The Russian source may be highly compressed and emotional. Expand it into clear target-language copy. Chinese marketing text often uses four-character phrases, idioms and parallel structures. These may not translate well. Replace them with concrete benefits. For example, a Chinese phrase meaning one step ahead may become reliable technology that keeps your business running. Quality assurance is the final stage. Never deliver a marketing translation without review. Use a three-step process: translation, editing and proofreading. The translator produces the draft. The editor checks meaning, tone, terminology and cultural fit. The proofreader checks spelling, grammar, punctuation, numbers and formatting. If possible, add a native-speaker reviewer for the target market. For African markets, a reviewer in Lagos may catch Nigerian English issues that a reviewer in London would miss. A reviewer in Cairo may catch Arabic phrasing that a reviewer in Beirut would find odd. A reviewer in Nairobi may catch Swahili or Kenyan English nuances. A reviewer in Johannesburg may catch South African English preferences. Build a reviewer network. Pay them properly. Their feedback improves your work and protects your reputation. Back-translation is useful for high-risk marketing claims. Translate the target text back into the source language and compare. If the meaning has shifted, revise. Back-translation is not a substitute for good translation, but it is a useful check for legal, medical and financial claims. Legal and regulatory review is essential for advertising. Many African countries have advertising standards authorities, consumer protection laws and sector regulators. Health claims, financial claims, environmental claims and comparative claims may be restricted. Translate the claim accurately and flag it for legal review. Do not invent certifications or awards. Do not exaggerate. In African markets, trust is hard to build and easy to lose. Finally, measure the results. Work with the marketing team to track response rates, website traffic, inquiries, sales and customer feedback. If a campaign underperforms, ask whether the translation or adaptation contributed. Sometimes the issue is the offer, the price or the channel. Sometimes it is the wording. Use data to improve future translations. Keep a record of what worked and what did not. Build a cultural style guide for each market you serve. Include preferred greetings, titles, date formats, currency formats, color notes, religious notes, humor notes and taboo notes. Update it regularly. Share it with clients and reviewers. In Module 11, you have learned foundations, contracts, negotiation correspondence, technical translation and marketing adaptation. The common thread is commercial judgment. You are not just converting words. You are helping Chinese, Arabic and Russian businesses communicate with African partners accurately, respectfully and profitably. Quality assurance is not a final step. It is a habit. Build it into every project from the first email to the final delivery.

<h3>Business-First Translation Principles for African Agri-Business</h3>

<p>Translation in agribusiness is not only a language task. It is a commercial risk management task. A contract for day-old chicks, a feed supply agreement or a vaccine import permit all depend on precise terminology. In African markets, buyers and sellers often work across multiple languages and legal systems. A Chinese supplier may draft in Mandarin, an Arabic-speaking distributor may handle the Gulf market, and a Russian manufacturer may supply equipment to East Africa. The translator becomes the bridge between these parties.</p>

<p>Three principles should guide every assignment. First, use the terminology of the importing country's regulator. If the Kenyan Directorate of Veterinary Services uses a specific term for <em>hatchery</em> or <em>feedlot</em>, use that term. Second, preserve commercial intent. A translation that is technically correct but commercially awkward can weaken a negotiation. Third, document every choice. Keep a glossary and a translation memory for each client. This reduces cost and error over time.</p>

<p>For Arabic, pay attention to right-to-left formatting in tables and certificates. For Chinese, watch for measure words and unit conversion. For Russian, check decimal commas and case endings in legal phrases. These small details determine whether a shipment clears customs or sits in a warehouse.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li>Confirm the official veterinary or regulatory term used by the importing African country before translating.</li>
<li>Verify all numbers, units and decimal separators against the source document and local conventions.</li>
<li>Check dosage, withdrawal period and storage temperature translations with a qualified veterinarian or pharmacist.</li>
<li>Use WOAH, FAO and national glossary terms for disease names and biosecurity concepts.</li>
<li>Maintain a client-specific glossary and translation memory for Chinese, Arabic and Russian agribusiness terms.</li>
<li>Back-translate critical clauses in contracts, labels and certificates before delivery.</li>
<li>Format tables, dates and numerals correctly for right-to-left and left-to-right target languages.</li>
<li>Record the source, date and reviewer for every health or safety-related translation.</li>
</ul>
