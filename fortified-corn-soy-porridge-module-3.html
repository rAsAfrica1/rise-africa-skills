3. Sourcing the Ingredients

Good porridge begins with good ingredients. This module shows you where to buy each one, how to tell good from bad in 60 seconds, and how to buy in bulk without losing money to spoiled stock.

Module 3 of 12 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · video resources throughout · quiz at the end

Lesson 3.1 — Maize: white vs yellow, whole grain vs refined, and why fresh matters

Maize is the base of your porridge. Getting the right type and quality is the foundation of everything else.

White vs yellow — the vitamin A question

In most of southern and eastern Africa, white maize dominates the market. It is what families know, what porridge traditionally looks like, and what shops stock. But yellow maize is nutritionally superior in one specific way that matters enormously for malnourished children.

FeatureWhite maizeYellow maize
ColourWhite/creamGolden yellow
Beta-carotene (vitamin A precursor)Very lowModerate — 1.5–2.5 µg/g
Traditional preferenceSouthern/East AfricaAnimal feed / West Africa
CostSlightly higher (demand)Slightly lower
Best forWhere culture demands whiteMalnourished children

For a malnourished child, yellow maize provides natural vitamin A that white maize does not. In a community where the child already has night blindness or frequent infections (both vitamin A deficiency signs), switching to yellow maize is a zero-cost improvement.

If white maize is what you can get, do not stop. You will add vitamin A through the premix and through the oil choice. But if you have a choice, yellow wins.

Whole grain vs refined — the nutrient stripping problem

Maize is milled into different grades in most African markets:

  • Whole grain (mugaiwa, chibage, samp-grade) — the entire kernel is ground. Highest in fibre, oils, B vitamins, iron and zinc. Shelf life is shorter due to oils.
  • Roller meal (mealie meal, grade 1) — germ and bran mostly removed. The most common household flour. Middle nutrition.
  • Super refined (breakfast meal, grade 2) — almost pure starch. Looks whitest, lasts longest, has the least nutrition.

The refining process removes the very layers where nutrients live. The bran holds the fibre and most B vitamins. The germ holds the oils, vitamin E and healthy fats. Removing them makes flour whiter and longer-lasting but strips a malnourished child's best natural source of iron, zinc and B vitamins.

Rule: For fortified porridge, always use whole grain or roller meal. Super-refined breakfast meal has almost nothing left to fortify. You are trying to feed a child, not produce a shelf-stable starch.

How to tell fresh maize flour from old

Maize flour has a shelf life of 3–6 months if stored properly. Old flour loses nutrition, develops off-flavours, and can harbour aflatoxin. Here are the tests:

  • Smell: Fresh flour smells like clean grain. Old flour smells musty, sour, or "cardboardy."
  • Colour: Fresh is uniform in colour. Old flour has dull patches or uneven tones.
  • Clumping: Squeeze a handful. It should flow freely. If it stays in a lump, moisture has entered — that means mould.
  • Taste test: Cook a small sample plain and taste it. Fresh is slightly sweet. Old is bitter or flat.
  • Pest check: Look for weevils, small holes in the sack, or web-like threads. Reject any bag with signs of insect activity.

Lesson 3.2 — Soybean: full-fat vs defatted, whole vs grits, and how to check for aflatoxin

Soybean is the most complex ingredient in this course. It comes in many forms, several of which look similar but behave very differently in the porridge. Choosing the wrong one ruins the nutrition.

The forms of soybean you will find in a market

FormWhat it isUse it?
Whole dry soybeanIntact dried beans, ~20% fat, ~38% protein✅ Best — you process yourself
Full-fat soy flourWhole beans ground without removing oil✅ Best ready-to-use option
Defatted soy flourOil pressed out, ~50% protein, ~1% fat⚠️ Use only if adding oil separately
Soy gritsCoarse pieces of defatted bean❌ Not suitable for porridge
Textured soy protein (TSP)Extruded chunks, meat substitute❌ Wrong product entirely
Soy milk / soymilk powderLiquid extract or dried⚠️ Can substitute if milk powder is unavailable

For this recipe, use whole dry soybean or full-fat soy flour. The oil in whole soybean is not a nuisance — it is a benefit. It adds 20% fat by weight to the mix, which raises the calorie count and provides fat-soluble vitamins with no extra cost.

Defatted soy has a place in industry — it is cheaper per kilogram of protein and lasts longer. But for a home or small-workshop producer, full-fat is the right choice. You are not optimizing cost per protein; you are feeding a malnourished child who needs calories.

The aflatoxin question

Aflatoxin is a poison produced by moulds (Aspergillus flavus and A. parasiticus) that grow on maize, groundnuts, tree nuts, and sometimes soybean. It is invisible, tasteless, and cannot be destroyed by cooking. Long-term exposure causes liver cancer, stunting, immune suppression and (in acute doses) death.

Africa has the highest aflatoxin exposure in the world. If you are feeding children, you must know how to spot and avoid it.

How to spot aflatoxin risk in soybean and maize

  • Visible mould: Any green, white or greyish fuzzy patches — reject the whole batch. Mould spores are far-reaching.
  • Discoloured or shrivelled beans: Healthy soybean is uniform, cream-coloured, and plump. Yellowish, brown or mouldy patches mean trouble.
  • Musty smell: Fresh soybean smells slightly nutty. Old or mouldy soybean smells earthy, sour, or like wet cardboard.
  • Insects and dust: Heavy insect damage means the storage was poor and mould has had time to develop.
  • Damp packaging: A sack with visible moisture inside is a mould factory.

There is no reliable home test for aflatoxin. The best protection is buy fresh, buy from a trusted supplier, and store dry. If you buy in bulk, sample-cook a small batch first and inspect the beans carefully.

Never feed visibly mouldy maize or soy to children. "Just cutting off the mouldy part" does not work. Aflatoxin spreads invisibly through the whole batch. If in doubt, throw it out. A kg of maize is cheap; liver damage in a child is not.

Lesson 3.3 — Milk powder: full-cream, skim, and the local brands you can actually trust

Milk powder is where quality variation is widest and prices swing most. A small difference in quality here shows up in the porridge's taste and nutrition.

Full-cream vs skim — the fat decision

As covered in Module 2, full-cream (whole) milk powder contains about 26% fat. Skim milk powder contains about 1%. For malnourished children, full-cream is the right choice.

TypeFat %CostBest for
Full-cream (whole)~26%HighestMalnourished children
Skim / low-fat~1%LowerAdult baking
Buttermilk powder~5%LowBaking, not fortification
Whey powder~1%MediumSports/protein supplements

If you must use skim (usually because full-cream is unavailable or unaffordable), add extra vegetable oil to the recipe — roughly 30 extra grams of oil per 100 g of skim milk powder used, to compensate for the missing fat.

Spotting good vs bad milk powder in 60 seconds

  • Smell: Fresh milk powder smells like fresh milk — clean, slightly sweet, dairy. Bad powder smells sour, cheesy, stale or "cooked."
  • Colour: Should be uniform cream-white. Yellow or brown patches indicate scorching during drying or age-related oxidation.
  • Texture: Pours freely and feels like fine powder. Hard lumps mean moisture has entered. Lumpy milk powder has lost vitamins and may have bacterial growth.
  • Clumping test: Squeeze a handful. It should flow out freely. If it stays in a compact ball, moisture is present.
  • Taste test: Dissolve a small spoon in warm water. Fresh milk powder tastes clean and milky. Old or bad tastes flat, sour, or slightly bitter.

Brands and sourcing

In African markets, milk powder comes in three tiers:

  • Premium imported brands (Nido, Anchor, Lakeland, Cowbell, Fernleaf): Consistent quality, higher price. Good for reliability when starting.
  • Local packaged (repacked by local dairies or importers): Cheaper, quality varies by supplier. Verify the package is sealed and dated.
  • Bulk unbranded (25 kg sacks from importers or feed stores): Cheapest per kilogram. Quality can vary wildly — always smell and inspect before buying.
Check the expiry date every time. Milk powder is a perishable product. Powder older than 12 months past manufacture has lost significant vitamin A, B vitamins and flavour. Some informal suppliers reprint dates — if the date looks newer than the packaging condition suggests, buy elsewhere.

Lesson 3.4 — Sugar and cooking oil: cheap sources and why vegetable oil beats palm

Sugar and oil are the two smallest inputs by weight but they determine calorie density. Getting them right and getting them cheap matters for both nutrition and business.

Sugar — simple but not always cheap

Any white granulated sugar works. Brown sugar also works but adds molasses flavour and colour, and may clump in humid storage. Do not use honey, syrups or artificial sweeteners — honey carries botulism risk in children under 12 months, and artificial sweeteners have no calories, which defeats the purpose.

Where to buy sugar cheaply:

  • Wholesale markets or millers — usually 15–25% cheaper than retail shops
  • Cash-and-carry bulk stores — 25 kg bags at significant discount
  • Supermarket specials — sometimes cheaper than wholesale when on promotion

Store in an airtight container. Sugar is hydroscopic — it absorbs moisture and hardens into lumps if stored in humid conditions. Cool, dry storage extends shelf life indefinitely (sugar doesn't spoil).

Vegetable oil — the energy hero

Vegetable oil is the single most calorie-dense ingredient in your recipe — 9 calories per gram, more than twice what sugar or maize provides. It is also the ingredient home producers most often skip because "it costs extra" — which is a mistake.

Best choices by country and price:

OilWhere popularNotes
Sunflower oilEast Africa, Southern AfricaMild taste, long shelf life, good all-rounder
Soybean oilWest Africa, Southern AfricaMild, cheap, widely available
Groundnut (peanut) oilWest Africa, SahelStronger flavour, slightly higher cost
Palm oil (red)West Africa, parts of Central AfricaHigher saturated fat, but high in vitamin A — bonus for this recipe
Canola / rapeseedSouth Africa, some importsMild, good fatty acid profile

Palm oil's strong colour and flavour will change the porridge significantly. In West Africa, where families already cook with red palm oil, this is culturally fine. In other regions, use sunflower or soybean oil.

Cost per calorie — the calculation that matters

Do not compare oils by price per litre. Compare by price per calorie. Here is a rough comparison at typical African market prices:

IngredientPrice per kgCalories/kgCost per 1,000 kcal
Maize flour$0.803,600$0.22
Sugar$1.204,000$0.30
Vegetable oil$2.009,000$0.22
Full-fat soy flour$1.004,400$0.23
Milk powder$8.005,000$1.60

Read this table carefully. Vegetable oil and maize flour are almost equally cheap per calorie. Sugar is more expensive. Milk powder is by far the most expensive calorie — but it delivers protein quality, calcium and vitamins that the others do not.

The strategy: use oil generously (it is the cheapest calorie after maize), use sugar moderately (it is more expensive and adds no nutrients), use milk powder at the recipe ratio (it is expensive but irreplaceable).

Lesson 3.5 — Micronutrient premix: what it is, where to buy it, and why UNICEF/WHO specify it

The micronutrient premix is the ingredient that separates a homemade porridge from a professional fortified blended food. It is not optional for anything you intend to sell to clinics, NGOs, or as a "fortified" product.

What the premix actually is

A micronutrient premix is a carefully blended powder of vitamins and minerals, mixed into a carrier like maltodextrin or starch. It is added to dry flour blends at a very small percentage — typically 0.3% to 1.5% by weight — to deliver the correct daily intake of each nutrient when the food is eaten in normal portions.

A typical child porridge premix contains around 13–15 nutrients:

NutrientTypical dose per kg of flourWhy it matters
Vitamin A2,000–3,000 µgVision, immunity, growth
Vitamin D350–100 µgBone growth, calcium absorption
Vitamin E80–150 mgAntioxidant, cell protection
Vitamin K200–400 µgBlood clotting
Vitamin C400–600 mgImmunity, iron absorption
Thiamine (B1)5–10 mgEnergy metabolism
Riboflavin (B2)5–10 mgEnergy, cell function
Niacin (B3)50–100 mgSkin, digestion, nerves
Vitamin B65–10 mgBrain development
Folic acid1,000–2,000 µgRed blood cells, DNA
Vitamin B1210–20 µgNerve function, red blood cells
Iron (ferrous fumarate)100–150 mgOxygen transport
Zinc50–80 mgGrowth, immunity, appetite
Copper3–5 mgIron metabolism
Selenium100–200 µgAntioxidant, thyroid

Doses are set by international standards (Codex Alimentarius, WHO, WFP specifications) and are designed to be safe for daily consumption across a wide range of children.

Why UNICEF and WHO specify premix

UNICEF and WFP have distributed fortified blended foods for decades. Their specifications exist because:

  • Natural food sources alone cannot meet children's micronutrient needs in food-insecure settings
  • Iron and zinc from plant foods are poorly absorbed without fortification
  • Deficiencies in vitamins A, D, B12 and folate are widespread and only correctable through fortification
  • Premix ensures consistency — every batch delivers a known, standard dose

If you want to sell to clinics, schools, NGOs or any institutional buyer, you must use a premix that meets these specifications. Without it, your product is "porridge" — with it, it is "fortified blended food," a recognized category of humanitarian nutrition product.

Where to buy premix in Africa

The premix is a B2B ingredient. It is not sold in ordinary shops. Reliable routes:

  • DSM Nutritional Products — offices in Kenya, South Africa, Nigeria, Egypt. Sells premix in 20 kg bags. Requires business registration to buy.
  • BASF Human Nutrition — similar structure, sells across Africa via distributors.
  • SternVitamin (Germany) — ships worldwide, smaller minimum orders, popular with small food processors.
  • Local fortified food producers — maize meal and cooking oil companies often import premix and will sell you 1–5 kg at a markup. Cheapest entry point for small producers.
  • NGO procurement channels — if you are registered as an NGO or community organisation, UNICEF Supply Division and WFP have prequalified supplier lists.

For most small producers, the best path is: find a local fortified maize meal miller, ask where they get their premix, and ask if they will sell you small quantities. Expect to pay a 20–50% markup over B2B pricing.

Rough costs

  • Bulk B2B (100 kg+): $15–$40 per kg depending on formula
  • Small quantity (1–5 kg via local producer): $40–$80 per kg
  • Cost in recipe: at 0.5% of dry mix, ~$0.20–$0.40 per kg of final flour

The premix adds a real but manageable cost to each kilogram of porridge. In a business plan, treat it as a fixed line item — you cannot make a saleable "fortified" product without it.

What if you cannot get premix at all?

For personal home use, use the natural fallbacks from Module 2 — yellow maize, red palm oil, moringa, iodised salt, groundnuts, eggs. The porridge will be nutritionally weaker but still far better than plain maize porridge.

For anything you intend to sell as "fortified," you must have premix. Selling without it and calling it fortified is misleading and may fall foul of food regulations.

Lesson 3.6 — Buying in bulk vs small quantities: cost per kilogram and shelf life

Your ingredient sourcing strategy will decide whether your porridge business makes money or loses it. This lesson is about the math of buying well.

The bulk-buying discount

Across most African markets, buying in bulk cuts the per-kilogram cost by 15% to 40%. A typical example for maize flour:

QuantityPrice per kgPremium over bulk
1 kg (small shop)$1.20+50%
10 kg (wholesale shop)$0.95+19%
50 kg (mill or bulk supplier)$0.80—

If you make porridge daily, buying 1 kg at a time costs 50% more than buying 50 kg at once. That difference alone is the difference between a profitable business and one that barely breaks even.

But bulk buying has risks

Every ingredient has a shelf life. Buy too much and you lose the savings to spoilage, insects or rancidity.

IngredientShelf life (proper storage)Safe bulk buy
Whole grain maize flour3–4 months1–2 months of production
Refined maize meal6–9 months3–4 months of production
Whole dry soybean12 months6 months of production
Full-fat soy flour4–6 months2–3 months of production
Full-cream milk powder9–12 months (sealed)3–4 months of production
SugarIndefinite (dry storage)1 year+
Vegetable oil12–18 months6–12 months of production
Micronutrient premix12–18 months (sealed, cool, dark)6 months of production

The general rule: buy no more of any ingredient than you can use within 60% of its shelf life. If full-fat soy flour lasts 6 months, buy at most 3–4 months' worth at a time. The savings from a bigger bulk buy are wiped out if the last 2 kg spoil.

Storage rules that protect your investment

  • Cool, dark, dry place — every 10°C rise in temperature roughly doubles the rate of spoilage. A cool pantry beats a hot garage every time.
  • Airtight containers — especially for flour, sugar and premix. Rodents and insects are a bigger threat than most home producers realise.
  • Off the ground — pallets or shelves. Concrete floors conduct moisture into sacks.
  • Away from strong-smelling items — flour and milk powder absorb odours from onions, spices, fuel or soap. Keep them separate.
  • Label every container — with the ingredient, date of purchase, and expiry date. You will forget.
  • Rotate stock — use the oldest first. First in, first out.

The three-supplier rule (your Iron-Clad Price Check)

Before every significant bulk purchase, call three suppliers and compare:

  1. Price per kilogram — not per bag, not per scoop, per kg
  2. Freshness — when was it harvested or manufactured?
  3. Minimum order — do they sell to your scale, or only in tonnes?
  4. Delivery — do they deliver, or do you collect?
  5. Payment terms — cash only, or can you pay on delivery?

Prices vary enormously between regions and seasons. In the maize harvest season (typically April–July in southern Africa, September–November in East Africa), prices fall 20–30%. Buying at the right time of year is as important as buying at the right place.
