1. Beet Washing and Slicing

When sugar beet arrives at the factory, it is covered in soil, stones, and leaf debris. Washing removes these impurities to prevent damage to slicing knives and to avoid contaminating the juice. In many African factories, beets are unloaded into a large concrete flume where water flows at about 1.5 meters per second, carrying beets to a rotating drum washer. The drum has perforated plates that let dirt fall through while beets tumble. Fresh water is added continuously; about 1,000 liters are used per tonne of beet. After washing, beets pass over a stone catcherâa vibrating screen that separates heavy stones. Then they go to a slicer.

The slicer uses sharp knives mounted on a rotating disc. The knives cut beets into V-shaped slices called cossettes, about 3-5 mm thick and 5-10 mm wide. Dull knives tear the cells, causing juice loss. Check knives every 8 hours and replace or sharpen as needed. A typical slicer runs at 200-300 rpm and can process 50 tonnes of beet per hour. The cossettes must be uniform; uneven slices lead to poor sugar extraction. After slicing, cossettes go directly to the diffusion tower within 10 minutes to avoid spoilage.

In small-scale operations in Kenya or Nigeria, a simple drum washer and a manual slicer can be used, but output is lower. For a 10-tonne-per-day plant, a motorized slicer with 8 knives is sufficient. Always wear gloves and safety glasses when handling knives. Keep the washing area clean to avoid bacterial growth that can sour the juice. Water from washing can be reused for irrigation after settling. Proper washing and slicing set the stage for efficient sugar extraction.

2. Diffusion Process: Principles and Equipment

Diffusion is the heart of sugar extraction. It uses hot water to pull sugar out of beet cossettes. The principle is simple: sugar moves from a high concentration inside the cossette to a low concentration in the surrounding water. To speed this up, cossettes are heated to about 70-75Â°C. At this temperature, cell walls become permeable, and sugar dissolves into the water. If the water is too hot (above 80Â°C), pectin and other impurities dissolve, making purification harder. If too cold, extraction is slow and incomplete.

The main equipment is a diffusion towerâa tall vertical cylinder, often 10-20 meters high. Cossettes enter at the top, and hot water enters at the bottom, flowing upward. This counter-current flow means fresh water meets the most exhausted cossettes, maximizing sugar removal. The cossettes stay in the tower for 60-90 minutes. A typical tower processes 100-200 tonnes of beet per day. The resulting juice, called raw juice, contains 12-15% sugar. The leftover cossettes, now called pulp, contain less than 0.5% sugar.

In Africa, many medium-scale factories use a continuous diffuser with a chain conveyor instead of a tower. This is easier to maintain and uses less water. For a 50-tonne-per-day plant, a diffuser with a 30-meter long trough works well. Key controls: maintain water temperature at 72Â°C, keep pH between 5.8 and 6.2 to avoid sugar breakdown, and ensure cossettes are not overcrowded. Add a small amount of sulfur dioxide (SO2) to the water to prevent bacterial growth. After diffusion, raw juice is screened to remove fine pulp, then sent to purification. Pulp is pressed and dried for animal feed. Proper diffusion can recover over 95% of sugar from beet.

3. Juice Purification: Carbonation and Liming

Raw juice from diffusion contains sugar but also impurities like proteins, pectins, and organic acids. Purification removes these to produce clear, clean juice. The main steps are liming and carbonation. First, milk of lime (calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2) is added to the raw juice. Lime raises the pH to about 11, which precipitates many impurities. It also neutralizes acids. For every tonne of beet, about 10-20 kg of lime is used. The lime is mixed in a large tank for 10-15 minutes.

Next, carbon dioxide (CO2) gas is bubbled through the limed juice. CO2 reacts with lime to form calcium carbonate (CaCO3), which forms a fine precipitate. This precipitate traps impurities and settles. The process is called carbonation. It is done in two stages: first carbonation to pH 10.5-11, then second carbonation to pH 8.5-9.0. After each carbonation, the juice is filtered to remove the precipitate. The filters are often rotary vacuum filters or plate-and-frame filter presses. The clear juice after first carbonation is called first carbonation juice; after second, it is thin juice.

In African factories, lime is often produced on-site by burning limestone in a kiln. CO2 from the kiln is used for carbonation, saving money. For a small plant, a simple lime slaker and a CO2 compressor are needed. Control pH carefully: too high pH causes sugar loss, too low pH leaves impurities. Temperature should be 60-70Â°C during carbonation. After purification, thin juice has 12-14% sugar and is ready for evaporation. The filter cake (lime mud) can be used as fertilizer. Proper liming and carbonation can remove over 90% of impurities.

4. Pressing and Drying of Pulp

After diffusion, the leftover beet cossettes, called pulp, still contain about 90% water. This wet pulp spoils quickly and is heavy to transport. Pressing and drying turn it into a stable, valuable animal feed. First, wet pulp is pressed in a screw press or hydraulic press to remove excess water. A screw press can reduce moisture from 90% to about 75%. The pressed water, called press water, contains some sugar and is recycled back to the diffusion tower. For every tonne of beet, about 500-600 kg of wet pulp is produced, which after pressing becomes 200-300 kg.

Next, pressed pulp is dried in a rotary drum dryer. Hot air at 300-400Â°C is blown through the drum, evaporating water. The pulp moves through the dryer for 20-30 minutes. The final dried pulp has less than 12% moisture. It is then cooled and stored in sacks or silos. Dried pulp is rich in fiber and is sold as cattle feed. In Africa, many small factories sun-dry pulp on concrete slabs, but this takes 3-5 days and risks contamination. A simple biomass-fired dryer using bagasse or wood chips is more reliable.

Key parameters: press pressure should be 20-30 bar for good water removal. Dryer temperature must not exceed 400Â°C to avoid burning. Dried pulp should be light brown and smell sweet. If it smells burnt, reduce temperature. For a 50-tonne-per-day plant, a dryer with 2-meter diameter and 10-meter length works well. Maintenance: clean the press screens daily to prevent clogging. Check dryer burners weekly. Properly dried pulp can be stored for 6 months and sold for extra income. This turns a waste product into profit.

5. Water Management in Beet Processing

Sugar beet processing uses a lot of water: about 15-20 cubic meters per tonne of beet. In Africa, where water is scarce, efficient management is critical. The main uses are washing, diffusion, and cooling. Washing uses 1-2 mÂ³ per tonne. Diffusion uses 0.5-1 mÂ³ per tonne as fresh water, but most is recycled. Cooling and cleaning use the rest. A factory processing 100 tonnes per day needs 1,500-2,000 mÂ³ daily. Without recycling, this is unsustainable.

The key is to reuse water in a cascade. Clean water is first used for diffusion, then for washing, and finally for cooling or irrigation. Press water from pulp pressing is returned to diffusion. Condensate from evaporators is used for washing. This can cut fresh water use by 70%. Install a settling pond to remove solids before reuse. Use screens to catch pulp. For small plants, a three-pond system works: first pond for heavy solids, second for settling, third for storage. Add lime to adjust pH and kill bacteria.

Monitor water quality: pH should be 6-8, and temperature below 40Â°C before reuse. High salt content can harm diffusion. In dry areas, use drip irrigation with final wastewater for crops like fodder. Rainwater harvesting from roofs can supplement supply. In Kenya, a 50-tonne-per-day factory reduced water use from 1,000 mÂ³ to 300 mÂ³ per day by recycling. This saved money and met environmental rules. Train workers to report leaks and keep valves closed when not in use. Proper water management lowers costs and protects local water sources.

6. Quality Control in Beet Juice Extraction

Quality control ensures that sugar extraction is efficient and the final sugar is pure. Key tests are done at each step. For beet, check sugar content with a refractometer or polarimeter. Good beet has 15-20% sugar. For cossettes, check thickness with a caliper; aim for 3-5 mm. For raw juice, measure pH (5.8-6.2), temperature (70-75Â°C), and sugar content (12-15%). For thin juice after purification, pH should be 8.5-9.0 and purity above 90%. Purity is the percentage of sugar in total dissolved solids.

In the diffusion tower, monitor water flow and cossette level. Take samples every hour. Test for sugar loss in pulp: if pulp has more than 0.5% sugar, adjust temperature or flow. Use a simple titration kit for lime and CO2 levels. For small factories, a handheld refractometer and pH meter are enough. Record all data in a logbook. If pH drops, add lime; if temperature falls, increase steam. In Africa, many factories lack labs, but basic tools cost under $500.

Train one worker as quality officer. They should calibrate instruments weekly. For juice clarity, hold a sample against light; it should be clear, not cloudy. Cloudy juice means poor filtrationâcheck filter cloths. For microbial control, add SO2 at 200-300 ppm in diffusion water. Test for bacteria with simple dip slides. If counts are high, clean tanks with hot water and caustic soda. Final sugar quality depends on this step. A well-run QC program can increase sugar recovery by 5-10% and reduce waste. Keep records for at least one year to trace problems.

Practical Reference: Juice Extraction from Sugar Beet

This practical reference is written for African agribusiness operators, cooperative managers, and technical staff running or planning a beet sugar line. Whether you are commissioning a 500 t/day plant in the Nile Delta, the High Atlas, or the Eastern Cape, the same physics and chemistry govern your juice extraction. This module condenses the operational knowledge behind Beet Washing and Slicing, Diffusion, Juice Purification (Carbonation and Liming), Pressing and Drying of Pulp, Water Management, and Quality Control into a business-first reference. Treat it as the document your shift supervisors pin to the control room wall.

1. Why Juice Extraction Determines Your Margin

In beet processing, roughly 16â18% of the beet mass is sucrose. Every percentage point of sucrose you leave in the pulp is lost revenue. A 1,000 t/day plant losing 0.5% sucrose in pulp loses about 5 t of sugar per day â at USD 500/t, that is USD 2,500/day, or USD 500,000 over a 200-day campaign. Juice extraction is therefore not a "technical" concern; it is the single largest controllable lever on gross margin after beet purchase price. In most African contexts, beet is trucked long distances and delivered warm, so the time between harvest and slicing is critical. Sucrose degrades through respiration and microbial activity; every 24 hours of delay can cost 0.1â0.3% sugar. Build your logistics around the slicer, not the other way round.

2. Beet Washing and Slicing

Beets arrive coated in soil, stones, tops, and trash. Washing removes this with a combination of dry cleaning (de-stoning, de-trashing), high-pressure spray, and a rotating drum or paddle washer. 3. Diffusion Process: Principles and Equipment

Diffusion extracts sucrose from cossettes by leaching with warm water. The driving force is the concentration gradient between cell sap (high sucrose) and surrounding water (low sucrose). In a continuous counter-current diffuser (tower, DDS, or BMA type), cossettes enter at one end and fresh water at the other. By the time cossettes exit, they are exhausted to 0.2â0.5% sugar.
