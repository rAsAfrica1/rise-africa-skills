Lessons

1. Family Members and Relationships

In Arabic, family terms are used daily and show respect. Start with immediate family: (ab) father, (um) mother, (ibn) son, (ibna) daughter, (akh) brother, (ukht) sister. For extended family: (jadd) grandfather, (jadda) grandmother, (amm) paternal uncle, (khal) maternal uncle, (amma) paternal aunt, (khala) maternal aunt. In-laws: (hama) father-in-law, (hamat) mother-in-law, (nasib) brother-in-law, (nasiba) sister-in-law. Practice with your own family tree. In many African countries like Nigeria, Senegal, or Kenya, Arabic is used in Muslim communities. For example, in Kano, Nigeria, you might greet an elder: ' ' (As-salaam alaykum ya amm) - Peace be upon you, uncle. Respond: ' ' (Wa alaykum as-salaam ya ibni) - And peace be upon you, my son. To ask 'Who is this?' say: ' ' (man hadha?) for male, ' ' (man hadhihi?) for female. To introduce: ' ' (hadha akhi) - This is my brother. ' ' (hadhihi ukhti) - This is my sister. Possessive suffixes: - (-i) my, - (-k) your (masc), - (-ki) your (fem), - (-hu) his, - (-ha) her. So 'my father' is (abi), 'your mother' is (umuk). In African contexts, respect is key: use titles like '' for older man, '' for older woman, even if not relatives. Practice by labeling photos of your family with Arabic words. Write 10 sentences: 'This is my...' and 'That is your...'. Use flashcards. Listen to Arabic family dialogues on YouTube. In 30 minutes daily, you can master these terms in a week.

2. Food and Drinks: Ordering at a Restaurant

Eating out in Arabic-speaking Africa? You need these phrases. Common foods: (khubz) bread, (aruz) rice, (lahm) meat, (dajaj) chicken, (samak) fish, (khudar) vegetables, (fawakih) fruits. Drinks: (ma') water, (shay) tea, (qahwa) coffee, (asir) juice, (halib) milk. To order: '...' (urid) I want..., ' ' (min fadlik) please. ' ' (urid dajaj mashwi ma'a aruz) - I want grilled chicken with rice. Waiter may ask: ' ' (madha turid an tashrab?) - What do you want to drink? Answer: ' ' (shay bidun sukkar) - Tea without sugar. In Senegal, thieboudienne is a popular rice dish; in Morocco, tagine. Ask: ' ' (ma huwa al-tabaq al-yawm?) - What is today's dish? For bill: ' ' (al-hisab min fadlik) - The bill please. To say delicious: '' (ladhidh). If you don't want something: ' ' (la urid). Practice role-play with a friend. Use menus from Arabic restaurants. In Egypt, '' (ayiz) means I want in dialect; in Standard Arabic, ''. In Sudan, '' (dayir). Learn both. Numbers: (wahid) one, (ithnan) two, (thalatha) three. Order two teas: ' ' (shayyan min fadlik). Tipping: '' (baqshish). In many African countries, service charge may be included. Always say '' (shukran) thank you. Practice by ordering in a local Arabic restaurant. Write a dialogue: greeting, ordering, asking for bill. Do this daily for a week.

3. Shopping: Clothes, Colors, and Prices

Shopping in Arabic markets like Marrakech or Cairo? Learn these. Clothes: (qamis) shirt, (bantal) trousers, (fustan) dress, (hijab) headscarf, (hidha') shoes, (jaket) jacket. Colors: (abyad) white, (aswad) black, (ahmar) red, (azraq) blue, (akhdar) green, (asfar) yellow. To ask price: ' ' (kam al-si'r?) - How much? Or ' ' (bikam hadha?) - How much is this? Seller: ' ' (ashara dularat) - Ten dollars. Bargaining: ' ' (ghali jiddan) - Too expensive. ' ' (hal yumkin takhfid?) - Can you reduce? ' ' (sa-adfa'a khamsa) - I will pay five. Numbers 1-10: . Sizes: (kabir) large, (saghir) small, (mutawassit) medium. Try on: ' ' (urid an aqis) - I want to try on. ' ' (hal ladayk maqas akbar?) - Do you have a bigger size? In African markets, bargaining is expected. Start at half price. Say: ' ' (a'tini si'ran jayyidan) - Give me a good price. Payment: (naqdan) cash, (bitaqa) card. In some places, mobile money like M-Pesa is used; ask ' ' (hal taqbal mobile money?) - Do you accept mobile money? Colors for clothes: (qamis azraq) blue shirt. Practice by listing 10 items in your wardrobe with colors. Visit a local market and practice. Write a shopping dialogue.

4. Directions and Transportation

Getting around in Arabic: directions first. '...' (ayna...?) - Where is...? ' ' (ayna al-mahatta?) - Where is the station? ' ' (ayna al-hammam?) - Where is the bathroom? Directions: (yamin) right, (yasar) left, (mustaqim) straight, (qarib) near, (ba'id) far. ' ' (idhhab yaminan) - Go right. ' ' (thumma istamir mustaqiman) - Then continue straight. Transportation: (sayyara) car, (hafila) bus, (qitar) train, (ta'ira) plane, (darraja) bicycle, (taksi) taxi. To ask for a taxi: ' ' (urid taksi). ' ' (kam al-ujra?) - How much is the fare? In many African cities, shared taxis are common. Say: ' ' (ila al-suq) - To the market. ' ' (qif huna) - Stop here. At the bus station: ' ' (mata yughadir al-bas?) - When does the bus leave? ' ' (al-sa'a al-thalitha) - Three o'clock. For train: ' ' (tadhkira dhahab faqat) - One-way ticket. ' ' (dhahab wa 'awda) - Round trip. In Egypt, microbus is '' (mikrobas). In Morocco, ' ' (taksi kabir) for grand taxi. Practice with a map. Give directions from your home to the market. Use Google Maps in Arabic. Learn 5 phrases daily. Role-play: you are lost, ask a passerby. Write a dialogue. In a week, you'll navigate confidently.

5. Telling Time and Scheduling

Time in Arabic is essential for appointments. ' ' (kam al-sa'a?) - What time is it? Answer: ' ' (al-sa'a al-wahida) - One o'clock. '' (al-thaniya) two, '' (al-thalitha) three, '' (al-rabi'a) four, '' (al-khamisa) five, '' (al-sadisa) six, '' (al-sabi'a) seven, '' (al-thamina) eight, '' (al-tasi'a) nine, '' (al-ashira) ten, ' ' (al-hadiya ashara) eleven, ' ' (al-thaniya ashara) twelve. For minutes: '' (wa) past, '' (illa) to. ' ' (al-thalitha wa rub') - Quarter past three. ' ' (al-thalitha illa rub') - Quarter to three. ' ' (al-thalitha wa al-nisf) - Half past three. AM: (sabahan), PM: (masa'). ' ' (al-sa'a al-thamina sabahan) - 8 AM. Days: (al-ahad) Sunday, (al-ithnayn) Monday, (al-thalatha) Tuesday, (al-arbi'a) Wednesday, (al-khamis) Thursday, (al-jum'a) Friday, (al-sabt) Saturday. Months: (yanayir) January, (fibrayir) February, (maris) March, (abril) April, (mayu) May, (yunyu) June, (yulyu) July, (aghushtus) August, (sibtambar) September, (uktobar) October, (nufambar) November, (disambar) December. To schedule: '' (maw'id) appointment. ' ' (indi maw'id al-sa'a al-ashira) - I have an appointment at 10. ' ' (hal yumkin ta'jil?) - Can we postpone? ' ' (araka ghadan) - See you tomorrow. ' ' (ba'da ghad) - Day after tomorrow. Practice by saying the time every hour. Make a daily schedule in Arabic. Use a clock app in Arabic.

6. Weather and Seasons

Weather talk is small talk. In Arabic: ' ' (kayfa al-taqs?) - How is the weather? ' ' (al-jaww har) - The weather is hot. '' (barid) cold, '' (mu'tadil) moderate, '' (mumtir) rainy, '' (mushmis) sunny, '' (gha'im) cloudy, '' (riyah) windy. Seasons: (al-sayf) summer, (al-shita') winter, (al-rabi') spring, (al-kharif) autumn. In Africa, climate varies: in the Sahel, hot and dry; in East Africa, rainy seasons. ' ' (fi al-sayf al-jaww har jiddan) - In summer, it's very hot. ' ' (fi al-shita' tumtir kathiran) - In winter, it rains a lot. ' ' (uhibb al-rabi') - I love spring. Temperature: ' ' (kam darajat al-harara?) - What is the temperature? ' ' (thalathun daraja) - 30 degrees. To say 'It's cold': ' ' (ash'ur bil-bard). ' ' (ash'ur bil-har) - I feel hot. Weather affects plans: ' ' (idha amtarat, lan nadhhab) - If it rains, we won't go. In many African countries, rainy season means farming. ' ' (mawsim al-amtar) - rainy season. ' ' (mawsim al-jifaf) - dry season. Practice by describing today's weather in Arabic. Listen to weather forecasts in Arabic. Write a 5-day forecast. Use these phrases daily. In a week, you'll chat about weather easily.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Everyday Arabic for African Agribusiness

This practical reference consolidates the seven lessons in the Everyday Vocabulary and Situations module into a single job-ready toolkit. It is written for African agribusiness professionalscoffee exporters in Ethiopia, sesame traders in Sudan, horticulture packhouses in Kenya, livestock aggregators in Nigeria and Niger, and input distributors across the Sahelwho need to negotiate, buy, sell, move goods, and schedule meetings in Arabic-speaking markets. The emphasis is on phrases and structures you can use the same day: at a wholesale market in Cairo, a restaurant in Khartoum, a clothing stall in Kano, a taxi rank in Amman, or a logistics office in Dubai.

Arabic is spoken across North Africa and the Middle East, and it is a working language of the African Union and the Arab League. For agribusiness, this means access to buyers, transporters, cold-chain operators, and port agents from Nouakchott to Port Sudan. Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) is understood in formal settings and written contracts; Egyptian, Sudanese, Levantine, and Gulf dialects dominate the street. This reference teaches MSA forms that travel well, with notes on regional usage. Where a dialect variant is common, it is flagged.

1. Family Members and Relationships

Agribusiness is relationship business. In Arabic-speaking markets, trust is often established through family and clan networks before contracts are signed. Knowing the terms for relatives helps you place a counterpart in their social context and shows respect.

Business-first tip: When a Sudanese or Egyptian counterpart says (hdha akh this is my brother), it may mean a business associate, not a sibling. Confirm the relationship before assuming a family discount is available.

2. Food and Drinks: Ordering at a Restaurant

Most agribusiness deals in North Africa are closed over a meal. Knowing how to order, ask about ingredients, and pay respects the host and keeps the meeting moving.

  • Menu: (qimat al-tam)
  • I would like: (urd)
  • Without sugar / salt: / (bidn sukkar / mil)
  • Is it halal? (hal huwa all?)
  • The bill, please: (al-isb, min falik)

In Egypt and Sudan, tea (, shy) and coffee (, qahwa) are negotiation tools. Accepting a second cup signals openness to a longer discussion. In the Gulf, cardamom coffee ( ) is served in small cups; holding the cup out for a refill is polite.

Safety warning price and contract negotiation

Never sign an Arabic-language supply contract you cannot read. Ask for a bilingual copy and have it reviewed by a licensed translator. In several North African markets, verbal price agreements at the souk are not legally binding; insist on a written purchase order with quantity, grade, Incoterms, and payment currency (USD, EUR, or local). Beware of cash-only, no receipt offers for seed, fertilizer, or pesticidethese may be counterfeit or unregistered. Cross-check product registration with the national agriculture ministry before payment.

4. Directions and Transportation

Moving produce from farm to port depends on clear directions. Learn these before you land in Khartoum, Cairo, or Casablanca.

  • Where is? (ayna?)
  • Right / left / straight: / / (yamn / yasr / mustaqm)
  • Near / far: / (qarb / bad)
  • Truck / pickup: / (shina / bk ab)
  • Cold storage: (makhzan mubarrad)
  • Port: (mn)
  • Customs: (jamrik)

In Cairo, the ring road ( ) connects the agricultural zones of the Delta to the port of Alexandria. In Sudan, the KhartoumPort Sudan corridor is the main export artery for gum arabic and livestock. Knowing the Arabic names of these corridors helps when speaking with drivers and freight forwarders.

5. Telling Time and Scheduling

Arabic-speaking markets often run on flexible time, but export logistics run on the clock. Use these phrases to confirm deadlines.

  • What time? (f ayy sa?)
  • Now / later / tomorrow: / / (al-n / liqan / ghadan)
  • Morning / afternoon / evening: / / (aban / bad al-uhr / masan)
  • Meeting: (ijtim)
  • Appointment: (mawid)
  • Deadline: (al-mawid al-nih)

Note that the Arabic week starts on Sunday in most countries, and Friday is the day of rest. In Saudi Arabia and the UAE, the weekend is FridaySaturday. Do not schedule loading or inspection on Friday mornings.

6. Weather and Seasons

Weather drives planting, harvest, and transport windows. In the Sahel, the rainy season ( , mawsim al-amr) determines when roads become impassable. In North Africa, the hot dry season (, al-ayf) affects cold-chain costs.

  • Hot / cold: / (r / brid)
  • Rain: (maar)
  • Wind: (r)
  • Drought: (jaff)
  • Flood: (fayan)
  • Harvest season: (mawsim al-id)

Use weather vocabulary when negotiating delivery windows: (bad mawsim al-amr after the rainy season) is a common clause in West African and Sudanese contracts.
