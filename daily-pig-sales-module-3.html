1. Gestation: 114 Days

A sow's pregnancy, called gestation, lasts 114 days on average. That is 3 months, 3 weeks, and 3 days. This number is the foundation of your breeding calendar. If you know the day the sow was served, you can calculate the farrowing date exactly. For example, if a sow is served on January 1st, she will farrow on April 25th. Count 114 days forward. You can use a simple wall calendar and mark the date, or use a phone app like Pig Breeding Calculator. In Africa, where many farmers rely on paper records, a calendar on the wall of the pig house is best. Write the sow's ID and service date in one colour, then count 114 days and write the expected farrowing date in red. This gives you a clear visual reminder. Gestation is divided into three stages: early (0-30 days), middle (30-90 days), and late (90-114 days). During early gestation, the embryos attach to the uterus. Stress, heat, or poor feed can cause them to be reabsorbed. So keep the sow calm and feed her well. In the middle stage, the sow's belly grows slowly. Feed her 2 kg per day of a good sow ration. In the late stage, the piglets grow fast. Increase feed to 2.5-3 kg per day. But do not overfeed, or the sow will get too fat and have difficulty farrowing. Always provide clean water. In hot African climates, make sure the sow has shade and wallows to cool down. Heat stress can cause abortion. Check the sow daily for signs of return to heat, which would mean she is not pregnant. If she returns to heat 21 days after service, she is empty. Re-serve her. If she does not return, she is likely pregnant. Mark the calendar accordingly. A good record system prevents surprises. You will know exactly when to prepare the farrowing pen, when to stop work, and when to expect piglets. This is the first step to daily pig sales.

2. Lactation: 28 Days

Lactation is the period when the sow is producing milk for her piglets. In a batch farrowing system, we wean the piglets at 28 days of age. This is a common practice in many African piggeries because it balances piglet health with sow productivity. At 28 days, piglets are strong enough to eat solid feed and no longer need only milk. The sow's lactation lasts exactly 28 days from farrowing to weaning. So if a sow farrows on April 25th, you will wean the piglets on May 23rd. Mark this on your calendar. During lactation, the sow needs high-quality feed and plenty of water. A lactating sow can drink up to 20 litres of water per day, especially in hot weather. Feed her a lactation diet with 16-18% protein. Give her 2 kg on the day of farrowing, then increase gradually to 5-6 kg per day by the second week. She needs this energy to produce milk. If she does not get enough feed, she will lose body condition and produce less milk, and the piglets will be small and weak. Keep the farrowing pen clean and dry. Change bedding daily. In Africa, use dry grass or wood shavings. Make sure the piglets have a warm, dry creep area with a heat lamp or a simple wooden box with a lid. At 28 days, weaning is done abruptly. Remove the sow from the pen and leave the piglets in the same pen for a few days to reduce stress. This is called 'weaner stay'. The sow goes back to the breeding area. After weaning, she will come into heat within 3 to 7 days. That is the next step. Record the weaning date on your calendar. It marks the end of lactation and the start of the sow's rest period. In a batch system, all sows in a group farrow within the same week, so you wean all litters on the same day. This makes management easier and allows you to clean and disinfect the farrowing house between batches. Daily pig sales depend on a steady flow of weaners. So stick to the 28-day lactation.

3. Weaning to Service: 7 Days

After weaning, the sow needs a short rest before she is served again. In a batch farrowing system, we plan for 7 days from weaning to service. This is the time it takes for the sow's reproductive tract to recover and for her to show a clear heat. The 7-day interval is not fixed for every sow, but in a well-managed batch system, you want all sows to be served within the same week. So you plan for 7 days. For example, if you wean on May 23rd, you will serve the sow on May 30th. Mark this on your calendar. During these 7 days, feed the sow a good flushing diet. Flushing means giving her extra feed to increase ovulation rate. Give her 3 to 4 kg per day of a sow ration. This will help her produce more eggs and potentially more piglets. Also, provide plenty of water and keep her cool. Heat stress can delay heat or cause silent heat. Check the sow twice a day for signs of heat: swollen vulva, mucus discharge, restlessness, and standing still when you press on her back. This is called the standing reflex. When you see these signs, she is ready to be served. In Africa, many farmers use natural mating with a boar. If you have a boar, bring him to the sow. If not, use artificial insemination (AI). AI is becoming more common and can be done by trained technicians. Serve the sow twice: once when you first see standing heat, and again 12 to 24 hours later. This increases conception rate. Record the service date on your calendar. That date is day 0 for the next gestation. So the cycle continues. If a sow does not show heat within 7 days, wait and check again. She may have a silent heat or a cyst. If she does not conceive, she will return to heat in 21 days. But in a batch system, you want to keep the group together. So if a sow fails to conceive, you may need to move her to another batch or cull her. The 7-day window is critical for keeping the batch on schedule. Without it, your farrowing dates will spread out, and you will not have daily pig sales.

4. Total Cycle: 149 Days

The total cycle from one service to the next service is 149 days. That is 114 days gestation plus 28 days lactation plus 7 days weaning to service. This is the heartbeat of your batch farrowing system. If you serve a sow on January 1st, she will farrow on April 25th, wean on May 23rd, and be ready for service again on May 30th. That is 149 days later. Mark this on your calendar. The 149-day cycle allows you to plan everything: feed purchases, labour, pen cleaning, and sales. In a batch system, you group sows so that they all follow the same cycle. For example, you might have 10 sows in Batch A, served on the same day. They will all farrow within a few days of each other, wean on the same day, and be served again on the same day. This synchronisation makes management efficient. You can clean and disinfect the farrowing house between batches. You can also plan to sell weaners or finishers in batches, which attracts better prices from buyers. To keep the cycle running, you need to serve sows on time. If a sow is not served within 7 days after weaning, the whole batch shifts. So you must check for heat diligently. Use a calendar or a record book. Write down the dates for each sow: service date, expected farrowing date, actual farrowing date, weaning date, and next service date. At the end of the 149 days, the sow should be pregnant again. If she is not, you have a gap. In Africa, where pig feed can be expensive, a gap means wasted feed and lost income. So stick to the 149-day cycle. It is the engine of daily pig sales. With 149 days, you can calculate how many sows you need to produce a certain number of piglets per week. For example, if you want to sell 20 pigs per week, and each sow produces 10 piglets per litter, you need 2 sows farrowing every week. Over 149 days, that means about 42 sows in total, but you can manage them in batches. The math is simple: 149 days divided by 7 days per week equals about 21 weeks per cycle. So each sow produces one litter every 21 weeks. Plan your batches accordingly.

5. Marking the Calendar

A breeding calendar is only useful if you mark it clearly and keep it updated. In Africa, many smallholder farmers use a wall calendar or a notebook. Choose a method that works for you. A large wall calendar in the pig house is best because you see it every day. Use different colours for different events. For example, use red for service dates, blue for expected farrowing, green for actual farrowing, and black for weaning. Write the sow's ID or tag number next to each event. At the start of each month, look ahead and mark all the expected dates for that month. For example, if a sow was served on January 1st, mark April 25th as expected farrowing. Then mark May 23rd as weaning, and May 30th as next service. Do this for every sow. If you have many sows, use a batch code. For example, Batch A served on January 1st, Batch B served on January 8th, and so on. Then you only need to mark the batch code on the calendar. Also, keep a record book with details: sow ID, service date, boar used, farrowing date, number of piglets born alive, number of stillborn, weaning date, and next service date. This book is your backup. If the calendar gets lost or damaged, you still have the data. In hot African climates, keep the calendar and book in a dry place, away from rain and rodents. Use a plastic sleeve or a metal box. Every day, check the calendar for tasks: who is due for farrowing, who needs to be checked for heat, who needs to be weaned. This daily check takes only 5 minutes but saves you from missing critical dates. If you use a smartphone, you can set reminders. But do not rely only on the phone; write it down. Technology can fail, but paper does not. Marking the calendar is a simple habit that keeps your batch system on track. It ensures you never miss a service, a farrowing, or a weaning. And that means daily pig sales.

6. What Happens If You Miss a Date

If you miss a date in the breeding calendar, the consequences can be costly. The most common missed date is the service date. If you fail to serve a sow within 7 days after weaning, she will come into heat again 21 days later. That adds 21 days to her cycle, making it 170 days instead of 149. This disrupts the batch. The sow will farrow later than the rest of the group, so you cannot clean the farrowing house between batches. You may have to keep the sow in a separate pen, which takes extra labour and feed. And you will have a gap in piglet production, meaning fewer pigs to sell. Another missed date is the farrowing date. If you are not prepared, the sow may farrow in a dirty pen or without supervision. Piglets can be crushed or chilled. You might lose the whole litter. So you must mark the expected farrowing date and prepare the pen 3 days before. If you miss the weaning date, piglets stay with the sow longer than 28 days. This drains the sow's body condition and delays her return to heat. It also means the piglets eat more of the sow's milk and less solid feed, so they may be slower to grow. In a batch system, all sows should be weaned on the same day. If you miss one, you break the batch. To avoid missing dates, use a calendar and a record book. Check them daily. Set alarms on your phone. Train your workers to recognise the signs of heat and to report any changes. If you do miss a date, act quickly. If a sow is not served on time, check for heat again and serve as soon as possible. If she is already pregnant, do not re-serve. If you miss a farrowing, check the pen immediately and assist if needed. If you miss weaning, wean as soon as you remember, but not later than 35 days. The key is to minimise the damage. A missed date costs money: extra feed, lost piglets, and delayed sales. In Africa, where margins are tight, you cannot afford to be careless. So treat your calendar as a tool of your business. Keep it accurate, and your pigs will reward you with daily sales.

<h2>Practical Reference: The Breeding Calendar</h2>

<p>In a batch farrowing system, the breeding calendar is the single most important management document on your farm. It determines when you breed, when you farrow, when you wean, and when you sell. Get it right, and you can plan cash flow, labour, feed purchases, and market timing with confidence. Get it wrong, and you lose money sometimes a lot of it in empty sow days, missed services, and inconsistent pig flow to market.</p>

<p>This practical reference lesson gives you the tools to build, mark, and manage a breeding calendar for a 149-day cycle: 114 days gestation, 28 days lactation, and 7 days weaning-to-service. It is written for African pig producers running batch systems whether you are managing 10 sows or 100. Every day counts. Every date matters.</p>

<h3>Why the Calendar Is a Business Tool, Not Just a Breeding Tool</h3>

<p>In Africa, pig producers face unique challenges: feed price volatility, inconsistent input supply, disease pressure (especially African Swine Fever), and market access constraints. A well-managed breeding calendar allows you to:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Forecast cash flow:</strong> Know exactly when pigs will be ready for sale and plan your income accordingly.</li>
<li><strong>Batch feed purchases:</strong> Buy feed in bulk when you know how many sows and piglets you will have at each stage.</li>
<li><strong>Plan labour:</strong> Farrowing, weaning, and service weeks require different labour inputs. Batch systems concentrate work, making it more efficient.</li>
<li><strong>Reduce disease risk:</strong> All-in/all-out batch management reduces disease transmission compared to continuous flow systems.</li>
<li><strong>Negotiate better prices:</strong> If you can supply consistent batches of pigs to market, you have more bargaining power with buyers.</li>
</ul>

<p>The 149-day cycle is the backbone of this system. Let us break it down.</p>

<h3>Marking the Calendar: A Practical System</h3>

<p>You need a calendar that works for your farm. It can be a wall chart, a notebook, a whiteboard, or a digital tool. The key is that it must be visible, updated daily, and understood by everyone who works with the pigs.</p>

<p>Here is a simple marking system that works in African farm conditions:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Use a large wall calendar</strong> (one month per page) or a whiteboard with a 12-month grid.</li>
<li><strong>Colour-code each phase:</strong> Red for service/breeding, Blue for gestation, Green for farrowing, Yellow for lactation, Orange for weaning.</li>
<li><strong>Write sow ID numbers</strong> on the date they are served, then mark the expected farrowing date (114 days later), weaning date (28 days after farrowing), and re-service date (7 days after weaning).</li>
<li><strong>Use a batch number</strong> for each group of sows served in the same week. This makes it easier to track and manage.</li>
<li><strong>Review the calendar every Monday morning</strong> with your team. Identify what is happening this week and next week.</li>
<li><strong>Record actual dates</strong> next to expected dates. This helps you identify problems (e.g., sows returning to heat, delayed farrowing).</li>
</ol>

<h4 style="color:#cc0000; margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING: African Swine Fever (ASF) Risk</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;">In African pig production, <strong>African Swine Fever (ASF) is the single biggest threat to your breeding calendar</strong>. If ASF enters your farm, you may lose your entire breeding herd within days. There is no vaccine and no treatment. The only protection is strict biosecurity. When managing your breeding calendar:</p>
<ul style="margin-bottom:0;">
<li><strong>Never bring new breeding stock onto your farm without quarantine.</strong> Isolate new pigs for at least 30 days before introducing them to your herd.</li>
<li><strong>Control all visitors.</strong> No one should enter your pig area without clean boots, clothing, and a footbath.</li>
<li><strong>Do not feed kitchen waste or swill</strong> unless it has been properly cooked (heated to 90C for 30 minutes). ASF spreads through contaminated feed.</li>
<li><strong>Monitor your sows daily.</strong> Sudden deaths, high fever, loss of appetite, or skin discolouration are emergency signs. Report to veterinary authorities immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Keep records.</strong> If ASF hits, your breeding calendar records will help you trace the source and plan recovery.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin-top:10px; margin-bottom:0;"><em>Source: World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), <a href="https://www.woah.org/en/disease/african-swine-fever/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">African Swine Fever</a>.</em></p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist: Breeding Calendar Management</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Set up your calendar before you breed.</strong> Mark expected farrowing, weaning, and re-service dates for every sow before the first service.</li>
<li><strong>Use batch numbers.</strong> Group sows served in the same week under one batch number. This simplifies management and record keeping.</li>
<li><strong>Check for heat twice daily.</strong> From Day 3 after weaning, check sows morning and evening with a boar. Miss the heat, miss the cycle.</li>
<li><strong>Record actual dates.</strong> Write down what actually happens (farrowing date, weaning date, service date) next to the expected date. This is your farm data.</li>
<li><strong>Review the calendar every Monday.</strong> Hold a 15-minute team meeting. Identify what is happening this week and next week. Assign tasks.</li>
<li><strong>Plan feed and labour around the calendar.</strong> Know how many sows are in each phase. Order feed accordingly. Schedule extra labour for farrowing week.</li>
<li><strong>Monitor for returns to heat.</strong> A sow that returns to heat 18-24 days after service was not pregnant. Re-breed her immediately and record the reason.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a 12-month view.</strong> Use a wall chart or whiteboard that shows the whole year. This helps you plan cash flow, feed purchases, and market sales.</li>
</ol>
