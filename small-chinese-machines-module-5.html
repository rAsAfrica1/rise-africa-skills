Lessons

1. Calculating the True Landed Cost

When you buy a small Chinese machine, the price you see on Alibaba or from a local dealer is never the final amount you pay. The true landed cost includes the machine price, shipping, import duties, clearing fees, transport to your workshop, and any minor repairs or missing parts. For example, a block-making machine advertised at $1,200 FOB (Free On Board) from China will cost you more. Sea freight to Mombasa or Lagos might be $300, import duty at 25% of the CIF value (cost, insurance, freight) adds another $375, clearing agent fees $150, and local transport $100. Your true landed cost is now $2,125 â nearly double the sticker price. Always ask for a CIF quote and then calculate duties yourself using your country's tariff code. In Kenya, for instance, many small machines fall under HS code 8474 with a 25% duty and 16% VAT. In Nigeria, duty can be 10-20% plus 7.5% VAT. Use a simple spreadsheet: list machine cost, freight, insurance, duty, VAT, clearing, transport, and a 10% contingency for surprises. If you are buying locally from a dealer, the landed cost is already baked into their price, but you should still ask what warranty and spare parts are included. Never forget the cost of power: a 3-phase machine may need a transformer or generator, adding $500-$1,500. Also budget for installation and training. A true landed cost calculation prevents you from sinking all your money into the machine and having nothing left to operate. Write down every number. If you cannot afford the true landed cost, you cannot afford the machine.

2. Micro-Leasing and Asset Financing Options

Micro-leasing is when a company buys the machine and rents it to you for a weekly or monthly fee, with the option to own it at the end. In many African countries, specialized asset finance companies like Rentworks in South Africa, or microfinance banks in Kenya and Nigeria, offer this. For example, a small maize mill costing $2,000 might be leased for $80 per week over 12 months. At the end, you pay a balloon payment of $200 and own it. The total cost is $4,160 â double the cash price â but you did not need collateral or a large lump sum. Asset financing works similarly but is often structured as a hire purchase. You pay a 20-30% deposit, then fixed monthly installments over 12-24 months. The machine itself is the collateral. In Ghana, companies like Sinapi Aba Trust offer asset financing for agro-processing machines. In Tanzania, CRDB Bank has a 'Imbeju' product for small businesses. The key is to read the contract: check the interest rate (often 2-4% per month), the total cost of credit, and what happens if you miss a payment. Some leases require you to insure the machine. Others charge a penalty of 5% per late installment. Before signing, calculate your monthly profit from the machine. If the machine earns you $300 per month and the lease is $150, you have $150 left for raw materials and your own income. If the lease is $250, you are too close to the edge. Always negotiate a grace period of one month before the first payment. Ask if you can pay weekly instead of monthly to match your cash flow. Micro-leasing is a powerful tool if you treat it as a business partnership, not a rescue loan.

3. Bank Loans and Collateral Requirements

Banks in Africa rarely lend to small businesses without collateral. For a machine loan, the bank will ask for land, a building, a vehicle, or a fixed deposit. If you do not have these, you have three options. First, use a group loan: join a cooperative or savings group where members guarantee each other. In Ethiopia, the 'Equb' system works this way. Second, use a credit guarantee scheme. In Kenya, the Credit Guarantee Scheme for SMEs covers up to 50% of the loan. In Nigeria, the Development Bank of Nigeria provides wholesale funds to banks for on-lending to SMEs. Third, build a credit history with a small loan first. Take a $500 loan, repay it in three months, then ask for $2,000. When you apply, the bank will want a business plan, cash flow projections, and invoices for the machine. They may also require the machine to be insured and registered in the bank's name until you finish paying. Interest rates vary: 12-18% per year in Kenya, 20-30% in Nigeria, 10-15% in South Africa. A $2,000 loan at 18% over 18 months means a monthly payment of about $128. You will pay $304 in interest. That is acceptable if the machine generates at least $400 per month. But if the bank asks for a 20% deposit and you do not have it, do not borrow from a loan shark to pay it. Instead, save for six months. Also, avoid 'loan insurance' that adds 5% to the loan. Ask for a amortization schedule. If the bank officer cannot explain it, walk away. A bank loan is cheaper than micro-leasing but harder to get. Start building your relationship with a bank today, even if you only open a savings account and deposit $20 weekly.

4. Vendor Credit and Dealer Financing

Some Chinese machine vendors and local dealers offer credit directly. This is called vendor credit or supplier financing. For example, a dealer in Nairobi might sell you a $1,500 welding machine and allow you to pay 50% now and the rest over six months with 10% interest. The dealer keeps the machine's serial number registered until you finish paying. This is simpler than a bank loan because the dealer knows the machine and trusts you more than a bank does. To get vendor credit, you need a relationship. Buy small spare parts from the dealer first. Pay cash on time. Then ask for terms. In China, many factories on Alibaba offer 'Trade Assurance' with 30% deposit and 70% before shipping. That is not credit. But some will accept 30% deposit and the balance in 60 days if you have a history. For local dealers, ask for a 'hire purchase agreement'. It should state the total price, deposit, installments, interest, and what happens if you default. In Uganda, companies like Toyota Uganda (for generators) offer leasing through their finance arm. In Senegal, dealers for rice milling machines often give 3-month credit to cooperatives. Always get the agreement in writing. Never rely on a handshake. If the dealer asks for a post-dated cheque or a promissory note, understand that they can sue you if you default. But vendor credit can be the fastest way to get a machine. Use it for machines that will pay for themselves quickly â like a popcorn machine that earns daily cash. Avoid vendor credit for machines that take months to generate income, like a brick-making machine that needs curing time. And always ask: what is the penalty for late payment? If it is more than 2% per month, negotiate.

5. Partnerships and Cooperative Ownership

If you cannot afford a machine alone, partner with others. A cooperative of five tailors can buy one industrial sewing machine for $800, each contributing $160. They share the machine on a schedule: Monday and Tuesday for one, Wednesday for another, and so on. The machine earns more because it is used every day. In Rwanda, the 'Umuganda' spirit of collective work has led to many such cooperatives. In Kenya, chamas (savings groups) often buy assets together. The key is a written agreement. It must state: who contributed how much, how the machine will be used, who maintains it, what happens if someone wants to leave, and how profits are shared. For example, a group of five maize millers buys a $3,000 mill. Each pays $600. They agree that each person mills for two days per week and pays $10 per day into a maintenance fund. After one year, they sell the mill and split the proceeds according to contribution. Or they keep it and continue. Another model is a partnership where one person provides the machine and another provides the workspace and labour. They split profits 50-50. This works for a car wash with a Chinese pressure washer. But partnerships fail when there is no clear leader. Choose a chairperson and a treasurer. Keep a bank account for the machine. Record every payment. If you buy as a cooperative, you may also qualify for government grants or NGO support. In Tanzania, the Small Industries Development Organisation (SIDO) gives matching grants to cooperatives. In Ethiopia, the Federal Micro and Small Enterprise Development Agency supports groups. Always register your group formally if you want to access these funds. Do not let friendship replace paperwork. A simple one-page contract signed by all members is enough to prevent most disputes.

6. Bootstrapping: Starting with One Machine

Bootstrapping means starting with what you have, not what you wish you had. If you only have $200, do not look at $2,000 machines. Look at a $150 manual block-making machine or a $100 popcorn maker. Your first machine should be small enough that you can pay cash and still have money for raw materials. For example, a hand-operated maize sheller costs $80 in Kampala. You can shell 200 kg of maize per day and charge $0.50 per kg. That is $100 per day gross. After paying for transport and your own labour, you save $50 per day. In 40 days, you have $2,000. Now you can buy a motorized sheller. This is the bootstrap cycle: buy small, earn, save, upgrade. Do not take a loan for your first machine. Use your own savings or a small family loan. The goal is to prove the business works before you take on debt. Start by renting the machine out to others. If you buy a welding machine, do not just weld your own gates. Weld for neighbours at $5 per joint. Reinvest every dollar into consumables and spare parts. Keep a notebook of daily income and expenses. After three months, calculate your return on investment. If you spent $200 and earned $600, your ROI is 200%. Now you can approach a bank or lease company with proof. Bootstrapping also means buying used. A used Chinese machine from a local dealer might cost half the price of new. Check the motor, bearings, and belts. If it runs for an hour without overheating, it is likely fine. Buy one machine, not two. Master it. Then expand. Many successful African workshops started with a single sewing machine or a single grinding mill. Your first machine is your teacher. It will show you what breaks, what sells, and what customers want. Only after that should you think about financing a bigger one.

1. The True Landed Cost Formula

Many African buyers compare only the FOB (Free On Board) price quoted by the Chinese supplier. That is a mistake. The true landed cost includes freight, insurance, customs duty, clearing, inland transport, installation, and the cost of financing itself. In Kenya, for example, a machine with a CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) value of KES 200,000 will attract import duty (often 0â25% depending on HS code), VAT at 16%, IDF at 3.5% of CIF, RDL at 2% of CIF, and clearing agent fees. The final landed cost can be 40â70% above the FOB price.

Formula: Landed Cost = FOB + Freight + Insurance + Duty + VAT + IDF + RDL + Clearing + Inland Transport + Installation + Financing Cost.

The lesson is clear: if you budget only the FOB price, you will run out of money before the machine is installed. Always add 40â70% for a realistic landed cost in most African markets.

Some micro-leasing companies and dealers in East and West Africa ask borrowers to sign blank or partially completed loan forms "for processing." This is illegal in most jurisdictions and has been used to inflate interest rates, add hidden fees, or seize machines without notice. Always demand a fully completed contract with the total cost of credit, the APR, the repayment schedule, and the repossession terms written in plain language. Keep a signed copy. If a lender refuses, walk away. Report to the Central Bank or the national consumer protection agency.

4. Vendor Credit and Dealer Financing

Many Chinese machine dealers in Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, and Dar es Salaam now offer in-house credit. The typical structure is 30â50% down payment, with the balance paid over 3â12 months. The effective interest rate is often hidden in the markup. For example, a machine quoted at USD 2,000 cash may be sold on credit at USD 2,400 over 6 months â an effective APR of roughly 40%. Always ask for the cash price and the credit price side by side, then calculate the difference as a percentage of the cash price.

5. Partnerships and Cooperative Ownership

For machines costing more than USD 3,000, a partnership of 3â5 people can spread the risk and the cost. The critical document is a written partnership agreement that covers: who contributes how much, who operates the machine, how revenue is split, how maintenance is funded, what happens if one partner wants out, and how the machine is valued if sold. Without this, partnerships collapse. Cooperatives and SACCOs are often the best route because they already have group guarantee structures and savings history that banks recognize.

6. Bootstrapping: Starting with One Machine

The safest financing is no financing. If you can save and buy one small machine â a maize mill at USD 800, a sewing machine at USD 300, a block maker at USD 1,200 â you avoid interest, collateral, and debt risk. Use the machine to generate cash flow, then reinvest in a second machine. This is the slowest route but the most resilient. Many successful African agribusinesses started with one machine and grew through retained earnings. The key is to keep the machine running at high utilization â at least 60% of available hours â so it pays for itself within 12â18 months.
