1. Chemical Reactions in Clarification: Lime and CO2

When sugarcane juice comes from the mill, it is cloudy and acidic, with a pH around 5.0 to 5.5. It contains tiny particles of bagasse, soil, waxes, gums, and dissolved impurities like organic acids and proteins. Clarification uses two simple chemicals to clean it: lime (calcium hydroxide) and carbon dioxide gas. Lime is a white powder that you mix with water to make milk of lime. When you add it to the juice, it raises the pH and reacts with acids to form insoluble salts. For example, lime reacts with phosphoric acid to form calcium phosphate, which traps tiny particles. Then you bubble carbon dioxide gas through the limed juice. CO2 reacts with the excess lime to form calcium carbonate (chalk), which is a fine precipitate. This chalk also captures impurities and drags them down. The key reactions are: Ca(OH)2 + CO2 â CaCO3 + H2O, and Ca(OH)2 + H3PO4 â Ca3(PO4)2 + H2O. In practice, you add lime to raise the pH to about 7.0â7.5, then add CO2 until the pH drops to about 6.8â7.0. You need a pH meter to check this. The lime must be fresh and mixed properlyâuse 1â2 kg of lime per tonne of cane, depending on juice quality. CO2 comes from a boiler or a lime kiln; you need a good sparger to make fine bubbles. The temperature should be 60â70Â°C for best reaction. After this treatment, the juice goes to a clarifier or filter to separate the mud. Remember, too much lime can make the juice dark and increase scaling; too little leaves impurities. So measure carefully. This step is the backbone of sugar processingâif you get it right, the rest becomes easier.

2. Defecation and Carbonation Processes

Defecation and carbonation are two main methods to clarify sugarcane juice. Defecation is simpler and cheaper: you add lime to raise the pH to about 7.0â7.5, heat the juice to 100â105Â°C, and let the impurities settle as mud. The mud is removed, and the clear juice goes to evaporation. This method works well for small-scale jaggery or brown sugar production, common in many African countries like Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania. You need a heating tank, a settling tank, and a way to remove mud. The drawback is that defecation removes less colour and turbidity than carbonation. Carbonation is more thorough: after liming, you bubble CO2 gas through the juice to form calcium carbonate precipitate, which adsorbs more impurities and colour. The pH is adjusted to about 7.0 with lime, then CO2 is added until pH drops to 6.8â7.0. The carbonated juice is heated to 70â80Â°C and sent to a clarifier or filter. Carbonation removes 30â50% more colour than defecation and produces clearer juice. However, it requires a CO2 source, more equipment, and careful pH control. In Africa, carbonation is used in large factories, while defecation is common in small to medium operations. For a typical 1000 TCD (tonnes cane per day) factory, you need about 1.5â2.5 kg lime per tonne cane and 0.5â1.0 kg CO2 per tonne cane. The mud from defecation can be used as fertilizer, while carbonation mud (calcium carbonate) can be recycled or used in agriculture. Both processes aim to produce clear juice with less than 0.5% suspended solids. Your choice depends on your budget, scale, and desired sugar quality. Always test pH and temperatureâthey are critical.

3. Filtration Techniques: Rotary Vacuum and Pressure Filters

After clarification, the juice contains fine mud that must be removed. Filtration separates the clear juice from the mud. Two common filters are the rotary vacuum filter and the pressure filter. The rotary vacuum filter is a large drum covered with a filter cloth. The drum rotates in a trough of mud slurry. A vacuum inside the drum sucks juice through the cloth, leaving a cake of mud on the outside. A knife scrapes off the cake, which is then disposed of. This filter works continuously and handles large volumesâup to 1000â2000 litres per square metre per hour. It needs a vacuum pump and a receiver. For a small African factory, a rotary vacuum filter of 10â20 mÂ² area can handle juice from 500 TCD. The pressure filter uses a pump to push slurry through filter cloths or plates under pressure (2â5 bar). It is batch or semi-continuous and gives clearer juice, but it is more expensive and needs more maintenance. Pressure filters are good for final polishing. In both cases, you may add filter aid like diatomaceous earth (0.5â1.0 kg per tonne cane) to improve flow and prevent clogging. The mud cake from filtration contains 50â70% moisture; you can dry it and use as fuel or fertilizer. Key parameters: vacuum should be 500â600 mm Hg for rotary filters; pressure should be 3â4 bar for pressure filters. Always check the filter cloth for holes and clean it regularly with hot water and mild acid. In Africa, where spare parts can be hard to get, choose a filter that is simple and robust. Rotary vacuum filters are popular because they are continuous and tolerate variations. But if you need very clear juice for white sugar, a pressure filter after carbonation is better. Train your operators to watch for cloudy filtrateâit means a torn cloth or wrong vacuum.

4. Removal of Impurities and Colorants

Sugarcane juice contains impurities like soil, bagasse, waxes, gums, proteins, and colourants such as melanoidins, caramels, and polyphenols. These must be removed to produce white sugar. Clarification with lime and CO2 removes much of the suspended matter, but dissolved colourants need extra treatment. One common method is adsorption using activated carbon or bone char. Activated carbon is made from coconut shells, wood, or coal. You pass the clear juice through a column of activated carbon; the colour molecules stick to the carbon. For a small plant, you can use 1â2 kg of activated carbon per tonne of sugar. The carbon can be regenerated by heating to 800â900Â°C, but in Africa, it is often used once and replaced. Another method is ion exchange resins, which swap colour-causing ions with harmless ones. These are expensive and need careful regeneration with salt and acid. A cheaper option is sulphitation: you bubble sulphur dioxide (SO2) gas through the juice. SO2 bleaches colour and also lowers pH. You add SO2 until the pH is about 4.5â5.0, then add lime to raise it to 7.0. This is common in India and some African countries for white sugar. However, sulphitation leaves residual sulphites, which some markets dislike. For organic or natural sugar, you avoid chemicals and rely on physical methods like ultrafiltration or microfiltration. These use membranes with tiny pores (0.01â0.1 microns) to trap colourants and microbes. They are costly but produce high-quality juice. In practice, most African factories combine defecation or carbonation with activated carbon or sulphitation. Always monitor colour using a spectrophotometer or simple colour comparison. The goal is to reduce colour to less than 100 ICUMSA units for white sugar. Remember, prevention is better than cure: harvest clean cane, avoid burning, and mill quickly to reduce colour formation. Regular cleaning of equipment also prevents colour build-up.

5. pH Control and Monitoring

pH control is the heart of juice clarification. pH measures how acidic or alkaline a liquid is, on a scale of 0 to 14. Sugarcane juice starts at pH 5.0â5.5. During clarification, you raise it with lime to 7.0â7.5, then lower it with CO2 or SO2 to 6.8â7.0. If pH is too low, impurities like proteins and gums do not coagulate well, and the juice remains cloudy. If pH is too high, you get dark colour, scaling on equipment, and loss of sucrose. So you must monitor pH constantly. The best tool is a digital pH meter with a glass electrode. Calibrate it daily with buffer solutions of pH 4.0, 7.0, and 10.0. Keep the electrode clean and store it in KCl solution. For field use, you can also use pH paper strips, but they are less accurate (Â±0.5 pH). In a factory, install pH meters at the liming tank, carbonation tank, and clarifier outlet. Take readings every 15â30 minutes. A typical target for limed juice is pH 7.2; after carbonation, pH 6.9. Adjust lime addition with a variable-speed pump or manual valve. If pH is rising too fast, reduce lime flow. If it drops, increase lime. For carbonation, control CO2 flow with a valve. Temperature affects pH: at higher temperatures, pH reads lower. So cool samples to 20â25Â°C before measuring. Also, pH electrodes need regular cleaning with acid or pepsin solution to remove protein build-up. In African conditions, where power can be unreliable, keep a backup battery-operated pH meter and spare electrodes. Train one operator as the pH champion. Record all readings in a logbook. A simple control chart can show trends and help you spot problems early. Remember, a 0.2 pH deviation can mean the difference between clear and cloudy juice. So take it seriously.

6. Clarification Efficiency and Troubleshooting

Clarification efficiency measures how well you remove impurities. It is calculated as the percentage of suspended solids and colour removed from the raw juice. For a good plant, you want 90â95% removal of suspended solids and 30â50% removal of colour. To measure efficiency, take samples of raw juice and clarified juice. Measure turbidity with a nephelometer or simple settling test. Measure colour with a spectrophotometer at 420 nm. If efficiency is low, troubleshoot step by step. First, check lime quality: it should be fresh, with at least 90% calcium hydroxide. If lime is old or damp, it reacts poorly. Second, check pH: it should be 7.0â7.5 after liming and 6.8â7.0 after carbonation. If pH is off, adjust. Third, check temperature: 60â70Â°C for carbonation, 100â105Â°C for defecation. If too cold, reactions are slow; if too hot, you may get caramelization. Fourth, check CO2 or SO2 supply: ensure gas flow is steady and bubbles are fine. Fifth, check retention time: juice should stay in the clarifier for 1.5â2.5 hours for proper settling. Sixth, check filter condition: torn cloth, low vacuum, or high pressure can cause cloudy juice. Seventh, check for scaling: calcium carbonate or calcium sulphate scale on heaters and pipes reduces heat transfer and flow. Clean with dilute hydrochloric acid (5%) or commercial descaler. Eighth, check raw juice quality: if cane is stale or burnt, impurities are harder to remove. Mill within 24 hours of harvest. Ninth, check chemical dosing: too much lime causes scaling; too little leaves impurities. Use a jar test to find optimal lime dose. Finally, keep records. A simple log of pH, temperature, lime dose, and efficiency will reveal patterns. In Africa, where cane quality varies, be flexible. Train your team to spot problems early. A clear juice is the first sign of a healthy process.

Practical Reference: Juice Clarification and Purification

In an African sugar factory, clarification is where money is either saved or lost. Raw mixed juice arriving from the mills carries 12â18% dissolved solids, plus suspended bagasse, sand, waxes, gums, colloids and colourants. If these are not removed before evaporation and crystallisation, they cause scale on evaporator tubes, poor crystallisation, high molasses losses and off-colour sugar that struggles to meet export or beverage-grade specifications. This practical reference gives shift supervisors, process chemists and maintenance teams the numbers, checks and troubleshooting logic needed to run a stable, low-cost clarification station.

Most African factories use either a limeâheat defecationâsulphitation route or the limeâCOâ carbonation route. Both depend on the same principle: raise pH with milk-of-lime, heat to coagulate proteins and gums, form a flocculant precipitate that adsorbs colour and suspended matter, then separate the floc by settling, flotation or filtration. Your job is to hold pH, temperature, chemical dose and retention time inside narrow windows so the floc is coarse, fast-settling and easy to filter.
