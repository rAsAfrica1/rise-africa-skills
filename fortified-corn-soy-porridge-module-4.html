4. The Recipe — Step by Step

Everything up to now was theory and preparation. This module is the actual recipe — with exact measurements for a 1 kg home batch, a 10 kg weekly batch, and a 50 kg monthly batch. If you follow this exactly, you will produce a food that meets WFP/UNICEF nutrition standards for children.

Module 4 of 12 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · printable batch cards · video demonstrations · business launch section at the end

Lesson 4.1 — The base ratio: 65% maize, 25% soy, 10% milk powder

This is the ratio used by the World Food Programme and UNICEF in their Corn-Soy Blend Plus (CSB+) — the international standard for child supplementary feeding. Deviate only if you have a specific reason.

The recipe is built on the dry mix, which is what you actually produce and package. Water and cooking are added later.

Ingredient% of baseWhy this amount
Maize flour (whole grain)65%Base energy — cheap, local, familiar taste
Soy flour (full-fat)25%Protein + fat — fills the amino acid gap
Milk powder (full-cream)10%Complete protein, calcium, fat-soluble vitamins

The base always totals 100%. Sugar, oil and premix are added on top of this base — that is why the final weight is higher than the base weight.

Why "base" and not just "final mix"? Because it makes scaling easy. The base ratio is always the same — 65:25:10 — whether you are making 1 kg or 500 kg. The additions (sugar, oil, premix) scale proportionally on top. One recipe, any size.

Why not just add more soy or milk?

You will be tempted. More soy means more protein. More milk means more calcium. Why not use 40% soy and 20% milk?

Three reasons the WFP ratio has held for decades:

  • Taste. Above 30% soy, the porridge becomes overpoweringly beany. Children refuse it. A rejected food has zero nutrition value — no matter what the label says.
  • Anti-nutrients. Soy contains trypsin inhibitors and phytates. At 25% and correctly processed, these are neutralized. At 40%+, tolerance for processing errors falls sharply. One under-roasted batch could make children sick.
  • Cost. Soy costs 20–40% more than maize. Milk powder costs 8–10× more than maize. Every extra percentage of either drives up the price families pay — often past what they can afford.

The 65:25:10 base is the point where nutrition, taste, safety and cost all balance. It is not a compromise — it is the best ratio that exists.

Lesson 4.2 — Adding sugar and oil: the energy-density calculation

Base + sugar + oil is what turns a protein drink into a food that actually makes a child gain weight. This lesson is the calculation behind the amounts.

The three additions

Addition% of basePurpose
Sugar (white, granulated)5%Palatability + quick energy
Vegetable oil (sunflower/soybean)5%Calorie density + fat-soluble vitamin absorption
Micronutrient premix0.5%Vitamins A, D, E, K, C, B-complex, iron, zinc, selenium

These three are measured as a percentage of the base weight, not the final mix. So for 1,000 g of base, you add 50 g sugar, 50 g oil and 5 g premix — giving a final dry mix of 1,105 g.

Why 5% sugar and not 10%?

Sugar is the cheapest way to make the porridge taste good — but it is not free, and too much is actively harmful:

  • Above 10%, the porridge is sickly sweet and children reject it
  • Sugar is an empty calorie — no protein, no vitamins, no minerals. Every gram of sugar is a gram of maize or soy that did not get used
  • Children fed heavy sugar from an early age develop a distorted palate for life — this is a real public health concern

5% sugar is enough to make the porridge palatable without dominating the taste.

Why 5% oil is the difference between success and failure

This is the single most important line in the recipe. Oil is what makes the porridge work. Here is the math:

VersionCalories per 100 g dry mixCalories per 100 ml cooked porridge
Plain maize porridge (no additions)~360~35
Base only (65:25:10)~385~38
+ 5% sugar~405~40
+ 5% sugar + 5% oil~450~45

The oil adds ~45 calories per 100 g of dry mix — which does not sound like much. But a 10 kg malnourished child needs 1,500–2,000 calories per day. To hit that target with porridge:

  • Without oil: the child would need ~4,400 ml of cooked porridge per day — physically impossible
  • With oil: the child needs ~3,300 ml per day — challenging but achievable across 5–6 feedings

Every additional percentage of oil in the recipe pushes the daily volume down further. This is why WFP formulas use 5–8% oil. Skipping oil is the single most common mistake home producers make.

Do not reduce the oil to save money. If you cannot afford 5% oil, make less porridge, not thinner porridge. A half-batch with full oil beats a full batch with none.

Lesson 4.3 — Micronutrient premix: the exact grams per kilogram

The premix is the ingredient that turns this into a truly fortified food. Most home producers skip it because it is not sold in shops — but if you plan to sell to clinics, schools or NGOs, it is mandatory.

The dose

5 g of premix per 1,000 g of base ingredients. That is 0.5% of base weight. This is the standard used by WFP for fortified blended foods.

Why so little? Because the premix is concentrated. A single kilogram of premix contains enough vitamins and minerals to fortify 200 kilograms of porridge base. That is why it is measured in grams, not tablespoons.

How to weigh 5 g without a scale

You probably do not have a jeweller's scale. That is fine. Here are practical equivalents:

  • 1 level teaspoon of premix ≈ 4–5 g (depends on premix density)
  • 1 heaped teaspoon ≈ 6–7 g
  • 1 small water-bottle cap ≈ 5–6 g for most premix powders

For home use, 1 level teaspoon per kilogram of base is close enough. For commercial production, buy a 0.1 g precision scale (~$30 online) and weigh exactly. Accuracy matters when you are selling.

Never guess the premix dose. Vitamin A toxicity is real — too much can cause liver damage, headaches and bone pain. Iron overdose in children is a medical emergency. If you cannot measure accurately, buy less premix and use it in smaller batches with a proper scale.

Why you mix the premix in the flour, not the cooked porridge

Two reasons:

  1. Heat destroys vitamins. Cooking at 100°C for 5+ minutes degrades vitamin C, thiamine and folate. Adding premix to the dry mix means the vitamins are protected until the porridge is eaten.
  2. Consistency. Premix mixed into dry flour distributes evenly through every serving. Premix stirred into a pot at the end can clump or concentrate at the bottom.

Always add the premix to the dry mix — this is a hard rule.

Lesson 4.4 — Mixing dry ingredients: order, method, and how to check for uniform colour

Mixing looks simple — but if you do it wrong, your premix concentrates in one part of the batch and leaves the rest unfortified. Children who eat from the "unfortified" side get no micronutrients. Children who eat from the "concentrated" side risk overdose.

The correct mixing order

  1. Weigh all dry ingredients separately — maize, soy, milk powder, sugar, premix. Do not eyeball.
  2. Combine maize + soy first. Mix for 2–3 minutes until the colour is uniform. These two are both flours and blend easily.
  3. Add milk powder. Mix another 1–2 minutes. Milk powder is finer and lighter — it needs extra time to blend in.
  4. Add sugar. Mix 1 minute. Sugar is granular and heavier — it drops to the bottom if not mixed enough.
  5. Add premix LAST. Mix for 3 full minutes. This is the most important step. Premix is dark and finely milled — you can SEE whether it is evenly distributed.

The colour test

Here is your quality-control check that requires no lab:

  1. After mixing, scoop out 5 small samples from 5 different spots (top, bottom, left, right, center).
  2. Spread each on a white plate.
  3. Look at the colour. All five should look identical.
  4. If any sample looks darker or lighter than the others, mix the whole batch for another 3 minutes and re-test.

This is what flour millers call the "uniformity check." It is the single most useful test a home producer can do.

Mixing methods by batch size

Batch sizeBest methodTime per batch
1 kg (home)Hand mix in a large bowl with a wooden spoon8–10 minutes
10 kg (weekly)Hand mix in a large tub, OR a cement mixer used only for food15–20 minutes
50 kg (monthly)Drum mixer, ribbon blender or cement mixer (food-only)20–30 minutes
200 kg+ (commercial)Ribbon blender — see Business Launch section below30–45 minutes

For anything over 20 kg, hand-mixing becomes unreliable. A cement mixer dedicated to food is the cheapest entry into proper commercial mixing — ~$150–$300, and it works.

Clean between batches. Always wash and dry the mixer between different recipes — especially if you produce multiple products (porridge, flour, spices). Cross-contamination causes off-flavours and can trigger allergies.

Lesson 4.5 — Small-batch version (1 kg): for a single household or clinic day

This is your home recipe. It makes enough dry mix for roughly 20 child servings or 10 adult breakfast servings.

🧪 1 kg Batch (Home)

Maize flour (whole grain)650 g
Soy flour (full-fat)250 g
Milk powder (full-cream)100 g
Base subtotal1,000 g
Sugar50 g
Vegetable oil50 g
Micronutrient premix5 g (1 level tsp)
Final dry mix1,105 g

Yield: ~22 child servings (50 g dry mix each) OR ~14 adult breakfast servings (80 g dry mix).

Approx cost (typical African market): $2.50–$3.50 USD

Prep time: 30 minutes (with pre-ground flours)

Step-by-step production

  1. Wash and dry a large mixing bowl. Dry = no water droplets. Moisture ruins dry mix shelf life.
  2. Weigh 650 g maize flour into the bowl.
  3. Weigh 250 g soy flour on top. Mix with a wooden spoon for 2–3 minutes until uniform.
  4. Weigh 100 g milk powder. Add and mix another 1–2 minutes.
  5. Weigh 50 g sugar. Add and mix 1 minute.
  6. Weigh 5 g premix (or 1 level teaspoon). Sprinkle evenly across the surface — do not dump in one spot. Mix 3 full minutes.
  7. Drizzle 50 g vegetable oil slowly while mixing. Do not pour it all at once. The oil should distribute evenly — the flour should look slightly darker and feel like damp sand, not form lumps.
  8. Do the colour test. If uniform, transfer to a clean, dry, airtight container.
  9. Label with date. Shelf life: 3–4 months in a cool, dry, dark place.

Cooking your 1 kg batch

Do not cook the whole batch at once. Cook only what you will eat in a day.

  1. Measure 55 g of dry mix (one child serving) into a bowl.
  2. Add 100 ml of COLD water and stir into a smooth paste. No lumps.
  3. Boil 200 ml of clean water in a pot.
  4. Slowly pour the cold paste into the boiling water while stirring constantly.
  5. Simmer 5 minutes, stirring frequently. The porridge should thicken.
  6. Remove from heat. Cool to safe eating temperature (test on your wrist).
  7. Optional flavour boost: add 1 spoonful of mashed banana, groundnut paste or a squeeze of lemon (helps iron absorption).
  8. Feed the child 3–5 times a day. Discard any porridge older than 2 hours at room temperature.

Lesson 4.6 — Medium-batch version (50 kg): for a small workshop or NGO distribution

Once you have made 1 kg successfully 10 times in a row, you are ready to scale to 50 kg. This is the size that starts being a business — a 50 kg batch gives ~1,000 child servings and lasts one child about 3 months of daily feeding.

🏭 50 kg Batch (Small Workshop)

Maize flour (whole grain)32.5 kg
Soy flour (full-fat)12.5 kg
Milk powder (full-cream)5 kg
Base subtotal50 kg
Sugar2.5 kg
Vegetable oil2.5 kg
Micronutrient premix250 g
Final dry mix55.25 kg

Yield: ~1,100 child servings (50 g each) OR ~690 adult servings (80 g each).

Approx cost (typical African market): $125–$175 USD (bulk-buying prices)

Cost per child serving: $0.11–$0.16 USD

Retail price per 500 g sachet: $2.00–$3.00 USD → margin of 40–55%

Prep time: 90 minutes with proper equipment

Equipment needed

  • Weighing scale — platform scale up to 60 kg, accuracy 50 g (~$40–$80)
  • Precision scale — 0.1 g accuracy for premix (~$30)
  • Large mixing tub or drum — food-grade, 80+ litres (~$30–$60)
  • Mixing paddle — long-handled, wooden or stainless (~$15)
  • OR cement mixer — dedicated to food (~$150–$300 used)
  • Sealer — impulse sealer for sachets (~$60–$150)
  • Scoop / dispenser — for filling sachets (~$10)
  • Storage bins — food-grade, airtight, 50 L (~$25 each)

Total startup equipment cost: $350–$700 USD. This is the single biggest investment you will make and it will last years.

Production process for 50 kg

  1. Day before: Clean all surfaces, equipment, containers. Dry completely.
  2. Weigh each ingredient separately into labelled bins. Triple-check the weights — a 1 kg error in a 50 kg batch is a 2% misformulation.
  3. Load the mixer in this order: maize → soy → run mixer 3 min → milk powder → run 2 min → sugar → run 2 min → premix → run 3 min.
  4. Add oil slowly while mixer runs. Use a slow drizzle or a pump. Do NOT pour the full 2.5 kg in at once — it will form lumps.
  5. Run mixer 5 more minutes after oil is fully added.
  6. Colour test: scoop 10 samples across the batch. All identical? Good. If not, 5 more minutes.
  7. Dispense into 500 g sachets or 1 kg bags using a scoop or filling machine.
  8. Seal immediately with impulse sealer. Do not leave open.
  9. Label each sachet with: product name, weight, ingredients, batch number, made-on date, best-before date, contact details, and "Not a medicine — supplementary food."
  10. Store in sealed bins in a cool, dry, dark room. Rotate stock — sell oldest first.

Batch number system

Every batch must have a unique number for traceability. Use a simple format:

FCS-20260912-A

  • FCS = Fortified Corn-Soy
  • 20260912 = Date (12 September 2026)
  • A = First batch of that day (B for second, etc.)

Keep a paper batch log. If a customer ever reports a problem, you can trace which ingredients went into that exact batch.

🚀 Business Launch — how to actually start selling

Your first 90 days — from kitchen to first sale

Here is the concrete path. Follow it step by step. Do not skip ahead — each stage funds the next.

Weeks 1–2 — Prove the product

  • Make 5 batches of 1 kg. Give samples to 10 families or 2 clinics.
  • Ask for honest feedback: taste, texture, colour, how children responded.
  • Adjust the recipe if needed. Do NOT change the base ratio — only adjust sugar or oil slightly.
  • Goal: 8 out of 10 families say their child ate it and asked for more.

Weeks 3–4 — Register and license

  • Zimbabwe-specific costs (adjust for your country): Food handler's certificate ~$30. Municipality health certificate ~$50–$100. Company registration ~$150 (agent) or DIY.
  • Required: ZIMRA registration (free), tax clearance certificate before selling to formal retailers.
  • For Kenya, Nigeria, Rwanda: equivalent food safety licenses exist — contact your national food authority (KEBS, NAFDAC, Rwanda FDA).
  • This is the boring part. Do it anyway. Formal buyers — clinics, schools, NGOs — will not engage without it.

Weeks 5–6 — First clinic partnership

  • Take samples + batch documentation to 3 local clinics.
  • Offer 5 kg free for their malnutrition cases, in exchange for feedback and a letter of support.
  • One clinic partner = one institutional customer = recurring monthly orders.

Weeks 7–8 — First paid customers

  • Sell 500 g sachets at $2–$3 each to families referred by the clinic.
  • Accept cash and mobile money (EcoCash, M-Pesa, etc.).
  • Target: $100–$200 revenue in weeks 7–8.
  • Reinvest all revenue into more raw materials.

Weeks 9–12 — Scale to 50 kg/week

  • Buy the mixing tub, sealer and platform scale.
  • Move to 50 kg batches (Lesson 4.6).
  • Target: 5 clinic/school contracts + 30 family customers = $500–$1,000/month revenue by month 3.
  • Hire one part-time helper at $100–$150/month.

Where the money comes from

  • 30% — Family sales — cash, small quantities, immediate
  • 40% — Clinics and schools — bulk orders, 30-day payment terms
  • 30% — NGO contracts — large orders, seasonal, competitive

Focus your first efforts on clinics — they are the fastest path to recurring revenue.
