Lessons

1. Selecting and Preparing Aluminum Frames

Aluminum frames protect the edges of your solar panel, hold the glass and backsheet together, and make mounting easy. In Africa, you'll find two common frame types: mill finish (plain silver) and anodized (coated for corrosion resistance). For coastal or humid areas, always choose anodized. Frames come in standard sizes for 60-cell and 72-cell panels, but you can cut them to length. You'll need a tape measure, hacksaw or mitre saw with a metal blade, file, and a rubber mallet. First, measure your laminated panel's length and width. Add 2 mm clearance on each side for the sealant. Cut four frame pieces: two long (length + 4 mm) and two short (width + 4 mm). Use a mitre box to cut 45-degree angles on both ends of each piece. After cutting, file the ends smooth to remove burrs. Wipe all frame surfaces with a clean cloth and isopropyl alcohol to remove dust and oil. This helps sealant stick. Check that the frame channel (the groove where the panel sits) is free of debris. If you're reusing old frames, scrape out old silicone with a putty knife. Lay out the four pieces on a clean, flat surface like a plywood board. Make sure the mitred corners meet tightly. If gaps are larger than 1 mm, re-cut or file. Pre-drill holes for corner keys if your frame doesn't have them. For small workshops, a simple jig made from wood scraps can hold the frame square while you work. Always wear gloves and safety glasses when cutting aluminum. In many African markets, you can buy pre-cut frame kits from solar suppliers in cities like Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg, which saves time and reduces waste.

2. Applying Silicone Sealant to Frame Edges

Silicone sealant creates a waterproof barrier between the frame and the laminated panel. Without it, rainwater and dust get inside, causing corrosion and electrical faults. Use a neutral-cure silicone (not acetic acid cure, which can corrode metals). In Africa, look for brands like Dow Corning or local equivalents; a 300 ml cartridge costs about $5-10 and does 2-3 panels. You'll need a caulking gun, a cartridge, a utility knife, and a smoothing tool or gloved finger. Before applying, ensure the frame channel and panel edges are clean and dry. Cut the nozzle at a 45-degree angle, about 5 mm wide. Load the cartridge into the gun and squeeze until silicone reaches the tip. Apply a continuous bead of silicone along the inside of the frame channel where the panel will sit. For a 1.6 m long frame, use about 20-30 ml of silicone. Don't be stingy; gaps cause leaks. Start at one corner and move steadily, keeping the nozzle in contact with the surface. If you stop, overlap the previous bead by 2 cm. After applying, immediately place the panel into the frame. Work in sections: do one long side, then the opposite long side, then the short sides. This prevents the silicone from skinning over before assembly. If you're in a hot climate (above 35C), work in the shade and in the morning. Silicone skins over in 10-15 minutes. Use a smoothing tool or your finger dipped in soapy water to press the silicone into the joint and remove excess. Wipe away excess with a dry cloth before it cures. Allow 24 hours to cure before moving the panel. Store unused cartridges in a cool place; they last 6-12 months.

3. Assembling Frame Corners with Corner Keys

Corner keys are L-shaped metal pieces that join the mitred frame corners. They come in two types: screw-fixed (with a set screw) and push-in (friction fit). For durability in African conditions, use screw-fixed keys with stainless steel screws. You'll need a hex key (Allen wrench) or screwdriver, a rubber mallet, and a flat work surface. First, insert a corner key into one frame piece's mitred end. The key should slide in snugly; if it's loose, use a punch to dimple the frame or apply a thin layer of epoxy. Tap gently with a mallet if needed. Then slide the mating frame piece onto the key. Check that the corner is square using a carpenter's square or by measuring diagonals (they should be equal within 1 mm). Tighten the set screw with a hex key until snug. Don't overtighten, as it can strip the aluminum. Repeat for all four corners. Work on a flat table or concrete floor to keep the frame flat. If you're using push-in keys, you may need to crimp the corner after assembly (see Lesson 5). For large panels (72-cell), use two keys per corner for extra strength. After assembly, lift the frame and check for wobbles. If a corner moves, disassemble and add a shim or use a larger key. In many African workshops, you can make your own corner keys from aluminum angle (20x20x2 mm) cut to 50 mm lengths and drilled for screws. This saves money and uses local materials. Always wear gloves to avoid cuts from sharp aluminum edges. Store assembled frames flat to prevent bending.

4. Attaching the Frame to the Laminated Panel

Now you'll place the laminated panel into the frame. The panel consists of glass, EVA, solar cells, and backsheet, already laminated. Handle it by the edges only; oils from your fingers can damage the backsheet. You'll need two people for panels larger than 1 m. Lay the frame face-down on a clean, padded surface (e.g., cardboard or foam). Ensure the silicone bead is still tacky. If it has skinned over, remove it and reapply. With one person at each end, lift the panel and lower it into the frame channel. Align the panel edges with the frame edges; there should be a 2 mm gap on all sides for expansion. Press down gently around the perimeter to seat the panel into the silicone. You should see silicone squeeze out slightly. If not, add more silicone. Check that the panel is centered; measure from panel edge to frame edge at several points. If it's off, lift and reposition. Once seated, apply gentle pressure along all four sides with a roller or your hands. This ensures good adhesion. For small panels (under 50 W), you can do this alone. For larger ones, use suction cup lifters. After seating, flip the panel over (with help) and check the front for any silicone that squeezed onto the glass. Wipe it off immediately with a clean cloth and isopropyl alcohol. Let the panel rest for 10 minutes, then check that the frame is flush with the glass. If the frame sits higher than the glass, the panel may not fit into mounting rails. In that case, remove the panel and check for debris in the channel. Allow 24 hours for the silicone to cure before moving or installing the panel.

5. Crimping Frame Corners for Structural Integrity

Crimping is a mechanical method to lock frame corners permanently. It's used with push-in corner keys or as a backup for screw-fixed keys. Crimping deforms the aluminum into the key, preventing movement. You'll need a crimping tool (also called a corner crimper) or a heavy-duty vice and a hammer. In Africa, you can buy manual crimpers from tool suppliers for $30-50, or make a simple one from a C-clamp and a metal plate. First, ensure the frame is assembled and square. Place the corner in the crimper's jaws. The jaws should press on the outer faces of the frame, not the glass. Tighten the crimper until you see the aluminum deform slightly into the key. You'll hear a slight crunch. Don't over-crimp; it can crack the frame. For a 45-degree corner, crimp at two points: one near the outer edge and one near the inner edge. This creates a strong joint. If using a hammer, place a metal block against the frame and strike it firmly with a 500 g hammer. Two or three strikes per corner are enough. After crimping, check for squareness again. If the corner moved, re-crimp or add a screw. Crimping is especially important for panels installed in windy areas or on vehicles. It also prevents water ingress by keeping the joint tight. For extra protection, apply a small bead of silicone over the crimped area after crimping. Let it cure. Test the frame by twisting it gently; it should not creak or flex. If it does, add more crimps or use a corner bracket. Always wear safety glasses, as aluminum chips can fly. In dusty environments, clean the crimper jaws after use.

6. Cleaning Excess Sealant and Final Inspection

After the silicone has cured (24 hours), clean up excess sealant and inspect the panel. You'll need a plastic scraper, a utility knife, isopropyl alcohol, a clean cloth, and a multimeter. First, use the plastic scraper to gently lift any large blobs of cured silicone from the frame and glass. Don't use metal tools, which can scratch the glass or anodized frame. For thin smears, dampen a cloth with isopropyl alcohol and rub. If silicone is stubborn, use a silicone remover (available at hardware stores) but test on a small area first. Avoid getting alcohol on the solar cells or backsheet. After cleaning, inspect the frame for gaps or cracks. Look at each corner: the crimps should be tight, and no light should pass through. Check the silicone bead around the panel; it should be continuous and smooth. If you find a gap, apply fresh silicone and smooth it. Next, inspect the glass for cracks or chips. Small chips at the edge can be sealed with silicone, but large cracks mean the panel is ruined. Check the backsheet for punctures; seal them with silicone or a patch. Use a multimeter to test the panel's open-circuit voltage (Voc) and short-circuit current (Isc). Compare to the panel's rating. If readings are within 5%, the panel is good. If zero, check connections. Finally, clean the glass with a mild soap solution and a soft cloth. Rinse and dry. Wipe the frame with a dry cloth. Your panel is now ready for installation. Store it upright in a dry place, with cardboard between panels to prevent scratching. In humid climates, add a desiccant packet to the storage area. Record the panel's serial number and test results in a logbook for future reference.

<h2>Practical Reference: Framing and Edge Sealing for Solar Panel Assembly</h2>

<p>In Africa's fast-growing solar assembly market, the frame is not a cosmetic accessory it is the structural spine, the mounting interface, and the first line of defence against moisture ingress. For an assembly business in Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, or Johannesburg, getting framing and edge sealing right directly determines warranty claims, customer trust, and repeat orders from EPC contractors, mini-grid developers, and off-grid distributors.</p>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a single field-ready guide. It assumes you are assembling standard 60-cell or 72-cell crystalline silicon modules (typically 1,650 992 mm or 1,956 992 mm) using anodised aluminium frames and neutral-cure silicone. Every step below is written for a workshop with basic hand tools, a crimping tool, and a clean assembly table.</p>

<h2>1. Selecting and Preparing Aluminium Frames</h2>

<p>Frame selection is a commercial decision as much as a technical one. In African markets, the two dominant profiles are the <strong>35 mm</strong> and <strong>30 mm</strong> anodised aluminium frames. The 35 mm profile is preferred for utility-scale and rooftop projects where wind uplift and mechanical load ratings (typically 5,400 Pa) matter. The 30 mm profile is common for smaller off-grid and portable modules where weight and cost are the priority.</p>

<p>Before assembly, inspect every frame length for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Anodising thickness (should be 10 m for coastal installations in Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, or Durban).</li>
<li>Straightness a 1 mm bow over 2 m will cause glass stress during crimping.</li>
<li>Clean channel aluminium swarf from cutting must be removed with compressed air or a dry brush.</li>
<li>Pre-drilled mounting holes and grounding holes present and deburred.</li>
</ul>

<p>Wipe the frame channel with isopropyl alcohol (IPA) before applying sealant. This removes cutting oil and oxide dust that would otherwise prevent silicone adhesion a leading cause of water ingress and potential-induced degradation (PID) in humid African climates.</p>

<h2>2. Applying Silicone Sealant to Frame Edges</h2>

<p>Use <strong>neutral-cure silicone</strong> only. Acid-cure silicone releases acetic acid during curing, which corrodes the aluminium frame and can attack the EVA encapsulant. In hot African workshops (3545 C), neutral-cure silicone skins over in 1020 minutes, so work in sections of 500 mm maximum.</p>

<p>Apply a continuous bead of 34 mm diameter into the frame channel the U-shaped groove that receives the laminate edge. Do not rely on a thin smear; the bead must compress and fill the gap when the laminate is inserted. Gaps, bubbles, or skips are the number one cause of field failures from moisture ingress.</p>

<h2>3. Assembling Frame Corners with Corner Keys</h2>

<p>Corner keys are the hidden structural connectors. Use <strong>aluminium or stainless steel corner keys</strong> never plastic, which embrittles under UV and thermal cycling. Insert the key into the frame corner, align the mitre, and tap gently with a rubber mallet. The joint should be tight enough that no light passes through the mitre line.</p>

<p>In high-volume workshops, a pneumatic corner press gives consistent insertion depth. In smaller operations, a manual jig with a stop block achieves the same repeatability. Record the insertion depth for traceability buyers increasingly ask for it during factory audits.</p>

<h2>4. Attaching the Frame to the Laminated Panel</h2>

<p>With the frame corners loosely assembled, slide the laminated panel into the frame channel. Work on a clean, padded table. Two workers are recommended for 72-cell modules (2528 kg). Push the laminate gently until it seats evenly on all four sides the edge should sit 12 mm inside the channel, never flush or protruding.</p>

<p>Check diagonal measurements before final tightening. A difference of more than 2 mm between diagonals indicates a twisted frame, which will cause glass stress and micro-cracks during crimping.</p>

<h2>5. Crimping Frame Corners for Structural Integrity</h2>

<p>Crimping locks the corner key inside the frame profile. Use a dedicated crimping tool with the correct die for your frame profile. Crimp at the marked positions typically two crimps per corner on each face. Over-crimping deforms the profile and can crack the glass; under-crimping leaves the joint loose and vulnerable to wind vibration.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:14px 18px; border-radius:6px; margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Crimping and Sealant Handling</strong>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">
<li>Wear safety glasses and cut-resistant gloves when crimping. Aluminium burrs cause serious cuts.</li>
<li>Never crimp over the glass area always crimp on the frame profile only.</li>
<li>Silicone sealant releases vapours during curing. Work in a ventilated area; avoid skin contact (sensitisation risk).</li>
<li>Do not use acid-cure silicone it corrodes aluminium and voids module warranty.</li>
<li>Keep IPA and sealant away from open flames IPA is highly flammable.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2>6. Cleaning Excess Sealant and Final Inspection</h2>

<p>Remove excess silicone immediately with a plastic scraper and IPA-dampened lint-free cloth. Do not use metal blades they scratch the anodising and create corrosion sites. After cleaning, inspect:</p>
<ul>
<li>Continuous sealant line around all four edges no gaps.</li>
<li>Corners flush, crimped, and free of burrs.</li>
<li>Laminate centred and not touching the frame metal directly.</li>
<li>No silicone on the glass surface or busbar area.</li>
<li>Frame label, serial number, and mounting holes intact.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li>Frame profile correct for application (35 mm utility / 30 mm off-grid).</li>
<li>Frame channel cleaned with IPA and free of swarf.</li>
<li>Neutral-cure silicone bead 34 mm, continuous, no gaps.</li>
<li>Corner keys aluminium or stainless inserted fully, mitre tight.</li>
<li>Laminate seated 12 mm inside channel, diagonals within 2 mm.</li>
<li>Corners crimped at marked positions no over-crimp, no glass contact.</li>
<li>Excess sealant removed with plastic scraper and IPA cloth.</li>
<li>Final visual inspection passed; serial number and traceability recorded.</li>
</ol>
