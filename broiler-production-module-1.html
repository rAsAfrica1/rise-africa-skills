1. Understanding the Broiler Value Chain and Market Demand in Africa

Welcome to Module 1 of Broiler Production. This first lesson sets the foundation for everything that follows. Before you buy a single chick or bag of feed, you must understand the business you are entering. Broiler production is not just farming; it is a manufacturing business where you convert feed into meat as efficiently as possible, and then sell that meat into a market that is hungry, growing, and increasingly urban. Across Africa, the demand for chicken meat is rising faster than almost any other protein. Urbanisation, rising incomes, and changing diets mean that more families are buying chicken for Sunday lunch, for weddings, for restaurants, and for fast food outlets. In countries like Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, South Africa, Zambia, and Tanzania, the poultry sector is one of the fastest growing agricultural sub-sectors. Yet local production often cannot meet demand. Imported frozen chicken still fills a large share of the market in many countries, which tells you there is room for local producers who can deliver fresh, affordable, and reliable supply. The broiler value chain has several links. It starts with the grandparent and parent stock farms that produce hatching eggs. Then hatcheries incubate those eggs and sell day-old chicks to farmers like you. Feed mills produce the rations that make up 65 to 70 percent of your production cost. Veterinary input suppliers provide vaccines, drugs, and supplements. You, the broiler grower, are the engine of the chain. After you, there are processors, wholesalers, retailers, and finally consumers. Each link has its own economics, and your profit depends on how well you manage your link and how smartly you connect to the next one. Understanding demand patterns is critical. In most African cities, demand is highest for live birds in informal markets, followed by fresh dressed chicken in butcheries and supermarkets. There is also growing demand from hotels, restaurants, and catering companies. Each buyer has different requirements. The live bird trader wants a healthy bird of about 1.8 to 2.2 kilograms, active and well feathered. The supermarket wants uniform, well-dressed carcasses with proper packaging and cold chain. The restaurant wants consistent size and quality, delivered on schedule. Your production plan must match the buyer you are targeting. Do not produce first and look for market later. That is the fastest way to lose money. Seasonality also matters. Demand for chicken spikes during festive periods such as Christmas, Easter, Eid, and Independence Day celebrations. It also rises during wedding seasons and harvest periods when rural incomes improve. During these peaks, prices can be 20 to 40 percent higher than normal. Smart producers plan their placement dates so that birds reach market weight during these high-demand windows. For example, if Christmas is your target, you count back six weeks from the desired market date and place your chicks then. This simple planning tool can add significant profit to your bottom line. Competition is another factor. You are not alone. There are large commercial integrators, medium-scale farmers, and backyard producers. Large integrators have advantages in feed cost, chick cost, and market access. But they also have high overheads and sometimes slow response to local needs. Small and medium producers can compete by being flexible, by serving niche markets, by delivering fresh local chicken, and by building direct relationships with customers. Many successful African broiler farmers sell directly to consumers through farm shops, WhatsApp groups, and delivery arrangements. This cuts out middlemen and captures more margin. Finally, consider the policy and regulatory environment. Some countries restrict imports to protect local producers. Others have quality standards for poultry meat, environmental rules for waste disposal, and licensing requirements for feed and drugs. Know the rules in your country. Register your business. Keep records. Pay taxes where required. A formal, compliant business can access bank loans, government support programmes, and contracts with institutional buyers that informal producers cannot. In summary, the broiler business in Africa is a growth business. Demand is strong, local supply is often short, and there is room for producers at many scales. But success requires more than just buying chicks. It requires understanding the value chain, knowing your market, planning your production calendar, and positioning yourself competitively. In the next lesson, we will look at the different capital tiers you can enter at, from backyard starter to commercial scale, and how to choose the right one for your situation.

2. Choosing Your Capital Tier: From Backyard Starter to Commercial Producer

One of the most important decisions you will make as a broiler producer is choosing your capital tier. This is not just about how much money you have. It is about matching your resources, your skills, your market access, and your risk tolerance to a production scale that you can manage profitably. Many farmers fail not because broilers are difficult to raise, but because they start too big too fast, or too small to be viable. This lesson will walk you through five practical capital tiers used across Africa, with realistic numbers and expectations. Tier one is the backyard starter. This is where most successful commercial farmers begin. You are looking at 50 to 200 birds per cycle. Capital requirement is low, often between 200 and 800 US dollars equivalent, depending on your country and whether you build or improvise housing. You can use a spare room, a corner of a shed, or a simple pen made from local materials. Equipment includes feeders, drinkers, a charcoal or kerosene brooder, and basic biosecurity items. Feed is bought in small bags from local agro-dealers. Chicks come from a nearby hatchery. At this tier, your main goal is not maximum profit. It is learning. You learn how to brood chicks, how to manage temperature, how to spot disease early, how to calculate feed conversion, and how to sell birds. You also learn the discipline of record keeping. Many people skip this tier and jump straight to 1000 birds. That is a mistake. The skills you gain with 100 birds will save you thousands of dollars when you scale up. Tier two is the small commercial producer. This is 200 to 1000 birds per cycle. Capital requirement ranges from about 1000 to 5000 US dollars. At this level, you need dedicated housing with proper ventilation, a reliable water source, and a small store for feed and equipment. You may hire one helper. You should be buying feed in 50 kilogram bags directly from a mill or major distributor to reduce cost. You should have a vaccination programme and a basic biosecurity protocol. At this tier, you can start supplying local butcheries, restaurants, and individual customers on a regular basis. Your profit per bird may be modest, but with 500 birds per cycle and five to six cycles per year, you can generate a meaningful income. This is the tier where many African farmers build a stable family business. Tier three is the established medium producer. This is 1000 to 5000 birds per cycle. Capital requirement is typically 10,000 to 50,000 US dollars. You need purpose-built broiler houses, automated or semi-automated drinkers and feeders, a feed storage facility, and possibly a small vehicle for delivery. You will employ two to five workers. You should be buying feed in bulk, possibly by the tonne, and negotiating directly with feed mills for credit terms. You should have contracts or standing orders with supermarkets, hotels, or processors. At this tier, you can also consider processing your own birds to capture more value. A small processing unit with a plucker, scalding tank, and cold room can increase your margin significantly. This is also the tier where you can access bank financing, because you have assets, records, and a track record. Tier four is the large commercial producer. This is 5000 to 20,000 birds per cycle. Capital requirement is 50,000 to 200,000 US dollars or more. You need multiple houses, automated feeding and drinking systems, climate control, backup power, and a professional management team. You may integrate backwards into feed production or forwards into processing and distribution. You will be selling to large supermarket chains, processors, and institutional buyers. Your main challenges are biosecurity, labour management, and cash flow. A disease outbreak at this scale can wipe out a year of profit. Tier five is the integrator or industrial producer. This is above 20,000 birds per cycle, often with own hatchery, feed mill, and processing plant. This is a different business model altogether, requiring significant capital and technical expertise. Most readers of this module will operate in tiers one to three, and that is perfectly fine. The key message is this: choose a tier that matches your current capacity, not your ambition alone. It is better to be a profitable 500-bird farmer than a struggling 5000-bird farmer. As you grow, reinvest profits into better housing, better equipment, and better stock. Move up one tier at a time. Also remember that capital tier is not just about money. It is about management capacity. Can you manage five workers? Can you keep records for 5000 birds? Can you negotiate feed credit? Can you handle a disease outbreak? If not, stay at your current tier until you build those skills. Finally, consider your market before you choose your tier. If you have a guaranteed buyer for 200 birds per week, then a 1000-bird cycle every five weeks makes sense. If your market is uncertain, start smaller and build demand as you build supply. In the next lesson, we will look at the numbers: how to calculate cost of production, break-even price, and profit per bird at each tier.

3. The Profit Plan: Cost of Production, Break-Even, and Margins

In this lesson, we get into the numbers. Broiler production is a business, and businesses survive on margins. If you do not know your cost of production, you are farming blind. You might feel busy and see money coming in, but you will not know whether you are actually making a profit until it is too late. This lesson will show you how to build a simple profit plan for your broiler operation, using realistic African figures. We will use a 500-bird cycle as our example, but the same method works for any size. The first step is to list all your costs. These fall into two categories: variable costs and fixed costs. Variable costs change with the number of birds you raise. They include day-old chicks, feed, vaccines and drugs, litter, and casual labour for specific tasks. Fixed costs do not change much with flock size in the short run. They include housing depreciation, equipment depreciation, rent, salaries for permanent workers, electricity, water, and interest on loans. For a 500-bird cycle in a typical African setting, here is a realistic cost breakdown. Day-old chicks at 1 US dollar each equals 500 dollars. Feed is the biggest cost. A broiler eats about 3.5 to 4 kilograms of feed to reach 2 kilograms live weight. With feed at 0.50 US dollars per kilogram, that is 1.75 to 2.00 dollars per bird, or 875 to 1000 dollars for 500 birds. Vaccines and drugs might be 0.10 to 0.15 dollars per bird, so 50 to 75 dollars. Litter and miscellaneous costs might be 50 dollars. Casual labour for brooding and catching might be 50 dollars. Total variable cost is roughly 1525 to 1675 dollars. Now add fixed costs allocated to this cycle. If your housing and equipment cost 3000 dollars and you depreciate over 5 years with 6 cycles per year, that is 100 dollars per cycle. If you pay 100 dollars per month for rent or permanent labour and the cycle is 6 weeks, that is about 150 dollars. Electricity and water might be 50 dollars. Total fixed cost allocated is about 300 dollars. So total cost for the cycle is roughly 1825 to 1975 dollars. Now let us look at revenue. If you sell 480 birds after accounting for 4 percent mortality, and you sell at 4.50 dollars per bird live weight, your revenue is 2160 dollars. Your profit is 2160 minus 1900, which is about 260 dollars per cycle. That is a margin of about 13 percent. If you sell at 5.00 dollars per bird, your profit rises to 500 dollars. If you sell at 4.00 dollars, you make only 20 dollars, and any disease outbreak or feed price increase will push you into loss. This shows you how sensitive broiler profit is to selling price and feed cost. Your break-even price is total cost divided by number of birds sold. In this example, 1900 divided by 480 equals about 3.96 dollars per bird. That is the minimum price you must get to avoid losing money. Anything above that is profit. You should know your break-even price before you place chicks. Write it on the wall of your broiler house. Now, how do you improve your margin? There are only three ways: increase revenue, reduce cost, or both. To increase revenue, you can sell at a higher price by targeting premium markets, selling during festive periods, processing your birds, or selling directly to consumers. You can also reduce mortality so you sell more birds from the same number of chicks. To reduce cost, you can negotiate better feed prices, buy in bulk, improve feed conversion so birds eat less per kilogram of gain, reduce energy costs, and control disease to avoid medication costs. Feed conversion is a key metric. If your birds convert at 1.8 instead of 2.0, you save 0.2 kilograms of feed per bird. On 500 birds, that is 100 kilograms of feed, or about 50 dollars per cycle. Over six cycles, that is 300 dollars per year. Small improvements add up. Record keeping is essential. You must record chick cost, feed used, mortality, medication, labour, and sales. At the end of each cycle, calculate your cost per bird, your revenue per bird, and your profit. Compare cycles. If profit is falling, find out why. Is feed conversion getting worse? Is mortality rising? Is selling price dropping? Fix the problem before the next cycle. Finally, remember that cash flow is not the same as profit. You may be profitable on paper but run out of cash because buyers pay late or because you spent money on fixed assets. Plan your cash flow carefully. Keep a reserve for emergencies. Do not expand too fast. A solid profit plan, reviewed every cycle, is the foundation of a sustainable broiler business. In the next lesson, we will look at housing, equipment, and biosecurity, which are the physical foundations of your profit plan.

4. Housing, Equipment, and Biosecurity for Profitable Broiler Production

Good housing and biosecurity are not optional extras. They are the foundation of broiler profitability. Poor housing leads to stress, disease, poor feed conversion, and high mortality. Weak biosecurity brings disease onto your farm, and one outbreak can destroy an entire cycle. This lesson covers practical, low-cost and medium-cost solutions suitable for African conditions, from backyard to medium commercial scale. Let us start with site selection. Choose a site that is well drained, not prone to flooding, and away from other poultry farms, live bird markets, and busy roads. If possible, keep at least 100 metres from other poultry operations. The site should have reliable water and electricity, or space for a borehole and solar backup. Orient the house east to west so that the sun does not beat directly on the long side, which helps control heat. Good ventilation is critical in hot climates. Now housing design. For a starter flock of 50 to 200 birds, you can use a simple deep litter house with mud or concrete walls, a corrugated iron roof, and wire mesh windows. The floor should be concrete or compacted earth covered with 5 to 10 centimetres of litter such as wood shavings, rice husks, or chopped maize stalks. Litter absorbs moisture, cushions birds, and dilutes droppings. Never use wet or mouldy litter. For 500 to 1000 birds, you need a more permanent structure. A good broiler house is typically 8 to 10 metres wide and any length you need, with a low wall and open sides covered with wire mesh and curtains. Curtains allow you to control ventilation and temperature. In hot weather, you open them. In cold or rainy weather, you close them. The roof should have a ceiling or reflective lining to reduce heat. The floor should be concrete for easy cleaning and disinfection. Space requirements matter. Broilers need about 10 birds per square metre in open-sided houses in tropical conditions. In hot climates, reduce to 8 birds per square metre to prevent overheating. So a 5 by 10 metre house gives 50 square metres, enough for 400 to 500 birds. Do not overcrowd. Overcrowding is one of the most common and costly mistakes. Equipment includes feeders, drinkers, brooders, and lighting. For the first week, use chick feeders and drinkers, then switch to larger ones as birds grow. Provide one feeder space per 25 to 30 birds and one drinker space per 20 to 25 birds. Automatic drinkers and feeders save labour and reduce waste, but they cost more. Start with manual equipment and upgrade as you grow. Brooders are essential for the first two to three weeks. You can use charcoal stoves, kerosene lamps, gas brooders, or electric bulbs. The key is to provide a temperature of about 32 to 34 degrees Celsius in the first week, reducing by about 3 degrees per week until you reach 21 to 24 degrees. Watch the chicks. If they huddle under the brooder, they are cold. If they spread out and pant, they are too hot. If they are evenly spread and active, the temperature is right. Biosecurity is your insurance policy. It has three parts: isolation, traffic control, and sanitation. Isolation means keeping your birds away from other birds, wild birds, and visitors who keep poultry. Do not allow live birds or used feed sacks into your farm. Traffic control means limiting visitors and using footbaths with disinfectant at the entrance of every house. Provide boots and overalls for workers. Sanitation means cleaning and disinfecting the house between cycles. Remove all litter, wash the floor and walls, disinfect, and allow the house to dry for at least three days before bringing in new chicks. Vaccination is part of biosecurity. Follow a standard programme for your area. Typical vaccines include Newcastle disease, infectious bronchitis, Gumboro, and fowl pox. Your vet or extension officer can advise on the right schedule. Keep vaccines cold and use them before they expire. Do not vaccinate sick birds. Record every vaccination. Mortality disposal is also important. Remove dead birds immediately. Burn, bury deeply, or compost them far from the house. Do not throw them into rivers or bushes. This attracts predators and spreads disease. Finally, keep records of everything: feed used, mortality, vaccinations, medication, and sales. Records help you spot problems early and prove your performance to lenders and buyers. Good housing, proper equipment, and strict biosecurity will reduce mortality, improve feed conversion, and increase your profit. They are not costs. They are investments. 5. Feed Management, Health, and Marketing for Maximum Profit

Feed and health are the two biggest drivers of broiler profitability. Feed alone accounts for 65 to 70 percent of your production cost. Health determines how many birds you sell and how efficiently they convert feed. Marketing determines the price you receive. This final lesson brings these three together into a practical action plan. Let us start with feed. Broilers need different rations at different stages. The standard programme is starter, grower, and finisher. Starter feed is high in protein, usually 22 to 24 percent, and is fed from day one to about four weeks. Grower feed is 20 to 22 percent protein, fed from four to six weeks. Finisher feed is 18 to 20 percent protein, fed from six weeks to market. Some producers use only starter and finisher, but a three-phase programme usually gives better feed conversion. Buy feed from a reputable mill. Cheap feed is often poor quality and leads to poor growth and high mortality. Check the feed for freshness, smell, and mould. Store feed in a dry, raised place away from walls. Use pallets or planks. Do not store feed on the floor. Keep it away from chemicals and do not use old pesticide bags. Feed conversion ratio, or FCR, is your key performance indicator. It is the kilograms of feed eaten per kilogram of live weight gained. A good FCR for broilers is 1.6 to 1.8. If your FCR is above 2.0, you are losing money. Causes of poor FCR include poor-quality feed, disease, heat stress, overcrowding, and feed wastage. Fix these and your FCR improves. Water is often overlooked. Birds drink about twice as much water as feed by weight. Clean, cool water is essential. In hot weather, birds drink more and eat less, so growth slows. Provide shade, increase ventilation, and consider adding electrolytes during heat stress. Now health. The main diseases affecting broilers in Africa are Newcastle disease, infectious bronchitis, Gumboro, coccidiosis, and chronic respiratory disease. Newcastle is the most feared. It can kill 50 to 100 percent of an unvaccinated flock. Vaccinate against it. Coccidiosis is a gut disease that causes bloody droppings and poor growth. It is controlled with coccidiostats in feed or with vaccines. Respiratory diseases are often triggered by poor ventilation and ammonia build-up. Keep litter dry and ventilate well. A simple health plan includes: buy chicks from a reputable hatchery, vaccinate on schedule, keep the house clean and dry, control visitors, provide clean water, and remove dead birds immediately. If you see sick birds, isolate them and call a vet. Do not guess with antibiotics. Use the right drug at the right dose for the right duration. Withdraw drugs before slaughter as required by law. Now marketing. You can sell live birds, dressed fresh chicken, or frozen chicken. Live birds are easiest for beginners. You can sell to local traders, neighbours, and at markets. Dressed chicken requires a processing area, clean water, and cold storage. It gives a higher price but also higher cost and more regulation. Frozen chicken requires a cold chain and packaging, but it opens doors to supermarkets and hotels. Choose your market before you place chicks. Build relationships with buyers. Offer consistent quality and reliable supply. Use WhatsApp and social media to advertise. Many African farmers now sell directly to consumers through farm pickup and delivery. This captures the retail margin. Price your birds based on your break-even cost plus a target profit. Do not just match the market price. If the market price is below your break-even, do not sell at a loss unless you have no choice. Instead, find a better market or reduce your cost. Consider processing during festive periods when demand and prices are high. Consider selling manure as a by-product. Poultry litter is valuable fertiliser. It can add 5 to 10 percent to your revenue. Finally, keep records of every cycle. Calculate your cost per bird, revenue per bird, profit, FCR, mortality, and average selling price. Compare cycles and set targets. If mortality is above 5 percent, find out why. If FCR is above 1.8, find out why. If profit is falling, find out why. Continuous improvement is the secret to long-term success in broiler production. This module has given you the foundation: understanding the value chain, choosing your capital tier, building a profit plan, setting up housing and biosecurity, and managing feed, health, and marketing. Now it is your turn. Start small, keep records, learn from every cycle, and grow steadily. The demand for chicken in Africa is rising. With discipline and good management, you can build a profitable broiler business that supports your family and creates jobs in your community. Thank you for completing Module 1. Quick Reference Checklist â Daily and Weekly Actions

  1. Check water first, every morning. If water consumption drops, disease or heat stress follows within 24 hours. Clean drinkers daily.
  2. Walk the flock twice daily. Look for huddling (too cold), panting (too hot), or uneven distribution (draft or overcrowding).
  3. Record feed intake per day. A sudden drop of more than 10% is your earliest disease warning â earlier than mortality.
  4. Weigh 10% of birds weekly. Compare to Table 1. If below African average by 10%, investigate immediately.
  5. Remove dead birds immediately. Record cause if known. A rising mortality curve over 3 days means call a vet, not a supplier.
  6. Check biosecurity daily. Footbath at entrance, no visitors into pens, separate boots for each house.
  7. Calculate your break-even price weekly. Know your cost per bird. Never sell below it, even to clear stock â you are training the market to undervalue your product.
  8. Confirm your buyer before day 28. Do not reach day 42 with 5,000 birds and no buyer. Contract or confirm by day 28 at the latest.
