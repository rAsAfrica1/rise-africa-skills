8. Cooling System Setup for Stationary Use

A car engine cools itself using airflow from vehicle motion plus a fan. A stationary engine gets no motion airflow. You must create the airflow and design the cooling system from scratch. Get it wrong and the engine overheats within 20 minutes of running. This module shows you exactly how to build a reliable stationary cooling system — different steps for petrol and diesel.

Module 8 of 12 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end · Separate petrol and diesel procedures

Lesson 8.1 — Why stationary cooling is harder

In a car, three things cool the engine:

  • Airflow from driving — at 60 km/h, huge volumes of air pass through the radiator
  • Mechanical fan — pulls additional air at low speed
  • Liquid circulation — water pump moves coolant through block, head, radiator

In a stationary engine, you lose the first one entirely. The fan must do all the work. If the fan or radiator is undersized, the coolant temperature rises, the engine loses power, and eventually the head gasket fails or the block cracks.

Ambient tempRequired airflow
20°C~800 m³/h per 10 kW
30°C~1,100 m³/h per 10 kW
40°C~1,500 m³/h per 10 kW

Notice: hotter climate needs much more airflow. In African conditions, design for the hottest day of the year, not the average.

The margin rule: Design your cooling system to keep the engine at 80–90°C on a 40°C day at full load. If it runs hot on a mild day, it will boil over in the field. Oversize the radiator and fan by 20–30%.
Practical exercise 8.1: Note the highest summer temperature in your area. Add 5°C for margin. Design your cooling system for that temperature at full load. This is your design point.

Lesson 8.2 — Radiator placement and airflow

Correct placement is critical. Airflow must pass cleanly through the radiator without recirculation or restriction.

Optimal placement:

  • Front of the frame. Air must enter the front and exit behind.
  • Radiator cap at highest point. Air bubbles rise — they need a clear path to the cap.
  • 200 mm clearance behind the radiator for the fan. Do not crowd.
  • Fan in a shroud that seals against the radiator. This forces air through the core, not around it.
  • Shield the radiator from exhaust heat. Route the exhaust to the side, or add a heat shield.

Airflow path layout:

  1. Air enters the front of the frame unobstructed.
  2. Passes through the radiator core — hot coolant gives up heat.
  3. Fan pulls air through and pushes it back over the engine.
  4. Warm air exits at the back or top of the frame.

Bad placements to avoid:

  • Radiator behind the engine — you push hot air from the engine through the radiator instead of cool air
  • Radiator on top of the frame — hot air pools, poor natural convection
  • Radiator directly behind another radiator — no fresh air reaches the second
  • Radiator with no shroud — fan pulls air around the core instead of through it (30–40% efficiency loss)
Practical exercise 8.2: Position the radiator on the frame. Stand 2 m in front and confirm you can see through the core. The fan should be visible through the core from the front. No obstruction.

Lesson 8.3 — Electric fan conversion (recommended for both petrol and diesel)

Electric fans are better than mechanical fans for stationary engines because they only run when needed, saving fuel and noise.

Fan diameterAirflowPowerCost
250 mm (10")~700 m³/h60 W$25–$60
305 mm (12")~1,200 m³/h100 W$35–$80
355 mm (14")~1,800 m³/h150 W$50–$120
406 mm (16")~2,400 m³/h200 W$70–$180
Twin 305 mm~2,400 m³/h200 W$80–$200

Recommended: one or two 305 mm electric fans with a thermostatic switch.

Wiring the electric fan with thermostatic switch — step by step:

  1. Mount the fan to the radiator using the plastic zip-tie kit or a custom metal bracket. Fan must be flush against the core with the shroud sealing the edges.
  2. Install the thermostatic switch. Two options:
    • Screw-in type — replaces a radiator drain plug or fits a dedicated port
    • Probe type — thin capillary tube inserted between radiator fins
  3. Mount the relay (30 A automotive relay) on the frame, near the battery.
  4. Wire the fan power circuit:
    • Battery positive → 30 A fuse → relay terminal 30
    • Relay terminal 87 → fan positive
    • Fan negative → chassis earth
  5. Wire the fan control circuit:
    • Ignition switch ON → relay terminal 86
    • Relay terminal 85 → thermostatic switch → earth
  6. Test: With ignition ON and engine cold, the fan should be off. Heat the switch (with a heat gun or by running the engine) — fan should kick in when the switch closes.
  7. Fit a manual override switch (bypass the thermostat) so you can run the fan continuously in extreme heat.

Petrol — fan specs

  • Typical airflow: 1,200–2,000 m³/h per 100 kW
  • Single 305 mm fan usually enough for 60–80 kW petrol engines
  • Consider twin fans for larger engines

Diesel — fan specs

  • Typical airflow: 1,500–2,500 m³/h per 100 kW (diesel runs hotter under load)
  • Twin 305 mm or single 405 mm recommended for 2.5–3.0 L diesels
  • Manual override is more important — diesels heat up faster under load
Never rely on a mechanical fan alone. Mechanical fans are designed for a moving vehicle. In a stationary unit, they move much less air than needed. Convert to electric — even if your engine has a mechanical fan fitted.
Practical exercise 8.2 (redo as practical): Buy and install the electric fan with thermostatic switch. Test activation. Confirm fan turns on at 85°C and off at 80°C.

Lesson 8.4 — Radiator cap, expansion tank, and overflow

The radiator cap is not just a lid — it is a pressure valve. A working cap lets the cooling system operate above atmospheric pressure, raising the boiling point and improving cooling.

Cap pressureBoiling point at sea levelBoiling point at 2,000 m
0.9 bar (13 psi)119°C112°C
1.1 bar (16 psi)123°C115°C
1.3 bar (19 psi)126°C118°C

Higher pressure = higher boiling point = more thermal margin. But do not exceed the radiator's rated pressure.

Expansion tank (also called coolant recovery tank):

  • Catches coolant that expands out of the radiator when hot
  • Returns it when the system cools — self-bleeding
  • Prevents air from entering the system
  • Simplifies checking coolant level

Install an expansion tank if your radiator does not have one:

  1. Buy a universal expansion tank ($10–$25).
  2. Mount it beside or behind the radiator — level with or slightly above the radiator cap.
  3. Connect the overflow hose from the radiator cap neck to the tank inlet.
  4. Fill the tank to the LOW line when cold.
  5. Mark the HOT and COLD levels on the tank with a marker.
  6. Check weekly: coolant should be between LOW and HOT when hot.
The cap rule: Replace the radiator cap with a new one rated for 1.1 bar. This one small part is often the cause of mysterious overheating on old radiators. $5 for a cap, $500 in avoided damage.
Practical exercise 8.4: Buy a new 1.1 bar radiator cap. Buy an expansion tank. Install both. Fill the system and check for leaks.

Lesson 8.5 — Coolant type and mixture

Coolant is not optional. It does three jobs:

  • Cools — carries heat away
  • Lubricates the water pump
  • Prevents corrosion inside the block and radiator

Never use plain water except in an emergency.

Coolant typeColourLifeCost per litre
Conventional (IAT)Green2 years$3–$6
OAT (organic acid)Pink/orange5 years$5–$10
HOAT (hybrid)Yellow/blue3 years$5–$9
Ready-mixed (any)VariesAs above$6–$12

Recommended: pre-mixed OAT coolant — 5-year life, best corrosion protection.

Mixture ratio:

  • 50% coolant + 50% water — standard
  • 60% coolant + 40% water — better for hot climates (higher boiling point)
  • 70% coolant + 30% water — extreme heat (not more — higher concentration has worse heat transfer)

Water quality matters:

  • Use distilled or deionised water if available
  • Rainwater is acceptable
  • Borehole water is acceptable if not hard
  • Do not use tap water in areas with very hard water

Petrol cooling tips

  • Petrol engines run cooler at idle but hotter at high RPM
  • Standard 50:50 mix works well in most conditions
  • Check coolant level weekly — petrol engines consume a bit more via the overflow

Diesel cooling tips

  • Diesel engines run hotter under sustained load
  • Consider 60:40 mix in hot climates for the higher boiling point
  • Add a coolant filter if your water is questionable
  • Check coolant level weekly, especially after long runs
Practical exercise 8.5: Buy 5 litres of pre-mixed OAT coolant. Fill your cooling system. Start the engine and run until hot. Top up if needed. Note the running temperature with the engine hot.

Lesson 8.6 — Temperature monitoring and overheating prevention

The final layer of cooling protection is monitoring. Even a perfect system can fail — a leaking hose, a stuck thermostat, a fan failure.

InstrumentCostPriority
Temperature gauge (mechanical or electric)$15–$40Essential
Warning light (high temp)$5–$15Essential
Audible alarm (buzzer)$10–$30Recommended
Infrared thermometer (handheld)$20–$50Recommended

Setup:

  1. Fit a temperature gauge in the dashboard of the frame. Electrical gauge is easier (sensor screws into head or thermostat housing).
  2. Fit a warning light that comes on when coolant exceeds 100°C. Wire to a temperature switch on the head.
  3. Optional: fit a buzzer that sounds when temperature exceeds 105°C. This alerts you if you are away from the pump.
  4. Mark normal operating temperature on the gauge with green tape (typically 80–90°C).
  5. Mark danger zone with red tape (above 100°C).
  6. Train operators to check the gauge every 30 minutes and to shut down if it hits the danger zone.

What to do if it overheats:

  1. Reduce load — throttle down.
  2. Check fan is running. If not, switch on the manual override.
  3. Check coolant level. If low, let it cool before adding.
  4. Check for leaks — hoses, radiator, water pump.
  5. If temperature still rises, shut down the engine. Do not run a hot engine "to see if it cools down."
  6. Let it cool completely before removing the radiator cap. Never open a hot radiator.
Never remove a hot radiator cap. Pressurised steam and boiling coolant will spray out and cause serious burns. Wait 30 minutes minimum, or until the radiator is cool to the touch.
Practical exercise 8.6: Install a temperature gauge and warning light. Run the engine up to operating temperature. Note the gauge reading. Mark normal and danger zones. Then test the warning light by briefly shorting the switch.
