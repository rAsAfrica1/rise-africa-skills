1. Identifying Good Clay Soil by Feel, Colour, and Smell

To find good clay for brick making, start by looking at the soil around you. Good clay soil is usually reddish-brown, grey, or yellowish. Avoid soil that is very dark black or has a lot of white salt patches. Pick up a handful of moist soil and rub it between your fingers. Good clay feels smooth and sticky, not gritty like sand. When you squeeze it, it should hold together in a ball without crumbling. If it feels like plasticine, that's a good sign. Smell the soil after digging a small hole. Fresh clay smells earthy and clean, like wet bricks. If it smells rotten or like sewage, it has too much organic matter and will not make strong bricks.

Another simple test is the ribbon test. Take a ball of moist soil and squeeze it between your thumb and forefinger, pushing out a ribbon. If the ribbon stays intact for more than 5 cm (2 inches) before breaking, the soil has good clay content. If it breaks quickly, there is too much sand. Also, rub a wet sample on your palm. If it leaves a shiny, sticky film, it's clay. If it leaves a gritty residue, it's sandy.

In Africa, termite mounds are often a sign of good clay. Termites bring clay from deep underground. You can dig into a termite mound and test the soil. Also, look for areas where water stands after rain. Clay holds water, so these spots often have good clay. But be careful: if the area floods too much, the clay may be too heavy and need sand.

Avoid soil from the top 15 cm (6 inches) because it contains roots, leaves, and other organic matter. Dig at least 30 cm (1 foot) deep to get clean clay. If the soil is too dry, add a little water to test it. If it's too wet, let it dry a bit. Always test a small sample before digging a large amount. With practice, you will learn to identify good clay by feel, colour, and smell in minutes.

2. Testing Soil for Sand, Silt, and Clay Content Using a Bottle Test

The bottle test is a simple way to find out how much sand, silt, and clay is in your soil. You need a clear glass bottle or jar (at least 1 litre), a ruler, and some water. Fill the bottle one-third with soil from your digging site. Remove stones and roots. Add water until the bottle is three-quarters full. Add a pinch of salt or a drop of dish soap to help separate the particles. Shake the bottle hard for 2 minutes, then set it down where it won't be disturbed.

After 1 minute, mark the level of the first layer that settles. This is sand. After 2 hours, mark the next layer. This is silt. After 24 hours, mark the top layer. This is clay. Measure the thickness of each layer with a ruler. The total thickness of all layers is the total soil sample. To find the percentage, divide each layer's thickness by the total and multiply by 100.

For good bricks, you want about 30-40% clay, 20-30% silt, and 30-50% sand. If clay is less than 20%, the bricks will be weak. If clay is more than 50%, the bricks will crack when drying. If sand is more than 60%, the bricks will crumble. Adjust by adding sand or clay as needed.

In Africa, many soils are laterite, which is rich in iron and clay. Laterite often has good clay content but may need sand to reduce cracking. If your test shows too much clay, add river sand or crushed granite. If too much sand, add more clay from another source. Always repeat the test two or three times from different spots to get an average. Write down your results and keep them for future reference. This test costs almost nothing and helps you avoid wasting time and fuel on bad soil.

3. Digging and Storing Clay Properly During Rainy and Dry Seasons

Digging clay at the right time and storing it well is key to making quality bricks. In the rainy season, clay is wet and heavy, making it hard to dig and mix. Wait for a dry spell of at least 3 days. If you must dig in the rainy season, dig from higher ground where water drains quickly. Use a hoe, shovel, and pickaxe. Remove the top 30 cm (1 foot) of soil and put it aside. Dig out the clay below and pile it on a clean surface, like a tarpaulin or concrete floor.

In the dry season, clay becomes very hard. Water the area the day before digging to soften it. Dig in the early morning or late afternoon to avoid extreme heat. Break the clay into small lumps with a hoe or mallet. If the clay is too dry, add water gradually while mixing.

Storage is important. Never leave clay in the open. In the rainy season, cover the pile with a plastic sheet or tarpaulin to keep it dry. In the dry season, cover it to prevent it from drying out completely. Store clay under a shed or in a covered area. If you have no shed, make a simple roof with poles and grass thatch. Keep the pile off the ground by placing it on a raised platform of stones or wooden pallets. This prevents moisture from the ground and keeps the clay clean.

If you store clay for more than a month, turn it over every two weeks to prevent mould and keep it workable. Add a little water if it dries out. In Africa, many brick makers store clay in pits lined with stones or plastic. This keeps it moist and ready to use. Always keep your clay covered and protected from rain and sun. Proper digging and storage save you time and produce stronger bricks.

4. Mixing Clay with Sand and Water for the Right Consistency

Mixing clay with sand and water is the heart of brick making. The right mix gives you strong, crack-free bricks. Start by testing your clay and sand. A common mix is 3 parts clay to 1 part sand by volume. If your clay is very sticky, use 2 parts clay to 1 part sand. If it's sandy, use 4 parts clay to 1 part sand. Always mix dry first: spread clay on a clean floor, add sand, and mix with a hoe until the colour is uniform.

Then add water slowly. Use a watering can or bucket. For every 10 shovels of dry mix, add about 2-3 litres of water. Mix with a hoe, turning the pile over and over. Add water until the mix holds together when squeezed but doesn't drip. The ideal consistency is like stiff dough or peanut butter. If it's too wet, add more dry clay and sand. If too dry, add water.

Let the mix rest for 30 minutes to 1 hour. This allows the clay to absorb water evenly. Then mix again. You can also tread the mix with your feet (wear boots) to remove lumps. In Africa, many use a pug mill or a simple pit where the mix is trampled. Test the mix by making a ball and dropping it from waist height. If it flattens but doesn't break, it's good. If it shatters, add more clay. If it splats, add more sand.

For quality bricks, the mix should be homogeneous. Any dry lumps will cause weak spots. Mix enough for one day's production. Don't leave mixed clay overnight unless covered with wet cloth. The next day, add a little water and remix. With practice, you will know the right feel. Always keep your tools clean and your mixing area free of stones and roots.

5. Avoiding Common Soil Problems Like Too Much Salt or Organic Matter

Soil problems can ruin your bricks. Two common issues are too much salt and too much organic matter. Salt comes from salty water, coastal areas, or soils with white crusts. Salt causes bricks to crack, crumble, or have white stains (efflorescence). To test for salt, take a soil sample, add distilled water, shake, and taste a drop. If it's salty, don't use that soil. Or, mix soil with water and let it dry on a glass; if white crystals form, there's salt. If salt is mild, you can leach it by soaking the clay in fresh water for 2 days, then draining. But it's better to find another source.

Organic matter includes roots, leaves, manure, and dark topsoil. It burns during firing, leaving holes and weak bricks. To test, put a small sample in a jar with water. Organic matter floats. If more than 5% floats, the soil is bad. Also, smell the soil: if it smells like rotten eggs or manure, it has too much organic matter. Avoid digging topsoil. Dig at least 30 cm (1 foot) deep. If you must use soil with some organic matter, spread it in thin layers and let it dry for weeks, turning it often. Or, burn it slightly to burn off organics, but this is tricky.

Other problems include too much iron (makes bricks red but can cause cracking) and too much limestone (causes popping during firing). In Africa, laterite soils often have high iron, which is fine if mixed with sand. If you see white lumps that fizz when you add acid (vinegar), that's limestone. Remove them. Always test your soil before making bricks. A simple bottle test and a smell test can save you from disaster. If you find salt or organic matter, find a new source or treat the soil. Never use soil from a garbage dump or near a latrine.

6. Using Local Additives Like Rice Husks or Sawdust for Better Bricks

Adding local materials like rice husks, sawdust, or groundnut shells can make your bricks lighter, stronger, and cheaper. These additives are called pore formers or fillers. They burn out during firing, leaving tiny holes that help the brick dry faster and reduce cracking. They also reduce the amount of clay needed, saving you money. Use them at 5-10% of the total dry mix by volume. For example, for 10 buckets of clay-sand mix, add half a bucket to one bucket of rice husks.

Rice husks are common in Africa. They are hard and burn slowly. Use them dry. Sawdust from woodworking shops works well too, but avoid sawdust from treated wood or painted wood because it releases toxins. Groundnut shells, palm kernel shells, and coffee husks are also good. Make sure they are dry and free of dirt. Mix them evenly into the dry clay and sand before adding water. If they clump, break them up.

When using additives, you may need a little more water because the additives absorb moisture. Test the mix by making a brick and drying it. If it cracks, reduce the additive to 5%. If it's too heavy, increase to 10%. Additives also make the brick lighter, which is good for transport. But too much additive (over 15%) makes the brick weak. So stick to 5-10%.

In Africa, many brick makers use rice husks to fire the kiln as well. You can mix ash from burnt rice husks into the clay. Ash acts like a flux, making the brick stronger. Use up to 5% ash. Always store additives in a dry place. Before using, test a small batch of bricks with and without additives to see the difference. With practice, you will find the right amount for your soil. This is a simple way to improve quality and reduce cost.

<h2>Practical Reference: Field Guide to Soil, Clay and Brick Mixes</h2>

<p>This practical reference is the working companion to the RISE AFRICA module "<strong>Understanding Soil and Clay for Quality Bricks</strong>." It is written for the small-scale brick and paver producer who must turn locally available soil into a product that sells. Every test, table and checklist below is designed to reduce rejects, cut fuel costs and protect your reputation in the market.</p>

<p>In most African brick enterprises, raw material (soil and clay) accounts for 6080% of production cost. Getting the soil wrong means cracked bricks, weak pavers, wasted firewood or diesel, and lost customers. Getting it right means you can price confidently, supply consistently, and scale.</p>

<h3>1. Why Soil Testing Is a Business Decision</h3>
<p>Every bag of cement, every litre of water and every hour of labour you spend on a bad soil mix is money lost. A 30-minute bottle test costs almost nothing but can save you thousands in failed production runs. Treat soil testing as quality control, not as a science project.</p>

<h3>2. Identifying Good Clay Soil by Feel, Colour and Smell</h3>
<p>Before you dig, do a quick sensory assessment:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Feel:</strong> Moisten a small ball. Good brick clay feels smooth and slightly sticky, holds together when squeezed, and does not crumble easily. If it feels gritty, it is sandy; if it feels slippery and slimy, it has too much silt or organic matter.</li>
<li><strong>Colour:</strong> Red, reddish-brown and dark grey clays are common in African brick belts. Uniform colour suggests consistent mineral content. Mottled or very pale soils often indicate high salt or lime.</li>
<li><strong>Smell:</strong> Fresh clay smells earthy. A sour, rotten or manure-like smell means high organic matter bad for bricks because it burns out and leaves voids.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. The Bottle Test: Measuring Sand, Silt and Clay</h3>
<p>This is the single most useful field test for a brick producer. It tells you the percentage of each fraction so you can plan your mix.</p>
<ol>
<li>Fill a clear 1-litre bottle one-third with soil, add water to two-thirds, and a pinch of salt or a drop of dish soap as a dispersant.</li>
<li>Shake hard for 3 minutes. Mark the total level.</li>
<li>After 12 minutes, mark the sand layer.</li>
<li>After 2 hours, mark the silt layer.</li>
<li>After 24 hours, mark the clay layer.</li>
<li>Measure each layer as a percentage of the total.</li>
</ol>

<h3>4. Digging and Storing Clay in Rainy and Dry Seasons</h3>
<p>In Africa's two-season climate, how you dig and store clay determines whether you can produce year-round.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Dry season:</strong> Dig when soil is moist but not wet. Stockpile under shade. Cover with tarpaulin or palm fronds to prevent surface cracking.</li>
<li><strong>Rainy season:</strong> Dig from raised areas or use a pit with drainage. Store clay on a raised platform, covered, and never let it sit in standing water. Excess water changes plasticity and causes mould growth.</li>
<li><strong>Ageing:</strong> Let dug clay rest (age) for 37 days. This improves workability and reduces cracking.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Mixing Clay with Sand and Water</h3>
<p>The goal is a homogeneous mix with the right plasticity. A simple field test: roll a ball of mix into a 3 mm thread. If it holds without breaking, the mix is good. If it cracks, add water; if it sags, add sand.</p>

<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING SILICA DUST</strong>
<p>Dry clay and sand contain crystalline silica. Breathing silica dust causes silicosis, an incurable lung disease. Always:</p>
<ul>
<li>Wear a fitted N95 or FFP2 respirator when sieving, mixing or sweeping dry materials.</li>
<li>Work upwind and keep dry material damp to reduce dust.</li>
<li>Never dry-sweep; use water or a vacuum with HEPA filter.</li>
<li>Provide washing facilities and change out of dusty clothes before eating.</li>
</ul>
<p><em>Source: WHO (2018) "Preventing silicosis in small-scale brick and tile production"; ILO (2020) "Safety and health in brickmaking."</em></p>
</div>

<h3>6. Avoiding Common Soil Problems</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>High salt:</strong> White crust on dry soil, poor strength after firing. Leach with water or blend with low-salt soil. Test by tasting a tiny amount salty taste means high salt.</li>
<li><strong>Organic matter:</strong> Dark, spongy soil with sour smell. Causes voids, shrinkage and low strength. Avoid or compost out before use.</li>
<li><strong>High lime:</strong> Fizzes with vinegar. Causes popping and cracking during firing. Blend with clay-rich soil.</li>
</ul>

<h3>7. Using Local Additives for Better Bricks</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rice husks:</strong> 510% by volume. Reduce weight, improve insulation, and provide internal fuel during firing. Common in Nigeria, Tanzania and Uganda.</li>
<li><strong>Sawdust:</strong> 510%. Similar benefits but finer; watch for organic burn-out voids.</li>
<li><strong>Rice husk ash:</strong> 515%. Acts as a pozzolan in stabilised blocks, improving strength and water resistance.</li>
<li><strong>Wood ash:</strong> 35%. Can improve plasticity but check for salt content.</li>
</ul>
<p><em>Source: Practical Action (2021) "Brickmaking: Technical brief"; FAO (2017) "Use of local materials in construction."</em></p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li> Do a bottle test before every new soil source.</li>
<li> Aim for 2030% clay, 2030% silt, 4060% sand for solid bricks.</li>
<li> Age clay 37 days before mixing.</li>
<li> Store clay covered and off the ground in rainy season.</li>
<li> Roll a 3 mm thread to check mix plasticity.</li>
<li> Add 510% rice husks or sawdust for lighter, fuel-efficient bricks.</li>
<li> Reject soil with white salt crust, sour smell or vinegar fizz.</li>
<li> Wear an N95/FFP2 respirator when handling dry clay and sand.</li>
</ol>
