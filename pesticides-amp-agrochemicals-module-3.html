1. Contact vs. Systemic Pesticides: What Works Best

When you spray a pesticide, it either stays on the surface where it lands (contact) or gets absorbed into the plant and moves inside it (systemic). Knowing the difference saves you money and gets better pest control. Contact pesticides kill pests only when they are hit directly by the spray. They work fast but have no residual effect. Examples include pyrethroids like deltamethrin and organophosphates like malathion. These are good for pests that sit on the outside of leaves, like aphids or beetles. But if the pest hides under the leaf or inside the stem, contact sprays miss them. Systemic pesticides are absorbed by leaves or roots and travel through the plant's vascular system. Pests die when they feed on any part of the plant. Examples include imidacloprid and thiamethoxam. These are excellent for sap-sucking pests like whiteflies, mealybugs, and stem borers. In Africa, systemic pesticides are popular for cotton, maize, and vegetables because they protect new growth. However, systemic pesticides can harm pollinators if used on flowering crops. Always check the label for bee warnings. For best results, match the pesticide type to the pest's behaviour. If pests feed openly, contact sprays work. If they hide, use systemic. Also consider cost: contact pesticides are usually cheaper, but systemic ones last longer. In smallholder farms, knapsack sprayers are common. With contact pesticides, you must spray the undersides of leaves thoroughly. With systemics, you can spray the whole plant and the chemical will move. Remember, no pesticide works if the pest isn't there. Scout your field first. Use a hand lens to check for eggs, larvae, or adults. Then choose the right type. Always wear protective gear and follow label instructions.

2. Selective vs. Non-Selective Herbicides

Herbicides are chemicals that kill weeds. Selective herbicides kill only certain types of plants, while non-selective herbicides kill almost all plants they touch. Choosing the right one is critical for your crop. Selective herbicides are designed to kill weeds without harming your crop. For example, 2,4-D kills broadleaf weeds in maize and wheat but does not harm the grass-like maize. Atrazine is another selective herbicide used in maize to control broadleaf and some grass weeds. These work because crops can break down the chemical while weeds cannot. Non-selective herbicides, like glyphosate and paraquat, kill any plant they contact. They are used for land clearing, pre-planting, or directed spraying between crop rows. Glyphosate is systemic and kills the whole plant including roots. Paraquat is contact and burns down foliage quickly. In Africa, non-selective herbicides are often used to clear fields before planting or to control weeds in orchards. But be careful: drift from non-selective herbicides can damage your crop if it lands on leaves. Always spray on a calm day and use a shield if needed. Selective herbicides are safer for crops but may not kill all weed species. You may need a mix or a sequence. For example, in maize, you might use atrazine early and then a grass-specific herbicide like nicosulfuron later. Read the label to see which weeds are controlled. Also, rotate herbicides with different modes of action to prevent resistance. Weeds like pigweed have developed resistance to glyphosate in many countries. If you use the same herbicide every year, resistant weeds will take over. Always calibrate your sprayer to apply the correct dose. Too little won't kill weeds; too much wastes money and can harm the crop. For smallholder farmers, hand weeding is still common, but herbicides save labour. Use selective herbicides when your crop is growing and non-selective ones only before planting or with a shield.

3. Understanding Active Ingredients and Formulations

Every pesticide product has two main parts: the active ingredient (AI) and the formulation. The AI is the chemical that actually kills the pest. The formulation is everything elsesolvents, emulsifiers, carriersthat makes the AI easy to mix, spray, and stick to surfaces. Understanding this helps you choose the right product and use it safely. Active ingredients have common names (like glyphosate) and trade names (like Roundup). The same AI can be sold under many trade names. Always look for the AI on the label. The concentration of AI is given as a percentage or grams per litre. For example, a product might say '480 g/L glyphosate' or '50% WP' (wettable powder). This tells you how much actual chemical is in the container. Formulations come in different types. Emulsifiable concentrates (EC) are liquids that mix with water to form an emulsion. They are easy to handle but can be harmful to skin. Wettable powders (WP) are dry powders that mix with water. They need constant agitation because they settle. Suspension concentrates (SC) are thick liquids that form a suspension. They are safer and easy to pour. Granules (G) are solid particles applied directly to soil. They release slowly. Each formulation affects how you spray. ECs and SCs are good for foliar sprays. WPs can clog nozzles if not mixed well. Granules are used for soil pests. In Africa, common formulations include ECs like cypermethrin 10% EC and WPs like mancozeb 80% WP. Always check the expiry date. Expired pesticides may not work or can become more toxic. Store pesticides in a cool, dry place away from children and animals. When mixing, use the correct dose. For example, if the label says 2 ml per litre, measure with a syringe or measuring cup. Never use more than recommended. Overdosing wastes money and can harm your crop. Also, some formulations require adjuvants like stickers or spreaders to improve coverage. These are added to the spray tank. Read the label for specific instructions. Understanding AIs and formulations helps you compare products and get the best value.

4. Common Active Ingredients in African Markets

African farmers have access to a wide range of active ingredients, but some are more common than others. Knowing these helps you choose effective and affordable pesticides. Here are the most frequently used AIs in African agriculture. For insecticides, pyrethroids like cypermethrin, deltamethrin, and lambda-cyhalothrin are widely available. They are contact insecticides that kill a broad range of pests including aphids, caterpillars, and beetles. They are cheap but can harm beneficial insects. Organophosphates like malathion and chlorpyrifos are also common, especially for stored grain pests. However, chlorpyrifos is banned in some countries due to health risks. Neonicotinoids like imidacloprid and thiamethoxam are systemic and used for sucking pests. They are popular in cotton and vegetables but are toxic to bees. For fungicides, mancozeb and copper-based products (like copper oxychloride) are staples for diseases like late blight in tomatoes and downy mildew in grapes. Mancozeb is a protectant fungicide, meaning it must be applied before disease starts. Systemic fungicides like metalaxyl and triadimefon are also available. For herbicides, glyphosate is the most common non-selective herbicide. Atrazine is widely used in maize. 2,4-D is used for broadleaf weeds in cereals. Paraquat is still used in some areas but is highly toxic and restricted in many countries. For storage pests, phosphine fumigants (like aluminium phosphide tablets) are common. They are dangerous and require professional handling. When buying pesticides, always check for the registration number from your national regulatory authority. In Kenya, look for PCPB registration. In Nigeria, NAFDAC. In South Africa, DALRRD. Avoid buying unregistered or repackaged products. They may be fake or expired. Also, consider the pre-harvest interval (PHI) the time you must wait after spraying before harvesting. For example, cypermethrin has a PHI of 7 days on many vegetables. Always follow the label. If you cannot find a specific AI, ask an extension officer for alternatives. Rotate AIs with different modes of action to prevent resistance. For example, alternate between a pyrethroid and a neonicotinoid for aphid control. This keeps pests guessing and preserves the effectiveness of your chemicals.

5. Reading Pesticide Labels and Safety Data Sheets

Pesticide labels are legal documents. They tell you how to use the product safely and effectively. Ignoring them can lead to crop damage, health problems, or legal trouble. Every label has key sections you must read before buying or using. First, look for the product name and active ingredient. The active ingredient is the chemical that kills the pest. The label also lists the concentration, like '480 g/L glyphosate'. Next, check the signal word: 'DANGER' means highly toxic, 'WARNING' means moderately toxic, and 'CAUTION' means slightly toxic. In Africa, you may also see a skull and crossbones for highly toxic products. The label tells you what pests or weeds the product controls. It also gives the dose rate, like '2 ml per litre of water' or '1 kg per hectare'. Follow this exactly. Using more can harm your crop and leave residues. Using less may not work. The label also states the pre-harvest interval (PHI) the number of days you must wait after spraying before you can harvest and eat the crop. For example, if the PHI is 14 days, do not harvest before then. The label also lists safety precautions: wear gloves, goggles, and a mask. Avoid spraying on windy days. Do not eat, drink, or smoke while spraying. Store the pesticide in its original container, locked away from children and animals. Never store pesticides in food or drink bottles. The Safety Data Sheet (SDS) is a more detailed document. It gives information on toxicity, first aid, firefighting, and spill cleanup. You can request an SDS from the seller or manufacturer. In case of poisoning, the SDS tells you what to do. For example, if someone swallows a pesticide, the SDS may say to induce vomiting or not, depending on the chemical. Always keep the label and SDS handy. If you don't understand something, ask an extension officer. Many African governments require labels in local languages or English. If the label is only in a foreign language, ask for a translation. Remember, the label is there to protect you and your crop. Read it every time, even if you have used the product before.

6. Modes of Action: How Pesticides Kill Pests

Modes of action (MoA) describe how a pesticide kills a pest. Knowing MoA helps you choose the right product and prevent resistance. Pesticides work in different ways, targeting specific systems in the pest. For insecticides, common MoA groups include nerve poisons, muscle poisons, and growth regulators. Pyrethroids and organophosphates attack the nervous system. They cause paralysis and death. Neonicotinoids block nerve signals, leading to paralysis. These are systemic and affect sucking pests. Insect growth regulators (IGRs) like methoprene disrupt the pest's ability to molt or reproduce. They are slow-acting but very targeted. For fungicides, MoA includes respiration inhibitors, sterol biosynthesis inhibitors, and multi-site inhibitors. For example, strobilurins (like azoxystrobin) block respiration in fungal cells. Triazoles (like tebuconazole) inhibit sterol production, weakening the fungal membrane. Multi-site inhibitors like mancozeb attack many sites, making resistance less likely. For herbicides, MoA includes photosynthesis inhibitors, amino acid synthesis inhibitors, and cell membrane disruptors. Atrazine inhibits photosynthesis. Glyphosate inhibits an enzyme (EPSPS) needed for amino acid production. Paraquat disrupts cell membranes. Weeds die because they cannot produce energy or build proteins. Resistance happens when pests survive a pesticide application and reproduce. If you use the same MoA repeatedly, resistant individuals become dominant. To prevent resistance, rotate pesticides with different MoA groups. For example, alternate between a pyrethroid (MoA group 3) and a neonicotinoid (group 4A) for aphid control. Also, use non-chemical methods like crop rotation, biological control, and hand picking. The Insecticide Resistance Action Committee (IRAC) and Herbicide Resistance Action Committee (HRAC) publish MoA classification lists. You can find them online or from extension officers. In Africa, resistance is a growing problem. For example, fall armyworm has developed resistance to some pyrethroids. By rotating MoA, you can keep your pesticides effective. Always read the label to find the MoA group. Many labels now include a group number. Use this to plan your rotation. Remember, no pesticide works forever if used alone. Integrated pest management (IPM) combines MoA rotation with other tactics for long-term control.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Pesticides & Agrochemicals for African Agribusinesses

This practical reference module is designed for agribusiness owners, farm managers, and agro-dealers operating in African markets. It consolidates the essential knowledge from the seven lessons on pesticide types, modes of action, and safe handling into a single, actionable resource. Whether you are managing a maize farm in Kenya, a tomato greenhouse in Nigeria, or a distribution network in Ghana, understanding how pesticides workand how to use them profitably and safelyis critical to protecting yields, margins, and reputations.

Pesticides are not just inputs; they are business decisions. A wrong choice can destroy a crop, harm workers, and trigger regulatory penalties. A right choice can save a harvest and secure premium contracts. This reference guide gives you the technical clarity to make those decisions with confidence.

Contact vs. Systemic Pesticides: What Works Best

Contact pesticides kill pests on direct contact and leave no residual protection inside the plant. They are fast-acting but wash off in rain and require thorough coverage. Systemic pesticides are absorbed by the plant and transported through its vascular system, killing pests that feed on any part of the plant. For African smallholders facing erratic rainfall and labor constraints, systemics often offer better reliability. However, contact pesticides are preferable for late-season applications close to harvest, as they have shorter residual periods. Agribusinesses should match the mode of action to the pest pressure, crop stage, and weather forecast.

Selective vs. Non-Selective Herbicides

Selective herbicides kill specific weed species without harming the cropessential for broad-acre maize, wheat, and rice. Non-selective herbicides (e.g., glyphosate) kill all vegetation and are used for land clearing or pre-planting. In Africa, where intercropping is common, non-selective herbicides can be disastrous if drift occurs. Always calibrate sprayers, use shielded nozzles, and avoid spraying on windy days. For agro-dealers, stocking both types is necessary, but training customers on correct use is a competitive advantage.

Understanding Active Ingredients and Formulations

The active ingredient (AI) is the chemical that kills the pest. The formulation includes solvents, emulsifiers, and carriers that determine how the product mixes, spreads, and persists. Common formulations include emulsifiable concentrates (EC), wettable powders (WP), suspension concentrates (SC), and granules (GR). In African markets, EC and WP are most common due to lower cost, but SC formulations are gaining popularity for safety and ease of handling. Knowing the formulation helps you advise on mixing, nozzle selection, and storage stability.

Reading Pesticide Labels and Safety Data Sheets

Labels are legal documents. In Africa, many products are repackaged or sold in unlabeled containersa serious risk. Always read the label for: active ingredient concentration, formulation type, target pests, crop restrictions, pre-harvest interval (PHI), re-entry interval (REI), and first aid instructions. Safety Data Sheets (SDS) provide detailed toxicity, handling, and disposal information. Agribusinesses should keep SDS files for every product and train staff on label comprehension. Failure to follow label instructions can void insurance, violate national regulations, and endanger lives.

Modes of Action: How Pesticides Kill Pests

Mode of action (MoA) describes the biochemical process by which a pesticide kills or inhibits a pest. Understanding MoA is essential for resistance management. Rotating between different MoA groups prevents pests from developing resistancea growing problem in Africa, especially with cotton bollworm and fall armyworm. Common MoA groups include:

  • Nerve poisons: Pyrethroids, organophosphates, carbamates (e.g., cypermethrin, chlorpyrifos).
  • Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor disruptors: Neonicotinoids (e.g., imidacloprid).
  • Photosynthesis inhibitors: Triazines, ureas (e.g., atrazine).
  • Cell membrane disruptors: Dithiocarbamates (e.g., mancozeb).
  • Growth regulators: Benzoylureas, triazoles.

SAFETY WARNING

Never mix pesticides without wearing personal protective equipment (PPE): gloves, goggles, respirator, long sleeves, and boots. In many African markets, counterfeit or unregistered products are common. Always purchase from licensed agro-dealers and verify the product registration number with your national pesticide authority. Improper handling has caused acute poisoning and death among farm workers. Store pesticides in a locked, ventilated area away from food, feed, and children. Triple-rinse empty containers and puncture them to prevent reuse. Follow national disposal regulations.

Quick Reference Checklist for Agribusinesses

  1. Identify the pest correctly. Misdiagnosis leads to wrong product selection and wasted money.
  2. Read the label before purchase. Confirm active ingredient, formulation, target pest, and crop suitability.
  3. Check registration status. Only buy products registered by your national authority (e.g., KEPHIS in Kenya, NAFDAC in Nigeria).
  4. Rotate modes of action. Avoid using the same MoA group repeatedly to prevent resistance.
  5. Calibrate spray equipment. Incorrect calibration causes under-dosing (resistance) or over-dosing (crop damage, residue violations).
  6. Observe pre-harvest intervals (PHI). Harvesting too early leaves illegal residues and can cost you export contracts.
  7. Train workers annually. Document training for compliance and insurance purposes.
  8. Keep records. Log every application: date, product, rate, weather, operator, and pest pressure. This data drives better decisions and proves stewardship.
