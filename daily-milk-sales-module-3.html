1. Introduction and Why It Matters

The lactation curve is the pattern of milk production from a cow from the day she calves until she dries off. For a dairy herd system selling milk daily, understanding this curve is the difference between profit and loss. A healthy cow typically peaks in milk yield about 6 to 8 weeks after calving, producing up to 20-30 litres per day for a crossbreed like Friesian x Zebu. After that peak, production slowly declines by about 5-10% each month. If you know the curve, you can plan feed, labour, and sales. For example, a cow giving 20 litres at peak will give about 15 litres at month 4 and 10 litres at month 8. Without this knowledge, you might overfeed or underfeed, leading to thin cows and low milk. In Africa, where many farmers rely on daily milk sales for cash flow, missing the peak means missing the highest income period. Also, the curve helps you decide when to rebreed. A cow should be bred again 60-90 days after calving to maintain a 12-13 month calving interval. If she is not bred, her lactation extends but total daily milk drops, and you lose money. The curve also signals health problems: a sudden drop can mean mastitis, foot rot, or poor feed. By recording daily milk with a simple notebook or a phone app, you learn your herd's normal curve. Then you can act fast. In this module, you will learn the tools, steps, mistakes, safety, and practice to master the lactation curve for your daily milk sales.

2. Tools and Materials

To track and manage the lactation curve, you need simple, affordable tools. First, a milk record book or a notebook with columns for date, cow ID, morning milk (litres), evening milk (litres), and total. A hardcover notebook costs less than 5,000 shillings and lasts a year. Second, a measuring jug or graduated bucket, preferably plastic with clear markings up to 30 litres. Metal buckets can dent and hide markings. Third, a permanent marker to label the bucket with cow ID if you have multiple cows. Fourth, a calendar or wall chart to mark calving dates and breeding dates. You can use a simple wall calendar and a red pen. Fifth, a weighing scale for feed, because feed affects the curve. A hanging scale up to 50 kg is useful. Sixth, a thermometer to check for fever if milk drops suddenly. Seventh, a phone with a calculator and a simple app like 'Dairy Record' or even a spreadsheet. If you have no smartphone, a notebook is enough. Eighth, a feed chart showing how much concentrate and forage each cow needs at different lactation stages. For example, a cow giving 20 litres needs about 1 kg of dairy meal per 2.5 litres of milk plus 2 kg for maintenance. Ninth, a body condition score (BCS) chart: you can print one from the internet or draw simple silhouettes. Tenth, a first-aid kit for udder injuries: antiseptic, cotton wool, and gloves. Finally, a whiteboard or chalkboard in the milking shed to write today's totals and compare to yesterday. All these tools cost less than 20,000 shillings total and are available in local agro-vet shops or markets. With them, you can plot your cow's lactation curve on graph paper or just list weekly averages.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to track and use the lactation curve. Step 1: Record calving date. Write it on the calendar and in your notebook. This is day 0. Step 2: Measure milk every milking. Use your graduated bucket. Record morning and evening yields separately. Do this for at least the first 10 days, then weekly if labour is tight. Step 3: Calculate daily total and weekly average. Add morning and evening. For a week, sum all daily totals and divide by 7. Step 4: Plot the curve. On graph paper, put weeks on the x-axis and litres on the y-axis. Mark each weekly average. You will see a rise to peak around week 6-8, then a gradual decline. Step 5: Compare to expected. For a crossbreed, peak should be 15-25 litres. If peak is below 10 litres, check feed, water, and health. Step 6: Adjust feed. As milk rises, increase concentrate by 0.5 kg for every extra litre above 10 litres. For example, if she gives 20 litres, feed 4 kg dairy meal plus 2 kg for maintenance = 6 kg per day, split into two feeds. Step 7: Monitor decline. After peak, milk drops about 5% per month. If it drops more than 10% in a month, investigate: mastitis, worms, or heat stress. Step 8: Decide breeding date. At 60-90 days after calving, if the cow is healthy and giving above 10 litres, breed her. Step 9: Dry off at 7 months pregnant or when milk falls below 5 litres per day. Step 10: Review records. At drying off, calculate total milk for the lactation. Compare to previous lactations. Use this to cull poor producers and keep good ones. Repeat for each cow. This method works for one cow or fifty. The key is consistency: record every day, even if you only note totals.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not recording milk daily. Many farmers guess. Fix: Keep a notebook in the milking shed. Write with a pencil if ink runs. Mistake 2: Feeding the same amount all year. A cow at peak needs more feed than a dry cow. Fix: Use the 1 kg dairy meal per 2.5 litres rule. Adjust weekly. Mistake 3: Ignoring sudden drops. A drop of 3 litres in one day can mean mastitis. Fix: Check udder for heat, swelling, or clots. Strip milk onto a dark cloth to see clots. Call a vet if fever. Mistake 4: Breeding too late. Waiting beyond 90 days reduces lifetime milk. Fix: Mark calendar 60 days after calving. If cow is healthy, breed at observed heat. Mistake 5: Overmilking or incomplete milking. This reduces yield and causes mastitis. Fix: Milk until udder is soft, but no longer than 8 minutes per cow. Use clean hands and a clean bucket. Mistake 6: Poor water access. A cow giving 20 litres needs 80-100 litres of water daily. Fix: Provide clean water at all times. A trough with a float valve is best. Mistake 7: Heat stress. In hot African afternoons, milk drops. Fix: Provide shade, fans, or sprinklers. Mistake 8: Not drying off properly. Cows that are milked until calving have weak calves and low next lactation. Fix: Dry off 60 days before expected calving. Reduce milking frequency over one week. Mistake 9: Using wrong BCS. Thin cows (BCS below 2.5) give less milk. Fix: Score monthly. Feed extra to thin cows. Mistake 10: No records for individual cows. You cannot cull poor performers. Fix: Use ear tags or names. Record per cow. Each mistake has a simple fix. Start with daily recording and correct feeding.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety first: When handling cows for milking or checking the lactation curve, wear clean overalls and boots. Wash hands with soap before milking. Use a clean bucket and a strip cup to check for mastitis. Never milk a cow with a fever or obvious udder infection without gloves. If you suspect mastitis, isolate the cow and milk her last. Dispose of infected milk properly; do not sell it. Best practice for record keeping: Use a pencil and a notebook that stays in the milking shed. At the end of each week, transfer totals to a master sheet. Keep records for at least three lactations. This helps you see patterns. Best practice for feeding: Weigh feed, don't guess. Use a hanging scale. Store feed in dry, raised place to avoid mould. Mouldy feed can cause abortion and milk drop. Best practice for water: Clean troughs daily. A cow drinks 3-5 litres per litre of milk. So a 20-litre cow needs 60-100 litres. Best practice for milking hygiene: Use a single-use paper towel or clean cloth to dry udder. Use teat dip after milking. This prevents mastitis. Best practice for breeding: Keep a breeding calendar. Observe heat twice daily for 30 minutes. Record all heats. If a cow is not bred by 90 days, consult an AI technician. Best practice for drying off: Reduce milking gradually over 7 days. Give dry cow therapy if available. Best practice for heat stress: Provide shade trees or a simple roof. Sprinklers in the afternoon can increase milk by 10-15%. Best practice for safety of people: Never stand directly behind a cow. Use a crush or head gate for examinations. Keep children away from bulls. Finally, keep a first-aid kit with antiseptic, bandages, and gloves. If you get kicked or injured, clean the wound and seek medical help. Following these practices keeps you, your cows, and your milk safe.

6. Practical Exercise

For this exercise, you will track one cow's lactation curve for 4 weeks. If you have no cow, borrow records from a neighbour or use the example below. Step 1: Choose a cow that calved 1-2 weeks ago. Write her ID and calving date. Step 2: Every milking (morning and evening), measure milk with your graduated bucket. Record in a notebook. Step 3: At the end of each day, add morning and evening. Write the total. Step 4: At the end of each week, calculate the average daily milk for that week. Example: Week 1: 12, 13, 14, 14, 15, 15, 16 litres = total 99 litres / 7 = 14.1 litres average. Week 2: 16, 17, 18, 18, 19, 19, 20 = 127 / 7 = 18.1 litres. Week 3: 20, 20, 21, 21, 20, 20, 19 = 141 / 7 = 20.1 litres. Week 4: 19, 19, 18, 18, 17, 17, 16 = 124 / 7 = 17.7 litres. Step 5: Plot these averages on graph paper. You will see a peak at week 3 (20.1 litres) then a decline. Step 6: Compare to expected peak of 6-8 weeks. If your cow peaks earlier or lower, note possible reasons: feed, water, health. Step 7: Calculate feed needed. At peak of 20 litres, feed 20/2.5 = 8 kg dairy meal? No, rule is 1 kg per 2.5 litres above maintenance. Maintenance is 2 kg. So total = 2 + (20/2.5) = 2 + 8 = 10 kg? That is too high. Correct rule: 1 kg dairy meal per 2.5 litres of milk total, but not less than 2 kg. For 20 litres, 20/2.5 = 8 kg. That is correct for high producers. If that is too expensive, feed 1 kg per 3 litres. Step 8: Write a short report: cow ID, peak week, peak litres, average for 4 weeks, and any health issues. Step 9: Discuss with a fellow farmer or extension officer. Step 10: Repeat for another cow. This exercise teaches you to see the curve and make decisions. Do it for at least one full lactation to master it.

<h2>Practical Reference: The Lactation Curve in African Dairy Herd Systems</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the working knowledge you need to read, record, and act on lactation curves in a smallholder or emerging commercial dairy herd. In an African context where cows are often crosses of Friesian, Jersey, Ayrshire or Sahiwal with local zebu, where feed is seasonal, and where every litre sold daily matters for cash flow the lactation curve is not an academic graph. It is a management tool that tells you when a cow is peaking, when she is likely to conceive again, when to increase concentrate, and when she is quietly losing you money.</p>

<p>Use this page alongside the earlier lessons. It gives you the standard 305-day reference figures, a field-ready recording table, a safety warning on milk-withdrawal and drug residues, a checklist, and free official sources for further study.</p>

<h3>1. What the curve looks like the standard shape</h3>

<p>A normal lactation curve rises steeply for the first 68 weeks after calving, reaches a peak (usually 48 weeks post-calving in well-fed temperate breeds, and often 610 weeks in tropical crosses under African conditions), then declines gradually until drying off. Persistency how slowly milk yield falls after peak determines total 305-day yield far more than peak height alone. A cow that peaks at 18 litres and persists well can out-yield a cow that peaks at 22 litres but crashes by month four.</p>

<h3>3. How to read and act on the curve</h3>

<p>Three practical rules matter most in African herds:</p>

<p><strong>Rule 1 Feed the peak, not the average.</strong> Peak yield drives total lactation yield. A cow peaking at 15 L needs roughly 1 kg of concentrate per 22.5 L above maintenance. If you underfeed between weeks 4 and 10, the peak itself is suppressed and the whole curve shifts down permanently.</p>

<p><strong>Rule 2 Watch persistency, not just peak.</strong> Persistency is the rate of decline after peak. A monthly decline of 68% is normal for crossbreds; above 10% per month signals a problem heat stress, mastitis, poor forage, or a ration short on energy or protein. Persistency is where the money hides in a daily-sales business.</p>

<p><strong>Rule 3 Breed back by week 12.</strong> A cow that does not conceive by 90 days post-calving will drift into a longer lactation and a longer dry period, pushing her next calving out and cutting the number of calves and litres per year. The curve tells you when she is cycling and when she is losing weight. Use both signals.</p>

<h3>4. Safety and best practice drug residues</h3>

<div style="border-left:6px solid #b00020;background:#fdecec;padding:14px 18px;margin:18px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<p style="margin:0;font-weight:bold;color:#b00020;"> RED WARNING Milk withdrawal and drug residues</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;">If a cow on the lactation curve is treated with an antibiotic, dewormer, or other veterinary drug, <strong>her milk must be withheld from sale and from calf feeding for the full withdrawal period stated on the label or by the veterinarian</strong>. Selling milk containing drug residues is illegal in most African markets, destroys buyer trust, and creates antimicrobial resistance that harms both people and animals. Record the treatment date, drug name, dose, and withdrawal end date on the same sheet as the daily yield. Never mix withheld milk into the bulk tank or the morning delivery. When in doubt, keep the milk out and consult the veterinarian or the nearest veterinary office.</p>
</div>

<h3>5. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Record every milking.</strong> AM and PM yields for each cow, every day. Without records there is no curve.</li>
<li><strong>Weigh or measure, don't guess.</strong> Use a calibrated bucket, milk meter, or scale. Eyeballing litres loses money.</li>
<li><strong>Plot weekly, review monthly.</strong> A simple line graph per cow or per group shows the peak and the decline clearly.</li>
<li><strong>Check the peak window (weeks 58).</strong> If the peak is low or late, review the transition ration and cow comfort immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Track persistency.</strong> Flag any cow dropping more than 10% per month after peak for a health and nutrition check.</li>
<li><strong>Breed back by 90 days.</strong> Use the curve plus heat records to hit a 1213 month calving interval.</li>
<li><strong>Plan the dry period.</strong> Dry off around 60 days before expected calving; use dry cow therapy where advised.</li>
<li><strong>Log all treatments and withdrawal dates.</strong> Keep drug-residue milk out of the sale chain always.</li>
</ol>
