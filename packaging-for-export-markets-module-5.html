Lessons

1. Ocean Freight: Containerization and Lashing

When you ship by sea, your goods go inside a steel container. The most common is the 20-foot container (TEU), which holds about 33 cubic meters and can carry up to 28,000 kg. A 40-foot container (FEU) holds about 67 cubic meters. Your packaging must survive weeks at sea, high humidity, and rough handling. Start with strong corrugated boxesdouble-wall or triple-wall for heavy items. Stack boxes tightly to fill gaps; empty spaces let cargo shift. Use pallets: standard 1200mm x 1000mm or 1200mm x 800mm. Stretch-wrap pallets with 20-30 micron film, then strap with plastic or steel bands. For heavy machinery, bolt it to a wooden base and brace with timber. Lashing is critical: use ratchet straps or chains rated for at least 2,000 kg. Attach to container lashing rings (available every 60 cm along the floor). For bags of cocoa or coffee, use woven polypropylene bags with inner liners to block moisture. Add silica gel desiccant (500g per cubic meter) to prevent mold. Label boxes with handling instructions: 'This Side Up', 'Keep Dry', and 'Fragile'. In Africa, ports like Mombasa, Durban, and Lagos have high humidity. Use VCI (vapor corrosion inhibitor) paper for metal parts. Always check container condition: no holes, no rust, doors seal tight. If you ship less than a full container (LCL), your cargo gets consolidated with othersso your packaging must be extra tough. Use edge protectors and corner boards. Finally, document everything: packing list, photos of loading. That helps with insurance claims if damage occurs.

2. Air Freight: Lightweight and Secure Packaging

Air freight is fast but expensiveyou pay by volumetric weight, so light packaging saves money. The formula: length x width x height (cm) / 6000 = volumetric kg. If your actual weight is less, you pay for the volume. So use lightweight materials: single-wall corrugated boxes for light items, foam inserts, and air pillows. But don't sacrifice protection. Air cargo gets stacked, thrown, and squeezed. Use double-wall boxes for anything over 10 kg. For electronics, wrap in anti-static bubble wrap (pink or blue) and place in a box with 5 cm of cushioning on all sides. Seal with pressure-sensitive tapenot masking tape. Use 'H' taping method: one strip down the center, two along edges. For perishables like flowers or fresh fish, use insulated boxes with gel packs. Keep weight under 30 kg per box for easy handling. Label with 'Air Freight' and 'This Way Up'. Avoid wooden crates unless requiredthey're heavy and may need fumigation (ISPM 15). Instead, use heavy-duty plastic crates or reinforced cardboard. For high-value goods, use tamper-evident tape and serialized labels. In Africa, many airports have limited handling equipment, so boxes must be easy to lift. Use hand holes or straps. Also, consider pressure changes: sealed bags can burst. Use vented bags for live plants. Finally, check airline restrictions: lithium batteries, liquids, and aerosols have special rules. Always declare accurately. A well-packed air shipment arrives in 1-3 days, but poor packaging means damage and delays. Invest in good tape and cushioningit's cheaper than replacing goods.

3. Rail and Road Transport: Packaging for Overland Routes

Overland transport in Africa often means rough roads, potholes, and long distances. Whether by truck or train, your packaging must handle vibration, shock, and stacking. For road freight, use strong corrugated boxes or wooden crates. Stack boxes no more than 2 meters high unless palletized. Use pallets and stretch-wrap to keep loads stable. For truck shipments, load heavy items at the bottom, light on top. Use dunnage bags (air bags) to fill gapsthey cost about $5 each and prevent shifting. For rail, containers or boxcars are common. Rail gives a smoother ride than road, but coupling impacts can be strong. Use double-wall boxes and cushioning. For bagged goods like maize or fertilizer, use woven polypropylene bags with UV protectionsunlight degrades plastic. Stitch or heat-seal bags. For liquids, use jerrycans or drums with tight lids, and pack in crates with sawdust or foam. Label with 'Handle with Care' and 'Keep Upright'. In Africa, dust is a big problem. Use inner plastic liners for bags and seal boxes with tape. For long-distance trucking (e.g., Mombasa to Kampala), consider vibration: use foam or paper cushioning. Also, theft is a risk. Use tamper-evident seals and avoid branding that advertises high-value goods. For rail, secure cargo to prevent sliding during braking. Use straps or wooden braces. Finally, plan for transloadinggoods may move from truck to train to truck. Each transfer increases damage risk. So pack for multiple handlings. Test your packaging: drop a box from 1 meter onto concrete. If it survives, it's likely road-worthy. Keep records of damage claims to improve future packaging.

4. Multimodal Transport: Packaging Challenges

Multimodal transport means your goods travel by more than one modee.g., truck to port, ship to another port, then rail to final destination. Each transfer adds risk: more handling, different climates, and varying regulations. Your packaging must be a 'jack of all trades'. Start with a strong outer boxdouble or triple-wall corrugated or a wooden crate. Inside, use cushioning that absorbs shock and vibration. For example, foam-in-place or air pillows. Use pallets that fit both container and truck dimensions. Standard pallets work, but check if your destination uses different sizes (e.g., Euro pallets 1200x800mm). Label with all handling instructions in multiple languages if needed. For sea legs, add desiccants. For air legs, keep weight low. For road legs, protect against dust and theft. Use tamper-evident seals that can survive multiple inspections. Documentation is key: each mode has different paperwork (bill of lading, air waybill, road manifest). Attach a packing list inside the box and on the outside. In Africa, multimodal often involves crossing borders. Customs may open boxesso use re-sealable tape or lids. Avoid nails that splinter. For perishables, use temperature-controlled packaging that works across modes: insulated boxes with phase change materials (PCMs) that hold temperature for 48-72 hours. Test your packaging by simulating the worst: drop, vibration, compression. A common test is ASTM D4169. If you can't test, over-pack. Remember, a failure in one mode ruins the whole journey. So invest in quality materials: 3M tape, 200-lb test boxes, and 2-mil stretch wrap. Also, consider using a freight forwarder who specializes in multimodal. They can advise on packaging. Finally, track your shipment. If damage occurs, you'll know which mode caused it and can improve.

5. Temperature-Controlled Packaging for Perishables

Perishables like fresh fruit, vegetables, fish, and flowers need temperature control from farm to market. If you export from Africa to Europe or the Middle East, the journey can take 2-5 days by air or 2-4 weeks by sea. Without proper packaging, goods spoil. Start with pre-cooling: remove field heat before packing. For air freight, use insulated boxes (e.g., polystyrene or polyurethane) with gel packs or dry ice. Dry ice is -78C, good for frozen items, but airlines limit it to 2.5 kg per box. Gel packs are cheaper and safer. For sea freight, use refrigerated containers (reefers). But you still need packaging: ventilated boxes for produce to allow airflow. For bananas, use vented cartons with polyethylene liners. For fish, use waxed boxes with ice. For flowers, use boxes with water tubes and cold packs. The key is to maintain a specific temperature: e.g., 0-2C for fish, 7-10C for tropical fruit. Use phase change materials (PCMs) that melt at a set temperaturethey cost about $2-5 per pack and hold temperature for 48-72 hours. For last-mile delivery, use small insulated bags with PCMs. Also, monitor temperature with data loggers (e.g., TempTale) that cost $20-50. They show if there was a break. In Africa, power outages are common, so PCMs are more reliable than electric coolers. Use vacuum insulation panels (VIPs) for high-value itemsthey're thin and effective. Finally, train your staff: pack quickly, don't leave boxes in the sun. A well-designed cold chain reduces spoilage from 30% to under 5%. That's real money. So invest in quality insulation and test your packaging with a dummy shipment before you export.

6. Last-Mile Delivery Packaging Considerations

Last-mile delivery is the final stepfrom a warehouse or hub to the customer's door. In African cities, this often means motorcycles, bicycles, or on foot. Your packaging must be light, easy to carry, and protect goods from heat, rain, and theft. For e-commerce, use poly mailers for soft items (clothing, textiles). They're cheap, waterproof, and lightweight. For electronics, use padded envelopes or small boxes with bubble wrap. For food, use insulated bags with ice packs. Keep packages smallmost delivery bikes can carry up to 20 kg. Use handles or straps for easy carrying. Label clearly with the customer's address and phone number. In many African countries, addresses are informal, so include a landmark or GPS coordinates. Use tamper-evident seals to prevent theft. Also, consider the environment: reusable bags or boxes can reduce waste. For cash-on-delivery, have a secure pouch for money. For fragile items, use 'Fragile' stickers and pack with 5 cm of cushioning. Test by dropping from 1 meter. For same-day delivery in cities like Nairobi or Lagos, speed matters. So use packaging that's quick to open and reseal. Avoid excessive packagingcustomers dislike it. But don't under-pack: a damaged item costs more than the packaging. Use QR codes for tracking. Finally, train delivery riders to handle packages with care. A simple rule: if you can't drop it from waist height without damage, repack it. Last-mile is where the customer judges your brand, so make it a good experience.

<h2>Practical Reference: Packaging for Different Transport Modes</h2>

<p>For African agribusiness exporters, packaging is not a cost centre it is a market-access decision. The same consignment of avocados, cashew kernels, or shea butter can arrive in Rotterdam or Dubai with premium value intact, or with 2040% rejections, depending entirely on whether the packaging was engineered for the transport mode it actually travelled on. This practical reference consolidates the standards, figures, and decision rules you need when selecting and specifying packaging for ocean, air, rail, road, multimodal, temperature-controlled, and last-mile movements.</p>

<p>Use it alongside the individual lessons in this module. It is written for operations managers, export coordinators, and packaging procurement staff who must justify choices to buyers, insurers, and freight forwarders.</p>

<h3>1. Matching packaging to transport mode</h3>

<p>The stress profile above drives every specification decision. Ocean freight imposes sustained compression stacked containers, humidity, and 1835 days at sea. Air freight imposes handling stress multiple transfers, quick turnaround, and pressure changes. Rail and road impose vibration and shock, especially on the poor road surfaces common on many African corridors. Multimodal combines all of them. Last-mile adds the highest handling frequency of any leg.</p>

<h3>2. Temperature-controlled packaging for perishables</h3>

<p>Africa's perishable exports avocados, berries, cut flowers, green beans, fish live or die by the cold chain. Packaging must maintain product temperature within the buyer's tolerance band for the full journey, not just at the airport or port. The two dominant systems are reefer containers (ocean) and insulated air cargo units with phase-change materials (air).</p>

<p>Key rules:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Respect the rule.</strong> Reefer containers should be loaded to about 75% of floor area so cold air can circulate. Overloading is the single most common cause of hot spots and rejected pallets.</li>
<li><strong>Pre-cool before packing.</strong> Field heat must be removed before the carton is closed. Packing warm produce into a reefer is a guaranteed quality failure.</li>
<li><strong>Use validated phase-change materials.</strong> For air freight, gel packs or vacuum-insulated panels must be validated for the specific transit time, not assumed.</li>
<li><strong>Control condensation.</strong> Liners and desiccants prevent moisture damage and mould, especially on long ocean legs from Mombasa or Durban.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. Multimodal and last-mile: the compounding problem</h3>

<p>Most African exports are multimodal by default. A Ugandan coffee consignment may travel by truck to Kampala, rail to Mombasa, ocean to Jebel Ali, and road to a Dubai roaster. Each transfer adds handling, and each mode adds its own stress. The packaging rule is simple: <strong>design for the highest-stress leg, then verify on the lowest.</strong></p>

<p>For last-mile, the packaging must survive repeated handling and often uncontrolled temperature. Consumer-facing packaging must also carry the buyer's branding, barcode, and regulatory information. For African exporters selling into EU or UK retail, this means the outer carton and the inner consumer pack are usually specified by the buyer but the transit packaging that protects them is your responsibility.</p>

<h3>4. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li>Identify every transport mode in the journey, including transhipment and last-mile, before specifying packaging.</li>
<li>Confirm stacking strength for the maximum stack height in the container or pallet configuration.</li>
<li>Validate cold chain for the full transit time, including port dwell and customs delays.</li>
<li>Use standard pallet footprints (1200 1000 mm or 1200 800 mm) to avoid repalletisation costs.</li>
<li>Include moisture barriers and desiccants for ocean and multimodal movements.</li>
<li>Test a pilot consignment before scaling, and record temperature and damage data.</li>
<li>Check buyer, insurer, and phytosanitary packaging requirements in writing.</li>
<li>Label clearly with handling symbols, batch codes, and country of origin.</li>
</ul>
