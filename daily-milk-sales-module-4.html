1. Introduction and Why It Matters

A dairy cow is a milk factory. What she eats determines how much milk she gives and how rich it is. In Africa, many farmers keep local cows that give 1-2 litres per day. With good feeding, a crossbreed can give 10-15 litres per day. That extra milk means more money for your family. Feeding is not just about grass. It is about giving the cow a balanced diet every single day.

Think of your cow like a person who works hard. She needs energy to walk, chew, and produce milk. She needs protein to build milk and keep her body strong. She needs minerals like calcium and phosphorus for strong bones and good milk. She needs clean water, because milk is 87% water. If any of these are missing, milk drops. You cannot fix poor feeding with medicine.

In Africa, the biggest challenge is dry season. When grass dries up, cows lose weight and milk falls. You must plan ahead. Make hay or silage when grass is plenty. Grow fodder trees like Leucaena or Calliandra. Store crop residues like maize stover and groundnut haulms. A cow that is well-fed in the dry season will keep giving milk when other farmers have none.

Feeding also affects milk quality. Cows that eat poor feed produce milk with low fat and protein. That milk sells for less. Cows that eat well produce rich milk that makes good cheese and yoghurt. Good feeding also keeps the cow healthy. A well-fed cow gets sick less often. She gives birth more easily and has stronger calves. She lives longer.

Finally, feeding is the biggest cost in dairy farming. It can be 60-70% of your expenses. So, you must be smart. Use local feeds like Napier grass, sweet potato vines, and brewers waste. Make your own mineral blocks. Do not waste money on expensive concentrates if you can grow good fodder. This module will show you exactly how to feed your cow for maximum milk, step by step.

2. Tools and Materials

To feed your cow well, you need the right tools and materials. You do not need expensive machines. Most things can be found locally. Start with a feed trough. A simple wooden or concrete trough, 1.5 meters long and 30 cm deep, works for one cow. It must be clean and smooth inside so the cow does not hurt her tongue. If you have several cows, make a longer trough. A water trough is also essential. Cows drink 40-80 litres of water per day. A clean plastic or concrete water trough that holds at least 50 litres is good. Place it in the shade.

For cutting and carrying feed, you need a sharp panga (machete), a sickle, and a wheelbarrow or a sack. A chaff cutter is very useful. It cuts Napier grass and maize stover into small pieces, which cows eat better and waste less. You can buy a manual chaff cutter for a small price. A weighing scale helps you measure feed accurately. If you cannot afford one, use a bucket or a tin as a standard measure. For example, a 10-litre bucket can measure chopped fodder.

You also need materials for making silage or hay. For silage, you need a pit or plastic bags. A pit is 2 meters wide, 2 meters deep, and 3 meters long. Line it with plastic sheet. For hay, you need a rake, a fork, and a dry storage shed. For mineral blocks, you need a mould, cement, and mineral premix. You can also buy ready-made mineral blocks from agrovets.

For feeding, you need a feed scoop or a container. Always use the same container so you know how much you are giving. A simple plastic basin works. For concentrates, you need a mixing area. A concrete floor or a wooden board is fine. You also need a record book and a pen. Write down how much you feed and how much milk you get. This helps you know if your feeding is working.

Finally, personal safety gear: gumboots, gloves, and a dust mask when mixing feeds. Keep tools clean. Wash the troughs daily. Store feed in a dry place away from rats. A well-organised feeding area saves time and keeps your cow healthy.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Know your cow's needs. A cow giving 10 litres of milk needs about 10 kg of dry matter per day. Dry matter is what is left after removing water. Good quality fodder like Napier grass has about 20% dry matter. So 10 kg dry matter means 50 kg of fresh Napier grass. But you also need to give concentrates. A simple rule: for every 2.5 litres of milk above 5 litres, give 1 kg of dairy meal. So a cow giving 10 litres needs (10-5)/2.5 = 2 kg of dairy meal. Also give minerals: 100 g per day.

Step 2: Plan your fodder. You need 50 kg of fresh fodder per cow per day. That is 18,250 kg per year. If you plant Napier grass, you can get 20-30 tonnes per acre per year. So one acre can feed one cow. Plant in rows, add manure, and cut when 1-1.2 meters tall. Cut and carry to the cow. Do not let the cow graze freely; she wastes feed and picks up worms.

Step 3: Make silage for dry season. When grass is plenty, cut it, wilt it for a few hours, chop it into 2-3 cm pieces, and pack it tightly into a pit or plastic bag. Add salt (0.5 kg per 100 kg) and molasses (2 kg per 100 kg) if you have them. Seal it airtight. After 3-4 weeks, it is ready. Feed 10-15 kg per cow per day.

Step 4: Mix and feed. Divide the day into three feedings: morning, midday, and evening. Give half the fodder in the morning, half in the evening. Give concentrates before milking. Give minerals mixed with concentrates. Always provide clean water. Change water twice a day.

Step 5: Monitor and adjust. Weigh or measure milk every day. If milk drops, increase feed or check for sickness. If the cow gets fat, reduce concentrates. Keep records. Adjust feeding based on milk production and body condition. A cow should not be too thin or too fat. You should see 2-3 ribs but not hip bones sticking out.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Feeding only dry grass. Dry grass has very little protein and energy. The cow will lose weight and milk will drop. Fix: Mix dry grass with fresh green fodder, legumes, and concentrates. Even a small amount of Leucaena leaves can boost protein.

Mistake 2: Not giving enough water. A cow giving 15 litres of milk needs up to 80 litres of water. If water is dirty or not enough, milk drops. Fix: Provide clean water at all times. Use a trough, not a small bucket. Change water at least twice a day. In hot weather, add more water points.

Mistake 3: Sudden change of feed. If you change from grass to silage suddenly, the cow may get diarrhoea and stop eating. Fix: Change feed gradually over 7-10 days. Mix small amounts of new feed with old feed and increase slowly.

Mistake 4: Overfeeding concentrates. Too much dairy meal can cause acidosis, a serious stomach problem. The cow may stop eating, breathe fast, and even die. Fix: Do not give more than 1 kg of concentrates per 2.5 litres of milk. Always give fodder first. If you must increase, do it slowly.

Mistake 5: Not using minerals. Cows need calcium, phosphorus, and salt. Without them, milk production drops and the cow may get weak. Fix: Buy mineral premix or make your own block. Give 100 g per day. You can also add crushed bones or oyster shells.

Mistake 6: Feeding mouldy or spoiled feed. Mouldy silage or hay can cause poisoning. Fix: Check feed before giving. If it smells bad or has mould, throw it away. Store feed in a dry place.

Mistake 7: Not keeping records. Without records, you do not know if feeding is working. Fix: Write down daily feed amounts and milk yield. Compare weekly. Adjust as needed.

Mistake 8: Letting the cow graze on wet pasture. This can cause bloat, a deadly condition. Fix: Do not graze on lush, wet legumes like lucerne early in the morning. Feed dry hay first.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety first. When handling feed and tools, wear protective gear. Gumboots protect your feet from sharp objects and manure. Gloves protect your hands from cuts and chemicals. A dust mask is important when mixing dry feeds or cleaning mouldy hay, as dust can damage your lungs. Always wash your hands with soap after handling feed or animals.

Keep your feeding area clean. Sweep the floor daily. Remove leftover feed before it spoils. Wash water troughs with clean water and a brush every day. Dirty water spreads disease. Store feed in a dry, raised place. Use pallets or stones to keep bags off the ground. This prevents mould and rats. Keep tools like pangas and sickles sharp and clean. Store them safely after use.

Handle cows gently. Cows are creatures of habit. Feed them at the same time every day. Avoid sudden loud noises or hitting them. A stressed cow gives less milk. When feeding, approach calmly. Use a halter if needed. Never stand directly behind a cow; she may kick. If a cow is sick, isolate her and call a vet.

Best practice: Rotate your fodder plots. Cut Napier grass every 6-8 weeks. Do not cut too low; leave 10 cm stubble. Apply manure after cutting. This keeps the grass healthy. For silage, ensure airtight sealing. Any air will cause mould. For hay, dry it properly before storage. It should crackle when twisted. If it is damp, it will heat and spoil.

Keep records of everything: feed given, milk produced, health treatments, and costs. This helps you know your profit. Also, train your family. Everyone who feeds the cow should follow the same rules. Finally, plan for the dry season. Never wait until grass is finished. Make silage or hay when you have plenty. A well-prepared farmer never runs out of feed.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is time to put what you learned into practice. This exercise has three parts. Do them over one week. Write down your answers in a notebook.

Part 1: Calculate your cow's daily feed. Choose one cow. Find out how much milk she gives per day. Use the rule: for every 2.5 litres above 5 litres, give 1 kg of dairy meal. For example, if she gives 12 litres, give (12-5)/2.5 = 2.8 kg of dairy meal. Also, she needs 10-15 kg of dry matter from fodder. If you use fresh Napier grass (20% dry matter), that is 50-75 kg of fresh grass. Write down your cow's milk yield and calculate her feed. Then measure out that amount using a bucket or scale. Feed her for three days and record how much milk she gives. Did milk increase?

Part 2: Make a simple mineral block. You need: 2 kg of cement, 2 kg of salt, 2 kg of mineral premix (from agrovet), and 1 kg of molasses (optional). Mix dry ingredients, add water to make a thick paste. Put in a mould (a plastic basin works). Let it dry for 2-3 days. Remove from mould and dry in shade for one week. Place it in the feed trough. Your cow will lick it as needed. Observe how much she licks. This provides essential minerals.

Part 3: Plan for dry season. Look at your farm. What fodder do you have? If you have excess grass now, make hay or silage. For hay: cut grass, dry it in the sun for 2-3 days, turn it daily. Store in a dry shed. For silage: chop grass, pack in a plastic bag, remove air, and seal. Make at least 50 kg of silage or hay. Store it properly. In the dry season, feed it to your cow and see if milk holds steady.

After one week, review your records. Did your cow give more milk? Did she eat all the feed? If not, why? Adjust your feeding. Share your results with other farmers. This practical work is the heart of dairy farming. Do it well, and your milk sales will grow.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Feeding for Milk Daily Milk Sales Dairy Herd System

Feeding is the single largest cost in a dairy enterprise typically 6070% of total production costs and it is the factor you control most directly. For a smallholder or emerging commercial dairy selling milk daily, the difference between profit and loss usually sits in the feed trough. A cow that eats well produces more litres per day, holds body condition, conceives faster, and stays in the herd longer. A cow that is underfed drops milk within days, loses weight, and costs you money twice: once in lost sales, and again in veterinary bills and delayed calving.

This reference lesson gives you the practical numbers, tools, and rules of thumb you need to feed a dairy herd for consistent daily milk sales in an African context using locally available feeds, managing dry-season gaps, and keeping feed cost per litre under control.

How a Dairy Cow Uses Feed

A lactating cow partitions nutrients in a strict priority order: maintenance first, then milk production, then body condition (weight gain), then reproduction. If feed is short, she maintains herself and milks off her back literally. This is why a thin cow that is still giving milk is a warning sign, not a success. The rumen (the cow's fermentation vat) needs a steady supply of forage, energy, protein, minerals, and water to function. Feed the rumen, and the rumen feeds you.

Daily Feed Requirement: The Numbers You Need

As a working rule, a lactating dairy cow (Friesian cross, 400450 kg) needs roughly 34% of body weight in dry matter (DM) per day, plus about 0.4 kg of concentrate per litre of milk above maintenance. Water is the most overlooked input: a milking cow drinks 60100 litres per day, and milk is 87% water. If water is short, milk drops before anything else.

Feeding for Profit: Cost per Litre

Your business metric is not "how much did I feed" but "what did each litre cost me to produce?" Track it monthly:

Feed cost per litre = Total monthly feed cost Total litres sold that month. If this figure is above 5060% of your milk price, you have a feeding efficiency problem usually too much concentrate for the milk returned, or too little forage quality.

Three levers move this number: (1) improve forage quality so the cow gets more from cheap feed; (2) feed concentrate according to milk produced, not by habit; (3) cut waste spilled, spoilt, or uneaten feed is pure loss.

SAFETY WARNING

Never feed mouldy, fermented, or chemically treated forage or silage to milking cows. Mouldy feed (especially maize silage and hay) can carry mycotoxins that pass into milk and harm people, and can cause abortion. Never feed fresh, wilted, or frosted legume forage in large amounts it causes bloat, which can kill a cow within hours. Do not use feed or water containers that previously held pesticides, herbicides, or veterinary drugs. Keep all agrochemicals locked away from the feed store. If a cow shows sudden bloating, staggering, or salivation after feeding, call a veterinarian immediately do not wait.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Water first: Confirm 60100 L of clean water per milking cow per day, available at least twice daily.
  • Forage free-choice: Offer 2535 kg fresh forage (or 812 kg hay) daily; never let the trough stay empty.
  • Concentrate by milk: Feed 1 kg maintenance + 0.4 kg per litre produced, split into two feeds.
  • Protein check: Include 12 kg of cake or legume forage daily for cows giving over 10 L.
  • Minerals always: Keep a mineral lick and salt available; keep it dry and clean.
  • Dry-season plan: Conserve silage or hay in the wet season do not wait until feed runs out.
  • Track cost per litre: Record feed cost and milk sales monthly; keep feed cost below 60% of milk income.
  • Watch body condition: Score cows monthly; a thin cow still milking means the ration is short.
