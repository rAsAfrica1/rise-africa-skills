1. Why Food Spoils: Causes and Signs in African Climates

Food spoils when microorganisms like bacteria, yeasts, and moulds grow on it. In hot, humid African climates, these microbes multiply very fast. At 30C, bacteria can double every 20 minutes. A pot of stew left out overnight can go from safe to dangerous in 6 hours. Other causes include enzymes in the food itself (causing browning in cut yams or avocados), oxidation (rancidity in palm oil or groundnuts), and physical damage from insects or handling.

Signs of spoilage you can see, smell, or feel: slime on meat or fish, off-smells (sour, rotten, or like ammonia), mould (green, white, or black patches), gas bubbles in fermented foods that shouldn't be there, colour changes (grey meat, brown cut fruit), and soft or mushy texture. Taste is the last signnever taste to check if food is safe. If in doubt, throw it out.

In African markets, spoilage hits hardest where cold chains are weak. Tomatoes in Kano might lose 40% of their value in two days without shade. Fresh fish in Lagos can spoil within 12 hours if not iced. Dried products like pepper or okra can still spoil if they absorb moisture from humid airmould grows at 70% relative humidity. Your job as a small business is to slow or stop these causes.

Key factors you can control: temperature (keep cold below 5C or hot above 60C), moisture (dry foods to below 10% moisture), oxygen (vacuum seal or use oil), acidity (add lime or vinegar), and salt or sugar (draw out water). Each preservation method in this course works by targeting one or more of these factors. Knowing why food spoils helps you choose the right method for your local produce and climate.

2. The 10 Preservation Methods Overview: Drying, Salting, Smoking, Fermenting, Canning, Pickling, Freezing, Sugaring, Vacuum Sealing, Irradiation

Here are the 10 methods you will learn, with practical examples for African small businesses.

1. Drying: Removing moisture using sun, solar dryers, or electric dehydrators. Good for tomatoes, peppers, mangoes, fish, and herbs. Sun drying is cheapest but slow and risky in rainy season. A solar dryer reaches 5060C and dries tomatoes in 23 days.

2. Salting: Using salt to draw out water and create an environment where bacteria cannot grow. Used for fish, meat, and vegetables. Dry salting: rub 1 kg salt per 5 kg fish, leave 24 hours, then dry. Brine: soak in 20% salt solution.

3. Smoking: Using smoke from wood to dry and flavour meat, fish, and cheese. Hot smoking cooks and dries at 6080C for 24 hours. Cold smoking flavours at 2030C for hours or days. Use hardwoods like mango or cashew, not softwoods.

4. Fermenting: Using beneficial microbes to preserve and add flavour. Examples: ogi (fermented maize), garri (fermented cassava), iru (fermented locust beans), and yoghurt. Fermentation produces acid that stops harmful bacteria.

5. Canning: Sealing food in jars or cans and heating to kill microbes. Requires a pressure canner for low-acid foods (meat, vegetables) to reach 116C. High-acid foods (tomatoes with added lime, fruit) can be water-bath canned at 100C.

6. Pickling: Preserving in vinegar or brine. Common for cucumbers, onions, peppers, and eggs. Use vinegar with at least 5% acetic acid. Add salt and sugar for taste.

7. Freezing: Storing at -18C or below. Works for most foods but needs constant electricity. A chest freezer uses less power than an upright. Blanch vegetables for 23 minutes before freezing to stop enzymes.

8. Sugaring: Using high sugar concentration to preserve fruits, e.g., jams, jellies, and candied fruits. Sugar draws out water. Use 1 part fruit to 0.8 parts sugar for jam.

9. Vacuum Sealing: Removing air and sealing in plastic bags. Slows oxidation and microbial growth. Works well with dried and frozen foods. A vacuum sealer costs from $50.

10. Irradiation: Using controlled radiation to kill microbes. Not common for small businesses in Africa due to cost and regulation, but used for spices and some exports. You will likely not use this method, but you should know it exists.

3. Choosing the Right Method for Your Local Produce

The best preservation method depends on your produce, climate, budget, and market. Start by asking: what do I have plenty of, and what do people want to buy?

For leafy vegetables like spinach, kale, or amaranth: drying is cheapest. A solar dryer or shade drying preserves vitamins better than sun. You can also blanch and freeze if you have reliable electricity. Pickling works for cabbage and cucumbers.

For fruits like mango, pineapple, banana, and pawpaw: drying makes chewy snacks. Sugaring (jam, fruit leather) adds value. Freezing works for pulp. Avoid canning low-acid fruits unless you add lime or lemon juice.

For tomatoes, peppers, and onions: drying is king. Sun-dried tomatoes sell well in cities. You can also make tomato paste and can it with added lime juice. Pickling peppers and onions is a growing market.

For fish and meat: smoking, salting, and drying are traditional and effective. Combine salting and smoking for longer shelf life. Freezing works if you have a cold chain. Vacuum sealing extends shelf life of smoked fish from 2 weeks to 3 months.

For grains and legumes: drying and vacuum sealing are best. Keep moisture below 12%. Add ash or neem leaves to repel insects. Fermenting works for some legumes like locust beans.

Consider your climate: in humid areas like the Niger Delta, drying is harduse a solar dryer or combine with salting. In dry areas like the Sahel, sun drying is easy. In hot, rainy seasons, freezing or canning may be more reliable.

Consider your market: if you sell to supermarkets, you need consistent quality and packaging. If you sell in open markets, traditional methods like smoking and salting are accepted. For export, vacuum sealing and irradiation may be required.

Finally, start with one method and one product. Master it, calculate your costs, and then expand. For example, start with solar-dried tomatoes. Once profitable, add dried peppers or mangoes. Do not try all 10 methods at once.

4. Food Safety and Hygiene Basics for Preservation

Food safety is non-negotiable. One case of food poisoning can destroy your business. Follow these basics.

Personal hygiene: Wash hands with soap for 20 seconds before handling food. Wear clean clothes and tie back hair. Do not handle food if you have diarrhoea, vomiting, or open cuts. Use gloves if you have them, but washing is more important.

Workspace hygiene: Keep your workspace clean and dry. Wash surfaces with soap and water before and after use. Use separate cutting boards for raw meat and vegetables. Sanitise with a bleach solution (1 tablespoon bleach per 4 litres of water). Control pestsflies, rats, and cockroaches carry bacteria. Use screens, traps, and keep food covered.

Water quality: Use clean water for washing and processing. If unsure, boil water for 1 minute and cool before use. Do not use stagnant water.

Temperature control: Keep hot foods above 60C and cold foods below 5C. The danger zone is 560C where bacteria multiply fastest. When drying, ensure food reaches safe temperatures (5060C for solar dryers). When canning, follow tested recipes and process times exactly.

Cleaning produce: Wash fruits and vegetables with clean water. For leafy greens, soak in salt water (1 tablespoon per litre) for 10 minutes to remove insects. Peel where possible. For meat and fish, rinse with clean water and pat dry.

Packaging and storage: Use clean, dry containers. Glass jars with lids are good for pickles and jams. Food-grade plastic bags for freezing. Vacuum bags for dried products. Label with date and product name. Store in a cool, dark, dry place away from chemicals.

Record keeping: Keep a simple log of batches, dates, and any issues. This helps you trace problems and improve. If a batch spoils, note whymaybe moisture was too high or seal failed.

Remember: If in doubt, throw it out. Never sell spoiled food. Your reputation is your business.

5. Costing and Pricing Your Preserved Products

To make a profit, you must know your costs and set a price that covers them and gives you a margin. Here is how to do it simply.

Step 1: Calculate your direct costs per batch. For example, making solar-dried tomatoes: 10 kg fresh tomatoes at $0.50/kg = $5.00. Salt and spices = $0.50. Packaging (bags, labels) = $1.00. Labour: 4 hours at $1/hour = $4.00. Fuel/electricity for solar dryer fan (if any) = $0.50. Total direct cost = $11.00. This batch yields about 1 kg dried tomatoes.

Step 2: Add overhead costs. These are costs not tied to one batch: rent, equipment depreciation, market fees, phone, transport. If your monthly overhead is $100 and you produce 50 batches per month, add $2 per batch. So total cost per batch = $13.00.

Step 3: Decide your profit margin. A common margin for food products is 3050%. If you want 40% profit on cost, multiply cost by 1.4. $13.00 x 1.4 = $18.20. So you should sell the 1 kg dried tomatoes for at least $18.20. Check market prices: if competitors sell at $15, you may need to reduce costs or differentiate (better packaging, organic).

Step 4: Price per unit. If you package in 100g bags, your cost per bag is $1.30 (since 1 kg = 10 bags). Add profit: $1.30 x 1.4 = $1.82. Round to $2.00. Selling 10 bags at $2 = $20, giving you $7 profit per batch.

Step 5: Track and adjust. Keep records of all expenses and sales. After one month, see if your actual costs match estimates. Adjust prices if needed. Consider value-added: smoked fish with vacuum sealing can fetch double the price of unsmoked fish.

Remember: Do not underprice. Many small businesses fail because they forget to pay themselves. Include your labour as a cost. Also, offer bulk discounts only if you still make a profit. Test prices with small batches before scaling.

6. Setting Up a Small-Scale Preservation Workspace

You don't need a factory to start. A clean, organised space in your home or a small shed can work. Here is how to set up.

Location: Choose a room or covered area with good ventilation and no direct sunlight. It should be easy to cleantiled floor or concrete is best. Avoid areas near toilets, animals, or chemicals. If possible, have a separate area for washing, processing, and packaging to avoid cross-contamination.

Essential equipment: Start with what you can afford. For drying: solar dryer (can be built with wood and polythene, cost $50$200) or electric dehydrator ($100$300). For salting/smoking: large plastic buckets, salt, and a smoking kiln (can be made from a drum, cost $30$100). For fermenting: food-grade plastic or glass containers, airlocks ($5 each). For canning: large pot with rack for water-bath canning ($30), pressure canner ($100$300). For freezing: chest freezer ($200$500) and generator if power is unreliable. For vacuum sealing: vacuum sealer ($50$150) and bags. For pickling: glass jars, vinegar, spices. For all: knives, cutting boards, scales, measuring cups, thermometers, and pH strips.

Layout: Arrange your workspace in a flow: receiving and washing preparation preservation cooling packaging storage. Keep raw and cooked separate. Have a hand-washing station with soap and running water. Use tables that are easy to sanitise. Store packaging materials off the floor.

Utilities: You need clean water, electricity (or alternative like solar), and proper drainage. If you smoke, ensure smoke is vented away from food areas. For freezing, a backup generator or solar freezer is wise.

Safety: Keep a fire extinguisher, first aid kit, and gloves. Store chemicals like bleach away from food. Have a pest control plan.

Costs: You can start with under $500. Prioritise based on your chosen method. For example, if you start with drying, invest in a solar dryer and packaging. Add other equipment as profits grow.

Regulations: Check local food safety rules. You may need a license or inspection. Even if not required, following good practices builds trust.

Start small: Set up one corner, produce a few batches, and improve as you learn. Your workspace should grow with your business.

<strong style="color:#b00020"> SAFETY WARNING Clostridium botulinum and Low-Acid Canning</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0">Low-acid foods (vegetables, meats, fish, beans, soups) canned in oil, water, or brine can support <em>Clostridium botulinum</em>, which produces a deadly toxin in sealed, oxygen-free conditions. In African small-scale canning, the most common causes are insufficient heat treatment, incorrect pH, and poor jar sealing. <strong>Never sell low-acid canned products unless you have a validated pressure-canning process, a calibrated thermometer, and batch records.</strong> High-acid foods (pH 4.6) such as tomato, mango, and pickles are safer for water-bath processing. When in doubt, refrigerate, freeze, dry, or ferment instead of canning. Follow WHO five keys to safer food and national food safety authority guidance.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist for Your Preservation Workspace</h3>
<ol style="line-height:1.7">
<li><strong>Separate zones:</strong> Keep raw produce, washing, processing, cooling, and packaging areas physically apart to prevent cross-contamination.</li>
<li><strong>Water and ice:</strong> Use potable water only. If water quality is uncertain, filter, boil, or chlorinate before washing or adding to products.</li>
<li><strong>Clean, dry, and sanitise:</strong> Wash hands, knives, boards, and jars; sanitise food-contact surfaces; dry equipment before use to avoid mould.</li>
<li><strong>Control time and temperature:</strong> Keep cold products below 5C, hot products above 60C, and never leave cooked or blanched produce in the danger zone (560C) for more than 2 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Measure, dont guess:</strong> Use a thermometer, pH strips or meter, refractometer for sugar, and a scale for salt and ingredients.</li>
<li><strong>Package and label:</strong> Use food-grade packaging, seal properly, and label with product name, batch, production date, best-before date, and contact details.</li>
<li><strong>Record every batch:</strong> Keep a simple log of raw material source, quantities, processing time/temperature, packaging, and yield. This supports traceability and buyer trust.</li>
<li><strong>Test shelf life:</strong> Store samples at ambient and accelerated conditions; check for off-odours, colour changes, mould, gas, and texture loss before scaling up.</li>
</ol>
