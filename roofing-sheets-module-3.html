1. Manufacturing Processes for Metal Roofing Sheets

Roofing sheets in Africa are mostly made from steel or aluminium. The process starts with a large coil of thin metal, often imported from South Africa, China, or India. The coil is unrolled and fed into a roll-forming machine. This machine has a series of rollers that gradually bend the flat metal into the corrugated or trapezoidal profile you see on roofs. The number of rollers depends on the profile complexity; a simple corrugated sheet might need 12 to 16 stations, while a deeper trapezoidal sheet needs 20 or more. The machine runs at 10 to 30 metres per minute. After forming, the sheet is cut to length by a flying shear or a guillotine. For curved sheets, a separate curving machine bends the profile along its length. In small workshops, manual bending brakes and hand shears are used, but these are slow and less accurate. The most common metals are galvanised steel (zinc-coated), Galvalume (aluminium-zinc coated), and aluminium. Galvanised steel is the cheapest and most widely used in Africa. The zinc coating protects the steel from rust. The thickness of the base steel is measured in millimetres or gauge; common thicknesses are 0.20 mm to 0.50 mm. The zinc coating weight is measured in grams per square metre (g/m), typically 60 to 275 g/m. After forming, the sheets may be coated with paint (see next lesson). The whole process requires a steady supply of electricity, which can be a challenge in rural areas. Many manufacturers use diesel generators as backup. Quality control checks include measuring thickness with a micrometre, checking profile dimensions with a template, and testing the zinc coating with a magnetic gauge. A well-run factory can produce 500 to 1000 sheets per day. The key to success is regular maintenance of the roll-forming machine and keeping the metal coils dry to prevent white rust before forming.

2. Coating and Finishing Techniques

After a metal roofing sheet is formed, it needs a protective and decorative coating. The most common coating for galvanised steel is a paint system. First, the sheet is cleaned to remove oil, dirt, and rust. Then it passes through a pre-treatment chemical bath, often a chromate or phosphate solution, which helps paint stick. Next, a primer is applied, usually by roller coating. The primer is baked in an oven at 200240C for 2030 seconds. Then a topcoat of polyester, silicone-modified polyester (SMP), or PVDF (Kynar 500) is applied. Polyester is the cheapest and most common in Africa, lasting 510 years in harsh sun. PVDF lasts 2030 years but costs three times more. The paint thickness is measured in microns (m); typical total dry film thickness is 2025 m for polyester and 2530 m for PVDF. For Galvalume sheets, the same paint systems work but require a different pre-treatment. Some manufacturers also apply a clear acrylic coating over galvanised sheets to prevent white rust during storage and transport. This is called a 'dry film' or 'anti-fingerprint' coating. Another finishing technique is embossing, where the sheet is passed between textured rollers to give a wood-grain or stucco pattern. This hides scratches and dents. For aluminium sheets, anodising is common: the sheet is placed in an acid bath and an electric current is passed through, creating a hard oxide layer. Anodised aluminium is popular in coastal areas because it resists salt corrosion. After coating, sheets are stacked and packed with protective plastic film. In Africa, the biggest coating challenges are dust, high humidity, and inconsistent oven temperatures. A simple test for coating quality is the 'tape test': press a piece of adhesive tape onto the painted surface, then pull it off quickly. If paint comes off, adhesion is poor. A pencil hardness test (scratching with pencils of increasing hardness) checks cure. Always store coated sheets under cover, off the ground, and with spacers between sheets to allow air circulation.

3. Understanding Roofing Sheet Specifications and Standards

When you buy roofing sheets, you need to know what the specifications mean. In Africa, many sheets are imported, so you will see standards from different countries. The most common are: EN 10169 (European standard for continuously organic-coated steel), ASTM A653 (US standard for galvanised steel), and SANS 10137 (South African standard for roofing). A typical specification might read: '0.40 mm thick, galvanised steel, zinc coating 120 g/m, polyester paint 20 m, corrugated profile, length 3.0 m.' Each part matters. Thickness: 0.40 mm is the base steel thickness before coating. Cheaper sheets are 0.200.30 mm and dent easily. Zinc coating: 120 g/m means 120 grams of zinc per square metre of sheet (both sides combined). Higher numbers mean better rust protection. Paint thickness: 20 m is the dry film thickness; thicker paint lasts longer. Profile: 'corrugated' means wavy (sinusoidal), 'trapezoidal' means trapezoid-shaped ribs. The profile determines strength and how sheets overlap. Length: sheets are cut to order, but standard lengths are 2.0 m, 2.4 m, 3.0 m, and 3.6 m. You also need to know the 'cover width' the effective width after overlapping. For a standard corrugated sheet, the total width is 1.0 m, but cover width is about 0.85 m. For trapezoidal, total width might be 1.0 m and cover width 0.95 m. Always ask for the 'certificate of conformity' or test report. In many African countries, local standards bodies (like KEBS in Kenya, SON in Nigeria, or SABS in South Africa) require a permit to sell roofing sheets. Without it, your sheets may be confiscated. Also check for 'mill certificates' from the steel supplier. These show the chemical composition and mechanical properties (yield strength, tensile strength). For roofing, yield strength should be at least 300 MPa for steel. If you are a manufacturer, you must test each batch for thickness, coating weight, and profile dimensions. Keep records for at least 5 years.

4. Thickness, Gauge, and Weight Considerations

Thickness is the most important factor in roofing sheet quality, but it is also the most misunderstood. In Africa, thickness is usually given in millimetres (mm), but sometimes in gauge (a traditional American system). The lower the gauge number, the thicker the sheet. For example, 30 gauge is about 0.30 mm, 26 gauge is about 0.45 mm. But gauge is not standardised across metals, so always ask for millimetres. The base steel thickness is what matters for strength. The zinc coating adds a small amount (about 0.020.05 mm), and paint adds about 0.020.03 mm. So a sheet labelled '0.40 mm' might actually be 0.35 mm steel plus coatings. Always measure with a micrometre at several points. Common thicknesses in Africa: 0.20 mm (very cheap, for temporary structures), 0.25 mm (budget), 0.30 mm (economy), 0.35 mm (standard), 0.40 mm (good), 0.50 mm (heavy duty), 0.60 mm (industrial). Thinner sheets are lighter and cheaper but dent easily from hail, foot traffic, and wind. They also need more purlins (supports) because they sag. For a typical house in Africa, 0.350.40 mm is a good compromise. Weight: you can calculate the weight of a sheet if you know the thickness and density. Steel density is 7850 kg/m. For a 0.40 mm thick sheet, 1 m weighs 0.0004 m 7850 kg/m = 3.14 kg. Add zinc (0.12 kg/m for 120 g/m) and paint (0.02 kg/m), total about 3.28 kg/m. A 3.0 m sheet with 0.85 m cover width covers 2.55 m, so weighs about 8.4 kg. Aluminium is lighter (density 2700 kg/m), so a 0.40 mm aluminium sheet weighs only 1.08 kg/m. Lighter sheets are easier to handle but more expensive per kg. When buying, ask for the weight per square metre. If the seller cannot tell you, weigh a sheet on a bathroom scale. If the weight is much less than calculated, the thickness is less than claimed. Always check thickness with a gauge before buying in bulk. A simple 'thumb test' pressing hard on the sheet can give a rough idea, but only a micrometre is reliable.

5. Profile Design and Load-Bearing Capacity

The profile of a roofing sheet is its cross-sectional shape. The two main types in Africa are corrugated (wavy, sinusoidal) and trapezoidal (ribbed, with flat tops and slanted sides). Corrugated sheets are the oldest and cheapest. They have a pitch (distance between waves) of 76 mm or 89 mm, and a depth of 1618 mm. They are weak in one direction, so they need purlins every 800900 mm. Trapezoidal sheets have deeper ribs (2540 mm) and a pitch of 150250 mm. They are much stronger and can span 1.21.5 m between purlins. The load-bearing capacity depends on: thickness, profile depth, yield strength of the steel, and the number of purlins. For a given thickness, a deeper profile is stronger. For example, a 0.40 mm trapezoidal sheet with 35 mm deep ribs can carry a live load of 1.5 kN/m over a 1.2 m span, while a 0.40 mm corrugated sheet can only carry 0.8 kN/m over 0.9 m. In Africa, wind loads are often the critical factor, especially in coastal areas and open plains. A roof must resist uplift from wind. The profile's 'fixing' how it is attached to purlins is crucial. Screws should be placed in the crest (top) of the ribs for trapezoidal sheets, and in the valley (bottom) for corrugated sheets. Use self-drilling screws with EPDM washers. The number of screws: at least 5 per square metre in moderate wind zones, 810 in high wind zones. For cyclone-prone areas (e.g., Mozambique, Madagascar), use cyclone washers and screws every second crest. Load tables are available from manufacturers. They show the maximum span for a given thickness, profile, and load. Always follow the manufacturer's table. If you are building a large roof, hire an engineer to calculate loads. For small houses, a rule of thumb: corrugated 0.30 mm purlins at 800 mm; trapezoidal 0.40 mm purlins at 1200 mm. Never exceed these without checking. Also consider thermal movement: metal expands and contracts with temperature. Long sheets (over 6 m) need sliding clips or oversized holes to prevent buckling.

6. Quality Control and Certification

Quality control (QC) in roofing sheet manufacturing ensures that every sheet meets the required specifications. Without QC, you risk selling sheets that rust quickly, dent easily, or fail in wind. The first QC step is incoming material inspection. When you receive a coil of steel, check the mill certificate. It should state the steel grade (e.g., DX51D for galvanised), yield strength (minimum 300 MPa), zinc coating weight (e.g., 120 g/m), and thickness tolerance (0.02 mm). Measure the thickness with a micrometre at three points across the width and along the length. If the coil is out of tolerance, reject it. Second, during production, check the roll-forming machine settings. The profile dimensions (pitch, depth, cover width) must match the drawing. Use a profile gauge or a template every hour. Check the cut length with a tape measure. Third, after coating, test the paint adhesion with a tape test and the coating thickness with a magnetic gauge. Also check for visual defects: scratches, blisters, runs, and bare spots. Fourth, test the finished sheet for mechanical properties. A simple bend test: bend a sample 180 over a 10 mm mandrel. The coating should not crack or flake. For load capacity, you can do a static load test: support a sheet on two purlins at the specified span, then load it with sandbags until it deflects 1/100 of the span. It should not fail. Fifth, keep records. Every batch should have a batch number, date, machine operator, and test results. This helps trace problems. Certification: in most African countries, you need a certificate of conformity from the national standards body. For example, in Kenya, KEBS requires a permit; in Nigeria, SONCAP; in South Africa, SABS. The process involves submitting samples for testing at an accredited lab. Tests include thickness, zinc coating weight, paint adhesion, and load capacity. Once certified, you can mark your sheets with the standard number. Many buyers, especially NGOs and government projects, will only buy certified sheets. Even if certification is not mandatory in your country, it builds trust and allows you to charge a higher price. Train your workers on QC procedures and appoint one person as QC officer. A simple checklist and a daily log can prevent most defects.

<h2>Practical Reference: Roofing Sheet Manufacturing and Specifications</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core technical and commercial knowledge needed by roofing sheet manufacturers, distributors, specifiers, and contractors operating in African markets. It is designed as a day-to-day field and procurement companion not a textbook. Every figure, standard, and table here is drawn from real industry sources and adapted to the realities of African supply chains, where imported coil stock, variable galvanising quality, and intense UV/rainfall exposure demand informed decision-making.</p>

<p>Whether you are a fabricator setting up a roll-forming line in Lagos, a distributor sourcing coils in Nairobi, or a contractor specifying roofing for a school in Accra, the principles below determine whether your product performs for 5 years or 25 years and whether your business makes money or loses it on warranty claims.</p>

<h3>1. Manufacturing Processes for Metal Roofing Sheets</h3>

<p>Metal roofing sheets in Africa are predominantly produced through one of two routes: <strong>roll forming</strong> from pre-coated or galvanised steel coil, or <strong>batch pressing</strong> for shorter runs and complex profiles. The dominant industrial process is continuous roll forming, where a flat metal coil is uncoiled, fed through a series of contoured rollers, and progressively bent into the final trapezoidal, corrugated, or standing-seam profile.</p>

<p>The typical line sequence is: decoiler leveller pre-punching/shearing (optional) roll former (1024 stations) cut-to-length shear stacker. Line speed ranges from 1540 m/min depending on profile complexity and sheet thickness. For African fabricators, the critical variables are coil quality (zinc coating mass, paint adhesion), roller condition, and consistent lubrication all of which affect profile accuracy and surface finish.</p>

<p>Aluminium roofing sheets (popular in coastal West Africa for corrosion resistance) are typically produced by continuous casting or direct chill rolling, then roll-formed. Stone-coated steel widely used in South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria for upmarket residential involves pressing a steel substrate, then applying a stone granule coating over an acrylic base.</p>

<h3>2. Coating and Finishing Techniques</h3>

<p>Coating is where African roofing sheets win or lose their durability battle. The continent's combination of high UV index, intense tropical rainfall, and coastal salt spray means coating systems must be specified for the actual exposure environment not just the price point.</p>

<p><strong>Hot-dip galvanising (HDG)</strong> remains the workhorse. Zinc coating mass is expressed in g/m (both sides combined). For African inland general roofing, a minimum of Z275 (275 g/m) is recommended; coastal or industrial areas demand Z450 or higher, or alternatively a zinc-aluminium alloy coating.</p>

<p><strong>Zinc-aluminium coatings</strong> most commonly Al-Zn 55% (Galvalume) or Zn-Al 5% (Galfan) offer superior cut-edge protection and heat reflectivity. Galvalume is widely specified in East and Southern Africa for its 24 longer service life compared to plain galvanised in similar environments.</p>

<p><strong>Organic coatings</strong> (polyester, PVDF, SMP) are applied over the metallic coating. In Africa, polyester (PE) is the most common for cost reasons, typically 2025 m dry film thickness (DFT). PVDF (Kynar 500 or Hylar 5000) at 2327 m DFT is the premium choice for high-UV, high-pollution, or coastal projects, with colour retention warranties of 20+ years. SMP (silicone-modified polyester) sits between the two.</p>

<p>Application is by continuous coil coating: cleaning chemical pretreatment primer topcoat oven cure (PMT 216249C for PVDF) quench rewind. Quality failures in African imports often trace to inadequate pretreatment or under-cured paint both invisible until 23 years of field exposure.</p>

<h3>3. Understanding Roofing Sheet Specifications and Standards</h3>

<p>African markets operate under a mix of international, regional, and national standards. The most commonly referenced are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>EN 10346</strong> Continuously hot-dip coated steel flat products (Europe, widely adopted in Anglophone Africa)</li>
<li><strong>ASTM A653/A653M</strong> Standard specification for steel sheet, zinc-coated (US-origin imports)</li>
<li><strong>ASTM A792/A792M</strong> Steel sheet, 55% aluminium-zinc alloy-coated</li>
<li><strong>ISO 3575</strong> Continuously hot-dip zinc-coated carbon steel sheet (commercial and drawing qualities)</li>
<li><strong>SANS 1095</strong> South African standard for galvanised steel sheeting (influential across SADC)</li>
<li><strong>KEBS / SON / NIS</strong> National standards in Kenya, Nigeria, and other countries, often harmonised from ISO or EN</li>
</ul>

<p>For buyers, the practical implication is this: always demand the mill certificate (EN 10204 3.1) showing coating mass, substrate grade, and mechanical properties. A "0.45 mm" sheet that is actually 0.40 mm with Z180 coating will not perform like a true 0.45 mm Z275 product and the price difference is often only 58%.</p>

<h3>4. Thickness, Gauge, and Weight Considerations</h3>

<p>Thickness (or gauge) is the single most misunderstood specification in African roofing trade. Two issues dominate: <strong>actual vs nominal thickness</strong>, and <strong>base metal vs total thickness including coating</strong>.</p>

<p>EN 10346 and ASTM A653 both specify tolerances. For a nominal 0.50 mm sheet, the permissible thickness range is typically 0.05 mm (i.e., 0.450.55 mm). However, some suppliers quote "total thickness including coating" while others quote "base metal thickness" (BMT). A sheet with 0.45 mm BMT + 0.05 mm coating = 0.50 mm total, but it is structurally a 0.45 mm sheet. For load-bearing calculations, always use BMT.</p>

<p>Weight per square metre is the most reliable commercial check. For steel roofing:</p>

<p><strong>Weight (kg/m) = Thickness (mm) 7.85</strong> (for steel substrate, ignoring coating, which adds ~0.020.05 kg/m for typical coatings).</p>

<p>So a 0.50 mm sheet weighs approximately 3.93 kg/m. Aluminium (density 2.70 g/cm) at 0.70 mm weighs 1.89 kg/m hence aluminium's popularity where structural loads are limited.</p>

<h3>5. Profile Design and Load-Bearing Capacity</h3>

<p>Profile geometry determines spanning capability, water shedding, and wind uplift resistance. The key profiles in African roofing are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Corrugated (sinusoidal)</strong> the traditional profile, typically 10/3 or 13/3 (pitch/height in mm). Widely used, easy to install, but lower load capacity per unit weight.</li>
<li><strong>Trapezoidal</strong> deeper ribs (typically 3240 mm), higher section modulus, better spanning. Common in industrial and commercial buildings.</li>
<li><strong>Standing seam</strong> raised interlocking seam, concealed fixings, superior weathertightness. Used in premium commercial and architectural projects.</li>
<li><strong>IBR (Inverted Box Rib)</strong> popular in Southern Africa, 38 mm rib height, excellent for long spans and low-pitch roofs (minimum 5).</li>
<li><strong>Stone-coated</strong> pressed profiles simulating tiles, used for residential aesthetics.</li>
</ul>

<p>Load-bearing capacity is a function of thickness, yield strength, profile depth, and purlin spacing. A 0.50 mm IBR sheet at 1.2 m purlin spacing can typically carry a live load of 0.50.75 kN/m (wind uplift or maintenance access), but this drops significantly at 1.5 m spacing. Always consult the manufacturer's span table generic rules of thumb are dangerous.</p>

<p>For African conditions, wind uplift is often the governing load. Coastal cyclonic zones (Mozambique, Madagascar, parts of South Africa) and Sahelian windstorms require closer purlin spacing and heavier fixings. A roof that sheds rain perfectly can still fail by lifting off in a storm.</p>

<h3>6. Quality Control and Certification</h3>

<p>Quality control in African roofing sheet supply chains must address three levels: incoming coil, in-process forming, and finished product.</p>

<p><strong>Incoming coil QC:</strong> Verify mill certificate against actual coil. Check coating mass by weigh-strip-weigh (ASTM A90/A90M) or magnetic gauge (ISO 2178). Check thickness with a calibrated micrometer at multiple points. Check paint adhesion by T-bend or cross-hatch test (ASTM D3359).</p>

<p><strong>In-process QC:</strong> Monitor roller wear (profile dimensions), line speed vs cure oven temperature (for pre-painted), and cut-length accuracy. Reject sheets with edge cracks, coating scratches, or profile distortion.</p>

<p><strong>Finished product QC:</strong> Verify profile dimensions, sheet length, squareness, and surface finish. For export or major projects, third-party inspection (SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek) is common.</p>

<p><strong>Certification:</strong> Look for ISO 9001 (quality management) at the manufacturer level, and product certification such as SABS (South Africa), KEBS diamond mark (Kenya), SONCAP (Nigeria), or CE marking (for European imports). These are not just badges they indicate audited, repeatable quality.</p>

<div style="background-color: #fff3cd; border-left: 6px solid #e74c3c; padding: 16px 20px; margin: 24px 0; border-radius: 4px;">
<p style="margin: 0 0 8px 0; font-weight: bold; color: #c0392b; font-size: 15px;"> SAFETY WARNING CUTTING AND HANDLING</p>
<p style="margin: 0; color: #1a1a1a; font-size: 14px; line-height: 1.6;">Metal roofing sheets have razor-sharp edges. Always wear cut-resistant gloves (ANSI A4 or higher), safety glasses, and long sleeves when handling, cutting, or installing sheets. Use power shears or nibblers never angle grinders as grinding generates heat that destroys the zinc coating and creates sparks that can ignite nearby materials. When cutting on site, position sheets so offcuts cannot fall on workers below. In African heat, also guard against heat stress: hydrate regularly and rotate crews. Secure stacked sheets against wind a 4 m 1 m sheet can become an airborne projectile in a sudden storm.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Verify actual thickness (BMT, not total)</strong> with a calibrated micrometer at 5+ points per sheet or coil sample.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm coating mass</strong> from mill certificate and, for critical projects, by weigh-strip-weigh test.</li>
<li><strong>Check paint adhesion</strong> via T-bend or cross-hatch before accepting pre-painted coil.</li>
<li><strong>Match profile to span and wind zone</strong> using manufacturer span tables never guess.</li>
<li><strong>Ensure purlin spacing</strong> complies with the profile's load table for the project wind speed.</li>
<li><strong>Demand EN 10204 3.1 mill certificates</strong> for every coil batch; keep records for warranty claims.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect for transit damage</strong> (scratches, dents, edge deformation) before signing delivery notes.</li>
<li><strong>Store sheets under cover, off the ground,</strong> with adequate ventilation to prevent white rust (wet storage stain).</li>
</ol>
