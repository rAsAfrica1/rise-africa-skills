Lessons

1. Customer and Sales Records

Every sale you make tells a story about your farm business. Keeping a simple customer and sales record helps you know who buys from you, what they buy, how much they pay, and when they buy. Start with a notebook or a basic spreadsheet on your phone. For each sale, record the date, customer name or description (e.g., 'Mama Ada market stall'), product sold, quantity, unit price, total amount, and payment method (cash, mobile money, credit). If you sell on credit, note the amount owed and the agreed payment date.

In Africa, many farmers sell to multiple buyers: local traders, neighbours, schools, hotels, or at roadside stands. Without records, you may forget who owes you money or which customer buys the most. A simple customer record can be a page per regular buyer, showing their contact, typical purchase, and payment history. For example, if you sell tomatoes, you might note that Mama Ada buys 20 kg every Tuesday and pays cash, while Hotel Kilimanjaro buys 50 kg on Fridays but pays after 7 days.

Use a cash book to track daily sales. At the end of each day, total your sales and compare with the cash and mobile money received. Discrepancies show theft, errors, or unpaid credit. If you use a smartphone, apps like Farmbiz or simple Google Sheets templates can work offline. Even a calendar can serve as a sales diary: write the total sales for each day in the box.

Review your customer records monthly. Identify your top 5 customers and their favourite products. This helps you plan production and avoid disappointing them. Also, follow up on overdue payments politely. Good records build trust and make your business look professional. Start today with a notebook and pen it costs almost nothing but pays off in better decisions.

2. Pricing and Margin Analysis

Setting the right price is critical for profit. Many farmers guess a price and later wonder why they have no money. To price properly, you must know your costs. List all costs for a batch of product: seeds, fertiliser, labour, transport, packaging, and any market fees. Add them up to get total cost. Then divide by the quantity produced to get cost per unit. For example, if you spend 200,000 shillings to produce 100 kg of maize, your cost per kg is 2,000 shillings.

Next, check the market price. Visit your local market, call traders, or ask other farmers. If the market price is 2,500 shillings per kg, your margin is 500 shillings per kg (2,500 2,000). That is a 25% margin. If the market price is only 1,800 shillings, you would make a loss. You must either reduce costs or find a better market.

Record your prices and margins for each product in a simple table. Include date, product, cost per unit, selling price, and margin. Update it weekly because prices change with seasons. For example, tomato prices may be high in dry season and low after harvest. If you sell at a low price, note why maybe you needed quick cash. Over time, you will see patterns.

Use margin analysis to decide what to grow. If maize gives a 10% margin but beans give 40%, consider growing more beans. But also consider risk: beans may need more labour. Record everything. Even a small notebook with columns for cost, price, and margin will guide you. Remember, profit is not the same as revenue. A high sale does not mean high profit if costs are higher. Always calculate margin before you commit to a price.

3. Direct Marketing and CSA Records

Direct marketing means selling straight to consumers, not through middlemen. This includes farm stands, farmers' markets, home delivery, and Community Supported Agriculture (CSA). In a CSA, customers pay in advance for a weekly or monthly box of produce. This gives you guaranteed income and a loyal customer base. But you must keep careful records to manage orders, deliveries, and payments.

For direct marketing, record every customer's name, contact, delivery address, and preferences. For CSA, note the subscription type (e.g., small box for 50,000 shillings per month), start date, and payment schedule. Keep a delivery log: date, box contents, and whether delivered. If a customer complains, note it and how you resolved it. For example, if you deliver tomatoes that are too ripe, you might offer a replacement next week.

Use a simple receipt book for every payment. For mobile money, screenshot the confirmation and write the transaction ID in your record. At the end of each week, tally your direct sales and CSA payments. Compare with your production plan. If you promised 20 boxes but only harvested enough for 15, you need to adjust or find extra supply. This record helps you avoid breaking trust.

Also, track customer feedback. Ask customers what they liked or disliked. Write it down. If many ask for more leafy greens, you can plant more. Direct marketing is about relationships. Good records show you care and help you keep customers. A notebook with sections for orders, deliveries, and payments is enough. Review it weekly to plan your harvest and deliveries.

4. Contract Farming Documentation

Contract farming is when you grow crops for a buyer under a written agreement. The buyer may provide seeds, fertiliser, or a guaranteed price. This can be good, but without proper documentation, you may be cheated. Always insist on a written contract. If you cannot read or write well, ask a trusted extension officer or friend to help. The contract should state: product, quantity, quality standards, price, delivery date, payment terms, and what happens if either side fails.

Keep a copy of the contract safe. Also, keep records of every transaction: inputs received from the buyer, dates, quantities, and their value. For example, if the buyer gives you 10 kg of seed worth 50,000 shillings, record it as an advance. When you deliver your harvest, note the date, quantity delivered, and amount paid or deducted. If the buyer deducts the seed cost, your record should show that.

Take photos of deliveries and get a signed delivery note from the buyer's representative. If there is a dispute, your records are your evidence. Many farmers lose money because they rely on memory. For instance, a farmer delivered 500 kg of cotton but the buyer claimed only 400 kg. Without a delivery note, the farmer had no proof.

Also, record your own production costs for the contracted crop. This helps you know if the contract is profitable. If the buyer pays 300 shillings per kg but your cost is 350 shillings, you are losing money. You may need to renegotiate or find another buyer. Keep all documents in a folder or a plastic bag to protect from rain. Review your contract records before each delivery to ensure you meet the terms. Good documentation protects you and builds a reputation as a reliable partner.

5. Inventory of Marketable Products

Inventory is the stock of products you have ready to sell. Without inventory records, you may sell more than you have, or let produce spoil because you forgot it. Start by listing all marketable products: grains, vegetables, fruits, eggs, milk, etc. For each, record quantity, quality grade, storage location, and date of harvest or production. For example, you might have 50 kg of maize in the granary, 20 kg of tomatoes in crates, and 30 eggs in a basket.

Update your inventory every time you add or remove stock. Use a simple stock card for each product: date, in/out, quantity, balance. For perishable goods, note the expected shelf life. If you have 20 kg of tomatoes that will spoil in 3 days, you need to sell them quickly. A daily inventory check helps you prioritise sales and reduce waste.

For grains, store in airtight bags or silos and record the moisture level. If moisture is too high, they may rot. For eggs, record collection dates and keep them cool. If you sell at a market, take only what you plan to sell that day and record what you take and what you bring back. The difference is your sales.

Inventory records also help with pricing. If you have a surplus, you might lower the price to clear stock. If stock is low, you can raise the price. At the end of each month, count your physical stock and compare with your records. Any difference is a loss due to theft, spoilage, or errors. Investigate and fix the problem. A simple notebook with columns for product, quantity, and date is enough. Keep it where you store your products so you remember to update it.

6. Using Sales Data for Market Planning

Sales data is the history of what you sold, when, and to whom. By analysing it, you can plan for the future. Start by reviewing your sales records from the past 6-12 months. Look for patterns: which months have high sales? Which products sell best? Who are your best customers? For example, you might notice that tomato sales peak in December and January, while maize sells steadily all year.

Use this to plan your planting and production. If tomatoes sell well in December, plant them in September so they are ready for harvest then. If a certain customer buys 100 kg of onions every month, make sure you have enough. Also, identify slow-moving products. If you have 100 kg of carrots that took 3 months to sell, consider reducing carrot production or finding new buyers.

Calculate your average monthly sales for each product. If you sold 600 kg of maize over 6 months, your average is 100 kg per month. Use this to set targets. If you want to increase sales by 20%, aim for 120 kg per month. Then plan how: maybe add a new market or offer a discount for bulk buyers.

Sales data also helps with cash flow planning. If you know you will have low sales in February, you can save money from high months. Record your sales in a simple spreadsheet or notebook with columns for date, product, quantity, and amount. At the end of each month, total sales per product. Draw a simple bar chart to see trends. Even a hand-drawn chart works. Use this information to make better decisions: what to grow, when to sell, and who to sell to. Without data, you are guessing. With data, you are planning for profit.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Record Keeping for Marketing and Sales

Sales records are the sharpest tool an African agribusiness has for protecting margin, managing cash flow, and planning the next season. Whether you sell at a rural assembly market in Ghana, supply a supermarket chain in Kenya, run a community-supported agriculture (CSA) box scheme in South Africa, or deliver under contract to a processor, the discipline is the same: capture every transaction, price every cost, and reconcile stock against cash. This reference consolidates the practical rules, templates, and benchmarks you need to run marketing and sales records that hold up to scrutiny from buyers, lenders, and your own board.

Treat this module as an operating manual. Each section gives you a record type, the fields that matter, and the business decision that record supports. Where possible, figures are drawn from official African and international sources so you can benchmark your own performance.

1. Customer and Sales Records

A sales register is only useful if it answers four questions: who bought, what they bought, at what price, and whether they paid. For smallholder-facing agribusinesses, the minimum viable sales record is a numbered sales book or a spreadsheet with the following columns: date, invoice/receipt number, customer name and type (trader, processor, retailer, consumer, institution), product and grade, quantity, unit price, gross value, discount, net value, payment method (cash, mobile money, bank transfer, credit), amount paid, balance owing, and due date.

Customer records sit alongside the sales register. Keep a customer card or database entry with contact details, location, preferred products, typical order size, payment behaviour, and any credit terms agreed. In many African markets, credit sales to traders are common but risky. A simple ageing schedule current, 30 days, 60 days, 90+ days turns a pile of receipts into a collections worklist.

2. Pricing and Margin Analysis

Price is not what you charge; it is what remains after every cost is recovered. Build a unit-level margin card for each product. For a 50 kg bag of maize, the card should list: seed, fertiliser, agrochemicals, labour (land prep, planting, weeding, harvest), transport, bagging, storage loss, levies, and overhead allocation. Subtract these from the selling price to get gross and net margin.

Two ratios matter most. Gross margin ratio = (revenue variable costs) revenue. Break-even volume = fixed costs (unit price unit variable cost). If your break-even volume is higher than your realistic harvest, the price or cost structure must change. Many African agribusinesses underprice because they omit family labour, transport, and post-harvest loss often 1030% for grains and perishables.

3. Direct Marketing and CSA Records

Direct marketing farm-gate sales, farmers' markets, box schemes, online orders, and community-supported agriculture (CSA) changes the record-keeping burden. You now manage many small customers, repeat orders, and delivery logistics. Keep a subscription register for CSA: member name, contact, box size, delivery day, payment status, and weeks remaining. Keep a packing list per delivery: box number, contents, weight, and any substitution.

Reconcile three numbers weekly: boxes packed, boxes delivered, and boxes paid for. A gap between packed and paid is either a delivery failure or a collections problem. For online and WhatsApp orders, screenshot or export the order thread into your register the same day. In markets where mobile money dominates (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money), record the transaction reference number, not just the amount.

4. Contract Farming Documentation

Contract farming records protect both the grower and the buyer. A complete file contains: the signed contract (quantities, quality grades, price formula, delivery window, payment terms), input advance records, field inspection notes, delivery notes, weighbridge or scale tickets, quality rejection notes, and payment advices. In out-grower schemes common in cotton, sugar, tea, tobacco, and horticulture across Africa, disputes usually trace back to missing delivery notes or unclear grade standards.

SAFETY WARNING Cash handling and credit risk

Never leave daily cash collections unbanked overnight, and never issue credit without a signed delivery note and a named customer. In high-inflation or high-default environments, an unrecorded credit sale is effectively a donation. Keep cash and mobile money float separate from personal funds, use a locked cash box with a two-person count, and reconcile bank/mobile money statements weekly. If a buyer refuses to sign a delivery note, treat the sale as high risk and require prepayment.

5. Inventory of Marketable Products

Inventory records connect production to sales. Keep a stock card per product and grade showing opening balance, receipts from harvest or processing, issues to sales, losses (spoilage, theft, shrinkage), and closing balance. Physical counts should be done at least monthly and always before a major sale or after a delivery. For perishables, record storage temperature and duration this feeds directly into your loss assumptions and pricing.

Use the formula: Closing stock = Opening stock + Receipts Sales Losses. If the physical count does not match, investigate before adjusting. Unexplained gaps are usually unrecorded sales, unrecorded home consumption, or theft.

6. Using Sales Data for Market Planning

Sales data becomes valuable when it feeds planning. Build a simple monthly dashboard: total revenue, gross margin, top five customers by value, top five products by volume, average price per unit, credit sales as a percentage of total sales, and stock turnover. Compare month-on-month and year-on-year. Look for seasonality, price trends, and customer concentration risk if one buyer is more than 40% of revenue, you are exposed.

Use the dashboard to set next season's planting or procurement plan, negotiate contracts earlier, and decide which products to drop. In African markets where price volatility is high, a three-month rolling average price is more reliable than a single spot price.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Every sale has a numbered receipt or invoice no exceptions, including cash sales.
  2. Customer records include contact, payment terms, and credit limit.
  3. Each product has a margin card showing all variable and fixed costs.
  4. Credit sales are aged weekly; 90+ days triggers a stop-supply decision.
  5. CSA and direct orders are reconciled weekly: packed = delivered = paid.
  6. Contract farming files contain contract, delivery notes, quality reports, and payment advices.
  7. Physical stock counts are done monthly and reconciled to stock cards.
  8. Monthly dashboard reviewed with the owner or board before the next buying or planting decision.
