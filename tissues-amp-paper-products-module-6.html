1. Calculating Cost per Roll: Materials, Labour, and Overheads

To make a profit, you must know exactly what each roll of tissue costs you to produce. Start by calculating your material cost per roll. Suppose you buy waste paper at 10,000 shillings per tonne. If you produce 1,000 rolls from one tonne, your paper cost per roll is 10 shillings. Add chemicals (bleach, dye) at 2,000 shillings per tonne, so 2 shillings per roll. Packaging: a plastic wrapper costs 1 shilling per roll. Total material cost = 13 shillings per roll.

Next, calculate labour. If you pay two workers 15,000 shillings each per day, and they produce 1,000 rolls per day, labour cost per roll = (2 x 15,000) / 1,000 = 30 shillings per roll. If you work yourself, decide a fair wage and include it. Overheads include electricity, water, rent, machine maintenance, and transport. Suppose these total 20,000 shillings per day for 1,000 rolls, so 20 shillings per roll. Total cost per roll = 13 + 30 + 20 = 63 shillings.

Now you know your break-even price. Any price above 63 shillings gives profit. But remember, production isn't always perfect: some rolls may be defective. If 5% are wasted, your effective cost per good roll rises. To account, divide total cost by (1 - waste rate). With 5% waste, cost per good roll = 63 / 0.95 = 66.3 shillings. Always round up to 67 shillings for safety.

Use a simple notebook to record daily expenses. At the end of each day, sum materials, labour, and overheads, then divide by the number of good rolls produced. This gives your true cost per roll. Update it weekly because material prices change. Knowing your cost per roll is the foundation for all pricing decisions.

2. Setting Wholesale and Retail Prices

Once you know your cost per roll, you can set prices that earn profit. Wholesale price is what you charge bulk buyers like shops or hotels who buy in large quantities. Retail price is what individual customers pay per roll or per pack. A common rule is to set wholesale at cost plus 20-30% profit, and retail at cost plus 50-80% profit. For example, if your cost per roll is 67 shillings, wholesale might be 85 shillings (27% markup) and retail 110 shillings (64% markup).

But check what competitors charge. Visit local shops and markets. If imported tissues sell at 100 shillings retail, you can price slightly lower, say 95 shillings, to attract buyers while still making 28 shillings profit per roll. For wholesale, offer a price that gives shops a good margin so they prefer your product. If a shop buys at 85 shillings and sells at 110, they make 25 shillings per roll. That's attractive.

Consider pack sizes. Many customers buy in packs of 10 or 20 rolls. For a 10-roll pack, retail price could be 1,000 shillings (100 per roll) to encourage larger purchases. Wholesale for a 10-roll pack might be 800 shillings (80 per roll) if they buy 50 packs or more. Always calculate your profit per pack to ensure you're not selling at a loss after discounts.

Don't forget transport costs for delivery. If you deliver to a wholesaler, add a delivery fee or include it in the price. A simple formula: Price = Cost + Desired Profit + Delivery. Write down your prices and review them monthly. As your costs change, adjust prices accordingly. Communicate price changes to your customers politely and in advance.

3. Competing with Imported Tissues on Price and Quality

Imported tissues often dominate shelves because they are soft, strong, and well-packaged. But they are expensive due to shipping and import duties. You can compete by offering similar quality at a lower price. First, study imported brands: buy a pack, feel the texture, check the number of plies, and see how it tears. Most imports are 2-ply or 3-ply. If you produce 2-ply, you can match them. If you only have 1-ply, improve by adding a second layer or using better pulp.

Quality matters. Customers will not buy rough, dusty tissue twice. Invest in good raw materials: clean waste paper, proper bleaching, and softeners if possible. Ensure your rolls are tightly wound and perforated neatly. Packaging should be clean and attractive. Print your brand name and contact details. A simple logo and clear plastic wrapper can make your product look professional.

Price competitively. Calculate your cost and set a price 10-20% below imported brands. For example, if imported 10-roll pack sells for 1,500 shillings, sell yours for 1,200 shillings. Highlight the savings to customers: "Same quality, save 300 shillings." For wholesale, offer even better rates to shops so they push your product.

Use local advantages: you can deliver faster, offer smaller order quantities, and build personal relationships. Schools, hotels, and churches often prefer local suppliers who respond quickly. Offer free samples to potential bulk buyers. Ask for feedback and improve. Over time, your brand will gain trust. Remember, competing on price alone is risky; combine fair prices with reliable quality and service.

4. Discounts for Bulk Buyers: Schools, Hotels, and Churches

Bulk buyers like schools, hotels, and churches purchase large quantities regularly. They expect discounts. A discount is a reduction in price for buying more. But you must ensure the discount still leaves you a profit. Start by calculating your cost per roll. Suppose it's 67 shillings. Your normal wholesale price is 85 shillings. For orders of 100 rolls or more, you might offer a 5% discount, selling at 80.75 shillings. That still gives 13.75 shillings profit per roll. For 500 rolls or more, offer 10% discount, selling at 76.5 shillings, profit 9.5 shillings per roll. Never go below your cost.

Create a simple discount structure: 1-99 rolls: no discount; 100-499 rolls: 5%; 500-999 rolls: 10%; 1000+ rolls: 15%. Write this on your price list. When approaching a school, ask how many rolls they use per term. If they use 2,000 rolls, offer the 15% discount. Show them the savings: normal price 85 x 2000 = 170,000; with 15% discount, 72.25 x 2000 = 144,500. They save 25,500 shillings. That's a strong incentive.

For hotels, offer a monthly supply contract with a fixed discount. For churches, offer a special price for events or regular orders. Always get a written order or agreement. Deliver on time and maintain quality. Bulk buyers value reliability. If you offer discounts but fail to deliver, you lose them. Also, track your profit per bulk order to ensure you're not over-discounting. Use a notebook to record each bulk sale: buyer name, quantity, discount given, total revenue, and profit. Review monthly to see which discounts work best.

5. Tracking Expenses and Profit with Simple Records

Without records, you cannot know if you are making a profit. Use a simple notebook or a cheap exercise book. Divide each page into columns: Date, Description, Money In, Money Out, Balance. Every time you buy materials, pay labour, or pay for electricity, write it under Money Out. Every time you sell tissues, write the amount under Money In. At the end of each day, calculate the balance by adding Money In and subtracting Money Out from the previous balance.

At the end of the week, total all Money In and all Money Out. The difference is your profit or loss. For example, if you spent 200,000 shillings on materials, labour, and overheads, and sold 250,000 shillings worth of tissues, your profit is 50,000 shillings. But this is gross profit. You must also set aside money for machine repairs, loan repayment, and your own salary. A simple rule: save 10% of profit for emergencies, 20% for business growth, and take 70% as your income.

Track your cost per roll weekly. If material prices change, your cost changes. Update your records. Also track how many rolls you produce and sell. If you produce 1,000 rolls but only sell 800, you have 200 rolls in stock. That stock has value but is not cash. Your profit calculation should only count sold rolls. Use a separate page for stock: Date, Rolls Produced, Rolls Sold, Rolls in Stock.

For better tracking, use a mobile phone app like a simple expense tracker or even a spreadsheet on a computer if available. But pen and paper work well. The key is consistency. Record every transaction, no matter how small. At the end of the month, review your records to see where you can cut costs or increase prices. Good records lead to good decisions.

6. Adjusting Prices When Raw Material Costs Change

Raw material costs for tissue productionwaste paper, chemicals, packagingchange often due to fuel prices, import duties, or seasonal availability. When your costs rise, you must adjust your prices to maintain profit. But do it carefully. First, calculate your new cost per roll. Suppose waste paper increases from 10,000 to 12,000 shillings per tonne. Your paper cost per roll rises from 10 to 12 shillings. If other costs stay the same, total cost per roll goes from 67 to 69 shillings. To keep the same profit margin, you need to increase your selling price.

Decide how much to increase. If you want to maintain the same profit per roll (say 18 shillings), your new wholesale price should be 87 shillings instead of 85. For retail, increase from 110 to 112 shillings. But customers may resist. Consider absorbing part of the increase by reducing other costs. Can you negotiate a better price for chemicals? Can you reduce waste? If you can cut overheads by 2 shillings per roll, you may not need to raise prices.

If you must raise prices, inform your customers in advance. For bulk buyers, give them one month's notice. Explain that raw material costs have gone up. Offer them a chance to buy at the old price if they order before the increase date. This builds goodwill. For retail customers, change the price on your packs and tell regular customers politely. Most will understand if you explain.

Monitor costs weekly. Keep a record of supplier prices. If costs fall, you can lower prices to stay competitive or keep prices and increase profit. But don't be too quick to lower; build a buffer for future increases. Always recalculate your break-even price after any cost change. Use a simple formula: New Price = New Cost + Desired Profit. Update your price list and records accordingly.

Practical Reference: Pricing and Costing for Profit in African Tissue & Paper Production

This practical reference is the working document for the Pricing and Costing for Profit module of the Rise AFRICA Skills course on Tissues & Paper Products. It assumes you already run or intend to run a small tissue converting operation buying jumbo reels (parent rolls), converting them into toilet rolls, kitchen towels, serviettes or facial tissues, and selling to wholesalers, retailers, schools, hotels and churches. The numbers below are benchmarks and frameworks, not fixed laws. Your local prices for waste paper, pulp, electricity, labour and transport will differ, but the structure of the calculation is the same across Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, Kampala, Lusaka and Johannesburg.

The single most common reason small tissue businesses fail in Africa is not poor quality it is pricing below true cost. A converter who does not know the cost per roll cannot know whether a bulk discount to a hotel is profit or charity. This reference gives you the arithmetic, the pricing logic, the record-keeping discipline and the triggers for repricing when raw material costs move.

1. Calculating Cost per Roll: Materials, Labour and Overheads

Work in cost per kilogram of finished paper first, then convert to cost per roll. This avoids the trap of guessing per-roll costs when reel weights and roll counts vary.

Formula: Cost per kg = (Jumbo reel cost + conversion materials + direct labour + overheads) usable kilograms produced. Usable kilograms = reel weight (1 waste rate). A realistic waste rate for a small converter is 410% (trim, core start-up, breaks, rejected rolls).

Then: Cost per roll = cost per kg grams per roll 1000. If a standard toilet roll uses 70 g of paper and your cost per kg is KES 145, cost per roll = 145 70 1000 = KES 10.15. Add packaging and you have your true ex-factory floor price.

2. Setting Wholesale and Retail Prices

Two prices, two customers, two jobs. Wholesale is a volume price to distributors and large buyers who collect or take delivery in bulk. Retail is the price to shops, kiosks and end consumers, and must absorb the retailer's own margin (typically 1530%).

A simple rule used by many African converters: wholesale = ex-factory cost 1.151.25; retail = wholesale 1.251.40. Never set retail below wholesale it destroys your distribution network. Publish a price list with a validity date (e.g. "prices valid 30 days") so you can reprice when pulp or waste-paper prices move.

3. Competing with Imported Tissues on Price and Quality

Imported tissue from Egypt, Turkey, China and South Africa often lands cheaper per roll because of scale and cheap shipping but it carries freight, duty, clearing and forex risk, and long lead times. Your advantages are freshness, local delivery, small order sizes, credit terms and brand trust.

Compete on total landed value, not headline price. A hotel that buys imported tissue may wait six weeks and hold three months of stock; you can deliver weekly. Quantify that: offer a slightly higher unit price with same-week delivery and 14-day credit, and you often win. On quality, match the key measurable: ply, sheet count, grammage, wet strength and core diameter. Buyers compare sheet count and ply far more than brand.

4. Discounts for Bulk Buyers: Schools, Hotels and Churches

Bulk buyers are the backbone of a tissue business in Africa but discounts must come from cost savings you actually make, not from margin you give away. You save on: fewer deliveries, larger order sizes, lower selling commission, reduced packaging handling and predictable cash flow.

SAFETY & BUSINESS WARNING

Never quote a bulk price before you have calculated cost per roll for the current month. Raw material and power costs in Africa can move 1530% in a single quarter; a discount agreed on last quarter's costs can become a loss-making contract. Also: do not store jumbo reels or finished tissue near open flame, generators or damp floors tissue is highly flammable and moisture ruins it. Keep a fire extinguisher and dry, raised storage in every warehouse.

5. Tracking Expenses and Profit with Simple Records

You do not need accounting software to start. You need four books, kept daily:

  1. Purchases book every jumbo reel, core, glue, fuel, spare part, with date, supplier and amount.
  2. Production book reels used, kilograms produced, rolls packed, waste kilograms.
  3. Sales book customer, quantity, unit price, discount, cash or credit.
  4. Cash book money in, money out, daily balance.

At month end, calculate: Gross profit = sales direct costs (materials + labour + power); Net profit = gross profit overheads (rent, salaries, transport, interest). Compare net profit to sales a healthy small tissue converter targets 815% net margin. If it is below 5%, reprice or cut waste.

6. Adjusting Prices When Raw Material Costs Change

Build a trigger rule into your price list: if the landed cost of jumbo reels or waste paper changes by more than 5%, review prices within 14 days. Communicate early and factually to customers "pulp prices rose 12% this quarter, so from 1 March our wholesale price per roll rises by 8%" and give bulk buyers 30 days' notice on existing contracts. Keep a simple index: track your cost per kg each month on one sheet; the trend line tells you when to move before your margin disappears.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • I know my cost per kg and cost per roll for this month, not last year.
  • My waste rate is measured, not guessed (target under 8%).
  • Wholesale price is at least 15% above ex-factory cost.
  • Retail price is above wholesale, allowing retailer margin.
  • Every bulk discount is tied to a real cost saving and written down.
  • I update purchases, production, sales and cash books daily.
  • I review prices whenever raw material cost moves more than 5%.
  • I keep at least 30 days' notice for contract price changes.
