Lessons

1. Heating water to the correct temperature for scalding

Scalding a pig is the key step that loosens the hair so it can be scraped off easily. The water must be hot enough to work but not so hot that it cooks the skin. The ideal temperature is between 60C and 65C (140F to 149F). If you don't have a thermometer, use the elbow test: dip your elbow into the water. It should feel very hot but not scalding. Another test is to dip a small area of the pig's back for a few seconds; if the hair comes off easily when you rub it, the temperature is right. If the water is too cool, the hair will be hard to remove. If it's too hot, the skin will turn pale and start to cook, making it tough and prone to tearing.

To heat water, use a large pot or a metal drum over a fire or gas burner. For a whole pig, you'll need 50 to 100 litres of water, depending on the size of the pig. Start with clean water. Bring it to a boil, then let it cool slightly or mix with cold water to reach the right temperature. Stir the water well before testing.

In many African settings, people use a large drum cut in half or a built-in concrete tank. If you're using a drum, make sure it's clean and free of rust. You can also add a little wood ash to the water; this helps break down the hair and makes scraping easier. But don't add too muchabout one handful per 20 litres is enough.

Always have a source of cold water nearby to adjust the temperature if it gets too hot. Keep a thermometer if possible, but learn the feel test. With practice, you'll know by touch. Remember, the goal is to scald, not boil. Work quickly once the pig is in the water, as the temperature will drop. If you're scalding more than one pig, reheat the water between animals.

Safety note: Never leave the fire unattended, and keep children away from the hot water. Use gloves and long sleeves to protect yourself from splashes.

2. Immersion and scraping techniques for removing hair

Once the water is at the right temperature, it's time to immerse the pig. If you have a large enough container, lower the whole pig into the water. If not, you can scald one half at a time. Hold the pig by the legs or use ropes to lower it carefully. Make sure the water covers the area you want to scald. Leave the pig in the water for 2 to 5 minutes. The time depends on the pig's size and hair thickness. For a small pig, 2 minutes may be enough; for a large one, up to 5 minutes.

After soaking, pull the pig out and place it on a clean surface, like a wooden pallet or a tarp. Immediately begin scraping the hair off with a sharp knife or a specialized scraper. A dull knife will tear the skin, so keep it sharp. Use a pulling motion, not pushing, to avoid cutting the skin. Scrape in the direction of the hair growth. You'll see the hair and a thin layer of dirt come off. Rinse the scraper often in clean water.

Work quickly because the hair becomes harder to remove as the pig cools. If you're scalding half at a time, do the first half, then flip the pig and scald the other half. For hard-to-reach areas like the head, ears, and between the legs, use a smaller knife or even a spoon to scrape. Be careful around the eyes and snout.

After scraping, check for any remaining hair. If there are patches, you can re-dip that area in hot water for a few seconds and scrape again. But don't over-scald. Once the hair is mostly off, rinse the pig with clean water to remove loose hair and dirt.

In many African communities, people work together: one person holds the pig, another scrapes, and a third keeps the water hot. This teamwork makes the job faster. Remember to keep the work area clean and dispose of hair properly to avoid attracting flies.

3. Using a blowtorch or fire to singe remaining hair

After scraping, there will still be fine hairs and sometimes stubborn patches. Singeing removes these and also helps to kill bacteria on the skin. You can use a blowtorch, a gas torch, or even a fire made with dry grass or wood. If using a blowtorch, keep the flame at a medium setting and hold it about 15-20 cm away from the skin. Move the flame back and forth quickly so you don't burn the skin. You want to singe the hair, not cook the meat.

If you don't have a blowtorch, you can use a fire. Build a small fire with dry grass or straw. Once it's burning well, pass the pig over the flames, turning it so all sides get exposed. Be careful not to let the pig catch fire. You can also use a metal sheet to reflect heat onto the pig. Another method is to use a hot iron rod or a piece of metal heated in the fire and run it over the skin.

Singeing should be done in a well-ventilated area, as the burning hair produces a strong smell and smoke. Wear gloves and keep a bucket of water nearby in case of emergencies. After singeing, the skin will be blackened in spots. That's normal. You'll scrape off the burnt layer in the next step.

In some African traditions, singeing is done with a blowtorch fueled by kerosene or gas. If you use a blowtorch, check the hose and connections for leaks before lighting. Never point the torch at yourself or others. Keep children away.

Singeing not only removes hair but also gives the skin a nice golden color and a crispy texture after cooking. It's an important step for a clean, professional finish.

4. Scraping and washing the skin for a clean finish

After singeing, the pig's skin will have burnt hair and a black layer. You need to scrape this off to get a clean, white skin. Use a sharp knife or a metal scraper. Work on a clean surface. Scrape firmly but carefully, using short strokes. You'll see the black layer come off, revealing the clean skin underneath. Rinse the scraper often in water.

For tough spots, you can dip the scraper in water or use a little ash as an abrasive. Ash also helps to remove any remaining hair and neutralizes odors. After scraping, wash the entire pig with clean water. You can use a brush to scrub the skin, especially in crevices like the armpits, groin, and between the toes.

If you want an extra clean finish, you can wash the pig with a mixture of water and lime juice or vinegar. This helps to remove any lingering smell and brightens the skin. Rinse thoroughly with clean water.

After washing, dry the pig with a clean cloth or let it air dry. The skin should be smooth and free of hair. Any remaining hair can be removed with a razor or a small knife. Check the skin for any cuts or bruises.

In many African markets, a clean, well-singed pig fetches a better price. Take your time with this step. A clean carcass is not only more appealing but also safer to eat because dirt and bacteria are removed.

Finally, inspect the skin for any wounds or abscesses. If you find any, cut them out and discard. Wash your hands and tools thoroughly after handling raw meat.

5. Removing hooves and lower legs

Removing the hooves and lower legs is done after the skin is cleaned. You can do this with a sharp knife, a meat saw, or a cleaver. First, locate the joint between the hoof and the leg. It's usually a visible line where the hair changes or where the leg narrows. Place the pig on its back or side.

If using a knife, make a cut around the leg just above the hoof, cutting through the skin and tendons. Then bend the hoof backward to expose the joint. Cut through the joint with a knife or saw. For larger pigs, a saw is easier. If you have a cleaver, you can chop through the bone with a firm strike.

After removing the hooves, you may want to remove the lower legs up to the knee or hock. This is optional but often done for easier handling. Cut through the joint at the knee. Again, use a saw or cleaver for the bone.

In some African cultures, the hooves and lower legs are cleaned and cooked as delicacies, often in soups or stews. If you plan to use them, wash them thoroughly and remove any hair or dirt. You can singe them again if needed.

If you're not using them, dispose of them properly. Don't leave them lying around, as they can attract flies and rodents.

Always use a stable cutting surface, like a sturdy table or chopping block. Keep your knives sharp and cut away from your body. Wear gloves if you have them. After removing the hooves, wash the legs with clean water and continue with the next steps of processing.

Remember, clean cuts make the carcass look better and reduce the risk of contamination.

6. Inspecting the skin for wounds or abscesses

Inspecting the skin is a crucial step for food safety. After the pig is scalded, scraped, and singed, look over the entire skin carefully. You're looking for wounds, abscesses, bruises, or any signs of disease. A healthy pig skin should be smooth, pale, and free of lumps or discharges.

Start at the head and work your way down. Check the ears, eyes, snout, and mouth. Look for cuts, sores, or swelling. Then move to the neck, shoulders, back, and sides. Feel the skin with your hands; you might detect lumps that you can't see. Abscesses are pockets of pus that can be felt as soft or firm lumps under the skin. If you find one, cut it out with a clean knife, making sure to remove all the infected tissue. Discard it properly.

Also check the belly, groin, and legs. Look for ticks, lice, or other parasites. If you see ticks, remove them with tweezers and dispose of them. Wash the area with disinfectant.

If you find many abscesses or signs of serious disease, like widespread redness or foul smell, the meat may not be safe to eat. In that case, consult a veterinarian or discard the carcass.

In African settings, where pigs may be raised in free-range conditions, wounds from fighting or accidents are common. Minor cuts can be trimmed away. But always err on the side of caution.

After inspection, wash the carcass again with clean water. This step ensures that only healthy meat goes to market or to your family's table. It also protects you and your customers from foodborne illnesses.

Keep records of any abnormalities you find, as this can help you improve your pig husbandry practices.

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Scalding, Dehairing, and Singeing the Carcass</h2>

<p>In small and medium pig-processing enterprises across Africa from backyard slaughter slabs in Uganda to semi-commercial abattoirs in Nigeria and Kenya the scalding, dehairing, and singeing stage determines both the saleable yield and the market price of the carcass. A clean, evenly white skin with no residual bristles, no scorch marks, and no abscesses can raise the value of a dressed pig by 1020% compared with a poorly finished carcass. Buyers in butcheries, hotels, and open markets judge quality first by the skin. This lesson gives you the practical numbers, timings, and techniques to get it right every time.</p>

<h3>1. Heating Water to the Correct Temperature for Scalding</h3>
<p>Scalding loosens the hair follicles and the outer layer of the epidermis so the bristles can be scraped off without tearing the skin. The target water temperature is <strong>6065 C (140149 F)</strong> for adult pigs. Below 58 C the hair will not release; above 70 C the skin cooks, turns grey, and becomes difficult to scrape this is called "scalding burn" and it damages the hide permanently.</p>
<p>In field conditions, most processors judge temperature by eye and hand, which is unreliable. A simple thermometer (even a cheap digital kitchen probe) pays for itself within one pig. If no thermometer is available, the traditional test is to dip a finger: at 6065 C you can hold your hand in the water for 23 seconds before it becomes painful. This is a rough guide only invest in a thermometer.</p>

<h3>2. Immersion and Scraping Techniques for Removing Hair</h3>
<p>Immersion must be complete and even. The pig should be fully submerged including the head, ears, armpits, and the inside of the legs for the full time. In practice, most African small-scale operations use a half-drum or a purpose-built scalding tank. The pig is held under with a hook or a wooden pole; it should be rolled and turned so that all surfaces contact the hot water.</p>
<p>After immersion, lift the pig onto a scraping table or clean concrete slab. Use a <strong>bell scraper</strong> (also called a hog scraper) or, if unavailable, a blunt knife, a piece of broken hoe blade, or a metal pot lid. Scrape with the grain of the hair, working from the head towards the tail. Do not press too hard you want to remove hair and the outer skin layer, not cut into the flesh.</p>
<p>Scrape in sections: head and ears first, then the back and flanks, then the belly and inner thighs, then the legs and feet. Re-dip the pig briefly if the hair starts to set again. A common mistake is to leave the head and ears until last by then the water has cooled and the hair is difficult to remove. Do the head first.</p>

<h3>3. Using a Blowtorch or Fire to Singe Remaining Hair</h3>
<p>Even after careful scraping, fine hairs and bristle roots remain, especially on the head, ears, legs, and around the belly. Singeing removes these and also helps to firm the skin and reduce surface bacteria. Two methods are common in Africa:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Blowtorch (gas):</strong> Fastest and most controllable. Use a standard propane or butane torch. Keep the flame moving constantly never hold it in one spot. The skin should darken slightly and the hair should curl and burn off, not char the skin. Work from head to tail, then the legs and belly.</li>
<li><strong>Open fire (straw, wood, or dried grass):</strong> Traditional and cheap. The carcass is passed over a low flame or laid on a clean metal sheet over hot coals. This requires two people: one to turn the carcass, one to control the fire. Avoid smoke from treated or painted wood it contaminates the meat.</li>
</ul>
<p>After singeing, scrape the skin again with a blunt scraper or knife to remove burnt hair and the scorched outer layer. This second scrape is what gives the carcass its clean, white appearance. Then wash thoroughly with clean water.</p>

<h3>4. Scraping and Washing the Skin for a Clean Finish</h3>
<p>The final finish is achieved by scraping, washing, and in premium markets a light shave with a sharp knife. Use clean, potable water. If the local water supply is questionable, add a small amount of food-grade sanitiser (e.g., 50100 ppm chlorine) to the final rinse. This is especially important for pork destined for urban butcheries or export.</p>
<p>Hang the carcass on a rail or gambrel and wash from top to bottom with a hose or a bucket and clean cloth. Pay attention to the armpits, groin, ears, and between the toes. A clean carcass dries to a pale pink-white colour; a poorly washed one looks grey and smells off within hours.</p>

<h3>5. Removing Hooves and Lower Legs</h3>
<p>Hooves are removed after scalding and singeing, while the carcass is still warm. Use a sharp knife, a meat saw, or a cleaver. The cut is made at the <strong>fetlock joint</strong> (the joint just above the hoof), not through the middle of the leg bone. Cutting through the bone leaves sharp splinters that can injure workers and contaminate the meat.</p>
<p>In many African markets, hooves and lower legs are a valuable by-product sold separately for soup, stew, or "paya." Do not discard them. Clean them thoroughly, singe off any remaining hair, and package them for sale. In Nigeria and Ghana, a set of four cleaned pig trotters can fetch 1525% of the value of the whole carcass. Treat them as a profit centre, not waste.</p>

<h3>6. Inspecting the Skin for Wounds or Abscesses</h3>
<p>After washing, inspect the entire skin under good light. Look for:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Abscesses:</strong> Firm, raised, pus-filled swellings, often at injection sites on the neck or rump. These must be trimmed away with a wide margin (at least 2 cm of clean tissue around the abscess) and condemned. Never cut into an abscess over the carcass it spreads bacteria.</li>
<li><strong>Wounds and scars:</strong> Open wounds, bite marks, or healed scars. Trim and condemn affected tissue.</li>
<li><strong>Skin lesions:</strong> Ringworm, mange, or erysipelas lesions. These may indicate a wider disease problem consult a veterinarian before selling the meat.</li>
<li><strong>Bruises:</strong> Dark, discoloured areas from rough handling. Trim them off; bruised meat spoils faster.</li>
</ul>
<p>Record every defect in your slaughter log. Over time, this data tells you which suppliers or transport methods are causing losses, and lets you negotiate price or change practices.</p>

<li><strong>Hot water (6065 C) causes severe burns in seconds.</strong> Always wear waterproof gloves, boots, and an apron. Never reach into the scalding tank with bare hands.</li>
<li><strong>Blowtorches and open fires are a fire and explosion risk.</strong> Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand and water within reach. Never use a blowtorch near fuel, dry grass, or gas cylinders. Check hoses and connections for leaks before every use.</li>
<li><strong>Sharp knives and cleavers cause serious cuts.</strong> Use cut-resistant gloves on the non-knife hand. Keep blades sharp a blunt knife requires more force and slips more easily.</li>
<li><strong>Ensure good ventilation.</strong> Burning hair and singeing produce smoke and fumes. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Never singe in an enclosed room.</li>
<li><strong>Zoonotic disease risk:</strong> Wear gloves and cover any open cuts on your hands. Wash hands and equipment with soap and clean water after handling any carcass.</li>
</ul>
