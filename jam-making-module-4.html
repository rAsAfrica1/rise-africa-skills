JAM MAKING · MODULE 4 OF 12

The Cooking Process

From raw fruit to finished jar. The pot, the batch, the stages, the temperature, and the moment you stop cooking.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Choose the right pot and equipment for the batch size you make
  • Scale a jam recipe correctly — including the equipment limits you cannot ignore
  • Identify the four stages of cooking and know what happens at each
  • Control temperature throughout the batch, not just at the end
  • Recognise the gel point and stop cooking at exactly the right moment
  • Cool and rest the jam so it firms properly before jarring
Lesson 4.1

The right equipment

Jam is simple to make, but the equipment matters more than beginners expect. Cheap or wrong equipment causes burnt batches, failed sets, and burns. The kit list is short and most of it is a one-time purchase.

The essentials

ItemWhyWhat to look for
A heavy-bottomed preserving pan or stockpotThin pots scorch jam on the bottom and burn the fruitStainless steel, aluminium core, or enamel. Not thin steel. Wide rather than tall — more surface area, faster evaporation
A long wooden spoonStirring hot jam with a short spoon risks burnsHandle long enough that your hand is well clear of the pot. Wood does not conduct heat. Buy two.
A jam thermometerThe most reliable way to know when jam will setMetal, clip-on, reads 0–200°C. Digital probe also works. Essential for any serious jam maker.
A wide-mouthed funnelFilling jars without spilling hot jamStainless steel or heatproof plastic. Wide neck lets fruit chunks through.
A jar lifterLifting hot jars out of boiling water safelyPurpose-made, with rubber-coated grips. Do not improvise with tongs.
A large slotted spoon or skimmerRemoving scum and seedsWide, flat, stainless.
A heatproof jug or ladlePouring jam into jars1L capacity is ideal. Do not use a plastic measuring jug — it will warp.
Clean clothsWiping jar rims before sealingCotton. Washable. Buy half a dozen.

Nice to have, but not essential to start

  • A refractometer — measures sugar concentration directly. Around $30. Useful once you are producing commercially.
  • A pH meter or pH strips — tells you if the acid level is right. More important for low-sugar jams.
  • A food mill or mouli — for pulpier jams where you want to remove seeds and skins mechanically.
  • A large muslin bag — for straining fruit juice and making jellies rather than jams.

Do not use these pots

  • Bare aluminium or unlined copper pots — react with fruit acid and change the colour and flavour of the jam. Copper is traditional in Europe for certain jams but only when properly tinned; unlined copper turns bright red with acid fruit.
  • Non-stick pots — the coating scratches and flakes into the jam at the temperatures you cook at. Never use them for jam.
  • Anything with a chipped enamel interior — the chip exposes the metal underneath, which reacts with the fruit.
  • Anything less than 2L capacity for anything more than a test batch — jam boils up and a small pot overflows.
Lesson 4.2

Batch sizes and scaling recipes

Most jam recipes are written for a fixed size — 1kg of fruit. When you scale, you cannot just multiply every ingredient by the same number. Jam has limits that individual recipes do not explain.

The rule of thumb

Maximum batch = about 4× the base recipe. A 1kg-fruit recipe can be scaled to 4kg, not to 20kg.

Why? Because larger batches:

  • Take much longer to heat and to reach setting temperature. During that extra time, colour and flavour are being destroyed by heat. A 4kg batch is fine. A 20kg batch tastes stewed.
  • Evaporate more slowly — the ratio of surface area to volume drops as the batch gets bigger. A large batch can take an hour longer to reach set than a small one.
  • Cannot be stirred properly in domestic equipment. You need a very large pot and a proper stirring paddle for anything over 5kg.
  • Set differently. The extra cooking time degrades pectin. A large batch sometimes needs more pectin than the linear calculation suggests.

Smaller batches produce better jam. Once you need more volume, make more batches, not bigger ones.

How to scale properly

  1. Scale the fruit and sugar linearly. Double the fruit, double the sugar. That part is linear.
  2. Scale the lemon juice linearly. Same reason.
  3. Scale the pectin carefully. Follow the packet ratio for the total fruit weight. Do not assume pectin scales linearly — sometimes large batches need slightly more.
  4. Do not scale the cooking time linearly. A double batch does not take double the time. It takes about 1.5×. Always cook by temperature and set test, never by clock.
  5. Reduce heat source. A very large batch on a domestic stove may need to cook in two pots rather than one, because a single burner cannot heat it evenly.

What commercial jam makers do

Commercial kitchens do not make 100kg batches in one pot. They make six or eight smaller batches side by side, or use a purpose-built steam-jacketed kettle that heats evenly across the whole volume. When you scale up, buy a bigger pot — do not stretch the same pot.

Lesson 4.3

The four stages of cooking

Jam goes through four distinct stages. Each has a purpose. Skipping one, or rushing through it, produces a worse jar.

A full walkthrough of a jam cook, showing the four stages and how the mixture changes.

Stage 1 — Preparing the fruit

Wash, peel, stone, chop. Put the fruit in the pot with a splash of water (usually 100ml per kg of fruit) and cook gently until soft. This stage can take 10–20 minutes depending on the fruit.

Why: cooking the fruit first extracts pectin, releases juices, and softens the fruit so the sugar can penetrate evenly. Skipping this stage means some fruit stays firm and the jam has an uneven texture.

Stage 2 — Adding sugar and dissolving

Add the sugar and stir gently over low heat until every grain of sugar has dissolved. You can tell when it is dissolved because the mixture goes from grainy-looking to clear and the spoon slides smoothly.

Do not boil before the sugar is fully dissolved. Undissolved sugar crystals become seeds for larger crystals later, giving a grainy jam. Stir gently and patiently until the mixture is clear.

Alternative traditional method: warm the fruit and sugar together overnight in the pot. By morning, the sugar has dissolved into the fruit juice without heat. Then proceed straight to Stage 3. This is the method used by many European jam makers and produces a very clear, fruit-forward jam.

Stage 3 — The hard boil

Once the sugar is dissolved, bring the mixture to a hard, rolling boil. This is where the jam sets. It typically takes 10–25 minutes depending on the fruit, the batch size, and the pot.

What is happening: water is evaporating from the mixture. As the water leaves, the sugar concentration rises. When it reaches 60–65%, the setting point arrives. Meanwhile, the pectin is slowly breaking down under the heat. This is why you must not overcook — after a point, the pectin has broken down and the jam will not set no matter how long you cook it.

The temperature you are looking for

At sea level, jam reaches setting point at 104°C (220°F). On a rolling boil, the mixture should reach this within 10–25 minutes. Start testing for set once you pass 102°C. If it takes more than 30 minutes, something is wrong — usually too much water in the fruit, or too small a pot for the batch, or too low a heat.

At altitude, subtract about 1°C for every 300m. See Module 3.

Stage 4 — Resting and jarring

Once the jam is set, take it off the heat. Wait 5 minutes — this is important — then stir gently to distribute any fruit that has floated to the top. Then jar it while hot (Module 5 covers filling).

Why wait 5 minutes: if you jar immediately, the fruit chunks float to the top of the jar and the liquid stays at the bottom. This is called "floating fruit" and is one of the most common signs of a beginner's jam. Allowing the jam to sit for 5 minutes lets the mixture thicken slightly, so the fruit stays evenly distributed.

The temperature rises through the fruit-cooking and sugar-dissolving stages, then climbs fast to the setting point at 104°C.

Lesson 4.4

Temperature control and thermometers

Temperature is the single most reliable guide during cooking. If you watch only one thing, watch the temperature.

What to look for at each stage

TemperatureStageWhat you should see
Below 60°CFruit prep, sugar dissolvingGentle simmer, fruit softening, sugar dissolving
60–90°CRapid sugar dissolveClear mixture, steam starting
90–100°CApproaching boilActive bubbling, mixture thickening
100–103°CRolling boilVigorous bubbling, rapid steam, mixture visibly reducing
104–105°CSet pointSlow, heavy bubbling, darker colour, strong fruit smell

The right thermometer

Two types work:

  • Clip-on jam thermometer — a metal dial thermometer with a clip that holds it to the side of the pot. Traditional, easy to read, stands up to jam temperatures. The probe sits in the jam and you can watch the needle.
  • Digital probe thermometer — faster, more accurate, but you need to hold it or clip it. Many are only rated to 100°C, so check that yours goes to at least 150°C. The cheaper ones fail in hot jam.

Do not use an infrared thermometer

Infrared (laser) thermometers measure surface temperature, not the temperature inside the jam. They are useless for jam making and will give you a reading 10–20°C below the true jam temperature. Use a probe that goes into the mixture.

The three thermometer mistakes

  1. Touching the bottom of the pot. The pot bottom is much hotter than the jam. If the probe rests on the bottom, you will read 110°C when the jam is at 100°C and overcook every batch. Keep the probe in the middle of the mixture.
  2. Reading only at the end. Watch the temperature throughout so you know when you are approaching the set point. If you only look when you think it might be done, you have already missed the moment.
  3. Not calibrating. Boil water and check the thermometer reads 100°C (or the correct figure for your altitude). If it reads 98°C, subtract 2°C from all your target temperatures. Test your thermometer once a month.
Lesson 4.5

Achieving the gel point

The gel point is the moment the jam sets — the point at which the sugar concentration is high enough that the mixture will form a gel as it cools. Reach it and the jam is good. Miss it by 5°C in either direction and the jam either will not set or will become too stiff.

The three tests, applied during cooking

You learned these in Module 3, but the practical application matters here. In order of reliability:

  1. Temperature. The most reliable. Watch for 104°C at sea level.
  2. Wrinkle test on a frozen plate. Reliable, but takes 1–2 minutes per check. Use this when temperature is close to target.
  3. Spoon sheet test. Useful as a rough guide, but not enough on its own.

Start testing early

Do not wait until you think the jam is done. Start testing for set the first time the temperature reaches 102°C. From there, check every 2 minutes. The distance between "almost set" and "over-cooked" is often only 3 minutes at high heat.

What "set" looks like when you have it right

A jam at the gel point, tested on a frozen plate:

  • Cools to a soft skin within 1 minute
  • Wrinkles when pushed with your fingertip
  • Holds the wrinkle — does not flow back into a flat pool
  • Has a slight "sheen" — the surface reflects light

A jam not yet at the gel point:

  • Stays liquid on the plate
  • Runs when tilted
  • Fails to hold any shape

The moment to stop cooking

The moment you confirm set, take the pot off the heat. Do not cook "one more minute" for safety. Every extra minute degrades pectin and darkens colour. The jam will firm up further as it cools — a jam that seems slightly loose on the plate will set properly in the jar.

The over-cooking trap

Beginners who cannot tell whether jam has set often cook it longer "to be safe." This is the wrong instinct. Over-cooked jam becomes stiff, dark, and can develop a burnt or caramel flavour. If you are unsure, test again in 2 minutes. Do not just add 5 minutes to be sure.

Lesson 4.6

Cooling and resting

The jam is set. The cooking is not quite done. What happens in the next 10 minutes decides the final texture of the jar.

Stage 1 — Take off heat and rest for 5 minutes

Remove the pot from the heat. Do not stir immediately. Let the jam sit for 5 minutes. During this time:

  • The jam thickens slightly as it cools
  • Any foam or scum rises to the surface and can be skimmed off
  • Fruit that was suspended in the boil settles back into the liquid

Stage 2 — Skim the scum

A pale foam forms on the surface. This is denatured fruit protein, harmless but it looks unprofessional and it can cloud the finished jam. Skim it off with a slotted spoon and discard. Do not stir it back in.

Some cooks add a small knob of butter (about 10g per kg of jam) at the end of cooking — it prevents the foam from forming in the first place. Optional, but it works.

Stage 3 — Stir to distribute fruit

After skimming, stir gently once or twice to distribute any fruit that has risen. Do not beat the mixture — you are distributing, not aerating.

Stage 4 — Rest for another 5–10 minutes

Let the jam rest again for 5–10 minutes before jarring. During this time, the jam continues to thicken slightly. Jarring too soon gives you:

  • Fruit that floats to the top of the jar
  • Thin liquid at the bottom of the jar
  • A visible separation when the jar is opened

Waiting until the jam has thickened to the point that a spoon drawn through it leaves a slow-moving track is the right moment. This is a visual cue, not a temperature cue.

But do not wait too long

Jam thickens quickly as it cools. If you wait 30 minutes, it will be too stiff to pour into jars and you will end up spooning it in. The window to jar is roughly 10–20 minutes after the cooking stops. Test it every few minutes with a spoon — when it is thick but still pourable, jar it.

Stage 5 — Jar, seal, cool, and hold

Filling and sealing are Module 5. But note here: the jam should be poured into jars while it is still hot (around 85°C), so the heat helps sterilise the jar and creates a vacuum as it cools. As soon as it drops below 80°C, the vacuum is weaker and the shelf life drops.

So the workflow is:

  1. Jam reaches set point — take off heat
  2. Rest 5 minutes
  3. Skim
  4. Stir once
  5. Rest 5–10 more minutes until pourable
  6. Jar and seal (Module 5)
  7. Cool jars at room temperature for 12–24 hours
  8. Check seals, label, store

Practical assignment — Module 4

Cooking assignment. Make a 2kg batch of jam. Follow the four stages explicitly.

  1. Set up your workspace before you start. Pot, wooden spoon, thermometer (calibrated — check it in boiling water), frozen plate, funnel, jars ready. Everything within reach.
  2. Stage 1 — cook the fruit first. 2kg of prepared fruit with 200ml water. Simmer until soft (10–20 min depending on the fruit). Note the time this takes.
  3. Stage 2 — add 2kg sugar and dissolve. Stir over low heat until every grain has gone. Note how long this takes.
  4. Stage 3 — boil hard to set. Once dissolved, raise the heat. Start checking temperature and set test once past 102°C. Note the temperature at which the jam set, and total time from start of boil to set.
  5. Stage 4 — cool and jar. Rest 5 min, skim, stir, rest 5 more min, then jar and seal.
  6. Photograph the four stages. One photo per stage showing what the mixture looked like. This is your visual reference for future batches.
  7. Write a full batch record including timings and temperatures. Note anything that went differently to what you expected.

Points 3, 4 and 7 carry the most marks. Point 3 tests whether you dissolved the sugar fully (grainy jam is the giveaway). Point 4 records the actual set temperature for your altitude and equipment. Point 7 gives you your own recipe notes for next time.

Module 4 knowledge check

1. Why should you not use a bare aluminium pot for jam?

  1. It is too heavy
  2. It reacts with fruit acid and changes the colour and flavour of the jam
  3. It is too expensive
  4. It conducts heat too slowly
Show answer

b. Bare aluminium reacts with acid fruit, turning the jam darker and giving it a metallic taste. Stainless steel, enamel, or properly tinned copper are the correct choices. Non-stick pots are also unsuitable because the coating can flake at jam temperatures.

2. What is the practical maximum batch size for a domestic jam pot?

  1. 20kg
  2. About 4× the base recipe — a 1kg base can scale to 4kg, not to 20kg
  3. Whatever the pot holds
  4. 50kg
Show answer

b. Large batches take much longer to reach set, so flavour and colour suffer. They also cannot be stirred properly with domestic equipment and can set unevenly. When you need more volume, make more batches — do not make one enormous one.

3. Why must you dissolve the sugar fully before boiling hard?

  1. To save fuel
  2. Undissolved sugar crystals become seeds for larger crystals later, giving a grainy jam
  3. It prevents the fruit from burning
  4. It stops the jam from setting too fast
Show answer

b. Any sugar crystal not dissolved before the boil will act as a nucleation point when the jam cools, forming visible sugar crystals in the finished jam. Stir gently and patiently until the mixture is completely clear before raising the heat.

4. What is the correct set point temperature at sea level?

  1. 100°C
  2. 104–105°C
  3. 110°C
  4. 95°C
Show answer

b. 104–105°C is the standard set point at sea level. At altitude, subtract about 1°C per 300m. Start testing once you pass 102°C and stop cooking the moment you confirm set — every extra minute degrades the pectin and darkens the colour.

5. Why should you wait 5 minutes after taking the jam off the heat before jarring?

  1. To let it cool enough to touch
  2. To skim scum, then stir to distribute the fruit so it does not float to the top of the jar
  3. To let the sugar crystals form
  4. To allow the pectin to break down
Show answer

b. If you jar immediately, fruit floats to the top and liquid sinks to the bottom — a classic beginner's jam. Resting for 5 minutes lets the mixture thicken slightly, then skimming removes foam and stirring distributes the fruit. Do not wait more than 20 minutes, or the jam becomes too stiff to pour.
