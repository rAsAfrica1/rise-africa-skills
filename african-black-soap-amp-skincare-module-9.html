1. Identifying Your Target Customers: Local and International

Start by listing who actually buys black soap in your area. Walk through your local market or ask friends who already sell. You will find three main local groups: women buying for family use (they care about price and quantity), young adults with acne or sensitive skin (they care about results and gentle ingredients), and small resellers like salons or boutiques (they care about wholesale margins). For international customers, focus on the African diaspora in the US, UK, and Canada, plus conscious consumers who want natural, ethical products. They care about authenticity, fair trade, and shipping reliability.

Use free tools to confirm demand. On Facebook, search for groups like 'Nigerian Natural Hair' or 'Ghanaian Skincare' and see what members ask about. On Instagram, check hashtags like #blacksoap, #africanskincare, and #naturalskincare. Note the questions people ask: 'Does it fade dark spots?' 'Is it good for eczema?' 'How long does shipping to London take?' These are your selling points.

Create two simple customer profiles. For local: 'Amina, 34, mother of three in Accra, buys 1kg of black soap every month for 15 cedis, wants it to last and not dry out skin.' For international: 'Chidi, 28, in Atlanta, buys 500g for $25 plus shipping, wants vegan, palm-oil-free, and a story about the women who made it.' Write these down and keep them visible when you make marketing decisions.

Test your assumptions cheaply. Make 10 small samples and give them to five local and five international contacts (via post or a friend traveling). Ask them to use it for two weeks and tell you what they would pay. Their answers will tell you more than any online course. Adjust your prices and packaging based on real feedback, not guesses.

2. Using Social Media (WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook) to Sell

WhatsApp is your most powerful tool for local sales. Set up a WhatsApp Business account (free) with your business name, a clear profile photo of your soap, and a catalog. Post a status update every morning showing your soap in use: someone washing hands, a close-up of the texture, a customer testimonial. Use broadcast lists to send weekly specials to past customers. For example: 'Buy 2 bars of 200g black soap for 10 cedis this week only.' Reply to every message within one hour. Save quick replies for common questions like 'How much?' and 'Do you deliver?'

Instagram works best for visual storytelling and international customers. Post 3-4 times a week: a photo of your soap with a natural background (leaves, wooden table), a 15-second video of you cutting a fresh batch, and a customer photo with their permission. Use hashtags like #blacksoap #africanskincare #naturalskincare #handmade. Add a link in your bio to a free WhatsApp order form or a simple Google Form. Go live once a week to answer questions and show your process. People buy from people they trust.

Facebook is for community and groups. Join 5-10 groups related to African hair, skincare, or diaspora living. Do not spam. Instead, answer questions helpfully: 'For dark spots, use black soap with shea butter at night.' Then mention your product only when relevant. Create a Facebook Page and post your Instagram content there too. Run a low-cost ad ($5-$10) targeting your city plus interests like 'natural hair' or 'shea butter'. Facebook ads can reach thousands for very little money.

Cross-promote everywhere. Put your WhatsApp number on your Instagram bio and Facebook page. In your WhatsApp status, mention your Instagram for photos. Ask happy customers to tag you. Consistency matters more than perfection. Post daily, even if it is just a short video of you packaging orders. That builds trust and keeps you top of mind.

3. Photographing Your Products with a Smartphone

You do not need an expensive camera. A smartphone from the last five years works. The key is natural light. Take photos near a window between 10am and 2pm, but avoid direct sunlight. A sheer white curtain diffuses light beautifully. Never use flashâit makes soap look greasy and harsh. Turn off overhead lights. If you must shoot at night, use a white bedsheet as a backdrop and two lamps with white bulbs, one on each side.

Create a simple background. Use a plain wooden board, a woven African cloth (kente, ankara, or mudcloth), or a clean white wall. Add natural props: fresh leaves, shea nuts, cocoa pods, or a small bowl of water. Keep it simpleâtoo many props distract. Place your soap on the surface at a slight angle. For a stack of bars, arrange them like a pyramid. For a single bar, shoot from a 45-degree angle and also straight down. Take at least 10 shots per setup.

Edit with free apps like Snapseed or Lightroom Mobile. Adjust brightness (+10 to +20), contrast (+5 to +10), and saturation (+5). Do not over-editâyour soap should look like soap, not plastic. Crop to square for Instagram or 4:5 for Facebook. Add a subtle watermark with your business name using the 'Text' tool. Save as high quality (at least 1080 pixels wide).

Shoot short videos too. A 15-second clip of you cutting a fresh block of soap, wrapping it in paper, or showing the lather in your hands gets high engagement. Use the rear camera, keep steady (rest your elbows on a table), and film in landscape. Add captions with the free app CapCut. Post one video per week. Customers love seeing the processâit proves your soap is real and handmade.

4. Writing Product Descriptions that Highlight African Heritage

Your product description is not just a list of ingredients. It is a story that connects the buyer to Africa. Start with a short, vivid sentence: 'Handmade in Ghana from plantain skins and cocoa pods, this black soap has been used for generations to cleanse and nourish.' Then explain the heritage: 'Our recipe comes from our grandmother in Kumasi, who learned it from her mother. We still use the same sun-drying and roasting methods.' This builds trust and justifies a higher price.

List the ingredients clearly and honestly. For example: 'Plantain skins, cocoa pods, shea butter, palm kernel oil, water.' If you use palm oil, say so and explain it is sustainably sourced. If you are palm-oil-free, highlight that. Mention the benefits simply: 'Gently exfoliates, helps fade dark spots, and leaves skin soft.' Avoid medical claims like 'cures eczema'âinstead say 'traditionally used for sensitive skin.'

Include practical details. Weight: 'Each bar is approximately 200g.' Dimensions: '10cm x 6cm x 3cm.' Scent: 'Natural smoky scent from roasted plantain skins.' Shelf life: 'Use within 12 months.' Shipping: 'Ships from Accra within 2 days. International shipping to US/UK takes 7-14 days.' For international customers, add a note about customs: 'We declare as soap, no extra fees.'

End with a call to action and a guarantee: 'Try it for 30 days. If you don't love it, we'll refund your money.' Add a personal touch: 'Every bar is cut by hand by our team of five women in Tamale. Your purchase supports their families.' This turns a simple soap into a meaningful purchase. Write in clear, simple English. Avoid jargon. Read it aloudâif it sounds like a friend talking, it is right.

5. Selling at Local Markets, Trade Fairs, and Pop-Up Shops

Local markets are the fastest way to get cash and feedback. Find a market that already sells natural products or cosmeticsânot a food market. In Accra, try Makola Market or the Arts Centre. In Lagos, try Lekki Market. In Nairobi, try Maasai Market. Rent a small table or mat for the day. Cost: 20-50 cedis or equivalent. Arrive by 7am to get a good spot. Bring a banner with your business name, a price list, and a mirror so customers can see the soap on their skin.

Set up your stall to invite touch and smell. Put a bowl of water and a small piece of soap for hand-washing demos. Let people lather up. Have samples in small paper bags (10g each) to give away. Price clearly: '200g bar â 15 cedis. 3 bars â 40 cedis.' Offer a 'buy 2 get 1 free' for first-time customers. Keep a notebook to record what people ask and what they buy. That data is gold for your next event.

Trade fairs and pop-up shops attract higher-income and international customers. Look for events like 'Africa Fashion Week', 'Natural Hair Fest', or local craft fairs. A booth may cost 100-500 cedis, but you can sell 50-100 bars in a day. Bring a card reader (like Paystack or Flutterwave) and a mobile money number. Display your soap in woven baskets and wooden bowls. Wear African printâit draws people in. Have a sign that says 'Handmade in [your country]' and 'Free sample with every purchase.'

After the event, follow up. Collect phone numbers or emails from everyone who bought or showed interest. Send a WhatsApp message the next day: 'Thank you for buying! Here is a 10% discount for your next order.' Ask for a photo of them using the soap. This turns a one-time market sale into a repeat customer. Track your sales: how many bars, total revenue, best-selling size. Use that to plan your next market day.

6. Building Customer Loyalty with Samples and Follow-Ups

Samples are the cheapest marketing you can do. Cut your regular 200g bars into 20g pieces. Wrap each in a small piece of paper with your WhatsApp number and Instagram handle. Give one sample with every purchase, and give five samples to anyone who says they will share with friends. Cost per sample: about 1 cedi. If one in five sample recipients buys a full bar, you make 15 cedis for every 5 cedis spent. That is a 3x return. Always ask for feedback: 'Did you like it? What would make it better?'

Follow up within 48 hours of every sale. Send a WhatsApp message: 'Hi [Name], thanks for buying our black soap! How is your skin feeling? Reply with a photo and get 10% off your next order.' For international customers, send an email with a simple survey: 'On a scale of 1-10, how likely are you to recommend us?' Offer a small discount for a review. Reviews on your Instagram or Facebook page are social proof that sells for you while you sleep.

Create a simple loyalty program. After 5 purchases, the 6th bar is free. Track it with a notebook or a free app like Loyverse. Send a birthday message with a 20% discount code. For repeat customers, offer a subscription: 'Get 2 bars delivered every month for 25 cedis instead of 30.' This gives you predictable income and them a steady supply. Announce new products (like liquid black soap or shea butter) first to your loyal customers.

Surprise and delight. Include a small extra gift with every 5th orderâa lip balm, a soap dish, or a handwritten thank-you note. For international orders, add a small piece of African fabric or a bead. These small touches cost little but create stories that customers share with friends. Keep a list of your top 20 customers. Message them personally every 2-3 months: 'We just made a fresh batch. Want me to reserve 5 bars for you?' That personal care turns buyers into fans.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Marketing and Selling African Black Soap

African black soap (also called ose dudu in Yoruba, alata samina in Ghana, or sabulun salo in Hausa) is one of Africa's most recognisable skincare exports. For a small producer, the difference between a hobby and a business is not the recipe â it is how you find customers, present the product, price it, and keep people coming back. This Practical Reference gives you the operational tools to sell black soap locally and internationally, whether you work from a kitchen in Kumasi, a workshop in Lagos, or a co-operative in Nairobi.

Treat every lesson below as a checklist you can apply this week, not theory. Marketing is a cost centre until it produces repeat buyers; your job is to make each cedi, naira, or shilling of marketing spend traceable to a sale.

1. Identifying Your Target Customers: Local and International

Black soap sells to two very different buyers. Locally, your customer is often a household buyer purchasing for family use, a salon owner buying in bulk, or a reseller at a market. Internationally, your customer is usually a diaspora consumer, a natural-cosmetics retailer, or a wellness influencer sourcing "authentic African" products.

Profile each segment before you spend money on marketing. Write down: age, income, where they shop, what problem the soap solves for them (eczema, acne, hyperpigmentation, hair care), and what they are willing to pay. A Lagos salon owner cares about bulk price and consistency; a London-based buyer cares about packaging, ingredient transparency, and shipping reliability.

2. Using Social Media (WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook) to Sell

In Africa, WhatsApp is not a messaging app â it is a shopfront. Set up WhatsApp Business with a catalogue, an away message, and a greeting message. Post daily status updates showing soap being made, cut, cured, and packed. Status views are free reach; a 30-second clip of hot soap being poured converts better than a polished advert.

Instagram and Facebook serve different roles. Instagram is visual and skews younger and diaspora-focused; use Reels and carousels with before/after skin results (with consent). Facebook is stronger for local community groups, market days, and older buyers; join groups such as "Nigerians in Diaspora" or "Ghana Business Network" and post value-first content, not spam.

Practical rules: post at least four times a week, reply to DMs within one hour during business hours, and always include a call to action ("DM 'SOAP' to order"). Keep a simple content calendar â Monday: ingredient story, Wednesday: customer review, Friday: price list and delivery options, Sunday: restock reminder.

3. Photographing Your Products with a Smartphone

You do not need a studio. You need natural light, a clean background, and a steady hand. Position the soap near a window between 9am and 11am or 3pm and 5pm, avoiding harsh midday sun. Use a plain cloth (brown hessian, white cotton, or a woven African textile) as a backdrop. Wipe the soap to remove dust, and include one prop that signals origin â raw shea butter, cocoa pods, or palm kernel.

Shoot at least five angles: top-down flat lay, 45-degree hero shot, close-up of texture, in-hand shot for scale, and a lifestyle shot (soap beside a basin). Edit only for brightness and crop using free apps such as Snapseed or Lightroom Mobile. Avoid over-filtering â international buyers distrust heavily edited images.

4. Writing Product Descriptions that Highlight African Heritage

Your description must do three jobs: explain what the soap does, prove it is authentic, and make the buyer feel connected to a tradition. Open with a benefit, follow with heritage, close with a clear instruction.

Example structure: "Handmade in [town/country] using a recipe passed down through three generations, this African black soap is rich in shea butter and plantain ash â traditionally used across West Africa for gentle cleansing and blemish-prone skin. Each bar is cured for 21 days. Order today and we ship within 48 hours."

Use words buyers search for: handmade, authentic, shea butter, plantain ash, palm kernel oil, fair trade, diaspora. Avoid vague claims like "miracle soap" â they trigger scepticism and, in some markets, regulatory problems. For international sales, always state the net weight in grams, ingredients in INCI-compatible language where possible, and country of origin.

5. Selling at Local Markets, Trade Fairs, and Pop-Up Shops

A market stall is your cheapest customer-research lab. Book a stall at a busy local market for one weekend, then a trade fair, then a pop-up in a mall or co-working space. At each, track three numbers: footfall, conversations, and sales. If 100 people pass and 10 buy, your display or price is the problem â not the soap.

Stall essentials: a tablecloth in earthy colours, a price list in large print, a QR code linking to your WhatsApp catalogue, a sample bowl with cut slivers, and a notebook for customer phone numbers. For trade fairs, prepare a one-page wholesale price sheet and business cards. For pop-ups, partner with a skincare or wellness brand to share footfall and cost.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Cosmetic Claims and Regulation

Never claim that African black soap "cures" eczema, acne, psoriasis, or any medical condition. In the EU, UK, US, and most African markets, cosmetics may only make cosmetic claims (cleanses, moisturises, exfoliates). Medical claims can trigger product recalls, fines, or bans â for example under the EU Cosmetics Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009, the US FDA, or Nigeria's NAFDAC cosmetics rules. If a customer reports a reaction, stop sale of that batch, record the batch number, and consult your national regulator. Always list ingredients and a batch code on the label.

6. Building Customer Loyalty with Samples and Follow-Ups

Repeat customers cost far less than new ones. Build loyalty with four habits: (1) include a free sliver sample with every order, especially for first-time buyers; (2) follow up by WhatsApp 7 days after delivery asking how the soap performed; (3) offer a refill or bundle discount for the second purchase; (4) ask for a review or testimonial in exchange for a small gift.

Keep a simple customer register (name, phone, product, date, next follow-up). A monthly message to past buyers â "Your soap may be running low, reply REFILL for 10% off" â typically recovers 10â20% of dormant customers. For international buyers, use email and include a tracking link; diaspora customers value reliable communication as much as the product itself.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Define two target segments (local + international) with a written profile before spending on ads.
  • Set up WhatsApp Business with catalogue, greeting, and away messages.
  • Post at least four times weekly across WhatsApp Status, Instagram, and Facebook.
  • Shoot five smartphone photos per product using natural light and a heritage prop.
  • Write descriptions that lead with benefit, prove heritage, and end with a clear order instruction.
  • Track footfall, conversations, and sales at every market, fair, or pop-up.
  • Include a free sample and a 7-day WhatsApp follow-up with every first order.
  • Never make medical claims; label every bar with ingredients and a batch code.
