The Four Ingredients: Greens, Browns, Water, Air

Composting is like cooking: you need the right ingredients in the right amounts. The four essential ingredients are greens, browns, water, and air. Greens are nitrogen-rich materials that provide protein for the microbes that break down the compost. Examples include kitchen scraps (fruit and vegetable peels, coffee grounds, tea leaves), fresh grass clippings, animal manure (cow, goat, chicken, but not dog or cat), and green plant trimmings. Browns are carbon-rich materials that provide energy for microbes. Examples include dry leaves, straw, hay, cardboard, newspaper, sawdust, and wood ash (in small amounts)..Trim()

.Trim()

The ideal ratio is about 2 to 3 parts browns to 1 part greens by volume. If you have too many greens, the pile becomes slimy and smelly. Too many browns, and it breaks down very slowly. A simple way to remember: if it's wet and green, it's a green; if it's dry and brown, it's a brown. For every bucket of kitchen scraps, add two buckets of dry leaves or straw..Trim()

.Trim()

Water is the third ingredient. The pile should be as moist as a wrung-out spongeânot dripping wet, not bone dry. In dry climates, you may need to water the pile every few days. In rainy season, you might need to cover it to prevent it from getting too wet. Air is the fourth ingredient. Composting microbes need oxygen to work efficiently. Without air, the pile becomes anaerobic, producing bad smells and slow decomposition. Turning the pile introduces air. You can also add coarse materials like small sticks or maize cobs to create air pockets..Trim()

.Trim()

To assemble: start with a layer of browns about 15 cm deep, then add a layer of greens, then a thin layer of soil or old compost (to introduce microbes). Repeat these layers until the pile is about 1 meter high. Water each layer lightly. Your pile will heat up within a few days as microbes get to work. If it doesn't heat up, check your ratio: add more greens if it's too dry, or more browns if it's too wet. With the right balance, you'll have compost in 4-8 weeks..Trim()

Layering Browns and Greens: The Ideal Ratio

The secret to a hot, fast-working compost heap is the right mix of 'browns' and 'greens'. Turning Schedule: When and How Often

Turning your compost heap is like stirring a pot of stewâit mixes everything, adds oxygen, and speeds up decomposition. What is Bokashi and How Does It Work?

Bokashi is a Japanese term meaning "fermented organic matter." Unlike traditional composting that relies on oxygen-loving microbes to break down waste over weeks or months, bokashi uses anaerobic (oxygen-hating) microbes to ferment food waste in a sealed container. The process is fastâoften just 10 to 14 daysâand produces a nutrient-rich pre-compost that you bury in soil to finish breaking down. The key microbes are lactic acid bacteria, yeasts, and phototrophic bacteria. They come in a bran carrier, which you sprinkle on food waste. The bran is inoculated with a starter culture, often a mix of molasses, water, and a commercial bokashi inoculant or a homemade version using rice water and milk. In Africa, you can make bokashi bran using locally available materials like wheat bran, rice husks, or sawdust. The fermentation process works because the sealed bucket creates an oxygen-free environment. The microbes produce lactic acid and other compounds that lower the pH, preventing rotting and foul smells. Instead, you get a pickled smell, like fermented vegetables. The waste does not decompose visually; it just softens and ferments. After fermentation, you bury the waste in a hole or trench, where soil microbes and worms finish the job, turning it into compost within 2 to 4 weeks. Calculating Production Costs and Profit Margins

To turn your compost into a business, you must know your costs. Start by listing every input: raw materials (crop waste, manure, ash), labour, water, bags, and transport. Your profit margin is $105 per tonne, or about 66%. But don't forget hidden costs: tools, rent, and your own time. Track everything for one month to get real numbers. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Excel on your phone. In Kenya, a small compost business with 10 tonnes monthly can net $1,050. But if you underprice, you'll work for nothing. Always add 10% for unexpected expenses. Then set your price: cost per bag plus 30-50% profit. For example, if cost per 50kg bag is $2.75, sell at $4.00. That's a 45% margin. Calculate break-even: if fixed costs are $200 monthly (rent, phone), you need to sell 2 tonnes at $4 per bag to cover it. Anything above is profit. Keep records of every sale and expense.
