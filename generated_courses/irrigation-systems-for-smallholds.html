Overview of Irrigation Methods for Smallholders

Understanding Soil Texture and Structure

Soil texture is the mix of sand, silt, and clay particles. Sand feels gritty, silt feels like flour, and clay feels sticky when wet. To test your soil, put a handful in a jar with water, shake it, and let it settle. Sand sinks first, then silt, then clay. After 24 hours, you can measure the layers. A loam soilâabout 40% sand, 40% silt, and 20% clayâis best for most crops because it holds water and drains well. In many parts of Africa, soils are sandy (like in the Sahel) or clayey (like in parts of the Congo Basin). Sandy soils drain fast but need frequent watering. Clay soils hold water but can become waterlogged. Structure is how soil particles stick together into crumbs. Good structure means roots can grow and water moves easily. You can improve structure by adding compost or manure. For example, add 2-3 handfuls of well-rotted manure per square meter before planting. Avoid tilling wet soil because it destroys structure. A simple test: dig a small hole, fill it with water, and see how fast it drains. If it drains in less than 30 minutes, your soil is too sandy; if it takes more than 2 hours, it's too clayey. Knowing your soil helps you choose irrigation methods. Sandy soils need drip irrigation or frequent small waterings. Clay soils can use furrow irrigation but avoid overwatering. In Africa, many smallholders have sandy soils, so adding organic matter is key. Mulching with crop residues also protects structure and reduces evaporation..Trim()

Soil Moisture Basics: Field Capacity and Wilting Point

Field capacity is the amount of water soil holds after excess water has drained away. Think of a sponge after you squeeze itâit's still wet but not dripping. Wilting point is when soil is so dry that plants can no longer pull water from it and they wilt permanently. The difference between field capacity and wilting point is called available water capacity (AWC). For sandy soil, AWC is about 5-10% by volume; for loam, 15-20%; for clay, 18-25%. To check your soil moisture, squeeze a handful. If it forms a ball and leaves a wet outline on your hand, it's near field capacity. If it crumbles and won't hold together, it's near wilting point. You can also use a simple tensiometer, a tool that measures how tightly water is held. In Africa, you can make a DIY tensiometer with a ceramic cup, a tube, and a vacuum gauge, but buying one for $20-$50 is easier. For smallholders, a cheaper method is to use a soil auger or a spade to dig 15 cm deep and feel the soil. Irrigate when soil at root depth feels dry but before plants wilt. For most crops, irrigate when 50% of available water is used. For example, if your loam soil holds 20% AWC, irrigate when it drops to 10%. This avoids stressing plants and saves water. In sandy soils, check moisture every 2-3 days; in clay, every 5-7 days. Overwatering wastes water and leaches nutrients. Underwatering reduces yields. A simple rule: if you can't squeeze a ball, it's time to water..Trim()

Determining Infiltration Rates

Infiltration is how fast water enters the soil. If water runs off instead of soaking in, you're wasting water and causing erosion. To test infiltration, use a tin can with both ends removed. Push it 5 cm into the soil, pour in 2.5 cm of water, and time how long it takes to disappear. Do this three times. The rate is the depth divided by time. For example, if 2.5 cm soaks in in 10 minutes, that's 0.25 cm per minute. Sandy soils infiltrate at 2.5 cm/hour or more; loams at 1-2 cm/hour; clays at less than 0.5 cm/hour. If your rate is very low, you may need to improve soil structure with compost or use drip irrigation. High infiltration (sandy) means you need to water more often but in smaller amounts. In Africa, many soils are crusted or compacted from overgrazing or plowing. To improve infiltration, add organic matter, avoid tilling wet soil, and plant cover crops like cowpeas or lablab. You can also make small basins around plants to hold water. For furrow irrigation, the furrow length and slope affect infiltration. A slope of 0.5-2% is ideal; steeper slopes cause runoff. If infiltration is too fast, you can add clay or compost. If too slow, add sand or gypsum. A simple infiltration test helps you decide how long to run your irrigation. For example, if your soil takes in 1 cm/hour and you need to apply 3 cm of water, you must run your sprinkler for 3 hours. Without this test, you might overwater or underwater. Always test before planting and after heavy rains..Trim()

Crop Water Requirements and Evapotranspiration

Crop water requirement is the amount of water a crop needs from planting to harvest. It depends on the crop, climate, and growth stage. Evapotranspiration (ET) is water lost from soil evaporation and plant transpiration. In hot, dry areas, ET can be 5-8 mm per day; in humid areas, 2-4 mm per day. To estimate ET, use a simple pan evaporimeter: a shallow pan filled with water. Measure how much water evaporates in 24 hours. Multiply by a crop factor (0.6-1.2 depending on crop and stage) to get crop water use. For example, if pan evaporation is 6 mm and your maize is at tasseling (factor 1.1), crop water use is 6.6 mm. That means you need to apply 6.6 liters per square meter per day. For a 10 m x 10 m plot, that's 660 liters daily. In Africa, many smallholders grow maize, sorghum, millet, cowpea, and vegetables. Maize needs 500-800 mm total; sorghum 400-600 mm; cowpea 300-500 mm. Critical periods: maize needs most water at tasseling and silking; sorghum at booting and flowering; vegetables throughout. You can reduce ET by mulching, which cuts evaporation by 30-50%. Also, water early morning or evening to reduce evaporation. Drip irrigation saves water by applying it directly to roots. If you don't have a pan, use a simple rule: if the top 5 cm of soil is dry, irrigate. But for better accuracy, keep a daily record of rainfall and irrigation. A rain gauge costs $5-$10. Knowing crop water needs helps you avoid over- or under-irrigation, which can reduce yields by 20-50%..Trim()

Identifying Critical Irrigation Periods for Common Crops

Critical irrigation periods are when water shortage causes the biggest yield loss. For maize, the most critical stage is tasseling and silking (about 60-70 days after planting). Water stress then can cut yields by 50%. Next is grain filling. For sorghum, critical periods are booting (when the head emerges) and flowering. For millet, it's flowering and grain filling. For cowpea, it's flowering and pod filling. For tomatoes, it's flowering and fruit set; for onions, it's bulb formation. In Africa, many smallholders grow these crops. To manage critical periods, plan your irrigation schedule. For example, if you grow maize in a dry area, ensure you have enough water during tasseling. If water is scarce, prioritize that stage. You can use a simple calendar: mark planting date, then count days to critical stages. For maize, tasseling at 60 days, silking at 65 days. For sorghum, booting at 50 days, flowering at 60 days. For cowpea, flowering at 40 days, pod fill at 55 days. During these periods, check soil moisture daily. If the top 10 cm is dry, irrigate. Apply enough water to wet the root zone (about 30 cm deep). For sandy soil, water every 2-3 days; for clay, every 5-7 days. Avoid waterlogging, which can also reduce yields. In many parts of Africa, rainfall is erratic, so supplemental irrigation is key. Even small amounts (10-20 mm) at critical stages can make a big difference. For example, a 10 mm irrigation at maize tasseling can increase yields by 20%. Use mulch to conserve moisture. If you have a drip system, run it daily during critical periods. Keep records to learn your crop's needs..Trim()

Simple Tools for Soil Moisture Monitoring

You don't need expensive equipment to monitor soil moisture. The cheapest tool is your hand. Dig 15 cm deep with a spade, take a handful of soil, and squeeze. Designing a Gravity-Flow System from a Tank

To design a gravity-flow system, you need a water source higher than your field. A tank on a stand, a rooftop tank, or a small dam on a hill works. The key is elevation difference, called head. .Trim()

For a 1-hectare field with maize, you might need 30,000 liters per day. If you have 10 hours to irrigate, that's 3,000 liters per hour, or 50 liters per minute. A 50mm (2-inch) pipe can carry about 100 liters per minute at low pressure, so it's enough..Trim()

.Trim()

Choose your pipe size based on flow and distance. A 32mm (1.25-inch) pipe can carry about 30 liters per minute over 50 meters. If you need more, use 50mm. Always use larger pipes for the main line and smaller for laterals. Install a filter if you use drip, and a valve at the tank to control flow. Bury pipes 30cm deep to protect them from sun and animals. At the field, you can use a manifold to split into smaller lines..Trim()

.Trim()

Test your system before planting. Fill the tank and open the valve. Check that water reaches the farthest point with enough pressure. If it's too slow, raise the tank or increase pipe size. A well-designed gravity system saves fuel and labor, and works even when there's no electricity..Trim()

Head is the vertical distance water falls. It creates pressure. Flow rate is how much water comes out per minute. To calculate head, measure from the water surface in your tank to the outlet. Pressure loss in pipes is real. For every 10 meters of 25mm pipe, you lose about 0.1 bar at 20 liters per minute. So if you have 50 meters of 25mm pipe, you lose 0.5 bar. Furrow irrigation is the oldest and cheapest gravity method. You dig small channels (furrows) between crop rows and let water flow from a head ditch or pipe. Without one, you risk overwatering (wasting water, leaching nutrients) or underwatering (stressing crops, reducing yields). Start by knowing your soil type. Sandy soils hold little water and need frequent, small applicationsâmaybe every 2-3 days. Clay soils hold more but drain slowly; irrigate every 5-7 days. Loam is ideal: every 3-4 days. Next, know your crop's root depth and critical growth stages. For example, maize needs more water during tasseling and silking. Tomatoes peak during flowering and fruit fill. A simple schedule: check soil moisture by hand (dig 10 cm, squeeze a handfulâif it crumbles, it's dry; if it forms a ball, it's moist). If dry, irrigate until the top 30 cm is wet. For a typical 1-hectare vegetable plot in Kenya, you might apply 20-30 mm per irrigation (20-30 litres per square metre) every 3 days. Use a rain gauge or a simple tin can to measure how much water you apply. If you have a 200-litre drum and a watering can, you can calculate: 1 mm of water = 1 litre per square metre. So 20 mm = 20 litres per square metre. For a 10 m x 10 m plot (100 mÂ²), you need 2000 litres. That's 10 drums. Adjust based on rainfall: if it rains 10 mm, skip that day's irrigation. Write your schedule on a calendar or phone reminder. Review weeklyâcrops change, weather changes. A good schedule saves water, time, and money..Trim()

Using Evapotranspiration Data for Scheduling

Evapotranspiration (ET) is the combined water lost from soil evaporation and plant transpiration. Knowing ET helps you replace exactly what the crop uses. In Africa, many weather stations and online tools provide ET data. For example, the FAO's AquaCrop or the online tool 'CropWat' (free from FAO) can estimate ET for your location. But you don't need a computer. A simple pan evaporimeterâa shallow pan of water exposed to sunâcan give you a rough ET estimate. Place a pan (like a frying pan) in an open area, fill with water, and measure daily loss. Pan evaporation is usually 1.2 to 1.5 times higher than crop ET. So if the pan loses 8 mm, your crop might use about 6 mm. For a maize field at flowering, daily ET might be 5-7 mm in hot, dry conditions. That means you need to apply 5-7 litres per square metre per day. If you irrigate every 3 days, apply 15-21 mm. For a 100 mÂ² plot, that's 1500-2100 litres. But ET varies with crop stage: young seedlings use 1-2 mm/day, while full canopy uses 5-8 mm/day. Use a simple table: for leafy vegetables, ET is about 3-5 mm/day; for tomatoes, 4-6 mm/day; for maize, 5-7 mm/day. Check local weather forecastsâif a heatwave is coming, increase water. If cloudy and cool, reduce. Many African farmers use the 'crop coefficient' method: ETc = ETo x Kc. ETo is reference ET (from a weather station or pan), and Kc is crop coefficient (e.g., 0.4 for seedling, 1.1 for mid-season maize). You can find Kc values in FAO-56. Start with these numbers, then adjust by watching your plantsâif leaves wilt in the afternoon, you're behind. Keep a simple notebook: date, pan reading, crop stage, water applied. Simple Tools for Scheduling: Tensiometers and Apps

Deficit Irrigation Strategies for Water Scarcity

When water is scarce, you can't keep soil at full moisture all the time. Deficit irrigation means deliberately applying less water than the crop's full requirement, accepting some yield reduction but saving water. The key is to target drought-tolerant stages. For many crops, flowering and grain filling are criticalâdon't stress then. But vegetative stages can tolerate some stress. For example, maize can tolerate 50% water deficit during vegetative growth (before tasseling) with little yield loss. Tomatoes can tolerate deficit during early vegetative stage, but not during flowering. A common strategy: 'regulated deficit irrigation' (RDI). For fruit trees like mango, apply 50-70% of full ET during fruit set, then full water during fruit sizing. For vegetables, use 'partial root-zone drying' (PRD): water only one side of the plant's root zone, alternating sides every 10-14 days. This saves 30-50% water while maintaining yield. In Africa, PRD works well for tomatoes, peppers, and maize. Another method: 'skip irrigation'âif you normally irrigate every 3 days, skip one day when soil is still moist. Use a tensiometer: allow readings to reach 60-70 centibars before irrigating, instead of 30-40. But watch for wilting. If leaves roll or droop in early morning, you've gone too far. Mulching helps: a 5 cm layer of dry grass or crop residues reduces evaporation by 30-50%, so deficit irrigation is less risky. Also, water at dawn or dusk to reduce evaporation. For a 1-hectare plot, deficit irrigation might save 1000-2000 cubic metres per season. But don't deficit irrigate young seedlings or during floweringâthat can kill the crop. Start with a small trial: divide your field into two, apply full water on one half, deficit on the other, and compare yields. Many African farmers have found that 70% of full water gives 90% of yieldâa good trade when water is short..Trim()

Monitoring and Adjusting for Weather Variability

Weather in Africa is unpredictable. A week of hot, dry winds can dry soil fast; a sudden downpour can waterlog. You must monitor and adjust. Start with a simple rain gaugeâa straight-sided tin can (like a baked bean can) placed in an open area. Measure rainfall daily at the same time. If you get 10 mm of rain, that's 10 litres per square metre. Subtract that from your irrigation requirement. For example, if your crop needs 20 mm and it rained 10 mm, irrigate only 10 mm. Also, watch temperature and wind. On hot days (above 35Â°C), ET increases by 20-30%. On windy days, more water evaporates. Use a simple thermometer and a windsock (a piece of cloth on a stick) to gauge. If it's very hot and windy, irrigate more frequentlyâmaybe every 2 days instead of 3. If cloudy and cool, stretch to 4-5 days. Keep a daily log: date, rainfall, max temp, wind, irrigation applied. After a season, you'll see patterns. For example, in the Sahel, the rainy season may bring 5 mm one day, then none for a week. In East Africa, short rains can be intense. Adjust by checking soil moisture: dig 10 cm, squeeze. If it's dry, irrigate even if you irrigated yesterday. If it's wet, wait. Also, watch for signs of waterlogging: yellowing leaves, wilting despite wet soil. So for 30 cm root zone, 45 mm available. Irrigate when 22 mm remains. This method works with just a rain gauge and a thermometer. Adjust weekly based on forecastâif rain is predicted, skip irrigation. If a heatwave is coming, pre-irrigate. Being flexible is key..Trim()

Routine Maintenance Checklist for Irrigation Systems

A good maintenance routine saves you money and water. Start with a daily walk-through. Every morning before you irrigate, check the pump, mainline, and valves. Look for leaks, listen for unusual noises, and check the pressure gauge. If pressure is below 1 bar (15 psi) for drip or 2 bar (30 psi) for sprinklers, stop and find the problem. Weekly, clean the filter. A dirty filter is the number one cause of poor irrigation. Also, check emitters and sprinklers for clogs. In sandy areas, you may need to clean filters every two days. Monthly, flush the mainline and sub-mainlines. Open the flush valves at the end of each line and let water run for 2-3 minutes. This removes sediment and roots. Inspect all pipes and fittings for cracks or loose connections. Tighten clamps and replace worn gaskets. Check the pump oil level if it has a dipstick, and lubricate moving parts. Every three months, test the water quality. High salt or iron can clog emitters. If you see white crust, your water is hard. You may need to acid-treat the system. Annually, before the rainy season, do a full system audit. Measure flow at the pump and at the farthest emitter. If flow drops by more than 10%, there is a blockage or leak. Replace any damaged parts. Keep a logbook. Write down dates of maintenance, problems found, and actions taken. This helps you spot patterns. For example, if filters clog every week, your water source has too much debris. You may need a settling pond or a sand filter. A simple checklist on a wooden board near the pump house works well. Include: pump oil, filter clean, leaks, pressure, emitters, flush lines, and valve operation. Tick each item. This takes 15 minutes but prevents big breakdowns. Remember, preventive maintenance is cheaper than repairs. A new pump can cost $200, but a bearing replacement is $20. So walk your system daily..Trim()

Cleaning Filters and Emitters

Filters and emitters are the heart of your drip system. If they clog, your crops suffer. Start with the filter. Most smallholder systems use a screen filter or a sand filter. For screen filters, turn off the pump. Open the drain valve at the bottom and let the water out. Unscrew the filter cap. Pull out the screen. Use a soft brush and clean water to remove debris. Never use a wire brush; it tears the mesh. If the screen is torn, replace it. A 120-mesh screen is common for drip. For sand filters, backwash them. Open the backwash valve and run water backwards for 3-5 minutes until the water runs clear. Do this weekly. If your water is very dirty, backwash every two days. Emitters clog from sediment, algae, and chemical deposits. First, check if the clog is at the emitter or the lateral line. If only one emitter is blocked, pull it off and clean it with a thin wire or a toothbrush. Soak it in vinegar overnight to dissolve calcium. If many emitters are clogged, flush the whole line. Open the end cap and let water run for 2 minutes. If that fails, use a chlorine treatment. Mix 1 liter of household bleach (5% chlorine) in 1000 liters of water. Run it through the system for 30 minutes, then flush with clean water. For iron clogs, use a commercial acid cleaner, but follow safety instructions. Always wear gloves and goggles. In Africa, many farmers use gravity-fed systems. Pressure is low, so emitters clog faster. Clean filters daily. Also, install a simple settling tank before the filter. Let water sit for a few hours so sand and silt settle. Then draw from the top. This reduces filter cleaning. For sprinklers, clean nozzles with a small brush. Check for worn nozzles; replace them if the spray pattern is uneven. Keep spare emitters and filters on hand. A pack of 50 emitters costs about $5. Train one person on your farm to do this weekly. Repairing Leaks in Pipes and Hoses

Leaks waste water and money. A small leak can lose 20 liters per day. Find leaks by walking the line when the system is running. Look for wet spots, spraying water, or hissing sounds. In buried pipes, look for unusually green patches. Once you find a leak, turn off the pump and drain the line. For PVC pipes, cut out the damaged section with a hacksaw. Make clean, square cuts. Dry the ends. Apply PVC primer, then PVC cement. Slide a coupling over both ends and twist a quarter turn. Hold for 30 seconds. Let it cure for 1 hour before pressurizing. For poly pipe, use a barbed coupling and stainless steel clamps. Heat the pipe end with a lighter or hot water to make it flexible, then push the coupling in. Tighten clamps with a screwdriver. For hoses, if the leak is at a fitting, cut off the damaged end and reinsert the fitting. Use a hose clamp. If the hose is split, use a hose repair kit with a barbed connector. For a temporary fix, wrap self-fusing silicone tape around the leak. Stretch it tight and overlap. It bonds to itself in minutes. For large holes, use a rubber patch and a clamp. In Africa, many farmers use old inner tubes as patches. Cut a piece, place it over the leak, and secure with wire or a clamp. For buried pipes, dig around the leak carefully. Don't use a pickaxe; you may crack the pipe. Use a hand trowel. After repair, backfill with sand to protect the pipe. Then test. Run water and check for drips. If the leak persists, redo the repair. Always keep spare parts: couplings, clamps, tape, and a small saw. A repair kit costs about $15 and saves many trips to town. Also, check your pump connections. Vibration can loosen fittings. Tighten them monthly. If you have a metal pipe, rust can cause pinhole leaks. Replace rusted sections. For drip tape, leaks are common at the emitters. You can plug a leaking emitter with a goof plug. These cost $0.10 each. Inspect your system after every storm or heavy use. Quick action prevents small leaks from becoming big breaks..Trim()

Winterizing and Seasonal Startup

In many parts of Africa, you don't have freezing winters, but you do have dry seasons and rainy seasons. Winterizing means preparing your system for a period of non-use. If you have frost, water left in pipes can freeze and burst them. Even without frost, leaving water in pipes for months can cause algae and sediment buildup. For small systems, you can disconnect the pipes and tip them. Remove filters and clean them. Store them dry. Remove the pump and store it indoors. If you can't remove it, drain it and add a little oil to the impeller. Cover the pump with a tarp. For drip tape, roll it up and store it in a cool, dark place. Reconnect everything. Clean the filter and install a new screen if needed. Check the pump oil and fuel. Prime the pump. Open all valves slowly to avoid water hammer. Diagnosing Pressure and Flow Problems

Pressure and flow problems are common. If your sprinklers don't reach, or drip emitters barely drip, you have a problem. Start with the pressure gauge. Install one at the pump and one at the end of the mainline. Normal pressure for drip is 1-1.5 bar (15-22 psi). For sprinklers, 2-3 bar (30-45 psi). If pressure is low at the pump, check the pump. Is it primed? Is the impeller worn? Is the suction line clogged? If pressure is high at the pump but low at the end, you have a blockage or a leak. Check filters first. A clogged filter drops pressure. Clean it. Then check for closed valves. A partially closed valve restricts flow. Open all valves fully. Next, check for leaks. A large leak will drop pressure. Walk the line. If pressure is low everywhere, your pump may be too small, or your pipe diameter is too small. For example, a 1-inch pipe can only carry about 30 liters per minute. If you need 50, you need a 1.5-inch pipe. If flow is low but pressure is normal, you have a clogged emitter or a broken lateral. Check each emitter. If only the first few emitters work, the line is clogged. Flush it. If pressure is high, you may have a closed valve or a clogged nozzle. High pressure can burst pipes. Install a pressure regulator. For gravity systems, pressure depends on tank height. Every 1 meter of height gives 0.1 bar. So a 10-meter tank gives 1 bar. If pressure is low, raise the tank. Also, check for air locks. Air in the line causes sputtering and low flow. Open the highest point and let air out. If you have a centrifugal pump, check the foot valve. A stuck foot valve causes no flow. Clean or replace it. Use a flow meter to measure actual flow. Compare to the design flow. If flow is 20% below design, find the cause. Keep a log of pressure and flow. This helps you spot trends. For example, if pressure drops slowly over a week, your filter is clogging. Clean it. If it drops suddenly, you have a leak or a broken pump..Trim()

Preventive Maintenance for Pumps and Valves

Pumps and valves are the most expensive parts of your system. Take care of them. For pumps, check the oil level daily if it has a dipstick. Use SAE 30 oil for most small pumps. Change the oil every 100 hours of operation. Clean the air filter every 50 hours. If you use a petrol pump, clean the spark plug every 100 hours. Replace it yearly. Check the impeller for wear. A worn impeller reduces flow. If you see cracks or heavy pitting, replace it. For solar pumps, clean the panels weekly. Dust reduces power by up to 30%. Check the wiring for corrosion. Tighten all connections. For valves, operate them monthly. A valve that sits closed for months can seize. Turn it open and closed a few times. Lubricate the stem with silicone grease. Check for leaks around the packing. If it leaks, tighten the packing nut a quarter turn. If that doesn't stop it, replace the packing. For gate valves, check the gate for debris. For ball valves, check the handle for smooth operation. In Africa, water can be gritty. Grit wears out valves quickly. Install a filter before the valves. Also, check the pressure relief valve. It should open at the set pressure. Test it by adjusting the pressure. If it doesn't open, replace it. For pump starters, check the contacts for burning. Clean them with fine sandpaper. If you have a foot valve, clean it monthly. A clogged foot valve causes the pump to lose prime. Keep spare parts: a spare impeller, a set of gaskets, a foot valve, and a pressure switch. A spare pump is ideal if you can afford it. But at least have a repair kit. Train one person to do daily checks. They should look for leaks, listen for noises, and check pressure.
