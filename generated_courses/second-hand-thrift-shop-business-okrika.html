What is Okrika? The Second-Hand Clothing Trade in Africa

Okrika is the popular name for second-hand clothing in Nigeria, derived from the port city of Warri, where early imports arrived. In other parts of Africa, it's known as 'mitumba' in East Africa, 'salaula' in Zambia, and 'kafa' in Ghana. The trade involves importing used clothes from Europe, North America, and Asia, then selling them in local markets or boutiques. Africa imports over $1 billion worth of used clothing annually, with Nigeria alone importing about 100,000 tonnes per year. Washing and Sanitizing Second-Hand Clothes Safely

When you buy bales of okrika, the first step is to wash and sanitize every item before selling. This removes dirt, germs, and odors. Start by sorting clothes by color: whites, lights, and darks. Never mix a red shirt with white ones, or you will end up with pink whites. For a standard load, use a large plastic basin or a washing machine if you have one. Fill it with cold water. Add one cup of powdered detergent and half a cup of disinfectant like Dettol or a local bleach (e.g., Jik) for whites only. For colored clothes, use a color-safe disinfectant like Savlon. Let the clothes soak for 30 minutes. Then scrub gently with a soft brush, focusing on collars, cuffs, and underarms. Rinse twice in clean water until no soap remains. For stubborn odors, add half a cup of white vinegar to the final rinse. Wring gently and hang to dry in direct sunlight for at least 4 hours. Sunlight kills bacteria and bleaches stains naturally. If you are in a humid area, use a dryer on medium heat. Never use hot water on delicate fabrics like silk or wool; it causes shrinkage. For heavily soiled items, pre-treat with a paste of detergent and water before washing. Always wear gloves to protect your hands. After washing, inspect each item for remaining stains or damages. This step is crucial because customers will not buy dirty or smelly clothes. A clean, fresh-smelling item sells faster and at a higher price. Remember, your reputation depends on the quality you deliver..Trim()

Removing Stains, Odors, and Common Damages

Even after washing, some stains and odors may remain. Common stains on okrika include oil, ink, rust, and mildew. For oil stains, apply a small amount of dishwashing liquid directly to the stain, rub gently with your fingers, and let it sit for 10 minutes before washing. For ink stains, dab with rubbing alcohol or nail polish remover on a cotton ball, but test on a hidden area first. For rust stains, squeeze lemon juice on the spot and sprinkle salt, then leave in sunlight for 30 minutes before rinsing. Mildew appears as black spots and smells musty. Soak the item in a mixture of one part white vinegar to four parts water for one hour, then wash normally. For odors that persist, soak in a solution of baking soda (half a cup per gallon of water) overnight. For damages like small holes or tears, you can patch them. Use an iron-on patch or a piece of similar fabric with fabric glue. For missing buttons, replace them with matching ones from your sewing kit. Zippers that stick can be rubbed with a graphite pencil or candle wax. If a zipper is broken, you can replace it or sew the opening shut and sell as a pullover. Always check pockets for tissues or money before washing. After treating, wash again to remove any chemical residue. Selling damaged items as-is will hurt your business. Fix what you can, and price accordingly. A little effort turns a rejected item into a bestseller..Trim()

Basic Repairs: Sewing Buttons, Fixing Zippers, and Patching

Basic repairs can save you money and increase the value of your okrika. You need a small sewing kit: needles, thread (black, white, and common colors), scissors, thimble, and a seam ripper. To sew a button, thread your needle with a double strand and knot the end. Position the button over the mark, and push the needle from the back of the fabric through one hole. Place a toothpick or matchstick on top of the button to create a shank (space for fabric thickness). Sew through the holes in a crisscross pattern four to six times. Remove the toothpick, bring the needle to the back, and wrap the thread around the stitches under the button a few times to secure. Knot and cut. For zippers, if the slider is stuck, rub it with soap or wax. If the zipper pull breaks, you can attach a paperclip or a small key ring as a temporary pull. If the zipper teeth are broken, you can replace the zipper by removing the old one with a seam ripper and sewing a new one. For patching holes, cut a patch from similar fabric, slightly larger than the hole. Pin it inside the garment, and sew around the edges with a small running stitch or use fabric glue for a no-sew option. For tears on seams, turn the item inside out and sew the seam closed with a backstitch. Always match thread color to the fabric. Practice on old rags first. These skills take minutes but can turn a $1 item into a $5 item. Offer free repairs on items you sell to build customer trust..Trim()

Ironing and Folding for a Neat Appearance

Ironing and folding make okrika look new and professional. Start with a clean iron and a ironing board or a table covered with a thick blanket. Set the iron temperature according to the fabric: low for silk, rayon, and synthetics; medium for wool; high for cotton and linen. Always test on a small hidden area first. For shirts, iron the collar first, then cuffs, then sleeves, then the body. For trousers, iron the waistband, then pockets, then legs, creating a sharp crease if desired. Use steam for stubborn wrinkles, but be careful with dark colors as steam can leave water spots. For delicate items, place a thin cotton cloth over the fabric before ironing. After ironing, fold immediately while the item is still warm to set the fold. For folding, use a clean flat surface. For shirts, button them up, fold sleeves back, and fold in half or thirds. For jeans, fold in half lengthwise, then fold in half again. For dresses, fold in half and then in thirds. You can also hang items on hangers to save time. Use clip hangers for skirts and trousers. For a neat display, group similar items together. Ironing and folding not only make items look better but also save space in your shop. Customers appreciate neatness and are willing to pay more. A well-ironed shirt can sell for double the price of a wrinkled one. Invest in a good iron and a spray bottle for water. Always unplug the iron when not in use..Trim()

Packaging Items for Sale or Delivery

Packaging protects your items and creates a good impression. For in-person sales, use simple clear plastic bags or paper bags with your shop name. Separating Business and Personal Money

Many okrika sellers mix business and personal money. This is the fastest way to kill your business. You buy stock with money meant for rent, or you spend sales money on personal needs, and soon you cannot restock. The fix is simple: separate your money. Open a separate mobile money wallet or bank account for the business. Use a different SIM card if possible. Every sale goes into that account. Every business expense comes out of it. Your personal money stays in your personal wallet. If you must take money for personal use, record it as 'owner's draw' and subtract it from profit. For example, if you sell 20 dresses at 50 birr each, that's 1,000 birr. Deposit all 1,000 birr into the business account. Then pay yourself a fixed salary, say 300 birr per week. This way, you always know how much the business has. At the end of each day, count your cash and compare with your sales record. Any difference must be explained. This discipline helps you qualify for loans and grow. Start today: get a separate wallet or account. Label it 'Okrika Business'. Never mix..Trim()

Tracking Daily Sales and Expenses

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Every day, write down your sales and expenses. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Wave or Keeper. At the end of each sale, record: date, item sold, quantity, price, total. For expenses, record: date, what you bought (stock, transport, rent, airtime), amount. For example: '15/06/2025 - Sold 3 shirts at 100 birr = 300 birr'. '15/06/2025 - Bought 10 shirts from supplier = 500 birr'. At the end of the day, total your sales and expenses. Subtract expenses from sales to get daily profit. Keep receipts for all expenses. If you use mobile money, the transaction history is your record. At the end of the week, review your notebook. You will see which items sell fast and which don't. You will also see if you are spending too much on transport or airtime. This information helps you make better decisions. For example, if you see that you spend 200 birr per day on transport, consider buying in bulk once a week. Tracking takes only 10 minutes a day but can double your profit..Trim()

Budgeting for Restocking and Overhead Costs

A budget is a plan for your money. Without it, you may spend all your profit on small things and have nothing to restock. Start by listing your monthly overhead costs: rent for your stall or storage, transport to buy stock, airtime and data, packaging bags, and any helper's salary. Add them up. For example: rent 1,000 birr, transport 500 birr, airtime 200 birr, bags 100 birr, helper 300 birr. Total overhead = 2,100 birr per month. Next, estimate your monthly sales. If you sell 300 items at average 80 birr, that's 24,000 birr. Subtract overhead: 24,000 - 2,100 = 21,900 birr. From this, you need to restock. Aim to spend at least 50% of your sales on new stock: 12,000 birr. That leaves 9,900 birr for profit and savings. But remember, sales vary. So set a restocking budget: decide how much you will spend each week, say 3,000 birr. Stick to it. If you see a great deal, you can adjust, but always keep enough for overhead. Write your budget on paper and keep it where you see it daily. Review it weekly and adjust based on actual sales..Trim()

Simple Profit Calculation and Reinvestment

Profit is what remains after you subtract all business expenses from your sales. Many sellers think all the money in their pocket is profit, but that's wrong. You must subtract the cost of goods sold (what you paid for the items), transport, rent, airtime, and any other business expense. For example, you buy 10 dresses for 500 birr total. You sell them for 1,000 birr. Your sales = 1,000 birr. Your cost of goods = 500 birr. Other expenses (transport, airtime) = 100 birr. Profit = 1,000 - 500 - 100 = 400 birr. That 400 birr is your true profit. Now, reinvestment: use part of your profit to buy more stock or improve your business. A good rule is to reinvest at least 50% of profit. So from 400 birr, reinvest 200 birr into new stock. The remaining 200 birr can be your personal income or savings. If you reinvest consistently, your business grows. For example, if you start with 10 dresses and reinvest 200 birr each week, after a month you might be buying 20 dresses. Track your profit weekly. If profit is low, find ways to reduce costs or increase prices slightly. Never eat your seed capital..Trim()

Using Mobile Money and Cash Handling Safely

Mobile money is safe and convenient, but you must use it wisely. In many African countries, services like M-Pesa, Airtel Money, MTN Mobile Money, and Tigo Pesa are common. Register for a business account if possible. Always confirm the transaction SMS before handing over goods. If you must carry money, split it between pockets or use a money belt. At the end of the day, deposit excess cash into your mobile money or bank account. Keep small change for customers. Count your cash before you leave the market. If you have helpers, set clear rules: only you handle money. If you accept mobile money, have a dedicated phone for business. Never share your PIN. If you suspect fraud, report immediately to your network provider..Trim()

Every business has slow seasons. For okrika, sales may drop during rainy seasons, holidays, or when customers have no money. You must plan ahead. During good months, save at least 10% of your profit in a separate savings account or mobile money savings. For example, if you make 5,000 birr profit in a good month, save 500 birr. After six good months, you have 3,000 birr for slow times. Also, build an emergency fund for unexpected costs: sickness, theft, or urgent restocking. Aim for at least one month's overhead costs. If your overhead is 2,000 birr per month, save 2,000 birr as emergency fund. Understanding Import Laws and Customs for Second-Hand Clothes

In many African countries, importing second-hand clothes (okrika) is regulated to protect local industries and ensure safety. For example, in Kenya, the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) and the Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA) require importers to have an Import Declaration Form (IDF) and pay duties. In Nigeria, the Nigeria Customs Service (NCS) enforces a ban on importing used clothes through land borders, but allows some through seaports with proper documentation. You must know your country's specific rules. Start by visiting your local customs office or their website to get a list of required documents. Typically, you need a business permit, tax identification number (TIN), and a customs agent to clear goods. The process involves: 1) Obtaining a Proforma Invoice from your supplier, 2) Applying for Form M (in Nigeria) or IDF (in Kenya), 3) Paying import duty (usually 20-35% of value) and VAT, 4) Inspection by customs and standards officials. For small-scale traders, consider joining a cooperative or using a consolidated container to share costs. Always insist on a Bill of Lading and Packing List. Avoid smuggling; it leads to confiscation and fines. In Tanzania, the Tanzania Bureau of Standards (TBS) checks for harmful chemicals. In Ghana, the Ghana Standards Authority (GSA) inspects for hygiene. Budget for these costs: a 20-foot container from Europe to West Africa might cost $2,000-$4,000 in shipping plus $1,500-$3,000 in duties. Keep all receipts. If you buy from local wholesalers who import, ask for customs clearance documents to ensure legality. This protects you from legal trouble and builds trust with customers who fear stolen goods..Trim()
