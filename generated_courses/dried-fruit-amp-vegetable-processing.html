Why Drying Food is a Smart Business in Africa

Drying food is one of the oldest and smartest businesses in Africa. Every year, farmers lose up to 40% of their harvest because they cannot eat or sell it fast enough. Tomatoes, mangoes, and leafy greens rot within days. But when you dry them, they last for months. That means you can buy cheap during harvest season and sell when prices are high. For example, a crate of tomatoes costs 5,000 Naira in Nigeria during peak season, but dried tomato flakes can sell for 15,000 Naira per kilogram year-round. The profit margin is huge because drying removes water, so 10 kg of fresh tomatoes become about 1 kg of dried product. You also solve a real problem: people in cities want healthy, natural snacks without preservatives. Dried mango, pineapple, and banana chips are in high demand in supermarkets, schools, and bus parks. Unlike fresh fruit, dried products don't need refrigeration, so you can transport them to distant markets without spoilage. Starting a drying business requires very little capital. You can begin with a solar dryer made from wood and polythene, or even sun-drying on clean mats. The main costs are raw materials, packaging, and your time. With consistent quality, you can supply local shops, hotels, and even export to Africans in the diaspora who miss home foods. The business is also climate-smart: you use the sun, a free resource, and reduce waste. Many successful African entrepreneurs started exactly this wayâdrying one product, then expanding. So if you want a low-risk, high-demand business that turns perishable crops into lasting wealth, drying is your answer..Trim()

Identifying Local Fruits and Vegetables for Drying

Not all fruits and vegetables dry well. You need produce with high sugar or low water content, and firm texture. In Africa, the best fruits for drying include mangoes, pineapples, bananas, papayas, guavas, and jackfruit. These have natural sweetness and hold their shape after drying. Avoid watery fruits like watermelon or orangesâthey turn to mush. For vegetables, good choices are tomatoes, onions, peppers, okra, leafy greens (like spinach, kale, and amaranth), carrots, and pumpkin. Leafy greens dry quickly and become nutritious powders for soups. Tomatoes and peppers dry into flakes or powder for stews. Okra dries into chips or powder for soups. When selecting produce, choose ripe but firm fruits. Overripe fruits are mushy and may ferment; underripe ones lack flavor. Buy from local farmers during harvest when prices are lowest. For example, in Kenya, mangoes are abundant in January and February, so you can buy a bucket for 100 KES. In West Africa, tomatoes are cheapest in the dry season. Always inspect for bruises, mold, or insect damageâthese spoil the batch. Wash everything thoroughly in clean water, then cut into uniform slices (3-5 mm thick) for even drying. Remove seeds from peppers and tomatoes if you want powder. For bananas and plantains, peel and slice. Some fruits like apples and pears brown quickly; dip them in lemon juice or salt water for 5 minutes before drying. Remember, the goal is to preserve local, seasonal abundance. You can also dry herbs like mint, basil, and moringa. Moringa leaves are highly nutritious and sell well as powder. Start with one or two products you can source easily and consistently, then add more as you learn..Trim()

Setting Up a Small-Scale Drying Workspace at Home

You don't need a factory to start drying food. A clean, well-ventilated space at home is enough. Choose a room or covered area away from dust, smoke, and animals. If you have a kitchen with a window, that works. For sun-drying, you need direct sunlight for at least 6-8 hours a day. A concrete slab, a raised wooden platform, or a table covered with clean mesh works well. Never dry food directly on the groundâdirt and insects will contaminate it. For solar drying, you can build a simple cabinet dryer using wood, a clear polythene sheet or glass, and a black-painted metal sheet as a heat absorber. Place it facing the sun. The black sheet absorbs heat, and air enters from bottom vents and exits from top vents, carrying moisture away. This protects food from rain, dust, and flies. Your workspace must have good air circulation. If the air is stagnant, food won't dry and may mold. Also, keep the area dry and free of standing water. For safety, store your tools and packaging materials on shelves, not on the floor. If you have pets, keep them out. A small table for cutting and weighing, a sink or basin for washing, and a drying rack are the main zones. You can start with a 1m x 1m drying rack that holds about 5 kg of fresh produce. As you grow, add more racks or build a larger solar dryer. Remember, cleanliness is critical: wash your hands, wear clean aprons, and sanitize surfaces before handling food. A simple setup like this can produce 20-30 kg of dried product per month, enough to test the market and earn extra income..Trim()

Basic Tools and Equipment You Need to Start

To start a dried fruit and vegetable business, you need only a few affordable tools. First, a sharp stainless steel knife and a cutting board. Stainless steel doesn't rust or react with food. A good knife costs about 2,000-5,000 Naira or 500-1,000 KES. Next, you need drying trays. These can be made from wooden frames with food-grade mesh (like mosquito netting or stainless steel mesh). A simple solar dryer can be built for 20,000-50,000 Naira. If you prefer electric drying, a small food dehydrator costs 50,000-150,000 Naira, but it uses electricity. Understanding Costs and Setting Your First Prices

To make a profit, you must know your costs. Let's break down a simple example: drying 10 kg of fresh mangoes. In many African markets, 10 kg of mangoes cost about 2,000 Naira (or 200 KES). After peeling and slicing, you get about 8 kg of usable fruit. Drying removes about 80-90% of water, so you end up with 1-1.5 kg of dried mango. If you sell dried mango at 5,000 Naira per kg, your revenue is 5,000-7,500 Naira. Your costs include: raw materials (2,000), packaging (500 for bags and labels), and maybe transport (500). Total costs = 3,000 Naira. Profit = 2,000-4,500 Naira. That's a 40-60% margin. But you must also factor in your time and equipment depreciation. If you dry 10 batches per month, you can earn 20,000-45,000 Naira profit. To set your price, check what competitors charge. Visit local markets, supermarkets, and online (Jumia, Kilimall). If dried mango sells for 6,000 Naira per kg, price yours at 5,500 to attract customers while still making profit. For vegetables like dried tomatoes, a kg of fresh tomatoes costs 500 Naira, yields about 100g dried, which sells for 2,000-3,000 Naira per kg. So 10 kg fresh (5,000 Naira) yields 1 kg dried (2,000-3,000 Naira)âthat's a loss if you only sell 1 kg. So you need to dry larger quantities or add value (e.g., tomato powder blends). Always calculate your yield ratio: for every 10 kg fresh, you get 1-1.5 kg dried. Price accordingly. Start with small batches, track every expense, and adjust. Offer samples to build trust. As your brand grows, you can charge premium prices for organic or well-packaged products..Trim()

Health and Safety Rules for Handling Food

Food safety is non-negotiable. If your dried products make someone sick, your business is finished. Always follow these rules. First, personal hygiene: wash your hands with soap and clean water before touching food, after using the toilet, and after handling waste. Wear clean clothes, an apron, and a hairnet. Keep fingernails short and clean. If you are sick, especially with diarrhea or vomiting, do not handle food. Second, clean and sanitize all surfaces, knives, and trays before and after use. Use hot water and food-safe sanitizer, or a mixture of vinegar and water. Third, wash all fruits and vegetables thoroughly in clean running water. For leafy greens, soak in salt water for 10 minutes to remove insects, then rinse. Fourth, dry food properly. Incomplete drying leads to mold, which produces dangerous toxins. To test dryness: fruits should be leathery with no moist spots; vegetables should be brittle. If you bend a dried tomato, it should snap, not bend. Fifth, store dried products in airtight containers in a cool, dark, dry place. Moisture is the enemy. Add a small oxygen absorber or silica gel packet to prevent mold. Sixth, protect from pests: use sealed containers, keep the workspace clean, and avoid leaving food exposed. Seventh, label your products with the production date and best-before date (usually 6-12 months). Finally, never mix spoiled produce with good ones. If you see mold, throw away the entire batchâdo not try to salvage it. Following these rules ensures your products are safe, and customers will trust your brand. You can also take a free online food safety course for more details..Trim()

Module 3 · Traditional Sun Drying Methods

Building a Simple Solar Dryer with Local Materials

A solar dryer speeds up drying and protects food from dust, rain, and animals. You can build one using cheap, local materials. Start with a wooden frame: four posts 1.5 m tall, and a rectangular box 1 m wide, 2 m long, and 0.5 m deep. Use planks or bamboo for the sides, leaving the front open for a door. The base should be solid to keep out rodents. For the drying chamber, use a black-painted metal sheet or dark stones on the bottom to absorb heat. Cover the top with a clear plastic sheet (UV-treated if possible) or glass. The plastic should slope slightly so rain runs off. At the back, cut a small vent (10 cm x 10 cm) near the top for moist air to escape. At the front bottom, leave an air inlet (10 cm x 20 cm) covered with mesh to keep insects out. Inside, place 3-4 removable trays made of wood frames with mesh bottoms (mosquito net or wire). Trays should sit 10 cm apart. The dryer works by trapping sun heat; temperatures reach 50-70Â°C, drying food in 1-2 days instead of 3-5 days. Place the dryer on a level surface in full sun, facing the equator (north in southern Africa, south in northern Africa). For best results, load trays with a single layer of food. Check the plastic for tears; repair with tape. This design costs less than $30 if you use scrap wood and local plastic. It can dry 10 kg of fresh produce per batch..Trim()

Using Raised Racks and Trays for Sun Drying

Raised racks and trays keep food off the ground, away from dirt, insects, and moisture. Build racks from local wood or bamboo. A simple rack is a table 1 m high, 1 m wide, and 2 m long. Legs should be sturdy; bury them 20 cm in the ground or set in stones for stability. The top surface can be a wooden frame with a mesh bottom (mosquito net or chicken wire). Mesh size should be small enough to hold small pieces like sliced tomatoes. Trays are shallow boxes (5 cm deep) that fit on the rack. Make trays from wood with mesh bottoms. You can also use clean woven mats, but mesh allows better air flow. Spread food in a single layer, not overlapping. For fruits like mangoes, slice 5-8 mm thick. For vegetables like tomatoes, cut into halves or 1 cm slices. Leave 2 cm gaps between pieces for air circulation. Turn food every 2-3 hours with clean hands or tongs. At night or during rain, stack trays and cover with a plastic sheet or bring them inside. In hot, dry areas, drying takes 1-2 days for thin slices; in humid areas, 3-4 days. Raised racks also keep food away from dogs, chickens, and rodents. Clean racks and trays after each use with soap and water, then sun-dry. This simple setup costs almost nothing if you use local materials and greatly improves food safety..Trim()

Protecting Food from Dust, Insects, and Rain

Sun drying fails if dust, insects, or rain spoil the food. Dust carries germs and dirt. Insects lay eggs, causing maggots. Rain rehydrates food, leading to mold. To protect food, always use raised racks (at least 1 m off the ground). Cover the drying area with a fine mesh screen or mosquito net. Build a simple frame over the rack and drape netting over it, securing the edges. This keeps flies, bees, and beetles out. For extra protection, use a double layer of netting at the sides. If you see insects, shake the net gently to dislodge them. For rain, have a plastic sheet ready. At the first sign of clouds, cover the racks. Better yet, use a solar dryer with a plastic roof. If drying in the open, bring trays inside at night or when rain threatens. In humid areas, dry food in the morning sun and bring it in by 4 pm. Never dry food directly on the ground or on roadsides where dust and animal waste are present. Also, keep animals away with a fence or by drying on a high platform. Wash hands before handling food. Use clean trays. If food gets wet, dry it immediately in full sun or use a solar dryer. If mold appears, throw the batch away. These simple steps prevent most losses and keep your dried food safe to eat..Trim()

Monitoring Sun Drying Time for Different Foods

Drying time depends on the food, slice thickness, weather, and method. In hot, dry weather (30-35Â°C, low humidity), thin slices dry in 1-2 days. In cooler or humid weather, it may take 3-5 days. Here are typical times for common African foods: Mango slices (5 mm): 1-2 days. Banana slices (5 mm): 1-2 days. Tomato halves: 2-3 days. Onion rings (3 mm): 1-2 days. Okra slices (5 mm): 1-2 days. Leafy greens (collards, kale): 1 day if blanched first. Pepper strips: 2-3 days. Meat (beef jerky, 5 mm strips): 2-3 days in very hot sun. To monitor, check food every 2-3 hours. Turn pieces. If the surface feels dry but inside is moist, it needs more time. Weigh a small sample before drying and after; when it loses 80-90% of its weight, it's likely dry. But weight alone is not enough; use the touch and cut test (next lesson). Start drying early in the morning (8-9 am) so food gets maximum sun. Bring food in before evening dew. If drying takes more than 3 days, consider using a solar dryer or a dehydrator. Record your start time and weather conditions to learn for next time..Trim()

Testing for Dryness Without Special Equipment

You don't need a moisture meter to know when food is dry. Use your senses: touch, sight, and taste. For most fruits and vegetables, properly dried pieces are leathery, pliable, and not sticky. They should not squirt juice when squeezed. Cut a piece open: no moisture should appear inside. For leafy greens, they should crumble easily. For tomatoes, they should be dry but still flexible, like raisins. For onions, they should be crisp and snap when bent. For peppers, they should be brittle. For meat, it should be hard and fibrous, not soft. Another test: take a few pieces, put them in a sealed jar for 24 hours. If condensation forms on the inside of the jar, they are not dry enough. Return to the dryer. Also, weigh a sample: if it stops losing weight after two consecutive weighings (2 hours apart), it's dry. A simple rule: fruits should retain about 15-20% of original weight; vegetables about 5-10%. But always combine tests. If in doubt, dry longer. Under-dried food grows mold within days. Over-dried food loses flavor and vitamins but is safe. Store only fully dry food in clean, dry jars with tight lids. Keep in a cool, dark place. Check after one week for any moisture or mold; if found, re-dry..Trim()

Common Mistakes in Sun Drying and How to Avoid Them

Many people lose their dried food due to avoidable mistakes. Here are the top ones and how to fix them. 1) Drying on the ground or on dirty surfaces: food picks up dirt, animal waste, and insects. Always use raised racks with mesh. 2) Cutting pieces too thick: thick slices dry slowly and unevenly, leading to mold. Cut fruits and vegetables 3-8 mm thick. 3) Not turning food: food sticks to trays and dries unevenly. Turn every 2-3 hours. 4) Leaving food out at night: dew and cool air rehydrate food. Bring trays inside or cover with plastic. 5) Ignoring weather: sudden rain can ruin a batch. Watch clouds and have a cover ready. 6) Using dirty trays or hands: germs cause spoilage. Wash hands and trays before and after. 7) Overloading trays: crowding slows drying. Spread in a single layer with gaps. 8) Not testing for dryness: food that looks dry may be moist inside. Use the jar test and cut test. 9) Storing before cooling: warm food creates condensation in jars. Let food cool completely before packing. 10) Using moldy or bruised produce: start with fresh, healthy food. Remove spoiled parts. 11) Drying in smoke or dusty wind: off-flavors and dirt. Dry in a clean, sunny spot. 12) Not protecting from insects: use mesh covers. By avoiding these mistakes, you will produce high-quality dried food that lasts months..Trim()
