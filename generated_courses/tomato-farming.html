Tomato Varieties: Determinate vs Indeterminate

When you buy tomato seeds, you will see two main types: determinate and indeterminate. Determinate tomatoes, also called bush tomatoes, grow to a fixed height, usually 60 to 90 cm. They stop growing when they set fruit, and all the tomatoes ripen within 2 to 3 weeks. This is perfect for farmers who want a single big harvest for market or processing. Common determinate varieties in Africa include Roma VF, UC82B, and Petomech. They need less staking and are easier to manage. If you are growing for tomato paste factories or selling in bulk, choose determinate..Trim()

.Trim()

Indeterminate tomatoes, also called vine tomatoes, keep growing and producing fruit until frost or disease kills them. They can reach 2 metres or more, so you must stake them with tall sticks or trellis them with strings. They produce fruit over many months, giving you a steady income. Popular indeterminate varieties in Africa include Money Maker, Tanya, and Anna F1. These are good for fresh market sales because the fruits are often larger and tastier. However, they need more labour for pruning and staking. If you have a small plot near a city market, indeterminate tomatoes can give you weekly harvests for 6 to 8 months..Trim()

.Trim()

How do you choose? First, check your market. If you sell to a processing plant, determinate is better because they ripen all at once. If you sell to hotels, restaurants, or individual consumers, indeterminate gives you a longer selling window. Second, check your climate. In hot, dry areas like northern Nigeria, determinate varieties like Roma VF tolerate heat better. In cooler highlands like Kenya's Rift Valley, indeterminate varieties like Anna F1 thrive. Third, consider your budget. Determinate tomatoes need less staking and fewer sprays, so they cost less to grow. Indeterminate tomatoes need more inputs but can earn more per plant. Always buy certified seeds from a reputable agro-dealer. Fake seeds are a major cause of crop failure in Africa..Trim()

Understanding Tomato Growth Stages

Tomato plants go through five clear stages from seed to harvest. Knowing each stage helps you give the right care at the right time. Stage 1 is germination, which takes 5 to 10 days after sowing. The seed absorbs water, cracks open, and a tiny root emerges. You must keep the seedbed moist but not flooded. Use a nursery tray or a raised bed with fine soil. In Africa, many farmers sow seeds in plastic trays filled with a mix of loam soil and compost. Keep the nursery shaded for the first 3 days, then expose it to morning sun..Trim()

.Trim()

Stage 2 is seedling growth, from day 10 to day 30. The plant develops its first true leaves and a strong root system. This is when you thin out weak seedlings so each plant has space. Water gently every morning. If leaves turn yellow, add a little nitrogen fertiliser, like 1 teaspoon of urea in 5 litres of water. Stage 3 is vegetative growth, from day 30 to day 60. The plant grows tall and bushy. You must stake indeterminate varieties now. Apply NPK 17-17-17 fertiliser at 50 kg per hectare, or about 2 handfuls per plant. Remove side shoots (suckers) that grow between the main stem and branches, because they steal nutrients..Trim()

.Trim()

Stage 4 is flowering, from day 60 to day 80. Small yellow flowers appear, and bees or wind pollinate them. Do not spray insecticides during the day when bees are active. Instead, spray in the evening. Too much nitrogen now causes flowers to drop. Switch to a fertiliser with more potassium, like NPK 15-15-30. Stage 5 is fruiting and ripening, from day 80 to day 120. Green fruits swell, then turn red, yellow, or pink depending on variety. Water regularly to prevent cracking. Harvest when fruits are firm and fully coloured. For determinate varieties, harvest all at once. For indeterminate, pick every 3 to 4 days. A single plant can produce 2 to 5 kg of fruit. With good care, you can harvest 20 to 40 tonnes per hectare..Trim()

Climate and Soil Requirements for Tomatoes

Tomatoes grow best in warm, sunny conditions. The ideal temperature is 20Â°C to 27Â°C during the day and 15Â°C to 20Â°C at night. When temperatures go above 35Â°C, flowers drop and fruits get sunburn. When they drop below 10Â°C, growth stops and fruits do not ripen. In Africa, you can grow tomatoes in many regions, but you must choose the right season. In West Africa, plant at the start of the rains (April to June) or in the dry season with irrigation (October to February). In East Africa, the long rains (March to May) and short rains (October to December) are good. In southern Africa, plant from August to November. Avoid planting in the hottest, driest months unless you have irrigation..Trim()

Tomatoes need at least 6 to 8 hours of direct sunlight every day. Shade reduces yield and increases disease. Choose a field that is open and not shaded by trees or buildings. Also, tomatoes are sensitive to waterlogging. They need well-drained soil. If water sits on your field for more than a few hours after rain, your plants will get root rot and die. You can fix this by making raised beds 20 to 30 cm high. The best soil for tomatoes is loam or sandy loam. It holds water but drains well. Heavy clay soil can be improved by adding 2 to 3 wheelbarrows of compost or manure per 10 square metres. Sandy soil needs more compost to hold water and nutrients..Trim()

Soil pH is critical. Tomatoes grow best at pH 6.0 to 6.8. If your soil is too acidic (pH below 5.5), add agricultural lime at 2 to 4 tonnes per hectare. If it is too alkaline (pH above 7.5), add gypsum or compost. You can test your soil at a local agricultural office for a small fee. Before planting, plough or dig the soil to 30 cm deep. Remove all weeds and stones. Mix in well-rotted manure or compost at 20 to 30 tonnes per hectare. This improves soil structure and feeds your plants slowly. Finally, ensure you have a reliable water source. Tomatoes need 2 to 3 litres of water per plant per day during fruiting. Drip irrigation is best because it saves water and keeps leaves dry, reducing fungal diseases..Trim()

Overview of Tomato Farming Systems

There are four main ways to grow tomatoes in Africa: open-field, greenhouse, hydroponic, and mixed cropping. Each has advantages and challenges. Open-field farming is the most common. You plant tomatoes directly in the soil in rows. It is cheap and simple, but you cannot control the weather, pests, or diseases. Yields are lower, often 10 to 20 tonnes per hectare. You need at least 0.5 hectares to make a good profit. Open-field is best for farmers with limited capital who can monitor their crops daily..Trim()

.Trim()

Greenhouse farming uses a structure covered with polythene or net to protect plants. It allows you to grow tomatoes year-round, even in harsh weather. Greenhouses reduce pests and diseases, and you can harvest 40 to 60 tonnes per hectare. But they cost money: a small 8m by 15m greenhouse costs $1,500 to $3,000. You also need drip irrigation and ventilation. Greenhouses work well in Kenya, South Africa, and Nigeria for high-value markets. Hydroponic farming grows tomatoes in water mixed with nutrients, without soil. It uses 80% less water and no soil, so you can grow in cities or on poor land. But it requires technical knowledge, electricity, and expensive nutrients. It is not for beginners..Trim()

.Trim()

Mixed cropping means growing tomatoes with other crops like maize, beans, or onions. This saves space and reduces pest damage because some crops repel insects. For example, planting onions between tomato rows reduces thrips. But mixed cropping makes it harder to spray and harvest. It also increases competition for water and nutrients. For a beginner, start with open-field farming on a small plot, like 0.2 hectares. Prepare raised beds, use certified seeds, and follow a spraying schedule. Once you master that, you can invest in a greenhouse or drip irrigation. Many African farmers also use a system called 'staking and pruning' for indeterminate varieties. You drive wooden stakes 1.5 metres tall next to each plant and tie the stem with soft string. This keeps fruits off the ground, reducing rot and making harvesting easier..Trim()

Economic Potential and Market Opportunities

Tomato farming can be very profitable, but you must understand the numbers. On 1 hectare of good land, you can harvest 20 to 40 tonnes of tomatoes. In a typical African market, a 50 kg crate sells for $20 to $50, depending on the season. If you harvest 25 tonnes, that is 500 crates. At $30 per crate, your gross income is $15,000. Your costs for seeds, fertiliser, pesticides, labour, and irrigation might be $5,000 to $7,000. So your net profit could be $8,000 to $10,000 per season. You can grow two seasons per year in many regions. That is a serious income. But prices crash when everyone harvests at the same time. In Nigeria, tomato prices often fall to $5 per crate in March when harvests peak. You can avoid this by planting early or using irrigation to harvest off-season when prices are high..Trim()

.Trim()

There are many market opportunities. First, fresh market: sell to local markets, supermarkets, hotels, and restaurants. Build relationships with chefs who need consistent supply. Second, processing: tomato paste, ketchup, and juice factories buy tonnes of tomatoes. In Nigeria, the Dangote Tomato Processing Plant in Kano needs 1,200 tonnes per day. Third, export: African tomatoes are exported to Europe, but you must meet strict quality and pesticide residue standards. Fourth, value addition: you can make tomato paste, sun-dried tomatoes, or tomato powder at home. A small solar dryer costs $200 and can dry 50 kg of tomatoes in 2 days. Dried tomatoes sell for $5 to $10 per kg in cities..Trim()

.Trim()

To succeed, you need a market plan. Before you plant, find out who will buy your tomatoes. Talk to traders at the local market. Visit hotels and restaurants. Join a farmer cooperative that can aggregate produce and negotiate better prices. Many African banks and microfinance institutions now give loans to tomato farmers if you show a solid plan. Also, consider contract farming: a company gives you seeds and inputs, and you sell your harvest to them at a fixed price. This reduces risk. Finally, keep records. Write down every cost and every sale. After one season, you will know exactly how to improve. Tomato farming is not easy, but with the right knowledge, it can lift you and your family out of poverty..Trim()

Module 2 · Soil Preparation and Fertility Management

Testing Soil pH and Nutrient Levels

Before you plant tomatoes, you need to know your soil's pH and nutrient levels. Tomatoes grow best in soil with a pH between 6.0 and 6.8. If pH is too low (acidic) or too high (alkaline), nutrients get locked up and plants can't absorb them. You can test pH with a simple DIY kit from an agro-vet shop or use a digital pH meter. For a cheap test, mix soil with distilled water (1:1) and dip a pH strip. For accurate results, send a sample to a local agricultural extension office. They often charge a small fee but give detailed nutrient reports..Trim()

.Trim()

To test nutrients, look for signs: yellowing leaves mean nitrogen deficiency; purple leaves suggest phosphorus deficiency; brown leaf edges indicate potassium deficiency. But visual signs appear late. Better to use a soil test kit that measures nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). In many African countries, you can buy NPK test kits from companies like Soil Test or local agro-dealers. Follow instructions carefully: take soil from 10-15 spots in your field, mix them in a bucket, and take a cupful for testing. Test before each planting season because nutrients change with crops and rains..Trim()

.Trim()

If pH is below 6.0, add agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) at 2-4 kg per 10 square meters, mix into top 15 cm, and water. Wait 2-4 weeks before planting. If pH is above 7.0, add elemental sulfur or organic matter like compost or manure. For nutrients, if nitrogen is low, add manure or urea; if phosphorus is low, add rock phosphate or bone meal; if potassium is low, add wood ash or potassium sulfate. Always test first to avoid wasting money on unnecessary fertilizers. Remember, healthy soil is the foundation of a good tomato harvest..Trim()

Amending Soil with Organic Matter

Organic matter is the life of your soil. It improves water holding, drainage, and feeds beneficial microbes. For tomatoes, aim for soil with 3-5% organic matter. Common sources in Africa include cattle manure, goat manure, poultry litter, compost, and crop residues. Never use fresh manureâit burns roots and carries diseases. Compost it first: pile manure with dry leaves, grass, and kitchen scraps, keep it moist, and turn every two weeks. In 6-8 weeks, it becomes dark, crumbly compost..Trim()

Apply compost or well-rotted manure at 2-4 kg per square meter (about 2-4 large buckets per 10 square meters). Spread it evenly over the bed and mix into the top 15-20 cm of soil with a hoe or fork. If you have limited manure, concentrate it in planting holes: mix one part compost with two parts soil. For liquid fertilizer, soak manure in water for 3 days (1 part manure to 5 parts water), strain, and use to water plants every two weeks..Trim()

In dry areas, organic matter helps soil hold water. In heavy clay, it improves drainage. You can also grow green manures like cowpeas or lablab before tomatoes, then plow them in. In sandy soils, add compost plus clay to improve structure. Avoid using sawdust unless composted, as it ties up nitrogen. Also, don't over-apply manureâtoo much causes leafy plants with few fruits. Mulching with dry grass after planting also adds organic matter as it breaks down and conserves moisture..Trim()

Your choice of planting method depends on your land, water, and soil. Raised beds are best for poor drainage, heavy clay, or small gardens. Build beds 20-30 cm high and 1 meter wide, with paths 30-50 cm between. This improves drainage, warms soil earlier, and makes weeding easier. In flood-prone areas, raised beds save your crop. Use a fork to loosen soil, then shape beds with a hoe. Add compost and mix well. Raised beds dry out faster, so mulch heavily and water regularly..Trim()

.Trim()

Containers work for urban growers or those with very poor soil. Use buckets, sacks, or old tiresâat least 20 liters per plant. Drill holes for drainage. Containers are portable and allow you to control soil exactly, but they need more frequent fertilizing because nutrients leach out..Trim()

.Trim()

Field planting is cheapest for large areas with good soil. Plow or dig to 30 cm deep, remove rocks and roots, and level. Plant in rows 60-90 cm apart, with 45-60 cm between plants. Field planting exposes plants to more pests and diseases, so rotate crops and use mulch. If your field has hardpan, break it with a fork or chisel plow. In dry regions, sunken beds (zai pits) capture water. Choose based on your resources: raised beds for wet areas, containers for small spaces, fields for scale. Tilling loosens soil so roots can grow. But don't over-tillâit destroys soil structure and organic matter. For tomatoes, till to a depth of 20-30 cm. Use a hoe, fork, or tractor plow. Bed formation should be done 1-2 weeks before planting. This allows soil to settle and any added amendments to integrate. If you have a tractor, use a bedder. Applying Base Fertilizers and Compost

Base fertilizers are applied before planting to give tomatoes a strong start. The main nutrients are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Compost is your best base. Apply 2-4 kg per square meter, mixing it into the soil. If you have manure, use well-rotted manure at 2-3 kg per square meter. .Trim()

Timing matters: apply base fertilizers 1-2 weeks before transplanting. Water the bed after application to dissolve nutrients. Soil Solarization for Disease Prevention

Soil solarization uses the sun's heat to kill soil-borne diseases, nematodes, and weed seeds. It works best in hot, sunny climates with at least 6 weeks of intense sun. For best results, solarize during the hottest part of the year, when temperatures are above 30Â°C. Leave the plastic on for 4-6 weeks. In cooler areas, it may take 8 weeks. After removing the plastic, avoid re-contaminating the soil: don't walk on it with dirty boots, and don't mix in fresh manure. Solarization also increases available nutrients like nitrogen and potassium. It's especially useful if you've had problems with bacterial wilt, fusarium wilt, or root-knot nematodes. These diseases can wipe out your tomatoes. Solarization is cheaper than chemicals and safe for organic farming..Trim()

Understanding N-P-K and Micronutrients

Tomatoes need 16 nutrients to grow well. The three main ones are Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), and Potassium (K). These are the numbers you see on fertilizer bags, like 20-10-20. Nitrogen makes leaves and stems grow strong and green. Phosphorus helps roots and flowers form. Potassium makes fruit grow big, red, and sweet. When you buy fertilizer, look for a blend made for tomatoes. In Africa, common fertilizers include DAP (18-46-0) for planting, and CAN (27% N) for top dressing. But tomatoes also need small amounts of micronutrients: calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, boron, and others. Without them, you get problems like blossom end rot (calcium) or yellow leaves (magnesium). A simple way to supply micronutrients is to add well-rotted manure or compost. You can also buy micronutrient mixes at agro-dealers. Test your soil first. In many African soils, nitrogen is low, phosphorus is often low, and potassium is medium. But don't guess. A soil test costs little and saves money. For a 10 by 10 meter plot, you might need 2 kg of DAP at planting and 1 kg of CAN when fruits start forming. Always follow label instructions. Over-fertilizing wastes money and harms plants. Remember: N-P-K is the foundation, but micronutrients are the fine-tuning..Trim()

Side-Dressing and Fertigation Techniques

Side-dressing means adding fertilizer to the soil beside growing plants. It gives tomatoes a boost when they need it most. The first side-dress is when the first flowers appear. Use 1 tablespoon of CAN (calcium ammonium nitrate) per plant, placed 10 cm from the stem. Cover with soil and water. The second side-dress is when fruits are swelling. Use 1 tablespoon of a balanced fertilizer like 17-17-17. Never put fertilizer directly on the stem; it burns. For larger farms, you can use a hoe to make a small furrow between rows and sprinkle fertilizer, then cover. Fertigation is mixing fertilizer into irrigation water. It saves labor and gives nutrients directly to roots. You can use a bucket and watering can for small plots. Mix 1 teaspoon of water-soluble fertilizer (like 20-20-20) per liter of water. Apply 1 liter per plant every week. For drip irrigation, use a venturi injector. Common in Kenya and South Africa, these cost about $50. Always flush the lines with plain water after fertigation to prevent clogging. If you use organic methods, you can fertigate with compost tea. Dilute 1 part tea to 5 parts water. Apply every 2 weeks. Both methods work best when soil is moist. Never fertigate dry soil; it can burn roots. Also, avoid side-dressing when heavy rain is expected, as nutrients wash away..Trim()

Organic Fertilizers: Compost Tea, Manure, and More

Organic fertilizers feed both plants and soil. They are cheap and locally available. Compost tea is made by soaking compost in water. Put 1 part compost in 5 parts water in a bucket. Stir daily for 3 days. Strain and spray on leaves or pour around roots. It adds nutrients and beneficial microbes. Manure is another option. Use only well-rotted manure, at least 6 months old. Fresh manure burns plants and carries weeds. Apply 2-3 kg per square meter before planting. Poultry manure is rich in nitrogen; cow manure is milder. You can also use kitchen scraps, banana peels, and eggshells. Bury them near plants. Wood ash is a good source of potassium and calcium. Sprinkle a handful around each plant, but not too close to the stem. Bone meal adds phosphorus. Blood meal adds nitrogen. In Africa, you can find these at agro-dealers or make your own. For liquid fertilizer, soak manure in water for a week (1 part manure to 10 parts water). Use the liquid to water plants. Organic fertilizers release nutrients slowly, so apply them early. Foliar Feeding for Quick Nutrient Uptake

Foliar feeding means spraying liquid fertilizer directly on leaves. Plants absorb nutrients through their leaves faster than through roots. Fertilizer burn happens when too much fertilizer touches roots or leaves. Symptoms include yellowing, wilting, and brown, scorched leaf edges. The plant may die. Tomatoes follow legumes like beans or groundnuts, which add nitrogen. Use organic matter to improve soil slowly. Remember, more fertilizer is not better. It is about balance. Keep a calendar of when you apply what. If you are unsure, apply half the recommended rate and see how plants respond..Trim()

Identifying Common Tomato Pests

To manage pests, you must first know your enemy. In Africa, the most destructive tomato pests are: African bollworm (Helicoverpa armigera), whiteflies (Bemisia tabaci), aphids, spider mites, thrips, and leafminers. African bollworm larvae are green or brown, up to 4 cm long, with light stripes. They bore holes into fruits and feed on leaves. Check for dark frass (droppings) near holes. Whiteflies are tiny (1-2 mm), white, and cluster under leaves. When you shake a plant, they fly up in a cloud. They transmit tomato yellow leaf curl virus. Aphids are small (2-3 mm), green, black, or red, and cluster on new shoots. They secrete honeydew, which attracts ants and sooty mold. Spider mites are red or brown, barely visible, and spin fine webs on the undersides of leaves. Thrips are slender, 1-2 mm, and cause silvery streaks on leaves and fruits. Leafminers are small flies whose larvae tunnel inside leaves, leaving winding white trails. Scout your field twice a week, early morning or late afternoon. Walk in a zigzag pattern, inspect 10 plants per row, and look at the top and bottom of leaves, stems, and fruits. Carry a hand lens (10x) and a notebook. Record which pests you see and their numbers. For example, if you find 5 or more whiteflies per leaf, you need to act. Early detection saves your crop..Trim()

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Strategies

IPM is a smart, cost-effective way to control pests by combining methods: cultural, physical, biological, and chemical (as a last resort). Start with cultural controls. Rotate crops: do not plant tomatoes where you grew tomatoes, peppers, or potatoes in the last 2-3 years. This breaks pest life cycles. Plant resistant varieties like 'Tanya' or 'Moneymaker' if available. Sanitation: remove and burn crop debris after harvest. Weeds like black nightshade host aphids and whitefliesâkeep fields clean. Spacing: plant tomatoes 60 cm apart within rows and 1 m between rows to improve airflow and reduce humidity, which discourages fungal diseases and some pests. Stake or trellis plants to keep fruits off the ground, away from soil pests. Physical controls: handpick bollworm and aphids; use a strong jet of water to knock off whiteflies and mites. Mulch with straw or plastic to reduce soil-borne pests. Biological controls: conserve natural enemies like ladybirds, lacewings, and parasitic wasps by avoiding broad-spectrum pesticides. Chemical control: only when pest numbers exceed thresholds. For example, treat African bollworm when you find 1-2 larvae per plant. Use selective pesticides like Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) or neem oil. Rotate chemicals to prevent resistance. Always follow label instructions and wear protective gear. IPM saves money, protects your health, and keeps pests from ruining your harvest..Trim()

Biological Controls: Beneficial Insects and Predators

Nature provides free pest control if you encourage it. Beneficial insects eat or parasitize tomato pests. Ladybirds (ladybugs) are your friends. Both adults and larvae eat aphids, whiteflies, and spider mites. One ladybird can eat 50 aphids a day. Lacewing larvae, called 'aphid lions,' are voracious predators of aphids, thrips, and mites. You can buy them from suppliers, but it's cheaper to attract them. Plant flowers like marigolds, sunflowers, and coriander around your tomato field. These provide nectar and pollen for adult beneficials. Parasitic wasps (e.g., Encarsia formosa) lay eggs inside whitefly nymphs, killing them. You can buy cards with parasitized pupae and hang them in the field. Predatory mites (Phytoseiulus persimilis) control spider mites. To keep these helpers, avoid broad-spectrum insecticides like cypermethrin, which kill both pests and beneficials. Use selective products like neem oil or soap sprays, which have less impact. Also, provide shelter: hedgerows and intercropping with maize or beans create habitat. If you must spray, do it in the evening when beneficials are less active. Monitor your field: if you see many ladybirds and few pests, you may not need to spray at all. Biological control is cheap, safe, and sustainable. It takes patience, but it works..Trim()

Using High Tunnels and Greenhouses

A high tunnel is a simple, unheated greenhouse made of metal hoops covered with clear plastic. It protects tomatoes from rain, wind, and cold, and can extend your growing season by 4 to 8 weeks in spring and fall.
