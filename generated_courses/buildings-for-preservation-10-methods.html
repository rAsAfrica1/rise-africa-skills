Sourcing and Preparing Local Clay and Mud for Repairs

To repair a mud building, you need the right clay. Not all soil is good. Test soil from different spots: dig 30 cm down, take a handful, add water, and roll it into a sausage. If it holds together without cracking, it has enough clay. If it crumbles, too sandy. Good clay feels sticky and smooth when wet. For fine cracks, use sieved soil. For filling large holes, unsieved is fine. Add water slowlyâabout 1 part water to 3 parts soil by volume. Test brick strength: drop from waist heightâif it shatters, add more clay or straw. Always wear gloves if soil is from unknown areas to avoid parasites. Mixing Mud with Additives: Cow Dung, Straw, and Lime for Durability

Plain mud cracks and erodes quickly. Additives make it last. Cow dung is the most common in Africa. Use fresh dung from cattle that eat grass, not commercial feed. Mix 1 part dung to 3 parts mud by volume. Break dung into slurry with water before adding. Dung contains fibers and bacteria that bind clay particles and reduce cracking. It also makes the surface water-resistant. Straw or grass: chop into 5-10 cm lengths. Add 1 part straw to 4 parts mud. Straw acts like rebar in concreteâit holds the mud together when it dries. Use dry straw, not green. For floors or walls exposed to rain, add lime. Agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) or hydrated lime (calcium hydroxide) works. Use 1 part lime to 10 parts mud. Lime hardens the mix and kills insects. But too much lime makes it brittle. Never use cement in traditional mud repairsâit's too hard and causes cracks at the edges. If too sticky, add more soil. This mix works for both new patches and surface coatings. In dry areas like the Sahel, increase dung to 1:2 ratio. In humid areas, add more lime. Techniques for Patching Cracks and Eroded Walls

Before patching, clean the area. Remove loose mud, dust, and old coatings with a wire brush or stick. Wet the surface with waterâdry mud sucks moisture from new mud, causing poor bond. For cracks: if crack is less than 5 mm wide, fill with a slurry of sieved mud and water (like paint). Use a brush or cloth to push it in. For cracks 5-20 mm, widen the crack slightly with a trowel to create a key, then fill with mud-straw mix. Pack it in layers of 1 cm, letting each layer dry for 2 hours before adding the next. For cracks wider than 20 mm, fill with small stones or broken bricks first, then mud. For eroded walls (where rain has washed away mud): remove all loose material until you hit solid mud. Cut the edges of the hole straight with a trowel so the patch has vertical sides. Wet the edges. Mix mud with more straw (1:3 ratio) for strength. Apply in layers no thicker than 5 cm. Score each layer with a stick to help the next layer stick. Let dry 24 hours between layers. For deep holes, build up in steps. After filling, smooth with a wet hand or trowel. Types of Thatching Materials: Grass, Reeds, Palm Fronds

Thatch is any dried plant material used to cover a roof. In Africa, the three most common thatching materials are grass, reeds, and palm fronds. Each has different properties, costs, and lifespans. Grass thatch is the most widespread. In East Africa, elephant grass (Pennisetum purpureum) or thatching grass (Hyparrhenia spp.) is cut, dried, and bundled. A good grass thatch roof lasts 5â10 years if maintained. It is cheap and easy to repair, but it catches fire easily and attracts insects. Reeds, such as Phragmites mauritianus, grow in wetlands. Reeds are more resistant to rot and fire than grass, but they are heavier, so the roof structure must be stronger. They are common in the Okavango Delta and along the Nile. Preventing Fire and Pest Infestation in Thatch

When to Use Cement vs. Traditional Materials

When repairing an old building in Africa, you must decide whether to use modern cement or traditional materials like mud, lime, or cow dung. Traditional materials are breathable and flexible, but they are weak against water and heavy loads. Cement is strong, water-resistant, and quick to set, but it can crack old walls if used incorrectly. Use cement when the damage is structural: cracked foundation, broken floor slab, or a wall carrying a heavy roof. For example, if a mud wall has a deep hole from water erosion, cement mortar (1 part cement to 4 parts sand) can fill it, but first remove all loose mud and dampen the area. Mixing Concrete for Small Repairs

Patching Concrete Floors and Walls

Sources of Damp: Rising Damp, Leaks, and Condensation

Damp is the enemy of any building. In Africa, we deal with three main types: rising damp, leaks, and condensation. Rising damp happens when ground water climbs up through porous walls by capillary action. You'll see a tide mark up to 1.5 meters high, white salt deposits, and peeling paint. It's common in older buildings without a damp-proof course (DPC). Leaks come from broken pipes, cracked roofs, or faulty gutters. They are often sudden and localized. Condensation occurs when warm, moist air meets a cold surface, like a cold wall or window. In humid areas like the coast, condensation can cause mould and mildew. To identify the source, check for patterns: rising damp is continuous along the base, leaks are patchy and may drip, condensation appears on cold surfaces and disappears when ventilated. Use a moisture meter (cost about $30) to measure. A simple test: tape a square of aluminium foil to the wall for 24 hours. If moisture appears on the outside, it's condensation; if on the inside, it's rising damp or a leak. Fixing the source is the first step before any waterproofing..Trim()

Installing Damp-Proof Courses

A damp-proof course (DPC) is a barrier that stops moisture from rising up through walls. In Africa, we use materials like bitumen felt, plastic sheets (polyethylene), or slate. For existing buildings, you can install a DPC by cutting a horizontal slot into the wall about 150mm above ground level, using a angle grinder or masonry saw. The slot should be about 12mm wide and deep enough to go through the wall. Then insert a DPC membrane, such as a 0.5mm thick polyethylene sheet, and seal it with mortar. For new construction, place the DPC between the foundation and the first course of bricks. Ensure it's continuous and overlaps at corners. Cost: about $2 per meter for polyethylene. Tools: angle grinder, mortar, trowel, DPC membrane. Safety: wear goggles and gloves. After installation, replaster the wall. This method can last 20-30 years if done correctly. If you can't cut a slot, you can inject a chemical DPC (silane/siloxane) into holes drilled every 150mm. This is more expensive but less invasive..Trim()

Using Waterproof Membranes and Coatings

Waterproof membranes and coatings are your second line of defence after fixing the source. For roofs, use bituminous membranes (torch-on) or EPDM rubber. For walls, cementitious coatings (like Thoroseal) are popular in Africa because they are affordable and easy to apply. First, clean the surface: remove loose paint, dirt, and mould with a wire brush and water. Let it dry. Mix the coating according to the instructions (usually 1 part water to 3 parts powder). Apply two coats with a brush or roller, waiting 4-6 hours between coats. Coverage: 1kg covers about 1 square meter per coat. For extra protection, embed a fibreglass mesh into the first coat. For below-ground walls, use a bituminous coating or a dimpled drainage membrane. For flat roofs, use a polyurethane coating. Cost: cementitious coating is about $5 per kg; bitumen is $3 per kg. Always apply when rain is not expected for 24 hours. Temperature should be between 10Â°C and 35Â°C. Ventilate the area. These coatings can last 5-10 years if maintained..Trim()

Improving Drainage Around Buildings

Water pooling around your foundation is a major cause of damp. Improve drainage by creating a slope away from the building. The ground should fall at least 150mm over the first 3 meters (a 5% slope). If you have a garden, dig a swale (a shallow ditch) 300mm deep and 600mm wide, lined with gravel, to channel water away. For downspouts, extend them at least 1.5 meters from the wall using plastic pipes. Install a French drain: dig a trench 300mm wide and 600mm deep around the perimeter, fill with 20mm gravel, and lay a perforated pipe at the bottom. Cover with geotextile fabric to prevent clogging. Slope the pipe 1% towards a soakaway or storm drain. In areas with heavy rain, consider a soakaway pit: a 1m x 1m x 1m hole filled with rubble, allowing water to seep into the ground. Cost: about $50 for a 10-meter French drain. Tools: shovel, wheelbarrow, level, gravel. Clean gutters regularly to prevent overflow. These simple steps can reduce damp significantly..Trim()

Ventilation Strategies to Reduce Moisture

Good ventilation removes moist air before it causes condensation. In Africa, many buildings rely on natural ventilation, but you can improve it. Install air bricks (also called vents) in walls, especially in kitchens, bathrooms, and laundry areas. A 215mm x 65mm air brick provides about 1000mmÂ² of free area. Identifying Termite and Pest Damage

Termites and other pests can silently destroy a building from the inside out. In Africa, the most common culprits are subterranean termites (especially the genera Macrotermes and Odontotermes), drywood termites, and carpenter ants. Subterranean termites build mud tubes on foundation walls, pillars, and wooden structures. These tubes are about the thickness of a pencil and are made of soil, saliva, and feces. Break a tube open: if you see soft, creamy-white worker termites, you have an active infestation. Drywood termites leave tiny holes (1-2 mm) in wood and push out six-sided fecal pellets that look like coarse sand. Carpenter ants don't eat wood but tunnel through it, leaving sawdust-like frass. Other pests include powderpost beetles, which reduce wood to powder, and rodents, which gnaw on wires and pipes..Trim()

.Trim()

To inspect, use a bright torch, a flat-head screwdriver, and a notebook. Tap wood with the screwdriver handle: a hollow sound means internal damage. Probe soft spots gently. Check dark, damp areas: under sinks, behind baseboards, in roof voids, and around foundations. Look for mud tubes, discarded wings (after swarming), and bubbling paint or wallpaper. In Africa, termite swarms often happen after the first heavy rains. If you see winged termites emerging from a crack in your wall, that's a swarm. Take photos and note locations..Trim()

.Trim()

Quantify the damage: measure the length of mud tubes, count exit holes per square meter, and note any sagging floors or doors that no longer close. A simple test: push an awl into wood; if it sinks more than 5 mm with little resistance, the wood is compromised. For a small fee, local extension officers or pest control companies can confirm species. Early detection saves thousands: treating a small infestation might cost 50-100 USD, while replacing a roof can cost 2,000 USD or more. Always wear gloves and a dust mask when inspecting, as termite dust and rodent droppings can carry disease..Trim()

Chemical and Natural Termite Barriers

A termite barrier is a continuous physical or chemical shield that stops termites from reaching your building. In Africa, you have two main options: chemical soil treatments and physical or natural barriers. For chemical barriers, the most common active ingredients are imidacloprid (e.g., Premise), fipronil (e.g., Termidor), and bifenthrin. These are mixed with water and applied to the soil around the foundation at a rate of 1-2 liters per square meter, depending on the product label. The goal is to create a treated zone 10-15 cm wide and at least 30 cm deep around all foundation walls, pillars, and plumbing penetrations. You need a watering can or a low-pressure sprayer, a measuring cup, and personal protective equipment (gloves, goggles, mask). Always follow the label: overdosing wastes money and harms the environment; underdosing fails..Trim()

.Trim()

Natural barriers are cheaper and safer for families. Sand barriers: termites cannot tunnel through uniform sand particles larger than 1.5 mm. Create a 10 cm layer of washed river sand (not fine sand) around the foundation. Diatomaceous earth (DE) is a powder made from fossilized algae; it scratches the termite's exoskeleton, causing dehydration. Apply a 5 cm band of DE around foundations and in wall voids. Keep it dryâwet DE is ineffective. Neem oil or neem cake: mix 200 g of neem cake per 10 liters of water and drench the soil. Neem is a natural repellent. For existing buildings, you can also use a physical barrier like stainless steel mesh (Termi-Mesh) over foundation cracks and vents, but this is expensive. .Trim()

Treating Infested Wood and Soil

Once termites or pests have infested wood or soil, you must treat both to stop the colony. For wood, you have three main approaches: injection, surface spraying, and fumigation. Conducting a Building Condition Survey

A building condition survey is a systematic inspection to record what is wrong and what is at risk. Start by gathering basic tools: a notebook, pencil, tape measure (5m), torch, camera or phone, and a simple checklist. Walk the building from top to bottom.
