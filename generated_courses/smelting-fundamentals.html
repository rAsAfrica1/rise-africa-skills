Basic Principles of Metal Extraction

Smelting is the process of extracting metal from its ore using heat and a chemical reaction. Ore is rock that contains metal compounds, usually metal oxides or sulfides. To get pure metal, you must remove the oxygen or sulfur. This is done by heating the ore with a reducing agent, often carbon from charcoal or coke. The carbon grabs the oxygen, leaving behind the metal..Trim()

.Trim()

For example, iron ore (hematite, Fe2O3) is mixed with charcoal and heated. The carbon in charcoal reacts with oxygen in the ore to form carbon dioxide, and the iron is left as a liquid. The overall reaction is: Fe2O3 + 3C â 2Fe + 3CO. This happens at about 1,200â1,400Â°C. You also need a flux, like limestone, to remove impurities. Flux combines with sand and dirt to form slag, which floats on top of the molten metal and can be tapped off..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many small-scale smelters use charcoal because it is cheap and locally available. But charcoal burns at lower temperatures than coke, so furnace design must be efficient. A typical clay furnace is about 1 meter tall, with a tuyere (pipe) for air blast from bellows. You need to control the air supply: too little air, and the fire is too cold; too much, and you burn extra charcoal and may oxidize the metal back..Trim()

.Trim()

The key principles are: (1) ore must be crushed to increase surface area, (2) the right ratio of ore to charcoal to flux (often 1:2:0.5 by volume), (3) sufficient air blast, and (4) a furnace that holds heat. You can test if smelting is working by watching the slag: it should flow like thick honey. If it is too thick, add more flux; if too runny, add more ore. Practice with small batches before scaling up..Trim()

Overview of Smelting Furnaces

There are many types of smelting furnaces, but for small-scale operations in Africa, three are most common: the pit furnace, the clay shaft furnace, and the drum furnace. Each has pros and cons depending on your budget, materials, and desired output..Trim()

.Trim()

A pit furnace is simply a hole in the ground, lined with clay. It is cheap and easy to build, but it loses heat quickly and is hard to control. You can use it for low-temperature smelting of copper or lead. Dig a hole 50 cm deep and 30 cm wide, line it with termite mound clay, and add a tuyere near the bottom. A drum furnace uses a 200-liter steel drum lined with clay. It is portable and durable. Cut a hole near the bottom for a tuyere and a hole at the base for tapping. Key Terms and Definitions

Before you start smelting, you must know the language. Here are the essential terms you will hear and use..Trim()

.Trim()

Ore: The natural rock or sediment that contains valuable metal. Examples: hematite (iron), malachite (copper), galena (lead). Ore is never pure metal; it is a compound. Grade: The percentage of metal in the ore. High-grade ore has more metal and is cheaper to smelt. For copper, 2% grade is good; for iron, 30% is workable. Gangue: The worthless minerals mixed with ore, like sand, clay, and rock. You must remove it as slag..Trim()

.Trim()

Flux: A substance added to help remove gangue. Limestone (calcium carbonate) is common. It lowers the melting point of impurities and makes slag runny. Slag: The molten waste that forms on top of the metal. It is a mixture of flux, gangue, and ash. You tap it off and discard it. Reducing agent: A material that removes oxygen from the ore. Carbon (charcoal or coke) is the most common. It combines with oxygen to form carbon monoxide or dioxide..Trim()

.Trim()

Tuyere: A pipe or nozzle that blows air into the furnace. It can be made from clay, iron, or steel. Bellows: A device that pumps air. Traditional bellows are made from goat skin and wood; modern ones use a fan or blower. Tap hole: An opening at the bottom of the furnace to drain molten slag and metal. Crucible: A ceramic or clay pot that holds molten metal. It must withstand high temperatures..Trim()

.Trim()

Charge: The mixture of ore, flux, and reducing agent that you put into the furnace. The ratio is critical. Matte: A mixture of metal sulfides, often produced when smelting sulfide ores. It needs further processing. Assay: A test to determine the metal content of ore. You can do a simple assay by crushing ore, mixing with flux, and smelting a small sample. Smelting affects the environment, but you can reduce the damage and stay legal. In many African countries, small-scale smelting is regulated to protect air, water, and soil..Trim()

Air pollution: Smelting releases carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, and particulate matter. Sulfur dioxide causes acid rain. Module 2 · Ore Preparation and Handling

Ore Types and Characteristics

Before you smelt anything, you need to know what you're working with. In Africa, common smelting ores include iron oxides like hematite (Fe2O3) and magnetite (Fe3O4), copper sulfides like chalcopyrite (CuFeS2), and lead sulfides like galena (PbS). Each behaves differently in the furnace. Hematite is reddish and relatively easy to reduce, while magnetite is black, magnetic, and denser. Chalcopyrite is brassy yellow and needs roasting to remove sulfur before smelting. Galena is silvery-grey and heavy, but it releases toxic lead fumes, so you must handle it carefully..Trim()

.Trim()

You can identify ores with simple tests. Use a streak plate: hematite leaves a red-brown streak, magnetite leaves a black streak. A magnet picks up magnetite but not hematite. For sulfides, roast a small sample on a charcoal fire: chalcopyrite gives off sulfur dioxide (rotten egg smell) and turns into copper oxide. Galena melts easily and gives off white lead oxide fumes. Always work outdoors or with good ventilation..Trim()

.Trim()

Ore grade matters. A good iron ore contains 50-65% iron. Copper ore might be only 0.5-2% copper, so you need to concentrate it. In Africa, many small-scale operations mine laterite (iron-rich soil) or alluvial deposits. Know your ore's hardness: quartz-rich ores are abrasive and wear out crushers quickly. Clay-rich ores stick and clog equipment. Test hardness with a knife: if it scratches, it's soft; if it scratches the knife, it's hard..Trim()

.Trim()

Moisture content affects handling. Wet ore is heavy and can freeze in cold highlands or turn to mud in rainy seasons. Dry ore is dusty and can be lost to wind. Always store ore under cover. Finally, check for harmful elements like arsenic or phosphorus. High phosphorus in iron ore makes brittle metal. High arsenic in copper ore is dangerous. If you can't test in a lab, send a sample to a local university or geological survey..Trim()

Crushing and Grinding Techniques

Crushing reduces ore from large rocks to small pieces. Start with a jaw crusher: feed rocks no larger than 80% of the jaw opening. For a 100 mm x 150 mm jaw crusher, feed rocks up to 80 mm. The output is typically 10-20 mm. If you don't have a jaw crusher, use a sledgehammer on a steel plate. Wear safety glasses and gloves. Break ore into pieces about the size of a fist..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, grind the ore to powder. A ball mill is best: a rotating steel cylinder with steel balls inside. For a small operation, a 1 m diameter mill turning at 30-40 rpm with 50 kg of 50 mm steel balls will grind 100 kg of ore per hour to less than 1 mm. If you lack a ball mill, use a stamp mill (common in Zimbabwe and South Africa) or a mortar and pestle for tiny batches. For gold ore, you need finer grinding: 75 microns (0.075 mm) or less to liberate gold particles. A ball mill can do this in 2-4 hours..Trim()

.Trim()

Control the particle size. After crushing, screen the ore. Oversize goes back to the crusher. After grinding, check with a sieve: 200 mesh (0.074 mm) is a common target. If too coarse, grind longer. If too fine, you waste energy and create slimes that are hard to handle. Add water during grinding to reduce dust and prevent overheating. The mixture of ore and water is called pulp. Keep pulp density at 60-70% solids by weight..Trim()

.Trim()

Maintenance is critical. Check crusher jaws for wear every 50 hours. Replace worn plates. In ball mills, check ball charge: add balls to maintain 40-50% of mill volume. Lubricate bearings daily. In Africa, power can be unreliable, so consider a diesel-powered mill. Always lock out equipment before maintenance. Train workers to never put hands into a running crusher. Use a rod to clear blockages..Trim()

Screening and Classification

Screening separates ore by size using woven wire mesh or punched plates. A vibrating screen is most efficient: it shakes at 800-1200 rpm with a stroke of 3-5 mm. For small operations, a manual screen (a frame with mesh) works. Common mesh sizes: 10 mm for coarse, 5 mm for medium, 1 mm for fine. Always screen before crushing to remove fines that would otherwise clog the crusher. After crushing, screen again to send oversize back to the crusher..Trim()

.Trim()

Classification separates particles by settling velocity in water or air. A hydrocyclone is a cone-shaped device that uses centrifugal force. Feed pulp at 50-100 kPa pressure. Coarse particles go to the underflow (bottom), fine particles to the overflow (top). A 100 mm hydrocyclone can handle 5-10 m3/h. For gold recovery, a cyclone can cut at 75 microns. If you don't have a cyclone, use a settling tank: let pulp stand for 30 minutes; coarse sand settles first, then silt, then clay. Decant the muddy water..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many artisanal miners use a simple sluice box for classification and concentration. A sluice is a long trough with riffles. Feed pulp at a steady rate. Heavy particles (gold, cassiterite) settle behind riffles; light particles wash away. Angle the sluice at 5-15 degrees. Use a gentle water flow. Check riffles every 30 minutes and clean them out..Trim()

.Trim()

Efficiency matters. Overloading a screen reduces efficiency: never feed more than 80% of screen capacity. For a 1 m x 2 m screen, capacity is about 10-20 t/h per mm of aperture. Keep screens clean: brush or wash them. Replace torn mesh immediately. For classification, monitor cyclone pressure and underflow density. If underflow is too wet, adjust the apex valve. Always wear a dust mask when handling dry screened ore. Silica dust causes silicosis..Trim()

Concentration Methods: Gravity Separation

Gravity separation uses density differences to separate heavy minerals from light waste. It works best for ores with a density difference of at least 2.5 g/cm3. Gold (19.3 g/cm3) is easy to separate from quartz (2.65 g/cm3). Cassiterite (7.0 g/cm3) separates from sand. But galena (7.5 g/cm3) and sphalerite (4.0 g/cm3) are close, so gravity alone may not work..Trim()

.Trim()

The simplest tool is a gold pan. Fill the pan with crushed ore and water. Swirl gently. Light particles wash over the edge; gold settles at the bottom. Practice with known samples. A good panner can recover 90% of gold coarser than 100 mesh. For larger scale, use a sluice box. Build a sluice 3 m long, 30 cm wide, with riffles every 20 cm. Line the bottom with a blanket or miners' moss. Feed pulp at 20-50 L/min. Angle at 10 degrees. Clean the sluice every 2 hours..Trim()

.Trim()

A shaking table is more efficient. It has a tilted deck with riffles that shakes side-to-side. Feed pulp evenly. Heavy particles move along the riffles to one side; light particles wash off the other. A small table (1 m x 2 m) handles 0.5-2 t/h. Adjust stroke length (10-20 mm) and speed (250-300 rpm). In Africa, tables are used for gold, tin, and tantalum. They are expensive but pay off in recovery..Trim()

.Trim()

Jigs are another option. A jig pulses water through a bed of ore. Heavy particles settle; light particles rise. A 0.5 m2 jig handles 1-3 t/h. Use a screen with 2-5 mm openings. Control water flow: too fast and you lose fine gold; too slow and separation is poor. Always pre-screen ore to remove slimes (less than 0.5 mm). Slimes interfere with gravity separation. For best results, classify ore into narrow size ranges before gravity separation. Test with a small sample first..Trim()

Concentration Methods: Flotation

Flotation separates minerals by making some particles water-repellent (hydrophobic) so they attach to air bubbles and float. It works for sulfide ores like chalcopyrite, galena, and sphalerite. You need a flotation cell, reagents, and aeration. A simple cell can be a 200 L drum with an impeller and air inlet. For small-scale, use a 20 L bucket and a hand mixer..Trim()

.Trim()

Reagents are key. Collectors make minerals hydrophobic. For copper sulfide, use potassium amyl xanthate (PAX) at 20-50 g/t. For lead, use sodium isopropyl xanthate. Add a frother like pine oil (20-50 g/t) to stabilize bubbles. Add a depressant like sodium cyanide (50-100 g/t) to keep unwanted minerals from floating. Add a pH modifier: lime (CaO) to raise pH to 10-11 for copper, or soda ash to lower pH. Always add reagents in order: pH modifier, then collector, then frother. Mix for 5-10 minutes..Trim()

.Trim()

Operate the cell. Feed pulp at 25-35% solids. Aerate at 0.5-1.0 m3/min per m3 of cell volume. Agitate at 800-1200 rpm. Bubbles rise and carry sulfide minerals to the surface. Skim the froth into a launder. Collect for 5-15 minutes. The froth is concentrate; the remaining pulp is tailings. For a 20 L bucket, use 1-2 g of collector, 1 g of frother, and 5 g of lime. Run for 10 minutes. You should see a mineralized froth..Trim()

.Trim()

Safety first. Many flotation reagents are toxic. Wear gloves, goggles, and a respirator. Never eat or smoke near reagents. Store reagents in sealed containers away from children. In Africa, flotation is used at large mines like Kansanshi in Zambia and Tenke Fungurume in DRC. For artisanal miners, flotation is complex, but it can be done with training. Always test ore in a lab to determine reagent dosages. Too much reagent wastes money and can kill fish if tailings are released. Treat tailings in a pond before discharge..Trim()

Drying and Roasting of Ores

Drying removes moisture before smelting. Wet ore causes explosions in furnaces and wastes heat. Sun-dry ore on a concrete pad or black plastic sheet. Spread ore in a layer 5-10 cm thick. Rake it every 2 hours. In hot African sun, ore dries in 1-3 days. For faster drying, use a rotary dryer: a horizontal cylinder heated by a diesel burner. Basic Thermodynamic Concepts

Thermodynamics is about energy and how it moves. In smelting, we care about heat and chemical reactions. Think of a charcoal furnace: you burn charcoal to make heat. That heat raises the temperature of the ore and helps drive chemical changes. The First Law says energy cannot be created or destroyed; it only changes form. So the chemical energy in charcoal becomes heat, which heats the ore and gases. The Second Law says heat flows from hot to cold, and reactions have a natural direction. For example, at high temperatures, carbon in charcoal can pull oxygen away from iron oxide, leaving iron metal. That's called reduction. .Trim()

.Trim()

Key terms: enthalpy (heat content), entropy (disorder), and Gibbs free energy (whether a reaction happens). For a smelter, the most useful is Gibbs free energy (ÎG). If ÎG is negative, the reaction can occur. If positive, it won't happen without extra energy. Temperature matters: many reduction reactions become favorable at high temperatures. For instance, reducing iron oxide with carbon becomes spontaneous above about 700Â°C. .Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, smelters often use charcoal or coal. Knowing these basics helps you control the furnace. If the fire is too cold, reduction stops. If too hot, you waste fuel and may melt the furnace. You can measure temperature with a thermocouple or an optical pyrometer. A simple test: watch the color of the glow. Dark red is about 600Â°C, bright orange is about 1000Â°C. Use these cues to adjust air blast or fuel feed. .Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, thermodynamics tells you what is possible, not how fast. Kinetics (lesson 5) tells you speed. So use thermodynamics to choose conditions, then kinetics to make it efficient. In a small African smelter, you might aim for 1200Â°C for copper smelting, but only 1000Â°C for lead. Always start with a small test batch to see what works with your local ore and fuel..Trim()

Gibbs Free Energy and Ellingham Diagrams

Gibbs free energy (ÎG) tells you if a reaction will go on its own. Negative ÎG means yes; positive means no. For smelting, we use Ellingham diagrams to see how ÎG changes with temperature for different reactions. These diagrams plot ÎG versus temperature for oxidation reactions (metal + oxygen â metal oxide). The lower the line, the more stable the oxide. For example, at 1000Â°C, the line for aluminum oxide is very low, meaning aluminum loves oxygen. Iron oxide's line is higher, so iron gives up oxygen more easily. .Trim()

.Trim()

How to use an Ellingham diagram: Find your smelting temperature on the horizontal axis. Look at the lines for your metal oxide and for carbon (C + O2 â CO2) or carbon monoxide (2C + O2 â 2CO). If the carbon line is below the metal oxide line, carbon can reduce the oxide. For iron, at 1200Â°C, the carbon line is below iron oxide, so reduction works. For chromium, you need much higher temperatures because chromium oxide is very stable. .Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, you might smelt iron in a bloomery furnace at 1200-1300Â°C using charcoal. The Ellingham diagram shows that at these temperatures, carbon monoxide reduces iron oxide. But for titanium, you can't use carbon because titanium oxide is too stable. You need other methods. .Trim()

.Trim()

Practical tip: You can find Ellingham diagrams online or in textbooks. Focus on the temperature range of your furnace. If you don't have a diagram, remember that the more reactive metals (like aluminum, magnesium) have very stable oxides and are hard to reduce with carbon. Less reactive metals (like copper, lead) are easier. So copper smelting is simpler than iron smelting. .Trim()

.Trim()

Use this knowledge to select your reductant and temperature. If your local ore is copper oxide, you can reduce it with charcoal at 800-1000Â°C. If it's iron oxide, you need 1200Â°C or more. Always test with a small sample to confirm..Trim()

Heat Balance in Smelting

Heat balance means tracking where heat comes from and where it goes. In a smelter, heat comes from burning fuel (charcoal, coal, or gas) and from exothermic reactions (like oxidation of sulfur in sulfide ores). Heat leaves with the off-gas, with the slag and metal, and through the furnace walls. If heat in equals heat out, the furnace stays at the right temperature. If heat out is more, the furnace cools and reactions stop. .Trim()

.Trim()

To calculate heat balance, you need: the heat value of your fuel (charcoal ~30 MJ/kg), the mass of ore, flux, and fuel, and the specific heat of materials. For example, heating 1 kg of iron ore from 25Â°C to 1200Â°C requires about 1.2 MJ (assuming specific heat ~0.8 kJ/kgÂ°C). Melting slag also takes heat. Burning 1 kg of charcoal gives about 30 MJ, but only part goes to the ore; much is lost in hot gases. .Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many smelters use charcoal because it's locally available. But charcoal is often made inefficiently, so its energy content varies. You can improve heat balance by preheating the air blast, insulating the furnace with clay or refractory bricks, and keeping the ore dry. A simple method: measure the temperature of the off-gas. If it's very hot (>600Â°C), you're losing heat. Add a heat exchanger to preheat incoming air. .Trim()

.Trim()

Another factor: exothermic reactions. For sulfide copper ores, oxidation of sulfur releases heat, so you may need less fuel. For oxide ores, you need more fuel. Always do a trial run: weigh your fuel and ore, measure the temperature at the tuyeres (air blast area), and adjust. A well-balanced furnace runs steadily and produces good slag. If the slag is thick and pasty, it's too cold; add more fuel or air. If the metal is too hot and burning, reduce fuel..Trim()

Phase equilibria is about which phases (solid, liquid, gas) exist at a given temperature and composition. In smelting, you want a liquid slag that flows easily and separates from the metal. Slag is a mixture of oxides (SiO2, Al2O3, CaO, FeO, etc.) that forms from gangue minerals and flux. The right slag composition lowers the melting point and viscosity. .Trim()

.Trim()

For example, pure silica (SiO2) melts at 1713Â°C, too high for most furnaces. Adding lime (CaO) or iron oxide (FeO) lowers the melting point to 1100-1300Â°C. .Trim()

Use a ternary phase diagram (e.g., CaO-FeO-SiO2) to find the right composition. You don't need a computer; simple diagrams are available in textbooks. If you don't have a diagram, do a test: mix different proportions of your ore, sand, and lime, heat them in a crucible, and see which mixture flows best. .Trim()

.Trim()

In practice, you can adjust slag by adding limestone (CaCO3) or dolomite. But too much lime raises the melting point. The key is to keep the slag in the liquid region. Also, slag should have low viscosity so it drains easily. Viscosity increases if the slag is too siliceous or too cold. .Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, slag also carries away some metal as dissolved oxides. Too much slag means metal loss. So aim for a slag volume that is just enough to collect impurities. A typical slag-to-metal ratio is 2:1 to 4:1. Test with small batches to find the best recipe for your ore..Trim()

Reaction Kinetics in Smelting

Thermodynamics tells you if a reaction can happen; kinetics tells you how fast. In smelting, slow reactions mean you need more time or higher temperature. The main factors are temperature, surface area, and mixing. Higher temperature speeds up reactions because particles move faster. Smaller ore particles have more surface area, so they react faster. Good mixing brings reactants together. .Trim()

.Trim()

For example, reducing iron oxide with carbon monoxide gas is faster at 1200Â°C than at 900Â°C. But if your ore is in big chunks, the gas can't reach the inside. So crush and grind ore to 5-10 mm for good kinetics. In a bloomery furnace, you use charcoal and ore in layers; the gas flows through the gaps. If the ore is too fine, it blocks gas flow. So there's a balance. .Trim()

.Trim()

In African smelters, you often use manual crushing. Aim for a consistent size. You can also add a flux to lower the melting point of the ore, which helps reactions occur in the liquid state, where kinetics are faster. .Trim()

.Trim()

Another kinetic factor: residence time. The ore must stay in the hot zone long enough. In a continuous furnace, you control feed rate. If you feed too fast, reactions don't finish. If too slow, you waste fuel. Start with a feed rate that gives 30-60 minutes in the furnace. You can tell if reactions are incomplete by checking the slag for unreacted ore or high metal content. .Trim()

.Trim()

Also, catalysts can help. For example, small amounts of iron oxide can speed up the reduction of some oxides. But in general, keep it simple: optimize temperature, particle size, and mixing. Use a stopwatch and a thermometer to track your process. If reactions are slow, increase temperature by 50Â°C or reduce particle size. Always test changes one at a time..Trim()

Now you can use thermodynamics to solve real smelting problems. Start with a small test furnace. For example, you have copper oxide ore from Zambia. You want to smelt it in a clay furnace with charcoal. First, check the Ellingham diagram: copper oxide is easily reduced at 800-1000Â°C. So you don't need extreme heat. Electric Arc Furnace Fundamentals

Reverberatory Furnace Principles

A reverberatory furnace is a rectangular or cylindrical furnace where the charge is melted by heat reflected from the roof and walls. It is used for melting copper, tin, and aluminum, and for refining metals. In Africa, it is common in small-scale copper smelting in Zambia and the DRC. The furnace has a shallow hearth, a curved roof, and a chimney at one end. Fuel (coal, oil, or gas) is burned in a firebox at one end, and the hot gases pass over the charge and exit through the chimney. The roof is made of high-alumina bricks to withstand temperatures up to 1600Â°C. The hearth is lined with magnesite or chrome-magnesite bricks. The charge is introduced through doors on the side. Fuel consumption is high: about 1â2 kg of coal per kg of metal melted. To improve efficiency, preheat the combustion air using a recuperator. Flash Smelting Furnace Technology

Flash smelting is a pyrometallurgical process for smelting sulfide ores, especially copper and nickel. It was developed in Finland but is used in Africa at large mines like Kansanshi in Zambia and Tenke Fungurume in the DRC. In flash smelting, dry concentrate is injected with oxygen-enriched air or pure oxygen into a hot furnace. The particles ignite and burn instantly, generating their own heat. The furnace is a tall, vertical shaft with a reaction chamber at the top and a settler at the bottom. The reaction shaft is lined with chrome-magnesite bricks, and the settler is lined with magnesite-chrome bricks. Temperatures reach 1300â1400Â°C. The products are a matte (molten metal sulfide) and a slag. The matte is tapped and further processed, while the slag is discarded or reprocessed. Key design parameters: oxygen enrichment (typically 30â70% oxygen), concentrate feed rate (e.g., 50â100 tonnes per hour for large units), and furnace pressure (slightly negative to prevent gas leakage). For small-scale operations, flash smelting is not practical because it requires fine, dry concentrate and sophisticated gas handling. However, the principles are useful to understand. In Africa, flash smelting is used at the Kansanshi smelter in Zambia, which produces about 300,000 tonnes of copper per year. Safety: the main hazards are sulfur dioxide gas and molten material. Crucible and Cupola Furnaces

Crucible furnaces are simple: a clay-graphite or silicon carbide crucible is placed inside a furnace heated by coke, oil, or gas. The metal is melted in the crucible, not in contact with the fuel, so it is clean. They are ideal for melting small amounts (5â50 kg) of aluminum, brass, bronze, and precious metals. In Africa, crucible furnaces are common in artisanal gold and jewelry workshops in Ghana, Mali, and South Africa. A typical furnace is a steel drum lined with firebrick, with a hole for the crucible and a lid. Fuel is coke or LPG. Melting times: 30â60 minutes for aluminum, 1â2 hours for copper alloys. The coke burns, melting the iron, which trickles down and is tapped continuously. A typical cupola is 1â3 m tall and 0.5â1 m in diameter, melting 1â5 tonnes per hour. Refractory Materials and Furnace Linings

Refractory materials are heat-resistant materials used to line furnaces. They must withstand high temperatures, thermal shock, chemical attack from slag and gases, and mechanical wear. In Africa, common refractories include fireclay bricks (for temperatures up to 1200Â°C), high-alumina bricks (up to 1600Â°C), magnesite bricks (basic, for steel furnaces), and chrome-magnesite bricks (for copper and nickel furnaces). For small-scale furnaces, locally made clay bricks mixed with sawdust or rice husks can be used, but they have lower performance. The lining is installed in layers: an insulating layer (e.g., diatomaceous earth) against the steel shell, then a firebrick layer, then a working lining of high-alumina or magnesite bricks. The working lining is the sacrificial layer that contacts the melt. Mortar must match the brick type: fireclay mortar for firebricks, magnesite mortar for magnesite bricks. Expansion joints are needed to allow for thermal expansion. In Africa, refractory suppliers include Vesuvius in South Africa and local brick makers in Nigeria. Maintenance: inspect linings after every campaign. Cracks can be patched with refractory cement. A lining typically lasts 6â24 months depending on operation. For a small foundry, a 1-tonne cupola might need 200â300 firebricks. Safety: always wear a dust mask when cutting or grinding refractories; some contain crystalline silica. Raw Material Characterization

Before you charge any material into a furnace, you must know exactly what you are dealing with. Raw material characterization means testing your ores, fluxes, and fuels to understand their chemical and physical properties. Weighing and Blending of Charge

Fluxes and Their Role in Smelting

Burden Distribution in Blast Furnace

Burden distribution refers to how the solid charge (ore, coke, flux) is arranged inside the blast furnace. It is critical because it affects gas flow, heat transfer, and chemical reactions. In a blast furnace, the charge descends while hot gases rise. If the burden is not distributed properly, gas will take the path of least resistanceâusually along the wallsâleaving the center cold and inefficient. The ideal distribution has more coke in the center and more ore near the walls. Why? Coke is larger and more permeable, so it allows gas to flow up the center. Ore is finer and less permeable, so it should be near the walls where it can be heated by the descending charge and rising gas. To achieve this, you control the charging pattern. In a bell-less top, you can set the chute to rotate and tilt to different angles. Common patterns are: center coke charging (coke to center, ore to periphery), and mixed charging (alternating layers). For a double bell, you can adjust the stockline (the level of charge) and the size of the bells. A higher stockline gives a flatter profile; a lower one gives a deeper center. You also control the ore-to-coke ratio in each batch. In Africa, many small blast furnaces use manual charging, which makes uniform distribution hard. But you can still improve by charging in small piles around the circumference. Use a template to guide where you dump each skip. Monitor the furnace by checking the temperature of the top gas and the color of the slag. If the center is too cold, add more coke to the center. If the walls are overheating, add more ore near the walls. Burden distribution is a dynamic skillâadjust as conditions change. Feed Rate Control and Automation

Feed rate control means regulating how fast charge enters the furnace to match the smelting rate. If you feed too fast, the furnace floods, gas flow is blocked, and you get poor reduction. If you feed too slow, the furnace cools down and production drops. The correct feed rate depends on furnace size, blast volume, and charge composition. For a small blast furnace producing 5 tonnes per day, you might feed 200 kg of charge every 10 minutes. For a larger one (50 tonnes per day), feed 1,000 kg every 5 minutes. The key is to keep the stockline steadyâthe charge level should stay within 0.5 meters of the target. You can measure stockline with a mechanical probe or a radar sensor. Manual control uses a timer and a weigh hopper: the operator opens the hopper gate for a set time, then closes it. But this is inconsistent. Automation uses a programmable logic controller (PLC) to control the feed rate based on signals from the stockline sensor and gas analysis. A simple automated system includes: a weigh feeder on the conveyor, a variable frequency drive (VFD) on the motor, and a PLC that adjusts belt speed to maintain the desired feed rate. For African smelters, cost-effective automation can be built with an Arduino or Raspberry Pi, a load cell, and a motor driver. You can also automate the charging sequence: when the stockline drops, the PLC starts the skip hoist, weighs the charge, and dumps it. Automation reduces labor, improves safety, and gives consistent results. But you must maintain sensors and calibrate weigh scales weekly. Start with manual control, then upgrade step by step. Principles of Reduction

Reduction is the chemical removal of oxygen from a metal compound to get pure metal. In smelting, we heat metal oxides with a reducing agent so the metal can flow out as liquid. For example, iron oxide (Fe2O3) plus carbon monoxide (CO) gives iron (Fe) and carbon dioxide (CO2). The key is that the reducing agent grabs the oxygen, leaving the metal behind. In Africa, traditional iron smelting used charcoal made from local trees like acacia. The charcoal burns to form CO, which reduces the iron ore. You can see this in action: when you heat black iron oxide with charcoal in a clay furnace, after about 2 hours at 1200Â°C, you get a bloom of iron. The reaction is: Fe2O3 + 3CO â 2Fe + 3CO2. To make reduction work, you need three things: a metal oxide (ore), a reducing agent (carbon or hydrogen), and enough heat. The temperature must be high enough to melt the metal but not so high that the reducing agent burns away too fast. In a small-scale furnace, use a ratio of 1 part ore to 2 parts charcoal by weight. Crush the ore to bean size (about 1 cm) so the gas can reach all surfaces. Stir the charge every 20 minutes to expose fresh material. You can test if reduction is happening by looking for a silvery or metallic shine on the ore pieces. If you see no change after 1 hour, add more charcoal and increase air blast. Remember, reduction is the heart of smeltingâwithout it, you just have hot rock..Trim()

A reducing agent is any substance that gives up electrons or takes oxygen away from a metal oxide. In African smelting, carbon is king. Oxidation is the opposite of reductionâit adds oxygen to a metal or removes electrons. In smelting, oxidation is both a problem and a tool. Roasting and calcination are heat treatments that prepare ore for reduction. Roasting uses air to convert sulfides to oxides.
