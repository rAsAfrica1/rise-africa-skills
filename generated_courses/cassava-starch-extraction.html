Determining Optimal Harvest Time

.Trim()

In Africa, many farmers harvest at 12 months for maximum yield, but if you want early cash, you can harvest at 8â9 months. However, for starch extraction, waiting until 10â12 months gives you more starch per root. Also consider the dry season: harvesting in dry weather reduces root rot and makes peeling easier. .Trim()

Tools for checking: a machete or hoe to dig, a knife to cut the root, a clear glass or jar for the starch test, and a scale to weigh sample roots. Manual Peeling vs. Mechanical Peeling

Peeling removes the outer brown skin and the inner pinkish layer (cortex) of cassava roots. Manual peeling uses a sharp knife or machete. Hold the root firmly, cut off the ends, then slice lengthwise through the skin and peel it back. For 100 kg of roots, one person can peel about 20-30 kg per hour. Mechanical peelers are machines with abrasive drums or blades that rub off the skin. They can process 500-1000 kg per hour but cost $500-$3000. In Africa, manual peeling is common for small-scale operations because it is cheap and creates jobs. However, mechanical peeling is faster and reduces labour costs for large processors. When choosing, consider your daily root volume. If you process less than 500 kg per day, manual peeling is better. If more, a mechanical peeler pays off within months. Also, mechanical peelers may remove too much flesh, leading to starch loss. Manual peeling allows you to control depth. For starch extraction, you want to remove all skin and cortex because they contain bitter compounds and dirt. After peeling, roots should be processed within 24 hours to avoid spoilage. Always use clean, sharp knives to avoid bruising. Train peelers to remove only the skin and thin cortex, leaving the white flesh intact. In many African villages, groups of women peel together, sharing tips and increasing speed. You can also use a simple hand-cranked peeler made from a metal drum with rough interior. This costs less than $100 and speeds up peeling. Remember, the goal is clean, white roots ready for washing. Peeling is the first step to high-quality starch..Trim()

Washing and Sanitizing Roots

After peeling, cassava roots carry dirt, sand, and microbes. Washing removes these to prevent starch contamination. You need three basins or drums: first for rough washing, second for rinsing, third for sanitizing. Use clean water. For 100 kg of roots, use about 200 litres of water. Cassava roots can contain stones, sand, pieces of wood, and damaged parts. These must be removed before grating. Foreign materials damage graters and lower starch quality. Grating breaks cassava root cells to release starch granules. You can use a manual grater (a metal sheet with punched holes) or a motorized rasping machine. Grating equipment must be cleaned and maintained daily. After use, rinse the grater with clean water to remove cassava pulp. Leftover pulp hardens and can break teeth or clog holes. Wash with water and a little detergent. Rinse well. Dry with a cloth. Every week, check the teeth or blades for wear. Dull teeth produce coarse pulp and reduce starch yield. Sharpen or replace them. For a manual grater, you can sharpen the punched holes with a file. For motorized, replace the drum or teeth as recommended by the manufacturer. Lubricate bearings and moving parts with grease or oil every 50 hours of use. Check belts and tighten if loose. A loose belt slips and reduces grating speed. Also check the motor: clean air filters, change oil every 200 hours. In dusty African environments, clean the motor weekly. Store equipment in a dry place, covered with a tarpaulin. Rust is a major enemy. If you use a mild steel grater, coat it with cooking oil after drying. Stainless steel is better but more expensive. For manual graters, hang them on a wall. For motorized, keep in a shed. Train one person to be responsible for maintenance. Keep a logbook: date, hours used, maintenance done. Replace worn parts before they break. A broken grater stops production. In many projects, equipment fails because of neglect. Simple daily cleaning adds years to life. Also, ensure the power source is stable. If using a diesel engine, check fuel and oil daily. For electric, use a voltage stabilizer. With good care, a motorized grater can last 5-10 years. Extraction Methods: Traditional and Modern

Traditional cassava starch extraction has been done in Africa for generations. The basic steps are: peel, wash, grate, mix with water, sieve, settle, and dry. .Trim()

Water is the backbone of starch extraction. You need it to grate, to wash, to separate, and to purify. But not all water is good. .Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

pH and Temperature Effects on Extraction

pH and temperature are two hidden factors that can make or break your starch extraction. pH measures how acidic or alkaline your water is. It runs from 0 (very acidic) to 14 (very alkaline), with 7 being neutral. Cassava starch extracts best when the water is slightly acidic to neutral, around pH 5.5 to 7.0. If the water is too alkaline (pH above 8), the starch granules can swell and become harder to separate. If it is too acidic (pH below 5), the starch may break down and you lose yield. In most cases, clean rainwater or well water is fine. .Trim()

Always use cool or room-temperature water. In hot African climates, water from a tank in the sun can reach 40Â°C, which is still okay, but if it feels hot to the touch, let it cool. The ideal temperature is 25-35Â°C..Trim()

.Trim()

Yield Calculation and Efficiency

Yield is how much starch you get from a given amount of cassava roots. It is usually expressed as a percentage. For example, if you start with 100 kg of fresh roots and end up with 20 kg of dry starch, your yield is 20%. Knowing your yield helps you see where you are losing starch and how to improve..Trim()

.Trim()

To calculate yield, you need to weigh your roots and your final dry starch. Use a simple scale. Weigh the roots after peeling and washing. Then after you have dried the starch, weigh it again. If your actual yield is much lower, you are losing starch..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, during sieving: if your sieve is too coarse, starch passes through with the fibre, or if too fine, it clogs and you leave starch behind. Third, during settling: if you do not let it settle long enough, fine starch particles remain in the water you pour off. Fourth, during washing: each time you wash, some starch goes with the water. To reduce losses, grate finely, use a two-step sieving (coarse then fine), let settle for at least 4-6 hours, and wash the fibre at least twice..Trim()

.Trim()

Efficiency is about getting more starch with less effort and less waste. One way to improve efficiency is to use a settling tank with a conical bottom. The starch collects at the bottom, and you can drain the water from the top without disturbing the starch. Another way is to use a centrifuge or hydrocyclone if you can afford it. But even simple improvements help: use clean water, control pH and temperature, and work quickly. Also, keep records. Write down the weight of roots, the weight of starch, and any problems. After a few batches, you will see patterns. Maybe your yield drops when you use river water. Maybe it goes up when you soak roots longer. That is how you learn and improve..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, remember that yield is not everything. Quality matters too. A slightly lower yield of pure white starch may sell for more than a higher yield of dirty starch. So aim for both: good yield and good quality..Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Module 6 · Screening and Fiber Separation

Using Screens and Sieves for Separation

Screening is the step where you separate the starch milk from the fiber and other particles. After you grind or grate the cassava roots, you get a mixture of starch, water, fiber, and sand. You need to pass this mixture through a screen or sieve to catch the fiber and let the starch milk flow through. The screen has holes that are small enough to block fiber but big enough to let starch granules pass. A common size is 100 to 200 mesh (about 0.15 to 0.075 mm). In Africa, many small processors use a simple nylon cloth or a fine wire mesh stretched over a wooden frame. You can also buy a stainless steel sieve from a hardware store..Trim()

.Trim()

To do the screening, you pour the grated cassava mixed with water onto the screen. Use your hands or a paddle to move the pulp around so the starch can wash through. Keep adding water to help the starch pass. The fiber stays on top of the screen. You then squeeze or press the fiber to remove as much starch as possible. The liquid that passes through is called starch milk. It still contains fine fiber and sand, so you may need to screen it again with a finer screen. A second screening with a 200-mesh screen will remove more impurities..Trim()

.Trim()

For best results, screen in two stages: first with a coarse screen (like 60 mesh) to remove large fiber, then with a fine screen (like 200 mesh) to remove fine fiber and sand. You can build a simple screening table by mounting a screen at a slight angle so the starch milk flows off while the fiber stays on top. Always use clean water. If the water is dirty, it will contaminate your starch. After screening, you should have a smooth, milky liquid. If it feels gritty, there is still sand. If it looks fibrous, screen again. This step is critical because fiber left in the starch will make the final product poor quality and lower its price..Trim()

Types of Screens: Rotary, Vibrating, Static

There are three main types of screens used in cassava starch extraction: rotary, vibrating, and static. Each has advantages and disadvantages for small-scale African processors. A static screen is the simplest and cheapest. It is just a frame with a screen cloth set at an angle. You pour the cassava mash onto the top and let gravity pull the starch milk through while the fiber slides down. Static screens are easy to build with local materials like wood and nylon mesh. They work well for small batches, but they clog quickly and require frequent cleaning. You can improve them by adding a water spray bar to wash the fiber as it moves down..Trim()

.Trim()

A vibrating screen uses a motor with an off-center weight to shake the screen. This shaking helps the starch pass through faster and keeps the screen from clogging. Vibrating screens can handle more cassava per hour than static screens. In Africa, you can adapt a vibrating screen from a locally made sieving machine used for garri or maize. The motor can be a small electric motor (0.5 to 1 HP) or a diesel engine. The screen is usually mounted on springs or rubber mounts. The vibration separates the starch milk from the fiber efficiently. However, vibrating screens need more maintenance and power..Trim()

.Trim()

A rotary screen is a cylinder made of screen material that rotates slowly. The cassava mash is fed into one end, and as it tumbles, the starch milk passes through the screen while the fiber moves to the other end. Rotary screens are good for continuous processing and are less likely to clog because the tumbling action keeps the screen clear. They are more expensive and complex to build, but you can make a small one using a metal drum with screen panels. Rotary screens are common in medium-scale plants in Nigeria and Ghana. For a beginner, start with a static screen. As your production grows, consider a vibrating or rotary screen. Always match the screen type to your budget and production volume..Trim()

Washing and Dewatering Screens

After you extract the starch milk, you need to wash it to remove remaining impurities and then dewater it to remove excess water. Washing and dewatering screens are designed for these tasks. A washing screen is similar to a screening screen but with a water spray system. You pass the starch milk over the screen while spraying clean water. The water washes the starch through the screen, leaving behind fine fiber and sand. The diluted starch milk is then collected. Washing screens are often used after the initial screening to improve starch purity. You can build a simple washing screen by placing a fine screen (200 mesh) over a basin and using a watering can to spray water as you pour the starch milk..Trim()

.Trim()

Dewatering screens are used to remove water from the starch milk after washing. The starch milk is very dilute, often containing only 5-10% starch. Dewatering concentrates it. A dewatering screen is usually a fine screen that allows water to drain while retaining the starch. You can use a vacuum or just gravity. In small operations, you can pour the starch milk into a cloth bag and let it drain. The bag acts as a dewatering screen. For larger operations, a vibrating dewatering screen works well. The vibration helps water pass through quickly. After dewatering, the starch is a thick paste with about 40-50% moisture. This paste is then dried..Trim()

.Trim()

It is important to use clean water for washing. If you use dirty water, you will introduce new impurities. Also, the water you use for washing should be soft if possible. Hard water can affect starch quality. After washing, you can test the starch milk by letting it settle. If the water on top is clear, the starch is clean. If it is cloudy, there is still fine fiber. In that case, repeat the washing step. Dewatering screens should be cleaned regularly to prevent clogging. A clogged screen will slow down your production and may cause starch to be lost with the water. Always check the screen for holes or tears, as these will let fiber through..Trim()

Fiber Removal Techniques

Fiber removal is the key to getting pure starch. Cassava roots contain about 20-25% fiber on a dry basis. If you don't remove it, your starch will be brownish and low quality. The first step in fiber removal is proper grinding. If you grind the cassava too coarsely, the fiber will be in large pieces and easy to remove by screening. If you grind too finely, the fiber breaks into small pieces that pass through your screens. So, aim for a medium grind. A hammer mill with a screen size of 1-2 mm is good. After grinding, you mix the mash with water to form a slurry. The ratio is about 1 part cassava to 3-4 parts water. This helps the starch granules separate from the fiber..Trim()

.Trim()

The main fiber removal technique is screening, as covered earlier. But you can improve fiber removal by using a combination of techniques. One is the use of a centrifugal sieve or a paddle washer. A paddle washer is a tank with rotating paddles that wash the fiber while the starch passes through a screen. This is common in medium-scale plants. Another technique is to use a series of screens with decreasing mesh sizes. Start with a 40-mesh screen to remove coarse fiber, then 80-mesh, then 120-mesh, and finally 200-mesh. Each screen catches finer fiber. You can set up a simple screening cascade using buckets and screens..Trim()

.Trim()

After screening, you can also use a hydrocyclone to remove fine fiber. A hydrocyclone is a cone-shaped device that uses centrifugal force to separate particles by density. Starch is denser than fiber, so it settles to the bottom while fiber goes out the top. Hydrocyclones are expensive but very effective. For small-scale processors, you can use a settling tank. Let the starch milk sit for 4-6 hours. The starch will settle to the bottom, and you can pour off the water with floating fiber. Then add fresh water, stir, and let it settle again. Repeat 2-3 times. This is called the settling method. It is slow but requires no special equipment. Always check for fiber by rubbing a small amount of starch between your fingers. If it feels smooth, it's good. If it feels gritty or stringy, remove more fiber..Trim()

Recovering Residual Starch from Fiber

After you remove the fiber, it still contains a lot of starch. In fact, fresh cassava fiber can hold 20-30% of the total starch if you don't recover it. Recovering this residual starch increases your yield and income. The simplest way is to rewash the fiber. Take the fiber you collected from your screens and put it in a clean container. Add water at a ratio of 1 part fiber to 2 parts water. Stir vigorously for 5-10 minutes. This washes the starch off the fiber. Then pour the mixture through a fine screen (200 mesh) to separate the fiber from the starch milk. The starch milk that passes through can be added to your main batch. You can repeat this washing 2-3 times until the water coming out is clear. Each washing recovers more starch..Trim()

.Trim()

Another method is to use a screw press or a hydraulic press. You put the fiber in a cloth bag and press it. The pressure squeezes out the starch milk. The liquid is collected and screened. A simple manual press can be made with a car jack and a wooden frame. You can also use a mangle (wringer) like the ones used for laundry. For larger operations, a continuous screw press is used. The fiber is fed into the press, and as it moves through, the starch milk is squeezed out. The pressed fiber comes out drier and with less starch. The recovered starch milk is then screened and added to the main process..Trim()

.Trim()

A third method is to soak the fiber in water overnight. The starch dissolves or suspends in the water, and then you can separate it by screening. This is called steeping. After soaking, stir and screen. The fiber can be used as animal feed. The recovered starch milk should be processed the same way as your main starch milk. It is important to recover as much starch as possible because it directly increases your profit. For every 100 kg of fresh cassava roots, you can get about 20-25 kg of starch if you recover well. Without recovery, you might only get 15-18 kg. So, spending time on fiber recovery pays off. Always keep your recovery water clean and use a fine screen to avoid losing starch..Trim()

Maintenance and Cleaning of Screening Equipment

Your screening equipment will not work well if you don't maintain and clean it. After each use, you must clean all screens, sieves, and the frames. Starch dries and hardens like glue, blocking the holes. If you leave it, the next batch will be slow and inefficient. Start by rinsing with clean water. Use a soft brush to scrub the screen. Never use a wire brush on fine screens because it will damage the mesh. For nylon or cloth screens, wash with mild soap and water, then rinse well. For stainless steel screens, you can use a pressure washer on low setting. After washing, let the screens dry completely before storing. Store them flat or hanging, not folded, to prevent creases and tears..Trim()

.Trim()

Check your screens regularly for holes, tears, or worn spots. A small hole will let fiber through and contaminate your starch. You can patch small holes with a piece of the same screen material and a strong adhesive or by sewing with nylon thread. For larger tears, replace the screen. Keep a spare screen on hand. Also check the frames and supports for cracks or rust. Wooden frames can rot if left wet. Paint them with a waterproof paint or use treated wood. Metal frames should be painted or galvanized to prevent rust. Lubricate moving parts of vibrating screens, such as bearings and motors, according to the manufacturer's instructions. If you use a rotary screen, check the bearings and drive belt regularly..Trim()

.Trim()

Establish a cleaning schedule. After every production day, clean all equipment. Once a week, do a deeper clean: disassemble screens if possible, soak in a mild bleach solution (1 tablespoon per 5 liters of water) for 10 minutes to kill bacteria, then rinse thoroughly. This prevents mold and sour odors. Before starting a new batch, inspect the screens for cleanliness and damage. Keep a logbook to record when you cleaned and when you replaced screens. This helps you track maintenance. Also, train everyone who uses the equipment on proper cleaning. A clean screen means better starch quality and faster production. Remember, maintenance takes time, but it saves money by preventing downtime and starch loss. If a screen is clogged, you lose starch and time. If it's torn, you lose quality. So, take care of your screens, and they will take care of your business..Trim()

Principles of Starch Washing

Starch washing removes impurities like fibre, protein, and soluble sugars from the starch slurry. After grating and screening, the slurry still contains about 5-10% non-starch solids. Washing with clean water dilutes these impurities and separates them based on size and density. The key principle: starch granules are denser (1.5 g/cmÂ³) than water and most impurities, so they settle faster. You need a series of washing steps with fresh water to gradually purify the starch. After three washes, starch purity can reach 95%. Test purity by drying a sample and checking colour: pure starch is bright white, impure starch is grey or yellowish. Module 9 · Milling and Sieving of Dried Starch

Grinding Dried Starch Cakes

After drying, cassava starch forms hard cakes or lumps. You need to grind them into powder. The most common tool in African villages is the hammer mill, powered by a diesel engine. A good hammer mill for starch has a screen with holes around 1-2 mm. You feed the dried cakes into the hopper. Do not feed too fast or the mill will clog. Feed slowly and steadily. The machine will break the cakes into fine powder. If you don't have a hammer mill, you can use a motorized grater or even a manual mortar and pestle for very small batches, but that is labour-intensive. For commercial production, a hammer mill is essential. In Nigeria, a hammer mill with a 10-15 horsepower engine can process about 500 kg per hour. In Ghana, many processors use a 'cassava grater' that has been modified with a different screen. The key is to adjust the mill so the powder is fine enough. After grinding, let the powder cool before sieving. The heat from grinding can cause moisture to condense. If the powder is too hot, it will clog the sieve. Also, check the mill's blades regularly. Dull blades produce uneven particles and overheat the starch. Sharpen or replace blades every 100 hours of operation. Always wear a dust mask and goggles when grinding. Starch dust can irritate your lungs and eyes. After grinding, you should have a fine powder that feels like flour. If you squeeze a handful, it should not stick together. If it does, the starch is still too moist and needs more drying before grinding. Store the ground starch in clean, dry sacks. Never use sacks that previously held fertiliser or chemicals. Proper grinding is the first step to getting high-quality starch powder that will fetch a better price at the market..Trim()

Particle Size Reduction Equipment

To turn dried starch cakes into powder, you need the right equipment. The most common machine in Africa is the hammer mill. It has a rotor with swinging hammers that beat the starch against a screen. Sieving and Classification

After grinding, the starch powder contains particles of different sizes. Sieving separates them into uniform grades. You need a set of sieves with different mesh sizes. The most common sieve in Africa is a woven wire mesh sieve, often mounted on a wooden frame. You can buy sieves from agricultural supply stores or have them made by a local artisan. For manual sieving, you shake the sieve back and forth. For larger operations, you use a mechanical sifter, which can be a vibrating screen or a rotary sifter. A vibrating screen is powered by a small motor and shakes the sieve rapidly. A rotary sifter has a rotating drum with mesh. In Nigeria, many processors use a 'gyratory sifter' that moves in a circular motion. The process is simple: pour the ground starch onto the top sieve. The fine particles fall through, while coarse particles stay on top. You can stack sieves with different mesh sizes. For example, a 60-mesh sieve on top, then 80-mesh, then 100-mesh. The powder that passes through all sieves is the finest grade. The powder that stays on the 60-mesh is the coarsest. You can then sell different grades at different prices. Fine starch is used in pharmaceuticals and cosmetics, while coarser starch is used in food. After sieving, you must clean the sieves regularly. The mesh size of your sieve determines the texture of your final starch powder. Mesh size is measured in numbers: a higher number means smaller holes. Quality Control for Particle Size

Quality control for particle size ensures your starch powder is consistent and meets customer expectations. You don't need a laboratory to do basic QC. Start with a simple test: take a handful of starch and rub it between your fingers. It should feel smooth, not gritty. If it feels gritty, there are coarse particles. You can also use a simple sieve test. Take a known weight of starch, say 100 grams, and sieve it through a 100-mesh sieve for 2 minutes. Weigh what passes through and what stays on top. Calculate the percentage. For fine starch, at least 90% should pass through. If less, you need to grind more or sieve longer. Another test is the sedimentation test. Put 10 grams of starch in a glass of water, stir, and let it settle. The particles will settle at different rates. The finest particles settle last. After 1 hour, measure the height of the sediment. A higher sediment means finer particles. You can also use a simple microscope or a magnifying glass to look at the particles. They should be uniform in size. If you see large lumps, your sieving is not working. For a more precise measurement, you can use a set of standard sieves and a mechanical shaker. This is called sieve analysis. You record the weight retained on each sieve and plot a graph. This is common in larger factories. But for small-scale, the feel test and the sieve test are enough. You should also check for moisture. Moist starch will clump and clog sieves. Use a moisture meter or the salt test: mix a pinch of starch with dry salt. If the salt sticks, the starch is too moist. Keep records of your tests. Write down the date, batch number, and results. This helps you trace problems. If a customer complains, you can check your records. Dust Control and Safety

Starch dust is a serious health and safety hazard. When you grind and sieve starch, fine dust fills the air. Breathing this dust can cause respiratory problems like asthma and bronchitis. It can also irritate your eyes and skin. In severe cases, starch dust can explode if it is concentrated and there is a spark. So you must control dust. The best way is to use a dust extraction system. This can be as simple as a cyclone attached to your mill. A cyclone separates dust from the air using centrifugal force. The dust falls into a collection bin, and clean air exits. You can also use a bag filter. For small-scale operations, you can wear a dust mask. A simple N95 mask is good. But masks are not enough; you need to reduce dust at the source. Enclose your mill and sieves with covers. Use flexible ducting to carry dust away. In many African workshops, you can build a simple dust collector using a 200-litre drum and a small fan. The fan pulls dusty air into the drum, where water sprays trap the dust. This is called a wet scrubber. It is cheap and effective. Also, keep your work area clean. Sweep or vacuum spilled starch immediately. Do not use compressed air to blow dust, as it will just spread it. Wear protective clothing: long sleeves, goggles, and gloves. Avoid wearing loose clothing that can get caught in moving parts. Ensure your electrical equipment is properly grounded to prevent sparks. Never smoke or use open flames near starch dust. Store starch in sealed containers. If you feel dizzy or have trouble breathing, stop work and get fresh air. Regular health checks for workers are important. In Nigeria, the Factory Act requires employers to provide a safe workplace. Even if you are a small business, you should follow these guidelines. Train your workers on dust hazards and safe practices. Post signs reminding them to wear masks. A safe workplace is a productive workplace..Trim()

Testing Starch Purity and Whiteness

Purity and whiteness determine the price and use of your cassava starch. Buyers reject starch that is grey, yellow, or gritty. To test purity, you need to separate starch from fibre, sand, and protein. A simple method is the sedimentation test. Take 10 grams of dry starch, mix with 100 ml of clean water in a clear glass jar. Stir well and let it stand for 2 hours. Pure starch settles to the bottom as a dense white layer. Fibre and impurities float or form a thin brown layer on top. Carefully pour off the water and top layer. Dry the settled starch and weigh it. If you started with 10 g and get 9 g or more, purity is above 90%. For whiteness, use a simple whiteness scale. You can buy a cheap whiteness meter (like a portable rice whiteness tester) for around $50. Or use a visual comparison: place a small pile of your starch next to a pure white tile or a commercial starch sample. In Africa, many buyers accept starch with a whiteness of 85% or higher. To improve whiteness, wash the starch slurry more times. After the first settling, add fresh water, stir, and settle again. Repeat 3 times. Also, avoid iron tools during processing because iron causes grey spots. Use stainless steel or plastic. Dry starch in a clean, shaded area away from dust. If you see black specks, those are sand or dirt. Sieve the dry starch through a 100-mesh sieve to remove them. Record your results in a notebook for each batch. This helps you track quality and adjust your process. Remember, consistent purity and whiteness build trust with buyers and allow you to sell at a premium price..Trim()

Moisture Content Determination

Moisture content is the amount of water left in your dried starch. Too much moisture causes mould, caking, and short shelf life. Too little wastes energy and reduces yield. The standard for cassava starch is 12-14% moisture. To measure moisture without a lab, use the oven-drying method. You need a small digital scale accurate to 0.1 g (cost about $20) and a heat source. An oven or a solar dryer works. Weigh a clean, dry metal plate or aluminium foil. Record its weight. Add 10 g of starch sample and spread it thin. Weigh the plate plus starch. Put it in an oven at 105Â°C for 3 hours. Remove and cool in a dry place (not open air, as it absorbs moisture). Weigh again. The difference in weight is the water lost. For example, if you started with 10 g and after drying you have 8.7 g, moisture is 1.3 g, which is 13%. That is acceptable. If you have no oven, use a frying pan on low heat. Stir constantly to avoid burning. When the starch stops steaming and feels bone dry, it is done. But this method is less accurate. For a quick field test, squeeze a handful of starch. If it holds together and feels damp, moisture is above 15%. If it crumbles and feels dry, it is below 12%. Always test each batch before packaging. Store dried starch in airtight bags or containers in a dry room. In humid African climates, moisture can rise during storage. So test again before selling. Keep records of moisture for every batch. If moisture is too high, spread the starch in the sun for a few hours or run it through a dryer again. Never pack hot starch; let it cool first. Viscosity and Gelatinization Tests

Viscosity is how thick your starch becomes when cooked. Gelatinization is the point when starch granules swell and thicken in hot water. High-quality cassava starch should have high viscosity and a clear gel. To test viscosity simply, make a paste. Mix 20 g of starch with 100 ml of cold water in a pot. Stir until smooth. Heat on a stove, stirring constantly. As it heats, the mixture will thicken. Note the temperature when it starts to thicken. For cassava starch, this is usually around 60-70Â°C. Continue heating to 80Â°C and hold for 5 minutes. You now have a gel. Let it cool. A good gel is thick, smooth, and slightly clear. If it is watery, your starch has low viscosity, possibly due to too much moisture or poor extraction. If it is lumpy, you did not stir well or the starch had impurities. To measure viscosity more objectively, use a simple funnel test. Fill a funnel with the hot gel and time how long it takes to flow out. Pure cassava starch gel takes about 30-60 seconds for 100 ml. If it takes longer, it is too thick (maybe high amylose, which is rare in cassava). If it flows too fast (under 20 seconds), viscosity is low. Another test is the finger test: dip a clean finger in the cool gel. It should coat the finger and drip slowly. For gelatinization, you can also check clarity. A clear gel indicates high purity. Microbiological Testing

Microbiological testing checks for harmful bacteria, mould, and yeast in your starch. High levels can cause food poisoning or spoilage. Detection of Adulterants

Adulterants are cheap substances mixed into starch to increase weight or profit. Common adulterants in cassava starch include talc, chalk, gypsum, wheat flour, and even sand. Adulterants like talc or sand leave a lot of ash or a hard residue. For sand, dry a sample and weigh. Then mix with water, stir, and let settle. Pour off the starch and water.
