Module 1 · Introduction to Yam Farming

Overview of Yam: Origin, Importance, and Global Production

Yam is a staple food crop in many parts of Africa, Asia, and the Caribbean. It belongs to the genus Dioscorea and is believed to have originated in West Africa and Asia. In Africa, yam is more than just food; it is a cultural symbol used in festivals, weddings, and traditional ceremonies. Nigeria is the world's largest producer, accounting for about 70% of global production. Other major producers include Ghana, CÃ´te d'Ivoire, Benin, and Togo. In 2021, global yam production was about 73 million metric tons, with Africa producing over 90% of that. The crop is important for food security because it provides carbohydrates, vitamins, and minerals. It also generates income for millions of smallholder farmers. Unlike other tubers, yams can be stored for months without refrigeration, making them a reliable food source during dry seasons. However, yam production faces challenges such as pests, diseases, and high labor costs. Despite these, yam remains a valuable crop for African farmers. Understanding its origin and importance helps you appreciate why yam farming can be a profitable venture. Types and Varieties of Yams (White, Yellow, Water, Chinese)

There are many types of yams, but the most common in Africa are white yam (Dioscorea rotundata), yellow yam (Dioscorea cayenensis), water yam (Dioscorea alata), and Chinese yam (Dioscorea esculenta). White yam, also known as Guinea yam, is the most popular in West Africa. It has white flesh, a rough brown skin, and can grow up to 2 meters long. It is preferred for pounded yam because of its texture. Yellow yam has yellow flesh and is similar to white yam but matures earlier. Water yam, also called winged yam, has a distinct winged stem and can grow in a wider range of climates. It is less preferred for pounding but good for boiling and frying. Chinese yam, also known as lesser yam, produces smaller tubers and is often grown in Asia and the Pacific. In Africa, white and yellow yams dominate the market. When choosing a variety, consider your local market demand, climate, and soil. For example, white yam does well in well-drained loamy soil with rainfall of 1000-1500 mm per year. Yellow yam tolerates drier conditions. Water yam can grow in poorer soils but needs good drainage. Chinese yam is often grown as a secondary crop. Farmers often plant a mix of varieties to spread risk. You can get seed yams from research institutes like IITA (International Institute of Tropical Agriculture) or from fellow farmers. Always select healthy, disease-free seed yams. Proper variety selection is the first step to a successful yam farm..Trim()

Nutritional and Economic Value of Yams

Yam is highly nutritious. A 100-gram serving of cooked yam provides about 118 calories, mainly from carbohydrates. It is a good source of dietary fiber, vitamin C, vitamin B6, potassium, and manganese. Unlike many staples, yam contains some protein (about 1.5 grams per 100 grams). It is low in fat and has a moderate glycemic index, making it suitable for people with diabetes when eaten in moderation. In Africa, yam is a major source of energy for millions. For example, in Nigeria, the average person consumes about 200-300 kg of yam per year. Economically, yam is a cash crop. A farmer can earn significant income from selling yams. In 2022, the price of a medium-sized yam tuber in Nigeria ranged from 1,500 to 3,000 naira (about $2-4 USD), depending on the season. A hectare of yam can yield 10-15 tons, generating revenue of $5,000 to $10,000. However, production costs are high, mainly for seed yams and labor. Yam also creates jobs in transportation, processing, and marketing. Value-added products like yam flour, chips, and flakes are gaining popularity. In Ghana, yam exports to Europe and the US earn foreign exchange. For a smallholder farmer, yam can be a pathway out of poverty if managed well. Understanding the nutritional and economic value helps you see yam farming as a business, not just subsistence..Trim()

Basic Requirements for Starting a Yam Farm

Starting a yam farm requires careful planning. First, you need land. A minimum of 0.25 hectares (about 2.5 plots) is good for a beginner. The land should be well-drained, loamy, and rich in organic matter. Clear the land of weeds and bushes, but avoid burning as it destroys organic matter. Next, you need seed yams. These are small tubers or cut pieces of larger tubers. Each seed yam should weigh 200-500 grams and be disease-free. You can buy from certified seed producers or research institutes like IITA. Expect to plant about 10,000 seed yams per hectare. You will also need tools: hoes, cutlasses, shovels, and a wheelbarrow. For larger farms, a tractor may be useful for land preparation. You need planting stakes or trellises. Yam vines need support to grow well. Use strong sticks or bamboo, about 2-3 meters long. Labor is a major requirement. Yam farming is labor-intensive, especially for planting, weeding, and harvesting. You may need to hire workers, especially during peak seasons. Fertilizer and manure are also important. Apply well-rotted manure or NPK 15-15-15 at planting. You will need a storage facility. Yams can be stored in a cool, dark, ventilated barn or in the ground. Finally, you need capital. A rough estimate for 0.25 hectares: seed yams (2,500 pieces) cost about $500, labor $300, tools $100, and other inputs $100, totaling around $1,000. With good management, you can harvest 2.5-3.5 tons, worth $1,500-$2,500. So, it is profitable. Start small, learn, and expand..Trim()

Challenges and Opportunities in Yam Farming

Yam farming comes with several challenges. The biggest is the high cost of seed yams, which can be 30-50% of total production cost. Pests like yam beetles and nematodes damage tubers, reducing market value. Diseases such as yam mosaic virus and anthracnose can wipe out a field. Weeds compete for nutrients, and weeding is labor-intensive. Climate change brings unpredictable rainfall, leading to drought or flooding. Post-harvest losses are also significant; poor storage can cause 20-30% of the harvest to rot. Market prices fluctuate, and middlemen often take a large share of profits. Additionally, access to credit and extension services is limited for smallholders. However, there are many opportunities. The demand for yam is growing in urban areas and for export. Value addition, such as processing yam into flour, chips, or flakes, can increase income. Organic yam fetches premium prices in international markets. With training, farmers can adopt improved varieties and better practices. Cooperatives can help with bulk buying of inputs and collective marketing. Government and NGO programs sometimes provide subsidized seed yams and loans. Technology, like mobile apps for market information, can help farmers get better prices. By addressing challenges and seizing opportunities, yam farming can be a viable and profitable business. The key is to plan well, manage risks, and continuously learn..Trim()

Determining Optimal Planting Time

In yam farming, timing is everything. Planting at the wrong time can mean poor sprouting, weak vines, and small tubers. The best time to plant yam setts is at the beginning of the rainy season, when the soil is moist but not waterlogged. .Trim()

You can also check local signs: when farmers start planting maize or when certain trees flower, it's often a good indicator. But don't rely only on signs; use a simple rain gauge or follow weather forecasts on radio or phone apps..Trim()

.Trim()

Planting too early in dry soil leads to setts drying out or being eaten by pests. Planting too late means the rainy season ends before tubers bulk up, resulting in small harvests. Yams need 6 to 8 months of consistent moisture. So if your rainy season ends in October, you must plant by March to give the crop enough time. Mounds are good for poorly drained soils because they raise the planting area above the water table. They also allow the tubers to expand easily. To make a mound, dig the topsoil, mix it with compost or manure, and pile it up. Plant one sett per mound, usually at the top. Mounds are labor-intensive but give good yields. In sandy soils, mounds may dry out quickly, so you might need to mulch..Trim()

.Trim()

Ridges are long, raised beds, like those used for potatoes. They are made by ploughing or hoeing soil into continuous ridges about 30-40 cm high and 60-80 cm wide. Ridges are easier to make with a tractor or ox-plough, saving labor. They are good for flat land and moderate drainage. Plant setts along the ridge at regular spacing. Ridges allow for mechanization and are easier to weed. However, in very wet areas, ridges may not be high enough to prevent waterlogging..Trim()

.Trim()

Flat beds are simply planting on level ground without raising the soil. This method is used in well-drained soils and in areas with low rainfall. It requires the least labor. You just dig holes and plant. But flat beds are risky in heavy rains because water can pool and rot the setts. If you use flat beds, make sure the field has good drainage and consider planting on slight slopes..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, most smallholders use mounds or ridges. If you have a small plot and plenty of labor, mounds are good. If you have a larger field, ridges save time. Flat beds are only for well-drained soils. Whichever method you choose, prepare the land early and add organic matter to improve soil fertility..Trim()

Spacing and Plant Density for Yam

Spacing determines how many yam plants you can grow per hectare and how well they grow. If you plant too closely, plants compete for light, water, and nutrients, and you get small tubers. If you plant too far apart, you waste land and get fewer plants per hectare. The right spacing depends on the yam variety, soil fertility, and your farming method..Trim()

.Trim()

For most yam varieties, a spacing of 1 meter by 1 meter is standard. This means each plant has 1 square meter of space. On one hectare (10,000 square meters), you can plant about 10,000 yam setts. But this is the maximum; in practice, you might plant 8,000 to 10,000 setts per hectare. If you use mounds, space the mounds 1 m apart in rows that are 1 m apart. For ridges, plant setts every 1 m along the ridge, with ridges 1 m apart. For flat beds, dig holes at 1 m by 1 m spacing..Trim()

.Trim()

Some farmers use closer spacing, like 0.8 m by 0.8 m, to get more plants per hectare. This can increase yield if the soil is very fertile and you provide enough water and nutrients. But if fertility is low, close spacing leads to competition and small tubers. On the other hand, wider spacing (1.2 m by 1.2 m) gives larger individual tubers but fewer plants. This might be better for high-value markets where big tubers fetch higher prices..Trim()

.Trim()

Plant density also affects disease and pest management. Dense plantings have more humidity and can spread diseases faster. So if you have disease problems, use wider spacing. Also, consider the growth habit of the variety. Some yam varieties have long vines that need more space; others are more compact..Trim()

.Trim()

To calculate the number of setts you need, measure your field. For example, a field 50 m by 20 m is 1,000 square meters. At 1 m by 1 m spacing, you need 1,000 setts. Add 5-10% extra for replanting. Always mark your spacing with a rope or sticks to keep rows straight. Good spacing makes weeding, harvesting, and other operations easier..Trim()

Seed Yam Preparation and Treatment Before Planting

Seed yams are small whole tubers or pieces of larger tubers used for planting. Proper preparation and treatment of seed yams before planting can prevent diseases and improve sprouting. Follow these steps to get your seed yams ready..Trim()

.Trim()

First, select healthy seed yams. Choose tubers that are firm, free from rot, and have at least one or two viable buds (eyes). Avoid tubers with soft spots, cracks, or signs of nematode damage (small holes or bumps). The size of the seed yam matters: small setts (100-200 g) are economical, but larger setts (300-500 g) sprout faster and produce bigger plants. If you cut larger tubers into setts, make sure each piece has at least one bud. Cut them into pieces of about 100-200 g. Use a clean, sharp knife to avoid crushing the tissue..Trim()

.Trim()

After cutting, treat the setts to prevent fungal and bacterial infections. You can dust the cut surfaces with wood ash, which is a traditional and effective treatment. Wood ash dries the cut and has some antimicrobial properties. Alternatively, use a fungicide like Benlate or Mancozeb, following the label instructions. For organic farmers, dip the setts in a mixture of neem extract and water. Another method is to dip the setts in a slurry of clay and water, which forms a protective coating..Trim()

.Trim()

Before planting, you can also pre-sprout the seed yams to ensure they are viable. Place the setts in a warm, shaded area with high humidity, such as in a box lined with moist sawdust or banana leaves. Keep them for 1-2 weeks until small sprouts appear. This is called pre-sprouting or chitting. It helps you identify and discard non-viable setts and gives the plants a head start..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, handle the setts carefully. Do not throw them; place them gently in the planting hole. Plant them with the sprout or bud facing up. If you treat and prepare your seed yams well, you will get uniform sprouting and healthier plants..Trim()

Planting Depth and Orientation of Setts

The depth at which you plant yam setts and the direction they face can affect sprouting, tuber development, and yield. Getting these details right is simple but important..Trim()

.Trim()

Planting depth: Yam setts should be planted about 5-10 cm deep. If you plant too shallow, the setts may dry out or be exposed to sun and pests. If you plant too deep, the sprouts may take longer to emerge, and the tubers may be small or deformed. A good rule is to plant the sett so that the top of the sett is about 5 cm below the soil surface. On mounds, you can plant at the top of the mound, covering with 5-10 cm of soil. On ridges, plant in the middle of the ridge. On flat beds, dig a hole 10-15 cm deep, place the sett, and cover with 5-10 cm of soil. The soil should be loose and well-drained..Trim()

.Trim()

Orientation: The bud or sprout should face upward. If you have a sett with a clear bud, plant it with the bud pointing up. If the sett is a piece of a larger tuber, the bud is usually at one end. Plant that end up. If you are not sure, plant the sett flat, but slightly tilted so that the bud end is higher. This helps the sprout emerge quickly. Some farmers plant setts horizontally, but vertical orientation with bud up is best..Trim()

.Trim()

After planting, you can mulch with dry grass or leaves to conserve moisture and suppress weeds. Mulching also keeps the soil temperature stable. Do not bury the setts too deep with mulch; just a light layer..Trim()

.Trim()

If you plant on mounds, make sure the sett is not directly exposed to the sun. You can plant on the side of the mound if the sun is intense. Also, avoid planting in waterlogged spots. If heavy rain is expected, plant on ridges or mounds to ensure drainage..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, proper depth and orientation lead to faster sprouting, stronger vines, and better tuber shape. Take a little extra time to plant each sett correctly..Trim()

Intercropping and Crop Rotation with Yams

Intercropping and crop rotation are age-old practices that can improve your yam farm's productivity and reduce pests and diseases. Yams are often grown with other crops, but you must choose the right companions and follow a good rotation plan..Trim()

.Trim()

Intercropping: Yams grow well with several crops. Good companions include maize, cowpea, egusi melon, and vegetables like okra and pepper. These crops have different growth habits and nutrient needs, so they don't compete too much. For example, maize provides shade for young yam vines, and cowpea fixes nitrogen in the soil. However, avoid planting yams with other root crops like cassava or sweet potato, as they compete for space and nutrients. Also, don't intercrop with tall, dense crops that shade yams too much. A common practice is to plant maize in rows between yam mounds, then plant cowpea after maize is harvested. This gives you extra food and income without hurting yam yields..Trim()

.Trim()

When intercropping, adjust spacing. If you plant yams at 1 m by 1 m, you can plant a row of maize or cowpea in between. But don't overcrowd. Too many plants will reduce yam tuber size. Also, consider the timing: plant yams first, then intercrops a few weeks later when yam sprouts emerge. This reduces competition during early growth..Trim()

.Trim()

Crop rotation: Yam should not be grown on the same land year after year. This depletes soil nutrients and encourages nematodes and diseases. A good rotation is to follow yam with a cereal like maize or sorghum, then a legume like cowpea or groundnut, then back to yam after 2-3 years. Or rotate yam with cassava, but note that cassava also depletes nutrients. Better to rotate with cereals and legumes. In traditional African farming, yams are often rotated with fallow periods. If you have enough land, let a field rest for 2-3 years to restore fertility. If not, use green manure crops like mucuna or pigeon pea..Trim()

.Trim()

Rotating crops also helps break pest cycles. For example, nematodes that attack yams may not survive on maize. So after yam, plant maize, and the nematode population drops. Then when you plant yam again, it's healthier..Trim()

.Trim()

In summary, intercropping can increase total farm output, but choose compatible crops. Crop rotation is essential for long-term soil health and yam productivity. Plan your rotation at least two seasons ahead..Trim()

Understanding Yam Nutrient Requirements

Yam is a heavy feeder. A crop of 10,000 plants (about 1 hectare) removes roughly 100 kg nitrogen (N), 30 kg phosphorus (P2O5), and 120 kg potassium (K2O) from the soil. That means if you harvest 20 tonnes of tubers, you are taking away those nutrients. You must replace them or the next crop will be small and sickly. Yam also needs calcium, magnesium, sulfur, and micronutrients like zinc and boron, though in smaller amounts..Trim()

.Trim()

The first 3 months after planting are critical. During this time, the vine grows rapidly and the tuber begins to form. If nutrients are missing now, you cannot make up for it later. Nitrogen is needed for leaf and vine growth. Phosphorus helps root development and tuber initiation. Potassium is the most important for tuber size, starch content, and storage quality. A shortage of potassium shows as yellowing between the veins of older leaves and small tubers..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, most yam is grown on soils that are sandy or loamy and low in organic matter. These soils hold few nutrients. After 2-3 years of continuous yam, yields drop sharply. You can see this in the leaves: pale green or yellow, and vines that stop growing. The best way to know what your soil needs is a soil test. Contact your local agricultural extension officer or a nearby university soil lab. A simple test costs little and tells you exactly which nutrients are low and what fertiliser to buy..Trim()

.Trim()

Without a soil test, a general recommendation for yam in West Africa is to apply 400-600 kg per hectare of a compound fertiliser like NPK 15-15-15 at planting, then top-dress with 100-150 kg per hectare of urea or NPK 20-10-10 about 8-10 weeks later. But these are only starting points. Your soil may need more or less. Also, remember that yam responds well to organic matter. Even if you use inorganic fertiliser, adding compost or manure will improve soil structure and water holding capacity, making fertiliser work better..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, yam does not like competition. Weeds take up nutrients faster than yam. Keep the field clean, especially in the first 3 months. Mulching with dry grass or crop residues also adds organic matter slowly and suppresses weeds. A healthy yam crop starts with knowing what the plant needs and what your soil can supply..Trim()

Organic Matter and Compost Application

Organic matter is the backbone of soil fertility. In Africa, many yam farmers cannot afford enough inorganic fertiliser, so compost and manure are essential. Good compost does three things: it feeds the soil life, it holds water and nutrients, and it improves soil structure so yam roots and tubers can grow straight and big. A soil with 2-3% organic matter will produce far better yam than a sandy soil with less than 1%..Trim()

.Trim()

To make compost, you need a mix of 'browns' (dry leaves, straw, sawdust) and 'greens' (fresh grass, kitchen waste, animal manure). A simple pile: alternate 15 cm layers of browns and greens, keep it as moist as a wrung-out sponge, and turn it every 2 weeks. In 6-8 weeks, you get dark, crumbly compost that smells like forest soil. For 1 hectare of yam, you need about 5-10 tonnes of compost. That sounds like a lot, but you can start small. Apply 2-3 handfuls (about 200-300 grams) per planting hole. That is 2-3 tonnes per hectare if you have 10,000 hills. Every bit helps..Trim()

.Trim()

Animal manure is also excellent. Poultry manure is rich in nitrogen, but fresh manure can burn plants. Let it dry or compost it for at least 4 weeks before use. Cow dung is milder and adds lots of organic matter. Apply 1-2 kg per hill (10-20 tonnes per hectare) if you have it. Spread it in the planting hole and mix with soil before planting the setts..Trim()

.Trim()

You can also grow cover crops like mucuna (velvet bean) or pigeon pea and plough them into the soil before planting yam. These 'green manures' add nitrogen and organic matter. Mucuna can produce over 100 kg of nitrogen per hectare in its biomass. Plant it at the start of the rains, let it grow for 8-10 weeks, then slash and incorporate it 2-3 weeks before planting yam..Trim()

.Trim()

Do not burn your crop residues. Burning destroys organic matter and soil life. Instead, chop residues and spread them as mulch. Mulch keeps the soil cool, reduces evaporation, and slowly releases nutrients. In dry areas, mulch can increase yam yield by 20-30%. Remember, building organic matter takes time. It may take 3-4 seasons of consistent compost and manure application to see big changes. But once your soil is rich, it will give you good yam with less fertiliser..Trim()

Inorganic Fertilizer Types and Application Rates

Inorganic fertilisers are concentrated nutrient salts. They are fast-acting and easy to apply, but they can be expensive and if used wrongly, they can damage your soil. The most common types for yam in Africa are NPK compounds, urea, and muriate of potash (MOP). NPK 15-15-15 means 15% nitrogen, 15% phosphorus (as P2O5), and 15% potassium (as K2O). Other common grades are NPK 20-10-10 (more nitrogen) and NPK 12-12-17 (more potassium for tuber filling)..Trim()

.Trim()

How much to apply? A standard recommendation for yam is 400-600 kg per hectare of NPK 15-15-15 at planting. That is about 40-60 grams per hill if you have 10,000 hills per hectare. Use a bottle cap or a small tin to measure. A full bottle cap of NPK 15-15-15 weighs about 15-20 grams, so you need 2-3 caps per hill. Place the fertiliser in a ring around the sett, about 10-15 cm away from the seed yam, and cover with soil. Never let fertiliser touch the sett directly; it will burn the young roots..Trim()

.Trim()

About 8-10 weeks after planting, when the vines are actively growing, top-dress with nitrogen. Use urea (46% N) at 100-150 kg per hectare, or NPK 20-10-10 at 150-200 kg per hectare. That is about 10-15 grams of urea per hill (half a bottle cap) or 15-20 grams of NPK 20-10-10. Apply in a band 15-20 cm from the plant base and cover lightly. If leaves are pale yellow, you need more nitrogen. If they are dark green and vines are too leafy, skip the top-dress..Trim()

.Trim()

For tuber bulking (from about 3 months onward), potassium is key. If you can find NPK 12-12-17 or potassium sulphate (SOP), apply 100-150 kg per hectare. Muriate of potash (MOP, 60% K2O) can also be used at 100-150 kg per hectare. Potassium helps tubers grow large and store well. Do not apply potassium too late; stop by 4 months after planting..Trim()

.Trim()

Always read the label on the fertiliser bag. Store fertiliser in a dry place, off the ground, and away from children and animals. Do not mix different fertilisers unless instructed. If you can afford only one bag, buy NPK 15-15-15 and use it at planting. That gives the young plant a balanced start. For best results, combine inorganic fertiliser with compost or manure. The organic matter helps hold the nutrients in the soil so they are not washed away by rain..Trim()

Timing and Methods of Fertilizer Application

When and how you apply fertiliser is as important as what you apply. Yam needs nutrients at three key stages: at planting (for root and sprout development), during rapid vine growth (8-12 weeks after planting), and during tuber bulking (3-5 months after planting). Missing these windows means wasted fertiliser and poor yields..Trim()

.Trim()

At planting, apply your base fertiliser. This is usually NPK 15-15-15 or a similar compound. The method is simple: dig your planting hole, place the seed yam, then put the fertiliser in a ring around the sett, about 10-15 cm away. Cover with soil. Do not put fertiliser directly under the sett or touching it. If you are using manure or compost, mix it thoroughly with the soil in the hole before planting. For a 1-hectare field with 10,000 hills, you need 400-600 kg of NPK. That is 40-60 grams per hill. Use a small tin or bottle cap to measure. One level bottle cap (from a 1-litre bottle) holds about 15-20 grams..Trim()

.Trim()

The first top-dressing comes when vines are growing fast, usually 8-10 weeks after planting. At this time, apply urea or NPK 20-10-10. The best method is banding: make a shallow furrow 15-20 cm from the base of the plant, sprinkle the fertiliser along the furrow, and cover with soil. Do not broadcast on the surface; nitrogen can be lost to the air. If the soil is dry, apply just before rain or irrigate lightly after application. For urea, 100-150 kg per hectare is typical. That is 10-15 grams per hill..Trim()

.Trim()

The second top-dressing, if needed, is at tuber bulking, around 3-4 months after planting. Use a potassium-rich fertiliser like NPK 12-12-17 or muriate of potash. Apply in a band between the rows, 20-25 cm from the plants, and cover. This helps tubers fill out and improves storage quality. Do not apply nitrogen at this stage; it will encourage leaf growth instead of tuber growth..Trim()

.Trim()

Always apply fertiliser to moist soil. If the soil is dry, wait for rain or water the field first. Fertiliser on dry soil can burn roots and is not taken up. Also, avoid applying on waterlogged soil; nutrients can be lost through leaching or denitrification. Keep a simple record: date, type of fertiliser, amount per hill, and method. This helps you learn what works on your farm. After harvest, note the yield. Over 2-3 seasons, you will know exactly what your yam needs..Trim()

Soil pH Management for Yam

Soil pH is a measure of how acidic or alkaline your soil is. It runs from 0 to 14. A pH of 7 is neutral. Below 7 is acidic, above 7 is alkaline. Yam grows best in soils with a pH between 5.5 and 6.5. In this range, nutrients like phosphorus, potassium, calcium, and magnesium are most available to the plant. If the pH is too low (below 5.0) or too high (above 7.5), yam cannot take up nutrients properly, even if you apply fertiliser..Trim()

.Trim()

Most soils in the humid and sub-humid tropics of Africa are naturally acidic. Continuous farming with urea or ammonium sulphate fertilisers makes them even more acidic. You can test your soil pH with a simple kit from an agricultural supply store. It costs little and gives you a number. Or send a soil sample to a lab. To take a sample, dig 15-20 cm deep in 10-15 spots across your field, mix the soil in a clean bucket, and take about 500 grams to the lab or extension office..Trim()

.Trim()

If your soil pH is below 5.5, you need to add lime. Agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) or dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate) raises pH. The amount depends on how acidic your soil is and its texture. A general rule: to raise pH by 0.5 units on a sandy soil, apply 1-2 tonnes of lime per hectare. On clay soil, you may need 3-4 tonnes. Apply lime 2-3 months before planting yam. Spread it evenly over the field and plough or hoe it into the top 15 cm. Lime reacts slowly, so do not expect immediate results. It also adds calcium and magnesium, which yam needs..Trim()

.Trim()

If your soil pH is above 7.5 (rare in most yam areas, but possible in dry savannah), you can lower it by adding elemental sulphur or gypsum. Elemental sulphur is applied at 200-500 kg per hectare, but it takes several months to work. Organic matter like compost also helps buffer pH, bringing it closer to neutral over time. In most cases, adding compost is the safest way to improve pH without overdoing it..Trim()

.Trim()

Do not guess. A pH test is cheap and prevents wasting money on fertiliser that your crop cannot use. If you cannot afford a test, look at your weeds. If you see lots of sorrel (Oxalis) or bracken fern, your soil is likely acidic. If you see thistles or nettles, it may be more neutral. But these are only clues. For yam, aim for pH 5.5-6.5 and you will get the best response from your fertiliser and organic matter..Trim()

Foliar Feeding and Micronutrients

Foliar feeding means spraying liquid fertiliser directly onto the leaves. Yam leaves can absorb nutrients quickly, so foliar sprays are a fast way to correct deficiencies. They are not a replacement for soil fertiliser, but they can give a boost during critical growth stages or when the soil is too dry or too wet for roots to take up nutrients. Foliar feeding is especially useful for micronutrients, which yam needs in very small amounts..Trim()

.Trim()

Micronutrients include zinc, boron, manganese, iron, copper, and molybdenum. A shortage of zinc causes small leaves and shortened internodes (the space between leaves). Boron deficiency leads to poor tuber formation and hollow tubers. Manganese deficiency shows as yellowing between veins on young leaves. In most African soils, zinc and boron are the most common deficiencies. You can buy micronutrient mixes at agro-dealers. Look for a product that contains chelated micronutrients, which are more stable. Mix according to the label, usually 1-2 grams per litre of water. Spray in the early morning or late evening when it is cool and leaves are not wet from rain. Cover both sides of the leaves..Trim()

.Trim()

For a general foliar boost, you can use urea (nitrogen) or potassium nitrate. A common recipe: dissolve 20-30 grams of urea in 10 litres of water (about 2-3 tablespoons per litre) and spray on the leaves. This gives a quick nitrogen boost if vines are pale. For potassium, use potassium nitrate or potassium sulphate at 10-20 grams per 10 litres. Do not mix urea with lime or calcium sprays; they can react and burn leaves. Always test on a few plants first. If leaves show burn (brown spots) within 24 hours, dilute the spray next time..Trim()

.Trim()

Foliar feeding is most effective during rapid vine growth (8-12 weeks after planting) and during tuber bulking (3-5 months). Spray every 2-3 weeks, up to 3-4 times per season. Do not spray in the hot midday sun; droplets can act like lenses and burn leaves. Also, do not spray if rain is expected within 6 hours; it will wash off. Use a knapsack sprayer with a fine nozzle. Clean the sprayer thoroughly after use, especially if you previously used herbicides..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, foliar feeding is a supplement, not a cure-all. If your soil is severely lacking in nutrients, you must fix the soil first with compost, manure, and inorganic fertiliser. Use foliar sprays as a quick fix for visible deficiencies or to give your yam a boost during critical stages. Keep notes on what you spray, when, and how the plants respond. Over time, you will know exactly what your yam needs..Trim()

Water Needs of Yam at Different Growth Stages

Yam needs different amounts of water at each stage of growth. Knowing these stages helps you water correctly. The first stage is sprouting, from planting to about 4 weeks after planting. During this time, the seed yam is using its stored water to produce shoots. You only need to keep the soil moist, not soaked. If the soil is dry, give about 5 liters per plant every 3 days. Too much water now can rot the seed yam..Trim()

.Trim()

The second stage is vine growth, from about 4 to 12 weeks after planting. Now the plant is building leaves and vines. Water needs increase. In dry weather, give 10 liters per plant every 2 days. If you have sandy soil, which drains fast, water every day. In this stage, lack of water makes vines short and weak, reducing future tuber size..Trim()

.Trim()

The third stage is tuber bulking, from about 12 to 24 weeks after planting. This is when the yam tuber grows biggest. Water demand is highest now. Each plant may need 15 to 20 liters every 2 days if there is no rain. If the soil dries out now, the tuber stops growing and becomes small and misshapen. You should water deeply so water reaches the roots, which are 20 to 30 cm deep..Trim()

.Trim()

The fourth stage is maturation, from about 24 weeks until harvest. Now the leaves start to yellow and die back. Water needs drop. You can reduce watering to 5 liters per plant every 4 days. Too much water now can cause tuber rot. Stop watering completely about 2 weeks before harvest to let the soil dry and make harvesting easier..Trim()

.Trim()

A simple tool to check soil moisture is a hand trowel or a stick. Push it into the soil 10 cm deep. If it comes out dry, you need to water. If it comes out muddy, you are overwatering. In Africa, where rains can be unpredictable, always adjust based on rainfall. A rain gauge, like a simple plastic cylinder marked in millimeters, helps you know how much rain you received. If you get 10 mm of rain, that is about 10 liters per square meter, so you can skip watering that day..Trim()

Rainfed vs. Irrigated Yam Farming

Most yam in Africa is grown under rainfed conditions. This means you depend only on rainfall. Rainfed yam works well in areas with 1000 to 1500 mm of rain spread over 6 to 8 months. But if rains come late, stop early, or have dry spells, your yield drops. A dry spell of 2 weeks during tuber bulking can cut your harvest by 30% or more. Rainfed farming is cheaper because you do not buy irrigation equipment. But it is risky with climate change causing unpredictable rains..Trim()

.Trim()

Irrigated yam farming means you add water when rain is not enough. You can use simple methods like watering cans, buckets, or drip lines. Irrigation gives you control. You can grow yam in drier areas with only 600 mm of rain if you irrigate. You can also grow yam in the dry season, when prices are higher. But irrigation costs money for equipment, fuel, or labour. You need a water source like a well, borehole, river, or stored rainwater..Trim()

.Trim()

For a small farm of 0.5 hectares (about 5000 yam plants), a simple drip irrigation system can cost between $300 and $800. That includes a tank, pipes, and drip tape. You can also use a petrol water pump to move water from a river. A pump costs about $200. But you must calculate if the extra yield pays for the system. If irrigation increases your yield from 10 tons to 15 tons per hectare, and yam sells for $200 per ton, you get an extra $1000 per hectare. That pays back the system in one or two seasons..Trim()

.Trim()

Many African farmers use a mix: rainfed during the rainy season and small-scale irrigation during dry spells. You can harvest rainwater from roofs into a 5000-liter tank. That water can irrigate 100 plants for a week. Mulching also helps rainfed yam by keeping soil moist longer. The best choice depends on your rainfall, your budget, and your market. If you have a reliable market for dry-season yam, irrigation is worth it. If not, invest in mulching and good soil instead..Trim()

Designing an Irrigation System for Yam

To design an irrigation system for yam, you need to know three things: how much water your plants need, your water source, and your field layout. Yam is usually planted on mounds or ridges. For a 0.5-hectare field with 5000 plants, you need about 15 liters per plant every 2 days during peak growth. That is 75,000 liters every 2 days, or about 37,500 liters per day. That is a lot, so you need a good water source..Trim()

.Trim()

A simple design is a gravity-fed drip system. You place a tank on a stand at least 1.5 meters high. A 5000-liter tank can irrigate 500 plants for one day. You connect a main pipe (32 mm diameter) from the tank, then sub-main pipes (16 mm) along rows, and drip tape (16 mm) with emitters every 30 cm. Each emitter gives 2 liters per hour. If you run the system for 4 hours, each plant gets 8 liters. You can run it twice a day for 16 liters. This works well for yam..Trim()

.Trim()

If you have a borehole or river, you can use a petrol pump. A 1.5-inch pump can move 10,000 liters per hour. You connect it to lay-flat hose (50 mm) and then to drip lines or sprinklers. Sprinklers are cheaper but waste water and can cause leaf diseases. Drip is better for yam because it puts water at the roots and keeps leaves dry. For a small plot, you can use watering cans. One person can water 200 plants in an hour with a 15-liter can, giving each plant about 4.5 liters..Trim()

.Trim()

You must also filter your water. Sand, dirt, or debris can clog emitters. Use a screen filter (120 mesh) after the pump or before the drip lines. Clean it weekly. Also, plan your layout so that water reaches all plants evenly. Put the tank in the center of the field to reduce pressure loss. Use a pressure gauge to check that you have at least 1 bar (15 psi) at the drip lines. If pressure is low, water will not reach the end of the rows. Test your system by running it for 30 minutes and checking that all emitters drip. Adjust as needed..Trim()

Mulching to Conserve Soil Moisture

Mulching is covering the soil around your yam plants with dry plant material. It is one of the cheapest ways to save water. A good mulch layer reduces evaporation from the soil by 50% or more. That means you water half as often. Mulching also stops weeds, keeps soil cool, and adds organic matter as it breaks down. For yam, which needs consistent moisture, mulching is very important..Trim()

.Trim()

You can use many materials for mulch. Dry grass, straw, rice husks, maize stover, banana leaves, or cocoa pods are all good. Do not use fresh grass or weeds that have seeds, because they will sprout. Also, do not use plastic sheets unless you can remove them later; they do not add organic matter. The best mulch is a mix of dry grass and crop residues. Apply a layer 5 to 10 cm thick around each plant. Keep mulch 5 cm away from the yam stem to prevent rot. You can mulch right after planting or after the first weeding..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, a common method is to mulch with dry grass from savanna areas. One hectare needs about 10 to 15 tons of dry mulch. That is a lot, but you can start with a small area. For 100 plants, you need about 200 kg of dry grass. You can collect this from around your farm. Apply mulch after rain when the soil is wet. The mulch will keep that moisture in the soil for weeks. If you use rice husks, they last longer and do not blow away. In West Africa, farmers use cocoa pod husks, which also add potassium..Trim()

.Trim()

Mulching has a few challenges. It can hide pests like termites or rodents. Check under the mulch regularly. In very wet areas, mulch can keep soil too wet and cause tuber rot. If your soil is heavy clay and drains poorly, use a thinner mulch layer (2-3 cm) or skip mulching during the rainy peak. Also, mulch can be a fire risk in dry savanna. Keep it away from your house. Overall, mulching is a simple, low-cost tool that every yam farmer should use, especially in areas with dry spells..Trim()

Drainage Management to Prevent Waterlogging

Yam hates waterlogged soil. If water sits around the roots for more than 2 days, the seed yam and tubers can rot. Waterlogging also stops the tuber from growing properly, giving you small, poor-quality yams. In Africa, heavy rains can flood your field quickly. So you must plan drainage before you plant. The best time to design drainage is during land preparation..Trim()

.Trim()

The simplest drainage is to make ridges or mounds. Yam is usually planted on mounds 30 to 40 cm high. This raises the roots above the water table. But in flat areas, you also need drains between rows. Dig shallow channels (20 to 30 cm deep) every 4 to 6 rows. These channels carry excess water away from the field. Make sure the channels lead to a lower area or a ditch. If your field is on a slope, dig drains across the slope (contour drains) to slow water down. Do not dig drains straight down the slope, because that causes erosion..Trim()

.Trim()

For a 0.5-hectare field, you need a main drain at the lowest edge, about 50 cm deep and 50 cm wide. Connect all the smaller channels to this main drain. If you have clay soil, which drains slowly, make the drains deeper and closer together. You can also add organic matter like compost to clay soil to improve drainage. In sandy soil, drainage is usually good, but you still need shallow drains to stop water from pooling after heavy rain..Trim()

.Trim()

You can also use raised beds. Make beds 1 meter wide and 30 cm high, with 50 cm paths between them. The paths act as drains. This is common in market gardens. Another method is to plant on ridges that run up and down the slope, so water flows between the ridges. After a heavy rain, walk your field and look for standing water. Fix the drains immediately. Keep drains clear of weeds and debris. If you have a flood-prone field, consider growing yam on the highest part or making a perimeter ditch. Remember, prevention is better than cure: once tubers rot, you cannot save them..Trim()

Water Quality and Testing for Irrigation

Not all water is safe for yam irrigation. Water can carry salt, chemicals, or diseases that harm your crop. You should test your water before using it, especially if you use water from a river, stream, or industrial area. The most important things to test are salinity (salt level), pH, and harmful bacteria..Trim()

.Trim()

Salinity is measured as electrical conductivity (EC) in decisiemens per meter (dS/m). Yam is sensitive to salt. If your water has EC above 1.0 dS/m, it can reduce yield. Above 2.0 dS/m, yam may die. You can buy a simple EC meter for $20 to $50. Test your water every 3 months. If EC is high, mix it with rainwater or switch to another source. In coastal areas, seawater intrusion can make well water salty. Never use seawater for irrigation..Trim()

.Trim()

pH tells you if water is acidic or alkaline. Yam grows best in water with pH between 5.5 and 7.0. If pH is below 5.0, the water is too acidic and can damage roots. If above 8.0, it can lock up nutrients. You can test pH with a simple test strip or a digital pH meter ($30). If pH is wrong, you can add lime (to raise pH) or vinegar (to lower pH) to a small tank, but it is better to find a better water source..Trim()

.Trim()

Harmful bacteria like E. coli come from human or animal waste. If you use water from a river near a village, it may contain bacteria that cause diseases in yam, like soft rot. You cannot see bacteria, so test at a local agricultural lab. If you cannot test, avoid using water that smells bad or looks dirty. Let water settle in a tank for 24 hours before use. Also, check for chemicals: if water has oil or foam, do not use it. For safe irrigation, use rainwater, clean well water, or water from a protected spring. If you must use river water, filter it through sand and let it sit in the sun for a day. Always keep your water source clean and fence it from animals..Trim()

Common Weeds in Yam Fields and Control Methods

Weeds compete with yam vines for sunlight, water, and nutrients, reducing tuber size and yield by 30-50% if left uncontrolled. The most common weeds in African yam fields include spear grass (Imperata cylindrica), which has deep rhizomes that are hard to pull; pigweed (Amaranthus spp.), a fast-growing broadleaf; goosegrass (Eleusine indica), a tufted grass; and sedges (Cyperus spp.), which thrive in moist soils. Other troublesome weeds are Tridax procumbens, Chromolaena odorata (Siam weed), and Commelina spp. (dayflower)..Trim()

.Trim()

Manual control is the most practical for smallholders. Hand-hoe or machete weeding should be done 3-4 times per season: first at 3-4 weeks after planting, then at 8-10 weeks, again at 14-16 weeks, and finally when vines start to cover the ground. Avoid deep hoeing near yam roots to prevent tuber damage. Mulching with dry grass or crop residues (e.g., maize stover) at 2-3 tonnes per hectare suppresses weeds and conserves moisture. Intercropping with cover crops like cowpea, groundnut, or melon (egusi) shades the soil and reduces weed growth. For larger farms, selective herbicides such as glyphosate (pre-planting) or pendimethalin (pre-emergence) can be used, but follow label instructions carefully. Always wear protective clothing and avoid spraying on windy days. Crop rotation with cereals like maize or sorghum breaks weed cycles. Hand-pulling before weeds set seed is criticalâone pigweed plant can produce 100,000 seeds. Regular field scouting every 2 weeks helps spot new weed flushes early..Trim()

Integrated Weed Management Strategies

Integrated Weed Management (IWM) combines cultural, mechanical, biological, and chemical methods to control weeds sustainably and reduce reliance on herbicides. Start with prevention: use clean seed, compost that has been properly heated to kill weed seeds, and clean tools between fields to avoid spreading rhizomes like spear grass. Crop rotation is a powerful toolârotate yam with maize, sorghum, or cowpea. Maize and sorghum are tall and shade out weeds, while cowpea fixes nitrogen and covers the ground. A 2-year rotation with a cereal crop can reduce spear grass populations by 60%..Trim()

.Trim()

Mechanical control includes timely hoeing and mowing. For small plots, a hand hoe or cutlass works. For larger areas, a tractor-mounted cultivator can be used between rows, but avoid disturbing yam mounds. Mulching with 3-4 tonnes per hectare of dry grass or plastic mulch (where affordable) suppresses weeds and improves soil moisture. Biological control uses living organismsâfor example, the fungus Colletotrichum gloeosporioides is used against Siam weed in some African countries, but this is not yet widely available. Competitive cover crops like Mucuna pruriens (velvet bean) or Pueraria phaseoloides (tropical kudzu) can be planted between yam rows to smother weeds. Chemical control should be a last resort. Use selective post-emergence herbicides like fluazifop-p-butyl for grass weeds, or imazethapyr for broadleaf weeds. Always calibrate your sprayer, use the recommended dose (e.g., 2-3 litres per hectare for glyphosate), and rotate herbicides to prevent resistance. Combine at least three methods for best resultsâthis is the core of IWM..Trim()

Major Pests of Yam and Their Control

Yam is attacked by several insect pests that reduce yield and market value. The most damaging is the yam beetle (Heteroligus meles), whose larvae feed on tubers, causing holes and rot. Adults are dark brown, about 1.5 cm long, and active at night. Control: handpick adults during the day (they hide in soil), apply wood ash or neem seed powder around mounds, or use chlorpyrifos (2-3 kg a.i./ha) as a soil treatment before planting. The yam shoot beetle (Crioceris spp.) eats leaves; control by handpicking and spraying with neem oil (3-5 ml per litre of water)..Trim()

.Trim()

Nematodes (root-knot and yam nematodes) cause galls on roots and tubers, leading to poor growth. Control by crop rotation with cereals, solarization of soil (cover with clear plastic for 4-6 weeks), and applying organic matter like poultry manure (5-10 tonnes/ha). The yam scale insect (Aspidiella hartii) sucks sap and weakens plants; control by pruning infested vines and spraying with horticultural oil. Termites attack stored tubers and sometimes field plants; keep fields clean of dead wood, and use termite bait stations or treat storage with actellic dust. Grasshoppers and crickets chew leaves; control by handpicking and using neem-based sprays. Rodents (rats, mice) burrow and eat tubers; set traps and keep field borders clear. For all pests, scout weekly, act early, and combine cultural, biological, and chemical methods. Always follow safety precautions when using pesticides..Trim()

Common Diseases of Yam (Anthracnose, Mosaic Virus, etc.)

Yam diseases can cause 20-80% yield loss. Anthracnose (Colletotrichum gloeosporioides) causes dark, sunken lesions on leaves and vines, leading to dieback. It thrives in warm, wet conditions. Control: use resistant varieties (e.g., TDr 89/02665), plant certified disease-free setts, and apply fungicides like mancozeb (2-3 kg/ha) or copper oxychloride every 2 weeks during wet season. Remove and burn infected vines. Yam mosaic virus (YMV) causes mottling, curling, and stunting. It is spread by aphids. .Trim()

Yam rot (caused by fungi like Fusarium and Pythium) leads to soft, brown tubers in storage. Prevent by curing tubers in a dry, ventilated place for 1-2 weeks after harvest, storing in well-drained barns, and dusting with wood ash or fungicide (e.g., benomyl). Bacterial wilt (Ralstonia solanacearum) causes sudden wilting and brown vascular tissue. Control by rotating with maize or sorghum, avoiding waterlogged soils, and removing infected plants. Leaf spot (Cercospora spp.) causes brown spots with yellow halos; use mancozeb or chlorothalonil. For all diseases, sanitation is key: remove crop debris, disinfect tools, and avoid overhead irrigation. Plant on ridges to improve drainage. Scout fields weekly and act at first sign..Trim()

Biological control uses natural enemiesâpredators, parasites, and pathogensâto manage pests and diseases. For yam, neem (Azadirachta indica) is a powerful biopesticide. Trichoderma harzianum, a fungus, can be mixed with soil to control root rot and nematodes. Apply 5-10 g per mound at planting. Beauveria bassiana controls beetles and termites; mix with soil or spray. These biologicals are safe for humans and beneficial insects, and they leave no harmful residues..Trim()

.Trim()

Chemical control should be used as a last resort, following integrated pest management (IPM) principles. For insects: chlorpyrifos (2-3 kg a.i./ha) for yam beetle; imidacloprid (0.5 L/ha) for aphids; lambda-cyhalothrin (0.5 L/ha) for grasshoppers. For diseases: mancozeb (2-3 kg/ha) for anthracnose and leaf spot; copper oxychloride (3 kg/ha) for bacterial diseases; benomyl (1 kg/ha) for storage rot. Always wear gloves, mask, and boots; spray early morning or evening; observe pre-harvest intervals (e.g., 14 days for mancozeb). Rotate chemicals to avoid resistance. Store pesticides in original containers, away from food and children. Never mix chemicals unless instructed. Remember: chemicals are toxicâuse only when necessary and according to label..Trim()

Sanitation is the cheapest and most effective way to prevent pests and diseases in yam. Start with clean planting material: select healthy setts from disease-free tubers, and treat them with wood ash or a fungicide (e.g., benomyl) before planting. Remove and burn crop residues after harvestâdo not compost diseased vines. Clean tools (hoes, cutlasses) with 10% bleach solution or fire between fields to avoid spreading pathogens. Control weeds because they harbour pests and diseases. Practice crop rotation: do not plant yam on the same land for more than 2 years. Rotate with maize, sorghum, or cowpea, which are not hosts to major yam pests. Plant on ridges or mounds to improve drainage and prevent waterlogging, which encourages rot..Trim()

.Trim()

Use certified disease-free seeds and resistant varieties where available. Space plants properly (1m x 1m) to allow air circulation and reduce humidity. Avoid overhead irrigation; use drip or furrow irrigation instead. Scout fields weekly for early signs of pests and diseases. Remove and destroy infected plants immediately (rogueing). Keep field borders clear of bush and tall grasses that harbour rodents and insects. Store tubers in well-ventilated, dry barns; cure them for 1-2 weeks before storage. Dust stored tubers with wood ash or actellic dust to prevent insects. Maintain farm hygiene: no standing water, no piles of rubbish. Train workers on sanitation. These practices reduce pesticide use, lower costs, and produce healthier yams..Trim()

Importance of Trellising for Yam Vines

Yam vines are natural climbers. In the wild, they wrap around trees and bushes to reach sunlight. When you farm yams, you must provide something for them to climb. This is called trellising. Without a trellis, vines crawl on the ground, and you lose a lot of your harvest..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does trellising matter so much? First, sunlight. Yam leaves need full sun to make food for the tubers. When vines stay on the ground, leaves overlap and shade each other. Studies in Nigeria show that trellised yams yield 30 to 50 percent more than untrellised ones. That is a big difference for your pocket..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, disease control. Vines on the ground stay wet after rain or dew. This encourages fungal diseases like anthracnose and leaf spot. Trellised vines dry faster and get more air movement. You will spend less money on fungicides..Trim()

.Trim()

Third, easier harvesting. When vines are up on a trellis, you can see the base of the plant clearly. You know exactly where to dig. You avoid cutting tubers with your machete or hoe. Clean tubers sell for a higher price at market..Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, weed control. A trellis keeps vines off the ground, so you can weed around the yam mounds without disturbing the vines. Weeds compete for water and nutrients. Less weeds means bigger tubers..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, trellising saves space. With vines growing upward, you can plant yams closer together. Some farmers in Ghana plant 10,000 yam sets per hectare with trellises, compared to 6,000 without. More plants per hectare means more food and more income..Trim()

.Trim()

So trellising is not optional if you want a good harvest. It is a basic investment. You will spend time and money on stakes or strings, but the returns are worth it. In the next lessons, you will learn which trellis system to choose and how to build it properly..Trim()

Types of Trellis Systems (Stakes, Strings, Living Supports)

There are three main trellis systems for yams in Africa: stakes, strings, and living supports. Each has advantages and disadvantages. Your choice depends on your budget, land, and labor..Trim()

.Trim()

Stakes are the oldest and most common method. You cut wooden poles about 2 to 3 meters long and drive them into the ground beside each yam mound. The vine climbs the stake naturally. Good wood comes from trees like Gliricidia, Leucaena, or bamboo. A stake can last 2 to 3 seasons if treated. You need about 10,000 stakes per hectare. The problem? Cutting stakes destroys trees, and replacing them is costly..Trim()

.Trim()

Strings are a modern alternative. You set up a strong frame using tall posts (2.5 to 3 meters) at the corners and along the rows. Then you run wire or strong nylon string between the posts, creating a grid. From this grid, you drop thin strings down to each yam mound. The vine climbs the string. String trellises cost more to set up initially, but they last 5 to 10 years. They also allow more air and sunlight. Many commercial farmers in Nigeria and Ghana now use string trellises..Trim()

.Trim()

Living supports are trees or shrubs planted among the yams. Examples include pigeon pea, moringa, and Gliricidia sepium. These trees grow tall and provide natural climbing frames. They also fix nitrogen in the soil and provide fodder for animals. The downside is competition for water and nutrients. You must prune the trees regularly so they don't shade the yams. Living supports work well in agroforestry systems..Trim()

.Trim()

Which is best? For a smallholder with limited cash, stakes are fine. For a farmer planning to grow yams for many years, invest in a string trellis. For a farmer who wants extra benefits like fodder and soil improvement, try living supports. You can also mix systems. For example, use living supports around the edges and stakes inside. The key is to get the vines off the ground. Any of these three systems will do that if built correctly..Trim()

Building a good trellis takes planning. You need the right materials, correct spacing, and proper installation. Let us go through the steps for a stake trellis and a string trellis..Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Yam vines do not always climb on their own. You must train them. Training means guiding the vine to the trellis. Pruning means cutting off unwanted parts. Both are simple but important..Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Regular training and pruning will give you a healthy, open canopy. This means more sunlight, better air movement, and bigger tubers. It takes time, but it is one of the best things you can do for your yam crop..Trim()

Managing Vine Growth for Maximum Yield

Once your yams are climbing, your job is to manage the vines so they produce the biggest tubers possible. This means controlling leaf area, water, and nutrients..Trim()

.Trim()

The key idea is balance. You want enough leaves to make food through photosynthesis, but not so many that they shade each other or waste energy. A good yam canopy should cover about 70 to 80 percent of the trellis. If it is too thin, the plant is not making enough food. If it is too thick, the lower leaves die from lack of light..Trim()

.Trim()

To manage growth, start by removing excess vines. Each yam mound should have only one main vine. If two or three vines grow from the same set, remove the weaker ones when they are 15 to 20 cm long. This forces the plant to put energy into one strong vine..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, control the height. Yam vines can grow 10 meters or more if left alone. But you only have a 2.5 to 3 meter trellis. When the vine reaches the top, let it grow along the top wire or stake for another 50 cm, then pinch off the tip. This creates a bushy top that catches sunlight without becoming a tangled mess..Trim()

.Trim()

Water management is critical. Yams need consistent moisture, especially during tuber bulking (months 4 to 6). In dry areas, irrigate once a week with 20 to 30 liters per plant. .Trim()

.Trim()

Pest and disease watch: Check vines weekly for aphids, beetles, and anthracnose. Remove infected leaves immediately. If you see many insects, spray with neem oil (5 ml per liter of water) in the evening..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, keep records. Note when you train, prune, fertilize, and harvest. This helps you improve next season. With good vine management, you can harvest 20 to 30 tonnes per hectare instead of 10..Trim()
