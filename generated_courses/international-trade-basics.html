Global trade has ancient roots. The Silk Road connected China to Europe for over 1,500 years, moving silk, spices, and ideas. In Africa, the trans-Saharan trade routes linked West Africa to North Africa and the Mediterranean, with gold, salt, and slaves. Module 2 · Theoretical Foundations of Trade

Absolute Advantage Theory

Absolute advantage is the simplest trade theory. It says a country should export goods it can produce more efficiently than other countries, and import goods others produce more efficiently. Efficiency means using fewer resourcesâlike labor hours or capitalâto make one unit. Adam Smith introduced this in 1776. For example, if Ghana can produce 1 ton of cocoa with 10 labor hours, while Brazil needs 20 hours, Ghana has an absolute advantage in cocoa. If Brazil can produce 1 ton of coffee with 15 hours, while Ghana needs 30 hours, Brazil has an absolute advantage in coffee. Trade benefits both: Ghana sells cocoa to Brazil, Brazil sells coffee to Ghana. Each gets more of both goods than if they tried to produce everything themselves. In Africa, absolute advantage often shows up in raw materials. Kenya has ideal conditions for teaâit can produce high-quality tea at lower cost than most countries. Nigeria has abundant oil reserves, giving it an absolute advantage in crude oil extraction. But absolute advantage is limited: it only explains trade when one country is better at everything. What if a country has no absolute advantage in anything? That's where comparative advantage comes in. Still, absolute advantage is a useful starting point. To apply it, ask: What can my business produce using fewer resources than competitors? For a small African exporter, this might mean unique local skills, cheaper labor, or access to raw materials. For instance, a Moroccan leather workshop might have an absolute advantage in handmade bags because of traditional craftsmanship and low labor costs. Use this theory to identify your strongest export product, then focus on scaling it. But don't stop thereâabsolute advantage alone rarely sustains a business. You must also consider quality, branding, and market access. In practice, many African countries export raw materials where they have absolute advantage, but import manufactured goods. That pattern can limit growth. So while absolute advantage helps you spot opportunities, it's just the first step in building a trade strategy..Trim()

Comparative Advantage Theory

Comparative advantage is the most important idea in trade theory. It says even if one country is better at producing everything, both countries still gain by specializing in what they do relatively best. The key is opportunity cost: what you give up to produce one more unit. David Ricardo explained this in 1817. Suppose South Africa can produce 1 ton of wine with 10 hours, and 1 ton of wool with 5 hours. Lesotho can produce 1 ton of wine with 30 hours, and 1 ton of wool with 15 hours. South Africa has an absolute advantage in both. But opportunity cost: South Africa gives up 2 tons of wool to make 1 ton of wine (10/5). Lesotho gives up 2 tons of wool to make 1 ton of wine (30/15). Wait, that's the same? Let me recalculate. Actually, if South Africa uses 10 hours for wine, it could have made 2 tons of wool (10/5). Lesotho using 30 hours for wine could have made 2 tons of wool (30/15). So opportunity costs are equal? That would mean no comparative advantage. Let me pick better numbers. Suppose South Africa: 1 wine = 10 hours, 1 wool = 5 hours. Lesotho: 1 wine = 30 hours, 1 wool = 10 hours. Then South Africa's opportunity cost of wine = 2 wool; Lesotho's = 3 wool. So South Africa has comparative advantage in wine (lower opportunity cost). Lesotho's opportunity cost of wool = 1/3 wine; South Africa's = 1/2 wine. So Lesotho has comparative advantage in wool. Both gain by specializing: South Africa makes wine, Lesotho makes wool, then trade. For Africa, this means don't try to make everything. Focus on what you sacrifice least to produce. Example: Kenya has a comparative advantage in cut flowers because its climate allows year-round growing, and the land could otherwise be used for less profitable crops. Ethiopia has a comparative advantage in coffee and leather. A small business: if you can produce shea butter at low opportunity cost (using local women's cooperative labor that has few alternative jobs), you have a comparative advantage. Use it to export to cosmetic companies. But remember: comparative advantage is dynamic. It can change with technology, infrastructure, and education. So invest in improving your skills and efficiency. Also, trade barriers and transport costs can erase gains. So check shipping costs from your country to target markets. Finally, comparative advantage doesn't mean you should accept low-value products forever. You can upgrade by moving into processing and brandingâlike turning cocoa beans into chocolate..Trim()

Heckscher-Ohlin Model

The Heckscher-Ohlin (H-O) model explains trade based on a country's factors of production: land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship. It says countries export goods that use their abundant factors intensively, and import goods that use their scarce factors intensively. For example, a country with lots of low-skilled labor will export labor-intensive goods like textiles, while a country with lots of capital will export capital-intensive goods like machinery. In Africa, many countries have abundant land and low-skilled labor. So they export agricultural products (cocoa, coffee, cotton) and simple manufactured goods (garments, shoes). They import machinery, vehicles, and electronics, which require capital and skilled labor. This pattern fits H-O. But H-O also predicts that trade will equalize factor prices across countries. That hasn't happened; wages in Africa remain lower than in Europe. Why? Because of barriers: poor infrastructure, tariffs, and unequal power. Still, H-O gives practical guidance. Ask: What factors does my country have in abundance? If labor is cheap and abundant, start a labor-intensive business like garment sewing or fruit processing. If land is abundant, consider agro-export. For instance, Tanzania has abundant land and labor, so it exports cashew nuts and tobacco. But to move up, you need to accumulate capital and skills. That's why many African governments offer incentives for processing instead of raw export. A small entrepreneur can use H-O by choosing a product that uses local abundant factors. If you're in a region with many unemployed youth, train them for assembly work. If you have access to cheap land, grow crops for export. But also consider factor intensity: don't invest in heavy machinery if capital is scarce and expensive. Instead, use labor-intensive methods. Example: a Kenyan flower farm uses many workers for picking and packing, which fits H-O because labor is abundant. Finally, H-O is not static. You can create new factors: build a skilled workforce through training, or improve infrastructure to attract capital. So use H-O to identify your current advantage, then invest in upgrading your factors. That's how Mauritius moved from sugar to textiles to financial services..Trim()

New Trade Theory

New Trade Theory (NTT) emerged in the 1980s with economists like Paul Krugman. It explains trade when countries are similar and products are differentiated. Unlike classical theories, NTT says trade can happen even without differences in technology or factors. Why? Because of economies of scale and network effects. Economies of scale mean the more you produce, the cheaper each unit becomes. So a country can specialize in a few products and export them, while importing other varieties. For example, Germany and France both make cars, but Germany specializes in luxury cars and France in compact cars. They trade with each other. In Africa, NTT applies to industries with increasing returns: mobile money, music, fashion. Consider Nigeria's film industry, Nollywood. It produces many movies for local and diaspora markets. Because of scale, it can compete with Hollywood in African markets. Another example: Kenya's M-Pesa. It started as a local mobile money service, but now it's exported to other African countries. NTT also highlights first-mover advantage. If a firm enters a market early, it can build brand loyalty and lock in customers. For a small African business, NTT suggests: find a niche where you can scale. Don't try to compete on price alone with mass-produced goods from China. Instead, differentiateâuse unique African designs, organic ingredients, or cultural stories. Then use digital platforms to reach global niches. For instance, a Ghanaian shea butter brand can sell to natural cosmetic consumers in the US via Amazon. Scale matters: the more you sell, the lower your average cost, so you can offer competitive prices. But you need upfront investment. Consider partnering with others in a cooperative to achieve scale. Also, NTT explains why trade is dominated by a few large firms. To survive, African businesses must innovate and build networks. The theory also justifies government support for infant industriesâlike protecting local car assembly until it reaches scale. But be careful: protection can lead to inefficiency. Use NTT to think strategically: can you achieve enough scale to compete? If not, focus on a niche where scale is less important, like high-end crafts. Overall, NTT is practical for African entrepreneurs because it emphasizes branding, innovation, and digital reach..Trim()

National Competitive Advantage: Porter's Diamond

Michael Porter's Diamond explains why some countries are more competitive in certain industries. It has four parts: factor conditions, demand conditions, related and supporting industries, and firm strategy, structure, and rivalry. Factor conditions include skilled labor, infrastructure, and natural resources. But Porter argues that advanced factors (like research institutes) matter more than basic ones. Demand conditions: if local customers are demanding, firms innovate. Related industries: a strong supplier network helps. Firm strategy and rivalry: intense local competition forces firms to improve. For Africa, apply the Diamond to find your competitive edge. Example: Kenya's cut flower industry. Factor conditions: good climate, airport infrastructure. Demand: European buyers demand high quality, forcing Kenyan farms to meet standards. Related industries: packaging, cold storage, and transport companies. Rivalry: many Kenyan flower farms compete, driving efficiency. So Kenya became a world leader. Another example: South Africa's wine industry. Factor conditions: Mediterranean climate, skilled winemakers. Demand: local wine culture and export demand. Related industries: tourism, restaurants. Rivalry: many estates compete. For a small business, use the Diamond to assess your industry. Do you have access to skilled workers? Can you get quality inputs locally? Is there local competition that pushes you? If not, you may need to build those conditions. Government can help: invest in training, improve roads, and set quality standards. But Porter warns that government should facilitate, not control. For African entrepreneurs, the Diamond suggests: cluster with other businesses. If you're in a region with many similar producers, you can share suppliers and knowledge. Example: the Ethiopian leather cluster in Addis Ababa. Also, target demanding export marketsâthey force you to upgrade. Finally, rivalry at home is good; it prepares you for global competition. So don't fear competitors. Instead, learn from them and differentiate. The Diamond is a tool for strategy: identify your strengths and fix weaknesses. Then you can build a national competitive advantage that lasts..Trim()

Critiques of Classical Trade Theories

Classical trade theoriesâabsolute advantage, comparative advantage, Heckscher-Ohlinâhave been criticized for ignoring real-world complexities. First, they assume perfect competition, but many markets are dominated by large corporations. Second, they assume factors of production are mobile within a country but not across borders. In reality, capital moves freely, and labor migrates. Third, they ignore income distribution: trade can benefit a country overall but hurt specific groups, like workers in import-competing industries. For Africa, these critiques matter. Many African countries followed comparative advantage and specialized in raw materials, but they remained poor. Why? Because prices of raw materials fluctuate, and gains from trade often go to foreign buyers and local elites. Also, colonial history shaped trade patterns: Africa exported cheap resources and imported expensive manufactures. This is called unequal exchange. Another critique: classical theories ignore environmental costs. Exporting timber or minerals can degrade land and water. Also, they assume full employment, but many African economies have underemployment. So shifting workers to export sectors may not increase total output. New trade theory and Porter's Diamond address some critiques by including scale, innovation, and clusters. But even they have limits. For a small business, the lesson is: don't blindly follow classical advice. Instead, analyze your value chain. Are you capturing enough value? If you export raw cocoa, you get a small share; if you process it into chocolate, you capture more. So consider upgrading. Also, protect against price swings: diversify your export markets and products. Use contracts and cooperatives to negotiate better prices. And advocate for fair trade policies. Finally, classical theories often ignore power dynamics. African countries have less bargaining power in global trade negotiations. So join trade associations and regional bodies like the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) to increase leverage. In summary, classical theories are useful starting points, but they are not gospel. Use them with a critical eye. Combine them with modern strategies: branding, digital marketing, and value addition. That's how you build a resilient export business in Africa..Trim()

Tariffs: Types and Effects

A tariff is simply a tax on imported goods. In Africa, tariffs are a major source of government revenue and a tool to protect local industries. There are two main types: ad valorem tariffs, which are a percentage of the value of the goods (e.g., 20% of the price), and specific tariffs, which are a fixed amount per unit (e.g., $5 per kilogram). A third type, the compound tariff, combines both. For example, Nigeria charges a 20% ad valorem tariff on imported rice plus a 10% levy, making imported rice more expensive than local rice..Trim()

.Trim()

Tariffs affect prices, quantity, and who wins or loses. When a tariff is imposed, the domestic price rises to the world price plus the tariff. This encourages local production but also raises costs for consumers and businesses that use imported inputs. For instance, a 25% tariff on imported steel in Kenya would help local steel producers but hurt construction companies that rely on cheap steel. The government collects revenue, but consumers pay more. In many African countries, tariffs account for 10-20% of total tax revenue..Trim()

.Trim()

To implement tariffs, you need to know the Harmonized System (HS) code for your product. This is a six-digit code used worldwide to classify goods. For example, HS 1006 is rice. You can find your country's tariff schedule on the website of the customs authority, like the Kenya Revenue Authority or the Nigerian Customs Service. The tariff rate depends on the HS code and the country of origin. Under the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), tariffs on 90% of goods will be eliminated over time, but currently many tariffs still apply..Trim()

.Trim()

As a trader, you must calculate the landed cost: the world price plus freight, insurance, and tariffs. If you import a machine worth $1,000 with a 15% tariff, you pay $150 in tariff, making the landed cost $1,150. This affects your selling price and competitiveness. Always check if your country has a preferential trade agreement that reduces or eliminates the tariff. For example, under the EU's Everything But Arms scheme, most African exports enter Europe duty-free. Understanding tariffs helps you price your goods and choose suppliers..Trim()

Non-Tariff Barriers: Quotas and Standards

Non-tariff barriers (NTBs) are trade restrictions that are not taxes. Two common types are quotas and standards. A quota is a physical limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported. For example, a country might allow only 10,000 tons of sugar to be imported per year. Once the quota is filled, no more can enter, or a higher tariff applies. Quotas raise prices because they restrict supply. In Africa, quotas are often used for sensitive products like sugar, poultry, and rice. For instance, South Africa has a quota on imported poultry to protect local farmers..Trim()

.Trim()

Standards are regulations that products must meet to be sold. They can be technical standards (e.g., electrical safety), sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) measures for food safety, or labeling requirements. For example, the EU requires that imported peanuts have aflatoxin levels below 4 parts per billion. Many African exporters struggle to meet such strict standards, effectively blocking their exports. Similarly, Kenya requires that imported maize be tested for genetically modified organisms (GMOs). These standards can be legitimate health measures, but they can also be used as protectionist tools..Trim()

.Trim()

To overcome NTBs, you need to understand the specific requirements of your target market. For SPS measures, you must ensure your product meets the importing country's food safety rules. This may involve laboratory testing, certification, and traceability. For example, to export fish to the EU, you need a health certificate from your national competent authority and your processing plant must be approved by the EU. For standards, you may need to obtain certification like ISO 9001 or HACCP. These can be costly but are necessary for market access..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, the AfCFTA aims to reduce NTBs by harmonizing standards and simplifying procedures. The African Organization for Standardization (ARSO) is working on common standards. As a trader, you can check the AfCFTA's Non-Tariff Barriers online reporting mechanism to report barriers you face. Also, consider working with trade promotion organizations like the International Trade Centre (ITC) for help. Always factor in the cost of compliance when planning your export. For instance, testing a shipment of peanuts for aflatoxin can cost $200-$500, which may be prohibitive for small volumes. Pooling resources with other exporters or using a shared testing facility can reduce costs..Trim()

Export Subsidies and Incentives

Export subsidies are government payments to domestic producers to help them sell abroad. They lower the cost of exports, making them cheaper in foreign markets. For example, a government might pay a cotton farmer $0.10 per kilogram exported. This allows the farmer to sell at a lower price than competitors. However, under World Trade Organization (WTO) rules, most export subsidies are prohibited because they distort trade. But many countries still use other incentives, such as tax breaks, preferential loans, and export credit guarantees..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, export incentives are common. For instance, Egypt offers a cash subsidy to exporters of certain goods, reimbursing a percentage of the value exported. In Ethiopia, exporters of leather and leather products get a 10% subsidy. Kenya has an Export Promotion Zone (EPZ) where companies enjoy tax holidays and duty-free imports if they export at least 80% of their production. These incentives can make your product more competitive, but they often come with conditions, like using local raw materials or meeting export targets..Trim()

.Trim()

To access these incentives, you must register with the relevant authority. For example, in Kenya, you register with the Export Processing Zones Authority (EPZA). In Nigeria, the Nigerian Export Promotion Council (NEPC) administers the Export Expansion Grant (EEG). You need to provide documentation: export invoices, customs declarations, and proof of repatriation of foreign exchange. The process can be bureaucratic, so hire a customs broker or consultant if needed. Also, note that subsidies can be challenged by trading partners. If another country believes your subsidy is unfair, they can impose countervailing duties (a tax on your exports) to offset the subsidy..Trim()

.Trim()

As a small exporter, focus on incentives that are easy to access, like duty drawback schemes. Duty drawback refunds the import duties you paid on inputs used to produce export goods. For example, if you import fabric to make shirts for export, you can get back the import duty on the fabric. This is allowed under WTO rules. In many African countries, you can apply for duty drawback through the customs authority. Keep detailed records of imports and exports. Also, look for export credit guarantees from institutions like the African Export-Import Bank (Afreximbank) to reduce risk. These incentives can improve your margins, but always calculate the net benefit after compliance costs..Trim()

Import Licensing and Customs Procedures

Import licensing is a requirement that importers obtain a permit before bringing goods into a country. It is often used to control imports of sensitive goods, like pharmaceuticals, weapons, or agricultural products. For example, in Tanzania, you need an import license for sugar, and in Ghana, for rice. The license specifies the quantity, value, and sometimes the source. The process can be complex: you apply to the Ministry of Trade, provide invoices, certificates, and pay fees. Delays can take weeks, which disrupts supply chains..Trim()

.Trim()

Customs procedures are the steps your goods go through at the border. These include declaration, valuation, inspection, and payment of duties. Inefficient customs can be a major non-tariff barrier. For instance, a truck crossing from Kenya to Uganda might wait days at the border due to paperwork. To speed up, many African countries have implemented electronic systems like the Kenya Trade Network Agency (KenTrade) and the Ghana Community Network (GCNet). These allow you to submit documents online and pay duties electronically. The World Customs Organization's Harmonized System is used for classification, and the WTO Trade Facilitation Agreement (TFA) aims to simplify procedures..Trim()

.Trim()

To navigate import licensing and customs, first determine if your product requires a license. Check with your country's trade ministry or customs website. If yes, apply early and ensure all documents are correct. Common documents include a bill of lading, commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, and import declaration. For customs valuation, the WTO Valuation Agreement sets rules: generally, the transaction value (the price you paid) is used. But customs may challenge if they suspect under-invoicing. Keep records of your payments..Trim()

.Trim()

Use a licensed customs broker to handle clearance. They know the procedures and can avoid delays. For example, in Nigeria, you must use a licensed agent to clear goods. For small traders, the Simplified Trade Regime (STR) under COMESA allows low-value consignments (up to $2,000) to be cleared with a simple form. This is useful for cross-border traders. Always factor in clearance time and costs when pricing your imports..Trim()

Voluntary Export Restraints

A Voluntary Export Restraint (VER) is an arrangement where an exporting country agrees to limit its exports to a particular country. It is 'voluntary' because the exporting country imposes the limit itself, but often it is done under pressure from the importing country to avoid harsher trade barriers. For example, in the 1980s, Japan agreed to limit its car exports to the US. In Africa, VERs are less common but can occur. For instance, in 2018, the US threatened tariffs on African apparel exports, and some countries considered voluntary limits to avoid them. VERs are technically prohibited by the WTO, but they still exist in informal forms..Trim()

.Trim()

VERs raise prices in the importing country because supply is restricted. They also create economic inefficiency: the exporting country loses revenue, and the importing country's consumers pay more. However, for the exporting country, a VER can provide a short-term benefit: if the quota is set above current exports, it may not restrict trade, but it creates uncertainty. For example, if Kenya voluntarily limits its flower exports to the EU, it might avoid a tariff, but it also caps its growth. VERs are often used in agriculture and textiles, where domestic industries are politically powerful..Trim()

.Trim()

As a trader, you are unlikely to negotiate a VER directly, but you may be affected by one. If your country has a VER with a major market, you may need to obtain an export license to ship under the quota. For example, if there is a VER on sugar to the EU, the government allocates quota rights to exporters. You would apply to the ministry of trade for a share. The allocation might be based on historical exports or first-come, first-served. You must monitor announcements from your trade ministry..Trim()

.Trim()

To mitigate the impact of VERs, diversify your markets. If one market is restricted, look for others. Under the AfCFTA, intra-African trade is growing, so consider selling to neighboring countries. Also, VERs often lead to trade diversion: countries that are not subject to the VER increase their exports. You could set up production in a third country to bypass the restriction, but this is complex. For small businesses, focus on products not subject to VERs. Check the WTO's Trade Monitoring Database for any VERs involving your country. If you are hit by a VER, consider lobbying through your industry association to negotiate a better deal or to have the VER removed..Trim()

Trade Remedies: Anti-Dumping and Countervailing Duties

Trade remedies are actions a country takes against unfair trade practices. The two main types are anti-dumping duties and countervailing duties. Dumping occurs when a company exports a product at a price lower than its domestic price or below cost. For example, if a Chinese company sells solar panels in Kenya for $100 each, but sells them in China for $150, that is dumping. The Kenyan government can impose an anti-dumping duty to raise the import price to fair levels. Countervailing duties (CVDs) are used when a foreign government subsidizes its exports, giving them an unfair advantage. For instance, if Egypt subsidizes its cotton exports, a importing country can impose a CVD to offset the subsidy..Trim()

.Trim()

To impose these duties, the importing country must conduct an investigation. The process starts with a complaint from a domestic industry. For example, the South African poultry industry complained about dumped chicken from Brazil, leading to anti-dumping duties. The investigating authority (like the International Trade Administration Commission in South Africa) collects evidence, calculates the dumping margin, and determines if the domestic industry is injured. If yes, duties are imposed, usually for five years. In Africa, countries like Egypt, South Africa, and Morocco frequently use anti-dumping measures..Trim()

.Trim()

As an exporter, you must be aware of these risks. If you are accused of dumping, you will receive a questionnaire from the investigating authority. You must respond with detailed cost and pricing data. If you don't, the authority may use the 'facts available' (often the complainant's data) and impose high duties. To avoid this, keep accurate records of your domestic sales and costs. Also, ensure your export price is not below your cost. If you receive government subsidies, be transparent; otherwise, you may face CVDs. For example, Vietnamese exporters of shoes faced anti-dumping duties in the EU because they couldn't prove they were not dumping..Trim()

.Trim()

If you are a domestic producer facing dumped imports, you can file a complaint with your trade ministry. You need to show that imports are dumped and causing injury. Collect data on import prices, your sales, and profits. The process is lengthy and costly, but it can protect your business. In Africa, the AfCFTA has a dispute settlement mechanism, but trade remedies are still handled nationally. The WTO's Anti-Dumping Agreement sets rules. For small businesses, it's often better to compete by improving quality or finding niche markets rather than relying on trade remedies. But if you are severely affected, consider joining an industry association that can file a case on your behalf..Trim()

GATT and the WTO: History and Functions

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) started in 1948 with 23 countries. Its goal was to reduce tariffs and expand trade. Over decades, GATT rounds cut average tariffs from over 40% to under 5% by the 1990s. In 1995, GATT became the World Trade Organization (WTO), now with 164 members. For African exporters, the WTO provides a rules-based system. For example, if Kenya exports coffee to the EU, it benefits from Most-Favored-Nation (MFN) tariffs that apply to all WTO members. The WTO also has a dispute settlement system. If a country unfairly blocks your goods, your government can file a case. The WTO has handled over 600 disputes. However, African countries have filed fewer than 30 cases, often due to lack of legal capacity..Trim()

.Trim()

The WTO's main functions include: administering trade agreements, acting as a forum for negotiations, handling disputes, and reviewing trade policies. For a small African business, the WTO matters because it sets the rules that your government must follow. For instance, if you export textiles to the US under AGOA (African Growth and Opportunity Act), that's a unilateral preference, but the WTO ensures such preferences don't discriminate among developing countries. The WTO also provides technical assistance to African countries to help them negotiate better deals..Trim()

.Trim()

To use the WTO system, start by understanding your country's commitments. Every WTO member has a 'schedule of concessions' listing tariff caps. You can find these on the WTO website. If you face a trade barrier, document it and contact your trade ministry. They can raise it at the WTO. Also, the Enhanced Integrated Framework (EIF) helps least-developed countries (LDCs) like many in Africa to build trade capacity. For example, EIF funded projects in Rwanda to improve cross-border trade. So, the WTO is not just for diplomats; it affects your ability to export and import..Trim()

WTO Agreements and Dispute Settlement

The WTO has several key agreements that affect African traders. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) covers goods. The General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS) covers services. The Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS) sets minimum IP standards. The Agreement on Agriculture (AoA) reduces subsidies and tariffs. For African farmers, the AoA matters: many African countries have low tariffs, but developed countries still subsidize their farmers heavily. For example, US cotton subsidies have hurt West African cotton producers. In 2004, Benin, Burkina Faso, Chad, and Mali filed a WTO dispute against US cotton subsidies. The WTO ruled that some subsidies were inconsistent with AoA. Although the US didn't fully comply, the case showed that African countries can use the system..Trim()

.Trim()

Dispute settlement is a core WTO function. The process: consultation (60 days), panel (6-9 months), appeals (3 months), and implementation. If a country loses, it must change its practices or face retaliation. For an African exporter, if your goods are blocked unfairly, your government can file a complaint. But it's costly. Legal fees can exceed $500,000. The Advisory Centre on WTO Law (ACWL) provides subsidized legal help to developing countries. Many African countries are members. For example, ACWL helped Kenya in a dispute with the EU over bananas..Trim()

.Trim()

To use dispute settlement, gather evidence: contracts, customs documents, correspondence. Then approach your trade ministry. They will decide whether to file. The system works, but it takes time. Meanwhile, you can use regional courts like the COMESA Court of Justice for faster resolution. So, know your rights and use the tools available..Trim()

Regional Trade Agreements: FTAs and Customs Unions

Steps to Start Exporting

Starting to export from Africa might seem complex, but breaking it into steps makes it manageable. First, identify a product you can supply consistently. In Kenya, register with the Kenya Revenue Authority and get an export permit. After that, find buyers. Many African export promotion agencies offer training and subsidies. For example, the Ghana Export Promotion Authority provides export readiness programs. Remember, exporting requires patience and attention to detail, but it opens doors to global markets..Trim()

Importing into Africa can be a great way to access goods not available locally or at lower prices. Start by identifying what you want to import. It could be electronics, machinery, or raw materials. Customs Clearance Process

Customs clearance is the process of getting your goods through customs control. It involves declaring goods, paying duties and taxes, and obtaining release. In most African countries, this is done electronically. For example, in Kenya, you use the Kenya Trade Network Agency (KenTrade) system; in Nigeria, the Nigeria Customs Service uses the NICIS II. The process starts before goods arrive. You or your customs broker submit a customs declaration. This includes details like HS codes, value, quantity, and origin. HS codes determine duty rates. For instance, the HS code for raw shea butter is 1515.90. You then pay assessed duties. In many countries, you can pay online. After payment, customs may inspect the goods. Inspections can be physical or documentary. If everything is in order, customs releases the goods. You then arrange transport. Delays often happen due to missing documents, undervaluation, or incorrect HS codes. To speed up, ensure all documents are accurate and complete. Incoterms 2020: Rules for Delivery

Incoterms 2020 are standard trade terms published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC). They define who is responsible for costs, risks, and tasks like transport, insurance, and customs clearance. There are 11 terms, but for African traders, the most common are FOB, CIF, EXW, and DDP. EXW (Ex Works) means the buyer picks up goods at the seller's premises. The buyer bears all costs and risks. This is simple for the seller but risky for the buyer. FOB (Free On Board) means the seller delivers goods on board the vessel at the port of shipment. The buyer pays for sea freight and insurance. This is common for African exports. CIF (Cost, Insurance, and Freight) means the seller pays for freight and insurance to the destination port. The buyer handles import clearance. This is common for imports into Africa. DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) means the seller delivers goods to the buyer's location, paying all duties and taxes. This is convenient for the buyer but risky for the seller. Other terms include FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU. When negotiating, choose terms that match your capacity. If you're new to exporting, FOB is safer because you don't have to arrange international transport. If you're importing, CIF might be easier. Always specify the Incoterm and the named place, e.g., FOB Mombasa. Incoterms do not cover payment terms or ownership transfer. Use them with a sales contract. Many African trade promotion agencies offer training on Incoterms. Understanding them helps avoid disputes and unexpected costs..Trim()

Freight forwarders and customs brokers are essential partners in international trade. A freight forwarder arranges the transport of goods from origin to destination. Letters of Credit: Types and Uses

A Letter of Credit (L/C) is a bank's promise to pay the seller once the seller presents compliant documents. Trade in Services and Intellectual Property

Trade in services is often overlooked, but it's huge. Services include consulting, IT, tourism, and finance. In Africa, services are 50% of GDP. You can export services without shipping anything. Specify payment terms, deliverables, and IP ownership. Use tools like DocuSign for e-signatures. Also, be aware of digital services taxes: some countries tax foreign digital companies.
