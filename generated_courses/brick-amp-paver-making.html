Choosing a Production Site Near Clay and Water Sources

Your production site must be close to clay and water. Clay is heavy and costly to transport. Ideally, the site should be within 1 kilometre of your clay pit. Basic Tools and Equipment You Need to Begin

You don't need expensive machines to start. Begin with hand tools. You'll need a hoe and a shovel for digging clay, a pickaxe for hard soil, and a wheelbarrow for transporting clay and water. A metal mould for bricks or pavers is essential. A simple brick mould (for 9x4x3 inch bricks) costs about 30,000 UGX. A paver mould (for 20x10x6 cm pavers) costs 50,000 UGX. Setting Your First Production Targets and Budget

Set realistic targets. For a beginner, aim to produce 500 bricks per day with 2 workers. That's 10,000 bricks per month if you work 20 days. For pavers, 200 per day is good. These numbers assume you have clay and water nearby and you work 6 hours a day. Don't overpromise. Start with 300 bricks daily and increase as you get faster..Trim()

Module 2 · Understanding Soil and Clay for Quality Bricks

Identifying Good Clay Soil by Feel, Colour, and Smell

To find good clay for brick making, start by looking at the soil around you. Good clay soil is usually reddish-brown, grey, or yellowish. Avoid soil that is very dark black or has a lot of white salt patches. Pick up a handful of moist soil and rub it between your fingers. Good clay feels smooth and sticky, not gritty like sand. When you squeeze it, it should hold together in a ball without crumbling. If it feels like plasticine, that's a good sign. Smell the soil after digging a small hole. Fresh clay smells earthy and clean, like wet bricks. If it smells rotten or like sewage, it has too much organic matter and will not make strong bricks..Trim()

.Trim()

Another simple test is the ribbon test. Take a ball of moist soil and squeeze it between your thumb and forefinger, pushing out a ribbon. If the ribbon stays intact for more than 5 cm (2 inches) before breaking, the soil has good clay content. If it breaks quickly, there is too much sand. Also, rub a wet sample on your palm. If it leaves a shiny, sticky film, it's clay. If it leaves a gritty residue, it's sandy..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, termite mounds are often a sign of good clay. Termites bring clay from deep underground. You can dig into a termite mound and test the soil. Also, look for areas where water stands after rain. Clay holds water, so these spots often have good clay. But be careful: if the area floods too much, the clay may be too heavy and need sand..Trim()

.Trim()

Avoid soil from the top 15 cm (6 inches) because it contains roots, leaves, and other organic matter. Dig at least 30 cm (1 foot) deep to get clean clay. If the soil is too dry, add a little water to test it. If it's too wet, let it dry a bit. Always test a small sample before digging a large amount. With practice, you will learn to identify good clay by feel, colour, and smell in minutes..Trim()

Testing Soil for Sand, Silt, and Clay Content Using a Bottle Test

The bottle test is a simple way to find out how much sand, silt, and clay is in your soil. You need a clear glass bottle or jar (at least 1 litre), a ruler, and some water. Fill the bottle one-third with soil from your digging site. Remove stones and roots. Add water until the bottle is three-quarters full. Add a pinch of salt or a drop of dish soap to help separate the particles. Shake the bottle hard for 2 minutes, then set it down where it won't be disturbed..Trim()

.Trim()

After 1 minute, mark the level of the first layer that settles. This is sand. After 2 hours, mark the next layer. This is silt. After 24 hours, mark the top layer. This is clay. Measure the thickness of each layer with a ruler. The total thickness of all layers is the total soil sample. To find the percentage, divide each layer's thickness by the total and multiply by 100..Trim()

.Trim()

For good bricks, you want about 30-40% clay, 20-30% silt, and 30-50% sand. If clay is less than 20%, the bricks will be weak. If clay is more than 50%, the bricks will crack when drying. If sand is more than 60%, the bricks will crumble. Adjust by adding sand or clay as needed..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many soils are laterite, which is rich in iron and clay. Laterite often has good clay content but may need sand to reduce cracking. If your test shows too much clay, add river sand or crushed granite. If too much sand, add more clay from another source. Always repeat the test two or three times from different spots to get an average. Write down your results and keep them for future reference. This test costs almost nothing and helps you avoid wasting time and fuel on bad soil..Trim()

Digging and Storing Clay Properly During Rainy and Dry Seasons

Digging clay at the right time and storing it well is key to making quality bricks. In the rainy season, clay is wet and heavy, making it hard to dig and mix. Wait for a dry spell of at least 3 days. If you must dig in the rainy season, dig from higher ground where water drains quickly. Use a hoe, shovel, and pickaxe. Remove the top 30 cm (1 foot) of soil and put it aside. Dig out the clay below and pile it on a clean surface, like a tarpaulin or concrete floor..Trim()

.Trim()

In the dry season, clay becomes very hard. Water the area the day before digging to soften it. Dig in the early morning or late afternoon to avoid extreme heat. Break the clay into small lumps with a hoe or mallet. If the clay is too dry, add water gradually while mixing..Trim()

.Trim()

Storage is important. Never leave clay in the open. In the rainy season, cover the pile with a plastic sheet or tarpaulin to keep it dry. In the dry season, cover it to prevent it from drying out completely. Store clay under a shed or in a covered area. If you have no shed, make a simple roof with poles and grass thatch. Keep the pile off the ground by placing it on a raised platform of stones or wooden pallets. This prevents moisture from the ground and keeps the clay clean..Trim()

.Trim()

If you store clay for more than a month, turn it over every two weeks to prevent mould and keep it workable. Add a little water if it dries out. In Africa, many brick makers store clay in pits lined with stones or plastic. This keeps it moist and ready to use. Always keep your clay covered and protected from rain and sun. Proper digging and storage save you time and produce stronger bricks..Trim()

Mixing Clay with Sand and Water for the Right Consistency

Mixing clay with sand and water is the heart of brick making. The right mix gives you strong, crack-free bricks. Start by testing your clay and sand. A common mix is 3 parts clay to 1 part sand by volume. If your clay is very sticky, use 2 parts clay to 1 part sand. If it's sandy, use 4 parts clay to 1 part sand. Always mix dry first: spread clay on a clean floor, add sand, and mix with a hoe until the colour is uniform..Trim()

.Trim()

Then add water slowly. Use a watering can or bucket. For every 10 shovels of dry mix, add about 2-3 litres of water. Mix with a hoe, turning the pile over and over. Add water until the mix holds together when squeezed but doesn't drip. The ideal consistency is like stiff dough or peanut butter. If it's too wet, add more dry clay and sand. If too dry, add water..Trim()

.Trim()

Let the mix rest for 30 minutes to 1 hour. This allows the clay to absorb water evenly. Then mix again. You can also tread the mix with your feet (wear boots) to remove lumps. In Africa, many use a pug mill or a simple pit where the mix is trampled. Test the mix by making a ball and dropping it from waist height. If it flattens but doesn't break, it's good. If it shatters, add more clay. If it splats, add more sand..Trim()

.Trim()

For quality bricks, the mix should be homogeneous. Any dry lumps will cause weak spots. Mix enough for one day's production. Don't leave mixed clay overnight unless covered with wet cloth. The next day, add a little water and remix. With practice, you will know the right feel. Always keep your tools clean and your mixing area free of stones and roots..Trim()

Avoiding Common Soil Problems Like Too Much Salt or Organic Matter

Soil problems can ruin your bricks. Two common issues are too much salt and too much organic matter. Salt comes from salty water, coastal areas, or soils with white crusts. Salt causes bricks to crack, crumble, or have white stains (efflorescence). To test for salt, take a soil sample, add distilled water, shake, and taste a drop. If it's salty, don't use that soil. Or, mix soil with water and let it dry on a glass; if white crystals form, there's salt. If salt is mild, you can leach it by soaking the clay in fresh water for 2 days, then draining. But it's better to find another source..Trim()

.Trim()

Organic matter includes roots, leaves, manure, and dark topsoil. It burns during firing, leaving holes and weak bricks. To test, put a small sample in a jar with water. Organic matter floats. If more than 5% floats, the soil is bad. Also, smell the soil: if it smells like rotten eggs or manure, it has too much organic matter. Avoid digging topsoil. Dig at least 30 cm (1 foot) deep. If you must use soil with some organic matter, spread it in thin layers and let it dry for weeks, turning it often. Or, burn it slightly to burn off organics, but this is tricky..Trim()

.Trim()

Other problems include too much iron (makes bricks red but can cause cracking) and too much limestone (causes popping during firing). In Africa, laterite soils often have high iron, which is fine if mixed with sand. If you see white lumps that fizz when you add acid (vinegar), that's limestone. Remove them. Always test your soil before making bricks. A simple bottle test and a smell test can save you from disaster. If you find salt or organic matter, find a new source or treat the soil. Never use soil from a garbage dump or near a latrine..Trim()

Using Local Additives Like Rice Husks or Sawdust for Better Bricks

Adding local materials like rice husks, sawdust, or groundnut shells can make your bricks lighter, stronger, and cheaper. These additives are called pore formers or fillers. They burn out during firing, leaving tiny holes that help the brick dry faster and reduce cracking. They also reduce the amount of clay needed, saving you money. Use them at 5-10% of the total dry mix by volume. For example, for 10 buckets of clay-sand mix, add half a bucket to one bucket of rice husks..Trim()

.Trim()

Rice husks are common in Africa. They are hard and burn slowly. Use them dry. Sawdust from woodworking shops works well too, but avoid sawdust from treated wood or painted wood because it releases toxins. Groundnut shells, palm kernel shells, and coffee husks are also good. Make sure they are dry and free of dirt. Mix them evenly into the dry clay and sand before adding water. If they clump, break them up..Trim()

.Trim()

When using additives, you may need a little more water because the additives absorb moisture. Test the mix by making a brick and drying it. If it cracks, reduce the additive to 5%. If it's too heavy, increase to 10%. Additives also make the brick lighter, which is good for transport. But too much additive (over 15%) makes the brick weak. So stick to 5-10%..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many brick makers use rice husks to fire the kiln as well. You can mix ash from burnt rice husks into the clay. Ash acts like a flux, making the brick stronger. Use up to 5% ash. Always store additives in a dry place. Before using, test a small batch of bricks with and without additives to see the difference. With practice, you will find the right amount for your soil. This is a simple way to improve quality and reduce cost..Trim()

A brick mould is a simple box without a bottom or top. You can make it from hardwood planks (like mahogany or iroko) or from 2 mm mild steel sheet. Building Drying Racks and Stacking Bricks for Air Flow

After moulding, bricks must dry evenly to gain strength. In Africa, where sun and rain can be extreme, a good drying rack is essential. Module 5 · Firing Bricks in a Traditional Kiln

Choosing a Kiln Site Away from Homes and Trees

Choosing the right site for your kiln is the first step to safe and successful firing. You need an open area at least 30 meters from any house, hut, or animal pen. Smoke and heat can damage property and harm people, especially children and the elderly. Also keep the kiln at least 15 meters away from trees, bushes, and dry grass. A spark can easily start a bush fire, especially during the dry season. Clear all vegetation within a 10-meter radius of the kiln. The ground should be level and firm, not on a slope where fire can spread uphill. Avoid areas that flood during rains; wet ground makes loading and firing difficult. Check the wind direction: position the kiln so smoke blows away from homes and working areas. If possible, choose a site near your clay source and water, but safety comes first. Also consider access for delivering wood, charcoal, or coal, and for trucks or carts to carry fired bricks away. Mark the site with stones or pegs. Dig a shallow trench around the perimeter to prevent fire from escaping along the ground. Never build a kiln under power lines or near fuel storage. In many African communities, kilns are built on the edge of the village, near a road for transport. Talk to local leaders and neighbours before you start. They can help you avoid conflicts and ensure everyone knows the firing schedule. A good site reduces accidents, saves time, and protects your community. Remember, a kiln fire can burn for days, so plan for constant supervision. Once you have chosen the site, clear it, level it, and compact the soil. That foundation will support your kiln for many firings..Trim()

Building a Clamp or Scove Kiln with Local Materials

A clamp or scove kiln is simple and cheap to build using materials you already have. You will need about 1,000 to 2,000 unfired bricks, depending on the size. For a clamp kiln, start by laying a base of bricks with gaps for air. Arrange them in a grid pattern, leaving 5 cm gaps between bricks. Build up the sides with bricks stacked at a slight angle, leaving small openings for air. The kiln should be about 1.5 meters high and 2 to 3 meters wide. For a scove kiln, you first build a dome or arch shape using bricks, then cover it with mud or clay mortar. Leave small holes (about 10 cm diameter) around the base for air and at the top for smoke. You can also use a scove kiln made by piling bricks in a rectangular shape and plastering the outside with a mixture of clay and water. This plaster holds heat and reduces fuel use. Use dry bricks for the kiln structure; wet bricks will crack. If you have no bricks yet, you can use stones for the base and cover with mud. The key is to create a chamber where heat can circulate evenly. Make sure the kiln has a chimney or top vent to let smoke out. A scove kiln is more permanent and can be reused. After firing, you can repair cracks with fresh mud. In many parts of Africa, builders use anthill soil or termite mound clay for mortar because it is strong and resistant to heat. Mix it with water to a thick paste. Build the kiln on a dry day. If rain is coming, cover it with a tarpaulin or banana leaves. A well-built kiln will last for several firings and produce strong bricks..Trim()

Loading Bricks into the Kiln for Even Firing

Loading bricks correctly is critical for even firing. Start by sorting your unfired bricks: remove any that are cracked or misshapen. They can cause uneven heat flow. Load the driest bricks first, as they are less likely to crack. Arrange bricks in the kiln with small gaps (about 2-3 cm) between them so hot gases can pass. Do not stack them too tightly or too loosely. A good pattern is to lay bricks flat, then stack them on edge in alternating rows. This creates channels for heat. Leave a central space or flue for hot air to rise. Build the walls of the kiln with bricks placed at an angle, leaving air holes every 30 cm. For a clamp kiln, start with a base layer of bricks with gaps, then build up in layers, each layer slightly smaller to form a dome. For a scove kiln, load bricks through the top opening, again leaving gaps. The top layer should be covered with a layer of mud or broken bricks to seal in heat. Do not overload the kiln; leave at least 20 cm of free space below the roof or cover. If you are firing different types of bricks, place the denser ones in the center where it is hottest. Keep the loading process organised: have a team of two or three people to pass bricks. Wear gloves to protect your hands. Once loaded, check that the kiln is stable and that air holes are not blocked. A well-loaded kiln will fire evenly, saving fuel and reducing waste. In Africa, many kilns hold 2,000 to 5,000 bricks. Adjust your loading based on kiln size and desired output..Trim()

Starting and Controlling the Fire Using Wood, Charcoal, or Coal

Start the fire slowly. Use dry twigs, grass, or paper as kindling. Place kindling in the air holes at the base of the kiln. Light it and let it catch. Gradually add small pieces of wood or charcoal. Do not use petrol or paraffin; they cause dangerous flare-ups. Once the fire is established, add larger wood pieces or coal. For wood, use dry hardwoods like acacia or mango; they burn hot and long. Softwoods burn fast and produce less heat. Charcoal burns hotter and cleaner but is more expensive. Coal gives the highest heat but needs good ventilation. Control the fire by adjusting the air holes. Open more holes to increase heat, close them to reduce it. A kiln fire should reach 900Â°C to 1,100Â°C for good bricks. You can use a pyrometer if available, but most traditional kilns rely on colour and sound. Feed the fire every 30 minutes to an hour. Do not overload the fire; add small amounts often. Keep a constant watch. The firing process takes 24 to 72 hours, depending on kiln size and fuel. During the first 6 hours, keep the fire low to drive off moisture. Then increase heat gradually. If you see steam, the bricks are still wet; slow down. If smoke turns from white to black, the fire is too rich; reduce fuel. Use a long stick to poke the fire and check air flow. Wear protective clothing: gloves, boots, long sleeves, and a mask. Never leave the fire unattended. Have water or sand nearby to control runaway flames. In Africa, many kilns use a mixture of wood and charcoal. Experiment to find what works best with your local materials..Trim()

Monitoring Firing Temperature by Colour and Sound

You can judge the temperature inside a kiln by looking at the colour of the bricks or the glow through the air holes. At 500Â°C, bricks glow a dull red. At 700Â°C, they are cherry red. At 900Â°C, they become orange. At 1,000Â°C, they are yellow-orange. At 1,100Â°C, they are white-hot. For good bricks, you want a consistent orange to yellow-orange glow. If you see white-hot spots, the fire is too hot and may melt or crack bricks. If the glow is dark red, the fire is too cool; add more fuel or open air holes. You can also tap two fired bricks together. A clear ringing sound means the brick is well fired. A dull thud means it is underfired. During firing, use a long metal rod to pull out a test brick from the kiln. Let it cool, then tap it. If it rings, the batch is ready. If not, continue firing. Another method is to insert a pyrometric cone (if available) that bends at a specific temperature. But in most African villages, colour and sound are the main tools. Check the kiln every hour. Look for smoke colour: white smoke means water vapour is escaping; black smoke means unburnt fuel; clear or light grey smoke means good combustion. Also listen for popping or cracking sounds; that means bricks are breaking. If you hear that, reduce heat slowly. Keep a log of your observations: time, colour, smoke, and any sounds. This helps you repeat a good firing. Remember, temperature varies inside the kiln. The centre is hottest, edges cooler. So rotate test bricks from different spots. Cooling and Unloading Fired Bricks Without Breakage

After firing, do not open the kiln immediately. Sudden cooling will crack the bricks. Let the kiln cool slowly. Seal all air holes and the top vent with mud or bricks. This stops air from entering and prevents rapid cooling. Leave it for 24 to 48 hours. The kiln should be cool enough to touch. If you can hold your hand on the outside of the kiln for a few seconds, it is safe to unload. Start by removing the top cover carefully. Use gloves and a face mask; dust and heat can still be present. Remove bricks from the top, working downwards. Do not throw or drop bricks; place them gently on a soft surface like grass or sand. Inspect each brick: good bricks are uniform in colour, ring when tapped, and have no cracks. Reject any that are cracked, warped, or underfired. Underfired bricks can be re-fired later. Sort bricks into grades: first grade for exposed walls, second grade for foundations or internal use, and rejects for crushing into aggregate. Stack fired bricks in a dry place, off the ground, to avoid moisture. Use wooden pallets or stones. Cover with a tarp if rain is expected. Unloading is hard work; take breaks. In Africa, many communities unload as a team, with women and men working together. After unloading, clean the kiln site and prepare for the next firing. Repair any damage to the kiln. With careful cooling and unloading, you will have strong bricks for building homes, schools, and businesses..Trim()

Module 6 · Making Cement-Stabilised Pavers

Selecting Sand and Aggregate for Strong Pavers

The quality of your sand and aggregate determines how strong and durable your pavers will be. In Africa, you can find good materials locally if you know what to look for. For sand, use clean, sharp river sand or pit sand. Avoid sand with too much clay or silt â it weakens the cement bond. A simple test: put a handful of sand in a clear bottle, add water, shake, and let it settle. If the silt layer on top is more than 10% of the total height, the sand is too dirty. Wash it or find better sand. For aggregate, you need small stones or crushed rock, usually 6mm to 10mm in size. In many areas, you can use quarry dust or crushed granite. Avoid using round river stones â they don't lock together well. A good mix for pavers is 1 part cement, 2 parts sand, and 3 parts aggregate (by volume) for strong pavers. But for cabro-style, you might use 1:2:2. Always measure with consistent containers â a bucket or a wheelbarrow. Test your materials: make a small batch, mould a paver, cure it for 7 days, and drop it from waist height. If it breaks easily, adjust your materials. Remember, dirty materials mean weak pavers, and weak pavers mean wasted money and time..Trim()

Mixing Cement, Sand, and Water in Correct Ratios

Getting the mix right is the difference between pavers that last for years and pavers that crumble in months. For cement-stabilised pavers, a common ratio is 1 part cement to 4 parts sand to 2 parts aggregate (1:4:2) for general use, but for heavy traffic, use 1:3:2. Always use fresh cement â if it has lumps, it's spoiled. Measure accurately using buckets or a scale. For a wheelbarrow mix, use one 50kg bag of cement (which is about 4 buckets) and match with sand and aggregate accordingly. Mix dry ingredients first on a clean concrete slab or mixing board. Turn the pile three times until the colour is uniform â no streaks of sand or cement. Then add water slowly while mixing. The correct water amount is about half the weight of cement â so for 50kg cement, use 25 litres of water. But adjust based on moisture in your sand. The mix should be stiff, not runny. Test: squeeze a handful; it should hold its shape but not drip water. Too much water weakens the paver; too little makes it hard to compact. Mix only what you can use within 30 minutes. In hot African sun, work fast. Never add more water to a mix that's starting to set â throw it away and start fresh. Consistent mixing gives consistent strength..Trim()

Using Manual or Vibrating Machines for Paver Production

You can make pavers with simple manual tools or with a vibrating machine. Manual production uses a steel mould and a tamper. It's cheap but slow and labour-intensive. You fill the mould with mix, then compact it by pounding with a heavy tamper (at least 10kg) about 20 times per paver. This works for small batches, but the pavers may be less dense and weaker. A vibrating machine â either a table vibrator or a hand-held poker vibrator â compacts the mix much better. A small table vibrator costs around $500-$1000, but it can produce 200-400 pavers per day with 2-3 workers. If you can't afford one, you can hire one or share with other producers. For manual, use a strong steel mould (not plastic â it wears out fast). For vibrating, place the mould on the vibrating table, fill it, and let it vibrate for 5-10 seconds until the mix settles and air bubbles stop. Then strike off the excess and remove the mould. Always lubricate the mould with used engine oil or soapy water to prevent sticking. In Africa, many successful producers start manual and upgrade to a vibrating machine after saving profits. The key is to compact well â whether by hand or machine â to remove air pockets. Air pockets are the enemy of strength..Trim()

Moulding Interlocking and Cabro-Style Pavers

Interlocking and cabro-style pavers are popular because they lock together and don't shift easily. Interlocking pavers have shaped edges â like zig-zags or curves â that fit into each other. Cabro-style pavers are rectangular with chamfered edges, often used for roads and driveways. To mould them, you need the right mould. For interlocking, use a mould that matches the shape you want. You can buy steel moulds locally or make them with a welder. For cabro, a simple rectangular mould with a slight taper works. The process: place the mould on a flat, clean surface (a concrete slab or steel plate). Fill it halfway with mix, then compact with a tamper or vibrator. Fill to the top and compact again. For interlocking, pay attention to the edges â they must be sharp and clean. After compacting, strike off the top with a straight edge (a piece of wood or steel). Curing Pavers with Water for 7 to 14 Days

Curing is the most important step that many beginners skip. Curing means keeping the pavers wet so the cement can continue to harden. If you don't cure, your pavers will be weak and dusty. After moulding, leave the pavers in the shade for 24 hours to set. Then, start curing. The best method is to cover them with a plastic sheet or wet burlap and keep them moist. You can also stack them and pour water over them twice a day â morning and evening. In hot African climates, you may need to water three times a day. Never let them dry out completely. Cure for at least 7 days for normal pavers, but 14 days is better for high strength. If you're in a hurry, 7 days is the minimum. After 7 days, the paver reaches about 70% strength; after 14 days, about 90%; after 28 days, full strength. But for practical use, 14 days is good. You can cure pavers by immersing them in a water tank â that's the best if you have one. Otherwise, keep them under a tree or in a shed. Avoid direct sunlight during curing â it causes rapid drying and cracks. Also, don't cure in the first 24 hours â the pavers are too soft. After curing, let them dry for a day before transporting. Proper curing makes your pavers strong enough to drive a car on. Skip curing, and they'll break under foot traffic..Trim()

Testing Paver Strength with Simple Drop and Load Tests

You don't need a laboratory to test your pavers. Simple field tests can tell you if they're strong enough. If it breaks into 2-3 pieces or stays intact, it's good. For a more precise test, drop it from 1.5 metres â it should not break. The load test: place a paver on two supports (like bricks) with a gap of 20cm. Stand on it with your full weight (about 70kg). If it cracks, it's not strong enough for driveways.
