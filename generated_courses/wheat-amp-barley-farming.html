Module 1 · Getting Started with Wheat & Barley in Africa

Why Wheat and Barley Matter for African Markets

Africa imports over 40 million tonnes of wheat each year, costing more than $15 billion. That's a massive opportunity for local farmers. Countries like Egypt, Nigeria, Ethiopia, and South Africa are among the world's top wheat importers. But only a fraction is grown locally. For example, Nigeria imports about 5 million tonnes annually, while local production is under 100,000 tonnes. This gap means millers and food processors are desperate for local grain. Barley is also in high demand, especially for beer production. Africa's beer market is growing at 5% per year, and breweries like Heineken, Diageo, and SABMiller source barley locally when possible. In Ethiopia, barley is a traditional crop used for food and local beer (tella). The African Development Bank estimates that import substitution in wheat could create 5 million jobs. For you as a small-scale farmer, this means if you can produce quality grain, you have a ready market. You don't need to export; you can sell to local millers, breweries, or feedlots. The key is to understand that wheat and barley are not just cropsâthey are strategic commodities that African governments want to support. Many countries offer subsidies, improved seeds, and extension services. By growing these crops, you contribute to food security and earn good money. Start small, but think big: one hectare of wheat can yield 3-5 tonnes, and with a price of $300 per tonne, that's $900-$1,500 per harvest. Barley yields similar but often sells for $250-$400 per tonne. The demand is there. Your job is to meet it..Trim()

Identifying Local Demand: Bread, Malt, and Animal Feed

Before you plant, find out who will buy your grain. There are three main markets: bread, malt, and animal feed. Bread is the biggest. In cities like Nairobi, Lagos, and Cairo, bakeries run daily. A single large bakery can use 10 tonnes of wheat flour per day. That flour comes from millers. So your target is the miller, not the bakery directly. Millers like United Milling in Kenya or Flour Mills of Nigeria buy thousands of tonnes. They prefer consistent quality: protein content of 11-13% for bread wheat, moisture below 12%, and low foreign matter. Visit your local miller and ask about their requirements. For malt, breweries need barley with specific qualities: high germination, low protein (9-11%), and uniform size. In South Africa, SAB (now AB InBev) contracts farmers directly. In Ethiopia, breweries like BGI Ethiopia buy local barley. If you are near a brewery, that's a golden opportunity. Animal feed is the third market. Feedlots and poultry farms use wheat and barley as energy sources. They often buy lower-grade grain that doesn't meet milling or malting standards. This is a good fallback if your crop has defects. To identify demand, talk to: 1) Millers (look for 'roller mills' or 'flour mills' in your area). 2) Breweries (check for 'maltings' or 'breweries'). 3) Feedlots (large cattle or poultry operations). Also check with local traders and cooperatives. Attend farmer field days and agricultural shows. Ask about prices, volumes, and contract terms. A simple phone call to a miller can save you months of guesswork. Remember: demand is local. Don't assume you must sell to a big city. A small feedlot 20 km away might be your best customer..Trim()

Understanding African Climates: Highlands vs. Lowlands

Africa's climate varies hugely. For wheat and barley, altitude matters more than latitude. Highlands (above 1,500 m) are cooler and often have reliable rainfall. Lowlands (below 1,000 m) are hotter and drier. Wheat thrives in highlands. In Ethiopia, wheat is grown at 1,800-2,800 m. In Kenya, the Rift Valley (1,800-2,500 m) is the wheat belt. In South Africa, the Free State (1,200-1,800 m) is key. Barley is more flexible. It grows in highlands but also in lowlands with irrigation or winter rain. In Morocco, barley is grown in lowlands (0-500 m) with 300-500 mm rainfall. In Egypt, barley is grown in the Nile Delta (lowland) in winter. Temperature: Wheat needs a cool growing season. If temperatures exceed 30Â°C during grain filling, yields drop. Barley can handle up to 35Â°C. Rainfall: Wheat needs 400-600 mm well-distributed. Barley needs 300-500 mm. In lowlands, you need irrigation. For example, in Nigeria's savannah, wheat is grown under irrigation in the dry season (November-March) when temperatures are cooler. Soil: Highlands often have acidic soils (pH 4.5-5.5). You may need to add lime. Lowlands have more alkaline soils. Test your soil before planting. Tools: Use a simple soil test kit (cost $20) or send samples to a lab. Also consider frost: Wheat is more frost-tolerant than barley at flowering. In highlands like Lesotho, frost can kill barley. Practical tips: 1) Find your altitude using a GPS or phone app. 2) Check local weather records for temperature and rainfall. 3) Talk to neighbors about what they grow. 4) If you are in a lowland area, choose barley with irrigation. 5) If you are in a highland area, wheat is usually better. Basic Costs and Potential Profits in Small-Scale Farming

Testing Your Soil: Simple pH and Nutrient Checks

Understanding Wheat Classes: Bread, Biscuit, and Durum

Wheat is not just wheat. There are three main classes you need to know: bread wheat, biscuit wheat, and durum wheat. Each has different uses and growing requirements. Bread wheat is the most common. Reading Fertilizer Labels: NPK Explained Simply

Every fertilizer bag has three big numbers on it, like 23-21-0 or 12-24-12. These numbers tell you the percentage of three key nutrients: Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), and Potassium (K). The first number is always nitrogen, the second is phosphorus (as phosphate), and the third is potassium (as potash). For example, a 50 kg bag of 23-21-0 contains 11.5 kg of nitrogen, 10.5 kg of phosphorus, and 0 kg of potassium. The rest is filler like sand or limestone to make the bag weigh 50 kg..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does this matter? Wheat and barley need these nutrients in different amounts at different stages. Nitrogen makes leaves green and promotes tillering. Phosphorus helps roots grow strong and aids in grain filling. Potassium improves stem strength and disease resistance. If you apply the wrong ratio, you waste money and may harm your crop..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, common fertilizers include DAP (18-46-0), Compound D (7-14-7), Urea (46-0-0), and CAN (27-0-0). DAP is high in phosphorus, so it's good for planting. Urea is almost all nitrogen, so it's used for top-dressing. Compound D has a balance of all three, often used as a basal fertilizer. Always check the label for the analysis and the net weight. Some bags show percentages that don't add up to 100 because they include other nutrients like sulphur or calcium..Trim()

.Trim()

To calculate how much nutrient you are applying, multiply the bag weight by the percentage. For example, 50 kg of Urea (46-0-0) gives 23 kg of actual nitrogen. If your soil test recommends 60 kg of nitrogen per hectare, you need about 130 kg of Urea per hectare. Always read the label carefully and store fertilizer in a dry place, away from moisture and children. Never mix different fertilizers unless instructed, as they can react and become ineffective..Trim()

Basal fertilizer is the foundation of your crop's nutrition. It is applied at planting time, placed near the seed so young roots can access nutrients immediately. The two most common basal fertilizers in Africa are DAP (18-46-0) and Compound D (7-14-7). DAP is high in phosphorus, which is critical for root development and early tillering. Compound D contains nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, plus sulphur, making it a balanced starter..Trim()

.Trim()

For wheat and barley, apply basal fertilizer at a rate of 200 to 250 kg per hectare for DAP, or 300 to 400 kg per hectare for Compound D. The exact rate depends on your soil test. If you don't have a soil test, a general recommendation is 200 kg/ha of DAP. Apply it in a band about 5 cm to the side and 5 cm below the seed at planting. This placement prevents seed burn and ensures the roots find the nutrients. Do not broadcast basal fertilizer on the surface; phosphorus does not move in the soil, so it must be placed near the roots..Trim()

.Trim()

If you are planting on a small plot, say 0.5 hectares, you need 100 kg of DAP. A standard matchbox holds about 20 grams of fertilizer. Top-dressing means adding fertilizer after the crop has emerged, usually to supply nitrogen when the plants need it most. For wheat and barley, the critical time is during tillering and stem elongation, about 3 to 6 weeks after planting. Foliar feeding means spraying liquid fertilizer directly onto the leaves. Plants can absorb nutrients through their leaves quickly, so foliar feeds are good for correcting deficiencies fast. .Trim()

Common foliar fertilizers include urea (dissolved in water), potassium nitrate, and commercial micronutrient blends. Avoiding Fertilizer Burn and Runoff

Fertilizer burn happens when too much fertilizer is placed too close to seeds or roots, or when fertilizer granules stick to wet leaves. The salts in fertilizer draw water out of plant cells, causing leaves to turn brown, curl, and die. To avoid burn, always place basal fertilizer at least 5 cm away from the seed. For top-dressing, apply when leaves are dry, and if using Urea, incorporate it lightly. Never apply fertilizer on frozen or waterlogged soil. If you accidentally over-apply, irrigate heavily to leach salts away from the root zone..Trim()

.Trim()

Runoff occurs when fertilizer is washed off the field by rain or irrigation, carrying nutrients into rivers and lakes. This wastes money and pollutes water. To prevent runoff, avoid applying fertilizer just before heavy rain. Instead, apply when light rain is expected, or irrigate lightly after application. On sloping land, use contour farming or planting across the slope to slow water flow. Keep a vegetated buffer strip along water bodies. Also, do not apply fertilizer to bare soil; incorporate it or apply to a growing crop that can take it up..Trim()

.Trim()

Another key practice is split application. Instead of applying all nitrogen at once, split it into two or three doses. For example, apply half at planting (as basal) and half at tillering. This reduces the amount of nitrogen available to be lost through runoff or leaching. Also, use the right source: Urea is more prone to volatilization loss than CAN, so if you cannot incorporate it, CAN may be better. On sandy soils, nutrients leach quickly, so apply little and often..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, maintain soil health by adding organic matter. Compost and manure improve soil structure, allowing water to infiltrate and nutrients to be held. Cover crops like legumes can fix nitrogen and prevent erosion. Rotate cereals with legumes to break disease cycles and improve soil fertility. Test your soil regularly to know exactly what nutrients you need. Over-fertilizing is not only wasteful but harmful. By following these practices, you protect your crop, your wallet, and the environment..Trim()

Identifying Common Weeds in Wheat and Barley

Weeds compete with your wheat and barley for water, nutrients, and sunlight. If you don't control them early, you can lose 30-50% of your yield. The first step is to know your enemy. In African wheat and barley fields, the most common weeds are wild oats, blackjack, pigweed, thorn apple, and couch grass. Wild oats look like thin, tall grass with loose seed heads. They are dangerous because they look similar to wheat when young, so you might mistake them. Pull them out before they set seed. Blackjack (Bidens pilosa) has yellow flowers and sticky black seeds that cling to clothes. It grows fast and spreads quickly. Pigweed (Amaranthus) has broad, red-tinged leaves and tiny black seeds. It emerges early and can smother young seedlings. Thorn apple (Datura) has large, spiky seed pods and purple or white trumpet flowers. It is poisonous to livestock, so remove it completely. Couch grass is a creeping grass with long underground stems (rhizomes). It spreads aggressively and is hard to kill by hand; you must dig out the rhizomes. To identify weeds, walk your field weekly. Look for plants that are different from your crop in leaf shape, color, or growth habit. Young weeds are easiest to kill. Learn to recognize seedlings: broadleaf weeds have two seed leaves (cotyledons) that are rounded, while grass weeds have thin, blade-like leaves. Keep a simple notebook with sketches or photos. If you are unsure, ask an extension officer or an experienced farmer. Early identification saves money and labour. Remember: one wild oat plant can produce 200 seeds, so never let weeds go to seed. Scout your field every 5-7 days for the first 6 weeks after planting..Trim()

For larger fields, consider hiring labour or using a ox-drawn cultivator. A sharp hoe is safer and faster than a blunt one. Wear gloves to prevent blisters. Signs of Maturity: Color, Hardness, and Moisture

Knowing when to harvest is critical. If you harvest too early, the grain is green and shriveled, leading to low yield and poor quality. If you wait too long, the grain shatters and falls to the ground, and birds and rodents eat it. For wheat and barley, the best time to harvest is when the grain is physiologically mature but still hard. The straw (stems and leaves) turns from green to yellow or golden. The heads (spikes) droop slightly and the grain inside is hard. A simple test: bite a grain. If it is doughy or soft, it is not ready. If it is hard and makes a cracking sound when bitten, it is ready. Another sign: the grain is difficult to separate from the chaff by hand. Moisture content should be around 20-25% for harvest to avoid shattering, but for safe storage it must be dried to 12-13%. In Africa, many smallholders harvest by hand, so timing is even more critical. Watch the crop daily as it approaches maturity. In hot, dry areas, maturity can happen quicklyâwithin a week. In cooler highlands, it may take longer. Also, check for black point or fusarium damage; if you see black tips on grains, harvest immediately and dry well. Do not delay once the crop is ready. Use a moisture meter if available, but if not, use the bite test and visual signs. Remember, barley matures earlier than wheat. For barley, the awns (bristles) become brittle and the grain hard. For wheat, the glumes (husks) open slightly and grain is exposed. Plan your harvest labor and equipment ahead of time so you can act fast when the crop is ready. A delay of even a few days can mean significant loss..Trim()

Harvesting with Sickles, Knives, and Small Machines

Most smallholder farmers in Africa harvest wheat and barley by hand using a sickle or knife. A sickle is a curved blade with a short handle, used to cut the stems near the ground. A sharp knife or machete can also work, but a sickle is faster. To harvest, grab a handful of stems with one hand and cut with the other, about 10-15 cm above the ground. Leave the stubble; it helps protect the soil. Cut in the early morning or late afternoon when it's cooler and grain is less likely to shatter. Avoid cutting when wet with dew, as grain may mold. After cutting, lay the bundles in small heaps or tie them into sheaves. Sheaves should be left to dry in the field for a few days, turning them occasionally. If you have access to a small machine, like a reaper or a combine harvester, it can save time and labor. But these machines are expensive and may not be suitable for small, rocky, or terraced fields. For small machines, ensure they are properly adjusted: the reel speed, cutter bar, and concave clearance. A combine harvester both cuts and threshes, but it requires a large field and a lot of fuel. For most smallholders, manual harvesting is the norm. Organize labor groups or family members to work together. A person can harvest about 0.1 hectare per day by hand. That means for a one-hectare field, you need 10 person-days. Plan ahead. After cutting, gather sheaves and transport them to the threshing floor. Use a tarpaulin or clean floor to avoid contamination. Do not throw sheaves; handle gently to prevent grain loss. If using a machine, check for grain loss behind the machineâadjust settings if too much grain is blown out. Also, keep safety in mind: wear gloves, long sleeves, and closed shoes to avoid cuts. Threshing and Winnowing: Traditional and Improved Methods

Threshing is the process of separating grain from the stalks and husks. Winnowing removes the chaff. Traditionally, farmers thresh by beating sheaves with sticks or by trampling with animals. This is labor-intensive and can damage grain. An improved method is to use a threshing machine, either manual or motorized. A manual thresher costs less and can be shared among farmers. Motorized threshers are faster but need fuel. For small quantities, you can thresh by rubbing the heads between your hands or using a wooden mallet. After threshing, you have a mix of grain, chaff, and broken straw. Winnowing is done by tossing the mixture into the air with a winnowing basket or fork, allowing the wind to blow away the lighter chaff. Do this on a windy day, preferably in the morning or evening. If there is no wind, use a fan or a mechanical winnower. An improved method is to use a tarpaulin and a fan. Spread the threshed grain on a tarpaulin, then use a standing fan to blow chaff away while you pour grain slowly from a height. This is more controlled and reduces grain loss. Another method: use a sieve to separate larger pieces. For best results, thresh when grain moisture is about 15-18%; if too dry, grain cracks; if too wet, it smears. After winnowing, you should have clean grain. But even then, small stones and dirt may remain. You can further clean by washing? No, never wash grain for storageâit introduces moisture. Instead, use a sieve or hand-pick. Threshing and winnowing should be done on a clean, dry surface, not on bare ground, to avoid soil contamination. In many parts of Africa, farmers use a 'threshing floor' made of cow dung and mud, which is smooth and clean. But a tarpaulin is better. After winnowing, bag the grain and move to drying. Drying Grain to Safe Moisture Levels

After threshing and winnowing, grain must be dried to a safe moisture content for storage. For wheat and barley, safe moisture is 12-13%. If grain is stored wet, it will heat up, mold, and become toxic. To dry, spread grain in a thin layer (5-10 cm) on a clean, dry surface like a tarpaulin, concrete floor, or raised platform. Avoid drying on bare ground; it picks up moisture and dirt. Dry in the sun, stirring the grain every hour with a rake or shovel. This ensures even drying. In hot, dry weather, grain may dry in 2-3 days. In humid conditions, it may take longer. If you have a moisture meter, test daily. If not, use the salt test: put a handful of grain in a dry bottle with a spoonful of salt, shake for a minute, and if salt sticks to the sides, grain is still too wet. Another test: bite a grain; if it shatters and is hard, it is dry. You can also use a glass jar: put grain in a jar, seal, and leave in the sun for an hour; if moisture forms on the glass, it's too wet. For large quantities, consider a mechanical dryer, but these are expensive. Solar dryers are a good intermediate option. They use a black-lined box with a glass or plastic cover and vents. They dry faster and protect from rain and dust. Never dry grain on the road or where animals roam. Also, do not dry on metal roofs as it can cook the grain. Once dry, allow grain to cool before storage. Store in a cool, dry place. If grain is too dry (below 10%), it can crack during handling, so aim for 12-13%. For barley, ideal moisture is 12%; for wheat, 13%. Proper drying prevents aflatoxin and insect infestation. It also improves milling quality and market price. Take drying seriously; it is the most important step after harvest..Trim()

Cleaning and Grading for Better Market Prices

Clean grain fetches a higher price. After drying, remove all foreign matter: stones, dirt, broken grains, weed seeds, and chaff. Use a sieve or a cleaning machine. A simple method is to use a set of sieves with different mesh sizes. First, a coarse sieve removes large debris like straw and stones. Then a finer sieve removes smaller seeds and sand. You can also use a fan or winnowing to blow away light chaff. For small quantities, hand-picking is possible but tedious. Grading means separating grain by size, color, and quality. Buyers pay more for uniform, bold grain. For wheat, grade by test weight (hectoliter weight) and protein content. For barley, grade by plumpness and color. In Africa, many markets have grades: Grade 1, Grade 2, etc. Grade 1 is clean, bold, and uniform. To grade, use a sieve with round holes (e.g., 2.5 mm for wheat). Grain that stays on top is larger and higher grade. Broken grains and shriveled ones fall through. You can also use a gravity separator or a spiral separator, but these are for large operations. For smallholders, simple sieving and hand sorting work. After cleaning and grading, package grain in clean bags. Do not mix different grades. Label the bags with grade and date. Store in a clean, dry place. Clean grain also stores better because insects and molds are less likely to attack. When selling, present samples to buyers. A clean, well-graded sample can increase price by 10-20%. Also, remove any discolored or moldy grains; they can lower the entire lot's value. If you have black point or fusarium, remove affected grains. For barley, keep the color uniform; discolored grain is rejected for malting. Remember, cleaning and grading take time but pay off. Invest in a set of sieves and a tarpaulin. Work on a clean surface. Wear a mask to avoid dust. After grading, store in hermetic bags or silos to maintain quality. Storage: Hermetic Bags, Silos, and Pest Control

Proper storage protects grain from insects, rodents, moisture, and mold. The key is to keep grain dry and airtight. Hermetic bags are a great option for smallholders. These are airtight plastic bags that seal out oxygen. Insects die due to lack of oxygen. Brands like GrainPro and PICS are available in Africa. To use, fill the bag with dry grain (12-13% moisture), squeeze out air, and seal tightly. Store bags on pallets, not directly on the floor. Keep in a cool, dark room. Hermetic bags can store grain for 1-2 years without chemicals. Silos are another option. Metal silos are popular in East and Southern Africa. They come in various sizes (e.g., 500 kg, 1 ton, 5 tons). A silo keeps grain airtight and protects from pests. But they are more expensive. For community storage, a large silo can be shared. If using a silo, ensure grain is dry and clean before filling. Also, check for leaks. Traditional storage methods like mud silos or gourds are used but are less effective. For pest control, avoid chemical pesticides if possible. Instead, use hermetic storage, diatomaceous earth (DE), or botanicals like neem leaves. DE is a powder that kills insects by drying them out; mix 1 kg per 100 kg grain. Neem leaves can be layered in bags. For rodents, keep storage area clean and use traps. Cats can help. For larger infestations, you may need phosphine tablets, but these are dangerous; use only if trained. Always follow safety instructions. Inspect stored grain monthly for signs of insects, moisture, or mold. If you see weevils or moths, act quickly. Sun-dry the grain again if needed. Never store grain on the ground; use pallets. Also, keep storage area well-ventilated but not humid. In humid areas, use hermetic bags. For long-term storage, keep grain cool. Remember, a good storage system reduces post-harvest losses, which can be 20-30% in Africa. With proper storage, you can sell when prices are high. Milling Wheat into Flour for Local Bakeries

Milling wheat into flour for local bakeries starts with clean grain. You need wheat that is dry (12-14% moisture) and free of stones, dirt, and broken kernels. Quality Control: Moisture, Ash, and Protein Tests

Quality control ensures your flour or malt meets buyer expectations. Three key tests are moisture, ash, and protein. Moisture: too high moisture causes mould; too low makes flour dry. For flour, aim for 12-14% moisture. For malt, below 5%. To test moisture without a lab, use a simple moisture meter ($50-$200). If you don't have one, use the oven method: weigh 10 g sample, dry in oven at 130Â°C for 1 hour, cool, and reweigh. Moisture % = (initial weight - final weight) / initial weight x 100. Ash: ash indicates mineral content and bran contamination. For white flour, ash should be 0.4-0.6%. For whole wheat, 1.5-2.0%. To test ash, burn a sample in a furnace at 550Â°C for 4 hours, then weigh the ash. This requires a furnace, so small processors can send samples to a lab. A simple test: mix flour with water to make dough, wash under running water to remove starch, and weigh the remaining gluten. Gluten content is roughly 80% of protein. For malt, you can test diastatic power (enzyme activity) by mashing a sample and checking sugar conversion. Keep records of each batch. If moisture is too high, dry the grain more before milling. If ash is too high, sift more to remove bran. If protein is low, blend with higher-protein wheat. For malt, ensure germination is even. Regular testing helps you maintain consistency. Buyers, especially breweries and bakeries, will ask for specs. Having test results builds trust. You can also use simple sensory tests: smell (fresh, no musty odour), touch (smooth, no lumps), and taste (neutral). Start with basic moisture and gluten tests, and add more as you grow.
