Your Role: Operator, Hirer, or Reseller

Module 2 · Finding the Right Machine for Your Market

Assessing Local Demand: What Do Farmers, Builders, and Processors Need?

Before you buy any machine, you must know who will pay to use it. Start by walking through your local area and talking to at least 20 potential customers. For farmers, ask what tasks take the most time and labour. In many African communities, maize shelling, cassava grinding, and groundnut oil pressing are top needs. A farmer with 2 hectares of maize may spend 10 days shelling by hand; a small maize sheller can do that in 2 hours. That saves 9 days of labour, which is worth about 50,000 to 100,000 shillings depending on local wages. For builders, demand often centres on block-making, concrete mixing, and cutting iron sheets. A manual block-making machine can produce 500 blocks per day, while a motorised one can do 1,500. Check how many building projects are within 20 km of your location. For processors, look at what raw materials are abundant. If there is a lot of cassava, a cassava grater and press are needed. If there are many oilseeds, an oil expeller is profitable. Visit local markets and ask traders what products they buy from outside the area. That tells you what processing is missing. Also check existing machines: if three people already own a maize mill, the market may be saturated. A simple demand survey tool is a notebook and pen. Record names, phone numbers, and specific needs. After 20 interviews, you will see a pattern. Choose the machine that solves the most common, most painful problem. That is your best bet for steady income..Trim()

Matching Machine Capacity to Your Market Size

Buying a machine that is too big wastes money; too small loses customers. First, estimate your daily demand. Count how many farmers, builders, or processors need your service within a 10 km radius. For example, if 50 farmers each have 1 hectare of maize, that is 50 hectares. A hectare yields about 2 tonnes of maize. Total 100 tonnes. Shelling season lasts 3 weeks (21 days). So you need to shell 100,000 kg / 21 days = 4,762 kg per day. A small sheller does 1,000 kg per hour. You need 5 hours of work per day. That is manageable. If you buy a machine that does 5,000 kg per hour, you will only work 1 hour per day and waste fuel and capital. Second, check your power source. Chinese machines often come with diesel or electric motors. A 5 HP diesel engine uses about 1 litre per hour. If you run 5 hours, that is 5 litres = $5. Electric motors are cheaper per hour but need reliable power. Third, consider transport. If your customers are far, you may need to move the machine. A small, portable machine (under 100 kg) is better than a heavy one. Fourth, think about growth. If you expect demand to double in 2 years, buy a machine that can be upgraded or run at higher speed. But do not overbuy. A good rule: buy a machine that can handle 120% of your current peak daily demand. That gives room to grow without sitting idle. Always ask the seller for the machine's capacity in kg per hour or blocks per day. If they cannot tell you, walk away. Real numbers matter..Trim()

New vs. Refurbished: Pros and Cons

New Chinese machines come with a warranty, usually 6 to 12 months. They are cleaner, more reliable, and often have better fuel efficiency. But they cost 30% to 50% more than refurbished ones. For example, a new maize sheller might cost $500, while a refurbished one is $300. Refurbished machines are used machines that have been repaired and repainted. They can work well if the repair was done properly. However, you must inspect them carefully. Check for cracks in the frame, worn bearings, and rust in the engine. Ask for a demonstration with actual maize or cassava. Listen for unusual noises. A good refurbished machine can last 2-3 years, while a new one can last 5-7 years. If you have limited capital and a reliable mechanic nearby, refurbished can be a good start. But if you need the machine for daily income and cannot afford downtime, buy new. Also consider spare parts: new machines often use standard parts that are easy to find. Refurbished machines may have modified parts that are hard to replace. One smart strategy: buy a new machine for your main income earner, and a refurbished one for backup or for less critical tasks. Another factor is resale value. New machines hold value better. If you plan to upgrade in 2 years, new is better. If you plan to use it until it dies, refurbished is fine. Always get a receipt and a written guarantee, even for refurbished. Test the machine under load for at least 30 minutes before paying. Never buy without seeing it run..Trim()

The Importance of Spare Parts Availability

A machine without spare parts is a pile of metal. Before you buy any Chinese machine, find out where you can buy parts. Ask the seller: Where is your nearest parts dealer? How long does it take to order a replacement? What are the most common parts that break? For maize shellers, the rubber rollers and sieves wear out. For oil expellers, the pressing screws and rings wear. For block-making machines, the moulds and bearings wear. If you cannot find these parts locally, you may wait weeks for a shipment from China. That means lost income. A simple rule: only buy a machine if you can find at least two suppliers of its spare parts within 100 km. If not, consider a different brand or model. Also, learn to do basic maintenance yourself. Change oil every 100 hours of use. Grease bearings every 50 hours. Keep the machine clean. A well-maintained machine breaks less often. But even with care, parts wear out. So budget 10% of the machine's cost per year for spare parts. For a $500 machine, set aside $50 per year. That is $4 per month. When a part breaks, you can fix it immediately. Another tip: buy a machine that uses common parts. For example, many Chinese diesel engines use standard oil filters that are also used in motorcycles. That makes them easy to find. Avoid machines with unique, proprietary parts. Ask other owners in your area what they use and where they buy parts. Their experience is your best guide..Trim()

When a Small Machine Beats a Large One

Bigger is not always better. In many African markets, a small machine outperforms a large one. Here is why. First, small machines cost less to buy and maintain. A small maize sheller at $300 can be paid off in 2 months. A large one at $3,000 might take 2 years. Second, small machines are easier to move. If your customers are scattered, you can carry a 50 kg machine on a motorcycle. A 500 kg machine needs a truck. Third, small machines use less fuel. A 3 HP engine uses 0.5 litres per hour; a 10 HP engine uses 2 litres per hour. If you only have 2 hours of work, the small machine is cheaper. Fourth, small machines are less risky. If demand drops, you can sell it easily or use it for other tasks. A large machine may sit idle. Fifth, small machines are easier to repair. You can learn to fix them yourself. Large machines need specialised mechanics. When does a large machine win? When you have a guaranteed, high-volume contract. For example, if a large farm hires you to shell 50 tonnes of maize in one week, a large machine is better. But most small businesses start with small machines and upgrade later. A good strategy: start with a small machine, serve your local market, save profits, then buy a second small machine or a larger one when demand is proven. Do not buy a large machine hoping to find customers. Buy a small machine that you know you can keep busy. That is the safest path to profit..Trim()

Calculating the True Landed Cost

When you buy a small Chinese machine, the price you see on Alibaba or from a local dealer is never the final amount you pay. The true landed cost includes the machine price, shipping, import duties, clearing fees, transport to your workshop, and any minor repairs or missing parts. For example, a block-making machine advertised at $1,200 FOB (Free On Board) from China will cost you more. Sea freight to Mombasa or Lagos might be $300, import duty at 25% of the CIF value (cost, insurance, freight) adds another $375, clearing agent fees $150, and local transport $100. Your true landed cost is now $2,125 â nearly double the sticker price. Always ask for a CIF quote and then calculate duties yourself using your country's tariff code. In Kenya, for instance, many small machines fall under HS code 8474 with a 25% duty and 16% VAT. In Nigeria, duty can be 10-20% plus 7.5% VAT. Use a simple spreadsheet: list machine cost, freight, insurance, duty, VAT, clearing, transport, and a 10% contingency for surprises. If you are buying locally from a dealer, the landed cost is already baked into their price, but you should still ask what warranty and spare parts are included. Never forget the cost of power: a 3-phase machine may need a transformer or generator, adding $500-$1,500. Also budget for installation and training. A true landed cost calculation prevents you from sinking all your money into the machine and having nothing left to operate. Write down every number. If you cannot afford the true landed cost, you cannot afford the machine..Trim()

Micro-Leasing and Asset Financing Options

Micro-leasing is when a company buys the machine and rents it to you for a weekly or monthly fee, with the option to own it at the end. In many African countries, specialized asset finance companies like Rentworks in South Africa, or microfinance banks in Kenya and Nigeria, offer this. Banks in Africa rarely lend to small businesses without collateral. For a machine loan, the bank will ask for land, a building, a vehicle, or a fixed deposit. Bootstrapping means starting with what you have, not what you wish you had. If you only have $200, do not look at $2,000 machines. Look at a $150 manual block-making machine or a $100 popcorn maker. A contract protects you and your client. It doesn't have to be complicated. For small jobs, a one-page agreement is enough.
