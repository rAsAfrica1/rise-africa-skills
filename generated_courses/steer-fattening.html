Basic Anatomy and Physiology of Beef Cattle

To fatten steers well, you need to know their basic body parts and how they work. A steer is a castrated male bovine, usually raised for beef. Its digestive system is unique: it has a four-chambered stomach (rumen, reticulum, omasum, abomasum). The rumen is the largest and acts as a fermentation vat where microbes break down fibrous feeds like grass and hay. This is why cattle can eat roughages that humans cannot digest..Trim()

The rumen works best when cattle eat small amounts frequently. Sudden diet changes can cause acidosis, a serious condition. Always transition feeds over 7-10 days. Water is critical: a steer drinks 30-50 liters per day. Ensure clean water is always available. The liver, heart, and lungs are also important. A healthy steer has a steady appetite, clear eyes, and a shiny coat..Trim()

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a practical tool. Use a scale of 1 to 5, where 1 is emaciated and 5 is obese. For fattening, aim for a BCS of 3.5 to 4 at slaughter. Check the ribs, backbone, and tailhead. If ribs are visible, the steer needs more feed. If the tailhead is buried in fat, it's too fat. Weighing is better: use a weigh tape or scale. Average daily gain (ADG) of 1.0-1.5 kg per day is good..Trim()

The rumen is on the left side, so injections are often given in the neck. Knowing these basics prevents injuries and stress, which can reduce weight gain. Healthy, stress-free steers grow faster and yield better meat..Trim()

Objectives and Economic Importance of Fattening

Fattening steers is a business. The main objective is to add weight quickly and efficiently, turning cheap feed into high-value beef. A well-fattened steer can gain 1.2-1.5 kg per day. Over 90 days, that's over 100 kg of gain. If you buy a steer at 250 kg for $300 and sell at 350 kg for $500, you make $200, minus feed costs. With good management, profit margins can be 20-30%..Trim()

Economic importance goes beyond individual profit. Fattening creates jobs, improves local food supply, and adds value to crop residues. In many African countries, cattle are a store of wealth. Fattening allows farmers to sell when prices are high, rather than during droughts when prices crash. It also reduces the need for imports, saving foreign exchange..Trim()

Key objectives include: achieving target slaughter weight (e.g., 400-450 kg live weight), improving carcass quality (more muscle, less bone), and minimizing feed costs. Feed is 60-70% of total costs. Understanding Growth Curves and Fat Deposition

Cattle growth follows a curve: slow at birth, fast during adolescence, then slowing as they mature. The sigmoid curve shows weight gain over time. For fattening, you want to target the rapid growth phase, usually from 6 to 24 months. After that, feed efficiency drops. A typical steer gains 0.5 kg/day on pasture, but with supplementation, you can push it to 1.5 kg/day..Trim()

.Trim()

Fat deposition happens in phases. First, internal fat (around organs), then intermuscular fat (between muscles), then subcutaneous fat (under the skin), and finally intramuscular fat (marbling). Marbling is what gives beef its flavor and juiciness. Too much external fat is wasteful and costs money. Aim for a moderate fat cover: about 5-8 mm of backfat. Use a backfat scanner or simply feel the ribs and tailhead..Trim()

.Trim()

Growth rate is influenced by genetics, nutrition, and health. Feed a balanced diet with 12-14% crude protein and 60-70% total digestible nutrients (TDN). Energy is the main driver of fattening. Maize, molasses, and vegetable oils are good energy sources. Protein is needed for muscle growth. Provide minerals and vitamins, especially calcium, phosphorus, and vitamin A..Trim()

Monitor growth with regular weighing. Weigh every 2 weeks. Plot weights on a graph. If growth stalls, check for parasites, disease, or feed quality. Adjust the ration. The goal is to reach slaughter weight (400-450 kg) before the growth curve flattens. If you wait too long, feed costs rise without much gain. So sell at the right time..Trim()

Key Performance Indicators in Steer Fattening

To know if your fattening operation is profitable, track key performance indicators (KPIs). The most important are: average daily gain (ADG), feed conversion ratio (FCR), cost per kg gain, and mortality rate. ADG is weight gained per day. Target 1.0-1.5 kg/day. If ADG is below 0.8 kg, your steers are not performing. Weigh regularly to calculate ADG..Trim()

.Trim()

FCR is the amount of feed consumed per kg of weight gain. A good FCR is 6:1 to 8:1. If it takes 10 kg of feed to gain 1 kg, you are wasting money. Improve FCR by using high-quality feed, treating diseases early, and reducing stress. Cost per kg gain is total feed and health costs divided by total weight gain. Keep it below the market price per kg. In many African markets, if cost per kg gain is $1.50 and selling price is $3.00 per kg live weight, you are profitable..Trim()

.Trim()

Mortality rate should be under 2%. Each death is a total loss. Prevent diseases through vaccination (e.g., anthrax, blackleg), deworming, and tick control. Also track feed intake per day. A 300 kg steer eats about 2.5-3% of body weight in dry matter, so 7.5-9 kg of feed. If intake drops, investigate..Trim()

.Trim()

Other KPIs include days on feed, dressing percentage (carcass weight divided by live weight, usually 50-55%), and carcass grade. Use a notebook or simple spreadsheet to record daily. Review weekly. Use KPIs to make decisions: if FCR is poor, change the ration. If ADG is low, check health. Good records turn fattening from guesswork into a science..Trim()

Module 2 · Selection and Evaluation of Steers

Criteria for Selecting Steers for Fattening

When selecting steers for fattening, your goal is to buy animals that will gain weight quickly and efficiently. The best candidates are young, intact males (steers) that have been castrated early, typically before 6 months of age. Look for steers with a good appetite and a calm temperament. Age matters: steers between 12 and 24 months old are ideal because they are still growing and will put on muscle and fat rapidly. Avoid very old steers (over 3 years) as they convert feed less efficiently. Weight should be between 250 kg and 400 kg at purchase, depending on breed and target market. In Africa, common fattening breeds include Boran, Sahiwal, and crossbreeds with exotic breeds like Holstein or Angus. These breeds respond well to improved feeding. Health status is critical: only buy steers that are alert, have bright eyes, and no signs of diarrhea or coughing. Check for lameness and abscesses. Also consider the source: steers from known farms with vaccination records are safer than those from open markets where disease risk is higher. Finally, assess body condition: steers with a body condition score (BCS) of 2 to 3 on a 1-5 scale (where 1 is emaciated and 5 is obese) are ideal. They have some fat cover but are not overly fat, so they will gain efficiently. Avoid extremely thin steers (BCS 1) as they may have health issues and require costly recovery. Selecting the right steers at the start saves you money on feed and veterinary bills later..Trim()

Evaluating Body Condition and Frame Size

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a hands-on method to assess fat cover on a steer. Use a 1-5 scale: 1 = emaciated (bones prominent, no fat), 2 = thin (some fat over ribs, but ribs easily felt), 3 = moderate (ribs felt with slight pressure, fat cover over tailhead), 4 = fat (ribs hard to feel, fat deposits visible), 5 = obese (heavy fat cover, mobility issues). For fattening, aim for steers with BCS 2 to 3. They have enough energy reserves to start gaining but are not too fat, which would reduce feed efficiency. To check BCS, run your hands over the ribs, spine, and tailhead. Feel for the amount of fat. Also assess frame size: steers with a larger frame (taller, longer body) can grow to heavier weights before finishing. Frame score is often estimated by hip height at a given age. For example, a 12-month-old steer with a hip height of 120 cm is considered medium-frame; 130 cm is large-frame. In Africa, many local breeds are smaller-framed, but crossbreeds can be larger. Choose steers with a frame size that matches your market: if you sell to local butcheries, a 350-400 kg finished weight is fine; for premium markets, you may need 450-500 kg, requiring larger-framed steers. Also evaluate muscle thickness: look at the width of the stance, the roundness of the hindquarters, and the thickness of the forearm. A steer with good muscle and moderate fat will yield more meat. Remember, frame size and body condition together determine how long you need to feed and the final weight. Always handle the steers before purchase to evaluate both..Trim()

Assessing Health and Soundness

A healthy steer is a profitable steer. Before buying, inspect each animal carefully. Start with the eyes: they should be bright, clear, and free of discharge. Cloudy eyes or excessive tearing can indicate pinkeye or respiratory disease. Check the nose for discharge; a runny nose with coughing suggests pneumonia. Listen for breathing: normal breathing is quiet and regular. Rapid or labored breathing is a red flag. Examine the mouth for sores or excessive salivation, which could be foot-and-mouth disease. Look at the coat: it should be smooth and shiny. A dull, rough coat often means internal parasites or poor nutrition. Check the skin for lumps, wounds, or abscesses. Any open wound can become infected and reduce weight gain. Next, assess mobility: have the steer walk on a flat surface. It should move freely without limping. Lameness can be caused by foot rot, abscesses, or injuries, and will reduce feed intake. Inspect the hooves for cracks or swelling. Also check the anus for signs of diarrhea, which could be due to worms or bacterial infections. Finally, look at the overall posture: a healthy steer stands alert with head up. A depressed steer with droopy ears and head down is likely sick. In Africa, common diseases to watch for include East Coast fever (caused by ticks), trypanosomiasis (spread by tsetse flies), and foot-and-mouth disease. If possible, ask for vaccination records. If buying at a market, avoid animals that have been transported long distances and are stressed. Quarantine new steers for at least 14 days before mixing with your herd. During quarantine, observe them daily and treat any health issues immediately. A thorough health check saves you from buying a problem that will cost more than the steer itself..Trim()

Genetic Considerations and Expected Progeny Differences

Genetics determine how fast a steer grows, how efficiently it converts feed, and the quality of its meat. When selecting steers, you cannot change their genetics, but you can choose animals with superior genetic potential. In Africa, indigenous breeds like Boran, N'Dama, and Ankole have good heat tolerance and disease resistance but slower growth. Crossbreeds with exotic breeds like Angus, Hereford, or Simmental can grow faster and reach heavier weights. If you are buying steers for fattening, look for crossbreeds that combine local hardiness with exotic growth. Expected Progeny Differences (EPDs) are tools used by breeders to predict the genetic merit of an animal as a parent. For fattening steers, you are not breeding, but EPDs can still indicate the steer's own genetic potential for growth. Key EPDs include birth weight (lower is better for calving ease, but not relevant for steers), weaning weight (higher means faster early growth), yearling weight (higher means faster overall growth), and carcass weight (higher means more meat). If you buy from a registered breeder, ask for EPDs. For example, a steer with a yearling weight EPD of +30 kg is expected to be 30 kg heavier at one year than the breed average. However, EPDs are only useful if the breeder has accurate records. In many African markets, such data is scarce. Instead, you can assess genetics by looking at the steer's parents if available, or by choosing steers from herds known for good growth. Also consider hybrid vigor (heterosis): crossbred steers often outperform purebreds in growth and health. For small-scale fatteners, a simple strategy is to buy crossbred steers from reputable farms. Avoid mixing breeds with different mature sizes if you want uniform batches. Remember, genetics set the ceiling for performance; nutrition and management determine how close you get to that ceiling..Trim()

Purchasing Strategies: Auctions, Direct Sales, and Contracts

Where you buy your steers affects price, health, and convenience. Three common methods in Africa are auctions, direct sales, and contracts. Auctions: Livestock auctions are held in many towns. You can buy steers at competitive prices, but there are risks. Animals may be stressed from transport and mixing, increasing disease risk. You also have limited time to inspect. To succeed at auctions, arrive early, inspect animals before the sale, set a maximum price, and stick to it. Check for auctioneer fees (often 5-10% of the sale price). Direct sales: Buying directly from a farmer or feedlot allows you to inspect the animals in their home environment. You can negotiate price, ask about health and feeding history, and build a relationship. This is often the best option for beginners. Visit the farm, check records, and observe the steers. Agree on a price per kilogram live weight or per head. For example, in Kenya, a 300 kg steer might sell for KES 200-250 per kg live weight (about USD 1.50-2.00). Contracts: Some large feedlots or meat processors offer contract farming. You agree to raise steers to a certain weight and they buy at a predetermined price. This reduces market risk but may lock you into a low price. Contracts often require you to follow specific feeding and health protocols. Read the fine print. For all methods, ensure you get a receipt and health certificate if possible. Also consider transport costs: buying from a distant market may be cheaper but add transport and stress. Always quarantine new arrivals for 14 days. If buying in bulk, try to source from a single herd to reduce disease spread. Finally, timing matters: prices are often lower during dry seasons when farmers sell off animals. Buy then and fatten during the wet season when feed is cheaper..Trim()

Transport and Handling of Newly Purchased Steers

Transport is a critical period that can make or break your fattening operation. Stress during transport leads to weight loss, disease, and even death. Plan ahead to minimize stress. Before loading, ensure steers are healthy and have been off feed for 6-8 hours (but have access to water). This reduces defecation and urine in the truck. Use a well-ventilated truck with non-slip flooring. Cover the floor with sand or wood shavings for grip. Load steers calmly; shouting and hitting will stress them. Use flags or sticks to guide them. Do not overload: allow about 1.5 to 2 square meters per adult steer. For a 300 kg steer, that's roughly 1.5 mÂ². If traveling long distances (over 4 hours), stop every 4-6 hours to check animals and offer water. In hot African climates, transport during early morning or evening to avoid heat stress. Never transport in the heat of the day. If possible, use a truck with a roof for shade. During the journey, avoid sudden braking and sharp turns. Upon arrival, unload gently into a clean, dry quarantine pen. Provide fresh water and good quality hay. Do not feed concentrates immediately; wait 12-24 hours to avoid digestive upset. Observe for signs of stress: rapid breathing, drooling, or collapse. If any steer shows these, consult a vet. Keep new steers separate from your herd for at least 14 days. During quarantine, monitor temperature and appetite daily. Treat for internal and external parasites as advised by a vet. Vaccinate if not already done. Handle steers quietly and patiently; they learn routines. Use a race or crush for restraint when needed. Remember, good handling reduces bruises and injuries, which lowers meat quality. A calm steer gains weight faster. So invest time in proper transport and handling; it pays off in better health and performance..Trim()

Types of Growth Promotants: Implants and Beta-Agonists

Growth promotants are tools that help cattle grow faster and convert feed more efficiently. In steer fattening, the two main types used in Africa are hormonal implants and beta-agonists. Hormonal implants are small pellets placed under the skin of the ear. They release natural or synthetic hormones like estradiol, testosterone, or progesterone. These hormones tell the steer's body to deposit more lean muscle and less fat. Common brands include Revalor, Synovex, and Compudose. For example, a single Revalor-S implant can increase daily weight gain by 0.2 to 0.3 kg and improve feed efficiency by 8-12% over 90 to 120 days. Implants are widely used in South Africa, Namibia, and Botswana, but are banned in the European Union. Beta-agonists are feed additives, not implants. They are drugs like zilpaterol (Zilmax) and ractopamine (Paylean). They are fed during the last 20 to 40 days before slaughter. They redirect nutrients to muscle growth, increasing carcass weight by 5 to 15 kg and improving feed efficiency. However, beta-agonists are controversial. Ractopamine is banned in the EU, China, and Russia. In Africa, only South Africa has approved zilpaterol for use under strict withdrawal periods. Many small-scale farmers avoid both implants and beta-agonists because of market restrictions and consumer concerns. Instead, they focus on good nutrition, health, and genetics. If you plan to use growth promotants, first check your local regulations and your buyer's requirements. For example, if you sell to a local butcher who exports to Europe, you cannot use implants or beta-agonists. If you sell to a local feedlot or butcher within South Africa, you may use them legally. Always keep records of what you use and when. This helps you avoid residue problems and meet market demands. Remember, growth promotants are not a substitute for poor management. They work best when cattle are healthy, well-fed, and have good genetics..Trim()

Regulations and Residue Avoidance

Using growth promotants without following regulations can get you into serious trouble. In Africa, rules differ by country. South Africa allows hormonal implants and beta-agonists like zilpaterol, but only with a veterinary prescription and strict withdrawal periods. Namibia and Botswana allow implants but not beta-agonists. Kenya, Nigeria, and most other African countries ban hormonal implants entirely. The European Union bans all hormonal implants and beta-agonists, and any meat exported there must be free of residues. Residues are traces of the drug left in the meat. To avoid residues, you must follow the withdrawal period exactly. For implants, there is no withdrawal period because the hormones are released slowly and are natural. For beta-agonists like zilpaterol, the withdrawal period is typically 3 to 7 days before slaughter. If you slaughter too early, the drug will still be in the meat. Keep a record for each animal: date implanted or fed, product name, dose, and withdrawal end date. Use a calendar or notebook. Before selling, check that the withdrawal period has passed. If you sell to a feedlot or abattoir, they will test for residues. If found, you may be fined or banned from selling. In some countries, residue testing is done at slaughterhouses. Implanting Techniques and Timing

Implanting is a simple procedure, but it must be done correctly to work and to avoid problems. You need a clean implant gun, a sharp needle, and the implants themselves. The implant site is the middle third of the ear, between the skin and the cartilage. Do not implant near the head or the tip of the ear. Load the implant gun with the correct number of pellets. For example, Revalor-S has 10 pellets per dose. Push the needle through the skin at a shallow angle, then pull back slightly to create a pocket. Deposit the pellets in a straight line under the skin. Implants are most effective when given to growing cattle. For steer fattening, implant at the start of the fattening period, usually when the steer weighs 250 to 300 kg. Also, do not implant sick cattle or cattle with ear infections. Wait until they are healthy. Use a new needle for each animal or disinfect between animals to prevent spreading disease. Growth promotants can significantly improve growth rate and feed efficiency, but the effects vary. Hormonal implants typically increase average daily gain (ADG) by 0.1 to 0.3 kg per day. Alternatives to Hormonal Growth Promotants

If you cannot or do not want to use hormonal growth promotants, there are many alternatives to improve steer growth and feed efficiency. The most important is good nutrition. Poor Growth Rates: Causes and Solutions

If your steers are not gaining at least 1.2 kg per day, you are losing money. First, weigh your animals monthly using a weigh tape or scale. Poor growth often starts with feed quality. Test your feed for protein and energy. A good fattening ration should have 12-14% crude protein and 70-75% total digestible nutrients. If you are using maize stover or poor hay, the steers cannot grow. Supplement with cottonseed cake, molasses, and urea-treated straw. For example, treat 100 kg of chopped straw with 4 kg urea dissolved in 40 liters of water, cover for 21 days, then feed. Second, check for internal parasites. Deworm every 3 months with albendazole or ivermectin. A heavy worm load can reduce growth by 30%. Third, ensure fresh water is always available. A steer drinks 30-50 liters per day. If water is dirty or too far, intake drops. Fourth, look at stocking density. Give each steer at least 10 square meters of pen space. Overcrowding causes stress and reduces feed intake. Fifth, check teeth and feet. Broken teeth or foot rot make eating painful. Fix these issues quickly. Finally, consider breed. Local zebu crosses gain slower than exotic crosses like Boran or Sahiwal. If you want faster growth, upgrade your genetics gradually. Track your feed conversion: it takes about 6-8 kg of feed to produce 1 kg of gain. If it is higher, adjust your ration. Keep records of weight, feed used, and costs. Without records, you cannot know what works. Act on the worst problem first: usually feed quality or parasites..Trim()

Digestive Upsets: Bloat and Acidosis

Bloat and acidosis are killers. They happen when you change feed too fast or feed too much grain. Bloat is a build-up of gas in the rumen. The left side of the belly swells up like a balloon. The steer stops eating, kicks at its belly, and breathes fast. If you do not act, it dies in hours. Immediate treatment: drench with 500 ml of vegetable oil or mineral oil to break the foam. Then walk the steer for 10-15 minutes. If that fails, a vet can insert a trocar into the rumen to release gas. To prevent bloat, introduce lush green feeds like lucerne or clover gradually over 7-10 days. Never turn hungry steers onto wet, frosty pasture. Acidosis happens when steers eat too much starch (maize, sorghum) at once. The rumen becomes acidic (pH below 5.5). Symptoms: loose dung, no cud chewing, off-feed, and staggering. Treat with 200 g of baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) in 5 liters of water as a drench. Then feed only hay for 24 hours. To prevent acidosis, always feed roughage first, then grain. Limit grain to 1% of body weight per day, split into two meals. For a 300 kg steer, that is 3 kg of grain per day. Increase grain slowly over 3 weeks. Add 1% bicarbonate to the grain mix if you feed high grain. Also, use ionophores like monensin (Rumensin) at 200 mg per head per day to reduce acidosis risk. Always have clean water available. If you see bloat or acidosis, act immediately. Delay costs you the animal..Trim()

Lameness and Foot Problems

A lame steer cannot walk to feed or water, so it loses weight fast. The most common causes are foot rot, foot abscess, and laminitis. Foot rot starts with swelling between the toes, bad smell, and pain. It happens when steers stand in wet, muddy pens. Treat foot rot by cleaning the hoof with water and antiseptic (like iodine), then inject long-acting oxytetracycline (20 mg per kg body weight) for 3-5 days. Also, trim the hoof to drain pus. For an abscess, you may need to open it with a clean knife and flush with hydrogen peroxide. Laminitis is inflammation of the hoof wall, often from too much grain. The hoof becomes hot and the steer walks on its heels. Treat by removing all grain for 3 days and feeding only hay. Give 200 g of baking soda in water. To prevent lameness, keep pens dry. Use concrete or gravel in high-traffic areas. Scrape manure daily. Provide a footbath with 5% copper sulfate solution at the entrance to the pen. Trim hooves every 6 months using hoof nippers and a rasp. Check hooves weekly for cuts or stones. If you see a steer limping, separate it and examine the foot. Do not ignore it. Cost of Production and Break-Even Analysis

To make money fattening steers, you must know your costs. Start by listing all expenses for one steer from purchase to sale. Example: buying a 250 kg steer at $2.50/kg = $625. Feed for 120 days: 8 kg/day of a mix costing $0.30/kg = $288. Veterinary and drugs: $20. Labour: $30. Transport: $25. Total cost = $988. If you sell at 400 kg and get $3.00/kg, revenue = $1,200. Profit = $212 per steer. But prices change. Break-even is the sale price where revenue equals total cost. Break-even price = total cost / final weight. Here, $988 / 400 kg = $2.47/kg. If market price falls below $2.47, you lose money. To lower break-even, reduce feed costs. For example, grow your own maize and silage. Or buy steers when prices are low. Also, improve feed conversion: if you use 7 kg/day instead of 8, feed cost drops to $252, total cost $952, break-even $2.38/kg. Keep records daily. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Farmbrite. Track every bag of feed, every dose, every hour of labour. At the end of each batch, calculate your actual cost per kg gain. Compare with other farmers. If your cost is higher, find out why. Maybe your steers are sick, or feed is wasted. Break-even analysis helps you decide whether to sell now or wait. If market price is $2.60 and your break-even is $2.47, you make $0.13/kg profit. But if you expect price to rise to $2.80 in two weeks, you could waitâbut you must add the extra feed cost for those two weeks. Always calculate before you act. Risk Management: Insurance and Futures
