Module 1 · Why Bread Is Daily Cash

Bread is not just food; it is a daily cash machine. In almost every African community, from Lagos to Nairobi to Accra, people buy bread every single day. Unlike a wedding cake or a birthday cake, bread is bought by the same customers over and over. That means if you bake good bread, you don't need to find new customers every day. .Trim()

Think about your own street. How many people eat bread for breakfast? How many buy it on the way to work? How many send children to the shop for a loaf in the evening? Step by Step Method

Baking bread is a science, but it is also simple. Follow these steps for a basic white bread recipe that makes 10 loaves..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 1: Gather and measure. For 10 loaves, you need 5 kg of flour, 50 g of yeast, 100 g of salt, 200 g of sugar, 2.5 litres of warm water, and 100 ml of oil. Use your scale. Don't guess..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 2: Activate the yeast. In a small bowl, mix yeast, sugar, and 1 cup of warm water (not hot, or it will kill the yeast). Let it sit for 10 minutes until it foams. This tells you the yeast is alive..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 3: Mix the dough. In a large bowl, put flour and salt. Make a well in the center. Pour in the yeast mixture, remaining water, and oil. Mix with your hands or a spoon until a sticky dough forms..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 4: Knead. Turn the dough onto a floured table. Knead for 10 to 15 minutes. Push, fold, turn, repeat. The dough should become smooth and elastic. If it sticks, add a little flour. If it's too dry, add a little water..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 5: First rise. Put the dough in a lightly oiled bowl, cover with a damp cloth, and let it rise in a warm place for 1 to 2 hours. It should double in size..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 6: Punch down and shape. Punch the dough to release air. Divide into 10 equal pieces. Shape each into a loaf and place in greased loaf pans..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 7: Second rise. Cover and let rise again for 30 to 45 minutes..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 8: Bake. Preheat oven to 200°C (400°F). Bake for 25 to 30 minutes until golden brown. The bottom should sound hollow when tapped..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 9: Cool and package. Remove from pans and cool on a rack. Once cool, bag and label with date. Sell same day for best quality..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, consistency is key. Use the same recipe, same measurements, same oven temperature. That way, your customers get the same great bread every day..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced bakers make mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 1: Yeast not foaming. You mix yeast with water and nothing happens. Fix: Your water was too hot (above 45°C) or too cold, or the yeast is expired. Use lukewarm water and check the expiry date. Always proof your yeast before mixing..Trim()

Mistake 4: Bread is dense and heavy. Fix: You didn't knead enough or didn't let it rise long enough. Knead for at least 10 minutes and give it the full rising time. Also, check your flour�??old flour can cause dense bread..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 8: Selling stale bread. Fix: Bake daily. If you have leftovers, sell them as day-old at a discount or make breadcrumbs. Never sell stale bread as fresh..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Safety in the bakery is not just about avoiding accidents; it is about producing clean, safe bread that won't make your customers sick. Here are the key safety and best practices..Trim()

.Trim()

Personal hygiene: Always wash your hands with soap before handling dough. Wear a clean apron, hairnet, and if possible, gloves. Tie back long hair. Don't bake if you are sick, especially with cough, cold, or stomach issues. Cover any cuts with a waterproof bandage..Trim()

.Trim()

Clean workspace: Keep your tables, tools, and floor clean. Wash mixing bowls, trays, and utensils after every use. Use food-safe sanitizer. Don't let raw dough sit on surfaces for hours. Clean up spills immediately to avoid slips..Trim()

.Trim()

Food safety: Store flour in a dry, cool place in sealed containers. Keep yeast in the fridge or a cool dark place. Check expiry dates on all ingredients. Don't use mouldy flour or expired yeast. Use clean water. Don't mix old dough with new dough unless you know what you're doing (some bakers use old dough as a starter, but it must be handled properly)..Trim()

.Trim()

Oven safety: Gas ovens can leak. Check for gas smells. Never light an oven with a match if you smell gas. Use the built-in igniter or a long lighter. Keep flammable items away from the oven. Use oven mitts when handling hot trays. Have a fire extinguisher nearby and know how to use it..Trim()

.Trim()

Knife and tool safety: Keep knives sharp; dull knives slip. Cut away from your body. Store knives safely. Don't leave tools on the floor..Trim()

.Trim()

Best practices for quality: Measure accurately. Use a thermometer to check water temperature (35-40°C for yeast). Keep a baking log: date, time, temperature, and results. This helps you repeat success. Cool bread completely before packaging to prevent sweating and mould. Label with date and price. Rotate stock: sell older bread first..Trim()

.Trim()

Customer safety: Never sell bread that is burnt, undercooked, or dropped on the floor. If in doubt, throw it out. Your reputation is your business. A single case of food poisoning can destroy your bakery..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, train your workers. Everyone who touches dough must know these rules. Safety is a habit. Make it part of your daily routine..Trim()

Module 2 · The Bakery Production Formula

Introduction and Why It Matters

Welcome to Module 2. If you want to sell bread daily and make a steady profit, you cannot guess. You need a formula. The Bakery Production Formula is a simple set of steps that tells you exactly how much flour, water, yeast, salt, and sugar to use for any number of loaves. Without it, you waste ingredients, your bread comes out different every day, and customers stop buying. With it, you produce the same good bread every single time, so people trust you and come back..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does this matter in Africa? Because most small bakeries here operate by 'feel' �?? a cup of this, a pinch of that. That works for home cooking, but for business, it fails. If you use too much yeast, your bread rises too fast and collapses. Too little salt, and the bread tastes flat. Too much water, and the dough is sticky and hard to shape. The formula fixes all that. It lets you scale up or down. Want to bake 10 loaves? Use the formula. Want 50? Multiply. You control your costs and your quality..Trim()

.Trim()

Think about your profit. Flour is your biggest expense. If you use 10% more flour than needed because you didn't measure, you lose money on every loaf. The formula prevents that. It also helps you plan. You know that one bag of flour (50 kg) makes about 80 loaves of standard bread. So if you sell 80 loaves a day, you need one bag. That's how you order supplies and manage cash flow..Trim()

.Trim()

In this module, we will cover the tools you need, the step-by-step method, common mistakes, safety, and a practical exercise. By the end, you will be able to write your own production sheet for any size order. Remember: baking is chemistry, but business is math. The formula combines both. Let's get started..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To follow the bakery production formula, you need the right tools and materials. You don't need expensive equipment, but you must have accurate measuring tools. A digital kitchen scale that measures in grams is the most important tool. Cups and spoons are not accurate enough for business baking. A scale costs about 5,000 to 15,000 Naira (or equivalent) and will save you money in wasted ingredients. You also need a large mixing bowl or a kneading table. A wooden table is best because dough doesn't stick as much. If you don't have a table, a clean plastic basin works..Trim()

.Trim()

For measuring liquids, use a graduated jug. For yeast, use a small spoon and the scale. For salt and sugar, the scale again. You need a dough scraper (a flat metal or plastic tool) to cut and lift dough. A rolling pin is optional for some breads, but useful for shaping. You need baking pans or trays. In many African bakeries, loaves are baked on flat trays or in locally made metal pans. Make sure they are clean and greased. You also need a way to control temperature: a thermometer for the oven if possible, or at least a reliable oven with a dial..Trim()

.Trim()

Ingredients: flour (bread flour or all-purpose, but bread flour gives better rise), water (clean, preferably filtered), yeast (instant dry yeast is easiest), salt (iodized or non-iodized, but consistent), sugar (optional, but helps browning and yeast activity), and oil or fat (vegetable oil or butter). You may also use milk powder for softer bread. For African markets, you can find these in bulk at lower cost. Buy yeast in small packets to keep it fresh. Store flour in a dry, cool place, off the floor, in a sealed container to prevent weevils..Trim()

.Trim()

Other useful items: a timer or clock, a notebook to record your formulas and results, and a clean apron. A dough cutter (bench knife) helps divide dough evenly. If you plan to sell, you need packaging: bread bags, twist ties, and labels. Keep everything clean. A dirty workspace ruins bread. Soap, water, and a clean cloth are part of your tool kit. With these basics, you can produce consistent bread daily..Trim()

Step by Step Method

The bakery production formula is based on baker's percentage. This means flour is always 100%. Every other ingredient is a percentage of the flour weight. For a standard white bread, the formula is: Flour 100%, Water 60%, Yeast 1.5%, Salt 2%, Sugar 2%, Oil 2%. So if you use 1 kg of flour, you need 600 g water, 15 g yeast, 20 g salt, 20 g sugar, and 20 g oil. This makes about 1.6 kg of dough, which yields two 800g loaves or three 500g loaves. Always weigh, don't guess..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 1: Weigh all ingredients. Use your scale. Put flour in a large bowl. In a separate jug, mix water (lukewarm, about 30-35°C �?? if you don't have a thermometer, it should feel like baby bath water), yeast, sugar, and salt. Stir to dissolve. Let it sit 5 minutes until foamy. This proves the yeast is alive. If it doesn't foam, your yeast is dead �?? get new yeast..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 2: Make a well in the flour. Pour the liquid mixture in. Add oil. Mix with your hands or a wooden spoon until a shaggy dough forms. Then knead on a clean table for 10-15 minutes. Kneading develops gluten, which makes bread chewy and helps it rise. The dough should become smooth and elastic. If it's too sticky, add a little flour (but not too much �?? too much flour makes dry bread). If too dry, add water a spoon at a time..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 3: First rise (proof). Put dough in a lightly oiled bowl, cover with a clean cloth or plastic, and leave in a warm place (25-30°C) for 1 hour, or until doubled in size. In hot African climates, it may take only 45 minutes. Don't let it over-rise; if it collapses, it's over-proofed..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 4: Punch down. Knock the air out. Divide into equal pieces using your scale. Shape into balls or loaves. Place in greased pans. Cover and let rise again for 30-45 minutes until nearly doubled..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 5: Bake. Preheat oven to 200°C (400°F). Bake for 30-35 minutes for 800g loaves, or 20-25 minutes for smaller rolls. The bread is done when golden brown and sounds hollow when tapped on the bottom. Remove from pans and cool on a wire rack. Don't slice hot bread �?? it gets gummy. This method gives you consistent bread every time..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even with the formula, things can go wrong. Here are the most common mistakes and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Not measuring accurately. If you use cups instead of a scale, your bread will vary. Fix: Buy a digital scale and use grams for everything. Write down your formula and stick to it..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 2: Water too hot. Hot water kills yeast. If the water is above 45°C, yeast dies and dough won't rise. Fix: Use lukewarm water (30-35°C). Test with your wrist �?? it should feel neither hot nor cold. If you don't have a thermometer, err on the cooler side; yeast works slower but survives..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 3: Too much flour during kneading. Adding flour because dough is sticky seems right, but it makes dry, dense bread. Fix: Lightly oil your hands and table instead of flouring. Let the dough rest 10 minutes �?? it absorbs water and becomes less sticky..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 4: Under-kneading or over-kneading. Under-kneaded dough doesn't develop gluten, so bread is crumbly. Over-kneaded dough is tough. Fix: Knead for 10-15 minutes by hand. The dough should be smooth and spring back when poked. If you have a mixer, 8-10 minutes on medium..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 5: Skipping the second rise. Many bakers shape and bake immediately. That gives small, dense loaves. Fix: Always let shaped dough rise again until nearly doubled, about 30-45 minutes. Cover it to prevent drying..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 6: Oven temperature wrong. If oven is too hot, bread browns outside but is raw inside. Too cool, and it doesn't rise well. Fix: Use an oven thermometer. If you don't have one, test with a piece of bread: it should toast in 1 minute at 200°C. Preheat for at least 15 minutes..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 7: Not cooling properly. Cutting hot bread makes it gummy. Fix: Cool completely on a wire rack for at least 30 minutes. This also lets the crust set..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 8: Storing bread wrongly. Bread in plastic gets soggy; in paper it dries out. Fix: For daily sales, keep bread in paper bags or breathable cloth. For longer storage, freeze. Never store in direct sun. By avoiding these mistakes, your bread will be consistent and sellable..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Safety in the bakery is not just about avoiding burns �?? it's about producing clean, safe food that won't make customers sick. Start with personal hygiene. Wash your hands with soap and water before touching any ingredients. Tie back hair, wear a clean apron, and avoid jewelry that can fall into dough. If you are sick, especially with stomach issues, don't bake for others. Your hands carry bacteria..Trim()

.Trim()

Workspace safety: Keep floors dry to prevent slips. Clean up spills immediately. Store knives and sharp tools safely. When using the oven, use thick dry cloths or oven mitts. Never use wet cloths �?? steam burns. Open oven doors slowly to avoid a blast of hot air. Keep children away from the oven area..Trim()

.Trim()

Ingredient safety: Check expiration dates on yeast, flour, and oil. Old yeast won't rise. Rancid oil smells bad and ruins bread. Store ingredients in sealed containers, off the floor, away from chemicals. Never use the same container for soap and flour. If you have pests, use traps, not poison near food..Trim()

.Trim()

Food safety: Bread can grow mold if stored damp. For daily sales, bake fresh each day. If you have leftovers, sell them at a discount the same day or turn them into breadcrumbs. Do not sell moldy bread �?? it's dangerous and destroys your reputation. Keep samples covered. Use clean packaging. If you handle money, wash hands before touching bread again..Trim()

Dough making and fermentation are the heart of your bakery. If you get this stage wrong, no amount of baking skill will save your bread. In Africa, where many bakers start small, understanding fermentation can mean the difference between a profitable business and daily losses. Fermentation is the process where yeast eats sugars in the flour and produces gas (carbon dioxide) that makes dough rise, plus alcohol and acids that build flavour. Without proper fermentation, your bread will be flat, dense, and taste like cardboard. With it, you get light, airy loaves that customers love..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does this matter for your daily bread sales? Because consistency sells. If your bread tastes different every day, customers will go elsewhere. Fermentation also affects shelf life. Well-fermented dough develops natural preservatives that keep bread fresh longer, reducing waste. In hot African climates, fermentation happens faster, so you must control time and temperature carefully. A dough that over-ferments becomes sour and sticky; under-fermented dough is tough and doesn't rise well in the oven..Trim()

.Trim()

You don't need fancy equipment. A clean bowl, a cloth, and your hands are enough. But you do need to understand the signs: when dough is ready, it feels soft, springs back slowly when poked, and smells slightly yeasty, not like alcohol. In this module, you'll learn exactly how to mix, knead, and ferment dough using local ingredients like wheat flour, water, salt, and yeast (or natural starters). We'll cover tools, step-by-step methods, common mistakes, safety, and a practical exercise. By the end, you'll be able to produce consistent, high-quality dough every single day, which means consistent sales and happy customers. Remember: good bread starts long before the oven..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To make dough and ferment it properly, you need a few basic tools and quality ingredients. You don't need a commercial mixer to start. A large plastic or wooden bowl (at least 5 litres) works well. Wood is traditional and holds warmth, which helps fermentation in cooler mornings. You'll also need a clean cloth or plastic wrap to cover the dough, a wooden spoon or your hands for mixing, and a flat surface for kneading�??a clean table or a wooden board. A dough scraper (a simple plastic card or metal blade) helps handle sticky dough. For measuring, a simple kitchen scale is best, but if you don't have one, use consistent cups and spoons. A thermometer is useful to check water temperature; aim for lukewarm (about 30-35°C)..Trim()

.Trim()

Ingredients: flour, water, salt, and yeast. In Africa, you can find strong bread flour (often imported or local brands like 'Snowflake' or 'Azam'). For yeast, you can use instant dry yeast (e.g., Saf-Instant) or fresh yeast. If you want natural fermentation, you can make a sourdough starter from flour and water, but that takes days. For daily sales, instant yeast is reliable and fast. Salt is important for flavour and controlling fermentation�??use fine table salt, not coarse. Water should be clean and drinkable. If your tap water is heavily chlorinated, let it sit overnight to dissipate chlorine, which can kill yeast..Trim()

.Trim()

Optional but helpful: a dough divider (to cut equal pieces), a proofing basket (or a bowl lined with a floured cloth), and a spray bottle for water. Keep everything clean and dry. In hot climates, fermentation can run away, so a cool spot (like a shaded corner) or even a refrigerator can slow it down. For a small bakery, invest in a digital scale and a thermometer�??they cost little and prevent costly mistakes. Remember, tools don't make bread; knowledge does. But the right tools make consistency easier..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Follow these steps for a basic white bread dough that yields about 4 loaves (each 500g). You'll need: 1 kg strong bread flour, 600 ml lukewarm water (30-35°C), 20 g salt, and 10 g instant dry yeast (or 30 g fresh yeast). First, measure flour into your bowl. Add salt and yeast on opposite sides (salt can kill yeast if directly mixed). Make a well in the centre. Pour in most of the water. Mix with a wooden spoon or your hand until a shaggy mass forms. Add remaining water if needed�??dough should be slightly sticky but not runny..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, knead. Turn dough onto a lightly floured surface. Knead for 8-10 minutes by hand: push with the heel of your hand, fold, turn, repeat. In hot climates, work quickly to avoid over-warming. Dough is ready when smooth, elastic, and springs back when poked. If using a mixer, knead on low for 5-7 minutes. Third, first fermentation (bulk rise). Place dough in a lightly oiled bowl, cover with a damp cloth or plastic wrap. Leave in a warm spot (25-30°C) for 1 to 1.5 hours, until doubled in size. In African heat, check after 45 minutes. To test, poke with a floured finger: if indentation stays, it's ready..Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, punch down and shape. Gently deflate dough, divide into 4 equal pieces (use a scale). Shape each into a round or loaf. Place on a floured baking tray or in proofing baskets. Fifth, second fermentation (proofing). Cover and let rest 30-45 minutes, until slightly puffed. Don't over-proof; if it collapses when touched, it's too far. Meanwhile, preheat oven to 220°C. Sixth, bake. Score tops with a sharp knife, bake for 25-30 minutes until golden and hollow-sounding when tapped. Cool on a wire rack. This method works for daily sales: start early, keep notes on time and temperature, and adjust as you learn your local conditions..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced bakers make mistakes. Here are the most common dough and fermentation problems in African bakeries, and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Dough doesn't rise. Causes: old yeast, water too hot (above 40°C kills yeast), or too cold (below 20°C slows it). Fix: Check yeast expiry, use thermometer, and find a warm spot (e.g., near a stove but not on it). If your kitchen is cold at night, use a warm water bath or an insulated box. Mistake 2: Dough is too sticky. Causes: too much water, not enough kneading, or flour with low protein. Fix: Add flour a spoonful at a time while kneading, but don't overdo it. Knead longer to develop gluten. Use strong bread flour, not cake flour..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 3: Dough over-ferments. Signs: sour smell, collapsed top, sticky and wet. In hot climates, this happens fast. Fix: Reduce fermentation time, use cooler water, or refrigerate dough after mixing (cold retardation). For daily sales, mix in the evening and let it rise slowly in a cool place, then bake in the morning. Mistake 4: Bread is dense and heavy. Causes: under-fermentation, under-kneading, or too much salt. Fix: Ensure dough doubles in size, knead until elastic, and measure salt accurately (2% of flour weight). Mistake 5: Bread tastes sour or yeasty. Causes: too much yeast or long fermentation. Fix: Reduce yeast to 1% of flour weight, and shorten bulk rise. If you want sourdough flavour, use a starter, not extra yeast..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 6: Dough dries out during fermentation. Fix: Always cover with a damp cloth or oiled plastic. In dry harmattan winds, place a bowl of water in the fermentation area. Mistake 7: Uneven loaves. Fix: Use a scale to divide dough equally, and shape tightly. Remember: fermentation is affected by altitude, humidity, and temperature. Keep a notebook. If a batch fails, note what you did. Adjust next time. Consistency comes from learning from mistakes, not avoiding them..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Bakery work involves heat, sharp tools, and raw ingredients. Safety first: always wash your hands with soap before handling dough. Keep your work area clean. Raw flour can contain bacteria like E. coli, so don't eat raw dough. Clean spills immediately to avoid slips. When using ovens, use gloves and keep a fire extinguisher nearby. In many African bakeries, ovens are wood-fired or gas; ensure proper ventilation to avoid carbon monoxide. Never leave a hot oven unattended. For dough making, be careful with sharp knives when scoring bread. Keep children away from hot surfaces and electrical equipment..Trim()

.Trim()

Best practices for fermentation: maintain a consistent temperature. Yeast is a living organism; sudden temperature changes shock it. Use lukewarm water (30-35°C) always. If you use a refrigerator for cold retardation, allow dough to come to room temperature before shaping. Keep a fermentation log: record time, room temperature, water temperature, and how long dough took to double. This helps you predict and control. For daily sales, plan your schedule: mix dough at a set time, ferment, shape, proof, and bake so bread is ready when customers arrive. Don't rush fermentation with hot water; it ruins flavour and texture..Trim()

.Trim()

Hygiene: clean bowls, cloths, and surfaces after each use. Use food-grade containers. Store yeast in an airtight container in a cool, dry place; once opened, keep in the fridge. Salt should be dry. Water should be clean. If you have staff, train them on these practices. Also, consider food safety regulations in your country. For example, in Kenya, public health officers may inspect your bakery. Keep records of cleaning and ingredient sourcing. Finally, take care of yourself: baking is physical. Lift heavy flour bags with your legs, not your back. Take breaks. Stay hydrated in hot kitchens. Good safety habits protect your health, your customers, and your business reputation..Trim()

Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. This exercise will help you master dough making and fermentation. You'll make a simple white bread dough, observe fermentation, and bake it. Gather your tools and ingredients: 500 g strong bread flour, 300 ml lukewarm water, 10 g salt, and 5 g instant dry yeast. You'll also need a bowl, cloth, scale, and baking tray. First, mix flour, salt, and yeast. Add water and mix to a shaggy dough. Knead for 10 minutes until smooth. Place in an oiled bowl, cover, and note the time. Let it rise in a warm spot. Every 20 minutes, check and record the size. In hot weather, it may double in 45 minutes; in cooler, up to 2 hours. Write down the time it takes..Trim()

.Trim()

Once doubled, punch down, shape into a round loaf, and place on a floured tray. Cover and proof for 30 minutes. Meanwhile, preheat your oven to 220°C. Score the top with a knife. Bake for 25-30 minutes until golden. Cool, then cut. Taste and evaluate: Is it light? Does it have a good crust? Note any problems. Then repeat the exercise, but change one variable: use cooler water (20°C) and see how fermentation time changes. Or reduce yeast to 2.5 g and observe. This teaches you how temperature and yeast amount affect fermentation..Trim()

.Trim()

For a real business scenario, scale up: use 2 kg flour, 1.2 L water, 40 g salt, 20 g yeast. Divide into 8 loaves. Time yourself from start to finish. Can you produce consistently? Keep a log for one week, baking daily. Adjust based on your climate. If you're in a coastal area with high humidity, you may need less water. If in a dry region, more. After a week, you'll know your own conditions. Share your results with a fellow baker or in a local business group. This practical experience is what turns knowledge into skill. Remember, every batch teaches you something. Don't fear mistakes; learn from them..Trim()

Module 5 · Baking and Oven Management

Introduction and Why It Matters

Baking is the heart of your bakery business. It turns your prepared dough into the golden, aromatic bread that customers buy. Without proper baking, even the best dough becomes a waste of money and time. In Africa, where many bakeries rely on charcoal, wood, or gas ovens, managing the oven well is critical. You need to understand that baking is not just about putting dough in an oven; it's about controlling temperature, time, and steam to get consistent results..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does this matter? Because your customers expect the same quality every day. If your bread is sometimes burnt, sometimes pale, or sometimes raw inside, they will go to another bakery. Consistent baking builds trust and repeat sales. Also, fuel is expensive. If you bake at the wrong temperature, you waste fuel and may lose a whole batch. In many African communities, bread is a daily staple. Your ability to bake well directly affects your income and your family's well-being..Trim()

.Trim()

Good oven management also reduces accidents. Burns from hot ovens or fires from uncontrolled flames are common in small bakeries. By learning proper techniques, you protect yourself and your workers. Moreover, efficient baking means you can produce more loaves in less time, increasing your daily bread sales. This module will guide you through the tools, methods, mistakes, safety, and practice needed to master baking and oven management. Whether you use a simple mud oven or a modern gas oven, the principles remain the same. Let's get started on turning your dough into profit..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To bake bread, you need more than just an oven. You need the right tools to load, monitor, and unload your bread safely and efficiently. First, you need a reliable oven. In Africa, common types include wood-fired brick ovens, charcoal ovens, gas ovens, and electric ovens. Each has its own temperature control method. For wood-fired ovens, you'll need firewood or charcoal, a poker, and a way to measure temperature, such as an oven thermometer or a simple trick like sprinkling flour to see if it browns..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, you need baking trays or pans. These should be sturdy and fit your oven. If you're baking round loaves directly on the oven floor, you might use a peel (a long-handled flat shovel) to slide them in and out. For loaves in pans, you need a tray lifter or thick gloves. A timer is essential; you can use a clock or a phone timer. A spray bottle with water is useful for creating steam, which gives bread a crispy crust. You'll also need a cooling rack to let bread cool after baking..Trim()

.Trim()

For safety, have oven mitts or thick cloths, a fire extinguisher or sand bucket, and a first-aid kit. A thermometer is non-negotiable; guessing temperature leads to inconsistent results. If you use a gas oven, check the gas hose and regulator regularly for leaks. For wood ovens, keep a metal ash bucket. Finally, a notebook to record baking times, temperatures, and results helps you improve. Investing in these tools may cost a little, but they save money by preventing burnt batches and accidents. Remember, in baking, preparation is half the battle..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Follow these steps for successful baking. First, preheat your oven. For most bread, you need 200°C to 220°C (400°F to 425°F). If using a wood oven, light the fire and let it burn until the oven is hot, then push the coals to the side or remove them. For gas ovens, set the dial and wait 15-20 minutes. Use an oven thermometer to confirm. Second, prepare your loaves. Shape your dough and let it proof (rise) until it's ready. Score the top with a sharp knife to allow expansion..Trim()

.Trim()

Third, load the bread. If using a peel, dust it with flour or cornmeal, place the loaf on it, and slide it into the oven. If using pans, place them on the oven rack. Leave space between loaves for heat circulation. Fourth, create steam. For a crispy crust, spray water into the oven (avoiding the glass) or place a pan of hot water on the bottom rack. Steam helps the bread rise fully before the crust hardens. Fifth, set your timer. Most loaves bake in 25-40 minutes, depending on size. Rotate the bread halfway through for even baking..Trim()

.Trim()

Sixth, check for doneness. Tap the bottom of a loaf; it should sound hollow. The internal temperature should be around 90°C (195°F) for soft bread. If not done, bake 5 more minutes. Seventh, remove bread and cool on a rack. Don't slice hot bread; it continues to cook and may become gummy. Finally, clean your oven after it cools to prevent buildup. Record your times and temperatures for next time. With practice, you'll develop a rhythm. Remember, every oven behaves differently, so adjust based on your observations..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced bakers make mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Oven not hot enough. If bread comes out pale and dense, your oven was too cool. Fix: Preheat longer and use a thermometer. For wood ovens, add more fuel and wait. Mistake 2: Oven too hot. Bread browns too fast outside but raw inside. Fix: Lower temperature by opening the oven door briefly or reducing fuel. Next time, start with lower heat and increase..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 3: No steam. Bread has a thick, dull crust. Fix: Add a pan of water or spray the oven walls (not the glass) with water before closing. Mistake 4: Overcrowding. Bread touches and steams instead of bakes, resulting in pale sides. Fix: Bake in batches, leaving 2-3 cm between loaves. Mistake 5: Not rotating. One side burns. Fix: Rotate trays halfway through baking. Mistake 6: Underbaking. Bread is doughy in the center. Fix: Use a thermometer to check internal temp (90°C) or tap the bottom. Bake longer if needed..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 7: Burning due to sugar or toppings. If your bread has sugar or seeds, they can burn. Fix: Lower temperature by 10-20°C or cover loosely with foil. Mistake 8: Ignoring oven hot spots. Some ovens have areas that are hotter. Fix: Learn your oven's quirks and place bread accordingly, or use a baking stone for even heat. Mistake 9: Not cleaning. Burnt bits smoke and affect flavor. Fix: Clean after each use. Mistake 10: Using wet dough. Too much water makes bread spread. Fix: Adjust hydration or use a bit more flour. By anticipating these issues, you'll waste less and sell more..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Safety in the bakery is not optional. Burns, fires, and injuries can ruin your business. Always wear protective gear: oven mitts, long sleeves, and closed shoes. Never use wet cloths to handle hot pans; they conduct heat and cause steam burns. Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand near the oven. For gas ovens, check for leaks with soapy water; if bubbles form, tighten connections or call a technician. Never use a gas oven if you smell gas; ventilate and turn off the supply..Trim()

.Trim()

For wood or charcoal ovens, manage the fire carefully. Keep flammable materials away. Use a metal ash bucket and never dispose of ashes in plastic bins. Ensure good ventilation to avoid carbon monoxide buildup. When loading bread, use a peel or tongs; never reach into a hot oven with bare hands. Keep children and pets away from the baking area. Establish a routine: preheat, bake, cool, clean. Clean your oven regularly to prevent grease fires and maintain efficiency..Trim()

.Trim()

Best practices include: maintaining a consistent baking schedule, recording temperatures and times, and training all workers on safety. Store fuel safely away from heat. Use a thermometer to avoid guesswork. Cool bread on racks, not on the floor. Keep your workspace tidy to prevent slips. In Africa, many bakeries operate in shared spaces; ensure your oven area is well-marked. Finally, have a first-aid kit and know basic burn treatment: cool with running water for 10 minutes, cover with a clean cloth, and seek medical help if severe. Your safety ensures your business continues..Trim()

Gather your tools: dough (made from 500g flour, 300ml water, 10g yeast, 10g salt, 10g sugar), oven, thermometer, timer, baking tray, cooling rack, and oven mitts. Preheat your oven to 200°C. If using a wood oven, burn wood until the oven reaches that temperature, then remove coals..Trim()

Shape your dough into a loaf and place it on a greased tray. Score the top. Once the oven is ready, load the bread and set your timer for 30 minutes. After 15 minutes, rotate the tray. Also, create steam by placing a small pan of hot water in the oven. At 25 minutes, check the bread: tap the bottom; if it sounds hollow, it's done. If not, bake 5 more minutes. Remove and cool on a rack. Record the actual baking time and any issues (e.g., uneven browning)..Trim()

.Trim()

After cooling, evaluate: Is the crust golden? Is the inside soft? If not, adjust next time. For example, if the bottom burned, place the tray higher. If the bread is pale, increase temperature by 10°C. Repeat this exercise three times, changing one variable each time (e.g., temperature, steam, position). Keep notes in a notebook. This practice builds your skill and confidence. Remember, consistent baking leads to consistent sales. Share your results with fellow bakers to learn together..Trim()

Introduction and Why It Matters

When you pull hot bread from the oven, it smells wonderful and looks golden. But if you pack it right away, you will trap steam inside the bag. Proper cooling, packing, and shelf life management are what turn a good baker into a profitable business. .Trim()

Cooling is not just about waiting. It is about letting moisture escape while the bread is still safe from dust, flies, and rain. In hot, humid climates, bread can cool too slowly if left in a closed room. In dry, dusty areas, it can dry out too fast. You need to find a balance. A simple method is to place loaves on a wire rack or a clean wooden pallet in a well-ventilated room. The bread should be at least 5 cm apart so air can move around each loaf. .Trim()

Packing protects the bread from contamination and keeps it from drying out. But you must pack only when the bread is completely cool. How do you know? Touch the bottom of the loaf. .Trim()
