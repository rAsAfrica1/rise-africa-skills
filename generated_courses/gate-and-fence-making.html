Module 1 · Introduction to Gate and Fence Making

Overview of Gate and Fence Types

In Africa, fences and gates serve many purposes: security, privacy, marking boundaries, and keeping livestock in or out. The most common types you will build are chain-link, barbed wire, wooden picket, and welded wire mesh. Chain-link is popular in urban areas because it is affordable and see-through. A standard chain-link fence uses 2-inch mesh, 9-gauge wire, and posts set every 2.5 to 3 meters. Barbed wire is used for farms and large plots; it is cheap but mainly for livestock and deterrence. Wooden picket fences give a decorative look for homes and gardens, often using treated pine or local hardwoods like mahogany or iroko. Welded wire mesh is stronger than chain-link and often used for security around shops and warehouses..Trim()

Gates come in two main categories: swing gates and sliding gates. Swing gates are the most common for residential driveways. A single swing gate can be up to 3.5 meters wide; wider openings need double swing gates. Sliding gates are used where there is no room to swing open, such as on steep driveways or tight urban plots. They run on a track or cantilever system. For pedestrian access, a small gate 1 meter wide is standard. All gates need strong hinges, latches, and posts set in concrete..Trim()

When choosing a fence type, consider the purpose, your budget, and local climate. In coastal areas like Mombasa or Lagos, salt air rusts metal quickly, so galvanised or powder-coated steel is best. In rainy regions, wood needs treatment against termites and rot. In dry areas, metal fences last longer but may need expansion joints. Always check local by-laws: some municipalities limit fence height to 1.8 meters or require permits. A good fence balances security, cost, and appearance. As a beginner, start with a simple chain-link fence around a small plot, then progress to gate installation..Trim()

Tools and Equipment for Gate and Fence Making

You will also need a shovel, a pickaxe for hard ground, and a wheelbarrow for mixing concrete. Safety Practices in Gate and Fence Construction

Fence and gate construction involves sharp metal, heavy posts, power tools, and concrete. Safety must come first. Always wear personal protective equipment (PPE): safety glasses when cutting or grinding, gloves when handling wire or rough wood, ear defenders when using power tools, and steel-toe boots. When welding, wear a welding helmet with a proper shade lens, leather gloves, and a leather apron. Never weld without eye protection; arc eye is painful and can cause permanent damage..Trim()

Materials: Wood, Metal, Vinyl, and Composite

Choosing the right material for your fence or gate depends on cost, durability, climate, and purpose. In Africa, wood and metal are the most common, but vinyl and composite are gaining popularity in upscale areas. Wood is affordable and easy to work with. Local hardwoods like iroko, mahogany, and teak are naturally resistant to termites and rot. Softwoods like pine are cheaper but must be pressure-treated with preservative (e.g., copper azole) to last. A wooden picket fence using 2x4 rails and 1x4 pickets can last 10-15 years if treated and maintained. However, wood needs painting or staining every 2-3 years..Trim()

Aluminium is lightweight and rust-proof but more expensive and less rigid. For gates, mild steel square tubing (e.g., 40x40x2 mm) is common; it can be welded and painted. Reading and Interpreting Fence Plans

A fence plan is a drawing that shows the layout, dimensions, and materials for a fence or gate. As a beginner, you must learn to read simple plans. Plans usually include a site plan (top view) and elevation drawings (side view). The site plan shows the property boundary, fence line, gate positions, and corners. It uses a scale, such as 1:100, meaning 1 cm on paper equals 100 cm (1 m) on the ground. You will need a scale ruler or a normal ruler and a calculator. For example, if the fence line on paper is 8 cm long at 1:100, the real length is 8 m..Trim()

Plans include dimensions: total length, post spacing, gate width, and height. Post spacing is often 2.5 m to 3 m for chain-link, 2 m for wooden fences. Gate width is typically 1 m for pedestrian, 3.5 m for single vehicle. Height is usually 1.2 m to 2.4 m. The plan also shows symbols: a circle with a cross for posts, a rectangle for gates, and a dashed line for underground cables. A legend explains these symbols. Always check the north arrow to orient the plan with the actual site..Trim()

For rails, you need 30 m of top rail and 30 m of bottom rail..Trim()

Site Assessment and Measurement Basics

Before you build, you must assess the site and take accurate measurements. Start by walking the boundary. Note the terrain: is it flat, sloping, or rocky? A slope affects post height and fence line. For a slope, you can step the fence (each panel level) or rack it (follow the slope). Chain-link can be racked up to 1:4 slope; steeper needs stepping. Rocky ground may require a rock drill or concrete anchors instead of digging. Check for obstacles: trees, buildings, underground pipes, and overhead cables. In many African compounds, you may find old foundations or buried debris..Trim()

.Trim()

Measure the total length of the fence line. Use a 30 m tape measure. For long distances, use a measuring wheel or pace it out (1 pace â 0.75 m). Measure each straight run separately. Note corners: a corner post needs bracing. Measure gate openings precisely; a gate that is too small or too large is a problem. For a vehicle gate, measure the width of the widest vehicle plus 1 m clearance. For pedestrian gates, 1 m is standard. Also measure the height: from ground to desired top of fence. Remember that posts go 60 cm into the ground, so a 2.4 m post gives a 1.8 m fence..Trim()

Check the soil type. Sandy soil drains well but may not hold posts; use more concrete. Clay soil holds water and can cause posts to rot; use gravel at the bottom of the hole. Rocky soil may need a breaker bar. In black cotton soil (common in parts of East Africa), posts can shift; use deeper holes and wider concrete footings. Also note the wind direction. In coastal areas, strong winds require closer post spacing (2 m instead of 3 m) and deeper footings..Trim()

For a long fence, set up a string line at the desired height and check with a line level. Measure diagonals to check for square corners: for a rectangular plot, the diagonals should be equal. If not, adjust the corners. Always add 10% to your material estimate for waste and mistakes. A thorough site assessment saves time and money later..Trim()

Determining Property Lines and Setbacks

Before you dig any hole or buy any material, you must know exactly where your property ends and your neighbour's begins. In most African countries, the legal boundary is marked by beaconsâconcrete pillars or iron pins placed by a licensed surveyor. If you don't have a survey diagram, visit your local lands office or hire a surveyor. A typical survey costs between $100 and $300 depending on the country and plot size. Never rely on an old fence or a neighbour's word; those can be wrong by metres. Once you have the beacons, use a 50-metre measuring tape and a string line to mark the boundary. Clear grass and bushes along the line so you can see the ground. Then check your local building code for setbacks. Setbacks are the minimum distance your fence or gate must be from the property line. For example, in many Kenyan municipalities, a front fence must be at least 3 metres from the road reserve, while side fences can sit on the boundary. In Nigeria, some states require a 1.5-metre setback from the boundary for walls. If you build too close, the county can fine you or force you to demolish. Write down the setback distances and mark them with pegs and string. Also note any easementsâareas where the government or utility company has right of way for pipes or cables. You cannot build a permanent fence over a sewer line. Finally, take photos of your marked lines and keep copies of your survey and any permits. This simple step saves you from disputes, fines, and wasted concrete..Trim()

Designing Fence Layouts for Function and Aesthetics

A fence must do a job: keep animals in or out, mark a boundary, provide privacy, or look good. Start by listing your main purpose. For security, use a solid wall or tight metal palisade with no gaps wider than 10 cm. For privacy, choose 2-metre-high corrugated sheets or woven bamboo. For livestock, a simple post-and-rail or chain-link works. Now sketch your layout on graph paper. Measure the total length of each straight run. For a typical residential plot of 15m by 30m, the perimeter is 90 metres. Divide the fence into panels of 2.4 metres (standard for many metal and wood fences). That gives 90 Ã· 2.4 = 37.5, so you need 38 panels. Mark where gates will go: usually one main gate for vehicles (3.5 to 4 metres wide) and one small pedestrian gate (1 metre wide). Place the vehicle gate near the road for easy access, not at a corner. For aesthetics, repeat the same panel design along the front. You can add variation by using pillars every 3 metres or changing the top rail height. In hot climates, leave a 5 cm gap at the bottom for airflow and to prevent rust from ground moisture. In rainy areas, slope the top of solid walls away from the property so water runs off. Use a string line and pegs to mark the layout on the ground. Walk the line and check for obstacles: trees, drainage channels, or existing structures. Adjust the layout to avoid cutting down a mature tree or blocking a natural water path. A good layout balances strength, cost, and appearance..Trim()

Selecting Gate Styles and Swing Directions

Gates are the most used part of any fence, so choose a style that fits your space and daily needs. The three main types are swing gates, sliding gates, and cantilever gates. Swing gates are cheapest and easiest to build. They hinge on one side and open inward or outward. For a single vehicle gate, a 4-metre wide swing gate needs strong hinges and a sturdy post. If you have limited driveway space, a double swing gate (two 2-metre leaves) opens faster and puts less strain on each post. Sliding gates run on a track or rollers along the fence line. They need at least 1.5 times the gate width in clear space to slide. Cantilever gates have no bottom track and slide on top rollersâgood for uneven ground but more expensive. Swing direction matters. Inward-swinging gates are safer because they don't block the road or pavement. But if your driveway slopes up, an inward gate may hit the ground. Outward-swinging gates give more clearance but can hit pedestrians. For pedestrian gates, always swing inward and away from the latch side so you can't get trapped. For security, add a heavy-duty drop bolt or a ground socket to hold the gate open. In Africa, metal gates are common: use 40mm square tubing for the frame and 12mm round bars for infill. Wooden gates need treated hardwood like mahogany or eucalyptus to resist termites. Always hang gates on posts set at least 60 cm deep in concrete. Test the swing before you weld or screw the final piece. A gate that opens smoothly with one hand is a gate that will last..Trim()

Permits and Building Codes for Fences and Gates

Many people build fences without permits and later pay fines or tear them down. In most African countries, you need a permit for any fence over 1.8 metres high, or for any fence in a front yard. Start by visiting your local municipal or county offices. Ask for the building department or physical planning department. Tell them you want to build a fence and gate. They will give you a list of requirements: a site plan, a survey diagram, and sometimes a structural drawing. In Kenya, for example, you submit a PPA1 form and pay a fee based on the fence lengthâabout $50 for a 90-metre fence. In Nigeria, you need a development permit from the state planning authority; fees vary from $100 to $500. In South Africa, you need approval from the local municipality if the fence is over 1.8m or if it's a solid wall. Building codes also control materials. Some areas ban solid walls over 1.8m because they block wind and can fall in storms. Others require fire breaks between fences. For gates, check if you need a road opening permit if your gate opens onto a public road. That permit ensures your gate doesn't swing into traffic. Always get your permit before you buy materials. Keep a copy on site. If an inspector visits, show your approved plans. If you skip permits, your neighbour can report you, and the county can demolish your fence at your cost. The process takes 2 to 6 weeks, so plan ahead. A small fee now saves a big headache later..Trim()

Inspecting Fences and Gates for Damage

Start your inspection at the main gate and walk the entire fence line. Carry a notebook, a hammer, a torch, and a pair of pliers. In Africa, common damage comes from termites, rust, livestock pushing through, and strong sun cracking wood. Check every post: tap it with the hammer. A dull thud means rot inside; a sharp ring means solid wood. Look for leaning posts, loose rails, and missing pickets. For metal gates, check hinges, latches, and welds for rust or cracks. Open and close the gate fully. It should swing freely without dragging. Listen for squeaks or grinding. Inspect the ground around posts for erosion or insect mounds. Termites leave mud tubes; treat them immediately. Also look for vines or tall grass hiding damage. In rainy seasons, check for water pooling at post bases, which speeds rot. Write down every problem: location, type of damage, and severity. Use a simple scale: 1 (minor, fix soon), 2 (moderate, fix this week), 3 (urgent, fix today). For example, a loose picket is 1, a leaning post is 2, a broken hinge on a gate used daily is 3. Take photos with your phone if you have one. This record helps you plan repairs and buy materials. Inspect at least once a month, and after every storm. A 30-minute inspection saves hours of emergency work. Teach a family member or worker to do the same. Early detection prevents small cracks from becoming big holes. Remember: a fence that is not inspected is a fence that will fail when you least expect it..Trim()
