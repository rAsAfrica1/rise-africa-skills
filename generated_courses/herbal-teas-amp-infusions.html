Common African Herbs for Tea: Rooibos, Hibiscus, Moringa, Lemongrass

Start with four herbs that grow well across much of Africa and sell reliably at markets. Rooibos (Aspalathus linearis) is a South African shrub grown mainly in the Cederberg region. It needs sandy, acidic soil and winter rainfall. You harvest the needle-like leaves and fine stems, then ferment them in heaps for 8 to 12 hours to get the deep red colour and sweet, woody flavour. Unfermented green rooibos is also sold at a higher price. A small plot of 100 plants can yield 20 to 30 kg of dried tea per year..Trim()

.Trim()

Hibiscus (Hibiscus sabdariffa), called zobo or bissap in West Africa, is grown from seed in warm, dry conditions. The calyces, not the petals, are what you dry and brew. Harvest 3 to 4 weeks after flowering, when the calyces are fleshy and bright red. Sun-dry them for 3 to 5 days on clean mats until brittle. One hectare can produce 500 to 800 kg of dried calyces. Hibiscus is tart and high in vitamin C, so it blends well with ginger or pineapple peel..Trim()

.Trim()

Moringa (Moringa oleifera) is a fast-growing tree that tolerates poor soil and drought. For tea, harvest young leaves, strip them from stems, and dry them in the shade for 2 to 3 days. Shade drying keeps the leaves green and preserves vitamins. Do not boil moringa leaves; pour hot water at about 80 degrees Celsius over 1 to 2 teaspoons of dried leaf and steep for 3 minutes. Moringa is bitter if over-steeped, so measure carefully..Trim()

.Trim()

Lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus) grows in clumps and spreads easily. Cut stalks 15 to 20 cm above the ground every 6 to 8 weeks. You can use fresh stalks or dry them in bundles for 1 to 2 weeks. Lemongrass is a good starter crop because it needs little water and few inputs. For a small tea business, plant at least 50 clumps so you can harvest continuously. Always label your dried herbs with the harvest date and location. Buyers pay more when they can trust the source and the quality..Trim()

Where to Find Herbs: Wild Harvesting, Farms, and Markets

You have three main sources for tea herbs: wild harvesting, farms, and markets. Each has different costs, risks, and quality levels. Choose based on what you can control and what your customers will pay..Trim()

.Trim()

Wild harvesting is cheapest but riskiest. In many parts of Africa, common tea herbs grow along roadsides, riverbanks, and bushland. Lemongrass often grows near water. Hibiscus and rooibos are usually cultivated, not wild. Moringa grows in villages and towns. Before harvesting wild plants, get permission from the landowner or local authority. Never harvest from roadsides where exhaust and herbicides contaminate plants. Avoid areas near mines, rubbish dumps, or spraying programmes. Farms give you consistent quality and volume. Visit smallholder farms within 50 to 100 km of your processing site. Ask what they grow, when they harvest, and how they dry. A good farm keeps records of planting dates and drying methods. Offer to buy a fixed quantity at a fair price before harvest. For example, agree on 50 kg of dried hibiscus calyces at a set price per kg. This gives the farmer income security and gives you reliable supply. If you can, visit during harvest to check the crop yourself..Trim()

.Trim()

Markets are the fastest way to buy herbs, but quality varies. In cities like Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, and Johannesburg, you can find dried hibiscus, moringa powder, and lemongrass in bulk. Bring a small scale, a clean container, and a moisture meter if you have one. Check for mould, dust, insects, and strong off-smells. Ask the trader where the herb came from and how old it is. How to Identify Herbs Safely and Avoid Toxic Plants

Misidentifying a plant can make your customers sick or kill them. You must learn to identify herbs with certainty before you sell any tea. Use three tools: a local plant guide, a knowledgeable elder or extension officer, and your own senses. Never rely on one tool alone..Trim()

.Trim()

Start with leaves. Rooibos has fine, needle-like green leaves on a low shrub. Hibiscus has broad, lobed leaves with serrated edges and red stems. Moringa has small, oval leaflets arranged along a long stem, and the whole leaf looks feathery. Lemongrass has long, sharp-edged blades that smell strongly of lemon when crushed. If a plant looks similar but smells wrong, do not use it. Smell is one of your best safety checks..Trim()

.Trim()

Learn the dangerous lookalikes in your area. In many African regions, the toxic plant known as "wild castor" or "mkakati" has leaves that can be confused with hibiscus. Some species of Solanum (nightshade) have leaves similar to moringa but produce toxic berries. Datura, also called jimsonweed, has large leaves and trumpet flowers and is highly poisonous. If you are not 100% sure, throw the plant away. There is no tea worth a hospital visit..Trim()

.Trim()

Use a simple identification checklist for every new plant. First, confirm the leaf shape and arrangement. Second, crush a leaf and smell it. Third, check the stem, flowers, and fruit if present. Fourth, compare with a photo in a trusted guide such as "Medicinal Plants of East Africa" or a local extension pamphlet. Fifth, ask two experienced people to confirm. Only then harvest a small test batch..Trim()

.Trim()

When you harvest, keep each herb in a separate labelled bag. Never mix unknown plants with known ones. Wear gloves if you are handling sap. Wash your hands after harvesting. If you plan to sell, keep a written record of who identified the plant and when. This protects you and builds trust with buyers. Teach your workers the same rules. A safe tea business is a lasting tea business..Trim()

Sustainable Harvesting Practices for Small Businesses

Sustainable harvesting means taking herbs in a way that lets the plant and the ecosystem recover. If you strip a wild patch bare, you will have nothing to sell next season. Good practices also improve quality and reduce waste..Trim()

.Trim()

For leafy herbs like moringa and lemongrass, never cut more than one-third of the plant at a time. For moringa, harvest only the young stems and leaves, leaving the main trunk and at least half the canopy. Wait 4 to 6 weeks before harvesting the same tree again. For lemongrass, cut stalks 15 to 20 cm above the ground and leave the base to regrow. You can harvest the same clump every 6 to 8 weeks for years if you do not pull it up..Trim()

.Trim()

For hibiscus, harvest only the mature red calyces and leave the green ones to develop. Pick in the cool of the morning. Do not uproot the plant. Hibiscus is an annual in many areas, so save some seed for the next season. For rooibos, harvest in summer when the bushes are 18 months to 3 years old. Cut the stems 30 cm above the ground. The plant will regrow, but it needs a rest period. Never harvest rooibos from the same bush two years in a row..Trim()

.Trim()

Wild harvesting needs extra care. Follow the "one in three" rule: for every three plants you find, harvest only one. Leave the strongest plants to seed. Use a sharp knife or secateurs, not a machete that damages the plant. Fill your basket loosely so leaves do not crush. Walk lightly and avoid trampling young plants. If a patch is small, skip it and find a larger one..Trim()

.Trim()

Record what you harvest. Write the date, location, herb, and amount in a notebook. This helps you rotate harvest sites and avoid overuse. It also shows buyers that you manage your supply responsibly. Some buyers pay a premium for herbs harvested sustainably. You can also plant your own herb garden to reduce pressure on wild areas. A 10 m by 10 m plot of lemongrass and moringa can supply a small tea business year-round..Trim()
