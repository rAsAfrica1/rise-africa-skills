What is Peanut Butter? Varieties and Uses in African Cuisine

Peanut butter is made by grinding roasted peanuts into a smooth or chunky paste. In Africa, peanuts (groundnuts) are boiled, roasted, or fried, then ground with salt, sugar, or oil. The simplest version uses 100% peanuts. Commercial varieties add stabilizers (like palm oil or hydrogenated oil) to stop separation, and sweeteners (sugar, honey) or salt for taste. In West Africa, 'groundnut paste' is often unsweetened and used in soups like groundnut stew. In East Africa, peanut butter is spread on bread or mixed into uji (porridge). In Southern Africa, it's used in relishes and sauces. Varieties include smooth, crunchy (with chopped nuts added), natural (no additives), and flavored (chocolate, honey, chili). You can also make nut pastes from cashew, almond, or sesame (tahini). For a small business, start with smooth and crunchy peanut butter. Use local peanuts: in Nigeria, 'Kano groundnut' is popular; in Senegal, 'Touba groundnut'; in Malawi, 'CG7' variety. Roast at 160Â°C for 20-30 minutes until golden and oily. Grind while warm using a food processor or a local mill. Add 1-2% salt and 5-10% sugar if desired. For crunchy, stir in 10% chopped roasted peanuts. Uses in African cuisine: groundnut soup (Ghana, Nigeria), domoda (Gambia), peanut sauce with rice (Tanzania), kuli-kuli (Nigeria) from peanut paste, and as a snack with banana or cassava. In markets, sell in clean jars or sachets. Label with production date and ingredients. This lesson sets the foundation: know your product, its varieties, and how it fits local dishes..Trim()

Overview of Nut Pastes: Groundnut, Cashew, Almond, and Sesame

Nut pastes are made by grinding nuts or seeds into a spreadable paste. Groundnut (peanut) paste is the most common in Africa. Cashew paste is made from roasted cashew nuts; it's creamy and slightly sweet. Cashew apples are also used, but the nut is key. In Tanzania, cashew paste is used in curries. Almond paste is made from almonds; it's expensive because almonds are imported in many African countries. But if you have local almond trees (e.g., in Morocco), it can be viable. Sesame paste (tahini) is made from hulled sesame seeds, roasted and ground. It's used in hummus, halva, and as a base for sauces. In Sudan, sesame paste is used in salads. Each paste has different oil content: groundnut has about 50% oil, cashew 44%, almond 50%, sesame 55%. This affects grinding: sesame needs longer grinding to release oil. For a small business, start with groundnut paste because peanuts are cheap and widely available. Then diversify: cashew paste for premium markets, sesame paste for Middle Eastern foods. Identifying Good Quality Groundnuts: Aflatoxin Prevention and Testing

Aflatoxin is a poison produced by moulds that grow on groundnuts, especially when they are damaged, stored wet, or dried on the ground. In Africa, aflatoxin is a serious health risk and can ruin your peanut butter business because buyers test for it. To prevent aflatoxin, start at harvest: never let nuts touch soil. Dry them on clean mats or tarps, not on bare ground. Dry until moisture is below 10% â you can test with a cheap moisture meter (around $20) or bite a nut: it should crack, not bend. Sort out any shrivelled, discoloured, or mouldy nuts. Mouldy nuts often have a yellow-green or black powdery coating, or they smell musty. Aflatoxin is invisible, so you must also test. Simple field tests include the black light test: aflatoxin glows greenish-yellow under UV light, but this only works for high levels. Better: use a rapid test kit like GIPSA or AgraStrip. These cost about $2-5 per test. For bulk, send a sample to a lab. In many African countries, the standard limit is 10 ppb (parts per billion) for total aflatoxin. If you sell to exporters or processors, they will test. Keep records of your tests. Also, buy from farmers who dry on raised platforms and store in jute bags, not plastic. Never mix good and bad nuts. If you find mould, discard the whole batch â one bad nut can contaminate many. Remember: prevention is cheaper than rejection. Train your suppliers on proper drying and storage. By controlling moisture and damage, you reduce aflatoxin risk by over 90%..Trim()

Storage of Raw Nuts: Preventing Moisture, Pests, and Mold

Proper storage keeps nuts dry, cool, and pest-free. Moisture is the enemy: at above 10% moisture, mould grows and produces aflatoxin. Use airtight containers or jute bags. Never store in plastic bags because they trap moisture. Jute breathes, but it absorbs humidity. Better: use hermetic bags like GrainPro or PICS. These cost about $2-3 each and can hold 50 kg. They block moisture and insects. If hermetic bags are too expensive, use clean metal drums with tight lids. Add a desiccant like silica gel (1 kg per 100 kg nuts) to absorb moisture. Store in a dry, well-ventilated room. Keep bags on pallets or wooden planks, not on the floor. Leave 50 cm gap from walls. Control pests: clean the store before loading. Use traps or approved insecticides like Actellic dust (apply 1 kg per 1000 kg nuts). But never spray directly on nuts for consumption. For rats, use traps or cats. Check stored nuts every 2 weeks. Look for webbing (moth larvae), live insects, or musty smell. If you find insects, sun-dry the nuts for 2-3 hours on a clean mat â this drives out insects. But do not over-dry. Temperature: keep below 25Â°C if possible. In hot climates, store in a shaded area. Never store near fuel, chemicals, or strong smells â nuts absorb odours. Label each bag with date and moisture level. Use first-in, first-out. With good storage, you can keep nuts for 6-12 months with minimal quality loss. Remember: aflatoxin can increase in storage if moisture rises. So monitor with a moisture meter monthly. A $20 meter saves thousands in rejected batches..Trim()

Costing Raw Materials: Calculating Cost per Kilogram

To know if you are making a profit, you must calculate the true cost per kilogram of usable nuts. Start with the purchase price. For example, you buy 100 kg of groundnuts at $0.80/kg = $80. But you must account for losses: sorting removes 5% (5 kg), drying removes 8% moisture (8 kg), and shelling (if in-shell) removes 30% (30 kg). So usable nuts = 100 - 5 - 8 - 30 = 57 kg. Your cost per usable kg = $80 / 57 = $1.40/kg. That is your real raw material cost. Now add other costs: transport, bags, and labour for sorting. If transport is $10, bags $2, labour $5, total extra $17. New total cost = $97. Cost per kg = $97 / 57 = $1.70/kg. Always use a simple spreadsheet or notebook. Record every purchase: date, supplier, weight, price, moisture, and losses. For mixed nuts, calculate separately. For example, 20 kg cashew at $4/kg = $80, and 80 kg groundnut at $0.80/kg = $64. Total cost $144 for 100 kg mix. But cashew has no shell, groundnut may have 30% shell loss. So usable: cashew 20 kg, groundnut 80*0.7=56 kg, total 76 kg. Cost per kg = $144/76 = $1.89/kg. You also need to add aflatoxin test cost: $3 per test, if you test every 100 kg, that's $0.03/kg. Packaging and labour come later. For pricing, aim for at least 30% gross margin. If cost per kg is $1.70, sell at $2.50/kg or more. Use a simple formula: Selling price = Cost per kg / (1 - desired margin). For 40% margin, price = $1.70 / 0.6 = $2.83/kg. Always recalculate when prices change. Buy in bulk to lower cost per kg. For example, buying 1 tonne instead of 100 kg can reduce price by 15%. Keep records for at least 2 years. Module 3 · Cleaning, Roasting, and Peeling Groundnuts

Cleaning Groundnuts: Removing Stones, Dirt, and Bad Nuts

Cleaning groundnuts is the first and most important step in making good peanut butter. Dirty nuts can spoil the taste and even make people sick. Start by spreading your raw groundnuts on a clean tarpaulin or a large wooden tray in a thin layer. Pick out stones, sticks, and any foreign matter by hand. This is best done in small batches so you don't miss anything. A simple trick is to run your fingers through the nuts and feel for hard objects. For larger amounts, use a sieve or a locally made wire mesh to shake out dirt and small stones. You can also use a fan or the wind to blow away light chaff and dust. After dry cleaning, wash the nuts in clean water if they are very dirty. Soak them for 5-10 minutes, rub them between your hands, then rinse until the water runs clear. Spread them out to dry in the sun for 1-2 days, or use a solar dryer. Never roast wet nuts; they must be completely dry. Next, remove bad nuts: discolor, shriveled, mouldy, or insect-damaged nuts. Mouldy nuts can contain aflatoxins, which are dangerous. Discard them immediately. A good rule: if in doubt, throw it out. For consistent quality, do this cleaning step every time, even if the nuts look clean. Aim to remove at least 99% of foreign matter. A 50 kg bag of raw groundnuts typically yields about 45 kg of clean nuts after removing stones, dirt, and bad nuts. This step takes time but ensures a safe and tasty final product. Remember, clean nuts make clean peanut butter..Trim()

Roasting Methods: Pan Roasting, Sand Roasting, and Oven Roasting

Roasting brings out the flavour and makes peeling easier. There are three common methods: pan roasting, sand roasting, and oven roasting. Pan roasting: Use a heavy-bottomed pan, like a cast iron or thick aluminium pot. Heat it over medium fire. Add a single layer of clean, dry groundnuts. Stir constantly with a wooden spoon to avoid burning. Roast for 10-15 minutes until they are golden brown and smell nutty. This method is good for small batches (1-2 kg). Sand roasting: Heat clean, dry sand in a large pot or pan. The sand should be hot but not smoking. Add groundnuts and stir continuously. The sand helps distribute heat evenly and prevents burning. Roast for 8-12 minutes. Then separate the nuts from the sand using a sieve. This method is faster and works well for larger batches (5-10 kg). Oven roasting: Preheat an oven to 160-180Â°C. Spread groundnuts in a single layer on a baking tray. Roast for 15-20 minutes, stirring every 5 minutes. This method is less labour-intensive but requires an oven. For all methods, the key is constant stirring and watching closely. The nuts will darken and release oil. Test by taking a few out, cooling, and tasting. They should be crunchy, not soft. Over-roasting makes them bitter and oily; under-roasting makes peeling hard and flavour weak. After roasting, cool the nuts completely before peeling. Roasting also kills bacteria and reduces moisture, which helps the peanut butter last longer. For a 10 kg batch, pan roasting takes about 45 minutes, sand roasting 30 minutes, and oven roasting 25 minutes. Always roast in a well-ventilated area..Trim()

Determining Perfect Roast: Color, Aroma, and Taste Tests

Knowing when groundnuts are perfectly roasted is a skill you can learn with practice. You need to check three things: colour, aroma, and taste. Colour: Raw groundnuts are pale, whitish-yellow. As they roast, they turn light brown, then golden brown, and finally dark brown. The perfect roast is a uniform golden brown. If they are still pale, they need more time. If they are dark brown or black, they are over-roasted and will taste bitter. Aroma: Raw nuts smell earthy. As they roast, they release a rich, nutty aroma. When you can smell that strong, pleasant nutty smell, they are close to done. If you smell a burnt or acrid smell, they are over-roasting. Taste: The most reliable test. Take a few nuts out, let them cool for a minute (they are hot!), and taste. Perfectly roasted nuts are crunchy, slightly sweet, and have a deep nutty flavour. If they taste raw or bland, roast longer. If they taste bitter or burnt, you've gone too far. Another test: rub a nut between your fingers. The skin should come off easily. If the skin sticks, they need more roasting. Also, listen for a crackling sound as they roast; when the crackling slows down, they are nearly done. For consistent results, roast in small batches and check every 2 minutes after the first 10 minutes. Record the time and temperature that works for your setup. Different groundnut varieties and sizes roast at different rates. For example, small nuts roast faster than large ones. Always preheat your pan or oven. After roasting, spread the nuts on a cool tray to stop the cooking process. Do not pile them up, as they will continue to cook from residual heat. Perfect roast is key to great peanut butter..Trim()

Peeling Groundnuts: Manual and Mechanical Methods

After roasting and cooling, the next step is peeling off the skins. Peeling is important because skins can make peanut butter bitter and gritty. There are manual and mechanical methods. Manual peeling: This is the most common for small-scale producers. Rub the cooled nuts between your hands or on a rough surface like a woven basket or a clean piece of burlap. The skins will flake off. For larger quantities, put nuts in a sack and beat them gently with a wooden stick, then winnow. Another manual method is to rub them on a wire mesh. This takes time: one person can peel about 2-3 kg per hour. It's labour-intensive but requires no special equipment. Mechanical peeling: If you produce larger volumes, consider a groundnut peeler. These machines range from simple hand-cranked models to motorised ones. A small motorised peeler can process 50-100 kg per hour. They work by rubbing the nuts against a rotating drum or abrasive surface. You can find these machines from agricultural suppliers or fabricators. They cost between $200 and $1000, depending on capacity. When using a mechanical peeler, adjust the spacing so it doesn't break the nuts. Broken nuts are fine for peanut butter, but whole nuts look better if you sell them. After peeling, you must separate the skins from the nuts. This is done by winnowing (see next lesson). Peeling efficiency: manual methods remove about 80-90% of skins; mechanical methods can remove 95% or more. Some skins may remain, especially in the crevices. You can rub the nuts again or use a fan to blow away loose skins. Don't worry if a few skins remain; they won't ruin the peanut butter. But too many skins make it bitter. After peeling, use the nuts immediately or store them in an airtight container to prevent them from absorbing moisture. Peeling is easier when nuts are roasted and cooled completely. If nuts are still warm, skins stick..Trim()

Cooling and Winnowing to Remove Skins

After roasting, groundnuts must be cooled completely before peeling. Cooling stops the cooking process and makes skins brittle and easy to remove. Spread the hot nuts in a single layer on a clean, dry tray or mat. Do not pile them up, as trapped heat will cause them to steam and become soft. Let them cool for 20-30 minutes at room temperature. You can speed cooling by using a fan, but avoid blowing dust onto the nuts. Once cooled, the skins will be loose and flaky. Now, winnowing: This is the process of separating the skins from the nuts. The traditional method is to toss the nuts in the air using a winnowing basket or a flat tray. The wind blows away the lighter skins while the heavier nuts fall back. Do this on a windy day or use a fan. Stand with your back to the wind, hold the basket, and toss the nuts gently. The skins fly away, and the nuts land back in the basket. Repeat until most skins are removed. For small quantities, you can also use a hair dryer on cool setting or an electric fan. Another method is to pour the nuts from one container to another in front of a fan. The skins blow away. For larger scale, use a mechanical winnower, which is a machine that uses air to separate skins. These are available from agricultural equipment suppliers. After winnowing, check for any remaining skins. You can rub the nuts again and winnow once more. Aim to remove at least 95% of skins. Remaining skins are okay but can add bitterness. Winnowing also removes any small stones or debris that were missed. Do this in a well-ventilated area, as the dust can be irritating. Wear a mask if you are sensitive. After winnowing, your nuts are ready for grinding into peanut butter. Store them in a clean, dry container. If you are not grinding immediately, keep them in an airtight container in a cool, dry place. They will stay fresh for up to 2 weeks. Proper cooling and winnowing ensure a smooth, tasty peanut butter..Trim()

Quality Control: Ensuring Consistent Roast and Cleanliness

Quality control is what separates a good peanut butter from a great one. You need to ensure every batch is consistent in roast and cleanliness. Start by setting standards. For roast, decide on a target colour (e.g., golden brown) and aroma. Use a colour chart or sample photos. Train yourself to recognise the perfect roast by sight, smell, and taste. For cleanliness, aim for zero foreign matter, stones, or bad nuts. Implement checks at each step. After cleaning, inspect a handful of nuts for stones or dirt. After roasting, check colour and taste. After peeling and winnowing, check for remaining skins. Keep a record of times, temperatures, and batch sizes. This helps you repeat success. Use simple tools: a thermometer for oven roasting, a timer, and a scale. For pan roasting, use a timer and your senses. Consistency also means uniform roasting. If you roast unevenly, some nuts will be burnt and others raw. To avoid this, roast in small batches, stir constantly, and use a single layer. For large batches, use a mechanical roaster, like a drum roaster, which rotates for even roasting. Cleanliness also means hygiene. Wash your hands, wear clean clothes, and keep your work area clean. Use clean water for washing. Store nuts in clean, covered containers. Avoid contamination from pests. If you find mouldy nuts, discard them and clean the area. Aflatoxins are a serious health risk. Test for moisture: good roasted nuts should have less than 6% moisture. You can use a moisture meter or the salt test: put a nut in a saturated salt solution; if it floats, moisture is too high. For small producers, simply ensure nuts are dry and crunchy. Finally, do a final quality check before grinding. Taste a few nuts. They should be crunchy, nutty, and free of off-flavours. If they are not perfect, adjust your process. Consistent quality builds customer trust and allows you to charge a better price. Keep a notebook and review your notes regularly..Trim()

Aflatoxin Testing and Control Measures

Aflatoxins are poisons produced by molds that can grow on peanuts, especially in hot, humid conditions. They can cause liver cancer and are especially dangerous for children. Calculating Cost of Production per Unit

To know if you are making a profit, you must first know exactly how much it costs to produce one jar of peanut butter. This is your cost of production per unit. Start by listing all your direct costs for a batch. For example, to make 20 jars of 500g peanut butter, you might use 10 kg of raw groundnuts at 2,000 XAF per kg = 20,000 XAF. You also need 1 kg of sugar (1,000 XAF), 0.5 kg of salt (500 XAF), and 1 litre of oil (2,000 XAF). Your total ingredient cost is 23,500 XAF. Next, add packaging: 20 jars with lids at 200 XAF each = 4,000 XAF, and labels at 50 XAF each = 1,000 XAF. So packaging costs 5,000 XAF. Now add direct labour: if you pay a helper 1,500 XAF to assist for the day, that's 1,500 XAF. Total direct costs = 23,500 + 5,000 + 1,500 = 30,000 XAF. Divide by 20 jars: 30,000 / 20 = 1,500 XAF per jar. But you also have overheads like electricity, rent, and equipment depreciation. Suppose monthly overheads are 30,000 XAF and you produce 300 jars per month, that's 100 XAF per jar. So your full cost per jar is 1,500 + 100 = 1,600 XAF. Always calculate per batch and per unit. Keep a notebook recording every purchase. Use a simple calculator or phone app. If you produce different sizes, calculate cost per gram or per jar separately. Review your costs every month because prices change. This number is the foundation for setting your price..Trim()

Setting a Profitable Selling Price

Once you know your cost per unit, you can set a selling price that covers all costs and gives you a profit. A simple method is cost-plus pricing. Decide on a profit margin. For example, if your cost per jar is 1,600 XAF, you might want a 40% profit margin. Multiply cost by 1.4: 1,600 x 1.4 = 2,240 XAF. Round up to 2,500 XAF for easy pricing. But also check what competitors charge. If similar peanut butter sells for 2,200 XAF, you might price at 2,300 XAF to stay competitive while still making 700 XAF profit per jar (2,300 - 1,600 = 700). That's a 44% markup on cost. However, if your costs are higher, you may need to reduce costs or differentiate your product (e.g., organic, no sugar) to charge more. Consider your target market: in cities, people may pay more for quality; in rural areas, price sensitivity is higher. You can also offer bulk discounts: sell 10 jars for 22,000 XAF instead of 23,000 XAF. Always ensure your price covers variable costs (ingredients, packaging) so you don't lose money on each sale. Test prices: start at a level where you make at least 20-30% profit, then adjust based on sales. Keep records of what you charge and how many you sell. If sales are slow, don't immediately drop price; first try to reduce costs or improve marketing. Remember, profit is not greedâit's what allows you to grow, pay yourself, and reinvest..Trim()

Managing Cash Flow for Small Business

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. Even if you are profitable on paper, you can run out of cash if you don't manage timing. To manage cash flow, follow these steps: First, forecast your cash needs. Look at your monthly expenses: raw materials, packaging, labour, rent, transport. Second, speed up cash inflows: offer a small discount for early payment (e.g., 5% off if paid within 7 days). Third, slow down cash outflows: negotiate with suppliers for credit terms (e.g., pay for groundnuts in 14 days). Buy in bulk when prices are low, but don't tie up all your cash. Fourth, keep a cash reserve for emergencies. Aim to have at least one month's expenses in cash. Fifth, separate business and personal cash. Pay yourself a fixed salary rather than dipping into the till. Use a cash flow spreadsheet: list opening cash, add expected inflows, subtract expected outflows, and see closing cash. If closing cash is negative, you need to adjust: delay purchases, increase sales, or get a short-term loan. For example, if you expect to have 10,000 XAF at month end but need 15,000 XAF for rent, you must find 5,000 XAF. Track your actual cash daily.
