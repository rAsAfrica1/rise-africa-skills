Module 1 · Introduction to Grasscutter Farming

Overview of Grasscutter Farming

Grasscutter farming, also known as cane rat farming, is the practice of raising wild grasscutters (Thryonomys swinderianus) in captivity for meat. In many African countries, grasscutter meat is a delicacy, selling for two to three times the price of chicken or goat meat. A mature grasscutter weighs between 4 and 7 kg, and a well-managed farm can produce 50 to 100 offspring per year from a foundation stock of 10 females and 2 males..Trim()

Benefits and Challenges of Grasscutter Farming

Grasscutter farming offers several benefits. First, it is a low-capital business. You can start with 2 males and 10 females, which cost between $100 and $300 depending on your country. You can build pens using local materials like bamboo, mud bricks, and thatch, though concrete is better for hygiene. Second, grasscutters multiply quickly. A single female can produce 20 to 30 offspring in her lifetime. Third, feed costs are low because they eat grasses and weeds that you can grow or collect for free. Fourth, the meat is highly nutritious: low in fat, high in protein, and rich in iron. Fifth, the market is strong. Hotels, restaurants, and supermarkets in cities like Accra, Lagos, and Nairobi buy all available supply. Sixth, grasscutter farming is environmentally friendly; it does not compete with human food because they eat plants that people do not eat..Trim()

Basic Biology and Behavior of Grasscutters

Grasscutters (Thryonomys swinderianus) are large rodents native to sub-Saharan Africa. They are also called cane rats or marsh rats. An adult weighs 4 to 7 kg, with males larger than females. They have a lifespan of 4 to 6 years in captivity. Their body is covered with coarse, brownish-grey fur. They have short tails, small ears, and prominent orange-yellow incisors that grow continuously. They have poor eyesight but excellent hearing and smell. They are nocturnal, meaning they are most active at night. In the wild, they live near water bodies, in tall grass, and in abandoned burrows..Trim()

Grasscutters are herbivores and monogastric (single-stomach) animals. They eat a variety of grasses, leaves, stems, roots, and fruits. They need a diet high in fiber (18-24% crude fiber) for proper digestion. Their digestive system is similar to that of rabbits; they practice coprophagy (eating their soft feces) to extract more nutrients. They drink water frequently, especially when eating dry feed. They are social animals and can be kept in groups, but adult males may fight. A typical group consists of one male and 4-6 females. They communicate through squeaks, grunts, and teeth chattering..Trim()

Breeding biology: Females reach sexual maturity at 6-8 months, males at 8-10 months. The estrous cycle is about 7-9 days. Gestation lasts 150-160 days (about 5 months). They give birth to 1-6 young, average 3-4. The young are born with fur, eyes open, and are able to move within hours. They suckle for 4-6 weeks. Females can conceive again 2-3 weeks after giving birth. With proper nutrition, a female can produce 2-3 litters per year. Males should be removed from the pen before kindling to prevent cannibalism of the young..Trim()

Behavioral considerations: Grasscutters are creatures of habit. They are easily stressed by sudden changes in environment, diet, or handling. Stress can lead to disease and death. Handle them gently using a net or by grasping the base of the tail. They are curious and can escape through small holes, so pens must be secure. They are also good climbers and jumpers. Keep pens covered. They are most active at dusk and dawn. Provide nesting material like dry grass or wood shavings. They are generally docile but can bite if cornered. Understanding their behavior helps you manage them better and reduces mortality..Trim()

If for breeding, you might aim to sell 50 young animals per year. Next, assess your resources: land, capital, labor, and time. A small backyard farm can start with $500, while a commercial farm may need $5,000 or more. Set realistic production targets based on your resources. Module 2 · Housing and Equipment

Types of Grasscutter Housing Systems

When starting grasscutter farming, you must choose a housing system that fits your space, climate, and budget. The three main types are: deep litter pens, raised hutches, and cage batteries. Deep litter pens are the cheapest and most common in Africa. You build a concrete floor pen with walls about 1 metre high, and cover the floor with dry bedding like wood shavings or rice husks. This system works well in villages where space is available. However, it makes cleaning harder and can lead to disease if bedding is not changed regularly. Raised hutches are wooden or metal frames with wire mesh floors, raised about 0.8 to 1 metre off the ground. This allows droppings to fall through, keeping the animals clean and reducing parasite problems. Hutches are good for humid areas because they allow air circulation. They cost more to build but save money on veterinary bills. Cage batteries are rows of small cages stacked in tiers, often used in commercial farms. Each cage holds one adult grasscutter or a female with her young. Cages are usually made of galvanised wire mesh with a wooden or metal frame. They are easy to manage and save space, but the animals need careful handling to avoid stress. For a beginner in Africa, a raised hutch system is often the best compromise: it is affordable, reduces disease, and can be built with local materials like bamboo, timber, and wire mesh. Many farmers start with a simple deep litter pen and later add hutches as they expand. The key is to match the system to your climate: in hot, dry areas, deep litter with good ventilation works; in wet, humid areas, raised hutches prevent dampness. Always ensure the floor is easy to clean and the structure is predator-proof. Remember, grasscutters are rodents and can chew through soft wood, so use hard wood or wire mesh on edges. Choose a system you can manage daily, because grasscutters need fresh food and water every day. A good housing system will pay for itself by keeping your animals healthy and productive..Trim()

Constructing a Suitable Hutch or Cage

Building a hutch or cage for grasscutters requires careful planning. A standard hutch for one adult grasscutter should be at least 0.6 metres long, 0.5 metres wide, and 0.4 metres high. For a female with young, double the length to 1.2 metres. Use hardwood timber for the frame, because grasscutters can chew through softwood like pine. Treat the wood with a non-toxic preservative or used engine oil to deter termites and rot. The floor should be wire mesh with 1.5 cm by 1.5 cm holes, strong enough to support the animal but allow droppings to fall through. Cover the mesh with a removable tray underneath to collect waste. For the sides, use galvanised wire mesh with 2.5 cm by 2.5 cm holes. The front can have a small door with a latch for feeding and cleaning. The roof should be corrugated iron sheets or plastic, sloping slightly to shed rain. Raise the hutch 0.8 to 1 metre above ground using concrete blocks or wooden posts. This prevents dampness and keeps predators like snakes and rats away. In Africa, you can use bamboo poles for the frame if hardwood is expensive, but wrap the edges with wire mesh to prevent chewing. For a cage battery, build a wooden frame and attach wire mesh cages in two or three tiers, with a gap of 10 cm between tiers for ventilation. Each cage should have a feeder and drinker. Use 2.5 cm by 2.5 cm mesh for the cage body and 1.5 cm mesh for the floor. Fix the mesh with U-nails or fencing staples. Always sand rough edges to prevent injury. A simple hutch can be built in one day with basic tools: hammer, saw, pliers, tape measure, and nails. Cost of materials for one hutch: timber 10,000 Naira, wire mesh 5,000 Naira, roofing sheet 3,000 Naira, nails and staples 1,000 Naira. Total about 19,000 Naira (approx. $25). That is a small investment for a structure that will last 3-5 years. Remember to place the hutch under shade or build a simple roof to protect from direct sun and rain. Good construction means fewer escapes and healthier animals..Trim()

Safety and Security Measures

Grasscutters are prey animals, so they are easily stressed by predators and theft. Your housing must be secure against snakes, rats, owls, hawks, dogs, and humans. Use strong wire mesh with holes no larger than 1.5 cm to keep out snakes and rats. Bury the wire mesh 30 cm into the ground if you have a floor-level pen, or raise the hutch on smooth concrete pillars that predators cannot climb. For raised hutches, fit a metal cone or a sheet of zinc around each leg to prevent snakes and rats from climbing. Lock the cage doors with padlocks or strong latches. In Africa, theft is a real risk, so build the farm near your house or in a fenced compound. A guard dog can help, but ensure it cannot reach the grasscutters. Keep the area around the hutch clear of tall grass and bushes where snakes hide. Use traps and rodenticides carefully: poison can kill grasscutters if they eat it. Better to use snap traps outside the hutch. For fire safety, keep dry grass and bedding away from electrical wires. Use a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand near the farm. For animal safety, handle grasscutters gently. Never lift them by the tail; support the body with both hands. Use gloves to prevent bites. Keep young children and strangers away from the cages to reduce stress. Quarantine new animals for 2 weeks before introducing them to the main group. This prevents disease outbreaks. Module 3 · Feeding and Nutrition

Natural Diet of Grasscutters

Grasscutters are strict herbivores. In the wild, they eat a wide variety of grasses, leaves, roots, fruits, and bark. They are not rodents that eat meat or insects. Their natural diet is high in fibre and low in fat. In Africa, common wild foods include elephant grass, guinea grass, cassava leaves, sweet potato leaves, banana leaves, and the bark of certain trees. They also eat fruits like mango, pawpaw, and pineapple when available. Grasscutters are crepuscular, meaning they feed mainly at dawn and dusk. This is when they are most active and when you should offer most of their food. They have a large caecum (a pouch in the gut) that ferments fibre, similar to a rabbit. This means they need a constant supply of roughage to keep their gut healthy. If you feed too much soft, starchy food, they can get diarrhoea and die. In a farm setting, you must mimic this natural diet. Offer a mix of fresh grasses, legumes, and kitchen vegetables. Avoid mouldy or sprayed plants. Collect forage from clean areas away from roadsides where exhaust fumes and chemicals settle. A good rule: 70% grasses and leaves, 20% legumes and fruits, 10% supplementary feed like pellets or grains. Never feed meat, fish, or oily foods. Grasscutters also need to gnaw to wear down their teeth, so include tough stalks and branches. This natural feeding pattern will keep your animals healthy and reduce feed costs..Trim()

Nutrient Requirements for Different Stages

Grasscutters need different amounts of nutrients depending on their age and purpose. Newborn grasscutters (pups) drink only their mother's milk for the first two weeks. After that, they start nibbling soft grasses. From weaning (about 4 weeks) to 3 months, they need high protein for rapid growth. A diet with 16-18% crude protein is ideal. You can achieve this by mixing legumes like lucerne or moringa leaves with grasses. From 3 to 6 months, they are growing steadily and need 14-16% protein. Pregnant and lactating females need extra energy and protein: 16-18% protein and plenty of calcium for milk production. Add crushed oyster shells or bone meal to their feed. Breeding males need 14-16% protein to maintain fertility. Adult maintenance (non-breeding) requires only 12-14% protein. Fibre is critical at all stages: at least 20-30% crude fibre. Too little fibre causes digestive problems. Energy needs are moderate; grasscutters do not need high-energy feeds like maize in large amounts. Excess energy leads to fat deposits and poor breeding. Vitamins and minerals: always provide a mineral lick or mix. Calcium and phosphorus in a 2:1 ratio are important for bone and teeth development. Salt (sodium chloride) should be available as a lick. Vitamin A from green leafy plants is essential for eyesight and immunity. Vitamin D from sunlight is made naturally if they get some sun. Vitamin E from fresh greens supports reproduction. A simple rule: young and breeding animals get more protein and minerals; adults get more fibre. Adjust rations every month by observing body condition. If animals look thin, increase protein. If they look fat, reduce energy feeds and increase grasses..Trim()

Common Feed Ingredients and Formulation

You can feed grasscutters using locally available ingredients. The main ingredients are roughages (grasses, leaves, stalks), concentrates (grains, legumes, oilseed cakes), and supplements (minerals, vitamins). Common roughages: elephant grass, guinea grass, napier grass, cassava leaves (dried to remove cyanide), sweet potato vines, banana leaves, plantain leaves, and water hyacinth (washed). Legumes: lucerne, moringa, groundnut haulms, cowpea leaves, and soya bean leaves. Concentrates: maize (whole or crushed), sorghum, millet, wheat bran, rice bran, palm kernel cake, groundnut cake, and soya bean meal. For a simple home-made pellet or mash, mix by weight: 40% maize, 20% wheat bran, 15% groundnut cake, 10% soya bean meal, 10% dried cassava leaves, 4% bone meal, 1% salt. This gives about 16% protein. For young animals, increase groundnut cake and soya bean meal to 20% each and reduce maize to 30%. For adults, reduce protein sources and increase wheat bran to 30%. Always grind ingredients into a coarse meal. Mix thoroughly. You can add a little water to make a crumbly mash, but do not make it too wet. Feed the mash in clean troughs. Do not store mixed feed for more than 3 days in a dry place, as it can grow mould. Mouldy feed causes poisoning. You can also buy commercial rabbit pellets as a base, but they are often too low in fibre for grasscutters. If you use pellets, mix with chopped grasses. A good formulation is not expensive: use what you grow or can buy cheaply at local markets. Always introduce new feeds gradually over 5-7 days to avoid digestive upsets..Trim()

Feeding Schedule and Rations

Grasscutters do best on a routine. Feed them twice a day: early morning (6-7 am) and late afternoon (4-5 pm). This matches their natural feeding times. Give fresh green forage first, then a small amount of concentrate. A typical adult grasscutter (2-3 kg) eats about 300-500 grams of fresh forage per day. That is roughly one large handful of grasses and leaves. They also need about 50-100 grams of concentrate (mash or pellets). Pregnant and lactating females need double the concentrate: 100-150 grams. Young weaned grasscutters (1-2 months) eat 100-200 grams of forage and 20-30 grams of concentrate per day. Growing grasscutters (3-6 months) eat 250-400 grams forage and 40-60 grams concentrate. Always provide more forage than they can finish; they will eat what they need. Remove leftovers before the next feeding to prevent mould. Never feed only concentrate; always mix with roughage. If you feed only grasses, they may lose weight. If you feed only pellets, they may get diarrhoea. A good ratio: 70-80% forage, 20-30% concentrate by weight. For a colony of 10 adults, you need about 4-5 kg of fresh forage and 1 kg of concentrate daily. Plan your forage collection accordingly. During the dry season, when fresh grass is scarce, you can use dried grasses, hay, or silage. Soak dried grasses in clean water for 30 minutes before feeding to soften them. You can also feed kitchen scraps like carrot tops, cabbage leaves, and fruit peels, but avoid onions, garlic, and citrus peels. Always provide fresh drinking water. Adjust rations based on body condition: if animals are thin, increase concentrate; if fat, reduce it. Keep a simple record of how much you feed and how much they eat..Trim()

Supplements and Additives

Even with a good diet, grasscutters may need supplements to stay healthy. The most important is a mineral lick. You can make one by mixing equal parts of bone meal, crushed oyster shells, and salt. Add a little clay or red soil. Put it in a shallow dish or hang a block in the cage. They will lick it as needed. This provides calcium, phosphorus, and sodium. For vitamins, fresh green leaves are usually enough. But in the dry season, when greens are scarce, add a commercial vitamin premix for rabbits or poultry (follow the label, usually 1-2 grams per kg of feed). Do not overdose; too many vitamins can be toxic. Another useful supplement is brewer's yeast (1-2% of feed) for B vitamins, especially for pregnant females. For digestion, you can add a little charcoal or kaolin clay to the feed (1% of feed) to prevent diarrhoea. Some farmers add probiotics (e.g., Lactobacillus) to improve gut health. You can make a simple probiotic by fermenting rice water or milk with a little sugar for 2 days, then mixing 10 ml per litre of drinking water. For strong bones and teeth, provide cuttlebone or old bones (boiled and dried) for gnawing. Avoid adding antibiotics routinely; use only when prescribed by a vet. Never add growth hormones; they are illegal and harmful. Salt licks should always be available. If animals are off feed, check for mould or spoilage. Supplements should not replace good forage. They are called supplements for a reason. Always introduce any new supplement slowly over a week. Observe animals for any negative reactions like diarrhoea or refusal to eat. Keep records of what you supplement and when..Trim()

Water Management and Hygiene

Water is the most important nutrient. Grasscutters need clean, fresh water every day. They drink about 100-200 ml per adult per day, more in hot weather or when lactating. Use clean water from a borehole, well, or tap. Do not give stagnant or dirty water. Provide water in shallow, heavy bowls that they cannot tip over. Or use nipple drinkers designed for rabbits. Change water at least twice a day, morning and evening. Wash the bowls with clean water and a little soap, then rinse well. Dirty water spreads diseases like coccidiosis and diarrhoea. In hot African climates, water can grow algae quickly. Keep waterers in the shade. If you use a trough, clean it daily. Never let animals walk in their water. Place waterers away from feeding areas to avoid contamination with feed and droppings. Hygiene of feed and feeders is also critical. Feed troughs should be cleaned daily. Remove leftover feed before it spoils. Store feed in dry, airtight containers to prevent mould, rats, and insects. Mouldy feed contains aflatoxins that can kill grasscutters. Never feed mouldy or fermented feed. Forage should be washed if dusty or from areas with animal droppings. But do not soak leafy greens for too long; they lose vitamins. If you collect forage from the wild, check for snails, slugs, or insects that can carry parasites. Rinse in salt water (1 tablespoon per litre) for 5 minutes, then rinse with clean water. Keep the cage dry. Wet floors cause foot rot and respiratory problems. Provide good drainage. Clean cages weekly and disinfect monthly with a mild solution (e.g., 1% bleach), then rinse well and let dry before returning animals. Sick animals should be isolated and given fresh water with electrolytes (a pinch of salt and sugar in water). Always have water available; grasscutters can die from dehydration within a day..Trim()

Common Diseases and Parasites

Grasscutters are hardy, but they still get sick. The most common diseases you will see are coccidiosis, mange, pneumonia, and intestinal worms. Coccidiosis is caused by a tiny parasite in the gut. It mostly hits young grasscutters, causing bloody diarrhoea, weight loss, and death if not treated. You will see them sitting hunched with rough fur. Treat with amprolium or sulphonamides in drinking water for 5 days. Mange is caused by mites that burrow into the skin. You will notice hair loss, crusty patches on the ears, nose, and feet, and the animal scratching constantly. Treat by injecting ivermectin (0.2 mg per kg body weight) under the skin, and repeat after 10 days. Also clean the cage with a flame or strong disinfectant to kill mites. Pneumonia is common when the hutch is damp or poorly ventilated. Signs include coughing, noisy breathing, and a runny nose. Keep the hutch dry and use antibiotics like oxytetracycline if severe. Intestinal worms are very common in grasscutters that eat fresh grass. You may see worms in droppings or a pot-bellied look. Deworm every 3 months with albendazole or levamisole. External parasites like ticks and fleas can also attack. Use tick grease or spray the hutch with acaricide. Always isolate sick animals immediately to stop spread. Many diseases come from dirty water, wet bedding, or introducing new animals without quarantine. Prevention is cheaper than treatment: clean cages daily, provide clean water, and avoid overcrowding. If you see sudden deaths, take the dead animal to a vet for post-mortem to know the real cause..Trim()

Safe Handling Techniques

Grasscutters are strong, fast rodents with sharp incisors. Never handle them by the tail or scruff roughly. Always wear thick leather gloves and long sleeves. Approach calmly from the side, not from above. Use both hands: one under the chest, one supporting the hindquarters. For a small grasscutter, you can lift it by cupping your hands under its belly. For larger ones, hold it close to your body to prevent kicking. If you must move a grasscutter from a cage, use a transfer box or a net with a solid bottom. Never grab a grasscutter by the ears or legs. If it bites, do not pull away; instead, push gently toward its mouth to make it release. Have a first aid kit with antiseptic and bandages ready. In Africa, many farmers use a thick cloth sack as a makeshift handler: place the sack over the grasscutter, then scoop it up. This reduces stress and protects your hands. Always wash your hands after handling. If a grasscutter escapes, do not chase it; use a towel to corner it and guide it into a cage. Practice handling young grasscutters from 4 weeks old for 5 minutes daily so they get used to you. This makes adult handling easier. Remember, a calm handler makes a calm grasscutter. Speak softly and move slowly. With practice, you can safely handle even a 6 kg adult..Trim()

Reducing Stress During Handling

Stress can kill grasscutters, especially pregnant females or young ones. Handle only when necessary: for weighing, health checks, or moving. Do it in the cool of the morning or evening, not midday heat. Keep the handling session short: under 5 minutes. Have everything ready before you pick up the animal: scale, record book, medicine. Work in a quiet, dimly lit area. Loud noises, dogs, or shouting cause panic. Use a cloth to cover the grasscutter's eyes; this calms it. Hold it firmly but gently. If the animal struggles, pause and wait for it to settle. Never lift a grasscutter by the tail; that causes pain and stress. After handling, return it to its cage and give a treat like a piece of sweet potato or cassava. Observe for signs of stress: rapid breathing, trembling, or refusal to eat. If stressed, leave it alone for 24 hours. In African farms, many use a 'handling box' â a small wooden box with a sliding door â to confine the animal during examination. This reduces struggle. Also, handle grasscutters in groups if they are used to each other; isolation increases stress. Regular gentle handling from a young age reduces fear. Restraint Methods for Examinations

For a full examination, you need to restrain the grasscutter safely. Use a 'V' shaped wooden trough or a thick towel wrapped around the body, leaving the head and hindquarters exposed. For small grasscutters, you can hold them in your lap with one hand over the shoulders and the other under the belly. Transportation of Grasscutters

Transporting grasscutters requires careful planning to avoid injury and stress. Use a sturdy wooden or plastic crate with ventilation holes (1 cm diameter, spaced 10 cm apart). After arrival, let the grasscutter rest for 24 hours before handling or mixing with others. Check for signs of stress: drooling, rapid breathing, or aggression. Behavioral Enrichment

Grasscutters are intelligent and curious. Without enrichment, they become bored, stressed, and may develop bad habits like biting bars or over-grooming. Enrichment means giving them things to do. Provide chew toys: untreated wooden blocks, branches from safe trees like mango or guava (avoid toxic ones like eucalyptus). Give them cardboard tubes or PVC pipes to crawl through. This reduces stress. In African farms, many use old tires or clay pots as hiding spots. Also, scatter food around the cage instead of using a bowl; this encourages foraging. Give them a variety of foods: grasses, legumes, fruits, and vegetables. A varied diet is mental stimulation. Play soft radio music during the day to mask sudden noises. Observe your grasscutters: if they ignore a toy, try a different one. Enrichment reduces aggression and improves breeding success. It also makes handling easier because the animals are calmer. Ethical Considerations in Farming

Farming grasscutters ethically means treating them with respect and minimizing suffering. Provide clean water, nutritious food, and enough space. Use pain relief if available. For slaughter, use a quick, humane method: a sharp blow to the back of the head followed by bleeding. This is faster and less stressful than other methods. Respect cultural and religious practices, but avoid unnecessary pain. In Africa, many farmers sell live grasscutters; ensure they are transported humanely. Do not separate young from mothers before 4 weeks. Wean at 4-6 weeks. Provide enrichment to prevent boredom. Keep records to monitor health and welfare. If you see signs of stress or disease, act quickly. Ethical farming also means being responsible to the environment: do not release grasscutters into the wild where they are not native. Dispose of waste properly. Treat your workers fairly; they should also handle animals gently. By farming ethically, you produce healthier meat and build a better reputation. Types of Waste Produced

Grasscutter farming produces several types of waste that you must manage daily. The main solid waste is bedding material mixed with droppings. Knowing these types helps you plan storage and disposal. For example, urine and wastewater should not run into a neighbor's plot. Solid waste can be composted. Common Problems and Solutions

Grasscutter farmers in Africa often face sudden deaths, poor breeding, and disease outbreaks. The most common problem is pneumonia, caused by damp bedding and poor ventilation. Solution: keep cages dry, use wire mesh floors, and provide dry bedding like wood shavings. If you see coughing or nasal discharge, isolate the animal and treat with oxytetracycline injection (1 ml per 5 kg body weight) for 3 days. Another common issue is cannibalism, especially when overcrowded. Provide at least 0.5 square meters per adult grasscutter and give them plenty of fresh forage like elephant grass, sweet potato vines, and cassava leaves. If a female rejects her kits, check for mastitis (swollen, hot teats). Treat with penicillin injection and warm compresses. Kits may need hand-feeding with goat milk using a syringe every 2 hours. Diarrhea in young grasscutters is often due to wet, spoiled feed. Remove wet feed immediately and offer dry hay and clean water. Add a tablespoon of honey to 1 liter of water to rehydrate. If diarrhea persists, use kaolin-pectin oral suspension (1 ml per kg) twice daily. Skin problems like mange are treated with ivermectin injection (0.2 mg per kg) under the skin, repeated after 10 days. Always consult a vet if unsure. Record every problem and treatment in a notebook to learn patterns. Many issues are preventable with good hygiene, proper spacing, and consistent feeding schedules.
