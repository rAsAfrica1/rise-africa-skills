Module 1 · Introduction to Welding and Safety

Overview of Welding Processes and Careers

Welding is the process of joining metals by heating them to melting point and allowing them to fuse. In Africa, welding is a high-demand skill in construction, mining, agriculture, and manufacturing. Common welding processes you will learn include stick welding (SMAW), MIG welding (GMAW), TIG welding (GTAW), and oxy-acetylene welding. Stick welding is the most common in rural areas because it is cheap and works outdoors. MIG welding is faster and used in workshops for production. TIG welding is for precise work on thin metals like aluminium and stainless steel..Trim()

As a welder, you can work in fabrication shops, on construction sites, in mines, or start your own business making gates, windows, wheelbarrows, and water tanks. In many African countries, a skilled welder can earn between $200 and $800 per month, depending on location and experience. With additional training, you can become a welding inspector, supervisor, or instructor. You can also specialize in pipeline welding, which pays very well in oil and gas regions..Trim()

.Trim()

To start, you need basic tools: a welding machine (stick welder is cheapest), electrode holder, ground clamp, chipping hammer, wire brush, and personal protective equipment (PPE). You also need a safe workspace with good ventilation and a fire extinguisher. Practice on scrap metal first. Learn to strike an arc, maintain a stable arc length, and control travel speed. A good weld is smooth, consistent, and penetrates the metal..Trim()

.Trim()

Formal certification is not always required, but it helps you get better jobs. Look for local vocational training centres or apprenticeships with experienced welders. In many countries, you can take a test to become a certified welder, such as the AWS D1.1 or ISO 9606. Start small, build your skills, and gradually invest in better equipment. Welding can be a lifelong career with opportunities across Africa and abroad..Trim()

Welding Shop Safety Rules and PPE

Welding is dangerous if you ignore safety. Every year, thousands of welders suffer eye injuries, burns, and respiratory problems. The first rule is to wear proper personal protective equipment (PPE). This includes a welding helmet with a shaded lens (at least shade 10 for stick welding), safety glasses under the helmet, leather gloves, a leather apron, and steel-toe boots. Never weld in shorts, sandals, or synthetic clothing that can melt..Trim()

.Trim()

Your welding helmet protects your eyes from arc flash, which can cause 'arc eye'âa painful burn of the cornea. If you experience arc eye, see a doctor immediately; do not rub your eyes. Use a auto-darkening helmet if you can afford one; it makes welding easier and safer. Ear protection is also important because welding can be noisy, and sparks can enter your ears..Trim()

.Trim()

Keep your work area clean and dry. Remove flammable materials like paper, cloth, and solvents within 10 meters. Use a fire blanket or extinguisher nearby. Never weld on containers that held fuel or chemicals unless they have been thoroughly cleaned and purged. Use a fume extractor or weld outdoors to avoid breathing toxic fumes. In enclosed spaces, forced ventilation is mandatory..Trim()

.Trim()

Other rules: Inspect your equipment before use. Check for cracked cables, loose connections, and damaged electrodes. Do not weld in wet conditions or while standing in water. Use a dry wooden board or rubber mat to insulate yourself. Never point the electrode at anyone. Turn off the machine when not in use. Always have a fire watcher if welding near combustibles. Follow these rules, and you will avoid most accidents..Trim()

Fire Prevention and Hot Work Permits

Welding produces sparks and hot slag that can travel up to 10 meters and start fires. In Africa, many workshops are made of wood or have thatched roofs, making fire prevention critical. Before you start welding, clear the area of all flammable materials: paper, cardboard, cloth, dry grass, petrol, paint, and solvents. If you cannot move them, cover them with fire-resistant blankets. Have a fire extinguisher (dry chemical or CO2) within easy reach, and know how to use it. A bucket of sand or water can also help..Trim()

.Trim()

A hot work permit is a formal check before welding in a new location, especially on construction sites or in factories. It ensures that someone has inspected the area, removed hazards, and assigned a fire watcher. Even if your shop does not use permits, you should do your own check: Is the floor clean? Are there flammable gases? Is there a fire exit? If you are welding on a wall, check the other side for hidden combustibles. Sparks can fall through cracks and start a fire in a hidden space..Trim()

.Trim()

After welding, stay in the area for at least 30 minutes to watch for smoldering fires. Many fires start after the welder leaves. Use a thermal camera if available, or just feel for heat with the back of your hand (do not touch hot metal). Never weld near fuel tanks, gas cylinders, or paint booths. If you must weld near them, use fire blankets and have a dedicated fire watcher..Trim()

.Trim()

In many African countries, fire services are far away, so prevention is your only protection. Train everyone in your shop on fire response. Keep a phone charged to call for help. If a fire starts, turn off the welding machine, alert others, and fight the fire only if it is small and you have a clear escape route. Otherwise, evacuate and call for help..Trim()

Ventilation and Fume Hazards

Welding fumes are a mixture of metal oxides, gases, and particulates that can cause serious health problems. Common fumes include manganese, chromium, nickel, and iron oxide. Exposure can lead to metal fume fever (flu-like symptoms), lung damage, and even cancer. In Africa, many welders work in small, poorly ventilated workshops, increasing the risk. You must take ventilation seriously..Trim()

.Trim()

The best ventilation is natural: weld outdoors or with doors and windows open. If you must weld indoors, use a fume extractor or a fan to blow fumes away from your breathing zone. A simple cross-draftâopening two opposite windowsâcan help. Never weld in a confined space without forced ventilation and a respirator. For stick welding, use a respirator with P100 filters. For stainless steel, use a supplied-air respirator because chromium fumes are very toxic..Trim()

.Trim()

Position your work so that fumes rise away from your face. Keep your head out of the plume. Use a welding table with a down-draft system if possible. In many African workshops, you can build a simple fume hood using a metal box and a fan. Even a cardboard box with a fan can reduce fume exposure..Trim()

.Trim()

Know the signs of overexposure: dizziness, nausea, headache, or metallic taste in your mouth. If you feel these, stop welding, get fresh air, and drink water. If symptoms persist, see a doctor. Long-term exposure can cause chronic bronchitis or nervous system damage. Wear a respirator even if you think the area is well-ventilated. Your health is more important than speed. Also, keep your work area clean to avoid dust that can mix with fumes..Trim()

Electrical Safety in Welding

Welding machines use high currentsâoften 80 to 300 ampsâthat can kill you. The open-circuit voltage of a stick welder is typically 50 to 80 volts, which is enough to cause a fatal shock if you are sweaty or standing in water. Electrical safety is non-negotiable. First, ensure your machine is properly grounded. Use a three-prong plug and a grounded outlet. Never cut off the ground pin. If your shop has no proper ground, install a grounding rod..Trim()

.Trim()

Inspect all cables and connections daily. Look for cracks, exposed wires, and loose lugs. Replace damaged cables immediately. Do not use tape as a permanent repair. Keep cables dry and off the floor. Use rubber mats or wooden boards to stand on. Wear dry leather gloves and boots. Never weld with bare hands or wet gloves. If you feel a tingle, stop immediately and fix the problem..Trim()

.Trim()

Do not weld in wet conditions. If you must weld outdoors in the rain, use a tent or shelter. Never weld while standing in water or on a wet floor. Use a GFCI (ground fault circuit interrupter) if available. In many African countries, power supply is unstable, so use a voltage stabilizer or surge protector to protect your machine. Also, avoid overloading circuits. Use the correct fuse or breaker size..Trim()

.Trim()

Turn off the machine when changing electrodes, adjusting the wire, or leaving the work area. Lock out the power if you are doing maintenance. Never touch the electrode and the ground clamp at the same time. Keep your body insulated from the work piece. If you are welding on a metal structure, use a insulating mat. Remember, electricity seeks the path of least resistanceâoften through you. Stay dry, stay insulated, and stay alive..Trim()

Emergency Procedures and First Aid

Even with all precautions, accidents happen. You must know how to respond. The first step in any emergency is to turn off the welding machine and remove the victim from the hazard if safe. For electrical shock, do not touch the person with your bare hands. Use a dry wooden stick or rope to pull them away. Then check for breathing and pulse. Call for help immediately. In Africa, emergency numbers vary: 112 in many countries, 999 in Kenya, 10111 in South Africa. Know your local number..Trim()

.Trim()

For burns, cool the burn with clean running water for at least 10 minutes. Do not apply ice, butter, or oil. Cover with a sterile bandage or clean cloth. Do not break blisters. For arc eye, cover the eyes with a cool, damp cloth and seek medical attention. Do not rub. For cuts, apply pressure to stop bleeding, clean with antiseptic, and bandage. If bleeding is severe, use a tourniquet only as a last resort..Trim()

.Trim()

For fume inhalation, move the person to fresh air. If they are not breathing, start CPR if trained. For eye injuries from flying particles, do not try to remove the object; cover the eye and get medical help. Keep a first aid kit in your shop with bandages, antiseptic, burn gel, eye wash, and scissors. Check it monthly and replace expired items..Trim()

.Trim()

Fire emergencies: If a fire starts, turn off the machine, alert everyone, and evacuate. Use a fire extinguisher only if the fire is small and you have a clear exit. Never turn your back on a fire. If your clothes catch fire, stop, drop, and roll. Practice fire drills with your team. In many African workshops, fire extinguishers are not available, so keep sand and water buckets. Also, have a phone with credit to call for help. Being prepared saves lives..Trim()

Hand Tools for Welding and Cutting

As a welder in Africa, you will often work without electricity, so hand tools are your lifeline. Start with a good quality chipping hammerâthe kind with a spring handle reduces shock. Use it to remove slag after each weld pass. A wire brush (stainless steel for stainless steel, carbon steel for mild steel) cleans the weld before and after. For cutting, you need a hacksaw with 18 or 24 teeth per inch blade for metal. In many workshops, you'll also find a cold chisel and hammer for cutting rivets or bolts. Always wear safety glasses when using these tools..Trim()

.Trim()

For oxy-fuel cutting, you need a cutting torch, oxygen and acetylene cylinders, regulators, hoses, and a spark lighter. In Africa, many welders use propane or LPG instead of acetylene because it's cheaper and more available. But LPG burns cooler, so you need a different torch tip. Always check hoses for cracksâleaking gas is deadly. Use soapy water to test for leaks; never use a flame..Trim()

.Trim()

A bench vise is essential for holding work while you cut or grind. Mount it securely on a heavy table. Files are used for deburring and smoothing edges. A half-round file is most versatile. For scraping slag from tight corners, a slag hammer with a pointed tip works well. Keep your tools clean and oiled to prevent rustâhumidity in coastal areas like Mombasa or Lagos will rust tools quickly..Trim()

.Trim()

When buying hand tools, look for forged steel, not cast iron. Brands like Stanley, Irwin, and local equivalents are fine. A good chipping hammer costs about $5-10. A hacksaw frame with blade costs $10-15. Invest in a file card to clean files. Store tools in a dry box with a little oil. Remember: a sharp tool is a safe tool. Dull blades require more force and slip easily, causing injuries..Trim()

Power Tools: Grinders, Drills, and Saws

Power tools speed up your work, but in Africa, power supply can be unreliable. A 4.5-inch angle grinder is the most important power tool for a welder. Use it with a grinding wheel to smooth welds, a cutting wheel to cut metal, and a wire cup brush to clean rust. Always unplug the grinder before changing wheels. Wear a face shield and gloves. In many African markets, you can buy a decent 4.5-inch grinder for $30-50. Brands like Bosch, Makita, or Chinese equivalents like Total or Ingco work well..Trim()

For drilling, a 1/2-inch electric drill with variable speed is useful. Use high-speed steel (HSS) drill bits for metal. Apply cutting oil or even used engine oil to keep the bit cool. Drill at slow speed for large holes. If you don't have electricity, a hand drill or a brace and bit works, but it's slow. A portable band saw or a chop saw is great for cutting metal accurately. A chop saw with an abrasive wheel cuts angle iron and pipe quickly. Always clamp your work securely..Trim()

In areas with frequent power cuts, consider a generator or inverter. A 2-3 kVA generator can run a grinder and small welder. But remember: power tools draw a lot of current. Use an extension cord with the correct wire gauge (at least 2.5 mmÂ² for 20 meters). Never use a damaged cord. Check the plug and cable before each use..Trim()

.Trim()

Maintenance: blow out dust from the motor vents weekly. Replace carbon brushes when they wear down. Keep grinding wheels dry and free from cracks. Store power tools in a dry place. If you work in a dusty environment, clean the tool after each use. A grinder that overheats will burn out quickly. Let it cool down. Always use the guard on a grinderâit protects you from flying debris. Safety first: wear goggles, gloves, and hearing protection..Trim()

Measuring and Layout Tools

Accurate measurement is the difference between a strong weld and a failed joint. You need a steel measuring tape (5 meters is standard), a steel rule (150 mm or 300 mm), and a try square. A try square checks 90-degree angles. In Africa, many welders use a combination square because it can also mark 45 degrees. A scribe or a sharp pencil marks metal. For layout, use a soapstone or a permanent markerâsoapstone is better because it doesn't burn away when you weld..Trim()

A center punch marks drilling points. A dividers or compass draws circles. For pipe work, a wrap-around or a pipe tape measures circumference. Buy stainless steel tools to resist rust..Trim()

When measuring, always measure twice and cut once. Use the same tape for all measurements on a project to avoid errors. For welding, you often need to allow for shrinkage. Steel expands when heated and contracts when cooled. For a 1-meter weld, allow about 1-2 mm shrinkage. Use a welding gauge to check fillet weld sizes and reinforcement. Mark clearly with soapstone. If you need precision, use a scriber and a hammer to make a small mark. For layout on curved surfaces, use a flexible curve or a piece of wire. In the field, you can use a chalk line for long straight lines. Always keep your measuring tools clean and dry. Store them in a case. A bent tape or a damaged square will give wrong readings. Clamps, Jigs, and Fixtures

Clamps hold your work steady so you can weld accurately. The most common are C-clamps, F-clamps, and bar clamps. In Africa, you can buy cheap C-clamps from local markets. A 4-inch C-clamp costs about $3-5. Use at least two clamps for a butt joint. For pipe welding, use a pipe clamp or a chain clamp. Magnetic clamps are very useful for holding steel plates at 90 degrees. A magnetic welding clamp costs $10-20 and can hold up to 50 kg. But remember: magnets lose strength when heated, so don't weld too close..Trim()

Jigs and fixtures are custom-made tools that hold parts in the correct position for welding. They save time and ensure repeatability. For example, if you are making 100 identical gates, a jig will hold the frame square while you weld. You can make a simple jig from angle iron and clamps. Use a steel table as a fixtureâweld a few tabs to hold your work. But be careful: welding directly to a table can distort it. Use spacers or copper backing..Trim()

Tack welds are your friend. Before fully welding, put small tack welds (about 10-15 mm long) at the corners and every 150-200 mm along the joint. Check squareness and alignment. If something is off, you can break the tacks with a chipping hammer and re-clamp. Always use a square to check 90-degree angles. For round parts, use a pipe clamp or a vise..Trim()

When clamping, don't over-tightenâyou can distort thin metal. Use a piece of wood or copper as a pad. For aluminum, use aluminum clamps to avoid contamination. Keep clamps clean and oil the threads. A rusty clamp will seize. Store clamps hanging on a rack. In the field, you can use a chain and a turnbuckle as a clamp. Be creative, but always ensure the work is secure before welding. A loose part can cause a dangerous kickback..Trim()

Welding Cables, Connectors, and Grounding

Welding cables carry high current from the machine to the electrode holder and work clamp. In Africa, you'll find cables with different insulation. Use rubber-insulated cable (like SOOW or H07RN-F) because it's flexible and oil-resistant. The cable size depends on amperage. For up to 200 amps, use 35 mmÂ² cable. For 300 amps, use 50 mmÂ². Never use undersized cableâit will overheat and cause a fire. A 10-meter length of 35 mmÂ² cable costs about $30-50..Trim()

Connectors must be tight. The electrode holder (stinger) should be rated for your amperage. A 300-amp holder costs $10-20. The work clamp (ground clamp) must make good contact. Clean the metal before clamping. A poor ground causes erratic arc and shocks. Use a brass or copper clamp. Never clamp to painted or rusty metal. In the field, you can clamp to a steel table or a piece of scrap..Trim()

Grounding is critical for safety. The work clamp should be connected to the workpiece, not to the ground. The welder's case must be grounded to earth. Use a ground rod if required. In many African workshops, grounding is ignored, but it can save your life. If you feel a tingle, stop immediately. Check cables for cuts, exposed wires, and damaged insulation. Replace damaged cables. Use cable connectors (quick connectors) to join cables. They must be tight and insulated..Trim()

.Trim()

Inspect cables daily. Look for burns, cracks, and exposed copper. Repair with heat shrink or electrical tapeâbut tape is temporary. Better to replace. Keep cables coiled loosely when not in use. Don't drag cables over sharp edges. Store in a dry place. For long runs, use a cable cover or ramp to prevent tripping. Remember: a good ground is 50% of a good weld..Trim()

Equipment Maintenance and Inspection

Maintenance keeps your tools safe and working. Start with a daily check: inspect cables, holders, clamps, and power tools. Look for damage, loose connections, and wear. A 5-minute check can prevent accidents. For welding machines, check the cooling fan and vents. Blow out dust with compressed air. Check the electrode holder for loose parts. Replace worn tips. For engine-driven welders, check oil and fuel daily. Change oil every 100 hours. Clean the air filter every 50 hours..Trim()

.Trim()

Grinders need regular care. Replace carbon brushes every 100-200 hours. Check the cord for cuts. Clean the vents. Replace grinding wheels when they are worn down to 2/3 of original diameter. Never use a cracked wheel. Store wheels flat. For drills, check the chuck and replace if worn. Lubricate the chuck. For saws, check blades for missing teeth. Replace dull bladesâthey cause kickback..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, dust and humidity are enemies. After work, wipe tools with an oily rag. Store in a dry box with silica gel. For welding machines, keep them covered when not in use. Use a voltage stabilizer if power fluctuates. Check the ground connection monthly. Test the insulation resistance with a megohmmeter if available. A simple test: use a multimeter to check for continuity between the case and the ground pin..Trim()

.Trim()

Create a maintenance log. Record dates of inspection, repairs, and replacements. This helps you track when to service. For example, if you use your grinder daily, plan to replace brushes every 3 months. Keep spare parts: fuses, brushes, tips, and wheels. In remote areas, order spares before you run out. Train yourself to notice unusual sounds, smells, or vibrations. A bearing whine means it's failing. Fix small problems before they become big. A well-maintained tool lasts years and keeps you safe..Trim()

SMAW Principles and Equipment Setup

Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW), often called stick welding, is the most common welding process in African workshops because it is cheap, portable, and works outdoors. .Trim()

GTAW Equipment and Setup

Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW), also called TIG welding, uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode to create an arc. The arc melts the base metal, and you add filler rod separately if needed. The main equipment includes a welding machine (constant current type), a TIG torch with a tungsten electrode, a shielding gas cylinder (usually argon), a gas regulator and flowmeter, a foot pedal or amperage control, and cables. In Africa, you can find affordable TIG machines from brands like Riland, Jasic, or even modified stick welders with TIG kits. For basic steel and stainless steel up to 6mm thick, a 200-amp inverter machine is sufficient. You'll need a gas flow rate of about 10-15 litres per minute (LPM) for most jobs. Set up your machine: connect the ground clamp to your workbench or workpiece, plug the torch cable into the negative terminal (for DCEN, which is standard for steel), and connect the gas hose from the regulator to the torch. The tungsten electrode goes into the torch collet, and you tighten the collet body. The electrode should stick out about 3-6mm from the ceramic cup. Choose a cup size based on access: a #7 (11mm) cup is a good all-rounder. Before welding, open the gas valve on the cylinder, set the flowmeter to 12 LPM, and check for leaks with soapy water. Turn on the machine, set it to DCEN (electrode negative), and set your amperage. For 3mm steel, start with 80-100 amps. Practice striking an arc on a scrap piece: with the foot pedal, press down slowly until the arc starts, then maintain. Remember, GTAW requires clean metalâgrind off rust, paint, and oil. In many African workshops, you'll use a generator or mains power; ensure stable voltage. A stable 220V supply is ideal. Keep your work area dry and well-ventilated..Trim()

Tungsten Electrode Preparation

The tungsten electrode is the heart of GTAW. It carries the arc and must be prepared correctly to get a stable, focused arc. There are several types: pure tungsten (green) for aluminum on AC, 2% thoriated (red) for steel on DC, and 2% ceriated (grey) or lanthanated (gold) for general use. Thoriated tungsten is common but slightly radioactive; ceriated or lanthanated are safer and work well on both AC and DC. In Africa, 2% lanthanated (gold) is a good choice because it handles both steel and aluminum and is readily available from welding suppliers. To prepare the electrode, you need a dedicated grinding wheelânever use the same wheel for steel, as contamination ruins the tungsten. Grind the electrode lengthwise (parallel to its axis), not radially. This creates a sharp point that directs the arc. For DCEN on steel, grind a sharp taper about 2.5 times the electrode diameter. For example, a 2.4mm (3/32 inch) electrode should have a taper about 6mm long. For AC on aluminum, you can ball the tip slightly by welding on a scrap piece, but a slightly truncated point also works. Always wear safety glasses and a dust mask when grinding thoriated tungsten. After grinding, insert the electrode into the torch collet so it sticks out about 3-6mm from the cup. If you're welding at low amps (under 50A), you can have a sharper point; at high amps, a slightly blunter tip lasts longer. Check the tip often: if it gets contaminated (touches the weld pool or filler), stop, remove it, and regrind. A contaminated electrode causes an erratic arc and poor weld quality. Keep several pre-ground electrodes on hand. Store them in a clean tube. In a busy workshop, you might grind 5-10 electrodes at a time. Remember, tungsten is expensive, so grind only when needed. A properly prepared electrode makes welding easier and gives you clean, strong welds..Trim()

Shielding Gas and Torch Angle

GTAW relies on shielding gas to protect the molten weld pool from air. The most common gas is argon (100% Ar) for both steel and aluminum. For thicker stainless steel (over 6mm), you can add 2-5% hydrogen to argon for faster welding, but pure argon is fine for most jobs. In Africa, argon cylinders are available from gas suppliers like Afrox, BOC, or local distributors. You'll need a regulator and flowmeter. Set the flow rate between 10 and 15 litres per minute (LPM). For flat welding, hold the torch perpendicular to the work (90 degrees) or tilted back 10-15 degrees in the direction of travel. This angle lets you see the puddle and directs the arc. For butt welds, a slight push angle (tilted forward) helps. For fillet welds, angle the torch about 45 degrees between the two plates. Keep the arc length short: about 2-3mm. A long arc spreads the heat and reduces penetration. The tungsten should be close to the puddle but not touching. Watch the puddle: it should be shiny and calm. If it looks turbulent or has black specks, you have contamination or poor gas coverage. Move the torch steadily, and dip the filler rod into the front edge of the puddle. For left-handed welders, mirror the technique. Practice on scrap: run beads without filler to get used to the arc and gas flow. Then add filler. Foot Pedal and Amperage Control

The foot pedal (also called a foot control or amperage control) is what makes GTAW so precise. It lets you adjust the welding current while welding, which is essential for controlling heat input. Filler Rod Addition Techniques

Adding filler rod in GTAW is a skill that takes practice. Unlike stick welding, the filler is added separately, and you control the rate. GTAW Safety and Applications

GTAW safety is similar to other welding processes but with some specific hazards. The arc produces intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation that can burn your eyes and skin. Always wear a welding helmet with a shade 10-13 lens, depending on amperage. For low amps (under 50A), shade 10 is fine; for high amps (over 150A), use shade 12 or 13. Wear flame-resistant clothing, leather gloves, and a leather apron. Never weld with bare skin exposed. The UV can cause 'arc eye' (painful eye inflammation) and skin burns. Also, the high-frequency (HF) start on some TIG machines can interfere with pacemakers; consult a doctor if you have one. Shielding gas (argon) is an asphyxiant: it displaces oxygen. Weld in a well-ventilated area. If welding in a confined space, use forced ventilation and a gas monitor. Argon is heavier than air, so it can pool in low areas. In African workshops, many are open-air, which is good, but if you weld inside, open doors and windows. Never weld on containers that held fuel or chemicals without proper cleaning. GTAW is used for many applications: welding stainless steel in food and dairy industries (tanks, pipes), aluminum in automotive and aerospace, and thin-gauge metals in art and furniture. In Africa, common applications include repairing aluminum radiators, fabricating stainless steel kitchen equipment, and building bicycle frames. GTAW gives clean, precise welds, but it is slower than stick or MIG. It's ideal for thin materials (0.5mm to 6mm) and for metals that need high quality. For thicker steel, stick or MIG is more economical. To start, practice on mild steel. Then try stainless and aluminum. Always clean your metal before welding. Keep your work area tidy. Have a fire extinguisher nearby. Never weld near flammable materials. Use a welding screen to protect others from UV. Remember, safety is your responsibility. A safe welder is a productive welder..Trim()

Flux-cored wire comes in two main types: gas-shielded and self-shielded. Gas-shielded wire, like E70T-1, requires an external shielding gas (usually CO2 or a mix). Gas-Shielded vs. Self-Shielded FCAW

The biggest choice in FCAW is whether to use gas shielding or not. Gas-shielded FCAW uses a separate gas cylinder to protect the weld pool. Parameter Adjustment for FCAW

Getting the right parameters is the difference between a strong weld and a failed one. The three main settings are voltage, wire feed speed (WFS), and travel speed. Voltage controls arc length and bead width. Wire feed speed controls amperage and penetration. Travel speed controls bead size and fusion. If the arc is too long (voltage too high), you'll get a hissing sound, excessive spatter, and a flat, wide bead. Decrease voltage. Wire feed speed: if the wire burns back to the tip, increase WFS. If the wire stubs into the plate, decrease WFS. A good rule: set WFS first to get the amperage you want, then adjust voltage to fine-tune the arc. Porosity is the most common FCAW defectâsmall holes in the weld. Causes: gas flow too low or too high (for gas-shielded), wind blowing away shielding gas, dirty base metal, damp wire, or too long stickout. Module 8 · Reading Welding Symbols and Blueprints

Basic Blueprint Lines and Views

A blueprint is a drawing that shows how a metal part should be made. In welding, you must read blueprints to know where to weld, what size weld, and what type. The first thing to learn is the lines. A visible line is a thick solid line that shows the outline of the part. A hidden line is a dashed line that shows edges you cannot see from the front. A centerline is a thin line with long and short dashes, used to mark the center of holes or round parts. A dimension line has arrows at both ends and shows the size, like 50 mm. An extension line is a thin line that extends from the part to the dimension line. A break line is a wavy or zigzag line that shows where a part is broken to save space on the drawing..Trim()

.Trim()

Blueprints also show different views. The most common is the front view, which shows the part as you look at it from the front. The top view shows the part from above, and the side view shows it from the side. In Africa, many workshops use third-angle projection, where the top view is above the front view and the side view is to the right. But some countries use first-angle projection, where the top view is below the front view. Always check the projection symbol on the drawing. It looks like a small cone or funnel. If the wide end is on the left, it is third-angle. If the wide end is on the right, it is first-angle..Trim()

.Trim()

When you look at a blueprint, start by finding the title block. It is usually in the bottom right corner. It tells you the part name, material, scale, and date. The scale might be 1:1, meaning the drawing is the same size as the part. Or 1:2, meaning the drawing is half the size. Use a ruler to measure dimensions if they are not written. But always trust the written dimensions over measuring..Trim()

.Trim()

Practice by getting a simple bracket drawing from a local technical college or online. Trace the lines with a pencil and label each type. Then look at the views and imagine the 3D shape. In many African welding shops, blueprints are printed on A3 paper. Keep them clean and dry. If you cannot read blueprints, you will waste metal and time. So spend time learning these basics..Trim()

Welding Symbol Components

A welding symbol is a drawing note that tells the welder what weld to make. It has several parts. The horizontal line is called the reference line. Everything else attaches to it. At the left end, there is an arrow that points to the joint. The arrow can have a break, called an arrow-side or other-side designation. If the symbol is below the reference line, the weld is on the arrow side. If it is above, the weld is on the other side. If it is on both sides, you weld both sides..Trim()

.Trim()

On the reference line, you may see a tail. The tail is where additional information goes, like a welding process (e.g., SMAW for stick welding) or a specification. If there is no tail, it means no special notes. The tail can also have a circle, which means weld all around. A flag at the junction of the arrow and reference line means field weld, meaning weld on site, not in the shop..Trim()

.Trim()

The most important part is the weld symbol itself. It sits on the reference line. For a fillet weld, the symbol is a right triangle. For a groove weld, it is a shape like a V, U, or square. The size of the weld is written to the left of the symbol. For a fillet weld, the size is the leg length. For example, a 6 mm fillet weld has a 6 mm leg. For a groove weld, the size is the depth of penetration or the groove size..Trim()

.Trim()

Length and pitch are written to the right of the symbol. Length is how long the weld is. Pitch is the distance between centers of welds if you have intermittent welds. For example, 50-100 means a 50 mm long weld every 100 mm. If there is no length, the weld is continuous..Trim()

.Trim()

Other components include the angle of countersink, the root opening, and the groove angle. These are written near the symbol. Always read from left to right. In African workshops, symbols are often drawn by hand on a chalkboard or paper. Practice drawing each component. Get a welding symbol chart from a local welding supplier or download one. Memorize the common ones: fillet, V-groove, square groove, and plug weld. When you see a symbol, say out loud what it means. This will help you remember..Trim()

Types of Welds and Joints

There are many types of welds, but in African welding shops, you will mostly use four: fillet, groove, plug, and spot. A fillet weld is a triangular weld that joins two surfaces at an angle, like a T-joint or a lap joint. It is the most common and cheapest. A groove weld is used to join two pieces edge to edge. It can be a V-groove, U-groove, J-groove, or square groove. Groove welds are stronger but require more preparation. A plug weld is a round weld made in a hole in one plate to join it to another. A spot weld is like a plug weld but smaller and usually made with a resistance welder..Trim()

.Trim()

Joints are how the metal pieces fit together. The five basic joints are butt, corner, T, lap, and edge. A butt joint is when two pieces are placed end to end. A corner joint is when they meet at a corner, like an L. A T-joint is when one piece sits on top of another, forming a T. A lap joint is when one piece overlaps another. An edge joint is when two pieces are placed side by side and welded along the edge..Trim()

.Trim()

Each joint can have different welds. For example, a T-joint usually uses a fillet weld on one or both sides. A butt joint can use a square groove weld for thin metal or a V-groove weld for thick metal. In Africa, you often work with mild steel plates from 3 mm to 12 mm thick. For 3 mm, a square groove with a 2 mm root gap works well. For 6 mm, a single V-groove with a 60-degree included angle is common. For 12 mm, you might need a double V-groove to reduce distortion..Trim()

.Trim()

When you read a blueprint, first identify the joint type. Then look at the weld symbol to see what weld to make. The symbol will tell you the size, length, and type. For example, a fillet weld symbol on a T-joint means you weld along the corner. If the symbol has a tail with 'SMAW', you know to use stick welding. Practice by making sample joints from scrap metal. Interpreting Size and Length Dimensions

Size and length are the two most important dimensions on a welding symbol. Size tells you how big the weld should be. Length tells you how long it should be. For a fillet weld, the size is the leg length. It is written to the left of the fillet symbol. For example, a symbol with '6' on the left means a 6 mm fillet weld. The leg length is measured on the longest leg. If the weld is on both sides, the size is still the leg length for each weld. If the symbol has a size on both sides of the reference line, you may have different sizes for each side..Trim()

.Trim()

For a groove weld, the size is the depth of the groove or the required penetration. For a V-groove, the size is the depth of the V. It is written to the left of the groove symbol. For example, a '6' on a V-groove symbol means a 6 mm deep V-groove. If there is no size, the weld must penetrate the full thickness of the material. That is called a complete joint penetration (CJP) weld. For partial joint penetration (PJP), the size is given..Trim()

.Trim()

Length is written to the right of the symbol. If there is no length, the weld is continuous along the whole joint. If a length is given, the weld is intermittent. For example, '50-200' means a 50 mm long weld every 200 mm. The first number is the length, the second is the pitch (center-to-center distance). If there are three numbers, like '50-200-3', the third number is the number of welds. Sometimes you see a length with a dash and another number, like '50-100', which means 50 mm long welds with 100 mm pitch. The pitch includes the weld length and the gap. So if you have a 50 mm weld and 100 mm pitch, the gap between welds is 50 mm..Trim()

.Trim()

In African workshops, you often use a tape measure and a soapstone or chalk to mark lengths. For a 6 mm fillet weld, you need to set your welding machine to about 120-140 amps for stick welding with a 3.2 mm electrode. For a 50 mm long weld, you start at one mark and end at the other. If the symbol says 'weld all around', you weld the entire joint. Always check the drawing for any notes about length. If you are unsure, ask your supervisor. Measuring wrong can cause weak joints and wasted material..Trim()

Supplementary Symbols and Notes

Supplementary symbols give extra information about the weld. They are small marks added to the basic welding symbol. The most common is the all-around symbol, which is a circle at the junction of the arrow and reference line. It means you weld all around the joint. For example, if you are welding a pipe to a plate, the all-around symbol means you weld the entire circumference. Another common symbol is the field weld symbol, which is a flag. It means the weld is made at the site, not in the shop. In Africa, many construction projects use field welds for on-site assembly..Trim()

.Trim()

The melt-through symbol is a filled-in circle between the arrow and the reference line. It means you must get complete penetration, often used for root passes. The consumable insert symbol is a rectangle with a line through it, placed on the reference line. It means you use a backing material that becomes part of the weld. The backing symbol is a rectangle on the other side of the reference line, meaning you use a backing strip that does not melt..Trim()

.Trim()

The tail of the welding symbol is where you put notes. For example, you might write 'SMAW' for shielded metal arc welding, or 'GMAW' for gas metal arc welding. You might also write a specification number, like AWS D1.1. In Africa, many workshops follow AWS or ISO standards. If you see 'ISO 5817', that is a quality standard for welds. The tail can also have a process number, like '111' for stick welding, '135' for MIG welding, or '141' for TIG welding..Trim()

.Trim()

Other notes might be written directly on the drawing, like 'grind flush' or 'remove slag'. These are not symbols but important instructions. Always read all notes on the drawing. Sometimes a note says 'weld size per detail A', so you must find detail A. In many African vocational exams, you will be given a drawing with symbols and notes. You must interpret them correctly. Practice by making a chart of all supplementary symbols and their meanings. Then look at real drawings from local fabricators. Ask them what each symbol means. Now you have learned the parts of a welding symbol, types of welds and joints, and how to read size and length. The final step is to apply this to real fabrication. Vertical and overhead welding are the most challenging positions because gravity pulls the molten metal down. In vertical welding, you can go uphill or downhill.
