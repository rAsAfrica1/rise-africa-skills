Historical Development of Roofing Materials

Before roofing sheets, African builders used local materials: thatch from grass or palm fronds, mud, stone, and later baked clay tiles. Thatch was cheap and cool but needed replacing every 2 to 5 years and caught fire easily. Clay tiles lasted longer but were heavy and required skilled labour. In the 1800s, European traders brought corrugated iron sheets to coastal ports like Accra, Mombasa, and Cape Town. These sheets were light, strong, and fire-resistant, so they spread quickly along trade routes..Trim()

The first corrugated iron was made in Britain in the 1820s by rolling hot iron between grooved rollers. It was galvanized with zinc to stop rust. By the 1900s, steel replaced iron, and sheets became cheaper. In colonial Africa, railways and missions used CGI for warehouses, churches, and staff housing. After independence in the 1960s, local factories in Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, and Egypt began producing sheets, cutting import costs..Trim()

Safety Practices for Roofing Work

Roofing is dangerous. Falls are the leading cause of death in construction. In Africa, many workers die each year from falling off roofs. Before you start, check the weather: never work on a wet or windy roof. Wind can lift a sheet and throw you off. Use a safety harness anchored to a strong point, and wear non-slip shoes. .Trim()

When handling sheets, wear thick gloves. Cut edges are razor-sharp. Carry sheets on edge, not flat, to avoid bending. Two people should carry long sheets. Metal roofing sheets are the most common in Africa because they are strong, lightweight, and easy to install. Steel sheets are the cheapest and most widely used. Module 3 · Roofing Sheet Manufacturing and Specifications

Manufacturing Processes for Metal Roofing Sheets

Roofing sheets in Africa are mostly made from steel or aluminium. The process starts with a large coil of thin metal, often imported from South Africa, China, or India. The coil is unrolled and fed into a roll-forming machine. This machine has a series of rollers that gradually bend the flat metal into the corrugated or trapezoidal profile you see on roofs. The number of rollers depends on the profile complexity; a simple corrugated sheet might need 12 to 16 stations, while a deeper trapezoidal sheet needs 20 or more. The machine runs at 10 to 30 metres per minute. After forming, the sheet is cut to length by a flying shear or a guillotine. For curved sheets, a separate curving machine bends the profile along its length. In small workshops, manual bending brakes and hand shears are used, but these are slow and less accurate. The most common metals are galvanised steel (zinc-coated), Galvalume (aluminium-zinc coated), and aluminium. Galvanised steel is the cheapest and most widely used in Africa. The zinc coating protects the steel from rust. The thickness of the base steel is measured in millimetres or gauge; common thicknesses are 0.20 mm to 0.50 mm. The zinc coating weight is measured in grams per square metre (g/mÂ²), typically 60 to 275 g/mÂ². After forming, the sheets may be coated with paint (see next lesson). The whole process requires a steady supply of electricity, which can be a challenge in rural areas. Many manufacturers use diesel generators as backup. Quality control checks include measuring thickness with a micrometre, checking profile dimensions with a template, and testing the zinc coating with a magnetic gauge. A well-run factory can produce 500 to 1000 sheets per day. The key to success is regular maintenance of the roll-forming machine and keeping the metal coils dry to prevent white rust before forming..Trim()

Coating and Finishing Techniques

After a metal roofing sheet is formed, it needs a protective and decorative coating. The most common coating for galvanised steel is a paint system. First, the sheet is cleaned to remove oil, dirt, and rust. Then it passes through a pre-treatment chemical bath, often a chromate or phosphate solution, which helps paint stick. Next, a primer is applied, usually by roller coating. The primer is baked in an oven at 200â240Â°C for 20â30 seconds. Then a topcoat of polyester, silicone-modified polyester (SMP), or PVDF (Kynar 500) is applied. Polyester is the cheapest and most common in Africa, lasting 5â10 years in harsh sun. PVDF lasts 20â30 years but costs three times more. The paint thickness is measured in microns (Âµm); typical total dry film thickness is 20â25 Âµm for polyester and 25â30 Âµm for PVDF. For Galvalume sheets, the same paint systems work but require a different pre-treatment. Some manufacturers also apply a clear acrylic coating over galvanised sheets to prevent white rust during storage and transport. This is called a 'dry film' or 'anti-fingerprint' coating. Another finishing technique is embossing, where the sheet is passed between textured rollers to give a wood-grain or stucco pattern. This hides scratches and dents. For aluminium sheets, anodising is common: the sheet is placed in an acid bath and an electric current is passed through, creating a hard oxide layer. Anodised aluminium is popular in coastal areas because it resists salt corrosion. After coating, sheets are stacked and packed with protective plastic film. In Africa, the biggest coating challenges are dust, high humidity, and inconsistent oven temperatures. A simple test for coating quality is the 'tape test': press a piece of adhesive tape onto the painted surface, then pull it off quickly. If paint comes off, adhesion is poor. Profile Design and Load-Bearing Capacity

The profile of a roofing sheet is its cross-sectional shape. The two main types in Africa are corrugated (wavy, sinusoidal) and trapezoidal (ribbed, with flat tops and slanted sides). Corrugated sheets are the oldest and cheapest. They have a pitch (distance between waves) of 76 mm or 89 mm, and a depth of 16â18 mm. They are weak in one direction, so they need purlins every 800â900 mm. Trapezoidal sheets have deeper ribs (25â40 mm) and a pitch of 150â250 mm. They are much stronger and can span 1.2â1.5 m between purlins. The load-bearing capacity depends on: thickness, profile depth, yield strength of the steel, and the number of purlins. For a given thickness, a deeper profile is stronger. In Africa, wind loads are often the critical factor, especially in coastal areas and open plains. A roof must resist uplift from wind. Quality Control and Certification

Quality control (QC) in roofing sheet manufacturing ensures that every sheet meets the required specifications. Without QC, you risk selling sheets that rust quickly, dent easily, or fail in wind. The first QC step is incoming material inspection. When you receive a coil of steel, check the mill certificate. It should state the steel grade (e.g., DX51D for galvanised), yield strength (minimum 300 MPa), zinc coating weight (e.g., 120 g/mÂ²), and thickness tolerance (Â±0.02 mm). Measure the thickness with a micrometre at three points across the width and along the length. If the coil is out of tolerance, reject it. Second, during production, check the roll-forming machine settings. The profile dimensions (pitch, depth, cover width) must match the drawing. Use a profile gauge or a template every hour. Check the cut length with a tape measure. Third, after coating, test the paint adhesion with a tape test and the coating thickness with a magnetic gauge. Also check for visual defects: scratches, blisters, runs, and bare spots. Fourth, test the finished sheet for mechanical properties. A simple bend test: bend a sample 180Â° over a 10 mm mandrel. The coating should not crack or flake. For load capacity, you can do a static load test: support a sheet on two purlins at the specified span, then load it with sandbags until it deflects 1/100 of the span. It should not fail. Fifth, keep records. Every batch should have a batch number, date, machine operator, and test results. This helps trace problems. For example, in Kenya, KEBS requires a permit; in Nigeria, SONCAP; in South Africa, SABS. The process involves submitting samples for testing at an accredited lab. Tests include thickness, zinc coating weight, paint adhesion, and load capacity. Once certified, you can mark your sheets with the standard number. Many buyers, especially NGOs and government projects, will only buy certified sheets. Even if certification is not mandatory in your country, it builds trust and allows you to charge a higher price. Roof Design Principles for Sheet Roofing

When designing a roof for sheet roofing, start by understanding the building's purpose and local climate. In Africa, intense sun and heavy rain are common, so your roof must shed water quickly and resist heat. A simple gable roof (two sloping sides) is easiest for beginners and works well with metal sheets. Avoid complex shapes like valleys or dormers unless you have experience. The roof should overhang the walls by at least 600mm to protect against driving rain and provide shade. For a typical 10m x 8m house, a gable roof with a 30-degree pitch gives good drainage. Use treated timber or steel trusses spaced at 1.2m centres for sheets up to 0.5mm thick. Always check local building codes; in many African countries, minimum roof pitch for metal sheets is 15 degrees. Consider wind uplift: in coastal areas, use cyclone washers and extra fasteners. Also plan for ventilationâa hot roof can radiate heat into the house. Ridge vents or eave gaps help. Finally, think about maintenance: keep the design simple so you can access and replace sheets easily. Draw a simple plan showing the roof outline, slope direction, and sheet layout. To calculate roof area, you need the building length, width, and roof pitch. For a gable roof, the area of one slope is: length x (width/2) / cos(pitch). Slope (or pitch) is the angle of the roof, and it determines how fast water runs off. For metal roofing sheets, the minimum pitch is usually 15 degrees (about 1:4). Fire Safety and Wind Uplift Standards

Fire safety and wind uplift are two big risks for roofs. Fire can spread through roofing materials, and wind can lift sheets off.
