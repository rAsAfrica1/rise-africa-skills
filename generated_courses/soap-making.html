History and Chemistry of Soap

Soap making is one of Africa's oldest crafts. In ancient Egypt, people combined animal fats with alkaline salts from wood ash to create cleansing pastes. In West Africa, palm oil and ash from cocoa pods were used for centuries. Today, you can make soap using the same basic chemistry: oil or fat plus lye (sodium hydroxide) equals soap plus glycerin. This reaction is called saponification. For example, 500g of palm oil requires about 68g of lye (NaOH) to fully saponify. Lye is dangerous in its raw form. It is caustic and can burn your skin and eyes. But once it reacts with oil, it becomes harmless soap. In Africa, you can source lye from chemical suppliers or make your own by leaching water through wood ash, though commercial lye is more reliable for consistent results..Trim()

The type of oil you use changes the soap's qualities. Palm oil makes a hard, long-lasting bar. Coconut oil creates big, fluffy lather. Shea butter, abundant in West Africa, adds creaminess and skin-conditioning properties. Olive oil, though more expensive, gives a mild, gentle bar. A common beginner recipe is 500g palm oil, 300g coconut oil, and 200g shea butter, with the correct amount of lye and 300ml water..Trim()

Soap making can go wrong in predictable ways. Learn to spot these fast. If your soap is soft and won't harden after 48 hours, you likely used too little lye or too much water.
