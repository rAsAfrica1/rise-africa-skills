Preparing Fruits and Vegetables for Drying

Start with fresh, ripe but firm produce. Wash everything in clean water. For fruits like mango, pineapple, banana, and papaya, peel and remove seeds or pits. Cut into uniform slices or pieces, about 5-8 mm thick. Uniform size ensures even drying. To prevent browning in apples, bananas, and potatoes, soak cut pieces in a solution of 1 teaspoon lemon juice or 1 tablespoon vinegar per litre of water for 5 minutes. Drain well..Trim()

.Trim()

Blanching is important for vegetables like carrots, green beans, and pumpkin. Bring a large pot of water to a rolling boil. Drop vegetables in for 2-4 minutes, then immediately transfer to cold water for the same time. This stops enzyme action that causes spoilage and helps keep colour and vitamins. .Trim()

For leafy greens like spinach or kale, wash, remove tough stems, and tear into small pieces. No blanching needed. For tomatoes, slice 1 cm thick and remove seeds to speed drying. For onions and garlic, peel and slice thinly. Spread everything in a single layer on clean drying trays or mats. Do not overlap. You can dry mixed vegetables together, but strong-smelling items like onion and garlic should be dried separately to avoid flavour transfer..Trim()

.Trim()

Work quickly and cleanly. Use clean hands, clean knives, and clean surfaces. In hot African climates, fruit flies and dust are a problem. Cover trays with a light cloth or mosquito net during preparation. Prepare only as much as you can dry in one day. If you have a solar dryer, load it immediately. Testing for Dryness and Preventing Mold

If you package food before it is fully dry, mould will grow and ruin everything. Mould produces poisons that can make people sick. So testing for dryness is critical. There are simple tests for different foods. For fruits and vegetables: cut a piece in half. There should be no moist spots inside. The texture should be leathery or brittle. For leafy vegetables, they should crumble easily. For fish and meat: bend a piece. It should snap, not bend like rubber. For herbs: leaves should crumble when rubbed. For roots and spices: they should be hard and snap when broken..Trim()

.Trim()

Another test: put a piece in a clean, dry jar with a lid. Wait 10 minutes. If moisture forms on the inside of the jar, it is not dry enough. Return it to the dryer for more hours. Mould prevention starts before drying. Use clean equipment. Dry food quickly and evenly. Do not dry in humid or rainy weather. In humid areas, use a solar dryer or add a small fire under a drying rack (smoke also helps preserve). After drying, cool the food completely before packaging. Any warmth creates condensation. Package in airtight containers â glass jars, sealed plastic bags, or metal tins. Keep in a cool, dark, dry place. Never store dried food near fresh produce or liquids..Trim()

If you find mould, throw the whole batch away â do not just cut off the mouldy part. Mould spreads invisible threads. If you find insects, you can kill them by putting the food in the freezer for 48 hours, or heating in an oven at 60Â°C for 30 minutes. Then repackage. Understanding Canning: Water Bath vs Pressure Canning

Canning preserves food by sealing it in jars and heating it to kill microorganisms. There are two main methods: water bath canning and pressure canning. Water bath canning is for high-acid foods like fruits, tomatoes with added acid, pickles, and jams. You need a large pot with a lid and a rack to keep jars off the bottom. Fill the pot with water to cover jars by 1-2 inches, bring to a rolling boil, and process for the time given in a tested recipe. For example, canned peaches in syrup require 20-30 minutes for pint jars, depending on altitude. Pressure canning is for low-acid foods like vegetables, meats, and legumes. These foods need higher temperatures (240Â°F/116Â°C) to kill dangerous bacteria like Clostridium botulinum. You need a pressure canner with a gauge or weighted regulator. Follow the manufacturer's instructions for venting and bringing to pressure. For green beans, process at 10 pounds pressure (psi) for 20 minutes for pints, adjusting for altitude. Never use a water bath for low-acid foods. In Africa, where electricity may be unreliable, a pressure canner can be used on a gas stove or open fire if it has a sturdy base. Always use tested recipes from reliable sources like the USDA or your local agricultural extension. Altitude matters: above 1,000 feet, increase pressure or processing time. For water bath, add 1 minute per 1,000 feet above sea level. For pressure canning, increase pressure by 1 psi per 1,000 feet if using a gauge. Investing in a pressure canner is worth it if you grow vegetables. Start with water bath canning for fruits and tomatoes, then move to pressure canning for vegetables and legumes..Trim()

Preparing Jars and Lids for Safe Canning

Before you start canning, you must clean and sterilize your jars and lids. Use glass jars made for canning, like Mason jars. Check for cracks or chips; damaged jars can break during processing. Wash jars in hot soapy water and rinse well. To sterilize, place jars in a large pot, cover with water, and boil for 10 minutes. Keep them hot until filled. Alternatively, wash in a dishwasher on a hot cycle. Lids: use two-piece metal lids (flat disc and screw band). The flat lids have a sealing compound and should not be reused. Screw bands can be reused if not rusted. Wash lids in hot soapy water and keep them in hot water until use; this softens the sealing compound. Do not boil lids for long periods as it can damage the seal. For water bath canning, you don't need to sterilize jars if processing time is more than 10 minutes, but it's good practice. For pressure canning, sterilization is not required because the high heat kills everything. However, clean jars are essential. Always use new lids for a proper seal. In Africa, you can find canning jars at supermarkets or online. If not, use glass jars with new lids that fit. Never use jars from commercial products like mayonnaise; they may not seal properly. After filling, wipe the rim with a clean, damp cloth to remove any food residue. Place the lid on and screw the band until fingertip-tight. Do not overtighten, as air needs to escape during processing. After processing, let jars cool undisturbed for 12-24 hours. You should hear a 'pop' as they seal. Check the seal by pressing the center of the lid; it should not move. If it pops up and down, it's not sealed. Refrigerate unsealed jars and use within a few days..Trim()

Canning Tomatoes and Tomato Paste

Tomatoes are a high-acid food, but their acidity varies. To can safely, add lemon juice or citric acid. For each pint of tomatoes, add 1 tablespoon bottled lemon juice or 1/4 teaspoon citric acid. For quarts, use 2 tablespoons lemon juice or 1/2 teaspoon citric acid. Use ripe, firm tomatoes. Wash, remove stems, and peel. To peel, dip in boiling water for 30 seconds, then transfer to cold water; skins slip off. Cut into quarters. For crushed tomatoes, simmer in a large pot until thick, stirring often. For tomato paste, cook down further until very thick, then pack into hot jars, leaving 1/2 inch headspace. For whole or halved tomatoes, pack raw into jars, add acid, and cover with hot tomato juice or water. Remove air bubbles with a plastic spatula. Wipe rims, put on lids, and process in a water bath canner. For pints, process 35 minutes; for quarts, 45 minutes. Adjust for altitude: add 5 minutes for 1,000-3,000 feet, 10 minutes for 3,001-6,000 feet. In Africa, tomatoes are abundant in season. You can make paste on a solar dryer or over a fire. For tomato paste, after cooking, you can also freeze in ice cube trays. But for canning, ensure it's thick enough to mound on a spoon. Never add oil, garlic, or herbs to canned tomatoes unless using a tested recipe; these can lower acidity. If you want seasoned tomatoes, add dried herbs just before serving. Always use new lids. After processing, check seals. Store in a cool, dark place. Home-canned tomatoes are best used within 12-18 months. Discard any jars with off odors, mold, or bulging lids..Trim()

Canning Fruits in Syrup

Canning fruits in syrup preserves them for months. Choose ripe but firm fruits: mangoes, peaches, pears, apples, guavas, or pineapple. Wash, peel, and cut into uniform pieces. To prevent browning, dip apples and pears in a solution of 1 tablespoon lemon juice per 1 liter water. Make syrup: for light syrup, mix 1 cup sugar with 4 cups water; medium syrup uses 2 cups sugar to 4 cups water; heavy syrup uses 3 cups sugar to 4 cups water. Heat until sugar dissolves. Keep syrup hot. Pack fruit into hot jars, leaving 1/2 inch headspace. Cover with hot syrup, ensuring fruit is covered. Remove air bubbles. Wipe rims, put on lids, and process in a water bath canner. Processing times: for pints, 20 minutes for most fruits; for quarts, 25 minutes. Adjust for altitude: add 5 minutes for 1,000-3,000 feet, 10 minutes for 3,001-6,000 feet. In Africa, you can use honey instead of sugar for a local twist, but honey can darken fruit. If using honey, use 3/4 cup honey per 4 cups water. For a no-sugar option, use fruit juice or water, but the fruit may not hold up as well. After processing, cool jars and check seals. Store in a cool, dark place. Home-canned fruits are best within 12 months. Serve as dessert or with yogurt. Always use new lids. If you live at high altitude, increase processing time. For a variation, add spices like cinnamon or cloves to the syrup, but use only dried spices, not fresh. Never add fresh herbs. Canning fruits is a great way to enjoy seasonal produce year-round..Trim()

Canning Vegetables and Legumes

Vegetables and legumes are low-acid foods and must be canned using a pressure canner. This is critical to kill Clostridium botulinum spores. Never use a water bath for these. Start with fresh, tender vegetables: green beans, carrots, beets, corn, peas, or legumes like beans and lentils. Wash and prepare: trim ends, peel, and cut into uniform sizes. For legumes, soak dried beans overnight, then drain and rinse. For green beans, pack raw or hot pack. Hot pack: boil beans for 5 minutes, then pack into hot jars, leaving 1 inch headspace. For raw pack, pack tightly and cover with boiling water. For legumes, fill jars halfway with beans, then add boiling water or broth, leaving 1 inch headspace. Add 1/2 teaspoon salt per pint if desired. Remove air bubbles. Wipe rims, put on lids. Process in a pressure canner at 10 psi for pints and quarts. Times: green beans, pints 20 minutes, quarts 25 minutes; carrots, pints 25 minutes, quarts 30 minutes; beans (soaked), pints 75 minutes, quarts 90 minutes. Adjust for altitude: increase pressure by 1 psi per 1,000 feet if using a gauge; if using a weighted gauge, use 15 psi above 1,000 feet. Always vent the pressure canner for 10 minutes before pressurizing. After processing, let pressure drop naturally. Do not force-cool. In Africa, pressure canners are available in some cities; if not, you can use a large pot with a tight lid and a pressure regulator from a pressure cooker, but only if it can reach and maintain 10-15 psi. This is risky; invest in a proper canner. After cooling, check seals. Store in a cool, dark place. Home-canned vegetables are best within 12 months. Boil all home-canned low-acid foods for 10 minutes before eating to ensure safety..Trim()

Labeling and Storing Canned Goods

After canning, label each jar with the contents and date. Use a permanent marker or sticker. Include any special notes like 'unsweetened' or 'with salt'. This helps you rotate your stock and avoid spoilage. Store jars in a cool, dark, dry place. Ideal temperature is 50-70Â°F (10-21Â°C). Avoid areas with temperature swings, like near a stove or in direct sunlight. Heat can cause spoilage and reduce quality. A cupboard or pantry works well. Keep jars off the floor to prevent moisture and pests. Use shelves or crates. Do not stack jars too high; the bottom jars can crack under weight. For best quality, use within 12-18 months. After that, food may still be safe but quality declines. Check jars regularly for signs of spoilage: bulging lids, leaking, mold, cloudiness, or off odors. If you see any, discard immediately. Never taste suspect food. When opening a jar, listen for a hiss or pop; that indicates a good seal. If the lid is loose or the food looks or smells wrong, throw it out. In Africa, where humidity can be high, consider using a dehumidifier or silica gel packets in the storage area. Label with the date you canned and the method used (water bath or pressure). Keep a log of what you canned and when. This helps with meal planning and ensures you use older jars first. For long-term storage, you can also use vacuum-sealed bags for dry goods, but for canned goods, glass jars are best. Always use clean hands when handling jars. If you sell your canned goods, check local regulations for labeling requirements. Proper labeling and storage ensure your hard work pays off with safe, delicious food..Trim()

Making Vinegar from Local Fruits

To make vinegar, you need a clean glass jar, a breathable cover like a cloth, and fruit scraps. Use overripe mangoes, pineapple peels, banana peels, or sugarcane juice. Chop fruits into small pieces. For every 1 kilogram of fruit, add 2 liters of clean water and 100 grams of sugar. Stir well. Pour into the jar, leaving 5 cm of space at the top. Cover with cloth and secure with a rubber band. Keep in a dark, warm place (25-30Â°C). Stir daily for the first week. After 2-3 weeks, you will see bubbles and smell alcohol. That is fermentation. Now strain the liquid into a new jar. Cover again and leave for 2-4 weeks. A thin film (mother of vinegar) may form. Taste after 3 weeks: it should be sour. If not, wait another week. Strain again and bottle. Store in a cool, dark place. It keeps for 6 months. For stronger vinegar, let it ferment longer. Use this vinegar for pickling. Do not use metal containers; use glass, ceramic, or food-grade plastic. Always label with date..Trim()

Pickling Vegetables: Onions, Cucumbers, Peppers

Start with fresh, firm vegetables. Wash and cut onions into rings, cucumbers into slices, peppers into strips. For a basic brine, mix 1 part vinegar (your homemade or bought) with 1 part water. Add 1 tablespoon of salt per liter of brine. For sweetness, add 1 tablespoon of sugar per liter. Bring brine to a boil, then cool. Pack vegetables tightly into clean glass jars. Add spices: mustard seeds, peppercorns, garlic cloves, or chili flakes. Pour cooled brine over vegetables, covering them completely. Leave 1 cm headspace. Seal jars with lids. For quick pickles, refrigerate and eat after 24 hours. For long-term storage, process jars in a hot water bath: place jars in a pot, cover with water, boil for 10 minutes. Remove and let cool. Check seals: lid should not pop when pressed. Store in a cool, dark place. Onions and peppers pickle in 1-2 weeks; cucumbers in 3-4 weeks. Always use clean jars and utensils. If you see mold, discard. For spicy pickles, add whole chilies..Trim()

Pickling Fish and Meat

Pickling fish and meat preserves them and adds flavor. Use fresh fish like tilapia or mackerel, or meat like beef or goat. Cut into small pieces (5 cm cubes). For fish, soak in salt water (100g salt per liter) for 1 hour. For meat, rub with salt and leave for 2 hours. Rinse well. Preserving in Oil: Garlic, Herbs, and Peppers

Preserving in oil is different from pickling. Oil does not kill bacteria; it blocks air. You must first dry or salt the food to remove moisture. Balancing Acidity and Flavor

The key to safe pickles is acidity. The pH must be below 4.6. You can buy pH strips from pharmacies or agricultural supply shops. Test your brine before pouring. If pH is too high, add more vinegar. What is Food Irradiation and How It Works

Food irradiation is a technique that uses controlled amounts of ionizing radiation to kill bacteria, insects, and parasites in food. It also slows ripening and sprouting. The process does not make food radioactive. Think of it like a very strong X-ray that passes through the food, damaging the DNA of pests and microbes so they cannot survive or reproduce..Trim()

.Trim()

There are three main types of radiation used: gamma rays from cobalt-60, electron beams, and X-rays. Gamma rays are the most common worldwide. In Africa, gamma irradiation facilities exist in countries like South Africa, Egypt, and Ghana. The food is exposed to a measured dose, called a Gray (Gy). For example, to kill insects in grains, a dose of 0.2 to 0.7 kGy (200-700 Gy) is enough. To kill harmful bacteria like Salmonella in spices, you need 5 to 10 kGy..Trim()

.Trim()

The process works in three steps: First, the food is loaded into special containers. Second, it moves through a shielded chamber where the radiation source is. Third, it exits and is ready for storage or sale. The whole process takes minutes to hours, depending on the dose. No chemicals are added, and the food does not become radioactive because the energy used is too low to induce radioactivity..Trim()

.Trim()

Irradiation can be used on many foods: spices, dried herbs, grains, fresh fruits, vegetables, meat, and seafood. It is especially useful for dried products because they are often contaminated with insects and microbes. For a small business, irradiation is not something you do yourselfâit requires a licensed facility. But you can send your products to an irradiation service. Food irradiation is one of the most studied food technologies. Decades of research show it is safe when used correctly. To build a successful food preservation business, you need to create product lines that combine two or more preservation methods. This extends shelf life, adds value, and sets you apart. For example, dried mango slices can be further preserved by vacuum sealing; smoked fish can be dried and then packaged in oil. Sourcing Raw Materials Locally

Sourcing raw materials locally is key to keeping costs low and ensuring freshness. Start by visiting your local market or farms. Build relationships with farmers. For example, if you're in Kenya, buy tomatoes from farmers in Loitokitok during the dry season when prices are low. In Nigeria, source cassava from local processors. Always negotiate bulk prices. Quality Control and Food Safety Standards

Costing, Pricing, and Profit Calculation

To make a profit, you must know your costs. Calculate direct costs: raw materials, packaging, labor, and utilities. For example, to make 1 kg of dried mango: fresh mangoes (5 kg at $0.50/kg = $2.50), labor (2 hours at $1/hour = $2), packaging ($0.50), and drying fuel ($0.50). Total cost = $5.50. This yields about 1 kg dried mango (since 5 kg fresh yields 1 kg dried). If you sell at $10/kg, your profit is $4.50 per kg. But you also have overheads: rent, equipment depreciation, marketing. Add 20% overhead to your cost. So total cost = $6.60, profit = $3.40..Trim()

Pricing strategy: check competitors. If similar products sell for $12/kg, you can price at $11 to attract customers. But ensure you cover costs. Use cost-plus pricing: cost + desired profit margin. For example, if you want 50% margin, price = cost / (1 - 0.5) = $6.60 / 0.5 = $13.20. Round to $13. For jams, a 250 ml jar costs $1.50 to make; sell at $3.50. For smoked fish, 500g costs $4 to make; sell at $8..Trim()
