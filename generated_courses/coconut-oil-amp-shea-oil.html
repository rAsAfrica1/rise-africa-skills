What Are Coconut and Shea Oils?

Coconut oil and shea oil are two of Africa's most valuable natural oils. Coconut oil comes from the white meat inside mature coconuts. You crack open a coconut, grate the white flesh, and press or boil it to release the oil. In Africa, coconut oil is produced in coastal countries like Ghana, Nigeria, Kenya, Tanzania, and Mozambique. The oil is solid below 24Â°C and liquid above that. It has a mild, sweet taste and a light texture. Shea oil, on the other hand, comes from the nuts of the shea tree (Vitellaria paradoxa), which grows wild across the Sahel regionâfrom Senegal to Sudan and down to Uganda. Women traditionally harvest shea nuts, boil and dry them, then crush and knead them to extract a butter-like fat. Shea oil is the liquid fraction of shea butter. It is rich in vitamins A, E, and F, and is widely used in cosmetics and cooking. Both oils are cold-pressed or hot-pressed. Cold-pressing keeps more nutrients but gives lower yield. Hot-pressing gives more oil but may reduce some vitamins. For a small business, you can start with manual presses or a simple boiling method. A 20-litre bucket of coconuts can yield about 2-3 litres of oil. A 10 kg bag of shea nuts can yield about 3-4 kg of shea butter, which can be further separated into oil. These oils are not just for cookingâthey are used in soap, skin cream, hair products, and even biodiesel. Understanding the difference between the two is your first step. Coconut oil is faster to produce and works well for cooking and soap. Shea oil takes more time but fetches higher prices in cosmetic markets. Traditional Uses in African Communities

For centuries, African communities have used coconut and shea oils for food, medicine, and beauty. In coastal West Africa, coconut oil is a staple cooking fat. Women grate fresh coconut, mix it with warm water, and squeeze out the milk. They let it sit, then skim off the cream and boil it to get pure oil. This oil is used to fry plantains, fish, and doughnuts. It is also used to make soap. In Ghana, traditional soap makers mix coconut oil with wood ash and water to make a dark, hard soap called 'alata samina'. In East Africa, along the Swahili coast, coconut oil is used in rice dishes like wali wa nazi (coconut rice). It is also applied to the skin to protect against sun and dry wind. Shea oil, meanwhile, is the 'women's gold' of the Sahel. In Burkina Faso, Mali, and northern Ghana, women collect shea nuts from wild trees. They boil the nuts, dry them in the sun, crack the shells, and crush the kernels into a paste. They then knead the paste with water to extract a creamy butter. This butter is used for cooking, as a moisturiser, and to treat rashes, burns, and insect bites. In Nigeria, shea butter is mixed with herbs to make traditional medicine for joint pain. In Uganda, it is used to waterproof mud walls and to condition leather. These traditional methods are still used today, but they are labour-intensive. A woman might spend 8-10 hours to produce 1 kg of shea butter by hand. Modern tools like hydraulic presses and grinding mills can cut that time to 2-3 hours. But traditional knowledge is valuableâit tells you which nuts are ripe, how long to boil, and how to filter. Overview of the Production Process

Making coconut oil and shea oil follows different steps, but both can be done with simple tools. Let's start with coconut oil. You need mature coconuts (brown, not green). Crack them open with a machete or hammer. Remove the white meat and grate it using a manual grater or a blender. You will get about 500g of grated coconut from one large coconut. Put the grated coconut in a clean cloth and squeeze out the milk. You can also mix it with warm water (1 litre per 500g) and squeeze again to get more milk. Let the milk sit for 2-3 hours. The cream will rise to the top. Skim off the cream and boil it gently for 20-30 minutes. The oil will separate and float. Strain through a cloth into a clean bottle. This is called the 'boiling method'. It yields about 200-250ml of oil per coconut. For cold-pressed oil, use a manual screw press on the grated coconut. You get less oil (about 150ml per coconut) but higher quality. For shea oil, the process is longer. Collect shea nuts. Boil them for 30 minutes to soften. Dry them in the sun for 3-5 days. Crack the shells to remove kernels. Crush the kernels into a paste using a grinding mill or a mortar and pestle. Add water and knead the paste for 30-60 minutes. The fat will rise to the top. Scoop it off and boil it to remove water. This gives shea butter. To get shea oil, heat the butter gently and filter it through a fine cloth. The liquid that passes through is shea oil. It solidifies below 30Â°C. A 10 kg bag of shea nuts yields about 3 kg of butter and 2.5 kg of oil. You need: a large pot, a wooden spoon, a filter cloth (muslin), a manual press (optional), and clean bottles. Always work in a clean space. Dirty tools cause rancidity. Store oil in dark, cool place. Coconut oil lasts 12-18 months. Shea oil lasts 12-24 months. Start with 5 kg of nuts or 10 coconuts per batch. Test your oil by smelling itâit should smell fresh, not sour..Trim()

Cracking and Grinding Shea Nuts

After harvesting shea fruits and removing the pulp, you have nuts with hard shells. Cracking is the first step to reach the kernel inside. In rural Africa, women often crack nuts by hand using a stone or hammer. Place the nut on a hard surface and strike it sharply with a smooth stone or metal hammer. .Trim()

Next, grinding reduces the kernels to a coarse powder or paste. Traditionally, women pound kernels in a wooden mortar with a pestle. This is labor-intensive; pounding 5 kg of kernels can take 2-3 hours. A better option is to take kernels to a local mill that grinds grains. Many villages have diesel-powered grinding mills. Grind the kernels into a fine meal, similar to coarse flour. The finer the grind, the more oil you can extract later. If you grind too coarse, oil extraction will be inefficient. Aim for a consistency like wet sand. You can also use a kitchen blender for small batches, but it may overheat. After grinding, you should immediately proceed to roasting to prevent spoilage. Roasting is a critical step that develops flavor and makes oil extraction easier. After grinding, you have a moist, coarse meal. Traditional shea butter processing has been passed down through generations in West Africa. It involves manual cracking, grinding, roasting, kneading, and skimming. Module 8 · Costing and Pricing Your Products

Calculating Cost of Production per Unit

To know if you are making a profit, you must first know exactly how much it costs to produce one unit of oil. For example, one 500ml bottle of coconut oil or shea butter. Start by listing all your direct costs: raw materials (coconuts or shea nuts), packaging (bottles, labels, caps), and labour (yours and any helpers). If you bought a machine, like a press or grinder, divide its cost by the number of units it will produce over its lifetime. For instance, a $500 press that lasts 5 years and produces 1000 bottles per year gives $0.10 per bottle. Add indirect costs: rent, electricity, transport, and any fees. Suppose you make 100 bottles of coconut oil in a month. Raw coconuts cost $50, bottles and labels $30, labour $40, rent $20, electricity $10, transport $15. Total cost = $165. Cost per unit = $165 / 100 = $1.65. That means each bottle costs you $1.65 to make. You must sell above this to profit. Keep a notebook or use a simple Excel sheet to track every expense. Even small costs like a matchbox or phone call add up. Review your costs monthly because prices of raw materials change. If you buy in bulk, your cost per unit drops. For shea oil, if you process 20kg of shea nuts to get 5 litres of oil, and nuts cost $10, labour $15, packaging $10, total $35, cost per litre = $7. Always include a small buffer for spoilage or spillage. This number is your foundation for pricing. Without it, you are guessing. So calculate carefully, write it down, and update it regularly..Trim()

Setting a Profitable Price

Once you know your cost per unit, you can set a price that gives you a profit. A simple method is cost-plus pricing: add a markup to your cost. For example, if your cost per bottle is $1.65, you might add 50% markup, giving a price of $2.48. But you must also consider what customers are willing to pay. If the market price for similar oil is $3, you can price at $2.80 to be competitive and still make profit. Another method is target profit pricing: decide how much profit you want per unit. If you want $1 profit per bottle, price at $2.65. But be realistic. In many African markets, customers are price-sensitive. So test your price with a few customers. Start with a price that covers cost and gives a small profit, then adjust as you grow. Also consider value-based pricing: if your oil is organic, cold-pressed, or in fancy packaging, you can charge more. For export, you might use a higher markup because of extra costs like export documentation and shipping. Always calculate your break-even point: how many units you must sell to cover all costs. If your total fixed costs are $200 per month and you make $1 profit per bottle, you need to sell 200 bottles to break even. Anything above that is profit. Don't forget to include a margin for discounts and returns. A good rule: your selling price should be at least 30-50% above your cost. But in competitive markets, you might need to lower costs instead of raising prices. So focus on reducing production costs through bulk buying or efficient methods. Finally, review your prices regularly. What works today may not work next year..Trim()

Understanding Local Market Prices

Before you set your price, you must know what others are charging. Visit local markets, shops, and supermarkets that sell coconut oil or shea butter. Note the prices for different sizes: 250ml, 500ml, 1 litre. For example, in a Ghanaian market, a 500ml bottle of coconut oil might sell for $2.50 to $4.00, while shea butter might be $3.00 to $5.00. Prices vary by region and quality. Talk to vendors. Ask them what sells best and why. Some customers prefer cheaper oil even if quality is lower; others will pay more for pure, unrefined oil. Check online platforms like Jumia or Kilimall to see prices in other areas. Also consider the season: during harvest, prices may drop; during scarcity, they rise. If you are in a rural area, prices might be lower than in cities. But your costs might also be lower. So compare your cost per unit with local prices. If your cost is $1.65 and local price is $2.00, your profit margin is small. You might need to find ways to reduce costs or differentiate your product. For example, offer a unique scent or use recycled bottles to lower packaging costs. Also, look at imported oils. They are often cheaper due to large-scale production, but customers may prefer local, natural products. Use that to your advantage. Attend local trade fairs and farmer markets to gauge demand. Ask potential customers what they would pay. You can also do a simple survey: show them your product and ask if they would buy at $2.50, $3.00, etc. This gives you real data. Remember, pricing is not just about covering costs; it's about what the market will bear. So do your homework before you set a price..Trim()

Pricing for Export Markets

Exporting your coconut or shea oil can bring higher prices, but it also comes with extra costs. You need to calculate these carefully. First, research the target market. For example, in Europe or the US, organic shea butter sells for $10 to $20 per 500g. But you must meet their standards: organic certification, fair trade, quality testing. These add costs. You also need export documentation: certificates of origin, phytosanitary certificates, customs clearance. These can cost $200 to $500 per shipment. Shipping is a big cost. A 20-foot container from West Africa to Europe might cost $2000 to $4000. If you ship 1000 bottles, that's $2 to $4 per bottle just for freight. Then there are import duties and taxes in the destination country. Also, you need to package according to their regulations: labels in English or French, nutritional information, barcodes. You might need to hire a freight forwarder. All these costs must be added to your production cost. Suppose your cost per bottle is $1.65. Add $0.50 for certification, $0.30 for documentation, $2.50 for shipping, $0.50 for import duty, total extra = $3.80. So your total cost per bottle becomes $5.45. To make a profit, you might sell at $8 to $10. But you also need a distributor or agent in the destination country; they take a commission, often 20-30%. So your final price to the distributor might be $8, and they sell at $12. You must negotiate well. Start with small trial shipments to test the market. Use platforms like Alibaba or export portals. Also, consider fair trade pricing: many buyers are willing to pay a premium if you can prove fair labour and environmental practices. But be prepared for paperwork and delays. Exporting is not for everyone, but if you do it right, it can be very profitable..Trim()

Offering Discounts and Bulk Pricing

Discounts and bulk pricing can help you sell more and attract loyal customers, but you must do it without losing money. First, know your break-even point. If your cost per bottle is $1.65, you cannot sell below that. A common strategy is to offer a discount for larger quantities. For example, single bottle at $3.00, but if a customer buys 10 bottles, sell at $2.50 each. That still gives you $0.85 profit per bottle, and you move more stock. But you must calculate: if you sell 10 at $2.50, total revenue is $25, cost is $16.50, profit $8.50. If you sold 10 at $3.00, profit would be $13.50. So you are giving up $5 to encourage a bigger sale. That can be worth it if it brings repeat business. Another tactic: offer a free bottle for every 10 bought. That's effectively a 9% discount. Or give a discount for early payment. For shea butter, you might sell in bulk to soap makers or cosmetics companies. They buy in 5kg or 20kg containers. You can offer a lower price per kg because your packaging and handling costs are lower. For example, retail 500g at $5, but bulk 5kg at $40 ($4 per 500g). That's a 20% discount. But ensure your bulk price still covers costs. Also, consider seasonal discounts: during harvest, you might have more supply and can afford to lower prices to clear stock. But don't devalue your product. Always explain why the discount is offered: for bulk, for loyalty, for off-season. Use a simple price list: 1-5 units: $3.00; 6-20 units: $2.80; 21+ units: $2.50. This encourages larger orders. Track your discounts: if you give too many, you might erode profits. Set a limit: no more than 20% off retail. And never discount below cost. Finally, train your sales people to upsell: when a customer buys one, ask if they want to buy three at a discount. This increases average sale..Trim()

Tracking Expenses and Profit

To stay profitable, you must track every expense and every sale. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Wave or Excel. Record daily: money spent on raw materials, packaging, transport, labour, rent, electricity, and any other costs. Also record money received from sales. At the end of each week or month, calculate total expenses and total revenue. Profit = revenue minus expenses. For example, if you sold 200 bottles at $3 each, revenue = $600. Your expenses: raw materials $100, packaging $60, labour $80, rent $40, transport $30, other $20, total = $330. Profit = $600 - $330 = $270. That's your gross profit. But you also need to set aside money for reinvestment and emergencies. A good practice is to pay yourself a salary as an expense. If you don't, you might think you're making profit when you're just working for free. Decide on a monthly salary, say $100, and include it in expenses. Then your profit is $170. Also, track your inventory: how many bottles you have at the start and end of the month. If you started with 50 bottles, made 200, sold 200, you should have 50 left. If not, investigate theft or spillage. Use a simple cash book: date, description, money in, money out, balance. At the end of the month, review your numbers. Are your costs going up? Are your sales increasing? If profit is low, find ways to cut costs or increase prices. If profit is high, consider expanding. Also, separate business and personal money. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for the business. This makes tracking easier. And always keep receipts. Even for small purchases, ask for a receipt or write it down. This discipline will help you grow your business and secure loans or grants in the future..Trim()

Understanding Export Regulations for Oils

Exporting coconut and shea oil from Africa requires knowing the rules of both your country and the destination country. Start with your national export authority. In Ghana, it's the Ghana Export Promotion Authority (GEPA); in Nigeria, the Nigerian Export Promotion Council (NEPC); in Kenya, the Kenya Export Promotion and Branding Agency. These bodies provide free guidance and sometimes subsidized training. You must register your business and obtain an export license. For oils, you also need a certificate of origin from your chamber of commerce, a phytosanitary certificate from your agriculture ministry, and a quality certificate from a lab like SGS or Bureau Veritas. The destination country sets its own rules. The EU, for example, requires that shea oil meet strict pesticide residue limits and be accompanied by a health certificate. The US FDA requires prior notice for food imports. For organic export, you need certification from a recognized body like ECOCERT or USDA Organic. Fair trade certification from Fairtrade International or WFTO adds value. Costs vary: a phytosanitary certificate might cost $50, a lab test $200, and organic certification $2,000-$5,000 annually. But these open doors to premium markets. Always check the latest regulations because they change. The International Trade Centre (ITC) has a free online tool called Market Access Map where you can look up tariffs and requirements for your product and target country. Use it. Also, join a trade association like the African Shea Butter Association or the Coconut Coalition of the Americas (for coconut). They lobby for easier rules and share updates. Without proper paperwork, your shipment can be stuck at customs, costing you storage fees and lost sales. So invest time in understanding regulations before you ship..Trim()

Export Documentation and Customs

Export documentation can make or break your shipment. The core documents are: commercial invoice (details of sale), packing list (what's in each box), bill of lading (contract with shipping line), certificate of origin (proves where goods were made), phytosanitary certificate (proves plants are pest-free), and quality certificate (lab test results). For shea and coconut oil, you also need a health certificate from your ministry of health and, for organic, a certificate of organic inspection. Each document must be accurate and consistent. A misspelled buyer name or wrong HS code can cause delays. The HS code for crude coconut oil is 1513.11; for crude shea oil, it's 1515.90. Check with your customs authority for the exact code in your country. You'll also need an export declaration, often filed electronically through your customs system (e.g., Ghana's ICUMS, Nigeria's NICIS). Work with a customs broker if you're new; they charge around $100-$300 per shipment but save you from costly mistakes. Customs duties in the destination country are based on the HS code and the trade agreement. The EU has duty-free access for many African countries under the Everything But Arms (EBA) agreement, but you must prove origin with a EUR.1 certificate. The US has the African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA), which allows duty-free entry for many products if you meet rules of origin. Always keep copies of all documents for at least five years. Use a cloud storage system like Google Drive. Also, consider getting an ATA Carnet for temporary exports like samples. Finally, be aware of non-tariff barriers: labeling requirements (e.g., EU requires allergen warnings, nutrition facts), packaging rules, and quotas. Check the ITC's Market Access Map for details. Shipping and Logistics for Small Businesses

Shipping oils internationally requires careful planning. For small volumes (under 100 kg), use air freight or courier services like DHL, FedEx, or UPS. Air freight costs $5-$10 per kg, but it's fast (3-7 days). For larger volumes (over 500 kg), sea freight is cheaper: $0.10-$0.30 per kg, but takes 20-40 days. You'll need to choose between FCL (full container load) and LCL (less than container load). LCL is good for small businesses; you share a container with others. But LCL can cause damage if not packed well. Always use food-grade, leak-proof containers. For shea oil, use HDPE drums or jerrycans; for coconut oil, use food-grade plastic buckets or glass jars for retail. Pack them in strong cartons with cushioning. Label each box with product name, batch number, and expiry date. Work with a freight forwarder who specializes in food products. They handle booking, customs, and insurance. Get quotes from at least three forwarders. Insurance is crucial: goods can be lost or damaged. Cost is about 0.5% of value. For sea freight, you need a bill of lading; for air, an air waybill. These are contracts and must be accurate. Consider Incoterms: FOB (Free on Board) means you pay until goods are on the ship; CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) means you pay until destination port. For beginners, FOB is safer. Also, plan for storage at the port. In Africa, ports like Mombasa, Lagos, and Tema can have delays. So add buffer time. Track your shipment with the container number.
