Module 1 · Introduction to Fish Pond Construction

Overview of Aquaculture and Pond Systems

Aquaculture is simply fish farming �?? raising fish in controlled water bodies. In Africa, it is a fast-growing business because demand for fresh fish is high, especially in cities. You can farm tilapia, catfish, or even Nile perch. A fish pond is a man-made pool where you control water, feed, and predators. The most common system is a static pond, where water stays in one place and you add fresh water only to replace evaporation and seepage. Another system is a flow-through pond, where water constantly enters and exits, keeping oxygen high but requiring a steady water source. For a beginner, a static pond of 100 to 500 square meters is manageable. You can stock about 3 to 5 fish per square meter for tilapia, or 10 to 20 per square meter for catfish with good aeration. A pond can produce 500 to 2,000 kg of fish per year if managed well. Key inputs are fingerlings, feed, and labour. In Africa, many farmers use chicken manure or rice bran to fertilize the pond and grow natural fish food, reducing feed costs. You also need to test water pH (6.5�??8.5 is ideal) and temperature (25�??30°C for tilapia). Ponds can be built with simple tools: hoes, shovels, wheelbarrows, and a water pump. Before you start, visit a working fish farm nearby to see how it operates. This module will teach you to build a pond from scratch, step by step..Trim()

Types of Fish Ponds: Earthen, Lined, and Concrete

There are three main types of fish ponds you can build in Africa: earthen, lined, and concrete. Each has advantages and costs. Earthen ponds are the cheapest and most common. You dig a hole in the ground and let the natural soil hold water. The soil must have at least 30% clay to seal properly. If your soil is sandy, you can mix in bentonite clay at 5�??10 kg per square meter. Earthen ponds are good for large areas (500�??5,000 m²) and allow natural food to grow. But they can lose water through seepage and are hard to harvest completely. Lined ponds use a plastic liner (HDPE or PVC, 0.5�??1.0 mm thick) over the soil. This prevents water loss and works even in sandy areas. A 100 m² lined pond needs about 120 m² of liner (with overlap). Cost is higher: a 0.5 mm HDPE liner costs about $2�??$4 per square meter in many African countries. Lined ponds are easy to clean and harvest, but the liner can be damaged by sharp objects or sun. Concrete ponds are the most expensive but also the most durable. They are built with cement, sand, and gravel (1:2:3 mix) and reinforced with wire mesh. A 50 m² concrete pond costs roughly $1,500�??$3,000. They are excellent for intensive fish farming, easy to drain and harvest, and can be built above ground. However, concrete must be cured for 2�??3 weeks before filling with water, and the pH can rise initially �?? you must soak and drain it several times. For a beginner with limited funds, start with an earthen pond if your soil is clayey; otherwise, a lined pond is a good compromise..Trim()

Site Selection Criteria: Soil, Water, and Topography

Choosing the right site is critical. A bad site will cost you money and time. First, check the soil. Dig a test hole 1 meter deep and 50 cm wide. Fill it with water and see how long it holds. If water stays for 24 hours, the soil has enough clay. If it drains within an hour, you need a liner or to import clay. Avoid rocky or gravelly soil �?? it leaks and is hard to dig. Second, water supply. You need a reliable source that does not dry up in the dry season. This could be a river, stream, well, borehole, or even rainwater harvested from roofs. Test the water quality: pH should be 6.5�??8.5, and it should be free of pesticides or industrial waste. A simple test kit costs $20�??$50. Third, topography. A gentle slope (1�??2%) is ideal because it allows you to drain the pond by gravity. Avoid steep slopes �?? they require terracing and are expensive. Also avoid flood plains; a heavy rain can wash away your fish. The water table should be at least 1 meter below the pond bottom to prevent the pond from floating or mixing with groundwater. Fourth, access. You need a road or path to bring in feed, fingerlings, and to take fish to market. Fifth, security. The site should be safe from thieves and predators like birds, snakes, and otters. Fencing with netting or bamboo helps. Finally, consider sunlight �?? ponds need 6�??8 hours of direct sunlight per day for natural food production. Shade from trees reduces productivity. In Africa, many successful ponds are built near homesteads for easy monitoring. Take your time to choose well; a good site saves you 30�??50% of construction costs..Trim()

Basic Pond Design Principles

A good pond design ensures easy management, good water quality, and high fish survival. Start with shape: rectangular ponds are easiest to harvest with a net. A length-to-width ratio of 2:1 or 3:1 is ideal. For a 100 m² pond, make it 20 m long and 5 m wide. Depth: 1.0�??1.5 m at the shallow end and 1.5�??2.0 m at the deep end. This allows fish to escape heat and allows you to drain completely. The bottom should slope gently (1�??2%) towards the drain. The drain is a pipe or channel at the lowest point, fitted with a screen to prevent fish from escaping. You also need an inlet pipe or channel at the highest point, with a screen to keep out wild fish and debris. Build a dyke (embankment) around the pond. The dyke should be at least 30 cm above water level and 50�??100 cm wide at the top. Compact the soil well to prevent leaks. If you use a liner, lay it over a smooth, stone-free surface and cover the edges with soil. For concrete ponds, reinforce with wire mesh and use a 1:2:3 cement-sand-gravel mix. Include a overflow pipe to release excess water during heavy rain. A monk or standpipe is a simple drainage structure: a vertical pipe with holes that can be raised or lowered to control water level. Also, plan for a harvest basin �?? a small depression at the deep end where fish gather when you drain. Finally, leave space for a storage shed for feed and a small area for sorting fish. A well-designed pond costs 20�??30% more upfront but saves labour and prevents fish kills. Sketch your design on paper before you dig..Trim()

Safety and Tools for Pond Construction

Building a pond is hard physical work. Safety first. Wear sturdy boots, gloves, and a hat. Digging in wet clay is slippery �?? use a mattock or hoe with a long handle to avoid back strain. Never dig alone; work in pairs or a group. If you use a pump or generator, keep it on dry ground and check cables for damage. When working with concrete, wear goggles and a dust mask; cement burns skin. Have a basic first aid kit with bandages, antiseptic, and painkillers. Drink plenty of water to avoid heat stroke. Tools you need: shovels (round and square), hoes, mattocks, wheelbarrow, pickaxe, spirit level, measuring tape (30 m), string and pegs for marking, a machete for clearing grass, and a bucket. For compacting, a hand tamper or a heavy log works. For water testing, a simple pH kit and thermometer. For liner ponds, a sharp knife and a broom to smooth the ground. For concrete, a mixing board, trowel, float, and wire mesh cutter. You can rent a plate compactor for large ponds. A water pump (2�??5 HP) is useful for draining or filling. Budget: basic hand tools cost $100�??$300. Rent or borrow what you can. In Africa, many farmers share tools through cooperatives. Before you start, check the weather �?? avoid the rainy season when holes fill with water. Mark the pond outline with pegs and string, then remove topsoil and store it for later use on dykes. Dig in layers, and keep the site tidy to avoid accidents. When using a liner, wear gloves to avoid cuts. Conducting a Topographic Survey

A topographic survey shows the shape and slope of your land. You need it to design a pond that fills and drains by gravity, avoiding pumps. Start by walking the site at the driest time of year. Use a dumpy level, tripod, and levelling staff. If you don't have these, a water-filled hose and two clear plastic pipes work as a water level. Set up your level where you can see the whole area. Take a backsight on a known point (like a concrete benchmark or a large rock). Then take foresights at each grid point. Subtract foresight from backsight to get the height difference. If the difference is positive, the ground is lower; negative, higher. Write these numbers down. Slope Stability and Embankment Design

Embankments are the walls of your pond. They must hold back water and not collapse. The key is slope stability. A safe slope depends on soil type. The top of the embankment should be at least 0.5 m wide to allow walking and maintenance. The outside slope can be steeper, like 1.5:1, to save space. Designing Gravity-Fed Water Supply

Gravity-fed water supply uses the natural slope of the land to move water from a source to your pond without a pump. This saves money on fuel and electricity, and it works even during power cuts. To design a gravity system, you need a water source that is higher than your pond. This could be a stream, a spring, a roof catchment tank, or a reservoir you build on a hill. The key measurement is the vertical drop, called head. For every 1 metre of drop, you get about 0.1 bar of pressure. A typical pond needs a flow of 1 to 5 litres per second, depending on its size. To calculate the flow, you can use a bucket and a stopwatch: time how long it takes to fill a 20-litre bucket from your source. If it takes 10 seconds, that is 2 litres per second. Next, measure the distance from source to pond. A gentle slope of 1% (1 metre drop per 100 metres distance) is enough for a smooth flow in a 50mm pipe. Use a spirit level and a string line to check the slope. For the pipe, use uPVC or HDPE pipes. A 50mm diameter pipe can carry about 2 litres per second over 100 metres with a 1-metre drop. Bury the pipe at least 30cm deep to protect it from sun and animals. At the pond inlet, install a simple gate valve to control flow. Also add a screen or gravel filter to keep out leaves and small fish. In Africa, many farmers use bamboo or PVC pipes for gravity systems. Ensure the source is protected from contamination by fencing and planting grass. Test the system by letting water run for one hour and checking that the pond fills evenly. If flow is too low, increase pipe diameter or reduce bends. Gravity systems are cheap to run but require careful planning and maintenance..Trim()

Pump Selection and Installation

When gravity is not possible, you need a pump. Pumps come in two main types for fish ponds: surface pumps (centrifugal) and submersible pumps. Surface pumps sit above water and suck water through a hose. They are good for shallow wells or rivers up to 7 metres deep. Submersible pumps go inside the water and push water up. They can lift water from deeper than 7 metres and are quieter. To choose a pump, you need to know two things: total head and flow rate. Total head is the vertical lift plus friction losses in pipes. For every 10 metres of pipe, add 1 metre of head for friction. For example, if you lift water 5 metres and have 30 metres of pipe, total head is 5 + 3 = 8 metres. Then decide flow rate: a 1000 m² pond needs about 2 to 5 litres per second. A pump rated at 10,000 litres per hour (about 2.8 L/s) at 8 metres head is suitable. In Africa, common brands include Pedrollo, Leo, and Dayuan. Solar pumps are increasingly popular because they avoid fuel costs. A 500W solar panel can run a 0.5 HP pump for 6 hours a day. Install the pump on a concrete base above flood level. Use a non-return valve on the suction line to keep water primed. For submersible pumps, hang them at least 1 metre above the bottom to avoid silt. Connect pipes with unions for easy removal. Wire the pump with a circuit breaker and earth leakage protection. Test the pump by running it for 30 minutes and checking for leaks and overheating. Always keep a spare pump or repair kit. Regular maintenance includes cleaning impellers and checking seals. With proper installation, a good pump can last 5 to 10 years..Trim()

Constructing Inlet Pipes and Channels

Inlet pipes and channels bring water from your source to the pond. They must be strong, clog-free, and easy to control. For small ponds, use 50mm PVC pipes. For larger ponds, use 75mm or 100mm pipes, or open channels lined with concrete or plastic. Start by digging a trench from the water source to the pond. The trench should be at least 30cm deep and 20cm wide. Slope it gently towards the pond (1% slope). If using pipes, lay them on a bed of sand to prevent damage from rocks. Join pipes with PVC glue and fittings. Use 45-degree bends instead of 90-degree elbows to reduce friction. At the pond end, the pipe should enter above the water level, angled down at 45 degrees, so water splashes and adds oxygen. Fit a gate valve just before the pond to control flow. For open channels, line the bottom and sides with bricks or concrete (1:3 cement-sand mix). Make the channel U-shaped, 30cm wide and 20cm deep. Add a screen of wire mesh (5mm holes) at the inlet to keep out debris and wild fish. In Africa, many farmers use bamboo pipes for temporary inlets. Split a bamboo pole, hollow it, and join with rubber seals. For a permanent system, PVC is best. Also install a small sediment trap: a box or pit before the pond where water slows down and drops sand. Clean it weekly. Test the inlet by running water and checking for leaks and blockages. If water flows too fast, it can erode the pond bank. Place a flat stone or concrete slab at the entry point to break the flow. Keep the inlet shaded to prevent algae growth. Regular cleaning ensures a steady supply..Trim()

Building Drainage and Harvesting Structures

Drainage is as important as water supply. You need to empty the pond for harvesting, cleaning, or repairs. A good drainage system also prevents overflow during heavy rains. The main drainage structure is a monk or a standpipe, but here we focus on the outlet channel and harvesting pit. At the lowest point of the pond, install a drain pipe (50-100mm diameter) that goes through the dyke. The pipe should slope down towards the outside. Inside the pond, cover the pipe opening with a screen to stop fish escaping. Outside, the pipe leads to a harvesting pit. This pit is a concrete or plastic-lined basin, about 1m x 1m x 1m, where water and fish collect. The pit has a sloped floor so fish slide to one corner. From the pit, water can flow to a drainage channel or be reused for irrigation. Build the pit with bricks and cement (1:4 mix). Add a wooden or metal grate on top for safety. For overflow control, create a spillway: a shallow channel at the top of the dyke that lets excess water out. Line it with grass or concrete to prevent erosion. In Africa, many farmers use a simple bamboo or PVC pipe as a drain, but a proper pit makes harvesting easier. To harvest, open the drain valve and let water out slowly. Fish will follow the current into the pit. Use a net to scoop them out. After harvest, flush the pond with clean water. Clean the pit and drain pipe. Check for cracks and repair with cement. Also, consider a sedimentation pond downstream to trap mud before water leaves your farm. This protects the environment. A well-built drainage system saves labour and reduces fish stress..Trim()

Installing Monks and Standpipes

A monk is a vertical structure that controls water level and drains the pond from the bottom. It is common in Asian and African fish farms. A standpipe is a simpler version: a vertical pipe inside the pond that you can raise or lower to set water level. Both are installed at the deepest point of the pond. For a monk, you build a concrete box (about 50cm x 50cm x 100cm) with three openings: one at the bottom for the drain pipe, one at the top for overflow, and one on the side for a wooden board or valve. The monk sits on a firm foundation. Inside, you place removable boards to block water. To drain, you remove boards from top to bottom. The water flows out through the bottom pipe. For a standpipe, use a 50-100mm PVC pipe. Install a 90-degree elbow at the bottom connected to the drain pipe through the dyke. The vertical pipe rises above the water level. To set water level, cut the pipe to the desired height and add a swivel joint or a series of pipes with O-rings. You can also use a threaded cap with holes. In Africa, many farmers use a simple standpipe made from a plastic drum with holes. The drum sits over the drain pipe. Water enters the drum and exits through the drain. To adjust level, you move the drum up or down. For both monks and standpipes, use rubber seals or cement to prevent leaks. Test by filling the pond and checking that water holds at the desired level. If it leaks, apply more cement or replace seals. Monks are more durable but cost more. Standpipes are cheap and easy to make. Both allow you to drain the pond without digging. Keep the area around the monk clear of mud and debris. Inspect monthly for cracks and blockages..Trim()

Automating Water Level Control

Automation saves time and prevents disasters. A simple float valve can keep your pond at the right level. A float valve works like a toilet cistern: when water drops, the float sinks and opens a valve; when water rises, the float closes it. You can buy a plastic float valve for about $5-$10. Install it on the inlet pipe at the pond edge. Set the float height to your desired water level. For a more advanced system, use a float switch connected to a pump. A float switch is a small device that turns the pump on when water is low and off when full. It costs $10-$20. Connect it to a relay and contactor for safety. For drainage automation, use a timer or a soil moisture sensor. But the simplest is a standpipe with an overflow. If you want to automate draining, install a motorized valve on the drain pipe. A 12V solenoid valve ($20) can be controlled by a timer or a float switch. Power it with a small solar panel and battery. In Africa, many farmers use a mobile phone SMS controller to turn pumps on and off. These cost $50-$100 and work with a SIM card. For low-tech automation, use a siphon system: a pipe that starts draining when water reaches a certain height. You can also use a bucket with a hole as a slow drain. Always include a manual override in case of power failure. Test your automation weekly. Clean sensors and valves. Keep electrical connections dry. Automation is not a replacement for daily checks. Walk around your pond every morning to look for leaks, stuck valves, or dead fish. With simple automation, you can reduce labour and maintain stable water quality..Trim()

Module 9 · Water Quality and Treatment

Testing Water Parameters: pH, DO, Ammonia

Water quality is the foundation of fish health. You must test regularly. For pH, use a liquid test kit or electronic meter. Ideal range for tilapia and catfish is 6.5 to 8.5. Test at least twice a week, early morning. If pH is below 6.5, add agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) at 100-200 kg per hectare. If above 8.5, add gypsum or partially exchange water. Dissolved oxygen (DO) is critical. Fish need at least 4 mg/L; below 2 mg/L they gasp at surface. Use a DO meter or chemical test kit. Test at dawn when DO is lowest. If low, increase aeration by splashing water or using a paddle wheel aerator. Ammonia is toxic. Unionized ammonia (NH3) is deadly above 0.1 mg/L. Test with a kit. Ammonia comes from fish waste and uneaten feed. To reduce, stop feeding for a day, increase aeration, and do a 30% water exchange. In Africa, many farmers use simple tools: a secchi disk for clarity, a thermometer, and locally made test kits from agricultural suppliers. Record all readings in a notebook. Always test before and after adding any treatment. Remember, consistent monitoring prevents fish kills. Start with a basic kit: pH, ammonia, and DO. These three tests will save your fish..Trim()

Water Treatment: Chlorination and Dechlorination

Chlorine is used to disinfect water, killing bacteria and viruses. But chlorine is also toxic to fish. You must dechlorinate before adding water to your pond. For chlorination, use calcium hypochlorite (bleaching powder) at 5-10 mg/L for empty ponds. For water supply, if using municipal water, it may already contain chlorine. Test with a chlorine test kit. To dechlorinate, you can use sodium thiosulfate. Dose: 1 gram per 100 liters of water to neutralize 1 mg/L chlorine. Alternatively, let water sit in an open tank for 24-48 hours in sunlight; chlorine evaporates. In rural Africa, many farmers use rainwater or well water, which may not need chlorination. But if you must treat, always dechlorinate. For emergency fish health, potassium permanganate at 2-4 mg/L can treat external parasites, but it also kills beneficial bacteria. Use only in isolated tanks. Never chlorinate a pond with fish. If you accidentally add chlorine, immediately add sodium thiosulfate or do a large water exchange. Store chemicals in a dry, dark place. Wear gloves and goggles. Measure carefully; overdose can kill everything. After dechlorination, test again to confirm zero chlorine. Then add water to pond. Always have a backup water source. Filling and Testing the Pond

Start filling your pond only after the walls and bottom are fully cured. For concrete ponds, cure for 7 days by keeping the surface wet. For lined ponds, check that the liner has no wrinkles or sharp objects underneath. Fill with clean water from a borehole, well, or stream. If using stream water, filter it through a fine mesh to keep out wild fish and insects. Fill the pond to about 10 cm below the top of the dyke or the overflow pipe. This takes 1-2 days for a 10m x 10m pond that is 1.5m deep, which holds about 150,000 litres. Use a water pump if you have one, or a hose from a tap. While filling, watch for leaks. Mark the water level with a stick. After 24 hours, check if the level has dropped. A drop of more than 2 cm means you have a leak. For concrete ponds, leaks are often at joints or cracks. Drain and repair with cement mortar. For lined ponds, check for punctures. Also test the overflow pipe: pour a bucket of water near it and see if it drains freely. Test the inlet: open the tap and see if water flows without splashing. After filling, let the water sit for 2-3 days. This allows chlorine to evaporate if you used tap water. If you used borehole water, still wait 24 hours. Then test the pH with a simple pH test kit (available at agro-vet shops). The pH should be between 6.5 and 8.5. If it is too low (acidic), add agricultural lime at 100g per square metre. If too high (alkaline), add gypsum at 50g per square metre. Also test for hardness if you can. Finally, check water temperature. It should be between 25°C and 30°C for tilapia and catfish. If it is too cold, wait for a warmer day or add a shade net to reduce evaporation but not temperature. Record all test results in a notebook. This baseline helps you later if problems arise..Trim()

Conditioning Water for Fish Stocking

Conditioning water means making it safe and productive for fish. After filling and testing, you need to build a natural food base. The easiest way is to add organic matter. For a 10m x 10m pond, add 20 kg of dry chicken manure or 30 kg of cow dung. Put it in a sack and hang it in the water near the inlet. This releases nutrients slowly. Alternatively, add 5 kg of NPK fertiliser (20-10-10) dissolved in a bucket of water and spread over the pond. Wait 3-5 days. The water should turn a light green colour. This means plankton (tiny plants and animals) are growing. If it stays clear, add another 10 kg of manure. Do not over-fertilise: dark green water with a bad smell means too many nutrients, which can kill fish. Use a Secchi disk (a white plate on a stick) to check clarity. Lower it into the water until it disappears. The depth should be 30-40 cm. If less than 20 cm, the water is too cloudy with plankton; stop feeding. If more than 50 cm, add more manure. Also check dissolved oxygen. You can buy a simple test kit. Oxygen should be above 5 mg/l. If it is low, increase water movement by splashing water or using a small pump. For catfish, you can add salt at 1 kg per 1000 litres to reduce stress and prevent fungus. For tilapia, salt is not needed. Adjust pH again after fertilising. If pH drops below 6.5, add lime. If above 8.5, add gypsum. Also check ammonia and nitrite if you have test kits. They should be near zero. High ammonia means too much waste; do a partial water change (20%) and stop feeding. Conditioning takes 7-10 days. During this time, run a pump or aerator for at least 6 hours a day if you have one. If not, manually splash the water with a bucket twice a day. By the end, the water should be green, warm, and smell earthy, not rotten. Then you are ready to stock..Trim()

Acclimating and Releasing Fish

Never dump fish straight into the pond. They will die from shock. First, prepare the pond: stop feeding for 24 hours before stocking. This reduces waste. Bring the fish in plastic bags or buckets from the hatchery. The water in the bag has a different temperature and pH. Float the sealed bags on the pond surface for 15-20 minutes. This equalises temperature. Then open the bags and add a cup of pond water every 5 minutes for 20 minutes. This mixes the waters slowly. After that, gently net the fish out of the bag and release them into the pond. Do not pour the bag water into the pond; it may contain waste or diseases. For tilapia, stock at 3 fish per square metre if you have no aeration. For a 10m x 10m pond, that is 300 fish. With aeration, you can stock 5-6 per square metre. For catfish, stock at 5-10 per square metre without aeration, and up to 20 with aeration. Buy fingerlings that are at least 5-10 grams each. Smaller ones are cheaper but have lower survival. Release fish in the early morning or late evening when it is cool. Do not release in the heat of the day. Spread them around the pond, not all in one spot. Watch them swim away. If some float or swim sideways, they are stressed. If many die within 24 hours, check water quality. Also, do not mix species unless you know they are compatible. Tilapia and catfish can be mixed, but catfish may eat small tilapia. If you mix, stock catfish after tilapia have grown to at least 20g. After release, do not feed for 24 hours. Let them settle. Then start with small amounts. Record the number and size of fish stocked. This helps you calculate feed later. Always buy from a reputable hatchery. Ask for a health certificate if possible..Trim()

Initial Feeding and Health Checks

After stocking, wait 24 hours before feeding. Start with a high-quality floating pellet feed, 2mm size for fingerlings. Feed them twice a day, morning and evening. Give only what they can eat in 15 minutes. For 300 tilapia fingerlings, start with 100g per day total. For catfish, start with 150g per day. Broadcast the feed evenly across the pond. Watch the fish come to eat. If they do not eat, check water quality. Overfeeding wastes money and pollutes water. Remove any uneaten feed after 30 minutes with a fine net. Increase feed gradually as fish grow. After one week, increase to 200g per day for tilapia and 300g for catfish. Always check fish health daily. Look for signs of disease: white spots on skin, frayed fins, red sores, or fish gasping at the surface. If you see these, isolate affected fish if possible. Common problems are fungus and bacteria. For fungus, add salt at 3g per litre as a bath for 5 minutes, or treat the whole pond with 1kg salt per 1000 litres. For bacteria, use oxytetracycline at 50mg per kg of fish, mixed with feed for 5 days. But prevention is better: keep water clean, do not overstock, and avoid handling fish roughly. Also check for predators like birds, snakes, and frogs. Cover the pond with netting if needed. Keep a daily log: date, feed amount, water temperature, fish behaviour, and any deaths. Remove dead fish immediately; they spread disease. If many fish die, test pH and ammonia. Do a 30% water change and stop feeding for a day. After two weeks, you can start sampling fish to check growth. Use a cast net to catch a few, weigh them, and return them. If they are growing well, adjust feed. Healthy fish are active and have shiny skin..Trim()

Monitoring Post-Stocking Behavior

After stocking, spend at least 30 minutes each morning and evening watching your fish. Normal behaviour: fish swim calmly, come to the surface at feeding time, and scatter when you approach. Abnormal behaviour tells you problems. If fish gasp at the surface in early morning, oxygen is low. Increase aeration by splashing water or running a pump. If they stay at the bottom and do not eat, check temperature and pH. If they rub against the pond walls, they may have parasites. If they swim erratically or spin, it could be a toxin or disease. Also watch for grouping. If all fish crowd in one corner, there may be a predator or low oxygen in other areas. Check water temperature daily with a simple thermometer. It should be 25-30°C. If it drops below 20°C, fish stop eating. If it rises above 35°C, they stress. Use shade nets or increase water depth to buffer temperature. Check pH every 3 days. Keep a record. If pH changes by more than 0.5 in a day, test again and adjust slowly. Also check for algae blooms. Green water is good, but if it turns pea-soup thick and smells bad, it can crash at night, causing oxygen loss. If that happens, do a 20% water change and reduce feeding. Look for dead fish every morning. Remove them immediately. A few deaths in the first week are normal (stress), but more than 1% per day is a problem. Also check for insects or frogs. Dragonfly larvae eat small fish. Net them out. Birds like herons can eat many fish. Put a net over the pond. If you see fish with white spots or red patches, treat as in Lesson 4. Keep a notebook with daily observations. After one week, you will know your pond's rhythm. Then you can relax but still check twice a day..Trim()

Troubleshooting Common Start-Up Issues

Even with good preparation, problems happen. Here are common start-up issues and fixes. 1) Fish dying within 24 hours: likely temperature or pH shock. Next time, acclimate longer. Check pH now; adjust if needed. Remove dead fish. 2) Fish gasping at surface: low oxygen. Add a pump or splash water. Stop feeding for 24 hours. 3) Water turns milky white: bacterial bloom. Do a 30% water change. Add salt at 1kg per 1000 litres. 4) Water turns dark green and smells rotten: too much fertiliser. Stop feeding, do 50% water change, add fresh water. 5) Fish not eating: check temperature (below 20°C or above 35°C), pH, or ammonia. Fix accordingly. 6) Fish have white spots: fungus. Treat with salt bath as in Lesson 4. 7) Fish have red sores: bacteria. Use oxytetracycline in feed. 8) Many fish hiding: predator. Check for birds, snakes, or frogs. Net the pond. 9) Leaking pond: water level drops. Find leak and repair. For concrete, use cement. For liner, use patch kit. 10) Algae too thick: use a rake to remove some, or add tilapia that eat algae. 11) Ammonia high: do 50% water change, stop feeding for 2 days. 12) pH too low: add lime at 100g per square metre. 13) pH too high: add gypsum at 50g per square metre. 14) Fish jumping out: stress or poor water. Check water quality. 15) No growth after 2 weeks: feed more or check feed quality. Always keep records. If you cannot solve a problem, ask your local fisheries officer or an experienced farmer. Do not guess. Many start-up issues are caused by overstocking, overfeeding, or poor water quality. Test your water weekly. A simple test kit costs little and saves fish. Also, buy healthy fingerlings from a trusted source. If you follow the steps in this module, you will have a successful stocking. Remember, patience is key. Do not rush to stock before the water is ready..Trim()
