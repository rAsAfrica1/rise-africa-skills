Module 2 · Selecting and Preparing Fruits

Identifying Ripe and High-Quality Fruits for Juicing

To make great juice, you must start with great fruit. Ripe fruit gives more juice and better flavour. For mangoes, look for a sweet smell at the stem end and slight softness when pressed. Avoid fruits with dark spots or bruises. For oranges, choose ones that feel heavy for their size and have smooth skin. Pineapples should have a sweet aroma at the base and green leaves that pull out easily. In Africa, you can buy fruits at local markets, but inspect them carefully. Ask the seller for a taste if possible. For guavas, pick ones that are slightly soft and yellow-green. Bananas for juice should be fully yellow with brown spotsânot green. Overripe fruits can be used but may have off-flavours. Underripe fruits are too hard and acidic. Always smell the fruit: a sweet, fruity aroma means it's ready. Also, check the bottom of the fruit for mould. A good rule: if it doesn't smell like fruit, it won't taste like fruit. Buy fruits in season when they are cheapest and best. For example, mangoes in West Africa from April to June. If you buy in bulk, sort them immediately. Use ripe fruits within two days. Store unripe fruits at room temperature to ripen. Never juice rotten fruitsâthey can make you sick. High-quality fruits are firm but give slightly to pressure, have bright colour, and no soft spots. Remember, juice is only as good as the fruit you put in..Trim()

Cleaning and Sanitizing Fruits to Remove Pesticides and Dirt

Fruits often carry dirt, bacteria, and pesticide residues. Proper cleaning is essential for safe juice. Start by washing your hands with soap and water. Then, rinse fruits under running water. Do not use soap on the fruits themselvesâit can leave harmful residues. For firm fruits like oranges and pineapples, use a clean vegetable brush to scrub the skin. For delicate fruits like grapes or berries, soak them in a bowl of water with 1 tablespoon of baking soda per litre for 10 minutes. This helps remove pesticides. Then rinse well. For a sanitizing step, you can use a diluted bleach solution: 1 teaspoon of unscented bleach per 4 litres of water. Soak fruits for 2 minutes, then rinse thoroughly with clean water. This kills most germs. Alternatively, use vinegar: 1 part white vinegar to 3 parts water, soak for 5 minutes, then rinse. In Africa, where clean water may be scarce, use boiled and cooled water for the final rinse. Never use dirty water. After washing, dry fruits with a clean cloth or paper towel to prevent mould. For fruits with rough skin like melons, scrub well because bacteria can hide in crevices. Even if you peel the fruit, washing first prevents contamination when cutting. Always clean your knives, cutting boards, and juicer before use. Sanitize them with hot water and bleach solution. Remember, juice is not cooked, so any germs on the fruit end up in your drink. Clean fruits mean safe juice that lasts longer..Trim()

Peeling, Coring, and Cutting Techniques for Different Fruits

Different fruits need different preparation. For oranges and citrus, cut off both ends, then slice off the peel in strips following the curve. Remove any white pithâit's bitter. Cut the fruit in half and remove seeds. For pineapple, cut off the top and bottom, slice off the skin in downward strips, then cut into quarters and remove the tough core. For mangoes, stand the fruit upright and slice down each side of the flat seed. Score the flesh in a grid and scoop out with a spoon. For apples and pears, peel with a vegetable peeler, cut into quarters, and remove the core with a knife. For guavas, cut in half and scoop out the seeds with a spoon. For bananas, simply peel and cut into chunks. Always use a sharp knife and a clean cutting board. A dull knife is dangerous because it slips. Cut fruits into similar-sized pieces so they juice evenly. For juicers, smaller pieces work better. For blending, chunks are fine. Remove all seeds and pitsâthey can damage your juicer and add bitterness. For fruits with edible skins like grapes, you can leave them on if you wash well. For tough skins like kiwi, peel first. Work quickly to prevent browning. Use a stainless steel knife, not carbon steel, which can discolour fruit. After cutting, use immediately or store in airtight containers in the fridge. Practice proper cutting techniques to avoid waste and injuries. Remember, the goal is to get clean, seedless, uniform pieces ready for juicing..Trim()

Preventing Enzymatic Browning in Cut Fruits

When you cut fruits like apples, bananas, or avocados, the flesh turns brown. This is called enzymatic browning. It happens because oxygen in the air reacts with enzymes in the fruit. Browned fruit looks bad and can taste slightly bitter, but it's not harmful. However, for juice, you want bright colour and fresh taste. To prevent browning, you can use several methods. First, work quickly and juice immediately after cutting. Second, add an acid: lemon juice or lime juice. For every 2 cups of cut fruit, add 1 tablespoon of lemon juice and mix. The acid slows the browning reaction. You can also use ascorbic acid (vitamin C) powder: dissolve 1 teaspoon in 1 cup of water and dip cut fruits. Third, use salt water: soak cut fruits in a solution of 1 teaspoon salt per litre of water for 5 minutes, then rinse. This works for apples and pears. Fourth, blanch the fruit: dip in boiling water for 1-2 minutes, then transfer to ice water. This stops the enzymes. But blanching can change texture, so it's best for fruits you will blend. Fifth, store cut fruits in airtight containers with little air, or cover with water or juice. In Africa, where electricity may be unreliable, use the lemon juice methodâit's cheap and effective. For bananas, you can also use honey water: 1 tablespoon honey in 1 cup water. Always use stainless steel or plastic bowls, not aluminium or iron, which speed up browning. If browning occurs, skim it off or blend it inâit's safe but not ideal. Prevention is better than cure..Trim()

Blanching and Freezing Fruits for Year-Round Juice Production

Fruits are seasonal. To make juice all year, you need to preserve them. Blanching and freezing are two methods. Blanching means briefly boiling fruit, then cooling it quickly. This stops enzymes that cause spoilage and browning. To blanch, bring a large pot of water to a rolling boil. Add cut fruit in small batches. For most fruits, blanch for 1-2 minutes. Then immediately transfer to a bowl of ice water for the same time. Drain and pat dry. Blanching works well for apples, peaches, and mangoes. It softens the fruit, so it's best for fruits you will blend or juice later. After blanching, pack fruit into airtight bags or containers, remove as much air as possible, and freeze. For freezing without blanching, some fruits like berries and bananas can be frozen directly. Spread them on a tray, freeze until solid, then transfer to bags. This prevents them from sticking together. For citrus, you can freeze juice in ice cube trays, then store cubes in bags. Frozen fruit lasts 6-12 months. In Africa, if you don't have a freezer, you can dry fruits or make fruit leather, but freezing is best for juice. When ready to use, thaw fruit in the refrigerator overnight. Use thawed fruit within 2 days. You can also blend frozen fruit directly into smoothies. Always label bags with the fruit type and date. Never refreeze thawed fruit. This method lets you enjoy mango juice in December or strawberry juice in June. It saves money and reduces waste..Trim()

Assessing Fruit Yield and Calculating Juice Output

To run a juice business, you must know how much juice you get from your fruits. This is called yield. Yield depends on the fruit type, ripeness, and juicing method. For example, oranges yield about 50% juice by weight. That means 1 kg of oranges gives about 500 ml of juice. Watermelons yield 70-80%, so 1 kg gives 700-800 ml. Mangoes yield 60-70%, pineapples 50-60%, apples 70-80%, and grapes 60-70%. To calculate yield, weigh your fruit before juicing. Then juice it, and measure the juice in millilitres. Use this formula: Yield % = (Volume of juice in ml / Weight of fruit in grams) x 100. For example, if 1 kg (1000 g) of oranges gives 500 ml, yield = (500/1000) x 100 = 50%. Keep records for each fruit. This helps you price your juice. If 1 kg of oranges costs 500 shillings and gives 500 ml, your cost per ml is 1 shilling. Add other costs like labour, bottles, and electricity. Then set a selling price that gives profit. Also, consider waste: peels and seeds can be composted or used for animal feed. To improve yield, choose juicy varieties, use ripe fruits, and use an efficient juicer. A manual press gives less yield than an electric juicer. For small businesses, test each batch. Note that yield can vary by season and fruit quality. Always weigh and measure accurately. Use a kitchen scale and a measuring jug. This simple practice will help you avoid losses and grow your business..Trim()

Module 3 · Juice Extraction Methods

Manual Extraction: Hand Squeezing and Reamers

Manual extraction is the simplest and cheapest way to start making fruit juice. For citrus fruits like oranges, lemons, limes, and tangerines, hand squeezing or using a reamer is very effective. You need ripe, fresh fruits. Roll the fruit on a hard surface with your palm to soften the internal membranes. Cut it in half crosswise. For hand squeezing, simply squeeze the halves over a bowl, using your fingers to catch seeds. For a reamer, push the fruit half onto the ridged cone and twist. A wooden or plastic reamer costs very little and extracts more juice than hand squeezing alone. In Africa, many households use a fork or a clean stick as a reamer. For larger quantities, a manual citrus press with a lever gives more force and yields more juice with less effort. Always work on a clean surface. Wash fruits thoroughly with clean water. For fruits like mangoes, pawpaw, or guava, manual extraction is harder because they are soft and pulpy. You can mash them with a clean potato masher or your hands, then strain through a clean cloth or fine sieve. This method is slow but requires no electricity. It is ideal for small-scale juice businesses starting with little capital. Yields vary: one large orange gives about 70-100 ml of juice. A lemon gives 30-50 ml. To get 1 litre of orange juice, you need about 10-14 oranges. Always discard seeds and excess pulp. For best hygiene, wear clean gloves and use food-grade containers. After extraction, strain the juice to remove pulp if desired. Drink fresh or pasteurize for longer shelf life. Manual extraction is labour-intensive but teaches you the feel of the fruit and helps you avoid waste. It is a good starting point before investing in machines..Trim()

Mechanical Pressing: Basket Press and Hydraulic Press

Mechanical pressing uses physical force to squeeze juice from fruits, especially hard fruits like apples, pears, and pineapples. The basket press is a simple wooden or metal cylinder with a pressing plate. You load crushed or chopped fruit into the basket, then turn a screw or lever to press down. Juice flows out through holes or a spout. A basket press can extract 5-10 litres per batch, depending on size. For apples, first crush them into a pulp using a clean wooden mallet or a manual crusher. Then wrap the pulp in a clean cloth (cheesecloth or muslin) and place it in the basket. Press slowly and steadily. Too much force can tear the cloth and mix pulp into juice. A hydraulic press uses a bottle jack or hydraulic cylinder to apply much greater pressure. It can extract 20-50 litres per batch and is more efficient. Hydraulic presses are common in small commercial juice operations in Africa. They are faster and yield more juice, but they cost more and need maintenance. For both presses, hygiene is critical. Clean and sanitize all parts before and after use. Use food-grade lubricant on moving parts. After pressing, strain the juice through a fine mesh or cloth to remove particles. You can then pasteurize or ferment. Mechanical pressing works best with firm fruits. Soft fruits like berries may clog the press; for those, use a steam juicer or blender. Yields: 1 kg of apples yields about 600-700 ml of juice with a basket press; a hydraulic press can yield up to 750 ml. Pineapples yield about 500-600 ml per kg. Always press within a few hours of crushing to avoid browning and fermentation. Store juice in clean glass or plastic containers. If you are scaling up, a hydraulic press is a good investment. It saves time and labour, and gives consistent results..Trim()

Centrifugal Juicers: Operation and Maintenance

Centrifugal juicers are the most common electric juicers. They use a fast-spinning shredding disc to grate fruit against a mesh filter. The centrifugal force pushes juice through the mesh, while pulp is thrown to the sides. These juicers work well for hard fruits and vegetables like carrots, apples, beetroot, and pineapple. They are fast: you can juice 1 litre in 1-2 minutes. However, they produce more foam and less juice than slow juicers, and the heat from the spinning can destroy some nutrients. To operate: wash and cut fruits into pieces that fit the feed chute. Remove large seeds and pits (e.g., mango, peach). Turn on the machine, then feed pieces with the pusher. Do not force too much at once; let the machine work. Juice flows into a jug, and pulp collects in a container. For best yield, alternate soft and hard fruits. After use, disassemble the parts: pusher, feed chute, lid, mesh filter, and pulp container. Rinse under warm water immediately. Use a brush to clean the mesh filter; pulp dries quickly and clogs holes. Most parts are not dishwasher-safe unless stated. Wash with mild soap and warm water, then air dry. Once a week, check the blade for dullness or damage. A dull blade reduces yield and can overheat the motor. Lubricate the motor bearings if recommended by the manufacturer. Never immerse the motor base in water. In Africa, power fluctuations can damage the motor. Use a voltage stabilizer if possible. Centrifugal juicers are affordable (from $30-$100) and widely available. They are a good choice for small juice bars. But they are noisy and not ideal for leafy greens or wheatgrass. For maximum yield, drink the juice immediately. Clean the machine within 30 minutes of use to prevent staining and bacterial growth..Trim()

Masticating Juicers: Slow Juicing for Maximum Yield

Masticating juicers, also called slow juicers or cold-press juicers, crush and squeeze fruit at low speed (80-100 RPM). They use a single or twin auger to grind fruit into a pulp, then press it through a fine screen. Because they run slowly, they produce less heat, preserving enzymes and vitamins. They yield more juice than centrifugal juicers: up to 30% more. They also handle leafy greens, wheatgrass, and soft fruits well. They are quieter and can process continuous batches with less foaming. However, they are slower: juicing 1 litre may take 5-10 minutes. They cost more ($150-$500). To operate: assemble the auger, screen, and pulp container. Cut fruits into small pieces. Feed slowly; do not overload. For hard fruits like carrots, cut into thin strips. For soft fruits like berries, feed with other fruits to avoid clogging. The juice comes out one spout, pulp another. You can run the pulp through again for extra yield. After use, disassemble and rinse all parts. Use the cleaning brush to remove pulp from the screen. Most parts are not dishwasher-safe. Wash with warm soapy water and air dry. Once a month, lubricate the auger with food-grade oil if recommended. Check the screen for cracks. In Africa, where power can be unstable, consider a manual masticating juicer (hand-crank) for off-grid use. These are cheaper and don't need electricity, but require more effort. Masticating juicers are ideal for health-conscious juice businesses. They produce dry pulp, meaning less waste. You can compost the pulp. For maximum yield, juice fresh, ripe fruits. Store juice in airtight glass bottles in the fridge for up to 48 hours. Clean immediately after use to prevent staining and odours..Trim()

Steam Juicing for Soft Fruits and Berries

Steam juicing is a gentle method for extracting juice from soft fruits like berries, grapes, tomatoes, and stone fruits. It uses a pot with a colander or steamer basket on top. Water boils in the bottom pot, steam rises through the fruit, and the juice drips into a collection jar. This method yields clear juice with little pulp. It is excellent for making jelly, syrup, and cordials. You need a steam juicer (also called a juice steamer). It has three parts: a bottom water pot, a middle fruit basket, and a top lid with a hose. If you don't have one, you can improvise with a large pot, a colander, and a clean cloth. But a proper steam juicer is safer and more efficient. To use: fill the bottom pot with water (about 1/3 full). Place the fruit basket on top. Add clean, ripe fruit. You can leave small berries whole; larger fruits should be cut. Do not fill more than 3/4 full. Put the lid on and turn on the heat. Once steam builds, juice will start dripping from the hose into a jar. It takes 30-60 minutes for 2-4 litres of juice, depending on fruit. For berries, 1 kg yields about 700-800 ml of juice. For grapes, 1 kg yields 600-700 ml. The juice is hot; handle with care. After juicing, let the fruit cool, then compost it. The juice can be drunk hot or cold, or processed into jelly. Clean the steamer with warm soapy water. The hose can be cleaned with a pipe cleaner. Steam juicing is popular in Africa for making local berry juices like baobab, hibiscus, and tamarind. It requires no electricity if you use a gas or charcoal stove. It is a low-cost method for small-scale production. However, it is slower and uses more fuel. For best results, use fully ripe fruit. Avoid overfilling, as it can boil over. Store juice in sterilized bottles. It keeps for several weeks if refrigerated..Trim()

Blender-Based Extraction and Straining Techniques

Blender-based extraction is a quick and affordable way to make juice at home or in small businesses. You blend fruit with a little water, then strain the mixture through a cloth or fine sieve. This method works for soft fruits like mango, pawpaw, banana, guava, and watermelon. It is not ideal for hard fruits like apples unless you cook them first. To start, wash and peel fruits as needed. Remove seeds and pits. Cut into chunks. Put fruit in the blender. Add water: about 1 cup of water per 2 cups of fruit. For juicy fruits like watermelon, no water is needed. Blend on high for 30-60 seconds until smooth. For a smoother juice, blend longer. Then strain. You can use a clean muslin cloth, cheesecloth, or a fine mesh sieve. Pour the blended mixture into the cloth, then squeeze or press to extract juice. For larger quantities, use a jelly bag or a clean pillowcase. You can also use a manual potato ricer to press the pulp. After straining, you may need to strain again for clarity. The leftover pulp can be used in baking or composted. Yields: 1 kg of mango yields about 700-800 ml of juice. 1 kg of watermelon yields 800-900 ml. This method is fast: 5 minutes to blend and 10 minutes to strain. It requires electricity for the blender, but you can use a manual hand-crank blender or a mortar and pestle for off-grid. Hygiene is key: wash the blender jar and blades after each use. Do not let pulp dry on the blades. For commercial use, a heavy-duty blender (e.g., Vitamix) costs more but lasts longer. In Africa, blenders are common in urban areas. This method is great for starting a juice business with low investment. You can experiment with fruit combinations. Always serve fresh or pasteurize for longer shelf life. Strain well to avoid choking hazards, especially for children..Trim()

Understanding Cloudy vs. Clear Juice Preferences

When you make fruit juice, you must decide whether to sell it cloudy or clear. Cloudy juice is thick, opaque, and often sweet because it still contains fruit pulp. Clear juice is transparent, looks like expensive imported juice, and has a longer shelf life. In many African markets, customers have different preferences. For example, in Nigeria, cloudy mango juice is popular as a fresh drink, while in Kenya, clear passion juice is preferred for formal events. To make cloudy juice, you simply press the fruit and stop. .Trim()

Your choice affects your equipment, time, and cost. Cloudy juice requires only a blender and a sieve. Clear juice requires enzymes, filter bags, and possibly a centrifuge. Start by asking your customers what they want. If you sell at a busy market, offer both types and label them clearly. Cloudy juice should be sold fresh within 3 days. Clear juice can last up to 6 months if pasteurized and bottled properly. .Trim()

.Trim()

One common mistake is mixing cloudy and clear juice. If you start with cloudy juice and try to filter it later, you waste time and lose yield. Decide early. .Trim()

.Trim()

Module 11 · Business and Marketing of Fruit Juices

Market Research and Identifying Target Customers

Before you squeeze a single orange, you need to know who will buy your juice. Start by walking around your neighbourhood, local market, and bus park. Count how many people sell fresh juice. Note their prices, cup sizes, and flavours. If five people sell mango juice at 500 Naira per cup, you know the market is crowded. Look for gaps: maybe nobody sells ginger-pineapple blend, or nobody delivers to offices. Talk to at least 20 potential customers. Ask: What juice do you buy? How often? How much do you pay? What would make you switch? Write down their answers. For example, in Kano, you might find that students want cheap 200ml sachets at 100 Naira, while office workers want 500ml bottles at 500 Naira. That tells you to make two product lines. Also check who else sells similar drinks: supermarkets, kiosks, street hawkers. Your target customer is a group of people with similar needs. Pick one group to start: e.g., mothers in your church who want healthy drinks for their kids, or taxi drivers who need quick energy. Once you choose, focus your money and time on them. Don't try to serve everyone. Use a simple notebook to record your findings. After one week, you will have real data, not guesses. This research costs you nothing but time and will save you from making juice nobody wants..Trim()

Costing, Pricing, and Profit Margin Calculation

If you don't know your costs, you will lose money even when sales are high. Start by listing every single expense for one batch of juice. For example, to make 20 litres of orange juice: 30 kg oranges at 500 Naira per kg = 15,000 Naira. Sugar: 2 kg at 800 Naira = 1,600 Naira. Preservative (sodium benzoate): 100 Naira. Bottles: 40 bottles at 50 Naira = 2,000 Naira. Labels: 40 at 20 Naira = 800 Naira. Transport: 1,000 Naira. Gas or firewood: 500 Naira. Labour (your time): 2,000 Naira. Total cost = 23,000 Naira. That makes 40 bottles of 500ml. Cost per bottle = 23,000 / 40 = 575 Naira. Now add your profit margin. A common mistake is to add only 10%. For small food businesses, aim for 30-50% margin. If you want 40% margin, selling price = cost / (1 - 0.40) = 575 / 0.6 = 958 Naira. Round to 1,000 Naira per bottle. Your profit per bottle = 1,000 - 575 = 425 Naira. For 40 bottles, total profit = 17,000 Naira. But remember: you must sell all 40 bottles to get that profit. If you sell only 30, you still cover costs but profit drops. Always track your actual costs weekly. Prices of fruit change with season. In dry season, oranges may cost 800 Naira per kg, raising cost per bottle to 725 Naira. Then you either raise price or reduce bottle size. Use a simple exercise book to record every purchase and sale. At the end of each week, calculate your real profit. This habit separates successful juice businesses from those that close after three months..Trim()

Starting a Small-Scale Juice Business

You can start a juice business with as little as 20,000 Naira if you are smart. First, decide your production scale. Start with 10 litres per day. You need: a clean table, a sharp knife, a manual or electric juicer (manual costs 5,000-15,000 Naira), food-grade buckets, bottles, labels, and a cooler. You don't need a factory. Many successful juice sellers start in their kitchen. Next, get your legal basics: register your business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) for about 10,000 Naira. Get a food handler's certificate from your local government health office (often free or 2,000 Naira). Open a separate bank account for the business. Then set a daily routine: buy fruit early morning at the market (cheaper), wash and sanitise all equipment, juice, bottle, label, and deliver or sell by afternoon. For sales, start with people you know: family, neighbours, church members. Offer free samples to 10 people and ask for feedback. If they like it, ask them to buy a bottle and tell two friends. Use WhatsApp to take orders. For example, in Accra, a woman started by selling 20 bottles per day to her office colleagues. Within three months, she was selling 100 bottles daily and hired one helper. Reinvest your profit: 50% back into buying more fruit, 30% for equipment, 20% as your salary. Keep records of every sale and expense. Don't mix business money with personal money. After one month, review: What sold best? What time do customers buy? Adjust. The key is to start small, keep quality high, and grow slowly. Many fail because they buy expensive equipment before they have customers. You don't need a stainless steel tank on day one. You need customers..Trim()

Branding, Packaging Design, and Labeling for Retail

Your brand is what makes customers choose you over the next seller. Start with a simple name that is easy to remember and say. Avoid long names. 'Mama Ada Juice' is better than 'Ada's Freshly Squeezed Natural Fruit Juice Enterprise'. Create a logo: you can pay a local artist 5,000 Naira to draw a fruit with your name. Use bright colours: orange for orange juice, green for pineapple, red for watermelon. Your packaging must protect the juice and look clean. For small scale, use clear PET bottles with screw caps. By law in most African countries, your label must show: product name, ingredients (in descending order by weight), net volume (e.g., 500ml), your business name and address, batch number, production and expiry dates, and storage instructions (e.g., 'Keep refrigerated after opening'). Also include a phone number. Print labels at a local print shop: 1,000 labels cost about 15,000 Naira (15 Naira each). Design your label with a bold fruit image and your logo. Use a font that is easy to read from one metre away. Don't lie: if you add water, say 'juice drink' not '100% juice'. If you add preservative, list it. Customers trust honesty. For retail, you need a barcode if you want to sell in supermarkets. GS1 barcodes cost about 50,000 Naira per year, so start with direct sales to shops that don't require barcodes. Regulatory Compliance and Licensing for Juice Sales

Selling juice without following health rules can get you shut down, fined, or worse, make people sick. In most African countries, you need at least three things: business registration, health certificate, and food safety compliance. Start with business registration. In Nigeria, register with CAC as a sole proprietorship for about 10,000 Naira. In Kenya, register with eCitizen for 1,000 Ksh. In Ghana, register with the Registrar General's Department for 50 GHS. This gives you a legal name and tax ID. Next, get a food handler's certificate. Your local government health office or ministry of health issues this after you and your workers take a short course on hygiene. It costs 2,000-5,000 Naira and must be renewed yearly. They will inspect your kitchen: you need clean water, a handwashing station, a fridge, and separate areas for raw fruit and finished juice. You also need a laboratory test for your juice. Take a sample to a government lab or private lab (e.g., in Lagos, the Lagos State Environmental Protection Agency lab). They test for bacteria like E. coli and salmonella. A test costs 10,000-20,000 Naira. If your juice passes, you get a certificate. Then you need a product registration number from NAFDAC (Nigeria) or equivalent (FDA in Ghana, KEBS in Kenya). This can take 3-6 months and cost 50,000-100,000 Naira. Many small sellers start by selling locally without NAFDAC, but you cannot sell in supermarkets without it. Always label your juice with production and expiry dates. Keep records of every batch: date made, fruit source, preservative used. If a customer complains, you can trace the problem. Finally, pay your taxes. Even small businesses must pay a presumptive tax. In Nigeria, turnover under 25 million Naira pays 0.5% tax. Compliance may seem expensive, but it builds trust and opens bigger markets..Trim()
