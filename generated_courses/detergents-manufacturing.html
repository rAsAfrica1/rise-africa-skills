History and evolution of detergents

Before commercial detergents, Africans cleaned with locally available materials: palm kernel oil soap, ash from burnt cocoa pods or plantain peels, and sand for scouring pots. In West Africa, women made black soap from cocoa pod ash and palm oil, a tradition still alive today. These early soaps worked because ash contains potash (potassium carbonate), which reacts with oils to form soapâa process called saponification. But soap has a weakness: in hard water, it forms scum and loses cleaning power..Trim()

.Trim()

The first synthetic detergent, invented in Germany in 1916, used short-chain alkyl naphthalene sulfonates. By the 1940s, petrochemical-based detergents like alkyl benzene sulfonate (ABS) became popular because they performed well in hard water and didn't leave scum. However, ABS caused massive foam in rivers. In the 1960s, scientists replaced it with linear alkyl benzene sulfonate (LAS), which biodegrades faster. Today, LAS is the most common surfactant in laundry powders worldwide, including Africa..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, detergent evolution followed a different path. Multinationals like Unilever and P&G introduced brands such as Omo and Ariel in the 1960s and 70s. But local production grew in the 1980s and 90s, with small-scale manufacturers in Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa making powders from locally sourced soda ash, STPP, and LABSA. For example, in Kano, Nigeria, entrepreneurs blend LABSA with soda ash from Lake Magadi in Kenya to produce affordable sachet detergents..Trim()

.Trim()

Today, the trend is toward concentrated liquids, eco-friendly formulations, and sachet packaging for low-income markets. In Africa, sachets dominate because they cost as little as 50 naira or 10 Kenyan shillings. The evolution continues: from ash and palm oil to high-tech surfactants, but the goal remains the sameâclean clothes and dishes at a price people can afford..Trim()

Types of detergents: anionic, cationic, nonionic, amphoteric

Detergents are classified by the electrical charge of their head group. Anionic detergents have a negative charge. They are the workhorses for cleaning clothes and dishes because they lift dirt and grease effectively. Common examples: linear alkyl benzene sulfonate (LAS), sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), and sodium laureth sulfate (SLES). In Africa, LAS is the main surfactant in laundry powders; you'll find it as a viscous brown liquid called LABSA (linear alkyl benzene sulfonic acid) that you neutralize with caustic soda to make LAS. Anionics work best in alkaline water (pH 9-10) and are cheap..Trim()

.Trim()

Cationic detergents carry a positive charge. They are poor cleaners alone but excellent at killing bacteria and softening fabrics. Examples: benzalkonium chloride (in disinfectants) and ester quats (in fabric softeners). In Africa, cationic surfactants are used in Pine Gel and toilet cleaners. They also help condition hair in shampoos. Note: never mix cationics with anionicsâthey cancel each other out and form a scum..Trim()

.Trim()

Nonionic detergents have no charge. They are excellent at removing oily stains and work well in both hard and soft water. Examples: alcohol ethoxylates (like Neodol) and alkyl polyglucosides (APGs, which are plant-based). In Africa, nonionics are often blended with anionics in liquid dishwash to boost grease cutting. They also produce less foam, making them ideal for front-load washing machines and for handwashing in areas where rinsing water is scarce..Trim()

.Trim()

Amphoteric detergents have both positive and negative charges depending on pH. They are mild, good foam stabilizers, and biodegradable. Examples: cocamidopropyl betaine (from coconut oil) and coco amphoacetate. In Africa, amphoterics are used in baby shampoos, handwash, and in some traditional black soap formulations. They are expensive, so they are used in small amounts (2-5%) to reduce irritation. For a beginner, focus on anionics for cleaning, cationics for softening and disinfecting, nonionics for grease, and amphoterics for mildness..Trim()

Key raw materials overview

To make detergent, you need five main groups of raw materials: surfactants, builders, fillers, auxiliary agents, and water. Surfactants are the active cleaning agents. The most common in Africa is LABSA (linear alkyl benzene sulfonic acid), a dark brown liquid. You neutralize it with caustic soda (sodium hydroxide) or soda ash (sodium carbonate) to form LAS. Other surfactants include sodium lauryl ether sulfate (SLES) for liquids, and alcohol ethoxylates for nonionics. A typical laundry powder contains 15-25% LAS..Trim()

.Trim()

Builders soften water and boost cleaning. The key builder is sodium tripolyphosphate (STPP), but many countries restrict it due to eutrophication. In Africa, STPP is still widely used, often at 20-30%. Alternatives include zeolites (sodium aluminosilicate) and sodium citrate. You also need sodium carbonate (soda ash) to provide alkalinity, and sodium silicate to prevent corrosion and keep powder free-flowing. For every 100 kg of powder, use about 25 kg soda ash, 20 kg STPP, and 5 kg silicate..Trim()

.Trim()

Fillers add bulk and reduce cost. The most common is sodium sulfate (also called salt cake or Glauber's salt). In Africa, you can buy it cheaply from chemical suppliers in Lagos, Nairobi, or Durban. A typical formulation uses 30-40% sodium sulfate. For liquid detergents, water is the main fillerâuse soft or distilled water to avoid hardness ions. You also need preservatives (like formaldehyde or isothiazolinones) to prevent bacterial growth in liquids..Trim()

.Trim()

Auxiliary agents include enzymes (protease, amylase) to break down protein and starch stains, optical brighteners to make clothes look whiter, perfumes, and dyes. In Africa, enzymes are often omitted in low-cost powders because they are heat-sensitive and expensive. Instead, you might add 0.5-1% optical brightener and 0.2% perfume. Finally, you need packaging: sachets, bottles, or cartons. Source raw materials locally where possibleâsoda ash from Lake Magadi, caustic soda from local suppliers, and LABSA from importers in Mombasa or Lagos..Trim()

Environmental and regulatory landscape

Detergents can harm the environment if not formulated responsibly. The main issues: eutrophication from phosphates, toxicity from surfactants, and non-biodegradable ingredients. In Africa, many countries lack strict enforcement, but regulations are tightening. For example, Kenya's National Environment Management Authority (NEMA) requires that detergents be biodegradable. Nigeria's NAFDAC regulates chemicals and insists on labeling. South Africa has the most advanced rules, banning phosphates in laundry detergents since 2010..Trim()

.Trim()

Phosphates, mainly STPP, cause algae blooms in lakes and rivers. When algae die, bacteria consume oxygen, killing fish. In Lake Victoria, which borders Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania, phosphate pollution from detergents and fertilizers has caused hyacinth infestations. To avoid this, use zeolites or citrates as builders. Also, choose linear alkyl benzene sulfonate (LAS) over branched ABS because LAS biodegrades in 2-3 days, while ABS persists for weeks..Trim()

.Trim()

Surfactants can be toxic to aquatic life. Nonylphenol ethoxylates (NPEs) are particularly harmful, so the EU banned them. In Africa, NPEs are still used in some industrial cleaners, but you should avoid them. Use alcohol ethoxylates instead. Also, avoid chlorine bleach in detergents because it forms dioxins. For disinfecting, use hydrogen peroxide or quaternary ammonium compounds..Trim()

.Trim()

Regulations you must know: In Nigeria, NAFDAC requires product registration before sale. You need to submit formulation, safety data, and labeling. In Kenya, the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) sets standards for detergents, including pH and active matter content. In South Africa, the SABS (South African Bureau of Standards) certifies products. Even if not enforced, follow basic rules: label ingredients, include warnings, and provide a safety data sheet. Also, join a local manufacturers associationâthey can help you navigate regulations. Safety and handling basics

Detergent manufacturing involves hazardous chemicals. You must protect yourself and your workers. The biggest risks: caustic soda burns, LABSA burns, and dust inhalation. Always wear personal protective equipment (PPE): chemical splash goggles, rubber gloves (nitrile or neoprene), a PVC apron, and rubber boots. For dust, wear a dust mask (N95). .Trim()

First aid: If caustic soda or LABSA touches your skin, flush immediately with plenty of water for 15 minutes. Do not use vinegar or other neutralizersâthey generate heat. If it gets in your eyes, flush with water for 20 minutes and go to a clinic. If you inhale dust, move to fresh air. If you swallow, do not induce vomiting; drink water and seek medical help. Keep a first aid kit with eyewash bottles and burn gel..Trim()

Storage: Store caustic soda in a dry, cool place away from acids. LABSA is corrosive and should be kept in plastic drums (not metal) because it reacts with metals. Keep all chemicals out of reach of children. Label everything clearlyânever store chemicals in food containers. Use secondary containment (like a larger tray) to catch spills. Have a spill kit with absorbent material (sand or vermiculite) and a neutralizing agent (soda ash for acids, vinegar for bases)..Trim()

Ventilation: Mixing powders creates dust. Work in a well-ventilated area or use a fume hood. If you are making liquids, fumes from caustic soda can irritate lungs. Open windows and use a fan. Never mix bleach with acids or ammoniaâit releases toxic chlorine gas. Also, be careful with enzymes: they can cause allergies, so wear a mask. Finally, train your workers. Module 2 · Surfactants: Chemistry and Function

Structure and classification of surfactants

A surfactant molecule has two ends: a hydrophilic (water-loving) head and a hydrophobic (water-hating) tail. The tail is usually a hydrocarbon chain from oils or petrochemicals. When you mix a surfactant with water, the heads dive into the water while the tails stick out. This is why surfactants can lift grease and dirt from clothes or dishes..Trim()

.Trim()

Surfactants are classified by the charge on their head group. Anionic surfactants have a negative charge. They are the workhorses in laundry powders and dish liquids. Common examples are linear alkylbenzene sulfonate (LAS) and sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS). Cationic surfactants have a positive charge. They are used in fabric softeners and disinfectants. Nonionic surfactants have no charge. They are excellent at removing oily stains and are often mixed with anionics. Amphoteric surfactants have both positive and negative charges depending on pH. They are mild and used in shampoos and baby products..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, you can make anionic surfactants from locally available materials. For example, you can sulfonate benzene with sulfuric acid to make LAS. But this requires careful handling of strong acids. A safer start is to buy pre-made surfactant concentrates from chemical suppliers in Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg. Look for products labeled 'LABSA' (linear alkylbenzene sulfonic acid) or 'SLES' (sodium lauryl ether sulfate)..Trim()

.Trim()

To test the type of surfactant you have, do a simple foam test. Put 1 gram of surfactant in a clean 500 ml bottle, add 200 ml of water, and shake for 10 seconds. Anionic surfactants produce lots of foam that lasts. Nonionics produce less foam. Cationics produce little foam. This helps you decide which surfactant to use in your formula..Trim()

.Trim()

Always wear gloves and goggles when handling surfactants. They can irritate skin and eyes. Store them in labeled plastic containers away from sunlight. For a small-scale detergent business, start with anionic and nonionic surfactants. They cover most cleaning needs and are readily available in 25 kg drums or 200 kg barrels..Trim()

Micelle formation and critical micelle concentration

When you add a surfactant to water, the molecules first spread out individually. As you add more, they start to cluster into tiny balls called micelles. In a micelle, the hydrophobic tails point inward, away from water, and the hydrophilic heads point outward, into the water. This happens at a specific concentration called the critical micelle concentration (CMC)..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does CMC matter? Below the CMC, the surfactant does not clean well because there are not enough micelles to trap dirt. Above the CMC, cleaning power increases rapidly. For most anionic surfactants like LAS, the CMC is around 0.1 to 0.5 grams per liter (100-500 ppm) in pure water. But in hard water, which contains calcium and magnesium ions, the CMC can be higher because some surfactant is used up reacting with these ions..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, water hardness varies widely. In cities like Accra or Cairo, water may be very hard (over 300 mg/L as CaCO3). In rural areas, rainwater is soft. To find the right amount of surfactant, do a simple jar test. Fill a 1-liter jar with your local water. Add 0.5 grams of surfactant, shake, and observe foam. Keep adding 0.1 gram increments until you get a stable foam that lasts 5 minutes. That amount is close to the CMC for your conditions..Trim()

.Trim()

For a typical laundry detergent, you want the surfactant concentration in the wash water to be well above the CMC, often 1-2 grams per liter. That means if you use 50 liters of water per wash, you need 50-100 grams of surfactant. If your detergent powder is 15% surfactant, you need about 333-667 grams of powder per wash. Adjust based on soil level and water hardness..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, micelles also help solubilize oils. Once micelles form, they can trap oily dirt inside their hydrophobic cores and carry it away in the rinse water. So achieving CMC is essential for effective cleaning. Always test your detergent with local water and local dirt (e.g., red clay soil or palm oil stains) to find the best formulation..Trim()

Hydrophilic-lipophilic balance (HLB) concept

The hydrophilic-lipophilic balance (HLB) is a number from 0 to 20 that tells you whether a surfactant prefers water (hydrophilic) or oil (lipophilic). A low HLB (3-6) means the surfactant is oil-soluble and good for water-in-oil emulsions. A high HLB (8-18) means it is water-soluble and good for oil-in-water emulsions. For detergents, you generally want surfactants with HLB between 10 and 15..Trim()

.Trim()

Each surfactant has its own HLB value. For example, sodium lauryl sulfate (anionic) has an HLB of about 40, but it is often used at low levels. Nonionic surfactants like alcohol ethoxylates have HLB values that depend on the number of ethylene oxide (EO) units. A nonionic with 6 EO units might have HLB 12, while one with 10 EO units might have HLB 14. You can blend surfactants to get the right HLB for your cleaning task..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, you can calculate HLB using simple formulas. For a blend, HLB_mix = (HLB1 x weight1 + HLB2 x weight2) / (weight1 + weight2). Suppose you want an HLB of 12 for a dishwashing liquid. You have two nonionics: one with HLB 10 and one with HLB 14. Mix equal parts: (10+14)/2 = 12. That blend will work well..Trim()

.Trim()

But HLB is not the only factor. Temperature, water hardness, and the type of dirt also matter. For example, in hot climates, nonionic surfactants may become less soluble and cloud out. That is why many African detergent makers use a mix of anionic and nonionic surfactants. Anionics like LAS have good cleaning power but can be sensitive to hard water. Nonionics are more tolerant of hard water but may foam less..Trim()

.Trim()

To apply HLB, start with a simple recipe: 10% LAS, 5% nonionic (HLB 12), 2% sodium carbonate, and water to 100%. Test it on oily plates and greasy clothes. If it does not clean well, adjust the nonionic HLB by blending different ethoxylates. Keep records of your trials. Over time, you will develop a feel for what works in your local market..Trim()

Anionic surfactants: LAS, AES, AS

Anionic surfactants are the most widely used in detergents because they are excellent at removing dirt and are relatively cheap. The three main types are LAS (linear alkylbenzene sulfonate), AES (alcohol ether sulfate), and AS (alcohol sulfate)..Trim()

.Trim()

LAS is the backbone of many laundry powders. It is made by sulfonating linear alkylbenzene with sulfur trioxide or oleum, then neutralizing with sodium hydroxide. In Africa, you can buy LAS as a powder (sodium salt) or as a liquid (LABSA, which is the acid form). LABSA is 90-96% active and must be neutralized carefully. To neutralize, slowly add LABSA to a 20% sodium hydroxide solution while stirring. Keep the temperature below 40Â°C to avoid darkening. The result is a thick paste or liquid with about 30% active LAS. Use 5-15% LAS in your detergent formula..Trim()

.Trim()

AES, also known as SLES (sodium lauryl ether sulfate), is milder than LAS and produces richer foam. It is common in dishwashing liquids and shampoos. AES is made by ethoxylating lauryl alcohol (from coconut or palm kernel oil) and then sulfating. In Africa, you can buy SLES as a 70% paste. To use, dilute it with water to 28-30% active and add preservative. A typical dish liquid has 10-20% SLES..Trim()

.Trim()

AS, or sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), is a simple sulfate with no ethoxylation. It is a good foamer but can be harsh on skin. It is often used in toothpaste and industrial cleaners. You can make SLS by sulfating lauryl alcohol with chlorosulfonic acid, but this is dangerous. It is safer to buy SLS powder or needles (90% active) and dissolve it in water..Trim()

.Trim()

When using anionic surfactants, avoid mixing with cationic surfactants because they will neutralize each other and form a precipitate. Also, anionic surfactants are sensitive to hard water. Add a builder like sodium carbonate or zeolite to soften the water. In Africa, many small-scale producers use locally available soda ash (sodium carbonate) from Lake Magadi in Kenya or Lake Natron in Tanzania. A simple laundry powder formula: 15% LAS, 10% sodium carbonate, 50% sodium sulfate, 5% sodium silicate, and 20% water (spray dried or mixed). Test with local dirt and water..Trim()

Nonionic surfactants: alcohol ethoxylates, alkylphenol ethoxylates

Nonionic surfactants have no charge. They are excellent at removing oily and greasy dirt, and they work well in hard water. The two main types are alcohol ethoxylates (AEs) and alkylphenol ethoxylates (APEs)..Trim()

.Trim()

Alcohol ethoxylates are made by reacting fatty alcohols (from coconut or palm oil) with ethylene oxide. The number of ethylene oxide (EO) units determines the properties. A typical laundry surfactant has 7-9 EO units. It is a liquid or paste at room temperature. In Africa, you can buy AEs under trade names like Neodol or Lutensol. For a dishwashing liquid, use 5-10% of an AE with 7 EO. For laundry, use 3-5% of an AE with 9 EO. AEs are biodegradable and generally considered safe..Trim()

.Trim()

Alkylphenol ethoxylates (APEs), such as nonylphenol ethoxylates (NPEs), were once very common but are now restricted in many countries because they break down into toxic compounds. In Africa, some markets still sell them, but it is better to avoid them. If you must use them, handle with care and follow local regulations. A safer alternative is to use AEs or fatty acid ethoxylates..Trim()

.Trim()

To use nonionic surfactants, you often need to blend them with anionics. For example, a heavy-duty laundry liquid: 10% LAS, 5% AE (7 EO), 2% sodium carbonate, 1% sodium chloride (to adjust viscosity), and water to 100%. Mix LAS and AE first, then add water slowly. Nonionics can be thick, so warm them to 40Â°C to make pouring easier. Do not overheat above 60Â°C as they can degrade..Trim()

.Trim()

Nonionics are also good for making emulsifiable concentrates, like a degreaser for engines. Mix 20% AE, 10% butyl glycol, and 70% water. Spray on greasy engine parts, wait 5 minutes, and rinse. In Africa, this can be a profitable product for auto mechanics..Trim()

.Trim()

One challenge with nonionics is cloud point. As temperature rises, they become insoluble and the solution turns cloudy. The cloud point for an AE with 7 EO is about 50-60Â°C. In hot African climates, this can be a problem. To raise the cloud point, blend with an anionic surfactant or add a hydrotrope like sodium xylene sulfonate. Always store nonionics in a cool, shaded place..Trim()

Biodegradability and ecotoxicity

When your detergent goes down the drain, it enters rivers, lakes, and groundwater. If it doesn't break down, it can harm fish, plants, and people. This lesson explains biodegradability and ecotoxicity in simple terms, and how to make safer products..Trim()

.Trim()

Biodegradability is the ability of a substance to be broken down by microorganisms (bacteria, fungi) into simple, harmless compounds like water, carbon dioxide, and minerals. .Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Life cycle assessment

Life cycle assessment (LCA) is a tool to measure the environmental impact of your detergent from cradle to grave. It helps you identify where you can make the biggest improvements. This lesson explains LCA in simple terms and how to do a basic version for your business..Trim()

.Trim()

LCA looks at all stages: raw material extraction, production, distribution, use, and disposal. For each stage, you measure inputs (energy, water, materials) and outputs (emissions, waste). The goal is to find hotspotsâthe stages with the highest impact. For example, for a liquid detergent, the raw materials (surfactants, packaging) often contribute 60-70% of the carbon footprint. For a powder detergent, the production process (spray drying) may use more energy..Trim()

.Trim()

To do a simplified LCA, follow these steps:.Trim()

.Trim()

1. Define your goal and scope. What product are you assessing? What functional unit? For example, "one wash load" or "1 kg of detergent." Choose a functional unit that makes comparison fair..Trim()

.Trim()

2. Inventory: List all inputs and outputs for each stage. For raw materials, get data from your suppliers (e.g., kg CO2 per kg of surfactant). For energy, note electricity and fuel use. For transport, calculate distance and mode. For disposal, consider how your packaging is treated (landfill, recycle, litter)..Trim()

.Trim()

3. Impact assessment: Use simple metrics like carbon footprint (kg CO2e), water use (litres), and waste (kg). You can use free tools like the OpenLCA software or the GHG Protocol. For a small business, a spreadsheet is enough. Multiply quantities by emission factors. For example, 1 kWh of grid electricity in Kenya emits about 0.1 kg CO2e (because of hydro and geothermal). In Nigeria, it's about 0.4 kg CO2e (gas)..Trim()

.Trim()

4. Interpret results: Identify the biggest contributors. If packaging is a hotspot, switch to recycled or lightweight packaging. If transport is high, source locally. If the use phase (heating water) is high, formulate for cold-water washing..Trim()

.Trim()

5. Improve and communicate: Make changes and re-assess. You can use your LCA results to make credible claims like "30% lower carbon footprint than conventional detergent." But be transparent about your assumptions..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, data can be scarce. Use regional averages from databases like Ecoinvent or the African LCA Network. You can also do a streamlined LCA focusing on carbon and water. Remember, LCA is iterative. Start simple, then refine. By understanding your product's life cycle, you can make better decisions for your business and the planet..Trim()
