Module 1 · Introduction to Preservation Methods

Overview of the 10 Preservation Methods

Preservation is about keeping a building's historic character while making it useful today. In Africa, we have old buildings made of stone, mud, timber, and coral that need care. This course covers 10 methods: 1) Documentation, 2) Cleaning, 3) Structural Stabilization, 4) Repair of Materials, 5) Replacement in Kind, 6) Adaptive Reuse, 7) Protective Coatings, 8) Environmental Control, 9) Fire Protection, and 10) Community Engagement. Each method has its place. For example, in Lamu, Kenya, coral stone walls are cleaned with soft brushes and water, not harsh chemicals. Documentation means taking photos, measurements, and notes before any work. Cleaning removes dirt without damaging surfaces. Structural stabilization uses props, grouts, or new foundations to stop collapse. Repair fixes cracks and rotten wood. Replacement uses same materials, like matching mud bricks. Adaptive reuse gives old buildings new functions, like turning a warehouse into a market. Protective coatings shield against rain and sun. Environmental control manages humidity and pests. Fire protection adds alarms and extinguishers. Community engagement involves local people in decisions. These methods are not expensive; many use local skills and materials. Start by assessing what you have. A simple checklist: What is the building made of? What is damaged? What can be saved? Then choose methods. For a mud mosque in Mali, you might use repair and protective coatings. For a colonial-era school in Ghana, adaptive reuse and fire protection. The goal is minimum intervention: do only what is needed. This module will teach you each method step by step. You will learn to think like a preservationist: respect the old, use local resources, and plan for the future..Trim()

Historical Context of Building Preservation

Building preservation in Africa has deep roots. Before colonialism, communities maintained structures like the Great Zimbabwe (11th-15th century) using dry stone techniques. They repaired walls after rains and kept granaries waterproof. In the 19th century, European colonizers built churches, forts, and administrative buildings using imported materials. After independence, many African countries began to value their own heritage. For example, in 1964, the Venice Charter set international standards, but Africa adapted them. The 1972 UNESCO World Heritage Convention helped protect sites like Robben Island in South Africa and the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela in Ethiopia. In the 1980s, countries like Nigeria and Kenya passed laws to protect old buildings. But preservation is not just about monuments. It is about everyday places: markets, homes, and schools. In the 1990s, the concept of 'living heritage' emerged, recognizing that buildings are used by people. For instance, the old town of Mombasa, Kenya, has Swahili houses that are still homes. Preservation there involves repairing coral rag walls and mangrove pole ceilings. In West Africa, the Sudano-Sahelian mud architecture of Djenné, Mali, is re-plastered each year by the community in a festival called Crepissage. This shows that preservation can be a social event. Today, African preservation faces challenges: rapid urbanization, lack of funds, and climate change. But there are successes. The Kigali Genocide Memorial in Rwanda preserves history while serving as a place of remembrance. The Bagamoyo Old Fort in Tanzania was restored using local labor and materials. Learning from history helps us avoid mistakes. Early preservation often used cement, which traps moisture and damages old walls. Now we know better: use lime, mud, and other breathable materials. Understanding this context helps you appreciate why we preserve and how to do it right..Trim()

Key Principles: Minimum Intervention and Reversibility

Two core principles guide all preservation work: minimum intervention and reversibility. Minimum intervention means do as little as possible to keep the building stable and useful. Do not replace a whole wall if only a few stones are loose. Do not repaint a historic surface if a gentle cleaning will do. For example, in the stone town of Zanzibar, Tanzania, preservationists clean coral stone with soft brushes and water, not sandblasting. Sandblasting removes the protective patina and causes erosion. Minimum intervention saves money and preserves original fabric. Reversibility means any change you make should be undoable without damaging the historic building. If you add a new roof, use bolts, not permanent adhesives. If you install a new floor, lay it on a removable subfloor. In practice, this means avoiding cement, epoxy, and other irreversible materials. Instead, use lime mortar, which is softer than historic stone and can be removed later. For a mud brick building in Burkina Faso, you might add a protective lime render that can be brushed off if needed. Reversibility also applies to repairs. If you replace a rotten timber beam, use a new beam that can be removed and replaced again. Document everything: take photos, keep samples, and write notes. This helps future caretakers. In Africa, where resources are limited, these principles are practical. They reduce waste and allow for future improvements. For instance, at the Ruins of Kilwa Kisiwani in Tanzania, stabilization work used stainless steel pins that can be removed if better methods are found. Remember: you are a temporary guardian. The building will outlive you. So make choices that respect the past and keep options open for the future. Always ask: Can this be undone? If not, reconsider..Trim()

Assessing Building Significance and Value

Before you preserve, you must assess what makes a building significant. Significance is the value people place on a building. It can be historical, architectural, social, spiritual, or economic. In Africa, significance often includes community memory. For example, a simple church in Ethiopia might be significant because of its ancient manuscripts and pilgrimage tradition. A market hall in Senegal might be significant for its role in local trade. To assess significance, start with research. Talk to elders, look at old photos, and check government records. Use a simple form: What is the building's age? Who built it? What events happened there? What materials are original? Then rate each value: high, medium, or low. For instance, the Great Mosque of Djenné is high for spiritual and architectural value. A colonial-era post office might be medium for historical value but high for adaptive reuse potential. Next, assess condition. Walk around with a notebook and camera. Note cracks, damp, pests, and structural issues. Use a scale: good, fair, poor, critical. For a mud building, check for erosion at the base. For a stone building, check for missing mortar. For timber, check for termites. Combine significance and condition to decide what to preserve. If a building is highly significant but in poor condition, you might stabilize it first. If it is low significance, you might document it and let it go. Always involve the community. In Kenya, the Maasai people assess their manyattas based on cultural use. A building used for ceremonies is more significant than a storage shed. Document your assessment in a report. Include photos, sketches, and notes. This becomes your guide. Without assessment, you might waste money on unimportant features or destroy what matters. Remember: significance is not fixed; it can change over time. A building once seen as ordinary might become valued later. So be thorough and respectful..Trim()

Role of Preservation in Sustainability

Preservation is one of the greenest ways to build. When you keep an old building, you save the energy and materials already invested in it. This is called embodied energy. A new concrete building uses huge amounts of energy to make cement, transport materials, and construct. An old mud or stone building already has that energy locked in. In Africa, where many people lack decent housing, preserving existing buildings is both sustainable and affordable. For example, in Ghana, a project in Accra restored an old warehouse into a community center using local labor and recycled materials. They saved 60% of the cost compared to new construction. Preservation also reduces waste. Demolition sends tons of rubble to landfills. In Nairobi, Kenya, a study found that renovating an old office building produced 80% less waste than building new. Old buildings are often designed for local climate. Thick mud walls keep interiors cool in hot Sahel. High ceilings and verandas in colonial buildings allow air circulation. By preserving these features, you reduce the need for air conditioning. Preservation also supports local economies. Skilled craftsmen like masons, carpenters, and thatchers get work. In Morocco, restoring riads in Marrakech employs hundreds of artisans. Materials like lime, earth, and stone are locally available, reducing transport emissions. Furthermore, preservation protects cultural identity. A community that keeps its old buildings maintains a sense of place. This attracts tourism, which can fund further preservation. For instance, the rock churches of Lalibela, Ethiopia, generate income for the local community. But sustainability also means planning for climate change. Old buildings may need adaptation: stronger roofs for heavier rain, raised foundations for floods. Use reversible methods. For example, in Mozambique, coastal forts are being protected with new drainage systems that can be removed if needed. In summary, preservation is not just about the past; it is a smart, sustainable strategy for the future. It saves money, energy, and culture. As you work, think: how can this building serve the community for another 100 years?.Trim()

Case Study: Successful Preservation Projects

Let us look at three successful preservation projects in Africa. First, the Old Fort of Bagamoyo in Tanzania. Built in the 18th century as a slave trade post, it later became a prison and then a ruin. In 2000, the Tanzanian government and UNESCO began restoration. They used local coral stone and lime mortar, matching original materials. Workers were trained in traditional masonry. The project cost about $200,000 and took three years. Today, the fort is a museum and cultural center, attracting 10,000 visitors a year. It created jobs for 50 local people. Second, the Kigali Genocide Memorial in Rwanda. After the 1994 genocide, mass graves were found. Instead of building a new monument, architects preserved the existing buildings and added a memorial garden. They used local stone and brick. The site now includes a museum, education center, and burial grounds. It is a place of remembrance and learning, visited by over 100,000 people annually. The project emphasized community involvement and minimal intervention. Third, the restoration of the Great Mosque of Djenné in Mali. This mud-brick mosque is the largest in the world. Every year, before the rainy season, the community re-plasters it with mud in a festival called Crepissage. In 2005, a major restoration replaced damaged timbers and reinforced walls. They used traditional methods: mud mixed with rice husks and shea butter. The project cost $1 million, funded by the Aga Khan Trust for Culture. It trained 100 local masons. The mosque remains a center of faith and tourism. These cases show common keys to success: use local materials, involve the community, respect original design, and plan for ongoing maintenance. They also show that preservation can be affordable and impactful. For your own projects, start small. Document before you touch. Test methods on a small area. Learn from these examples. Whether it is a village church or a town hall, you can preserve it for future generations..Trim()

Module 2 · Method 1: Preventive Conservation

Understanding Deterioration Agents

Preventive conservation means stopping damage before it starts. To do that, you must know the ten agents that destroy buildings: fire, water, pests, pollutants, light, incorrect temperature, incorrect relative humidity, physical forces, thieves and vandals, and neglect. In Africa, water and pests cause the most damage. Water comes from rain, leaking roofs, rising damp, and burst pipes. Pests include termites, borers, rodents, and birds. Termites alone can destroy a wooden roof structure in two to three years if untreated. .Trim()

.Trim()

Temperature and humidity work together. In hot, humid coastal cities like Mombasa or Lagos, relative humidity often stays above 80%. That encourages mould, rust, and wood rot. In dry areas like Timbuktu, low humidity cracks mud brick and timber. Daily swings between hot days and cool nights cause materials to expand and contract, leading to cracks. .Trim()

.Trim()

Light, especially ultraviolet (UV) from the sun, fades paint, dyes, and textiles. In Africa, direct sun is intense. A south-facing window in Nairobi can receive over 6,000 hours of bright light per year. Physical forces include earthquakes, floods, and poor maintenance. Thieves steal copper roofing, brass fittings, and even carved doors. Neglect is the slowest but deadliest agent: a blocked gutter or a broken window leads to bigger problems. .Trim()

.Trim()

To fight these agents, you need a plan. Start by walking around the building once a week. Look for damp patches, cracked walls, insect frass (powder), rodent droppings, and broken locks. Write down what you see in a notebook with dates. That log becomes your early warning system. Without it, you react too late. With it, you can act before small faults become expensive disasters..Trim()

Environmental Monitoring: Temperature and Humidity

Temperature and relative humidity (RH) are the two most important things to measure. RH is the amount of moisture in the air compared to the maximum it can hold at that temperature. For historic buildings in Africa, aim for a stable RH between 45% and 60%. Avoid rapid changes of more than 5% in an hour. High RH above 65% invites mould and rust. Low RH below 40% dries out wood and leather, causing cracks. .Trim()

.Trim()

You do not need expensive equipment. A simple dial hygrometer costs about $10 and gives a rough reading. A digital thermo-hygrometer with a memory function costs $30�??$50 and is better because it records highs and lows. Place one in each main room, away from direct sun, doors, and vents. Read it at the same time every day, morning and afternoon. Write the numbers in a logbook. After one month, you will see patterns: for example, RH rises after rain and falls in the afternoon. .Trim()

.Trim()

In hot, dry regions like the Sahel, you may need to add moisture. A shallow tray of water near a wooden artifact can raise local RH by 5�??10%. In humid coastal areas, you need to remove moisture. Use a dehumidifier (a small one uses about 300 watts) or improve ventilation with fans and open windows during dry hours. Never seal a building completely; air must move to prevent mould. .Trim()

.Trim()

If you cannot afford a dehumidifier, use calcium chloride desiccant bags. One kilogram absorbs about 1 litre of water. Hang them in corners and replace when they turn to liquid. Also, fix leaks immediately. A single drip can raise RH in a room by 10% and rot a wooden floor in one season. Monitor, record, and act. That is preventive conservation..Trim()

Light and UV Control Strategies

Light damages buildings in two ways: visible light fades colours, and ultraviolet (UV) radiation breaks down materials like wood, textiles, paper, and paint. In Africa, sunlight is strong. Pest Management in Historic Buildings

Pests destroy historic buildings faster than almost anything else. In Africa, the worst are termites, powder-post beetles, rodents, birds, and bats. Termites eat wood from the inside out. You often see only a thin skin left. Powder-post beetles leave fine sawdust (frass) under wooden beams. Rodents gnaw wires and leave urine that corrodes metal. Birds and bats drop acidic droppings that stain stone and spread disease. .Trim()

.Trim()

Start with prevention. Remove food and water sources. Fix leaking taps. Store grains and seeds in sealed metal bins. Clear vegetation at least 1 metre away from walls. Trim tree branches that touch the roof. Seal cracks in walls and foundations with mortar or expanding foam. Install wire mesh over vents and chimneys. Keep gutters clean. .Trim()

.Trim()

Inspect monthly. Use a torch and a screwdriver. Tap wooden beams: a hollow sound means termites. Look for mud tubes on walls (termites) and small holes in wood (beetles). For rodents, look for droppings, gnaw marks, and grease trails along walls. For birds, look for nests under eaves. .Trim()

.Trim()

If you find pests, act fast. For termites, use a borate-based treatment (e.g., Tim-bor) injected into wood. For beetles, brush or spray the wood with a borate solution. For rodents, use snap traps, not poison, because poison can kill owls and other helpful animals. For birds, install bird spikes or netting. For bats, call a wildlife expert; bats are protected in many countries. Always wear gloves and a mask when cleaning droppings. .Trim()

.Trim()

Never use strong pesticides inside a historic building unless a professional applies them. They can stain surfaces and harm people. Integrated pest management (IPM) means using the least toxic method first: cleaning, sealing, trapping, then chemicals as a last resort..Trim()

Housekeeping and Maintenance Schedules

Good housekeeping is the cheapest preventive conservation. It means cleaning regularly, fixing small faults quickly, and keeping records. In Africa, dust, sand, and insects are constant. A weekly routine prevents bigger problems. .Trim()

Make a schedule. Every day: sweep floors, empty bins, check for leaks after rain. Every week: dust surfaces with a soft cloth, check for pest signs, test smoke detectors. Every month: clean gutters, check roof tiles, inspect window seals, test locks. Every three months: clean drains, check ventilation, inspect wooden elements for rot. Every year: repaint exterior wood, service dehumidifiers, review your logbook. .Trim()

Implementing a Preventive Conservation Plan

A preventive conservation plan is a written document that says what you will do, who will do it, and when. It turns good intentions into action. Start small. You do not need a 100-page report. A 10-page plan with photos and a calendar is enough. .Trim()

.Trim()

Step 1: Survey the building. Walk around with a notebook and camera. List every problem: leaking roof, cracked wall, termite damage, faded paint, broken lock. Note the location and severity (low, medium, high). Take photos. .Trim()

.Trim()

Step 2: Set priorities. Fix high-severity problems first. For example, a leaking roof is high because water destroys everything below. A faded sign is low. Use a simple table: Problem, Location, Severity, Action, Who, When, Cost. .Trim()

.Trim()

Step 3: Assign tasks. Who will check the hygrometer every morning? Who will clean gutters? Who will call the pest control officer? Write names, not titles. If you are a small team, share tasks. .Trim()

.Trim()

Step 4: Set a budget. Preventive conservation is cheap. A year of monitoring might cost $200 for a hygrometer, logbook, and basic tools. Repairs cost more, but prevention reduces repairs. Ask for donations from local businesses or heritage trusts. .Trim()

.Trim()

Step 5: Train and monitor. Hold a one-hour training session every three months. Review the logbook monthly. Adjust the plan when you find new problems. .Trim()

.Trim()

Step 6: Document and share. Keep your plan in a binder. Share copies with staff and volunteers. When you apply for grants, your plan shows you are serious. .Trim()

Diagnosing Common Building Defects

Before you repair anything, you must know what is wrong and why. Start by walking around the building with a notebook, a torch, a small mirror, and a camera or phone. Look for cracks, damp patches, crumbling mortar, rotten timber, and loose roof sheets. Masonry Repair: Mortars and Pointing

Structural Consolidation: Foundations and Walls

Consolidation means making a building stronger without changing its look. Start by checking the foundation. In many African towns, old buildings sit on stone or brick footings that have been weakened by water. Dig a test pit next to the wall, about 1 metre deep, to see what's there. If the footing is loose, you can inject a cement grout mix (1 part cement to 3 parts sand, with water to make it flow like thick cream) into the gaps. Use a manual grout pump or a large syringe for small cracks. For walls, look for bulges or leaning. A simple plumb bob on a string tells you if a wall is straight. If a wall leans more than 1 cm per metre of height, you need to act. One method is to add a reinforced concrete ring beam at the top of the wall. Drill holes 15 cm deep into the wall every 50 cm, insert 12 mm steel bars, and tie them with wire. Then build a wooden formwork and pour concrete (1:2:4 mix). This ties the walls together. Another method is to stitch cracks with stainless steel staples. Cut a groove across the crack, 30 cm long and 3 cm deep, every 40 cm along the crack. Fill with epoxy mortar and press in a U-shaped stainless steel bar. This stops the crack from opening further. Always work from the bottom up and support the wall with temporary props (wooden posts) before you start. In Africa, use local materials: timber props from eucalyptus, sand from a clean river, and cement that is less than 3 months old. Never work in the rain. Keep the work dry for at least 7 days after grouting..Trim()

Grouting and Injection Techniques

Grouting fills empty spaces inside walls and foundations to make them solid. You need a grout mix that flows easily but doesn't shrink. A common mix is 1 part cement, 2 parts fine sand, and 0.5 part water, plus a plasticiser (like a small amount of washing-up liquid, about 1 teaspoon per 10 litres) to make it slippery. For very fine cracks (less than 2 mm), use a thin epoxy resin instead. First, clean the crack with a wire brush and compressed air (or a bicycle pump with a nozzle). Then seal the surface with a quick-setting mortar, leaving small plastic tubes (injection ports) sticking out every 30-50 cm along the crack. Let the seal dry for 24 hours. Then inject the grout using a manual grease gun or a hand pump. Start at the lowest port and pump until grout comes out of the next port up. Then cap that port and move up. Keep pressure low (about 0.2 to 0.5 bar) to avoid blowing out the wall. For foundations, drill holes 45 degrees into the footing and inject a thicker mix (1:1 cement-sand). After injection, leave the ports in place for 3 days, then cut them off flush. In hot African climates, mix small batches (5 litres at a time) because grout sets fast. Test the mix on a spare brick first. If it cracks when dry, add more sand. If it's too stiff, add a little water. Always wear gloves and goggles. Grouting is messy but cheap and effective for stabilising old masonry..Trim()

Reinforcement with Modern Materials

Modern materials can strengthen old buildings without heavy concrete. One popular method is to use fibre-reinforced polymer (FRP) strips. These are thin, strong bands made of carbon or glass fibres in a plastic resin. They are glued to the surface of walls or beams with epoxy. For a cracked wall, clean the surface, apply a two-part epoxy (mix equal parts from two tubes), and press on the FRP strip. Overlap strips by 10 cm. FRP is expensive, but you need very little. A 10 cm wide strip costs about $5 per metre in many African cities. Another option is to use stainless steel mesh and render. Fix a 1 mm thick stainless steel mesh to the wall with nails every 20 cm, then apply a 2 cm thick render of 1:3 cement-sand mortar. This adds strength and stops cracks from spreading. For timber beams that are rotting, you can splice in new timber with steel plates and bolts. Use 6 mm steel plates and 10 mm bolts, spaced 15 cm apart. For columns, wrap them with a steel jacket: a 3 mm thick steel plate bent around the column, welded at the seam, and filled with non-shrink grout. This is common for historic concrete columns in Africa. Always check that the new material doesn't trap moisture. Use breathable paints and leave small drainage holes. In coastal areas, use stainless steel or galvanised fixings to avoid rust. Modern materials are quick to install but require trained labour. Start with a small test area and monitor for 3 months before doing the whole building..Trim()

Monitoring Movement and Cracks

You can't fix a problem if you don't know how fast it's getting worse. Monitoring is cheap and simple. For cracks, buy a crack monitor: a small plastic gauge with two overlapping scales. Stick one half on each side of the crack with epoxy. Read the movement every week. If the crack opens more than 1 mm per month, it's active and you must act. For wall movement, use a plumb bob. Hang a string with a weight from the top of the wall. Mark the position on the ground. Check it every month. A lean that increases by 5 mm per month is serious. For foundation settlement, use a level and a staff. Mark a point on the wall and measure its height above a fixed benchmark (a concrete block set in the ground away from the building). Record the height every two weeks. If it drops more than 2 mm per month, the foundation is moving. In Africa, you can also use a smartphone app like 'Crack Monitor' to take photos and compare. For large buildings, set up 3-4 tell-tales: glass slides glued across cracks. If the glass breaks, the crack has moved. Keep a logbook with dates, measurements, and weather. Rain and temperature changes can cause daily movement of up to 0.5 mm, so measure at the same time of day. After consolidation work, monitor for at least 6 months. If movement stops, you can reduce checks to once every 3 months. Share your data with a local engineer if you can. Monitoring costs almost nothing but saves buildings..Trim()

Consolidation of Historic Concrete

Historic concrete, common in African buildings from the 1920s to 1960s, is often weaker than modern concrete. It may have been mixed with sea sand, which causes salt damage, or it may be carbonated (lost its protective alkalinity). First, test the concrete. Tap it with a hammer: a dull thud means it's hollow or delaminated. Use a phenolphthalein solution (from a pharmacy) to test carbonation: spray it on a fresh break; if it stays clear, the concrete is carbonated. To consolidate, remove loose concrete with a chipping hammer and wire brush. Then apply a repair mortar. For historic concrete, use a mortar that is weaker than the original: 1 part cement, 3 parts sand, and 10% lime by volume. This is softer and won't crack. For carbonated concrete, apply a breathable anti-carbonation coating (like a silane-siloxane paint) after repairs. For corroded steel reinforcement, chip out around the bar, clean it to bare metal with a wire brush, and paint it with a zinc-rich primer. Then patch with the repair mortar. For large areas, use a sprayed concrete (shotcrete) with a small hand-held gun. Mix 1:3:0.5 cement, sand, water. Apply in 2 cm layers, letting each layer set for 2 hours. In hot climates, keep the repair moist for 7 days by covering with wet burlap. Never use modern high-strength concrete on historic concrete; it will crack the old material. Always match the strength and breathability. If in doubt, do a small test patch and check after 6 months..Trim()

Case Study: Stabilizing a Leaning Tower

In a coastal town in Ghana, a 120-year-old church tower leaned 30 cm out of plumb at the top. The tower was 12 m tall, built of lime mortar and local stone. The foundation was on sandy soil that had been eroded by rainwater. First, we monitored for 3 months: the lean increased by 2 mm per month. We dug a trench around the base, 1.5 m deep, and found the footing was only 60 cm wide. We consolidated the foundation by injecting a cement-bentonite grout (1 part cement, 1 part bentonite, 4 parts water) into the sand under the footing. We used a manual pump and injected at 1 bar pressure through 1.5 m long pipes driven at 45 degrees. After grouting, we added a reinforced concrete collar around the base: 30 cm wide, 40 cm deep, with 12 mm bars. Then we addressed the lean. We used a technique called 'underpinning with jacks'. We dug small tunnels under the high side of the foundation and placed 10-tonne hydraulic jacks. Every week, we jacked up the high side by 2 mm and inserted stainless steel shims. After 6 months, the tower was vertical within 1 cm. Finally, we repointed the mortar joints with a lime mortar (1:3 lime-sand) and installed a drainage channel around the tower to divert rainwater. Total cost: about $8,000, mostly labour. The tower has been stable for 5 years. Key lessons: monitor first, fix the foundation before the walls, go slowly (2 mm per week), and use local materials. This method can be used on any leaning building in Africa, but always consult an engineer for towers over 10 m..Trim()
