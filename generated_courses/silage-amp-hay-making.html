Land Preparation with Hand Hoes and Oxen

Before you plant any fodder crop, you must prepare the land well. Start by clearing the field of bushes, stones, and old crop residues. Use a machete or slasher to cut tall weeds. For small plots, a hand hoe (jembe) is enough. For larger areas, oxen pulling a plough will save you many days of work. In many parts of Africa, a pair of oxen can plough one acre in about 4 to 6 hours, while a person with a hand hoe may take 10 to 15 days for the same area..Trim()

.Trim()

First, slash the vegetation and let it dry for a week. Then, if you have oxen, plough the field to a depth of 15-20 cm. This loosens the soil and buryes weeds. After the first ploughing, wait for two weeks to let weeds regrow, then plough again. This is called double ploughing and it reduces weeds without chemicals. If you only have hand hoes, dig the soil and turn it over, breaking large clods. Remove roots of perennial weeds like couch grass because they will regrow..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, level the field. Use a rake or a wooden board dragged by oxen to create a smooth surface. This helps with even water distribution and seed germination. If the land slopes, make contour ridges or cut-off drains to prevent soil erosion. For fodder grasses like Napier or Brachiaria, you can plant on flat land or on terraces. For legumes like lablab or cowpea, a fine seedbed is better..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, incorporate well-rotted manure or compost during land preparation. Spread 2 to 3 tonnes per acre (about 20 to 30 wheelbarrows) and mix it into the top 10 cm of soil. This adds nutrients and improves soil structure. Do this at least two weeks before planting so the manure can decompose further. With good land preparation, your fodder crops will establish faster and give higher yields..Trim()

Good seeds are the foundation of a healthy fodder crop. Always buy certified seeds from a reputable agro-dealer or research station. Seed treatment also includes coating with rhizobium bacteria for legumes. Rhizobium helps the plant fix nitrogen from the air. You can buy rhizobium inoculant from agro-dealers. Fodder crops need nutrients to grow fast and produce high yields. The cheapest and safest source is manure and compost. Fresh manure can burn plants, so always use well-rotted manure. Irrigation Methods for Dry Season Fodder Production

In the dry season, fodder crops need water to keep growing. Without irrigation, grasses dry up and legumes die. You can use simple, low-cost methods. The cheapest is manual watering with buckets or watering cans. For a small plot (0.1 acre), you need about 200-300 litres per day. Water early morning or late evening to reduce evaporation. Determining the Best Harvest Stage for Hay Crops

For hay, the best time to cut is when the crop has the most feed value and will dry quickly. Most grasses like Rhodes, Napier, or Brachiaria should be cut at the early heading stage, just before the seed heads fully emerge. At this stage, protein is high, fiber is digestible, and the stems are not too thick. If you wait until seeds are mature, the hay becomes stemmy and low in protein. For legumes like lucerne or desmodium, cut when 10% of the plants are flowering. This balances yield and quality. Cutting too early gives a very watery crop that is hard to dry. Cutting too late gives tough, woody stems that animals refuse to eat..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, timing also depends on the rains. You want at least 3-5 dry days after cutting. If you cut when rain is coming, the hay may mold. In the highlands of Kenya or Ethiopia, cut early in the morning after the dew has dried, around 9-10 am. In hotter areas like Tanzania or Zimbabwe, cut in the late afternoon to avoid excessive moisture loss. Use a sharp machete or sickle. For small plots, cut at 5-10 cm above the ground to allow regrowth. For Napier grass, leave 10-15 cm stubble. This helps the plant recover quickly..Trim()

.Trim()

To test if the crop is ready, grab a handful and twist it. If it snaps easily and feels dry but still green, it's ready to cut. If it bends without breaking, it's too wet. If it crumbles, it's too dry. Aim for 60-65% moisture at cutting. After cutting, spread the grass in a thin layer in the sun to dry. Turn it every 2-3 hours for the first day. In good sun, it can dry to 15-20% moisture in 2-3 days. Then bale or store in a dry shed. Remember, hay made at the right stage will smell sweet and be leafy. Poor timing gives dusty, moldy hay that animals may reject. So plan your cutting around the weather and the plant's growth stage..Trim()

Determining the Best Harvest Stage for Silage Crops

Silage is made from crops that are still green and moist, so the harvest stage is different from hay. The goal is to cut when the crop has maximum energy and enough moisture to ferment properly. For maize silage, the ideal stage is when the grain is at the dough stageâabout 50% of the kernels are dented and the leaves are still green. At this point, the whole plant has 30-35% dry matter, which is perfect for fermentation. If you cut too early (before tasseling), you get low yield and too much water. If you cut too late (after black layer), the stalks become woody and hard to compact..Trim()

.Trim()

For sorghum or millet silage, cut when the seeds are at the soft dough stage. For grasses like Napier or Rhodes, cut when they are about 1-1.2 meters tall, before they flower. For legumes like cowpea or lablab, cut when pods are half-formed. In all cases, you want the crop to be wilted to 30-35% dry matter. If it's too wet (above 70% moisture), you'll get smelly, butyric silage. If it's too dry (below 25% dry matter), it won't ferment well and may mold..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, small-scale farmers often use pit silos or trench silos. The best time to harvest is during a dry spell, so you can chop and pack without rain spoiling the crop. Cut in the morning after dew has dried. Chop the material into 1-2 cm pieces using a chaff cutter or a sharp panga. The shorter the chop, the better the compaction. Fill the silo quickly and pack it tightly to remove air. You can test dry matter by squeezing a handful: if juice runs out easily, it's too wet; if it feels crumbly, it's too dry. Aim for a firm ball that holds together but doesn't drip. After filling, seal the pit with plastic and soil. Good silage will be ready in 3-4 weeks. It should smell like vinegar or fresh bread, not like manure. Harvesting at the right stage is the key to high-quality silage that will feed your animals well..Trim()

Cutting Tools and Techniques for Small-Scale Farmers

Choosing the right cutting tool saves time and labor. For small-scale farmers, the most common tools are the sickle, machete (panga), and scythe. A sickle is good for harvesting cereals like maize, sorghum, and millet. It has a curved blade with a serrated edge. Use it to cut stems at about 10-15 cm above the ground. A machete is better for cutting tall grasses like Napier or elephant grass. It has a straight, heavy blade. Swing it low and close to the ground for a clean cut. A scythe is used for cutting hay crops like Rhodes grass or lucerne. It has a long curved blade and a long handle. You swing it in a semi-circle, keeping the blade close to the ground. This is faster for large areas but requires practice..Trim()

.Trim()

Maintain your tools. A sharp blade cuts easily and reduces effort. Sharpen your sickle or machete with a file or whetstone every day before use. A dull blade bruises the plants, which can lead to poor regrowth and disease. For motorized options, a brush cutter with a metal blade can cut 1-2 hectares per day, but it's expensive. For most smallholders, hand tools are sufficient for 0.5-1 hectare..Trim()

.Trim()

Technique matters. For grasses, cut at the base but leave 5-10 cm stubble. This helps the plant regrow. For legumes, cut higher, about 15-20 cm, because they store energy in the lower stems. Cut in the morning after dew, but not too early when plants are wet. Wet plants are heavy and can cause fungal problems. When cutting, work in a systematic pattern: cut a swath, then move to the next. Collect the cut material immediately to avoid sun bleaching. For hay, leave it in the field to dry; for silage, chop and pack immediately. Always wear gloves and closed shoes to avoid cuts. Keep a first aid kit nearby. Avoiding Soil Contamination During Harvest

Soil contamination is a major cause of poor silage and hay quality. When soil gets mixed with the crop, it introduces harmful bacteria like clostridia, which cause butyric fermentation and spoilage. Soil also carries mold spores and dirt that reduce palatability. To avoid this, start by cutting cleanly. Set your sickle or machete to cut at least 5-10 cm above the ground. Never scrape the soil. If you cut too low, you pick up soil and stones. For hay crops, use a clean surface. If you dry hay on the ground, place it on a tarpaulin or a clean concrete slab. Never dry on bare soil, especially after rain, because mud will splash onto the hay..Trim()

.Trim()

Field Drying: Sun and Wind Techniques

After cutting your grass, the first job is to get it dry as fast as possible. In most of Africa, the sun is strong, but humidity and unexpected rain can ruin your hay. The goal is to reduce moisture from about 75-80% (fresh grass) down to 15-20% for safe storage. Field drying relies on sun and wind. Cut early in the morning after the dew has dried, so the grass has the whole day to dry. Use a sharp sickle or machete, or a simple mower if you have one. Spread the grass in a thin layer, not clumps. A thin layer exposes more surface to sun and wind. In very hot areas, you might need to dry for only 1-2 days; in cooler or humid areas, 3-5 days. Turn the grass once or twice a day with a wooden rake or pitchfork to expose the underside. If you have a tractor, a hay tedder helps, but manual turning works. Watch the weather: if clouds build up, cover the hay with a tarpaulin or move it under shelter. In windy areas, use windrows (long rows) perpendicular to the wind to speed drying. But don't make rows too thick. Test dryness by twisting a handful: if it breaks easily and no moisture squeezes out, it's close. Aim for 18-20% moisture. If you dry too much (below 15%), leaves shatter and you lose nutrients. If too wet, mould grows. So check daily. In humid regions like coastal West Africa, field drying may take longer; consider adding salt (1-2 kg per 100 kg grass) to help preserve, but that's not traditional hay. Better to dry thoroughly. Remember, hay quality depends on quick drying. The longer it stays wet, the more sugars and vitamins are lost. So start early, spread thin, turn often, and bring in before evening dew if not fully dry..Trim()

Tedding and Raking to Speed Up Drying

Tedding and raking are simple actions that can cut drying time by a day or more. Tedding means fluffing up the grass to expose more surface to air and sun. You can do this with a pitchfork or a wooden rake. After the grass has wilted for a few hours, go through the swath and toss it lightly. This is especially important if the grass is thick or if humidity is high. Do it once in the morning and once in the early afternoon. Raking, on the other hand, is gathering the partially dried grass into windrows (long rows) to dry evenly and to prepare for baling. In humid areas, tedding is critical to prevent mould. Be careful not to rake too early when grass is too wet, as it can clump and mould. Testing Moisture Content Without a Meter

You don't need an expensive moisture meter to know if your hay is ready to bale or store. Use your senses and simple tests. The most common field test is the twist test: take a handful of hay and twist it tightly. If it feels soft and cool, and no moisture drips out, it's likely around 20-25% moisture. You can also use a simple oven method: weigh a small sample (say 100g), dry it in an oven or solar dryer at low heat until constant weight, then reweigh. The weight loss is moisture. For example, if 100g becomes 80g, moisture is 20%. But in the field, use the twist and feel test. Another method: press a handful of hay into a ball. If it springs back and feels dry, it's ready. If you store hay at 25% moisture, it will heat up and mould. So test before baling and again before storage. In humid areas, aim for 15-18% moisture. If you're unsure, better to dry an extra day. Avoiding Rain Damage and Mould Growth

Rain is the biggest enemy of hay. A single rain shower on nearly dry hay can ruin it. When hay gets wet, moulds and bacteria grow, producing toxins that can harm livestock. So you must protect hay from rain. First, watch the weather. In many African regions, rains come in the afternoon. So plan to cut early, dry fast, and bale before rain. To prevent mould, ensure hay is properly dried before storage. Also, store in a dry place. Another method is to make haylage (wrapped silage) but that's different. For hay, the key is moisture below 20%. Proper storage protects hay from rain, ground moisture, and pests. The two main methods are stacks (outdoor or indoor) and lofts (above animal housing). For stacks, choose a well-drained site.
