Module 1 · Introduction to Beekeeping and Bee Biology

The Role of the Hive in a Colony

A beehive is not just a box; it is the colony's home, nursery, food store, and defense fortress. In Africa, where we have both wild and managed colonies, understanding the hive's role helps you build better boxes. The hive provides shelter from rain, sun, and predators. It allows bees to regulate temperature and humidity, essential for brood rearing. Without a proper hive, a colony cannot survive for long. In traditional beekeeping, people use hollow logs, clay pots, or gourds. These work, but they are hard to manage and often destroy the colony during harvest. Modern hives like the Langstroth, Top-Bar, and Warre are designed to mimic natural cavities and allow beekeepers to inspect and harvest without killing bees. The hive also stores honey and pollen. In Africa, honey is a valuable cash crop. A strong colony can produce 20-30 kg of honey per year in a good hive. The hive must be dark, dry, and well-ventilated. Bees need a small entrance that they can defend against robber bees and pests like ants and wax moths. Inside, the hive has frames or bars where bees build comb. The comb is used for brood, honey, and pollen. The hive also serves as a social hub. The queen lays eggs, workers communicate, and drones mate. A well-constructed hive increases colony productivity and makes beekeeping profitable. As you build your hive, remember: you are creating a home for thousands of bees. Every detail matters, from the size of the entrance to the thickness of the walls. A good hive is an investment that pays off in honey and healthy bees..Trim()

Basic Anatomy of the Honey Bee

To work with bees, you must know their body parts. The honey bee has three main sections: head, thorax, and abdomen. The head has two large compound eyes, three simple eyes (ocelli), and antennae. The antennae smell and feel. The mouthparts are built for sucking nectar and chewing. The thorax is the middle section, packed with muscles for flight. It has two pairs of wings and three pairs of legs. The legs are specialized: front legs clean antennae, middle legs groom, and hind legs have pollen baskets (corbiculae) for carrying pollen. The abdomen contains the digestive system, heart, and reproductive organs. Workers have a sting at the tip. The sting is a modified ovipositor. When a worker stings, it dies because the sting is barbed and pulls out. The queen's sting is smooth and she can sting multiple times. Drones have no sting. Inside the bee, there is a honey stomach (crop) for carrying nectar. Enzymes in the crop start converting nectar to honey. Bees also have wax glands on the underside of the abdomen. These glands produce beeswax, which they use to build comb. A worker bee can produce about 1/8 of a teaspoon of wax in its lifetime. That's why building comb is energy-intensive. Bees have a tracheal system for breathing, not lungs. Air enters through spiracles on the sides of the body. Understanding anatomy helps you handle bees gently. For example, you avoid crushing them, which releases alarm pheromones. You also know that smoke masks their sense of smell, reducing aggression. Knowing bee anatomy is the first step to becoming a skilled beekeeper..Trim()

The Lifecycle of the Honey Bee

The honey bee undergoes complete metamorphosis: egg, larva, pupa, adult. The queen lays eggs in hexagonal cells. Fertilized eggs become females (workers or queens); unfertilized eggs become males (drones). The egg hatches in 3 days into a tiny larva. For the first 3 days, all larvae are fed royal jelly. After that, worker and drone larvae get bee bread (pollen and honey), while queen larvae continue on royal jelly. This diet determines whether a female becomes a queen. The larva grows rapidly, molting five times in about 6 days. Then it spins a cocoon and becomes a pupa. During pupation, the body transforms. The adult emerges by chewing through the wax cap. The development time varies: queen takes 16 days, worker 21 days, drone 24 days. In Africa, these times may be slightly shorter due to warmer temperatures. A queen can lay up to 2,000 eggs per day during peak season. That's more than her own body weight. Workers live 4-6 weeks in summer, but up to 6 months in winter or dearth periods. Drones live about 8 weeks, then are kicked out before winter. The lifecycle is crucial for beekeeping. When you inspect a hive, you look for eggs, larvae, and capped brood to assess queen health. If you see eggs, the queen was there in the last 3 days. If you see only capped brood and no eggs, the queen may be failing. Understanding the timeline helps you plan harvests and colony splits. For example, to raise a new queen, you need to know that she emerges in 16 days. This knowledge is power in beekeeping..Trim()

Cast Systems: Queen, Workers, Drones

A honey bee colony has three castes: queen, workers, and drones. Each has a specific role. The queen is the only fertile female. She lays eggs and produces pheromones that control the colony's behavior. A queen can live 2-3 years, but beekeepers often replace her after 1-2 years to maintain productivity. She mates once in her life with 10-20 drones, storing sperm for years. The workers are sterile females. They do all the work: cleaning, nursing, building comb, foraging, guarding, and regulating temperature. Worker jobs change with age. Young workers are nurses; older ones forage. A worker bee visits 50-100 flowers per trip. To make 1 kg of honey, bees must visit about 4 million flowers and fly 90,000 km. That's a lot of work. Workers communicate through dances. The waggle dance tells direction and distance to food. Drones are male bees. They have no sting, no pollen baskets, and no wax glands. Their only job is to mate with a virgin queen. After mating, they die. In a colony, there are a few hundred to a few thousand drones, mostly in the warm season. In Africa, drones are produced year-round in tropical areas. Beekeepers sometimes remove drone comb to control varroa mites, but in Africa, varroa is less of a problem. Understanding castes helps you manage the hive. For example, if you see many drones, it may indicate a failing queen or preparation for swarming. If you see queen cells, the colony may be about to swarm. You can prevent swarming by splitting the colony or removing queen cells. Each caste is essential for the colony's survival. A balanced colony has one queen, thousands of workers, and a few hundred drones..Trim()

Understanding Bee Space and Propolis

Bee space is the gap that bees leave open between combs. It is about 6-9 mm (1/4 to 3/8 inch). If the gap is smaller, bees fill it with propolis. If larger, they build burr comb. This discovery by Lorenzo Langstroth revolutionized beekeeping. In Africa, bee space is critical for building hives that are easy to work with. When you build a Langstroth hive, you must maintain 6-9 mm between frames. If you don't, bees will glue everything together with propolis, making inspection difficult. Propolis is a resinous mixture that bees collect from tree buds, sap, and other plant sources. They use it to seal cracks, strengthen comb, and sanitize the hive. Propolis has antimicrobial properties. In Africa, propolis is harvested for medicinal use and sold in markets. It can fetch good prices. But too much propolis is a nuisance for beekeepers. To minimize propolis, keep your hive tools clean and avoid leaving gaps. Also, choose bee strains that use less propolis. In Top-Bar hives, bee space is maintained by the width of the bars. The bars are usually 32-35 mm wide, which gives the correct spacing. In Warre hives, the boxes are designed with proper bee space. When building your hive, measure carefully. A small mistake can lead to a glued-up hive. Also, bees use propolis to narrow the entrance. You can scrape it off during inspections. But leave some, as it helps with defense. Understanding bee space and propolis is key to a manageable hive. It saves you time and reduces stress on the bees..Trim()

Overview of Hive Types: Langstroth, Top-Bar, Warre

There are three main modern hive types used in Africa: Langstroth, Top-Bar, and Warre. Each has advantages and disadvantages. The Langstroth hive is the most common. It has stacked boxes with movable frames. The frames allow you to inspect and harvest honey without destroying comb. A standard Langstroth hive has 10 frames per box. The boxes are called supers. You can add supers as the colony grows. Langstroth hives produce the most honey, up to 30-40 kg per year in good conditions. They are easy to transport and manage. However, they require precise construction and are more expensive. The Top-Bar hive is simpler. It has a single box with bars across the top. Bees build comb hanging from the bars. You can inspect each bar individually. Top-Bar hives are cheaper to build and easier for beginners. They are good for areas where wood is scarce. But they produce less honey, about 15-20 kg per year, and the comb is fragile. You cannot extract honey with a centrifuge; you must crush and strain. The Warre hive is like a Top-Bar but with boxes stacked vertically. It is designed to mimic a tree cavity. Warre hives are less intrusive; you add boxes to the bottom. They produce about 20-25 kg per year. They are good for natural beekeeping. In Africa, the choice depends on your goals, budget, and local conditions. Langstroth is best for commercial honey production. Top-Bar is good for small-scale and beginners. Warre is for those who want a more natural approach. Many African beekeepers use a mix. For example, you can start with a Top-Bar and later switch to Langstroth. Whatever you choose, ensure the hive has proper bee space, ventilation, and a secure entrance. Your hive is your farm; choose wisely..Trim()

Personal Protective Equipment: Suits, Gloves, Veils

Personal protective equipment (PPE) is your first line of defense against bee stings. In Africa, where African honeybees are more defensive, you need a full suit. A good suit is made of thick cotton or ventilated mesh, light in colour (white or khaki) because bees are calmer around light colours. It must cover your whole body, with elastic at wrists and ankles to prevent bees entering. A full suit costs between $30 and $80, but you can sew your own using heavy cotton drill. Always check for holes and repair them before each use..Trim()

Your veil is critical. It must be black mesh so you can see through, and it should be attached to a hat or helmet to keep it away from your face. A veil that touches your skin invites stings. Many beekeepers use a fencing mask or a wide-brimmed hat with a veil sewn on. Ensure the veil zips or ties securely to your suit. Never approach a hive without your veil properly fastened..Trim()

Gloves protect your hands but reduce dexterity. Leather gloves are durable but can carry alarm pheromones from previous stings, making bees more aggressive. Wash them regularly with soap and water. Alternatively, use thick rubber gloves, which are easier to clean. Some beekeepers use no gloves for delicate work, but beginners should always wear gloves. Keep a spare pair..Trim()

Boots are also important. Wear sturdy closed shoes or gumboots. Tuck your suit legs into the boots or use elastic bands to seal them. Bees often crawl up from the ground. In hot African climates, choose breathable fabrics to avoid overheating. Drink plenty of water before and after working. Always work in pairs, so one person can help if bees become too aggressive. Remember, no PPE is 100% sting-proof, but it dramatically reduces the risk..Trim()

The Smoker: Operation and Maintenance

A smoker is your most important tool for calm hive inspections. It produces cool, white smoke that masks the bees' alarm pheromones and triggers a feeding response, making them less aggressive. In Africa, where bees can be highly defensive, a well-lit smoker is essential. You can buy a stainless steel smoker for $20-$40, or make one from a tin can and a piece of leather for the bellows. The fuel you use matters: dry, rotten wood, coconut husks, dried grass, or cardboard work well. Avoid using wet or green material, as it produces hot, black smoke that irritates bees..Trim()

Safe Handling of Power Saws and Drills

Power tools make hive construction faster but require strict safety. In Africa, circular saws and drills are common in workshops. Before using any power tool, read the manual and inspect it for damage. Check that the blade or bit is sharp and securely tightened. Dull blades cause kickback and accidents. After cutting, wait for the blade to stop completely before setting the saw down. Use a push stick for narrow cuts. Always cut away from your body. First Aid for Bee Stings and Workshop Injuries

Bee stings are painful but rarely life-threatening unless you are allergic. In Africa, many beekeepers work without knowing they are allergic until a severe reaction occurs. Always carry a first aid kit and know the signs of anaphylaxis: difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, dizziness, nausea, and a rapid pulse. If these occur, seek emergency medical help immediately. If you have a known allergy, carry an epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen) and inform your partner..Trim()

For a normal sting, scrape the stinger out with a fingernail or a credit card. Do not squeeze it, as this injects more venom. Wash the area with soap and water. Apply a cold compress to reduce swelling and pain. Take an antihistamine if available. A paste of baking soda and water or a slice of onion can soothe the sting. In Africa, many beekeepers use crushed plantain leaves or aloe vera gel. Keep the area clean and monitor for infection. Multiple stings (more than 10 per kilogram of body weight) can be toxic; seek medical attention..Trim()

.Trim()

Workshop injuries include cuts, splinters, and eye injuries. For minor cuts, clean with water, apply antiseptic, and cover with a sterile bandage. For deep cuts, apply pressure to stop bleeding and get medical help. For splinters, remove with sterilized tweezers and clean the wound. For eye injuries, flush with clean water for 15 minutes and seek medical attention. Never rub your eyes with dirty hands. Always wear safety glasses when cutting or drilling..Trim()

Prevention is best. Wear your PPE, work calmly, and keep your first aid kit stocked. Check it every month for expired items. Include bandages, antiseptic, antihistamines, pain relievers, tweezers, scissors, and a cold pack. In remote areas, have a plan for getting to a clinic. Share your location with someone before working. And remember, if you feel unwell after a sting, stop working and get help..Trim()

Choosing Rot-Resistant Woods: Cedar, Pine, Cypress

In beekeeping, your hive sits outside in rain, sun, and humidity. Wood that rots quickly means rebuilding hives every year. Choose rot-resistant species. In Africa, three good options are cedar, pine, and cypress. Cedar (like Juniperus procera in East Africa) is naturally rot-resistant due to oils. It lasts 10-15 years untreated. It's light, easy to work, but can be expensive and splinters easily. Pine (Pinus species) is widely available and cheap, but untreated pine rots in 2-3 years in wet conditions. You must treat pine with preservative or paint. Cypress (Cupressus lusitanica) is a middle ground: moderately rot-resistant, affordable, and grows in many African highlands. It lasts 5-8 years untreated. .Trim()

.Trim()

For beehives, avoid softwoods like balsa or fast-growing eucalyptus that warp and crack. Hardwoods like mvule or iroko are durable but heavy and hard to nail. For a Langstroth hive, 20mm thick boards are standard. Cedar at 20mm gives good insulation. Pine at 25mm is stronger but heavier. Cypress at 20mm is a good compromise. .Trim()

.Trim()

When buying, look for straight grain, few knots, and no signs of fungus (black streaks or white patches). In dry areas, termites are a bigger threat than rot. Cedar and cypress resist termites better than pine. If using pine, treat with borate or creosote (but let it dry fully before bees enter). .Trim()

.Trim()

Source locally: in Kenya, cedar from Aberdare forests; cypress from Kiambu; pine from sawmills in Nakuru. In Nigeria, try iroko or mahogany for bases. .Trim()

Remember: bees need dry hives. Rot-resistant wood keeps your investment safe. Spend a little more on good wood now, save money later..Trim()

Understanding Wood Grades and Defects

Wood grades tell you the quality of lumber. In Africa, most sawmills use simple grades: First, Second, Third, and Utility. First grade is clear, few defects, for visible parts. .Trim()

.Trim()

For beehive parts: use First or Second grade for hive bodies, supers, and frames. Use Third grade for bottom boards, stands, and roofs (where defects won't affect bees). Avoid boards with more than one defect per meter. .Trim()

.Trim()

Pre-Drilling and Countersinking for Screws

Screws hold beehives together. But driving screws into wood without pre-drilling causes splits, especially near ends. Pre-drilling makes a pilot hole smaller than the screw. Countersinking makes a cone-shaped hole for the screw head to sit flush. .Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Step 1: Mark screw locations. For hive body corners, use 3 screws per joint, spaced 50mm apart. .Trim()

.Trim()

Step 2: Pre-drill. Use a bit equal to the screw's minor diameter (the shank without threads). For a 4mm screw, use 3mm bit. Drill through the first board and 20mm into the second. .Trim()

.Trim()

Step 3: Countersink. Use a countersink bit to make a cone for the screw head. Depth: just enough so head sits flush or slightly below surface. .Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

For hardwoods like cedar, always pre-drill. For softwoods like pine, pre-drill near ends. .Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Alternative: nails. But screws are stronger and can be removed. Use galvanized or stainless screws to resist rust. .Trim()

.Trim()

If you don't have a countersink bit, use a larger drill bit (8mm) to make a shallow cone. .Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Remember: pre-drilling takes time but prevents splits. A split board is wasted. .Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Practice on scrap wood first. You'll quickly learn the right bit sizes and screw torque..Trim()

Module 7 · Constructing Frames and Foundations

Frame Anatomy: Top Bar, Bottom Bar, Side Bars

A bee frame is a rectangular wooden structure that holds honeycomb. It has four parts: top bar, bottom bar, and two side bars. The top bar is the longest piece, usually 48.3 cm (19 inches) for a standard Langstroth hive. It has a thicker profile to support the weight of honey and to hang on the hive body's frame rest. The bottom bar is the same length but thinner, often 1.9 cm (3/4 inch) wide and 1 cm (3/8 inch) thick. The side bars are 23.2 cm (9 1/8 inches) tall, connecting the top and bottom bars. They have a notch at the top to fit under the top bar and a hole or slot at the bottom for the bottom bar. In Africa, many beekeepers use local timber like cypress, pine, or eucalyptus. Avoid termite-prone woods like soft mango. Cut all pieces accurately; a frame that is 2 mm off will not sit properly in the hive. Use a tape measure, pencil, and a mitre saw or hand saw. Sand rough edges to prevent bee injuries. The top bar often has a groove on the underside to hold foundation. The side bars may have holes for wiring. Understanding these parts helps you build strong, interchangeable frames. A well-built frame lasts many years and makes hive inspections easy..Trim()

Cutting and Assembling Wooden Frames

To cut frames, start with dry, straight timber. Use a table saw or circular saw for speed, but a hand saw works fine. Set a stop block to cut multiple pieces to the same length. For a standard Langstroth frame, cut top bars to 48.3 cm, bottom bars to 48.3 cm, and side bars to 23.2 cm. You need two side bars per frame. Cut a notch at the top of each side bar to fit the top bar. The notch is usually 1.5 cm deep and 2 cm wide. At the bottom, cut a slot for the bottom bar. Sand all pieces with 80-grit sandpaper. Assembly: apply waterproof wood glue (like Titebond III) to all joints. Use a frame jig to hold pieces square. Nail with 4 cm (1.5 inch) galvanised nails: two nails through each side bar into the top bar, and two into the bottom bar. Pre-drill holes to avoid splitting. Check for square by measuring diagonals; they must be equal. Let glue dry for 24 hours. In Africa, use nails that won't rust in humid conditions. If using local hardwood, pre-drill all holes. A good frame is rigid and does not twist. Build at least 10 frames per hive box. Stack them flat to dry. Poor assembly leads to burr comb and broken frames..Trim()

Wiring Frames for Strength

Wiring a frame reinforces the comb so it doesn't break during extraction or hot weather. You need frame wire (26 or 28 gauge, stainless steel or galvanised), a wire embedder (spur or electric), and wire cutters. First, drill four small holes in the side bars: two near the top and two near the bottom. They should be about 1 cm from the edges. Thread one end of the wire through a hole and secure it with a small nail or a figure-eight knot. Pull the wire across to the opposite hole, then back, creating a zigzag pattern: two horizontal wires across the frame. Keep the wire tight; it should twang when plucked. Use a wire tensioner or a nail to twist the wire at the end. For embedding, place a sheet of wax foundation on the frame. Run a spur embedder (a tool with a small wheel) along the wires, pressing them into the wax. Alternatively, use an electric embedder that heats the wire. In Africa, many beekeepers use a 12V car battery and a length of resistance wire to heat and embed. Do not overheat; wax melts at 63Â°C. Let the wax cool and set. Check that wires are embedded but not cutting through. Properly wired frames last many seasons and produce straight comb..Trim()

Installing Wax Foundation: Crimp-Wire and Pin

Wax foundation is a thin sheet of beeswax with a hexagon pattern. It guides bees to build straight comb. You can install it using crimp-wire or pins. Crimp-wire method: after wiring the frame, place the foundation on the wires. Use a crimping tool (or a small pliers) to press the wire into the wax. Start at one end and work along each wire. The wire should sit just below the surface. Then, use a spur embedder to press the wire deeper. For extra security, use a hot wire embedder. Pin method: use foundation pins (small metal pins with a flat head) or a stapler. Place the foundation on the frame and push pins through the wax into the top bar and side bars. Space pins every 5 cm. In Africa, many beekeepers make their own foundation using a silicone mould and melted beeswax. When installing, do it in a warm room (25-30Â°C) so the wax is pliable. Do not install in direct sun; it will melt. If foundation is brittle, warm it slightly. Ensure the foundation is centred and flat. Bees will draw comb from it. A well-installed foundation saves the colony energy and reduces drone comb. Replace foundation every 2-3 years to avoid disease buildup..Trim()

Building Foundationless Frames with Starter Strips

Foundationless frames have no full wax sheet, only a starter strip at the top. This allows bees to build natural comb, which many African beekeepers prefer because it saves money and wax. To make a starter strip, cut a piece of beeswax foundation 2-3 cm tall and as wide as the top bar. Alternatively, use a wooden strip or a plastic strip. Attach it to the underside of the top bar using melted beeswax or small nails. You can also run a bead of melted wax along the top bar and press the strip into it. The strip acts as a guide. For strength, wire the frame as usual, but leave the wires exposed. Bees will build comb around the wires. Another method is to cut a V-shaped groove in the top bar and pour melted wax into it. The bees will follow the wax line. In Africa, use local beeswax from your own hives. Make sure the starter strip is straight and centred. When placing the frame in the hive, ensure it is level. Bees build comb downward. Check frames every week; if comb is crooked, straighten it early. Foundationless frames require more frequent inspections but produce high-quality wax. They are ideal for top-bar hives and for beekeepers who want to avoid buying foundation..Trim()

Assembling Plastic Frames and Foundations

Plastic frames and foundations are one-piece units, often made of food-grade plastic. They are durable, resistant to wax moths, and easy to clean. To assemble, you simply snap the plastic foundation into the plastic frame. No wiring or embedding is needed. Some plastic frames come pre-assembled; others require you to fit the foundation into the frame's grooves. Use your thumbs to press the foundation into the frame until it clicks. Make sure it is seated evenly. Plastic frames are heavier than wood but last many years. They are ideal for African beekeepers who lack woodworking tools or who want to reduce labour. However, bees may be slow to accept plastic, especially if it is smooth. To encourage acceptance, coat the foundation with a thin layer of melted beeswax. Use a paintbrush or dip the foundation in warm wax. Let it dry before placing in the hive. Plastic frames can be cleaned with a hive tool and warm water. Do not use boiling water; it will warp. In hot African climates, plastic can become brittle; store them out of direct sun. They are also less forgiving if dropped. Use plastic frames in all hive boxes for uniformity. They are a good choice for beginners and for areas with high humidity..Trim()

Purpose of Inner Covers and Ventilation

An inner cover sits directly on top of the top bars of your hive, just under the outer cover. Its main jobs are to provide insulation, prevent the bees from gluing the outer cover down with propolis, and allow moisture to escape. Without an inner cover, condensation from the bees' breath and nectar ripening can drip back onto the cluster, chilling bees in cold weather or causing mold in humid conditions. In hot African climates, an inner cover with a ventilation notch also helps hot air rise and exit, reducing bearding and fanning..Trim()

.Trim()

You need an inner cover that fits snugly but not too tight. A common design uses a 10 mm thick plywood or solid wood board cut to the same length and width as your hive's top opening. For a standard Langstroth hive, that's 508 mm by 413 mm. Cut a rectangular hole in the centre, about 100 mm by 150 mm, and cover it with 3 mm wire mesh (hardware cloth) to stop bees from passing through while letting air move. On one short side, cut a notch 20 mm wide and 10 mm deep into the edge; this becomes an upper entrance and ventilation gap when the outer cover is on..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many beekeepers use local materials: offcuts of plywood from carpentry shops, flattened metal drums, or even sturdy cardboard sealed with propolis. Whatever you use, the inner cover must be flat and free of gaps that let rain in. If you use a solid board without a notch, drill two 25 mm holes near opposite corners and screen them. This gives cross-ventilation. During honey flow, you can leave the notch open; in cool highland areas, close it with a small wedge to retain warmth..Trim()

.Trim()

Proper ventilation also reduces absconding. Bees in a hot, stuffy hive with no air movement will often leave. An inner cover with a mesh centre and a notch gives them a chimney effect: hot air rises through the mesh and out the notch, drawing cooler air in from the entrance. Check your inner cover every few months. Scrape off propolis with a hive tool, and replace mesh if it's torn. A well-made inner cover costs little but pays you back in healthier, calmer colonies..Trim()

Constructing an Inner Cover with Bee Escape

A bee escape is a small device that lets worker bees go down but not back up, useful for clearing bees from a honey super before harvest. You can build an inner cover with a built-in bee escape using simple tools: a hammer, saw, drill, and 6 mm plywood. Start with a board 508 mm by 413 mm. Cut a central hole 100 mm by 150 mm. Instead of mesh, you will fit a bee escape. You can buy a plastic or metal escape (like a Porter escape) or make one from a 20 mm thick wooden block with a 19 mm tunnel and a small wire grid..Trim()

.Trim()

To make a wooden escape: take a block 80 mm by 80 mm by 20 mm. Drill a 19 mm hole through the centre. On the top face, cut a slot 3 mm wide and 15 mm deep across the hole. Insert a piece of 3 mm wire mesh over the slot, stapled down. The bees enter the hole from below, walk through the tunnel, and exit through the mesh into the super above. Because the mesh is flat, they cannot figure out how to go back down. This is a one-way valve..Trim()

.Trim()

Fix the escape over the central hole of your inner cover using four 25 mm screws. Make sure the escape sits flush and there are no gaps around it. On the short side of the cover, cut a 20 mm by 10 mm notch for ventilation and as an upper entrance when the escape is not in use. Sand rough edges. Paint the cover with linseed oil or food-grade paint, avoiding the escape area..Trim()

.Trim()

When using the escape, place the inner cover with escape under the honey super you want to clear. The bees will move down into the brood box overnight. After 24 to 48 hours, most workers will be gone. Then remove the super. In Africa, where small hive beetles and ants are common, a bee escape can also help reduce pests by letting bees concentrate in the brood nest. Clean the escape after each use; propolis can block the tunnel. Store the cover dry. This tool saves you from shaking bees off frames, which stresses the colony and loses honey..Trim()

Building a Telescoping Outer Cover

The telescoping outer cover is the roof of your hive. It slides over the inner cover and the top edges of the hive body, protecting against rain, sun, and wind. It must be at least 50 mm larger than the hive on all sides so it overhangs and sheds water away from the walls. For a standard 10-frame Langstroth hive, the outer cover is about 560 mm long, 460 mm wide, and 100 mm tall. In Africa, you can adapt these dimensions to your local hive size, but keep the overhang..Trim()

.Trim()

Build the cover from 20 mm thick timber. Cut two side pieces 560 mm long and two end pieces 460 mm long, each 100 mm wide. Nail or screw them together into a rectangular frame. Then cut a top board 560 mm by 460 mm from 12 mm plywood or solid wood. Fix it to the frame with 40 mm screws. For extra strength, add a 20 mm by 20 mm batten along the inside edges of the frame. This frame creates a dead air space that insulates the hive from the hot sun..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, cover the top with metal roofing. You can use galvanised iron sheet, aluminium, or even flattened tin cans. Cut the metal 50 mm larger than the top board on all sides. Bend the edges down over the sides by 50 mm and nail them to the frame with roofing nails. This metal reflects sunlight and keeps rain out. In hot areas, paint the metal white or silver to reflect heat. In cooler highlands, black paint absorbs heat, but white is safer for bees..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, add a ventilation gap. Drill two 25 mm holes in the end pieces, near the top, and cover them with 3 mm mesh to stop bees from entering. This lets hot air escape. The telescoping cover should fit loosely enough to lift off easily but not so loose that wind blows it off. You can add a brick or strap on windy sites. Check annually for rust and repaint. A good outer cover lasts 5 to 10 years and is the first line of defence for your colony..Trim()

Cutting and Fitting Metal Roofing

Metal roofing for your hive cover can be cut from old corrugated iron sheets, flat galvanised sheet, or aluminium flashing. You need tin snips (aviation snips) for straight cuts, a measuring tape, a marker, and gloves to protect your hands from sharp edges. For a telescoping cover of 560 mm by 460 mm, cut the metal 660 mm by 560 mm, giving a 50 mm overhang on all sides to bend down..Trim()

.Trim()

Mark your cut lines with a marker. Use the tin snips to cut along the lines. If the metal is thick, use a hacksaw with a fine-tooth blade. For corrugated sheets, cut along the ridges to keep the sheet strong. After cutting, file the edges smooth with a metal file. This prevents cuts and helps the metal bend without tearing. Wear gloves and eye protection; metal shards fly..Trim()

.Trim()

Bend the overhang down over the sides of the wooden cover. You can do this by hand for thin aluminium, but for thicker galvanised iron, use a hammer and a block of wood. Place the metal on the cover, align it, and tap the edges down gently. Work from the centre of each side outward to avoid wrinkles. At the corners, make a diagonal cut from the corner of the top board to the corner of the metal, then fold the flaps over each other and nail. This creates a neat, watertight corner..Trim()

Fix the metal to the wooden frame with roofing nails or screws with rubber washers. Space them every 100 mm along the edges. In Africa, where intense sun can make metal expand, leave a 2 mm gap between sheets if you use more than one piece. Paint the metal with anti-rust paint every two years. A well-fitted metal roof keeps your hive dry and cool, and it can be removed for inspection without disturbing the bees..Trim()

Adding Insulation and Moisture Control

Insulation in the hive cover helps keep temperatures stable. In hot African regions, it reduces heat stress; in cool highlands, it stops condensation from dripping on the bees. The simplest insulation is a 25 mm layer of rigid foam board (like Styrofoam) cut to fit inside the telescoping cover, between the top board and the inner cover. You can also use dry grass, sawdust, or shredded paper in a cloth bag, but foam is cleaner and lasts longer..Trim()

.Trim()

To install foam: cut it to the inside dimensions of your outer cover, about 510 mm by 410 mm for a Langstroth. Place it on top of the inner cover, then slide the outer cover over. The foam should not block the ventilation holes in the outer cover. If your outer cover has mesh vents, cut the foam to leave those areas open. In very hot areas, add a second layer of foam or use a reflective foil facing upward. In humid areas, moisture is the bigger enemy..Trim()

.Trim()

Moisture control: bees produce water vapour from respiration and from ripening nectar. If that moisture condenses on a cold inner cover, it drips back. To prevent this, ensure your inner cover has a ventilation notch and a mesh centre. You can also add a moisture quilt: a shallow box (50 mm deep) placed between the inner cover and outer cover, filled with wood shavings or dry rice husks. The shavings absorb moisture and let it evaporate through the top vents. In Africa, rice husks are cheap and available. Replace them when they feel damp, about every 2 to 3 months..Trim()

.Trim()

Another trick: tilt the hive slightly forward so condensation runs out the entrance, not onto the cluster. A 5 mm tilt is enough. Also, avoid sealing the hive completely. Bees need air movement. In the rainy season, check the inner cover for mold. If you see black spots, scrape and sun-dry the cover. A dry hive is a healthy hive. Insulation and moisture control are not expensive, but they can mean the difference between a colony that survives the season and one that absconds..Trim()

Weatherproofing Covers for Longevity

Your covers face sun, rain, wind, and insects. Without weatherproofing, wood warps, metal rusts, and bees suffer. Start with the wood. Use hardwood or treated pine for the frame. If you use untreated wood, paint it with two coats of exterior latex paint or linseed oil. Avoid creosote or toxic paints; bees are sensitive. Paint all surfaces, including edges and inside the frame, but do not paint the inner cover's bee-contact areas if you use a bee escape. Let it dry for 48 hours before putting it on the hive..Trim()

.Trim()

For the metal roof, apply a rust-resistant paint. In coastal Africa, salt air rusts metal fast, so use galvanised or aluminium sheets and paint them with zinc-rich paint. Check for scratches every few months. Sand any rust spots and touch up with paint. If you use corrugated iron, make sure nails have rubber washers and are not overdriven. Replace missing nails. Seal seams with roofing silicone..Trim()

.Trim()

Add a wind strap. In windy areas, a simple strap over the outer cover, anchored to the hive stand, prevents the cover from blowing off. You can use a ratchet strap or a piece of inner tube. Also, ensure the cover overhangs enough so rain drips off away from the hive body. A 50 mm overhang is minimum; 75 mm is better in heavy rain..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, inspect your covers every season. Look for cracks, rot, rust, and loose mesh. Repair small problems immediately. A cracked inner cover lets rain in; a rusted roof leaks. Keep a spare cover ready so you can swap out a damaged one without exposing the colony. With proper care, a well-built telescoping cover lasts 8 to 10 years in African conditions. This saves you money and keeps your bees productive. Remember, the cover is the hive's umbrella and its winter coat. Treat it well, and your bees will reward you with honey..Trim()

Building a Queen Excluder Frame

A queen excluder keeps the queen out of the honey supers so she doesn't lay eggs there. You can buy one, but making your own saves money and lets you fit it to your hive. The critical part is the mesh: use galvanized wire mesh with holes exactly 4.2 mm (or 4.1â4.5 mm). If holes are too big, the queen passes; too small, workers can't pass. Choosing Fasteners: Nails, Screws, Staples

When building a beehive, the fasteners you choose determine how long the hive lasts and how well it handles African heat, rain, and termites. Sanding and Surface Preparation

Sanding and surface preparation might seem like extra work, but it makes your beehive last longer and look better. Choose a site that gets morning sun and afternoon shade. In hot African regions, hives facing east or northeast get early warmth to start foraging, but avoid full-day sun that melts comb.
