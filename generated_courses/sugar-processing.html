History and Global Importance of Sugar

Sugar has been produced and traded for over 2,000 years. It likely originated in New Guinea around 8000 BCE, then spread to India, where people learned to crystallize sugar around 500 BCE. Arab traders brought it to the Middle East, North Africa, and Spain. By the 1500s, European colonizers established sugarcane plantations in the Caribbean, Brazil, and West Africa, using enslaved African labor. This brutal history shaped today's sugar industry..Trim()

Today, sugar is a major global commodity. In 2022, the world produced about 180 million metric tons of sugar. Brazil is the largest producer, followed by India, Thailand, and China. In Africa, major producers include South Africa, Egypt, Morocco, Nigeria, and Sudan. Africa produces about 12 million tons annually but consumes more, so many countries import sugar. For example, Nigeria produces around 1.5 million tons but needs over 2 million tons, creating a supply gap..Trim()

.Trim()

Sugar is more than a sweetener. It is a source of energy (about 4 calories per gram), a preservative in jams and beverages, and a key ingredient in baking, confectionery, and soft drinks. It also produces ethanol, a biofuel. In many African countries, sugar is a strategic crop that supports millions of smallholder farmers. For instance, in Kenya, the sugar industry employs over 250,000 people directly and indirectly. In Eswatini, sugar accounts for about 60% of agricultural exports..Trim()

Understanding this history helps you see why sugar processing matters. It is not just about making sweets; it is about food security, jobs, and rural development. As you learn processing skills, you join a long chain of producers who turn a simple grass into a globally traded product. Your work can help reduce imports and create local wealth..Trim()

Types of Sugar: Sucrose, Glucose, Fructose

When we talk about 'sugar', we usually mean sucrose, the white crystals you buy in the market. But there are many types of sugars, and they behave differently in cooking, fermentation, and the human body. The three most common are sucrose, glucose, and fructose. All are carbohydrates, but their chemical structures differ..Trim()

.Trim()

Sucrose is a disaccharide, meaning it is made of two smaller sugars: glucose and fructose linked together. Table sugar, brown sugar, and sugarcane juice are mostly sucrose. Sucrose is extracted from sugarcane or sugar beet. It is about 100% sweet, stable, and easy to crystallize. In Africa, most sucrose comes from sugarcane. For example, a ton of sugarcane can yield about 100 kg of sucrose if processed well..Trim()

.Trim()

Glucose is a monosaccharide, also called dextrose. It is the main sugar in blood and the primary energy source for cells. Glucose is less sweet than sucrose (about 70% as sweet) and is often used in energy drinks and medical drips. It is also produced when you break down starch from maize, cassava, or potatoes. In Africa, glucose syrup is made from cassava starch in countries like Nigeria and South Africa..Trim()

.Trim()

Fructose is another monosaccharide, found in fruits and honey. It is the sweetest of the three, about 1.7 times sweeter than sucrose. High-fructose corn syrup (HFCS) is made from maize and is common in soft drinks. Fructose is absorbed differently and can be problematic in large amounts, but in whole fruits it comes with fiber and vitamins..Trim()

.Trim()

For a sugar processor, knowing these types matters. When you make invert sugar (a mix of glucose and fructose), you heat sucrose with acid or enzymes. Invert sugar is used in candies and soft drinks to prevent crystallization. In Africa, many small processors make invert sugar from local sugarcane or beet. Understanding the differences helps you control sweetness, texture, and shelf life of your products..Trim()

Basic Steps in Sugar Production

Sugar production from sugarcane involves six main steps: harvesting, extraction, purification, evaporation, crystallization, and refining. Each step is critical for quality and yield. Here is how it works in a typical African mill..Trim()

.Trim()

1. Harvesting: Cane is cut by hand or machine. Remove leaves and tops because they contain little sugar. Transport cane to the mill within 24 hours. In Kenya, smallholders cut cane and load it onto tractors or lorries..Trim()

.Trim()

2. Extraction: At the mill, cane is washed and shredded, then passed through a series of roller mills (usually 4â6 mills). Hot water is sprayed on the cane to dissolve sugar. 3. Purification: The raw juice is heated to 70Â°C and mixed with lime (calcium hydroxide) to raise the pH. This causes impurities to settle. The juice is then clarified, and the mud is filtered out. 4. Evaporation: The juice is boiled in a series of vacuum pans (multiple-effect evaporators) to remove water. It becomes a thick syrup called 'meladura' with 60â65% solids. This step uses steam from the boilers, often powered by bagasse..Trim()

Small-scale processors can make jaggery or non-centrifugal sugar by boiling juice in open pans, but this is less efficient. Safety and Environmental Considerations in Sugar Processing

Sugar processing involves heavy machinery, hot liquids, and chemicals. Safety must be your first priority. In Africa, many mills have poor safety records, so you must be vigilant. Here are key risks and how to manage them..Trim()

.Trim()

Physical hazards: Roller mills can crush hands or arms. Never reach into a mill while it is running. Use lockout/tagout procedures during maintenance. Hot juice and steam can cause severe burns. Wear heat-resistant gloves, aprons, and face shields. Boilers can explode if not maintained; check pressure gauges daily. Bagasse dust can cause fires or explosions; keep areas clean and use dust suppression systems..Trim()

.Trim()

Chemical hazards: Lime (calcium hydroxide) is corrosive. Wear goggles and gloves when handling it. Sulphur dioxide can irritate lungs; use in well-ventilated areas. Phosphoric acid is also corrosive. Always add acid to water, never water to acid..Trim()

.Trim()

Environmental concerns: Sugar processing produces wastewater with high organic load (BOD and COD). If released untreated, it depletes oxygen in rivers, killing fish. Mills should install anaerobic digesters or aerated lagoons. Bagasse fly ash from boilers must be captured with scrubbers. Molasses spills can contaminate soil and water. In many African countries, environmental regulations require an Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) before building a mill..Trim()

.Trim()

Best practices: Train workers on safety every month. Keep a first-aid kit and fire extinguishers nearby. Use personal protective equipment (PPE) at all times. Monitor noise levels; use earplugs in noisy areas. For the environment, recycle water where possible, use bagasse for energy, and compost filter mud. In Kenya, the sugar industry has adopted cogeneration, burning bagasse to produce electricity for the grid. This reduces waste and generates income..Trim()

.Trim()

As a small processor, you may not have a big mill, but the same principles apply. Boiling juice in open pans can cause burns; use long-handled ladles and keep children away. Dispose of wastewater properly; do not dump it in streams. By following safety and environmental rules, you protect yourself, your workers, and your community..Trim()

Module 2 · Sugarcane Agriculture and Harvesting

Sugarcane Varieties and Selection Criteria

Choosing the right sugarcane variety is the first step to a profitable harvest. In Africa, common varieties include NCo376 (high sucrose, good for irrigated areas), CP72-1312 (drought-tolerant, suited to drylands), and R570 (resistant to smut, widely grown in South Africa). Local agricultural research stations, like the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organization (KALRO) or the Sugarcane Research Institute in Tanzania, can advise on varieties best for your region. Key selection criteria are: maturity period (early, mid, or late), sucrose content (measured as pol% cane, ideally above 12%), resistance to pests and diseases (especially smut, rust, and mosaic), and tolerance to drought or waterlogging. For rain-fed areas, choose early-maturing varieties like Co421 that can be harvested in 12 months. For irrigated farms, late-maturing high-yield varieties like N14 can be grown for 18 months. Always buy certified seed cane from reputable sources to avoid disease. Inspect seed cane for signs of pests like scale insects or borers. Plant at least two or three different varieties to spread risk. For example, if you plant only one variety and it succumbs to rust, you lose everything. A good practice is to allocate 70% of your land to a proven high-yielder and 30% to a new variety you want to test. Remember, variety choice affects everything from planting density to harvest timing. Consult your local extension officer before planting..Trim()

Soil Preparation and Planting Techniques

Sugarcane grows best in deep, well-drained loamy soils with a pH between 6.0 and 7.5. Start by clearing the land of bushes and previous crop residues. Plough to a depth of 30-40 cm, then harrow to break clods. If the soil is acidic (pH below 5.5), apply agricultural lime at 2-4 tonnes per hectare and incorporate it into the top 15 cm. For planting, use the furrow method: dig furrows 20-30 cm deep and 90-120 cm apart. In dry areas, make furrows 15 cm deep and space them 1.5 m apart to conserve moisture. Place seed cane (setts) in the furrows, overlapping them slightly. Each sett should have 2-3 buds. A common spacing is 3-4 setts per meter of furrow. Cover with 5-8 cm of soil. Planting time depends on rainfall: in East Africa, plant with the long rains (March-May) or short rains (October-December). In West Africa, plant at the onset of the rainy season. Apply a starter fertilizer at planting: 200 kg/ha of DAP (18-46-0) or 150 kg/ha of TSP. For organic options, use well-rotted manure at 10-15 tonnes per hectare. After planting, apply a pre-emergence herbicide like atrazine to control weeds. If you plant on slopes, use contour furrows to prevent erosion. For smallholder farmers, hand planting with a hoe is common, but for larger fields, tractor-drawn planters save time. Ensure the soil is moist at planting; if dry, irrigate lightly. Germination occurs in 2-4 weeks. Gap-fill after 4 weeks to maintain plant population..Trim()

Irrigation and Fertilization Practices

Sugarcane is a thirsty crop, needing 1500-2500 mm of water per year. In dry areas, irrigation is essential. The most common methods in Africa are furrow irrigation (flooding the furrows), sprinkler, and drip. Furrow irrigation is cheap but uses a lot of water; drip irrigation saves up to 50% water but has high initial cost. Irrigate every 7-10 days in sandy soils and every 14 days in clay soils. Critical stages for water are tillering (2-4 months) and grand growth (4-10 months). Stop irrigation 30 days before harvest to increase sucrose. For fertilization, conduct a soil test first. A typical recommendation for a 12-month crop is 120 kg N, 60 kg P2O5, and 120 kg K2O per hectare. Split nitrogen: apply half at planting and half at 6-8 weeks after planting. Use urea (46% N) or ammonium sulphate. Phosphorus is applied at planting as DAP or TSP. Potassium as muriate of potash (MOP) at planting. In sandy soils, apply 30 kg/ha of magnesium sulphate if deficiency symptoms (yellowing between veins) appear. Organic farmers can use poultry manure (5-10 t/ha) or compost. Foliar sprays of micronutrients like zinc and boron can correct deficiencies. Over-fertilizing with nitrogen causes lodging (falling over) and reduces sugar content. Always apply fertilizer when soil is moist and cover lightly. Keep records of all applications for future planning..Trim()

Pest and Disease Management in Sugarcane

The most destructive sugarcane pests in Africa are the spotted stem borer (Chilo sacchariphagus), the African sugarcane borer (Eldana saccharina), and termites. Borers tunnel into stalks, reducing sugar yield and causing dead hearts. Monitor with pheromone traps or by scouting for shot-hole damage on leaves. For control, release biological agents like Cotesia flavipes (a parasitic wasp) or apply insecticides such as carbofuran at planting. Termites attack setts and roots; treat setts with imidacloprid before planting. Diseases include smut (caused by Sporisorium scitamineum), ratoon stunting disease (RSD), and mosaic virus. Smut shows as black whip-like structures on the growing point. Uproot and burn infected plants. RSD causes stunted growth and is spread through infected seed cane; use disease-free seed and sterilize cutting tools with 10% bleach. Mosaic virus causes yellow-green mottling; control aphids that spread it. For all diseases, plant resistant varieties. Practice crop rotation with legumes like beans or groundnuts to break pest cycles. Keep fields weed-free because weeds host pests. Avoid over-irrigation, which promotes fungal diseases. Regular field scouting is key: walk your fields weekly and look for unusual symptoms. If you see more than 5% of plants affected, take action. Consult your extension officer for approved pesticides and follow safety instructions. Always wear protective clothing when spraying..Trim()

Harvesting Methods: Manual vs. Mechanical

Harvesting sugarcane at the right time is crucial for maximum sugar recovery. The crop is ready when it is 12-18 months old, depending on variety, and when a sample of stalks shows a Brix reading of 18-22% using a hand refractometer. Harvesting too early gives low sugar; too late gives fibrous, low-juice cane. Manual harvesting is common in Africa: workers use machetes or cane knives to cut stalks at ground level, then remove leaves and tops. A skilled worker can cut 1-2 tonnes per day. Manual harvesting is labor-intensive but gives cleaner cane and is suitable for small fields and uneven terrain. Mechanical harvesting uses combine harvesters that cut, chop, and load cane into trailers. These machines can harvest 200-400 tonnes per day but are expensive and require large, flat fields. They also leave more trash in the cane, which can reduce sugar recovery. In Africa, mechanical harvesters are used on large estates in South Africa, Zambia, and Zimbabwe. For most smallholders, manual harvesting is the only option. After cutting, leave the cane in windrows for a day to dry slightly, then load onto trucks. Avoid burning before harvest unless necessary; burning removes leaves but can damage cane if too hot. If you burn, do it 2-3 hours before cutting and ensure the fire does not spread. Harvest during the dry season when sugar content is highest. Cut stalks close to the ground (within 2 cm) to maximize yield. After harvest, ratoon the crop for 2-3 more cycles before replanting..Trim()

Post-Harvest Handling and Transport to Mill

After cutting, sugarcane starts losing sugar immediately. The sucrose content drops by about 0.5% per day if left in the field. Therefore, transport to the mill should happen within 24-48 hours. First, load cane onto trucks or tractor-drawn trailers. For manual loading, use pitchforks or hands; for mechanical, use grab loaders. Stack cane loosely to allow air circulation and prevent heating. Do not overload trucks; keep loads within legal weight limits. If the mill is far (over 50 km), cover the load with a tarpaulin to reduce moisture loss. In hot weather, sprinkle water on the cane to keep it cool. At the mill, cane is weighed and tested for sucrose (pol% cane). Payment is based on weight and quality. To maintain quality, avoid mixing old and fresh cane. If you must store cane temporarily, keep it in a shaded area and do not pile it more than 1.5 m high. For smallholder cooperatives, schedule harvesting so that trucks arrive at the mill just in time. Delays cause sugar loss and lower income. Also, clean the cane: remove excess trash, roots, and soil. Mud on cane adds weight but reduces sugar content and can damage mill equipment. After delivery, the mill may return molasses or bagasse as by-products; use them for animal feed or compost. Keep records of each load: weight, date, and quality. This helps you track performance and plan better..Trim()

Chemical Reactions in Clarification: Lime and CO2

When sugarcane juice comes from the mill, it is cloudy and acidic, with a pH around 5.0 to 5.5. It contains tiny particles of bagasse, soil, waxes, gums, and dissolved impurities like organic acids and proteins. Clarification uses two simple chemicals to clean it: lime (calcium hydroxide) and carbon dioxide gas. Lime is a white powder that you mix with water to make milk of lime. When you add it to the juice, it raises the pH and reacts with acids to form insoluble salts. For example, lime reacts with phosphoric acid to form calcium phosphate, which traps tiny particles. Principles of Multiple-Effect Evaporation

In sugar processing, evaporation removes water from clarified juice to concentrate it to about 60-65 Brix. Multiple-effect evaporation uses a series of vessels (effects) where steam heats juice, and the vapour produced in one effect heats the next effect. This saves energy because each kilogram of steam evaporates multiple kilograms of water. The first effect uses live steam from the boiler, typically at 120-130Â°C. The vapour from the first effect (at about 100Â°C) goes to the second effect, and so on. Each subsequent effect operates at lower pressure and temperature. A common setup in African sugar factories is a quadruple or quintuple effect evaporator. For example, a factory crushing 100 tonnes of cane per hour produces about 100 tonnes of juice at 15 Brix. To concentrate to 65 Brix, you need to evaporate about 77 tonnes of water per hour. With a quintuple effect, steam economy can be 4.5 kg water evaporated per kg steam, so you need only about 17 tonnes of steam per hour. That saves huge amounts of bagasse, which can be used for cogeneration. The key principle: the number of effects determines steam economy. More effects mean better fuel efficiency but also higher capital cost and more complexity. In Africa, where bagasse is often used as fuel, a 4 or 5 effect evaporator is typical. The temperature difference between live steam and the last effect condenser drives the evaporation. The last effect usually operates under vacuum at about 55-60Â°C to avoid caramelisation and to use low-pressure vapour. Understanding this cascade is essential for any operator..Trim()

Evaporator Design and Operation

Evaporators in sugar factories are usually of the Robert or falling-film type. Robert evaporators have vertical tubes with juice inside and steam outside. Falling-film evaporators have juice falling as a thin film inside tubes, which gives better heat transfer and shorter residence time. In Africa, many older factories still use Robert evaporators, but new installations often choose falling-film for energy efficiency. Key design parameters include heating surface area, tube length and diameter, and vapour space. For a 100 t/h factory, total heating surface might be 3000-5000 mÂ² across all effects. The first effect may have 800 mÂ², and the last effect 1200 mÂ² because the vapour is less dense. Operation requires careful control of juice level, steam pressure, and vacuum. Juice level should cover the tubes but not flood the vapour space. If the level is too low, tubes dry out and scale bakes on. If too high, juice carries over into the vapour line, contaminating condensate. Steam pressure to the first effect is typically 1.5-2.5 bar gauge. Vacuum in the last effect is maintained by a condenser and vacuum pump, usually at 0.15-0.2 bar absolute. Operators must monitor temperatures, pressures, and Brix at each effect. A common problem is entrainment, where juice droplets get carried with vapour. This is prevented by installing demister pads or cyclones. Regular checking of condensate for sugar (using a refractometer or simple taste) helps detect entrainment early. In many African factories, evaporator operators manually adjust valves, but modern plants use PLC control. Steam Economy and Energy Efficiency

Steam economy is the kilograms of water evaporated per kilogram of steam supplied to the first effect. In a well-run quintuple evaporator, economy can reach 4.5 to 5.0. That means 1 tonne of steam evaporates 4.5 tonnes of water. If your factory uses 17 tonnes of steam per hour to evaporate 77 tonnes of water, your economy is 4.5. To improve economy, you can add more effects, but there are limits. The temperature difference between live steam and last effect vapour must be enough to drive heat transfer. If you have too many effects, the temperature drop per effect becomes too small, and boiling point elevation (due to dissolved solids) reduces available driving force. In Africa, bagasse is often the fuel. Saving steam means more bagasse for cogeneration or sale. Simple measures can improve efficiency: (1) Insulate steam lines and evaporator bodies to reduce heat loss. (2) Remove air from steam spacesâair is a poor conductor. Install air vents and ensure they work. (3) Clean tubes regularlyâscale acts as insulation. (4) Maintain vacuum in the last effectâpoor vacuum raises boiling temperature and reduces economy. (5) Use vapour bleeding: take vapour from middle effects to heat juice heaters or for pan boiling. This reduces steam demand. For example, bleeding 2 tonnes/h of vapour from the third effect to a juice heater saves 2 tonnes/h of live steam. Monitor steam flow, juice flow, and Brix to calculate economy daily. A drop in economy indicates a problem: scale, air leaks, or entrainment. In many African factories, steam economy is not measured, so operators miss opportunities. Train staff to record data and calculate economy weekly. Even a 5% improvement can save thousands of dollars in bagasse or fuel oil..Trim()

Scaling and Cleaning of Evaporators

Scale is the hard deposit that forms on evaporator tubes when dissolved solids precipitate. In sugar juice, scale is mainly calcium sulphate, calcium carbonate, silica, and organic matter. Scale reduces heat transfer, so you burn more steam to evaporate the same water. A 1 mm scale layer can increase steam consumption by 10-15%. In Africa, where juice quality varies, scaling can be severe. Symptoms include rising steam pressure, falling evaporation rate, and higher Brix in later effects. To control scale, first ensure proper clarificationâremove as much calcium and impurities as possible. Add scale inhibitors if needed, but many African factories rely on regular cleaning. Cleaning methods: (1) Mechanical: for Robert evaporators, operators may use brushes or scrapers, but this is labour-intensive and can damage tubes. (2) Chemical: circulate dilute hydrochloric acid (2-5%) or sulphamic acid to dissolve carbonate and sulphate scale. Caustic soda (1-2%) removes organic scale. Always neutralise and rinse well after cleaning. (3) Boiling out: fill with water and add soda ash, then boil to soften scale. Frequency depends on juice quality. Some factories clean every 2 weeks, others every 3 months. A common practice is to clean when steam economy drops by 10% or when evaporation rate falls. In African conditions, high silica in cane (from soil) can cause hard scale. Use of a pre-evaporator or juice softening can help. After cleaning, inspect tubes for cracks and replace if needed. Safety: acid cleaning requires goggles, gloves, and ventilation. Never add water to acidâadd acid to water. Train a cleaning team and keep records of cleaning dates and results. Monitoring Brix and Concentration Control

Brix measures the concentration of dissolved solids in juice or syrup. In evaporation, you must control the final syrup Brix to about 60-65 Brix. Too low, and you waste energy in the pans; too high, and you risk crystallisation in the evaporator or sugar loss. The most common tool is a refractometer. In African factories, handheld refractometers are used to check Brix at each effect. For continuous monitoring, inline refractometers or density meters are installed. Operators should take samples from each effect every hour. The Brix should increase gradually: first effect maybe 20 Brix, second 30, third 40, fourth 50, fifth 60-65. If Brix is not rising as expected, there may be scale, low steam, or high juice flow. Control is done by adjusting juice feed rate or steam pressure. If final Brix is too low, reduce feed rate or increase steam. If too high, increase feed. In many African factories, the evaporator operator manually controls a valve on the juice feed. This requires constant attention. A simple rule: if the last effect Brix is below 60, you are evaporating too much water later in the pans, wasting energy. If above 65, you may get sugar colour problems and scaling. Also monitor syrup temperatureâit should be about 60-65Â°C. Use a thermometer. Record Brix, temperature, and steam pressure every hour in a logbook. This data helps troubleshoot. For example, if Brix drops suddenly, check for a leak in the juice line or a drop in steam pressure. Train operators to trust their refractometer and to calibrate it daily with distilled water. A clean prism is essential. In Africa, where ambient temperatures are high, refractometers should be temperature-compensated. Consistent Brix control leads to better sugar quality and lower energy costs..Trim()

Troubleshooting Evaporator Performance

When an evaporator is not performing, you see symptoms: low evaporation rate, high steam consumption, low Brix, or high Brix in early effects. Start by checking the basics: steam pressure, vacuum, juice level, and feed rate. Common problems and solutions: (1) Low steam pressure: check boiler and steam valves. If pressure is below 1.5 bar, evaporation drops. (2) Poor vacuum in last effect: check condenser water flow, vacuum pump, and air leaks. A vacuum of 0.2 bar absolute is typical. If vacuum is lost, boiling point rises, reducing temperature difference. (3) Scale: if steam pressure is normal but evaporation is low, scale is likely. Clean tubes. (4) Entrainment: if condensate tastes sweet or refractometer shows Brix in condensate, juice is carrying over. Check demister, reduce juice level, or lower steam pressure. (5) Air in steam space: air reduces heat transfer. Open air vents and ensure they are not blocked. (6) High juice level: floods tubes, reduces evaporation, and causes entrainment. Adjust level control. (7) Low juice level: tubes dry, scale bakes, and tubes can crack. Maintain level. (8) Blocked tubes: in Robert evaporators, tubes can block with scale or debris. Clean mechanically. (9) Condensate blockage: if condensate cannot drain, steam space floods. Check traps and valves. (10) Brix too high: reduce feed or increase juice flow. In African factories, power cuts are common, causing vacuum loss and juice sitting in tubes. After a power cut, restart carefully: restore vacuum, then steam, then feed. Keep a troubleshooting chart near the evaporator. Train operators to identify the most likely cause first: scale and air leaks are the most common. Regular maintenance and record-keeping prevent many problems. If performance drops suddenly, check for a recent change in juice quality or a mechanical failure. A systematic approach saves time and energy..Trim()

Affination and Melting of Raw Sugar

Raw sugar from a mill is covered in a film of molasses. Affination removes this film by mixing the raw sugar with a heavy syrup (about 70-75 Brix) in a magma mixer. The mixture is then spun in centrifugals to separate the syrup from the sugar crystals. In Africa, many refineries use batch centrifugals for this step. The syrup, called affination syrup, is recycled or sold as a byproduct. After affination, the sugar is melted with hot water or sweet water to form a liquor of 60-65 Brix. This melting step is done in a melter equipped with steam coils and agitation. The liquor is then screened to remove debris like bagasse, sand, and metal pieces. In small-scale operations in countries like Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa, a simple tank with a stirrer and steam injection can be used. The melted liquor is then pumped to clarification. Key parameters: affination syrup Brix should be 70-75, temperature around 60-70Â°C. Melting water temperature should be 80-90Â°C. The purity of the melted liquor should be at least 98%. This step is crucial because it reduces color and ash, making subsequent refining easier. Without proper affination, the final white sugar will have high color and may fail quality standards..Trim()

Clarification and Decolorization: Carbonatation and Phosphatation

After melting, the raw liquor still contains impurities like gums, waxes, and colorants. Clarification removes these. Two main methods are used: carbonatation and phosphatation. In carbonatation, lime (calcium hydroxide) is added to the liquor to raise pH to about 8.5-9.0. Then carbon dioxide gas is bubbled through, forming calcium carbonate precipitate. This precipitate traps impurities and color. The mixture is then filtered. Carbonatation is common in large refineries in South Africa and Egypt. In phosphatation, phosphoric acid is added, followed by lime to form calcium phosphate precipitate. This method is simpler and used in smaller plants. The pH is adjusted to about 7.5-8.0. After precipitation, a flocculant is added to help settle the precipitate. The clear liquor is then decolorized using activated carbon or bone char. In Africa, bone char is used in some refineries, but activated carbon is more common due to availability. The decolorization step reduces color from about 1000 ICUMSA to less than 100. Temperature during clarification is kept at 70-80Â°C. Proper control of pH and chemical dosage is critical. Over-liming can cause scaling, while under-liming leaves impurities. This step ensures the final sugar is bright and pure..Trim()

Filtration and Ion Exchange Resins

Filtration removes the precipitate formed during clarification. Common equipment includes plate-and-frame filter presses, rotary vacuum filters, and pressure leaf filters. In African refineries, plate-and-frame presses are often used because they are robust and easy to maintain. Filter aids like diatomaceous earth (DE) are used to improve filtration. The filtered liquor should be clear, with turbidity less than 5 NTU. After filtration, the liquor may still contain dissolved color and ions. Ion exchange resins are used for further decolorization and demineralization. Strong base anion resins remove color bodies, while cation resins remove metal ions like calcium and magnesium. In a typical setup, the liquor passes through a series of columns: first a cation resin (to remove cations), then an anion resin (to remove anions and color). The resins are regenerated with sodium chloride (for anion) and hydrochloric acid (for cation). In small-scale operations in Africa, ion exchange may be skipped if the sugar is for local markets with less strict color standards. However, for export-quality white sugar, ion exchange is essential. The resin beds must be backwashed regularly to prevent fouling. The flow rate should be controlled to ensure proper contact time. This step can reduce color to below 20 ICUMSA and ash to less than 0.01%..Trim()

Crystallization in Refining: White Sugar Production

Crystallization is the heart of white sugar production. The clarified and decolorized liquor is concentrated in evaporators to about 65-70 Brix, then further boiled in vacuum pans to form crystals. In refining, the process is done in multiple stages: first, a high-purity liquor is boiled to produce white sugar crystals. The vacuum pan is operated under vacuum to lower the boiling temperature to about 60-70Â°C, preventing color formation. Seed crystals are added to start crystallization. The pan is then fed with more liquor until the crystals reach the desired size (typically 0.4-0.6 mm). The mixture, called massecuite, is then dropped into crystallizers for cooling and further crystal growth. In Africa, many refineries use batch vacuum pans. The boiling time can be 2-4 hours. The purity of the mother liquor drops as sugar crystallizes. The massecuite is then sent to centrifugals. The first crop of crystals is the white sugar. The mother liquor (run-off) is reboiled to produce lower-grade sugars, which are recycled back to affination or sold as brown sugar. Key control points: vacuum level (about 600-650 mmHg), temperature, and supersaturation (1.1-1.3). Proper seeding and boiling ensure uniform crystal size and good yield..Trim()

Centrifugation and Drying of Refined Sugar

After crystallization, the massecuite is separated into crystals and mother liquor using centrifugals. In refining, high-speed batch centrifugals are used for white sugar. The centrifugal basket has a fine screen (0.06-0.08 mm openings) that retains crystals while allowing syrup to pass. The cycle includes loading, spinning, washing, and discharging. Washing is done with hot water (about 80-90Â°C) to remove the film of mother liquor from the crystals. The wash water is then spun off. The spinning time is typically 3-5 minutes. The sugar is then discharged and sent to dryers. Drying reduces moisture from about 1-2% to less than 0.05%. Common dryers are rotary drum dryers and fluidized bed dryers. In African refineries, rotary dryers are common. Hot air at 60-80Â°C is passed through the dryer. After drying, the sugar is cooled to below 30Â°C and then screened to remove lumps and fines. The final product is stored in silos or bags. Proper centrifugation and drying are critical for sugar quality: moisture above 0.05% can cause caking, and residual syrup can cause color development. The centrifugals must be well maintained to avoid screen damage, which can lead to sugar loss..Trim()

Refined sugar must meet strict quality standards. Key parameters include: color (ICUMSA), moisture, ash, invert sugar, and particle size. For white sugar, ICUMSA color should be less than 45 (often 20-30 for premium grade). Moisture should be below 0.05%. Ash content should be less than 0.01%.
