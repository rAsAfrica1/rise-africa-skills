Module 1 · Why Milk Is Daily Cash

Introduction and Why It Matters

Milk is money that walks on four legs. Unlike a goat you sell once at the market, a dairy cow or goat gives you cash every single day. In many parts of Africa, a good dairy cow can produce 10 to 20 litres of milk per day. If you sell that milk at 50 shillings per litre, that is 500 to 1,000 shillings daily. Over a month, that is 15,000 to 30,000 shillings. That is school fees, medicine, and food on the table..Trim()

.Trim()

But why is milk called 'daily cash'? Because it does not wait for harvest season. Every morning and evening, you milk your animals and you have a product that people need every day. Tea shops, hotels, and families buy milk daily. You do not need a big farm to start. One improved dairy cow or three dairy goats can be enough to begin. The key is to keep the animal healthy and milking steadily..Trim()

.Trim()

Many farmers fail because they treat dairy as a side activity. They milk when they remember, feed poor-quality grass, and sell to anyone who passes by. That is not a system. A dairy herd system means you plan: you feed, you breed, you milk, you sell, and you save. You keep records of how much milk each animal gives, how much feed you use, and how much money comes in. That way you know if you are making a profit or just working hard for nothing..Trim()

By the end, you will understand why milk is the fastest way to turn grass into cash. You will be ready to start or improve your own daily milk business. Remember: the cow does not know it is a business. You must be the one who makes it one..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To sell milk daily, you need a few basic tools and materials. You do not need expensive equipment to start. But you must have clean, food-grade items. A food-grade bucket or milking can is the first tool. It should be stainless steel or plastic that is approved for milk. Never use a bucket that held chemicals or petrol. You need a clean cloth or filter to strain the milk after milking. A simple cotton cloth, boiled and dried, works. You also need a chiller or refrigerator to keep milk cold. If you have no electricity, you can use a charcoal cooler or a deep freezer with ice packs. In many African markets, you can sell milk immediately, but if you want to keep it for hours, cold is your friend..Trim()

For feeding and health, you need a water trough, a mineral block, and a feed scoop. A dairy cow drinks 60 to 100 litres of water per day. Without water, milk production drops fast. You also need a record book and a pen. Write down the date, animal name, amount of milk in litres, and amount of money from sales. This record is your business brain. You also need a clean milking parlour or a simple stall where the animal stands still. A rope or halter keeps the cow calm..Trim()

.Trim()

For selling, you need clean containers for customers. One-litre or half-litre plastic bottles or jerrycans with tight lids. If you sell to a milk collection centre, they may provide their own cans. You also need a weighing scale or a measuring jug to measure litres accurately. Customers trust you when you measure correctly. Finally, you need a simple first-aid kit for the animal: iodine for the teat after milking, and a wound spray. Keep these tools clean every day. Wash buckets with warm water and soap, then rinse with boiling water. Dirty tools spoil milk and make customers sick. Your tools are your reputation..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Step 1: Prepare the milking area. Tie your cow or goat in a clean, quiet place. Wash your hands with soap and water. Wash the animal's udder with clean warm water and dry it with a clean cloth. This removes dirt and helps milk let down..Trim()

Step 2: Milk gently but quickly. Use your whole hand, not just fingers. Squeeze and pull down in a rhythm. The first few squirts should go into a separate cup, not the main bucket, because they may have bacteria. Milk each teat until it is empty. Emptying the udder fully tells the cow to produce more next time. A good cow takes 5 to 10 minutes to milk..Trim()

Step 3: Strain the milk immediately. Pour it through a clean cloth into a clean container. This removes dust, hair, and insects. Then cool it quickly. If you have a fridge, put it in the fridge. If not, put the container in a basin of cold water or a charcoal cooler..Trim()

Step 4: Measure and record. Use a measuring jug to see how many litres you got. Write it in your record book. For example, 'Cow A: 12 litres morning.' Do this every milking, morning and evening..Trim()

Step 5: Sell your milk. You can sell to neighbours, tea shops, hotels, or a milk collection centre. Set a fair price. In many areas, fresh milk sells for 40 to 60 shillings per litre. If you sell 10 litres, that is 400 to 600 shillings. Sell quickly while it is fresh. If you have extra, you can make yoghurt or cheese to avoid waste..Trim()

Step 6: Clean everything. Wash buckets, cloths, and containers with hot water and soap. Rinse well. Leave them to dry in the sun. Clean tools mean safe milk and happy customers..Trim()

Step 7: Feed and water your animal after milking. Give her good hay, silage, or fresh grass, plus dairy meal. A cow giving 15 litres needs about 1 kg of dairy meal for every 2.5 litres of milk. Always provide clean water. Happy, well-fed animals give more milk tomorrow. Repeat this every day, without fail. That is the daily cash system..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not milking at the same time every day. Cows are creatures of habit. If you milk at 6 a.m. one day and 9 a.m. the next, production drops. Fix: Set a fixed schedule, morning and evening, 12 hours apart. Write it on your wall..Trim()

Mistake 2: Leaving milk in a warm place. Warm milk spoils in 2 hours. Fix: Cool it immediately after straining. Use a fridge, or a charcoal cooler with wet sand and a wet cloth on top. In hot areas, sell within 1 hour if you cannot cool..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 3: Using dirty containers. Milk absorbs smells and bacteria. A bucket that held soap will ruin the taste. Fix: Use only food-grade buckets. Wash with hot water and no strong soap. Rinse with boiling water before use..Trim()

Mistake 4: Not feeding enough. A cow cannot give milk from nothing. If you give only dry grass, she will give 2 litres instead of 10. Fix: Give balanced feed: green fodder, hay, silage, and dairy meal. Add mineral salt. Water is the cheapest feed. Always have clean water available..Trim()

Mistake 5: Selling to anyone without measuring. If you guess litres, you lose money. Fix: Use a measuring jug or scale. Record every sale. Know your daily average..Trim()

Mistake 6: Ignoring animal health. A sick cow gives less milk. Mastitis (udder infection) is common. Fix: Wash udder before and after milking. Dry teats with iodine. If you see lumps or blood, call a vet. Treat quickly..Trim()

Mistake 7: Not keeping records. You cannot know profit without records. Fix: Keep a simple notebook. Write milk litres, feed cost, and sales. At the end of the week, subtract costs from sales. That is your profit. If it is low, find the problem..Trim()

Mistake 8: Giving up too soon. Dairy takes 2 to 3 months to stabilise. Fix: Be patient. Follow the system. Improve one thing at a time. Soon you will have daily cash..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Safety first for you and your customers. Milk can carry diseases like tuberculosis and brucellosis. Always boil milk before drinking or selling to customers who will not boil it. If you sell raw milk, tell customers to boil it. Wash your hands with soap before and after milking. Keep your fingernails short and clean. Do not milk if you have open cuts on your hands unless you cover them with a waterproof plaster and glove..Trim()

.Trim()

Keep the milking area clean. Remove manure daily. Do not let animals lie in mud. A clean shed reduces flies and bacteria. Use safe cleaning chemicals. Never mix bleach with acid. Store chemicals away from milk containers. Use food-grade sanitisers if available. Rinse everything with clean water after washing..Trim()

Handle animals calmly. Do not hit or shout at cows. Stress reduces milk let-down and can cause kicking. Use a halter and tie the cow short so she cannot step in the bucket. If she kicks, use a leg rope gently. Never kneel behind a cow. Stand to the side..Trim()

Best practice for milk quality: cool milk to 4Â°C within 2 hours. Keep it cold until sale. Do not mix warm milk with cold milk. Use a thermometer if you can. If you sell to a collection centre, follow their quality rules. They may test for water, antibiotics, and bacteria. Never add water to milk. It is cheating and can make people sick. Never sell milk from a cow treated with antibiotics until the withdrawal period is over (usually 3 to 5 days). Ask your vet..Trim()

Keep records for traceability. If a customer gets sick, you can check your records. Also, keep your animals vaccinated. Common vaccines: anthrax, blackquarter, and foot-and-mouth. Deworm every 3 months. These steps protect your business and your community. Safe milk is profitable milk..Trim()

Module 2 · The Dairy Herd Formula

Introduction and Why It Matters

Welcome to Module 2. In Module 1, you learned why selling milk daily can change your life. Now we get to the heart of the business: the dairy herd formula. This formula is simple: your daily milk sales depend on three numbers: how many cows you milk, how much milk each cow gives, and how many days you sell. But the real magic is in the fourth number: your cost per litre. If you know these numbers, you can predict your profit and fix problems before they eat your money. In Africa, many small dairy farmers fail because they guess. They guess how much milk they get, guess how much feed costs, and guess why some cows give less. Guessing leads to losses. The dairy herd formula replaces guessing with counting. For example, if you have 5 cows giving 8 litres each, that's 40 litres a day. If you sell at 50 KES per litre, that's 2,000 KES daily. But if your feed, labour, and health costs are 1,500 KES, your profit is only 500 KES. Now imagine you improve feed and each cow gives 12 litres. You now have 60 litres, earning 3,000 KES, and profit jumps to 1,500 KES. That's the power of the formula. It also helps you decide when to cull a cow, when to buy feed in bulk, and when to hire help. This module will teach you the tools, the step-by-step method, common mistakes, safety, and give you a practical exercise. By the end, you will be able to calculate your own herd's daily milk sales and profit with confidence. Remember: what gets measured gets managed. Let's start measuring..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To use the dairy herd formula, you don't need expensive equipment. You need a few simple tools that cost little but save you big money. First, a record book. Any exercise book will do, but a hardcover one lasts longer. You will record daily milk yield per cow, feed given, and any health issues. Second, a measuring jug or a calibrated bucket. You must measure milk accurately, not by eye. A 10-litre plastic bucket with litre markings is perfect. Third, a weighing scale for feed. A simple hanging scale or a bathroom scale works. You need to know exactly how much feed each cow gets. Fourth, a chalkboard or whiteboard for your shed. Write today's milk total and feed cost so everyone sees it. Fifth, a mobile phone. Use it for a calculator, a calendar, and to take photos of records. You can also use free apps like 'Milk Manager' or 'Farm Records' if you have internet. Sixth, a notebook for individual cow cards. Each cow gets a page: her name or number, calving date, daily milk, and breeding dates. Seventh, a simple first aid kit for udder care: clean cloths, iodine, and teat dip. Eighth, a feed storage container with a lid to keep feed dry and safe from rats. Ninth, a water bucket or trough with measurements so you know water intake. Tenth, a pen and permanent marker to label cow cards. All these tools together cost less than 2,000 KES if you buy locally. In Africa, you can find them at agrovet shops or markets. The most important tool is discipline: use them every day, at the same time. Without records, the formula is just numbers on paper. With records, it becomes your roadmap to profit..Trim()

At each milking (morning and evening), use your calibrated bucket. Record the litres for each cow separately. If you milk twice, add both. Step 3: Total daily milk. Add all cows' litres. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not measuring milk per cow. Many farmers just look at the total bucket. Fix: Use individual cow records. You cannot manage what you don't measure. Mistake 2: Guessing feed amounts. If you feed by handfuls, you overfeed some and underfeed others. Fix: Weigh feed with a scale. Use a standard measure like a 2kg tin. Mistake 3: Ignoring cost per litre. You might sell milk at 50 KES but spend 45 KES to produce it. Fix: Calculate cost per litre weekly. If it's above 70% of selling price, find savings. Mistake 4: Feeding low-quality forage. In dry season, poor grass gives less milk. Fix: Invest in hay or silage. One bale of hay can add 2 litres per cow per day. Mistake 5: Not culling unproductive cows. A cow that gives 3 litres but eats like a 15-litre cow is a parasite. Fix: Use the formula: if a cow's daily feed cost exceeds her milk value for 3 months, sell her. Mistake 6: Water shortage. Cows need 60-100 litres of water daily. If water is limited, milk drops. Fix: Provide clean water always. A cow giving 20 litres needs 80 litres of water. Mistake 7: Poor record keeping. Records on loose papers get lost. Fix: Use a single notebook or a phone app. Back up with photos. Mistake 8: Selling milk on credit. You lose cash flow and can't buy feed. Fix: Sell for cash or mobile money. If you must give credit, limit to trusted customers and keep a ledger. Mistake 9: Ignoring cow health. Mastitis, ticks, and worms reduce milk. Fix: Dip cows weekly, deworm every 3 months, and check udders daily. Mistake 10: Overstocking. Too many cows on small land leads to feed shortage. Fix: Keep only as many cows as your land and budget can feed well. Each mistake has a fix. Start with the biggest one in your herd today..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Safety in dairy farming protects you, your workers, and your cows. First, personal safety. Always wear clean overalls and gumboots when handling cows. Cows can kick, especially during milking. Never stand directly behind a cow. Use a kick bar or tie her legs gently if she is nervous. Wash your hands with soap before and after milking. Second, milk safety. Clean the udder with a clean cloth and warm water before milking. Use a separate cloth for each cow to prevent mastitis spread. After milking, dip teats in iodine solution. Filter milk immediately using a clean cloth or filter. Cool milk to 4Â°C within 2 hours if you sell to a processor. If you sell raw, keep it in a clean container and sell within 4 hours. Third, feed safety. Never feed mouldy hay or silage. Mould causes aflatoxin, which passes into milk and harms people. Store feed on pallets, off the floor, in a dry place. Keep rats out. Fourth, chemical safety. Store veterinary drugs, pesticides, and dips away from feed and milk. Use gloves when handling. Follow withdrawal periods: do not sell milk from a cow treated with antibiotics for at least 3 days after last dose. Fifth, animal welfare. Provide shade, clean bedding, and space. Cows that are stressed give less milk. Handle them calmly. Sixth, record safety. Keep records in a safe, dry place. Take photos as backup. Seventh, financial safety. Keep business money separate from household money. Pay yourself a salary. Save 10% of profit for emergencies. Eighth, best practice: milk at the same times daily, 12 hours apart. Keep a regular routine. Train your workers on these practices. Post a simple checklist on the shed wall. Review safety monthly. A safe farm is a profitable farm..Trim()

Module 3 · The Lactation Curve

Introduction and Why It Matters

The lactation curve is the pattern of milk production from a cow from the day she calves until she dries off. For a dairy herd system selling milk daily, understanding this curve is the difference between profit and loss. A healthy cow typically peaks in milk yield about 6 to 8 weeks after calving, producing up to 20-30 litres per day for a crossbreed like Friesian x Zebu. After that peak, production slowly declines by about 5-10% each month. If you know the curve, you can plan feed, labour, and sales. For example, a cow giving 20 litres at peak will give about 15 litres at month 4 and 10 litres at month 8. Without this knowledge, you might overfeed or underfeed, leading to thin cows and low milk. In Africa, where many farmers rely on daily milk sales for cash flow, missing the peak means missing the highest income period. Also, the curve helps you decide when to rebreed. A cow should be bred again 60-90 days after calving to maintain a 12-13 month calving interval. If she is not bred, her lactation extends but total daily milk drops, and you lose money. The curve also signals health problems: a sudden drop can mean mastitis, foot rot, or poor feed. By recording daily milk with a simple notebook or a phone app, you learn your herd's normal curve. Then you can act fast. In this module, you will learn the tools, steps, mistakes, safety, and practice to master the lactation curve for your daily milk sales..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To track and manage the lactation curve, you need simple, affordable tools. First, a milk record book or a notebook with columns for date, cow ID, morning milk (litres), evening milk (litres), and total. A hardcover notebook costs less than 5,000 shillings and lasts a year. Second, a measuring jug or graduated bucket, preferably plastic with clear markings up to 30 litres. Metal buckets can dent and hide markings. Third, a permanent marker to label the bucket with cow ID if you have multiple cows. Fourth, a calendar or wall chart to mark calving dates and breeding dates. You can use a simple wall calendar and a red pen. Fifth, a weighing scale for feed, because feed affects the curve. A hanging scale up to 50 kg is useful. Sixth, a thermometer to check for fever if milk drops suddenly. Seventh, a phone with a calculator and a simple app like 'Dairy Record' or even a spreadsheet. If you have no smartphone, a notebook is enough. Eighth, a feed chart showing how much concentrate and forage each cow needs at different lactation stages. For example, a cow giving 20 litres needs about 1 kg of dairy meal per 2.5 litres of milk plus 2 kg for maintenance. Ninth, a body condition score (BCS) chart: you can print one from the internet or draw simple silhouettes. Tenth, a first-aid kit for udder injuries: antiseptic, cotton wool, and gloves. Finally, a whiteboard or chalkboard in the milking shed to write today's totals and compare to yesterday. All these tools cost less than 20,000 shillings total and are available in local agro-vet shops or markets. With them, you can plot your cow's lactation curve on graph paper or just list weekly averages..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to track and use the lactation curve. Step 1: Record calving date. Write it on the calendar and in your notebook. This is day 0. Step 2: Measure milk every milking. Use your graduated bucket. Record morning and evening yields separately. Do this for at least the first 10 days, then weekly if labour is tight. Step 3: Calculate daily total and weekly average. Add morning and evening. For a week, sum all daily totals and divide by 7. Step 4: Plot the curve. On graph paper, put weeks on the x-axis and litres on the y-axis. Mark each weekly average. You will see a rise to peak around week 6-8, then a gradual decline. Step 5: Compare to expected. For a crossbreed, peak should be 15-25 litres. If peak is below 10 litres, check feed, water, and health. Step 6: Adjust feed. As milk rises, increase concentrate by 0.5 kg for every extra litre above 10 litres. For example, if she gives 20 litres, feed 4 kg dairy meal plus 2 kg for maintenance = 6 kg per day, split into two feeds. Step 7: Monitor decline. After peak, milk drops about 5% per month. If it drops more than 10% in a month, investigate: mastitis, worms, or heat stress. Step 8: Decide breeding date. At 60-90 days after calving, if the cow is healthy and giving above 10 litres, breed her. Step 9: Dry off at 7 months pregnant or when milk falls below 5 litres per day. Step 10: Review records. At drying off, calculate total milk for the lactation. Compare to previous lactations. Use this to cull poor producers and keep good ones. Repeat for each cow. This method works for one cow or fifty. The key is consistency: record every day, even if you only note totals..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not recording milk daily. Many farmers guess. Fix: Keep a notebook in the milking shed. Write with a pencil if ink runs. Mistake 2: Feeding the same amount all year. A cow at peak needs more feed than a dry cow. Fix: Use the 1 kg dairy meal per 2.5 litres rule. Adjust weekly. Mistake 3: Ignoring sudden drops. A drop of 3 litres in one day can mean mastitis. Fix: Check udder for heat, swelling, or clots. Strip milk onto a dark cloth to see clots. Call a vet if fever. Mistake 4: Breeding too late. Waiting beyond 90 days reduces lifetime milk. Fix: Mark calendar 60 days after calving. If cow is healthy, breed at observed heat. Mistake 5: Overmilking or incomplete milking. This reduces yield and causes mastitis. Fix: Milk until udder is soft, but no longer than 8 minutes per cow. Use clean hands and a clean bucket. Mistake 6: Poor water access. A cow giving 20 litres needs 80-100 litres of water daily. Fix: Provide clean water at all times. A trough with a float valve is best. Mistake 7: Heat stress. In hot African afternoons, milk drops. Fix: Provide shade, fans, or sprinklers. Mistake 8: Not drying off properly. Cows that are milked until calving have weak calves and low next lactation. Fix: Dry off 60 days before expected calving. Reduce milking frequency over one week. Mistake 9: Using wrong BCS. Thin cows (BCS below 2.5) give less milk. Fix: Score monthly. Feed extra to thin cows. Mistake 10: No records for individual cows. You cannot cull poor performers. Fix: Use ear tags or names. Record per cow. Each mistake has a simple fix. Start with daily recording and correct feeding..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Safety first: When handling cows for milking or checking the lactation curve, wear clean overalls and boots. Wash hands with soap before milking. Use a clean bucket and a strip cup to check for mastitis. Never milk a cow with a fever or obvious udder infection without gloves. If you suspect mastitis, isolate the cow and milk her last. Dispose of infected milk properly; do not sell it. Best practice for record keeping: Use a pencil and a notebook that stays in the milking shed. At the end of each week, transfer totals to a master sheet. Keep records for at least three lactations. This helps you see patterns. Best practice for feeding: Weigh feed, don't guess. Use a hanging scale. Store feed in dry, raised place to avoid mould. Mouldy feed can cause abortion and milk drop. Best practice for water: Clean troughs daily. A cow drinks 3-5 litres per litre of milk. So a 20-litre cow needs 60-100 litres. Best practice for milking hygiene: Use a single-use paper towel or clean cloth to dry udder. Use teat dip after milking. This prevents mastitis. Best practice for breeding: Keep a breeding calendar. Observe heat twice daily for 30 minutes. Record all heats. If a cow is not bred by 90 days, consult an AI technician. Best practice for drying off: Reduce milking gradually over 7 days. Give dry cow therapy if available. Best practice for heat stress: Provide shade trees or a simple roof. Sprinklers in the afternoon can increase milk by 10-15%. Best practice for safety of people: Never stand directly behind a cow. Use a crush or head gate for examinations. Keep children away from bulls. Finally, keep a first-aid kit with antiseptic, bandages, and gloves. If you get kicked or injured, clean the wound and seek medical help. Following these practices keeps you, your cows, and your milk safe..Trim()

Module 7 · Health: Mastitis and Other Issues

Introduction and Why It Matters

Mastitis is the most costly disease for small-scale dairy farmers in Africa. It reduces milk yield, lowers milk quality, and can kill cows if not treated. A cow with mastitis may drop from 15 litres per day to 5 litres, and the milk may be unfit for sale. In Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, studies show that up to 40% of dairy cows have subclinical mastitis at any time. That means you lose money without even seeing sick cows. Mastitis is an infection of the udder, usually caused by bacteria entering through the teat canal. It happens when hygiene is poor, when milking is rough, or when cows lie in dirty, wet bedding. The disease comes in two forms: clinical and subclinical. Clinical mastitis shows clear signs: hot, swollen udder, clumpy or bloody milk, and a sick cow. Introduction and Why It Matters

A cow must calve to produce milk. The calving interval is the time from one calving to the next. For a dairy cow to be profitable, she should calve every 12 to 13 months. If the interval stretches to 15 months or more, you lose money. In Africa, many smallholder dairy farms have calving intervals of 16 to 20 months. .Trim()

Why does a long calving interval hurt? A cow gives milk for about 10 months after calving. Then she needs a 2-month dry period before the next calf. .Trim()

In Africa, common problems are poor heat detection, poor nutrition, and diseases like brucellosis. Cows may not show strong signs of heat, especially in hot weather or if they are thin. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Calf rearing involves safety for both you and the calf. Follow these best practices. Personal safety: Always wash your hands with soap after handling calves, especially before eating. Introduction and Why It Matters

Every day you sell milk, money leaves your pocket before you see any profit. These are your expenses. If you don't track them, you might think you're making money when you're actually losing it. For example, a cow eats 10 kg of feed per day at 50 KES per kg, that's 500 KES. She gives 15 litres of milk sold at 60 KES per litre, earning 900 KES. But you also pay for labour, transport, and vet care. Without tracking, you might spend 800 KES on expenses, leaving only 100 KES profit. That's why every shilling counts..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, dairy farmers often face hidden costs: water for cleaning, electricity for cooling, and repairs for milking machines. A smallholder with 5 cows might spend 2,500 KES daily on feed alone. .Trim()

Tracking expenses helps you set the right price for your milk. If your cost per litre is 40 KES and you sell at 50 KES, you make 10 KES per litre. You can save small amounts daily..Trim()
