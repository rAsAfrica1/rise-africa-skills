The Problem With Continuous Farrowing

If you keep a boar running with your sows all year, you get piglets born every week. That sounds good until you try to sell them. Say you have 10 sows. With continuous farrowing, each sow gives birth about twice a year, so you get roughly 20 litters spread across 52 weeks. That's a litter every 2.6 weeks, or about 2-3 piglets weaned per week if each litter has 8 piglets. You end up with a trickle of pigs of all different ages and sizes. Buyers want 20 or 50 pigs of the same size at one time. You can't fill that order. You also waste time: every week you're moving a few pigs, mixing feeds for different age groups, and trying to find a buyer for 3 pigs. Transport costs eat your profit because you're making many small trips. Health suffers too: piglets of different ages mix and pass diseases. And your records become a mess�??you can't tell which sow is performing. The worst part is cash flow: you get small amounts of money irregularly, so you can't plan investments or pay bills. Continuous farrowing is the default for many smallholders, but it's a trap. It keeps you busy but poor. The solution is to group your sows so they give birth at the same time. They need to know that on the 15th of every month, you will have 40 pigs ready. That reliability lets them sign contracts with their customers. They also care about biosecurity: mixing pigs from different sources or ages increases disease risk. Uniform batches are safer. And they want value: if you can deliver 50 pigs at once, you can negotiate a better price per kg because you save them the trouble of sourcing from many farmers. Litters Per Sow Per Year

Litters per sow per year (LPSY) is the number of times a sow gives birth in 12 months. It is the first number you must know because it controls how many pigs you can sell. In Africa, many small farms get only 1.2 to 1.5 litters per sow per year. Good farms get 2.0 to 2.2. The difference is management, not magic..Trim()

To calculate LPSY, use this formula: LPSY = 365 ÷ (gestation + lactation + weaning-to-service interval). Gestation is fixed at 114 days (about 3 months, 3 weeks, 3 days). Lactation is how long you leave piglets on the sow. Weaning-to-service is the days from weaning until the sow is bred again. For example, if you wean at 28 days and the sow is bred 7 days later, the cycle is 114 + 28 + 7 = 149 days. 365 ÷ 149 = 2.45 litters per year. But that is theoretical. In reality, some sows return to heat late, some abort, some die. So you must track actual dates..Trim()

Use a simple notebook or a wall chart. Write the sow's ID, date farrowed, date weaned, date bred back. At the end of the year, count how many litters each sow gave. Average them. If your average is below 2.0, look at three things: (1) Are you weaning too late? Wean at 21-28 days, not 8 weeks. (2) Are you missing heat? Check for standing heat twice a day, every day, after weaning. (3) Are sows too thin or too fat? Body condition score 3 out of 5 is ideal. Feed a flush of 2.5 kg per day from weaning until breeding..Trim()

In Africa, heat stress is a major killer of LPSY. When temperatures rise above 30°C, sows stop cycling. Provide shade, wallows, or sprinklers. Breed early morning or late evening. Module 3 · The Breeding Calendar

Gestation: 114 Days

A sow's pregnancy, called gestation, lasts 114 days on average. That is 3 months, 3 weeks, and 3 days. This number is the foundation of your breeding calendar. If you know the day the sow was served, you can calculate the farrowing date exactly. For example, if a sow is served on January 1st, she will farrow on April 25th. Count 114 days forward. You can use a simple wall calendar and mark the date, or use a phone app like Pig Breeding Calculator. In Africa, where many farmers rely on paper records, a calendar on the wall of the pig house is best. Write the sow's ID and service date in one colour, then count 114 days and write the expected farrowing date in red. This gives you a clear visual reminder. Gestation is divided into three stages: early (0-30 days), middle (30-90 days), and late (90-114 days). During early gestation, the embryos attach to the uterus. Stress, heat, or poor feed can cause them to be reabsorbed. So keep the sow calm and feed her well. In the middle stage, the sow's belly grows slowly. Feed her 2 kg per day of a good sow ration. In the late stage, the piglets grow fast. Increase feed to 2.5-3 kg per day. But do not overfeed, or the sow will get too fat and have difficulty farrowing. Always provide clean water. In hot African climates, make sure the sow has shade and wallows to cool down. Heat stress can cause abortion. Check the sow daily for signs of return to heat, which would mean she is not pregnant. If she returns to heat 21 days after service, she is empty. Re-serve her. If she does not return, she is likely pregnant. Mark the calendar accordingly. A good record system prevents surprises. You will know exactly when to prepare the farrowing pen, when to stop work, and when to expect piglets. This is the first step to daily pig sales..Trim()

Lactation: 28 Days

Lactation is the period when the sow is producing milk for her piglets. In a batch farrowing system, we wean the piglets at 28 days of age. This is a common practice in many African piggeries because it balances piglet health with sow productivity. At 28 days, piglets are strong enough to eat solid feed and no longer need only milk. The sow's lactation lasts exactly 28 days from farrowing to weaning. So if a sow farrows on April 25th, you will wean the piglets on May 23rd. Mark this on your calendar. During lactation, the sow needs high-quality feed and plenty of water. A lactating sow can drink up to 20 litres of water per day, especially in hot weather. Feed her a lactation diet with 16-18% protein. Give her 2 kg on the day of farrowing, then increase gradually to 5-6 kg per day by the second week. She needs this energy to produce milk. If she does not get enough feed, she will lose body condition and produce less milk, and the piglets will be small and weak. Keep the farrowing pen clean and dry. Change bedding daily. In Africa, use dry grass or wood shavings. Make sure the piglets have a warm, dry creep area with a heat lamp or a simple wooden box with a lid. At 28 days, weaning is done abruptly. Remove the sow from the pen and leave the piglets in the same pen for a few days to reduce stress. This is called 'weaner stay'. The sow goes back to the breeding area. After weaning, she will come into heat within 3 to 7 days. That is the next step. Record the weaning date on your calendar. It marks the end of lactation and the start of the sow's rest period. In a batch system, all sows in a group farrow within the same week, so you wean all litters on the same day. This makes management easier and allows you to clean and disinfect the farrowing house between batches. Daily pig sales depend on a steady flow of weaners. So stick to the 28-day lactation..Trim()

Weaning to Service: 7 Days

After weaning, the sow needs a short rest before she is served again. In a batch farrowing system, we plan for 7 days from weaning to service. This is the time it takes for the sow's reproductive tract to recover and for her to show a clear heat. The 7-day interval is not fixed for every sow, but in a well-managed batch system, you want all sows to be served within the same week. So you plan for 7 days. For example, if you wean on May 23rd, you will serve the sow on May 30th. Mark this on your calendar. During these 7 days, feed the sow a good flushing diet. Flushing means giving her extra feed to increase ovulation rate. Give her 3 to 4 kg per day of a sow ration. This will help her produce more eggs and potentially more piglets. Also, provide plenty of water and keep her cool. Heat stress can delay heat or cause silent heat. Check the sow twice a day for signs of heat: swollen vulva, mucus discharge, restlessness, and standing still when you press on her back. This is called the standing reflex. When you see these signs, she is ready to be served. In Africa, many farmers use natural mating with a boar. If you have a boar, bring him to the sow. If not, use artificial insemination (AI). AI is becoming more common and can be done by trained technicians. Serve the sow twice: once when you first see standing heat, and again 12 to 24 hours later. This increases conception rate. Record the service date on your calendar. That date is day 0 for the next gestation. So the cycle continues. If a sow does not show heat within 7 days, wait and check again. She may have a silent heat or a cyst. If she does not conceive, she will return to heat in 21 days. But in a batch system, you want to keep the group together. So if a sow fails to conceive, you may need to move her to another batch or cull her. The 7-day window is critical for keeping the batch on schedule. Without it, your farrowing dates will spread out, and you will not have daily pig sales..Trim()

Total Cycle: 149 Days

The total cycle from one service to the next service is 149 days. That is 114 days gestation plus 28 days lactation plus 7 days weaning to service. This is the heartbeat of your batch farrowing system. If you serve a sow on January 1st, she will farrow on April 25th, wean on May 23rd, and be ready for service again on May 30th. That is 149 days later. Mark this on your calendar. The 149-day cycle allows you to plan everything: feed purchases, labour, pen cleaning, and sales. In a batch system, you group sows so that they all follow the same cycle. For example, you might have 10 sows in Batch A, served on the same day. They will all farrow within a few days of each other, wean on the same day, and be served again on the same day. This synchronisation makes management efficient. You can clean and disinfect the farrowing house between batches. You can also plan to sell weaners or finishers in batches, which attracts better prices from buyers. To keep the cycle running, you need to serve sows on time. If a sow is not served within 7 days after weaning, the whole batch shifts. So you must check for heat diligently. Use a calendar or a record book. Write down the dates for each sow: service date, expected farrowing date, actual farrowing date, weaning date, and next service date. At the end of the 149 days, the sow should be pregnant again. If she is not, you have a gap. In Africa, where pig feed can be expensive, a gap means wasted feed and lost income. So stick to the 149-day cycle. It is the engine of daily pig sales. With 149 days, you can calculate how many sows you need to produce a certain number of piglets per week. For example, if you want to sell 20 pigs per week, and each sow produces 10 piglets per litter, you need 2 sows farrowing every week. Over 149 days, that means about 42 sows in total, but you can manage them in batches. The math is simple: 149 days divided by 7 days per week equals about 21 weeks per cycle. So each sow produces one litter every 21 weeks. Plan your batches accordingly..Trim()

Marking the Calendar

A breeding calendar is only useful if you mark it clearly and keep it updated. In Africa, many smallholder farmers use a wall calendar or a notebook. Choose a method that works for you. A large wall calendar in the pig house is best because you see it every day. Use different colours for different events. For example, use red for service dates, blue for expected farrowing, green for actual farrowing, and black for weaning. Write the sow's ID or tag number next to each event. At the start of each month, look ahead and mark all the expected dates for that month. For example, if a sow was served on January 1st, mark April 25th as expected farrowing. Then mark May 23rd as weaning, and May 30th as next service. Do this for every sow. If you have many sows, use a batch code. For example, Batch A served on January 1st, Batch B served on January 8th, and so on. Then you only need to mark the batch code on the calendar. Also, keep a record book with details: sow ID, service date, boar used, farrowing date, number of piglets born alive, number of stillborn, weaning date, and next service date. This book is your backup. If the calendar gets lost or damaged, you still have the data. In hot African climates, keep the calendar and book in a dry place, away from rain and rodents. Use a plastic sleeve or a metal box. Every day, check the calendar for tasks: who is due for farrowing, who needs to be checked for heat, who needs to be weaned. This daily check takes only 5 minutes but saves you from missing critical dates. If you use a smartphone, you can set reminders. But do not rely only on the phone; write it down. Technology can fail, but paper does not. Marking the calendar is a simple habit that keeps your batch system on track. It ensures you never miss a service, a farrowing, or a weaning. And that means daily pig sales..Trim()

What Happens If You Miss a Date

If you miss a date in the breeding calendar, the consequences can be costly. The most common missed date is the service date. If you fail to serve a sow within 7 days after weaning, she will come into heat again 21 days later. That adds 21 days to her cycle, making it 170 days instead of 149. This disrupts the batch. The sow will farrow later than the rest of the group, so you cannot clean the farrowing house between batches. You may have to keep the sow in a separate pen, which takes extra labour and feed. And you will have a gap in piglet production, meaning fewer pigs to sell. Another missed date is the farrowing date. If you are not prepared, the sow may farrow in a dirty pen or without supervision. Piglets can be crushed or chilled. You might lose the whole litter. So you must mark the expected farrowing date and prepare the pen 3 days before. If you miss the weaning date, piglets stay with the sow longer than 28 days. This drains the sow's body condition and delays her return to heat. It also means the piglets eat more of the sow's milk and less solid feed, so they may be slower to grow. In a batch system, all sows should be weaned on the same day. If you miss one, you break the batch. To avoid missing dates, use a calendar and a record book. Check them daily. Set alarms on your phone. Train your workers to recognise the signs of heat and to report any changes. If you do miss a date, act quickly. If a sow is not served on time, check for heat again and serve as soon as possible. If she is already pregnant, do not re-serve. If you miss a farrowing, check the pen immediately and assist if needed. If you miss weaning, wean as soon as you remember, but not later than 35 days. The key is to minimise the damage. A missed date costs money: extra feed, lost piglets, and delayed sales. In Africa, where margins are tight, you cannot afford to be careless. So treat your calendar as a tool of your business. Keep it accurate, and your pigs will reward you with daily sales..Trim()

Module 7 · The Sow: Feeding and Management

Feeding the Dry Sow

A dry sow is a sow that is not pregnant and not lactating. She still needs good care to get back into condition for the next breeding. Feed her a maintenance diet of about 2 kg per day of a good sow ration. This ration should have 14% protein and be high in fibre, like maize bran or wheat bran. If she is too thin, increase to 2.5 kg; if too fat, reduce to 1.5 kg. Always provide plenty of clean water�??at least 10 litres per day. In hot African climates, sows may eat less, so feed early morning and late evening when it is cooler. Group-housed dry sows need space to avoid bullying; give each sow at least 2 square metres. If you keep them in individual stalls, make sure they can lie down and stand up comfortably. Watch for signs of heat (oestrus) every day�??a dry sow that is not cycling may need a boar nearby or a change in feed. Flushing (increasing feed to 3 kg per day) for 10-14 days before mating can increase ovulation rate. But do not overfeed, as fat sows have difficult births. Keep a record of each sow's feed and body condition. In Africa, many farmers use local feeds like sweet potato vines, cassava leaves, and kitchen waste, but these must be balanced with a commercial or home-mixed concentrate. A simple home mix: 50% maize, 20% bran, 20% protein concentrate (like soy or fish meal), 10% minerals and salt. Always store feed in a dry place to prevent mould. A dry sow that is well managed will give you many healthy litters..Trim()

Feeding the Pregnant Sow

Once a sow is confirmed pregnant, her feeding changes. For the first 80 days of pregnancy, feed her about 2 kg per day of a 14% protein sow ration. Do not overfeed, because too much energy can cause the embryos to die. From day 80 to farrowing (about 114 days), increase to 2.5-3 kg per day. This is when the piglets grow fast and the sow needs extra nutrients. Use a ration with 16% protein and added calcium and phosphorus for strong bones. Provide 15-20 litres of water daily. In the last week before farrowing, reduce feed slightly to 2 kg and add bran to prevent constipation. A pregnant sow should have a body condition score of 3 out of 5�??not too thin, not too fat. Check her udder: it should start to develop and may leak milk a few days before birth. Keep her in a clean, quiet pen away from other sows. In Africa, many farmers use group housing for pregnant sows, but individual pens are better to prevent fighting and ensure each sow gets her share. If you use local feeds, make sure they are not mouldy�??mouldy feed can cause abortion. Add a mineral supplement if your soil is deficient in selenium or zinc. Weigh feed with a simple scale or use a tin can as a measure (a standard 2-litre tin holds about 1.5 kg of maize). Record feed intake daily. A sow that is too thin will have small piglets; a sow that is too fat will have difficulty farrowing. Adjust feed based on her condition. Always consult a vet if you see signs of illness. Good feeding during pregnancy means strong, healthy piglets and a sow that recovers quickly after birth..Trim()

Feeding the Lactating Sow

A lactating sow is feeding piglets, so she needs a lot of energy and protein. Feed her free-choice, meaning she can eat as much as she wants, but start slowly after farrowing. On day 1 after birth, give only 1 kg of feed. Increase by 0.5 kg each day until she is eating 2 kg plus 0.5 kg for each piglet she is nursing. For example, a sow with 10 piglets should eat 2 + (10 x 0.5) = 7 kg per day. Use a high-energy, 18% protein lactation ration. Provide unlimited clean water�??she may drink 20-30 litres daily. If she does not eat enough, she will lose body condition and produce less milk. In Africa, you can supplement with local feeds like cooked sweet potato, pumpkin, or milk from cattle, but always include a balanced concentrate. Feed her 2-3 times a day, and remove any leftover feed to prevent spoilage. Keep the farrowing pen cool�??use shade, ventilation, or wet the floor. A hot sow will eat less. Watch her udder for mastitis (hard, hot, painful). If she has mastitis, she may refuse to feed piglets. Consult a vet. Also, ensure piglets have access to creep feed from 2 weeks old to reduce demand on the sow. Weigh the sow weekly or use body condition scoring. If she is losing too much weight, increase feed. If she is too fat, reduce slightly. After weaning, reduce feed to 2 kg per day to help her dry off. Good lactation feeding means heavy piglets and a sow that returns to heat quickly. Remember, a sow that is well fed will produce 20-30 piglets per year. Poor feeding means fewer, smaller piglets and longer time to rebreed..Trim()

Body Condition Scoring

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a simple way to check if your sow is too thin, too fat, or just right. Use a scale of 1 to 5, where 1 is very thin and 5 is very fat. A score of 3 is ideal. To score, look at the sow from the side and behind. Feel her backbone, ribs, and hip bones. If you can easily feel the bones with light pressure, she is thin (score 2). If you cannot feel them even with firm pressure, she is fat (score 4 or 5). If you can feel them with firm pressure but not see them, she is ideal (score 3). Also check the tailhead and pin bones. In Africa, where feed can be scarce, many sows are too thin. A thin sow will have weak piglets and poor milk. A fat sow will have difficulty giving birth and may crush piglets. Score your sows every month. Keep a record for each sow. If a sow scores 2, increase her feed by 0.5-1 kg per day. If she scores 4, reduce feed by 0.5 kg and increase exercise. For pregnant sows, aim for score 3 at mating, then maintain until day 80, then allow slight increase. For lactating sows, they will naturally lose condition; aim to have them at score 3 when weaned. Use a simple chart with pictures to help you. You can draw the scores on a paper or use a notebook. In group housing, score each sow individually because dominant sows may eat more. If you cannot feel the bones, use a measuring tape to check girth. A sow with a girth of more than 1.5 metres may be too fat. Body condition scoring is free and takes only a few minutes. It helps you adjust feeding and prevent problems. Train your workers to do it. Good BCS means healthy sows and more profit..Trim()

Culling Old Sows

Culling means removing a sow from the breeding herd. You cull old sows when they are no longer productive. Most sows stay productive for 6-8 litters, or until they are 4-5 years old. Signs it is time to cull: she has fewer than 8 piglets per litter, she has difficulty farrowing, she has poor milk, she has lost body condition and does not recover, she has repeated health problems like mastitis or lameness, or she is aggressive and injures other sows. Also cull if she fails to conceive after two matings. In Africa, where feed is expensive, keeping a low-producing sow wastes money. A good sow should wean at least 20 piglets per year. If she weans fewer than 16, consider culling. To cull, first confirm she is not pregnant. Sell her to a butcher or another farmer. In some areas, you can sell cull sows for meat�??they are often used for sausages or minced meat. Before selling, check local regulations and ensure she is healthy. Do not sell a sow with disease. If you cannot sell, you may slaughter for home consumption. After culling, replace her with a gilt (young female pig) that you have raised or bought. Keep records of each sow's performance: number of piglets born alive, weaned, and weaning weight. Use a notebook or a simple spreadsheet. This helps you decide which sows to keep. Culling is not failure�??it is good management. It keeps your herd productive and profitable. Plan to cull 20-25% of your sows each year. This means if you have 10 sows, replace 2-3 each year. Always have replacement gilts ready. In batch farrowing, culling must be done between batches to avoid disruption. A well-culled herd has healthy, productive sows that give you daily pig sales..Trim()

Managing the Boar

A boar is a male pig used for breeding. A good boar is calm, has strong legs, and comes from a healthy line. Keep one boar for every 10-15 sows. In Africa, many farmers cannot afford artificial insemination, so a boar is essential. Feed your boar a special boar ration with 14% protein. Give him 2-2.5 kg per day. If he is used for mating often, increase to 3 kg. Do not overfeed, as fat boars have poor libido and difficulty mating. Provide plenty of water and exercise. A boar needs space�??at least 6 square metres. If he is tethered, give him a chain and a comfortable pen. Boars can be aggressive, so handle them calmly and never turn your back. Use a pig board (a flat board) to move him safely. Check his feet and legs regularly; lame boars cannot mate. Trim his tusks if they grow too long. A boar can mate from 8 months old, but wait until he is 10-12 months for heavy use. Use him for at most 2 matings per week when young, and up to 4-5 per week when mature. Always bring the sow to the boar's pen for mating�??this reduces fighting. Mating takes 5-10 minutes. If the boar is not interested, check for heat in the sow or health problems in the boar. In hot climates, boars may be less fertile; provide shade and cool water. Rotate boars every 2 years to prevent inbreeding. If you have only one boar, replace him after 2 years with a new one from a different farm. Keep records of each mating: date, sow, and boar. This helps you predict farrowing dates. A well-managed boar will give you many healthy litters. Remember, a boar is half your herd's genetics, so take good care of him..Trim()

Module 8 · Pre-Weaning Survival

Farrowing Supervision

Farrowing supervision means being present when the sow gives birth. Most piglets die during the first 3 days, so your presence can save many lives. Start by preparing a clean, dry farrowing pen with a heat lamp or heat box for the piglets. The sow needs a quiet, dark area with no drafts. Write down the expected farrowing date (114 days after mating) and check the sow every 4 hours starting 2 days before. When she starts nesting, restless, and her udder swells, she will farrow within 12-24 hours. Stay nearby. As each piglet is born, tear the sac if it covers the face, wipe mucus from the nose and mouth with a clean cloth, and dry the piglet with a towel or dry straw. Tie the umbilical cord with a clean string about 2 cm from the belly and cut it with disinfected scissors. Dip the stump in iodine. Put the piglet under the heat lamp or in a warm box. Make sure it gets colostrum within 30 minutes. If a piglet is born without breathing, rub its chest vigorously and hold it upside down for a few seconds. If the sow is in distress or no piglet comes after 30 minutes of straining, call a vet. Count the placentas; there should be one per piglet. Keep a record of births, deaths, and any problems. Supervised farrowing can reduce stillbirths and crushing. In Africa, where many farmers cannot afford constant vet care, simple supervision by the farmer is the cheapest way to save piglets. Stay calm, keep the area clean, and act quickly..Trim()

Colostrum in the First 6 Hours

Colostrum is the first milk a sow produces. It is thick, yellowish, and rich in antibodies that protect piglets from disease. Piglets are born with no immunity, so they must drink colostrum within the first 6 hours of life. After 6 hours, their gut can no longer absorb the antibodies. The first 30 minutes are best. Make sure every piglet gets colostrum. If a piglet is weak, hold it to a teat and squeeze a few drops into its mouth. The best teats are the front ones, which give more milk. If a sow has more piglets than teats, rotate the smaller piglets to the front and the stronger ones to the back. If a sow dies or has no milk, use colostrum from another sow that farrowed within 24 hours, or use a commercial colostrum replacer. In Africa, you can also use cow colostrum as a last resort, but pig colostrum is best. Never give water or glucose before colostrum; it can damage the gut. Keep the piglets warm while they suckle. A cold piglet will not suckle. If a piglet has not suckled within 2 hours, assist it. Check that the sow has enough teats; if not, consider cross-fostering to another sow that farrowed at the same time. Cross-fostering works best within 24 hours. Always wash your hands before handling piglets. After the first 6 hours, piglets can drink regular milk. But colostrum is the foundation of their health. Without it, they will likely die from diarrhea or pneumonia. So be there at farrowing and make sure every piglet gets its share..Trim()

Keeping Piglets Warm

Newborn piglets cannot control their body temperature. They need a warm, dry place away from the sow. The ideal temperature for a newborn piglet is 32-35°C. For a 3-week-old piglet, 24-28°C is fine. If piglets are cold, they huddle, shiver, and refuse to suckle. They can die within hours. In Africa, many farmers use a heat lamp (infrared bulb) hung 45-60 cm above the floor. A 100-watt bulb is enough for a small pen. If you have no electricity, use a heat box: a wooden box with a lid and a small entrance, filled with dry straw. The piglets' body heat warms the box. You can also use a charcoal stove, but keep it outside the pen to avoid fumes. Another option is a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel. Place the heat source in a corner, away from the sow. The piglets will learn to go there. Bedding must be dry and clean. Wet bedding draws heat away. Use dry straw, wood shavings, or shredded paper. Change it daily. Avoid drafts. Fix holes in walls or use curtains. In hot climates, piglets may need cooling during the day, but nights can be cold. Monitor piglets: if they are spread out and panting, it's too hot; if they are piled up and shivering, it's too cold. Adjust the heat lamp height or add more bedding. A simple thermometer helps. Keep the farrowing pen at 20°C for the sow, but provide a warmer microclimate for piglets. This reduces crushing because piglets will not seek warmth under the sow. Warm piglets are active, suckle well, and grow faster. So invest in a heat source; it pays back in saved piglets..Trim()

Iron Injection and Iron Deficiency

Piglets are born with very little iron. Sow's milk has almost no iron. Without extra iron, piglets become anemic: their skin looks pale, they breathe fast, and they grow slowly. Anemia can kill them. In nature, piglets get iron from soil, but in confinement, you must inject iron. Give 200 mg of iron dextran injection to each piglet at 3 days of age. Use a 1 ml syringe with a 20-gauge needle. Inject into the neck muscle, behind the ear, or into the ham. Clean the skin with alcohol first. Do not inject into the same spot twice; rotate sides. If you cannot get iron dextran, some farmers use iron paste or iron-rich soil, but injection is best. In Africa, iron dextran is available from agrovets. Store it in a cool, dark place. Do not use expired product. If piglets are very pale at birth, give iron at 1 day old. Repeat if needed at 2 weeks. Signs of iron deficiency include pale gums, rough hair, and poor weight gain. If you see these, inject iron immediately. Also, ensure piglets have access to clean soil or a mineral block after 1 week. Some farmers give iron via the sow's feed, but that does not pass enough to piglets. So injection is necessary. Keep records of dates and doses. A simple notebook works. Iron injection is quick and cheap. One bottle of iron dextran treats many piglets. It prevents anemia and helps piglets survive to weaning. So do not skip it..Trim()

Castration and Tail Docking

Castration and tail docking are done to reduce boar taint and tail biting. Castrate male piglets at 3-7 days old. At this age, they are small and heal fast. Use a sharp, clean scalpel or a castration knife. Wash your hands and the piglet's scrotum with soap and water. Hold the piglet upside down by the hind legs. Make a small cut in the scrotum, push out the testicle, and pull it out with a gentle twist. Cut the cord. Repeat on the other side. Apply iodine to the wounds. Do not use a knife that is rusty. Do not castrate sick or weak piglets. If you are not confident, get a vet or experienced farmer to show you. Tail docking: cut the tail 2-3 cm from the base with clean scissors or a hot iron. This prevents tail biting later. Do it at the same time as castration. Use a hot iron to cauterize and reduce bleeding. After docking, apply iodine. Keep piglets in a clean, dry pen. Watch for bleeding or infection. If a piglet bleeds a lot, press a clean cloth on the wound. In Africa, some farmers use rubber rings for castration, but they are not recommended because they cause pain and tetanus risk. Always use sterile tools. Do not castrate after 2 weeks; it is more stressful. If you sell piglets for meat, castration is important because uncastrated males have a strong smell. Tail docking reduces injuries from biting. Both are quick if done correctly. So learn the technique and do it early..Trim()

Reducing Crushing Deaths

Crushing is the biggest killer of piglets before weaning. The sow accidentally lies on them. To reduce crushing, you need to change the pen and the sow's behavior. First, provide a safe creep area for piglets: a corner with a heat lamp and bedding, blocked off by a low wall so the sow cannot enter. Piglets will learn to sleep there. Second, keep the pen warm for piglets but cool for the sow. If the sow is too hot, she will lie spread out and may crush piglets. Use a heat lamp only over the creep area, not the whole pen. Third, handle the sow gently. A stressed sow is more likely to lie down suddenly. Fourth, install anti-crushing rails: a horizontal bar 20-25 cm from the floor along the walls. When the sow lies down, the rail gives piglets a gap to escape. Fifth, supervise farrowing and the first 3 days. When the sow is about to lie down, watch her. If a piglet is under her, move it. Sixth, keep the floor dry and non-slip. Wet floors cause sows to slip and fall on piglets. Seventh, feed the sow in a trough away from the creep area. Eighth, if a sow is known to crush piglets, consider using a farrowing crate, but crates are expensive and can stress the sow. In Africa, simple pens with a creep area and rails work well. Also, ensure piglets are not too cold; cold piglets huddle near the sow and get crushed. So warmth is key. Check pens every few hours, especially at night. Remove dead piglets immediately. With these steps, you can cut crushing deaths by half..Trim()

Module 12 · Selling Every Day: Marketing and Buyers

Butchers vs Live Traders

When you sell pigs daily, you have two main buyer types: butchers and live traders. A butcher buys slaughtered carcasses or live pigs to slaughter and sell as meat. A live trader buys live pigs to resell, often at markets or to other farmers. Each has different needs and payment terms. Butchers want consistent weight and quality. They pay per kilogram of carcass or live weight. In many African markets, a butcher might pay 2,500 to 3,500 shillings per kg of carcass, depending on the country and season. They prefer pigs weighing 80-100 kg live, yielding about 70-75% carcass. Live traders often pay less per kg because they need a margin for transport and resale. They may pay 1,800 to 2,500 shillings per kg live weight. But they buy in bulk and pay cash quickly. Your choice depends on your volume and location. If you are near a town with butcheries, focus on butchers. If you are remote, live traders may be your only option. Butchers demand regular supply�??if you promise 5 pigs every Monday, you must deliver. Live traders are more flexible but less reliable. Start by visiting local butcheries and market stalls. Ask about their weekly needs, prices, and payment terms. Keep a notebook of each buyer's requirements. For daily sales from batch farrowing, you need at least 3-4 regular buyers to avoid dependence on one. Mix butchers and live traders to balance price and reliability. Remember, a live trader might pay cash today but disappear tomorrow. A butcher may pay later but buys every week. Build relationships slowly. Offer consistent quality, and you can negotiate better prices..Trim()

Contract With a Butcher

A contract with a butcher protects you from price drops and guarantees a market. It does not need to be a legal document�??a simple written agreement signed by both parties works in most African settings. Include these key points: number of pigs per week, weight range (e.g., 80-90 kg live), price per kg (or a formula based on market price), payment terms (cash on delivery or within 7 days), and penalties for missed deliveries. For example, you agree to supply 4 pigs every Tuesday. The butcher agrees to pay 2,800 shillings per kg live weight, with a 5% bonus if you deliver before 9 AM. If you miss a delivery without notice, you pay a penalty of 10% of the value. If the butcher fails to pay within 7 days, you charge 2% interest per week. Both parties should keep a copy. In practice, many butchers prefer verbal agreements, but a written contract reduces disputes. Start with a 3-month trial. Review after 3 months and adjust prices based on feed costs and market trends. For daily sales from batch farrowing, a contract ensures you can plan your batches. You know exactly how many pigs you need each week. That helps you schedule farrowing and weaning. Without a contract, you might have pigs ready but no buyer. With a contract, you can invest in better feed and health care. Some butchers will also pay a deposit upfront�??use that to buy feed. Always deliver on time and at the agreed weight. If your pigs are underweight, the butcher may reject them or pay less. Weigh your pigs before delivery. Use a weighing crate or a scale. If you don't have a scale, use a weigh tape. Consistency builds trust. After 6 months, you can negotiate a higher price or a longer contract. A good butcher relationship can last years..Trim()

Pricing to Protect Margin

Your selling price must cover feed, labor, health, and transport, and still leave a profit. Many pig farmers fail because they price based on what buyers offer, not on their costs. Calculate your cost per kg of live weight. Start with feed�??it is 60-70% of costs. A pig eats about 250 kg of feed from weaning to 90 kg live weight. If feed costs 1,200 shillings per 50 kg bag, that is 24 shillings per kg of feed. So feed cost per pig = 250 kg x 24 = 6,000 shillings. Add health (vaccines, dewormers) at 500 shillings, labor at 1,000 shillings, and transport at 500 shillings. Total cost = 8,000 shillings. If the pig weighs 90 kg live, your cost per kg = 8,000 / 90 = 89 shillings per kg. But that is only cost�??you need profit. A 30% margin means you sell at 89 x 1.3 = 116 shillings per kg. However, market prices in many African countries range from 1,800 to 3,500 shillings per kg live weight. Wait�??that is per kg of carcass? No, live weight prices are often 1,500 to 2,500 shillings per kg in local currency. Check your local market. The key is to know your cost and set a floor price. Never sell below cost. If a live trader offers 1,200 shillings per kg and your cost is 1,500, you lose money. Instead, find a butcher who pays 2,000. Also consider weight: heavier pigs have lower cost per kg because feed efficiency improves. But too heavy (over 110 kg) may be rejected. Aim for 80-100 kg. Track every sale in a notebook: date, buyer, weight, price, and costs. After one month, calculate your average margin. If margin is below 20%, raise prices or cut feed costs by mixing your own rations. Protect your margin by selling directly to consumers or butchers, not through middlemen who take a cut. Remember, a small margin on many pigs is better than a big margin on few. Daily sales mean steady cash flow..Trim()

Direct to Consumer

Selling directly to consumers�??families, restaurants, and small shops�??can earn you 20-40% more than selling to traders. Consumers pay for convenience and quality. You can sell live pigs, butchered cuts, or processed products like sausages. Start by targeting neighbors, friends, and local eateries. A restaurant might buy 2 pigs per week if you offer consistent supply and good cuts. Advertise using WhatsApp groups, Facebook Marketplace, and community notice boards. Post photos of your pigs and prices. For example, 'Fresh pork available every Friday. 1 kg = 3,500 shillings. Order before Thursday.' Offer delivery for a small fee. To sell direct, you need a reliable slaughter and cutting setup. If you don't have a slaughter slab, partner with a local butcher who charges a fee per pig (e.g., 500 shillings). You can also sell live pigs to consumers who slaughter at home�??common in rural areas. Price your cuts: a whole carcass might sell for 2,800 shillings per kg, but individual cuts like ribs or loin can go for 4,000-5,000 shillings per kg. That increases your margin. However, direct selling takes time. You must handle orders, slaughter, cutting, packaging, and delivery. Start small: sell to 5 regular customers. Use a cooler box with ice to keep meat fresh. For daily sales from batch farrowing, direct-to-consumer works best if you have a steady supply of pigs every week. You can also offer subscription boxes: '10 kg pork per month for 30,000 shillings.' This gives you predictable income. Keep records of each customer's preferences. Ask for feedback. Happy customers refer others. In many African cities, demand for pork is growing. Position yourself as a reliable local supplier. Avoid selling on credit�??cash on delivery. If a customer wants credit, set a limit and charge a small interest. Direct selling is more work but more rewarding..Trim()

When to Add Value

Adding value means turning live pigs into products like sausages, bacon, ham, or cooked pork. It can double or triple your income, but only if you have the right conditions. First, check demand. Are there supermarkets, hotels, or delis in your area that sell processed pork? If not, you might need to create demand through sampling and promotion. Second, check regulations. In many African countries, processing meat requires a license from the health department. You need a clean facility, running water, and refrigeration. If you cannot afford a facility, start with simple value addition: sell pork cuts in smaller packages, or offer marinated ribs ready for grilling. That requires only a freezer and packaging. Third, calculate costs. A sausage-making machine costs 200,000 to 500,000 shillings. Spices and casings add 200 shillings per kg. If you sell sausages at 6,000 shillings per kg and your cost is 3,500, you make 2,500 per kg. But you must sell enough volume. Start by making 10 kg per week. Test the market. If it sells, scale up. For daily pig sales from batch farrowing, value addition helps you use lower-value cuts. Instead of selling a whole pig to a butcher at 2,500 per kg, you can turn the shoulders and bellies into sausages and sell the prime cuts fresh. That increases your overall revenue per pig by 30-50%. However, value addition takes time and labor. You might need to hire one person. Only add value when you have more pigs than you can sell live, or when live prices are low. Also consider seasonality: during festivals, demand for fresh pork is high, so sell live. During slow periods, process. Start small with one product, like sausages, and master it. Get a food handler's certificate. Keep your facility clean. Bad meat can ruin your reputation. Many successful pig farmers in Kenya, Uganda, and Nigeria started with direct sales and then added value after 2 years. Be patient..Trim()

A 12-Month Growth Plan

A 12-month growth plan helps you move from selling a few pigs to a steady daily business. Start by setting clear monthly targets. For example, Month 1-3: sell 2 pigs per week to 2 butchers. Month 4-6: increase to 4 pigs per week and add one live trader. Month 7-9: add direct-to-consumer sales, targeting 10 regular customers. Month 10-12: start value addition with sausages, selling 20 kg per week. To achieve this, you need to plan your batches. With batch farrowing, you group sows to farrow together every 4-6 weeks. That gives you a consistent number of pigs ready for sale each week. For 4 pigs per week, you need about 8 sows farrowing every 5 weeks (assuming 10 piglets weaned per sow, 90% survival, and 24 weeks to market weight). Adjust based on your performance. Keep records of farrowing dates, weaning weights, and sales. Use a simple notebook or a smartphone app like Farmbrite or PigCHAMP. Each month, review your progress. Are you meeting sales targets? If not, why? Maybe feed quality is poor, or disease. Fix problems early. Reinvest profits: 30% into feed, 20% into expanding housing, 10% into marketing, and keep 40% as savings or emergency fund. Build relationships with buyers. Invite them to see your farm. Show them your healthy pigs. That builds trust. As you grow, consider hiring one worker to help with feeding and cleaning. Pay them fairly. Also, join a pig farmers' cooperative to access cheaper feed and share transport. In Month 12, evaluate: can you sell 6 pigs per week? If yes, plan for Year 2: add a second batch farrowing group, buy a feed mixer, and maybe open a small retail outlet. Remember, growth is gradual. Don't overstock. Many farmers fail because they expand too fast without a market. Secure buyers first, then increase production. Your 12-month plan should be written down and reviewed monthly. Celebrate small wins.
