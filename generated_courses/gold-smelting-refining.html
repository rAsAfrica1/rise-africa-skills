Module 1 · The Business of Gold Smelting

Introduction and Why It Matters

Gold smelting is the process of melting gold-bearing material to separate pure gold from impurities. In Africa, small-scale miners often produce raw gold with mercury or other contaminants. Smelting turns that dirty gold into a clean bar you can sell at a higher price. For example, a gram of impure gold might fetch $40, but after smelting to 99% purity, the same gram can sell for $60 or more. That difference is your profit. This business matters because Africa is rich in gold, yet much of it is exported raw. By smelting locally, you add value, create jobs, and keep more money in your community. You don't need a big factory. A simple charcoal furnace, a crucible, and borax can get you started. Many successful smelters in Ghana, Tanzania, and South Africa began with less than $500 in tools. The key is knowing the right steps and safety rules. This module will teach you the business side: how to set up, what tools to buy, how to smelt safely, and how to avoid common mistakes. You'll also learn how to test your gold and sell it to reputable buyers. Whether you're a miner wanting to upgrade your product or an entrepreneur looking for a new venture, gold smelting offers a practical way to earn income. Remember, gold is heavy and valuable, so even small amounts matter. A single gram of pure gold is worth more than a day's wage for many. With the right skills, you can turn that gram into a reliable business. Let's get started..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To start smelting gold, you need a few essential tools. First, a furnace. You can build a simple charcoal furnace using a metal bucket, fire bricks, and a blower. A used brake drum works well as a crucible holder. For fuel, charcoal is cheap and available in most African markets. You'll also need a crucible �?? a clay-graphite crucible can handle temperatures up to 1200°C and costs about $10. Never use a steel pot; it will melt. Next, a crucible tongs to handle hot crucibles safely. A mold for pouring the molten gold �?? a steel or cast iron ingot mold. For flux, use borax (sodium borate) and soda ash. Borax helps remove impurities and makes the gold flow. You'll also need safety gear: leather gloves, safety goggles, a face shield, and a leather apron. For testing, get a small digital scale accurate to 0.01 grams, and a gold testing kit with acids. A hammer and anvil for shaping. A magnetic stirrer is not needed; a simple graphite rod for stirring. Also, a bucket of water for quenching. For ventilation, set up your furnace outdoors or under a hood. A fire extinguisher is mandatory. In terms of materials, you'll need your raw gold concentrate, borax, soda ash, and possibly a little silver or copper to adjust alloys. Keep a notebook to record weights and temperatures. All these tools can be bought in local markets or online for under $300 total. Start small, and upgrade as you earn..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to smelt gold safely and effectively. Step 1: Prepare your workspace. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Put on all safety gear. Step 2: Weigh your raw gold. For example, you might have 50 grams of gold concentrate. Record the weight. Step 3: Mix flux. For 50 grams of concentrate, use 10 grams of borax and 5 grams of soda ash. Mix well. Step 4: Preheat your furnace. Fill it with charcoal and light it. Use a blower to get the temperature up to 1100°C. You can check with a pyrometer or by watching the crucible glow bright orange. Step 5: Place the crucible in the furnace and let it heat for 10 minutes. Step 6: Carefully add your gold and flux mixture into the hot crucible. Close the furnace and let it melt for 20-30 minutes. Stir occasionally with a graphite rod. Step 7: Once the gold is fully molten and shiny, pour it into your preheated mold. Preheat the mold to avoid cracking. Step 8: Let the gold cool for 5 minutes, then quench in water. Step 9: Remove the gold bar and weigh it. You should have about 45 grams of pure gold if your concentrate was 90% pure. Step 10: Clean the bar with a wire brush. Now it's ready to sell. Remember, never pour molten gold into a wet mold �?? it can explode. Always preheat. Also, never leave the furnace unattended. This method works for small batches up to 100 grams. For larger batches, scale up the flux and use a bigger crucible..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many beginners make mistakes that cost them gold and money. Here are the most common and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Using too much flux. Excess borax can eat through your crucible and mix with the gold, lowering purity. Fix: Use the ratio 1 part flux to 5 parts gold. For 50g gold, use 10g flux. Mistake 2: Not preheating the mold. A cold mold causes the gold to splatter and stick. Fix: Heat the mold with a torch until it's hot to the touch (about 200°C) before pouring. Mistake 3: Melting in a steel pot. Steel melts at 1400°C, close to gold's 1064°C, so it can contaminate your gold. Fix: Always use a clay-graphite crucible. Mistake 4: Poor ventilation. Smelting releases toxic fumes like lead and mercury. Fix: Work outdoors or use a fume hood. Wear a respirator. Mistake 5: Not stirring. Unstirred gold can trap impurities. Fix: Stir gently every 10 minutes with a graphite rod. Mistake 6: Overheating. If you get gold too hot, it can vaporize (though only above 2800°C, but you can lose silver and copper). Fix: Keep temperature around 1100-1200°C. Use a pyrometer. Mistake 7: Skipping the quench. Quenching cools the gold quickly and makes it easier to handle. Fix: Always quench in water after 5 minutes of air cooling. Mistake 8: Not recording weights. You can't calculate your yield or profit. Fix: Weigh before and after. If you started with 50g and got 45g, your recovery is 90%. If it's lower, adjust your flux or temperature. Fix these mistakes and your smelting will be profitable..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional in gold smelting. Molten gold is over 1000°C and can cause severe burns. Fumes from impurities like mercury, lead, and arsenic are toxic. Follow these rules to stay safe. First, always wear personal protective equipment (PPE): leather gloves, safety goggles, a face shield, a leather apron, and closed-toe boots. Never wear synthetic clothing �?? it melts. Second, work in a well-ventilated area. Outdoors is best, but if indoors, use an exhaust fan and open windows. A fume hood is ideal. Third, keep a fire extinguisher nearby. A Class D extinguisher for metal fires is best, but a CO2 extinguisher works for small fires. Never use water on a metal fire. Fourth, never smelt alone. Have a buddy who can help in an emergency. Fifth, store chemicals like borax and acids in labeled containers, away from children. Sixth, handle crucibles with tongs only. Never touch a hot crucible. Seventh, preheat molds and tools to avoid steam explosions. Eighth, after smelting, clean your workspace. Gold dust can be swept and recovered. Ninth, dispose of waste properly. Flux waste can contain heavy metals; take it to a hazardous waste facility. Tenth, get trained. If possible, take a short course or apprentice with an experienced smelter. Best practices include keeping a logbook of every smelt: date, weight in, weight out, temperature, and notes. This helps you improve. Also, use a calibrated scale. And always test your gold before selling. A simple acid test can tell you purity. Remember, your health and safety are more valuable than any gold bar. Follow these rules and you'll smelt for years..Trim()

Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. This exercise will guide you through a small smelt using safe methods. You will need: 10 grams of gold concentrate (or clean gold flakes), 2 grams of borax, 1 gram of soda ash, a small clay-graphite crucible, a charcoal furnace, a mold, tongs, and full PPE. If you don't have gold, you can practice with copper or brass to learn the process. Step 1: Set up your furnace outdoors. Light charcoal and use a blower to reach 1100°C. Step 2: Weigh your gold and flux. Mix them in a small container. Step 3: Preheat the crucible in the furnace for 5 minutes. Step 4: Carefully add the gold-flux mixture to the crucible. Close the furnace. Step 5: Wait 20 minutes, stirring once after 10 minutes. Step 6: Preheat your mold. Step 7: Pour the molten gold into the mold. Step 8: Let cool for 5 minutes, then quench in water. Step 9: Weigh your gold button. Calculate recovery: (final weight / initial weight) x 100. For 10g input, if you get 9g, recovery is 90%. Step 10: Test purity with an acid test kit. If purity is below 95%, repeat the smelt with fresh flux. Record everything in a notebook. After the exercise, clean your tools and dispose of waste properly. Discuss with your trainer: What went well? What could be improved? How would you scale this up for 100 grams? This exercise costs about $20 in materials if you use copper, or more if using gold. But the experience is invaluable. Do it three times to build confidence. Then you're ready to start your own small smelting business..Trim()

Module 2 · Safety Before Anything Else

Introduction and Why It Matters

Gold smelting and refining can be dangerous. Every year, small-scale miners and refiners in Africa suffer burns, lung damage, and even death from accidents that could have been prevented. The biggest threats are mercury and lead poisoning, acid burns, and fires from open flames. For example, mercury vapour from heating amalgam can cause permanent brain damage. Lead from old car batteries or pipes used in smelting can cause severe anaemia and kidney failure. Inhaling metal fumes like zinc or copper can give you metal fume fever, which feels like a bad flu but can be worse. .Trim()

.Trim()

Why does safety matter so much? Because you cannot earn a living if you are sick or dead. Your family depends on you. A single accident can put you in the hospital for weeks, costing more than you earn in months. Also, gold buyers and international markets are increasingly refusing gold that is not produced safely and ethically. If you want to sell to serious buyers, you must show you follow safety rules. .Trim()

This module will teach you the basics: what tools you need, how to work step by step, common mistakes that cause accidents, and how to set up a safe workspace. You will also do a practical exercise to practice safety habits. Remember, safety is not about being scared; it is about being smart. Most accidents happen because people skip steps or use wrong equipment. By the end of this module, you will know how to protect yourself, your workers, and your community. Let's start with the right mindset: no gold is worth your life. .Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many refining operations happen in small workshops or even at home. That increases risk because children and family members may be nearby. You must keep your work area separate, well-ventilated, and away from living spaces. Also, never work alone. Have a buddy who can help if something goes wrong. This introduction is a wake-up call: take safety seriously from day one..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To work safely, you need the right tools. First, personal protective equipment (PPE). You need safety goggles or a face shield that covers your whole face. A respirator with cartridges for acid gases and metal fumes, not just a dust mask. Heavy leather gloves that go up to your elbows. A leather apron or fire-resistant overalls. Closed leather boots, not sandals. And a hard hat if you work near overhead hazards. .Trim()

.Trim()

Second, ventilation. Never smelt indoors without proper airflow. A fume hood or a forced-air fan that pulls fumes away from you and out of the building. If you work outside, stay upwind of the furnace. A simple test: light a match and see if the smoke blows away from you. If not, reposition. .Trim()

.Trim()

Third, fire safety. You must have a fire extinguisher rated for metal fires (Class D) and one for electrical fires (Class C). Also keep a bucket of dry sand to smother small metal fires. Never use water on a metal fire; it can explode. Have a first aid kit with burn gel, sterile bandages, and eye wash. .Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, the furnace and crucibles. Use a proper crucible made of clay-graphite or silicon carbide. Never use a makeshift container like a tin can. The furnace must be stable and away from walls. Keep a long-handled tongs and a mould that is dry and preheated. .Trim()

.Trim()

Fifth, chemicals. If you use acids like nitric acid or aqua regia, store them in acid-resistant containers, labelled clearly. Keep a spill kit with baking soda to neutralise acid spills. Never store acids near metals or organic materials. .Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, a safe work area. Clear the floor of clutter. Keep a minimum of 2 metres around the furnace. Use a metal table for hot items. Mark the area with warning signs. Train everyone who enters. These tools and materials may cost money, but they cost far less than a hospital bill or a funeral. Start with the basics and add as you can..Trim()

Follow these steps every time you smelt or refine gold. Step 1: Prepare yourself. Put on all your PPE: respirator, goggles, gloves, apron, boots. Tie back long hair. Remove jewellery that can catch fire or conduct heat. Tell someone you are working and where. .Trim()

.Trim()

Step 2: Check your workspace. Ensure ventilation is on. Test the fan with a smoke match. Make sure the fire extinguisher is within reach. Clear the floor. Have your first aid kit open and ready. .Trim()

Step 5: Monitor the heat. Use a pyrometer or observe colour: gold melts at 1064°C, so the crucible will glow bright orange. Do not leave the furnace unattended. Keep a log of time and temperature. .Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not wearing a respirator. Many people think a cloth mask is enough. It is not. Metal fumes and acid gases pass right through. Fix: Buy a proper respirator with P100 or acid gas cartridges. Replace cartridges regularly. .Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 2: Working in a closed room. Fumes build up and you inhale them. Fix: Always work outdoors or with forced ventilation. If you must work indoors, install a fume hood and keep a window open. .Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 3: Using water on a metal fire. This causes a violent explosion. Fix: Keep dry sand or a Class D extinguisher. Never use water. .Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 4: Storing acids in unlabelled bottles. People mistake them for water or juice. Fix: Use clearly labelled acid-resistant containers. Store in a locked cabinet away from food. .Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 5: Pouring molten metal into a wet mould. The moisture turns to steam and explodes, splashing molten gold. Fix: Preheat moulds to at least 200°C before pouring. .Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 6: Leaving the furnace unattended. A fire can start in seconds. Fix: Never leave a hot furnace alone. If you must step away, turn it off. .Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 7: Not having a first aid kit. Small burns become big infections. Fix: Keep a kit with burn gel, bandages, and eye wash. Check it monthly. .Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 8: Working alone. If you get hurt, no one can help. Fix: Always have a buddy. Even a family member nearby who can call for help. .Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 9: Ignoring mercury safety. Mercury is a neurotoxin. Fix: Never heat mercury indoors. Use a retort to capture fumes. Store mercury in sealed containers. .Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 10: Skipping clean-up. Leftover chemicals and slag can harm children or animals. Fix: Clean up immediately after every session. Dispose of waste properly. .Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Best practice means doing things the right way even when no one is watching. Start with a safety plan. Write down the hazards in your workshop: fire, fumes, acid burns, heavy lifting. For each, write what you will do to prevent it and what to do if it happens. Share this plan with everyone who works with you. .Trim()

.Trim()

Training is key. Every new worker must be trained before they touch any equipment. Do not assume they know. Show them how to use the respirator, how to light the furnace, how to pour safely. Let them practice under supervision. Refresh training every six months. .Trim()

.Trim()

Emergency preparedness. Know the emergency numbers for your area: fire, ambulance, police. Keep a phone charged and accessible. Practice a fire drill once a month. Make sure everyone knows where the extinguisher and first aid kit are. .Trim()

.Trim()

Health monitoring. If you work with lead or mercury, get regular blood tests. In many African countries, clinics can test for lead levels. If you feel dizzy, have headaches, or tingling in your hands, see a doctor and tell them about your work. .Trim()

.Trim()

Environmental responsibility. Do not dump chemicals or slag into rivers or soil. It poisons your community. Use a lined pit or take waste to a proper disposal site. Recycle acids when possible. .Trim()

.Trim()

Community awareness. Teach your neighbours about the dangers. If you smelt near homes, let people know when you will work and that they should stay upwind. Better yet, site your workshop away from homes. .Trim()

.Trim()

Record keeping. Keep a log of every smelt: date, materials, temperature, any incidents. This helps you improve and shows buyers you are professional. .Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, lead by example. If you are the boss, wear your PPE every time. If you see a unsafe act, correct it politely. Create a culture where safety is normal, not optional. Remember: safe gold is better gold. It protects you, your family, and your future..Trim()

Module 3 · Understanding Gold and Gold Alloys

Introduction and Why It Matters

Gold is a soft, dense, yellow metal that has been valued for thousands of years. In Africa, gold has been mined and traded for centuries, from the ancient kingdoms of Ghana and Mali to modern artisanal mining in Tanzania, Ghana, and South Africa. Understanding gold and its alloys is the foundation of smelting and refining. Pure gold, known as 24 karat (24K), is too soft for most jewellery and industrial uses. It is mixed with other metals to make alloys that are harder, change colour, or lower the melting point. For example, adding copper makes gold reddish and harder, while adding silver makes it greenish-yellow. Adding palladium or nickel makes it white. The purity of gold is measured in karats or fineness. 24K is 99.9% pure, 18K is 75% gold, 14K is 58.3%, and 10K is 41.7%. In Africa, most artisanal gold is smelted into doré bars, which are a mix of gold and silver, often 70-90% gold. Knowing the composition of your gold is critical because it affects how you smelt, refine, and sell it. If you smelt gold with too much copper, you may create a brittle bar that cracks. If you refine gold without removing silver, you cannot reach high purity. This module will teach you the properties of gold and common alloys, how to identify them, and why this knowledge matters for your business. You will learn to test gold, calculate alloy mixes, and avoid costly mistakes. Whether you are a small-scale miner, a jeweller, or a refiner, this knowledge will help you get the best price and produce quality products. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Determine the gold content of your starting material. If you have doré bars or scrap jewellery, test it. Use a touchstone and acid test or an electronic tester. Record the karat or percentage. For example, if you have 10 grams of 18K gold, it contains 7.5 grams of pure gold (10 x 0.75). Step 2: Decide the alloy you want to make. Common alloys: 22K (91.7% gold, 8.3% copper/silver), 18K (75% gold, 12.5% copper, 12.5% silver), 14K (58.3% gold, 41.7% copper/silver), and 9K (37.5% gold, 62.5% copper/silver). Split alloy as desired (e.g., 2.5g copper, 2.5g silver). Step 4: Melt the pure gold first in a preheated crucible. Use a torch or furnace. When molten, add the alloy metals. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not testing the purity of your starting gold. If you assume your gold is pure when it's not, your alloy will be off. Fix: Always test with acid or electronic tester before alloying. Mistake 2: Using dirty or oxidised metals. Copper and silver can have oxides that affect the alloy. Fix: Clean metals with sandpaper or pickle before use. Mistake 3: Adding alloy metals to molten gold too quickly. This can cause splattering or incomplete mixing. Fix: Preheat alloy metals and add slowly. Mistake 4: Overheating the melt. Gold boils at 2856°C, but alloy metals like zinc boil at 907°C and can fume off, changing the alloy. Fix: Keep temperature just above melting point (around 1100°C). Use a reducing flame to prevent oxidation. Mistake 5: Not using flux. Without flux, oxides and impurities remain, making the alloy brittle. Fix: Add borax or commercial flux before pouring. Mistake 6: Pouring into a cold mould. This causes the gold to cool too fast and crack. Fix: Preheat mould to 200-300°C. Mistake 7: Not stirring the melt. This leads to uneven alloy. Fix: Stir gently with a graphite rod for 10-15 seconds. Mistake 8: Quenching too soon. If you quench before the bar solidifies, it can warp. Fix: Wait until the bar loses its red glow, then quench. Mistake 9: Using the wrong acid for testing. Nitric acid alone won't test gold above 14K; you need aqua regia or a testing kit. Fix: Use proper acid test solutions. Mistake 10: Ignoring safety. Fumes from alloying can be toxic (e.g., zinc fumes). Fix: Always wear a respirator and work outdoors or with ventilation. In Africa, many smelters face these mistakes due to lack of training. For example, in Tanzania, some smelters add copper without flux, resulting in brittle bars that break when dropped. By avoiding these mistakes, you save time, money, and gold. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is non-negotiable when working with gold and alloys. First, protect your eyes. Wear safety glasses or a face shield. Molten gold can splatter. Second, wear heat-resistant gloves and a leather apron. Never wear synthetic clothing; it melts and sticks to skin. Third, work in a well-ventilated area. Fumes from flux, zinc, and other metals are toxic. Use a fume hood or work outdoors. If indoors, use a fan to blow fumes away from you. Fourth, keep a fire extinguisher and a bucket of sand nearby. Never use water on a metal fire. Fifth, store acids safely. Nitric acid and sulfuric acid are corrosive. Keep them in labelled, tightly closed containers away from metals and bases. Sixth, never eat or drink in the workspace. Wash hands thoroughly after handling metals and chemicals. Seventh, use a respirator when melting alloys with zinc or nickel. These metals produce harmful fumes. Eighth, preheat crucibles and moulds gradually to avoid thermal shock. Ninth, never leave a torch or furnace unattended. Tenth, have a first aid kit and know how to treat burns. For chemical burns, rinse with plenty of water. For thermal burns, cool with water and cover. Best practices: Always weigh and record everything. Use clean tools. Keep your workspace tidy. Label all alloys and chemicals. Test your gold before and after alloying. Calibrate your scale regularly. Replace worn crucibles. Use a pyrometer to monitor temperature. Train others before letting them work. Introduction and Why It Matters

Fluxes are chemicals added to gold ore or concentrate before smelting to help separate the gold from impurities. Without fluxes, the smelting process would be inefficient, and you would lose a lot of gold in the slag. In Africa, many small-scale miners smelt gold using simple furnaces and crucibles. The right flux mix can increase gold recovery from 70% to over 95%. That is a huge difference in income..Trim()

.Trim()

Fluxes work by lowering the melting point of impurities and making them flow easily. They also help remove oxides and other unwanted materials. Common fluxes include borax, soda ash, silica sand, and fluorspar. Each has a specific job. For example, borax helps dissolve metal oxides and prevents gold from sticking to the crucible. Soda ash helps reduce melting point and improves slag fluidity. Silica sand adds silica to balance the slag chemistry..Trim()

.Trim()

The type and amount of flux depend on the ore. If your ore is high in iron, you need more silica. If it is high in silica, you need more soda ash or limestone. In Africa, ores vary widely: from quartz veins in Ghana to alluvial deposits in Zimbabwe. So you must test your ore and adjust your flux recipe. A common mistake is using too much borax, which can make the slag too thin and cause gold to be lost in the slag. .Trim()

Smelting is not just about melting gold; it is about creating two distinct layers: a metal layer (gold and other heavy metals) and a slag layer (light impurities). Fluxes make this separation clean. Introduction and Why It Matters

Gravity concentration is the step where you separate heavy gold particles from lighter sand, rock, and dirt using water and motion. Before you smelt, you want your material as rich in gold as possible. Smelting raw ore is wasteful: you burn extra fuel, create more slag, and lose gold in the process. By concentrating first, you increase the gold content, sometimes from a few grams per tonne to hundreds of grams per tonne. This makes smelting faster, cheaper, and cleaner..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many small-scale miners already use simple gravity methods like panning and sluicing. But if you rush or do it wrong, you leave gold behind. Studies in Ghana and Tanzania show that proper gravity concentration can recover over 90% of gold, while poor methods recover less than 50%..Trim()

.Trim()

Gravity concentration works because gold is about 19 times denser than water and 6-7 times denser than common rock. When you swirl a pan or run a sluice, lighter materials wash away while gold settles. The key is to use the right equipment, classify your material into similar sizes, and control water flow. You don't need expensive machines. A simple pan, a sluice box, or a spiral concentrator can do the job if you understand the principles..Trim()

Module 7 · Mercury Amalgamation: Techniques and Dangers

Introduction and Why It Matters

Mercury amalgamation is a method used by small-scale gold miners across Africa to capture fine gold from crushed ore or concentrates. It works because mercury binds with gold particles to form an amalgam, a soft, silvery-gold blob. This method is popular because it is cheap, fast, and requires little equipment. In many African countries like Ghana, Tanzania, Zimbabwe, and Burkina Faso, miners use mercury in sluice boxes, pans, or small ball mills. For example, a miner might add 10 grams of mercury to a bucket of concentrate and stir for 20 minutes. The amalgam is then squeezed through a cloth to remove excess mercury, leaving a hard ball that is later burned to evaporate the mercury, leaving raw gold. However, mercury is highly toxic. It damages the brain, kidneys, and lungs, and it never breaks down in the environment. When burned, it becomes an invisible vapor that can be inhaled by families, including children. In water, bacteria convert it to methylmercury, which builds up in fish and can poison entire communities. Despite these dangers, many miners continue because they lack safer alternatives or training. This module will teach you the correct techniques to minimize risk, but more importantly, it will show you why you should consider moving away from mercury altogether. You will learn how to use it safely if you must, but also how to protect yourself, your family, and your community.
