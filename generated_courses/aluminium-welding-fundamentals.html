Module 1 · The Aluminium Welding Business

Why Aluminium Is Different from Steel

Aluminium is not just a softer steel. If you treat it like steel, you will fail. Steel is heavy and strong; aluminium is light and conducts heat about five times faster. That means when you weld aluminium, the heat runs away from the weld pool quickly. You need more amps and a faster travel speed. Steel welds at around 1,500Â°C; aluminium melts at about 660Â°C, but its oxide layer melts at over 2,000Â°C. That oxide skin forms instantly and must be broken before you can weld. With TIG, you use AC current to clean the oxide. With MIG, you need a push technique and a spool gun or a teflon liner because aluminium wire is soft and birds-nests easily..Trim()

.Trim()

Aluminium also does not change colour when it gets hot. Steel glows red before it melts; aluminium stays silver until it suddenly collapses. You must learn to judge the weld pool by its fluidity and sound, not by colour. Porosity is a big enemy. Aluminium absorbs hydrogen when molten, so you must clean the metal with a stainless steel brush dedicated to aluminium, then wipe with acetone or alcohol. Do not use the same brush on steel..Trim()

.Trim()

For a beginner in Africa, the most common aluminium jobs are window frames, door frames, roofing sheets, and repairs on cooking pots or water tanks. These are often thin materials, 1mm to 3mm. You cannot weld thin aluminium with a stick welder. You need TIG or MIG. A basic inverter TIG machine with AC/DC capability costs around $500 to $800 new. A MIG with a spool gun is similar. If you cannot afford that yet, start by practising on scrap with a rented machine or borrow one. The key difference is preparation: clean, clean, clean. Then use a stainless steel wire brush, then weld hot and fast. That is the aluminium way..Trim()

Module 2 · Aluminium Metallurgy and Properties

The Aluminium Family of Alloys

Aluminium is rarely used pure. It is mixed with other metals to make alloys that are stronger, easier to weld, or more resistant to corrosion. The main alloying elements are copper, magnesium, manganese, silicon, and zinc. In Africa, you will commonly find alloys from the 1000, 3000, 5000, and 6000 series. The 1000 series is almost pure aluminium (99% or more). It is soft, very weldable, and used for electrical bus bars and cooking pots. The 3000 series contains manganese. It is stronger than 1000 series and used for roofing sheets and heat exchangers. The 5000 series contains magnesium. It is strong, corrosion-resistant, and used in boat hulls, truck bodies, and fuel tanks. The 6000 series contains magnesium and silicon. It is heat-treatable and used for window frames, ladders, and structural beams..Trim()

.Trim()

When you pick up a piece of aluminium, you cannot tell its alloy by looking. It might have a stamp like '6061-T6' or '5083-H321'. The first four digits tell you the alloy family. The letter and numbers after the dash tell you the temper, or how it was strengthened. If there is no stamp, you must be careful. Welding a mystery alloy can lead to cracks or weak joints..Trim()

.Trim()

In many African workshops, you will work with recycled aluminium from car rims, engine blocks, or old window frames. These are often 6000 or 7000 series, but they may be mixed. To identify them, use a spark test: touch the metal to a grinding wheel. 5000 series gives a short, dull spark. 6000 series gives a longer, brighter spark. 7000 series gives a very bright spark with many forks. This is not exact, but it helps. For critical jobs, buy certified new stock from a supplier. Common suppliers include Hulamin in South Africa, AluAfrica in Nigeria, and Egyptalum in Egypt. Always ask for the mill certificate..Trim()

.Trim()

As a welder, your job is to match the filler wire to the base alloy. For 5000 series, use 5356 or 5183 filler. For 6000 series, use 4043 or 5356. For 1000 and 3000 series, use 4043. Never use 4043 on 5000 series if the joint will be exposed to salt water, because it can corrode. Understanding the alloy family is the first step to a strong weld..Trim()

Heat-Treatable vs Non-Heat-Treatable

Aluminium alloys are divided into two big groups: heat-treatable and non-heat-treatable. This difference decides how you weld them, how you strengthen them after welding, and what jobs they are good for. Non-heat-treatable alloys get their strength from working the metalârolling, hammering, or stretching it. You cannot make them stronger by heating and cooling. The 1000, 3000, 4000, and 5000 series are non-heat-treatable. The most common in Africa are 1100, 3003, and 5052. They are easy to weld and form. They do not lose much strength in the weld zone, so you can weld them and use them without extra heat treatment. However, if you weld a heavily cold-worked 5052-H34, the heat will soften the area near the weld. You cannot restore that strength without re-rolling, which is impossible on a finished job..Trim()

.Trim()

Heat-treatable alloys get their strength from a process called precipitation hardening. You heat the metal to about 500Â°C, quench it in water, then let it age at room temperature or in an oven. This traps alloying elements in the metal and makes it hard. The 2000, 6000, and 7000 series are heat-treatable. Common ones are 6061, 6063, 2024, and 7075. When you weld these, the heat from welding destroys the heat treatment in the weld zone. The area becomes soft and weak. For 6061-T6, the weld zone can lose up to 50% of its strength. You can recover some strength by re-heat-treating the whole part after welding, but this is expensive and often not practical in a small workshop. Instead, you design the joint so the weld is not carrying the full load, or you use a thicker piece..Trim()

.Trim()

In practice, many African welders work with 6061 because it is common in window frames and truck parts. If you must weld 6061, use 4043 or 5356 filler. After welding, you can do a local heat treatment with a torch, but it is tricky. Better to accept the loss and over-engineer the joint. For non-heat-treatable alloys like 5052, you can weld without worry about losing strength, but you must use the right filler. 5052 is often used for boat hulls and chemical tanks because it is strong and corrosion-resistant. Remember: heat-treatable alloys are stronger but more difficult to weld. Non-heat-treatable are weaker but more forgiving. Choose based on the job..Trim()

The Oxide Layer Problem

Aluminium reacts with oxygen in the air to form a thin layer of aluminium oxide on its surface. This layer is only about 0.005 to 0.01 microns thickâthinner than a soap bubbleâbut it is very tough. It melts at 2072Â°C, while the aluminium underneath melts at 660Â°C. That means when you heat aluminium to weld it, the oxide layer stays solid and blocks the weld. If you do not remove it, your filler wire will ball up and not stick, or you will get a cold weld with no penetration. The oxide also traps dirt, oil, and moisture, which cause porosity and weak joints..Trim()

.Trim()

To weld aluminium, you must break up the oxide layer and keep it from forming during welding. The most common method is to use alternating current (AC) with a tungsten inert gas (TIG) welder. The AC cycle has two parts: the positive half cleans the oxide, and the negative half penetrates and melts the metal. The cleaning action is called 'arc cleaning' or 'etching'. You will see a white, frosted zone around the weld. That is normal. For thicker metal, you can use direct current electrode negative (DCEN) with helium shielding gas, but you must clean the oxide mechanically first. With MIG welding, you use direct current electrode positive (DCEP), which also provides some cleaning, but you still need to clean before welding..Trim()

.Trim()

Mechanical cleaning is essential. Use a stainless steel wire brush that is dedicated to aluminium only. Never use a brush that has touched steel, because steel particles will embed in the aluminium and cause rust. Brush in one direction, not back and forth, to avoid pushing oxide into the metal. Then wipe with acetone or isopropyl alcohol to remove oil and grease. Do not use chlorinated solvents like brake cleanerâthey create toxic fumes when heated. Clean the joint and at least 25 mm on each side just before welding. If you wait more than a few hours, the oxide will grow back. In humid African coastal areas, it can regrow in minutes. So clean, then weld immediately..Trim()

.Trim()

Another trick is to use a stainless steel wire brush on a drill or grinder for large areas. But be careful not to overheat the metal. Also, you can use a chemical etch, but that is messy and not common in small shops. The best practice is: brush, wipe, weld. Keep your brushes clean and stored in a plastic bag. If you see a dark, dull surface, that is oxide. If you see a bright, silvery surface, you are ready to weld. Remember, the oxide layer is your enemy. Defeat it with mechanical cleaning and the right welding current..Trim()

Thermal Conductivity and Distortion

Aluminium conducts heat about five times faster than steel. That means when you weld, the heat does not stay in one spotâit races away into the rest of the metal. This causes two big problems: you need more heat to start the weld, and the surrounding metal expands and then contracts, causing distortion. In steel, you can weld a small area and the heat stays local. In aluminium, you must use higher amperage and sometimes preheat the whole part to 100-150Â°C to help the weld fuse. But preheating also increases distortion. It is a balancing act..Trim()

.Trim()

Distortion happens because aluminium expands and contracts more than steel when heated and cooled. For a 1-meter length, aluminium expands about 23 microns per degree Celsius, while steel expands about 12. So if you weld a long seam, the metal around the weld expands, then cools and shrinks, pulling the part out of shape. You will see bending, twisting, or buckling. Thin sheets (1-3 mm) are the worst. They can warp like a potato chip. To control distortion, use these methods: First, tack weld frequentlyâevery 50-75 mmâbefore welding the full seam. Second, use a back-step welding technique: weld short segments in the opposite direction of the overall travel. For example, weld from B to A, then D to C, and so on. Third, use heat sinks like copper or aluminium blocks clamped to the workpiece to draw heat away. Fourth, use the lowest amperage that gives good fusion. Fifth, if possible, weld both sides alternately to balance the stress..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many workshops do not have fancy fixtures. You can use heavy steel plates or even wet sandbags as heat sinks. Clamp the work to a strong table. For long seams, you can also use a technique called 'stitch welding' where you weld 25 mm, skip 75 mm, then come back and fill the gaps. This gives the metal time to cool. Another trick is to pre-set the parts with a slight opposite bend, so when they distort, they end up straight. This takes experience..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, distortion is not just about looksâit can make a part unusable. A warped window frame will not fit. A distorted boat hull can leak. So plan your welds. Use as few passes as possible. Use a skip technique. And if you are welding thin sheets, practice on scrap first. The heat is your enemy, but with the right technique, you can control it..Trim()

Hot Cracking and Why It Happens

Hot cracking, also called hot shortness or solidification cracking, happens when the weld metal is cooling and is still weak. It occurs at temperatures just below the melting point, when the metal is mushy. If the stresses from shrinking are too high, the weld tears apart along the centerline or in the heat-affected zone. You will see a crack that looks like a jagged line, often right down the middle of the weld. It can happen immediately after welding or hours later. Hot cracking is a serious defect because it weakens the joint and can lead to failure..Trim()

.Trim()

The main cause is the wrong filler alloy or too much dilution from the base metal. For example, if you weld 6061 with 4043 filler, the silicon in the filler helps, but if you use 5356 filler on 6061, you might get cracking because 5356 contains magnesium, which can form brittle phases with copper in the base metal. The most crack-sensitive alloys are the 6000 series, especially 6061 and 6063. The 7000 series is also prone. The 5000 series is generally resistant to hot cracking. Another cause is a weld bead that is too wide or too deep. A deep, narrow bead cools faster and has more stress. A wide, shallow bead is better. Also, if you have a lot of restraintâlike a thick plate clamped tightlyâthe weld cannot shrink, so it cracks..Trim()

.Trim()

To prevent hot cracking, use the right filler. For 6061, use 4043 (ER4043) or 5356 (ER5356). 4043 is more crack-resistant but weaker. 5356 is stronger but more crack-sensitive. Many welders prefer 4043 for 6061. For 7000 series, use 4043 or 5356, but 4043 is often better. For 5000 series, use 5356 or 5183. Never use 4043 on 5000 series if the joint will be anodized, because it turns black. Also, control your heat input. Use a higher travel speed and lower amperage to reduce the size of the mushy zone. .Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Porosity and How to Stop It

Porosity is those little holes you see in a weld, like bubbles frozen in the metal. In aluminium, it happens when hydrogen gas gets trapped as the weld cools. The gas comes from moisture, oil, grease, paint, or even the air. Aluminium is very sensitive to this because it melts at around 660Â°C and absorbs hydrogen quickly when hot, then releases it as it freezes, forming bubbles..Trim()

.Trim()

To stop porosity, you must clean the metal properly. Use a stainless steel wire brush dedicated only to aluminium. Brush the joint and 5 cm around it until it shines. Then wipe with acetone or denatured alcohol to remove oil. Do this immediately before welding. Also, keep your filler wire clean and dry. Store it in a sealed plastic bag with silica gel. If you use a gas cylinder, make sure the gas is pure argon (99.99% or better). Flow rate should be 10-15 litres per minute for a #6 or #7 cup. Too little flow lets air in; too much causes turbulence that also pulls in air..Trim()

.Trim()

Your torch angle and travel speed matter. Keep the torch tilted about 10-15 degrees back (pushing angle) and move steadily. If you go too slow, the weld pool overheats and absorbs more hydrogen. If you go too fast, you might not get enough penetration, but that's a different defect. For a butt weld on 3 mm aluminium, aim for a travel speed around 20-30 cm per minute. Listen to the sound: a steady sizzling like bacon is good. A popping or hissing sound means trouble..Trim()

.Trim()

If you still get porosity, check your gas lines for leaks. A tiny leak will pull in moist air. Use soapy water on connections. Also, preheat thick sections to 100-150Â°C to drive off moisture. But don't overheat. For thin sheets, no preheat needed. If you're welding outside, set up a windbreak. Even a gentle breeze can blow away your shielding gas. A simple cardboard or metal sheet works..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, prevention is better than grinding out porosity. Once you have it, you must cut or grind out the porous area and re-weld. That wastes time and filler. So take the extra five minutes to clean and set up right. Your welds will be stronger and look better..Trim()

Cracks: Hot and Cold

Cracks in aluminium welds are serious. They can cause parts to fail suddenly. There are two main types: hot cracks and cold cracks. Hot cracks happen while the weld is still hot, just after it solidifies. Cold cracks happen later, sometimes hours or days after welding. Both are preventable if you know the causes..Trim()

.Trim()

Hot cracks usually occur in the weld metal itself. They happen when the weld metal is weak during cooling and shrinks. Aluminium alloys like 6061 are prone to hot cracking if you use the wrong filler. For 6061, use 4043 or 5356 filler. 4043 is more crack-resistant but less strong. 5356 is stronger but can crack if the weld is highly restrained. The best way to avoid hot cracks is to use the right filler and reduce restraint. Preheat to 150-200Â°C for thick sections to slow cooling and let the weld metal gain strength before shrinking. Also, use a backstep welding technique: weld short segments, then step back and weld over the start of the previous segment. This spreads out the heat and reduces stress..Trim()

.Trim()

Cold cracks, also called delayed cracks, happen in the heat-affected zone (HAZ) or at the weld toe. They are often caused by hydrogen embrittlement, similar to porosity but worse. Moisture or oil on the metal can introduce hydrogen. The crack may not appear until hours later. To prevent cold cracks, clean the metal thoroughly, use dry filler, and preheat to drive off moisture. Also, avoid welding on very thick sections without preheat, as fast cooling traps hydrogen. If you must weld a highly restrained joint, use a lower-strength filler like 4043, which is more ductile..Trim()

.Trim()

If you see a crack, you must remove it completely. Grind out the entire crack plus 2-3 cm beyond each end. Use a carbide burr or grinding wheel. Then re-weld with the correct filler and technique. Never try to just weld over a crack; it will come back. Inspect after welding with a magnifying glass or dye penetrant to ensure no cracks remain. For critical parts, like trailer frames or boat hulls, always use dye penetrant or X-ray after welding. Cracks can be hidden, so don't trust your eyes alone..Trim()

Lack of Fusion and Penetration

Lack of fusion (LOF) means the weld metal didn't bond to the base metal or to the previous weld pass. Lack of penetration (LOP) means the weld didn't go deep enough into the joint root. Both are weak points that can cause failures. They often happen because of wrong technique, insufficient heat, or dirty metal..Trim()

.Trim()

To prevent LOF, you need enough heat and the right torch angle. For TIG welding, set your amperage based on thickness: about 1 amp per 0.025 mm of thickness. For 3 mm aluminium, use 120-140 amps. For MIG, use 1 amp per 0.025 mm as a starting point, but adjust for travel speed. Keep a short arc length (for TIG, 2-3 mm; for MIG, 10-15 mm). Push the torch slightly into the joint so the arc digs in. If you move too fast, the edges won't melt. If you move too slow, you might get a wide, shallow weld that still lacks fusion at the sides. Use a weaving motion only if needed; for thin material, a straight drag is better..Trim()

.Trim()

LOP happens when the root doesn't melt. For butt welds, make sure you have a proper gap (1-2 mm for 3 mm thick aluminium) and use a backing plate if possible. For TIG, you can use a keyhole technique: melt through until you see a keyhole shape, then move forward. That ensures full penetration. For MIG, use a root gap and higher amperage. If you're welding from one side only, you might need to bevel the edges to get access. A 60-degree included bevel is common. Also, clean the joint faces; oxide and oil prevent fusion..Trim()

.Trim()

If you suspect LOF or LOP, you can check with a fillet weld break test: weld a T-joint, then break it in a vise. The weld should tear the base metal, not pull away cleanly. If it pulls away, you have LOF. For LOP, cut a cross-section and polish it to see the penetration depth. In the field, use a flashlight and mirror to look at the root. If you see a line where the weld meets the base metal without blending, that's LOF. Grind it out and re-weld. Prevention is easier: use enough heat, clean metal, and correct technique..Trim()

Distortion and Its Correction

Distortion is when your welded part bends, twists, or warps because of heat. Aluminium expands and contracts a lot when heated, about twice as much as steel. So distortion is a big problem. It can make your part unusable. But you can control it with good planning and technique..Trim()

.Trim()

First, minimize heat input. Use the lowest amperage that gives good fusion. Weld fast, but not so fast that you lose penetration. Use a smaller weld bead. For thin sheets, consider skip welding: weld a short segment, then move to another area, letting the first cool. This spreads the heat. Also, use a heat sink. A piece of copper or aluminium clamped next to the weld will draw heat away. You can also use a wet rag to cool the weld immediately after, but be careful not to quench too fast, as that can cause cracks..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, balance the stresses. If you're welding a T-joint, weld on both sides alternately. If you're welding a long seam, use backstep welding: start at the end, weld toward the start, then step back and weld over the start. This balances the shrinkage. For butt welds, tack the ends first, then weld from the center out. That way, the shrinkage pulls evenly. Also, pre-setting the parts in the opposite direction of expected distortion helps. For example, if you know the part will bend up, clamp it down with a slight downward bend before welding..Trim()

.Trim()

If distortion still happens, you can correct it. For thin sheets, you can hammer the weld area with a planishing hammer while it's still warm. That stretches the metal and flattens it. For thicker parts, you can use localized heating and quenching. Heat a small area with a torch until it's dull red (about 500Â°C), then quench with water. That shrinks the metal and pulls it back. But be careful: too much heat can weaken the metal. Another method is to use a hydraulic press or clamps to force it straight while heating. Always check with a straightedge or square after correction..Trim()

.Trim()

Prevention is better than correction. Plan your welds, use jigs and clamps, and don't rush. A little extra time setting up saves a lot of time fixing..Trim()

Visual Inspection

Visual inspection is the first and most important check you do on a weld. It's free, fast, and can catch most defects. But you need good lighting, a clean surface, and a keen eye. Do it before, during, and after welding..Trim()

.Trim()

Before welding, inspect the joint preparation. Check that the edges are clean, free of oil, grease, paint, and oxide. Look for cracks, nicks, or bends. Make sure the gap is correct: for 3 mm aluminium, a 1-2 mm gap is good for butt welds. Check the fit-up; misalignment should be less than 10% of thickness. Use a tape measure or feeler gauge. Also, check your equipment: gas flow, filler wire cleanliness, and torch condition..Trim()

.Trim()

During welding, watch the weld pool. It should be shiny and fluid. If it looks dull or has a scum, that's oxide or contamination. Listen to the sound: steady sizzling is good; popping means trouble. Check that the arc is stable and the filler is melting smoothly. If you see the pool getting too wide or too narrow, adjust your travel speed or amperage. Also, watch for undercut: a groove melted into the base metal along the weld toe. That happens if you move too fast or use too much heat. Stop and fix it before continuing..Trim()

.Trim()

After welding, let the part cool, then clean it. Use a stainless steel brush to remove smut and oxide. Then inspect with a bright light and a magnifying glass (10x). Look for porosity (small holes), cracks (especially at the ends), lack of fusion (a line where the weld doesn't blend), undercut, overlap, and spatter. Check the weld size: for a fillet weld, the leg should be at least the thickness of the thinner part. Use a weld gauge if you have one. Measure the length and check for uniformity. If you see a defect, mark it with a paint marker and grind it out. Don't try to cover it up..Trim()

.Trim()

For critical welds, like on a trailer or a boat, you might need to do a dye penetrant test after visual inspection. But visual is always first. Train your eye to spot problems early. A good welder is also a good inspector..Trim()

When to Use Dye Penetrant and X-Ray

Visual inspection catches surface defects, but some defects are hidden below the surface. That's when you need dye penetrant testing (PT) or X-ray (RT). PT finds surface-breaking defects like cracks, porosity, and lack of fusion that open to the surface. X-ray finds internal defects like porosity, inclusions, and lack of penetration. Both are used in critical applications, but they cost time and money, so you don't use them on every weld..Trim()

.Trim()

Dye penetrant testing is simple and cheap. You clean the weld, apply a red dye, let it soak for 10-15 minutes, wipe it off, then spray a white developer. Any dye that seeped into a defect will bleed out and show as a red line or spot. It works on non-porous materials like aluminium. Use it when you suspect surface cracks, especially after welding high-strength alloys like 6061 or 7075. It's also good for checking welds that will be machined or painted. PT kits cost about $50 and are available at welding supply shops. But PT only works on clean, dry surfaces. Don't use it on hot metal. And it only finds defects that reach the surface..Trim()

.Trim()

X-ray testing is more expensive and requires special equipment and training. A portable X-ray machine costs thousands of dollars, and you need radiation safety gear. But it gives a picture of the inside of the weld. You use it for critical welds: pressure vessels, structural beams, aircraft parts, and high-value projects. In Africa, you might find X-ray services in major cities like Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg. For a small shop, it's often outsourced. X-ray can find tiny porosity (0.5 mm) and lack of penetration. But it has limits: it's hard to detect cracks that are not aligned with the beam, and it's expensive. Typically, you X-ray a percentage of welds, like 10% or 20%, depending on the code..Trim()

.Trim()

When to use which? Start with visual. If you see a defect, fix it. If you suspect hidden defects, use PT for surface cracks. For internal defects, use X-ray. But remember, the best inspection is prevention: clean metal, correct technique, and proper filler. That reduces the need for expensive testing. In a small workshop, you might only use PT on critical parts and skip X-ray unless required by a client. Always follow the project specifications..Trim()

Gates, Frames and Doors

Aluminium gates, frames, and doors are in high demand across Africa because they resist rust, look modern, and last for years. To build a standard pedestrian gate, you need 40x40mm square tube for the frame, 25x25mm for the infill, and 2mm thick aluminium sheet for panels. Start by measuring the opening: a typical gate is 1m wide and 1.8m high. Cut your tubes with a mitre saw or a hacksaw with a mitre box, ensuring 45-degree corners for a clean join. Clean the cut ends with a stainless steel wire brush, then clamp the pieces on a flat welding table. Use a TIG welder with 2% thoriated tungsten electrode and 4043 filler rod (1.6mm diameter). Set the machine to 80-100 amps for 2mm thickness. Weld tack welds at each corner first, check for square by measuring diagonals, then weld the full joints. For doors, use 50x50mm frame with 3mm thickness; increase amps to 120-140. Add hinges by drilling and tapping, or weld pre-made steel hinges if you use a bi-metal insert. For frames around windows, use 40x40mm and weld at 90 degrees; a corner bracket helps alignment. Always wear a welding helmet with shade 10-12, leather gloves, and a respiratorâaluminium fumes are harmful. After welding, grind smooth with a flap disc, then finish with a wire brush and apply a clear lacquer or powder coat. A simple gate takes 2-3 hours and can sell for $150-$300 depending on design. Start with a small gate for a neighbour, then scale up..Trim()

Aluminium Boats and Canoes

Aluminium boats are ideal for African lakes, rivers, and coastal waters because they are lightweight, don't rot, and need less maintenance than wood. A typical 4-meter canoe or small fishing boat uses 3mm thick marine-grade aluminium sheets (5052 or 5083 alloy) for the hull and 40x40mm square tube for the gunwales and ribs. You will need a TIG welder capable of 200 amps, 5356 filler rod (1.6mm or 2.4mm), and a spool gun for MIG if you prefer faster welding on thicker sections. Start by building a jig from timber or steel to hold the hull shape. Cut the bottom sheet to size, then bend the sides using a brake or by clamping between two strong boards and hammering gently. Tack weld the hull seams every 100mm, then weld continuously with a slight back-step technique to control distortion. Use a heat sink (a copper bar) behind the seam to prevent burn-through. For ribs, space them every 400mm along the length. Weld the gunwales on top, then add a transom for an outboard motor using 6mm plate. Test for leaks by filling with water before painting. A 4m boat takes about 40-60 hours to build and can sell for $1,500-$3,000. Safety: always work in a well-ventilated area, and wear a P100 respirator when welding aluminium. Water Tanks and Containers

Aluminium water tanks are popular in rural Africa because they are lighter than concrete, don't rust like steel, and can be repaired easily. A typical 1000-litre tank is made from 3mm aluminium sheet rolled into a cylinder, with a 4mm bottom and top. You need a sheet roller or a local metal fabricator to roll the body. Cut the sheet to size: for a 1m diameter and 1.3m height, the circumference is 3.14m, so cut 3.2m to allow overlap. Weld the vertical seam using TIG with 4043 filler at 120-140 amps, using a back-step to reduce distortion. Then weld the bottom plate to the cylinder with a fillet weld, and add a top with a manhole cover. Reinforce the tank with two 25x25mm angle rings around the circumference, welded at 200mm intervals. For containers like jerry cans or small drums, use 2mm sheet and a MIG welder with 1.2mm wire for speed. Always pressure-test the tank by filling with water and checking for leaks; mark leaks and re-weld after draining. A 1000L tank takes about 8-10 hours to build and sells for $200-$400. For food-grade containers, use 5052 alloy and clean welds with a stainless brush, then passivate with nitric acid paste. Truck Bodies and Trailers

Aluminium truck bodies and trailers are big business in Africa because they are lighter than steel, which saves fuel and increases payload. Repair work is the bread and butter of an aluminium welder in Africa because everything from pots to truck bodies gets damaged and needs fixing. Common repairs include cracked water tanks, broken gate hinges, leaking boat seams, and torn truck sides. For truck body tears, use a MIG spool gun with 5356 wire at 180-200 amps, and add a backing plate if the tear is large.
