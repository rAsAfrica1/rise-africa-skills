Brainstorming Techniques for New Business Ideas

To generate business ideas, you don't need a genius momentâyou need a method. Start with the 'Problem Flood' technique. For one week, carry a small notebook or use a free app like Google Keep. Write down every frustration you see or experience: the long queue at the water point, the boda boda that overcharges, the maize flour that spoils quickly. Aim for 20 problems. Then, pick the top 5 that you or people you know face daily. For each, ask: 'How could I solve this with what I have?' This is the seed of a business..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, use 'SCAMPER' to twist existing products. SCAMPER stands for Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse. For example, a woman in Kenya sold mandazi (fried dough). She substituted wheat flour with cassava flour (Substitute), reducing cost by 40%. She combined mandazi with a delivery service for offices (Combine). You can do the same with any local product. Write each SCAMPER letter and force 3 ideas per letter. That's 21 ideas in 30 minutes..Trim()

.Trim()

Another powerful technique is 'Brainstorming Circles'. Gather 5-6 friends or fellow traders. Sit in a circle. One person states a problem, e.g., 'Farmers lose tomatoes after harvest.' Each person gives one idea, no matter how wild. No criticism allowed. Write all ideas on a flipchart or a wall using sticky notes. After 15 minutes, you'll have 30-50 ideas. Then vote for the top 3. This method works because it taps into collective knowledge and reduces fear of judgment..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, use 'Observation Walks'. Visit a busy market like Balogun in Lagos or Kariakoo in Dar es Salaam. Spend one hour watching what people buy, what they complain about, and what they carry. Note gaps: Is there no clean drinking water? No phone charging station? No affordable lunch? Each gap is a potential business. Combine these techniques weekly. Keep an 'Idea Journal'. Review it monthly. You'll soon have more ideas than you can pursue..Trim()

Evaluating Market Gaps and Unmet Needs

A market gap is a need that customers have but no one is satisfying well. To find one, look for three things: high demand, poor supply, and willingness to pay. Start by counting. Visit a market and count how many vendors sell a product, say, mobile phone accessories. If there are 10 vendors and 200 customers per day, the market is crowded. But if you see 10 vendors and they all run out of phone chargers by noon, that's a gap. In Kigali, a young man noticed that hardware stores sold paint but no one offered color mixing. .Trim()

Use the '5 Whys' to dig deeper. Ask 'Why?' five times about a problem. Example: Why do farmers in Uganda get low prices for maize? Because they sell immediately after harvest. Why? Because they need cash. Why? Because they have no storage. Why? Because storage costs money. Why? Because banks won't lend to small farmers. The gap: affordable storage or a loan product. Your business could be a small warehouse or a mobile money-based savings group..Trim()

.Trim()

Check demand with simple tools. Use Google Trends (free) to see if searches for a product are rising. In Nigeria, searches for 'solar inverter' grew 200% from 2020 to 2023. That's a gap. Also, talk to at least 20 potential customers. Ask: 'What do you currently do when you need X?' and 'How much do you pay?' If they already pay for a poor solution, they'll pay for a better one. For example, women in Ghana paid 5 cedis for a bucket of water from vendors. A new borehole with a small fee of 2 cedis per bucket would be a gap..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, look for 'underserved' groups: rural areas, low-income earners, women, and youth. A gap exists if a group is ignored by big companies. In Ethiopia, injera (flatbread) is made at home, but working women have no time. A business that delivers fresh injera batter daily fills a gap. Always verify: Can you reach these customers? Customer discovery interviews are conversations to learn what customers actually need, not to sell. The goal is to understand their problems, current solutions, and spending. For example, if you're thinking of a laundry service, ask: 'How do you currently wash your clothes? How long does it take? What do you do when you're busy?' Avoid pitching your idea. If you mention your solution, the customer will focus on being nice. Instead, keep the conversation about them. Take notes on exact words. After 20 interviews, look for patterns. Group answers into themes: price, convenience, quality, trust. The theme that appears most is your opportunity. Validating Problem-Solution Fit

Problem-solution fit means you have evidence that your solution solves a problem people care about and will pay for. It's not about a perfect product; it's about a minimum viable product (MVP) that tests your assumptions. Start by writing down your top 3 assumptions: e.g., 'Customers will pay 1000 naira for home delivery,' 'Farmers will use a mobile app to sell produce,' 'People will buy pre-cooked beans.' One low-cost test is the 'Landing Page' test. Use free tools like Carrd or Google Forms to create a one-page description of your service with a 'Buy Now' or 'Register Interest' button. Share the link on WhatsApp, Facebook, or local groups. If 100 people visit and 10 click 'Buy,' that's a 10% conversionâa good sign. If no one clicks, your solution may not fit. Another test is 'Pre-Sales.' For example, if you want to sell packaged garri, take orders and deposits before you buy packaging. If 20 people pay 500 naira deposit, you have validation..Trim()

For physical products, do a 'Pop-Up Stall.' Set up a small table at a market or outside a church. Sell a small batch. Watch how people react. Do they ask questions? Do they buy? Do they come back? .Trim()

Assessing Feasibility: Technical, Financial, and Legal

Before you invest money, check if your idea is feasible in three areas: technical, financial, and legal. Technical feasibility asks: Can you actually make or deliver this? Do you have the skills, equipment, and suppliers? For example, if you want to start a bakery, do you know how to bake? Do you have an oven? Can you get flour at a good price? If not, you need training or a partner. In Kenya, a group of youth wanted to make solar panels but lacked the technical know-how. They switched to assembling solar lamps from imported kitsâmuch easier. Write a list of every step from raw material to customer. Identify any step you can't do. That's a red flag..Trim()

.Trim()

Financial feasibility means the numbers work. Calculate your start-up costs: equipment, rent, licenses, initial stock. Then estimate monthly costs: materials, transport, salaries, airtime. Estimate your selling price and how many units you can sell per month. Use a simple formula: Break-even = Fixed Costs / (Price - Variable Cost per Unit). For example, if your fixed costs are 50,000 naira per month, price is 500 naira, and variable cost is 300 naira, then break-even = 50,000 / (500-300) = 250 units per month. Can you sell 250? If not, the idea is not financially feasible. Also, check if you can get a loan or grant. In Nigeria, you can apply for a BOI (Bank of Industry) loan or a Tony Elumelu Foundation grant..Trim()

.Trim()

Legal feasibility is about rules. Do you need a business registration? In most African countries, you can register a business name for as low as 5,000 naira (Nigeria) or 1,000 shillings (Kenya). Some businesses need special licenses: food handling, health permits, import/export. For example, selling bottled water requires a NAFDAC number in Nigeria. Selling alcohol requires a liquor license. If you ignore these, you risk fines or shutdown. Also, check local bylaws: can you run a business from home? Do you need a permit for a market stall? Visit your local government office or ask other traders. Spend one day researching. It's better to know now than to be closed later..Trim()

Introduction to Business Model Canvas

The Business Model Canvas is a one-page tool that helps you map out how your business creates, delivers, and captures value. It has nine building blocks: Customer Segments, Value Propositions, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams, Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partnerships, and Cost Structure. You can draw it on a large sheet of paper or use a free online tool like Canva or Miro. In Africa, where resources are limited, this canvas forces you to be clear and concise. For example, a poultry farmer in Ghana might identify her customer segments as local households and restaurants, her value proposition as fresh, affordable eggs, and her revenue streams as direct sales and subscriptions. The canvas is not a static document; you update it as you learn. It helps you see the big picture without getting lost in details. Start by filling in what you know, then validate each block with real customers. This tool is used by startups worldwide, from Silicon Valley to Nairobi's iHub. By the end of this lesson, you will have a draft canvas for your own business idea..Trim()

Mapping Key Partners, Activities, and Resources

Key Partners are the external companies or individuals you rely on to make your business work. They include suppliers, distributors, and even competitors you collaborate with. For a small bakery in Kenya, key partners might be the flour supplier, the local dairy for milk, and a delivery rider. Key Activities are the most important things your business must do to deliver your value proposition. For the bakery, that's baking bread, managing inventory, and marketing.
