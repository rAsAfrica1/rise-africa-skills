Module 1 · Introduction to Sweet Potato Farming

History and Global Importance of Sweet Potato

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) originated in Central and South America, but it has been grown in Africa for centuries. Portuguese traders brought it to West Africa in the 16th century, and it quickly spread because it grows well in poor soils and needs little water. Today, Africa produces about 30% of the world's sweet potatoes, with countries like Nigeria, Uganda, Tanzania, and Malawi leading. In many African communities, sweet potato is a staple food, especially during hunger gaps when maize and other cereals are not ready to harvest. It is also a cash crop for smallholder farmers..Trim()

Sweet potato is important because it is hardy. It can grow from sea level up to 2,500 meters, in rainfall as low as 500 mm per year, and in soils with low fertility. Unlike maize, it does not need expensive fertilizers to give a decent yield. Farmers can harvest as early as 3-4 months after planting, and they can leave the roots in the ground for months and dig them as needed. This makes it a reliable food security crop..Trim()

Globally, sweet potato is the seventh most important food crop. China is the largest producer, but Africa has the highest per capita consumption. In countries like Uganda, the average person eats over 100 kg of sweet potato per year. The crop also has industrial uses: starch, flour, animal feed, and even biofuel. Orange-fleshed varieties are promoted to fight vitamin A deficiency, which affects millions of African children..Trim()

With minimal investment, you can produce food for your family and surplus for sale. Understanding this history and importance helps you see sweet potato not just as a subsistence crop but as a business opportunity..Trim()

Nutritional Value and Health Benefits

Sweet potato is one of the most nutritious foods you can grow. A medium-sized root (about 150 g) provides more than 100% of the daily vitamin A requirement if it is an orange-fleshed variety. It is also rich in vitamin C, vitamin B6, potassium, and dietary fiber. The leaves are even more nutritious, containing protein, iron, and calcium. In many African households, sweet potato leaves are cooked as a vegetable, sometimes called 'sweet potato greens'..Trim()

The orange color comes from beta-carotene, which the body converts to vitamin A. Vitamin A deficiency is a major problem in Africa, causing night blindness and increasing the risk of death from infections in children. Eating just one small orange-fleshed sweet potato a day can prevent this deficiency. That is why organizations like HarvestPlus and the International Potato Center promote orange-fleshed varieties such as Beauregard, Kabode, and Vita..Trim()

Unlike white potatoes, sweet potatoes have a lower glycemic index, meaning they do not cause sudden spikes in blood sugar. This makes them suitable for people with diabetes when eaten in moderation. The potassium helps control blood pressure..Trim()

White-fleshed varieties are traditionally grown for their high starch content and are used for boiling, roasting, or making flour. Sweet Potato vs. Other Root Crops

Sweet potato is often compared to cassava, yam, and Irish potato. Each has strengths, but sweet potato stands out for speed and low input. Cassava takes 9-18 months to mature, while sweet potato matures in 3-5 months. Yam takes 6-10 months and needs staking and fertile soil. Irish potato takes 3-4 months but requires cool temperatures and is prone to diseases like late blight. Sweet potato is more flexible: it grows in warm lowlands and cooler highlands, and it tolerates poor soils..Trim()

.Trim()

In terms of nutrition, sweet potato beats cassava and yam. Cassava is mostly starch with very little vitamin A or protein. Yam is also starchy but has some vitamins. Irish potato has vitamin C but no vitamin A. Orange-fleshed sweet potato is the only root crop rich in beta-carotene. For fighting hidden hunger, sweet potato is the best choice. Sweet potato leaves also provide protein, which cassava and yam leaves do not (cassava leaves are edible but must be properly processed to remove toxins)..Trim()

Economically, sweet potato has a lower cost of production. You don't need to stake it like yam, and you can plant it on flat land or ridges. It responds well to little fertilizer, though yields improve with manure. Cassava can be left in the ground longer, making it a good famine reserve, but sweet potato can also be left for up to 6 months after maturity if the soil is not waterlogged. However, sweet potato is more perishable after harvest; it bruises easily and rots within weeks if not stored properly. Cassava and yam store longer..Trim()

As a new farmer, sweet potato is a good entry crop because it gives quick returns and requires less capital. You can also intercrop it with maize, beans, or cassava to maximize land use..Trim()

Market Demand and Economic Potential

Sweet potato is in high demand across Africa, both in rural and urban areas. In cities, consumers buy sweet potato for boiling, roasting, and frying. Street vendors sell roasted sweet potato, especially in the evening. Supermarkets and hotels buy orange-fleshed varieties for health-conscious customers. Schools, hospitals, and prisons are institutional buyers. The processing industry needs sweet potato for flour, chips, puree, and starch. In 2020, Africa produced about 29 million tonnes of sweet potato, but demand still outstrips supply in many countries..Trim()

The economic potential is huge. A smallholder can harvest 10-20 tonnes per hectare with good management, and sell at $0.20-$0.50 per kg. That translates to $2,000-$10,000 per hectare. Even with 5 tonnes per hectare on poor soil, you can earn $1,000. Costs are low: vines, land preparation, and labor. You can start with a quarter hectare and expand. Value addition increases profit: making sweet potato chips can triple the value. Milling into flour for bakeries is another option. Orange-fleshed sweet potato puree is used in baby food and juices..Trim()

Overview of the Sweet Potato Value Chain

The sweet potato value chain includes all activities from planting to consumption. It starts with input supply: vines, fertilizer, and tools. Then production: farmers plant, weed, and harvest. Next is post-harvest handling: sorting, grading, cleaning, and packaging. Then processing: making flour, chips, puree, or animal feed. Then distribution: transport to markets, wholesalers, retailers. Finally consumption: households, restaurants, and institutions. Each step adds value and creates opportunities..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, the chain is often informal. Most farmers sell fresh roots at farm gate or local markets. Middlemen take a large share. Processing is mostly small-scale: women's groups make chips and flour using simple equipment like solar dryers and manual mills. There is a growing number of small and medium enterprises (SMEs) producing orange-fleshed sweet potato puree for baby food, and bakeries substituting wheat flour with sweet potato flour. Governments and NGOs support these SMEs with training and equipment..Trim()

To succeed in the value chain, you can play different roles. You can be a producer, focusing on high yields and quality. You can be a vine multiplier, selling clean planting material. You can be a processor, adding value. You can be a trader, linking farmers to markets. Or you can be a service provider, offering transport or storage. The most profitable roles are often in processing and marketing, but they require more capital and skills. Starting as a producer is easiest..Trim()

Key to a strong value chain is coordination. Farmer groups can collectively buy inputs, share equipment, and negotiate prices. Contracts with processors ensure a market. Good storage extends shelf life. Improved packaging attracts buyers. Certification for organic or fair trade can open export markets. In countries like Rwanda, Uganda, and Nigeria, the sweet potato value chain is supported by government policies and research. As a new farmer, study your local chain: who buys, at what price, and what quality they want. Then position yourself to meet that demand. Module 2 · Understanding Sweet Potato Biology

Botanical Classification and Plant Structure

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) belongs to the morning glory family, Convolvulaceae. Unlike Irish potato, it is a root, not a tuber. The plant has two main types of roots: fibrous roots that absorb water and nutrients, and storage roots that swell to become the sweet potatoes you harvest. The leaves are heart-shaped or lobed, growing on vines that can spread 2â4 meters. Vines are green or purple, depending on variety. At each node (where leaf meets stem), roots can form if the vine touches moist soil. This is why farmers use vine cuttings for planting. The flowers are trumpet-shaped, white or purple, but in Africa many varieties rarely flower; seed is not used for planting. The storage roots develop from adventitious roots that thicken when conditions are right. Understanding this structure helps you: (1) plant vine cuttings at least 3 nodes deep so roots and shoots develop; (2) avoid damaging shallow roots during weeding; (3) recognize that leaf yellowing may indicate root problems. In Africa, common varieties include 'Tanzania', 'SPK004', and 'Ejumula', each with different root shapes and colors. A healthy plant has a balance: too many leaves and few roots means too much nitrogen; too few leaves means poor photosynthesis. By knowing the parts, you can diagnose problems early and take action, such as adding potassium to encourage root swelling..Trim()

Growth Stages: From Sprouting to Tuber Formation

Sweet potato growth has four clear stages. Stage 1: Sprouting (0â2 weeks after planting). Vine cuttings develop roots and new shoots. Keep soil moist but not waterlogged. Stage 2: Vegetative growth (2â8 weeks). Vines spread rapidly. Leaves and stems grow. This is when the plant builds its 'factory' for making food. Stage 3: Root initiation and development (4â12 weeks). Storage roots begin to form. They start as thin roots, then swell. This stage is critical: any stress (drought, poor soil, pests) now reduces final yield. Stage 4: Maturity and bulking (12â20 weeks). Roots reach full size. Leaves may start to yellow. In Africa, most varieties mature in 3â5 months, depending on altitude and rainfall. For example, in Nigeria, 'TIS 2532' matures in 4 months; in Uganda, 'NASPOT 1' takes 3â4 months. To manage stages: Water regularly during Stage 3; avoid flooding. Weed early (first 6 weeks) because weeds compete for nutrients. Do not harvest too earlyâroots are still small and starchy. Signs of maturity: skin does not rub off easily, and vines stop growing. You can also dig one plant to check root size. After harvest, cure roots in a shaded, ventilated place for 5â7 days to heal cuts and sweeten the taste. Proper stage management can double your yield from 5 to 10 tons per hectare..Trim()

Photosynthesis and Nutrient Uptake

Photosynthesis is how sweet potato makes food. Leaves capture sunlight, take in carbon dioxide from air, and absorb water from soil. They produce sugars, which are sent to storage roots. More leaf area = more sugar = bigger roots. But too many leaves (from too much nitrogen) can reduce root growth because the plant puts energy into leaves instead. Nutrient uptake: Sweet potato needs 16 essential nutrients. The big three are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Nitrogen makes leaves green; phosphorus helps root development; potassium is crucial for root swelling and starch formation. A typical recommendation for African soils: apply 200 kg/ha of NPK 15-15-15 at planting, then 100 kg/ha of potassium sulfate (0-0-50) at 6 weeks. But do not guessâtest your soil first. Many African soils are low in phosphorus and potassium. Organic matter (compost, manure) improves soil structure and holds nutrients. Micronutrients like zinc and boron are needed in small amounts; deficiency causes poor root shape. Water is essential: photosynthesis stops if leaves wilt. In dry areas, irrigate every 7â10 days. In humid areas, ensure drainage. Remember: healthy leaves = healthy roots. Remove diseased leaves to stop spread. If leaves turn yellow between veins, it may be magnesium deficiencyâapply Epsom salt (magnesium sulfate) at 20 g per plant. By understanding this process, you can time fertilizer applications for maximum root yield..Trim()

Soil and Water Needs for Optimal Growth

Sweet potato does best in well-drained, sandy loam or loam soils. Heavy clay soils hold water and cause root deformation. If your soil is clay, add compost, sand, or plant on ridges 30â40 cm high. Ideal soil pH is 5.5â6.5. Acidic soils (pH below 5) need lime; apply 1â2 tons per hectare of agricultural lime before planting. Saline soils are not suitable. Soil depth: at least 30 cm of loose soil for roots to expand. Compacted soil restricts root growth. Water needs: sweet potato is drought-tolerant but yields best with regular moisture. Critical periods: first 2 weeks after planting (root establishment) and weeks 6â12 (root swelling). Water stress during these times reduces yield. In rain-fed areas, plant with the rains. In dry areas, irrigate with 25â30 mm per week. Drip irrigation is efficient; use 2â4 liters per plant per day. Avoid overhead irrigation in humid areas as it spreads disease. Waterlogging for more than 2 days can kill roots. If your field floods, dig drainage channels. Mulching with dry grass or straw reduces evaporation and keeps soil cool. Test your soil every 2 years. Simple test kits are available at agricultural offices. Add organic matter yearly: 5â10 tons per hectare of manure or compost. Rotate sweet potato with cereals like maize or sorghum to reduce pests and improve soil. Never plant sweet potato after sweet potato in the same fieldâthis increases nematodes and diseases. Common Growth Disorders and Their Causes

Growth disorders reduce yield and quality. Learn to identify them early. 1. Yellowing leaves (chlorosis): often nitrogen deficiency. Apply urea (50 kg/ha) or manure. If yellowing is between veins (interveinal), it's magnesium deficiencyâspray Epsom salt (2% solution). 2. Stunted growth: could be phosphorus deficiency or root damage from nematodes. Add phosphate fertilizer (e.g., DAP) and rotate crops. 3. Root cracking: caused by uneven wateringâdry then wet. Keep soil consistently moist. 4. Root rot: soft, brown, smelly roots. Caused by waterlogging or fungi. Improve drainage, plant on ridges, and treat seeds (vines) with fungicide before planting. 5. Leaf spots and blight: fungal diseases. Remove affected leaves, spray with copper-based fungicide (e.g., copper oxychloride) every 10 days. 6. Vine borers: caterpillars that bore into vines. Handpick and destroy; use neem extract spray. 7. Sweet potato weevil: larvae tunnel into roots. Practice crop rotation, use clean planting material, and harvest promptly. 8. Sunscald: exposed roots turn white and bitter. Mulch to cover roots. 9. Premature flowering: stress (drought, low fertility) causes flowering, reducing root growth. Irrigate and fertilize. 10. Poor root shape: too much nitrogen or compacted soil. Balance fertilizer and loosen soil. In Africa, common disorders also include 'sweet potato virus disease' (SPVD) causing stunting and leaf distortion. Use resistant varieties like 'NASPOT 1' and control whiteflies (aphids) with neem oil. Always keep records: note symptoms, weather, and actions. Share with extension officers. Early action saves your harvest..Trim()

Macronutrients and Micronutrients for Sweet Potato

Sweet potato needs 16 nutrients to grow well. The big three are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Nitrogen makes leaves and vines grow strong. Phosphorus helps roots form and develop. Potassium is critical for tuber size, starch content, and storage quality. A typical sweet potato crop yielding 20 tonnes per hectare removes about 80 kg N, 40 kg P2O5, and 120 kg K2O from the soil. That means potassium is the most important nutrient for sweet potato. If your soil is low in potassium, you will get small, poor-quality tubers even if you add plenty of nitrogen..Trim()

.Trim()

Secondary nutrients are calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), and sulfur (S). Calcium builds strong cell walls and helps root development. Magnesium is part of chlorophyll, so it keeps leaves green. Sulfur helps form proteins. In many African soils, sulfur is low because farmers use little organic matter. Micronutrients like zinc (Zn), boron (B), iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), and copper (Cu) are needed in tiny amounts but are essential. Zinc deficiency causes small leaves and short internodes. Boron deficiency leads to cracked tubers and poor storage. Iron deficiency shows as yellow leaves between green veins, common in high-pH soils..Trim()

.Trim()

To know what your soil lacks, take a soil test. In Kenya, KALRO and private labs charge about 2,000 to 4,000 KES per sample for a full analysis. In Nigeria, IITA and state agricultural offices offer testing. Collect soil from 15 spots in your field, mix in a clean bucket, and take a 500g sample. Test before planting. If you cannot afford a lab test, use a simple field kit or observe deficiency symptoms. But remember: symptoms appear after yield is already lost. Prevention is better..Trim()

.Trim()

Organic matter is your best friend. Compost, manure, and crop residues supply all nutrients slowly. A good target is 5 to 10 tonnes of well-rotted manure per hectare per season. Combine that with the right mineral fertilizers based on soil test. For example, if your soil test shows low K, apply 100 kg of muriate of potash (0-0-60) per hectare. If P is low, apply 50 kg of triple superphosphate (0-46-0). Never guess. Sweet potato is a heavy feeder, but over-fertilizing with nitrogen gives you huge vines and tiny tubers. Balance is key..Trim()

Fertilizer Types: Organic vs. Synthetic

Organic fertilizers come from plant or animal sources: compost, manure, poultry litter, green manures, and biochar. They release nutrients slowly, improve soil structure, hold water, and feed soil microbes. A tonne of cattle manure contains roughly 5 kg N, 2 kg P2O5, and 5 kg K2O. Poultry litter is richer: about 20 kg N, 15 kg P2O5, and 15 kg K2O per tonne. Organic matter also helps sandy soils retain nutrients and clay soils drain better. But organic fertilizers are bulky, low in concentration, and may carry weed seeds or pathogens if not composted well. They are best applied 3-4 weeks before planting..Trim()

.Trim()

Synthetic fertilizers are manufactured, concentrated, and fast-acting. Common examples for sweet potato include urea (46-0-0), triple superphosphate (0-46-0), muriate of potash (0-0-60), and compound blends like NPK 17-17-17 or 12-24-12. A 50 kg bag of urea gives 23 kg of actual nitrogen. Synthetic fertilizers are easy to apply, easy to store, and you can precisely match soil test recommendations. But they can burn roots if over-applied, leach into groundwater, and acidify soil over time. They also do nothing to improve soil organic matter..Trim()

.Trim()

The best strategy is integrated soil fertility management (ISFM): combine organic and synthetic. Start with organic matter to build the soil. Then use synthetic fertilizers to supply the exact nutrients your crop needs at the right time. For example, apply 5 tonnes of compost per hectare at land preparation. Then at planting, band 100 kg/ha of NPK 17-17-17. Six weeks later, side-dress with 50 kg/ha of urea. This gives both slow-release and quick-release nutrients..Trim()

.Trim()

Cost matters. In many African markets, a 50 kg bag of urea costs about 2,500 to 3,500 KES (or 20,000 to 30,000 NGN). Compost is cheap if you make it yourself. Poultry litter may be free or low-cost from nearby farms. Calculate the cost per kg of nutrient. Synthetic nitrogen from urea costs about 100 KES per kg N. Organic nitrogen from manure may cost less but you need more labour to transport and spread. Choose based on what is available, affordable, and safe for your soil..Trim()

.Trim()

Do not mix synthetic and organic randomly. Apply organic first, incorporate it into the soil, then apply synthetic fertilizers just before planting or as side-dress. Never leave synthetic fertilizer on the soil surface without coveringâsun and rain waste it. Store fertilizers in a dry, dark place, off the floor, away from children and animals. Use gloves when handling. Both types have a place. The smart farmer uses both..Trim()

Calculating Fertilizer Rates Based on Soil Tests

A soil test report gives you numbers like 'phosphorus: 8 ppm' or 'potassium: 0.2 cmol/kg'. These are meaningless unless you convert them to fertilizer bags per hectare. First, know your target yield. For sweet potato, a realistic good yield is 20-30 tonnes per hectare. The crop removes about 4 kg N, 2 kg P2O5, and 6 kg K2O per tonne of fresh tubers. So for 25 tonnes, you need 100 kg N, 50 kg P2O5, and 150 kg K2O per hectare..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, subtract what your soil already provides. Soil tests usually report available nutrients in kg/ha or ppm. If your test shows 20 kg/ha of available P2O5, you only need to add 30 kg/ha (50 - 20). For nitrogen, soil tests are less reliable because N moves. Assume your soil supplies about 30 kg N/ha from organic matter. So you need 70 kg N/ha. For potassium, if your test shows 100 kg/ha K2O, you need 50 kg/ha extra..Trim()

.Trim()

Now convert nutrient needs to fertilizer products. Urea is 46% N. To get 70 kg N, divide 70 by 0.46 = 152 kg urea per hectare. Triple superphosphate (TSP) is 46% P2O5. .Trim()

Work in small plots first. If your field is 0.5 hectare, divide all rates by 2. So you would need 76 kg urea, 33 kg TSP, and 42 kg MOP for half a hectare. .Trim()

Always adjust for soil type. Sandy soils leach nitrogen and potassium quickly, so split applications. Clay soils hold nutrients better but may fix phosphorus. Test pH along with nutrients. Repeat soil tests every 2-3 years. Keep records of what you applied and your yields. That is how you learn your field's true needs..Trim()

Application Methods: Broadcast, Band, and Side-Dress

Broadcasting means spreading fertilizer evenly over the whole field, then incorporating it into the top 10-15 cm of soil with a hoe or plough. It is fast and good for phosphorus and potassium, which do not move much in soil. But broadcasting is wasteful for nitrogen because it can volatilize or leach. For sweet potato, broadcast all your phosphorus and potassium fertilizers plus half your nitrogen at land preparation, 2-3 weeks before planting. Use a cyclone spreader or broadcast by hand. A good rate: 100 kg/ha of TSP and 150 kg/ha of MOP. Incorporate immediately..Trim()

.Trim()

Banding means placing fertilizer in a narrow strip 5-10 cm to the side and 5-10 cm below the planting ridge. .Trim()

Side-dressing means applying fertilizer alongside the growing plants, usually 4-6 weeks after planting. This is the best way to give nitrogen because the vines are actively growing and roots are established. Use urea or ammonium sulphate. .Trim()

For organic fertilizers, broadcasting is common. Spread 5-10 tonnes of compost or manure per hectare and incorporate it 2-4 weeks before planting. For liquid organic fertilizers like compost tea, apply as a side-dress using a watering can, 1 litre per plant, diluted 1:5 with water..Trim()

.Trim()

Tools you need: a hoe, a bucket, a weighing scale, and a measuring cup. For banding, a simple stick can mark the furrow. For side-dressing, a hand hoe to make a shallow trench. Always cover fertilizer with soil. Never apply on wet leaves or before heavy rain. If rain is expected within 24 hours, wait. The goal is to get nutrients into the root zone, not into the air or runoff. Match the method to the nutrient: broadcast P and K, band starter NPK, side-dress N. That is the sweet potato way..Trim()

Sweet potato has a 3-5 month growing cycle. Nutrient uptake peaks between 6 and 10 weeks after planting, when vines are rapidly growing and tubers begin to form. If you apply all fertilizer at planting, much of the nitrogen will be gone by the time tubers need it. If you apply too late, you get vines but no tuber bulking. Timing is everything..Trim()

.Trim()

At land preparation (2-4 weeks before planting): Apply all organic matterâcompost, manureâand all phosphorus and potassium fertilizers. Incorporate into the top 15 cm. For a 0.5 ha plot, spread 1 tonne of lime and 50 kg of TSP plus 75 kg of MOP..Trim()

.Trim()

At planting: Apply a starter fertilizer banded near the vines. Use NPK 17-17-17 at 100 kg/ha (50 kg for 0.5 ha). .Trim()

At 4-6 weeks after planting: Side-dress with nitrogen. This is when vines are running and the plant needs a boost. Apply 50 kg/ha of urea (or 100 kg/ha of ammonium sulphate). On a 0.5 ha plot, that is 25 kg of urea total..Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Keep a calendar: mark planting date, then 4 weeks, 8 weeks, and 10 weeks. Set reminders on your phone. Rainfed farmers: apply fertilizer when soil is moist but not waterlogged. Avoiding Nutrient Deficiencies and Toxicities

Nutrient deficiencies show up as visual symptoms on leaves and tubers. Learn to read them early. Nitrogen deficiency: older leaves turn pale green to yellow, vines are thin and short. Phosphorus deficiency: leaves are dark green with purple or reddish tints, especially on undersides; roots are small and fibrous. Potassium deficiency: leaf edges scorch and turn brown, starting on older leaves; tubers are small and may have dark internal spots. Magnesium deficiency: yellowing between veins on older leaves, often called 'interveinal chlorosis'. Calcium deficiency: new leaves are distorted, curled, and may die at the tips; tubers have cavities. Boron deficiency: growing points die, leaves are thick and brittle, tubers crack and have brown patches inside..Trim()

.Trim()

To correct deficiencies, act fast. For nitrogen, side-dress 50 kg/ha of urea and water it in. For phosphorus, band 50 kg/ha of TSP. For potassium, side-dress 50 kg/ha of MOP. For magnesium, spray 2% Epsom salt solution (20g per litre) on leaves. For calcium, apply lime or gypsum at 500 kg/ha. For boron, spray 0.1% borax solution (1g per litre) â but be careful, boron toxicity is easy. Never exceed 2g per litre..Trim()

.Trim()

Toxicity happens when you over-apply. Nitrogen toxicity: dark green, lush vines with few tubers; leaves may be brittle. Potassium toxicity: causes magnesium and calcium deficiency because K competes for uptake. Boron toxicity: leaf edges turn yellow then brown and die; this is common if you apply too much borax. Manganese toxicity: occurs in very acidic soils (pH below 5.0); leaves show brown spots and crinkling. The best cure for toxicity is prevention: follow soil test recommendations, never double the dose, and always mix fertilizers well into the soil..Trim()

.Trim()

Soil pH is the master key. Sweet potato grows best at pH 5.8 to 6.5. If pH is below 5.5, lime it. If above 7.5, add elemental sulfur or organic matter like compost. At high pH, iron, zinc, and manganese become unavailable, causing deficiency. At low pH, aluminum and manganese become toxic. Test pH every year. A simple pH meter costs 5,000 KES or a test kit 2,000 KES. It is worth it..Trim()

.Trim()

Organic matter prevents most deficiencies and toxicities. It chelates micronutrients, buffers pH, and feeds soil life. Aim for 2-3% organic matter in your soil. If you see symptoms, do not guess. Take a soil sample and send it to a lab. In the meantime, foliar feed with a balanced micronutrient mix. Keep a notebook: record symptoms, what you applied, and the result. Over time, you will become your own soil doctor. Your sweet potato yield will thank you..Trim()

Water Requirements at Different Growth Stages

Sweet potatoes need about 500-700 mm of water over the whole growing season, but how that water is delivered matters as much as the total. In the first 3-4 weeks after planting, the vines are small and roots are shallow. Keep the top 15 cm of soil moist but not soaked. Too much water now can rot the slips. .Trim()

From week 5 to week 10, the plants grow fast and start forming storage roots. This is the most critical stage for water. The soil should be kept evenly moist to a depth of 30 cm. Lack of water now means small, woody roots..Trim()

.Trim()

Reduce irrigation when you see the leaves start to yellow naturally. Stop watering completely 10-14 days before you plan to harvest. This helps the skin set and improves storage life..Trim()

Common Weeds and Their Impact on Yield

Weeds are the silent thieves of your sweet potato harvest. In Africa, the most damaging weeds include couch grass (Agropyron repens), pigweed (Amaranthus spp.), blackjack (Bidens pilosa), and nutgrass (Cyperus rotundus). These weeds compete with your crop for water, nutrients, and sunlight. Research from Kenya and Nigeria shows that unchecked weeds can reduce sweet potato yields by 30 to 60 percent. For example, a plot that could produce 10 tonnes per hectare may yield only 4 tonnes if weeds are left to grow..Trim()

.Trim()

The first 6 to 8 weeks after planting are critical. Sweet potato vines spread slowly at first, leaving plenty of open soil for weeds to take over. Couch grass is especially bad because its underground stems (rhizomes) regrow even after you pull the top. Nutgrass is called the world's worst weed because it pierces through tubers, making them unsellable. Pigweed grows fast and tall, shading your vines..Trim()

.Trim()

Weeds also harbour pests and diseases. Aphids and whiteflies often live on weeds before moving to your sweet potatoes. Some weeds, like blackjack, can host viruses that later infect your crop. So weed control is not just about removing competition; it is also about breaking the pest cycle..Trim()

.Trim()

To measure impact, do a simple test: mark two 1m x 1m squares in your field. Keep one weed-free for the first 8 weeks, and leave the other unweeded. At harvest, dig up both squares and weigh the tubers. You will see the difference yourself. On average, each kilogram of weed dry matter per square meter can reduce tuber yield by 0.5 to 1 kilogram. So if you have 2 kg of weeds per square meter, you could lose 1 to 2 tonnes per hectare. That is money lost. Therefore, plan to weed early and often..Trim()

Integrated Weed Management (IWM) means using a combination of methods to control weeds, rather than relying on one. This is cheaper, safer, and more effective. The three main pillars are cultural, mechanical, and chemical control..Trim()

.Trim()

Cultural control starts before you plant. Choose a field with low weed pressure. If you have a weedy field, plant a cover crop like cowpea or mucuna for one season to smother weeds. Rotate sweet potatoes with maize or beans; different crops have different growth habits that disrupt weed cycles. Also, plant sweet potato vines denselyâspacing of 30 cm between plants and 1 m between ridgesâso the vines cover the ground faster and shade out weeds..Trim()

.Trim()

Mechanical control is hand weeding, hoeing, or using a hand cultivator. In Africa, most smallholders weed by hand. Do your first weeding 2 to 3 weeks after planting, and a second 5 to 6 weeks after planting. After that, the vines should cover the ground. Be careful not to damage the shallow roots of sweet potatoes. A sharp hoe used shallowly (2-3 cm deep) is best. You can also use a mower between ridges if you have one. Never weed when the soil is wetâit spreads weed seeds and compacts soil..Trim()

.Trim()

Chemical control uses herbicides, but only as a last resort. For sweet potatoes, few herbicides are registered in many African countries. Glyphosate can be used before planting to kill existing weeds, but it must be applied 2 weeks before planting and not after. .Trim()

Combine all three: start with cultural methods, add mechanical weeding, and use chemicals only on tough spots. This reduces costs and keeps your soil healthy..Trim()

Three insect pests cause the most damage to sweet potatoes in Africa: sweet potato weevil, whiteflies, and aphids. Learn to identify them and act early..Trim()

.Trim()

The sweet potato weevil (Cylas spp.) is the worst. It is a small, ant-like beetle, 5-7 mm long, with a dark body and orange legs. The female lays eggs in the soil near the roots or on the vines. The larvae bore into the tubers, making tunnels and causing a bitter taste. A single weevil can ruin a whole tuber. You will see small holes on the tuber surface and black spots inside. You can also use pheromone traps (available in some countries) or apply neem extract..Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Sweet potatoes suffer from several diseases, but three are most common in Africa: Fusarium wilt, black rot, and viral infections..Trim()

.Trim()

Fusarium wilt is caused by the fungus Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. batatas. It lives in the soil and enters through roots. Symptoms: leaves turn yellow, then brown, and wilt. The inside of the stem shows brown streaks. The plant may die within 2 weeks. The fungus can survive in soil for up to 5 years. .Trim()

Black rot is caused by the fungus Ceratocystis fimbriata. It affects tubers in storage and in the field. Symptoms: small, dark, sunken spots on tubers that expand into large black lesions. The rot smells sweet and fruity. It spreads through wounds during harvest. .Trim()

Viral infections are caused by viruses like Sweet Potato Feathery Mottle Virus (SPFMV) and Sweet Potato Chlorotic Stunt Virus (SPCSV). There is no cure. Control: use virus-free planting material (certified vines from tissue culture or clean nurseries), control aphids and whiteflies, and remove infected plants immediately. .Trim()

Biological and Chemical Control Options

When pests and diseases get out of hand, you can use biological or chemical controls. Biological control uses living organisms to fight pests. Chemical control uses synthetic pesticides. Both have a place, but biological is safer and cheaper for smallholders..Trim()

.Trim()

Biological control options: Neem extract is the most widely used. Crush 1 kg of neem seeds, soak in 10 litres of water for 24 hours, strain, and spray. It is sold as a powder and mixed with water. For sweet potato weevil, you can use the fungus Beauveria bassiana, which infects and kills the weevil. Mix 10 g of powder per litre of water and spray on soil around plants. Ladybirds, lacewings, and parasitic wasps are natural predatorsâavoid broad-spectrum insecticides that kill them..Trim()

.Trim()

Chemical control options: Use only when biological methods fail and pest pressure is high. For sweet potato weevil, apply chlorpyrifos (e.g., Dursban) at 2 litres per hectare in the soil before planting. For aphids and whiteflies, spray imidacloprid (e.g., Confidor) at 0.5 litres per hectare. For Fusarium wilt, there is no effective chemical; use resistant varieties. For black rot, dip tubers in benomyl (0.5 g per litre) before storage. Always wear gloves, mask, and long sleeves. Follow the label exactly. Do not spray within 7 days of harvest. Never mix chemicals unless the label says so..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember: chemicals can harm your health and the environment. Use them as a last resort. Rotate chemicals to prevent resistance. Store them locked away from children and animals..Trim()

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is a decision-making process that combines all control methods to keep pests and diseases below damaging levels. It is not about eliminating all pests; it is about managing them economically and safely..Trim()

.Trim()

The first step is prevention. Use clean planting material from certified sources. Rotate cropsânever plant sweet potatoes in the same field two seasons in a row. Plant resistant varieties like 'Kabode' or 'Naspot 1' where available. Keep your field free of weeds that harbour pests. Space plants properly to allow air movement, which reduces fungal diseases..Trim()

.Trim()

The second step is monitoring. Scout your field every week. Walk in a zigzag pattern and check 10 random plants. Look for weevil holes, whiteflies, aphids, yellow leaves, or wilting. Write down what you see. Use pheromone traps for weevils (1 trap per 100 square meters) and yellow sticky traps for whiteflies (10 traps per hectare). When pest numbers exceed thresholdsâfor example, 5 aphids per leaf or 10% of plants with weevil damageâtake action..Trim()

.Trim()

The third step is intervention. Start with biological controls: neem spray, Bt, or Beauveria. If those fail, use mechanical methods: handpick weevils, remove infected plants, or use a strong water spray for aphids. Only if pest pressure is still high, use chemicals. Always choose the least toxic option and spot-treat affected areas rather than spraying the whole field..Trim()

.Trim()

Record everything: what you saw, what you did, and the results. This helps you make better decisions next season. Share your records with other farmers. IPM reduces costs, protects your health, and gives you a better harvest. Start smallâtry one IPM practice this season and see the difference..Trim()

Sustainable Practices: Crop Rotation and Cover Crops

Sweet potatoes take a lot of nutrients from the soil, especially potassium. If you plant them in the same plot year after year, yields drop and pests build up. Crop rotation means changing what you grow in each plot each season. A good rotation for sweet potatoes is: Year 1 â sweet potatoes; Year 2 â maize or sorghum; Year 3 â legumes like beans or groundnuts; then back to sweet potatoes. Legumes fix nitrogen in the soil, which helps the next crop. This rotation reduces soil-borne diseases like sweet potato weevils and nematodes. Cover crops are plants grown to protect and improve the soil, not for harvest. Examples are mucuna (velvet bean), lablab, and cowpeas. Plant them after harvesting sweet potatoes. They cover the ground, prevent erosion, and add organic matter when you plough them back in. You can also use sweet potato vines themselves as a cover crop â they spread quickly and suppress weeds. Another sustainable practice is mulching with dry grass or crop residues. This keeps moisture in the soil, reduces weeding, and adds nutrients as it decomposes. Organic certification means your sweet potatoes are grown without synthetic chemicals and meet specific standards.
