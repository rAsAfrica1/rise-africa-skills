Introduction to Soldering

Soldering is the process of joining metal parts with a filler metal called solder. In electronics, we use soft solder made of tin and lead (or lead-free for health reasons). The soldering iron heats the joint, and the solder melts and flows to create an electrical and mechanical connection. A good solder joint is shiny, smooth, and concave. Module 2 · Through-Hole Component Assembly

Identifying Through-Hole Components

Through-hole components are the traditional electronic parts with long metal leads that go through holes in a circuit board. You will see them in almost every repair job across Africa, from radios in Accra to solar charge controllers in Nairobi. Start by sorting them into groups: resistors, capacitors, diodes, transistors, ICs, connectors, and switches. Resistors are small cylinders with coloured bands. A 10k ohm resistor has bands brown, black, orange, gold. Capacitors come in two main types: ceramic discs (small, no polarity) and electrolytic cans (larger, with a white stripe marking the negative lead). Diodes are black cylinders with a silver band at one end â that band marks the cathode. Transistors look like little black half-cylinders with three legs; the flat side shows the part number like BC547. ICs are black rectangular chips with 8, 14, or 16 legs. Connectors are plastic headers or screw terminals. Switches are mechanical. Use a cheap digital multimeter set to continuity or resistance to confirm values. For example, a good 100 ohm resistor reads about 100 ohms. A bad capacitor may read zero or infinite. Always check the part number printed on the component. In many African markets, components are salvaged from old TVs and computers, so markings may be faded. Clean the part with a cloth and use a magnifying glass. Keep a notebook listing each componentâs name, value, and polarity. This habit saves hours during assembly. Remember: polarity matters for electrolytic capacitors, diodes, LEDs, and ICs. Non-polar parts like resistors and ceramic capacitors can go either way. Practice identifying 20 random components from a mixed bag until you can name them in under 10 seconds each..Trim()

Component Preparation and Lead Forming

Before you insert any component, you must prepare its leads. This step prevents damage and ensures a proper fit. Start with a pair of small flush cutters and a lead-forming tool or a simple pair of round-nose pliers. For resistors and diodes, bend the leads to match the hole spacing on your board. A common spacing is 0.4 inches (10 mm). Hold the component body with one hand and use pliers to bend the leads 90 degrees downward. Do not bend right at the body â leave a small gap of about 1 mm to avoid cracking the seal. For capacitors, especially electrolytics, keep the leads straight unless the board requires a specific bend. Clean the leads with fine sandpaper or a pencil eraser to remove oxidation. In humid African climates, oxidation is common, and dirty leads cause bad solder joints. If leads are too long, trim them to about 5 mm below the board after insertion â but wait to trim until after soldering. For ICs, never bend the legs. Instead, use a DIP insertion tool or gently straighten them on a flat surface. If a leg is bent, use tweezers to realign it. For transistors, identify the emitter, base, and collector before forming. A BC547 has a flat side; the middle leg is base, the left is collector, right is emitter when viewed from the front. Form leads to fit the boardâs triangle pattern. Always wear safety glasses when cutting leads â flying metal can injure your eyes. Use a lead-forming jig if available; otherwise, a small vice and pliers work fine. After forming, place all prepared components in a clean tray sorted by value. This preparation step may take 10 minutes per 20 components, but it speeds up assembly and reduces rework. In many African workshops, technicians skip this step and later struggle with misaligned parts. Donât be that technician..Trim()

Inserting and Securing Components

Insertion is where your prepared components meet the board. Start with the lowest-profile parts: resistors, diodes, and small ceramic capacitors. Place the board on a flat surface or in a holder. Insert each componentâs leads through the correct holes. For polarized parts, match the positive lead to the square pad or the hole marked with a plus sign. On most boards, the square pad is positive for electrolytic capacitors and the round pad is negative. For diodes, the banded end goes to the pad marked with a line or a K. For ICs, align the notch or dot with the notch on the silkscreen. Push the component down until it sits flush against the board â no gap larger than 1 mm. If a component stands off the board (like power resistors), use a spacer or leave a 2-3 mm gap for cooling. Once inserted, bend the leads outward at about 45 degrees to hold the part in place. This is called clinching. Do not bend them flat against the board â that makes rework difficult. For multi-lead parts like ICs, insert one row first, then gently push the other row in. If a lead wonât go, do not force it â check for a bent leg or wrong hole. Use a small screwdriver to guide leads. After all components are inserted, flip the board over and check that each part is seated properly. You can use a piece of foam or a soft cloth to hold components in place while flipping. Secure large or heavy components with a small dab of silicone or hot glue after soldering, especially in high-vibration environments like solar installations in dusty areas. In African workshops, a common mistake is inserting tall components first, which makes it hard to reach shorter ones. Always work from short to tall. Practice inserting 10 resistors, 2 capacitors, and 1 IC on a scrap board until you can do it without thinking..Trim()

Soldering Through-Hole Components

Soldering is the heart of electronics assembly. You need a soldering iron with a fine tip, 60/40 or lead-free solder (0.8 mm diameter is ideal), a wet sponge or brass wool, and a fume extractor or small fan. Set your iron to 350Â°C for leaded solder or 380Â°C for lead-free. Let it heat for 5 minutes. Clean the tip on the sponge, then apply a small amount of solder to tin the tip. For each joint, follow this method: touch the iron tip to both the pad and the component lead at the same time. Count one second. Then feed solder into the joint â not onto the iron tip. The solder should melt and flow around the lead and pad like a small volcano. Remove solder first, then remove the iron. The whole process should take 2-3 seconds. A good joint is shiny, smooth, and concave. A bad joint looks dull, blobby, or ball-shaped. For African learners, practice on a scrap board with 20 resistors. Common problems: too little heat (cold joint), too much solder (bridge), or moving the component before solder cools. If you see a bridge between two pads, use solder wick or a desoldering pump to remove excess. Keep your tip clean every 3-4 joints. Do not use too much pressure â you can lift pads. For ground planes or large copper areas, you may need a higher wattage iron (40W or more). After soldering, trim the leads flush with the board using flush cutters. Hold the lead so it doesnât fly. Inspect each joint with a magnifying glass. In many African settings, power fluctuations can affect iron temperature; use a surge protector or a voltage stabilizer. If you are soldering in a humid environment, keep the board dry. Practice until you can solder 30 joints in 5 minutes with 90% perfect. That speed and quality will get you hired..Trim()

Inspecting Through-Hole Solder Joints

Inspection is not optional â it is how you catch defects before they cause failures. After soldering, let the board cool for 2 minutes. Use a magnifying glass (10x) and bright light. Start by checking each joint visually. A good joint has a shiny, smooth, concave fillet that wets both the pad and the lead. The solder should look like a tiny volcano. A cold joint is dull, lumpy, and may have a crack around the lead. A cold joint often results from insufficient heat or dirty surfaces. A solder bridge is a blob of solder connecting two adjacent pads â this causes short circuits. Use a multimeter set to continuity to check for bridges between neighbouring pads. A disturbed joint looks like a frozen drip â it happens if the component moved while solder was cooling. A blowhole or pinhole indicates trapped gas or contamination. For each defect, mark it with a small piece of tape or a marker. Then check component placement: is the part flush? Are polarized parts oriented correctly? Look for leads that are too long or too short. Use a dental pick or tweezers to gently tug each lead â a good joint will not move. If it moves, itâs a cold joint. In African repair shops, a common defect is insufficient solder on ground pads because the copper plane sucks heat away. Reheat those joints with a hotter iron and add a little solder. Also check for splashes of solder on the board â remove them with a brush. After inspection, clean the board with isopropyl alcohol and a toothbrush to remove flux. Flux residue can cause corrosion over time, especially in humid coastal areas like Mombasa or Lagos. A clean board is easier to inspect and more reliable. Document your findings: count the number of defects per 100 joints. A good target is less than 2 defects per 100. If you find more, review your soldering technique. Inspection takes 5 minutes for a small board, but it saves hours of troubleshooting later..Trim()

Rework and Touch-Up of Through-Hole Assemblies

Rework means fixing defects found during inspection. Touch-up means improving a joint that is not perfect but not yet failed. You need a soldering iron, solder sucker or solder wick, flux pen, tweezers, and a heat-resistant mat. Start by identifying the defect. For a cold joint, reheat the joint with the iron and add a tiny amount of fresh solder. The flux in the new solder will clean the joint. Hold the iron for 1-2 seconds, then remove. For a solder bridge, use solder wick: place the wick over the bridge, press with the iron, and the wick will absorb excess solder. Alternatively, use a solder sucker â heat the bridge and quickly suck the molten solder. For a component that needs replacement, first remove solder from all pads using wick or sucker. Then gently pull the component out with tweezers while heating one lead at a time. Do not force it â you can lift pads. If a pad lifts, you may need to run a jumper wire to the next component. For touch-up, if a joint looks dull but is electrically good, add a little flux and reheat. Do not add more solder unless needed. After rework, clean the area with isopropyl alcohol. Always test the board after rework with a multimeter to ensure no shorts and correct continuity. In African workshops, rework is common because components are often salvaged. Practice rework on a scrap board: create 10 cold joints, then fix them. Time yourself â a skilled technician can fix a cold joint in 10 seconds. Remember: never rework a joint more than 3 times â repeated heating can damage the pad and the component. If a pad is damaged, use a small wire to bridge to the nearest connected point. Keep your iron tip clean and tinned. After rework, re-inspect the joint. A good rework is invisible â it looks like a factory joint. Finally, log what you fixed and why. This helps you improve your assembly process and avoid repeating mistakes..Trim()

Wire stripping is the first step in almost every wiring job. You need to remove the insulation without nicking the copper. PCB Types and Materials

Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs) come in three main types: single-sided, double-sided, and multilayer. Single-sided PCBs have components on one side and copper traces on the other. They are cheap and used in simple devices like power supplies. Double-sided PCBs have copper on both sides and are common in consumer electronics. Multilayer PCBs stack multiple copper layers with insulating material between them; they are used in smartphones and computers. In Africa, most small workshops start with single-sided or double-sided boards because they are easier to repair and cost less..Trim()

.Trim()

The base material is usually FR-4, a fiberglass-epoxy composite. FR-4 is strong, flame-resistant, and handles heat well. Cheaper boards may use CEM-1 or paper-based materials, but they warp easily. The copper thickness is measured in ounces per square foot. 1 oz copper is about 35 micrometers thick; 2 oz copper handles more current for power circuits. The solder mask is the green (or other color) coating that protects copper traces and prevents solder bridges. The silkscreen is white text that shows component locations and polarity..Trim()

.Trim()

When you buy PCBs locally, check for warping, scratches, or exposed copper. A good board lies flat on a table. In hot, humid African climates, store PCBs in sealed bags with silica gel to prevent oxidation. Oxidized copper looks dull brown and will not solder well; clean it with fine steel wool or a pencil eraser before assembly..Trim()

.Trim()

For prototyping, you can make your own single-sided PCB using the toner transfer method: print the layout on glossy paper, iron it onto a clean copper board, then etch in ferric chloride. This is messy but works. Alternatively, use perfboard or stripboard for simple circuits. Always wear gloves and eye protection when etching..Trim()

.Trim()

Understanding PCB types helps you choose the right board for the job and handle it correctly. A well-chosen board saves time and prevents assembly problems..Trim()

ESD-Safe PCB Handling

Electrostatic discharge (ESD) is a sudden flow of electricity between two objects at different potentials. You feel a shock when you touch a doorknob after walking on a carpet. That same spark, too small to feel, can destroy sensitive electronic components like microchips. A human body can carry up to 25,000 volts in dry conditions. A CMOS IC can be damaged by as little as 10 volts. In many African workshops, dry harmattan winds or air-conditioned rooms increase ESD risk..Trim()

.Trim()

To prevent ESD damage, always work at an ESD-safe workstation. Use a wrist strap connected to a grounded mat. The wrist strap has a 1 megaohm resistor to protect you from electric shock. If you don't have a mat, touch a grounded metal object (like a water pipe) before handling boards. Never wear synthetic clothing (polyester, nylon) when working on PCBs; cotton is safer. Avoid plastic chairs and carpeted floors..Trim()

.Trim()

Store PCBs and components in anti-static bags (usually silver or pink). Do not use ordinary plastic bags. Handle boards by the edges; never touch the gold fingers or component leads. When shipping, use anti-static foam or pink bubble wrap. In humid climates, ESD is less of a problem, but still take precautions..Trim()

.Trim()

If you don't have a wrist strap, you can make a simple one: connect a 1M resistor to a wire, attach one end to your wrist with a band, and ground the other end. Test it with a multimeter to ensure the resistor is working. Always check your wrist strap daily..Trim()

.Trim()

ESD damage may not cause immediate failure; it can weaken components so they fail later. This is called latent damage. By following ESD-safe practices, you protect your work and your reputation. Teach everyone in your workshop to do the same..Trim()

Component Placement Guidelines

Placing components correctly on a PCB is critical for the circuit to work and for easy soldering. Start by organizing your components: resistors, capacitors, ICs, connectors, etc. Use the silkscreen markings and your schematic or layout diagram to identify each location. For through-hole components, bend the leads to fit the hole spacing. Insert the component so it sits flat against the board. For resistors, the color code should be readable from left to right or top to bottom. For capacitors, check polarity: electrolytic capacitors have a stripe marking the negative lead. For diodes, the band marks the cathode. For ICs, the notch or dot indicates pin 1. Align pin 1 with the square pad or silkscreen mark..Trim()

.Trim()

For surface-mount components (SMD), use tweezers to place them. Apply solder paste to the pads first, then place the component, then reflow with a hot air station or reflow oven. Keep components close to the board to avoid vibration damage. For heavy components like transformers, use adhesive or mounting screws. Polarity and Orientation Checks

Polarity and orientation mistakes are the most common assembly errors. They can destroy components, damage boards, and waste time. Always check polarity before soldering. Diodes have a band indicating the cathode (negative). The PCB silkscreen shows a band or arrow. For LEDs, the flat side of the rim and the shorter lead indicate the cathode. Integrated circuits (ICs) have a notch or dot at pin 1. The PCB has a matching mark, often a square pad or a dot. For DIP ICs, pin 1 is at the top-left when the notch is up. Transistors have three leads: base, collector, emitter. The flat side usually faces a certain direction. Check the datasheet and the PCB silkscreen. Connectors and plugs may have keying or polarity marks. For example, barrel jacks have center positive or center negative. Check before soldering. For battery holders, the '+' and '-' are marked. Use a multimeter to confirm continuity to the correct pads..Trim()

Adhesives and staking compounds secure components to the PCB, especially for surface-mount parts, heavy components, and wires. They prevent movement during soldering, vibration, and thermal cycling. PCB Cleaning and Contamination Control

After soldering, PCBs often have flux residue, dust, and other contaminants. Flux can be corrosive, conductive, and can cause short circuits or poor adhesion of conformal coating. Cleaning is essential for reliability, especially in humid African climates where moisture combines with flux to cause corrosion..Trim()

.Trim()

There are three main flux types: rosin (RA), water-soluble (WS), and no-clean (NC). Rosin flux leaves a sticky residue that must be cleaned with isopropyl alcohol (IPA) or a commercial flux remover. Water-soluble flux must be cleaned with deionized water, often with a brush, within a few hours. No-clean flux can be left on, but it's still best to remove it for high-reliability applications..Trim()

.Trim()

For manual cleaning, use a soft-bristle brush (like an anti-static brush) and 99% isopropyl alcohol. Pour IPA into a small container, dip the brush, and scrub the board gently. For water-soluble flux, use warm deionized water (about 60Â°C) and a brush. Rinse thoroughly and dry immediately with compressed air. Do not let water sit on the board. For no-clean flux, you can often skip cleaning, but if you need to remove it, use IPA..Trim()

.Trim()

Ultrasonic cleaning is effective but can damage certain components (crystals, relays). Use only if your components are compatible. In Africa, many workshops use simple manual cleaning. Always wear gloves and eye protection..Trim()

.Trim()

After cleaning, inspect the board under a magnifying glass for remaining residue, solder splashes, or loose metal. Remove any splashes with tweezers. If you plan to apply conformal coating, clean first and let the board dry completely. Contamination control also means storing boards in anti-static bags with desiccant. Clean boards solder better and last longer..Trim()

Visual Inspection Criteria

Visual inspection is your first and cheapest quality check. Before you power up any board, look at it under good light. You need a bright, white light sourceâa 10W LED desk lamp works well. A bridge between pins on an IC can destroy the chip when you power up. Also check for cold solder joints: they look dull and grainy, not shiny and smooth. In Africa, humidity and dust can cause problems, so inspect for corrosion or dirt on pads. Use a magnifying glass (10x) to see fine details. Continuity testing checks if a path is complete. Set your multimeter to continuity mode (beep). Touch probes to both ends of a wire. If it beeps, the wire is good.
