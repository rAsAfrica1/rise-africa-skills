History and Importance of Beekeeping

Beekeeping in Africa goes back thousands of years. Ancient Egyptians kept bees in clay pots along the Nile, and rock paintings in the Sahara show honey hunting. Today, traditional beekeeping using hollow logs, gourds, and clay pots is still common in many rural areas. But modern beekeeping with movable-frame hives is growing fast because it produces more honey and allows you to inspect the colony without destroying it. Honey is not just food; it has medicinal value, and beeswax is used in candles, cosmetics, and waterproofing. More importantly, bees pollinate crops like coffee, avocado, mango, and watermelon, increasing yields by 20â30%. In Africa, beekeeping is a low-cost, high-return business. Bee Species and Races

Not all bees are the same. The most important species for beekeeping in Africa is Apis mellifera, the Western honey bee. Types of Hives: Langstroth, Top Bar, Warre

When choosing a hive, you need to consider your budget, available materials, and how you plan to manage your bees. The three most common hives in Africa are the Langstroth, Top Bar, and Warre. Each has advantages and challenges..Trim()

.Trim()

The Langstroth hive is the most widely used worldwide. It consists of stacked rectangular boxes with removable frames. The bees build comb on the frames, which can be easily lifted out for inspection and honey harvesting. A standard Langstroth hive has a brood box (where the queen lays eggs) and supers (for honey storage). It requires precise construction because bee space must be exactly 3/8 inch (9.5 mm) to prevent bees from gluing frames together with propolis or building extra comb. Langstroth hives produce the most honey and are easiest to manage, but they are expensive to buy and require accurate woodworking skills..Trim()

.Trim()

The Top Bar hive is a simpler, low-cost alternative. It is a single long box with sloped sides and top bars resting across the top. Bees build natural comb hanging from the bars. You can inspect each comb by lifting a bar. Top Bar hives are easy to build with local materials and are good for beginners. However, the comb is fragile and can break during harvest, and honey yields are lower than Langstroth..Trim()

.Trim()

The Warre hive is a vertical, stackable hive similar to Langstroth but with top bars instead of frames. It is designed to mimic a hollow tree, with boxes added to the bottom rather than the top. Warre hives are less intrusive for bees and require less frequent inspections. They are popular in sustainable beekeeping but are harder to find and manage for maximum honey production..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many beekeepers start with Top Bar hives because they are affordable and can be made from locally available timber. As you gain experience, you may upgrade to Langstroth for higher yields. Choose the hive that matches your resources and goals..Trim()

Materials and Construction

To build a durable hive, you need the right materials. The best wood for hives is seasoned timber that is not treated with toxic chemicals. In Africa, good options include cypress, cedar, mahogany, or eucalyptus. Avoid softwoods like pine that rot quickly, and never use pressure-treated wood because the chemicals can kill bees. You will need planks of 1-inch (2.5 cm) thickness for the boxes, and 1-inch by 1-inch strips for frames and top bars..Trim()

.Trim()

Essential tools for hive construction include a saw (hand saw or circular saw), hammer, nails or screws, wood glue, measuring tape, square, and sandpaper. For Langstroth hives, you also need a table saw or router to cut precise grooves for frames. A bee space of 3/8 inch (9.5 mm) is critical: if spaces are smaller, bees fill them with propolis; if larger, they build burr comb. So measure twice and cut once..Trim()

.Trim()

Start by cutting all pieces to size according to your hive plan. For a Langstroth brood box, the standard dimensions are 19 7/8 inches long, 16 1/4 inches wide, and 9 5/8 inches high (internal). Assemble the boxes using waterproof wood glue and galvanized nails or screws. Reinforce corners with metal brackets or cleats. For Top Bar hives, cut the sides at a 30-degree angle to mimic natural tree hollows, and make top bars 1 3/8 inches wide to allow bee space between combs..Trim()

.Trim()

After assembly, sand rough edges and apply a protective finish. Use linseed oil or exterior latex paint on the outside only. Never paint the inside. Allow the hive to dry completely before introducing bees. Proper construction ensures your hive lasts for years and keeps bees healthy..Trim()

A hive stand raises your hive off the ground, protecting it from dampness, termites, and predators like ants and small animals. Protective Clothing and Tools

Beekeeping requires protective clothing to prevent stings and keep you safe. The essential items are a veil, gloves, and a bee suit. A veil protects your face and neck. .Trim()

Gloves protect your hands. Leather gloves are durable but can be clumsy. Rubber or nitrile gloves allow better dexterity but may get sticky with propolis. Always keep gloves clean and free of holes. Wash them regularly to remove alarm pheromones from previous stings..Trim()

You also need a hive tool, which is a small metal pry bar used to separate boxes and frames. It is essential for inspections. A bee brush is a soft brush to gently move bees aside. A smoker is a metal can with a bellows that produces cool smoke to calm bees. Use dry, clean fuel like dried grass, cardboard, or pine needles. Light the smoker before opening the hive and puff smoke at the entrance and under the lid..Trim()

Other useful tools include a frame grip for lifting frames, a uncapping knife for harvesting honey, and a bucket with a lid for holding tools. Always work with confidence and move slowly. An apiary is a place where you keep your beehives. Choosing the right site is crucial for bee health and honey production. Start by selecting an area with abundant bee forage. Bees need nectar and pollen from flowers, trees, and crops. Good sources in Africa include acacia, citrus, sunflower, eucalyptus, and indigenous wildflowers. Aim for a site with diverse flowering plants that bloom at different times of the year..Trim()

In hot regions, provide shade during the hottest part of the day, but ensure morning sun reaches the hives to warm them and encourage early foraging..Trim()

Water is essential. Bees need a reliable source of clean water within 100-200 meters. If none exists, create a shallow water station with floating sticks or stones so bees can drink without drowning. Keep the water fresh to prevent mosquito breeding..Trim()

Module 5 · Hive Inspection and Maintenance

Conducting a Hive Inspection

Inspecting a hive is like checking on a valuable but delicate friend. You want to know they are healthy and happy, but you don't want to disturb them too much. The best time to open a hive is on a warm, sunny day between 10am and 4pm, when most of the worker bees are out foraging. Avoid opening on windy, rainy, or very cold days. Wear protective clothing: a veil, gloves, and light-colored overalls. Use a smoker to calm the bees. Before you start, have your hive tool and a notebook ready. Stand to the side of the hive, not in front of the entrance, to avoid blocking the flight path. Give a few puffs of smoke at the entrance, wait 30 seconds, then lift the lid gently. Use your hive tool to pry it open. Work from the outside frames inward. Lift each frame slowly, keeping it over the hive to avoid dropping the queen. Look for signs of a healthy colony: eggs, larvae, capped brood, stored honey and pollen, and the queen. Check for pests, diseases, and swarm cells. Limit each inspection to 15-20 minutes to avoid stressing the bees. Close the hive carefully, ensuring no gaps. Record what you saw: date, time, weather, number of frames with brood, honey stores, queen seen (yes/no), and any problems. In Africa, many beekeepers use top-bar hives or Kenya Top Bar Hives (KTBH). The inspection method is similar, but you work with bars instead of frames. Always approach calmly and move slowly. If the bees become very aggressive, close the hive and try again another day. Regular inspections, every 7-10 days during the active season, help you catch problems early and keep your colony strong..Trim()

Reading Frames and Recognizing Queen Status

Reading frames is the skill of understanding what the bees are doing by looking at the comb. Each frame tells a story. You need to recognize the difference between eggs, larvae, and capped brood. Eggs are tiny, white, and stand upright at the bottom of cells. Larvae are white, curled, and lie in a pool of royal jelly. Capped brood is flat, biscuit-colored, and covers the cell. A healthy queen lays eggs in a tight pattern, often in the center of the frame. If you see eggs, you know the queen was there within the last three days. If you see larvae, she was there within the last nine days. If you see only capped brood, the queen may have stopped laying or is missing. The queen herself is longer than worker bees, with a pointed abdomen and shorter wings. She is usually surrounded by a circle of attendant bees. In Africa, local bee races like the African honey bee (Apis mellifera scutellata) are smaller and more defensive. Marking the queen with a small dot of paint (white, yellow, red, green, or blue) helps you find her quickly. If you cannot find the queen but see eggs, she is likely present. If you see no eggs and no larvae, the colony may be queenless. In that case, check for queen cells: peanut-shaped cells hanging from the comb. If you find queen cells, the colony is either swarming or replacing the queen. A laying worker can also occur in queenless colonies, but they only lay drone eggs, which are raised in larger cells. Knowing these signs helps you decide whether to requeen, add a frame of eggs from another hive, or leave the colony alone. Practice reading frames every inspection to build your confidence..Trim()

Detecting Pests and Diseases

Pests and diseases can weaken or kill your colony. Common pests in Africa include ants, termites, wax moths, small hive beetles, and varroa mites. Ants and termites can be controlled by placing the hive on stands with grease or oil barriers. Wax moths attack weak colonies, especially in stored combs. Keep your hive strong and remove unused combs. Small hive beetles are black, about 5mm long, and hide in corners. They can be trapped with oil traps or diatomaceous earth. Varroa mites are tiny, reddish-brown parasites that feed on bees. They are less common in Africa but can be a problem. Check for mites by using a sticky board or sugar shake method. Diseases include American foulbrood (AFB), European foulbrood (EFB), chalkbrood, and nosema. AFB causes sunken, perforated cappings and a foul smell. EFB causes twisted larvae that die before capping. Chalkbrood turns larvae into white, chalky mummies. Nosema causes dysentery and crawling bees. To detect diseases, inspect brood frames carefully. Look for discolored larvae, sunken cappings, or a bad smell. If you suspect AFB, burn the infected frames and sterilize your tools. For EFB, requeen and improve ventilation. Chalkbrood often clears up with better airflow and a stronger colony. Nosema can be treated with fumagillin, but prevention is better: keep hives dry and well-ventilated. Always maintain good hygiene: clean your hive tool between hives, and never exchange combs between sick and healthy colonies. Regular inspections help you spot problems early. If you see something unusual, take a photo and ask an experienced beekeeper or extension officer for advice. Early action saves colonies..Trim()

Managing Space: Adding Supers

Bees need space to store honey and raise brood. When a colony grows, it fills the brood chamber and may become crowded. This triggers swarming. To prevent swarming and increase honey production, you add supers. A super is a box placed on top of the brood chamber. It contains frames for honey storage. In Africa, many beekeepers use Langstroth hives with supers, or top-bar hives where you add more bars. The rule of thumb: when the brood chamber is 70-80% full, add a super. Check by looking at the frames: if 7 out of 10 frames are drawn and covered with bees, it's time. Add the super with frames that have foundation or starter strips. If you use a queen excluder, place it between the brood chamber and the super to keep the queen from laying in the honey super. However, some beekeepers avoid excluders because they can reduce honey yield. In Africa, African bees may be more reluctant to pass through excluders. Monitor the super: when the frames are 80% capped, add another super. You can add multiple supers, stacking them. Ensure the hive is level and stable. During a strong honey flow, bees can fill a super in 2-3 weeks. If you don't add space in time, the colony will swarm, and you lose half your bees. Conversely, adding too much space too early can stress the colony, as they must heat and defend a larger area. So add supers gradually. In top-bar hives, simply add more bars with guides. Always inspect the super every 7-10 days during the flow. Harvest honey only when frames are fully capped. Leave enough honey for the bees, especially before the dry season. Proper space management keeps your colony productive and healthy..Trim()

Dealing with Aggressive Colonies

Aggressive colonies are a common challenge, especially with African bee races. Aggressiveness can be genetic, environmental, or due to poor handling. Signs include bees following you, stinging without provocation, and boiling out of the hive when opened. First, check your own behavior: move slowly, wear clean, light-colored clothing, and avoid crushing bees. Use smoke gently. If a colony remains aggressive, consider requeening. Order a gentle queen from a reputable breeder. To requeen, find and kill the old queen, then introduce the new queen in a cage. Leave her caged for 2-3 days, then release her. Alternatively, you can split the aggressive colony and let them raise a new queen from eggs from a gentle colony. Another method is to move the aggressive hive to a new location, at least 3 km away, and place a gentle colony in its old spot. The foragers will join the gentle colony. If you cannot requeen, manage the colony defensively: work in the late afternoon when bees are calmer, use plenty of smoke, and keep inspections short. Wear extra protection: a thick suit, gloves, and boots. Never work an aggressive colony near people or animals. If the colony is too dangerous, consider destroying it by pouring soapy water into the hive. This is a last resort. In Africa, many beekeepers use local bees that are naturally aggressive. Some choose to work with them because they are productive and resistant to pests. But if you have neighbours or livestock, a gentle colony is safer. Always inform your neighbours before inspecting an aggressive hive. With patience and proper management, you can reduce aggression and make beekeeping enjoyable..Trim()

Seasonal Maintenance Tasks

Beekeeping follows the seasons. In Africa, seasons vary: wet and dry, or hot and cool. Your maintenance tasks should match the bees' needs. During the rainy season, ensure hives are dry and well-ventilated. Check for leaks and repair them. Raise hives on stands to prevent flooding and ant attacks. In the dry season, bees may need water. Provide a shallow water source with stones or sticks so bees don't drown. Also, watch for absconding due to lack of forage. If nectar is scarce, feed sugar syrup (1:1 sugar to water) to keep the colony alive. In hot regions, provide shade for the hive. In cool regions, reduce the entrance to keep warmth in. During the honey flow, add supers as needed and inspect weekly. After harvest, leave enough honey for the bees. In areas with a cold season, insulate the hive with grass or leaves, and reduce the entrance. In all seasons, keep the apiary clean: remove tall grass, and control pests. Check hives monthly during low activity. Replace old, dark combs with fresh foundation to reduce disease. Repair or replace damaged hive parts. In Africa, many beekeepers use locally available materials: mud, thatch, or metal sheets for roofs. Ensure the hive is predator-proof: use wire mesh to keep out honey badgers and monkeys. Record everything: date, tasks done, observations. This helps you plan for the next season. Remember, bees are resilient but need your help to thrive. By following a seasonal calendar, you keep your colonies strong and productive year-round..Trim()

How Bees Make Honey

Honey starts as nectar, a sugary liquid produced by flowers. Forager bees collect nectar using their proboscis, a tube-like tongue, and store it in their honey stomach. A single bee can carry about 40 milligrams of nectar. Back at the hive, the forager passes the nectar to a younger house bee through trophallaxis, mouth-to-mouth transfer. The house bee adds enzymes, mainly invertase, which breaks down complex sugars like sucrose into simple sugars glucose and fructose. The bee then deposits the nectar into a wax cell..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, the bees fan their wings rapidly over the open cells to evaporate water. Nectar is about 80% water, but honey must be under 20% water to prevent fermentation. A strong colony can evaporate several liters of water in a day. The fanning creates airflow that carries moisture out of the hive. Meanwhile, the enzyme glucose oxidase produces hydrogen peroxide, which helps preserve the honey. When the honey reaches the right moisture level, usually around 17-18%, the bees cap the cell with a thin layer of wax. This capping signals that the honey is ripe..Trim()

.Trim()

The whole process takes about one to three weeks, depending on humidity and nectar source. In hot, dry areas like northern Kenya or the Sahel, evaporation is faster, so honey ripens quicker. In humid forest zones, it may take longer. A healthy hive can produce 20 to 50 kilograms of surplus honey per year, but you should only harvest what the bees can spare. Always leave at least 10-15 kilograms of honey for the colony to eat during dry seasons. Understanding this process helps you know when to harvest and how to avoid disturbing the bees unnecessarily..Trim()

Determining When Honey is Ripe

Ripe honey is capped honey. That is the simplest rule: if at least 80% of the cells in a frame are sealed with white wax, the honey is ready to harvest. Uncapped honey contains too much water and will ferment, turning sour and ruining your crop. To check, lift a frame from the super (the upper box) and look at the surface. Capped cells are flat or slightly convex, while open cells glisten with liquid. If you see many open cells, wait another week and check again..Trim()

.Trim()

For a more precise test, use a refractometer. This small optical tool measures water content. Place a drop of honey on the glass, close the lid, and look through the eyepiece. Ripe honey should read between 15% and 18% water. Above 20% is risky. In humid regions like the Congo Basin or coastal West Africa, honey may never drop below 20% without extra drying. In that case, you can use a dehumidifier in a small room or dry the honey in shallow trays with a fan before bottling. But the capping test is usually enough for small-scale farmers..Trim()

.Trim()

Another sign is the 'shake test.' Hold a frame horizontally and give it a sharp jerk. If honey drips out, it is not ripe. If it stays in the cells, it is close. Also, listen to the hive. When bees are fanning to evaporate water, you hear a steady hum. Once capping begins, the fanning decreases. Always harvest on a sunny day when bees are out foraging, so fewer bees are in the hive. Never harvest uncapped honey unless you plan to eat it quickly or process it further. Selling unripe honey damages your reputation and can make customers sick. So be patient: ripe honey is worth the wait..Trim()

Harvesting Honey: Methods and Tools

Harvesting honey requires calm bees, the right tools, and a clean workspace. Start early in the morning or late afternoon when most foragers are away. Wear protective clothing: a bee suit, gloves, and boots. Smoke the entrance lightly with a bee smoker burning dry grass or sisal. Smoke masks alarm pheromones and makes bees docile. Open the hive gently and remove frames one by one. Use a hive tool to pry frames loose. Brush bees off with a soft bee brush or shake them gently into the hive. Never crush bees; that releases alarm pheromones and agitates the colony..Trim()

.Trim()

Place harvested frames in a clean, airtight container to prevent robbing by other bees. For small-scale farmers, a simple plastic bucket with a lid works. If you have many hives, use a wheelbarrow or pickup to move frames to a processing room. The room should be bee-proof, with screened windows, and clean surfaces. You can harvest honey in two ways: crush-and-strain or using an extractor. Crush-and-strain is cheapest: cut capped honeycomb into a bucket, mash it with a clean paddle, and let it drain through a sieve or cheesecloth. This method destroys the comb, so bees must rebuild it, which takes energy and honey. An extractor is better: it spins frames and throws honey out by centrifugal force, leaving comb intact. After extraction, return empty frames to the hive. Bees will clean them and refill. Always leave enough honey for the colony. In Africa, harvest twice a year in many regions: once after the main rainy season and once after the short rains. Never harvest during drought or when flowers are scarce. If you must harvest, feed the bees sugar syrup (1 part sugar to 1 part water) to keep them alive. Proper harvesting ensures strong hives and repeat harvests..Trim()

Extracting and Straining Honey

After harvesting, you need to separate honey from wax and debris. If you use an extractor, place uncapped frames in the extractor basket. Spin slowly at first, then faster. Honey flies out and collects at the bottom. Open the tap and strain the honey through a double sieve: a coarse mesh to catch wax bits, then a fine mesh to remove pollen and fine particles. Straining removes impurities but keeps beneficial pollen. Use food-grade plastic or stainless steel buckets. Avoid galvanized metal, which reacts with acids in honey. Clean all equipment with hot water and dry thoroughly. Never use soap, as residue affects taste..Trim()

In Africa, many farmers lose honey to fermentation because they bottle it too wet. After straining, check water content again with a refractometer. If above 20%, you can reduce moisture by placing honey in a shallow tray in a dry, warm room with a fan for a few days. Stir daily. Or use a dehumidifier. Once honey is below 18% water, it will not ferment. Store strained honey in a clean, dry container with a tight lid. Keep it in a cool, dark place. Proper extraction and straining give you clear, attractive honey that sells for a higher price at local markets and to urban buyers..Trim()

Bottling honey is simple but must be done hygienically. Use clean glass jars or food-grade plastic containers with airtight lids. Honey Varieties and Flavor Profiles

Honey's color, taste, and aroma depend on the flowers bees visit. In Africa, you can find many unique varieties. Acacia honey, from acacia trees in savannah regions, is light, mild, and slow to crystallize. It sells for a premium. Wildflower honey is a blend from many flowers; it is amber, robust, and varies by season. Baobab honey, from baobab blossoms, is creamy and tangy. Eucalyptus honey is dark, strong, and slightly medicinal. Miombo honey from miombo woodlands is dark and malty. Citrus honey from orange or lemon blossoms is light and fruity. Each region has its own signature honey..Trim()

.Trim()

Flavor also depends on processing. Raw, unheated honey retains delicate floral notes. Heating or fine filtering removes pollen and some aroma.
