Types of Raw Materials: Silica, Soda Ash, Limestone

Limestone (calcium carbonate, CaCO3) adds calcium oxide, which makes glass more durable and resistant to water. You need about 10-15% limestone in the batch. Batch Calculation and Mixing Procedures

In African settings, a simple visual check after melting is powerful. Also, check the final glass for seeds (bubbles) and stones (unmelted particles). Principles of Annealing: Stress Relief

When glass cools unevenly, internal stresses build up that can cause it to crack or shatter unexpectedly. Annealing is the controlled cooling process that relieves these stresses. In a small workshop in Nairobi or Lagos, you might make glass beads, fused glass art, or even simple window panes. After shaping the hot glass, you must cool it slowly through a specific temperature range. For soda-lime glass, the annealing point is around 540Â°C. At this temperature, the glass is still slightly plastic and can relieve stress in about 15 minutes. Then you cool it slowly to the strain point, about 510Â°C, where stress is frozen in if cooled too fast. The key is to keep the glass at the annealing point long enough for all parts to reach the same temperature, then cool at a controlled rate, usually 1-2Â°C per minute for thick pieces. A simple annealing oven, called a lehr, can be made from firebricks and a PID controller. Without annealing, glass may look fine but break from a slight bump or temperature change. In Africa, where daily temperature swings can be large, proper annealing is critical for durable products. Always test your annealed glass by checking for stress with a polariscope, which shows stress patterns as colored bands. If you see bright colors, you need to adjust your cooling schedule. Remember, annealing is not just about cooling; it's about giving the glass time to relax internally..Trim()

A lehr is a specialized oven for annealing glass. For small-scale operations in Africa, you can build a simple lehr using locally available materials. Tempering: Thermal and Chemical

Tempering makes glass stronger by creating compressive stress on its surface. Thermal tempering involves heating glass to about 620-680Â°C, then rapidly cooling it with air jets. This causes the surface to cool faster than the interior, putting the surface into compression and the interior into tension. The result is glass that is 4-5 times stronger than annealed glass. In Africa, thermal tempering is used for car windows, shower doors, and building facades. However, it requires precise control. You need a tempering furnace with rollers and air blowers. The glass must be cut and edged before tempering because any cutting after will cause it to shatter. Chemical tempering, also called ion exchange, is done by immersing glass in a molten salt bath, usually potassium nitrate, at about 400-450Â°C. Smaller sodium ions in the glass are replaced by larger potassium ions, creating surface compression. This method is used for thin glass, like smartphone screens. In Africa, chemical tempering is less common due to the cost of salts and equipment, but it can be done in small tanks. Safety is critical: molten salt can cause severe burns. Always wear protective gear. Both methods require strict quality control. Tempered glass breaks into small, blunt pieces, which is a safety feature. Fragmentation testing is how you verify that glass is properly tempered. The standard test involves breaking a sample and counting the number of fragments in a 50mm x 50mm square. Glass Cutting Tools and Techniques

To cut glass, you need a few basic tools: a glass cutter (wheel-type or carbide), a straight edge or T-square, a cutting oil (or kerosene), and a workbench covered with a felt or rubber mat. For straight cuts, measure twice with a tape measure and mark with a grease pencil. Apply a drop of oil along the cut line. For curves, use a pattern or template and a cutter with a smaller wheel. For tempered glass, you cannot cut itâit will shatter. So always cut before tempering. Store cutters in a dry place to prevent rust.
