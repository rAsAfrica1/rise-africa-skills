Overview of the Production Process from Start to Finish

For pellets, the process is similar but uses a pellet mill. You need to grind material finer (less than 6 mm) and control moisture (12-15%). .Trim()

.Trim()

Why Carbonization? Benefits for Smoke Reduction and Energy

When you burn raw agricultural waste like maize cobs or rice husks directly, you get a lot of smoke and not much heat. .Trim()

In Africa, where many families cook indoors with poor ventilation, smoke reduction is a health necessity. .Trim()

Carbonization also allows you to use a wider range of materials. Sawdust, coconut shells, palm kernel shells, and even paper waste can be carbonized. Finally, carbonization reduces waste volume. A pile of rice husks that takes up 1 cubic meter might shrink to 0.3 cubic meters of char. This makes storage and transport cheaper. Module 12 · Scaling Up and Expanding Your Business

When to Move from Manual to Motorized Production

You will know it is time to motorize when your manual production cannot meet orders and you are turning away customers. A typical manual briquette press produces 50-100 kg per day with 2-3 workers. If you regularly sell out and have a waiting list, or if you are spending more than 6 hours a day pressing, it is time to upgrade. Motorized presses range from 300 kg/hour to 1 ton/hour. For example, a 300 kg/hour motorized briquette machine costs between $2,500 and $5,000 in Kenya or Uganda. It can pay for itself in 6-12 months if you sell briquettes at $0.30 per kg. Before buying, calculate your current daily output and your potential sales. If you can sell at least 500 kg per day, motorization makes sense. Also consider the cost of fuel or electricity. A diesel-powered machine uses about 2 liters per hour, costing $2-3. Electric machines need a 3-phase connection, which may require a transformer. You also need more raw material. A motorized line requires 1-2 tons of biomass per day. Secure supply from sawmills, farms, or charcoal dust. You will need a larger drying area, at least 50 square meters. Train one operator to run and maintain the machine. Simple maintenance like greasing bearings and replacing screens can be done weekly. If you cannot afford a new machine, look for used ones from other producers who are upgrading. Always test the machine with your own material before buying. Motorization is a big step, but it doubles or triples your income and allows you to hire more workers..Trim()

Finding Partners and Investors for Growth

To grow, you need money and people. Start with your local community. SACCOs (Savings and Credit Cooperatives) in Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania offer loans of $500 to $5,000 at 10-15% annual interest. You need a business plan and sometimes collateral. Another option is a partnership with a local farmer or sawmill owner. They supply raw materials in exchange for a share of profits. For example, a sawmill in Nakuru might provide 2 tons of sawdust per week for 20% of your briquette sales. That saves you cash. Investors can come from diaspora Africans who want to invest back home. Platforms like Kiva, AgFunder, and local angel networks connect you. But be careful: an investor will want 20-40% ownership. Only give equity if you need large sums ($10,000+). For smaller amounts, take a loan. You can also apply for grants from organizations like the African Development Bank, USAID, or the Global Alliance for Clean Cookstoves. They offer $5,000 to $50,000 for clean energy businesses. Write a simple proposal: problem, solution, market, team, financials. Show your sales records. Partner with a local NGO that works on clean cooking. They can link you to buyers and funders. Always have a written agreement. Include roles, profit sharing, and exit terms. Do not rely on verbal promises. A lawyer or local business advisor can help draft it for $50-100. Start small with a partner you trust. Test the relationship for 3 months before signing a long-term deal. Remember, growth is a marathon, not a sprint..Trim()

Diversifying Products: Cooking Briquettes, Pellets, and Animal Bedding

Selling only cooking briquettes limits your income. You can make three products from the same biomass: cooking briquettes, pellets, and animal bedding. Cooking briquettes are your core. Standard size: 5 cm diameter, 5 cm tall, with a hole for airflow. Sell in 5 kg bags for $1.50. Pellets are smaller (6-8 mm diameter) and burn hotter. They are used in gasifier stoves and for chicken brooding. A pellet mill costs $1,500-$3,000. Pellets sell for $0.40-$0.60 per kg. You can sell to poultry farmers who use them for heating. Animal bedding is made from coarse biomass (rice husks, wood shavings) that is not carbonized. It absorbs moisture and ammonia. Sell to dairy farmers and horse stables. A 20 kg bale sells for $2-$3. To make bedding, just dry and bag the raw material. No pressing needed. This is a low-cost add-on. For pellets, you need a hammer mill to grind material to 5 mm, then a pellet mill, then a cooler. You can also make mosquito coils from sawdust and natural repellents like neem. Or make charcoal dust mixed with clay for cooking. Diversifying reduces risk. If briquette sales drop in the rainy season, bedding sales may rise. Start with one new product at a time. Test the market with 50 kg. Ask customers what they need. Train your workers on the new process. You can use the same drying racks and storage. Label each product clearly. For pellets, include instructions: use in pellet stoves only. For bedding, say it is untreated and safe for animals. With three products, you can keep your machines running and your workers employed year-round..Trim()

Exporting to Neighboring Countries: Opportunities and Challenges

Exporting to neighboring countries can double your market, but it comes with rules. First, check if there is demand. In Rwanda, briquettes sell for $0.35 per kg. In South Sudan, $0.50 per kg. In Malawi, $0.25 per kg. Your cost to produce is $0.15-$0.20 per kg. So exporting can be profitable. But you need to handle transport, customs, and quality standards. Start with a small trial: send 1 ton to a buyer in Kampala or Kigali. Use a truck that carries 7-10 tons. Transport cost from Nairobi to Kampala is about $1,000 per truck. That adds $0.10 per kg. You must register with your country's export promotion board. In Kenya, it is the Export Promotion Council. They help with paperwork. You need a certificate of origin, a phytosanitary certificate (for plant-based products), and a commercial invoice. Some countries require a pre-shipment inspection. The East African Community has simplified trade regimes for small exporters. For loads under $2,000, you can use the Simplified Certificate of Origin. That saves time. Quality is critical. Your briquettes must have consistent size, low moisture (below 10%), and high calorific value (above 4,500 kcal/kg). Buyers may test a sample. If it smokes or crumbles, they reject it. Pack in 20 kg bags with your brand and contact. Label in English and French if going to Francophone countries. Payment is a risk. Use letters of credit or get 50% deposit upfront. Work with a clearing agent at the border. They charge $100-$200 per shipment. Start with one country, learn the process, then expand. Join a trade mission or an export training workshop. Many are free through ITC or USAID..Trim()

Training Others and Creating Jobs in Your Community

Training others is a way to grow your business and help your community. You can train people to make briquettes and then buy their output or employ them. A 3-day training workshop can teach 10 people how to mix, press, and dry briquettes. Charge $20 per person or offer free training in exchange for them selling to you. For example, in Ghana, a briquette business trained 50 women who now supply 2 tons per week. That business pays them $0.10 per kg. The women earn $200 per week collectively. You can also hire workers for your own production. A manual press needs 3 workers: one to mix, one to press, one to dry and pack. Pay them $3-$5 per day. That is above average in many rural areas. As you grow, you need a supervisor, a salesperson, and a machine operator. Train them on safety: wear masks when handling dust, gloves when using the press, and keep the area ventilated. You can also train on business skills: record keeping, customer service, and pricing. Partner with local vocational schools or NGOs to run trainings. They may provide materials or a stipend. Create a job ladder: start as a helper, become a press operator, then a team leader. Offer a bonus for high quality or extra output. This reduces turnover. When you train others, you also build loyalty. They see you as a leader. You can form a cooperative where members share equipment and profits. Register it with the social services department. This allows you to access group loans and grants. Remember, your community is your biggest asset. Invest in them and they will invest in you..Trim()

Developing a Business Plan for Expansion

A business plan is your roadmap. It helps you get loans, partners, and stay focused. Write it in simple language. Start with a one-page summary: what you do, your market, your financial need, and your expected profit. Then detail each section. First, describe your product: briquettes, pellets, or bedding. Include size, packaging, and price. Second, market analysis: who buys, how many, at what price. For example, in your town, 500 households use charcoal. If you capture 10%, that is 50 households buying 10 kg per week = 500 kg per week. Third, operations: where you produce, what equipment you have, how many workers. Fourth, management: who runs the business, their skills. Fifth, financials: list your costs (raw materials, labor, rent, transport) and your revenue. Show profit for 12 months. Use a simple spreadsheet or a notebook. For expansion, state what you need: a new machine ($3,000), a truck ($8,000), or working capital ($2,000). Show how the money will increase sales. For example, a new machine will double output from 300 kg to 600 kg per day. That adds $45 per day in profit. Payback in 3 months. Include a risk section: what if raw material prices rise? What if a competitor opens? Have a plan B. Attach photos of your products and letters from customers. If you apply for a loan, the bank will want to see your records. Keep daily sales and expense logs. Update your plan every 6 months. A good plan is 5-10 pages. You can get templates from the local business development office or online. Have someone review it. Then use it to guide your growth..Trim()
