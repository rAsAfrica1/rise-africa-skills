Key terms: standards, certification, accreditation, conformity

To navigate export rules, you need to understand four key terms: standards, certification, accreditation, and conformity. A standard is a document that sets out requirements, specifications, guidelines, or characteristics for a product, service, or process. For example, the East African Standard for maize flour specifies moisture content, aflatoxin limits, and packaging. Standards are usually voluntary, but they become mandatory when referenced in regulations. The Codex Alimentarius is a set of international food standards that many countries use as a basis for their regulations..Trim()

.Trim()

Certification is the process by which an independent body confirms that a product, process, or service meets a standard. For example, a company can get ISO 22000 certification for its food safety management system. Certification is usually voluntary but can be required by buyers. It involves an audit and ongoing surveillance. The certifying body issues a certificate that is valid for a certain period, usually three years..Trim()

.Trim()

Accreditation is the formal recognition that a certification body is competent to carry out certification. In other words, accreditation is to certification bodies what certification is to companies. For example, the Kenya Accreditation Service (KENAS) accredits certification bodies in Kenya. Accreditation ensures that certificates are credible and accepted internationally. If a certification body is not accredited, its certificates may not be recognized by buyers or regulators..Trim()

.Trim()

Conformity is the act of meeting a standard. It is the result of following the standard's requirements. Conformity assessment is the process of demonstrating that a product meets a standard, which can include testing, inspection, and certification. For example, a conformity assessment for export might involve laboratory testing of your product, inspection of your facility, and certification of your quality management system..Trim()

.Trim()

Understanding these terms helps you communicate with buyers, regulators, and service providers. When a buyer asks for a certificate, you know it must come from an accredited certification body. When you test your product, you should use an accredited laboratory. This ensures your results are accepted. Remember: standards are the rules, certification is the proof, accreditation is the credibility, and conformity is the goal..Trim()

Who sets the rules? ISO, Codex, EU, and African bodies

Export standards are set by a mix of international, regional, and national bodies. The most important international bodies are ISO, Codex Alimentarius, and the WTO. ISO (International Organization for Standardization) develops voluntary standards for many products and processes. For example, ISO 9001 is a quality management standard, and ISO 22000 is for food safety. ISO standards are developed by experts from member countries and are widely used in global trade..Trim()

.Trim()

Codex Alimentarius, often called the "food code," is a collection of standards, guidelines, and codes of practice for food safety and quality. It is managed by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the World Health Organization (WHO). Codex standards are the basis for many national regulations and are recognized by the WTO as the benchmark for food safety. For example, Codex sets maximum levels for aflatoxins in peanuts and cereals..Trim()

The WTO (World Trade Organization) does not set standards but provides rules for how countries can apply standards. The Sanitary and Phytosanitary (SPS) Agreement and the Technical Barriers to Trade (TBT) Agreement require that standards be based on science and not be used as unjustified trade barriers. If a country sets a standard higher than international norms, it must provide scientific justification..Trim()

In Africa, several bodies set standards. The African Organization for Standardization (ARSO) harmonizes standards across Africa to facilitate trade under AfCFTA. The African Union has also developed the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) which includes provisions on standards. At the national level, each country has a national bureau of standards, such as KEBS in Kenya, SON in Nigeria, and SABS in South Africa. These bodies set national standards and often adopt international standards as their own..Trim()

.Trim()

The EU sets its own standards, which are often stricter than international norms. For example, the EU has maximum residue levels (MRLs) for pesticides that are lower than Codex limits. The US has its own set of standards, enforced by agencies like the FDA and USDA. To export, you must know which body sets the rules for your product and market. Start with your national bureau of standards; they can guide you to the relevant international or regional standards..Trim()

Your national bureau of standards is your first stop for export compliance. Examples include the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS), the Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON), the Ghana Standards Authority (GSA), and the South African Bureau of Standards (SABS). You can bring samples of your product to their laboratories to check if it meets requirements. This is often cheaper than private labs. Third, they offer certification. Fourth, they provide training and advisory services on quality management, food safety, and export documentation..Trim()

First, define your product precisely. For example, "dried hibiscus flowers" is not enough. You need to specify: is it whole or ground? Is it for human consumption or cosmetic use? Second, identify the target market's requirements. If you are exporting to the EU, go to the EU's Access2Markets database. It lists import rules, tariffs, and standards for each product and country. Third, compare your product's current quality to the standard. Get your product tested by an accredited lab. If your product fails, you need to improve your production process. This might mean changing your farming practices, investing in better drying and storage, or using approved pesticides..Trim()

Fourth, document your compliance. Keep records of your tests, certificates, and production processes. This is your evidence. Buyers and regulators may ask for it. Finally, review and update. Standards change. For example, the EU regularly updates its MRLs for pesticides. Subscribe to alerts from your national bureau of standards or trade promotion agency. How to read a buyer's specification sheet

A buyer's specification sheet is a document that lists all the requirements your product must meet. It often looks like a table with columns for parameter, unit, and limit (e.g., moisture content max 8%). First, identify the product name and the buyer's reference code. Then, scan for mandatory parameters like moisture, foreign matter, and defect percentage. For example, a European coffee buyer might require moisture â¤12%, defects â¤5%, and no live insects. Next, check for testing methods: they might specify ISO 6673 for moisture. You must use a lab that follows that method. Also, look for packaging and labeling details: bag size (e.g., 60 kg jute bags), labeling language (English/French), and batch numbering. If something is unclear, don't guessâemail the buyer for clarification. Many African exporters lose deals because they assume they know the spec. Private standards vs. public regulations: what buyers demand

Public regulations are laws set by governments, like the EU's maximum residue limits (MRLs) for pesticides. Private standards are rules set by buyers, industry groups, or NGOs. Examples include GlobalG.A.P. for fruits and vegetables, Fairtrade for cocoa, and Rainforest Alliance for coffee. Buyers often demand private standards even if public regulations are met. For instance, a German coffee buyer may require Rainforest Alliance certification, which covers environmental and social criteria. Public regulations are mandatory; private standards are voluntary but become mandatory if you want that buyer. In Africa, many exporters struggle because they only focus on public rules. To find out what buyers want, ask for their supplier manual or code of conduct. These documents list requirements like no child labor, traceability, and pesticide lists. For shea butter, a French buyer might demand organic certification (EU organic) and fair trade. For coffee, a German buyer might require 4C certification (Common Code for the Coffee Community). The cost of certification can be high: GlobalG.A.P. inspection can cost $1,000â$3,000. But it opens doors to premium prices. Some buyers offer to share costs or provide training. Always check if the standard is recognized in your country. For example, Kenya has a national organic standard. You can also join cooperatives to share certification costs. Remember, private standards often exceed public ones. If you meet them, you can negotiate better prices. But don't over-promise: if you claim to be organic, you need annual inspections. Using trade portals (ITC, AfCFTA) to find standards for your product

Negotiating standards with buyers without losing the deal

Sometimes a buyer's standard is too expensive or impossible for you right now. You don't have to say yes or no immediately. You can negotiate. First, understand the buyer's real need. Ask: 'Is this standard mandatory for your market, or can we phase it in?' For example, a French buyer might require organic certification, but you can propose to start with conventional and transition over 12 months. Second, offer alternatives. If they want a specific pesticide limit, show them test results from a local lab that meet the limit using a different method. Third, be transparent about costs and timelines. Say: 'I can meet this standard in 6 months if you pay 20% advance.' Many buyers will share costs if you show commitment. Fourth, use a phased approach. Propose a trial shipment of 1 ton that meets most standards, with a plan to meet the rest. Fifth, get help from trade support institutions. In Africa, organizations like the African Export-Import Bank (Afreximbank) or national TPOs can provide grants for certification. Sixth, never promise what you cannot deliver. If you fail, you lose the buyer and your reputation. Seventh, put the agreement in writing. Email is fine. State what you will do and by when. Eighth, if the buyer insists on an impossible standard, walk away politely. There are other buyers. For example, a Kenyan exporter of green beans was asked for GlobalG.A.P. but couldn't afford it. HACCP basics for small food processors

HACCP stands for Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points. It is a food safety system that prevents problems before they happen. As a small processor in Africa, you can implement HACCP without expensive consultants. Start by identifying where things can go wrong in your process. For example, if you dry maize on the ground, birds and rodents can contaminate it. That is a hazard. List all steps from receiving raw materials to shipping. For each step, ask: what could make the food unsafe? Common hazards include bacteria like Salmonella, chemicals like aflatoxins, and physical objects like stones..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, find the critical control points (CCPs). These are steps where you must take action to prevent or eliminate a hazard. For a small fruit juice processor, pasteurization is a CCP. You must heat the juice to 72Â°C for 15 seconds to kill E. coli and other pathogens. For a groundnut processor, roasting at 160Â°C for 15 minutes can reduce aflatoxins. Set a critical limit: the minimum or maximum value that keeps food safe. For pasteurization, the limit is 72Â°C for 15 seconds. If the temperature drops below that, you must stop and reheat..Trim()

.Trim()

Monitor each CCP. This means checking the temperature or time regularly. Use a simple thermometer that you calibrate monthly with ice water (0Â°C) and boiling water (100Â°C). Record the results in a logbook. If you find a deviation, take corrective action: reheat the juice, or discard the batch if it cannot be fixed. Keep records for at least two years; buyers and auditors will ask for them..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, verify your HACCP plan works. Test your final product for bacteria like E. coli and Salmonella. Send samples to a lab every three months. If results show contamination, adjust your CCPs. Also, train your workers. They must know why handwashing after using the toilet is critical. A simple sign in the local language at the toilet exit helps. HACCP is not paperwork for its own sake; it is a practical way to keep your export business alive. Start with one product line, then expand..Trim()

Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) on a budget

Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) are the basic conditions and activities needed to produce safe food. You do not need a fancy factory. You need discipline and cheap solutions. Start with your building. Walls should be smooth and washable. If you have rough mud walls, cover them with plastic sheeting or cement plaster. Floors must be sloped to a drain so water does not pool. Fix leaks immediately; stagnant water breeds bacteria. Keep doors and windows screened to keep out flies and rodents. A screen costs less than $20 at a local market..Trim()

.Trim()

Personal hygiene is free. Workers must wash hands with soap and water before handling food, after using the toilet, and after touching garbage. Provide a handwashing station with a bucket, tap, and soap. No towel needed; air dry or use single-use paper. Workers with cuts or diarrhea must not handle food. Provide clean aprons and hairnets. If hairnets are expensive, use clean caps or scarves. No jewelry, no eating, no smoking in production areas..Trim()

.Trim()

Equipment and utensils must be cleaned and sanitized. Use a three-compartment sink: wash with detergent, rinse with clean water, then sanitize with a bleach solution (1 tablespoon of bleach per gallon of water). Soak utensils for 1 minute, then air dry. For cutting boards, use plastic, not wood, because wood harbors bacteria. Clean floors and walls daily. Keep a cleaning schedule posted on the wall. Assign one person per area..Trim()

.Trim()

Pest control is critical. Store raw materials and finished products off the floor on pallets. Keep garbage in covered bins and empty daily. Use traps for rats and flies. Do not use poison near food. If you see cockroaches, call a pest control company, but first seal cracks. Water quality matters: use potable water. If you use well water, test it for E. coli every six months. Boil or chlorinate if needed. These GMP steps cost little but prevent costly rejections at the port..Trim()

Controlling aflatoxins in groundnuts and maize

Aflatoxins are poisons produced by molds (Aspergillus flavus and A. parasiticus) that grow on groundnuts, maize, and other crops. They cause liver cancer and are strictly regulated. The European Union allows only 2 parts per billion (ppb) of aflatoxin B1 in groundnuts for direct human consumption, and 4 ppb total aflatoxins. For maize, the limit is 5 ppb for B1 and 10 ppb total. Many African exports are rejected because of aflatoxins. You can prevent this..Trim()

.Trim()

Start in the field. Use good agricultural practices: plant early, irrigate properly, and control insects. Drought stress increases aflatoxin risk. Harvest when crops are mature but before they over-dry. For groundnuts, harvest when 75% of pods have dark veins inside. Do not leave harvested crops on the ground. Dry on clean tarps or raised platforms, not on bare soil. Dry to safe moisture: groundnuts to 8-10%, maize to 13-14%. Use a moisture meter ($50-$100). If you cannot afford one, use the salt test: mix a handful of groundnuts with dry salt in a jar; if salt sticks to the nuts, they are too wet..Trim()

.Trim()

During storage, keep moisture low. Store in clean, dry jute bags on pallets. Keep the store ventilated and cool. Do not mix new and old crops. Inspect weekly for mold, insects, and moisture. If you see green or yellow mold, remove and destroy affected kernels. Never blend moldy with good crops; that spreads aflatoxins. For maize, you can use a simple hand sorter to remove discolored kernels. For groundnuts, hand-sort to remove shriveled, discolored, or moldy nuts..Trim()

.Trim()

Testing is essential. Buy a rapid test kit like AgraStrip or RidaQuick. They cost about $5 per test. Test each lot before shipping. If aflatoxin is above 4 ppb for groundnuts or 10 ppb for maize, do not export. You can also use biocontrol like Aflasafe, which is a natural product that reduces aflatoxin by 80-90% when applied in the field. Aflasafe is available in several African countries. Finally, keep records of your drying, storage, and testing. Buyers want proof..Trim()

Pesticide residue limits: what the EU really checks

The European Union sets Maximum Residue Limits (MRLs) for pesticides on food. If your export exceeds the MRL, it is rejected. MRLs are in milligrams per kilogram (mg/kg), also called parts per million (ppm). For example, the MRL for chlorpyrifos on groundnuts is 0.01 mg/kg. For maize, it is 0.05 mg/kg. These are very low. Many African farmers use pesticides that are banned in the EU, like chlorpyrifos and dimethoate. Even if you use a legal pesticide, you must follow the pre-harvest interval (PHI): the number of days between the last spray and harvest. For example, if the label says PHI is 14 days, you must not harvest for 14 days after spraying..Trim()

.Trim()

The EU checks pesticide residues by taking a sample of your shipment at the port of entry. They test for over 500 different pesticides. If they find one above the MRL, they notify the Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed (RASFF). Your name and shipment are published. That can destroy your reputation. To avoid this, keep a spray diary: record the date, pesticide name, dose, and PHI. Use only pesticides registered in your country and allowed in the EU. Check the EU Pesticides Database online. If you cannot access the internet, ask your extension officer for a list..Trim()

.Trim()

Use integrated pest management (IPM) to reduce pesticide use. Scout your field weekly. Use pheromone traps for moths. Plant resistant varieties. Encourage natural enemies like ladybugs. If you must spray, use the correct dose. Do not mix pesticides unless the label says so. Wear protective clothing. After spraying, triple-rinse the empty container and puncture it so it cannot be reused. Never spray near harvest. For groundnuts, stop all spraying 21 days before harvest. For maize, stop 30 days before harvest..Trim()

.Trim()

If you are a smallholder, join a farmer group that can afford a shared sprayer and training. Some exporters provide lists of approved pesticides. Also, consider organic production. Organic exports have zero pesticide residue limits, but you must be certified. That takes three years of no synthetic pesticides. Start with a small plot. Finally, test your crop for residues before shipping. Labs in Kenya, Ghana, and Nigeria offer this for $50-$100 per sample. It is cheaper than a rejected container..Trim()

Traceability: from farm to shipping container

Traceability means you can follow a food product from the farm to the consumer. If a buyer finds a problem, you must quickly identify the source. The EU requires traceability for all food imports. Without it, your shipment can be rejected. You do not need a computer. A simple paper system works. Start by assigning a unique code to each farmer or field. For example, use the farmer's initials and a number: JD-001. When you receive their harvest, write that code on the bag. Keep a logbook with the date, farmer code, weight, and any test results..Trim()

.Trim()

During processing, keep batches separate. If you mix groundnuts from different farmers, you lose traceability. Instead, process one farmer's lot at a time. If you must mix, record the codes of all farmers in that batch. Assign a batch number to your finished product, like BATCH-2025-03-15. Write this number on every bag or carton. In your logbook, link the batch number to the farmer codes. Also record the processing date, machine settings, and operator name. For example, if you roast at 160Â°C for 15 minutes, write that down..Trim()

.Trim()

When you ship, create a shipping container code. For example, CONT-2025-04-01. Record which batches went into that container. If a buyer in Europe finds aflatoxin in one bag, you can trace it back to the batch and then to the farmer. That farmer's other bags can be recalled. Without traceability, you might have to recall the entire container, costing thousands of dollars. Keep all records for at least two years. Store them in a dry place. Use a simple filing cabinet or a plastic box..Trim()

.Trim()

Train your workers to fill in forms correctly. Use a checklist for each step. For example, when receiving raw materials, check: farmer code, weight, moisture, and visible mold. Sign and date. When shipping, check: batch numbers, container code, and destination. If you use a smartphone, you can take photos of forms and store them in Google Drive. But paper is fine. The key is consistency. Auditors will ask to see your records. If you cannot show them, you fail. So start today, even with a notebook. It is the cheapest insurance for your export business..Trim()

Preparing for a food safety audit

A food safety audit is an inspection by a buyer or certification body to check if you follow food safety standards. It can be scary, but with preparation, you can pass. First, know what the auditor will ask for. They will want to see your HACCP plan, GMP records, traceability logs, and test results. They will walk through your facility, look at your equipment, and interview workers. They may take samples. The audit usually takes 1-2 days. You will get a report with non-conformities (problems) that you must fix..Trim()

.Trim()

Start preparing at least one month before. Do a self-audit using the same checklist the auditor will use. For example, if you are audited against GlobalG.A.P. or BRC, download their checklist. Walk through your process and score yourself. Common non-conformities include: no handwashing station, missing pest control records, dirty equipment, and no calibration for thermometers. Fix these before the auditor arrives. Clean everything: floors, walls, ceilings, equipment, and storage areas. Remove all clutter. Make sure you have soap, paper towels, and bleach..Trim()

.Trim()

Organize your documents. Put them in a binder with tabs: HACCP plan, GMP records, cleaning schedules, pest control records, training records, traceability logs, and lab test results. Make sure they are up to date. For example, if you calibrate your thermometer monthly, the last calibration should be within the last month. If you test for aflatoxin, have results for each lot. Train your workers on what to say. They should know basic food safety: wash hands, no jewelry, report illness. Practice a mock audit with a friend playing the auditor. If you fix everything, you get certified. Certification opens doors to high-value markets. It is worth the effort. Remember, the auditor is not your enemy; they help you improve. ISO 9001 for small manufacturers: is it worth it?

ISO 9001 is a quality management system standard. It is not a product standard; it certifies that your factory has consistent processes. For small manufacturers in Africa, certification costs between $3,000 and $15,000 for the first year, including consultant fees, documentation, and the certification body audit. Annual surveillance audits cost $1,500 to $5,000. Is it worth it? If you export to Europe, North America, or large African retailers like Shoprite or Massmart, ISO 9001 is often a minimum requirement. It opens doors to contracts that un-certified factories cannot bid for. For example, a Kenyan garment factory supplying to a European brand may be asked for ISO 9001 as part of a supplier evaluation. The benefits include fewer customer complaints, less waste, and better traceability. However, many small manufacturers fail because they treat it as paperwork. The key is to implement simple systems: document your procedures (even handwritten), train staff on quality checks, and keep records of customer feedback. Start with a gap analysis using free tools like the ISO 9001 checklist from the International Trade Centre. EU labeling rules for food and cosmetics

If you want to sell food or cosmetics in the European Union, your label must follow strict rules. Mutual recognition agreements and their limits

A mutual recognition agreement (MRA) is a formal arrangement between countries or accreditation bodies to accept each other's conformity assessment results â tests, inspections, certifications. In Africa, the African Accreditation Cooperation (AFRAC) promotes MRAs among national accreditation bodies. Also, many African NABs are signatories to the ILAC Mutual Recognition Arrangement (MRA) and the International Accreditation Forum (IAF) MLA. This means a test report from an accredited lab in Kenya (KENAS) is accepted in Europe or Asia, provided the lab's accreditation scope covers the test. MRAs reduce the need for duplicate testing, saving time and money. However, MRAs have limits. First, they only cover accredited bodies. If a lab is not accredited, its results may not be accepted. Second, the MRA only covers the specific scope of accreditation; if the test method is not listed, the report may be rejected. Third, MRAs do not override national regulations. A country may still require its own testing for phytosanitary or food safety reasons, even if an accredited lab elsewhere has tested. For example, the EU requires that certain products be tested for pesticide residues by labs recognized by the EU, not just any accredited lab. Fourth, political and administrative barriers can exist; some customs officials may not understand MRAs and may insist on local testing. Fifth, MRAs are between accreditation bodies, not between countries directly, so they depend on the accreditation system functioning properly. To benefit, always ensure your test report comes from a lab accredited to ISO 17025 for the specific test, and that the accreditation body is a signatory to the relevant MRA. Sampling and testing: what to expect

Sampling is the process of taking a small portion of your product that represents the whole lot. If sampling is done wrong, even the best lab will give a wrong result. For export, sampling must follow a standard method, often specified by the buyer or the destination country. Common sampling standards include ISO 5555 for animal and vegetable fats, ISO 707 for milk and milk products, and ISO 24333 for cereals. For aflatoxin in groundnuts, the standard is often ISO 16050 or the USDA method. The key is to take a representative sample: from different parts of the lot, using a clean tool, and mixing them. For a container of 20 tons of maize, you might take 10-20 incremental samples, combine them, and then quarter down to a final sample of 1-2 kg. The sample must be sealed, labelled with date, time, location, lot number, and signed by all parties. A chain of custody form must accompany the sample to the lab. Testing then follows the method. You should ask the lab which method they use and whether it is accredited. For example, aflatoxin testing might use HPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography) or ELISA. HPLC is more accurate but costs more. Pesticide residue testing often uses GC-MS or LC-MS/MS. Expect turnaround times: rapid tests like ELISA can be done in a day; confirmatory tests take 3-7 days. Appealing a failed test result

A failed test result can be devastating, but you have options. First, don't panic. Review the test report carefully. If you suspect an error, contact the lab immediately and ask for clarification. They may re-check the raw data or re-run the test at no cost if it's their error. If you believe the result is correct but due to a sampling issue, you can request a retest on the retained sample. Most labs keep the sample for a certain period (e.g., 30 days). You will likely have to pay for the retest. If the retest confirms the failure, you need to investigate the cause: was it a batch problem, storage issue, or contamination? You can then decide to rework, reject, or sell to a less demanding market. If you disagree with the lab's result and believe the lab is at fault, you can file a formal appeal. The first step is to write to the lab manager, stating your case and providing evidence. If that fails, you can complain to the lab's accreditation body.
