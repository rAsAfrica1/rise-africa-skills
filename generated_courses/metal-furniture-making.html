Module 2 · Design and Drawing

Introduction and Why It Matters

In metal furniture making, design and drawing are not optional luxuries�??they are the foundation of every profitable job. Without a clear drawing, you waste metal, time, and money. In Africa, where steel and welding rods are costly, a simple sketch can save you thousands of shillings. Imagine a client in Nairobi ordering a set of six dining chairs. If you guess the dimensions, you might cut legs too short or too long, ending up with unstable chairs and an angry customer. But if you draw each part to scale, you calculate exactly how many metres of square tube you need, how many welding rods, and how many hours. That means you can quote a fair price and still make a profit..Trim()

.Trim()

Drawing also helps you communicate with your client. Many customers cannot read technical blueprints, but they understand a simple isometric sketch showing the front, side, and top views. You can mark dimensions in millimetres and point to where the seat will be, how high the backrest goes, and where the armrests attach. This avoids arguments later. In workshops from Accra to Johannesburg, the best welders always start with a pencil and paper. They draw the furniture piece at full size or to a scale like 1:10, using a ruler, set square, and compass. Even a rough drawing on cardboard is better than no drawing..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, design thinking lets you add value. You can modify a standard design to use locally available materials�??like using offcuts from a construction site or adapting to a client�??s small room. A good drawing also serves as a record. If a client wants a second identical chair six months later, you can pull out the drawing and repeat the job exactly. So treat design and drawing as your first tool, not an afterthought. It costs almost nothing but pays you back in saved materials, satisfied customers, and repeat business..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To draw metal furniture designs, you need a few basic tools. Most are cheap and available in any African town. Start with a drawing board or a flat piece of plywood at least 60 cm by 90 cm. You can make one yourself from a 12 mm plywood offcut. Cover it with a smooth sheet of paper taped down. For drawing, use a 2H pencil for fine lines and an HB pencil for darker lines. A pencil sharpener and eraser are essential. A 30 cm steel ruler with millimetre markings is your most important measuring tool. Also get a 60-degree set square and a 45-degree set square to draw perpendicular and angled lines. A compass for circles (like for round table tops) and a protractor for angles are useful..Trim()

.Trim()

For scale drawings, you need a scale ruler or simply use your steel ruler and divide by 10 for a 1:10 scale. For example, 1 metre becomes 10 cm on paper. Use A3 or A2 paper if you can get it; otherwise, tape two A4 sheets together. Graph paper is excellent because it already has a grid�??each square can represent 10 cm. For marking metal before cutting, you will need a scriber (a sharp steel point) or a permanent marker, a centre punch, and a hammer. A try square is used to check 90-degree angles on metal. A chalk line or soapstone is good for marking on dark metal..Trim()

.Trim()

Materials for drawing include pencils, erasers, masking tape, and paper. For the actual furniture, you will draw the metal sections: square tubes (e.g., 25 mm x 25 mm x 1.5 mm wall thickness), rectangular tubes (20 mm x 40 mm), round tubes (e.g., 25 mm diameter), and flat bars (e.g., 25 mm x 3 mm). You also need to know the standard lengths: usually 6 metres. When drawing, always note the material type, size, and length for each part. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to draw a simple metal chair. First, decide the overall dimensions. A standard dining chair is 450 mm wide, 450 mm deep, and 900 mm high at the backrest. Seat height is 450 mm. Write these numbers down. Second, choose a scale. For a chair, 1:10 is good�??so 450 mm becomes 45 mm on paper. Use your ruler to mark a horizontal line for the floor. Third, draw the front view. Start with the two front legs: they are vertical lines 450 mm apart (45 mm on paper). The seat is a horizontal line at 450 mm height. The back legs go up to 900 mm. Add the backrest top rail. Use your set square to keep lines perpendicular. Fourth, draw the side view. Show the depth: front leg to back leg is 450 mm. The seat is at 450 mm. The backrest leans back slightly, maybe 100 mm offset at the top. Fifth, draw the top view (plan). This shows the seat frame: a rectangle 450 mm x 450 mm. Mark where the legs attach at the corners. Now add dimensions: use arrows and numbers. For example, write '450' between the front legs. Sixth, make a parts list. For each part, note the material, size, and length. For example: front legs (2 off) 25x25 square tube, 450 mm long. Back legs (2 off) 25x25 square tube, 900 mm long. Seat rails (4 off) 20x40 rectangular tube, 450 mm long. Finally, transfer the drawing to a clean sheet or cardboard template. You can cut out cardboard pieces to test the fit before cutting metal. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced welders make drawing mistakes. Here are the most common and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Not drawing to scale. If you sketch freehand, proportions will be wrong. Fix: Always use a ruler and a scale. For example, if you draw a chair 10 cm tall on paper but the seat is only 2 cm from the floor, the real chair will have a seat at 200 mm�??too low. Fix by measuring twice. Mistake 2: Forgetting wall thickness. When you draw a 25 mm square tube, the inside is smaller. If you need a sliding fit, account for the 1.5 mm wall. Fix: Draw the outside dimensions and note the wall thickness in the parts list. Mistake 3: Ignoring weld gaps. Metal expands when heated. If you draw parts to butt together perfectly, after welding they may distort. Fix: Leave a 1-2 mm gap for weld penetration, or design with overlapping joints. Mistake 4: Not checking for square. A drawing might look right but if the diagonals of a rectangle are not equal, the frame will be twisted. Fix: Always draw and measure both diagonals�??they must be equal. Mistake 5: Using the wrong pencil. A hard pencil (like 4H) makes faint lines that are hard to see; a soft pencil (like 2B) smudges. Fix: Use 2H for construction lines and HB for final lines. Mistake 6: Forgetting the client�??s space. You draw a table 1.2 m x 0.8 m, but the client�??s doorway is only 0.7 m wide. Fix: Always ask for room dimensions and door widths before drawing. Mistake 7: No allowance for feet or floor unevenness. Fix: Add adjustable feet or design legs with a 10 mm tolerance. Mistake 8: Losing the drawing. Fix: Keep a folder or take a photo with your phone. In Africa, many workshops now use WhatsApp to share drawings with clients for approval before cutting metal. That simple step prevents costly errors..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Safety in design and drawing might seem odd�??after all, you are just sitting at a table. But poor drawings lead to dangerous furniture. A chair that collapses because you forgot to brace the legs can injure someone. So safety starts with the drawing. First, always design for stability. A table with a narrow base will tip over. Rule of thumb: the base should be at least 2/3 the height of the table. For a 750 mm high table, the base should be at least 500 mm wide. Second, avoid sharp edges in your design. Round off corners or specify a radius (e.g., 5 mm) on flat bar ends. Third, consider weight. A metal bed frame that is too heavy is hard to move and can fall on someone. Use thinner wall thickness where possible, like 1.2 mm instead of 2 mm, if strength allows. Fourth, think about fire safety. If you design a braai stand or stove, keep combustible materials away. Draw a heat shield..Trim()

.Trim()

Best practice includes clear, neat drawings. Use a title block with your name, date, scale, and client�??s name. Number each part and list them in a table. Use standard symbols: a circle with a cross for a hole, a zigzag line for a weld. Always draw the front, side, and top views. If the piece is complex, add an isometric view. Check your dimensions twice. Use a scale ruler, not a tape measure, for drawings. Keep your drawing board clean and dry. Store drawings flat, not rolled, to avoid creases. When marking metal, use a scriber and engineer�??s blue (layout fluid) for accuracy. If you don�??t have engineer�??s blue, use a permanent marker. Always wear safety glasses when cutting or grinding, but also when marking metal�??tiny chips can fly. Finally, get a second pair of eyes. Ask a fellow welder to check your drawing for obvious mistakes. In many African workshops, apprentices learn by copying the master�??s drawings. That is good, but always verify with a ruler. A safe design is a profitable design because it prevents accidents, returns, and bad reviews..Trim()

.Trim()

This module teaches you basic welding specifically for furniture. You will learn arc welding (stick welding), which is the most common and affordable method in Africa. It uses a welding machine that runs on 220V single-phase power (or a generator), a welding electrode (rod), and a ground clamp. No gas cylinders are needed, making it ideal for small workshops. You will learn to weld mild steel, which is what most furniture is made from�??square tubes, round tubes, angle iron, and flat bars..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does welding matter for furniture? Because furniture must bear weight, resist shaking, and look neat. A poorly welded joint will crack when someone sits on it, leading to returns, lost customers, and safety risks. A well-welded joint is stronger than the metal itself. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Beginners often make mistakes that weaken furniture or make it look ugly. Here are the most common and how to fix them. First, slag inclusion: this happens when you move too fast or don't clean between passes. Slag (the glassy coating on the weld) gets trapped inside, causing weak spots. Fix: clean thoroughly with a chipping hammer and wire brush after each pass, and slow down your travel speed. Second, porosity: small holes in the weld caused by moisture, dirt, or too long an arc. Finishing is the difference between a metal piece that looks like scrap and one that sells for top price. Step by Step Method
