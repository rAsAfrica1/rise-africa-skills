Module 1 · Introduction to Poultry Nutrition and Feed Formulation

Overview of the Poultry Feed Industry

The poultry feed industry in Africa is growing fast. In Nigeria alone, over 180 million birds are raised each year, and feed accounts for 60-70% of the total cost of production. Most small-scale farmers mix their own feed using maize, soybean meal, and fishmeal, but commercial feed mills produce pelleted feeds with consistent quality. Key players include companies like Chikun, Vital, and Amo Byng in Nigeria, and similar feed manufacturers in Kenya, South Africa, and Ghana. The industry faces challenges: high cost of imported ingredients like soybean meal and vitamins, poor storage leading to aflatoxin contamination, and lack of quality control. For a new entrepreneur, starting a small feed mixing business can be profitable if you source local ingredients like maize, cassava, and groundnut cake. You need a hammer mill, a mixer, and a weighing scale. A typical 50 kg bag of broiler starter feed sells for about 18,000 to 22,000 Naira (2024 prices). To compete, focus on consistent quality and sell directly to farmers. The demand for poultry products is rising due to population growth and urbanisation, so feed production is a viable business. However, you must understand nutrient requirements, ingredient quality, and regulations. Basic Anatomy and Digestive Physiology of Broilers and Layers

Birds have a unique digestive system. Unlike mammals, they have a beak for pecking, a crop for storing feed, a proventriculus (glandular stomach) and a gizzard (muscular stomach) that grinds feed with grit. The small intestine absorbs nutrients, and the ceca ferment fibre. The cloaca expels waste. Broilers and layers have similar anatomy, but layers have a longer digestive tract to handle more fibre. Feed passes through the gut in about 2-4 hours. To optimise digestion, feed particles should be 0.5-1.5 mm for mash, or pelleted for better intake. Grit helps grind grains. Enzymes like phytase break down phytic acid in maize and soybean, releasing phosphorus. Without phytase, birds need inorganic phosphorus, which is expensive. In Africa, many farmers use grit from crushed granite. The pH of the gut is acidic in the proventriculus (pH 2-3) and neutral in the intestines. Understanding this helps you choose ingredients: for example, high-fibre ingredients like rice bran are better for layers than broilers. Also, birds lack teeth, so feed texture matters. A good feed should be palatable and free of mould. If you see undigested feed in droppings, adjust particle size or add enzymes. This knowledge helps you avoid waste and improve feed conversion ratio (FCR). A broiler should convert 1.6-1.8 kg of feed into 1 kg of meat, while layers need 1.8-2.2 kg feed per kg eggs. Poor digestion increases FCR, raising costs..Trim()

Feed Ingredients: Classification and Nutritional Characteristics

Feed ingredients are grouped into energy sources, protein sources, minerals, vitamins, and additives. Energy sources include maize (3350 kcal/kg), sorghum (3200 kcal/kg), cassava chips (3000 kcal/kg), and wheat bran (1300 kcal/kg). Maize is king but costly; you can replace up to 30% with cassava in broiler diets if you add methionine. Protein sources: soybean meal (44-48% CP), groundnut cake (40-45% CP), fishmeal (55-65% CP), and cottonseed cake (32-38% CP) â but cottonseed has gossypol, toxic to layers. Feed Formulation Concepts and Objectives

Feed formulation is the process of mixing ingredients to meet the nutrient requirements of birds at the lowest cost. The main objectives are: 1) meet nutrient needs for health and productivity, 2) minimise cost, 3) use locally available ingredients, and 4) ensure feed is palatable and safe. You start with a nutrient specification table: for broiler starter, ME 3000 kcal/kg, CP 22%, lysine 1.2%, methionine 0.5%, calcium 1.0%, available phosphorus 0.45%. Then list available ingredients with their nutrient composition and cost. Use the Pearson Square method for simple two-ingredient mixes, or linear programming software for complex mixes. For example, to make a 100 kg broiler starter with maize and soybean meal, you can use Pearson Square to find the ratio of maize (8.5% CP) and soybean meal (44% CP) to get 22% CP. But you also need to add minerals, vitamins, and amino acids. A typical formula: maize 55%, soybean meal 30%, fishmeal 5%, wheat bran 5%, limestone 1%, dicalcium phosphate 1.5%, premix 0.5%, salt 0.3%, methionine 0.2%, lysine 0.1%, and oil 1.4%. Always check that the final mix meets all nutrient requirements. Use a computer or smartphone app like Feed Formulation (available on Google Play) to save time. The objective is not just least cost but also least cost per kg of meat or eggs. For layers, you might use more limestone and less protein. Remember, feed formulation is dynamic: ingredient prices change, so adjust formulas regularly. Keep records of your formulas and results. Test your feed on a small batch of birds before scaling up. This reduces risk and ensures you learn from mistakes..Trim()

Regulatory and Safety Considerations in Feed Production

Feed safety is critical. In Africa, many countries have feed regulations: Nigeria's NAFDAC and SON, Kenya's KEBS, and South Africa's NRCS. They set standards for maximum levels of toxins, heavy metals, and pathogens. Aflatoxin is the biggest threat: it causes liver damage and reduces egg production. Maize and groundnut cake are often contaminated. Test with aflatoxin test kits (e.g., ELISA) or send samples to labs. Safe level is below 20 ppb for poultry. Salmonella and E. coli can be controlled by proper storage and heat treatment (pelleting kills many pathogens). Avoid using mouldy ingredients. Also, some ingredients are banned: e.g., meat and bone meal from ruminants to prevent BSE. Antibiotics as growth promoters are banned in many countries; use alternatives like probiotics and organic acids. Label your feed with nutrient content, date, and batch number. Keep records for traceability. For a small business, register with the relevant authority if you sell feed commercially. Even for home mixing, follow good manufacturing practices: clean equipment, use gloves and masks, and store feed in dry, cool places. Train workers on hygiene. In Kenya, the Feed Act requires that feed be free from harmful substances. In Nigeria, NAFDAC registration is needed for commercial feed. Non-compliance can lead to fines or closure. Also, consider environmental safety: dust from mixing can cause respiratory problems. Use a dust mask. Dispose of waste properly. By following regulations, you protect your birds, your customers, and your business reputation. Safe feed leads to healthy birds and better profits..Trim()

Energy Systems: Metabolizable Energy (ME) and Net Energy (NE)

Energy is the most expensive part of any poultry feed, so you must get it right. In Africa, maize is the main energy source, but its quality varies. Metabolizable Energy (ME) is the energy birds can actually use after digestion and losses in urine and gases. Net Energy (NE) goes further, subtracting the energy lost as heat. For practical feed formulation, we use ME because tables are widely available. For broilers, starter feed needs 3000-3100 kcal ME/kg, grower 3100-3200, and finisher 3200-3300. Layers need about 2700-2800 kcal ME/kg. If you use cassava instead of maize, remember cassava has lower ME (about 2800 kcal/kg) and needs added oil or fat to reach target. A simple method: use a feed formulation software like Feedsoft or Excel. Enter ingredient ME values from NRC (1994) or your local feed lab. For example, maize ME = 3350 kcal/kg, soybean meal = 2440 kcal/kg, fishmeal = 2900 kcal/kg. To increase energy, add 1-2% vegetable oil (palm oil or sunflower oil), which gives about 9000 kcal/kg. But too much oil makes feed rancid and sticky. In hot African climates, birds eat less, so you must increase nutrient density. If birds eat 100g/day instead of 120g, you need 20% more energy per kg. Always check feed intake. A quick test: if broilers are not reaching target weight by day 21 (should be around 800-900g), energy may be low. Also, ME is not enough; consider NE for precision, but most African feed mills stick to ME. Remember, energy interacts with protein: too much energy and not enough protein gives fat birds; too little energy and birds eat more, wasting protein. Balance is key. Use local ingredients: maize, millet, sorghum, cassava chips, and by-products like wheat bran (lower ME, about 1300 kcal/kg). Always test your maize for moisture and aflatoxin. Aflatoxin reduces energy use and harms birds. So, buy good maize, dry it to below 14% moisture, and store in dry place. Thatâs how you control energy cost and bird performance..Trim()

Crude Protein vs. Amino Acid Requirements

Crude protein (CP) is a rough measure. It counts all nitrogen, but birds need specific amino acids (AA) to build muscle and eggs. Think of CP as the total bricks, but AA are the different shapes you need. The most critical AA are lysine, methionine, threonine, and tryptophan. For broilers, starter needs 22-23% CP, but more importantly, 1.2% lysine and 0.5% methionine. Layers need 16-18% CP, with 0.8% lysine and 0.35% methionine. In Africa, soybean meal is the gold standard for AA, but itâs expensive. You can use alternatives: groundnut cake (lower lysine), sunflower cake (low lysine, high fibre), fishmeal (excellent but costly), and cottonseed cake (use with caution, gossypol). To meet AA, you must combine ingredients. For example, maize is low in lysine, so you add soybean meal. If you use 60% maize and 25% soybean meal, you get about 18% CP and 0.9% lysine. For layers, add 0.1% synthetic methionine and 0.05% lysine. Synthetic AA are cheap and effective. Always use a least-cost formulation software. If you donât have one, use the Pearson Square method for two ingredients, but it only balances CP, not AA. So, you need AA tables. The NRC (1994) gives AA requirements. For example, broiler 0-3 weeks: lysine 1.10%, methionine 0.50%, methionine+cysteine 0.90%, threonine 0.80%, tryptophan 0.20%. For layers: lysine 0.80%, methionine 0.35%, methionine+cysteine 0.60%. In hot weather, birds eat less, so increase AA density by 5-10%. Also, watch for anti-nutritional factors: raw soybeans have trypsin inhibitors that reduce protein digestibility. Always toast or buy properly processed soybean meal. In Africa, many farmers use fishmeal from local markets; test for salt content (should be <3%) and rancidity. A practical tip: if your birds have poor feathering or cannibalism, it may be methionine deficiency. Add 0.05% methionine. For eggs, if egg size is small, increase methionine and lysine. Macro and Micro Minerals in Poultry Diets

Minerals are needed in small amounts but are critical. Macro minerals: calcium, phosphorus, sodium, chloride, potassium, magnesium. Micro minerals: zinc, manganese, iron, copper, iodine, selenium. In Africa, the biggest problems are calcium and phosphorus. For layers, calcium is key for eggshells. A layer needs 3.5-4.0% calcium. Use limestone or oyster shell. But particle size matters: give 50% as fine powder and 50% as small stones (2-3mm) for slow release. For broilers, calcium needs are lower: starter 1.0%, finisher 0.9%. Phosphorus: available phosphorus (not total) is what matters. Broilers need 0.45% available P, layers 0.35%. Most plant phosphorus is bound as phytate, so you must add phytase enzyme (500 FTU/kg) to release it. Without phytase, add dicalcium phosphate (DCP) or bone meal. But DCP is expensive. Vitamin Requirements and Supplementation

Vitamins are needed in tiny amounts but control many body functions. There are fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and water-soluble vitamins (B-complex, C). In African poultry, deficiencies are common because feed ingredients lose vitamins during storage. For example, maize stored for 6 months loses vitamin E. So, you must add a vitamin premix. For broilers, a standard premix provides per kg feed: vitamin A 10,000-15,000 IU, D3 2,000-3,000 IU, E 30-50 IU, K 2-3 mg, B1 2-3 mg, B2 6-8 mg, B6 3-5 mg, B12 0.01-0.02 mg, niacin 30-50 mg, pantothenic acid 10-15 mg, folic acid 0.5-1 mg, biotin 0.1-0.2 mg. For layers, similar but often higher vitamin D3 (3,000-4,000 IU) for eggshell quality. In hot climates, add vitamin C (100-200 mg/kg) to reduce heat stress. You can buy commercial premixes from companies like DSM, BASF, or local feed stores. Also, watch for interactions: excess vitamin A interferes with D. Excess D is toxic. In Africa, many farmers use liver or fish oil as vitamin source, but these are inconsistent. For example, cod liver oil has A and D, but it can go rancid. Better to use synthetic vitamins. For small-scale farmers, you can make a simple premix: for 100 kg feed, add 100 g of commercial vitamin premix. If you see symptoms like curled toe paralysis (riboflavin deficiency), add extra B2. Water Quality and Its Impact on Nutrition

Water is the most important nutrient. Birds drink 1.5-2 times their feed intake. If water is bad, feed is wasted. In Africa, water sources often have high salts, bacteria, or algae. You must test water at least once a year. Key parameters: pH (6.5-8.5), total dissolved solids (TDS) <1000 ppm, hardness, nitrate <50 ppm, sulfate <250 ppm, chloride <250 ppm, iron <0.3 ppm, manganese <0.05 ppm. High TDS (e.g., 2000 ppm) causes wet litter and poor growth. High sulfate (>500 ppm) causes diarrhea. High iron (>5 ppm) supports bacterial growth and clogs drinkers. High nitrate (>100 ppm) reduces oxygen in blood. In many African farms, borehole water has high iron and manganese. You can treat with chlorination (2-5 ppm chlorine) or use filters. But chlorine kills vitamins in water if you add vitamins via water. So, if you give vitamins in water, do it 2 hours after chlorination or use hydrogen peroxide. Also, water pH affects vaccine and drug efficacy. For example, pH >8 reduces amprolium. A practical method: test water with a simple kit from a pharmacy or agricultural store. If TDS is high, use reverse osmosis or mix with rainwater. Rainwater is soft but can be contaminated. Always cover tanks. Clean drinkers daily with a brush and disinfect weekly. In hot weather, birds drink more, so water quality becomes even more critical. If birds refuse water, check for off-flavors from algae or bacteria. Add 0.5% vinegar (acetic acid) to lower pH and control algae. But donât overdo it. Also, water temperature: birds prefer 10-20Â°C. In hot climates, water in black tanks can reach 40Â°C, reducing intake. So, bury pipes or use white tanks. A good rule: if you wouldnât drink it, donât give it to your birds. Poor water quality reduces feed conversion, increases disease, and costs you money. So, invest in water testing and treatment. Itâs cheaper than medication..Trim()

Using NRC and Commercial Feeding Standards

Feeding standards are tables that tell you how much of each nutrient to include in feed for different bird ages and types. The most common are NRC (National Research Council) and commercial company standards (e.g., Cobb, Ross, Hy-Line). NRC (1994) is the classic reference, but itâs old. Modern genetics have changed requirements. For example, NRC broiler 0-3 weeks: 3200 kcal ME/kg, 23% CP, 1.10% lysine. But Cobb 500 (2022) recommends 3000 kcal ME/kg, 22% CP, 1.20% lysine. So, commercial standards are more up-to-date. In Africa, you can get these manuals from breed company websites or local distributors. Use them as a starting point. But you must adjust for local conditions: heat stress, disease pressure, ingredient quality. For example, in hot climates, reduce CP by 1-2% and increase synthetic amino acids to lower heat production. Also, if your maize is low in energy (e.g., 3200 kcal/kg instead of 3350), you need to add oil. A practical method: take the commercial standard, then use a feed formulation software to match it with your available ingredients. For example, for layers, NRC says 16% CP, 0.8% lysine, but Hy-Line W-36 recommends 16.5% CP, 0.85% lysine for peak production. So, always use the breedâs recommendation. Also, consider feed form: mash, crumble, or pellet. Pelleting improves feed efficiency by 5-10%, so you can reduce nutrient density slightly. But pelleting is expensive. Module 3 · Feed Ingredients and Their Nutritional Profiles

Energy Sources: Corn, Wheat, Barley, Oats, and Fats/Oils

Energy drives growth and egg production. In Africa, maize (corn) is the gold standard, providing about 3,350 kcal/kg metabolizable energy (ME) for poultry. It is highly digestible and low in fibre. However, maize quality varies; aflatoxin contamination is common in humid regions. Always test for aflatoxin using a simple black light or lab test. Wheat has slightly less ME (3,200 kcal/kg) and contains arabinoxylans that make wet droppings. Limit wheat to 20-30% of the diet unless you add enzymes like xylanase. Barley and oats are lower in energy (2,800-2,900 kcal/kg) due to high fibre (beta-glucans). They are better for layers than broilers, and should not exceed 10-15% of the diet. Fats and oils are concentrated energy sources: vegetable oil provides 8,800 kcal/kg ME, while animal tallow gives 7,500 kcal/kg. Adding 2-5% oil improves feed intake and reduces dust. In Africa, palm oil, sunflower oil, and used cooking oil are common. Ensure oils are not rancid; smell them before use. For broilers, aim for 3,000-3,100 kcal/kg ME in starter, 3,100-3,200 in finisher. For layers, 2,700-2,800 kcal/kg is adequate. Always balance energy with protein: too much energy leads to fat birds, too little reduces growth. Use a feed formulation software like Brill or Excel to calculate exact ME. Remember, energy is the most expensive nutrient, so choose sources based on local price per kcal..Trim()

Protein Sources: Soybean Meal, Canola Meal, Fish Meal, and Animal By-Products

Protein builds muscle and eggs. Soybean meal (SBM) is the best plant protein: 44-48% crude protein (CP) and excellent amino acid profile. But raw soybeans contain trypsin inhibitors that stunt growth; always use toasted or solvent-extracted meal. In Africa, SBM is often imported and expensive. Canola meal has 34-36% CP but contains glucosinolates that can cause liver damage; limit to 10-15% for layers and 5-10% for broilers. Fish meal is a premium animal protein: 60-65% CP, rich in lysine and methionine. Use 5-10% in broiler starter and 3-5% in layer diets. Local fish meal from tilapia or sardines works if properly dried and stored away from moisture. Animal by-products include meat and bone meal (45-50% CP), blood meal (80-85% CP), and poultry by-product meal (55-60% CP). They are good sources of essential amino acids but vary in quality. Blood meal is high in lysine but low in methionine. Meat and bone meal can carry bacteria; buy from reputable renderers. For broilers, starter needs 22-24% CP, finisher 18-20%. Layers need 16-18% CP. Always balance amino acids, especially methionine and lysine. Use synthetic methionine (e.g., DL-methionine) at 0.1-0.2% to meet requirements. A simple test for protein quality: mix 1 part feed with 5 parts water, add a few drops of formalin; if it sets like jelly, protein is good. Store all protein meals in dry, cool places to prevent mould..Trim()

Mineral Sources: Limestone, Dicalcium Phosphate, Salt

Minerals are needed for bones, eggshells, and metabolism. Calcium and phosphorus are the main ones. Limestone (calcium carbonate) provides 36-38% calcium. For layers, use 3.5-4.5% limestone in the diet; for broilers, 1-1.5%. Particle size matters: coarse limestone (2-3 mm) stays in the gut longer and improves shell strength. Dicalcium phosphate (DCP) supplies both calcium (22-24%) and phosphorus (18-20%). It is the standard source of available phosphorus. Monocalcium phosphate is similar but more expensive. In Africa, bone meal is a local alternative but must be sterilized. Never use raw bone meal due to disease risk. Salt (sodium chloride) provides sodium and chlorine. Add 0.25-0.5% salt to all poultry diets. Too much salt causes wet droppings and kidney damage; too little reduces feed intake. Other minerals like zinc, manganese, and iron are usually added via premixes. For layers, calcium to phosphorus ratio should be 10:1 to 12:1. For broilers, 2:1 is adequate. A simple test for limestone quality: put a drop of vinegar on it; if it fizzes, it is calcium carbonate. For DCP, check for a greyish-white powder that is free-flowing. Store minerals in sealed bags away from moisture. In coastal regions, salt may already be in water; test your water source before adding full salt. Always mix minerals thoroughly into the feed to avoid toxicity..Trim()

Vitamin Premixes and Additives

Vitamins are needed in tiny amounts but are critical for health, growth, and egg production. A standard poultry vitamin premix contains vitamins A, D3, E, K, B1, B2, B6, B12, niacin, pantothenic acid, folic acid, and biotin. For broilers, add 0.1-0.2% of a commercial premix. For layers, 0.1-0.15%. Without premix, birds may show deficiency signs: curled toe paralysis (riboflavin), rickets (vitamin D3), or poor hatchability (vitamin E). In Africa, buy premixes from reputable suppliers and check expiry dates. Store in a cool, dark place; heat and light destroy vitamins. Additives include enzymes (phytase, xylanase), probiotics, prebiotics, organic acids, and coccidiostats. Phytase (0.01-0.02%) releases phosphorus from plant phytate, reducing DCP needs and saving money. Xylanase (0.01-0.02%) breaks down wheat and barley fibres. Probiotics like Lactobacillus improve gut health. Organic acids (e.g., formic acid at 0.1-0.3%) reduce Salmonella. Coccidiostats (e.g., amprolium) prevent coccidiosis, but follow withdrawal periods. For layers, never use coccidiostats after 16 weeks. Always follow manufacturer instructions. A simple rule: if you cannot afford a premix, at least provide a vitamin-mineral mix for layers to maintain shell quality. Mix additives uniformly by first blending with a small amount of feed, then adding to the main batch..Trim()

Alternative and Novel Ingredients: DDGS, Bakery Meal, Insect Meal

As conventional feed prices rise, African farmers are turning to alternatives. Distiller's dried grains with solubles (DDGS) is a by-product of ethanol production. It contains 25-30% CP and 2,800-2,900 kcal/kg ME. Use up to 10% in broiler diets and 15% in layer diets. DDGS is high in fibre and low in lysine, so adjust with synthetic lysine. Bakery meal is waste bread, biscuits, and cakes. It is high in energy (3,200-3,500 kcal/kg) but low in protein (8-12%). Limit to 10-20% for broilers and 15-25% for layers. Ensure it is free of mould and packaging. Insect meal, especially black soldier fly larvae (BSFL), is a promising protein source: 40-45% CP and good amino acid profile. It can replace 25-50% of soybean meal. To produce BSFL, use organic waste; dry and grind the larvae. Start with 5-10% inclusion and increase gradually. Other options include cassava peels (low protein, high energy), palm kernel meal (15-18% CP, high fibre), and moringa leaves (25-30% CP, but anti-nutritional factors). Always test new ingredients for nutrient content and toxins. Introduce them gradually over 1-2 weeks to avoid digestive upset. For small-scale farmers, insect meal can be produced on-farm using a simple bin system. Remember, alternative ingredients may vary batch to batch, so regular analysis is key..Trim()

Ingredient Quality Assessment and Anti-Nutritional Factors

Poor quality ingredients waste money and harm birds. Start with visual inspection: check for mould, discoloration, insects, and foreign matter. Smell for musty or rancid odours. For grains, test moisture content: safe storage is below 12%. A simple test: put a handful of grain in a sealed jar with a few grains of salt; if salt clumps, moisture is too high. For protein meals, test for protein using the formalin test described earlier. For aflatoxin, use a black light (UV) on ground samples; bright green-yellow fluorescence indicates contamination. Anti-nutritional factors (ANFs) are natural compounds that reduce nutrient absorption. Examples: trypsin inhibitors in raw soybeans (destroyed by heat), glucosinolates in canola (limit inclusion), tannins in sorghum and cassava (bind proteins), phytate in grains (binds phosphorus; use phytase), and gossypol in cottonseed meal (toxic to layers; limit to 5% and add iron). To reduce ANFs: heat treatment (toasting, boiling, autoclaving), soaking, fermentation, or adding enzymes. For cassava peels, sun-dry or ferment to reduce cyanide. For moringa leaves, dry and grind; limit to 5-10% due to saponins. Always buy from trusted suppliers and request a certificate of analysis. If not available, send samples to a local lab. In Africa, many universities and research institutes offer affordable feed testing. Keep records of each batch: date, supplier, visual assessment, and test results. This helps you trace problems and adjust formulations..Trim()

Module 4 · Feed Formulation Techniques and Software

Pearson Square Method for Simple Formulations

The Pearson Square is a simple tool to mix two ingredients to a target nutrient level, like protein. It works well for small-scale farmers who mix feed on-farm. For example, you want a 16% protein layer mash using maize (9% protein) and soybean meal (44% protein). Draw a square. Put the target 16 in the center. At the top left, write maize's 9; at bottom left, soybean's 44. Subtract diagonally: 16 - 9 = 7 parts soybean; 44 - 16 = 28 parts maize. Total parts = 35. So, for 100 kg feed, use (28/35)*100 = 80 kg maize and (7/35)*100 = 20 kg soybean meal. This gives 16% protein. But you must also add minerals, vitamins, and amino acids. For a more balanced mix, include limestone and bone meal. In Africa, maize and soybean meal are common, but prices vary. If soybean meal is expensive, try sunflower cake (28% protein) or cottonseed cake (32% protein). Always check local availability. The Pearson Square only balances one nutrient; for multiple nutrients, use software or trial and error. It's best for simple mixes like a 16% layer mash or 18% broiler starter. Remember to grind ingredients finely and mix thoroughly. A 50 kg bag of this mix might cost: maize at 500 Naira/kg = 40,000 Naira; soybean meal at 800 Naira/kg = 16,000 Naira; plus 2 kg minerals at 300 Naira/kg = 600 Naira. Total = 56,600 Naira for 100 kg, or 566 Naira/kg. That's affordable for many farmers. But if you sell eggs, you need a feed that supports high production. The Pearson Square is a starting point; adjust based on bird performance..Trim()

Linear Programming for Least-Cost Formulation

Linear programming (LP) is a mathematical method to find the cheapest feed mix that meets nutrient requirements. You list all available ingredients, their nutrient content, and their prices. Then you set constraints: minimum protein, maximum fiber, minimum calcium, etc. The LP algorithm calculates the best combination. For example, you have maize (9% protein, 300 Naira/kg), soybean meal (44% protein, 800 Naira/kg), fish meal (60% protein, 1200 Naira/kg), and limestone (0% protein, 100 Naira/kg). You need 18% protein for broiler starter. LP might choose 60% maize, 30% soybean, 5% fish meal, and 5% limestone, giving 18.2% protein and costing 540 Naira/kg. Without LP, you might overuse fish meal and spend more. LP also handles multiple nutrients: energy, lysine, methionine, calcium, phosphorus. You can set maximum inclusion rates for ingredients like cottonseed meal (due to gossypol) or fish meal (due to fishy taste in eggs). In Africa, LP is used by commercial feed mills. Free tools like Excel Solver can do LP. You need a computer or smartphone with a spreadsheet app. Start by building a table: ingredients in rows, nutrients in columns, and price in the last column. Then set target nutrient levels. Excel Solver will find the least-cost mix. It's fast and accurate. But LP assumes you can buy any amount of each ingredient, which may not be true in rural areas. Also, nutrient values vary by batch, so test ingredients. For small farmers, LP might be overkill, but for medium-scale producers, it saves money. A 10% reduction in feed cost can increase profit significantly. Learn LP basics; it's a valuable skill..Trim()

Using Formulation Software: Features and Best Practices

Feed formulation software helps you create least-cost diets quickly. Popular options include Brill Formulation, Feedsoft, and Bestmix. Some are free or low-cost, like WinFeed. These programs have databases of ingredients and their nutrient profiles. You select ingredients, set nutrient requirements, and the software calculates the optimal mix. Key features: nutrient analysis, cost calculation, and reports. For example, you can input local prices for maize, soybean meal, and fish meal, and the software will update the formula. Best practices: 1) Keep your ingredient database updated with local nutrient values. Send samples to a lab for analysis. 2) Set realistic constraints. Don't set protein too high; it wastes money. 3) Use the software's 'what-if' feature to see how price changes affect the formula. 4) Save formulas for different bird ages (starter, grower, finisher). 5) Print or export reports for record-keeping. In Africa, internet may be unreliable, so choose offline software. Many programs run on Windows. If you can't afford software, use Excel templates. For example, a simple Excel sheet with Solver can do linear programming. Training is available online, but you can also learn by doing. Start with a simple mix, then add complexity. Always double-check the software's output: does it make sense? If it suggests 50% fish meal, that's likely too high. Adjust constraints. Also, consider ingredient availability. Software might suggest an ingredient you can't buy. So, set maximum limits based on what you can source. Finally, test the feed with a small batch of birds before scaling up. Software is a tool, not a replacement for observation..Trim()

Setting Nutrient Constraints and Ingredient Limits

When formulating feed, you must set nutrient constraints (minimum and maximum levels) and ingredient limits (maximum inclusion rates). Nutrient constraints ensure birds get what they need. For broilers, starter (0-4 weeks) needs 22% protein, 3000 kcal/kg energy, 1% calcium, 0.5% available phosphorus, 1.2% lysine, 0.5% methionine. Layer mash needs 16-18% protein, 3.8-4.2% calcium, 0.45% available phosphorus. If you set protein too low, birds grow slowly. Too high, it's wasteful and can cause health issues. Nutrient Specifications for Broiler Starter (0-3 weeks)

Broiler starter feed is critical for the first three weeks of a chick's life. During this period, chicks grow very fast and their digestive system is still developing. A good starter feed must be high in protein and energy, and easy to digest. In Africa, common ingredients include maize, soybean meal, fishmeal, and sunflower cake. The typical nutrient specifications for broiler starter are: crude protein 22-24%, metabolizable energy 3000-3100 kcal/kg, crude fiber 3-5%, calcium 0.9-1.0%, available phosphorus 0.45-0.50%, and lysine 1.2-1.3%, methionine 0.5-0.55%. These numbers ensure proper bone development, muscle growth, and overall health. For example, a 100 kg batch of starter feed might include 55 kg maize, 30 kg soybean meal, 5 kg fishmeal, 5 kg wheat bran, 3 kg limestone, 1 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, and 0.5 kg premix. Always use a feed formulation software like Pearson's Square or a simple Excel sheet to balance nutrients. In Africa, where ingredient quality varies, test your ingredients for protein and moisture before mixing. Also, consider adding a coccidiostat to prevent coccidiosis, a common disease in brooding chicks. Remember, starter feed should be in crumble form to encourage eating. Feed and water should be available at all times. Clean feeders and drinkers daily. Monitor chick weight weekly; by 3 weeks, broilers should weigh around 800-900 g. If they are lighter, adjust the feed. Proper starter nutrition sets the stage for a profitable broiler cycle..Trim()

Nutrient Specifications for Broiler Grower (3-6 weeks)

From 3 to 6 weeks, broilers enter the grower phase. Growth is still rapid but the bird's digestive system is more developed, so you can use slightly less expensive ingredients. The goal is to maintain steady growth and prepare the bird for the finisher phase. Nutrient specifications for broiler grower are: crude protein 20-22%, metabolizable energy 3100-3200 kcal/kg, crude fiber 4-6%, calcium 0.8-0.9%, available phosphorus 0.40-0.45%, lysine 1.0-1.1%, and methionine 0.45-0.50%. Use a hammer mill to grind ingredients to a medium texture. Mix thoroughly for at least 10 minutes. Feed grower as mash or pellets. Pelleting improves feed conversion but requires a pellet machine. If you don't have one, mash is fine. Keep feed troughs clean and provide fresh water. At 6 weeks, broilers should weigh about 2.0-2.2 kg. Nutrient Specifications for Broiler Finisher (6+ weeks)

The finisher phase starts from 6 weeks until market weight, usually 7-8 weeks. At this stage, the bird's growth slows down, and feed conversion ratio worsens. The aim is to maximize weight gain while minimizing feed cost. Finisher feed is lower in protein but higher in energy to promote fattening. Formulating Broiler Diets for Optimal Feed Conversion Ratio

Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) is the amount of feed required to produce 1 kg of body weight. A low FCR means better efficiency and profit. To achieve optimal FCR in broilers, you must formulate diets that match the bird's nutrient needs at each stage. Start with accurate nutrient specifications for starter, grower, and finisher. Use a feed formulation software like Feedsoft, Brill, or a simple Excel spreadsheet. In Africa, many farmers use Pearson's Square for two-ingredient mixes, but for complete diets, software is better. First, list available ingredients and their nutrient composition. For example, maize has about 9% protein, soybean meal 44%, fishmeal 60%. Use linear programming to find the cheapest mix that meets all nutrient requirements. Consider constraints like maximum inclusion levels: fishmeal up to 10%, soybean meal up to 30%, limestone up to 2%. Also, account for anti-nutritional factors in ingredients like cottonseed (gossypol) and cassava (cyanide). To improve FCR, add enzymes like phytase (500 FTU/kg) to release phosphorus from plant sources, and xylanase to break down fiber. Probiotics and organic acids improve gut health, reducing mortality and improving absorption. Always mix feed thoroughly; poor mixing leads to nutrient segregation and poor FCR. Use a horizontal mixer or mix on a clean concrete floor with shovels for at least 15 minutes. Store feed in dry, cool place to prevent mold. Moldy feed reduces intake and causes disease. Monitor FCR by recording feed consumed and weekly weights. If FCR is poor, check for feed wastage, disease, or incorrect formulation. In Africa, where feed costs are high, improving FCR by 0.1 can save significant money. For example, if a bird eats 4 kg feed to reach 2.5 kg, FCR is 1.6. Use of Feed Additives in Broiler Diets: Enzymes, Probiotics, Organic Acids

Feed additives are non-nutritive substances added to broiler diets to improve health, digestion, and performance. In Africa, where disease pressure is high and feed quality varies, additives can make a big difference. The three main types are enzymes, probiotics, and organic acids. Enzymes are proteins that break down anti-nutritional factors and improve nutrient availability. Common enzymes include phytase, which releases phosphorus from phytate in maize and soybean meal. Adding 500 FTU of phytase per kg of feed can reduce the need for dicalcium phosphate by 1-1.5 kg per 100 kg feed, saving money. Xylanase and glucanase break down fiber in wheat and barley, improving energy use. Proteases break down proteins. Probiotics are live beneficial bacteria like Lactobacillus and Bacillus subtilis. They colonize the gut, preventing harmful bacteria like Salmonella and E. coli. Add 0.5-1 kg of probiotic per ton of feed. Probiotics improve feed conversion and reduce mortality. Organic acids like citric acid, formic acid, and lactic acid lower gut pH, inhibiting pathogens. They also improve nutrient absorption. Add 2-3 kg per ton of feed. In Africa, many commercial premixes already contain these additives, but you can buy them separately. Always follow manufacturer's instructions for dosage. Overuse can be harmful and costly. Store additives in a cool, dry place. When mixing, add small amounts to a premix first, then blend with the main feed. For example, mix 1 kg probiotic with 10 kg maize, then add to 990 kg feed. This ensures even distribution. Use additives especially during stress periods like vaccination, transport, or heat waves. They are not a substitute for good management, but they boost performance. In a study in Nigeria, broilers fed probiotics had 5% better FCR than controls. So, consider adding them to your formulation..Trim()

Enzymes: Phytase, Xylanase, Protease

Enzymes are proteins that break down feed components your birds cannot digest on their own. In Africa, most feed ingredients like maize, soybean meal, and wheat bran contain anti-nutritional factors that lock up nutrients. Adding the right enzyme unlocks them, saving you money on expensive ingredients like fishmeal and synthetic amino acids..Trim()

.Trim()

Phytase is the most important enzyme for African poultry. Up to 80% of phosphorus in plant seeds is bound as phytic acid, which chickens cannot absorb. Without phytase, you must add expensive dicalcium phosphate and your birds excrete most of the phosphorus, polluting the environment. Add 500 FTU of phytase per kg of feed. This releases about 0.1% available phosphorus, equivalent to 1 kg of dicalcium phosphate per 100 kg feed. It also improves calcium and zinc absorption. For layers, this means stronger eggshells and fewer broken eggs..Trim()

.Trim()

Xylanase breaks down arabinoxylans in wheat, barley, and maize by-products. These fibres form a gel in the gut, slowing digestion and causing wet litter. Adding 1,000-2,000 units of xylanase per kg of feed reduces gut viscosity, improves litter quality, and increases energy availability by 2-3%. In broilers, this can improve feed conversion ratio by 3-5 points. Use xylanase especially when your feed contains more than 20% wheat or wheat bran..Trim()

.Trim()

Protease digests proteins that survive normal digestion, especially in soybean meal and cottonseed meal. .Trim()

Nutrition and Gut Health in Poultry

A healthy gut is the foundation of profitable poultry production. In Africa, where heat, poor storage, and disease pressure are common, gut health often determines whether a flock thrives or barely survives. The gut must digest feed, absorb nutrients, and block pathogens. When it fails, you see wet litter, undigested feed in droppings, and poor feed conversion. .Trim()

Sampling Techniques for Feed Ingredients and Finished Feed

Sampling is the first step in quality control. If you take a bad sample, all lab results will be wrong. For ingredients like maize, soy cake, or fishmeal, use a grain probe or scoop to take small portions from at least 10 different spots in the bag, truck, or silo. Mix these portions in a clean bucket, then quarter down to about 500 grams. For finished feed, sample from the cooler discharge or from bags on the pallet. Take 10 to 20 samples per batch, mix well, and put 500g in a labelled airtight container. Always label with date, ingredient name, supplier, and batch number. In Africa, where humidity is high, avoid sampling during rain. Use a clean, dry scoop. Never sample from the top layer onlyâfeed can separate. For truck deliveries, sample from the top, middle, and bottom of the load. For bagged ingredients, take samples from different layers of the stack. Send samples to a lab within 24 hours, or store in a cool, dry place. A good sample represents the whole lot. If you are testing for aflatoxin, take extra care: sample at least 20 spots and mix thoroughly. Remember, a 500g sample from a 30-ton truck is like a teaspoon from a swimming poolâbut if taken correctly, it tells the truth. Train your workers to use the right tools: a brass probe for bags, a Pelican sampler for trucks, and a riffle splitter for reducing sample size. Always wear gloves and a mask when sampling dusty feed. Keep a record of who sampled, when, and how. This traceability helps if there is a problem later..Trim()

Proximate Analysis: Moisture, Protein, Fat, Fiber, Ash

Proximate analysis is the basic set of tests that tells you what is in your feed. It measures moisture, crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, and ash. Moisture is measured by drying a sample in an oven at 105Â°C for 3 hours. A 2-gram sample is weighed before and after. If moisture is above 12%, feed will go mouldy quickly. In Africa, maize often comes with 14-16% moisture; you must dry it to 12% before storage. Crude protein is measured by the Kjeldahl method: digest the sample with acid, distill, and titrate. It takes about 4 hours. For soy cake, you want 44-48% protein; for maize, 8-9%. Crude fat is measured by extracting with petroleum ether in a Soxhlet apparatus for 6 hours. Fat gives energy; too much can cause rancidity. Crude fiber is the tough part; it is measured by boiling in acid and alkali, then burning. High fiber means low digestibility. Ash is what remains after burning at 550Â°C for 4 hours; it shows mineral content. For fishmeal, ash should be less than 20%; if higher, it may be mixed with sand or bone. These tests are done in a lab, but you can do simple versions on the farm: use a moisture meter for grains, and a simple test kit for protein. Always send samples to a reputable lab. In many African countries, the national bureau of standards or universities offer these tests for a small fee. Record all results in a logbook. Compare with expected values. If protein is low, your birds will grow slowly. If fat is high, feed may go rancid. Proximate analysis is your first line of defence against poor-quality ingredients..Trim()

Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) for Rapid Feed Analysis

Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) is a fast, non-destructive method to analyse feed. It shines near-infrared light on a sample and measures how much light is absorbed. Each ingredient has a unique fingerprint. NIRS can predict moisture, protein, fat, fiber, and even amino acids in less than 2 minutes. In Africa, NIRS machines are becoming more common in large feed mills and research centres. They cost between $20,000 and $50,000, so small mills may not afford one. But you can send samples to a lab that has NIRS. The advantage is speed: you can test every truckload of maize before unloading. For example, if a truck of maize arrives, you take a sample, scan it with NIRS, and within minutes you know if protein is 8% or 10%. That helps you decide whether to accept or reject. NIRS needs calibration: the machine must be trained with hundreds of samples that have been tested by wet chemistry. Without good calibration, results can be wrong. Also, sample preparation matters: grind the sample to a uniform size, and keep it dry. Moisture affects the reading, so NIRS often predicts moisture first and corrects other values. In Nigeria, the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA) uses NIRS to analyse maize and cassava. You can partner with them. For a small mill, a practical approach is to use NIRS for rapid screening, then send 10% of samples to a wet lab for confirmation. This saves time and money. Train one staff member to operate the NIRS and maintain the machine. Keep the machine clean and calibrate regularly. NIRS is not a replacement for good sampling and traditional analysis, but it is a powerful tool for quality control..Trim()

Interpreting Laboratory Results

When you get a lab report, you need to understand what the numbers mean. A typical report shows moisture, protein, fat, fiber, ash, and sometimes amino acids, minerals, and aflatoxin. Start with moisture: if it is above 12%, the feed is too wet. You may need to dry it or use it quickly. For protein, compare with the expected value for that ingredient. For example, soybean meal should be 44-48% protein. If you get 40%, it is either poor quality or mixed with hulls. For maize, 8-9% protein is normal. If you are formulating a broiler starter, you need 22-24% protein in the final feed. If your lab result shows 20%, you must adjust the formula. Fat: for full-fat soy, 18-20% is normal. If fat is high in fishmeal, it may go rancid. Fiber: for maize, 2-3%; for soy hulls, 35%. High fiber in a broiler feed reduces energy. Ash: for fishmeal, 15-20%; for meat and bone meal, 30-40%. If ash is very high, there may be sand or dirt. Aflatoxin: the safe level for poultry feed is below 20 ppb. If above, do not use. Also look at the standard deviation or confidence interval if given. A single result can be misleading; take multiple samples. In Africa, labs may have different methods, so always ask which method they used. For example, protein by Kjeldahl vs. Dumas can differ slightly. Keep a record of all results for each supplier. If a supplier consistently gives low protein, change supplier. Use a spreadsheet to track. If you don't understand a result, call the lab and ask. They can explain. Remember, lab results are only as good as the sample you sent. So follow proper sampling. With practice, you will be able to read a report in 5 minutes and make quick decisions..Trim()

Quality Control Programs in Feed Mills

A quality control (QC) program is a set of rules and checks to ensure your feed is safe and consistent. In a small African feed mill, you can start with simple steps. First, write down your specifications for each ingredient: e.g., maize must have max 12% moisture, min 8% protein; soy cake must have min 44% protein, max 7% fiber. When a truck arrives, take a sample and test moisture with a meter. If it fails, reject the load. Second, check your weighing scale daily with a known weight (e.g., 10 kg). If it is off, fix it. Third, calibrate your mixer: mix a batch with a tracer (e.g., iron filings) and test for uniformity. The coefficient of variation should be less than 10%. Fourth, keep a logbook for every batch: date, ingredients, weights, mixer time, and operator. Fifth, do a weekly check of finished feed: send a sample for protein and moisture. If it is out of spec, adjust. Sixth, train your staff on hygiene: clean equipment, no rodents, no water leaks. Seventh, store ingredients properly: on pallets, away from walls, first-in-first-out. Eighth, have a pest control plan. Ninth, keep a sample of each batch for 3 months in case of complaints. Tenth, review your QC data monthly and look for trends. In Africa, many mills lose money because they don't have QC. A simple program can be set up with a notebook, a moisture meter, and a scale. You don't need a lab on site. Partner with a nearby lab for monthly tests. The key is consistency. If you always check, you will catch problems early. Also, involve your workers: they see the feed every day. Encourage them to report anything unusual. A good QC program builds trust with customers and reduces waste..Trim()

Traceability and Recall Procedures

Traceability means you can follow a batch of feed from raw materials to the customer. Recall is when you remove a bad batch from the market. In Africa, where feed contamination can cause disease or death, traceability is critical. Start by giving every batch a unique code. For example, use the date and a serial number: 20231015-01. Write this code on all bags from that batch. Record what ingredients went into the batch, their supplier, and the batch numbers of those ingredients. Keep this record for at least one year. If a customer complains about sick birds, you can trace back to the batch and the ingredients. If the problem is from a supplier, you can stop using that supplier. If you find a problem in your mill (e.g., aflatoxin), you can recall all bags with that code. To recall, you need a list of customers who received that batch. So keep a sales record: customer name, phone number, quantity, date. If a recall is needed, call each customer immediately. Tell them to stop using the feed and return it. Replace it with good feed. In many African countries, there are no strict laws, but it is your moral duty. Also, if you suspect a serious hazard (e.g., salmonella), report to the authorities. For small mills, a simple notebook works. For larger mills, use a computer. Practice a mock recall once a year: pick a batch, see if you can trace it within 4 hours. If not, improve your records. Also, work with your suppliers: ask them for a certificate of analysis for each delivery. Keep those certificates. If you use a transporter, record the truck number. Traceability also helps with customer trust: you can tell them exactly where their feed came from. In case of a recall, act fast. The longer you wait, the more birds may die. A good traceability system is like insurance: you hope you never need it, but when you do, it saves your business..Trim()
