Why cement blocks are in demand across African towns and villages

Across Africa, millions of families are moving from mud and thatch to permanent homes. Cement blocks are the number one choice because they are strong, fireproof, and can be made locally. In countries like Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, and South Africa, the demand for affordable housing is growing faster than the supply. Basic safety rules for working with cement and heavy blocks

Cement is a dangerous material if you do not respect it. When cement mixes with water, it becomes highly alkaline and can burn your skin. Always wear gloves, boots, and long trousers. Never work with bare hands or bare feet. If cement gets on your skin, wash it off immediately with plenty of water. If it gets in your eyes, rinse with clean water for 15 minutes and seek medical help. Also, wear a dust mask when mixing cement and sand. The dust can damage your lungs over time. A simple cloth mask is better than nothing, but a proper N95 mask is best..Trim()

Heavy blocks can cause serious injuries. A standard 6-inch block weighs about 15 to 20 kilograms. Lifting them repeatedly can hurt your back. Always bend your knees and keep your back straight when lifting. Use a wheelbarrow or a trolley to move blocks instead of carrying them by hand. Do not stack blocks too high; a stack can fall and crush you. Keep stacks stable and on level ground. When loading or unloading, work with a partner. Never stand under a raised load. If you use a machine, keep your hands away from moving parts. Turn off the machine before clearing jams..Trim()

Store cement bags on pallets, not on the floor, to prevent them from getting damp and hard. Keep a first aid kit with bandages, antiseptic, and eye wash. If you have employees, train them on safety rules and provide protective gear. In many countries, there are no strict laws for small yards, but it is your responsibility to keep everyone safe..Trim()

Module 2 · Understanding Cement and Aggregates

Types of cement available in local markets (OPC, PPC, etc.)

When you go to buy cement for making blocks, you will find different types. The most common are OPC (Ordinary Portland Cement) and PPC (Portland Pozzolana Cement). OPC is pure cement and gains strength quickly. It is good for high-strength blocks and for use in areas with a lot of water, like foundations. PPC is mixed with pozzolana (like fly ash or volcanic ash) and is cheaper. It gains strength slower but is more resistant to chemicals and is better for plastering and general block making. In many African markets, you will also see 'masonry cement' or 'rapid hardening cement'. Rapid hardening is more expensive and not needed for blocks. For block making, PPC is often the best choice because it is affordable and works well. But check the label: it should say 'Portland Pozzolana Cement' and have a strength grade like 32.5 or 42.5. The number means the compressive strength in megapascals (MPa) after 28 days. For load-bearing blocks, use 42.5. For non-load-bearing, 32.5 is fine. Also, beware of fake cement. Buy from reputable dealers and look for the manufacturer's logo and a production date. In some countries, cement is sold in 50 kg bags. A bag of OPC typically costs more than PPC. If you are making blocks for a small house, PPC 32.5 is usually sufficient. If you are making blocks for a commercial building, use OPC 42.5. Remember, the type of cement affects how much water you need and how fast the blocks cure. Always follow the manufacturer's recommendations..Trim()

How to check cement quality before buying: lumps, date, and packaging

Before you pay for cement, you must check its quality. Poor cement will make weak blocks that crumble. First, look at the packaging. The bag should be strong, not torn, and printed with the manufacturer's name, cement type, strength grade, and production date. If the date is older than 3 months, the cement may have absorbed moisture and lost strength. Ideally, buy cement that is less than 1 month old. Second, feel the bag. It should be soft and loose, not hard or lumpy. If you squeeze the bag and it feels like a rock, the cement has set. Walk away. Third, open a bag if the seller allows. Good cement should be a fine, smooth powder, like flour. If you see lumps, even small ones, it means moisture got in. You can also do a simple test: take a handful of cement and squeeze it in your fist. When you open your hand, it should fall apart easily. If it stays in a lump, it's bad. Another test: rub a little cement between your fingers. It should feel smooth, not gritty. Gritty cement has sand or dirt mixed in. Also, check for a strong smell. Fresh cement has a slightly earthy smell. If it smells musty or like chemicals, it's old. Finally, buy from a shop that stores cement on pallets, not directly on the floor. The floor can transfer moisture. If the bags are stacked against a wall, the bottom bags may be damp. Ask the seller how long the cement has been in the shop. If it's been there for months, find another supplier. Remember, good cement is the backbone of strong blocks. Don't gamble with bad cement..Trim()

Sourcing sand: river sand vs. pit sand and which is best for blocks

Sand is a key ingredient in blocks. It gives them strength and shape. There are two main types: river sand and pit sand. River sand is found in riverbeds. It has been washed by water, so it is usually clean and free of clay. The grains are round and smooth. This makes it easy to mix with cement and water. River sand is excellent for making blocks because it produces a smooth finish and good workability. However, river sand can be expensive, especially if you are far from a river. Pit sand is dug from the ground. It has sharp, angular grains. This can make stronger blocks because the grains interlock. But pit sand often contains clay and silt, which weaken the block if not washed. Pit sand is usually cheaper and more available. For block making, river sand is generally preferred, but if you use pit sand, you must wash it or test it for clay. In many parts of Africa, river sand is scarce, so pit sand is common. You can also find 'plaster sand' which is fine, and 'concrete sand' which is coarser. For blocks, use concrete sand or a mix of river and pit sand. The best sand for blocks has a mix of particle sizes: some fine, some coarse. This fills voids and makes a dense block. Avoid sand with too much clay. You can test by putting sand in a bottle with water, shaking it, and letting it settle. Clay will form a layer on top. If the clay layer is more than 10% of the total, the sand is not good for blocks unless you wash it. Also, avoid sand with salt (from sea sand) because salt attracts moisture and weakens cement. So, choose river sand if available, but test any sand before using it..Trim()

Testing sand for clay and silt content using a simple bottle test

Clay and silt in sand weaken your blocks. They prevent cement from binding properly. You can test your sand with a simple bottle test. You need a clear glass bottle or jar, water, salt, and a sample of sand. Fill the bottle about one-third with sand. Add water until the bottle is about two-thirds full. Add a teaspoon of salt. Salt helps clay particles settle faster. Shake the bottle vigorously for one minute. Then set it down and wait for 24 hours. The sand will settle at the bottom, silt in the middle, and clay on top. After 24 hours, measure the layers. If the clay layer is more than 10% of the total height, the sand has too much clay. For example, if the total settled material is 10 cm and the clay layer is 1.5 cm, that's 15% clayâtoo much. You can also use a ruler. If the clay is more than 10%, you must wash the sand. To wash sand, put it in a bucket, add water, and stir. Let it settle for a few seconds, then pour off the muddy water. Repeat until the water is clear. Another quick test: put some sand in a jar, add water, and shake. If the water becomes very cloudy and stays cloudy for hours, there is a lot of clay. If it clears quickly, the sand is clean. Also, rub wet sand between your fingers. If it feels slippery and sticky, it has clay. If it feels gritty, it's good. Remember, clay acts like a sponge and sucks up water, so your blocks will need more water and will be weaker. Always test your sand before starting a block-making project. It takes 24 hours but saves you from building with bad materials..Trim()

Choosing the right stone or gravel size for strong blocks

For strong blocks, you need the right size of stone or gravel. In block making, we often use coarse aggregate, which is crushed stone or gravel. The size matters. For solid blocks, the aggregate should be no larger than 20 mm (about the size of a small marble). For hollow blocks, you can use up to 25 mm. If the stones are too big, they create weak spots and the block may crack. If they are too small, you use more cement and the block may be less strong. The best aggregate for blocks is a mix of sizes: from fine dust to 20 mm. This is called 'well-graded' aggregate. It fills voids and makes a dense block. In many African markets, you can buy 'chippings' or 'crushed stone' in different sizes. For blocks, ask for 3/4 inch (19 mm) or 1/2 inch (12.5 mm) chippings. You can also use river gravel, but it should be clean and free of clay. Test the aggregate by washing a sample. If the water is dirty, wash the gravel before use. Also, avoid stones that are flat or elongated; they weaken the block. Round stones are better. If you are making blocks by hand, you can use a mix of 50% 20 mm stones and 50% 10 mm stones. For machine-made blocks, you can use up to 25 mm. Remember, the aggregate should be hard and durable. Do not use soft stones like sandstone or shale. Also, avoid stones with dust or dirt. If you can, sieve the aggregate to remove fines. A good ratio for block making is 1 part cement, 3 parts sand, and 3 parts aggregate by volume. But this can vary. Always test your mix by making a few blocks and checking their strength after 7 and 28 days. If they break easily, adjust the aggregate size or ratio..Trim()

Storing cement and aggregates to prevent moisture damage

Cement and aggregates must be stored properly to keep them dry and clean. Moisture is the enemy of cement. If cement gets wet, it starts to set and becomes useless. So, store cement in a dry, well-ventilated place. Keep it off the floor. Use wooden pallets or a raised platform. Stack bags at least 10 cm away from walls. Do not stack more than 10 bags high, or the bottom bags will compress and harden. Use the oldest cement first. If you buy cement in bags, keep them sealed. Once you open a bag, use it within a few days. If you must store opened cement, put it in an airtight container. For aggregates like sand and gravel, store them on a clean, hard surface, like a concrete slab. Do not store them on bare ground, because dirt and clay can mix in. Cover them with a tarpaulin or plastic sheet to keep off rain. But allow some air circulation to prevent mold. If you store sand in a pile, keep it covered but not sealed tight. Also, separate different sizes of aggregate. Do not mix sand and gravel. If you have space, build a simple storage shed with a roof and open sides. This protects from rain and sun. In humid areas, you can put a moisture absorber like quicklime in the storage area. But keep it away from cement. Check your cement regularly. If you see lumps, use it immediately for non-critical work, like filling, or throw it away. Never use lumpy cement for blocks. For aggregates, check for dirt and clay. If they get dirty, wash them before use. Good storage saves money and ensures strong blocks..Trim()

Module 3 · Block Making Materials and Mix Ratios

Standard mix ratios for common block types

When making blocks, the mix ratio is the amount of cement compared to the amount of sand and aggregate. In Africa, we usually measure by volume using buckets or wheelbarrows. The most common mix for solid load-bearing blocks is 1:5: meaning one part cement to five parts sand. For hollow blocks that are not load-bearing, you can use 1:6 or even 1:7. The ratio changes based on the strength you need. For a 9-inch solid block that will carry heavy walls, use 1:5. For a 6-inch hollow block for a fence, 1:6 is fine. Never go weaker than 1:7 for any block that will be exposed to weather. A common mistake is to add more sand to save cement. That makes weak blocks that crack and crumble. If you are making blocks for a foundation, use 1:4 or 1:5. For a single-storey house wall, 1:5 or 1:6. For a garden wall, 1:6 or 1:7. Remember, the ratio is by volume, not weight. So if you use one bucket of cement, you need five buckets of sand. But sand must be clean and free of clay or roots. If the sand is dirty, wash it or use more cement. In many parts of Africa, people use a wheelbarrow as the measure. One wheelbarrow of cement to five wheelbarrows of sand is too much cement; that is 1:5 but a wheelbarrow is large. Better to use smaller containers like buckets for accuracy. Always mix on a clean concrete floor or metal sheet. Never mix on bare ground because soil will contaminate the mix. Start by measuring sand first, then add cement on top. Mix dry until the colour is uniform. Then add water slowly. The mix should be stiff, not runny. A good test: squeeze a handful; it should hold shape without dripping water. If it drips, too wet. If it crumbles, too dry. Adjust with small amounts. These ratios work for most common blocks in Africa, but always test a few blocks first and let them cure for 7 days before using them in a wall..Trim()

Measuring materials without a weighing scale: using buckets and wheelbarrows

Most block makers in Africa don't have a weighing scale. That's fine. You can measure accurately with buckets, wheelbarrows, or even tins. The key is to use the same container for all measurements. A standard 20-litre bucket is common. For a 1:5 mix, you need 1 bucket of cement to 5 buckets of sand. But cement is often sold in 50kg bags. One 50kg bag of cement is about 33 litres by volume. So if you use a 20-litre bucket, one bag fills about 1.5 buckets. That means for a 1:5 mix, you would need 1.5 buckets of cement to 7.5 buckets of sand. But that's awkward. Better to use a smaller container, like a 10-litre bucket. Then one 50kg bag is about 3.3 buckets of cement. For 1:5, you need 3.3 buckets cement to 16.5 buckets sand. Still not round. Many block makers use a wheelbarrow as the main measure. A standard wheelbarrow holds about 60 litres. One bag of cement is about half a wheelbarrow. So for 1:5, you need half a wheelbarrow of cement to 2.5 wheelbarrows of sand. That's easy: 1 bag cement to 2.5 wheelbarrows sand. But wheelbarrows vary in size. So always check your own wheelbarrow. Fill it with water using a bucket to know its volume. Then you can calculate. Another method: use a wooden box. Make a box 30cm x 30cm x 30cm. That is 27 litres. One bag of cement is about 1.2 boxes. For 1:5, use 1.2 boxes cement to 6 boxes sand. The most important rule: never guess. Always use the same container for cement and sand. If you use a bucket for cement, use the same bucket for sand. Level off each bucket with a straight stick. Don't heap it. A heaped bucket has more material. Also, remember that sand is often wet. Wet sand takes more volume. So if sand is wet, use slightly less sand or add a little more cement. In the rainy season, sand can be soaked. Then you might need to adjust. But for dry sand, the bucket method works well. Practice with a small batch first. Make 10 blocks with your measurements. If they are strong after 7 days, your measurements are good. If they crack, add more cement. This is how you learn without a scale..Trim()

Adding water: how much is too much or too little

Water is the most common mistake in block making. Too much water makes weak, porous blocks that crack and shrink. Too little water makes a crumbly mix that won't hold shape. The right amount of water is about 5 to 7 litres per 50kg bag of cement for a 1:5 mix. But that depends on how wet your sand is. If your sand is dry, you might need 7 litres. If it's already damp, maybe 4 litres. Never just pour water from a hose. Use a bucket or watering can so you can control it. Add water slowly while mixing. The mix should be stiff, like thick porridge, not runny like soup. A good test: pick up a handful and squeeze. It should form a ball that holds together without dripping water. When you open your hand, the ball should stay intact. If water drips out, it's too wet. If it crumbles, it's too dry. Another test: drop the ball from knee height. It should flatten slightly but not splash. For block making, you want a mix that is drier than for concrete slabs. Blocks need to hold their shape immediately after pressing. If the mix is too wet, the block will sag and lose shape. If it's too dry, the block will have holes and weak edges. In hot African climates, water evaporates fast. So mix only as much as you can use in 30 minutes. If you leave the mix sitting, it will dry out. You can add a little water and remix, but never add water to a mix that has already started to set. That is dangerous. Also, use clean water. Dirty water with oil, salt, or organic matter will weaken the cement. In many rural areas, people use water from rivers or wells. That's fine if it's clear. But if it's muddy, let it settle and use the clear part. Never use seawater for reinforced blocks because salt rusts steel, but for plain blocks, seawater can be used in small amounts, though it may cause efflorescence (white stains). Best to use fresh water. Remember, the goal is a mix that is workable but stiff. Practice with small batches. If you can't shape a block that stands on its own without slumping, it's too wet. If the block crumbles when you touch it, it's too dry. Adjust by adding small amounts of water or dry cement and sand..Trim()

Using admixtures and waterproofers in rainy seasons

In the rainy season, block making becomes difficult. Too much moisture in the air and in the sand can ruin your blocks. Admixtures are chemicals you add to the mix to improve properties. Waterproofers are a type of admixture that makes blocks repel water. In Africa, you can buy liquid waterproofers at hardware stores. Common brands include Sika, Fosroc, or local equivalents. They are added at about 0.5 to 1 litre per 50kg bag of cement. For rainy season, a waterproofer helps blocks resist water penetration, reducing dampness and efflorescence. Another useful admixture is a plasticiser, which makes the mix more workable with less water. That means you can use a stiffer mix without adding too much water. This is great for block making because stiff mixes produce stronger blocks. A plasticiser is added at about 0.2 to 0.5 litres per bag. If you can't afford admixtures, you can use alternative methods. For example, add a small amount of lime (about 5% of cement weight) to improve workability and water resistance. Or use wood ash, but that reduces strength. In the rainy season, your sand will be wet. That means you must reduce the water you add. Test the sand first: squeeze a handful. If it drips, it's too wet. Spread it out to dry for a few hours before mixing. Also, cover your blocks with plastic sheets or tarpaulins after casting. Rain can erode fresh blocks. Cure them under cover for at least 7 days. If you must make blocks in the rain, work under a shed. Use a waterproofer to help the blocks shed water. But remember, admixtures are not a substitute for good mix ratios. You still need the right cement-to-sand ratio. Also, never mix admixtures with bare hands; use gloves. Store them away from children. In the dry season, you may not need waterproofers. But in the rainy season, they are worth the extra cost. They can reduce block breakage and improve durability. If you are making blocks for a foundation in a wet area, always use a waterproofer. It will save you money in the long run..Trim()

Mixing small batches by hand on a clean surface

For small-scale block making, you don't need a mixer. You can mix by hand on a clean, flat surface. This is how most blocks are made in Africa. First, choose a clean concrete floor or a metal sheet. Never mix on bare ground because soil will get into the mix and weaken it. If you must mix on ground, first lay a tarpaulin or plastic sheet. The surface should be hard and smooth. Start by measuring your sand. For a 1:5 mix, measure 5 buckets of sand. Spread it in a ring or a flat layer. Then measure 1 bucket of cement. Pour the cement on top of the sand. Now, using a shovel or hoe, mix the dry materials. Stand on one side and pull the sand and cement towards you, turning it over. Keep turning until the colour is uniform. There should be no streaks of brown sand or grey cement. This takes about 3 to 5 minutes. Next, make a crater in the middle of the dry mix. Pour water into the crater. Add water slowly. Start with about 4 litres for a 1:5 mix using one bag of cement. Then use your shovel to fold the dry mix into the water. Keep turning and folding. Add more water if needed, but a little at a time. The mix should become stiff but uniform. If you add too much water, you cannot remove it. So be careful. Once the mix is ready, use it within 30 minutes. If it starts to dry, you can add a tiny bit of water and remix, but never add water to a mix that has begun to set. After mixing, clean your surface immediately. Dried cement is hard to remove. For small batches, you can mix with a hoe in a wheelbarrow. That is even easier. Put sand and cement in the wheelbarrow, mix dry, then add water and mix with a hoe. This is good for making 10 to 20 blocks at a time. Always wear gloves and a dust mask when handling cement. Cement dust is harmful to your lungs. Also, work in a shaded area if possible. The sun can dry the mix too fast. With practice, you can mix a batch in 10 minutes. Remember, consistency is key. Use the same method every time so your blocks are uniform..Trim()

Calculating how many blocks one bag of cement can make

One of the most common questions is: how many blocks can I make with one bag of cement? The answer depends on the block size, mix ratio, and how much sand you use. Let's take a standard 9-inch solid block (450mm x 225mm x 225mm). Its volume is about 0.0228 cubic metres. For a 1:5 mix, the total volume of cement and sand needed for one block is about 0.0228 cubic metres. But cement is only 1/6 of that volume (since 1 part cement to 5 parts sand = 6 parts total). So cement volume per block = 0.0228 / 6 = 0.0038 cubic metres. One 50kg bag of cement has a volume of about 0.033 cubic metres (33 litres). So number of blocks per bag = 0.033 / 0.0038 = about 8.7 blocks. So you get about 8 to 9 solid 9-inch blocks per bag of cement. For a 6-inch hollow block (400mm x 200mm x 150mm), the volume is about 0.012 cubic metres. But hollow blocks have voids, so the actual material volume is less. Typically, a 6-inch hollow block uses about 0.008 cubic metres of mix. For 1:6 mix, cement volume per block = 0.008 / 7 = 0.00114 cubic metres. Blocks per bag = 0.033 / 0.00114 = about 29 blocks. So one bag can make about 25 to 30 hollow 6-inch blocks. For a 4-inch solid block (400mm x 200mm x 100mm), volume = 0.008 cubic metres. For 1:5 mix, cement volume = 0.008 / 6 = 0.00133 cubic metres. Blocks per bag = 0.033 / 0.00133 = about 25 blocks. So one bag makes about 25 solid 4-inch blocks. These numbers are estimates. Your actual yield depends on how well you measure and how much waste you have. Also, if you use a richer mix (1:4), you get fewer blocks per bag. If you use a leaner mix (1:7), you get more blocks but they are weaker. Always test a small batch to find your actual yield. For example, make 10 blocks and see how much cement you used. Then calculate. Keep a record. In Africa, many block makers sell blocks by the bag of cement. They know that one bag makes about 8 solid 9-inch blocks. So they price accordingly. If you are making blocks for a project, calculate the total number of blocks needed, then divide by the yield per bag to know how many bags to buy. Always add 5% extra for waste. Also, remember that the sand quality affects yield. Dirty sand needs more cement. So always use clean sand..Trim()

Checking block dimensions and shape consistency

Every block you produce must meet the required size for your market. In most African countries, the standard block size is 450mm long, 225mm wide, and 225mm high for a 9-inch block, or 450mm x 150mm x 225mm for a 6-inch block. Use a steel tape measure or a wooden gauge stick marked with the correct length, width, and height. Measure at least 10 blocks from each batch. If more than 2 blocks are off by more than 3mm, your mould is worn or your mix is too wet or too dry. Check the corners: they should be sharp and square. Use a carpenter's square to test the 90-degree angles. If corners are rounded, your mould needs repair or you are not compacting properly. Also check that the top and bottom faces are flat. Place the block on a flat concrete surface; if it rocks, the block is warped. Warped blocks cause uneven wall thickness and weak joints. For shape consistency, stack 5 blocks on top of each other. If the stack leans or wobbles, the blocks are not uniform. Record all measurements in a notebook. A simple wooden caliper made from two pieces of plywood can help you check width quickly. Remember, customers will reject blocks that are too small or too large because they waste cement or create thick mortar joints. Consistency saves you money and builds trust..Trim()

Identifying cracks, honeycombing, and weak spots

After demoulding, inspect each block for defects. Cracks are the most common problem. Hairline cracks on the surface are usually from rapid drying; they are not always weak, but they look bad and can grow. Structural cracks go deeper and often appear at corners or along the middle. If you can insert a thin wire into a crack, the block is weak. Honeycombing looks like small holes or a rough, pitted surface where the cement did not fill the spaces between aggregates. This happens when the mix is too dry or not compacted enough. Run your hand over the block: if it feels rough like a sponge, it has honeycombing. Weak spots can also be soft patches that crumble when you rub them. To test, scratch the surface with a nail. If it leaves a deep groove, the block is weak. Another sign is a dull sound when you tap two blocks together; good blocks ring slightly. Also check for efflorescenceâwhite powder on the surfaceâwhich means salts are present and can indicate poor curing. In Africa, where aggregates like river sand or quarry dust vary, always check for clay lumps or organic matter that create weak spots. Remove any block with visible cracks, honeycombing, or soft spots. Do not sell them as first-grade; you can break them and reuse as aggregate if clean. Keep a reject pile and count how many you throw away. If more than 5% of a batch is defective, adjust your mix or compaction..Trim()

Simple field tests for compressive strength

You cannot build a laboratory in your yard, but you can do simple field tests to estimate compressive strength. The most common is the drop test: lift a block 1 metre high and drop it onto a hard concrete surface. If it shatters into many pieces, it is weak (likely below 2.5 MPa). If it breaks into 2 or 3 large pieces, it is medium strength (around 3.5 MPa). If it barely cracks or stays intact, it is strong (above 5 MPa). Another test is the scratch test: use a screwdriver or a sharp nail to scratch the surface. If you can easily carve a groove deeper than 2mm, the block is too soft. For a more reliable estimate, use the 'brick hammer' test: hit the block with a hammer. A good block gives a clear ring and resists breaking. A weak block sounds dull and breaks easily. You can also do a water absorption test: weigh a dry block, soak it in water for 24 hours, then weigh again. If it absorbs more than 15% of its weight, it is porous and likely weak. For a rough compressive strength number, use a bathroom scale and a hydraulic car jack. Place the block between the jack and a strong frame, and pump until it cracks. Read the scale. Divide the load by the block's surface area (length x width). For example, if a 450mm x 225mm block cracks at 5000 kg, that is 5000 kg / (0.45m x 0.225m) = 49,383 kg/mÂ², which is about 0.5 MPaâtoo weak. Aim for at least 3.5 MPa (3500 kg on a 450x225 block). Always test at least 3 blocks per batch..Trim()

A quality log is a simple notebook where you record every batch you produce. It helps you trace problems and prove quality to customers. Start a new page for each day or each batch. Write the date, batch number, and the mix ratio you used (e.g., 1 part cement to 6 parts sand to 8 parts stone). Record the amount of water added in litres. Note the type and source of cement, sand, and aggregates. After demoulding, inspect 10 blocks and write down how many are good, how many have cracks, and how many are rejected. Adjusting mix ratios when blocks fail tests

When blocks fail tests, you must adjust your mix. The most common reason for failure is too much sand or too little cement. A standard mix for strong blocks is 1 part cement to 4 parts sand to 6 parts stone (1:4:6) for a 9-inch block, or 1:5:7 for a 6-inch block. If your blocks are weak (shatter on drop test), increase cement by 10-15%. For example, if you used 1 bag of cement (50kg) for 20 blocks, use 1.2 bags for the same number. If blocks are crumbly or honeycombed, you may have too little water or poor compaction. Add water slowly until the mix holds together when squeezed but does not drip. If blocks crack during curing, you are drying them too fast. Cover them with plastic or wet burlap for at least 7 days. If blocks are too porous (high water absorption), reduce sand and add more cement or use a plasticiser. In Africa, sand often contains clay or silt. Do a simple silt test: put 50mm of sand in a clear bottle, add water, shake, and let settle. If the silt layer is more than 10% of the total, wash the sand or use less of it. If you cannot get stone, use a 1:6 mix (cement:sand) but only for non-load-bearing walls. Always test a small batch of 5 blocks with the new mix before producing a full batch. Record the change in your quality log. Adjust graduallyâdo not double the cement at once. After 7 days of curing, retest. Module 10 · Scaling Up and Improving Production

When to buy a motorised block-making machine

If you are still using manual moulds and a hand tamper, you can produce about 40 to 60 blocks per day with two workers. That is fine for local orders, but when you start getting orders for 500 blocks or more per week, manual production becomes too slow and too expensive in labour. A motorised block-making machine can produce 400 to 800 blocks per day, depending on the model. The cost of a good used or new machine ranges from $2,000 to $8,000 in most African cities. Before buying, calculate your current daily output and your average weekly orders. If you regularly turn down orders because you cannot meet deadlines, it is time to buy a machine..Trim()

.Trim()

Also consider the cost of labour. With manual production, you might need 4 workers to make 200 blocks per day. With a motorised machine, 2 workers can make 600 blocks per day. If you pay each worker $5 per day, you save $10 per day in labour, which adds up to $300 per month. That savings can help pay back the machine over time. But you must also factor in the cost of electricity or fuel to run the machine. A diesel-powered machine may cost $10 to $15 per day in fuel, while an electric one may cost $5 to $8 per day. Check if your site has reliable electricity or if you need a generator..Trim()

.Trim()

Before you buy, visit other block makers who use machines. Ask them about maintenance, spare parts, and breakdowns. Some machines require frequent replacement of moulds or vibrator motors. Also, make sure you have enough space to store the raw materials and the finished blocks. A machine needs a concrete slab to sit on, and you need a curing area. If you don't have a proper curing area, your production will still be limited. Finally, consider financing. You can save up, get a loan, or lease a machine. Some suppliers offer hire-purchase agreements. Only buy when you have a steady stream of orders and can afford the down payment and monthly costs..Trim()

Increasing output with better moulds and curing racks

Even without a motorised machine, you can increase your output by using better moulds and curing racks. Most manual block makers use single moulds, which means you can only make one block at a time. By switching to multiple mouldsâfor example, a mould that makes 4 or 6 blocks at onceâyou can double or triple your output with the same effort. These multi-block moulds are made of steel and cost between $100 and $300. They are available from local welders or from suppliers in larger cities. When using a multi-block mould, you need to ensure the mix is consistent and you tamp each block evenly. It takes practice, but after a few days, your team will be faster..Trim()

.Trim()

Another way to boost output is to improve your curing method. Many small producers cure blocks on the ground, which means you have to wait for them to harden before you can use the space for the next batch. By building curing racksâsimple wooden or metal shelvesâyou can stack blocks vertically and cure more blocks in the same area. A curing rack can hold 200 to 300 blocks in the space that would normally hold 50 on the ground. This allows you to keep producing while earlier blocks cure. You can build racks yourself using timber or metal offcuts. The cost is low, maybe $50 to $100 for materials..Trim()

.Trim()

Also, consider using a vibrating table. A small vibrating table costs around $300 to $500 and helps compact the concrete better, so blocks are stronger and you can demould them sooner. This means you can reuse moulds faster. With a vibrating table and multi-moulds, a two-person team can produce 150 to 200 blocks per day manually. Remember to keep the curing area shaded and moist. Cover blocks with plastic sheets or wet sacks for the first 7 days. This prevents cracks and makes stronger blocks. Better moulds and racks are low-cost investments that pay off quickly..Trim()

Adding a cement mixer to save time and labour

Mixing cement, sand, and water by hand is hard work and takes time. If you are producing more than 100 blocks per day, you should consider buying a cement mixer. A small diesel or electric mixer with a capacity of 0.5 to 1 cubic metre costs between $500 and $1,500. It can mix a batch in 5 to 10 minutes, compared to 20 to 30 minutes by hand. That means you can produce more blocks in a day with the same number of workers. For example, if you currently spend 3 hours mixing for 100 blocks, a mixer can reduce that to 1 hour. You can use the saved time to make more blocks or to improve quality..Trim()

.Trim()

A mixer also gives a more consistent mix, which means stronger blocks and less waste. When mixing by hand, it's easy to add too much or too little water or cement. With a mixer, you can measure materials accurately and get the same quality every time. This reduces breakages and reject rates. If you are selling blocks, customers will notice the quality and come back. Also, a mixer reduces the physical strain on your workers, so they can work longer without getting tired..Trim()

As your production grows, you will likely outgrow your current site. You need space for raw materials (sand, cement, gravel), a mixing area, a production area, a curing area, and storage for finished products. A small backyard may not be enough. Look for a site of at least 500 square metres, preferably with good access for trucks to deliver materials and pick up orders. Sites on the outskirts of town are often cheaper. Another option is to have multiple small sites closer to customers. For example, you could keep your main production site on the outskirts and open a small sales yard in town. Proper planning will ensure that scaling up doesn't reduce your profitability..Trim()

Applying for small business loans or grants to grow

To scale up, you may need extra money for a machine, a mixer, a new site, or more raw materials. There are several ways to get funding. Separating business money from personal money

When you mix business money with personal money, you never know if your block business is making a profit or losing money. .Trim()

Next, pay yourself a fixed salary from the business. For example, if your block business earns 500,000 shillings per month, you might pay yourself 150,000 shillings as salary. .Trim()

Keep all business receipts and records in one place. Use a notebook or a simple app like a spreadsheet on your phone. Every time you spend business money, write it down. .Trim()
