Types of Waste: Municipal, Industrial, Electronic, Organic

Waste is not just 'garbage'âit comes in distinct streams, each with its own source, composition, and handling needs. Municipal solid waste (MSW) is what households, schools, and markets throw away daily. In African cities, MSW is often 50-60% organic matter (food scraps, leaves), 10-15% plastics, 5-10% paper, and the rest metals, glass, and textiles. Industrial waste comes from factories, workshops, and construction sites: scrap metal, sawdust, chemical sludge, and offcuts. Electronic waste (e-waste) includes old phones, computers, TVs, and batteriesâgrowing fast as Africa's tech use rises. Organic waste is biodegradable material from kitchens, gardens, and farms. Knowing these differences matters because each stream needs a different recycling method. For example, you can compost organic waste in 30-60 days, but plastics need sorting by resin type before processing. Start by observing your own community: visit a local dump, a market, and a repair shop. Count what you see. Material Identification: Plastics, Metals, Glass, Paper

To recycle, you must first identify materials correctly. Plastics are marked with a resin code (1-7) inside a triangle. PET (1) is clear and used for soda bottles; HDPE (2) is opaque and used for milk jugs; PVC (3) is rigid and used for pipes; LDPE (4) is flexible and used for bags; PP (5) is tough and used for buckets; PS (6) is brittle and used for disposable cups; and 'other' (7) includes mixed plastics. A simple test: squeeze the plasticâif it cracks easily, it's likely PS (6); if it bends, it's PP (5) or LDPE (4). Metals: use a magnet. Steel and iron stick; aluminium, copper, and brass do not. Aluminium cans are lightweight and shiny; copper wires are reddish and heavy. Glass: check colour and thickness. Clear, green, and brown glass are common. Glass is 100% recyclable infinitely, but mixed colours lower value. Paper: tear it. Long fibres (like in cardboard) are stronger; short fibres (like newspaper) are weaker. Office paper is white and high-grade; newspaper is grey and low-grade. In Africa, informal collectors often sort by handâtrain your eye and hands to recognise these quickly. A good tool is a simple sorting chart with pictures, laminated and hung at your collection point..Trim()

Waste Audits: How to Conduct a Business Waste Assessment

A waste audit tells you exactly what your business throws away, how much, and where it goes. It takes one day and costs almost nothing. Step 1: Pick a typical dayâavoid holidays. Step 2: Collect all waste from bins in labelled bags (e.g., 'office', 'kitchen', 'workshop'). Step 3: Weigh each bag using a hanging scale (accurate to 0.1 kg). Step 4: Open each bag on a tarp and sort into categories: organic, plastics, paper, metals, glass, e-waste, hazardous, and other. Step 5: Weigh each category separately. Step 6: Record everything in a table. For example, a small restaurant might find: 40 kg organic, 5 kg plastics, 3 kg paper, 2 kg glass, 1 kg metals per day. That's 51 kg total. Step 7: Calculate percentages. If organic is 78%, composting could cut waste by more than three-quarters. Step 8: Identify recyclables with market value. In Nairobi, PET bottles fetch 20-30 KES per kg; scrap metal 30-50 KES per kg. Step 9: Set a targetâe.g., reduce landfilled waste by 50% in three months. Repeat the audit monthly to track progress. Hazardous Waste: Identification and Safe Handling

Hazardous waste can harm people, animals, and the environment. Common hazardous items in Africa include: batteries (lead-acid, lithium), fluorescent tubes (mercury), paints and solvents, pesticides, medical waste (syringes, expired drugs), and electronic components (circuit boards with heavy metals). How to identify? Look for warning symbols: skull and crossbones (toxic), flame (flammable), corrosion symbol (acid), and exclamation mark (irritant). If unsure, treat it as hazardous. Safe handling rules: 1) Never mix hazardous with regular waste. 2) Wear gloves, goggles, and a mask. 3) Store in sealed, labelled containersânever in food or drink bottles. 4) Keep away from water sources and children. 5) For batteries, tape terminals to prevent sparks. 6) For medical waste, use sharp-safe boxes and incinerate or autoclave if available. 7) For e-waste, do not burn cables to recover copperâthis releases toxic fumes. 8) Contact local authorities or specialised recyclers. Composting Organic Waste: Methods and Benefits

Composting turns food scraps and garden waste into nutrient-rich soil. It reduces waste by up to 60% and saves money on fertiliser. Method 1: Pit composting. Dig a pit 1m deep, 1m wide, 1m long. Add alternating layers of 'greens' (kitchen scraps, fresh leaves) and 'browns' (dry leaves, cardboard, sawdust) in a 1:2 ratio. Keep moist like a wrung-out sponge. Turn every 2 weeks. Ready in 2-3 months. Method 2: Heap composting. Build a pile 1.5m high on a shaded spot. Same layering. Cover with a tarp to retain moisture. Turn weekly. Ready in 6-8 weeks. Method 3: Vermicomposting. Use a bin with red wiggler worms. Feed them fruit and vegetable scraps (no meat, dairy, or citrus). Worms produce castings in 4-6 weeks. Great for small spaces. Benefits: compost improves soil water retention by 20-30%, increases crop yields by 20-50%, and reduces methane from landfills. In Africa, composting is low-cost: a family can compost 5-10 kg of waste per week. Use the compost for urban gardens, tree nurseries, or sell itâa 50 kg bag can fetch 500-1000 KES. Avoid composting meat, bones, oily foods, and diseased plants. Keep the pile aerated to prevent smell. Composting is a simple business: collect organic waste from markets, compost, and sell to farmers..Trim()
