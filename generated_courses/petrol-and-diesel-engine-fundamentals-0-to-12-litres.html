The Four-Stroke Cycle Explained

The four-stroke cycle, invented by Nikolaus Otto, has four piston movements: intake, compression, power, and exhaust. One cycle takes two crankshaft revolutions (720 degrees). In the intake stroke, the piston moves down from TDC to BDC, the intake valve opens, and air (diesel) or air-fuel mixture (petrol) is drawn into the cylinder. In the compression stroke, both valves close, and the piston moves up, compressing the charge. Petrol engines compress to about 10:1; diesel engines to about 20:1. Near TDC, petrol engines fire the spark plug; diesel engines inject fuel, which ignites from the heat of compression..Trim()

.Trim()

In the power stroke, the expanding gases push the piston down, turning the crankshaft. This is the only stroke that produces power. In the exhaust stroke, the exhaust valve opens, and the piston moves up, pushing out burned gases. Then the cycle repeats. For a 4-cylinder engine, the power strokes are staggered so that one cylinder fires every 180 degrees of crankshaft rotation. This gives smooth power. Firing order for a 4-cylinder is typically 1-3-4-2. For a 6-cylinder, it is 1-5-3-6-2-4..Trim()

Valve timing is critical. The intake valve opens slightly before TDC (10-20 degrees) and closes after BDC (40-60 degrees). The exhaust valve opens before BDC (40-60 degrees) and closes after TDC (10-20 degrees). This overlap helps scavenge exhaust and fill the cylinder. In African workshops, you can check valve timing by aligning marks on the crankshaft and camshaft pulleys. For a Toyota 2Y engine, align the crankshaft pulley mark with the '0' on the timing cover, and the camshaft sprocket mark with the mark on the head. If off by one tooth, the engine will run poorly or not at all..Trim()

The Two-Stroke Cycle Explained

The two-stroke cycle completes in one crankshaft revolution (360 degrees). It has three phases: intake and compression (piston moving up), power (piston moving down), and exhaust and transfer (piston near BDC). Unlike four-stroke engines, two-strokes use ports in the cylinder wall instead of valves. The piston itself acts as a valve, opening and closing the intake, exhaust, and transfer ports. A two-stroke engine fires every revolution, so it produces twice as many power strokes as a four-stroke of the same size. This gives high power-to-weight ratio, which is why two-strokes power chainsaws, leaf blowers, and small motorcycles (50cc to 250cc) across Africa..Trim()

In a typical two-stroke petrol engine, the air-fuel mixture enters the crankcase through the intake port as the piston moves up. The mixture is compressed slightly in the crankcase. As the piston moves down, it compresses the mixture in the cylinder. At the same time, the falling piston uncovers the exhaust port, letting burned gases escape. Then the transfer port opens, and the compressed mixture from the crankcase flows into the cylinder, pushing out remaining exhaust. The spark plug fires near TDC, and the power stroke begins. This is called loop scavenging. Diesel two-strokes use a blower to force air in and have exhaust valves, but they are rare in small sizes..Trim()

Engine workshops contain hazards: moving parts, hot surfaces, toxic fumes, heavy components, and electrical systems. Before starting any job, put on personal protective equipment (PPE): safety glasses, steel-toe boots, gloves, and ear protection when using power tools. Never wear loose clothing or jewellery near a running engine. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO2) within reach. In Africa, many workshops are informal; you must create your own safety rules. Always disconnect the battery before working on the electrical system or fuel system. For petrol engines, relieve fuel pressure at the fuel rail before opening lines. Thermodynamics of Combustion

When you burn petrol or diesel in an engine, you are turning chemical energy into heat, and then heat into motion. This is thermodynamics. In a petrol engine, the spark plug ignites a mixture of air and fuel. The burning gas expands rapidly, pushing the piston down. That push is what turns the crankshaft. In a diesel engine, the air is squeezed so hard it becomes hot enough to ignite the diesel when it is injected. No spark plug needed. The key numbers: petrol has a stoichiometric ratio of about 14.7 parts air to 1 part fuel by weight. Diesel is about 14.5:1. But diesel engines run leanâoften 18:1 to 70:1âbecause they control power by how much fuel is injected, not by throttling air. Petrol engines need a throttle to control air, so they always try to stay near 14.7:1 for clean burning. In Africa, where fuel quality varies, running slightly rich (12.5:1 to 13:1) can help cool the engine and prevent knock, but it wastes fuel and fouls plugs. The three laws of thermodynamics matter: energy cannot be created or destroyed; heat always flows from hot to cold; and you can never get 100% efficiency. A typical petrol engine is about 25-30% efficient. Diesel is 30-40%. The rest is lost as heat out the exhaust and radiator. To feel this, touch the exhaust manifold after a short runâit will be hundreds of degrees. That heat came from your fuel. Mechanics in Nairobi or Lagos often see engines overheating because the cooling system cannot remove that heat fast enough. Understanding this helps you diagnose: if an engine runs hot, check ignition timing, fuel mixture, and coolant flow. If it runs cold, check the thermostat. Combustion is not magicâit is controlled burning that you can measure with a timing light, a compression gauge, and a exhaust gas analyser..Trim()

Air-Fuel Mixtures and Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry is the exact ratio of air to fuel that burns completely with nothing left over. For petrol, that is 14.7 kilograms of air to 1 kilogram of fuel. For diesel, it is about 14.5:1. But engines rarely run at exactly that ratio all the time. Petrol engines use a throttle plate to control how much air enters. The engine control unit (ECU) then adds fuel to match. At idle, you might have 14.7:1. At full throttle, you might go rich to 12.5:1 for more power. At cruise, you might go lean to 15.5:1 for economy. In Africa, where many vehicles are older and have carburettors, you adjust the mixture screw by hand. A good starting point is to turn the screw in until the engine stumbles, then out one and a half turns. Use a vacuum gauge: highest vacuum at idle means best mixture. For diesel, there is no throttle. Air is always unrestricted. You control power by how much fuel the injection pump delivers. So a diesel can run very leanâ30:1 or more at idle. That is why diesels are more efficient. But too lean at high load can melt pistons. Too rich wastes fuel and makes black smoke. To check mixture on a petrol engine, use a colour tune or an exhaust gas analyser. On a diesel, look at the exhaust: black smoke means too much fuel, white smoke means unburnt fuel or water, blue smoke means oil burning. In many African workshops, you can use a simple Gunson Gastester or even a narrowband oxygen sensor with a multimeter. The goal is to keep the mixture in a range that gives good power, acceptable economy, and does not damage the engine. Remember: air is free, fuel costs money. Running slightly lean saves fuel but can raise exhaust gas temperatures. Running rich wastes fuel but cools the engine. Find the balance for your conditionsâdusty roads, high altitude, or poor fuel quality..Trim()

Compression Ratios and Their Effects

Compression ratio is the volume of the cylinder when the piston is at the bottom divided by the volume when it is at the top. A petrol engine typically has a compression ratio of 8:1 to 12:1. A diesel engine has 14:1 to 23:1. Higher compression means more power and better efficiency because the air-fuel mixture is squeezed into a smaller space, so the burn is faster and more complete. But higher compression also means more heat and more risk of knock in petrol engines. In Africa, many older petrol engines run 8.5:1 to 9.5:1 because fuel octane is often low (91 or 93 RON). If you increase compression without increasing octane, you get knockâa pinging sound that can destroy pistons. Diesel engines use high compression to ignite the fuel. The air is compressed so much it reaches 700-900Â°C. That is why diesel engines are heavier and noisier. To measure compression, you need a compression gauge. Remove all spark plugs or injectors, screw the gauge into one cylinder, hold the throttle wide open, and crank the engine 4-6 times. A healthy petrol engine should read 10-14 bar (150-200 psi). A diesel should read 20-30 bar (300-450 psi). Low compression means worn rings, valves, or head gasket. In many African workshops, you can borrow a compression gauge from a tool library or buy a basic one for $20. If one cylinder is low, pour a teaspoon of oil into it and retest. If compression rises, the rings are worn. If it stays low, the valves are leaking. Compression ratio also affects fuel choice. High compression engines need high-octane fuel. In countries where only low-octane fuel is available, you must retard ignition timing to prevent knock. That is why some cars have a switch for 'regular' or 'premium' fuel. Understanding compression helps you diagnose power loss, rough idle, and overheating. It is the heart of engine health..Trim()

Ignition Timing and Combustion Phasing

Ignition timing is when the spark plug fires in a petrol engine. It is measured in degrees before top dead centre (BTDC). If the spark fires too early, the burning mixture pushes against the rising piston, causing knock and power loss. If it fires too late, the burn finishes after the piston is already going down, so you lose power and overheat the exhaust. A typical petrol engine idles at 8-12Â° BTDC and advances to 30-40Â° BTDC at high rpm. The engine control unit (ECU) adjusts timing based on engine speed, load, and knock sensor signals. On older engines with a distributor, you set base timing with a timing light. Connect the light to cylinder 1 spark plug wire, point it at the crankshaft pulley, and turn the distributor until the mark lines up with the specified degreesâoften 10Â° BTDC with the vacuum advance disconnected. In Africa, many mechanics use a simple inductive timing light that costs $30. If you do not have one, you can set timing by ear: advance until the engine starts to knock, then back off slightly. But that is risky. Better to use a light. For diesel engines, there is no spark. Ignition happens when fuel is injected into hot compressed air. The timing is controlled by the injection pump. You adjust it by rotating the pump slightly. Too advanced and you get diesel knock (harsh noise). Too retarded and you get white smoke and poor power. If ignition delay is too long, too much fuel has mixed, and the sudden burn is violentâthat is diesel knock. If delay is too short, the fuel may not mix well, causing incomplete combustion and black smoke. A carburettor mixes petrol and air in the right ratio for your engine. Diesel Fuel Properties and Grades

Diesel fuel is graded by its cetane number, sulphur content, and cold-flow properties. In Africa, you'll commonly see diesel with high sulphur (up to 5000 ppm) in older grades, but modern low-sulphur diesel (50 ppm or less) is becoming standard. Cetane number measures ignition quality; higher cetane (45-55) means easier cold starts and smoother running. In hot climates, cetane is less critical, but in high-altitude areas like the Ethiopian highlands, a cetane boost helps. Always check the fuel's cloud point and pour pointâthese tell you when wax crystals form and clog filters. Inline and Distributor Injection Pumps

Inline injection pumps (like Bosch PE) have one plunger per cylinder, driven by a camshaft. They're robust and common on older trucks and tractors. The plunger pressurizes fuel to about 200-300 bar. Distributor pumps (like Bosch VE or CAV DPA) use a single plunger to serve all cylinders, rotating to distribute fuel. They're lighter and used on smaller engines. For inline pumps, you adjust the rack for fuel delivery; for distributor pumps, you adjust the governor and timing piston. A common problem is worn plungers causing low pressure and hard starting. You can test pressure with a gauge at the injector line. If pressure is below spec, rebuild the pump. In Africa, dirty fuel kills these pumps quickly. Always install a good pre-filter. Bleeding the system is critical: open the bleed screw on the pump and hand-pump until bubble-free fuel flows. Then crank the engine. If it won't start, check the fuel shut-off solenoidâit often fails due to poor wiring. For distributor pumps, the timing advance mechanism can stick, causing poor performance. Common Rail Injection Systems

Common rail (CR) systems use a high-pressure pump to pressurize fuel in a rail, which feeds solenoid or piezo injectors. Pressures range from 300 to 2000 bar. Unit Injectors and HEUI Systems

Unit injectors combine the pump and injector into one unit, driven by the engine's camshaft. They're found on older Detroit Diesel and some Caterpillar engines. Lubrication System Components and Flow

The lubrication system in a small petrol or diesel engine (up to 12 litres) has one main job: keep metal parts from touching each other. It does this by pumping oil under pressure to bearings, camshafts, and cylinder walls. The main parts are the oil pan (sump), oil pump, pickup tube, oil filter, and oil galleries (passages drilled into the block and head). In most small engines, the pump is driven by the crankshaft or camshaft. Oil sits in the pan. The pump pulls it through a strainer on the pickup tube. Then it pushes oil through the filter and into the main oil gallery. From there, oil flows to the crankshaft main bearings, then through drilled passages to the connecting rod bearings. A small amount splashes onto cylinder walls and piston pins. The camshaft gets oil from a separate gallery. In overhead valve engines, oil also goes up to the rocker arms through pushrods or an external line. After lubricating, oil drains back to the pan by gravity. A pressure relief valve in the pump prevents too much pressure when the oil is cold. A bypass valve in the filter lets oil skip the filter if it gets clogged, so the engine still gets oil. Typical oil pressure for a small diesel is 2 to 4 bar (30-60 psi) at operating speed. For a petrol engine, 1.5 to 3 bar (20-45 psi). If you see the oil light come on at idle, the pressure is below about 0.5 bar (7 psi) â stop the engine immediately. In Africa, dusty conditions mean you must check the oil level daily and change oil more often. Use a clean rag, not your shirt. The dipstick has marks: keep the level between them. Overfilling causes foaming and leaks. Underfilling causes overheating and rapid wear. Always use the correct viscosity oil for your climate..Trim()

The cooling system keeps the engine at the right temperature â usually 80 to 95Â°C for most small engines. If it runs too hot, metal expands, oil breaks down, and parts seize. Overheating and low oil pressure are the two most common killers of small engines. Learn to diagnose them fast. For overheating, first check the coolant level. If it is low, look for leaks. .Trim()

B) Disconnect the battery
C) Drain the oil
D) Remove the radiator cap
Show answer
Correct: A — Removing all spark plugs allows the engine to crank freely and prevents compression from being affected by other cylinders.
2. A healthy petrol engine should have compression readings within what range?
A) 50-80 psi
B) 120-180 psi
C) 300-500 psi
D) 600-800 psi
Show answer
Correct: B — Typical petrol engines have compression between 120 and 180 psi, with no cylinder more than 10% below the highest.
3. During a leak-down test, air bubbles in the radiator indicate:
A) Worn piston rings
B) Leaking intake valve
C) Blown head gasket
D) Leaking exhaust valve
Show answer
Correct: C — Bubbles in the radiator mean combustion gases are entering the cooling system, typically from a blown head gasket or cracked head.
4. On a diesel engine, what should you do before removing injectors?
A) Disconnect the battery
B) Crack the high-pressure fuel lines
C) Drain the fuel tank
D) Remove the air filter
Show answer
Correct: B — Cracking the fuel lines releases residual pressure and prevents dangerous fuel injection into the skin.
5. If compression rises after adding oil to a low cylinder, the problem is likely:
A) Worn piston rings
B) Leaking valves
C) Blown head gasket
D) Cracked cylinder head
Show answer
Correct: A — Oil temporarily seals worn rings, so if compression improves, the rings are the cause.
6. What does a cylinder balance test measure?
A) Compression
B) Power contribution of each cylinder
C) Fuel pressure
D) Ignition timing
Show answer
Correct: B — A cylinder balance test checks how much each cylinder contributes to overall engine power by disabling cylinders one at a time.
7. On a petrol engine, when you disconnect a strong cylinder, RPM should drop by:
A) 0-10 RPM
B) 50-100 RPM
C) 200-300 RPM
D) 500-600 RPM
Show answer
Correct: B — A strong cylinder contributes significantly, so disabling it causes a noticeable RPM drop of about 50-100 RPM.
8. On a diesel engine, a cold exhaust port temperature indicates:
A) Rich mixture
B) Dead cylinder
C) Advanced timing
D) Normal operation
Show answer
Correct: B — A cold exhaust port means that cylinder is not firing, so no heat is being produced.
9. What is a common cause of low cylinder contribution?

B) High fuel pressure
C) Advanced ignition timing

C) Lean mixture

Correct: B — High HC means unburnt fuel is exiting the engine, often due to misfire or poor combustion.
