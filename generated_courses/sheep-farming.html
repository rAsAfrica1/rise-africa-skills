Module 1 · Introduction to Sheep Farming

Overview of the Sheep Industry

Sheep farming is one of the oldest and most adaptable livestock enterprises in Africa. From the arid lands of the Sahel to the highlands of Ethiopia and the grasslands of South Africa, sheep provide meat, milk, wool, and skins. In 2022, Africa had over 350 million sheep, with Sudan, Ethiopia, Nigeria, and South Africa leading. Most are indigenous breeds kept by smallholders, but commercial farms also thrive. The industry is split into two main systems: extensive pastoralism, where sheep roam over large areas, and intensive farming, where they are kept in pens and fed. Pastoral systems dominate in dry regions, while intensive systems are common near cities for meat production. Key products include mutton and lamb (meat), wool from breeds like Merino, milk from dairy breeds like Awassi, and hides for leather. Demand for sheep meat is rising due to population growth and urbanization. In 2020, Africa imported over 200,000 tonnes of sheep meat, showing a gap local farmers can fill. Opportunities exist in breeding, feed production, veterinary services, and processing. Challenges include disease, feed shortages, and climate change. However, with proper management, a small flock of 20 ewes can produce 30 lambs per year, generating significant income. This module will equip you with the basics to start or improve a sheep farm. You will learn about breeds, anatomy, behavior, economics, and regulations. By the end, you will be able to make informed decisions for a profitable sheep enterprise..Trim()

Breeds of Sheep and Their Purposes

Choosing the right breed is critical for success. Breeds are classified by purpose: meat, wool, milk, or dual-purpose. In Africa, indigenous breeds are hardy and adapted to local conditions. The Dorper, developed in South Africa, is a meat breed with high growth rates and good carcass quality. It thrives in arid areas and is popular across the continent. The Pelibuey (West African Dwarf) is a small meat breed resistant to trypanosomiasis, common in West Africa. The Red Maasai in East Africa is known for tolerance to internal parasites. For wool, the Merino, found in South Africa and Lesotho, produces fine wool. The Angora goat is not a sheep, but some farmers confuse it; for sheep, the Karakul produces fur pelts. Dairy sheep include the Awassi in the Middle East and North Africa, yielding up to 300 liters of milk per lactation. The East Friesian, a European breed, is also used for milk but requires better feeding. Dual-purpose breeds like the Tunis (meat and wool) and the Blackhead Persian (meat and fat) are common. When selecting a breed, consider your environment, market demand, and available feed. For meat, fast-growing breeds like Dorper or Suffolk crosses are ideal. For wool, Merino or Rambouillet. For milk, Awassi or East Friesian. Always buy from reputable breeders and check for health certificates. Crossbreeding indigenous ewes with exotic rams can improve growth while maintaining hardiness. For example, crossing a Red Maasai ewe with a Dorper ram can produce lambs that grow faster and resist parasites. Start with at least 10 ewes and one ram. Record performance to identify best animals..Trim()

Basic Sheep Anatomy and Physiology

Understanding sheep anatomy helps you manage health and productivity. Sheep have four stomach compartments: rumen, reticulum, omasum, and abomasum. The rumen is the largest, holding up to 30 liters in adult sheep, where microbes break down fibrous feed. This allows sheep to digest grasses and crop residues. The reticulum traps foreign objects; hardware disease occurs when sheep swallow wire. The omasum absorbs water, and the abomasum is the true stomach, secreting acid. Sheep have no upper front teeth; they bite grass with a dental pad and lower incisors. This makes them efficient grazers. Their lips are split, allowing close grazing. Sheep have a lifespan of 10-12 years, but productive life is 6-8 years. Ewes reach puberty at 6-8 months, but should be bred at 12-18 months when they weigh 35-45 kg. Gestation is about 147 days (5 months). Rams can breed at 6-8 months. Heat cycles occur every 17 days, and ewes are seasonally polyestrous, meaning they cycle in autumn (shorter days). In tropical Africa, some breeds cycle year-round. Body temperature is 38.5-40Â°C. Respiration is 12-20 breaths per minute. Sheep have sweat glands only on their noses, so they pant to cool down. Hooves need regular trimming every 6-8 weeks. Teeth check: lambs have baby teeth, replaced by permanent at 12-18 months. Age can be estimated by teeth. Wool grows continuously; shearing is done once or twice a year. Milk production in dairy ewes peaks at 2-4 weeks after lambing. Understanding these basics helps you detect illness early, such as bloat (swollen left flank) or pneumonia (rapid breathing)..Trim()

Sheep Behavior and Handling

Sheep are flock animals with strong herding instincts. They feel safe in groups and become stressed when isolated. A lone sheep will call loudly and try to rejoin the flock. This behavior makes moving sheep easier: use a trained dog or a handler to guide the flock, not individual animals. Sheep have a flight zone, a personal space of about 1-3 meters. If you enter it, they move away. To move sheep forward, position yourself behind the flock and apply pressure. To stop them, step into their flight zone from the front. Sheep have excellent hearing and can detect high-frequency sounds. They have poor depth perception, so they avoid shadows and dark spots. Use solid fences and avoid sudden movements. When handling, use a race or chute to restrain them. Grab the wool on the neck or flank, not the legs or ears. For examination, sit the sheep on its rump with its back against your legs. Sheep remember bad experiences; handle calmly and quietly. They are creatures of habit and prefer routine. Feeding at the same time daily reduces stress. Sheep are curious but cautious. They will investigate new objects but run if startled. Use this to your advantage: place feed near a new object to encourage exploration. Rams can be aggressive during breeding season; never turn your back on a ram. Use a shield or stick if needed. Ewes are protective mothers; avoid disturbing newborn lambs for the first 24 hours. Sheep have a strong sense of smell; they recognize their lambs by scent. Orphan lambs can be grafted onto another ewe by rubbing the lamb with the ewe's afterbirth or using a stockinette. Understanding behavior reduces injuries and improves welfare. Always move sheep in the cool of the day, and provide water and shade..Trim()

Economic Importance of Sheep Farming

Sheep farming contributes significantly to African economies and household incomes. A well-managed ewe can produce 1.5 lambs per year, and with 20 ewes, you can get 30 lambs annually. At a local market price of $50 per lamb, that's $1,500 per year. Add wool sales: a Merino fleece weighs 3-5 kg and sells for $5-10 per kg, giving $15-50 per sheep per year. Dairy sheep can yield 100-300 liters of milk per lactation, sold at $1 per liter, adding $100-300 per ewe. Skins and hides fetch $5-15 each. Manure is valuable fertilizer; a sheep produces 1-2 tonnes per year, worth $50-100. The initial investment for 20 ewes and one ram is about $2,000 (ewes at $80 each, ram at $200). Annual costs include feed ($300), veterinary ($100), and labor ($200). Net profit can be $1,000-1,500 per year, a 50-75% return. Sheep are also a savings account; sold in times of need, they provide quick cash. In many cultures, sheep are used for ceremonies, dowry, and religious festivals, creating steady demand. The growing urban population demands more meat; Africa's sheep meat consumption is projected to rise 3% annually. Export opportunities exist for live sheep, meat, and wool to the Middle East and Europe. However, challenges include feed scarcity in dry seasons, disease outbreaks, and price fluctuations. Value addition, such as processing wool into yarn or making leather goods, can increase income. Cooperatives help smallholders access markets and negotiate better prices. With proper record-keeping and business planning, sheep farming can be a profitable enterprise for African youth and women..Trim()

Regulations and Associations

Sheep farming in Africa is governed by national and local regulations. You must register your farm with the Ministry of Agriculture or Livestock Development. This ensures you receive extension services and can access subsidies. Animal movement permits are required when transporting sheep across districts or borders to prevent disease spread. Vaccination against notifiable diseases like anthrax, blackleg, and peste des petits ruminants (PPR) is often mandatory. Keep records of all treatments and sales for at least three years. Slaughter and meat processing must comply with public health laws; only licensed abattoirs can sell meat. If you plan to export, you need export certification and compliance with importing country standards. Associations provide support, training, and market linkages. In Africa, key organizations include the African Union Interafrican Bureau for Animal Resources (AU-IBAR), which coordinates livestock policies. At national level, examples are the National Sheep Association of Nigeria, the South African Meat Merino Breeders' Society, and the Kenya Sheep and Goat Breeders Association. These groups offer breed registration, performance recording, and field days. Joining a cooperative or association gives you access to subsidized feed, veterinary drugs, and collective bargaining. Many associations also facilitate access to credit and insurance. For example, the Eastern Africa Farmers Federation (EAFF) has programs for small ruminant farmers. Before starting, check local bylaws about land use, waste disposal, and noise. Some urban areas restrict livestock keeping. Also, be aware of breed-specific regulations: some countries restrict importation of exotic breeds to protect local genetic resources. Always consult your local veterinary office for the latest requirements. Compliance not only avoids fines but also builds trust with buyers and lenders. By following regulations and joining associations, you become part of a professional network that can help your farm grow..Trim()

Common Sheep Diseases

Sheep in Africa face several diseases that can wipe out your flock if you don't act fast. The most common are Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR), foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), bluetongue, sheep pox, and internal parasites like Haemonchus contortus (barber's pole worm). PPR is viral, spreads quickly, and kills up to 90% of infected sheep. Signs include high fever (40-41Â°C), mouth ulcers, nasal discharge, and diarrhea. There is no cure, so vaccination is your only defense. FMD causes blisters on the mouth and hooves, making sheep lame and unable to eat. It spreads through saliva, urine, and contaminated feed. Bluetongue, spread by midges, causes swelling of the face, tongue, and lips, and can kill within a week. Sheep pox causes skin nodules and fever; it spreads through direct contact and contaminated equipment. Internal parasites are the silent killer. Barber's pole worm sucks blood, causing anemia (pale gums) and sudden death. You can check for anemia using the FAMACHA eye color chart: pale white eyes mean severe infection. Other diseases include pneumonia (caused by stress, cold, or dusty environments), enterotoxemia (overeating disease from sudden grain intake), and caseous lymphadenitis (abscesses in lymph nodes). To identify diseases early, walk through your flock daily. Look for sheep that are separated from the group, have droopy ears, don't come to feed, or have abnormal droppings. Take note of any sudden deaths. For any suspected outbreak, isolate sick animals immediately and call a vet. In many African countries, government vets provide free or low-cost vaccination against PPR and FMD. Always report unusual deaths to your local veterinary office. Remember, prevention is cheaper than treatment. A single dose of PPR vaccine costs less than 1 USD and protects for 3 years. Treating a sick sheep can cost 10 times more and often fails. Keep records of all diseases you see, and share with other farmers to track outbreaks..Trim()

Vaccination Protocols

Vaccination is the cheapest insurance for your sheep. In Africa, the core vaccines are PPR, FMD, sheep pox, and anthrax (in endemic areas). Follow a strict schedule: lambs born to vaccinated ewes get their first PPR vaccine at 3 months old, then a booster 3-4 weeks later. If the ewe was not vaccinated, vaccinate the lamb at 1 month, then booster at 2 months. For FMD, vaccinate every 6 months because immunity is short-lived. Sheep pox vaccine is given once a year, preferably before the rainy season when outbreaks peak. Anthrax vaccine is given annually in areas with a history of the disease. Always buy vaccines from a reputable agro-vet store and check the expiry date. Keep vaccines in a cold chain: use a cooler box with ice packs during transport, and store at 2-8Â°C in a refrigerator. Never freeze PPR or FMD vaccines; freezing kills them. Use a new needle for each animal to prevent spreading diseases like caseous lymphadenitis. For small flocks, a 2 ml syringe with a 20-gauge needle works well. Inject subcutaneously (under the skin) in the neck, not intramuscularly, unless the label says otherwise. Restrain the sheep properly: hold it between your legs or use a halter. Clean the injection site with cotton wool and alcohol. After vaccination, watch for allergic reactions like swelling, drooling, or difficulty breathing. Keep epinephrine (adrenaline) on hand; dose is 0.5-1 ml per 50 kg body weight. Record every vaccination: date, vaccine name, batch number, and animal ID. If you have a large flock, organize a vaccination day with neighbors to share costs and labor. In many countries, the government runs annual vaccination campaigns against PPR and FMD. Take advantage of these free or subsidized programs. Do not vaccinate sick or stressed sheep; wait until they recover. Also, do not mix vaccines unless the manufacturer says it's safe. After vaccination, immunity takes 2-3 weeks to develop, so keep animals away from possible sources of infection during that time. A well-vaccinated flock has fewer deaths and higher profits..Trim()

Parasite Control

Parasites are the number one cause of poor growth and death in African sheep. They come in two types: internal (worms) and external (ticks, mites, lice, fleas). Internal parasites like Haemonchus contortus (barber's pole worm), Ostertagia, and Trichostrongylus live in the stomach and intestines. They cause diarrhea, weight loss, anemia, and bottle jaw (swelling under the jaw). To control them, use a combination of deworming, grazing management, and selective treatment. Deworm every 3-4 months, but rotate between different drug classes to prevent resistance. Common dewormers: albendazole (white drench), ivermectin, and levamisole. Dose correctly by weight; underdosing creates resistant worms. Weigh your sheep using a scale or a weigh tape. For example, a 40 kg sheep gets 1 ml of ivermectin 1% injection. After deworming, move sheep to a clean pasture that hasn't been grazed for at least 3 months. The FAMACHA system helps you treat only anemic sheep, reducing drug use. Check eye color: 1 (red) = healthy, 5 (white) = severe anemia, treat immediately. External parasites: ticks transmit heartwater and other diseases. Use tick grease (e.g., amitraz) applied to the ears, tail base, and under the tail every 2 weeks. For mites (scabies), inject ivermectin or use pour-on products. Lice cause itching and hair loss; dust with insecticidal powder or use a pour-on. Keep bedding clean and dry. Rotate pastures and avoid overstocking. If you see diarrhea, isolate the sheep and collect a fecal sample for a vet to check worm eggs. A simple fecal egg count (FEC) tells you the worm burden. In Africa, many farmers use local plants like neem (Azadirachta indica) and aloe vera as dewormers, but these are less effective than modern drugs. Use them only as supplements. Always store dewormers in a cool, dark place and check expiry dates. After handling dewormers, wash your hands. Never slaughter sheep within the withdrawal period (meat: 14-30 days depending on drug). Parasite control is a year-round job, but it pays off with healthier, faster-growing sheep..Trim()

Hoof Care and Lameness

Lameness is a major welfare and economic problem in sheep. It causes weight loss, reduced fertility, and early culling. The main causes are foot rot, foot abscess, and foot scald. Foot rot is a bacterial infection (Dichelobacter nodosus) that thrives in wet, muddy conditions. Signs: foul smell, blackening of the hoof, separation of the hoof wall, and severe lameness. Foot scald is milder, with red, inflamed skin between the toes. Foot abscess is a pocket of pus, often from a thorn or stone. To prevent lameness, keep pastures dry and well-drained. Avoid muddy areas near water troughs. Provide a dry, clean bedding area. Trim hooves every 6-8 months, or more often in wet seasons. Use sharp hoof shears or a hoof knife. Restrain the sheep by sitting it on its rump (shearing position) or use a tilt table. Clean the hoof with a wire brush, then trim the overgrown wall and sole. Be careful not to cut too deep; stop when you see pink tissue. For treatment of foot rot, trim away all dead tissue, then soak the foot in a 10% zinc sulfate solution for 30 minutes. Or use a copper sulfate footbath (5% solution) for 10 minutes. Repeat every 5-7 days for 3 weeks. For severe cases, inject long-acting oxytetracycline (20 mg/kg body weight) once, and apply a topical antibiotic spray. Isolate lame sheep to a dry pen until they recover. Foot abscesses need to be lanced and drained by a vet. After treatment, apply a bandage with iodine. To prevent spread, cull chronically lame sheep. In Africa, many farmers use a football (footbath) made from a plastic drum cut in half. Place it at the entrance of the pen so sheep walk through it daily. Add 5 kg of copper sulfate to 100 liters of water. Change the solution every 50 sheep. Also, avoid grazing on wet, marshy land. If you have rocky terrain, clear sharp stones. Regular hoof trimming and footbaths reduce lameness by up to 80%. Healthy hooves mean sheep can walk to feed and water, which keeps them productive..Trim()

Recognizing Signs of Illness

Sheep hide illness well because they are prey animals. By the time you see obvious signs, the disease may be advanced. Your best tool is daily observation. Spend 10 minutes each morning walking among your flock. Look for the 'sick sheep' signs: separation from the group, droopy ears, head down, not eating, lagging behind, or lying down more than usual. Check the eyes: pale or white gums and eyelids indicate anemia (worms). Yellow eyes and gums mean jaundice (liver fluke or leptospirosis). Normal temperature for a sheep is 38.5-40Â°C. Take it rectally with a digital thermometer. Fever above 40.5Â°C suggests infection. A low temperature (below 37.5Â°C) means shock or hypothermia. Check breathing: normal is 12-20 breaths per minute. Rapid, shallow breathing or coughing suggests pneumonia. Look at droppings: normal are pelleted. Diarrhea (scours) can be caused by worms, coccidia, or bacteria. Bloody diarrhea is an emergency. Check the nose: clear discharge is normal; thick, yellow or green discharge means infection. Check the feet: swelling, heat, or foul smell means foot rot. Check the mouth: ulcers, blisters, or drooling could be PPR or FMD. Check the body condition: run your hand over the ribs and spine. If you can feel bones easily, the sheep is thin (score 2 or less on a 1-5 scale). Also, watch for sudden deaths. If more than one sheep dies in a week, call a vet immediately. Keep a simple notebook: record date, animal ID, signs, and action taken. Take photos on your phone to show the vet. Isolate sick sheep in a quiet, shaded pen with easy access to water and hay. Provide supportive care: fresh water, electrolytes, and soft feed. Do not give antibiotics without a vet's advice; wrong drugs waste money and create resistance. Learn to do a basic physical exam: check gums, eyes, temperature, and breathing. If you suspect a notifiable disease like PPR or FMD, report it to the authorities. Early detection saves lives and money..Trim()

Biosecurity Measures

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out of your farm. It is the cheapest and most effective health strategy. Start with a closed flock: buy new sheep only from known, healthy sources. Quarantine all new animals for at least 30 days in a separate pen, far from your main flock. During quarantine, check for signs of illness, deworm, and vaccinate. Use separate boots, clothing, and equipment for the quarantine area. If you visit another farm or market, change clothes and shower before entering your sheep pen. Do not let visitors enter your pens unless they wear clean boots or disposable shoe covers. Provide a footbath with 5% copper sulfate or 10% bleach at the entrance. Keep vehicles and dogs away from feed and bedding. Control rodents and wild birds; they carry diseases. Store feed in sealed containers. Use clean water from a well or tap, not stagnant pools. Keep pastures free of garbage and sharp objects. Isolate sick sheep immediately. If a sheep dies, bury it deep (at least 2 meters) or burn it. Never sell or eat a dead sheep. Disinfect pens and equipment regularly. Use a 1:10 bleach solution or a commercial disinfectant like Virkon. Remove manure daily and compost it; composting kills many pathogens. Keep a record of all animals and their health history. Limit the number of people who handle your sheep. Train your workers on hygiene. If you share a ram or grazing land, be extra careful. In Africa, many farmers share communal grazing, which increases disease spread. To reduce risk, graze your sheep on your own land or rotate pastures. Avoid mixing with other flocks, especially at watering points. If you attend a livestock market, do not bring your sheep; send a healthy-looking animal as a sample, or better, sell through a middleman. After any contact with outside sheep, change clothes and disinfect. Vaccinate against common diseases. A good biosecurity plan includes: quarantine, vaccination, parasite control, and hygiene. Write it down and follow it every day. One outbreak of PPR can wipe out your entire flock. Biosecurity is not expensive; it is just a habit. Start today..Trim()

Module 10 · Pasture and Forage Management

Forage Species Selection

Choosing the right forage for your sheep depends on your climate, soil, and how you plan to graze. In Africa, you need plants that survive dry seasons and produce enough protein. For wet, high-rainfall areas like the Kenyan highlands, plant Rhodes grass (Chloris gayana) or Napier grass (Pennisetum purpureum). Rhodes grass yields 10-15 tonnes of dry matter per hectare per year and tolerates heavy grazing. Napier grass gives up to 30 tonnes but needs good rainfall and fertilizer. For drier zones, use buffel grass (Cenchrus ciliaris) or Bermuda grass (Cynodon dactylon). Buffel grass survives on 300 mm annual rainfall. Add legumes like lucerne (alfalfa), desmodium, or stylo (Stylosanthes) to boost protein. Lucerne has 18-22% crude protein and fixes nitrogen, reducing fertilizer costs. Desmodium works in humid areas and is shade-tolerant. Stylo thrives in poor, acidic soils. For a small farm, mix one grass and one legume. Example: Rhodes grass + desmodium. Sow grass at 10-15 kg per hectare and legume at 2-4 kg per hectare. In dry areas, plant at the start of the rains. Test germination before sowing: put 100 seeds in a wet cloth for 7 days and count how many sprout. If less than 70 sprout, buy new seed. Avoid toxic plants like bracken fern or Crotalaria species, which can poison sheep. Rotate species every 3-4 years to prevent pest buildup. Always buy certified seed from a registered agrodealer to avoid weeds and low yields..Trim()

Rotational Grazing Systems

Rotational grazing means moving sheep between paddocks to let grass recover. Without it, sheep overgraze and eat the best plants first, leaving weeds. A simple system for 20 sheep: divide your pasture into 4-6 paddocks using electric fencing or live fences like sisal or thorny hedges. Each paddock should be 0.5 to 1 hectare. Move sheep every 3-5 days in the rainy season and every 7-10 days in the dry season. The key rule: graze half, leave half. When grass is 15-20 cm tall, let sheep in. When they eat it down to 5-7 cm, move them. This gives plants enough leaf area to regrow. Rest each paddock for 30-45 days in the wet season and 60-90 days in the dry season. In dry periods, you may need to reduce flock size or buy supplementary hay. A practical tool is a grazing chart: draw your paddocks and mark the date you move sheep. Use a notebook or a simple phone app like Pasture Map. For 20 sheep, you need about 0.4 hectares per month of rest. So if you have 2 hectares, you can keep 20 sheep with 5 paddocks. In East Africa, many farmers use a 'cell grazing' system with daily moves using portable electric netting. This improves grass yield by 30-50% and reduces worm burden because sheep don't graze where they pooped 3 days ago. Always provide clean water and mineral licks in each paddock. Rotational grazing saves money on feed and vet bills..Trim()

Soil Fertility and Testing

Healthy soil grows healthy grass. You need to know your soil's pH, nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). In Africa, most soils are acidic (pH below 5.5) and low in P. Get a soil test from a local agricultural office or a private lab. In Kenya, KALRO does tests for about 1,000 KES per sample. Take samples from 10 spots in your pasture, mix them in a bucket, and send 500g to the lab. If you can't afford a lab, use a simple soil test kit from an agrodealer. For pH, you can also use vinegar: if soil fizzes, it's alkaline (pH above 7); if it doesn't, it's likely acidic. For acidic soil, apply agricultural lime at 2-4 tonnes per hectare. Lime raises pH and releases nutrients. Do this 2-3 months before planting. For nitrogen, plant legumes like desmodium or lucerne. They fix 50-200 kg of N per hectare per year. For phosphorus, apply triple superphosphate (TSP) at 100-200 kg per hectare at planting. For potassium, wood ash or muriate of potash (MOP) at 50-100 kg per hectare works. Manure is excellent: apply 5-10 tonnes of well-rotted cattle or goat manure per hectare per year. Spread it in the dry season and incorporate it lightly. Avoid fresh manure; it burns plants and spreads weeds. Test your soil every 2-3 years. Keep records of what you apply and when. If grass turns yellow, it needs nitrogen. If it's purple, it needs phosphorus. If leaf edges burn, it needs potassium. Fix deficiencies early to avoid poor grazing..Trim()

Weed and Pest Control

Weeds compete with grass for water and nutrients. The worst weeds in African pastures are thorny plants like Lantana camara, blackjack (Bidens pilosa), and couch grass. For small farms, hand-pull weeds before they flower. For larger areas, use a slasher or mower to cut weeds before they seed. Mulching with dry grass or straw suppresses weeds and keeps soil moist. For chemical control, use glyphosate (Roundup) carefully. Spray only on a calm day and avoid grazing for 7 days. For thorny bushes, cut them at the base and paint the stump with a herbicide like triclopyr. Always wear gloves, boots, and a mask. Pests include termites, armyworms, and locusts. Termites eat grass roots. Control them by destroying mounds and applying neem cake (200 kg per hectare) or a registered insecticide like imidacloprid. Armyworms appear in the rainy season. Scout your pasture weekly. If you see 5-10 caterpillars per square meter, spray with Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) or neem oil. Locusts are harder: report outbreaks to your local agriculture office. They may spray. For sheep, internal parasites like worms are a pest. Rotational grazing reduces worm larvae. Also, use copper sulfate foot baths for foot rot. Keep a weed and pest diary. Record what you see, when, and what you did. This helps you act faster next season. Never use a herbicide on a windy day or near water sources. Always store chemicals away from children and animals..Trim()

Irrigation and Water Management

Sheep need clean water daily: 4-10 litres per adult per day, more in hot weather. But your pasture also needs water. In dry seasons, irrigation keeps grass growing. For small farms, use a treadle pump or a solar-powered pump. A 1-horsepower solar pump can irrigate 0.5 hectares. Drip irrigation is best: it saves 30-50% water compared to sprinklers. Lay drip lines 30 cm apart along grass rows. For a 1-hectare pasture, you need about 20,000-30,000 litres per week in dry weather. Water early morning or late evening to reduce evaporation. Mulch around plants with dry grass to hold moisture. Build a small dam or water pan to catch rainwater. Line it with clay or a plastic liner to prevent seepage. A 10m x 10m x 2m pan holds 200,000 litres, enough for 2 hectares for a month. For sheep drinking water, use troughs with a float valve to keep them full. Clean troughs weekly to prevent algae and mosquitoes. In dry areas, harvest rainwater from roofs into tanks. A 5,000-litre tank costs about 30,000 KES and can water 20 sheep for 3 months. Test water quality: if it's salty or has a bad smell, send a sample to a lab. Sheep can tolerate up to 5,000 ppm total dissolved salts, but young lambs need cleaner water. Always provide shade near water points. In very dry periods, reduce stocking rate. You can also feed hay and silage instead of relying on irrigated pasture..Trim()

Hay and Silage Making

Hay and silage let you feed sheep when grass is scarce. Hay is dried grass; silage is fermented wet grass. To make hay, cut grass when it's tall and before it seeds. In the morning, cut with a sickle or mower. Let it dry in the sun for 2-3 days, turning it daily. Test dryness: twist a handful; if it breaks, it's ready. If it's still flexible, dry more. Bale it with a manual baler or store loose in a dry shed. Good hay is green, smells sweet, and has 8-12% moisture. Store on pallets to avoid dampness. For silage, cut grass when it's wet and wilt it for 4-6 hours. Chop into 2-3 cm pieces. Pack tightly into a pit, drum, or silo bag. Add molasses (2-3% of weight) to help fermentation. Seal with plastic and soil. It ferments in 3-4 weeks. Good silage smells like vinegar and is yellow-green. Feed 1-2 kg per sheep per day. A 200-litre drum holds about 150 kg of silage. For 20 sheep, you need 2-3 tonnes of hay or silage for a 3-month dry season. Make hay in the rainy season when grass is abundant. In Kenya, many farmers make hay from Rhodes grass or oats. In West Africa, use maize stover or groundnut haulms. Always store in a dry, ventilated place. Never feed mouldy hay or silage; it causes disease. Test by smelling: if it's musty or black, throw it away. With good hay and silage, you can keep your sheep healthy all year..Trim()

Budgeting and Cost Analysis

A budget is a plan for your money. For sheep farming, you need to know your costs and income. Start by listing all costs: feed, veterinary care, labour, housing, and transport. That's 6 bags, costing KES 15,000 per month. Add vet costs: deworming every 3 months at KES 50 per sheep, so KES 1,000 per quarter. Labour: if you hire one worker at KES 10,000 per month, that's KES 120,000 per year. Housing: initial cost of KES 50,000 for a simple shed, spread over 5 years, so KES 10,000 per year. Transport: KES 5,000 per month to take sheep to market. Total monthly costs: feed 15,000 + labour 10,000 + transport 5,000 + vet (1,000/3=333) + housing (10,000/12=833) = about KES 31,166 per month. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Excel or Google Sheets to track. Review your budget every month and adjust.
