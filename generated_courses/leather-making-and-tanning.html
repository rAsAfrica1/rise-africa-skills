Overview of the Leather Industry

The leather industry in Africa is a growing sector that turns animal hides and skins into valuable products like shoes, bags, belts, and upholstery. In countries like Ethiopia, Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa, leather is a major export earner. For example, Ethiopia exports over $100 million worth of leather products annually. Types of Hides and Skins

In leather making, 'hides' come from large animals like cattle, buffalo, and zebra, while 'skins' come from smaller animals like goats, sheep, and rabbits. The difference matters because hide thickness and fiber structure affect tanning and final use. Cattle hides are thick and strong, ideal for shoe soles, belts, and heavy bags. A typical cattle hide weighs 20-30 kg when fresh. Goat skins are thin, flexible, and have a distinct grain pattern, perfect for gloves, bookbinding, and luxury bags. Goat skins weigh about 2-4 kg. Sheep skins are softer and stretchier, used for clothing, linings, and chamois leather. Sheep skins weigh 1-3 kg. In Africa, you'll also find camel hides (thick, used for sandals and water bags), ostrich skins (luxury, with quill follicles), and fish skins (tilapia, catfish) for exotic leather. Each type requires different tanning approaches. For example, goat skins need gentler chemicals because they are thinner. Cattle hides can take stronger liming and longer tanning. When sourcing, inspect for defects: cuts, scars, tick bites, or rot. Fresh hides should be preserved with salt (25-30% of hide weight) or air-dried if you tan immediately. In rural areas, you can buy raw hides from local butchers for $2-5 per goat skin or $10-20 per cattle hide. Start with goat skins because they are easier to handle and tan quickly. Avoid hides from sick animals. Proper selection is the first step to quality leather..Trim()

Leather Grading and Quality

Leather grading determines price and use. Graders look at four things: grain surface, thickness, flexibility, and defects. Top-grade leather (Grade A) has a clean, unblemished grain, uniform color, and no holes or scars. It's used for luxury shoes, handbags, and car interiors. Grade B has minor defects like small scars or uneven color, used for belts, wallets, and upholstery. Grade C has visible defects, used for work boots, bags, or cut into small pieces. Grade D is heavily damaged, used for cheap items or ground up for reconstituted leather. In Africa, buyers often use a simple scale: A, B, C, reject. To grade, lay the leather on a table under bright light. Check both sides. The grain side (hair side) should be smooth; the flesh side should be clean. Measure thickness with a gauge: 1-2 mm for garments, 2-3 mm for bags, 3-5 mm for soles. Flexibility: bend the leather; it should not crack. Common defects include: flay cuts (from skinning), tick marks, brand marks, and dung marks. These reduce grade. For example, a cattle hide with more than 5 tick marks drops from A to B. Proper tanning and finishing can hide some defects, but not deep scars. To get top prices, sort your leather after tanning. Store it flat, away from moisture. In local markets, Grade A goat skin might sell for $10-15, while Grade C sells for $3-5. Learn to grade accuratelyâit protects your reputation and income..Trim()

Historical and Modern Tanning Methods

Tanning has been practiced in Africa for thousands of years. Traditional methods use natural materials: animal brains, liver, dung, tree bark (acacia, mimosa), and leaves. For example, in Ethiopia, tanners use acacia pods to make a tea-like solution. The hides soak for days to weeks. This 'vegetable tanning' produces firm, brown leather used for saddles, sandals, and drums. It's slow but eco-friendly and gives unique character. Another old method is brain tanning: the animal's brain is mashed with water and worked into the skin. Each brain tans its own hide. Smoke from wood fires finishes it, making it water-resistant. These methods are still used in rural areas and for cultural products. Modern tanning uses chemicals, mainly chromium salts (chrome tanning). Chrome tanning takes 1-2 days, produces soft, blue-grey leather that dyes easily. It's the most common worldwide because it's fast and cheap. But chrome can pollute water if not treated. In Africa, many small tanneries use chrome but lack proper waste treatment. A third method is 'combination tanning'âusing both vegetable and chrome to get benefits of both. For a beginner, start with vegetable tanning using local acacia or mimosa. You need pits or drums, water, and patience. Chrome tanning requires careful handling of acids and chromium. Both methods need proper safety gear. Modern tanneries also use machines for splitting, shaving, and staking. But you can tan by hand with simple tools. The key is to control pH, temperature, and time. Traditional methods are forgiving; modern methods are faster but less forgiving. Choose based on your market and resources..Trim()

Safety in Leather Workshop

Leather tanning uses chemicals that can harm your skin, eyes, and lungs. You must protect yourself. Always wear safety goggles, rubber gloves, and a face mask (N95 or better) when handling lime, acids, chromium, and ammonia. These chemicals can burn skin, cause blindness, or damage lungs. Wear an apron and rubber boots. Never eat, drink, or smoke in the workshop. Keep the area well-ventilatedâopen windows and use fans. For lime, use slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) and wear gloves. If chemical touches skin, rinse with plenty of water for 15 minutes. If in eyes, rinse and seek medical help. Have a first aid kit with eyewash, bandages, and burn cream. Also, be careful with sharp tools: knives, fleshing knives, and splitting machines. Cut away from your body. Tools and Equipment Overview

You don't need expensive machines to start tanning. Basic tools are affordable and available locally. First, a fleshing knife: a two-handled curved blade used to remove flesh and fat from the hide. In Africa, you can buy one for $10-20 or make one from a machete. Next, a beam: a wooden or metal log where you drape the hide for fleshing. You can use a smooth tree trunk. For soaking, you need drums or pits. Plastic drums (200L) are cheap and good for small batches. For liming and tanning, use wooden or plastic paddles to stir hides. A hide horse (a wooden frame) helps you work on the hide. For measuring, get a pH meter or pH strips (important for chrome tanning). A thermometer for checking water temperature. A weighing scale for chemicals and hides. For drying, you need ropes and frames. For finishing, a slicker (a flat tool) to smooth leather, and a staking tool to soften it. For splitting, a hand splitter or a simple machine if you can afford it. You'll also need buckets, measuring jugs, and a hose. Safety gear: goggles, gloves, mask, apron. In many African markets, you can find these at hardware stores or make them. For example, a fleshing beam can be a smooth log. A paddle can be a carved wooden stick. Start with a small set: fleshing knife, beam, 2 drums, paddle, pH strips, scale, and safety gear. That's under $200. As you grow, add a hide splitter, shaving machine, and staking machine. But master hand tools first. They teach you the feel of leather. Keep tools clean and sharp. Store them dry to prevent rust..Trim()

Module 2 · Hide and Skin Preparation

Selection of Raw Hides

Selecting the right raw hide or skin is the first step to good leather. In Africa, you will mostly work with goat, sheep, cow, or camel hides. Goat skins are thin and flexible, good for gloves and bookbinding. Cow hides are thick and strong, used for belts and shoes. Sheep skins are soft but delicate, best for garments. Inspect the hide for holes, cuts, scars, or insect damage. Reject any hide with green or black patches, which mean bacterial rot. A fresh hide should smell slightly meaty, not foul. If you buy from a slaughterhouse, ask for the hide immediately after slaughter. If you buy from a market, check that the hide has been kept cool or salted. In hot African climates, a hide can rot within 6 hours if left in the sun. So salt it or start soaking within 2 hours. For small-scale tanning, goat skins are easiest for beginners because they are thin and require less chemicals. Cow hides need more time and stronger solutions. Weigh the hide: a fresh goat skin weighs about 1.5â2 kg, a cow hide 20â30 kg. You will need this weight to calculate chemical amounts later. Also check the grain side (the hair side) for tick bites or scars. These will show in the final leather. A good hide has uniform thickness, no bad odour, and no visible decay. If you are not tanning immediately, salt the hide with coarse salt (about 1 kg salt per 1 kg hide) and roll it up. Store in a cool, dry place. Never use a hide that has been lying in water for daysâit will be weak and may already be rotting. Remember: poor selection means poor leather, no matter how good your tanning is..Trim()

Soaking and Rehydration

Soaking brings a dried or salted hide back to its original softness. If you skip this, the hide will be stiff and chemicals won't penetrate. For a dried goat skin, use a clean plastic drum or basin. You can add 0.5% of a wetting agent (like mild soap) to speed up soaking, but never use bleach or detergent. After soaking, the hide should be pliable and slightly swollen. If it smells bad, add 1 gram of sodium carbonate (washing soda) per litre and soak for another 2 hours, then rinse. For rehydration of salted hides, you must also remove dirt and blood. Use a soft brush to scrub the flesh side. Do not use hot waterâit can cook the hide. Always use clean water; dirty water introduces bacteria. In rural areas, rainwater is best because it's soft. Fleshing and Trimming

Fleshing removes the leftover meat, fat, and membrane from the flesh side of the hide. If you leave this on, it will rot and stop chemicals from working. You need a fleshing beam or a smooth log. Unhairing and Liming

Unhairing removes the hair and epidermis (outer skin) and swells the hide. The traditional method uses lime (calcium hydroxide) and water. For 10 kg of hides, use 1 kg of lime in 30 litres of water. After liming, the hide is swollen and alkaline (pH about 12). Deliming lowers the pH to about 8â9 and removes lime. Bating further softens the hide and removes non-structural proteins. After bating, the hide should be soft and floppy, like a wet cloth. If it still feels rubbery, bate longer. Then rinse well. Now the hide is ready for pickling. Test by pressing a finger into the hideâit should leave a mark that slowly disappears. For goat skins, 12 hours bating is enough; cow hides need 24 hours. Always use clean water. Pickling acidifies the hide to pH 2â3 and preserves it before tanning. It also allows tanning agents to penetrate. If higher, add more acid. After pickling, the hide can be stored for weeks if kept in the pickle solution. But for tanning, you must depickle. Depickling means raising the pH to about 4â5 before tanning. To depickle, add 0.5 kg of sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) or 1 kg of sodium acetate to the pickle bath slowly. Stir well. Add a little at a timeâit will fizz. Check pH every 30 minutes until it reaches 4.5. Then soak for another 2 hours. For vegetable tanning, you can depickle to pH 4.5; for chrome tanning, pH 3.5â4. After depickling, the hide is ready for the tanning process. If you skip depickling, the tanning agent won't work properly and the leather will be uneven. For small-scale tanners, pickling and depickling can be done in a plastic drum. Always test pH with litmus paper or a cheap pH meter. In Africa, many tanners use formic acid instead of sulfuric because it's safer. Use 1 litre formic acid per 20 litres water. After pickling, the hide should be plump and white. After depickling, it should be soft and slightly swollen. Now you can proceed to tanning. Store pickled hides in a cool place away from sunlight. Buffing and Snuffing

Buffing and snuffing are mechanical operations that prepare the leather surface for finishing. Buffing removes minor defects, raises a nap on the grain side, or cleans the flesh side. Snuffing, on the other hand, creates a velvety nap on the flesh side (suede) or corrects the grain side to make it more absorbent. In Africa, many small tanneries use drum or wheel buffers. A common setup is a 1.5 kW motor turning a 30 cm diameter buffing wheel at 1,200 rpm. The wheel is made of compressed cotton or felt. For buffing grain, use a fine abrasive wheel (240-320 grit) and light pressure. Move the leather back and forth to avoid burn marks. For snuffing flesh side, use a coarser wheel (80-120 grit) to raise a uniform nap. Always work with dry leather (moisture below 14%). If the leather is too damp, the wheel will clog and tear the surface. Safety first: wear a dust mask, goggles, and gloves. Buffing creates fine dust that can irritate lungs. In many African workshops, buffing is done manually with a hand-held sanding block for small items like belts and wallets. For larger hides, a buffing machine saves hours. After buffing, wipe the surface with a dry cloth to remove dust. If you are making suede, snuff the flesh side after tanning and drying. Test on a scrap piece first. Adjust wheel speed and pressure until you get an even, soft nap. Remember, over-buffing can thin the leather and weaken it. Practice on scrap until you can produce a consistent finish. In rural areas, you can improvise a buffing wheel using a bicycle wheel fitted with a leather pad, but ensure it is securely mounted and guarded..Trim()

Coating and Spraying

Coating and spraying apply a protective and decorative film to leather. This improves appearance, water resistance, and durability. Common coatings include acrylic, polyurethane, and nitrocellulose lacquers. In Africa, many artisans use locally available acrylic floor finishes or shoe polish as a base. For a professional finish, mix a topcoat: 1 part acrylic binder, 1 part water, and a few drops of defoamer. Apply with a spray gun (gravity feed, 1.4 mm nozzle) at 2-3 bar pressure. Hold the gun 20-25 cm from the leather and move in overlapping passes. For small items, use an airbrush or even a sponge. Always spray in a well-ventilated area or outdoors. Wear a respirator with organic vapor cartridges. Before spraying, clean the leather with a damp cloth and let it dry. If the leather is absorbent, apply a thin base coat of diluted binder (1:3 with water) and let it dry. Then apply 2-3 thin coats of topcoat, drying 15-20 minutes between coats. Too thick a coat will crack. For a matte finish, add 2% matting agent. For gloss, add 1% gloss enhancer. In humid African climates, add 1% crosslinker to improve adhesion and water resistance. After the final coat, let it cure for 24 hours before use. If you don't have a spray gun, you can pad on the coating with a lint-free cloth. This gives a more rustic look but works for belts, bags, and sandals. Test on scrap first. Store coatings sealed and away from direct sun. Embossing and Printing

Embossing and printing create raised or recessed designs on leather. Embossing uses heat and pressure to stamp a pattern. Printing uses ink or dye to transfer a design. In Africa, traditional techniques like Nigerian leather stamping and Moroccan tooling are common. For embossing, you need a heated embossing plate (brass or steel) and a press. A simple hand press with a 2-tonne jack works for small items. Heat the plate to 80-100Â°C for grain leather, 60-80Â°C for suede. Place the leather on a hard rubber pad, position the plate, and press for 5-10 seconds. Too much heat burns the leather; too little gives a faint impression. For deep embossing, dampen the leather slightly (12-14% moisture). For printing, use a rubber stamp or screen with leather dye or pigment. Apply the dye evenly, press the stamp, and hold for 3-5 seconds. For multi-color prints, use separate stamps and let each color dry. In many African workshops, artisans carve wooden blocks for printing. You can also use a pyrography tool to burn designs. Always test on scrap. For embossing, practice with different temperatures and pressures. A common mistake is overheating, which makes the leather brittle. After embossing, apply a light coat of wax to protect the design. For printing, seal with a clear acrylic topcoat. If you are embossing a logo, make a metal plate from a local welder. Embossing and printing add value to plain leather, turning it into unique cultural products. Keep your plates clean and store them dry to prevent rust..Trim()

Glazing and Plating

Glazing and plating give leather a smooth, glossy surface. Glazing is done with a glass cylinder or agate stone rubbed vigorously over the leather. Plating uses a heated metal plate under pressure. In Africa, glazing is often done by hand with a glass bottle or a smooth stone. For glazing, first case the leather (dampen slightly) and apply a thin coat of gum tragacanth or egg white. Then rub the glass cylinder back and forth with firm pressure. The friction heat polishes the surface. Work in one direction until you see a shine. For plating, use a heated platen press. Set the plate to 60-80Â°C and 100-200 bar pressure for 2-3 seconds. This flattens the grain and gives a mirror finish. For small items, you can use a hand iron (like a clothes iron) set to low heat, but keep it moving to avoid scorching. Always test on scrap. Glazing and plating are often used on upper-grade leather for shoes, belts, and bags. In many African tanneries, plating is done after dyeing and before final coating. The key is to have a smooth, clean surface. Any dust or grit will scratch the leather. After glazing, apply a light wax and buff with a soft cloth. For plating, ensure the leather is dry (moisture below 12%). Too much moisture causes steam and blisters. If you don't have a press, you can achieve a similar effect by ironing with a Teflon sheet between the iron and leather. Practice on different leathers. Polishing and Ironing

Polishing and ironing are final steps to give leather a clean, professional look. Polishing removes minor scratches and adds shine. Ironing flattens wrinkles and sets the finish. In Africa, many artisans use a simple hand iron or a heated stone. For polishing, apply a wax-based polish (beeswax mixed with carnauba wax) with a soft cloth. Rub in circles, then buff with a clean cloth or brush. For a high shine, use a polishing wheel (cotton) at 1,500 rpm. Hold the leather firmly and move it against the wheel. Don't press too hard or you'll burn the surface. For ironing, set an iron to 60-70Â°C (low setting). Place a thin cotton cloth or Teflon sheet between the iron and leather. Iron with slow, even strokes. This removes creases and gives a flat finish. For embossed or printed leather, iron on the reverse side or use a lower temperature. Always test on scrap. In humid climates, ironing also helps remove moisture and set the shape. After ironing, let the leather cool before handling. For a final polish, apply a small amount of leather conditioner and buff. In many African workshops, polishing is done with a piece of soft leather or a banana leaf. The goal is a uniform, attractive surface. Avoid over-ironing, which can make leather stiff and brittle. If you are working with suede, do not iron; instead, brush with a suede brush. For smooth leather, ironing is safe if you use a protective layer. Quality Control in Finishing

Quality control (QC) ensures every finished leather meets standards. In Africa, small tanneries often skip QC, leading to rejects. A simple QC routine can save money. Start with visual inspection: check for uneven color, scratches, spots, or missed areas. Use a bright light and a clean table. For coated leather, check adhesion by scratching with a fingernail. If the coating peels, it's a reject. For embossed leather, check depth and clarity. For glazed leather, check gloss uniformity. Measure thickness with a gauge; variation should be within Â±0.2 mm. Test flexibility by bending the leather over a 2 cm rod. It should not crack. Test water resistance by placing a drop of water on the surface; it should bead for at least 5 minutes. For suede, check nap uniformity and color fastness by rubbing with a white cloth. Record all results in a log. Common defects include: orange peel (rough coating), fish eyes (craters), and bleeding (dye migration). To fix minor defects, re-buff and re-coat. For major defects, downgrade the leather. In many African markets, buyers reject leather with visible flaws. Train your workers to check at each stage. Use a simple checklist: color, gloss, feel, thickness, and defects. Keep samples of good and bad finishes for reference. QC is not about perfection; it's about consistency. Even a small workshop can implement a 5-minute inspection per hide. This builds trust with buyers and reduces waste. Always document your QC results and review them weekly to improve your process..Trim()

Effluent Treatment Basics

Tanning uses a lot of water and chemicals. The dirty water that comes out is called effluent. If you dump it straight into a river or on the ground, it kills fish, plants, and makes people sick. .Trim()

First, you need to separate your flows. Keep the beamhouse (soaking, liming, fleshing) wastewater separate from the tanning (chrome) wastewater. .Trim()

.Trim()

You can build a three-pit system: first pit for screening and settling, second for pH adjustment, third for polishing with sand and charcoal. Use a pH meter or pH paper (litmus) to check. Combination Tanning

Combination tanning means using two or more tanning agents together to get the best properties of each. In Africa, many tanneries use a combination of vegetable tanning and chrome tanning. For example, first tan with 5% wattle extract (based on pelt weight) for 2 hours, then add 2% chrome powder (25% basicity) and drum for another 4 hours. This gives leather that is firm like vegetable-tanned but more heat-resistant and softer like chrome-tanned. You can also combine aluminium with vegetable tannins for washable leather. Use a drum that rotates at 8-12 rpm. The process: after bating, you pickle the pelt with salt and acid (pH 2.5-3.0). Then add the first tanning agent, drum for 2-4 hours, then add the second. Check penetration by cutting a small piece and looking for a uniform colour. If the centre is white, drum longer. After tanning, basify with sodium bicarbonate to pH 3.8-4.2 for chrome combination, or pH 4.5 for vegetable-aluminium. Then wash and fatliquor with 6% sulphated oil. This technique is good for goatskins and sheepskins, common in Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa. It reduces chrome use, which cuts costs and waste. A typical 100 kg pelt batch uses 5 kg wattle extract and 2 kg chrome powder. You need a drum, pH meter, and weighing scale. Always add chemicals slowly through the drum door to avoid spots. Combination tanning is a practical way to upgrade your leather without buying expensive new equipment..Trim()

Chrome-free leather is popular for baby shoes, car interiors, and export to Europe. In Ethiopia and South Africa, some tanneries get premium prices for chrome-free leather. Microwave and ultrasound can speed up tanning and improve chemical penetration. Ultrasound uses sound waves above 20 kHz to create tiny bubbles that push chemicals into the pelt. For 100 kg of pelt, after pickling, add tanning agents and turn on ultrasound for 30 minutes instead of 2 hours of drumming. This saves time and energy.
