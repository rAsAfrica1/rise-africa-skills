Module 1 · Introduction to Avocado Farming

History and Global Importance of Avocado

The avocado originated in south-central Mexico and Central America, where it was cultivated as early as 5000 BC. The Aztecs called it 'ahuacatl', which means testicle, referring to its shape. Spanish explorers took it to Europe in the 16th century, and it spread to the Caribbean, Africa, and Asia. In Africa, avocado was introduced by Portuguese traders in the 17th century, first in West Africa, then later in East Africa. Today, Africa produces about 10% of the world's avocados, with Kenya, South Africa, and Rwanda being major growers. Global demand has skyrocketed: world production reached 8.5 million metric tons in 2022, up from 2.4 million in 1990. Mexico leads with 2.4 million tons, followed by Colombia, Peru, and Indonesia. Kenya is the largest African producer, exporting over 100,000 tons annually, mainly to Europe. Avocado is now a major export crop for many African countries, providing income for smallholder farmers. The fruit is rich in healthy fats, vitamins, and minerals, and is used in salads, dips, smoothies, and even cosmetics. Its rising popularity as a superfood has created a lucrative market. For African farmers, avocado farming offers a chance to tap into this growing global demand, especially with improved varieties and better market access. Understanding this history helps you appreciate the crop's potential and its journey to your farm..Trim()

Avocado Varieties and Cultivars

Avocado varieties fall into three main groups: Mexican, Guatemalan, and West Indian. Mexican varieties are cold-hardy and have small, thin-skinned fruits with high oil content. Guatemalan types have thick, rough skin and are more heat-tolerant. West Indian avocados are tropical, with large, smooth-skinned fruits. In Africa, the most popular commercial varieties are Hass, Fuerte, and Persea Americana. Hass is the world's leading variety, known for its creamy texture, high oil content (around 18-22%), and long shelf life. It thrives in warm, subtropical climates and is grown extensively in Kenya, South Africa, and Tanzania. Fuerte is a Mexican-Guatemalan hybrid with a pear shape and green skin; it is popular in South Africa and Zimbabwe. Other varieties include Pinkerton, Reed, and Lamb Hass. For African farmers, choosing the right variety depends on your climate, market demand, and rootstock. For example, in Kenya's cooler highlands, Hass and Fuerte do well, while in hotter lowlands, varieties like Simmonds or local West Indian types may be better. Grafting is common: you plant a rootstock (often a local variety) and graft a desired scion onto it. This ensures disease resistance and consistent fruit quality. Always buy certified seedlings from reputable nurseries to avoid diseases like avocado sunblotch viroid. With the right variety, you can harvest for years and sell to local or export markets..Trim()

Overview of the Avocado Industry

The global avocado industry is booming, valued at over $13 billion in 2022 and expected to reach $20 billion by 2030. Africa plays a growing role, with exports increasing by 15% annually. Kenya is the continent's top exporter, earning over $100 million from avocado exports in 2022. Other key players include South Africa, Tanzania, Rwanda, and Zimbabwe. The main markets are the European Union (especially Netherlands, France, and UK), the Middle East, and increasingly China and Japan. In Africa, the industry is dominated by smallholder farmers who supply 70% of production, often through cooperatives. Basic Terminology in Avocado Farming

Understanding key terms will help you communicate with other farmers, extension officers, and buyers. Here are essential avocado farming terms: Grafting: joining a scion (desired variety) to a rootstock to create a new tree. Rootstock: the lower part of a grafted tree that provides roots and disease resistance. Scion: the upper part that produces the fruit. Cultivar: a cultivated variety, like Hass or Fuerte. Orchard: a plantation of fruit trees. Canopy: the upper branches and leaves of a tree. Mulching: covering soil around the tree with organic matter to conserve moisture. Drip irrigation: a system that delivers water directly to roots. Phytophthora: a soil fungus that causes root rot. Sunblotch: a viroid disease causing yellow streaks on fruit and bark. Pollination: transfer of pollen for fruit set; avocado needs cross-pollination for some varieties. Alternate bearing: a tendency to produce heavy crop one year and light the next. Harvest maturity: when fruit reaches oil content of 8-10% for Hass. Packhouse: facility where fruits are washed, graded, and packed. GlobalGAP: a certification for good agricultural practices. Farmgate price: price paid to farmer at the farm. Export market: selling to other countries. Local market: selling within your country. Cooperative: a farmer group that markets collectively. Extension officer: government or NGO advisor. Knowing these terms helps you access information, training, and markets. For example, when a buyer asks for 'Hass, 12-count, GlobalGAP certified', you'll understand. Keep a notebook of new terms and ask questions. This knowledge is the foundation for successful avocado farming..Trim()

Module 3 · Avocado Propagation Techniques

Seed Selection and Germination

To grow a strong avocado tree, start with the best seed. Choose seeds from healthy, high-yielding local trees or from known varieties like Hass or Fuerte. Avoid seeds from fruits that are diseased or misshapen. After eating the avocado, wash the seed thoroughly to remove any flesh, as this can cause rot. Soak the seed in water for 24 hours to soften the outer skin. Then, peel off the brown skin carefully; this helps the seed germinate faster. Next, you need to germinate the seed. There are two common methods: in water or in soil. For the water method, stick three toothpicks into the sides of the seed and suspend it over a glass of water, with the pointed end up and the flat end down, just touching the water. Place it in a warm, bright spot but out of direct sunlight. Change the water every week. Roots should appear in 2-4 weeks, and a shoot in 6-8 weeks. For the soil method, fill a pot with well-draining soil mix (like 1 part sand, 1 part compost, 1 part topsoil). Plant the seed with the pointed end up, leaving the top third exposed. Water regularly to keep the soil moist but not waterlogged. Germination takes 4-8 weeks. In Africa, many farmers use the soil method because it's simpler and avoids disturbing the roots later. Keep the pots in a shaded area or nursery. Once the seedling has 2-3 leaves, it's ready for potting on or grafting. Remember, seeds from hybrid avocados won't produce the same fruit, so for consistent quality, you'll need to graft. But seed selection and germination are the first steps to getting rootstocks for grafting..Trim()

Grafting Methods: Whip and Cleft

Grafting joins a scion (the desired variety) to a rootstock (the seedling) so they grow as one tree. The whip (or splice) graft is best for rootstocks and scions of similar thickness (about 1-2 cm). Use a sharp, clean knife or grafting tool. Make a smooth, diagonal cut about 3-5 cm long on both the rootstock and the scion. The cuts should match perfectly. Then, make a small vertical slit at the top of the rootstock cut and a corresponding tongue on the scion to interlock. Fit them together so the cambium layers (the green layer under the bark) align on at least one side. Wrap tightly with grafting tape or polythene strip, leaving the bud exposed. The cleft graft works when the rootstock is thicker than the scion. Cut the rootstock horizontally, then make a vertical split about 5 cm deep in the center. Sharpen the scion into a wedge shape and insert it into the split, ensuring the cambium matches on one side. Wrap and seal with tape. For both methods, cover the graft with a plastic bag to maintain humidity, but remove it after 2-3 weeks when the scion shows signs of growth. Keep the grafted plant in a shaded, humid place. Success rates are higher in warm weather (20-30Â°C). In Africa, whip grafting is common for young seedlings, while cleft grafting is used for top-working older trees. Always sterilize tools with alcohol or bleach to prevent disease. After grafting, remove any shoots from the rootstock below the graft to direct energy to the scion. With practice, you can achieve 80-90% success..Trim()

Budding Techniques

Budding is a form of grafting where a single bud, rather than a whole scion, is joined to the rootstock. It's useful when scion wood is scarce. The most common method for avocados is patch budding or T-budding. For T-budding, select a healthy bud from a mature branch of the desired variety. Make a T-shaped cut in the rootstock bark about 15-20 cm above the soil. Gently lift the bark flaps. Cut a bud from the scion with a small piece of wood and leaf stalk attached. Slide the bud into the T-cut and press the bark flaps back over it. Wrap with budding tape, leaving the bud exposed. After 2-3 weeks, if the bud is green and plump, it has taken. Cut the rootstock about 5 cm above the bud to force the bud to grow. Patch budding is similar but uses a rectangular patch of bark with a bud. Budding is faster and less damaging than grafting, and you can bud multiple rootstocks from one scion stick. However, it requires skill and clean tools. In Africa, budding is often done during the rainy season when the bark slips easily. Keep the budded plants in a nursery with partial shade. Remove any shoots from the rootstock below the bud. Once the bud grows into a shoot, you can train it into a new tree. Budding success rates can be 70-90% with practice. Always use disease-free budwood..Trim()

Rootstock Selection

The rootstock is the foundation of your avocado tree. It determines the tree's size, disease resistance, and tolerance to soil conditions. In Africa, common rootstocks include seedlings of local varieties like 'Fuerte' or 'Hass', but these may be susceptible to root rot (Phytophthora). For better resistance, use clonal rootstocks like 'Dusa' or 'Bounty', which are propagated from cuttings. However, these may not be available everywhere. If you're using seeds, choose seeds from trees that are healthy and productive. Avoid seeds from trees with root rot. The rootstock should be grown in well-draining soil to prevent waterlogging. For areas with heavy clay, consider grafting onto rootstocks that tolerate wet feet, such as 'Duke 7'. Also, consider the soil pH: avocados prefer 5.5-6.5. If your soil is too alkaline, you may need to add organic matter. In Africa, many smallholders use seedling rootstocks because they are cheap and easy. But for commercial orchards, clonal rootstocks are worth the investment. When selecting rootstocks, also consider the size: dwarfing rootstocks like 'Dusa' can keep trees small for high-density planting. Always source rootstock seeds from reputable suppliers or your own best trees. Test for root rot by planting in a small trial area. Remember, the rootstock is the base; a good one ensures a long-lived, productive tree..Trim()

Hardening off prepares nursery seedlings for the harsh conditions of the field. Start 2-3 weeks before transplanting. Reduce watering gradually, but don't let them wilt. Essential Nutrients for Avocado

Avocado trees need 16 essential nutrients to grow well and produce good fruit. The main ones are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), sulfur (S), zinc (Zn), boron (B), iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), copper (Cu), molybdenum (Mo), chlorine (Cl), nickel (Ni), and oxygen, hydrogen, and carbon from air and water. In Africa, many soils are old and weathered, so they often lack nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, zinc, and boron. For example, sandy soils in Ghana or Kenya may have less than 0.1% nitrogen, which is very low. Avocado trees need these nutrients for specific jobs. Nitrogen makes leaves green and helps trees grow. Phosphorus helps roots and flowers. Potassium helps fruit size and quality. Zinc prevents small, mottled leaves. Boron helps fruit set and prevents misshapen fruit. Iron keeps leaves green in high-pH soils. Without enough nutrients, trees grow slowly, produce few fruits, and fruits may be small or drop early. You can see signs: yellow leaves mean lack of nitrogen; purple leaves mean lack of phosphorus; small leaves with yellow spots mean lack of zinc. But sometimes signs are not clear. That's why soil and leaf testing is important. As a smallholder, you can start by applying manure or compost, which provides many nutrients. But to get high yields, you may need to add specific fertilizers. Remember, too much of a nutrient can also harm trees. For example, too much nitrogen can cause lots of leaves but few fruits. Too much boron can kill leaves. So always follow recommendations from soil tests or local extension officers. In Africa, organizations like the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organization (KALRO) and the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA) provide guides for avocado nutrition. Start by learning the signs of deficiency and apply nutrients carefully..Trim()

Soil and Leaf Tissue Testing

Soil and leaf testing tell you exactly what nutrients your avocado trees need. Without testing, you are guessing. In Africa, many farmers skip testing because they think it is expensive or difficult. But it can save you money and increase yields. Soil testing: Take soil samples from around the tree canopy, about 30 cm deep, from 10-15 spots in your orchard. Mix them in a clean bucket, then take about 500g to a laboratory. In Kenya, you can send samples to KALRO or private labs like Crop Nutrition Laboratory Services (Cropnuts). The test costs about 2,000-5,000 KES (20-50 USD). The report will show soil pH, organic matter, and levels of nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, zinc, and boron. It will also give recommendations on how much fertilizer to apply. Leaf testing: This shows what the tree has actually taken up. Take leaf samples from the middle of the current season's growth, from 4-6 month old leaves. Collect 20-30 leaves from different trees, avoid diseased or sunburnt leaves. Send them to a lab. Leaf testing is best done when trees are not flushing new leaves or fruiting heavily. The lab will compare nutrient levels to standard ranges. For avocado, optimal leaf nitrogen is 2.0-2.5%, phosphorus 0.1-0.3%, potassium 1.0-2.0%, zinc 30-100 ppm, boron 20-60 ppm. If levels are below, you need to fertilize. If above, reduce. In Africa, leaf testing is less common but very useful. Some labs like Cropnuts in Kenya offer both soil and leaf testing. If you cannot afford testing, you can use visual symptoms and local recommendations, but testing is more accurate. Always test before planting and then every 1-2 years. This helps you avoid over-fertilizing, which wastes money and can pollute water..Trim()

Fertilizer Types and Application Methods

Fertilizers come in many forms: inorganic (mineral) and organic (manure, compost). In Africa, common inorganic fertilizers include urea (46% N), calcium ammonium nitrate (CAN, 27% N), triple superphosphate (TSP, 46% P2O5), muriate of potash (MOP, 60% K2O), and compound fertilizers like NPK 17-17-17. Organic fertilizers include cattle manure (about 0.5% N, 0.2% P, 0.5% K), poultry manure (3% N, 2% P, 1% K), and compost. Application methods: 1) Broadcasting: Spreading fertilizer evenly under the tree canopy, then incorporating into soil. Good for phosphorus and potassium. 2) Banding: Placing fertilizer in a band around the drip line. 3) Fertigation: Injecting soluble fertilizers into irrigation water. Common in South Africa and increasingly in Kenya. 4) Foliar spraying: Spraying diluted fertilizer on leaves, good for quick correction of micronutrient deficiencies like zinc and boron. For young trees (1-3 years), apply 100-200g of NPK 17-17-17 per tree per year, split into 2-3 applications. For mature trees (5+ years), apply 1-2 kg of NPK per tree per year, plus additional nitrogen. Always apply fertilizer when soil is moist, not dry. Avoid placing fertilizer directly against the trunk, as it can burn. For organic manure, apply 10-20 kg per mature tree per year, spread under the canopy. In Africa, many farmers use manure because it is cheap and improves soil. But manure alone may not supply enough nutrients for high yields. Combine organic and inorganic for best results. Always read fertilizer labels and follow recommended rates. Over-application can harm trees and waste money..Trim()

Macronutrient Management (N, P, K)

Nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) are needed in large amounts. Nitrogen: Avocado trees need nitrogen for leaf growth and fruit production. A mature tree needs about 0.5-1.0 kg of actual N per year. In Kenya, common sources are urea (46% N) or CAN (27% N). Apply in split doses: one-third at flowering, one-third at fruit set, one-third after harvest. Too little N causes yellow leaves and poor growth; too much causes excessive leaf growth and few fruits. Phosphorus: Important for root development and flowering. Avocado trees need about 0.1-0.2 kg P2O5 per year. Apply as TSP or in NPK blends. Phosphorus is less mobile in soil, so place it near roots. In acidic soils (pH below 5.5), phosphorus is less available; apply lime to raise pH to 6.0-6.5. Potassium: Needed for fruit size, quality, and disease resistance. A mature tree needs about 0.5-1.0 kg K2O per year. Apply as MOP or potassium sulfate. Potassium deficiency causes small fruits and leaf scorch. In sandy soils, potassium leaches easily, so split applications. In Africa, many soils are deficient in N and P, but K may be adequate in some volcanic soils. Use soil tests to guide. For example, if soil test shows low N, apply 1 kg urea per tree; if low P, apply 0.5 kg TSP; if low K, apply 0.8 kg MOP. Always adjust based on tree age and yield. Remember, balanced nutrition is key. Too much of one nutrient can affect uptake of others. For instance, high potassium can reduce magnesium uptake. So follow soil test recommendations and apply in splits. In Kenya, KALRO recommends for mature avocado: 1 kg NPK 17-17-17 per tree per year plus 0.5 kg urea, split into three applications..Trim()

Micronutrient Management (Zn, B, Fe)

Micronutrients are needed in small amounts but are critical. Zinc (Zn), boron (B), and iron (Fe) are the most common deficiencies in African avocado orchards. Zinc deficiency: Causes small, narrow leaves with yellow spots between veins (mottled). New leaves are affected first. In Kenya, many soils are deficient in zinc. To correct, spray trees with zinc sulfate (0.5% solution, i.e., 50g in 10 liters of water) 2-3 times per year, at flushing and after fruit set. You can also apply zinc sulfate to soil at 20-50g per tree per year. Boron deficiency: Causes misshapen fruits, corky areas inside, and dieback of growing tips. Boron is toxic in excess, so be careful. Apply borax or boric acid at 10-20g per mature tree per year, broadcast under canopy. For quick correction, spray 0.1% borax solution (10g in 10 liters). Do not exceed recommended rates. Iron deficiency: Causes yellow leaves with green veins (interveinal chlorosis), especially on high-pH soils (pH above 7.0). Common in parts of Kenya and Tanzania. To correct, lower soil pH by adding elemental sulfur or organic matter. Spray with chelated iron (Fe-EDDHA) at 0.5g per liter, 2-3 times. In Africa, micronutrient deficiencies are often linked to soil erosion and continuous cropping without replenishment. Regular leaf testing helps detect deficiencies early. For smallholders, a simple approach is to apply well-decomposed manure, which contains micronutrients. But for severe deficiencies, specific sprays are needed. Always follow label instructions and avoid over-application, especially boron, which can kill trees. In South Africa, commercial growers use fertigation with micronutrient mixes. In Kenya, KALRO recommends foliar sprays of zinc and boron for avocado. Remember, healthy trees with good organic matter are less likely to have micronutrient problems..Trim()

Organic Fertilization Strategies

Organic fertilization uses natural materials like manure, compost, and cover crops to feed avocado trees. It improves soil health, water holding capacity, and nutrient availability. In Africa, where inorganic fertilizers can be expensive, organic strategies are essential. Manure: Cattle, goat, poultry, and rabbit manure are good sources. Apply 10-20 kg of well-decomposed cattle manure per mature tree per year, spread under the canopy but away from the trunk. Poultry manure is richer; use 5-10 kg per tree. Fresh manure can burn roots, so compost it first. Compost: Make compost from crop residues, kitchen waste, and animal manure. A good compost has about 1-2% nitrogen. Apply 20-30 kg per tree per year. Compost also adds organic matter, which helps sandy soils hold nutrients. Cover crops: Plant legumes like beans, cowpeas, or lablab between trees. They fix nitrogen from the air and add organic matter when cut. In Kenya, farmers use desmodium and mucuna. Mulching: Spread dry grass, leaves, or crop residues around the tree base. Mulch conserves moisture, suppresses weeds, and adds nutrients as it decomposes. Green manures: Grow fast-growing plants like tithonia (Mexican sunflower) and incorporate into soil. Tithonia is rich in nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. In Africa, many farmers use tithonia as a cheap fertilizer. Liquid fertilizers: Make compost tea or manure tea by soaking manure in water for 1-2 weeks, then dilute and apply to soil or leaves. This gives a quick nutrient boost. Combining organic and inorganic: For best results, combine organic materials with small amounts of inorganic fertilizers. For example, apply manure plus 200g of NPK per tree. This provides both immediate and long-term nutrients. Organic farming also improves soil life, which helps roots access nutrients. In Ethiopia, a study showed that combining compost with NPK increased avocado yields by 30% compared to NPK alone. Start by making compost and using manure from your animals. Over time, you will see healthier trees and better fruits..Trim()
