Module 2 · Basic Greetings and Introductions

Common Greetings: Hello, Goodbye, Good Morning

In Arabic, greetings change depending on the time of day and formality. The most common hello is 'As-salaam alaykum' (peace be upon you), replied with 'Wa alaykum as-salaam'. For a simple hi, use 'Marhaba' or 'Ahlan'. Good morning is 'Sabah al-khayr' (morning of goodness), replied with 'Sabah an-nur' (morning of light). Good evening is 'Masa al-khayr', replied with 'Masa an-nur'. Goodbye is 'Ma'a as-salaama' (go with safety), replied with 'Allah yisallimak' or simply 'Ma'a as-salaama'. In Africa, especially in countries like Egypt, Sudan, and Morocco, you'll hear these daily. For example, in Cairo, a shopkeeper might greet you with 'Ahlan wa sahlan' (welcome). In Zanzibar, Swahili greetings mix with Arabic: 'Habari' is common, but Arabic 'Marhaba' is understood. Practice with a partner: greet at different times of day. Record yourself on your phone and compare to native audio on YouTube. Use apps like Duolingo or Memrise for repetition. Write each greeting on a flashcard with the English on the back. Review 5 minutes daily. In 3 days, you'll know 6 greetings. In 1 week, you can greet anyone in Arabic. Remember: in Africa, greetings are long and friendly. Don't rush. Ask about family, health, and work. 'As-salaam alaykum' opens doors. Try it with a neighbor from Sudan or Somalia. They'll smile and reply. That's your first real conversation..Trim()

Introducing Yourself: Name, Origin, and Nationality

To introduce yourself in Arabic, start with 'Ismi' (my name is) followed by your name. For example, 'Ismi Amina' means 'My name is Amina'. To ask someone's name, say 'Ma ismuka?' (to a male) or 'Ma ismuki?' (to a female). To say where you are from, use 'Ana min' (I am from) plus your country. 'Ana min Nigeria' means 'I am from Nigeria'. For nationality, Arabic uses a suffix: 'Nigeriyy' (Nigerian male), 'Nigeriyya' (Nigerian female). Similarly, 'Misri' (Egyptian male), 'Misriyya' (Egyptian female). In Africa, you might say 'Ana min Kenya' (I am from Kenya) or 'Ana Sudani' (I am Sudanese). To ask 'Where are you from?' say 'Min ayna anta?' (male) or 'Min ayna anti?' (female). Practice with a friend: swap names, countries, and nationalities. Use a mirror to watch your mouth movements. Record a 30-second introduction on your phone. Listen for mistakes. Use online resources like ArabicPod101 or Forvo for pronunciation. Write your introduction in Arabic script and transliteration. For example: 'As-salaam alaykum. Ismi John. Ana min Ghana. Ana Ghanawi.' (Hello. My name is John. I am from Ghana. I am Ghanaian.) Repeat until fluent. In Africa, knowing how to introduce yourself in Arabic helps in business, travel, and making friends. Polite Expressions: Please, Thank You, Excuse Me

In Arabic, 'please' is 'Min fadlak' (to a male) or 'Min fadlik' (to a female). 'Thank you' is 'Shukran'. The response is 'Afwan' (you're welcome). 'Excuse me' is 'Afwan' as well, but for getting attention, say 'Law samaht' (if you please, male) or 'Law samahti' (female). For 'I'm sorry', say 'Asif' (male) or 'Asifa' (female). In Africa, these phrases are essential in markets, taxis, and offices. For example, in Morocco, when you want to pass, say 'Law samaht'. In Egypt, 'Shukran' is used constantly. To say 'Thank you very much', say 'Shukran jazeelan'. To say 'No, thank you', say 'La, shukran'. Practice with role-plays: buying bread, asking for directions, apologizing for being late. Days of the Week and Months

In Arabic, days of the week are: al-ithnayn (Monday), ath-thulatha (Tuesday), al-arbi'a (Wednesday), al-khamis (Thursday), al-jum'a (Friday), as-sabt (Saturday), al-ahad (Sunday). Note that Friday is the holy day, and Sunday is 'the first'. Months in Arabic follow two systems: Gregorian and Islamic. Gregorian months are: Yanayir (January), Fibrayir (February), Maris (March), Abril (April), Mayu (May), Yunyu (June), Yulyu (July), Aghustus (August), Sibtambar (September), Oktubar (October), Nufambar (November), Disambar (December). Islamic months are: Muharram, Safar, Rabi' al-awwal, Rabi' al-thani, Jumada al-ula, Jumada al-akhira, Rajab, Sha'ban, Ramadan, Shawwal, Dhu al-Qi'dah, Dhu al-Hijjah. In Africa, both are used. For example, in Egypt, Ramadan is a key month. In Morocco, Gregorian months are common in business. Practice saying the days in order. Use a calendar and point to each day. Write the Arabic name next to the English. Use songs or rhymes to memorize. For months, learn the Gregorian first, then Islamic. In 3 days, you'll know the days. In 1 week, the months. Use them in sentences: 'Al-yawm al-ithnayn' (Today is Monday). 'Shahru Ramadan' (the month of Ramadan). In Africa, knowing days and months helps with appointments, holidays, and business. Try scheduling a meeting in Arabic: 'Al-jum'a, min fadlak' (Friday, please)..Trim()

Nominative, Accusative, and Genitive Cases

In Arabic, nouns and adjectives change their endings based on their role in the sentence. This is called 'i'rab' or case. The three main cases are nominative (raf'), accusative (nasb), and genitive (jarr). For example, 'the teacher wrote the lesson' is 'kataba al-mu'allimu ad-darsa'. Here 'al-mu'allimu' (the teacher) is nominative because it's the subject, ending in -u. 'Ad-darsa' (the lesson) is accusative because it's the object, ending in -a. If we say 'I went to the teacher', 'dhahabtu ila al-mu'allimi', 'al-mu'allimi' is genitive because it follows the preposition 'ila', ending in -i. In African business contexts, you might use these cases when writing emails or contracts. For instance, 'The company signed the contract' â 'waqqa'at ash-sharikatu al-'aqda'. Here 'ash-sharikatu' is nominative (subject) and 'al-'aqda' is accusative (object). If you say 'The contract of the company', 'al-'aqdu ash-sharikati', 'ash-sharikati' is genitive because it's the second part of a possessive construction (idafa).
