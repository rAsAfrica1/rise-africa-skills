Module 1 · Introduction to Cooking Oils and Production Overview

History and Global Importance of Cooking Oils

Cooking oils have been extracted for thousands of years. In Africa, ancient Egyptians pressed castor and sesame seeds as early as 3000 BC. Today, palm oil from West Africa and olive oil from North Africa are major global commodities. Worldwide, we consume over 200 million tonnes of cooking oil annually. The most produced oils are palm (75 million tonnes), soybean (55 million), and rapeseed (25 million). In Africa, palm oil is dominant, with Nigeria, Ghana, and CÃ´te d'Ivoire being major producers. Small-scale oil pressing is a common income-generating activity. A manual press can process 5-10 kg of seeds per hour, yielding 2-3 litres of oil. The oil is used for cooking, cosmetics, and soap making. The demand for cooking oil is growing due to population growth and urbanization. This creates opportunities for local producers. However, Africa still imports large quantities of refined oil, so local production can reduce imports and create jobs. Starting a small oil business requires understanding the raw materials, extraction methods, and market. With basic equipment like a manual press or a small motorized expeller, you can produce oil for your community. The key is to focus on quality and consistency. For example, in Tanzania, small-scale sunflower oil producers have formed cooperatives to share equipment and access markets. They earn 20-30% more than selling raw seeds. In Kenya, women's groups press coconut oil for cooking and cosmetics, selling in local markets and online. These examples show that oil production can be a viable business. The global market for cooking oil is expected to reach $300 billion by 2027, so there is room for new entrants. By learning the basics, you can tap into this growing industry..Trim()

Types of Cooking Oils: Vegetable, Seed, Nut, and Fruit Oils

Cooking oils come from four main sources: vegetables, seeds, nuts, and fruits. Vegetable oils are extracted from the flesh of plants, like palm oil from palm fruit and olive oil from olives. Seed oils come from seeds: sunflower, groundnut, sesame, cottonseed, and soybean. Nut oils include coconut, shea, and peanut (groundnut is a legume but often grouped with nuts). Fruit oils are from fruits like avocado and palm. In Africa, palm oil is the most common fruit oil, while groundnut and sunflower are common seed oils. Each oil has different properties. Palm oil is semi-solid at room temperature and high in saturated fat, making it good for frying. Sunflower oil is high in polyunsaturated fats and is liquid at room temperature. Groundnut oil has a high smoke point (230Â°C) and is ideal for deep-frying. Coconut oil is high in saturated fat and solid below 24Â°C. Shea butter is not a cooking oil but is used in cosmetics and sometimes cooking. The extraction method varies: fruit oils are often pressed from the pericarp, while seed oils require cleaning, dehulling, and grinding before pressing. For example, to extract sunflower oil, you clean the seeds, remove foreign matter, then press them in an expeller. The oil is then filtered. For palm oil, you sterilize the fruit, strip it, and press the mesocarp. The choice of oil depends on local availability and market demand. In West Africa, red palm oil is preferred for its color and flavor. In East Africa, sunflower and groundnut oils are popular. Understanding these types helps you choose which oil to produce based on your region's crops and customer preferences..Trim()

Overview of Oil Production Processes

Oil production involves several steps: cleaning, preparation, extraction, and refining. For small-scale producers, the process starts with cleaning raw materials. For seeds like sunflower or groundnut, you remove stones, dirt, and damaged seeds. A simple winnowing or sieving can do this. Next, you may dehull seeds (remove the shell) to increase oil yield. For groundnuts, dehulling is done manually or with a dehuller. Then, you grind the seeds into a paste using a grinder or mortar and pestle. The paste is then pressed to extract oil. There are two main methods: manual pressing and mechanical pressing. Manual presses, like the ram press, can extract 60-70% of the oil. Motorized expellers can extract 80-90%. For example, a small motorized expeller (5-10 HP) can process 50-100 kg of seeds per hour. After pressing, the oil is filtered to remove particles. You can use a filter cloth or a filter press. The remaining cake is a valuable byproduct for animal feed. For fruit oils like palm, the process is different: you sterilize the fruit bunches, strip the fruits, and press the mesocarp. The oil is then clarified by boiling or settling. Refining is optional for small-scale producers. It involves degumming, neutralization, bleaching, and deodorization to remove impurities and improve shelf life. However, many local markets prefer unrefined oil for its flavor and color. For example, unrefined red palm oil is prized in West Africa. The equipment needed includes a cleaner, grinder, press, and filter. You can start with a manual press for under $500, or a motorized expeller for $2,000-$5,000. The key is to ensure proper cleaning and pressing to maximize yield and quality..Trim()

Key Quality Parameters and Standards

Quality determines whether your oil sells. Key parameters include free fatty acid (FFA), peroxide value, moisture content, color, and flavor. FFA measures how much the oil has broken down; lower is better. Good cooking oil should have FFA below 0.5% for refined oil, and below 2% for unrefined. Peroxide value indicates oxidation; it should be below 10 meq/kg. Moisture content should be below 0.1% to prevent spoilage. Color and flavor depend on the oil type and market preference. For example, red palm oil should have a deep red color, while sunflower oil should be light yellow. In Africa, many countries have standards for cooking oil. The East African Community (EAC) standard for edible oils specifies FFA max 0.5%, moisture max 0.1%, and peroxide max 10. The Nigerian Industrial Standard (NIS) for palm oil requires FFA max 5% for unrefined. To test quality, you can use simple methods: FFA can be tested with a titration kit, moisture with a moisture meter, and peroxide with test strips. For small-scale producers, regular testing ensures consistency. You should also follow hygiene practices: clean equipment, use food-grade containers, and store oil in a cool, dark place. Packaging is important: use opaque bottles or jerrycans to protect from light. Label with production date and oil type. In Kenya, the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) certifies edible oils, and getting certification can boost sales. Even without formal certification, maintaining quality builds trust. For example, a producer in Uganda who consistently tests FFA and moisture can command a higher price. Remember, poor quality oil can be harmful; rancid oil contains free radicals. So invest in basic testing and good manufacturing practices..Trim()

Major Oilseed and Oilfruit Crops

Africa grows many oil-rich crops. The major ones are palm, groundnut, sunflower, sesame, coconut, shea, and cottonseed. Palm is the highest yielding, producing 3-5 tonnes of oil per hectare. It grows in tropical regions, especially West and Central Africa. Nigeria is the largest producer, with over 1 million tonnes annually. Groundnut is grown in West Africa, with Senegal, Nigeria, and Sudan as major producers. It yields about 1-2 tonnes of oil per hectare. Sunflower is important in East and Southern Africa, especially Tanzania, South Africa, and Zambia. It yields 0.5-1 tonne of oil per hectare. Sesame is grown in Ethiopia, Sudan, and Nigeria; it is high-value and drought-tolerant. Coconut is grown in coastal areas like Ghana, Mozambique, and Tanzania. Shea trees grow in West Africa's savannah, and shea butter is extracted from the nuts. Cottonseed oil is a byproduct of cotton production in countries like Burkina Faso, Mali, and Egypt. Each crop has specific requirements. Palm needs 1500-2000 mm rainfall and temperatures above 24Â°C. Groundnut needs 500-1000 mm rainfall and sandy loam soil. Sunflower is drought-tolerant but needs 300-500 mm rainfall. For small-scale producers, choosing the right crop depends on local availability and market demand. For example, in Tanzania, sunflower is widely grown, so setting up a sunflower oil press near farms ensures a steady supply. In Ghana, palm fruit is abundant, so palm oil production is common. You can also use multiple crops to spread risk. For instance, a producer in Uganda might press sunflower in one season and groundnut in another. The key is to source raw materials locally to reduce transport costs. Also, consider the oil content: palm mesocarp has 45-55% oil, groundnut 40-50%, sunflower 35-45%, sesame 45-50%, coconut 60-70%, and shea 45-55%. Knowing these helps estimate yield and profitability..Trim()

Safety and Regulatory Considerations in Oil Production

Safety is critical in oil production. You must protect yourself and your workers from hazards. First, ensure proper ventilation when heating oil; fumes can be harmful. Use gloves and goggles when handling chemicals or hot oil. Keep equipment clean to prevent contamination. For example, if you use solvents like hexane (in large-scale refining), you need proper ventilation and fire safety. Small-scale producers usually avoid solvents, using mechanical pressing instead. Fire safety is important: keep a fire extinguisher or sand bucket near the press. Oil is flammable, so never leave heating oil unattended. Also, ensure electrical equipment is properly grounded. Regulatory compliance is essential for selling oil. In many African countries, you need a business permit and a food safety certificate. For example, in Kenya, you must register with the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) and get a permit from the county government. In Nigeria, you need NAFDAC registration for packaged oil. These regulations ensure your oil is safe for consumption. They cover hygiene, labeling, and quality. For small-scale producers, you can start by selling informally, but to grow, you should comply. The process involves submitting samples for testing and paying fees. It may take time, but it opens markets. Also, follow environmental regulations: dispose of waste properly. The seed cake can be used as animal feed, but wastewater from processing should not be dumped into rivers. In some countries, there are taxes or levies on oil production. For example, in Ghana, there is a 1% levy on palm oil exports. Stay informed about local laws. Finally, keep records of production and sales for tax purposes. By prioritizing safety and compliance, you build a sustainable business and protect your community..Trim()

Cleaning and Dehulling of Oilseeds

Before you press any oilseed, you must clean it. Dirt, stones, sand, and plant trash will damage your expeller and make dark, smelly oil. Start by winnowing: pour seeds from one basket to another in a breeze to blow away chaff and light rubbish. For groundnuts, sorghum, and sunflower, a simple vibrating screen or a hand sieve with 2-3 mm holes removes fines and small stones. For larger impurities like sticks and metal, pass a magnet over the seed or use a destoner. A good target is less than 1% foreign matter..Trim()

Dehulling means removing the seed coat. For groundnuts, you can do this by hand rubbing after drying, or use a groundnut decorticator. For sunflower, the hull is tough; a roller mill or impact dehuller works. For sesame, dehulling is often skipped because the hull is thin and oil is high quality. For palm kernel, you crack the shell with a nutcracker or a palm kernel cracker and separate the kernel from shell in a salt water bath (shells float, kernels sink)..Trim()

.Trim()

After dehulling, separate hulls from kernels. A winnowing fan or a vibrating screen does this. Hulls can be used for animal feed or fuel. If you leave hulls on, they absorb oil and reduce yield by 5-10%. For small-scale operations, a simple aspiration system (a fan pulling air through a cyclone) is enough..Trim()

.Trim()

Moisture matters. If seeds are too wet, they stick and clog. If too dry, they shatter and make too many fines. For groundnuts, aim for 6-8% moisture. Test by biting: the kernel should be firm but not brittle. For sunflower, 7-9% is good. Dry seeds in the sun on a clean tarp for 2-3 days, turning every few hours, or use a mechanical dryer at 40-50Â°C..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, store cleaned and dehulled seeds in clean, dry sacks in a cool, dry place. Never store on bare ground. Use pallets. This preparation step is the foundation of good oil yield and quality. Skip it, and you lose money..Trim()

Size Reduction: Grinding and Flaking

Size reduction breaks oilseeds into smaller pieces so oil can escape easily during pressing. For groundnuts, you want to grind them into a coarse meal, not a fine powder. A hammer mill with a 4-6 mm screen works well. If you grind too fine, the meal turns into paste and clogs the expeller. For sunflower, you flake the seeds: pass them through a roller mill with a gap of 0.2-0.3 mm. The flakes should be thin, like cornflakes, so cells rupture and oil flows..Trim()

.Trim()

For copra (dried coconut), you chop it into small pieces then grind in a hammer mill. For palm kernel, you grind to a coarse grit. For sesame, you can grind lightly or skip grinding and press whole. The rule: soft seeds like groundnut need coarse grinding; hard seeds like sunflower and palm kernel need flaking or fine grinding..Trim()

.Trim()

A simple hand-cranked grinder can work for small batches, but for 100 kg per hour, you need a motorized mill. A 5 HP hammer mill can handle 200-300 kg per hour. Set the screen size: 4 mm for groundnut, 3 mm for copra, 2 mm for palm kernel. Always check the particle size: if you see whole seeds or large chunks, adjust the mill..Trim()

.Trim()

Flaking requires a roller mill with two smooth rollers. The gap should be slightly less than the seed thickness. For sunflower, a gap of 0.25 mm gives good flakes. For soybeans, 0.3 mm. If flakes are too thick, oil extraction is poor. If too thin, you get fines that clog. Run a test: take a handful of flakes and squeeze; they should feel flat and slightly oily..Trim()

.Trim()

After grinding or flaking, use the material immediately. Do not let it sit for hours because oil can oxidize and become rancid. If you must store, keep it in a cool, airtight container for no more than 24 hours. For best yield, go straight to conditioning..Trim()

Conditioning and Tempering of Oilseeds

Conditioning means adjusting the moisture and temperature of the prepared seed before pressing. This step can increase oil yield by 10-20%. The goal is to make the oil less viscous and the cell walls easier to rupture. For most seeds, you heat and add or remove moisture to reach a specific range..Trim()

Solvent Extraction Feed Preparation

Solvent extraction is used for seeds with low oil content (like soybeans, rice bran, cottonseed) or to recover residual oil from expeller cake. The feed to a solvent plant must be prepared carefully. First, the material must be flaked or ground to a uniform size. For soybeans, flakes should be 0.2-0.3 mm thick. For rice bran, grind to pass a 20-mesh screen. Moisture is critical. The feed should be 8-12% moisture for most solvents. Too wet, and the solvent won't penetrate; too dry, and fines are created. Use a dryer or conditioner to adjust. For small-scale, you can sun-dry on a tarp, but for consistent results, use a rotary dryer..Trim()

Temperature also matters. The feed should be at 50-60Â°C when entering the extractor. This helps solvent penetrate and oil dissolve. Preheat the feed in a steam-jacketed conveyor or a simple heated trough. Do not exceed 70Â°C or you may degrade oil quality..Trim()

Remove fines. Fines clog the extractor and cause channeling. Use a vibrating screen or a cyclone to remove particles smaller than 0.5 mm. You can pelletize fines or mix them back into the main feed at a low ratio (less than 5%)..Trim()

For solvent extraction, the feed must be free of stones, metal, and other debris. A magnet and a destoner are essential. Also, ensure the feed is not too oily. If oil content is above 20%, pre-press with an expeller to reduce oil to 12-15%. Then the solvent can extract the rest..Trim()

Finally, store prepared feed in a sealed bin under nitrogen or with a blanketing gas to prevent oxidation. Use within 24 hours. If you are a small producer, solvent extraction is usually not economical; it is for large-scale plants (above 50 tonnes per day). First, keep everything dry. Moisture above 10% invites mold and bacteria. For groundnut meal, target 6-8% moisture. For sunflower flakes, 7-9%. For copra, 5-7%. Second, control temperature. Cool, dark conditions slow oxidation. Ideal storage is 15-25Â°C. Avoid direct sunlight and hot rooms. In hot African climates, store in a shaded, ventilated room. Third, keep out pests. Rodents, insects, and birds love oilseeds. Use metal bins with tight lids. For sacks, stack them on shelves or pallets, not against walls. Use traps or cats. Fourth, limit storage time. Prepared materials should be pressed within 24-48 hours for best yield and quality. If you must store longer, keep whole seeds instead of ground or flaked material. Principle of Mechanical Oil Extraction

Mechanical oil extraction is the process of squeezing oil out of oil-bearing seeds, nuts, or fruits using physical pressure, without chemicals. It works because oil is stored in tiny pockets inside the plant cells. When you apply enough pressure, the cell walls break and oil flows out. In Africa, common raw materials include groundnuts, sunflower seeds, sesame seeds, copra (dried coconut), palm kernels, and shea nuts. Each has a different oil content: groundnuts about 45-50% oil, sunflower seeds 40-45%, sesame 50-55%, copra 60-65%, and palm kernels 45-50%. The higher the oil content, the easier it is to extract, but you still need proper preparation..Trim()

.Trim()

Before pressing, seeds must be cleaned to remove stones, sand, and metal. Then they are usually ground into a paste or flaked. For hard seeds like palm kernels, you may need to heat them first to soften the oil. The principle is simple: the smaller the particle, the more oil you can release. A hammer mill or a manual grinder can do this. In many African villages, women use wooden mortars and pestles to pound seeds, but this is slow. A small motorized grinder can process 100 kg per hour, which is much faster..Trim()

.Trim()

The press itself creates pressure. There are two main types: hydraulic presses, which use a piston and cylinder to push a plate against the seed paste, and screw presses (expellers), which use a rotating screw inside a barrel to squeeze the paste continuously. Hydraulic presses work in batches, while screw presses work continuously. Both rely on the same principle: pressure forces oil out through small openings while the solid cake remains behind..Trim()

.Trim()

Temperature matters. Cold pressing (below 50Â°C) gives purer oil but lower yield. Hot pressing (80-120Â°C) gives more oil but may darken it and remove some flavor. In Africa, many small producers use hot pressing for groundnuts and palm kernels because it increases yield. However, you must control heat to avoid burning the oil. A simple thermometer helps..Trim()

.Trim()

After pressing, the oil is filtered to remove particles. Let it settle, then filter through cloth or a screen. The leftover cake is valuable animal feed or can be used for biogas. Mechanical extraction is affordable, safe, and does not require imported chemicals. With a good press and proper preparation, you can extract 80-90% of the oil in groundnuts and sesame, and 70-80% from palm kernels. This makes it a practical business for small-scale producers across Africa..Trim()

Cold Pressing vs. Hot Pressing

Cold pressing and hot pressing are two ways to extract oil mechanically. The difference is temperature. Cold pressing keeps the seed temperature below 50Â°C (122Â°F) during extraction. Hot pressing raises the temperature to between 80Â°C and 120Â°C (176-248Â°F) before or during pressing. Each method has advantages and disadvantages, and your choice depends on the seed, the oil quality you want, and your market..Trim()

.Trim()

Cold pressing is done without adding heat. You clean the seeds, grind them into a paste, and press them at room temperature or with slight warming from friction. The oil that comes out is called virgin or cold-pressed oil. It retains natural flavor, color, and nutrients like vitamin E. This is popular for sesame, groundnut, and sunflower oil sold as premium products. In Africa, cold-pressed sesame oil sells for a higher price in cities and for export. However, cold pressing leaves more oil in the cake. You might only recover 60-70% of the oil, compared to 80-90% with hot pressing. Also, cold pressing is slower and may require a stronger press because the oil is thicker..Trim()

.Trim()

Hot pressing involves heating the seed paste before pressing. You can roast seeds in a pan or use a steam jacket on the press. For groundnuts, heating to about 100Â°C for 20-30 minutes makes the oil flow more easily. For palm kernels, you might heat to 110Â°C. The heat breaks down cell walls and reduces oil viscosity, so more oil comes out. Hot pressing also kills bacteria and extends shelf life. But high heat can damage nutrients and create a darker oil with a roasted taste. If you overheat, the oil can become bitter or even toxic. You need a thermometer and constant stirring to avoid burning..Trim()

.Trim()

In practice, many African small-scale producers use a combination. They may warm the seeds slightly (50-60Â°C) to improve yield without losing the cold-pressed label. For example, in Uganda, sesame oil producers often warm seeds to 60Â°C before pressing, calling it 'warm-pressed' and getting 75% yield. In Nigeria, groundnut oil is often hot-pressed at 100Â°C for higher yield, then sold as regular cooking oil. The choice depends on your target market. If you sell to health-conscious buyers, cold press. If you sell to bulk buyers or need maximum oil, hot press. Always test small batches to see what works best for your seed and press..Trim()

Operation of Hydraulic Presses

A hydraulic press uses a piston and cylinder to create pressure. You fill a cylinder with hydraulic fluid (oil), then pump it to push a piston. The piston presses a plate against a container of seed paste. The oil squeezes out through small holes or cloth, and the solid cake stays behind. Hydraulic presses are common in Africa because they are simple, strong, and can be built locally. They work in batches, meaning you load, press, unload, and repeat..Trim()

.Trim()

To operate a hydraulic press, start by preparing the seed paste. Clean and grind your seeds. For groundnuts, grind to a fine paste. For palm kernels, grind coarsely and heat to 100Â°C. Next, wrap the paste in a strong cloth (like a cotton filter cloth) and place it in the press cage. The cage is a metal cylinder with small holes or slots for oil to escape. Make sure the paste is evenly distributed. Then close the cage with a top plate. Now pump the hydraulic jack. Most manual jacks can produce 10-20 tons of pressure. Pump slowly and steadily. You will see oil start to flow out within a minute. Keep pumping until the oil stops flowing, usually after 5-10 minutes. Then release the pressure, remove the cake, and reload..Trim()

.Trim()

A typical batch size for a small hydraulic press is 5-10 kg of seed paste. With 10 tons of pressure, you can extract 80-85% of the oil from groundnuts in one pressing. For higher yield, you can re-grind the cake and press again, but this takes more time. The oil that comes out is usually cloudy. Let it settle for 24 hours, then filter through a cloth. The cake can be used as animal feed..Trim()

.Trim()

Maintenance is important. Check the hydraulic fluid level every week. If it is low, top up with clean hydraulic oil. Check the seals and hoses for leaks. If the jack does not hold pressure, the seals may be worn. Replace them. Clean the cage and plates after every use to prevent rust. In Africa, many hydraulic presses are made from car jacks and steel pipes. They are affordable (around $200-500) and can be repaired by a local welder. However, they are slower than screw presses. For a small business, a hydraulic press can process 50-100 kg of seed per day, which is good for local markets. Always wear gloves and safety glasses when operating, as hydraulic fluid can be hot and oil can splash..Trim()

Operation of Screw Presses (Expellers)

A screw press, also called an expeller, uses a rotating screw inside a barrel to squeeze oil out of seeds continuously. The screw pushes the seed paste forward, and as the space gets narrower, pressure increases. Oil escapes through small openings in the barrel, while the solid cake is pushed out the end. Screw presses are popular in Africa because they can process large amounts quickly and run continuously. Small models can process 30-50 kg per hour, while larger ones do 200-500 kg per hour..Trim()

.Trim()

To operate a screw press, first prepare the seeds. Clean them and remove stones. For most seeds, you need to grind them into a paste or flake them. For groundnuts, you can press whole kernels if they are small, but grinding gives better yield. For sunflower seeds, dehull them first because hulls absorb oil and reduce yield. Then adjust the press. The key settings are the screw speed and the gap between the screw and the barrel. A smaller gap creates more pressure but can clog. Start with a medium gap. Turn on the motor and let it warm up. Then feed the seed paste slowly into the hopper. Do not overload it. The press will start to push out oil and cake. The oil flows out through the barrel openings and collects in a tray. The cake comes out at the end as a hard, dry material..Trim()

.Trim()

You can adjust the pressure while running. If the oil flow is low, tighten the gap slightly. If the press jams, stop and clear it. For best results, preheat the seeds to 80-100Â°C for groundnuts and 100-110Â°C for palm kernels. This makes the oil flow easier and increases yield. A typical screw press can extract 85-90% of the oil from groundnuts in one pass. The oil is usually darker and has more particles than hydraulic-pressed oil. Let it settle and filter it..Trim()

.Trim()

Maintenance is critical. After every 8 hours of use, stop and check the screw and barrel for wear. Clean out any stuck cake. Lubricate the bearings. If the screw becomes dull, it can be sharpened or replaced. In Africa, many screw presses are made in China, India, or locally in Kenya and Nigeria. A small motorized screw press costs around $500-1500. It needs a 2-5 horsepower motor, which can run on electricity or a diesel engine. Always follow safety rules: keep hands away from the moving screw, wear tight clothing, and use a mask to avoid dust. With proper care, a screw press can last for years and be the backbone of a cooking oil business..Trim()

Optimizing Oil Yield in Mechanical Extraction

Getting the most oil out of your seeds is key to profit. Even a 5% increase in yield can mean more income. Yield depends on seed preparation, press settings, temperature, and timing. Here are practical ways to optimize oil yield in mechanical extraction, based on what works in Africa..Trim()

.Trim()

First, clean and sort your seeds. Remove stones, sand, and immature seeds. Immature seeds have less oil. For groundnuts, remove the skins if possible, as skins absorb oil. For sunflower, dehull the seeds. Hulls can soak up oil and reduce yield by up to 10%. Use a simple sieve or winnowing basket. Then grind the seeds to the right particle size. Too coarse, and oil pockets remain unbroken. Too fine, and the paste clogs the press. For groundnuts, grind to a paste that holds together when squeezed. For palm kernels, grind coarsely after heating. A hammer mill with a 2-3 mm screen works well..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, control temperature. For hot pressing, heat the paste to the right temperature. Groundnuts: 80-100Â°C. Sesame: 70-90Â°C. Palm kernels: 100-110Â°C. Use a thermometer and stir constantly. Overheating burns oil and reduces yield. For cold pressing, keep seeds below 50Â°C. You can warm them slightly to 40Â°C to improve flow without losing cold-pressed status. In many African villages, women heat seeds in a pot over a fire, but this is hard to control. A simple electric hot plate or a steam jacket on the press is better..Trim()

.Trim()

Third, adjust your press. For screw presses, set the gap between the screw and barrel. Start with a gap of 1-2 mm. If oil flow is slow, tighten to 0.5-1 mm. For hydraulic presses, use the maximum pressure your jack can handle (10-20 tons). Press for 5-10 minutes until oil stops. For both, press the cake twice. Re-grind the cake and press again. This can recover another 5-10% oil. In Nigeria, groundnut oil producers often press twice to get 90% yield..Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, manage moisture. Seeds should be dry (below 10% moisture) before pressing. If too wet, oil emulsifies and is hard to separate. If too dry, the paste is dusty and oil is trapped. For groundnuts, dry to 6-8% moisture. Test by biting a seed: it should crack, not squash. For copra, dry to 6% moisture. In humid African climates, use a solar dryer or a hot air oven..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, filter and settle properly. Let the oil settle for 24-48 hours. Then filter through a fine cloth. You can also use a filter press. The particles you remove can be re-pressed. By following these steps, you can achieve 85-92% yield for groundnuts, 80-85% for sunflower, and 75-80% for palm kernels. Always keep records of your yield to see what works..Trim()

Troubleshooting Common Mechanical Extraction Issues

Even with good equipment, problems happen. Here are common issues in mechanical oil extraction and how to fix them, based on real experiences in Africa..Trim()

.Trim()

Problem 1: Low oil yield. If you are getting less oil than expected, check your seed preparation. Are the seeds clean and ground fine enough? For groundnuts, if the paste is too coarse, oil stays trapped. Re-grind to a finer paste. Check temperature: if cold pressing, try warming seeds to 50Â°C. If hot pressing, ensure you reach 80-100Â°C. Also, check press pressure. For screw presses, tighten the gap between screw and barrel. For hydraulic presses, pump to maximum pressure. Press the cake twice. If yield is still low, your seeds may be low in oil (e.g., old or immature). Test a small batch with fresh seeds..Trim()

.Trim()

Problem 2: Press jams or clogs. Screw presses often jam if the paste is too fine or too wet. Stop the press immediately. Open the barrel and remove the stuck cake. Clean the screw and barrel. Next time, use a coarser grind or dry the seeds more. If the press jams repeatedly, the gap may be too tight. Loosen it slightly. For hydraulic presses, jamming is rare, but if the piston does not move, check the hydraulic fluid level and seals..Trim()

.Trim()

Problem 3: Oil is dark or has burnt smell. This means you overheated the seeds. Reduce heating temperature. For groundnuts, do not exceed 100Â°C. Stir constantly. If you are using a direct fire, use a double boiler or a steam jacket. Burnt oil is not safe to eat and sells poorly. Discard burnt batches..Trim()

.Trim()

Problem 4: Oil is cloudy or has too much sediment. This is normal for mechanical extraction. Let the oil settle for 24-48 hours. Then filter through a fine cloth or a filter press. You can also add a little hot water (2-5% by volume) to help sediments settle faster, but this can cause emulsions if not done carefully. For small-scale, settling and cloth filtration is enough. If cloudiness persists, re-filter..Trim()

.Trim()

Problem 5: Cake is too wet or too dry. Wet cake means oil is still trapped. Re-press the cake. Dry cake means you extracted well. If cake is powdery, your paste was too dry. Add a little water (1-2%) to the paste before pressing. In Africa, some producers add a handful of sand to the paste to increase friction and pressure, but this contaminates the cake. Avoid sand..Trim()

.Trim()

Problem 6: Press makes unusual noise or vibrates. Stop immediately. Check for loose bolts, worn bearings, or a bent screw. Tighten or replace parts. Lubricate bearings. If the motor overheats, give it a rest. In many African workshops, a local mechanic can repair these issues. Always keep spare parts like belts, bearings, and seals. Regular maintenance prevents most problems. Train your operators to recognize early signs and act fast..Trim()
