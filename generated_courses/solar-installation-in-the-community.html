Understanding Solar Energy and Its Benefits

Solar energy is electricity from sunlight. A solar panel, also called a PV module, turns sunlight directly into DC electricity. A typical panel for a home is about 1 meter by 1.6 meters and produces 300 to 400 watts in full sun. In Africa, most places get 4 to 6 peak sun hours per day. That means a 300W panel can make about 1.2 to 1.8 kilowatt-hours (kWh) daily. A small home using lights, phone charging, and a radio might use 0.5 kWh per day. So one panel can cover that easily..Trim()

.Trim()

The benefits for a community are many. Solar reduces dependence on kerosene lamps, which are expensive, dim, and smoky. A family might spend 200 to 500 shillings per week on kerosene; a solar kit pays for itself in months. Solar also allows children to study at night, powers clinics for vaccine fridges, and pumps water for irrigation. Unlike diesel generators, solar has no fuel cost, makes no noise, and needs little maintenance..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, solar is especially useful because many rural areas are not connected to the grid, and where the grid exists, it often has outages. Solar works during the day and can charge batteries for night use. It is modular: you can start with one panel and add more later. A basic home system might include a 100W panel, a 12V 100Ah battery, a 10A charge controller, and a 300W inverter. That can run LED lights, a fan, a TV, and charge phones..Trim()

.Trim()

As an installer, you will help people choose the right size system for their needs and budget. You will also teach them how to use it properly, like not running heavy appliances all at once. Solar is a clean, reliable, and affordable solution that can transform daily life in African communities..Trim()

Community Energy Needs Assessment

Before installing any solar system, you must find out what the community actually needs. This is called a needs assessment. Start by talking to households, businesses, schools, and clinics. Ask questions: What do you use for lighting now? How much do you spend on kerosene, candles, or phone charging? What appliances do you want to power? For how many hours per day? Write down everything..Trim()

.Trim()

A simple method is a walk-through survey. Visit 10 to 20 homes. For each, list the devices they want to run: LED bulbs (5-10W each), phone chargers (5W), radio (10W), TV (50-100W), fan (50-75W), fridge (100-200W). Note how many hours each device runs. Multiply watts by hours to get watt-hours (Wh). For example, 3 LED bulbs of 5W for 5 hours = 75Wh. A phone charger 5W for 3 hours = 15Wh. Add them up. That gives daily energy need..Trim()

.Trim()

Also check the sun. In most of Africa, you get 4-6 peak sun hours. To find panel size, divide daily Wh by peak sun hours. If a home needs 300Wh and you get 5 sun hours, you need a 60W panel minimum. But add 30% for losses, so 80W. For battery, if you want two days of backup, double the daily Wh and divide by battery voltage (usually 12V). For 300Wh daily, two days = 600Wh, divided by 12V = 50Ah. So a 12V 50Ah battery..Trim()

.Trim()

Consider community needs too: a school might need lighting for evening classes, a clinic needs a vaccine fridge (which uses about 1 kWh per day), a water pump needs a bigger system. Also assess ability to pay. Some families can pay cash, others need pay-as-you-go. Your assessment should lead to a clear recommendation: system size, cost, and expected benefits. Always involve the community in decisions so they take ownership..Trim()

Overview of Solar Photovoltaic Systems

A solar photovoltaic (PV) system has four main parts: panels, charge controller, battery, and inverter. Panels make DC electricity from sunlight. A typical panel has 60 or 72 cells and produces 12V or 24V nominal, but open-circuit voltage can be 18V to 45V. Charge controllers regulate the voltage and current going to the battery to prevent overcharging. There are two types: PWM (pulse width modulation) and MPPT (maximum power point tracking). MPPT is more efficient, especially in cloudy conditions, and can increase charging by 10-30%..Trim()

.Trim()

Batteries store energy for night and cloudy days. Lead-acid batteries are common and cheap, but only 50% of their capacity is usable. Lithium (LiFePO4) batteries are more expensive but allow 80-90% depth of discharge and last longer. A 12V 100Ah lead-acid battery gives about 600Wh usable (12V x 100Ah x 0.5). Inverters convert DC to AC for appliances that need 220V. Choose an inverter with pure sine wave for sensitive electronics like TVs and computers. Size it to handle the total watts of all appliances running at once, plus a safety margin..Trim()

.Trim()

There are three system types: off-grid (standalone, with battery), grid-tied (no battery, feeds grid), and hybrid (battery plus grid). In rural Africa, off-grid is most common. A basic off-grid system: 200W panel, 20A MPPT controller, 12V 100Ah battery, 500W inverter. This can run lights, phone charging, a small TV, and a fan. For larger needs, you can connect panels in series or parallel to increase voltage or current. Always use correct cable sizes to avoid voltage drop. For a 200W system at 12V, 4mmÂ² cable is fine for short runs. Keep panels facing the equator (north in southern hemisphere, south in northern hemisphere) at an angle equal to your latitude..Trim()

Solar Energy in the Local Context

Solar energy is not new to Africa. For decades, small solar home systems have powered rural homes, and now larger mini-grids are appearing. In Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia, companies like M-KOPA and Zola Electric sell pay-as-you-go solar kits. A basic kit with a 10W panel, battery, and 3 lights costs about $50, and customers pay daily or weekly via mobile money. This makes solar affordable for low-income families..Trim()

.Trim()

In many communities, the main alternative is the national grid, but it can be unreliable. In Nigeria, for example, grid power is often available only a few hours a day. Diesel generators are noisy, pollute, and cost 500-1000 naira per liter. Solar avoids these problems. Also, extending the grid to remote villages can cost $10,000 to $50,000 per kilometer, so solar is cheaper for scattered homes..Trim()

.Trim()

Local context matters. In the Sahel, dust and high temperatures reduce panel output; you must clean panels weekly and allow ventilation. In humid coastal areas, corrosion is a problem, so use corrosion-resistant mounts. In conflict zones, theft can be an issue; mount panels securely and consider community watch. Culture also plays a role: some communities prefer shared systems, like a solar kiosk where people charge phones and batteries for a small fee. That creates local jobs..Trim()

.Trim()

As an installer, you must adapt to local conditions. Use locally available materials where possible. Train a local technician to do basic maintenance. Work with community leaders to build trust. Solar can power schools, health centers, and water pumps, but only if the system is designed for the actual environment and usage. Always ask: what happens during the rainy season? How will we replace parts? Plan for sustainability from the start..Trim()

Roles and Responsibilities of Community Solar Installers

A community solar installer does more than just mount panels. You are a technician, advisor, and educator. Your main tasks include: assessing needs, designing systems, installing components, testing, and training users. You must also provide after-sales service, like troubleshooting and replacing parts. In many African communities, you will be the only solar expert, so people will rely on you..Trim()

.Trim()

Key responsibilities: First, safety. Always turn off power before working, use insulated tools, and wear gloves and eye protection. Second, quality work. Use correct cable sizes, proper connectors (MC4), and secure mounting. Panels should face the sun and be tilted correctly. Keep cables out of walkways and water. Third, documentation. Write down the system size, battery type, and installation date. Give the customer a simple manual in their language. Fourth, training. Show the user how to clean panels, check battery water (for lead-acid), and avoid overloading the inverter. Fifth, ethics. Do not oversell. If a customer needs only 100W, do not sell them 500W. Be honest about costs and limitations..Trim()

.Trim()

You also act as a businessperson. You need to price your services fairly, keep records, and manage inventory. Many installers start by serving their own village, then expand. Join a local solar association or find a mentor. Keep learning: new products like lithium batteries and smart controllers are always coming. Attend trainings when possible..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, you are a community role model. By providing reliable solar power, you improve health, education, and economic opportunities. You help reduce kerosene use and carbon emissions. Your work builds trust and creates a market for more solar. Always remember: the goal is not just to install a system, but to ensure it works for years and benefits the whole community..Trim()

Safety and Environmental Considerations

Solar installation involves electrical, mechanical, and environmental risks. Always prioritize safety. Electrical hazards: solar panels produce DC electricity whenever exposed to light, even if disconnected. Cover panels with an opaque cloth during installation to prevent shock. Use insulated tools and wear rubber gloves. Batteries can release explosive hydrogen gas when charging, so keep them in a ventilated area away from sparks. Lead-acid batteries contain sulfuric acid; if it touches skin, rinse with plenty of water. Never short-circuit battery terminals..Trim()

.Trim()

Mechanical hazards: panels are heavy (about 20kg for a 300W panel) and can break if dropped. Use proper lifting techniques and have a helper. Mounting on roofs requires fall protection; use harnesses and secure ladders. In windy areas, ensure mounts are anchored to rafters, not just roof sheets. Use stainless steel bolts to avoid rust..Trim()

.Trim()

Environmental considerations: Solar is clean, but batteries and panels must be disposed of properly. Lead-acid batteries contain lead and acid; never dump them in rivers or fields. Find a certified recycler. Panels can last 25 years, but if broken, they may contain small amounts of heavy metals. Store damaged panels safely and send to a recycling facility. Also, minimize your own environmental impact: avoid spilling battery acid, clean up packaging, and use reusable materials..Trim()

.Trim()

In the community, teach users about safety: do not touch panels with wet hands, do not cover panels with heavy objects, and keep children away from batteries. During installation, be mindful of local wildlife and plants; avoid cutting trees unnecessarily. If you dig trenches for cables, refill them properly to prevent erosion. By following these practices, you protect yourself, your customers, and the environment. Remember, a safe installation is a successful installation..Trim()

Module 2 · Solar Site Assessment and Planning

Conducting a Site Survey for Solar Potential

A site survey is the first step to see if solar will work for a home or business. You will need a notebook, pencil, tape measure, compass, and a smartphone with a solar app like Solar Pathfinder or Sun Surveyor. Start by talking to the owner about their electricity needs: how many lights, phones, fridge, TV, or machines they use daily. Ask about their monthly energy bill if they have one, or estimate by listing appliances and their wattage. For example, a small home might use 5 LED lights (5W each), a phone charger (10W), and a radio (20W) for 4 hours a day: total about 50W x 4h = 200Wh per day. Write this down..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, walk the property and note possible mounting spots: roof, ground, or pole. Measure the area available. For a roof, measure length and width in meters. Check the roof material: corrugated iron, tiles, or thatch. Thatch is not suitable for solar panels. For ground mount, check soil type and if the area floods in rainy season. Use your compass to find true north (or south if you are in southern Africa). In most of Africa, panels should face north in the north hemisphere and south in the south hemisphere. But near the equator, a shallow angle works..Trim()

.Trim()

Take photos of the site from different angles, especially where you plan to put panels. Note any tall trees, buildings, or hills that could cast shadows. Also check where the battery and inverter will go â they need a dry, ventilated space. Measure the distance from panels to battery to estimate cable length. Cable runs over 10 meters may need thicker wire to reduce power loss..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, record the location using GPS coordinates or a nearby landmark. Note the local climate: rainy season, dust, or extreme heat. These affect panel performance. A good survey takes 1-2 hours and gives you all the data to design a system that fits the site and the user's budget..Trim()

Evaluating Solar Irradiance and Shading

Solar irradiance is the amount of sunlight energy hitting a surface, measured in kilowatt-hours per square meter per day (kWh/mÂ²/day). In Africa, this varies from about 4 kWh/mÂ²/day in cloudy highlands to over 6 kWh/mÂ²/day in the Sahara and Kalahari. You can find your local value using free online tools like Global Solar Atlas or NASA POWER. For a quick estimate, assume 5 kWh/mÂ²/day for most of sub-Saharan Africa. This means a 300W panel can produce about 1.5 kWh per day (300W x 5 hours = 1500Wh). But shading can cut this by half or more..Trim()

.Trim()

Shading is the biggest killer of solar production. Even a small shadow on one panel can reduce the output of the whole string. To evaluate shading, use a Solar Pathfinder or a smartphone app like Sun Surveyor. Stand where you will mount the panels and look at the sky. Identify trees, buildings, or poles that cast shadows. Note the time of day and year when shadows fall on the spot. For example, a tree to the east will shade panels in the morning; a building to the west shades in the afternoon. In the northern hemisphere, the sun is in the south, so shadows point north. In the southern hemisphere, the opposite..Trim()

.Trim()

Measure the height of obstacles and their distance from the panels. A simple rule: if an obstacle is twice as tall as its distance from the panel, it will shade the panel when the sun is low. For example, a 10m tree 5m away will shade the panel when the sun is below 63 degrees. In Africa, the sun is high most of the year, but in winter it is lower. Check shading at 9am, 12pm, and 3pm on a clear day. Also consider seasonal changes: in the rainy season, clouds reduce irradiance even without shadows..Trim()

.Trim()

If shading is unavoidable, you can move the panels, trim trees (with permission), or use micro-inverters or power optimizers to reduce the impact. But the best solution is to avoid shading at the design stage. Record all shading data in your survey notes. A site with heavy shading may need a ground mount in an open area instead of a roof mount..Trim()

Assessing Roof and Ground Mount Options

Choosing between roof and ground mount depends on space, shading, roof condition, and cost. Roof mount saves ground space and often uses existing structure, but it can be harder to install and maintain. Ground mount is easier to clean and angle, but needs land and may be vulnerable to theft or animals..Trim()

.Trim()

For roof mount, first check the roof type. Corrugated iron sheets are common in Africa and can support solar panels if the purlins (the horizontal beams) are strong. You will need to find the rafters and use lag bolts or special roof hooks. Never drill through the roof without sealing â use rubber washers and sealant to prevent leaks. For tile roofs, use tile hooks that replace a tile. For concrete roofs, use anchor bolts. The roof should be less than 10 years old; if it is old or rusted, repair or replace it first. Also check the roof pitch: panels work best at an angle equal to your latitude (e.g., 10Â° near the equator, 30Â° in South Africa). If the roof is too steep or flat, you may need tilted mounts..Trim()

.Trim()

For ground mount, you need a clear area with good sun. The soil should be stable â not sandy or waterlogged. Use concrete footings or ground screws to anchor the mounting frame. Keep panels at least 50cm above ground to avoid dust, water, and animals. In areas with theft risk, build a fence or use tamper-proof bolts. Ground mounts allow you to adjust the tilt seasonally for maximum output. But they require more cable to reach the battery, which adds cost..Trim()

.Trim()

Consider wind loads: in coastal or windy areas, use heavier frames and more anchors. Also think about access for cleaning and maintenance. A ground mount is easier to clean, which is important in dusty regions. If the roof is shaded or weak, ground mount is better. If land is scarce, roof mount is the only option. Always discuss with the owner about their preference and budget. A typical 1kW system needs about 6-8 square meters of roof or ground space..Trim()

Structural and Electrical Considerations

Before installing solar panels, you must ensure the structure can handle the weight and wind. A single solar panel weighs about 15-20 kg. A 1kW system (3-4 panels) adds 60-80 kg to the roof, plus mounting rails. That is like having an extra person standing on the roof. Check the roof trusses: they should be spaced no more than 1.2 meters apart and made of treated timber or steel. If they are weak, add bracing. For ground mounts, the frame must withstand wind speeds of at least 120 km/h. Use galvanized steel or aluminum to prevent rust. Concrete footings should be at least 60cm deep..Trim()

.Trim()

Electrical considerations start with voltage and current. Most solar panels produce 12V or 24V DC. You will need a charge controller to regulate the voltage to the battery. The controller must match the panel voltage and battery voltage. For example, a 12V battery needs a 12V panel and a 12V controller. If you use a 24V system, you can use two 12V panels in series. Calculate the total current: a 300W panel at 12V produces about 25A. Use cables rated for at least 1.25 times that current. For a 25A current, use 4mmÂ² copper cable for runs up to 5 meters. Longer runs need thicker cable (6mmÂ² or 10mmÂ²) to avoid voltage drop..Trim()

.Trim()

Safety is critical. Install a DC circuit breaker or fuse between the panel and controller, and between controller and battery. Use a grounding rod for the panel frames and mounting structure to prevent electric shock. All connections must be in waterproof junction boxes. Never connect panels directly to a battery without a controller â it will overcharge and damage the battery..Trim()

.Trim()

Also plan for the inverter if you need AC power. The inverter should be sized to handle the total wattage of appliances you want to run at once. For example, a 500W inverter can run a laptop, lights, and a small TV. Place the inverter and battery in a dry, ventilated area away from living spaces because they produce hydrogen gas. Label all cables clearly. A well-planned electrical system is safe, efficient, and easy to maintain..Trim()

Permitting and Community Approvals

In many African countries, installing solar panels requires permits and approvals from local authorities. The rules vary by country, region, and even by city. Always check with the local government office, energy ministry, or utility company before you start. In some places, small off-grid systems need no permit, but grid-tied systems or systems above a certain size (e.g., 1kW) require approval. For example, in Kenya, the Energy Regulatory Commission (ERC) requires licensing for systems above 1kW. In South Africa, you need a Certificate of Compliance (CoC) from a registered electrician for any grid-tied system..Trim()

.Trim()

Start by visiting the local council or municipal office. Ask for the requirements for solar installation. You may need to submit a site plan, electrical diagram, and proof of ownership or permission from the landowner. If you are renting, get written permission from the landlord. For community projects, you may need approval from a village committee or chief. In some areas, you must inform the utility company if you plan to feed excess power into the grid. That requires a bi-directional meter and a grid-tie agreement..Trim()

.Trim()

Community approvals are just as important. Talk to neighbors about your plans. They may have concerns about glare, noise from inverters, or access. Address these early. In some communities, you may need to present your project at a community meeting. Bring a simple drawing and explain the benefits: clean energy, lower bills, and local jobs. If the system is on a shared roof or land, get written consent from all users..Trim()

.Trim()

Keep copies of all permits and approvals. They protect you if there is a dispute later. Also check if there are any tax incentives or subsidies for solar in your area. Some governments offer rebates or reduced import duties on solar equipment. A little paperwork upfront saves big trouble later. If you are unsure, hire a local solar consultant who knows the regulations. Never install without the necessary permits â you could be fined or forced to remove the system..Trim()

Creating a Site Assessment Report

A site assessment report is a written document that summarizes your findings and recommends a solar system design. It helps the client understand what they are buying and protects you as the installer. The report should be clear, simple, and include photos and diagrams. Write it in the local language if needed, or use simple English..Trim()

.Trim()

Start with a cover page: client name, date, location, and your name. Then include a summary of the client's energy needs: list appliances, their wattage, hours used per day, and total daily energy in watt-hours (Wh). For example, 5 lights (5W x 4h = 100Wh), phone charger (10W x 2h = 20Wh), radio (20W x 4h = 80Wh) = 200Wh/day. Add a 20% safety margin, so design for 240Wh/day..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, describe the site: roof or ground area, dimensions, orientation, and shading analysis. Include photos with arrows showing north and any obstacles. State the solar irradiance value you used (e.g., 5 kWh/mÂ²/day). Then recommend a system size: for 240Wh/day, you need a 100W panel (100W x 5h = 500Wh, which is more than enough; but you can use a 50W panel if you have a smaller battery). Calculate battery size: for 240Wh, a 12V 20Ah battery (240Wh) gives one day of storage. Add an extra day for cloudy weather, so 40Ah. Recommend a charge controller rated for the panel's current (e.g., 10A for a 100W panel)..Trim()

.Trim()

Include a cost estimate: panels, battery, controller, inverter, cables, mounting, and labor. List permits needed. Add a simple wiring diagram. Finally, give a timeline: survey (1 day), design (1 day), installation (1-2 days), testing (1 day). End with your contact details and a place for signatures. The report should be 2-3 pages. Give a copy to the client and keep one for your records. A good report builds trust and ensures everyone agrees on the plan before you start digging or drilling..Trim()

Preparing Mounting Structures

Before you touch a panel, you need a solid mounting structure. In most African community solar projects, we use either galvanised steel angle iron (like 40mm x 40mm x 4mm) or treated timber (50mm x 100mm). Steel is better for longevity, especially in coastal or humid areas. You will need a hacksaw or angle grinder with a metal cutting disc, a power drill with cobalt bits, a spanner set, and a tape measure. Start by measuring the roof or ground area where the panels will go. Panels are typically 1.6m x 1m for a 250W-400W module. Plan for at least 10cm gap between panels for airflow and thermal expansion. Cut your steel to size. For a typical 4-panel array, you need two long rails (called stringers) that span the width of the panels. Each rail should be at least 4.2m long if panels are mounted in portrait. Drill holes in the rails where they will bolt to the roof mounts or ground posts. Use 10mm bolts with spring washers and nuts. If using timber, pre-drill to avoid splitting, and treat all cut ends with wood preservative or used engine oil. For ground mounts, dig holes 60cm deep for concrete footings. Mix concrete 1:3:5 (cement:sand:aggregate) and let it cure for at least 48 hours before loading. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. Deburr all cut metal edges with a file. Check that your structure is level using a spirit level before tightening all bolts. A wobbly structure will crack panels or cause micro-cracks that kill output. In dusty areas, consider raising the structure at least 50cm off the ground to reduce dust accumulation and allow cleaning. For pole mounts, use a 100mm diameter steel pole set in a 80cm deep concrete base. Weld or bolt a top plate to the pole to attach the panel frame. Never use mild steel without painting it with red oxide primer and two coats of enamel. Rust is the number one killer of mounts in Africa. Finally, lay out all parts and count your bolts, nuts, and washers before starting assembly. Missing one bolt means a trip to town..Trim()

Installing Roof Mounts and Flashing

Roof mounts are the most common in African villages, but they must be done right or you will have leaks and a dangerous installation. First, identify your roof type: corrugated iron sheets (most common), clay tiles, or concrete. For corrugated iron, use L-foot brackets with a rubber gasket and a 8mm x 100mm coach screw. The L-foot must sit on the crest (the high part) of the corrugation, never in the valley. Drill a 8mm pilot hole through the crest and into the roof purlin (the wooden or steel beam under the sheet). Apply a bead of polyurethane sealant (like Sikaflex) around the hole before inserting the screw. Tighten until the gasket just compresses â do not over-tighten or you will crush the gasket and leak. For each mount, use two screws diagonally opposite. For tile roofs, you need to lift the tile, fix a galvanised strap to the rafter, then notch the tile to fit over the strap. Replace the tile and seal the notch with roof cement. For concrete roofs, use expansion anchors (M10 x 100mm) with a chemical anchor if the concrete is weak. Drill a 10mm hole, clean dust, inject epoxy, insert anchor, wait 30 minutes, then tighten. Flashing is critical. After installing each L-foot, cover the base with a piece of lead or aluminium flashing (150mm x 150mm) bedded in sealant. Nail or screw the flashing to the roof sheet above the mount, and seal all edges. In heavy rain areas, add a second layer of sealant after 24 hours. Never use silicone sealant on metal roofs â it fails in UV. Use polyurethane or butyl tape. Check for leaks by pouring water on the roof after installation. If you see drips inside, re-seal. Also, ensure all mounts are in line: use a string line from the first to the last mount. A crooked array looks bad and stresses panels. Ground mounts are ideal where roofs are weak, shaded, or not owned by the user. They also allow easy cleaning and better cooling. Start by marking out your array footprint with pegs and string. Securing Panels and Torque Specifications

Once your panels are in place, you must secure them properly. Loose panels vibrate, crack, and can fly off in a storm. Use the correct clamps: mid-clamps between panels and end-clamps at the ends. Most panels have a 35mm or 40mm frame height. Clamps must match. Stainless steel clamps are best â they do not rust. Aluminium clamps are okay but can corrode in coastal areas. Never use mild steel clamps. The bolts that hold clamps to the rails are usually M8 (8mm) stainless steel. The torque specification is critical. Too loose and the panel moves; too tight and you crush the frame or crack the glass. For M8 bolts, the recommended torque is 15-20 Nm (Newton-meters). For M6 bolts, 8-10 Nm. For M10, 30-40 Nm. You need a torque wrench. If you don't have one, tighten until the spring washer is flat, then a quarter turn more. But a torque wrench is cheap insurance. Always use a flat washer and a spring washer under the nut. Apply a small amount of anti-seize compound to stainless bolts to prevent galling (cold welding). Tighten in a diagonal pattern, like you would a car wheel, to avoid stressing the frame. Start with the centre clamps, then the ends. After tightening, check that the panel is not distorted â look along the frame edge. If it bows, loosen and re-tighten. For ground mounts, also check the bolts holding the rails to the posts. Those should be M10 or M12, torqued to 40-50 Nm. After installation, re-torque all bolts after one week, then every six months. Vibration and thermal cycling loosen bolts. In dusty areas, clean bolts before torquing. Use a paint marker to mark tightened bolts so you can see if any move. For added security, use a thread-locking compound like Loctite 243 on all bolts. Never use ordinary nuts â always use nyloc nuts or two nuts jammed together. If you are in a high-theft area, use security bolts (Torx with pin) or weld the nuts to the bolts after tightening. But remember, you may need to remove panels for repair. A good rule: secure panels so they cannot be lifted by hand, but can be removed with tools. Finally, check that all clamps are fully seated on the panel frame â not on the glass. Clamping on glass voids warranty and breaks panels..Trim()

Inspecting Mounting Integrity

After installation, and then every six months, you must inspect the mounting system. A loose mount can cause micro-cracks in panels, reduce output, and create a safety hazard. Start with a visual inspection from the ground. Look for: sagging rails, rust streaks, bent brackets, missing bolts, and loose clamps. Use binoculars if the roof is high. Then climb up safely â use a ladder rated for your weight, and tie it off. Wear a harness if working at height. Check every bolt with a torque wrench. Set it to the specified torque (e.g., 18 Nm for M8) and try to turn the bolt. If it moves, it was loose. Re-torque and mark it. Check all clamps: they should be tight against the frame with no gap. Push the panel gently â it should not move. If it moves, find the loose clamp and tighten. Check for corrosion: white powder on aluminium, red rust on steel. Clean with a wire brush and apply zinc-rich paint or cold galvanising spray. Check the roof penetrations for leaks. Look for water stains inside the roof. Re-seal any suspect flashing with polyurethane sealant. Check the earthing: the copper wire should be firmly connected to the ground rod and to the mount. Measure resistance with a multimeter â it should be less than 1 ohm. Check cable management: cables should not rub against sharp edges or be pinched. Replace any UV-damaged cable ties. In dusty areas, clean the panels and mounts with a soft brush and water. Do not use a pressure washer â it can force water into connectors. After cleaning, inspect for cracks in the glass or frame. If you find a cracked panel, replace it immediately â it can cause a fire or shock hazard. For ground mounts, check the concrete footings for cracks. If cracks are wider than 2mm, consult an engineer. Check for soil erosion around footings â backfill with compacted gravel. In coastal areas, inspect every three months due to salt corrosion. Keep a logbook: date, inspector, findings, actions. This helps you track recurring issues. Finally, after any storm or high wind (over 60km/h), do an emergency inspection.
