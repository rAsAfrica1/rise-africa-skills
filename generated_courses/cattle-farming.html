Module 1 · Introduction to Cattle Farming

Overview of the Cattle Industry

Cattle farming is one of the most important agricultural activities in Africa. It provides meat, milk, hides, and manure, and supports the livelihoods of millions of people. In countries like Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa, cattle are a major source of income and food security. The industry is divided into two main systems: traditional pastoralism and commercial ranching. Pastoralists move with their herds in search of water and pasture, while commercial farms keep cattle in fenced areas and focus on high production..Trim()

In Africa, cattle are raised in diverse environments, from the dry savannas of the Sahel to the highlands of East Africa. The most common breeds are indigenous cattle such as the Boran, Nguni, and Ankole, which are adapted to local conditions and resistant to diseases. However, many farmers crossbreed with exotic breeds like Holstein-Friesian and Brahman to increase milk and meat yields. For example, a pure Boran cow may produce 2-3 litres of milk per day, while a crossbreed can produce 10-15 litres under good management..Trim()

.Trim()

The cattle industry faces challenges such as drought, diseases (e.g., foot-and-mouth disease, East Coast fever), and limited access to markets. Climate change is making pastures less reliable, forcing farmers to adopt new strategies like rotational grazing and water harvesting. Governments and NGOs are supporting farmers with training, veterinary services, and loans. For instance, the Kenya Livestock Commercialization Project helps smallholders improve breeds and access markets..Trim()

.Trim()

To succeed in cattle farming, you need to understand the local environment, choose the right breed, and manage health and nutrition carefully. Start small with a few cows and expand as you gain experience. The demand for beef and milk is growing in African cities, so there is a ready market if you can produce quality products. This module will introduce you to the basics of cattle farming, from terminology to regulations, and show you how to turn cattle into a profitable business..Trim()

Types of Cattle Operations

Cattle operations in Africa can be classified into three main types: cow-calf, stocker/backgrounder, and feedlot/finishing. Each has different goals, infrastructure, and management needs. Understanding these types helps you decide which fits your land, capital, and market..Trim()

.Trim()

Cow-calf operations are the most common. You keep a breeding herd of cows and bulls to produce calves. Calves are usually sold at weaning (6-8 months) or kept as replacements. This system requires good grazing land and a bull. For example, a farmer with 20 cows and 1 bull can produce about 15 calves per year. The main costs are bull maintenance, vaccines, and supplemental feed during dry seasons. In pastoral areas, cow-calf operations are mobile, following rains..Trim()

.Trim()

Stocker/backgrounder operations buy weaned calves and grow them on pasture until they reach a heavier weight. You might buy 200 kg calves and sell them at 350 kg after 6-12 months. This system needs good pastures and careful health management. It is common in areas with abundant grass but limited market for finished cattle. Profit comes from weight gain and buying low/selling high. You must watch for diseases and parasites, and provide mineral supplements..Trim()

.Trim()

Feedlot/finishing operations confine cattle in pens and feed them a high-energy diet (e.g., maize silage, molasses, and protein concentrates) to fatten them quickly for slaughter. A typical feedlot in South Africa might keep 1,000 cattle for 90-120 days, achieving daily gains of 1.2-1.5 kg. This requires significant capital for feed, facilities, and veterinary care. Feedlots are located near abattoirs and feed sources. They produce consistent, high-quality beef for supermarkets and export..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many smallholders combine cow-calf with some fattening. For example, a farmer might keep 10 cows for breeding and also buy 5 calves to fatten for festive seasons when prices are high. Choosing the right operation depends on your resources: land, water, capital, and labour. Start with what you have and scale up gradually..Trim()

Economic Importance of Cattle

Cattle are a cornerstone of rural economies across Africa. They provide direct income from the sale of meat, milk, hides, and manure, and serve as a store of wealth and a source of draft power. In many communities, cattle are used for bride price, ceremonies, and as insurance against crop failure. The livestock sector contributes up to 40% of agricultural GDP in some African countries, with cattle being the largest component..Trim()

.Trim()

Meat production is a major economic driver. Africa produces about 7 million tonnes of beef annually, but demand is growing faster than supply, leading to imports. For example, Nigeria imports over 100,000 tonnes of beef each year. This gap presents an opportunity for local farmers to increase production. A well-managed cow can yield a 250 kg carcass, which sells for around $2,000 in local markets. Milk is equally important: a crossbred cow can produce 3,000 litres per year, worth $1,500 at $0.50 per litre. In Kenya, the dairy industry supports over 1 million smallholder farmers..Trim()

.Trim()

Cattle also create jobs along the value chain: veterinarians, feed suppliers, transporters, and traders. A single feedlot employs dozens of workers. Hides and skins are used for leather goods, generating export earnings. Manure is used as organic fertilizer, improving crop yields and reducing costs. In mixed farming systems, cattle provide draft power for ploughing, saving on tractor hire..Trim()

.Trim()

However, the economic potential is often limited by poor infrastructure, diseases, and lack of market information. Farmers may sell at low prices during droughts and buy at high prices later. To maximize returns, you need to understand market cycles: prices are usually highest during festive seasons (e.g., Eid, Christmas) and lowest during dry spells. By planning breeding and fattening to coincide with high-demand periods, you can increase profits. With proper management, cattle farming can be a reliable source of income and a pathway out of poverty..Trim()

Basic Cattle Terminology

To succeed in cattle farming, you must learn the language. Here are key terms you will use daily. A cow is an adult female that has calved. A heifer is a female that has not yet calved. A bull is an intact adult male. A steer is a castrated male, often raised for beef. A calf is a young cattle under one year. A weaner is a calf that has been separated from its mother, usually at 6-8 months..Trim()

Breeding terms: Gestation is the pregnancy period, about 283 days (9 months) for cattle. Estrus (heat) is when a cow is ready to mate, occurring every 18-24 days. Service is mating. Calving is giving birth. A cow that has just calved is a fresh cow. Dry period is when a cow is not lactating, usually 2 months before calving..Trim()

.Trim()

Production terms: Lactation is the period a cow produces milk, typically 305 days. Yield is the amount of milk or meat produced. Carcass weight is the weight of the slaughtered animal after removing head, hides, and internal organs. Dressing percentage is carcass weight divided by live weight, usually 50-60% for cattle. Average daily gain (ADG) is weight gained per day, e.g., 1 kg/day..Trim()

.Trim()

Health and management terms: Vaccination is giving a vaccine to prevent disease. Deworming is treating internal parasites. Dipping is treating external parasites (ticks). Quarantine is isolating new or sick animals. Rotational grazing is moving cattle between paddocks to let grass recover. Stocking rate is the number of animals per hectare. Carrying capacity is the maximum number of animals the land can support..Trim()

.Trim()

Market terms: Live weight is the weight of the animal before slaughter. Cold dressed weight is carcass weight after chilling. Offtake is the number or percentage of animals sold or slaughtered per year. Herd is a group of cattle. Flock is not used for cattle (only for sheep/goats). Bullock is a castrated male used for draft. Heiferette is a female that has calved once but is not yet a cow. Knowing these terms helps you communicate with vets, buyers, and other farmers. Regulations and Compliance

Cattle farming in Africa is governed by national and local regulations to ensure animal health, food safety, and environmental protection. As a farmer, you must comply with these rules to avoid fines, market rejection, or disease outbreaks. Key areas include animal identification, movement control, vaccination, and slaughter hygiene..Trim()

Animal identification: Most countries require cattle to be tagged with unique numbers. In South Africa, all cattle must have an ear tag with a unique ID linked to a national database. In Kenya, the Kenya Veterinary Board issues movement permits. Tags help trace animals during disease outbreaks and prove ownership. You must tag calves within a few months of birth and keep records..Trim()

Movement control: Moving cattle across districts or borders often requires a movement permit from a veterinarian. This is to prevent the spread of diseases like foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) and contagious bovine pleuropneumonia (CBPP). In Botswana, strict fences separate livestock from wildlife to control FMD. If you buy cattle, ensure they have valid permits and health certificates..Trim()

.Trim()

Vaccination and disease control: Some vaccines are mandatory, such as annual FMD vaccination in many countries. You must keep vaccination records. Notifiable diseases (e.g., anthrax, rabies) must be reported to authorities immediately. Quarantine may be imposed on your farm if disease is suspected..Trim()

.Trim()

Slaughter and meat hygiene: If you slaughter for sale, you must use a licensed abattoir. Meat inspection is required to ensure it is safe for human consumption. On-farm slaughter for home use is usually allowed but must follow basic hygiene. Selling uninspected meat can lead to heavy fines..Trim()

.Trim()

Environmental regulations: Some countries regulate waste disposal, water use, and land clearing. For example, feedlots must manage manure to prevent water pollution. Overgrazing is discouraged, and you may need a permit for large herds..Trim()

.Trim()

Compliance may seem burdensome, but it protects your animals, your customers, and your business. Join a farmer association to stay updated on regulations. Keep all documents in a file. When in doubt, ask your local veterinary office or extension officer. They are there to help you comply and succeed..Trim()

Evaluating Conformation and Structure

Conformation refers to the shape and structure of an animalâs body. Good conformation indicates health, productivity, and longevity. When evaluating cattle, start from a distance: the animal should stand squarely on all four legs, with a straight back and a level rump. For beef breeds, look for a deep, wide body, thick hindquarters, and a broad chest. The ideal beef animal is rectangular from the side, with a well-muscled shoulder and round hindquarters. For dairy breeds, the ideal is a wedge shape: wide and deep at the rear (for udder and rumen), and narrow at the front. The udder should be large, soft, and well-attached, with teats of medium size, evenly spaced. Avoid cows with pendulous udders that hang below the hocks. Legs should be straight and strong; check for straight hocks and strong pasterns. Feet should be even, with no cracks or overgrowth. Genetic Selection and Breeding Goals

Genetic selection is the process of choosing which animals to breed to improve the next generation. Start by setting clear breeding goals: do you want more milk, faster growth, disease resistance, or all? For smallholders, a balanced goal is often best: moderate milk (10â15 liters/day), good growth (weaning weight 200 kg), and hardiness. Use estimated breeding values (EBVs) if available; these are numbers that predict an animalâs genetic merit for traits like milk yield, growth, and fertility. In Africa, many breed societies provide EBVs for Boran, Nguni, and Holstein. If EBVs are not available, use performance records: weigh calves at birth, weaning (6â8 months), and yearling (12 months). Select bulls from dams with high milk yield and good fertility. For dairy, select for milk yield, butterfat, and udder health. For beef, select for growth rate, carcass quality, and calving ease. Digestive System of Cattle

Cattle are ruminants, meaning they have a four-compartment stomach: rumen, reticulum, omasum, and abomasum. The rumen is the largest compartment and acts as a fermentation vat. It contains billions of microorganisms that break down fibrous plant material that humans cannot digest. When a cow eats, she swallows feed without chewing much, then later regurgitates it as cud to chew again. This process, called rumination, happens 6 to 8 hours a day. A healthy rumen has a pH between 6.0 and 7.0. If pH drops below 5.5, the cow gets acidosis, which can cause lameness and reduced milk. To keep the rumen healthy, feed long fibre like hay or straw daily. Sudden changes in diet disrupt rumen microbes; always transition over 7 to 10 days. Water is critical: a lactating cow drinks 60 to 100 litres per day. Without water, rumen microbes stop working. The reticulum catches heavy objects like nails; magnets can be given to prevent hardware disease. The omasum absorbs water and minerals. The abomasum is the true stomach, secreting acid and enzymes. In young calves, the rumen is not fully developed until about 3 months old, so they need milk or milk replacer. From 2 weeks, offer clean hay and calf starter to encourage rumen development. Understanding this system helps you feed cattle correctly and avoid digestive upsets..Trim()

A lactating cow giving 10 litres of milk per day needs 14-16% crude protein and 65-70% TDN. For each extra litre of milk, add 0.5 kg of concentrate feed. A pregnant cow in the last 2 months needs 12% protein and 60% TDN to support calf growth. Growing heifers need 14-16% protein and 65% TDN to reach breeding weight (about 300 kg for local breeds) by 18-24 months. A mature bull needs 10-12% protein and 55% TDN when working. Calves from birth to 3 months: milk or milk replacer plus calf starter with 18-20% protein. From 3 to 6 months: 16% protein and 70% TDN. Feedstuffs fall into three groups: roughages, concentrates, and supplements. Roughages are high-fibre feeds like pasture, hay, silage, and crop residues (maize stover, rice straw). Supplements include minerals, vitamins, and non-protein nitrogen like urea. A balanced ration mixes roughage and concentrate. For a dry cow: 2 kg maize bran, 0.5 kg cottonseed cake, and plenty of roughage. Pasture Management and Grazing

Good pasture management means rotating grazing, controlling stocking rate, and resting paddocks. In Africa, common pasture grasses: Napier grass, Rhodes grass, Guinea grass, and natural veld. Common Cattle Diseases

In Africa, cattle face several diseases that can wipe out your herd if you don't act fast. The most common are foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), lumpy skin disease, anthrax, blackleg, and East Coast fever. FMD causes blisters on the mouth and hooves, making cattle drool and limp. Anthrax and blackleg are soil-borne killers; anthrax can even infect you, so never open a carcass suspected of anthraxâcall a vet. East Coast fever, spread by brown ear ticks, kills calves within 3 weeks if untreated. Other frequent problems include mastitis (udder infection), pneumonia (often after transport), and foot rot from muddy pens. Recognizing these early saves money. For example, a cow with pneumonia breathes fast (over 40 breaths per minute) and coughs. Mastitis makes the udder hot and milk lumpy. In Africa, many diseases are preventable with vaccines. Vaccination Protocols

To breed cattle successfully, you must understand the basic parts of the cow and bull reproductive systems and how they work. The cow's reproductive tract includes the vulva, vagina, cervix, uterus, fallopian tubes, and ovaries. The ovaries produce eggs (ova) and hormones like estrogen and progesterone. Testicles produce sperm and testosterone. A healthy bull can produce billions of sperm daily..Trim()

.Trim()

The estrous cycle in cows lasts about 21 days (range 18-24). It has four phases: proestrus, estrus (heat), metestrus, and diestrus. Estrus lasts 12-18 hours. Ovulation occurs 10-14 hours after the end of estrus. If the cow is not bred, the cycle repeats. Pregnancy lasts about 283 days (9 months)..Trim()

.Trim()

Nutrition affects reproduction. Cows with a body condition score (BCS) of 2.5 to 3.5 (on a 1-5 scale) cycle regularly. Thin cows (BCS <2) may not show heat or may abort. Minerals like phosphorus, copper, and selenium are critical. In many African areas, dry-season feeding of crop residues or hay with mineral licks improves fertility..Trim()

.Trim()

Heat detection is key. Signs include standing to be mounted, clear mucus from the vulva, bellowing, restlessness, and swollen vulva. Use a chin-ball marker or heat-detection patches. Observe cows at least twice daily for 30 minutes, especially early morning and evening. Record heats on a calendar. A cow bred 12 hours after standing heat is most likely to conceive..Trim()

.Trim()

Age and genetics matter. Heifers should be bred at 15-18 months when they weigh about 65% of mature weight (e.g., 250-300 kg for local breeds). Bulls should be at least 15 months old and in good condition. Overworked bulls (more than 30 cows) may have poor fertility. Rotate bulls every 2-3 years to avoid inbreeding..Trim()

Estrus Detection and Timing

Detecting heat (estrus) accurately is the single most important factor for successful breeding. A cow in heat will stand still when mounted by another cow or bull. This is called 'standing heat' and is the primary sign. Other signs: clear stringy mucus hanging from the vulva, swollen red vulva, bellowing, restlessness, and reduced milk yield. .Trim()

Observe your herd at least twice daily, ideally three times, for 30 minutes each. The best times are early morning and late evening when temperatures are cooler. Use heat detection aids: chin-ball markers on teaser bulls, tailhead patches (e.g., Estrotect), or paint sticks. Record every heat on a calendar or notebook. Timing of insemination is critical. The rule is 'a.m./p.m.': if you see standing heat in the morning, breed that evening (12 hours later); if you see it in the evening, breed the next morning. Natural mating means keeping a bull with the cows. It is simple and requires no special equipment. A bull can detect heat and mate without your help. For small herds (10-30 cows), one bull is enough. Reproductive Health Issues

Reproductive problems reduce herd fertility and cost money. Common issues: (1) Infertility: a cow that does not conceive after 3-4 services. Causes: poor nutrition, disease, or anatomical defects. Prevention is better than cure. Vaccinate against brucellosis, leptospirosis, IBR, and BVD. In Africa, brucellosis is a major cause of abortion; test and cull positive animals. Treatment options: For metritis, antibiotics (e.g., oxytetracycline) and uterine flushing. For retained placenta, do not pull; give antibiotics and oxytocin. Forage Species Selection

Choosing the right forage species is the foundation of profitable cattle farming. In Africa, you need species that match your climate, soil, and cattle needs. Start by identifying your agro-ecological zone: arid, semi-arid, sub-humid, or humid. For dry areas, drought-tolerant grasses like Buffel grass (Cenchrus ciliaris) and Rhodes grass (Chloris gayana) are best. In wetter regions, Napier grass (Pennisetum purpureum) and Guinea grass (Panicum maximum) give high yields. Legumes like Desmodium, Lablab, and Leucaena add protein to the diet and fix nitrogen in the soil. Always mix grasses and legumes: a ratio of 70% grass to 30% legume works well. Consider your cattle's purpose: dairy cows need high-protein, high-energy forage like Napier and Lucerne; beef cattle can do well on lower-quality pastures. Also think about establishment: some species need seedbeds, others can be planted by cuttings. For example, Napier is planted using stems, while Rhodes grass is seeded. Check local availability of seeds or planting material. Buy certified seeds from reputable agro-dealers to avoid weeds and poor germination. Test soil before planting: most forages need pH 5.5-7.0. If your soil is acidic, add lime. Also consider seasonality: you need forage year-round. Combine cool-season and warm-season species if possible. In East Africa, farmers often use Rhodes grass with desmodium for cut-and-carry. In West Africa, Gamba grass (Andropogon gayanus) is common. Always start with a small trial plot to see what works before scaling up. Remember, the best species is the one that grows well on your farm and that your cattle eat readily..Trim()

Pasture Establishment and Renovation

Establishing a new pasture or renovating an old one requires careful planning. First, clear the land of bushes, stumps, and old grass. Use a tractor or ox-plough to plough to a depth of 20-30 cm. Then harrow to break clods and level the field. Soil testing is crucial: take samples from 10 spots per hectare, mix them, and send to a lab. Based on results, apply lime if pH is below 5.5, and add phosphorus (e.g., 200 kg/ha of triple superphosphate) and potassium if needed. For planting, choose the right season: just before the rains for dry areas, or at the start of rains. Sow seeds at recommended rates: Rhodes grass 10-15 kg/ha, Napier grass 2-3 stems per hole. For legumes like desmodium, use 2-3 kg/ha. Plant in rows 30-50 cm apart to allow weeding. Cover seeds lightly with soil, not deeper than 1 cm. Water if possible, but rain-fed is common. After planting, keep livestock off until the grass is well-establishedâusually 3-6 months. Renovating an old pasture: if it's overgrazed or weedy, you can overseed with legumes like stylo or siratro. First, graze heavily to reduce old grass, then broadcast legume seeds and let them set seed. Alternatively, plough and replant entirely. For weed control before planting, you can use glyphosate (e.g., Roundup) at 3-4 L/ha, but wait two weeks before planting. Fertilize established pastures with nitrogen (50-100 kg/ha of urea) after each grazing cycle in pure grass stands. In mixed pastures, legumes provide nitrogen. Always keep records of planting dates, inputs, and yields to improve management..Trim()

Grazing Systems and Rotational Grazing

Rotational grazing is the key to healthy pastures and better cattle performance. Instead of letting cattle roam freely, you divide your land into paddocksâat least 4 to 8âand move cattle regularly. The principle: graze a paddock for 1-3 days, then rest it for 21-35 days, depending on grass growth. In dry seasons, rest periods may be longer. This allows grass to recover, roots to deepen, and prevents overgrazing. You can use electric fencing, which is cheaper and easy to move. For a 10-hectare farm, divide into 8 paddocks of 1.25 ha each. With 20 cattle, each paddock can be grazed for 2 days, giving 14 days restâadjust based on grass availability. Other systems: strip grazing (move fence daily) gives maximum use but more labour; creep grazing allows calves to access better pasture ahead of cows. Zero grazing (cut-and-carry) is common in intensive dairy farms: cut forage daily and feed in stalls. This requires more labour but yields more milk per hectare. For rotational grazing, you need a grazing plan: map your paddocks, record grazing dates and rest periods. Monitor grass height: graze when grass is 20-30 cm tall, and stop when it's down to 5-10 cm. Never graze below 5 cmâit damages the plant. In dry periods, reduce stocking rate or provide supplementary feed like hay or silage. Rotational grazing improves soil health, reduces parasites (because larvae die in rested paddocks), and increases drought resilience. Start simple: use temporary electric fences and move cattle every 2-3 days. You'll see results in one season..Trim()

Hay and Silage Production

Hay and silage are conserved forages for dry seasons when pasture is scarce. Hay is dried grass; silage is fermented, wet forage. Making hay: cut grass when it's at early flowering stageâusually 50-70 days after planting for Rhodes grass. Cut in the morning after dew has dried. Leave to dry in the sun for 1-2 days, turning it once or twice. Aim for 15-20% moisture: the grass should be brittle and crack when twisted. Bale using a baler or manually with a box baler. Store in a dry, ventilated shed on pallets to avoid mould. Hay quality depends on leafiness and green colour. For silage: chop fresh forage (maize, sorghum, or Napier) to 2-3 cm pieces. Fill a pit, trench, or plastic tube (silo) quickly, compacting each layer to remove air. Add molasses (2-3% of fresh weight) to improve fermentation if forage is low in sugar. Cover with plastic sheet and weight down with tyres or soil. Fermentation takes 3-4 weeks. Good silage smells sweet, is slightly acidic, and retains green colour. For small-scale farmers, using airtight plastic bags (e.g., 50 kg bags) is practical. You can also make haylage (wilted silage) with 40-60% moisture. In Africa, many farmers make hay from natural pastures, but planted forages give better quality. Store enough for 3-6 months of dry season. For 10 cattle, you need about 10-15 tonnes of hay per year. Test your hay/silage for quality: crude protein should be 8-12% for hay, 10-15% for silage. Feed hay in racks to reduce waste. Always have water available..Trim()

Soil Fertility and Management

Healthy soil grows healthy pasture. Key soil properties: pH, organic matter, nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), and micronutrients. Test your soil every 2-3 years. In Africa, many soils are acidic and low in P. Lime application: if pH is below 5.5, apply agricultural lime at 2-4 tonnes per hectare, based on soil test. Lime takes months to act, so apply before planting. Phosphorus is critical for root development and legume growth. Apply triple superphosphate (TSP) at 100-200 kg/ha at planting, and maintain with 50-100 kg/ha annually. Potassium: apply muriate of potash (MOP) at 50-100 kg/ha if deficient. Nitrogen: for grass pastures, apply urea (46% N) at 50-100 kg/ha after each grazing cycle. But legumes fix nitrogen: inoculate legume seeds with appropriate rhizobia before planting. For example, Desmodium needs specific inoculant. Organic matter: add manure or compost (5-10 tonnes/ha) to improve soil structure and water holding. Rotational grazing helps build organic matter by leaving litter and roots. Avoid overgrazingâit exposes soil to erosion and depletes nutrients. In dry areas, use conservation agriculture: minimum tillage, cover crops. Also, manage soil erosion: plant contour grass strips, avoid grazing on steep slopes. For smallholder farmers, integrated soil fertility management (ISFM) combines organic and mineral fertilizers. Example: apply 2 tonnes/ha of manure plus 100 kg/ha of TSP for maize-legume rotation. Keep records of fertilizer applications and pasture yields to adjust. Remember, soil fertility is a long-term investment; consistent management pays off in higher forage yields and better cattle health..Trim()

Weed and Pest Control in Pastures

Weeds and pests reduce pasture yield and quality. Common weeds: couch grass, blackjack, thorny amaranth, and striga. Control starts with good establishment: healthy, dense pasture outcompetes weeds. For annual weeds, mow before they set seed. For perennial weeds like couch grass, plough and dry the roots, or use herbicides. Glyphosate (e.g., Roundup) at 3-4 L/ha kills most weeds but also kills your grassâuse only before planting or as spot treatment. For selective control in grass pastures, use 2,4-D at 1-2 L/ha to kill broadleaf weeds like blackjack. Always read labels and wear protective gear. In legume-grass mixtures, avoid 2,4-D as it kills legumes. Instead, use hand weeding or mowing. Striga (witchweed) is a problem in poor soils; improve fertility and rotate with non-host crops like legumes. Pests: termites, armyworms, and grasshoppers. Termites attack roots and stems; destroy mounds and use termite bait. Armyworms: scout weekly, and if you see 2-3 larvae per square metre, spray with Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) or cypermethrin. Grasshoppers: use bait or spray with malathion. For ticks and internal parasites, rotational grazing helps: rest paddocks for 30 days to break life cycles. Also, keep cattle healthyâwell-nourished animals resist pests. Biological control: encourage natural enemies like birds and ladybirds. For small farms, hand-pick large insects. Always integrate methods: cultural (grazing management), mechanical (mowing), biological, and chemical as last resort. Monitor your pasture weekly for signs of weeds or pests. Early action prevents spread. Keep a farm diary: note weed/pest outbreaks, treatments, and results. Train your workers to identify common problems. Healthy pasture is the best defence..Trim()

Low-Stress Handling Techniques

Cattle are prey animals. Their survival instinct makes them fearful of sudden movements, loud noises, and anything that feels like a predator. Low-stress handling means working with this instinct, not against it. The payoff is real: calmer cattle gain weight faster, bruise less, and are less likely to injure you or themselves. A stressed cow can lose 2-3 kg of body weight in a single day of rough handling, and bruises cost you money at the abattoir..Trim()

.Trim()

The flight zone is the space around an animal that triggers it to move away when you enter it. For most African cattle, this zone is 3-5 metres. If you walk directly toward a cow's head, she will turn and run. Instead, approach at an angle from the side, and work the edge of the flight zone. To move a cow forward, step into the zone behind her shoulder. To stop her, step back out. Use the point of balance at the shoulder: pressure behind it moves her forward, pressure in front moves her backward..Trim()

.Trim()

Cattle have a wide field of vision, nearly 300 degrees, but a blind spot directly behind them. They also see shadows and contrast poorly. A dark puddle on a concrete floor may look like a hole. A flapping plastic bag on a fence can stop a whole herd. Before moving cattle, walk the route and remove anything that flutters, shines, or looks unfamiliar. Use a solid barrier or a person to block the escape route you don't want them to take..Trim()

.Trim()

When you must move cattle, do it slowly. Walk, don't run. Speak in low tones or stay quiet. Use flags or sticks as extensions of your arm, not as weapons. Never hit an animal around the head or udder. If one animal panics, stop, let it settle, then continue. In Africa, many farms use donkeys or dogs to move cattle; trained dogs can work well, but untrained dogs cause chaos. If you use dogs, keep them under voice control and never let them bite..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, handle cattle in the cool hours of early morning or late afternoon. Heat stress adds to handling stress. Provide water and shade at the handling facility. A well-designed crush, race, and loading ramp reduces the need for force. We will cover equipment in the next lesson, but remember: good handling is 80% attitude and 20% equipment..Trim()

Use of Handling Equipment

Good equipment makes handling safer and faster. The basic kit for any cattle farm includes a crush (also called a chute), a head gate, a race, a loading ramp, and a set of hurdles or panels. In many parts of Africa, you can build these from local timber or welded steel. A simple wooden crush can be made from 10 cm x 10 cm hardwood posts set 60-70 cm apart, with a head gate that closes behind the neck. The floor should be concrete or packed gravel that drains well, because cattle slip on smooth, wet surfaces..Trim()

.Trim()

The crush should be 70-75 cm wide for mature zebu or Boran cattle, and 60-65 cm for smaller breeds. If it is too wide, the animal turns around; too narrow, and she panics. The head gate must open and close smoothly from the side, not from behind the animal. A good head gate has a neck opening of about 20-25 cm and a bottom bar that prevents the animal from going down. Always check that the gate cannot swing back and hit the operator..Trim()

.Trim()

A race is a narrow alley leading to the crush. It should be 60-70 cm wide, with solid sides at least 1.5 m high. Solid sides prevent the animal from seeing distractions and reduce balking. If you cannot afford solid sides, use tightly spaced horizontal rails. The race should curve gently; cattle naturally follow curves better than straight lines. A curved race with a 3-4 m radius works well. At the end of the race, a loading ramp should rise no more than 20 degrees. A steeper ramp frightens cattle and causes slips..Trim()

.Trim()

For restraining individual animals, you need a halter and a nose lead. A simple rope halter can be made from 8 mm nylon rope. Place it over the head behind the ears, with the nose loop over the muzzle. Never tie a cow by the horns alone; she can break her neck. For leg restraint, use a hobble or a rope around the hind legs above the hock. For castration or branding, use a crush with a head gate and side access. Always have a second person to help..Trim()

.Trim()

Maintain your equipment. Check for sharp edges, broken welds, and loose bolts. Oil hinges and latches regularly. Store ropes and halters out of the sun to prevent rot. A well-maintained crush lasts 10-15 years. If you cannot afford metal, hardwood treated with used engine oil will last 5-7 years. Remember, equipment is only as good as the handler. Even the best crush will not help if you rush and shout..Trim()

Animal Welfare Principles

Animal welfare is not just about being kind. It is about meeting the basic needs of cattle so they stay healthy, grow well, and produce more. The World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) recognises five freedoms: freedom from hunger and thirst, freedom from discomfort, freedom from pain, injury and disease, freedom from fear and distress, and freedom to express normal behaviour. In practical African cattle farming, these freedoms translate into specific actions..Trim()

.Trim()

Freedom from hunger and thirst means cattle must have access to clean water at all times. A mature cow drinks 30-50 litres per day, more in hot weather. A lactating cow can drink up to 80 litres. If water is more than 500 metres away, cattle will not walk to it often enough. Provide troughs or dams within 200 metres of grazing. Feed must be adequate for the season. In the dry season, supplement with hay, silage, or crop residues. A cow needs about 2-2.5% of her body weight in dry matter per day. For a 400 kg cow, that is 8-10 kg of dry feed..Trim()

.Trim()

Freedom from discomfort means shade and shelter. Cattle can suffer heat stress above 30Â°C, especially if humidity is high. Provide trees, a simple roof, or a wall for windbreak. In the Sahel, a thatched shade structure reduces heat load significantly. Also, keep bedding dry if cattle are housed. Wet, muddy floors cause foot rot and mastitis..Trim()

.Trim()

Freedom from pain, injury and disease means regular health care. Vaccinate against anthrax, blackquarter, and foot-and-mouth disease where available. Deworm every 3-6 months. Treat wounds immediately with antiseptic. If you castrate or dehorn, use pain relief if possible, and do it at a young age. Disbud calves before 8 weeks with a hot iron or caustic paste..Trim()

.Trim()

Freedom from fear and distress means low-stress handling as covered earlier. Never shout, hit, or use electric prods except as a last resort. Freedom to express normal behaviour means cattle need space to graze, socialise, and lie down. A cow lies down for 10-12 hours a day. If she cannot, her rumen stops working properly. Provide at least 5 square metres per cow in a pen, and rotate grazing to prevent overgrazing. Good welfare is good business..Trim()

Transportation of Cattle

Transporting cattle is one of the most stressful events in their lives. Poor transport causes weight loss, bruising, injuries, and even death. In Africa, cattle are moved by truck, pickup, trailer, or on foot. Each method has rules that protect both the animal and your wallet..Trim()

.Trim()

Before loading, prepare the truck. The floor must be non-slip. Use sand, wooden cleats, or rubber mats. The sides must be high enough that cattle cannot jump out: at least 1.8 m for adult cattle. The roof should provide shade. Never overload. A standard 7-tonne truck can carry about 20-25 head of 400 kg cattle. For a pickup with a cage, carry no more than 4-5 head. Overloading causes suffocation and broken legs. Also, check the ramp angle: no more than 20 degrees. If the ramp is too steep, build a step or use a loading bank..Trim()

.Trim()

During loading, move cattle quietly. Use a race and a flag. Do not use electric prods. If an animal goes down, stop and let her get up. Never drag a downer animal. Once loaded, secure the tailgate. Drive smoothly. Sudden braking throws cattle forward. On bad roads, reduce speed to 20-30 km/h. Avoid potholes. If the journey is longer than 8 hours, stop every 4 hours to check the animals. Provide water at stops, but do not feed heavily before transport; a full rumen increases stress and bloat risk..Trim()

.Trim()

In hot weather, transport early morning or at night. The temperature inside a metal truck can reach 40Â°C even if outside is 30Â°C. Ventilation holes should be at least 10% of the floor area. In cold or rainy weather, provide a tarpaulin on the windward side, but leave gaps for air. Never leave cattle standing in a parked truck in the sun. If you must wait, park in shade and open the sides..Trim()

.Trim()

For long distances, consider rest stops at quarantine stations or holding grounds. In many countries, you need a movement permit and veterinary health certificate. Check local regulations. On arrival, unload slowly. Let cattle settle in a pen with water and hay. Do not mix with resident cattle for at least 2 weeks to avoid disease spread. Weigh a sample before and after to monitor shrinkage. A well-managed 100 km trip should cause less than 2% weight loss. Poor transport can cause 10% or more. That is money lost..Trim()

Euthanasia and Emergency Procedures

Sometimes an animal is suffering and cannot be saved. Euthanasia means ending life humanely, without pain or distress. As a cattle farmer, you must know when and how to do it. The decision is never easy, but delaying causes more suffering. Indications include: a broken leg that cannot be set, severe bloat that does not respond to treatment, incurable disease, or a downer animal that cannot stand after 24 hours of proper care..Trim()

.Trim()

Before euthanasia, confirm the diagnosis with a veterinarian if possible. If not, use your judgement. The goal is a quick, painless death. The best method for cattle is a captive bolt pistol, followed by bleeding (exsanguination) or pithing. A captive bolt stuns the animal instantly. Place the bolt at the intersection of two lines drawn from the base of the horns to the opposite eye. For hornless cattle, aim at the centre of the forehead, about 2-3 cm above the eyes. The animal should collapse immediately. If she does not, repeat. After stunning, bleed by cutting the throat or sticking the jugular vein. Death occurs within 1-2 minutes..Trim()

.Trim()

If you do not have a captive bolt, a firearm is acceptable. Use a .22 calibre or larger, aimed at the same point. Never use a shotgun; it may not penetrate. For young calves, a sharp blow to the head with a heavy hammer can be used, but it must be followed by bleeding. Do not use poison; it is dangerous to humans and scavengers. Do not bury alive. Do not let an animal bleed out without stunning first..Trim()

.Trim()

Emergency procedures include bloat treatment and downer cow care. For bloat, if the animal is breathing heavily and the left flank is distended, pass a stomach tube or trocarise. A trocar is a sharp metal tube inserted into the left flank at the point of the triangle formed by the hip, last rib, and loin. Insert it 2-3 cm behind the last rib, 10-15 cm below the spine. Release gas slowly. For a downer cow, roll her every 2 hours, provide deep bedding, and lift with a hip hoist if available. Give calcium if milk fever is suspected..Trim()

.Trim()

Always have a euthanasia plan. Keep the captive bolt and ammunition clean and stored safely. Train at least two people on your farm. After euthanasia, dispose of the carcass by deep burial (at least 1.5 m deep) or burning, away from water sources. Notify your veterinarian if anthrax is suspected; do not open the carcass. Humane endings are part of good farming..Trim()

Stockmanship and Observation

Stockmanship is the art of knowing your cattle. A good stockman can spot a sick cow before she shows obvious signs. This skill saves money and suffering. Observation is not just looking; it is seeing. Spend at least 15-20 minutes each day walking among your herd, quietly, without disturbing them. Watch how they move, eat, drink, and interact. You will learn each animal's normal behaviour. Then, any change stands out..Trim()

.Trim()

Start with the head. A healthy cow has bright eyes, alert ears, and a moist nose. A sick cow may have dull eyes, droopy ears, and a dry nose. Check the mouth for drooling or sores. Look at the rumen: the left side of the abdomen should have a visible contraction every 1-2 minutes. You can also listen with a stethoscope or put your ear against the flank. A rumen that is not moving means trouble. Check the manure: it should be well-formed, like a soft pat. Diarrhoea or blood is a red flag. Urine should be clear to slightly yellow. Dark urine can mean kidney problems..Trim()

.Trim()

Watch the gait. A cow with foot rot will limp and favour one leg. A cow with laminitis will walk stiffly, like a soldier. A cow with a dislocated hip will drag a leg. Check hooves every month; trim overgrown hooves with hoof nippers. In wet conditions, foot rot is common. Treat with zinc sulphate footbath or antibiotics..Trim()

.Trim()

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a simple 1-5 scale. A score of 1 is emaciated, 5 is obese. For beef cattle, aim for 3. For dairy, 3.5 at calving. Check the backbone, ribs, and hip bones. If you can see individual ribs, she is too thin. If you cannot feel the ribs, she is too fat. Weigh or condition score every month. Sudden weight loss means parasites, poor feed, or disease..Trim()

.Trim()

Observe behaviour. A cow in heat will stand to be mounted. A cow about to calve will separate from the herd and have a swollen vulva. A cow with mastitis will kick when you touch the udder. A cow with a retained placenta will have a bloody discharge. Write down what you see. Keep a notebook or use a simple phone app. Record dates of vaccination, deworming, and calving. Good records help you spot patterns. Finally, train your workers. Everyone who handles cattle must know these signs. A good stockman is the best insurance you can have..Trim()

Business Planning for Cattle Farms

A cattle business plan is your roadmap to profit. Start by defining your goal: are you selling weaners, finished beef, milk, or breeding stock? Each has different costs and timelines. For a smallholder in Kenya or Nigeria, a 20-cow herd can be profitable. Write down your starting resources: land, cattle, water, and capital. Then list monthly costs: feed, veterinary care, labour, and transport. For example, in East Africa, a cow eats about 2% of its body weight in dry matter daily. A 400 kg cow needs 8 kg of feed. If you grow napier grass, you can cut costs. But if you buy hay at $0.20 per kg, that's $1.60 per day per cow. For 20 cows, that's $32 daily, or $960 monthly. Now add vet costs ($10 per cow per year), labour ($200 per month), and water. Your total monthly expenses might be $1,500. To break even, you need to sell at least 2 cows per month at $750 each, or 1 cow at $1,500. But prices vary. In Botswana, a good beef cow can fetch $800 to $1,200 at auction. So plan your sales. Also include a cash flow statement: when will you have money coming in? Cattle take 2-3 years to mature. You might sell weaners at 8 months for $300 each. That's faster cash. Your plan should cover 3-5 years. Include risks: drought, disease, price drops. Have a mitigation plan, like buying insurance or keeping a reserve fund. Finally, set SMART goals: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. For example, 'Increase herd from 20 to 30 cows by December 2025.' Write your plan down and review it every 3 months. Use simple tools like a notebook or free apps like Wave for accounting. A good plan helps you get loans from banks or microfinance institutions. Many African banks require a business plan for agricultural loans. So make it clear and realistic..Trim()

Record Keeping and Accounting

Keeping records is like keeping score in a football match. Without it, you don't know if you're winning or losing. For cattle farming, you need three types of records: production, financial, and health. Production records track births, deaths, weights, and milk yield. Financial records track income and expenses. Health records track vaccinations, treatments, and vet visits. Start with a simple notebook. Divide it into sections. For each cow, give an ID number or name. Record its date of birth, parentage, and weight every 3 months. Use a weighing tape to estimate weight: measure heart girth in centimetres. For a typical Zebu cow, weight (kg) = (heart girth in cm)^2 / 300. So a girth of 150 cm gives 75 kg? That's wrong. Actually, the formula for cattle is: weight = (heart girth^2 x body length) / 300. But for simplicity, use a weigh band. In many African markets, you can buy a cattle weigh tape for $5. For finances, record every sale and purchase. Example: On 1 March, you sell a bull for $600. On 5 March, you buy 10 bags of feed for $200. At the end of the month, subtract expenses from income. If income is $600 and expenses are $400, your profit is $200. But remember, some costs are annual, like vaccinations. Divide them monthly. Use a cash book: two columns, one for money in, one for money out. At the end of the year, calculate your profit. Also track accounts receivable (money owed to you) and payable (money you owe). For taxes, many African countries have smallholder exemptions, but check local laws. In Kenya, for example, agricultural income is taxed differently. Keep receipts for everything. A simple smartphone app like 'Farm Records' or 'AgriTracker' can help. If you have no smartphone, use a ledger book. Review your records monthly. Look for patterns: Are you spending too much on feed? Are your cows getting sick often? Records help you make decisions. They also prove your creditworthiness when applying for a loan. So start today, even with just one cow..Trim()

Marketing Channels for Cattle

You can sell cattle through many channels. Each has pros and cons. Choose based on your location, volume, and target market. 1. Local auctions: In countries like South Africa, Namibia, and Botswana, auctions are common. You bring your cattle, they are weighed and sold to the highest bidder. Auction fees are typically 5-10% of the sale price. For a $500 cow, you pay $25-$50. Auctions give you cash immediately, but prices can be low if many sellers show up. 2. Direct to butcheries: Butcheries often buy directly from farmers. You can negotiate a price per kg. In Kenya, a butchery might pay $2.50 per kg live weight. A 400 kg cow gives $1,000. But you need to transport the cow to the butchery. 3. To feedlots: Feedlots buy young cattle to fatten. They pay per kg. In Zimbabwe, feedlots may pay $2.00 per kg. They often prefer steers. 4. To other farmers: You can sell breeding stock or weaners to other farmers. This is common in Nigeria. You can advertise on WhatsApp groups or local radio. 5. Online platforms: In Kenya, platforms like 'Mkulima Young' or 'Farmers Market' connect farmers to buyers. You can post photos and prices. 6. Export: For high volumes, you can export to countries like Egypt or the Middle East. But this requires meeting health and quality standards. You need a veterinary certificate and export license. 7. Cooperatives: Join a cooperative to pool cattle and sell in bulk. This gives you better bargaining power. For example, in Ethiopia, cooperatives sell to meat processors. 8. Agritourism: If you are near a city, you can invite visitors to see your farm and buy beef directly. This is a growing trend. To choose the best channel, consider: distance to market, transport costs, price offered, and payment terms. Always have a backup channel. For example, if auction prices are low, sell directly to a butchery. Also, time your sales. Prices are often higher during festivals like Eid or Christmas. So plan your breeding to have cattle ready then. Finally, build relationships with buyers. Trust leads to repeat business..Trim()

Pricing and Risk Management

Pricing cattle is not guesswork. It depends on weight, breed, age, and market demand. In most African markets, price is based on live weight. For example, in Uganda, a local Zebu might sell for $1.50 per kg, while a crossbreed sells for $2.00 per kg. To get the best price, know your cow's weight. Use a weigh tape or scale. Then check current market prices. You can call local auctions or check online forums. Set a minimum price below which you won't sell. But be flexible. If you have many cattle, you might accept a lower price to avoid feeding costs. Risk management is about protecting your investment. Major risks: drought, disease, theft, and price fluctuations. For drought, build a reserve of hay or silage. In Kenya, a bale of hay costs $3. For 20 cows, you need 20 bales per month, so $60. Save money in good times. For disease, vaccinate regularly. Common vaccines: FMD, anthrax, blackleg. In Nigeria, a vaccine costs $0.50 per dose. Also, insure your cattle. In South Africa, livestock insurance costs about 3% of the animal's value per year. For a $500 cow, that's $15 per year. For theft, brand your cattle and keep them in a secure kraal. Price fluctuations: prices drop when many farmers sell at once. So stagger your sales. Sell some in dry season when prices are higher. Also, consider forward contracts: agree to sell at a fixed price in 6 months. This locks in your price. Another risk is feed cost. If you rely on bought feed, prices can rise. Grow your own fodder. For example, plant maize and make silage. Finally, keep a financial buffer. Aim for 3-6 months of operating expenses in savings. This helps you survive shocks. Remember, risk management is not about avoiding all risks, but about reducing their impact. So plan ahead..Trim()

Value-Added Products

Value-added products can double or triple your income from cattle. Instead of selling a live cow for $500, you can process it into products worth $1,500. Here are practical options: 1. Beef processing: Slaughter and sell cuts. A 400 kg cow yields about 200 kg of meat. If you sell at $5 per kg, that's $1,000. But you need a slaughter license and cold storage. In Kenya, you can rent a butchery space. 2. Milk and dairy: If you have dairy cows, sell milk. A good cow gives 10 litres per day. At $0.50 per litre, that's $5 daily, $150 monthly. Process into yoghurt or cheese for higher value. Yoghurt sells for $1.50 per litre. You need a pasteurizer and starter culture. 3. Hides and skins: A hide can sell for $10-$20. But if you tan it, it can become leather worth $50. Tanning is a skill; you can learn from local tanneries. 4. Manure: Cow dung is valuable fertilizer. A cow produces about 10 kg of manure per day. You can sell it at $0.10 per kg, earning $1 per day per cow. Or use it to grow crops. 5. Breeding stock: Sell pregnant cows or bulls. A proven bull can sell for $1,000. 6.
