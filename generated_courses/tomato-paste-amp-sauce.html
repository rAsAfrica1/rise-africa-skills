Why Tomato Paste and Sauce is a Profitable Business in Africa

Tomato paste and sauce is a daily staple in most African households. From jollof rice in Nigeria to ugali in Kenya, stews in Ghana, and pasta in South Africa, tomatoes are the base of many meals. But fresh tomatoes are seasonal, perishable, and often expensive off-season. That is why processed tomato paste and sauce are in constant demand. A small-scale producer can buy fresh tomatoes during harvest when prices are low and turn them into paste or sauce that sells all year round..Trim()

The profit margins are attractive. In many markets, a 70g sachet of tomato paste sells for 50 to 100 Naira (or equivalent) while costing about 20 to 30 Naira to produce. A 400g tin or jar sells for 500 to 1,000 Naira, with production costs around 250 to 400 Naira. If you produce 100 sachets a day, you can make 3,000 to 5,000 Naira profit daily. That is a real income for a household..Trim()

.Trim()

The business is scalable. You can start in your kitchen with a few pots, a blender, and a sealing machine. As demand grows, you can move to a small shed, buy a pulper, a boiler, and a filling machine. You can sell to neighbours, local shops, restaurants, and street vendors. You can also supply schools, hospitals, and catering services..Trim()

.Trim()

Africa imports billions of dollars worth of tomato paste every year. That is a huge opportunity for local producers. Governments and NGOs often support local agro-processing with training and grants. By producing locally, you reduce post-harvest losses, create jobs, and keep money in your community. The market is not saturated; in many areas, demand exceeds supply. With consistent quality and good packaging, you can build a loyal customer base and a profitable business..Trim()

Choosing the Right Tomato Varieties for Paste and Sauce

For tomato paste and sauce, you need tomatoes that are meaty, low in water, and high in natural sugars and acids. In Africa, the best varieties are often local landraces like 'Tengeru' in Tanzania, 'Marmande' in South Africa, or improved types such as 'Roma VF', 'UC82B', and 'Petomech'. These are determinate (bush) types that ripen all at once, making harvest and processing easier. Avoid watery salad tomatoes like 'Money Maker' â they give thin paste and need more cooking, which wastes fuel..Trim()

.Trim()

When selecting seeds or seedlings, look for varieties with resistance to local diseases like bacterial wilt and early blight. In West Africa, 'Ibadan Local' is popular for its deep red colour and thick walls. In East Africa, 'Tanya' is a good choice for paste because it has few seeds and high solids. If you are buying from a market, ask the seller which variety they have. If they don't know, look for tomatoes that are oval or pear-shaped, not round and juicy..Trim()

.Trim()

For sauce, you can use slightly softer tomatoes, but they should still be firm and ripe. A good rule: 1 kg of Roma tomatoes yields about 700 g of usable pulp after peeling and seeding. If you use watery tomatoes, you might only get 500 g. That difference matters for your profit. Also, consider colour: deep red tomatoes have more lycopene, which gives paste its rich colour. Pale or greenish tomatoes will make dull paste..Trim()

.Trim()

If you grow your own, plant determinate varieties in blocks, not rows, so they support each other. Space them 60 cm apart. Apply compost or NPK 17-17-17 at planting. Harvest when fruits are fully red but still firm. For paste, pick all at once when 90% are ripe. For sauce, you can harvest every 2-3 days. Never mix varieties in one batch â they cook at different rates and give inconsistent texture..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, test a small batch before committing. Boil 2 kg of tomatoes for 30 minutes, then strain. If you get at least 1.2 kg of thick pulp, the variety is good for paste. If it's watery, use it for juice or sauce instead. Remember: the right variety is the foundation of good paste and sauce..Trim()

How to Test Tomato Quality: Firmness, Colour, and Sweetness

Before you buy or process tomatoes, test them for firmness, colour, and sweetness. These three factors determine your final product's taste, texture, and shelf life. Start with firmness: gently squeeze a tomato in your palm. It should feel heavy for its size and give slightly, like a ripe avocado. If it's rock hard, it's unripe and will be acidic. If it's mushy, it's overripe and will spoil quickly. For paste, you want firm-ripe tomatoes. For sauce, you can use slightly softer ones, but never use tomatoes with soft spots or mould..Trim()

.Trim()

Colour is next. A good tomato is uniformly red from stem to bottom. Green shoulders mean it was picked too early; it will lack sweetness and have a bitter taste. Yellow or orange patches mean it's not fully ripe. For paste, deep red is best because it indicates high lycopene. Hold the tomato against a light â if you see dark spots inside, discard it. Also check the skin: it should be smooth and shiny, not wrinkled or cracked. Cracks let in bacteria..Trim()

.Trim()

Sweetness is measured with a refractometer, but you can also taste. Cut a wedge and chew it. A good paste tomato has a balance of sweet and tart, with a sugar content of at least 5% (5 Brix). If it tastes watery or bland, it won't make good paste. You can also use a simple float test: put tomatoes in a bucket of water. Those that sink are denser and better for paste; those that float are watery and better for juice. For sauce, you want a Brix of 4-5%..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many markets sell tomatoes in bulk. Take a random sample of 10 tomatoes. Check each for firmness, colour, and taste. If more than 2 are bad, reject the lot or negotiate a lower price. Also, smell the tomatoes â they should smell sweet and earthy, not sour or fermented. If they smell like alcohol, they are rotting..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, test acidity. High-acid tomatoes (pH below 4.6) are safer for canning. You can use pH strips. If pH is above 4.6, add lemon juice or citric acid when making sauce. For paste, you want pH 4.0-4.4. Remember: quality in, quality out. Spend time testing before you buy..Trim()

Washing and Sorting Tomatoes to Remove Dirt and Spoiled Fruits

Washing and sorting are critical steps to prevent dirt, stones, and spoiled fruits from ruining your paste or sauce. Start by filling a large basin or drum with clean water. For 20 kg of tomatoes, use about 40 litres of water. Add 1 tablespoon of food-grade bleach (sodium hypochlorite) per 10 litres to kill bacteria, or use 2 tablespoons of salt per 10 litres. Never use soap or detergent â it leaves residue..Trim()

.Trim()

Sort tomatoes before washing. Remove any with mould, deep cracks, bird pecks, or soft spots. Also remove green tomatoes, leaves, and stems. A good rule: discard at least 5% of the harvest. If you have 100 kg, expect to throw away 5 kg. Do this on a clean tarpaulin or table, not on the ground. Wear gloves to protect your hands from acids and dirt..Trim()

.Trim()

Wash in three stages. First, dump tomatoes into the first basin and swirl gently for 2 minutes. This removes loose dirt. Then transfer to a second basin with clean water and 1 tablespoon of bleach per 10 litres. Soak for 5 minutes. Finally, rinse in a third basin with clean water. Never reuse dirty water â it spreads bacteria. Change water every 20 kg of tomatoes..Trim()

.Trim()

After washing, inspect each tomato again. Cut out any remaining bad spots with a clean knife. If a tomato is more than 20% spoiled, discard it. For paste, you need perfect fruits. For sauce, you can use slightly blemished ones, but still remove all mould. Mould produces mycotoxins that survive cooking..Trim()

.Trim()

Dry the tomatoes on a clean rack or cloth for 10 minutes before peeling. This prevents water from diluting your paste. If you are processing immediately, you can skip drying, but excess water means longer cooking time and more fuel. Always wash your hands, knives, and basins with soap and hot water before starting. Sanitation is key to a safe product. In Africa, where water can be scarce, use the three-basin method to save water while still getting clean tomatoes..Trim()

Blanching and Peeling Tomatoes for Smooth Paste

Blanching and peeling tomatoes is essential for smooth paste. The skin is tough and bitter; if left on, it makes paste gritty and dark. Blanching also loosens the skin and kills surface bacteria. Here's how to do it with basic tools: a large pot, a slotted spoon, a bowl of ice water (or cold water), and a knife..Trim()

.Trim()

First, bring a large pot of water to a rolling boil. For 5 kg of tomatoes, use about 10 litres of water. While waiting, cut a shallow 'X' on the bottom of each tomato. This helps the skin split. When the water boils, drop 5-6 tomatoes into the pot at a time. Do not overcrowd. Boil for 30-60 seconds â just until you see the skin start to curl and pull away from the flesh. Over-blanching cooks the tomato and makes it mushy..Trim()

.Trim()

Immediately transfer the tomatoes to a bowl of ice water or very cold water. This stops the cooking and makes them cool enough to handle. Let them sit for 2 minutes. Then, using your hands or a small knife, peel the skin off. It should slip off easily. If it doesn't, blanch for another 15 seconds. Put the peeled tomatoes in a clean bowl. Discard the skins or save them for other products (see Lesson 6)..Trim()

.Trim()

For large-scale processing, you can use a steam blancher or a perforated basket. But for small businesses, the pot method works fine. Work in batches of 5 kg to keep the water hot. Change the water every 10 kg, or when it becomes cloudy. Add a little salt (1 tablespoon per litre) to the blanching water to help firm the flesh..Trim()

.Trim()

After peeling, remove the hard core with a knife. For paste, you want only the soft flesh. You can also squeeze out some seeds and juice. For every 1 kg of peeled tomatoes, you should have about 800 g of usable flesh. If you have less, you over-blanched. Practice makes perfect. Remember: blanching time depends on tomato size. Small cherry tomatoes need only 20 seconds; large Roma tomatoes need 60 seconds. Always test one tomato first..Trim()

De-seeding and Chopping for Chunky Sauces

For chunky sauces, you want pieces of tomato, not a smooth paste. De-seeding and chopping are done after peeling. Start with peeled tomatoes. Cut each tomato in half horizontally (across the middle). Use your thumb or a small spoon to scoop out the seeds and watery gel into a bowl. Save this â you can use it for juice or stock. For a chunky sauce, you want to remove most seeds because they add bitterness and make the sauce gritty..Trim()

.Trim()

Once de-seeded, chop the tomato flesh into cubes. For a rustic sauce, chop into 1-2 cm pieces. For a finer sauce, chop into 0.5 cm pieces. Use a sharp knife on a clean cutting board. A dull knife crushes the tomato and makes it mushy. Work quickly to avoid losing juice. If you are processing many tomatoes, use a manual food chopper or a stainless steel grater. But a knife gives better control..Trim()

.Trim()

For 5 kg of peeled tomatoes, you should get about 3.5 kg of chopped flesh after de-seeding. The seeds and gel make up about 30% of the weight. If you want a thicker sauce, let the chopped tomatoes sit in a colander for 10 minutes to drain excess water. Save that water for soups..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many traditional sauces use onions, peppers, and oil. Add these after chopping. For a chunky tomato sauce, sautÃ© onions and peppers first, then add the chopped tomatoes. Cook for 20-30 minutes until the sauce thickens. Do not overcook â you want visible pieces. If you want a smoother sauce, blend half of the chopped tomatoes and mix back in..Trim()

.Trim()

Always taste the chopped tomatoes. If they are too acidic, add a pinch of sugar. If too sweet, add a squeeze of lemon. For canning, add 1 tablespoon of lemon juice per 500 ml jar to ensure safety. Remember: de-seeding and chopping are about texture. For paste, you skip this step and blend everything. For sauce, you want chunks. Adjust your chop size to your market â some customers prefer large pieces, others small..Trim()

Reducing Waste: Using Tomato Skins and Seeds for Other Products

Tomato skins and seeds are not waste â they are valuable resources. After peeling and de-seeding, you can turn them into three products: tomato powder, tomato oil, and animal feed. This reduces waste and increases your profit. For every 10 kg of tomatoes, you get about 1 kg of skins and 0.5 kg of seeds. Don't throw them away..Trim()

.Trim()

First, tomato powder. Spread the skins on a clean tray and dry them in the sun for 2-3 days, or in a solar dryer for 6-8 hours. They should be brittle. Grind them in a blender or with a mortar and pestle. Sift to remove large pieces. The powder is rich in lycopene and fibre. Use it to thicken soups, stews, or even as a natural food colouring. Store in an airtight jar for up to 6 months. You can sell 100 g for about $2..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, tomato seed oil. Dry the seeds completely, then grind them. Mix with a little water and boil, then skim off the oil that floats. Or use a manual oil press. Tomato seed oil is rich in antioxidants and good for skin. You can also roast the dried seeds and eat them as a snack, or grind them into a protein-rich flour for animal feed.
