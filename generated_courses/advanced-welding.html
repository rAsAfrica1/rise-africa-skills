Module 1 · Beyond the Basics

Introduction and Why It Matters

Advanced welding is not just about joining metal; it is about creating strong, safe, and lasting structures that can withstand the demands of African industry, construction, and repair work. Whether you are welding a trailer chassis in Nairobi, repairing a water tank in Lagos, or building a gate in Accra, the quality of your weld determines safety and durability. Basic welding gets two pieces stuck together. Advanced welding ensures the joint can carry loads, resist vibration, and survive years of exposure to sun, rain, and dust. Tools and Materials

To do advanced welding, you need more than a basic arc welder and a chipping hammer. Start with a reliable welding machine. For stick welding (SMAW), a 200-amp inverter welder works well on 220V single-phase, common in many African workshops. For MIG welding, a 200-amp MIG with gas capability is ideal. You will also need an angle grinder with 115mm or 230mm discs for cutting and grinding. A wire brush, chipping hammer, and slag hammer are essential for cleaning welds. For measuring and marking, get a steel tape, combination square, and soapstone or marker. Clamps (C-clamps, magnetic clamps) hold parts in place. Personal protective equipment (PPE) is non-negotiable: auto-darkening welding helmet with at least shade 10-13, leather gloves, flame-resistant jacket, safety boots, and a respirator for fumes. For materials, mild steel is the most common and easiest to weld. You will also encounter stainless steel (needs different electrodes and lower heat) and aluminum (requires AC TIG or spool gun MIG). Electrodes: for mild steel, use E6013 for general work and E7018 for high-strength joints. For stainless, use 308L or 316L rods. Filler wire for MIG: ER70S-6 for mild steel. Shielding gas: for MIG, use 75% argon/25% CO2 mix for steel; for TIG, pure argon. Always store electrodes in a dry place; moisture causes porosity. In Africa, you can find these tools at hardware stores in major cities or order online. Invest in quality; cheap tools cost more in downtime and bad welds..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Follow these steps for a strong advanced weld. First, prepare the metal. Clean it with a grinder to bare shiny metal, removing rust, paint, and oil. Bevel edges for thick metal (over 6mm) to ensure penetration. Set your machine: for 3.2mm E6013 electrode on mild steel, set around 90-120 amps. For 4.0mm, use 120-160 amps. For MIG on 6mm steel, set voltage to 20-22V and wire speed to 4-5 m/min. Clamp your work securely. Second, tack weld the pieces at both ends and middle to prevent distortion. Third, start welding. For stick, strike the arc like a match, then maintain a short arc (arc length about equal to electrode diameter). Use a drag angle of 10-15 degrees. For a flat butt weld, move in a straight line or slight weave. For vertical up, use a triangle or Z weave, pausing at edges. For overhead, use lower amperage and faster travel. Fourth, control heat input. If the metal gets too hot (turns blue or grey), stop and let it cool. Overheating causes warping and weakens the weld. Fifth, after welding, clean the slag with a chipping hammer and wire brush. Inspect for defects: cracks, porosity, undercut. If you find a defect, grind it out and re-weld. Finally, let the weld cool naturally; do not quench with water unless specified, as rapid cooling can cause cracking. Practice each position on scrap metal before doing a real job. Remember, consistency in travel speed, angle, and arc length is key..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced welders make mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them. 1) Porosity: small holes in the weld caused by moisture, oil, or too long an arc. Fix: clean metal, use dry electrodes, and shorten arc. 2) Undercut: a groove melted into the base metal along the weld toe. Caused by too high amperage or wrong angle. Fix: reduce amps, adjust angle to 10-15 degrees, and use a slight weave. 3) Slag inclusion: trapped slag inside the weld. Happens when you don't clean between passes or use wrong polarity. Fix: clean thoroughly, use correct polarity (electrode positive for E6013). 4) Spatter: small metal droplets stuck to the workpiece. Caused by too high amperage or long arc. Fix: reduce amps, shorten arc, use anti-spatter spray. 5) Warping: metal bends due to uneven heating. Fix: clamp securely, use tack welds, alternate welding sides, and allow cooling. 6) Incomplete penetration: weld doesn't reach the root. Fix: increase root gap, higher amps, slower travel. 7) Cracking: often from rapid cooling or high carbon steel. Fix: preheat thick steel, use low-hydrogen electrodes like E7018, and cool slowly. 8) Wrong electrode angle: leads to uneven beads. Fix: maintain 10-15 degree drag angle. Always inspect your work with a flashlight and magnifying glass. If you find a defect, grind it out completely and re-weld. Never try to cover up a defect with another pass; it will fail. Practice on scrap until you can consistently produce clean welds..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Welding safety is not optional; it saves lives. In Africa, many workshops lack proper ventilation, so you must protect yourself. Always wear an auto-darkening helmet with correct shade: shade 10 for low amps, 13 for high amps. Never look at the arc without protection; it causes arc eye (painful burns to the cornea). Wear leather gloves, a flame-resistant long-sleeve jacket, and safety boots. Use a respirator when welding galvanized or painted metal, as fumes are toxic. Ensure good ventilation: open doors and windows, use a fan to blow fumes away, but not directly on the weld (it disrupts shielding gas). Keep a fire extinguisher nearby and a bucket of sand or water. Never weld near flammable materials. Check your cables for damage; exposed wires cause electric shock. Ground the work clamp securely. Work on a dry surface, not wet ground. When welding overhead, wear a cap and ear plugs to keep sparks out. Keep your work area clean and organized. Store gas cylinders upright and secured. Never weld a closed container; it can explode. Best practices: plan your weld, use the right process for the metal, and don't rush. Take breaks to avoid fatigue. Stay hydrated. If you feel dizzy, stop and get fresh air. Finally, get trained in first aid for burns and electric shock. Your safety is your responsibility..Trim()

Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. This exercise will help you apply what you've learned. You will need: mild steel plates (100mm x 100mm x 6mm), a stick welder (200A), E6013 electrodes (3.2mm), angle grinder, wire brush, chipping hammer, clamps, and PPE. Task 1: Butt weld in flat position. Clean two plates, bevel edges if needed, tack weld at both ends. Set amperage to 100-120A. Weld a straight bead along the joint. Clean slag, inspect for porosity and undercut. Task 2: T-joint in horizontal position. Place one plate vertical on another, tack weld. Weld along the joint using a slight weave. Use 110-130A. Task 3: Vertical up weld. Set two plates vertically with a gap, tack. Weld from bottom to top using a triangle weave, pausing at edges. Use 90-110A. Task 4: Overhead weld. Position plates overhead (secure safely). Use 80-100A, short arc, fast travel. After each task, grind out any defects and re-weld. Record your settings and observations. Aim for consistent bead width and penetration. If you don't have a welder, visit a local workshop and ask to practice under supervision. Alternatively, use a welding simulator app. After completing, compare your welds to the examples in the module. Take photos and share with your instructor or peers for feedback. Repeat until you can produce clean, strong welds in all positions. This practice is the foundation for advanced certification..Trim()

Module 2 · TIG Welding In Depth

Introduction and Why It Matters

TIG welding, or Tungsten Inert Gas welding, is a precise method that uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode to create an arc that melts the base metal. A separate filler rod is added by hand. This gives you control that stick or MIG welding can't match. In Africa, TIG is in high demand for food-grade stainless steel in dairy and brewery plants, aluminium in auto repair and window fabrication, and thin sheet metal in artisan workshops. For example, in Kenya's growing solar panel industry, TIG is used to weld aluminium frames that must be watertight and corrosion-resistant. Learning TIG opens doors to higher-paying jobs because it requires skill and patience. Unlike stick welding, TIG produces clean, spatter-free welds that often need no grinding. That saves time and money. But it's slower and demands good hand-eye coordination. You'll need a TIG machine with high-frequency start, a gas cylinder of 100% argon, and a regulator. In many African cities, you can find used TIG machines for around $300-$600. The gas costs about $50-$80 per refill, but a cylinder lasts many hours. TIG is also versatile: you can weld steel, stainless steel, aluminium, copper, and even titanium. For a self-employed welder, TIG means you can take on repair jobs for restaurants, dairies, and factories that need sanitary welds. This module will teach you from the ground up: tools, technique, fixing mistakes, safety, and a practical exercise. By the end, you'll be able to lay a clean bead on mild steel and stainless steel, and you'll know how to troubleshoot common problems. TIG is a skill that pays for itself quickly if you practice. Let's get started..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To TIG weld, you need the right equipment. First, a TIG welder. Look for one with adjustable amperage (at least 200A for aluminium) and high-frequency start. Inverters are lighter and more energy-efficient. Brands like Miller, Lincoln, or cheaper Chinese units like Everlast are common in Africa. Next, a tungsten electrode. For steel and stainless, use 2% thoriated (red) or ceriated (grey) tungsten. For aluminium, pure tungsten (green) works but lanthanated (gold) is better. You'll need a tungsten grinder or a dedicated grinding wheel to sharpen the tip. Never use the same wheel for steel. Shielding gas: 100% argon for most metals. For thicker aluminium, you can use a helium-argon mix, but argon is fine up to 6mm. Gas flow rate: 10-15 cubic feet per hour (CFH) for most jobs. A flowmeter is essential. Filler rods: for mild steel, use ER70S-6; for stainless, ER308L; for aluminium, ER4043 or ER5356. Sizes: 1.6mm, 2.4mm, and 3.2mm. You'll also need a welding helmet with a shade 9-13 lens, TIG gloves (thin and flexible), and a torch with a collet body, collet, and ceramic cup. Cups come in sizes #4 to #8; #6 is a good all-rounder. A foot pedal is highly recommended for amperage control. For cleaning, have a stainless steel wire brush (dedicated to aluminium only) and acetone or alcohol for wiping oil. In Africa, you can source these from welding supply shops in major cities or online. A basic TIG setup costs around $500-$1000. Don't skimp on gas quality; moisture in gas causes porosity. Keep your tungsten sharp and your metal clean. That's half the battle..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Start by cleaning your metal. Remove all oil, paint, and rust with acetone and a stainless brush. For aluminium, brush with a dedicated stainless brush until shiny. Set up your machine: for 3mm mild steel, set amperage to 80-100A; for 3mm stainless, 60-80A; for 3mm aluminium, 120-150A. Set gas flow to 12 CFH. Sharpen your tungsten to a point (for steel) or a balled tip (for aluminium). Clamp your work lead to the table or workpiece. Put on your helmet and gloves. Hold the torch like a pencil, with the cup resting on the metal if possible. Press the foot pedal to start the arc. Keep a short arc length (1-2mm). For steel, make a small puddle, then dip the filler rod into the leading edge of the puddle. Move forward steadily, dipping and moving. For a bead on plate, travel speed should be about 2-3mm per second. For a butt joint, tack the ends first, then weld from one end to the other. Use a slight forward push angle (10-15 degrees). For aluminium, you need to move faster and use more heat. The puddle should be shiny and fluid. Dip the filler as soon as the puddle forms. If you see black soot, you're too hot or moving too slow. For stainless, keep the heat low and move quickly to avoid warping. After welding, let the post-flow gas run for a few seconds to cool the tungsten. Practice on scrap metal first. Make 10 beads on a flat plate before trying joints. Remember: TIG is about consistency. Keep your arc length constant and your dip rhythm steady. A good exercise is to weld a series of overlapping beads, then cut and etch to see penetration. In Africa, many welders learn by doing repairs on pots, pans, and gates. Start with thin material and work up..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Tungsten touching the puddle. This contaminates the tungsten and causes a sputtery arc. Fix: Sharpen the tungsten again and restart. Keep a short arc but don't dip the tungsten. Mistake 2: Porosity (holes in the weld). Causes: dirty metal, low gas flow, or too much arc length. Fix: Clean with acetone, increase gas to 15 CFH, and shorten arc. Mistake 3: Lack of penetration. You're moving too fast or amps too low. Fix: Increase amps by 10-20A and slow down. For 3mm steel, you need at least 80A. Mistake 4: Burn-through on thin metal. Too many amps or too slow. Fix: Reduce amps, use a faster travel speed, or use a heat sink (copper backing). Mistake 5: Tungsten balling up on steel. This happens if you use pure tungsten on steel. Fix: Use 2% thoriated or ceriated tungsten. Mistake 6: Arc wandering. This is often due to a bent tungsten or wrong grind angle. Fix: Grind tungsten longitudinally, not radially, and keep it straight. Mistake 7: Black soot on aluminium. This is from too much heat or not enough cleaning. Fix: Reduce amps, increase travel speed, and brush the aluminium right before welding. Mistake 8: Cracking in stainless. This can happen from too much heat or fast cooling. Fix: Use less amps, backstep weld, or use a chill block. Mistake 9: Gas leaks. Check hose connections with soapy water. Replace cracked hoses. Mistake 10: Inconsistent bead. This is from poor hand position. Fix: Rest your hand on a block or use a torch guide. Practice makes perfect. In Africa, where materials can be expensive, always test on scrap first. Learn to read the puddle: it should be a calm, shiny pool. If it's turbulent, something is wrong. Keep a notebook of settings that work for different thicknesses. That's how you become a pro..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

TIG welding is safer than stick welding because there's no slag and less spatter, but hazards still exist. The arc emits intense ultraviolet (UV) and infrared light. Always wear a welding helmet with a proper shade. For TIG at 100A, use shade 10; for 200A, shade 12. Never look at the arc with naked eyes. Wear flame-resistant clothing, leather gloves, and a leather apron. TIG gloves are thin for dexterity, but they still protect from heat and UV. Ensure good ventilation. TIG welding produces ozone and nitrogen oxides, especially on aluminium. In a small workshop, use a fume extractor or open windows and doors. Never weld in a closed room. Argon gas is heavier than air and can displace oxygen. If you're welding in a pit or confined space, use a respirator and have someone watch you. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. The arc can ignite flammable materials. Clear the area of paper, cloth, and solvents. Electrical safety: check your cables for damage. Don't weld in wet conditions. Use a GFCI if possible. Ground the workpiece properly. When not welding, turn off the machine and close the gas valve. Store gas cylinders upright and secure them with a chain. Never roll a cylinder on its side. In Africa, power fluctuations can damage inverters. Use a surge protector or voltage stabilizer. Best practices: Keep your tungsten sharp and clean. Use the right gas flow. Don't overheat the metal; it can warp or crack. Let the weld cool before handling. Clean your work area daily. Wear safety glasses when grinding tungsten. And always practice good posture to avoid back strain. TIG welding can be done for hours, so take breaks. Drink water. Remember, safety is not just for you; it's for everyone in the shop. A safe welder is a productive welder..Trim()

Practical Exercise

For this exercise, you'll need: a TIG welder, 3mm mild steel plate (100mm x 100mm), ER70S-6 filler rod (2.4mm), argon gas, a #6 cup, 2% thoriated tungsten (2.4mm), a stainless brush, acetone, and a grinder. First, clean the steel with acetone and brush off any rust. Set your machine to DCEN (direct current electrode negative) for steel. Set amperage to 90A. Set gas flow to 12 CFH. Sharpen your tungsten to a sharp point. Clamp the plate to your table. Put on your helmet and gloves. Step 1: Make a bead on plate without filler. Practice moving the torch in a straight line, creating a puddle. Keep arc length 2mm. Do this for 5 passes. Step 2: Add filler. Hold the filler rod at a 15-degree angle. Dip the rod into the leading edge of the puddle, then move forward. Dip every 2-3mm. Aim for a consistent bead width of 4-5mm. Do 10 beads. Step 3: Butt joint. Cut two plates, place them with a 1mm gap, tack both ends. Weld from one end to the other. Use the same dip technique. Check penetration by cutting the weld crosswise and polishing with a grinder. You want full penetration without burn-through. Step 4: T-joint. Stand one plate on another and weld the corner. This is harder; use 80A and move faster. Step 5: Aluminium practice (optional). If you have aluminium, set to AC, use pure tungsten, and 120A. Clean with a stainless brush, weld a bead. After each step, inspect your weld. Look for undercut, porosity, and consistency. Write down the settings that worked. Repeat until you can make a clean bead without stopping. In Africa, you can use scrap metal from car panels or roofing sheets. Practice for at least 2 hours. This exercise builds muscle memory. When you can weld a butt joint on 3mm steel that passes a bend test, you're ready for real jobs..Trim()

Introduction and Why It Matters

MIG welding, also called Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW), is the workhorse of modern metal fabrication. In Africa, where industries from auto repair to construction are booming, MIG welding offers speed, versatility, and ease of learning. Unlike stick welding, MIG uses a continuously fed wire electrode and a shielding gas to protect the weld pool. This means you can weld thin sheet metal or thick plates with the same machine by adjusting voltage and wire speed. For a small workshop in Nairobi or Lagos, a 200-amp MIG welder running on 220V single phase can handle most jobs. The key advantage is productivity: you don't stop to change electrodes every few inches. You can weld steel, stainless, and aluminum (with the right setup). In Africa, MIG is increasingly used in fabrication of gates, window frames, truck bodies, and agricultural equipment. It also produces less slag than stick welding, so cleanup is faster. However, MIG requires a shielding gas cylinder, typically 75% Argon/25% CO2 for steel. This adds cost, but the speed and quality pay off. Learning MIG opens doors to jobs in manufacturing, repair shops, and even overseas contracts. This module will take you from setting up the machine to running clean beads on different joints. You'll learn to troubleshoot common defects and work safely. By the end, you'll be able to produce strong, neat welds that meet professional standards. Remember, MIG is a skill best learned by doing. So get your gloves on and let's start..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To MIG weld effectively, you need the right tools and materials. First, a MIG welder: for most African workshops, a 200-amp machine like the Lincoln Electric Power MIG 210 MP or a Chinese equivalent (e.g., Andeli MIG-200) works well. Ensure it can run on 220V single-phase power, common in many African countries. You'll need a shielding gas cylinder: for mild steel, use 75% Argon/25% CO2 (often called C25). Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to make a sound MIG weld. First, set up your machine: connect the gas cylinder to the regulator, set flow to 12 CFH. Install the wire spool, feed it through the drive rolls (tension should be snug but not crushing the wire), and into the torch liner. Use the correct contact tip for your wire size (0.8mm tip for 0.8mm wire). Set voltage and wire speed according to the thickness of your metal. For 3mm steel, start with voltage 18-19V and wire speed 3.5-4.0 m/min (check your machine's chart). Second, prepare your metal: grind the edges to bare metal, clamp it securely. For a butt joint, leave a 1-2mm gap. Third, position yourself: hold the torch at a 10-15 degree push angle (tilted away from the direction of travel). This gives better gas coverage and penetration. Fourth, strike the arc: touch the wire to the metal, pull the trigger, and move. Maintain a consistent travel speedâabout 3-5 mm per second for 3mm steel. Keep the contact tip to work distance (CTWD) at 10-12mm. Too close, and you'll clog the tip; too far, and you lose gas coverage. Fifth, watch the weld pool: it should be a smooth, oval shape. If it's too fluid, reduce voltage; if it's too cold and lumpy, increase voltage or wire speed. For a fillet weld, angle the torch at 45 degrees into the joint. Practice on scrap until you can run a straight bead without stopping. Remember to clean the nozzle regularly with pliers to remove spatter. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced welders make mistakes. Here are common MIG problems and how to fix them. First, porosity (holes in the weld): usually caused by insufficient gas flow, windy conditions, or dirty metal. Check your gas cylinder isn't empty, set flow to 12-15 CFH, and weld indoors or use a wind screen. Clean metal with a grinder before welding. Second, spatter: excessive spatter means voltage too low or wire speed too high. Increase voltage slightly or reduce wire speed. Also, ensure you're using the correct polarity: for MIG, the torch is positive (DCEP). If you accidentally set DCEN, you'll get poor penetration and spatter. Third, burn-through: on thin metal, you may blow holes. Reduce voltage and wire speed, or use a thicker wire (0.9mm) and move faster. For 1.5mm sheet, try 16V and 2.5 m/min. Fourth, lack of penetration: the weld sits on top without fusing. Increase voltage and wire speed, or slow down your travel. Also, ensure your joint has a small gap. Fifth, wire stubbing: the wire pushes back into the torch. This happens when wire speed is too high or voltage too low. Adjust the settings. Sixth, birdnesting: wire tangles at the drive rolls. This is caused by too much tension on the spool or a kinked liner. Check the spool tension and replace the liner if worn. Seventh, undercut: a groove melted into the base metal along the weld toe. This weakens the joint. Reduce travel speed or increase wire speed to fill the groove. In Africa, power fluctuations can affect weld quality. Use a voltage stabilizer if your machine is sensitive. Always practice on scrap before welding a critical part. Keep a notebook of settings that work for different thicknesses..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

MIG welding is safe if you follow basic rules. First, protect your eyes and skin: the arc emits intense UV and IR radiation. Always wear an auto-darkening helmet with shade 10-13. Never look at the arc with naked eyes. Wear leather gloves, a flame-resistant jacket (leather or cotton), and closed shoes. Synthetic clothing can melt and stick to skin. Second, prevent fires: welding sparks can travel up to 10 meters. Clear the area of flammable materials like paper, cloth, petrol, and paint thinners. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO2) within reach. In many African workshops, wooden structures are common; use a welding blanket to protect them. Third, ensure proper ventilation: MIG welding produces fumes, especially when welding galvanized or painted metal. Work in a well-ventilated area or use a fume extractor. If you feel dizzy, stop and get fresh air. Fourth, handle gas cylinders safely: secure them upright with a chain or strap. Keep them away from heat and never roll them on their side. When not in use, close the valve and remove the regulator. Fifth, avoid electric shock: check cables for damage, ensure the machine is grounded, and never weld in wet conditions. Wear dry gloves. Sixth, maintain your equipment: inspect the torch liner, contact tip, and nozzle daily. Replace worn parts. Clean the drive rolls. A well-maintained machine gives better welds and is safer. Seventh, practice good posture: weld at a comfortable height to avoid back strain. Use a stool or adjustable table. Finally, never weld alone if possible; have someone nearby in case of emergency. In Africa, where access to medical help may be limited, prevention is critical. Follow these practices and you'll weld for years without injury..Trim()

Practical Exercise

This exercise will build your MIG skills step by step. Module 12 · Running a Welding Business

Introduction and Why It Matters

Welding is a skill that can put food on your table every day if you treat it like a business, not just a job. In many African cities and towns, there is a constant demand for welding services: gates, window frames, burglar bars, wheelbarrows, ox-carts, and repairs for cars, trucks, and farm equipment. But most welders stay poor because they only sell their time by the hour and never build a business. This module will show you how to turn your welding skills into a profitable enterprise that can grow and employ others..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does running a welding business matter? Because Africa is building fast. New houses, schools, shops, and roads are going up everywhere. Every building needs metalwork. Every truck and taxi needs repairs. If you can organise yourself, buy the right tools, price your work correctly, and keep customers happy, you can earn far more than a daily wage. You can also create jobs for other young people in your community..Trim()

.Trim()

Running a welding business is not just about striking an arc. You need to find customers, quote jobs, buy materials at good prices, manage cash flow, and keep records. You need to understand your costs: electrodes, gas, steel, grinding discs, electricity or diesel, transport, and your own time. Many welders fail because they charge too little, don't separate business money from personal money, or take on jobs without a deposit..Trim()

.Trim()

This module covers the practical steps: the tools and materials you need to start, a step-by-step method to set up and run your business, common mistakes and how to fix them, safety and best practices, and a practical exercise to get you started. By the end, you will have a clear plan to turn your welding skills into a steady income. Remember: a welder who understands business will always have work, while a welder who only knows welding may struggle. Let's get started..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To run a welding business, you need more than just a welding machine. You need a full set of tools and a reliable supply of materials. Start with the basics: a welding machine. For most small workshops in Africa, a 200-amp inverter welder that runs on 220V single-phase is ideal. It can weld up to 4mm electrodes and is portable. If you don't have electricity, consider a diesel generator of at least 5kVA, but remember fuel costs. You also need an angle grinder (115mm or 230mm) with cutting and grinding discs, a chipping hammer, a wire brush, a set of files, a measuring tape, a square, a spirit level, a centre punch, and a good workbench. For safety, you need a welding helmet (auto-darkening is best), leather gloves, apron, safety boots, and goggles for grinding..Trim()

.Trim()

Materials are your biggest ongoing cost. You will need mild steel rods (electrodes) in sizes 2.5mm and 3.2mm, and possibly 4mm for heavy work. Buy in bulk from a steel merchant to get a discount. Common steel sections include square tubing (20x20mm, 25x25mm, 40x40mm), rectangular tubing, round tubing, angle iron, flat bar, and sheet metal. Keep a stock of common sizes so you can start jobs immediately. Also stock grinding discs, cutting discs, paint (red oxide primer and enamel), and anti-rust treatment..Trim()

.Trim()

You will need a way to transport materials and finished products. A small pickup truck or a three-wheeled cargo bike is useful. If you can't afford one, build a simple handcart. For mobile jobs, you need a portable welding machine and a long extension cable. A basic tool kit for on-site repairs should include a generator, grinder, and hand tools..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, set up a small workshop. It can be a rented space, a corner of your compound, or a roadside stall. Ensure it has a strong table, a vice, a place to store steel, and good ventilation. You don't need everything at once. Start with the essentials and reinvest profits to buy more tools. Many successful welders started with just a machine, a grinder, and a hammer. The key is to keep your tools in good condition and always have materials ready so you never turn away a customer..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Step 1: Register your business. Even a small welding business should be registered. In most African countries, you can register a business name at the local government office or online. This allows you to open a bank account, get a tax number, and bid for bigger contracts. It also makes you look professional. Keep the registration certificate and receipts safe..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 2: Find a good location. You need a place that is visible and accessible. Near a main road, a market, or a growing residential area is ideal. If you can't afford rent, start at home but put up a signboard. Make sure you have enough space for a workbench, steel storage, and parking for customers..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 3: Set your prices. Calculate your costs: materials, labour, electricity, transport, and a profit margin. For example, if a gate uses 50kg of steel at 20 birr/kg (or equivalent), that's 1000 birr. Add electrodes, grinding discs, paint, and 2 days of your labour at 500 birr/day = 1000 birr. Total cost = 2200 birr. Add 30% profit = 2860 birr. Quote 2900 birr. Always give a written quote and take a 50% deposit before buying materials..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 4: Market your business. Tell everyone you know. Put up a signboard with your phone number. Use social media like WhatsApp, Facebook, and TikTok to post photos of your work. Offer a small discount for referrals. Visit hardware stores, car repair garages, and construction sites to introduce yourself. Word of mouth is powerful: do good work and ask happy customers to recommend you..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 5: Manage your money. Open a separate bank account or mobile money account for the business. Pay yourself a fixed salary each week. Keep receipts for all purchases. Track every job: what you charged, what you spent, and your profit. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Wave or Keeper. Never mix business money with personal money..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 6: Deliver quality and on time. Always finish jobs when you promised. If you can't, communicate early. Check your welds: they should be strong, clean, and free of slag. Grind smooth and paint to prevent rust. A satisfied customer will return and tell others. A bad job will ruin your reputation..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 7: Grow slowly. Reinvest profits into better tools, more materials, and training. Hire an apprentice when you have more work than you can handle. Teach them properly and pay them fairly. Consider adding services like gate automation, aluminium welding, or mobile repairs. Keep learning new techniques. With discipline, your one-man workshop can become a thriving business..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Charging too little. Many welders quote low prices to win jobs, then struggle to buy materials or pay themselves. Fix: Calculate your costs properly and add a fair profit. Never work for free. If a customer says your price is too high, explain the quality and warranty you offer. It's better to lose a job than to lose money..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 2: No deposit. If you buy materials with your own money and the customer refuses to pay, you lose. Fix: Always ask for at least 50% deposit before starting. For large jobs, ask for 50% upfront and 50% on delivery. Put the agreement in writing, even if it's just a handwritten note signed by both parties..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 3: Mixing business and personal money. You buy steel with the money meant for school fees, then you can't pay fees. Fix: Open a separate business account. Pay yourself a fixed weekly or monthly salary. Keep all business transactions separate..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 4: Poor record keeping. You don't know if you made a profit or loss. Fix: Write down every job: date, customer, description, amount charged, materials used, and expenses. Use a simple notebook or a free app. At the end of each month, add up your income and expenses to see your profit..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 5: Ignoring safety. You weld without a helmet and get eye damage, or you don't wear gloves and get burned. Fix: Always wear proper PPE: welding helmet, gloves, apron, safety boots, and goggles for grinding. Keep a fire extinguisher or bucket of sand nearby. Ventilate your workspace..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 6: Taking on jobs you can't do. You promise a complex aluminium weld but you only know mild steel. Fix: Be honest. If you can't do it, refer the customer to someone who can, or ask for time to learn. You can also partner with a more experienced welder..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 7: Not maintaining tools. A grinder with a worn cable shocks you, or a welding machine overheats. Fix: Inspect tools daily. Replace damaged cables, clean the machine, and service the generator regularly. A small maintenance cost prevents big repair bills..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 8: Poor customer service. You arrive late, don't answer calls, or deliver shoddy work. Fix: Answer your phone, be polite, and deliver on time. If you can't, communicate. A happy customer is your best advertisement..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 9: Giving up too soon. Business is slow at first. Fix: Persist. Keep marketing, improve your skills, and save. Most successful welders took at least a year to build a steady customer base. Don't quit..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional. As a welder, you face hazards every day: electric shock, burns, eye damage, fumes, and fire. In Africa, many welders work in informal settings without proper protection. This must change. Always wear personal protective equipment (PPE). A welding helmet with a proper shade lens (shade 10-13 for arc welding) protects your eyes from arc flash. Leather gloves, a leather apron, and safety boots protect your skin from sparks and hot metal. When grinding, wear goggles and a face shield. Never weld in shorts or open shoes..Trim()

.Trim()

Ventilation is critical. Welding fumes contain toxic metals and gases. Work in an open area or use a fan to blow fumes away. If you weld inside, install an exhaust hood or weld near an open door. Never weld in a closed room with no airflow. If you feel dizzy or nauseous, stop and get fresh air immediately..Trim()

.Trim()

Prevent fires. Welding sparks can travel up to 10 metres and ignite flammable materials. Clear your workspace of paper, cloth, petrol, and paint thinners. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO2) or a bucket of sand and water nearby. Never weld near fuel tanks or gas cylinders. After welding, stay in the area for at least 30 minutes to watch for smoldering fires..Trim()

.Trim()

Electrical safety. Check your welding machine and cables for damage before use. Never touch the electrode or work clamp with bare hands when the machine is on. Ensure proper grounding. Use a circuit breaker. If you use a generator, place it outdoors and refuel only when it's off and cool..Trim()

.Trim()

Best practices for quality work: Prepare metal before welding. Clean off rust, paint, and oil with a grinder or wire brush. Fit parts properly with clamps and tack welds. Use the correct electrode and amperage for the metal thickness. For 3mm steel, use 2.5mm electrode at 70-90 amps; for 6mm steel, use 3.2mm at 100-130 amps. Weld in a steady, consistent motion. Remove slag after each pass. Inspect your welds: they should be smooth, with no undercut, porosity, or cracks. If you find a defect, grind it out and re-weld..Trim()

.Trim()

Keep your workspace organised. Store steel and tools safely. Put away cables to avoid tripping. Clean up at the end of each day. A clean workshop is a safe workshop. Finally, get first aid training. Know how to treat burns, cuts, and eye injuries. Keep a first aid kit with burn cream, bandages, and eye wash. Your safety and the safety of others depend on your habits..Trim()

Now it's time to put everything into practice. This exercise will guide you through setting up a simple welding business plan and completing a real job from start to finish. Follow these steps:.Trim()

.Trim()

For example, a standard 3m gate might be 8000 birr, a window frame 1500 birr, and a small repair 500 birr. Use the pricing method from Lesson 3..Trim()

.Trim()

2. Calculate your startup costs. List tools you already have and tools you need to buy. For example: inverter welder (15,000 birr), angle grinder (3,000 birr), helmet (1,500 birr), gloves (500 birr), measuring tape (200 birr), square (300 birr). Total needed: 20,500 birr. If you don't have all, start with the essentials and borrow or rent the rest..Trim()

.Trim()

3. Find your first customer. Talk to neighbours, friends, and local businesses. Offer a small job at a fair price. For example, repair a broken gate or make a simple burglar bar for a window. Agree on a price, take a 50% deposit, and buy materials..Trim()

.Trim()

4. Do the job safely and well. Wear your PPE. Measure twice, cut once. Weld strong joints. Grind smooth. Paint with red oxide primer and enamel. Clean up. Take before and after photos..Trim()

.Trim()

5. Get paid and record the job. Collect the remaining 50%. Write down in your notebook: date, customer, job description, amount charged, materials cost, and profit. For example: gate repair, charged 800 birr, materials 200 birr, profit 600 birr..Trim()

.Trim()

6. Ask for a testimonial. If the customer is happy, ask them to write a short recommendation or to tell friends. Take a photo of the finished work and post it on WhatsApp or Facebook with your phone number..Trim()

.Trim()

7. Review and improve. After the job, ask yourself: What went well? What could be better? Did I make a profit? Adjust your prices or methods for next time..Trim()

.Trim()

8. Repeat. Do at least three jobs this month. Each job will teach you something new. Keep records of all jobs. At the end of the month, calculate your total profit. Reinvest part of it into a new tool or more materials..Trim()

This exercise is your launchpad. Many successful welding businesses started with one small job and grew through word of mouth. Be reliable, do quality work, and manage your money. Within six months, you can have a steady stream of customers and a growing business. Good luck!.Trim()
