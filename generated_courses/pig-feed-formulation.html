Module 1 · Introduction to Pig Nutrition and Feed Formulation

Overview of Pig Production Systems and Feeding Stages

Pig production in Africa ranges from backyard scavenging to intensive commercial units. In backyard systems, pigs eat household waste, roots, and whatever they find, often resulting in slow growth (6-12 months to reach 80 kg). In semi-intensive systems, farmers provide some feed (e.g., 1-2 kg of maize bran per day) and let pigs forage. Intensive systems confine pigs and rely entirely on formulated feed, aiming for 100 kg in 5-6 months. Feeding stages depend on age and weight: creep feed for piglets (0-4 weeks, 20% protein), starter (4-10 weeks, 18% protein), grower (10-20 weeks, 16% protein), and finisher (20-28 weeks, 14% protein). Sows and boars have special diets: gestation (12-14% protein), lactation (16-18% protein). In Africa, many farmers mix their own feed using locally available ingredients like maize, cassava, soybean meal, and fish meal. However, without proper formulation, pigs may suffer from nutrient deficiencies, leading to poor growth and disease. For example, a 50 kg grower pig needs about 1.8 kg of feed per day (dry matter basis) containing 16% crude protein, 0.6% calcium, and 0.5% phosphorus. Water is critical: a pig drinks 2-3 times its feed intake. In hot climates, water intake can double. Understanding these stages helps you adjust feed to match the pig's changing needs, saving money and maximizing growth. Always weigh pigs monthly to track progress and adjust feed amounts. Use a simple scale or weigh tape to estimate weight..Trim()

Basic Nutrient Requirements of Pigs: Water, Energy, Protein, Minerals, Vitamins

Pigs need six nutrient groups: water, energy, protein, minerals, vitamins, and sometimes additives. Water is the most critical: a 50 kg pig drinks 5-10 litres daily; a lactating sow can drink 20-30 litres. Lack of water reduces feed intake and growth. Energy comes mainly from carbohydrates (maize, cassava, sorghum) and fats/oils. Pigs need energy for maintenance and growth. A grower pig requires about 13-14 MJ digestible energy per kg feed. In Africa, maize is the top energy source, but cassava can replace up to 50% if balanced with protein. Protein is needed for muscle and tissue. Crude protein (CP) requirements: piglets 20-22%, growers 16-18%, finishers 14-16%, gestating sows 12-14%, lactating sows 16-18%. Protein is made of amino acids; lysine is the most limiting. A grower diet should have 0.8-0.9% lysine. Sources include soybean meal (44-48% CP), fish meal (60-65% CP), and groundnut cake (40-45% CP). Minerals: calcium and phosphorus are key for bones. A grower diet needs 0.6-0.8% calcium and 0.5-0.6% phosphorus, with a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of 1.2:1 to 1.5:1. Salt (0.2-0.3%) provides sodium and chloride. Vitamins: pigs need vitamins A, D, E, K, and B-complex. Under African conditions, green forage like sweet potato vines or amaranth can supply some vitamins, but commercial premixes are safer. For example, add 0.25% vitamin-mineral premix to the diet. Always provide clean water and a balanced diet to avoid deficiencies like rickets (from lack of calcium/phosphorus) or poor growth (from lack of lysine)..Trim()

Digestive Anatomy and Physiology of Pigs

Pigs are monogastric (single-stomach) animals, meaning they cannot digest large amounts of fibre like ruminants. Their digestive system includes the mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, and large intestine. Digestion starts in the mouth: pigs chew feed and mix it with saliva containing amylase, which begins starch breakdown. The stomach is a simple, single-chamber organ that secretes hydrochloric acid and pepsin to digest proteins. The stomach has a capacity of about 2-3 litres in a 50 kg pig. From the stomach, feed enters the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum), where most digestion and absorption occur. The pancreas and liver release enzymes (trypsin, chymotrypsin, lipase) and bile to break down proteins, fats, and carbohydrates. The small intestine is about 15-20 metres long in adult pigs. Nutrients are absorbed through villi. The large intestine (caecum and colon) ferments some fibre and absorbs water and minerals. Pigs have a functional caecum but it is less developed than in horses. Fibre digestion is limited; therefore, high-fibre feeds like grass or straw should be restricted. For example, a diet with more than 10% crude fibre reduces feed intake and growth. In Africa, farmers often use fibrous by-products like maize cobs or rice bran; these should be limited to 5-10% of the diet. Understanding digestion helps you choose ingredients that pigs can actually use. For instance, finely ground maize is better digested than whole kernels. Also, pigs need constant access to water because digestion requires moisture. A pig's digestive tract is adapted to a omnivorous diet, so both plant and animal proteins can be used, but plant sources like soybean meal must be processed (heated) to destroy anti-nutritional factors like trypsin inhibitors..Trim()

Feed Ingredients Classification and Terminology

Feed ingredients are classified into eight groups by the Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO): dry forages and roughages, pasture, silage, energy feeds, protein supplements, mineral supplements, vitamin supplements, and additives. In Africa, common energy feeds include maize (8-9% CP, 3.3-3.5 Mcal/kg DE), cassava (2-3% CP, high starch), sorghum, and wheat bran. Protein supplements: soybean meal (44-48% CP), groundnut cake (40-45% CP), fish meal (60-65% CP), and cottonseed cake (30-35% CP, but limited by gossypol). Mineral supplements: limestone (calcium), dicalcium phosphate (calcium and phosphorus), and salt. Vitamin supplements: commercial premixes. Additives: antibiotics (for disease control, but use with caution), enzymes (phytase to improve phosphorus digestibility), and antioxidants. Terminology: 'as-fed' means feed as it is offered, including moisture; 'dry matter' (DM) is feed minus moisture. For example, maize at 12% moisture has 88% DM. 'Crude protein' (CP) is nitrogen content multiplied by 6.25. 'Digestible energy' (DE) is the energy available to the pig after digestion. 'Metabolizable energy' (ME) is DE minus energy lost in urine and gases. In feed formulation, you balance nutrients on a DM basis. For instance, a grower diet might be 88% DM, 16% CP, 0.7% calcium, 0.6% phosphorus. Locally available ingredients like palm kernel cake (15-18% CP) can be used but are high in fibre. Always test ingredients for quality: mouldy maize can contain aflatoxins, which are poisonous to pigs. Store feed in dry, cool places. Use a hammer mill to grind ingredients to the right particle size (500-800 microns for maize) to improve digestion. Mixing by hand or with a small mixer should be thorough to avoid segregation..Trim()

Role of a Feed Formulator in Pig Production

A feed formulator is the person who designs diets that meet the nutrient needs of pigs at the lowest cost. In Africa, this role is often taken by the farmer or a hired nutritionist. The formulator must know the nutrient requirements of different pig classes (e.g., grower, finisher, sow) and the nutrient composition of available ingredients. They use tools like the Pearson Square method for simple two-ingredient mixes, or computer software (e.g., WinFeed, Feedsoft) for complex formulations. The formulator's main tasks: (1) Determine the nutrient requirements based on pig weight, age, and production stage. (2) Select locally available and cost-effective ingredients. (3) Calculate the proportions of each ingredient to meet requirements. (4) Check that the final mix meets minimum and maximum limits for nutrients and anti-nutritional factors. (5) Ensure the feed is palatable and safe. For example, a formulator might use the Pearson Square to mix maize (8.5% CP) and soybean meal (44% CP) to make a 16% CP grower diet. The formula: parts of maize = (44-16) = 28; parts of soybean meal = (16-8.5) = 7.5; total parts = 35.5. So maize = 28/35.5 = 78.9%, soybean meal = 7.5/35.5 = 21.1%. Then add minerals and vitamins. The formulator also monitors feed costs: in many African countries, feed accounts for 60-70% of pig production costs. By using alternative ingredients like cassava or palm kernel cake, they can reduce costs. However, they must balance amino acids, especially lysine, methionine, and tryptophan. A good formulator keeps records of pig performance and adjusts formulas as needed. They also train farm workers on proper mixing and feeding. Without a skilled formulator, pigs may get too much or too little of certain nutrients, leading to poor growth, disease, or wasted money..Trim()

Current Trends and Challenges in Pig Feed Formulation

In Africa, pig feed formulation faces both opportunities and challenges. Trends: (1) Use of alternative feed ingredients: cassava, sweet potato, palm kernel cake, and brewery waste are increasingly used to reduce reliance on expensive maize and soybean meal. For example, cassava can replace up to 50% of maize in grower diets if supplemented with methionine and lysine. (2) Precision feeding: using phase feeding (adjusting diets as pigs grow) to match nutrients exactly, reducing waste. (3) Use of enzymes: phytase to release phosphorus from plant sources, reducing need for expensive dicalcium phosphate. (4) Antibiotic-free feeds: due to rising antimicrobial resistance, farmers use probiotics, prebiotics, and organic acids. (5) Digital tools: mobile apps like Feed Formulation for Pigs help farmers calculate rations. Challenges: (1) High cost and inconsistent supply of quality ingredients. Maize prices fluctuate, and aflatoxin contamination is common. (2) Lack of accurate nutrient composition data for local ingredients. For instance, the protein content of groundnut cake can vary from 30% to 50%. (3) Limited access to laboratory analysis. (4) Poor storage leading to mould and insect damage. (5) Inadequate training: many farmers mix feed by guesswork, leading to imbalances. (6) Climate change affecting crop yields. To overcome these, formulators should: test ingredients regularly, use least-cost formulation software, join cooperatives to buy ingredients in bulk, and attend training. For example, in Kenya, farmers use a simple spreadsheet to formulate rations based on local prices. In Nigeria, the use of cassava peel meal in pig diets has been researched. By staying updated, you can produce affordable, nutritious feed that boosts pig production and profits. Remember, a well-formulated feed can improve feed conversion ratio from 4:1 to 3:1, saving 25% on feed costs..Trim()

Energy Utilization and Efficiency in Pigs

Pigs don't eat calories; they eat feed, and how efficiently they convert that feed into meat depends on energy utilization. The key measure is feed conversion ratio (FCR): kg of feed per kg of gain. A good FCR for growing pigs is 2.5-3.0; if your FCR is above 3.5, energy is being wasted. Energy is used first for maintenance (body functions, temperature control), then for growth. In hot climates, pigs eat less to avoid heat, so they may not get enough energy. To improve efficiency, match energy density to the pig's needs: young pigs need 3,400 kcal DE/kg, growers 3,200 kcal DE/kg, and sows 3,000 kcal DE/kg. If energy is too low, pigs eat more but may not grow fast; if too high, they eat less and may not get enough protein. The ideal energy-to-protein ratio is about 18-20 kcal DE per gram of crude protein for growers. Grinding feed to 700-800 microns increases digestibility by 5-10%. Pelleting further improves FCR by 5-8% but is costly for smallholders. Adding fat increases energy density and reduces feed intake, so adjust protein upward. Water is critical: pigs need 2-3 litres of water per kg of feed, and restricted water reduces energy utilization. Health status matters: worms, coccidia, and respiratory disease divert energy to immune response. Deworm every 3 months. Also, avoid overfeeding: a 50 kg pig needs about 2 kg of feed per day (6,400 kcal DE) for 700 g gain. Weigh feed and pigs regularly to calculate FCR. Phytase and Other Enzymes to Improve Mineral Utilization

Phytate is a form of phosphorus stored in plant seeds like maize, soybean, and wheat. It binds phosphorus and other minerals (calcium, zinc, iron) making them unavailable to pigs. Up to 60-80% of phosphorus in these feedstuffs is phytate-bound. Pigs lack the enzyme phytase in their digestive tract, so they cannot break this bond. As a result, undigested phytate phosphorus is excreted in manure, causing environmental pollution and forcing farmers to add expensive inorganic phosphorus. Phytase is an enzyme that breaks down phytate, releasing phosphorus and other minerals for absorption. Adding phytase to pig feed can improve phosphorus digestibility by 30-50%, allowing you to reduce inorganic phosphorus by 0.1-0.15% and calcium by 0.1%. It is sensitive to heat, so if you pellet feed, use a heat-stable phytase. In Africa, phytase may be imported and costly, but the savings on inorganic phosphorus often justify the expense. Mineral Interactions and Antagonisms

Minerals do not work in isolation. They interact, and an excess of one can block the absorption of another. This is called antagonism. The most common antagonism in pig nutrition is between calcium and phosphorus. Too much calcium reduces phosphorus absorption by forming insoluble calcium phosphate in the gut. That's why the Ca:P ratio must be kept between 1.2:1 and 1.5:1. If you feed a diet with 1.5% calcium and only 0.4% phosphorus, pigs will show phosphorus deficiency even though there is enough phosphorus in the feed. Another key interaction is between zinc and copper. High zinc (over 1000 ppm) interferes with copper absorption, causing copper deficiency. Conversely, high copper (over 250 ppm) can reduce zinc absorption. So if you use high zinc oxide for diarrhea control, you must also increase copper..Trim()

Phytate, as discussed, binds many minerals. High levels of calcium also increase phytate's binding effect. Iron and zinc compete for absorption; excess iron reduces zinc uptake. Manganese and magnesium can also interfere with calcium and phosphorus. Selenium and vitamin E work together; a deficiency of one increases the need for the other. Iodine and selenium interact in thyroid function. In practical terms, you should avoid formulating diets with extreme levels of any single mineral. Use a balanced premix. If you use high levels of one mineral for a specific purpose (like zinc for diarrhea), do it for a short time and adjust others accordingly..Trim()

Pearson Square and Algebraic Methods for Simple Formulations

When you only need to mix two ingredients to hit a target nutrient level, the Pearson Square is your fastest tool. Suppose you want a 16% crude protein (CP) grower diet using maize (8.5% CP) and soybean meal (44% CP). Draw a square: put 16 in the centre. Top left: 8.5 (maize). Bottom left: 44 (soybean). Subtract diagonally: 44 - 16 = 28 parts maize; 16 - 8.5 = 7.5 parts soybean. Total parts = 35.5. So maize is 28/35.5 = 78.9%, soybean meal is 7.5/35.5 = 21.1%. That gives a 16% CP mix. In practice, you also need minerals and vitamins, so you'd scale back the maize and soybean to leave 3-5% for a premix. For example, if you want 3% premix, multiply the maize and soybean proportions by 0.97: maize 76.5%, soybean 20.5%, premix 3%. This method works for any two-ingredient blend, like mixing two energy sources to hit a desired fibre level, or two protein sources to hit a lysine target. The algebraic method is similar but uses equations. Let x = kg of maize, y = kg of soybean meal. You want 100 kg of feed with 16% CP: 0.085x + 0.44y = 16 and x + y = 100. Solve: x = 100 - y. Substitute: 0.085(100 - y) + 0.44y = 16 â 8.5 - 0.085y + 0.44y = 16 â 0.355y = 7.5 â y = 21.1 kg, x = 78.9 kg. Same answer. In Africa, where ingredient options are often limited, these hand methods let you formulate on a phone calculator or paper. But remember: they only handle two ingredients and one nutrient. For three or more ingredients or multiple nutrients (energy, lysine, calcium, phosphorus), you need linear programming. Still, mastering Pearson Square builds your intuition for how ingredient proportions affect nutrient levels. Practice with local ingredients: cassava peel meal (2% CP), palm kernel cake (16% CP), fish meal (60% CP). Try mixing maize and fish meal to get 18% CP for creep feed. Linear Programming for Least-Cost Formulation

Linear programming (LP) is the engine behind every least-cost feed formulation. It finds the cheapest combination of ingredients that meets all your nutrient requirements. Imagine you have 10 ingredients and 15 nutrient constraints (energy, protein, lysine, methionine, calcium, phosphorus, etc.). LP solves a system of equations to minimise cost. The maths is done by computer, but you need to understand the inputs. First, you list available ingredients with their nutrient composition and cost per kg. For example, maize: 3,350 kcal ME/kg, 8.5% CP, 0.30% lysine, cost $0.25/kg. Soybean meal: 2,230 kcal ME/kg, 44% CP, 2.8% lysine, cost $0.55/kg. Fish meal: 2,800 kcal ME/kg, 60% CP, 4.5% lysine, cost $1.20/kg. You also set minimum and maximum inclusion levels: maize max 70%, fish meal max 10%, etc. Then you specify the diet's nutrient requirements: 14.5 MJ ME/kg (or 3,400 kcal), 16% CP, 0.85% lysine, 0.65% calcium, 0.55% available phosphorus. The LP algorithm tests thousands of combinations and returns the cheapest mix. In Africa, where ingredient prices fluctuate and some ingredients are scarce, LP helps you adapt quickly. For instance, if soybean meal price jumps 30%, LP will substitute with groundnut cake or sunflower cake, provided you've set correct nutrient values. Free tools like Excel Solver can do small LP problems. For example, in Excel, you set up a spreadsheet with ingredient quantities in cells, calculate total cost and nutrient totals using SUMPRODUCT, then use Solver to minimise cost subject to constraints. Many African feed mills use commercial software like Brill, Format, or Bestmix, but Excel is a great start. The key is accurate data: wrong nutrient values or prices lead to wrong diets. Also, LP doesn't know about palatability, mycotoxins, or anti-nutritional factors unless you set maximum limits. So always review the output. A least-cost diet might use 40% cassava peel meal if you don't restrict it, but that could reduce feed intake. Use LP as a tool, not a black box. Start with simple problems: two ingredients, one nutrient, then add complexity. You'll soon see how LP saves money and improves precision..Trim()

Setting Nutrient Constraints and Ingredient Restrictions

Nutrient constraints are the minimum and maximum levels of nutrients you allow in the final diet. Ingredient restrictions are limits on how much of each ingredient you can use. Getting these right is critical for pig performance and cost. Start with nutrient requirements based on pig class: creep feed (7-10 kg), starter (10-20 kg), grower (20-50 kg), finisher (50-100 kg), gestating sow, lactating sow. For each, you need energy (ME or DE), crude protein, lysine, methionine, threonine, tryptophan, calcium, available phosphorus, and sometimes sodium, chloride, and trace minerals. For example, a grower diet might need 14.5 MJ ME/kg, 16% CP, 0.85% lysine, 0.65% calcium, 0.55% available phosphorus. Set these as minimums. You may also set maximums for some nutrients, like crude fibre max 5% for young pigs, or calcium max 1.0% to avoid interference with zinc. Ingredient restrictions: set minimum and maximum inclusion levels. Minimums ensure you use enough of a critical ingredient, like 0.3% salt for sodium, or 0.2% lysine HCl if needed. Maximums prevent problems: maize max 70% (high starch, low fibre), soybean meal max 30% (cost, trypsin inhibitors if under-processed), fish meal max 5% (cost, fishy taint), wheat bran max 20% (bulk, fibre), limestone max 1.5% (palatability). Conversely, if a constraint is not binding, it's not affecting cost. Use shadow prices to identify which constraints to adjust. Sensitivity Analysis and Shadow Prices

Sensitivity analysis shows how changes in prices or nutrient requirements affect the cost of your feed. Shadow prices (also called dual values) tell you how much the total cost would change if you relaxed a constraint by one unit. For example, if the shadow price for lysine is $0.02 per 0.01% increase, it means adding 0.01% more lysine would increase the diet cost by 2 cents per kg. If the shadow price for maize maximum is -$0.01 per 1% increase, it means allowing 1% more maize would reduce cost by 1 cent per kg. Shadow prices are powerful because they identify which constraints are limiting you. If a constraint has a zero shadow price, it's not binding; you can change it without affecting cost. If it has a high shadow price, it's costing you money. In Africa, where ingredient prices fluctuate, sensitivity analysis helps you decide when to switch ingredients. Suppose soybean meal price rises. You can check the shadow price for soybean meal maximum. If it's high, you might look for alternatives. Or if maize price drops, you can see how much you could save by increasing maize maximum. Most formulation software provides a sensitivity report. In Excel Solver, you can generate a sensitivity report after solving. It shows allowable increases and decreases for objective coefficients (prices) and constraints. Use it to answer questions like: 'If fish meal price increases by 10%, how much will my feed cost increase?' or 'If I reduce crude protein from 16% to 15.5%, how much can I save?' But remember: shadow prices are only valid for small changes. For large changes, you need to re-solve. Also, shadow prices assume linearity; in reality, nutrient responses are not always linear. Still, they are a great guide. Practical tip: Keep a record of shadow prices for your common diets. When prices change, you can quickly estimate the impact without re-running software. For example, if the shadow price for soybean meal is 0.8, and soybean meal price increases by $0.05/kg, the diet cost will increase by about $0.04/kg (0.8 * 0.05). That's 4 cents per kg, or $40 per tonne. That knowledge helps you negotiate prices or adjust your selling price. In small African operations, you might not have software that gives shadow prices, but you can approximate by re-running formulations with small changes. It's worth the effort..Trim()

For protein: soybean meal, groundnut cake, sunflower cake, cottonseed cake, fish meal, and synthetic amino acids. For each, record nutrient composition and current price. Update prices weekly, or even daily if you can. Use a simple spreadsheet to track prices and calculate cost per unit of nutrient, e.g., cost per kg of protein or per MJ of energy. Cost of Production and Feed Cost per Pig

Feed is the biggest cost in pig farming, often 60-75% of total production cost. To know if you are making money, you must calculate the cost of feed per pig from weaning to market weight (usually 90-100 kg). Start by recording all feed ingredients used and their prices. If you sell the pig at KES 35,000, profit = KES 10,000. But if feed cost rises to KES 70 per kg, total feed cost becomes KES 17,500, and profit drops to KES 7,500. So every shilling saved on feed matters. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Farmbrite to track costs. Also calculate feed conversion ratio (FCR): kg feed per kg gain. Keep records of feed purchases, pig weights, and sales. This data helps you make informed decisions and improve profitability..Trim()

Least-Cost Formulation vs. Maximum Profit Formulation

Least-cost formulation means creating a feed mix that meets the pig's nutrient requirements at the lowest possible cost. It uses linear programming software or simple Excel Solver to find the cheapest combination of ingredients. For example, if maize is expensive, you might use more cassava chips or sorghum. But least-cost feed may not always give the best growth or profit. Maximum profit formulation considers the pig's performance and the market price of pork. It might use a slightly more expensive ingredient that improves feed conversion or growth rate, leading to higher revenue. For instance, adding synthetic lysine (costing KES 300 per kg) might allow you to reduce soybean meal (KES 120 per kg) and still meet lysine needs. If that reduces feed cost by KES 2 per kg and improves FCR from 3.0 to 2.8, the extra profit from faster growth and lower feed intake can outweigh the higher ingredient cost. To decide, calculate the value of extra gain. If pork sells at KES 300 per kg live weight, and a pig gains an extra 5 kg, that's KES 1,500 more revenue. If the improved feed costs an extra KES 1,000 per pig, you still gain KES 500. So maximum profit formulation focuses on net return, not just feed cost. Reducing Nutrient Excretion and Environmental Impact
