Binders: Natural and Synthetic Resins

Binders are the glue that holds paint together and sticks it to the wall. Without them, you just have colored dust. In Africa, you have access to both natural and synthetic binders. Natural binders include plant gums (like acacia), casein from milk, and drying oils such as linseed, tung, and castor oil. These are cheap and locally available, but they vary in quality and can spoil. For example, linseed oil from local presses in Kenya or Nigeria works well for oil-based paints, but you must check its iodine value (should be above 170 for good drying). Synthetic binders are made in factories: alkyds, acrylics, epoxies, and polyurethanes. Alkyds are the workhorse for solvent-based paints in Africa because they are tough, glossy, and cheap. Acrylics are water-based and UV-resistant, ideal for exterior walls in sunny regions. Epoxies are two-part and give hard, chemical-resistant floors for workshops. When choosing a binder, consider: drying time (alkyds dry in 4-6 hours; acrylics in 1-2 hours), adhesion to local surfaces (mud, cement, wood), and cost. A common mistake is using too much binderâit makes paint brittle. A good starting formula for a simple wall paint: 30% binder, 20% pigment, 10% solvent, 40% extender. Test your binder by mixing a small batch and brushing on a cement block. If it peels after 24 hours, adjust the ratio or add a primer. Always store natural binders in airtight containers away from heat, and synthetic binders according to the manufacturer's safety data sheet..Trim()

Pigments: Inorganic and Organic Colorants

Pigments give paint its color and hiding power. They are divided into inorganic (from minerals) and organic (from carbon-based chemicals). In Africa, inorganic pigments are abundant: iron oxides (red, yellow, black) from local deposits in South Africa, Zimbabwe, and Nigeria; titanium dioxide (white) mostly imported but essential; and chromium oxides (green). These are cheap, lightfast, and heat-stable. For example, red iron oxide from a mine in Mpumalanga can be ground and used directly, but you must wash it to remove salts that cause efflorescence. Organic pigments are brighter and stronger but more expensive and less UV-stable. They include azo pigments (yellows, reds) and phthalocyanines (blues, greens). A small amount goes a long way: 1 kg of organic blue can color 200 liters of paint, while you might need 10 kg of iron oxide for the same volume. When buying pigments, check for: fineness (grind a sample with a spatulaâif it feels gritty, it needs milling), oil absorption (higher means more binder needed), and bleed resistance (mix with solventâif color runs, it's poor). To test hiding power, draw a black line on a white card, paint over it, and see if the line disappears after one coat. For exterior paints in high-sun areas, use inorganic pigments or blend organic with UV absorbers. Always wear a dust mask when handling dry pigmentsâmany are toxic, especially lead-based ones (avoid lead completely). Store pigments in dry, sealed containers; moisture causes lumps..Trim()

Solvents: Types and Selection Criteria

Solvents thin the paint so you can brush or spray it, then evaporate. In Africa, common solvents include mineral turpentine (white spirit), xylene, toluene, acetone, and water. Turpentine is the standard for alkyd paintsâit's cheap and available in most hardware stores. Xylene and toluene are stronger, used for epoxy and some industrial coatings, but they are toxic and flammable; use them outdoors with a respirator. Acetone is for cleaning tools and thinning nitrocellulose lacquers. Water is the solvent for acrylic and emulsion paintsâfree, safe, and easy. When selecting a solvent, match it to the binder: alkyds need turpentine; acrylics need water; epoxies need xylene or a dedicated thinner. Also consider evaporation rate. Fast solvents like acetone dry too quickly, causing brush marks; slow solvents like turpentine give better flow. A good rule: for brushing, use a solvent with a evaporation rate similar to turpentine (0.15 on the butyl acetate scale). For spraying, use a faster solvent to avoid runs. In hot African climates, add a retarder solvent (like butyl cellosolve) to prevent skinning in the can. Safety first: store solvents in metal cabinets away from sparks. Never use petrol (gasoline) as a paint thinnerâit's extremely dangerous and leaves an oily residue. Test your solvent by mixing a small amount with binder; if it curdles or separates, it's incompatible. For water-based paints, you can use clean tap water, but hard water (high calcium) can cause lumpsâuse distilled or rainwater if possible..Trim()

Additives: Driers, Anti-Skinning Agents, Thickeners

Additives are small amounts of chemicals that fix big problems. Three key types: driers, anti-skinning agents, and thickeners. Driers speed up the drying of oil-based paints. The most common are cobalt, manganese, and zirconium salts (octoates or naphthenates). In Africa, cobalt drier is available from chemical suppliers in Nairobi, Lagos, and Johannesburg. Use 0.05% to 0.2% of total binder weightâtoo much causes wrinkling. A simple test: add drier to a small batch, brush on glass, and check tack-free time. Common ones are methyl ethyl ketoxime (MEKO) and butanone oxime. Add 0.1% to 0.5% of total weight. Without it, your paint forms a rubbery skin in 24 hours, wasting product. Thickeners control viscosity and prevent pigment settling. For water-based paints, use cellulose ethers (like HEC) or associative polyurethanes. For solvent-based, use organoclays or fumed silica. A typical addition is 0.5% to 2%. To test thickening, mix a batch and let it sit for 24 hoursâif water separates on top, add more thickener. Kaolin gives better opacity and suspension, ideal for water-based paints. Silica (quartz) is hard and increases burnish resistance, but it's abrasiveâwear a mask. When choosing an extender, check: particle size (finer gives smoother finish), oil absorption (higher means more binder needed), and brightness (for light colors). Viscosity Measurement: Stormer, Krebs, Brookfield

Viscosity is a measure of a paint's resistance to flow. In simple terms, it tells you how thick or thin your paint is. If it's too thick, it won't spread easily; if it's too thin, it will sag and drip. In Africa, where temperatures can be high, viscosity can change quickly, so testing is crucial. You don't need expensive lab equipment to get started. The Stormer viscometer is a simple mechanical device that measures the time for a weighted paddle to rotate a certain number of times in the paint. It's robust and works well in dusty environments. The Krebs viscometer is similar but uses a rotating paddle and gives a reading in Krebs units (KU), which is common in the paint industry. To measure viscosity with a Stormer viscometer, you fill a container with paint, lower the paddle, and start the motor. The time it takes for 100 revolutions is noted. Always measure at a consistent temperature, ideally 25Â°C, because viscosity changes with heat. Density is how heavy a paint is for a given volume, usually measured in grams per millilitre (g/mL). Specific gravity (SG) is the density of the paint compared to water. Water has an SG of 1.0. A paint with high SG might settle hard in the can, while a low SG paint might be more prone to floating. In Africa, where pigments like iron oxide and titanium dioxide are common, SG can vary widely. Introduction to Emulsion Polymers and Latex

Emulsion paint is made of tiny plastic balls (polymer) suspended in water. These balls are called latex. When the water evaporates, the balls stick together to form a continuous film. In Africa, we often use acrylic, vinyl, or styrene-acrylic latex. Acrylic is best for exterior because it resists sunlight and rain. Vinyl is cheaper but not as durable. You can buy latex from chemical suppliers in Lagos, Nairobi, or Johannesburg. It comes as a milky liquid with 40-60% solid content. For a 20-litre bucket of paint, you need about 8-10 litres of latex. The rest is water, pigments, and additives. To test if latex is good, put a drop on a glass plate and let it dry. It should form a clear, flexible film. If it cracks, it's not good for exterior. Also check the pH; it should be between 7 and 9. If it smells like ammonia, that's normal. Store latex in a cool place, away from direct sun. Never let it freeze. In Africa, many small factories start with a simple mixer: a drum with a paddle driven by a motor. You can also mix by hand with a wooden stirrer, but it takes longer. Start with 50 litres to learn. Measure carefully: use a scale for solids and a measuring jug for liquids. Always wear gloves and goggles. Latex can cause skin irritation. With practice, you can make quality paint that competes with imported brands..Trim()

Formulation of Interior and Exterior Emulsions

Interior and exterior emulsion paints have different recipes. Interior paint needs good coverage, easy cleaning, and low odour. Exterior paint must resist UV light, rain, and temperature changes. Keep a bin card for each material. A bin card is a sheet stuck on the shelf showing date in, date out, and balance. When you receive 50 bags of filler, write it down. Common paint defects include settling, skinning, lumpiness, poor colour, and low coverage. Settling happens when heavy pigments sink to the bottom. Lean Manufacturing and Six Sigma in Paint Plants

Lean manufacturing is about removing waste. In a paint plant, the seven wastes are: overproduction, waiting, transport, overprocessing, inventory, motion, and defects. Start by walking through your plant and noting each waste. For example, if you produce 500 buckets but only sell 300, that is overproduction. If workers wait for raw materials, that is waiting. .Trim()

A clean plant produces fewer defects and works faster..Trim()

When a customer complains, your first response must be to listen and apologise. Do not argue. Write down the complaint: customer name, product, batch number, date of purchase, and exact problem.
