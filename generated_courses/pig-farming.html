Module 1 · Introduction to Pig Farming

History and Global Importance of Pig Farming

Pigs were domesticated around 9,000 years ago in Asia and the Middle East, and later in Africa. In Africa, traditional pig keeping dates back centuries, especially in areas with forest cover. Today, pig farming is one of the fastest-growing livestock sectors worldwide because pigs convert feed to meat efficiently. A pig can produce 100 kg of meat in 6 months, while a cow takes 2-3 years. Global pork production exceeds 100 million tonnes annually, with China producing half. In Africa, pork consumption is rising, especially in urban areas. Countries like Nigeria, South Africa, Kenya, and Uganda have growing pig industries. For example, Nigeria has over 7 million pigs, mostly in the south. Uganda has the largest pig population in East Africa, with over 4 million pigs. Smallholder farmers own over 80% of these pigs. Pig farming helps families earn income, improve nutrition, and create jobs. Pigs eat kitchen waste, crop residues, and by-products, turning them into high-quality protein. They require little land compared to cattle. A single sow can produce 20-30 piglets per year, which can be raised for meat or sale. In Africa, pig farming is a practical business for young people and women because it needs low startup capital. However, challenges like disease (African Swine Fever), feed costs, and market access exist. Understanding the history and global importance helps you see pig farming as a serious business, not just a side activity. You can start with 2-3 pigs and grow. The key is to learn modern methods and apply them locally..Trim()

Overview of Pig Production Systems

There are three main pig production systems in Africa: free-range, semi-intensive, and intensive. Free-range is the oldest: pigs roam freely to find food like roots, fallen fruits, and kitchen waste. It requires almost no capital, but pigs grow slowly and are exposed to diseases and theft. Semi-intensive is a step up: pigs are kept in a simple pen but allowed to scavenge during the day. You provide some feed like maize bran, cassava peels, and water. This system is common in rural Africa. Intensive system keeps pigs confined in pens or houses with controlled feeding, watering, and health care. This is best for commercial production. You can use deep litter or concrete floors. Deep litter uses dry bedding like wood shavings or rice husks, which absorbs waste and provides warmth. Concrete floors are easy to clean but need daily washing. For a small farm, start with a semi-intensive system: build a pen with local materials like bamboo, mud, or timber, and a corrugated iron roof. Provide a wallow or shade for cooling. Pigs need 1-2 square meters per pig in a pen. Feed them a mix of energy feeds (maize, cassava) and protein feeds (soybean meal, fish meal). A simple feed formula: 60% maize, 20% soybean meal, 15% wheat bran, 5% minerals and salt. Always provide clean water. Breeding stock: keep one boar for every 10 sows. Sows can be served naturally or through artificial insemination if available. Farrowing (giving birth) happens after 114 days of pregnancy. Prepare a farrowing pen with a creep area for piglets. Weaning at 4-6 weeks. Then grow pigs to market weight of 80-100 kg in 5-6 months. Record keeping is crucial: note feed used, births, deaths, and sales. This helps you know if you are making a profit. Choose a system that fits your budget, land, and market. Many successful African farmers start semi-intensive and upgrade to intensive as they grow..Trim()

Economic and Social Benefits of Pig Farming

Pig farming can be a profitable business with low initial investment. A weaner pig costs between $20 and $50 in many African countries. With good feeding, you can sell it at 80-100 kg after 5 months for $150-$250. That's a profit of $80-$150 per pig. If you keep a sow, she can produce 20 piglets per year, earning you $1,600-$3,000 annually from piglet sales alone. Pigs grow fast and multiply quickly, giving you a fast return on investment. They also provide manure, which is rich in nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. You can use it to fertilize crops like maize, vegetables, or bananas, reducing your fertilizer costs. A single pig produces about 1 tonne of manure per year. Pig farming creates jobs: you may need workers to feed, clean, and manage the pigs. It also supports related businesses like feed suppliers, veterinarians, and transporters. Socially, pig farming empowers women and youth. In many African cultures, women are responsible for small livestock, and pigs fit well because they can be kept near the home. Youth can start with a small pen and grow. Pigs also improve food security: pork is a rich source of protein, vitamins, and minerals. It helps fight malnutrition, especially in children. However, pig farming has challenges: diseases like African Swine Fever can wipe out your herd. Feed costs can be high if you rely on commercial feed. Market prices fluctuate. To succeed, join a pig farmers' cooperative to access better markets, training, and cheaper inputs. Keep records to track profit. Start small, reinvest profits, and expand gradually. With dedication, pig farming can lift you out of poverty and create a lasting business..Trim()

Common Pig Breeds and Their Characteristics

Choosing the right breed is key to success. In Africa, you will find local breeds, exotic breeds, and crossbreeds. Local breeds like the African indigenous pig (e.g., Mukota in Zimbabwe, Windsnyer in South Africa) are hardy, disease-resistant, and survive on poor feed. They grow slowly and have small litters (6-8 piglets), but they are good for free-range systems. Exotic breeds are from Europe, America, and Asia. The most common are Large White, Landrace, Duroc, Hampshire, and Berkshire. Large White (also called Yorkshire) is white, has a long body, and is known for large litters (10-12 piglets) and good mothering ability. It is popular in commercial farms. Landrace is white with a long body and droopy ears. It is also prolific and produces lean meat. Duroc is red or brown, hardy, and grows fast. It is often used as a terminal sire for crossbreeding. Hampshire is black with a white belt around the shoulder, and it produces lean meat. Berkshire is black with white points, known for tasty meat but smaller litters. Crossbreeds combine the hardiness of local breeds with the productivity of exotic breeds. For example, crossing a Large White boar with a local sow gives piglets that grow faster and survive better. In Africa, many farmers keep crossbreeds because they perform well under local conditions. When selecting breeding stock, look for pigs with good body shape, strong legs, and no defects. Avoid pigs from diseased herds. Buy from reputable breeders or government farms. A good sow should have at least 10 functional teats and a calm temperament. A boar should be active and have good libido. If you are starting, consider a crossbreed sow for your first herd. You can later upgrade by using an exotic boar. Remember, breed alone does not guarantee success; feeding, housing, and health care matter just as much. Always keep records of your pigs' performance to know which breed works best for you..Trim()

Regulatory and Environmental Considerations

Before starting pig farming, you must understand the rules and environmental impacts. In most African countries, you need a permit or license from the local government or veterinary department. This ensures your farm meets health and sanitation standards. For example, in Kenya, you must register with the Directorate of Veterinary Services. In Nigeria, you need a certificate of registration from the Ministry of Agriculture. These permits often require proof of proper waste disposal, disease control, and animal welfare. Environmental concerns are serious: pig waste can pollute water sources if not managed. A pig produces about 4-5 kg of manure per day. If you keep 50 pigs, that's 250 kg daily. You must have a waste management plan. Options include composting manure for crop fertilizer, building a biogas digester to produce cooking gas, or selling manure to gardeners. Never dump waste into rivers or streams. Also, control odors by cleaning pens regularly and using bedding that absorbs moisture. Pigs can also damage the environment if they roam freely, causing soil erosion and destroying crops. So, keep them confined or supervised. Another regulation is disease control: you must report outbreaks of notifiable diseases like African Swine Fever to authorities. Vaccinate against common diseases like swine fever, erysipelas, and foot-and-mouth where vaccines exist. Quarantine new pigs for 2-3 weeks before mixing with your herd. Animal welfare laws are also emerging. Pigs need space, clean water, and protection from extreme weather. Avoid cruelty. If you plan to sell pork, you must meet meat inspection standards. Slaughter only at approved abattoirs. For marketing, you may need a health certificate. Check with your local veterinary office for specific requirements. Following regulations protects your business from fines and closure, and it protects your community and the environment. It also builds trust with customers, who prefer safe, responsibly raised pork..Trim()

Career Opportunities in the Pig Industry

Pig farming is not just about raising pigs; it offers many career paths. You can be a pig farmer, either full-time or part-time. Start with 2-3 pigs and grow. As you gain experience, you can specialize: become a breeder, producing piglets for sale. A good sow can earn you $1,000 per year from piglet sales. You can also be a finisher, buying weaners and raising them to market weight. Other careers include pig nutritionist, who formulates feed to reduce costs and improve growth. You can train at agricultural colleges or through online courses. Veterinary services are in high demand: pig health technicians, veterinarians, and artificial insemination specialists. They help farmers prevent and treat diseases. You can also work in pig housing construction, building pens and farrowing crates. Marketing and sales: you can be a pig buyer, transporter, or butcher. Processed pork products like sausages, bacon, and ham are growing markets in African cities. You could start a small processing unit. Feed production is another opportunity: making pig feed from local ingredients like maize, soybean, and cassava. You can sell to other farmers. Extension services: government and NGOs hire trainers to teach farmers best practices. If you enjoy teaching, become a pig farming trainer. Research and development: work with institutes to improve breeds and management. Agribusiness management: run a pig enterprise, manage finances, and plan expansion. Many young Africans are taking up pig farming as a business, using social media to market. You can also offer consultancy services. To succeed, get practical training. Join a pig farmers' association. Network with others. Start small, keep records, and reinvest. The pig industry in Africa is growing, with rising demand for pork. This means more jobs and business opportunities. Whether you want to be your own boss or work for someone else, pig farming offers a viable career with low barriers to entry..Trim()

Module 2 · Pig Breeds and Selection

Identifying Major Pig Breeds

In Africa, pig farming relies on a mix of local and exotic breeds. The most common exotic breeds are Large White, Landrace, Duroc, Hampshire, and Pietrain. Large White (also called Yorkshire) is white with erect ears, known for large litters (10-12 piglets) and good mothering. Landrace is white with droopy ears and a long body, producing up to 14 piglets per litter but sensitive to stress. Duroc is red or brown with droopy ears, fast-growing and hardy, often used as a terminal sire. Hampshire is black with a white belt, lean and muscular. Pietrain is white with black spots, extremely muscular but stress-prone and poor mothering. Local African breeds like the Ashanti Black in Ghana or the Mukono in Uganda are smaller, slower-growing but resistant to diseases and heat. To identify a breed, look at ear shape (erect vs droopy), coat colour, body length, and litter size records. For example, if you see a white sow with droopy ears and a long body, it's likely Landrace. A red boar with droopy ears is Duroc. Keep a notebook to record each pig's breed, ear tag number, and performance. In markets, ask the seller about the breed and check teeth for age (piglets have small teeth, adults have worn teeth). Crossbreeds are common; they may show mixed colours and ear shapes. Start with at least two breeds to compare. For small farms, Large White x Landrace crosses are popular for good litter size and growth. Always buy from a reputable breeder who keeps records. Avoid buying pigs with unknown backgrounds, as they may carry diseases or poor genetics. In hot climates, breeds with dark coats like Duroc tolerate sun better, but they need shade and water. Local breeds are best for low-input systems; exotic breeds need balanced feed and vet care. Your choice depends on your market: if you sell to hotels, lean meat from Pietrain crosses is preferred; for local markets, hardy crossbreds work well..Trim()

Comparing Maternal and Terminal Breeds

Maternal breeds are chosen for their ability to produce and raise many piglets. They have good mothering instincts, large litters, and enough milk. Examples: Large White and Landrace. A good maternal sow weans 10-12 piglets per litter, with low piglet mortality. Terminal breeds are chosen for fast growth, feed efficiency, and meat quality. They are used as sires in crossbreeding to produce market pigs. Examples: Duroc, Hampshire, Pietrain. A terminal boar passes on rapid growth and lean meat to his offspring. In a commercial operation, you keep maternal line sows (e.g., Large White x Landrace) and mate them with a terminal boar (e.g., Duroc) to get piglets that grow fast and have good carcass quality. This is called a terminal cross. Never keep offspring from terminal crosses for breeding, because they may have poor mothering and inconsistent traits. Maternal breeds often have more backfat and are less muscular, while terminal breeds are leaner and more muscular. For example, a Pietrain boar will give piglets with high lean percentage but may cause stress-related deaths during transport. In Africa, where feed costs are high, using a terminal sire like Duroc improves feed conversion ratio (FCR) from 3.5 to 3.0, meaning less feed per kg gain. However, terminal breeds may need better housing and health care. Maternal breeds are more tolerant of poor management. If you are a smallholder with limited resources, start with a maternal breed like Large White and use a local boar for hardiness. If you supply to a premium market, invest in a terminal boar. Record performance: weigh piglets at birth, weaning (28 days), and 70 days. Compare litters from different sows. A sow that weans 10 piglets at 7 kg each is better than one weaning 8 at 6 kg. Keep sows that consistently perform. Replace sows after 6-8 litters. For boars, check libido and semen quality. Use a terminal boar for no more than 2 years. In hot climates, terminal breeds like Duroc handle heat better than Pietrain. Always match breed choice to your market and resources..Trim()

Selecting Breeding Stock Based on Performance

Selecting breeding stock based on performance means choosing pigs that have proven they can produce well. You need records. Start with a simple notebook or a smartphone app like PigChamp. Record for each sow: ear tag number, breed, date of birth, number of piglets born alive, number weaned, and weaning weight. For boars: growth rate, feed efficiency, and libido. Key performance indicators (KPIs) for sows: litter size (target 10-12 born alive), weaning weight (target 6-8 kg at 28 days), and farrowing interval (target 150-160 days). For boars: average daily gain (target 700-900 g/day from 30 to 90 kg), feed conversion ratio (target 2.8-3.2), and backfat thickness (target 12-15 mm at 90 kg). To measure backfat, use a backfat scanner or ultrasound; if not available, condition score by hand: ribs easily felt but not visible is ideal. Select replacement gilts from sows that consistently wean 10+ piglets. Gilts should reach puberty by 6-7 months and weigh 100-110 kg at first mating. Avoid gilts from litters with high mortality or slow growth. For boars, choose from litters with high growth and low backfat. Check testicles: two large, firm testicles of equal size. Check legs and feet for soundness. In Africa, where data may be scarce, use visual appraisal: a good gilt has a long body, 12-14 teats (spaced evenly), and a strong back. A good boar has a wide chest, strong legs, and active libido. Buy from breeders who provide performance records. If no records, ask to see parents and siblings. Select at 5-6 months of age. Never select pigs that have been sick or treated frequently. Keep replacement rate at 20-25% per year. Cull sows with poor mothering, small litters, or lameness. Cull boars with low libido or poor semen. Remember: performance is influenced by environment and management. A pig with good genetics can still perform poorly if fed poorly. So, provide balanced feed (16-18% protein for growers) and clean water. Weigh pigs monthly to track growth. Use a scale or weigh tape. If a pig is not meeting targets, check feed, health, and housing before blaming genetics..Trim()

Evaluating Conformation and Soundness

Conformation refers to the shape and structure of a pig's body; soundness means the pig can move and stand without pain. Good conformation and soundness are critical for breeding stock because they affect longevity, fertility, and ease of management. Start with legs and feet. A sound pig has straight, strong legs with even toes. Check for swollen joints, cracked hooves, or splayed legs. In Africa, where pigs often walk on hard, uneven ground, leg problems are common. A boar with weak legs cannot mount a sow; a sow with bad legs cannot farrow comfortably. Next, look at the back. A good breeding pig has a long, wide, and slightly arched back. A dipped or roached back indicates weakness. The body should be deep, with well-sprung ribs (ribs that curve outward) to allow room for the heart and lungs. For sows, a good underline means at least 12 evenly spaced, prominent teats. Inverted or blind teats are disqualifying because piglets cannot suckle. Check the vulva: it should be well-developed and not infantile. For boars, check testicles: two large, firm, symmetrical testicles in a well-descended scrotum. The sheath should be small and free of discharge. Evaluate the head: a good pig has a broad snout and bright eyes. Avoid pigs with runny noses, coughing, or skin lesions. When evaluating a pig, have it walk on a flat surface. Watch for limping, stiffness, or crossing over. Score each trait from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent). Only select pigs with scores of 4 or 5 in legs, back, and underlines. In hot climates, pigs with dark coats and loose skin tolerate heat better. Also check teeth: a pig with overshot or undershot jaw may have trouble eating. For breeding, avoid closely related pigs (inbreeding) to prevent poor conformation. If you are buying at a market, ask the seller to walk the pig. A pig that refuses to walk may have foot problems. Always quarantine new pigs for 30 days and observe for soundness. Remember, conformation is partly genetic and partly environmental. Provide good nutrition and soft bedding to prevent leg injuries..Trim()

Understanding Genetics and Heritability

Genetics is the study of how traits are passed from parents to offspring. In pig farming, understanding genetics helps you improve your herd. Every pig has genes, which come in pairs: one from the sire, one from the dam. Some genes are dominant (they show up even if only one copy is present), others are recessive (they show up only if both copies are the same). For example, the gene for white coat colour is dominant over black. Heritability is a measure of how much of a trait's variation is due to genetics versus environment. It ranges from 0 to 1. A high heritability (above 0.4) means the trait is mostly genetic and can be improved by selection. Examples: backfat thickness (0.5), growth rate (0.3), litter size (0.1). Litter size has low heritability, so selecting for it is slow; better to improve management (feed, health). To improve a trait, you need to select pigs with superior genes and cull those with poor genes. But you must also provide good environment. For instance, even a pig with high genetic potential for growth will not grow well if underfed. In Africa, many farmers use local breeds that have low growth but high disease resistance. Crossbreeding with exotic breeds can combine traits. However, crossbreeding does not change heritability; it just creates new gene combinations. To make genetic progress, keep records and use estimated breeding values (EBVs). EBVs predict the genetic merit of a pig for a trait. For example, an EBV for growth of +50 g/day means the pig's offspring will grow 50 g/day faster than average. Without EBVs, use performance records of the pig and its relatives. Select boars from litters with high growth and low backfat. Select gilts from sows with large litters. Avoid inbreeding: mating closely related pigs (e.g., father-daughter) increases the chance of recessive defects like hermaphrodism or cleft palate. Keep a herd book to track pedigrees. Rotate boars every 2 years. If you have a small herd, bring in a new boar from a different farm every 2-3 years. Remember, genetics is a long-term investment. It takes 3-5 generations to see major changes. Start with the best stock you can afford and keep records..Trim()

Crossbreeding Strategies for Commercial Production

Crossbreeding is mating pigs of different breeds to produce offspring with superior traits. This is called hybrid vigour or heterosis. For commercial production, crossbreeding can increase litter size, growth rate, and health. The most common strategy is the two-way cross: mating a Large White sow to a Landrace boar, or vice versa. The resulting F1 gilts are kept as mothers, and they are mated to a terminal boar like Duroc. This is a three-way cross: (Large White x Landrace) sow x Duroc boar. The piglets from this cross are all for market. They grow fast, have lean meat, and good feed efficiency. Another strategy is the rotational cross: use two or three breeds in rotation, e.g., breed 1 boar on breed 2 sows, then breed 2 boar on their daughters, and so on. This maintains hybrid vigour but requires more breeds. For small farms, a simple two-breed cross is easiest. For example, use a local sow (hardy) and an exotic boar (fast growth). The offspring will be hardier than pure exotic and faster-growing than pure local. In Africa, where heat and disease are challenges, crossbreeding with local breeds can improve survival. However, you must manage nutrition and health carefully. Crossbred pigs need balanced feed (16% protein for growers, 14% for finishers) and regular deworming. Record performance of crossbreds: weigh at birth, weaning, and market. Compare with purebreds. If crossbreds are not better, check management. Avoid keeping crossbred gilts for breeding if they come from a terminal cross, as they may not mother well. Always buy terminal boars from reputable breeders. In hot climates, use Duroc or local boars for heat tolerance. For market, crossbred pigs often fetch higher prices due to lean meat. Plan your crossbreeding: decide on maternal line (e.g., Large White x Landrace) and terminal line (e.g., Duroc). Keep 1 boar per 10-15 sows. Replace boars every 2 years. Keep records of each cross. If you have a small herd, consider artificial insemination (AI) to access superior genetics without keeping a boar. AI costs about $10-20 per dose in many African cities. Crossbreeding is not a magic bullet; it must be combined with good feeding, housing, and health..Trim()

Designing Farrowing and Nursery Units

A farrowing unit is where sows give birth and nurse piglets. It must be dry, warm, and safe for both mother and piglets. In Africa, many farmers use simple pens made from locally available materials like timber, bamboo, or concrete blocks. The floor should be smooth but not slippery, with a slope of 1-2% towards a drain. Each farrowing pen should be at least 2.5 meters by 2 meters, with a creep area for piglets that is heated to 30-32Â°C for the first week. Use a heat lamp or a wooden box with a lid and a 100-watt bulb. The sow needs a resting area that is cool, around 18-20Â°C, so provide shade and ventilation. A farrowing crate can reduce piglet crushing, but if not available, use a rail around the pen walls 20-25 cm from the floor to give piglets an escape zone. The nursery unit is for weaned piglets, typically from 4 to 10 weeks old. Keep them in groups of 10-15, with 0.3-0.4 square meters per piglet. The floor should be easy to clean, such as concrete with a slight slope to a urine drain. Provide a warm sleeping area at 26-28Â°C and a cooler dunging area. Use solid dividers between pens to prevent drafts and disease spread. Ensure there is a footbath at the entrance and hand-washing station. Natural lighting is good, but avoid direct sunlight that can overheat. In hot climates, orient the building east-west to reduce sun exposure on the long sides. Use local materials like mud bricks, thatch, or corrugated iron, but ensure they are predator-proof and secure. Always include a small storage area for feed and tools within the unit to reduce movement in and out. Clean and disinfect between batches to break disease cycles..Trim()

Constructing Grow-Finish Barns

Grow-finish barns house pigs from about 10 weeks old until market weight, usually 90-110 kg. These pigs need more space: 0.8-1.0 square meters per pig in a fully slatted system, or 1.2-1.5 square meters in a bedded system. In Africa, many use open-sided barns with deep litter or concrete floors. The floor should be concrete, at least 10 cm thick, with a 2% slope to a drain. For deep litter, use dry sawdust, wood shavings, or chopped straw, and add fresh material weekly. The walls can be low (1.2 meters) with wire mesh above for ventilation. The roof should be high (3-4 meters at the eaves) and insulated with thatch or reflective material to reduce heat. In hot climates, consider a ridge vent and open sides. In cooler highland areas, use curtains that can be rolled down. Provide 15-20 cm of trough space per pig for feeding, and one water nipple for every 10-15 pigs. Group pigs by size to reduce fighting. Stocking density is critical: overcrowding leads to poor growth, tail biting, and disease. A common mistake is to keep too many pigs in a small space. For a 20-pig pen, you need at least 24 square meters. Build a loading ramp at one end for easy transport to market. Use local materials like eucalyptus poles, bamboo, and mud bricks to cut costs, but ensure the structure is strong enough to withstand wind and rain. Always include a concrete apron around the barn to prevent mud and rodents. Clean the barn daily, removing wet bedding and manure. Disinfect weekly with a 2% caustic soda solution or commercial disinfectant. Keep records of feed intake and weight gain to monitor performance..Trim()

Ventilation and Temperature Control Systems

Pigs are sensitive to heat. Unlike humans, they cannot sweat effectively, so they need help to stay cool. The ideal temperature for a grow-finish pig is 18-22Â°C, for a lactating sow 18-20Â°C, and for piglets 30-32Â°C in the first week. In hot African climates, temperatures often exceed 35Â°C, causing heat stress. Signs include panting, reduced feed intake, and poor growth. To control temperature, use natural ventilation: open-sided buildings with high roofs, ridge vents, and large openings on the prevailing wind side. For every 10 pigs, provide at least 0.5 square meters of inlet area and 0.3 square meters of outlet area. If natural ventilation is not enough, use fans. A 50-cm diameter fan can move 10,000 cubic meters of air per hour, enough for 50-60 grow-finish pigs. In very hot areas, use evaporative cooling pads or foggers. A simple fogger system uses nozzles that spray fine water droplets; this can lower temperature by 5-10Â°C. For piglets, provide a heated creep area with a heat lamp or a wooden box with a 100-watt bulb. In cold highland areas, use curtains, deep bedding, and maybe a small stove, but ensure carbon monoxide does not build up. Always monitor temperature with a simple thermometer at pig level. Humidity should be 50-70%; too high causes respiratory problems, too low causes dust. Ventilation also removes ammonia and moisture. Ammonia levels should be below 20 ppm; if your eyes water when you enter, it's too high. Use a simple ammonia detector or your nose. Adjust ventilation by opening or closing curtains, vents, or doors. In deep litter systems, ventilation is critical to keep litter dry. Stir litter weekly and add fresh material. During the rainy season, ensure drainage to avoid flooding. In all cases, provide shade and plenty of cool drinking water..Trim()

Waste Management and Manure Handling

A pig produces about 4-6 kg of manure per day, which is a valuable resource but also a potential pollutant. In Africa, many farmers use pig manure to fertilize crops, but it must be handled properly to avoid disease and flies. The first step is to collect manure daily. In concrete-floor barns, scrape or flush manure into a drain that leads to a collection pit. In deep litter systems, remove wet litter and add fresh bedding. The collection pit should be concrete, covered, and located away from the barn to reduce odors and flies. For liquid manure, use a biogas digester. A simple plastic bag digester or fixed-dome digester can produce biogas for cooking and lighting. For a 10-pig farm, a 6-cubic-meter digester can produce enough gas for 2-3 hours of cooking per day. The effluent from the digester is a rich fertilizer. If you don't have a digester, compost the manure. Mix manure with crop residues like maize stalks or rice straw, and turn the pile every week. After 4-6 weeks, it becomes compost. For solid manure, you can also dry it and sell it as organic fertilizer. Never dump manure into rivers or streams; it causes water pollution and spreads diseases. Also, avoid spreading fresh manure on fields; it can burn plants and spread parasites. Store manure in a covered area to prevent rain from washing nutrients away. Use gloves and boots when handling manure, and wash hands after. Keep the manure storage area away from the pig barn (at least 30 meters) to reduce flies and disease transmission. In intensive systems, consider a lagoon for liquid waste, but ensure it is lined to prevent groundwater contamination. Regularly check for leaks and repair. Good waste management also reduces odors, which improves neighbor relations and pig health..Trim()

Biosecurity Infrastructure and Layout

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out of your farm. The layout of your pig housing is your first line of defense. Start with a perimeter fence at least 1.5 meters high to keep out people and animals. The entrance should have a gate with a lock and a sign saying 'No Entry Without Permission'. Provide a footbath at the entrance of each building: a shallow concrete basin filled with disinfectant (e.g., 2% caustic soda or commercial virucide). Change the disinfectant daily. Also, have a hand-washing station with soap and water. If possible, have a changing room where workers and visitors put on farm-specific boots and coveralls. The layout should follow a flow: from clean to dirty. That means the feed storage and nursery should be at the entrance, and the grow-finish and sick pens at the back. Never move from sick pens to healthy pens without changing clothes and boots. Keep different age groups in separate buildings at least 10 meters apart. This prevents disease spread between batches. Use solid walls between pens to prevent nose-to-nose contact. For new pigs, have a quarantine pen at least 50 meters from the main herd. Quarantine for 30 days and monitor for signs of disease. Keep feed in closed bins to prevent rodents and birds. Control flies with screens and traps. Dispose of dead pigs immediately by burning or deep burial (at least 1 meter deep) away from water sources. Keep records of all visitors and their contact with pigs. In African contexts, many farms are small and mixed with other livestock; keep pigs separate from poultry and cattle if possible. If you have a vehicle, park it outside the fence. Always wash and disinfect boots and equipment after use. A simple biosecurity plan can save you from devastating diseases like African Swine Fever, for which there is no vaccine..Trim()

Equipment for Feeding and Watering

Pigs need constant access to clean water and feed. For watering, the simplest method is a nipple drinker connected to a tank. Each nipple serves 10-15 pigs. Nipples should be placed at shoulder height of the pigs: for weaners at 30 cm, for growers at 45 cm, and for finishers at 60 cm. Check water flow daily; a pig drinks 5-10 liters per day, more in hot weather. Use a water tank of at least 100 liters for 10 pigs, and clean it weekly. For feeding, you can use troughs or self-feeders. Troughs are cheaper but require more labor. A concrete trough 30 cm wide and 15 cm deep can feed 10 pigs; allow 20-30 cm of trough space per pig. Feed twice daily, and remove leftovers to prevent spoilage. Self-feeders save labor and reduce waste. A simple wooden or metal hopper with a trough at the bottom can hold 50 kg of feed. Adjust the opening so feed flows freely but doesn't spill. For piglets, use creep feeders with small openings. Always provide feed on a clean surface; never feed on the floor where manure is. In Africa, you can make feeders from local materials like clay pots, wooden planks, or PVC pipes. For example, cut a PVC pipe lengthwise and attach it to a wooden frame. For water, you can use a plastic drum with nipples. Ensure all equipment is easy to clean and disinfect. Remove feed leftovers daily to prevent mold. Store feed in a dry, cool place, off the floor on pallets, to prevent aflatoxin. Use a simple scale to weigh feed for accurate rationing. Keep records of feed intake per pen to detect health issues early. Clean water and proper feeding equipment are the backbone of good pig performance..Trim()

Module 4 · Nutrition and Feeding

Nutrient Requirements for Different Production Stages

Pigs need different nutrients at different stages of life. A piglet from birth to weaning (0-4 weeks) depends on sow's milk, which provides high energy, protein, and antibodies. If you wean early, use a creep feed with 20-22% crude protein (CP) and 14-16 MJ/kg digestible energy (DE). From 4-8 weeks (weaner), feed 18-20% CP and 13.5-14.5 MJ/kg DE. From 8-12 weeks (grower), drop to 16-18% CP and 13-14 MJ/kg DE. From 12 weeks to market (finisher), use 14-16% CP and 12.5-13.5 MJ/kg DE. For pregnant sows, feed 12-14% CP and 12-13 MJ/kg DE, but limit feed to 2-2.5 kg per day to prevent fatness. Lactating sows need 16-18% CP and 13-14 MJ/kg DE, and should eat 4-6 kg per day to produce milk. Boars need 14-16% CP and 12.5-13.5 MJ/kg DE. In Africa, many smallholders use local ingredients like maize, cassava, and soybean meal. For example, a typical piglet creep feed might be 60% maize, 25% soybean meal, 10% fishmeal, and 5% minerals/vitamins. Always provide clean water and a mineral lick. If you see poor growth, check protein and energy first. Remember, overfeeding pregnant sows wastes money and causes farrowing problems. Underfeeding lactating sows reduces milk and piglet survival. Adjust feed based on body condition: thin sows get more, fat sows get less. Use a weighing scale or a plastic bucket marked in kilograms to measure feed accurately. In Africa, where feed costs are high, matching nutrients to stage is the cheapest way to get good growth..Trim()

Common Feed Ingredients and Formulation

In Africa, pig feed is usually made from energy sources, protein sources, and supplements. Energy sources include maize (corn), cassava, sorghum, and millet. Maize is the best, with about 8-9% CP and high energy. Cassava is cheaper but low in protein (2-3% CP) and must be dried to avoid cyanide poisoning. Protein sources include soybean meal (44-48% CP), groundnut cake (40-45% CP), sunflower cake (28-30% CP), and fishmeal (55-65% CP). Fishmeal is excellent but expensive; use 5-10% in piglet diets. Animal by-products like blood meal (80% CP) and meat-and-bone meal (45-50% CP) can be used but check quality. Minerals and vitamins are needed: limestone (calcium), bone meal (calcium and phosphorus), and salt. A simple formulation for a grower pig (50 kg) might be: 65% maize, 20% soybean meal, 10% groundnut cake, 3% fishmeal, 1% limestone, 0.5% salt, and 0.5% vitamin-mineral premix. This gives about 16% CP. For a finisher, reduce soybean meal to 15% and increase maize to 70%. Always mix ingredients thoroughly on a clean concrete floor or in a mixer. Use a weighing scale, not cups, for accuracy. In Africa, many farmers use a hammer mill to grind maize and other grains. Store ingredients in dry, raised bags to prevent mold. Aflatoxin in maize and groundnut cake is a major risk; never use moldy feed. If you cannot afford soybean meal, use cowpeas or pigeon peas, but they need cooking to destroy anti-nutritional factors. Test your feed by weighing pigs monthly; if they gain less than 500 g per day, adjust the formula. Formulating on-farm saves 30-50% compared to buying commercial feed..Trim()

Feed Processing and Storage Best Practices

Processing feed properly improves digestion and reduces waste. First, grind all grains to a medium texture (like coarse sand). Fine powder causes dust and respiratory problems; too coarse reduces digestion. Use a hammer mill or a mortar and pestle for small scale. After grinding, mix ingredients thoroughly for at least 5 minutes. For piglets, you may need to pellet the feed to prevent sorting. Pelleting requires a pellet machine, but you can also moisten the feed slightly and press it. However, wet feed spoils quickly, so only mix what pigs eat in one day. For storage, keep feed in a cool, dry, dark place. Use raised pallets or wooden planks to keep bags off the floor. Never store feed directly on concrete, as moisture causes mold. Airtight plastic containers or metal silos are best; if using bags, tie them tightly. In humid African climates, add a mold inhibitor like propionic acid if available, or mix in 1% calcium propionate. Check for pests: rats and weevils. Use traps or cats, and avoid chemical poisons near feed. Rotate stock: use older feed first. Never store feed for more than 4 weeks in hot weather; vitamins degrade. If you see caking, discoloration, or a musty smell, discard it. Aflatoxin is invisible but deadly; test with a black light or send a sample to a lab. For silage or fermented feed, use airtight containers and keep pH below 4.5. In Africa, many farmers use plastic drums for fermentation of cassava or other by-products. Always clean storage areas monthly. Proper storage saves money by preventing spoilage and keeps pigs healthy..Trim()

Feeding Systems and Delivery Methods

How you feed pigs affects growth, health, and feed waste. The three main systems are dry feeding, wet feeding, and automatic feeding. Water Quality and Availability

Water is the most important nutrient for pigs. A pig can survive weeks without feed but only days without water. Pigs need 2-3 litres of water per kilogram of dry feed. Managing Feed Costs and Efficiency

Feed is 60-80% of pig production costs. To make a profit, you must manage feed costs without sacrificing nutrition. First, buy ingredients in bulk when prices are low. Fourth, improve feed conversion ratio (FCR). FCR is feed eaten divided by weight gain. A good FCR is 3:1 for finishers (3 kg feed per 1 kg gain). If your FCR is 4:1, you are losing money. Understanding Market Channels and Pricing

When you raise pigs, you need to decide where and how to sell them. The main market channels in Africa include selling live pigs at local markets, to butcheries, to restaurants, or directly to consumers. Each channel has different prices and requirements. For example, a live pig might sell for 20,000 to 30,000 Naira in Nigeria, while a butchered carcass can fetch 35,000 to 45,000 Naira. Restaurants often pay premium prices but demand consistent size and quality. You must understand your local market: visit nearby markets, talk to butchers, and ask about current prices. Prices fluctuate with feed costs, holidays, and disease outbreaks. To get the best price, sell when demand is high, such as before Christmas, Eid, or New Year. Also consider selling in bulk to traders who come to your farm; they may offer lower prices but save you transport costs. Always negotiate. A simple rule: know your cost of production per pig. If it costs you 25,000 Naira to raise a pig to 80 kg, you need to sell above that to make a profit. Keep a notebook of prices from different buyers each week. Over time, you will see patterns and can plan your sales. Don't just accept the first offer. Build relationships with several buyers so you have options. Remember, the market channel you choose affects your profit and your workload. Selling directly to consumers takes more time but gives higher returns. Selling to traders is quicker but less profitable. Choose based on your capacity and goals..Trim()

Direct Marketing and Value-Added Products

Direct marketing means selling your pigs or pork products directly to consumers, bypassing middlemen. This can increase your profit significantly. In many African communities, you can sell pork by the kilo to neighbours, local bars, or at roadside stalls. For example, a live pig sold for 30,000 Naira might yield 60 kg of pork. To start, invest in a simple smoker made from a metal drum. Season the meat with salt, pepper, and local spices, then smoke for 4-6 hours. Another value-added product is pig feed made from local ingredients like maize, soya, and cassava peels. You can sell surplus feed to other farmers. Direct marketing requires good customer service. Record Keeping and Financial Analysis

Keeping records is not just for big farms; it is essential for any pig business. Without records, you cannot know if you are making a profit or loss. Start with a simple notebook or a cheap exercise book. Record every expense: feed, medicine, labour, water, electricity, and transport. Also record every income: sales of pigs, pork, manure, or other products. Write the date, description, and amount. At the end of each month, add up your total expenses and total income. Subtract expenses from income to get your profit. For example, if you spent 200,000 Naira on feed, 20,000 on medicine, and 30,000 on labour, total expenses = 250,000 Naira. If you sold pigs for 350,000 Naira, your profit is 100,000 Naira. But that is not the whole story. You also need to account for the cost of piglets, depreciation of equipment, and your own time. A simple tool is a cash book, which you can buy at any stationery shop. For better analysis, use a spreadsheet on a phone or computer. Track key numbers: feed conversion ratio (kg feed per kg gain), mortality rate, and average daily gain. If your feed conversion is above 3.5, you are wasting money. If mortality is above 5%, investigate disease or management issues. Financial analysis helps you make decisions: should you buy more piglets? Change feed? Increase prices? Compare your performance month to month. Also calculate your break-even point: the number of pigs you must sell to cover all costs. For example, if each pig costs 25,000 Naira to raise and sells for 35,000, you need to sell 10 pigs to cover 250,000 Naira in fixed costs. Keep records for at least three years to see trends and plan for the future..Trim()

Budgeting and Cost Control

A budget is a plan for how you will spend and earn money. For pig farming, you need both an operating budget (monthly expenses) and a capital budget (large purchases like pens, water tanks, or a feed mixer). Start by listing all your expected costs for the next 6 months. Feed is usually 60-70% of total costs. For a farm with 20 pigs, you might need 2,000 kg of feed per month at 500 Naira per kg = 1,000,000 Naira. Add labour (maybe 50,000 Naira), veterinary services (30,000 Naira), water and electricity (20,000 Naira), and miscellaneous (20,000 Naira). Total monthly expenses = 1,120,000 Naira. Now estimate income: if you sell 10 pigs per month at 35,000 Naira each, that's 350,000 Naira. You are running at a loss! So you must either reduce costs or increase income. Cost control means finding cheaper feed ingredients, buying in bulk, or reducing waste. For example, mix your own feed using maize, soya, and fishmeal. Buy maize directly from farmers after harvest when prices are low. Store it properly to avoid aflatoxin. Control labour costs by involving family members or using part-time workers. Reduce mortality by vaccinating and keeping pens clean. Also, consider raising pigs to heavier weights to get better prices per kg. But don't overfeed; find the optimal weight. A budget helps you set targets and monitor performance. Review your budget every month. If you overspend on feed, adjust next month. Risk Management and Insurance

Pig farming has many risks: disease outbreaks, feed price increases, theft, and natural disasters. You cannot eliminate all risks, but you can reduce their impact. Also, write a simple risk management plan: list each risk, its likelihood, and what you will do. For example, if feed prices rise, you will switch to alternative ingredients.
