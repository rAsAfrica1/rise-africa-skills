What is Honey? Composition and Properties

Honey is a thick, sweet liquid made by bees from flower nectar. In Africa, honey comes from many plants like acacia, baobab, eucalyptus, and wildflowers. Its main sugars are glucose and fructose, making up about 80% of its weight. The rest is water (usually 15â20%), plus small amounts of minerals, vitamins, enzymes, and antioxidants. This mix gives honey its energy boost and long shelf life. For example, 1 kilogram of honey contains roughly 3,040 calories. Because it is acidic (pH around 3.9) and low in water, bacteria cannot grow in it. That is why pure honey never spoils if kept sealed. When you harvest, the moisture content matters. In Africa, aim for below 20% moisture. You can check with a simple refractometer, a tool costing about $50. If honey has more water, it will ferment and taste like alcohol. Honey also crystallizes over time. This is normal and does not mean it is fake. To turn it back to liquid, place the jar in warm water (not above 40Â°C). Heating above 60Â°C destroys enzymes and darkens the honey. Different flowers give different colours and flavours. Acacia honey is light and mild; forest honey is dark and strong. For small-scale farmers, understanding these properties helps you decide when to harvest, how to store, and what price to ask. Always store honey in food-grade plastic or glass containers, away from sunlight. Never use metal containers because honey's acid reacts with metal, causing rust and bad taste. Keep your honey in a cool, dry place. With proper handling, your honey can last for years and fetch a good price at local markets or cooperatives..Trim()

Types of Bees in Africa: Apis mellifera and Stingless Bees

Africa has two main groups of honey-making bees: Apis mellifera (stinging honeybees) and stingless bees (Meliponula and others). Apis mellifera is the most common. Harvesting Honey: Timing and Methods

Harvesting at the right time is critical. In Africa, honey flow depends on rainfall and flowering. In most regions, harvest after the rainy season when flowers are abundant. For example, in East Africa, harvest from June to September and December to February. In West Africa, harvest from October to December. Never harvest when bees are hungry or during droughtâthey need honey to survive. Check the hive: combs should be at least 80% capped (sealed with white wax). If less, wait. Use a smoker to calm bees. Wear your suit. Open the hive gently. Remove one frame or top bar at a time. For Langstroth, use a bee brush to remove bees. For KTBH, lift the bar and brush bees off. Identifying Quality Honey vs. Adulterated Honey

Adulterated honey is a big problem in African markets. It is honey mixed with sugar syrup, corn syrup, or water. To protect your customers and your reputation, learn to spot fake honey. First, check the label. Pure honey should list only 'honey' as an ingredient. If it says 'honey blend' or 'honey syrup', it is not pure. But many local sellers do not label. So you need simple tests. The water test: put a drop of honey in a glass of water. Pure honey sinks and stays as a lump. Adulterated honey dissolves quickly. The thumb test: put a drop on your thumb. Pure honey stays put and does not run. Fake honey spreads or drips. The flame test: dip a cotton swab in honey and light it. Pure honey burns because it is flammable. Adulterated honey with water does not light easily. The taste test: pure honey has complex flavoursâfloral, fruity, or woodyâand a slight sting in the throat. Fake honey tastes simply sweet like sugar. The crystallization test: pure honey crystallizes over time (especially if stored cool). Fake honey often stays liquid forever. But note: some pure honey (like acacia) crystallizes slowly. Also, check moisture with a refractometer. Pure honey should be below 20% moisture. Above 20% means it may ferment or be watered down. In Africa, adulteration is common because honey is expensive. A 1-litre bottle of pure honey sells for $8â$15, while sugar syrup costs pennies. As a producer, you can build trust by letting customers test your honey. Sell in clear jars so they can see the colour and texture. Get your honey tested at a local lab or beekeeping association if possible. Always harvest fully capped combs and process cleanly. Your pure honey will speak for itself. Do not be tempted to mix. Safety and Hygiene in Honey Handling

Honey is food, so safety and hygiene are non-negotiable. Start with your hands. Wash them with soap and clean water before touching honey or equipment. Wear clean clothes and a hairnet if possible. Do not handle honey if you are sick, especially with diarrhoea or vomiting. Your harvesting toolsâknives, buckets, extractorsâmust be washed and dried. Use food-grade plastic or stainless steel. Never use containers that held chemicals, oil, or fuel. For cleaning, use hot water and a food-safe detergent. Rinse well. Dry with a clean cloth or air dry. During harvest, avoid contaminating honey with dirt, bees, or wax. Use a clean bucket with a lid. Strain honey through a clean cloth or mesh. Do not use your bare hands to squeeze combsâuse a masher or press. After extraction, let honey settle in a tall container. Impurities like wax and bee parts float to the top. Skim them off with a clean spoon. Then pour honey into clean, dry jars. Jars must be sterilized: wash with hot water, then dry in an oven at 100Â°C for 10 minutes, or sun-dry. Do not use wet jarsâmoisture causes fermentation. Seal jars tightly. Store in a cool, dark place, away from strong smells (honey absorbs odours). Never store honey in metal containers because the acid reacts and creates rust. Also, protect yourself from bee stings. Wear your suit and use smoke. If you are allergic, keep an antihistamine nearby. In Africa, many small-scale farmers process honey on the ground or in dirty sheds. This leads to contamination and low prices. Build a simple clean processing area: a table, a roof, and washed walls. Use clean water. Train your family or workers on hygiene. Remember, honey is eaten raw by many people, so any bacteria or dirt can cause illness. Good hygiene means better taste, longer shelf life, and higher prices. A manual centrifugal extractor uses spinning motion to pull honey out of the comb without destroying the wax. Minimizing Contamination During Extraction

Honey is a natural product, but it can easily be contaminated during extraction. The biggest risks are dirt, wax particles, bee parts, and moisture. To minimize contamination, follow strict hygiene. First, work in a clean, enclosed room with screened windows to keep out flies and dust. Wear clean clothes and wash your hands with soap and water before starting. Use only food-grade stainless steel or plastic buckets, and sanitize them with a weak bleach solution (1 tablespoon per 4 litres of water), then rinse thoroughly with clean water. Never use buckets that held pesticides, fuel, or chemicals. During extraction, keep the honey covered as much as possible. If using a centrifugal extractor, make sure it is clean and dry before loading frames. For crush and strain, use a clean straining cloth that has been boiled and dried. Avoid touching the honey with bare hands if you have cuts or sores. Also, control moisture: extract only capped honey (below 20% moisture) to prevent fermentation. Use a refractometer to check moisture content. If honey is too wet, you can dehumidify the room or use a honey dryer. After extraction, filter the honey through a double sieve (coarse then fine) to remove wax and debris. Let it settle, then skim off foam. Never add water or any other substance. Store honey in clean, dry glass or food-grade plastic containers with airtight lids. In Africa's warm climate, contamination can happen fast, so always keep your equipment spotless and your workspace tidy. A clean operation also gets you better prices from buyers..Trim()

Post-Extraction Cleanup and Storage

After extraction, clean up immediately. Honey left on equipment attracts bees, ants, and rodents, and it hardens, making cleaning harder. Never store honey in metal containers other than stainless steel, as it can react and cause off-flavours. Keep honey away from strong-smelling items like onions or petrol. For long-term storage, keep honey in sealed buckets in a dry room. Proper cleanup and storage ensure your honey stays high-quality for months, and you can sell it at premium prices. Moisture Content Testing: Refractometer Use

Moisture content is the single most important quality factor in honey. If moisture is above 20%, honey will ferment. In Africa, where humidity is often high, testing moisture is critical. Testing for Adulteration: Simple Methods

Adulteration means adding cheap sugars like cane sugar, corn syrup, or high-fructose syrup to honey. This cheats customers and damages your reputation. Basic Bookkeeping for Honey Businesses

Bookkeeping means writing down every shilling that comes in and goes out of your honey business. Without it, you cannot know if you are making a profit or losing money. Start with a simple cash book: a notebook with two columns, one for money in (sales of honey, honeycomb, beeswax) and one for money out (sugar for feeding bees, jars, labels, transport, labour). .Trim()

At the end of each month, add up both columns. Subtract total money out from total money in. If the result is positive, that is your profit. If negative, you are running at a loss and must find ways to cut costs or increase sales. For example, if you sold 50 jars at 2,000 shillings each, that is 100,000 shillings in. If you spent 30,000 on jars, 10,000 on labels, 20,000 on transport, and 15,000 on sugar, total out is 75,000. Profit is 25,000 shillings..Trim()

.Trim()

Separate business money from personal money. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for the business. Pay yourself a fixed salary, say 10,000 shillings per week, and record it as an expense. This prevents you from accidentally spending the business's money on personal needs..Trim()

.Trim()

Use simple tools: a hardcover notebook, a calculator, and a pen. If you have a smartphone, use a free app like Wave or QuickBooks Online Simple Start. Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your honey business. Even a profitable business can fail if cash runs out when bills are due.
