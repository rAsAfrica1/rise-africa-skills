Understanding the Iron-Carbon Phase Diagram

The iron-carbon phase diagram is a map that tells you what's happening inside steel at different temperatures and carbon contents. For bladesmithing, you mainly work with steel containing 0.5% to 1.2% carbon. The diagram shows three key regions: ferrite (soft, low carbon), austenite (high-temperature phase where carbon dissolves), and cementite (hard, brittle iron carbide). When you heat steel above the critical temperature (around 727Â°C for most simple steels), it transforms into austenite. This is where carbon atoms dissolve into the iron lattice. The exact temperature depends on carbon content: for 1080 steel (0.8% carbon), the critical temperature is about 727Â°C; for 5160 (0.6% carbon), it's around 760Â°C. In Africa, you can use a simple magnet to test: steel becomes non-magnetic just above the critical temperature. Heat until a magnet no longer sticks, then you're in the austenite zone. But don't overheatâif you go too high, you get grain growth, which weakens the blade. The diagram also shows the A1, A3, and Acm lines. The A1 line is at 727Â°C and separates ferrite+cementite from austenite. The A3 line slopes upward as carbon decreases, marking the boundary between ferrite+austenite and full austenite. Austenitizing, Quenching, and Martensite Formation

After heat treating, you need to test your blade to ensure it's hard enough and will hold an edge. The most common test is the file test: take a sharp file and run it across the edge. Troubleshooting Common Heat Treating Issues

Heat treating can go wrong in several ways. Here are common issues and how to fix them. First, soft spots: after quenching, parts of the blade don't harden. This happens if the steel wasn't fully austenitized (too low temperature or too short soak) or if quenching was too slow (oil instead of water for a fast-quenching steel). Fix: re-heat to the correct temperature, soak longer, and quench in a faster medium. Second, cracking: blades crack during quenching due to rapid cooling or uneven cooling. High-carbon steels in water are prone. Fix: use oil or brine, quench vertically, and avoid sharp corners. If cracked, the blade is ruinedâstart over. Third, warping: blades bend during quenching because of uneven cooling or stress. Fix: quench edge-first, use a quench tank that allows even cooling, and straighten immediately after quenching while still warm (but not too hot). You can straighten in a vise or with a hammer. Fourth, retained austenite: after quenching, some austenite remains, making the blade soft and unstable. This happens if you don't cool enough or if the steel is high-alloy. Fix: quench to room temperature, and temper multiple times. For severe cases, do a sub-zero treatment (freeze in dry ice or freezer) before tempering. Fifth, grain growth: overheating during austenitizing causes large grains, making the blade brittle. Fix: control temperatureâuse a thermocouple or magnet. If grain growth occurs, you can normalize (heat to just above critical, air cool) a few times to refine grains. Sixth, decarburization: the surface loses carbon during heating, making it soft. Fix: heat in a reducing atmosphere (charcoal forge with limited air) or coat the blade with anti-scale compound. If decarb occurs, grind away the soft layer. Seventh, tempering too high: if you temper above the recommended temperature, hardness drops too much. Fix: re-heat treat if possible, or use a lower tempering temperature next time. Eighth, quenching too fast: causes cracking; quenching too slow: causes softness. Match quenchant to steel. Pattern-Welded Steel: Introduction to Damascus

Pattern-welded steel, often called Damascus, is made by forge-welding layers of high-carbon and low-carbon steel together, then folding and welding repeatedly to create a billet with hundreds of layers. In Africa, you can use locally available steels: an old file (high carbon, around 1.0-1.2% carbon) and mild steel from a car spring or rebar (low carbon, 0.2-0.3% carbon). Start with 5-7 layers, each about 3mm thick, 40mm wide, and 150mm long. Clean all surfaces with a grinder or sandpaper to bare metal, stack them, and wire them tightly together. Heat the stack in your forge to 1250-1300Â°C (bright yellow-orange, almost white). Sprinkle borax on the seams as a flux to prevent oxidation. When the steel looks like a glowing lemon, pull it out and immediately set the weld with light hammer taps, then increase force. Work quickly; if the steel cools below 1100Â°C, stop and reheat. After the first weld, you have a solid billet. Now cut it into three pieces, stack, and weld again. Repeat this fold-and-weld cycle 5-7 times. Each cycle doubles the layer count, so 5 layers becomes 160 after 5 folds. Forge the billet into a blade shape, then grind and etch in ferric chloride (available from electronics suppliers) diluted 1:3 with water. The high-carbon layers turn dark, low-carbon stay bright, revealing the pattern. Common problems: poor welds from dirty steel or insufficient heat. Practice with cheap steel first. In many African markets, you can find old leaf springs and files at scrap yards for low cost. Always wear safety glasses and gloves; hot borax can pop..Trim()

Forge Welding: Techniques and Troubleshooting

Forge welding is the backbone of advanced bladesmithing. It joins two pieces of steel at high temperature with pressure. In Africa, you can use a simple charcoal forge with a hand-cranked blower. The key is reaching the right temperature: 1250-1300Â°C for most steels. At this heat, the steel surface looks like a bright yellow-orange, almost molten, and a sprinkle of borax (20-30 grams) will bubble and flow like water. Borax dissolves oxides and prevents new scale from forming. Clean your steel to bare metal with a grinder before heating. Stack the pieces, wire them together, and heat slowly to avoid thermal shock. When the flux becomes liquid and the steel is at welding heat, remove it from the fire and immediately tap lightly to set the weld, then hit harder with a 2-3 kg hammer. Do not delay; you have about 2-3 seconds before the temperature drops. If the weld fails, you will see a dark line or the pieces separate. Common causes: insufficient heat (steel not yellow enough), dirty surfaces, too little flux, or too much oxygen in the fire. Use a deep fire with plenty of charcoal to reduce oxygen. If welding a billet, weld one side at a time. After welding, normalize the steel by heating to 900Â°C and air cooling to relieve stress. Troubleshooting: if the weld cracks, reheat, re-flux, and try again. If the steel burns (sparks like a sparkler), you overheated it; cut off the burnt part. Practice with mild steel first, then move to high-carbon. In many African workshops, forge welding is done with a hammer and anvil, but a hydraulic press (even a 10-ton bottle jack press) can make it easier. Always wear leather gloves and a face shield..Trim()

Twisting and Laddering Patterns

Once you have a pattern-welded billet, you can manipulate it to create twists and ladder patterns. For a twist pattern, forge your billet into a square bar, about 20mm x 20mm x 200mm. Heat it to 1250Â°C (bright yellow) and clamp one end in a heavy vise. Use a twisting wrench or a large pipe wrench to turn the other end. For a tight twist, turn 3-4 full rotations per 50mm of length. Work while the steel is hot; if it cools, reheat. After twisting, forge the bar flat into a blade, which will reveal a star-like pattern. For a ladder pattern, take a stacked billet and cut shallow grooves (2-3mm deep) across the billet every 10-15mm using a chisel or a grinder. Then forge the billet flat. The grooves will create a ladder-like pattern when etched. Alternatively, you can drill holes through the billet before forging to create a raindrop pattern. In Africa, you can use a charcoal forge and a simple anvil. For twisting, a sturdy bench vise is essential; if you don't have one, bury a heavy metal post in the ground and use a pipe wrench. After patterning, always normalize the steel (heat to 900Â°C, air cool) to refine grain. Then grind and etch in ferric chloride to see the pattern. Safety: twisting hot steel can send scale flying; wear safety glasses and a leather apron. Practice on mild steel first to learn the feel. With a 5-layer billet, a tight twist can produce over 100 visible layers after forging..Trim()

San-Mai and Go-Mai Construction

San-Mai means 'three layers' in Japanese: a hard high-carbon steel core sandwiched between two layers of soft low-carbon steel. Go-Mai is five layers: a hard core with two soft layers on each side. This construction gives a blade a hard edge and a tough, shock-absorbing body. In Africa, you can use a piece of old file for the core (high carbon) and mild steel from a car body or rebar for the cladding. Start with a core piece 5mm thick, 30mm wide, and 150mm long. Cut two cladding pieces 3mm thick, 30mm wide, and 150mm long. Clean all surfaces to bare metal. Stack: cladding, core, cladding. Wire the stack together tightly. Heat to 1250-1300Â°C with borax flux and forge weld. Weld one side, then flip and weld the other. For Go-Mai, add two more cladding layers, making a 5-layer stack. After welding, forge the billet into a blade shape. Be careful not to forge too thin or the core might be exposed. The core should be centered; if it shifts, you can grind the sides to recenter. After forging, normalize, grind, and heat treat. The hard core will take a keen edge, while the soft cladding protects against breakage. Etching in ferric chloride will show the layers. Common mistake: welding temperature too low, leading to delamination. Use plenty of flux and a reducing fire (deep charcoal). In many African workshops, San-Mai is a cost-effective way to make high-performance blades from recycled materials. Practice with small billets first..Trim()

Forging Axes and Tomahawks

Forging an axe or tomahawk requires a different approach than a knife. Start with a piece of high-carbon steel, such as a car axle (0.5-0.6% carbon) or a leaf spring (5160). For a tomahawk head, cut a piece 100mm long, 25mm wide, and 10mm thick. Heat to 1200Â°C (orange-yellow) and forge the eye first. Use a drift punch (a tapered steel rod) to open the eye. Start with a slot punched through with a hot cutter, then drive the drift through to shape the eye. Work from both sides to keep it centered. For the blade, draw out the steel on the anvil to form the bit. Use a cross-peen hammer to spread the steel. For an axe, you may need to forge weld a high-carbon bit to a mild steel body. Forge the bit to about 3mm thick at the edge, then weld it into a slot in the body. Heat treat: heat the blade to 800-850Â°C (non-magnetic) and quench in oil. Temper at 200-250Â°C for one hour to reduce brittleness. In Africa, you can use a charcoal forge and a heavy anvil. A tomahawk can be forged in one session; an axe may take two. Handle material: local hardwood like mahogany or acacia. Shape the handle to fit the eye, then secure with a wedge. Safety: always wear safety glasses and gloves. Test the edge on a piece of wood; it should chop cleanly. Common problems: eye too small or off-center. Measure with calipers and adjust with a file. Practice on mild steel first..Trim()

Forging a Full-Tang Integral Knife

A full-tang integral knife is forged from a single piece of steel, with the blade, bolster, and handle all one piece. This makes a strong, durable knife. Start with a piece of high-carbon steel, such as a 5160 leaf spring, 6mm thick, 40mm wide, and 250mm long. Heat the steel to 1200Â°C (orange-yellow) and forge the blade first. Draw out the tip, then shape the profile with a hammer. For the integral bolster, leave a thicker section (about 10mm) between the blade and handle. Forge this section to shape, then use a file or grinder to create a smooth transition. The handle tang should be about 4mm thick and full length. Forge the tang to shape, leaving it slightly thicker than the final dimension. After forging, normalize the blade three times (heat to 900Â°C, air cool) to refine grain. Grind the blade to a smooth finish, leaving the edge about 1mm thick. Heat treat: heat the blade to 800-830Â°C (non-magnetic) and quench in oil. Temper at 200Â°C for two hours. For the handle, you can use local hardwood, bone, or horn. Drill holes in the tang for pins, then epoxy and pin the scales. Shape the handle with files and sandpaper. Etch the blade in ferric chloride to reveal the hardening line. In Africa, you can find leaf springs at auto repair shops. A full-tang integral knife takes time but is a mark of a skilled bladesmith. Safety: always wear safety glasses and a respirator when grinding. Practice on a smaller piece first..Trim()

Edge Retention Testing: Cutting Tests

Edge retention testing tells you how long your blade will keep cutting before it needs sharpening. You don't need fancy equipment. A simple test is to cut sisal rope or cardboard. Get a 1-meter length of 10mm sisal rope, tie it to a post, and make clean slices. Count how many cuts you get before the knife stops shaving hair or slicing paper easily. For a good carbon steel knife (like 1080 or 5160) hardened to 58-60 HRC, you should get 50-100 cuts on rope. If you get less than 20, your edge geometry is too thick or the heat treat is off. Another test: cut into a soft pine board 10 times, then check if it still slices newspaper cleanly. In Africa, many makers use old leaf springs (5160) or files (W1). Test with local materials: cut through a maize stalk or a thick banana trunk. Record results in a notebook. Compare blades. If edge rolls or chips, note it. For a working knife in rural Africa, edge retention matters more than extreme sharpness. A blade that holds a working edge for a day of farm work is better than one that shaves but dulls in 10 minutes. Always test on the same material for fair comparison. After testing, sharpen and repeat. This builds a baseline for your forging and heat treating..Trim()

Toughness Testing: Chopping and Flexing

Toughness is a blade's ability to absorb impact without cracking or breaking. A hard blade can be brittle; a tough blade bends before it breaks. To test chopping toughness, secure a dry hardwood log (like acacia or mahogany) and chop into it 20 times with moderate force. A good chopper (e.g., a machete or camp knife) should not chip, roll, or crack. If the edge chips, your steel is too hard or has a coarse grain. If it rolls, it's too soft. For flex testing, clamp the blade horizontally in a vise with the edge up, leaving 10 cm of the tip exposed. Pull the tip sideways with a pipe until it bends 20-30 degrees. It should spring back straight. If it stays bent, the steel is too soft; if it snaps, too hard. In Africa, many blades are made from car springs. A spring steel blade at 55-57 HRC should flex without breaking. Test small blades by pushing the tip into a softwood board and prying sideways. Never test a blade you intend to sell without safety glasses and gloves. A broken blade can fly. Record how much force you used. For a bush knife, you want a balance: hard enough to hold an edge, tough enough to survive hitting a bone or a knot. If a blade fails, adjust your tempering temperature. Tempering at 200Â°C gives more toughness than 150Â°C..Trim()

Hardness Testing: Files and Rockwell

Hardness testing tells you if your blade will hold an edge. The simplest test is the file test. Take a new, sharp mill file (like a Nicholson). Draw it across the blade's edge bevel at a 30-degree angle. If the file bites and cuts metal, the blade is too soft (below 50 HRC). If it skates without cutting, the blade is harder than the file (around 60 HRC). If it scratches slightly, it's around 55 HRC. This is a rough test but works in a rural workshop. For more precision, use a Rockwell hardness tester. Many city workshops or technical colleges have one. A typical knife should be 56-60 HRC. A machete or chopper: 52-56 HRC. A fine slicing knife: 60-62 HRC. Test at least three points along the edge: near the handle, middle, and tip. Variations of more than 2 HRC mean uneven heat treatment. In Africa, you can make a simple hardness tester using a hardened ball bearing and a hammer, but it's not accurate. Better to send a sample to a lab. If no lab, use the file test and compare to a known good knife. Always test after tempering, not before. If too soft, re-harden and temper at a lower temperature. If too hard, temper again at a higher temperature (e.g., 220Â°C for 1 hour). Record results. A consistent hardness across the blade means good forge control..Trim()

Inspecting for Cracks and Flaws

Cracks and flaws can cause a blade to fail catastrophically. Inspect your blade after forging, after hardening, and after tempering. Start with a visual inspection under good light. Look for fine lines, especially near the edge, spine, and tang. Use a magnifying glass (10x) if available. A common flaw is a hardening crack, which appears as a jagged line running perpendicular to the edge. Another is a cold shut, a fold in the steel from forging that didn't weld. To check for internal cracks, do a bend test: clamp the blade in a vise and bend it 10 degrees. If it snaps, examine the break for dark spots or grain boundaries. A bright, silky gray break is good; a dark, rough break means poor grain structure. Also check for delamination if you used layered steel (e.g., cable damascus). Tap the blade with a small hammer; a dull thud may indicate a crack. In Africa, many blades are made from recycled steel with unknown defects. Always normalize (heat to critical and cool in air) three times before hardening to refine grain and reduce stress. After quenching, temper immediately. If you find a crack, do not try to sell the blade. Rework it into a smaller knife or scrap it. A cracked blade can injure the user. Keep a log of flaws and their causes. Most cracks come from quenching too cold, too fast, or from steel with high sulfur. Use clean water or oil at the right temperature (oil at 50-60Â°C for most carbon steels)..Trim()

Hard/brittle edge: if it chips easily, temper again at a higher temperature (e.g., 230Â°C for 1 hour). Chipping can also be from a too-thin edge; sharpen at a wider angle (20-25 degrees per side). Always have a fire extinguisher or sand bucket. After correcting, retest. Never sell a blade that has been re-hardened more than twice; the steel may be compromised.
