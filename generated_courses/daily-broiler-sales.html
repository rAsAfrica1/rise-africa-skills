Module 1 · Why Daily Sales Need Batch Production

Introduction and Why It Matters

If you sell broilers every day, you know the struggle: some days you have too many birds, some days none. Customers come and go disappointed. You lose money and trust. Batch production solves this. Instead of buying chicks once and selling them all at once, you divide your flock into groups called batches. Each batch is started at a different time, so you always have birds ready for sale. For example, start 50 chicks every week. After 6 weeks, you sell 50 birds per week, every week. This gives you steady income and happy customers. In Africa, where demand for chicken is high in towns and cities, daily sales can double your profit compared to selling only once a month. You need a small space, basic equipment, and a reliable chick supplier. Many farmers in Kenya, Nigeria, and Ghana use this method to supply hotels, restaurants, and local markets. It also reduces risk: if disease hits one batch, you still have others. Start small with 50 chicks per batch. As you learn, increase to 100 or more. The key is consistency. With batch production, you become a reliable supplier, and customers will come to you daily. This module will show you exactly how to set up and run a daily sales operation using batches..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To run a batch production system for daily broiler sales, you need the right tools. First, housing: a simple deep litter house with wire mesh walls and a leak-proof roof. For 50 birds per batch, you need about 10 square meters. You can use local materials like bamboo or mud bricks, but ensure it's predator-proof. Second, feeders and drinkers: 10 tube feeders and 10 drinkers for 50 birds. Plastic ones are cheap and easy to clean. Third, heat source for brooding: charcoal stove, kerosene lamp, or electric bulb. For 50 chicks, one charcoal stove works. Fourth, bedding: wood shavings or rice husks. Avoid sawdust from treated wood. Fifth, feed: starter, grower, and finisher. Buy from reputable suppliers like Olam or local millers. You'll need about 4 kg of feed per bird total. Sixth, vaccines and medicines: Newcastle, Gumboro, and vitamins. Keep a cool box for vaccines. Seventh, record book and pen: track batch start dates, feed, deaths, and sales. Eighth, weighing scale: to sell by weight if needed. Ninth, transport crate: for delivering to customers. Tenth, water source: clean water daily. All these can be sourced locally. In many African markets, you can buy feeders and drinkers from agro-vet shops. Start with what you have and upgrade as you grow. Remember, cleanliness is key: have a footbath and disinfectant at the entrance..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to set up your batch production system. Step 1: Plan your batches. Decide how many birds you want to sell per week. If you want 50 birds per week, start 50 chicks every week. But start small: begin with 2 batches, then add more. Step 2: Prepare the brooder. Clean and disinfect the brooding area. Spread bedding 5 cm thick. Heat the room to 32-35°C for the first week. Step 3: Buy day-old chicks from a hatchery. Choose healthy, active chicks. Place them in the brooder. Give them glucose water for the first 6 hours. Step 4: Feed and water. Use starter feed for 0-4 weeks, grower for 4-6 weeks, finisher for 6-8 weeks. Provide feed and water ad libitum. Step 5: Vaccinate. Day 1: Marek's (at hatchery). Day 7: Newcastle + IB. Day 14: Gumboro. Day 21: Newcastle booster. Follow your vet's advice. Step 6: Manage each batch separately. Keep records of each batch's start date. Step 7: After 6-8 weeks, birds reach 1.5-2 kg. Start selling. But don't sell all at once. Sell 50 birds per week from the oldest batch. Step 8: As you sell, start a new batch. So every week you have a new batch of chicks and a batch ready for sale. Step 9: Keep daily records of sales, feed, and mortality. Step 10: Adjust batch size based on demand. If you sell out quickly, increase chicks per batch. This cycle repeats, giving you daily sales..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many farmers fail at batch production because of avoidable mistakes. Mistake 1: Starting too many batches at once. You get overwhelmed and run out of space or feed. Fix: Start with 2 batches, then add one batch per month. Mistake 2: Mixing batches. If you put different ages together, older birds bully younger ones and disease spreads. Fix: Keep each batch in separate pens. Mistake 3: Poor record keeping. You forget when to vaccinate or sell. Fix: Use a simple notebook or chalkboard. Write batch number, start date, and expected sale date. Mistake 4: Inconsistent feeding. Birds grow slowly and unevenly. Fix: Feed at the same times daily. Use a feeding schedule. Mistake 5: Skipping vaccinations. Disease wipes out entire batches. Fix: Vaccinate on time. Set phone reminders. Mistake 6: Selling all birds from one batch at once. You lose daily sales. Fix: Sell only the oldest batch, and only as needed. Keep a buffer. Mistake 7: Not isolating sick birds. One sick bird infects all. Fix: Have a sick bay. Remove sick birds immediately. Mistake 8: Overcrowding. Birds stress and die. Fix: Allow 0.2 square meters per bird in grow-out. Mistake 9: Poor biosecurity. Visitors bring disease. Fix: Limit visitors, use footbath, and disinfect equipment. Mistake 10: Underestimating costs. You run out of money for feed. Fix: Budget for feed, vaccines, and utilities before starting. Learn from these mistakes and your daily sales will be steady..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Safety and best practices protect your health, your birds, and your business. First, personal safety: wear boots, gloves, and a mask when cleaning poultry houses. Poultry dust can cause respiratory problems. Wash hands with soap after handling birds. Second, biosecurity: keep visitors out. If they must enter, give them boots and a lab coat. Use a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance. Clean and disinfect feeders and drinkers daily. Third, bird welfare: provide adequate space, fresh water, and feed. Avoid overcrowding. Keep litter dry to prevent ammonia burns. Fourth, vaccine handling: store vaccines in a cool box with ice packs. Never freeze Newcastle or Gumboro vaccines. Use clean water for mixing. Vaccinate in the cool of the day. Fifth, feed safety: store feed in a dry, raised place. Prevent mold. Never feed moldy feed. Sixth, waste management: dispose of dead birds properly by burning or deep burial. Compost litter away from the poultry house. Seventh, fire safety: if using charcoal stoves, keep them away from walls and bedding. Have a bucket of sand or water nearby. Eighth, record keeping: keep sales and expense records. This helps you know your profit and plan. Ninth, market safety: transport birds in clean crates. Do not mix with other birds. Tenth, continuous learning: join a local poultry association. Attend training. Best practices lead to healthy birds, happy customers, and more money. Always prioritize safety..Trim()

Even with formulas, farmers make mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Not accounting for mortality. Many farmers calculate chicks needed based only on sales, forgetting that some chicks will die. If you need 2,100 birds and mortality is 5%, you must start with 2,205 chicks. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in broiler production is not just about avoiding injury. It is also about biosecurity, feed safety, and financial safety. First, personal safety. When handling feed, wear a dust mask and gloves. Feed dust can cause respiratory problems. When using chemicals for disinfection, wear protective clothing and keep them away from children. In Africa, many farmers use formalin for fumigation. Formalin is dangerous. Use it only in a well-ventilated area, wear a mask, and never mix it with other chemicals..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, biosecurity. Disease can wipe out your entire batch. Follow these practices: Keep visitors away from your pens. Use a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance. Change clothes and boots before entering. Vaccinate chicks against Newcastle disease, Gumboro, and fowl pox. In Africa, Newcastle is common. Buy vaccines from reputable agro-vet shops and follow the cold chain. If you have multiple batches, handle the youngest birds first, then older ones. This prevents spreading disease from older to younger birds..Trim()

.Trim()

Third, feed safety. Store feed in a dry, cool place. Use pallets to keep bags off the floor. In humid areas, feed can grow mould. Mould produces aflatoxin, which is poisonous. Never feed mouldy feed. Buy feed from trusted suppliers and use within 4 weeks. If you mix your own feed, use aflatoxin binder. Also, protect feed from rodents and birds..Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, financial safety. Keep separate records for production and sales. Do not mix business money with personal money. Use a simple cash book. If you sell 50 birds a day, record each sale. At the end of the week, calculate your profit. If you are making a loss, adjust your formulas. Also, have a contingency fund for disease outbreaks or feed price hikes. In Africa, prices can change quickly. A fund of at least 10% of your monthly costs is wise..Trim()

.Trim()

Fifth, best practice for daily sales. Sell birds at a consistent time each day. This builds customer trust. Weigh birds in front of the customer if possible. Use a clean scale. Provide a receipt or record the sale. Keep the pen clean and remove dead birds immediately. Dead birds attract disease and predators. Bury or burn them far from the pen..Trim()

Finally, take care of yourself. Farming is hard work. Rest, drink clean water, and eat well. A healthy farmer makes better decisions. By following these safety and best practices, you protect your birds, your money, and your health..Trim()

Module 3 · The 42-Day Broiler Cycle

Introduction and Why It Matters

Broiler farming is one of the fastest ways to make money in African agriculture. A broiler chicken can be ready for market in just 42 days if you follow a strict cycle. This 42-day cycle is the backbone of a daily sales business. Instead of selling chickens once in a while, you produce batches every week so you always have birds to sell. Customers want fresh chicken every day, and if you can supply that, you build a loyal market. The 42-day cycle works because broilers grow quickly when given the right feed, water, and shelter. From day-old chicks to 2.5 kg live weight, it takes about six weeks. If you start a new batch every week, you will have a continuous harvest. For example, if you keep 100 birds per batch and start a batch every Monday, then after 42 days you will be selling 100 birds every week. That means daily sales of about 14 birds. At a profit of 500 naira per bird, that's 7,000 naira daily. This system turns a small space into a steady income. But it only works if you stick to the timeline. Delays in feeding, dirty water, or disease can push the cycle to 50 or 60 days, eating your profit. In Africa, many farmers fail because they treat broilers like local chickens�??free-ranging and irregular. The 42-day cycle demands discipline: exact feed amounts, clean water, vaccines on time, and proper ventilation. It also matters because feed is your biggest cost. A broiler eats about 4 kg of feed to reach 2.5 kg. If you waste feed or let it spoil, you lose money. The cycle helps you plan your cash flow. You know when to buy chicks, when to buy feed, and when to expect income. This module will teach you the exact steps to run a 42-day cycle, from preparing the brooder to selling the last bird. Tools and Materials

To run a successful 42-day broiler cycle, you need the right tools and materials. Without them, you will struggle with disease, waste, and slow growth. Here is a complete list for a batch of 100 birds. First, housing: a deep litter pen of 10 square meters (100 birds at 10 birds per square meter). The floor should be concrete or packed earth, covered with 5-10 cm of wood shavings or rice husks. Never use sawdust from treated wood. You need a brooder for the first 2-3 weeks: a charcoal stove, kerosene lamp, or electric bulb (100 watts for 100 chicks). A thermometer to monitor temperature (start at 35°C and reduce by 3°C each week). Feeders: 10 tube feeders (capacity 5 kg each) and 10 drinkers (capacity 3 liters each). For the first week, use chick feeders and drinkers (small trays). You will need feed: 1 bag of chick starter (25 kg), 3 bags of grower (75 kg), and 2 bags of finisher (50 kg) for 100 birds. That's about 150 kg total. Also buy vaccines: Newcastle disease (ND) and Infectious Bursal Disease (IBD). You need a vaccine carrier with ice packs. For biosecurity: a footbath with disinfectant, a sprayer for disinfecting, and overalls or boots. For record-keeping: a notebook and pen to track feed, water, mortality, and weight. For selling: a weighing scale (5 kg capacity), crates for transport, and a sharp knife for processing if you sell dressed chicken. Other useful items: a water hose, a broom, a shovel, and a bucket. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps exactly to complete the 42-day cycle. Step 1: Prepare the brooder 24 hours before chicks arrive. Clean and disinfect the pen. Spread litter 5 cm deep. Set up the brooder and heat it to 35°C. Put feed and water in chick trays. Step 2: Receive day-old chicks. Count them and place them gently near the heat source. Dip their beaks in water to teach them to drink. Keep the brooder temperature at 35°C for the first week. Step 3: Week 1 (Days 1-7). Feed chick starter mash free-choice. Give clean water with glucose or vitamins for the first 3 days. Vaccinate against Newcastle disease (ND) on day 7 via eye drops. Reduce temperature to 32°C. Step 4: Week 2 (Days 8-14). Continue starter feed. Vaccinate against IBD on day 14 in drinking water. Reduce temperature to 29°C. Expand the brooder space as chicks grow. Step 5: Week 3 (Days 15-21). Switch to grower feed. Reduce temperature to 26°C. If using charcoal, you can stop heating by day 21 if the weather is warm. Give ND booster on day 21. Step 6: Week 4 (Days 22-28). Continue grower feed. Increase feeder and drinker space. Weigh a few birds weekly to check growth. A broiler should weigh about 1 kg at day 28. Step 7: Week 5 (Days 29-35). Switch to finisher feed. Ensure plenty of ventilation. At day 35, average weight should be 1.8 kg. Step 8: Week 6 (Days 36-42). Continue finisher feed. Withdraw feed 8 hours before selling (to empty the crop). On day 42, weigh and sell. Average weight should be 2.5 kg. Throughout the cycle, give feed twice daily (morning and evening) and water always. Clean drinkers daily. Remove wet litter. Record daily mortality. If a bird dies, remove it immediately. By following these steps, you will achieve a 42-day cycle. Remember, any delay in feeding or vaccination will extend the cycle and reduce profit..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many farmers fail to complete the 42-day cycle because of avoidable mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Overcrowding. Putting more than 10 birds per square meter causes stress, poor growth, and disease. Fix: Stick to 10 birds per square meter. For 100 birds, use 10 square meters. Mistake 2: Poor brooding temperature. Chicks huddle if too cold or pant if too hot. Fix: Use a thermometer. Start at 35°C and reduce by 3°C weekly. Watch chick behavior: if they spread out evenly, temperature is right. Mistake 3: Dirty water. Water with droppings or feed will cause disease. Fix: Clean drinkers twice daily and refill with fresh water. Mistake 4: Skipping vaccines. Newcastle disease can kill 90% of your flock. Fix: Vaccinate on day 7, 14, and 21. Use a vaccine carrier with ice. Mistake 5: Using the wrong feed. Starter, grower, and finisher have different protein levels. Fix: Follow the feed schedule. Do not use layer feed. Mistake 6: Wet litter. Wet litter causes ammonia gas and respiratory disease. Fix: Stir litter daily and add fresh shavings. Fix leaks immediately. Mistake 7: Not recording. Without records, you cannot know your profit or losses. Fix: Keep a notebook. Write down feed used, deaths, and weights. Mistake 8: Selling too early or too late. Selling before 42 days gives low weight; after 42 days, feed conversion worsens. Fix: Weigh birds weekly. Sell at 2.5 kg or day 42. Mistake 9: No biosecurity. Visitors can bring disease. Fix: Use a footbath. Limit visitors. Wear boots. Mistake 10: Power failure for brooder. Chicks can chill and die. Fix: Have a backup charcoal stove or kerosene lamp. By avoiding these mistakes, you will achieve a successful 42-day cycle and steady profit..Trim()

Introduction and Why It Matters

Feed is the single largest cost in broiler production, often accounting for 70% or more of your total expenses. If you are selling broilers daily, you must manage feed carefully or you will eat your profits. A broiler eats about 3.5 to 4 kg of feed to reach 2 kg live weight. At a feed price of 500 shillings per kg, that is 1,750 to 2,000 shillings per bird just for feed. If you sell a 2 kg bird for 2,500 shillings, you are left with only 500 to 750 shillings to cover chicks, medication, labour, and profit. In a batch production system, you raise birds in groups (batches) and sell them daily as they reach market weight. This means you must plan feed purchases for each batch from day one to day sale. Without a budget, you may run out of feed, forcing you to buy expensive small quantities, or you may overbuy and tie up cash that could be used elsewhere. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not keeping feed records. Many farmers feed by guesswork and never write down how much they use. This leads to running out of feed or overspending. Fix: Keep a daily feed log. Write the date, batch, and amount given. At the end of the week, add it up. Compare with your budget. If you are using more than planned, investigate..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 2: Buying feed in small quantities. When you run out and buy a few kilograms from a local shop, you pay a higher price per kg. Fix: Plan your purchases. Buy in 50 kg bags from a supplier. If cash is tight, join with other farmers to buy in bulk and share the discount..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 3: Overfeeding. Some farmers think more feed means faster growth. But broilers have limited capacity. Overfeeding wastes feed and can cause health problems like ascites. Fix: Follow the feeding guide. Use a scale to weigh feed for the first two weeks. After that, use a scoop and stick to the recommended daily amount. If birds leave feed in the trough, reduce the amount..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 4: Underfeeding. This is worse because birds will not reach market weight on time, and you will sell at a loss. Fix: Monitor bird weight weekly. Weigh a few birds (5-10) each week. If they are below target, increase feed slightly. But do not overdo it..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 5: Poor storage. Feed that gets wet or is attacked by rats is money lost. Fix: Store feed in a dry, raised place. Use airtight containers. Keep only one week's feed in the poultry house. The rest stays in the store. Rotate stock: use older feed first..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 6: Not adjusting for feed price changes. Feed prices fluctuate. If you budgeted at 25,000 per bag and the price rises to 28,000, your budget is off. Fix: Check prices weekly. Keep a buffer of 10% in your budget. If prices rise, adjust your selling price or find a cheaper supplier..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 7: Feeding the wrong feed. Broilers need different feed at different stages: starter (0-4 weeks), grower (4-6 weeks), and finisher (6 weeks to market). Using the wrong feed wastes money. Fix: Buy the correct feed for each stage. Label your storage clearly..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Safety in feed management is about protecting your health, your birds, and your money. First, personal safety. Feed dust can cause breathing problems. Always wear a dust mask when handling feed, especially when mixing or scooping. Wash your hands after handling feed, especially before eating. Keep feed away from children and pets. Store feed in a locked area if possible..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, feed safety. Feed can carry bacteria like Salmonella. Never use mouldy or spoiled feed. Mould produces toxins that can kill your birds. If feed smells sour or has clumps, do not use it. Keep feed dry. Moisture leads to mould and spoilage. Store feed in a cool, dry place. Use pallets to keep bags off the floor. Close bags tightly after opening. Use opened bags within one week..Trim()

.Trim()

Third, bird safety. Feed must be fresh and free of contaminants. Do not use feed that has been spilled on the floor and swept up�??it may contain droppings or dirt. Use clean feeders. Clean feeders daily to prevent feed buildup. Remove any wet or caked feed. Provide enough feeder space so birds do not crowd. A good rule: 5 cm of feeder space per bird. For 100 birds, you need 5 metres of feeder length. If feeders are too small, some birds will not get enough feed..Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, best practices for feed budgeting. Keep a separate bank account or cash box for feed money. Do not mix feed money with personal money. Buy feed from reputable suppliers who provide quality feed. Ask for a receipt. Check the manufacturing date on the bag. Use feed within three months of manufacturing. Weigh feed for the first two weeks to calibrate your scoop. Then you can rely on the scoop. But re-calibrate if you change feed type or supplier..Trim()

.Trim()

Fifth, environmental safety. Store feed away from chemicals like pesticides or fuel. These can contaminate feed and poison your birds. Keep feed storage clean and free of rodents. Use traps, not poison, near feed. If you must use poison, place it far from feed..Trim()

.Trim()

Sixth, financial safety. Your feed budget should include a contingency of 10% for price increases or unexpected needs. Review your budget weekly. If you are overspending, find out why and fix it. Do not borrow money for feed unless you are sure of your selling price. Remember, feed is your biggest cost�??protect it like your life..Trim()

Module 7 · Brooding Management

Introduction and Why It Matters

Brooding is the first 14 to 21 days of a chick's life. It is the most critical period because chicks cannot regulate their own body temperature until they are about two weeks old. In Africa, where many farms rely on natural ventilation and power can be unreliable, brooding management makes the difference between a 95% survival rate and a 70% one. A well-brooded chick starts eating and drinking within hours, grows evenly, and reaches market weight in 5 to 6 weeks. A poorly brooded chick is stunted, prone to disease, and may die. For daily broiler sales, you need a steady supply of healthy birds. That starts with brooding..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does brooding matter so much? Chicks are fragile. They need a temperature of 32-35°C in the first week, dropping by about 3°C each week until they are feathered. They also need clean water, correct feed, and protection from predators and drafts. In many parts of Africa, the ambient temperature can swing from 40°C during the day to 15°C at night. Without proper brooding, chicks will huddle, suffocate, or chill. You will see them panting or shivering. Both stress them and reduce feed intake..Trim()

.Trim()

Brooding also sets the stage for feed conversion. A chick that is comfortable and healthy will eat well and convert feed efficiently. If it is cold, it uses energy to stay warm instead of growing. If it is too hot, it eats less. Either way, you lose money. Good brooding reduces mortality, improves uniformity, and increases your profit per bird..Trim()

.Trim()

In this module, you will learn the tools, steps, and best practices for brooding broiler chicks in an African context. Whether you are using a charcoal stove, a kerosene lamp, or an electric bulb, the principles are the same. You will learn how to prepare the brooder house, manage temperature, feed and water, and avoid common mistakes. By the end, you will be able to brood chicks successfully and set your batch up for a profitable harvest..Trim()

Module 8 · Health and Biosecurity

Introduction and Why It Matters

Broiler chickens grow fast, but they are also fragile. A disease outbreak can wipe out your entire batch in days, turning your investment into a loss. In Africa, common killers include Newcastle disease, Gumboro (infectious bursal disease), coccidiosis, and chronic respiratory disease. These spread quickly through droppings, air, equipment, and even your clothes. Biosecurity is the set of simple, cheap actions you take every day to keep germs out and stop them from spreading if they get in. It is not about expensive drugs; it is about discipline. For example, a farmer in Kenya who lets visitors walk into the pen without boots can bring in Newcastle virus on their shoes. One week later, 80% of the birds may be dead. But a farmer who uses a footbath, keeps visitors out, and cleans drinkers daily can keep mortality under 5%. Healthy birds eat well, gain weight, and sell at top price. Sick birds eat less, grow slowly, and may be rejected by buyers. Your profit margin in broiler production is thin�??often only 10-15% per bird. One disease outbreak can erase profits for three batches. Biosecurity also protects your family and community from diseases like avian flu that can pass to humans. And it protects your reputation: buyers return to farms with consistent, healthy birds. The core rules are: keep a closed flock (no mixing with other poultry), clean and disinfect regularly, control traffic, and act fast when you see signs of illness. This module will give you the tools, steps, and habits to make biosecurity a normal part of your daily routine. You do not need a veterinary degree�??just consistency and attention to detail..Trim()

Module 9 · Processing and Cold Chain

Introduction and Why It Matters

Processing and cold chain are the steps that turn a live broiler into safe, sellable meat. In Africa, many small-scale farmers lose money because they sell live birds or slaughter without proper cooling. When you process correctly and keep the meat cold, you can sell at a higher price, reduce spoilage, and reach customers like restaurants and supermarkets. A broiler that is slaughtered, cleaned, and chilled within 2 hours can last up to 5 days in a refrigerator at 4°C. Without chilling, bacteria double every 20 minutes, and meat can spoil in 4-6 hours in hot weather. This means you must plan your processing day carefully. You need a clean workspace, safe water, sharp knives, and a cooling method. In many rural areas, electricity is unreliable, so you might use ice, a chest freezer with a generator, or a cool room. The key is to keep the meat below 4°C from slaughter to sale. Also, proper processing improves food safety and builds trust with customers. If you sell spoiled meat, you lose repeat business and may face legal issues. This module will teach you the tools, steps, mistakes to avoid, safety practices, and a practical exercise to master processing and cold chain for daily broiler sales. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many processors make mistakes that lead to spoilage or customer complaints. One common mistake is scalding at too high a temperature. Water above 65°C cooks the skin, making plucking difficult and damaging the carcass. Fix: Use a thermometer and keep water at 60°C. Another mistake is not chilling quickly enough. In hot African weather, meat left at room temperature for over 2 hours can become unsafe. Fix: Have ice ready before slaughter and chill immediately after washing. A third mistake is using dirty water. Untreated water contains bacteria that contaminate meat. Fix: Use clean, potable water or treat with chlorine. Fourth, improper evisceration can spill intestines, contaminating the carcass. Fix: Cut carefully and wash hands and knife between birds. Fifth, not cleaning tools between batches. Bacteria build up on knives and tables. Fix: Sanitize tools with hot water and bleach solution every 10 birds. Sixth, selling meat without proper packaging. Open meat attracts flies and dries out. Fix: Use food-grade bags or trays and label with date. Seventh, storing meat at wrong temperature. A refrigerator at 8°C is too warm; bacteria grow. Fix: Keep fridge at 4°C or below, use a thermometer. Eighth, not wearing gloves or clean clothes. This transfers bacteria. Fix: Wear clean apron, boots, and gloves, and wash hands frequently. Ninth, overcrowding in cooler. Birds stacked too tightly don't cool evenly. Fix: Arrange birds in single layers with ice between. Tenth, ignoring customer feedback. If customers complain about smell or texture, adjust your process. Fix: Regularly review your methods and train helpers. Avoiding these mistakes will improve your product and profits..Trim()

Module 11 · Expenses at Every Stage

Introduction and Why It Matters

When you sell broilers daily from a batch, every stage of the bird's life costs money. If you don't track expenses, you might think you're making profit when you're actually losing. For example, a farmer in Ghana buys 100 day-old chicks at 5 cedis each = 500 cedis. Feed for 6 weeks costs about 1,200 cedis. Vaccines and medication: 150 cedis. Bedding and heating: 200 cedis. Total expenses before selling: 2,050 cedis. If you sell all birds at 30 cedis each, you get 3,000 cedis. Your profit is 950 cedis. But if you forgot to count the 200 cedis for electricity and water, your real profit is only 750 cedis. That's a 21% drop! So tracking expenses at every stage tells you the true cost per bird. It helps you set the right selling price. It shows where you can cut costs without harming the birds. For daily sales, you need to know your break-even point: how many birds you must sell each day to cover costs. Suppose fixed costs (rent, labour) are 300 cedis per week. Variable cost per bird is 20.5 cedis (2,050 total / 100 birds). If you sell a bird at 30 cedis, your contribution per bird is 9.5 cedis.
