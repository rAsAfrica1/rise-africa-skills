Introduction and Why It Matters

Metal casting is one of the oldest and most useful trades in the world. In Africa, foundries have been shaping metal for centuries�??from the bronze casters of Benin to the iron smelters of the Haya people in Tanzania. Today, the demand for cast metal parts is everywhere: water pumps, grinding mills, car parts, cooking stoves, farm tools, and construction fittings. If you can melt metal and pour it into a mould, you can make money solving real problems in your community..Trim()

Starting a foundry does not require a huge factory. Many successful African foundries began in a backyard with a simple charcoal furnace, a few crucibles, and locally sourced scrap metal. The key is to learn the basics: how to build a furnace that reaches the right temperature, how to prepare sand moulds that don't crack, and how to pour safely. You also need to understand the business side: what parts are in demand, how to price your work, and how to find customers..Trim()

The market for quality cast products in Africa is growing, and with the right skills, you can be part of it..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To start a small foundry, you need a few essential tools and materials. First, the furnace: you can build a simple charcoal or coke furnace using a 200-litre steel drum lined with refractory clay (a mix of clay, sand, and water). For melting aluminium, a charcoal furnace works well; for iron, you need coke and a forced air supply from a blower or vacuum cleaner. Next, crucibles: clay-graphite crucibles are best for holding molten metal. A #6 or #8 crucible can handle 3-6 kg of aluminium. You'll also need tongs to lift the hot crucible, a skimmer to remove slag, and a pouring shank or ring..Trim()

.Trim()

For moulding, you need casting sand. Good sand is fine, slightly damp, and holds shape when squeezed. You can mix silica sand with bentonite clay (about 5-8% by weight) and water. A simple moulding box (flask) made of wood or metal, with a bottom board and a top board, is essential. You'll also need a rammer to pack the sand, a strike-off bar to level it, and a sprue pin to make the pouring hole. For patterns (the shape you want to cast), you can use wood, metal, or even existing parts. A simple pattern like a gear or a pulley can be made from hardwood..Trim()

.Trim()

Other tools: a wire brush for cleaning castings, a hacksaw for cutting gates, and a file for finishing. For safety, you need leather gloves, a face shield, a leather apron, and boots. Never pour metal without eye and hand protection. Materials you'll need regularly: scrap metal (aluminium from old pots, car parts; iron from old engine blocks), charcoal or coke, and sand. You can source scrap from local mechanics, junkyards, or households. Keep a stock of sand and clay. With these tools and materials, you're ready to set up a basic foundry. Start small, and upgrade as you earn..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Casting a metal part follows a clear sequence. First, make your pattern. This is a replica of the part you want, slightly larger to allow for shrinkage (about 1% for aluminium, 2% for iron). You can carve it from wood or use an existing part. Next, prepare the mould. Place the pattern in the drag (bottom half of the flask) and fill with sand, ramming it firmly. Flip the drag, place the cope (top half) and the sprue pin, then ram sand in the cope. Remove the sprue pin and pattern, creating a cavity. Cut a pouring basin and vents. Now you have a mould ready for metal..Trim()

.Trim()

Melt your metal. Light the furnace and add charcoal or coke. Once hot, place the crucible with scrap metal inside. Use a blower to increase heat. For aluminium, you need about 660°C; for iron, 1200-1400°C. Watch the metal until it's fully liquid and shiny. Remove slag with a skimmer. Then, pour the molten metal into the mould through the sprue. Pour steadily and continuously�??don't stop halfway. Fill the mould completely; you'll see metal rise in the vents. Let it cool. Small parts cool in 10-15 minutes; larger ones may take an hour..Trim()

.Trim()

After cooling, break the sand mould and remove the casting. Cut off the sprue and gates with a hacksaw. Grind or file the rough edges. Inspect for defects like holes or cracks. If defective, you can remelt and try again. Finally, finish the part: sand it, paint it, or machine it as needed. Practice this process with simple shapes like a flat bar or a ring. Each step builds skill. Remember, the quality of your mould determines the quality of your casting. Take time to ram sand properly and ensure the pattern is clean. With practice, you'll cast complex parts efficiently..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced foundry workers make mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them. First, mould collapse: if the sand is too dry or not rammed hard enough, the mould walls can fall in when you pour. Fix: mix sand with the right amount of water (it should hold shape when squeezed) and ram it firmly in layers. Second, misrun: when metal cools too quickly and doesn't fill the mould. This happens if the metal isn't hot enough or the mould is too cold. Fix: heat metal until it's fully liquid and preheat the mould slightly (by pouring a little metal and letting it cool, or using a torch). Third, porosity: small holes in the casting due to gas trapped in the metal. This can come from wet sand, dirty scrap, or too much slag. Fix: use dry sand, clean scrap, and skim slag before pouring. Also, add vents to let gas escape..Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, shrinkage: a sunken area where metal contracts as it cools. Fix: add a riser (a reservoir of molten metal) that feeds the casting as it shrinks. Fifth, rough surface: if the sand is too coarse or the pattern is rough, the casting will be rough. Fix: use finer sand and smooth your pattern. Sixth, cold shuts: when two streams of metal meet but don't fuse. This happens if the metal is too cold or pouring is too slow. Fix: pour quickly and continuously with hot metal. Seventh, sand inclusion: sand particles stuck in the casting. Fix: ensure the mould is clean and the sprue is properly designed to avoid turbulence..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, safety mistakes: not wearing protective gear, pouring on wet ground (causes steam explosion), or using wet tools. Always wear leather gloves, face shield, and apron. Pour on dry sand or concrete. Keep a bucket of dry sand to smother fires. If you make a mistake, don't give up. Remelt the metal and try again. Keep a notebook to record what went wrong and what you changed. That's how you improve..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Foundry work is dangerous if you ignore safety. Molten metal can cause severe burns, and fumes can harm your lungs. Always wear proper protective equipment: a face shield (not just goggles), leather gloves, a leather apron, and steel-toed boots. Never wear synthetic clothing�??it melts and sticks to skin. Work in a well-ventilated area, preferably outdoors or with a chimney to draw fumes away. When melting scrap, avoid painted, galvanised, or oily metal�??these release toxic fumes. Use only clean scrap..Trim()

.Trim()

Before you start, check your equipment. Make sure the crucible is not cracked. Preheat it gradually to avoid thermal shock. Keep the furnace on a stable, non-flammable surface like concrete or bare earth. Clear the area of anything flammable. Have a fire extinguisher or a bucket of dry sand nearby. When pouring, stand to the side, not directly in front of the mould. Pour steadily and avoid splashing. Never pour on wet ground�??steam can explode and throw molten metal. After pouring, let the mould cool completely before opening. Use tongs and gloves to handle hot castings..Trim()

.Trim()

Best practices: work with a partner when possible. Have a first-aid kit ready for burns. Keep water away from molten metal�??a drop of water can cause an explosion. Store sand and clay in a dry place. Clean your tools after each use. Maintain your furnace: replace lining when worn. Learn to read the metal: when it's ready to pour, it looks like liquid silver and moves easily. Don't rush. Take breaks to avoid fatigue. Remember, safety is not just for you�??it's for everyone around you. A safe foundry is a productive foundry..Trim()

Practical Exercise

Now it's time to put your knowledge into practice. For this exercise, you will cast a simple aluminium keychain or a small flat bar. You will need: a small charcoal furnace (or a metal bucket with holes and a blower), a #4 crucible, aluminium scrap (old pots, cans), casting sand, a small wooden pattern (a flat rectangle 5 cm x 2 cm x 1 cm), a two-part flask (or two wooden boxes), and safety gear..Trim()

Evaluate your work: Does it have a smooth surface? Are there holes or rough spots? If yes, note what you might change next time (e.g., ram sand harder, use hotter metal). Repeat the exercise to improve. Once you can cast a simple shape reliably, try a more complex pattern like a small gear or a pulley. This exercise is the foundation of your foundry business. With each casting, you build skill and confidence. Soon, you can start selling your products. Keep a record of your pours, including metal temperature, sand mix, and results. That data will help you perfect your craft..Trim()

Module 2 · Furnace Construction

Introduction and Why It Matters

A furnace is the heart of any foundry. Without a reliable furnace, you cannot melt metal, and without melted metal, you cannot cast anything. In many African workshops, imported furnaces are expensive and hard to repair. Building your own furnace from locally available materials saves money, creates jobs, and gives you control over the melting process. This module teaches you how to construct a simple, effective furnace that burns charcoal, coke, or even waste oil. You will learn to build a furnace that reaches temperatures above 1200°C�??hot enough to melt aluminium, brass, and bronze. With the right design, you can also melt cast iron at around 1400°C..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does this matter? Across Africa, metal casting supports agriculture, construction, and transport. Farmers need plough parts, water pumps, and grinding mills. Builders need window frames, door hinges, and roofing sheets. Transporters need engine parts and brake drums. Most of these items are imported. By building your own furnace, you can produce them locally, create income, and reduce dependence on imports. A well-built furnace can last for years if maintained properly..Trim()

.Trim()

The furnace you will build in this module is a simple shaft furnace, also called a cupola or a crucible furnace. It uses a steel drum or clay-lined pit as the body, a firebrick or clay lining to hold heat, a tuyere (air pipe) to blow air into the fire, and a grate to hold the fuel. You will use a hand-cranked blower, a vacuum cleaner motor, or a small electric fan to supply air. The fuel can be charcoal, coke, or even dry wood, but charcoal and coke give the best heat..Trim()

.Trim()

Before you start, understand that furnace building is not just about stacking bricks. You must control air flow, fuel size, and insulation. Too much air cools the furnace; too little air starves the fire. Wet fuel creates smoke and lowers temperature. A good furnace builder checks every detail. In the next lessons, you will learn the tools, materials, step-by-step method, common mistakes, safety rules, and finally build your own furnace in a practical exercise. By the end, you will have a working furnace and the skills to melt metal safely..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To build a furnace, you need basic tools and materials that are available in most African towns. You do not need expensive equipment. Start with a steel drum (200-litre oil drum) or a large metal bucket. If you cannot find a drum, you can dig a pit and line it with clay. You will also need firebricks or clay soil. Firebricks are best because they withstand high heat, but if they are not available, use termite mound clay or river clay mixed with sand. Avoid ordinary cement bricks�??they crack at high temperatures..Trim()

.Trim()

For the lining, you need a refractory mixture. A good recipe is 3 parts clay, 1 part sand, and 1 part wood ash or sawdust. The ash makes the lining porous and resistant to cracking. Mix with water until it is like thick mud. You will also need a tuyere�??a metal pipe (about 2-3 cm diameter) that carries air into the furnace. A car exhaust pipe or galvanised pipe works well. For the air supply, use a hand-cranked blower from a blacksmith, a vacuum cleaner motor, or a 12V car heater fan. You will need a grate made from steel bars or a heavy metal mesh to hold the fuel and let ash fall through..Trim()

.Trim()

Other tools: a shovel for mixing clay, a bucket for water, a trowel or wooden float for shaping the lining, a hacksaw or angle grinder for cutting the drum and pipe, a measuring tape, and a marker. For safety, you need leather gloves, safety goggles, a face mask (for dust), and a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand. A wheelbarrow helps move materials. If you are using a steel drum, cut a hole near the bottom for the tuyere and a larger opening at the top for loading fuel and a crucible..Trim()

.Trim()

Gather everything before you start. Check that the drum is clean and free of oil or paint�??burn it out first if necessary. Test your air supply: it should blow a steady stream of air. Make sure the clay is free of stones and roots. If you use firebricks, soak them in water before laying them so they bond well with the clay. A good furnace builder organises tools and materials in one place. This saves time and prevents accidents. In the next lesson, you will use these tools to build the furnace step by step..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Step 1: Prepare the drum. If using a 200-litre steel drum, cut off the top lid with a hacksaw or angle grinder. Cut a 5 cm hole near the bottom for the tuyere. Also cut a 15 cm diameter hole in the side about 20 cm from the bottom�??this is the tap hole for removing slag and metal. Clean the inside with a wire brush. Burn out any paint or oil by building a small fire inside..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 2: Make the refractory lining. Mix 3 parts clay, 1 part sand, and 1 part wood ash. Add water slowly and mix with your feet or a shovel until it is thick and sticky. Let it rest for 30 minutes. Then apply a 5-8 cm thick layer inside the drum, starting from the bottom. Shape the bottom into a slight bowl so molten metal collects at the tap hole. Leave the tuyere hole open. Smooth the lining with wet hands. Allow it to dry for 2-3 days in the shade. If it cracks, fill the cracks with more clay mixture..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 3: Install the tuyere and grate. Insert a 2-3 cm diameter metal pipe through the tuyere hole, angled slightly downward. The pipe should reach about 10 cm inside the furnace. Seal around it with clay. Place a steel grate at the bottom, about 10 cm above the floor, to hold fuel and let ash fall. The grate can be made from 1 cm steel bars spaced 2 cm apart..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 4: Connect the air supply. Attach your blower or fan to the outer end of the tuyere with a hose or metal pipe. If using a vacuum cleaner motor, mount it on a wooden stand and connect with a flexible pipe. Test the air flow�??it should be strong but not so strong that it blows ash out of the top..Trim()

.Trim()

Step 5: Cure the furnace. Build a small fire inside with charcoal and let it burn slowly for 2 hours. Then increase the fire gradually over another 2 hours. This drives out moisture and hardens the lining. Do not rush�??fast heating causes cracks. After curing, your furnace is ready. Load charcoal or coke, light it, turn on the air, and in 15-20 minutes you can melt aluminium. For cast iron, use coke and preheat for 30 minutes. Always keep a lid or cover for the top to conserve heat..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Using ordinary cement or river sand without clay. Cement cracks at high temperatures and can explode. Fix: Use firebricks or a clay-sand-ash mixture. If you must use cement, mix it with fire clay (available at brick factories) and cure slowly..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 2: Too much air blast. Beginners often use a powerful fan that blows heat out of the furnace. The fire roars but the metal does not melt. Fix: Use a damper or a smaller fan. Aim for a gentle, steady blast. You can test by holding your hand near the tuyere�??it should feel like a strong breeze, not a hurricane..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 3: Wet fuel. Charcoal or coke stored in the rain absorbs moisture. This lowers the temperature and creates steam. Fix: Store fuel under cover. Dry it in the sun for a day before use. If you hear hissing, the fuel is wet..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 4: Cracks in the lining. If the lining dries too fast or is too thin, it cracks. Fix: Dry slowly in shade, not in direct sun. Make the lining at least 5 cm thick. Repair cracks with fresh clay mixture and re-cure..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 5: No tap hole or blocked tap hole. Molten metal and slag need to flow out. If the tap hole is too small or clogged, you cannot pour. Fix: Make the tap hole 15 cm wide and keep it clear with a metal rod. Angle it downward so metal flows easily..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 6: Poor grate design. If the grate is too high, fuel does not sit in the hottest zone. If too low, ash blocks air. Fix: Place the grate 10 cm above the bottom. Use bars spaced 2 cm apart. Clean ash after every melt..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 7: Not preheating the furnace. Cold furnace walls absorb heat. Fix: Burn a small fire for 10-15 minutes before loading the main fuel. This also drives out moisture..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 8: Using a crucible that is too small or too thin. The crucible must hold enough metal and withstand heat. Fix: Use a clay-graphite crucible or make one from fire clay. Preheat it before use. Always use tongs to handle hot crucibles..Trim()

Safety and Best Practice

Furnace work is dangerous. Molten metal can burn you, fires can spread, and fumes can poison you. Follow these safety rules every time. First, wear the right gear: leather gloves, safety goggles, a face mask, long trousers, and closed leather boots. Never wear synthetic clothes�??they melt and stick to skin. Tie back long hair. Remove jewellery..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, work in a well-ventilated area. Carbon monoxide from charcoal and coke is deadly. Build your furnace outdoors or in a shed with open sides. Never work in a closed room. If you feel dizzy or sleepy, move to fresh air immediately. Keep a bucket of water and a fire extinguisher nearby. For metal fires, use dry sand or a CO2 extinguisher�??never water on molten metal..Trim()

.Trim()

Third, handle fuel and fire carefully. Store charcoal and coke away from the furnace. Use a metal shovel to add fuel. Never use your hands. When lighting the furnace, use a long match or a gas torch. Do not pour petrol or kerosene on the fire�??it can explode. Keep a safe distance when the furnace is running. The outside of the furnace gets very hot. Mark a 2-metre safety zone with stones or tape..Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, prepare for spills. Place a sand tray under the tap hole to catch molten metal. Wear a leather apron. Use long-handled tongs to move the crucible. Never look directly into the furnace without goggles�??the infrared light can damage your eyes. When pouring, stand to the side, not in front of the crucible. Pour slowly and steadily..Trim()

.Trim()

Fifth, maintain your furnace. Check the lining before every use. Repair cracks. Clean the grate and ash pit. Check the air pipe for blockages. A well-maintained furnace is a safe furnace. Teach everyone in your workshop these rules. Keep a first aid kit with burn cream and bandages. If someone gets burned, cool the area with clean water for 10 minutes and seek medical help. Never ignore a burn. Finally, work with a partner. Never melt metal alone. A partner can help in an emergency and watch for danger..Trim()

Module 5 · Pattern Making

Introduction and Why It Matters

Pattern making is the first step in metal casting. A pattern is a replica of the part you want to cast, but slightly larger to allow for shrinkage and machining. Without a good pattern, you cannot make a good mold, and without a good mold, you cannot cast a good part. In Africa, where recycling scrap metal is common, a well-made pattern lets you turn old engine blocks and broken tools into new products like cooking pots, plough parts, or machine components. This module teaches you how to make patterns using local materials and simple tools..Trim()

.Trim()

Patterns are usually made from wood, metal, or plastic. In many African foundries, hardwood like mahogany or iroko is used because it resists warping and is easy to shape. You will learn to select the right wood, cut it to size, and finish it so it pulls cleanly from the sand. The pattern must be strong enough to withstand repeated ramming of sand. It also needs a smooth surface to leave a good impression..Trim()

.Trim()

A pattern must include allowances: shrinkage allowance (because metal shrinks when it cools), draft allowance (taper so it can be removed from the sand), and machining allowance (extra material for finishing). For example, if you cast aluminium, shrinkage is about 1.3%, so a 100 mm pattern becomes 101.3 mm. You will learn to calculate and apply these allowances..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does this matter? Because a poorly made pattern wastes time, metal, and fuel. In a small foundry in Nigeria or Kenya, every failed casting costs money. By mastering pattern making, you reduce defects like misruns, sand inclusions, and dimensional errors. You also gain the skill to make patterns for any part you need, from a simple pot to a complex gear. This module gives you the foundation to become a skilled pattern maker, a trade that is in demand across Africa's growing manufacturing sector..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To make patterns, you need a set of basic tools and the right materials. Most tools can be bought locally or made by a blacksmith. You will need: a try square, steel rule, marking gauge, mortise gauge, tenon saw, coping saw, wood chisels (6 mm, 12 mm, 25 mm), mallet, smoothing plane, spokeshave, rasps and files, sandpaper (80, 120, 220 grit), and a bench vice. For measuring, a vernier caliper is useful but not essential. You also need a workbench and a set of clamps. In many African workshops, these tools are shared, so learn to keep them sharp and oiled to prevent rust..Trim()

.Trim()

For materials, hardwood is the best choice. Mahogany, iroko, teak, and camphor are common in West and Central Africa. They are dense, stable, and take a smooth finish. Softwoods like pine are easier to work but wear out quickly. You can also use metal patterns (aluminium or cast iron) for long production runs, but they are harder to make without a lathe. For small patterns, you can use plastic resin or even hard wax. In some areas, you can find pattern makers who use compressed sawdust mixed with glue, but this is not as durable..Trim()

.Trim()

You also need finishing materials: shellac, varnish, or paint. These seal the wood so it does not absorb moisture from the sand. A sealed pattern releases better and lasts longer. For draft, you will file or plane a taper of 1 to 3 degrees on vertical faces. For fillets (rounded corners), you can use leather, wood filler, or wax. Remember, the pattern must be slightly larger than the final part to allow for shrinkage. For aluminium, add 1.3% to all dimensions; for cast iron, add 1%; for brass, add 1.5%. Use a calculator or a shrinkage rule (a special ruler that already accounts for shrinkage)..Trim()

.Trim()

Safety is important: wear eye protection when chiseling or sanding, and use a dust mask when sanding hardwood. Keep your tools sharp; a dull tool is more dangerous than a sharp one. Store patterns in a dry place to prevent warping. With these tools and materials, you are ready to start making your first pattern..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to make a simple pattern, such as a flat plate with a boss (a raised circular pad). First, study the drawing of the part. Note all dimensions, then add shrinkage allowance. For aluminium, multiply each dimension by 1.013. For example, a 200 mm length becomes 202.6 mm. Also add machining allowance if the part will be machined: typically 2-3 mm on surfaces that will be cut. Write down the pattern dimensions..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, select a piece of hardwood that is straight-grained and free of knots. Cut it slightly larger than the pattern dimensions using a tenon saw. Plane one face flat and true, then use a try square to mark the edges. Plane the edges square to the face. This is your reference surface. Next, mark the outline of the pattern on the wood using a marking gauge and rule. For the boss, mark a circle with a pair of dividers or a compass. Cut away waste wood with a coping saw, staying about 1 mm away from the line. Then use chisels and rasps to shape to the line. For the boss, you can turn it on a lathe if available, or shape it with a spokeshave and file..Trim()

.Trim()

Third, apply draft. On all vertical faces, file or plane a taper of 1 to 3 degrees. You can make a simple draft gauge by cutting a block at the desired angle. Check with a bevel protractor. For the boss, taper its sides too. Next, round all internal corners with fillets. You can glue in a piece of leather or use wood filler shaped with a file. Fillets prevent sand from breaking off and causing defects..Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, sand the pattern smooth, starting with 80 grit and finishing with 220 grit. Remove all scratches. Then seal the pattern with two coats of shellac or varnish, sanding lightly between coats. This protects the wood from moisture and makes it release easily from the sand. Finally, mark the pattern with its part number and the shrinkage allowance used. Store it in a dry place. Now you can use it to make a mold. Remember to check the pattern for accuracy before casting; a small error in the pattern becomes a big error in the casting..Trim()

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced pattern makers make mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them. First, forgetting shrinkage allowance. If you make a pattern exactly to the final dimensions, the casting will be too small. Fix: always add the correct shrinkage allowance. For aluminium, add 1.3%; for cast iron, 1%; for brass, 1.5%. Use a shrinkage rule or calculate. If you already made the pattern, you can sometimes use it for a smaller part, but often you must start again..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, insufficient draft. Without draft, the pattern sticks in the sand, and you damage the mold when removing it. Fix: file or plane a taper of at least 1 degree on all vertical faces. If you already made the pattern, you can carefully file the draft, but be careful not to change dimensions too much. For future patterns, always apply draft before finishing..Trim()

.Trim()

Third, rough surface. A rough pattern leaves a rough casting and may cause sand to stick. Fix: sand progressively from 80 to 220 grit, then seal with shellac or varnish. If the pattern is already sealed and rough, sand lightly and reseal..Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, warped pattern. Wood can warp if not dried properly or stored in damp conditions. Fix: use well-seasoned hardwood, seal all surfaces, and store flat in a dry place. If warped, you may need to make a new pattern..Trim()

.Trim()

Fifth, incorrect fillets. Sharp internal corners can cause cracks in the casting. Fix: add fillets using leather, wax, or wood filler. Shape them smoothly. If you forgot, you can add fillets after the pattern is made, but it may be tricky..Trim()

.Trim()

Sixth, no core prints. If your casting needs a hole, you need core prints on the pattern to support the core. Fix: add core prints (extra projections) at the ends of the hole. They should be slightly larger than the core. If you forgot, you can sometimes drill and insert dowels, but it's better to plan ahead..Trim()

.Trim()

Seventh, using softwood. Softwood patterns wear out quickly and can break during ramming. Fix: use hardwood or metal for production runs. If you must use softwood, reinforce with metal edges or use it for only a few castings. By avoiding these mistakes, you save time and material..Trim()

Pattern making involves sharp tools and wood dust, so safety is critical. Always wear safety glasses when chiseling, sawing, or sanding. Wood chips can fly into your eyes. Wear a dust mask when sanding, especially with hardwoods like iroko, which can cause allergic reactions. Use ear protection when using power tools, though most pattern making in Africa is done by hand. Keep your workbench clean and free of clutter. Clamp your work securely before cutting or chiseling. Never cut towards your body; always cut away from yourself. Keep chisels and planes sharp; a dull tool requires more force and can slip..Trim()

.Trim()

Best practices start with planning. Study the drawing carefully and calculate all allowances before cutting wood. Use a pencil and rule to mark clearly. Work to a tolerance of ±0.5 mm for most patterns. Check dimensions with a caliper or rule at each stage. Make a full-size drawing on paper or plywood to use as a template. This helps you avoid errors. For complex patterns, make a prototype from cheap wood or cardboard first..Trim()

.Trim()

Choose the right material for the job. For one-off castings, hardwood is fine. For hundreds of castings, consider making a metal pattern. Always seal patterns to protect against moisture. Store patterns in a dry, ventilated area, preferably on shelves, not on the floor. Label each pattern with its part number and shrinkage allowance. Keep a record book of patterns you have made, noting dimensions and any special features..Trim()

.Trim()

In the workshop, maintain a clean and organised space. Put tools back after use. Oil metal tools to prevent rust. Sweep sawdust regularly; it is a fire hazard. When using shellac or varnish, work in a well-ventilated area and avoid flames. Dispose of oily rags safely. Finally, learn from experienced pattern makers in your community. In many African cities, there are master craftsmen who can teach you tricks. Observe their methods and ask questions. By following these safety and best practices, you will produce high-quality patterns and avoid accidents..Trim()

Module 6 · Sand Casting

Introduction and Why It Matters

Sand casting is the oldest and most widely used metal casting method in the world. In Africa, it is the backbone of small-scale foundries from Nigeria to South Africa. The process involves pouring molten metal into a cavity shaped in sand, letting it cool, and breaking the sand away to reveal a solid metal part. It works for aluminium, bronze, brass, and cast iron. Why does it matter for you? Because it is cheap, flexible, and uses materials you can find almost anywhere. You can cast pots, machine parts, ploughshares, door hinges, and even engine components. A basic sand casting setup can be built for under $200 using a metal bucket as a furnace, charcoal or coke as fuel, and locally dug clay sand. Unlike welding or machining, sand casting lets you produce complex shapes in one pour. It also allows you to recycle scrap metal, turning old car parts and broken tools into new products. In many African communities, this skill creates immediate income: a single cast aluminium cooking pot sells for $5�??$15, and a dozen can be made in a day. The key advantage is low capital. You do not need electricity or expensive machines. You need sand, a pattern, a crucible, and heat. This module will teach you every step, from preparing sand to finishing your casting. By the end, you will be able to set up a small foundry in your backyard and start producing items for sale or personal use. The demand for locally made metal goods is high, especially in rural areas where imported items are expensive. Sand casting is not just a trade; it is a pathway to self-employment and community development. Let�??s get started..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To start sand casting, you need a few essential tools and materials. First, the flask: a two-part frame (cope and drag) made of wood or metal, typically 30 cm x 40 cm. You can make one from scrap wood. Second, a pattern: a replica of the part you want to cast, usually made of wood, metal, or plastic. It must be slightly larger than the final part to allow for shrinkage (about 1% for aluminium, 2% for brass). Third, sand: you need green sand, a mixture of silica sand (70�??85%), bentonite clay (10�??15%), and water (3�??5%). You can mix it yourself: for a 20-litre bucket, use 14 litres of fine river sand, 3 litres of bentonite clay powder, and 1.5 litres of water. Sixth, tools: a rammer (wooden or metal), a strike-off bar, a sprue pin, a vent wire, a trowel, and a mallet. For safety, you need leather gloves, a face shield, and a leather apron. Step by Step Method

Step 3: Flip the drag. Turn the drag over, place the cope (top half) on it. Insert a sprue pin (a tapered pin) at one end to form the pouring hole. Step 4: Fill the cope. Sprinkle facing sand, then fill with green sand and ram. Remove the sprue pin. Step 5: Vent the mould. Use a vent wire to poke small holes through the sand to the pattern to let gases escape. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced casters make mistakes. Here are the most common and how to fix them. 1. Sand too dry or too wet: If sand crumbles, add water (a little at a time). If it sticks to your hand, add dry sand. Test by squeezing; it should hold shape without leaving wet marks. 2. Weak mould: If the mould collapses when you pour, you did not ram enough. Sieve sand finer and apply a thin layer of facing sand against the pattern. 4. Misrun (incomplete casting): Metal cools too fast before filling the mould. Fix: preheat the mould slightly, pour faster, or increase metal temperature. Also, make sure the sprue and runner are wide enough. 5. Shrinkage cavities: Metal shrinks as it cools, leaving holes. Fix: add a riser (a reservoir of molten metal) above the mould to feed shrinkage. For aluminium, allow 1% shrinkage in pattern size. 6. Gas porosity (bubbles): Trapped gases cause holes. Fix: vent the mould with a vent wire, and make sure sand is not too wet. Also, melt metal properly and skim slag. 7. Sand inclusions: Loose sand falls into the mould. Fix: clean the mould cavity with a soft brush before closing, and handle the cope carefully. 8. Poor parting line: The two halves of the mould don't align. Fix: use alignment pins on the flask, and check before pouring. 9. Cold shuts: Two streams of metal meet but don't fuse. Fix: pour continuously and quickly, and increase metal temperature. 10. Pattern stuck: If the pattern won't come out, you didn't apply enough parting dust. Use talcum powder or oil. Always practice on scrap before casting a valuable part. Wear protective gear: Leather gloves, a face shield (not just safety glasses), a leather apron, and closed leather boots. Never wear synthetic clothing; it melts. 2. Work in a well-ventilated area: Melting metal releases toxic fumes, especially from painted or oily scrap. Set up your furnace outdoors or in a shed with open doors. 3. Keep water away: Molten metal explodes when it touches water. Dry all tools, crucibles, and moulds before pouring. Never pour on wet ground. 4. Handle crucible carefully: Use tongs designed for crucibles. Preheat the crucible before use to remove moisture. Do not overfill; leave 2 cm of space. 5. Pour steadily: Stand to the side, not directly in front of the mould. Pour in one continuous motion. If metal splashes, step back. 6. First aid: For burns, cool with clean water for 10 minutes, cover with a sterile cloth, and seek medical help. 9. Best practices: Keep your work area clean and organized. Store sand covered. Permanent mould casting is a method where molten metal is poured into a reusable metal mould, usually made of cast iron or steel. Unlike sand casting, where the mould is destroyed after each pour, a permanent mould can be used hundreds or even thousands of times. .Trim()

The process is best for metals with lower melting points, such as aluminium, zinc, and magnesium. The mould is typically made in two halves that open and close, with a pouring basin and runners cut into the joint. Gravity or low pressure fills the cavity. Module 8 · Investment Casting

Introduction and Why It Matters

Investment casting, also called lost-wax casting, is one of the oldest metal forming techniques. It lets you make complex parts with fine details and smooth surfaces that are hard to achieve with sand casting. In Africa, this method is used by artisans in places like Benin, Ghana, and Nigeria to create intricate brass and bronze items. For a small workshop, investment casting can open doors to making jewelry, machine parts, and decorative pieces that sell for higher prices..Trim()

.Trim()

The process works by creating a wax pattern of the part you want. You coat the wax with a ceramic shell, then heat it so the wax melts out, leaving a hollow mold. Molten metal is poured into that mold. Once cooled, the ceramic shell is broken away, and you have your metal part. The key advantage is accuracy: you can cast thin walls, sharp corners, and detailed textures without much machining..Trim()

.Trim()

Why does it matter for African businesses? Many local markets demand unique, high-quality metal goods. Tourists and export buyers look for handmade brass jewelry, door handles, and art pieces. Investment casting allows you to produce these in small batches with minimal equipment. You can start with a simple kiln, a wax melter, and some plaster. Unlike sand casting, you don't need heavy molding boxes or lots of sand..Trim()

.Trim()

But it's not without challenges. The process takes more time per piece, and materials like wax and ceramic slurry cost more than sand. You need patience and precision. However, the higher quality and detail can justify higher prices. For a learner with no formal training, this module will guide you step by step. You'll learn to make a wax pattern, build a shell, burn out the wax, and pour metal safely. With practice, you can create products that stand out in your local market and beyond..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To start investment casting, you need a few basic tools and materials. Most can be found locally or ordered from foundry suppliers. Don't be intimidated; you can improvise many items..Trim()

.Trim()

First, for making wax patterns: you need wax. Paraffin wax or beeswax works, but dedicated casting wax is better. You can melt wax in a small pot over a stove or a dedicated wax melter. For shaping, you need sculpting tools, knives, and a smooth surface like a glass plate. If you're making multiple identical patterns, you'll need a mold (e.g., silicone or metal) to pour wax into..Trim()

.Trim()

A small electric kiln or a charcoal-fired furnace can work..Trim()

.Trim()

For melting and pouring metal: you need a crucible, tongs, and safety gear (face shield, gloves, apron). A propane furnace or a simple pit furnace with a blower can melt brass, bronze, or aluminum. You'll also need a pouring basin and a mold support..Trim()

.Trim()

Other useful items: a scale for measuring, a timer, a thermometer (for wax and kiln), and a vacuum pump or vibration table to remove air bubbles from the slurry. Here is a practical step-by-step method for investment casting a small part, like a brass pendant. Follow these steps carefully..Trim()

Module 9 · Finishing and Machining

Introduction and Why It Matters

After you pour molten metal into a mould and it cools, what you pull out is a rough casting. It has sand stuck to it, rough edges, maybe some extra metal where the gates were, and dimensions that are close but not exact. Finishing and machining turn that rough casting into a usable product that fits, works, and lasts. In Africa, where many metal parts are made in small workshops, this step is often the difference between a part that works and one that fails. For example, a pump housing cast in a local foundry must have its bore machined to within 0.1 mm so the bearing fits tightly. If not, the pump will vibrate and leak. Similarly, a grinding mill hammer needs a smooth finish to avoid cracking. Finishing includes cleaning, grinding, sanding, and sometimes polishing. Machining involves cutting metal with lathes, milling machines, or drills to achieve precise dimensions and smooth surfaces. In many African countries, replacement parts for tractors, water pumps, and food processing equipment are expensive or unavailable. Local foundries can cast these parts, but they must be finished properly to work. This module teaches you how to take a raw casting and turn it into a finished part using hand tools, power tools, and basic machine tools. You will learn to remove sand, cut off gates and risers, grind smooth, drill holes, and even turn on a lathe. The skills are practical and can be done in a small workshop with limited electricity. By the end, you will be able to take a casting from your foundry and make it ready for use. This matters because it adds value to your product, reduces waste, and creates opportunities to sell to industries that need reliable parts. Whether you are making spare parts for a village or supplying a factory, finishing and machining are essential steps to get paid..Trim()

Tools and Materials

To finish and machine castings, you need a set of basic tools and materials. Start with safety gear: leather gloves, safety glasses, a dust mask (for sand and metal dust), and ear protection when using power tools. For cleaning sand off castings, you need a wire brush (hand or wheel), a chipping hammer, and a file. A stiff bristle brush helps for loose sand. For cutting off gates and risers, you can use a hacksaw with a 24-tooth blade for small parts, or an angle grinder with a cutting disc for larger ones. A cold chisel and hammer also work for brittle cast iron. For grinding and smoothing, an angle grinder with a grinding disc (for rough work) and a flap disc (for finishing) is essential. If you have electricity, a bench grinder is useful for sharpening tools and deburring. For machining, a basic lathe is the most versatile tool. A small 1-meter bed lathe with a 3-jaw chuck can turn diameters up to 200 mm. You also need a drill press or hand drill with HSS drill bits (high-speed steel) for drilling holes. For measuring, a vernier caliper (0-150 mm) and a micrometer (0-25 mm) are critical. A steel rule and try square help with layout. For materials, you need cutting oil (or used engine oil) for drilling and turning, emery cloth in grits 80, 120, 220 for polishing, and possibly a surface plate and marking blue for precision layout. In Africa, you can find many of these tools in local markets or from hardware suppliers. If you cannot afford a lathe, you can often rent time at a local workshop or use a friend's machine. Start with hand tools and add power tools as your work grows. Always keep tools sharp and clean; a dull file or grinder disc wastes time and gives poor results..Trim()

Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to finish and machine a typical casting, such as a pulley or a flange. First, remove the sand. Use a wire brush and chipping hammer to knock off loose sand and scale. For stubborn sand, soak the casting in water for an hour, then brush again. Second, cut off gates and risers. Use a hacksaw or angle grinder to cut them close to the casting, leaving about 2 mm for grinding. For cast iron, score a line with a chisel and hit it with a hammer to break off large gates.
