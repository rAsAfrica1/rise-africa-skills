Module 1 · Introduction to Agrochemicals for African Small Businesses

What Are Pesticides and Agrochemicals?

Pesticides and agrochemicals are substances used to protect crops from pests, diseases, and weeds. In simple terms, they are chemicals that help farmers grow more food by killing or controlling things that damage plants. Agrochemicals include pesticides (insecticides, herbicides, fungicides), fertilizers, and plant growth regulators. Pesticides are a subset of agrochemicals specifically designed to kill or repel pests. For example, a tomato farmer in Kenya might use an insecticide to kill whiteflies, a fungicide to stop blight, and a herbicide to clear weeds. Without these tools, a farmer could lose 30-50% of their harvest to pests and diseases. In Africa, common pests include fall armyworm in maize, cassava mosaic virus, and tomato leafminer. Agrochemicals are sold in various forms: liquids, powders, granules, and concentrates that must be mixed with water. They come in small sachets for smallholders or large drums for commercial farms. Understanding labels is crucial: every product has an active ingredient (the chemical that works), a formulation (how it is made), and a concentration (how strong it is). For example, a common insecticide might contain 50% active ingredient and 50% inert ingredients. Safety is paramount: these are poisons and must be handled with care. Always read the label, wear protective gear, and follow dosage instructions. As a small business, you can sell agrochemicals to local farmers, but you must know the products, their uses, and legal requirements. Start by learning the basics: what each product does, how to store it, and how to advise customers. This module will equip you with that knowledge..Trim()

Why African Farmers Need Them: Common Pests and Diseases

African farmers face devastating pests and diseases that can wipe out entire fields. In maize, the fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda) can destroy 20-50% of yield if untreated. It attacks leaves and cobs, and a single female moth can lay 1,000 eggs. Cassava, a staple for over 500 million Africans, is threatened by cassava mosaic virus, spread by whiteflies, causing up to 70% loss. Tomato farmers battle tuta absoluta (tomato leafminer), which can reduce yields by 80-100% in severe infestations. Other common pests include stem borers in maize, aphids in vegetables, and weevils in stored grains. Diseases like tomato blight (Phytophthora infestans) and maize rust (Puccinia sorghi) spread rapidly in humid conditions. Without control, a smallholder might harvest only 1 ton of maize per hectare instead of 3-4 tons. Agrochemicals provide rapid, effective control. For example, applying a recommended insecticide like emamectin benzoate can kill armyworm within 24 hours. Fungicides like mancozeb prevent blight if applied before symptoms spread. Herbicides like glyphosate clear weeds that compete for nutrients. But timing is critical: spraying after pest populations explode is less effective and more costly. Farmers need access to affordable, genuine products and knowledge of when and how to use them. As a small business, you can supply these solutions, but you must also educate farmers on integrated pest management (IPM) to avoid resistance. The demand is high: every season, farmers need reliable agrochemicals. By understanding local pests, you can stock the right products and build trust..Trim()

Types of Agrochemicals: Insecticides, Herbicides, Fungicides

Agrochemicals fall into three main categories: insecticides, herbicides, and fungicides. Insecticides kill insects. They work in different ways: contact insecticides (e.g., pyrethroids) kill on touch; systemic insecticides (e.g., imidacloprid) are absorbed by plants and kill insects that feed on them; stomach poisons (e.g., carbamates) kill after ingestion. Common African brands include Karate (lambda-cyhalothrin) for armyworm and Confidor (imidacloprid) for aphids. Always rotate active ingredients to prevent resistance. Herbicides kill weeds. They are either selective (kill only certain weeds, e.g., atrazine for maize) or non-selective (kill all plants, e.g., glyphosate). Pre-emergence herbicides (e.g., pendimethalin) are applied before weeds sprout; post-emergence (e.g., 2,4-D) after. Timing and dosage are crucial: too little fails, too much harms crops. Fungicides control fungi. Protectant fungicides (e.g., mancozeb) prevent infection; curative fungicides (e.g., metalaxyl) stop existing infections. They are used for blight, rust, and mildew. In tomatoes, a farmer might spray copper oxychloride every 7-10 days during rainy season. Each type comes in formulations: emulsifiable concentrates (EC), wettable powders (WP), suspension concentrates (SC), and granules. ECs mix with water and are easy to apply but can burn leaves if overdosed. WPs require constant agitation. Granules are applied directly to soil. For a small business, stock a range: insecticides for common pests, herbicides for maize and cassava, fungicides for vegetables. Learn the active ingredients and recommend based on pest pressure. Always sell genuine products from reputable manufacturers and provide clear instructions..Trim()

How Agrochemicals Are Used in Maize, Cassava, and Tomato Farming

Maize, cassava, and tomato are three key African crops, each with specific agrochemical needs. In maize, the biggest threat is fall armyworm. Scout fields twice a week. When you see 5-10% of plants with fresh damage, spray an insecticide like emamectin benzoate (e.g., Attack) at 10-15 ml per 20L knapsack. For stem borers, apply granules like carbofuran in the whorl. Weeds compete: apply atrazine (e.g., Atrazine 500 SC) at 3-4 L per hectare pre-emergence, or 2,4-D post-emergence. Fungal diseases like rust are controlled with triazoles (e.g., tebuconazole) if symptoms appear. Cassava is hardy but suffers from mosaic virus and bacterial blight. There is no cure for viral diseases, so control whiteflies (vectors) with imidacloprid (e.g., Confidor) at 5 ml per 20L every 2 weeks. For bacterial blight, copper-based fungicides (e.g., copper hydroxide) help. Weeds are a major issue early on: apply glyphosate before planting, or metribuzin post-emergence. Cassava also needs fertilizer, but that is separate. Tomato is the most demanding. Tuta absoluta requires weekly scouting. Use pheromone traps. Spray with spinosad (e.g., Tracer) or chlorantraniliprole (e.g., Coragen) alternating to avoid resistance. For blight, apply mancozeb (e.g., Dithane) every 7 days in wet weather. For bacterial wilt, use copper. Always observe pre-harvest intervals: for tomato, many insecticides require 7 days before picking. For maize, 14 days. For cassava, 30 days. As a business, you can package advice: sell a 'maize starter kit' with herbicide, insecticide, and fungicide. Train farmers on safe use. This builds loyalty and repeat sales..Trim()

Safety First: Protecting Yourself and Your Workers

Agrochemicals are poisons. Every year, thousands of African farmers and workers suffer pesticide poisoning due to careless handling. Protect yourself and your workers with these practical steps. First, wear protective gear: a long-sleeved shirt, trousers, rubber boots, gloves, goggles, and a face mask. Never spray in shorts or bare feet. A basic kit costs about $20 and can save your life. Second, read the label before opening any container. The label tells you the active ingredient, toxicity level (WHO Class Ia, Ib, II, III), dosage, and safety precautions. Follow it exactly. Third, mix chemicals outdoors or in a well-ventilated area, never near food or water sources. Use clean water and a dedicated measuring cup. Never mix different chemicals unless the label says it is safe. Fourth, spray only when wind is calm (early morning or late evening) to avoid drift. Never spray against the wind. Keep children and animals away. Fifth, after spraying, wash your hands, face, and clothes immediately. Take a shower. Wash protective gear separately. Never eat, drink, or smoke while handling pesticides. Sixth, store chemicals in a locked cabinet, away from food, feed, and children. Keep them in original containers with labels intact. Never store in soda bottles. Seventh, dispose of empty containers safely: triple-rinse, puncture, and bury or burn according to local regulations. Never reuse containers for water or food. Eighth, know the signs of poisoning: headache, dizziness, nausea, sweating, blurred vision. If someone shows these, call a doctor immediately and show the label. As a business, you must train your workers and customers. Provide gloves and masks with every sale. Offer a short training session. This reduces liability and builds trust. Safety is not optional; it is your responsibility..Trim()

Legal Basics: Registering Your Agrochemical Business

Selling agrochemicals is a regulated business in most African countries. You cannot just buy and resell without proper registration. The exact rules vary by country, but here are the general steps. First, register your business with the national companies commission (e.g., CAC in Nigeria, eCitizen in Kenya, CIPC in South Africa). You need a business name, tax ID, and physical address. Second, obtain a pesticide dealer license from the national pesticide regulatory authority. Examples: in Kenya, the Pest Control Products Board (PCPB); in Nigeria, the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) and the Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON); in Ghana, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). You will need to submit an application, pay a fee (often $100-$500), and provide details of your storage facility. Third, ensure your storage meets safety standards: locked, ventilated, away from water sources, with spill containment. An inspector will visit. Fourth, only sell registered products. Every agrochemical must be registered in your country. Selling unregistered or counterfeit products is illegal and dangerous. Keep records of your suppliers and batch numbers. Fifth, label and advise correctly: you must provide a label in the local language and a safety data sheet. Sixth, pay taxes and renew your license annually. Seventh, consider liability insurance. If a farmer claims your product damaged their crop, you could be sued. As a small business, start by visiting your local regulatory office to get a checklist. Join a trade association for support. Many governments offer free training for agro-dealers. Compliance may seem costly, but it protects you from fines, closure, and lawsuits. It also builds customer trust. A registered business can access credit and supply contracts. Do it right from the start..Trim()

Module 2 · Understanding Pests and Diseases in African Crops

Identifying Fall Armyworm in Maize

Fall armyworm (FAW) is a major pest of maize in Africa. The larva is the damaging stage. Look for small holes in young leaves, often in the whorl. As the plant grows, you may see ragged holes and large amounts of moist sawdust-like frass (excrement) around the whorl. The larva has a distinctive inverted Y-shaped mark on its head and four dark spots arranged in a square on the second-to-last abdominal segment. FAW can be confused with other caterpillars like African armyworm or stem borers. To confirm, check the head and tail: FAW has a light-colored inverted Y on the head and four dots on the tail. Scout your field twice a week, especially when maize is 2-6 weeks old. Check 10 plants in 5 different spots. If you find 2 or more larvae per 10 plants, consider action. Handpick larvae when few. Apply recommended biopesticides like Bacillus thuringiensis or neem-based products early. Chemical pesticides like emamectin benzoate or chlorantraniliprole can be used, but rotate to avoid resistance. Always follow label instructions and wear protective gear..Trim()

Recognizing Cassava Mosaic and Brown Streak Viruses

Cassava mosaic disease (CMD) and cassava brown streak disease (CBSD) are the two most destructive viral diseases of cassava in Africa. CMD causes yellow-green mosaic patterns on leaves, leaf distortion, and stunted growth. The virus is spread by whiteflies and through infected cuttings. CBSD causes yellowing along leaf veins, but the real damage is in the roots: brown, corky dry rot that makes tubers inedible. CBSD is also spread by whiteflies and infected cuttings. To identify CMD, look for mosaic patterns, especially on young leaves. For CBSD, pull up a few plants and check roots for brown streaks or rot. There is no cure for infected plants. Management relies on using certified disease-free cuttings, uprooting and burning infected plants, and controlling whiteflies with neem oil or imidacloprid. Plant resistant varieties like TME 419 or TMS 30572. Scout fields every two weeks. If you see symptoms on more than 5% of plants, take action. Always source cuttings from reputable suppliers..Trim()

Tomato Leafminer and Other Common Tomato Pests

Tomato leafminer (Tuta absoluta) is a devastating pest. The adult is a small moth. The larvae mine inside leaves, creating blotchy, irregular mines. They also bore into stems and fruits, causing rot. Scout by checking leaves for mines and fruits for entry holes. Use pheromone traps to monitor adult moths: hang 20 traps per hectare. If you catch more than 20 moths per trap per week, start control. Other common tomato pests include whiteflies (vector of viruses), aphids, and spider mites. Whiteflies cause yellowing and transmit tomato yellow leaf curl virus. Aphids cause leaf curling and secrete honeydew. Spider mites cause stippling and webbing. For control, use neem oil, insecticidal soap, or recommended chemicals like abamectin for mites, imidacloprid for whiteflies. Rotate crops and remove weeds that host pests. Practice integrated pest management: combine cultural, biological, and chemical methods. Always follow pre-harvest intervals..Trim()

Weeds That Threaten African Farms: Striga and Speargrass

Striga, also called witchweed, is a parasitic weed that attacks cereals like maize, sorghum, and millet. It attaches to the host roots and sucks nutrients, causing stunting and yellowing. Striga thrives in poor soils and dry areas. Speargrass (Imperata cylindrica) is a aggressive perennial grass with sharp leaves and deep rhizomes. It competes for water and nutrients and is hard to remove by hand. To manage Striga, practice crop rotation with legumes like cowpea or groundnut, which stimulate Striga seeds to germinate but do not support them. Pull Striga before it flowers. Apply nitrogen fertilizer to boost crop vigor. For speargrass, repeated tillage during dry season exposes rhizomes to sun. Mulching with thick organic matter can suppress it. Hand-weed before speargrass sets seed. Herbicides like glyphosate can be used, but careful not to harm crops. Always integrate methods for best results..Trim()

Using Local Knowledge to Scout for Pests

Local knowledge is a powerful tool for pest scouting. Farmers often know signs of pest outbreaks before scientists do. For example, in many African communities, farmers observe certain birds or insects that indicate pest presence. They also know plant indicators: when certain wild plants flower, pests may appear. Use this knowledge alongside systematic scouting. Start by walking your field in a zigzag pattern. Check 10 plants in 5 spots. Look for chewed leaves, holes, frass, discoloration, or wilting. Record what you see. Talk to elders and experienced farmers about pest cycles. Use simple tools: a hand lens, a notebook, and a stick to open plant whorls. For fall armyworm, check early morning or late evening when larvae are active. For diseases, look for symptoms on leaves and stems. Share findings with neighbors. If you see something unusual, take a photo and ask an extension officer. Combine local knowledge with modern methods for best results..Trim()

When to Act: Economic Thresholds for Small Farms

Economic threshold (ET) is the pest density at which control measures are justified to prevent economic loss. For small farms, ET helps avoid unnecessary pesticide use. For fall armyworm in maize, ET is 2 larvae per 10 plants. For tomato leafminer, ET is 20 moths per pheromone trap per week. For cassava mosaic, ET is 5% of plants showing symptoms. For weeds, ET depends on crop and weed species; generally, if weeds cover 10% of the field, act. To calculate ET, estimate the cost of control and the value of crop loss. If control cost is less than potential loss, act. Scout regularly to monitor pest levels. Use simple counts: for aphids, check 10 leaves per plant; if more than 50 aphids per leaf, treat. Always consider natural enemies: if many ladybirds are present, aphid numbers may decline without spraying. Keep records to make better decisions each season..Trim()

Module 3 · Types of Pesticides and How They Work

Contact vs. Systemic Pesticides: What Works Best

When you spray a pesticide, it either stays on the surface where it lands (contact) or gets absorbed into the plant and moves inside it (systemic). Knowing the difference saves you money and gets better pest control. Contact pesticides kill pests only when they are hit directly by the spray. They work fast but have no residual effect. Examples include pyrethroids like deltamethrin and organophosphates like malathion. These are good for pests that sit on the outside of leaves, like aphids or beetles. But if the pest hides under the leaf or inside the stem, contact sprays miss them. Systemic pesticides are absorbed by leaves or roots and travel through the plant's vascular system. Pests die when they feed on any part of the plant. Examples include imidacloprid and thiamethoxam. These are excellent for sap-sucking pests like whiteflies, mealybugs, and stem borers. In Africa, systemic pesticides are popular for cotton, maize, and vegetables because they protect new growth. However, systemic pesticides can harm pollinators if used on flowering crops. Always check the label for bee warnings. For best results, match the pesticide type to the pest's behaviour. If pests feed openly, contact sprays work. If they hide, use systemic. Also consider cost: contact pesticides are usually cheaper, but systemic ones last longer. In smallholder farms, knapsack sprayers are common. With contact pesticides, you must spray the undersides of leaves thoroughly. With systemics, you can spray the whole plant and the chemical will move. Remember, no pesticide works if the pest isn't there. Scout your field first. Use a hand lens to check for eggs, larvae, or adults. Then choose the right type. Always wear protective gear and follow label instructions..Trim()

Selective vs. Non-Selective Herbicides

Herbicides are chemicals that kill weeds. Selective herbicides kill only certain types of plants, while non-selective herbicides kill almost all plants they touch. Choosing the right one is critical for your crop. Selective herbicides are designed to kill weeds without harming your crop. For example, 2,4-D kills broadleaf weeds in maize and wheat but does not harm the grass-like maize. Atrazine is another selective herbicide used in maize to control broadleaf and some grass weeds. These work because crops can break down the chemical while weeds cannot. Non-selective herbicides, like glyphosate and paraquat, kill any plant they contact. They are used for land clearing, pre-planting, or directed spraying between crop rows. Glyphosate is systemic and kills the whole plant including roots. Paraquat is contact and burns down foliage quickly. In Africa, non-selective herbicides are often used to clear fields before planting or to control weeds in orchards. But be careful: drift from non-selective herbicides can damage your crop if it lands on leaves. Always spray on a calm day and use a shield if needed. Selective herbicides are safer for crops but may not kill all weed species. You may need a mix or a sequence. For example, in maize, you might use atrazine early and then a grass-specific herbicide like nicosulfuron later. Read the label to see which weeds are controlled. Also, rotate herbicides with different modes of action to prevent resistance. Weeds like pigweed have developed resistance to glyphosate in many countries. If you use the same herbicide every year, resistant weeds will take over. Always calibrate your sprayer to apply the correct dose. Too little won't kill weeds; too much wastes money and can harm the crop. For smallholder farmers, hand weeding is still common, but herbicides save labour. Use selective herbicides when your crop is growing and non-selective ones only before planting or with a shield..Trim()

Modes of Action: How Pesticides Kill Pests

Modes of action (MoA) describe how a pesticide kills a pest. Knowing MoA helps you choose the right product and prevent resistance. Pesticides work in different ways, targeting specific systems in the pest. For insecticides, common MoA groups include nerve poisons, muscle poisons, and growth regulators. Pyrethroids and organophosphates attack the nervous system. They cause paralysis and death. Neonicotinoids block nerve signals, leading to paralysis. These are systemic and affect sucking pests. Insect growth regulators (IGRs) like methoprene disrupt the pest's ability to molt or reproduce. They are slow-acting but very targeted. For fungicides, MoA includes respiration inhibitors, sterol biosynthesis inhibitors, and multi-site inhibitors. For example, strobilurins (like azoxystrobin) block respiration in fungal cells. Triazoles (like tebuconazole) inhibit sterol production, weakening the fungal membrane. Multi-site inhibitors like mancozeb attack many sites, making resistance less likely. For herbicides, MoA includes photosynthesis inhibitors, amino acid synthesis inhibitors, and cell membrane disruptors. Atrazine inhibits photosynthesis. Glyphosate inhibits an enzyme (EPSPS) needed for amino acid production. Paraquat disrupts cell membranes. Weeds die because they cannot produce energy or build proteins. Resistance happens when pests survive a pesticide application and reproduce. If you use the same MoA repeatedly, resistant individuals become dominant. To prevent resistance, rotate pesticides with different MoA groups. For example, alternate between a pyrethroid (MoA group 3) and a neonicotinoid (group 4A) for aphid control. Also, use non-chemical methods like crop rotation, biological control, and hand picking. The Insecticide Resistance Action Committee (IRAC) and Herbicide Resistance Action Committee (HRAC) publish MoA classification lists. You can find them online or from extension officers. In Africa, resistance is a growing problem. For example, fall armyworm has developed resistance to some pyrethroids. By rotating MoA, you can keep your pesticides effective. Always read the label to find the MoA group. Many labels now include a group number. Use this to plan your rotation. Remember, no pesticide works forever if used alone. Integrated pest management (IPM) combines MoA rotation with other tactics for long-term control..Trim()

Protecting Water Sources from Pesticide Runoff

Pesticides can travel from your farm into streams, rivers, wells, and lakes through runoff, drift, or leaching. In Africa, where many communities rely on surface water and shallow wells, this contamination can harm people, livestock, and fish. To protect water sources, first identify all water bodies within 100 meters of your fields. Never mix or rinse pesticide containers near a well, stream, or pond. Always leave a buffer zone of at least 10 meters (30 feet) between treated crops and any water body. For highly toxic pesticides, increase the buffer to 30 meters. Use vegetative filter strips: plant thick grasses or shrubs along the edges of fields and waterways. These strips slow runoff and trap pesticide particles. In dry areas, avoid applying pesticides before heavy rains. Check the weather forecast; if rain is expected within 24 hours, postpone spraying. Apply pesticides when wind is calm (less than 10 km/h) to prevent drift into water. For soil-applied pesticides, incorporate them into the top 5 cm of soil to reduce runoff. Never clean spray equipment in a river or near a well; do it in a designated area away from water, and collect rinse water for proper disposal. Store pesticides in a locked shed with a concrete floor and a lip to contain spills, at least 30 meters from any water source. If a spill occurs, contain it with sand or soil, then scoop it into a leak-proof container. Report any water contamination to local authorities. Remember: it is easier and cheaper to prevent contamination than to clean it up..Trim()

Recognizing Symptoms of Acute and Chronic Exposure

Pesticide exposure can cause acute (immediate) or chronic (long-term) health effects. Acute symptoms appear within minutes to hours after contact. Common signs include headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, stomach cramps, diarrhea, blurred vision, excessive sweating, salivation, tearing, muscle twitching, difficulty breathing, and confusion. Severe cases can lead to convulsions, coma, or death. Different pesticides cause different symptoms. Organophosphates and carbamates (e.g., malathion, carbaryl) cause cholinergic effects: pinpoint pupils, slow heart rate, and fluid in the lungs. Pyrethroids (e.g., permethrin) can cause tingling or burning skin, dizziness, and nausea. Paraquat causes severe mouth and throat burns, and kidney failure. Chronic exposure happens over months or years, often from repeated low-level contact. Symptoms are vague: fatigue, memory loss, anxiety, depression, sleep problems, skin rashes, and respiratory issues like asthma. Some pesticides are linked to cancer, birth defects, and infertility. In Africa, farmers may not connect these symptoms to pesticide use. To recognize exposure, keep a diary of symptoms and when you handle pesticides. If you or a family member has recurring headaches or dizziness after spraying, suspect pesticide poisoning. Get a medical check-up and tell the doctor about your pesticide use. Blood tests can measure cholinesterase levels for organophosphate exposure. Prevention: always wear protective gear, wash hands before eating, and change clothes after spraying. Never eat or smoke while handling pesticides. If you notice symptoms, stop work and seek fresh air. Train others to recognize these signs. Early detection prevents chronic illness..Trim()

Reducing Environmental Impact on Beneficial Insects

Beneficial insects like bees, ladybugs, lacewings, and parasitic wasps help pollinate crops and control pests naturally. Many pesticides kill these helpers along with target pests. To reduce harm, choose selective pesticides that target specific pests and break down quickly. Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides like organophosphates and pyrethroids when possible. Use biopesticides such as Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) for caterpillars, or neem oil, which is less toxic to bees. Apply pesticides when beneficial insects are least active: early morning or late evening, when bees are not foraging. Never spray on flowering crops. If you must treat during bloom, use a bee-safe pesticide and apply at night. Create buffer zones: leave strips of wildflowers and native plants around fields as habitat for beneficial insects. These areas also provide refuge if spraying occurs. Use integrated pest management (IPM) practices: scout for pests, use traps, and only spray when pest numbers exceed economic thresholds. For example, treat only when you find 5 aphids per leaf. Reduce pesticide drift by using low-drift nozzles and spraying when wind is below 10 km/h. Avoid tank mixes that increase toxicity. Rotate pesticides to prevent resistance. After spraying, monitor beneficial insect populations. If you see dead bees or ladybugs, adjust your methods. In Africa, farmers can encourage natural enemies by planting marigolds, sunflowers, and coriander. These plants attract predators and parasites. Remember: healthy beneficial insect populations mean less need for pesticides in the long run..Trim()
