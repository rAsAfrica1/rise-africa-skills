Storing Ingredients in Hot and Humid Climates

For cocoa beans, store in jute bags with a moisture barrier, like a plastic liner. Keep bags off the floor on pallets. In a room, use a dehumidifier if possible, or place silica gel packets in containers. For small amounts, add a few dry rice grains to absorb moisture..Trim()

Second, control temperature. Heat speeds up spoilage. Ideal storage is below 25Â°C (77Â°F). Fourth, rotate stock. Use older ingredients first. Label everything with the date. Cocoa beans keep for 1-2 years if stored well. Nuts keep for 6 months. Honey and palm sugar keep indefinitely if sealed. Dates keep for 6 months in the fridge. Butter keeps for 3 months frozen..Trim()

Making Hard Candy with Local Sugar and Fruit Flavors

Hard candy is the simplest candy to make without special tools. You need white sugar, water, and a flavoring like fruit juice or zest. Use a heavy pot, a wooden spoon, and a clean flat surface like a metal tray or banana leaf. The key is to cook sugar to the hard crack stage, which is 150Â°C (300Â°F). If you don't have a thermometer, test by dropping a small amount into cold water; it should become brittle and snap. Start with 2 cups of sugar and 1/2 cup of water in a pot. Stir only until the sugar dissolves, then stop stirring. Boil over medium heat. If you stir after boiling, the sugar will crystallize. For fruit flavor, add 2 tablespoons of fresh lemon or orange juice after the sugar reaches 140Â°C. If you add juice too early, it can burn. Also add a pinch of salt to balance sweetness. Once at 150Â°C, remove from heat and quickly pour onto a greased tray or banana leaf. Let it cool for 10 minutes, then break into pieces with a hammer or back of a spoon. For variations, use tamarind paste for a sour kick, or grated ginger for heat. In Africa, you can find sugarcane juice or palm sugar; these work but may darken the candy. Store in an airtight container to prevent stickiness. This recipe makes about 500g of candy. Practice with small batches until you master the color and texture. Never touch hot sugar; it causes severe burns. Always keep a bowl of cold water nearby..Trim()

Cooking Soft Caramels in a Pot over Charcoal

Soft caramels are chewy and rich, made from sugar, butter, and milk. Over a charcoal stove, you can control heat by moving the pot or adding small amounts of charcoal. Use a heavy pot to avoid burning. Combine 1 cup sugar, 1/2 cup butter (or palm oil for a local twist), and 1 cup milk (coconut milk works well). Also add 1/4 teaspoon salt. Melt everything over low heat, stirring constantly with a wooden spoon. Once the mixture boils, stop stirring and let it cook. The target temperature is 120Â°C (248Â°F) for soft caramels. Without a thermometer, test by dropping a bit into cold water; it should form a soft ball that flattens when pressed. This takes about 20-30 minutes over charcoal. Stir occasionally to prevent sticking. If the mixture starts to smoke, lift the pot off the heat for a moment. When ready, remove from heat and stir in 1 teaspoon vanilla or a spoon of honey. Pour into a greased tray or banana leaf. Let it cool completely, then cut into squares with a knife. Wrap each caramel in wax paper or banana leaf. For a African flavor, add groundnut paste or toasted coconut. These caramels stay soft for weeks if kept cool. Avoid making on a rainy day because humidity makes them sticky. Always test a small spoonful first. This recipe yields about 30 pieces. Clean your pot immediately with hot water; caramel hardens fast..Trim()

Creating Nut Brittles with Groundnuts and Cashews

Nut brittle is a hard, crunchy candy packed with nuts. It's easy and uses local ingredients. You need 1 cup sugar, 1/2 cup water, 1 cup roasted groundnuts or cashews, and 1/4 teaspoon salt. Also 1 tablespoon butter or oil. Use a heavy pot and a wooden spoon. Start by roasting the nuts if raw: spread on a dry pan over medium heat for 5-7 minutes until fragrant. Set aside. In the pot, combine sugar and water. Stir over low heat until sugar dissolves. Then increase heat and boil without stirring until the syrup turns a light amber color, about 150Â°C (300Â°F). If you don't have a thermometer, drop a bit into cold water; it should crack. Remove from heat and quickly stir in the nuts, salt, and butter. Pour onto a greased tray or banana leaf. Spread thinly with a greased spoon. Let it cool for 15 minutes until hard. Break into pieces with a hammer or the back of a knife. For variation, add a pinch of chili powder or ground ginger for a spicy kick. Cashews are softer than groundnuts, so add them at the last moment to avoid burning. Store in an airtight container; humidity makes brittle sticky. This recipe makes about 400g. Be careful: hot sugar syrup causes burns. Keep children away. If the brittle is too sticky, it wasn't cooked long enough; if it tastes burnt, you cooked too long. Practice with small batches. Use a charcoal stove with steady heat; avoid windy spots..Trim()

Tempering Chocolate on a Simple Stone Slab

Tempering gives chocolate a glossy finish and a snap. Without a tempering machine, you can use a clean stone slab or a marble countertop. You need 500g of good-quality dark or milk chocolate, chopped into small pieces. If you only have chocolate bars, break them up. You also need a pot, a bowl that fits over it, and a spatula. First, melt two-thirds of the chocolate in a bowl over hot water (not boiling). Stir until smooth and reaches 45Â°C for dark, 40Â°C for milk. Remove from heat. Add the remaining one-third chocolate and stir constantly. This cools the mixture. Keep stirring and testing: the chocolate should cool to 27Â°C for dark, 26Â°C for milk. If you don't have a thermometer, test by dipping a knife tip; it should look slightly thick but still fluid. Then, warm it gently over hot water again to 31Â°C for dark, 29Â°C for milk. Now it's tempered. Pour onto the stone slab and spread thin with a spatula. Let it set at room temperature (not in fridge). It should harden in 10-15 minutes with a shine. If it streaks or is soft, retemper. For African flavors, add a pinch of ground coffee or dried fruit before pouring. Use the slab for molding too. Clean the slab with warm water and dry thoroughly. Tempering is sensitive to humidity; work in a dry room. Practice with small amounts. This method works for coating nuts or fruits. Store tempered chocolate in a cool, dark place..Trim()

Molding Candies Using Clay or Wooden Molds

You can make beautiful candies without plastic molds. Use clay or wooden molds, which are cheap and local. First, make your candy mixture: hard candy, caramel, or chocolate. For clay molds, ensure they are food-safe and well-cleaned. Soak new clay molds in water for 24 hours, then dry. Rub with a little oil to prevent sticking. For wooden molds, sand them smooth and oil them. To mold hard candy, pour the hot syrup (at 150Â°C) into the molds. Let it cool completely. Tap the mold to release. If it sticks, warm the mold slightly. For caramels, pour the cooked caramel into molds and let set. For chocolate, temper it first, then pour into molds. Tap the mold on the table to remove air bubbles. Let set at room temperature. You can also press nuts or dried fruit into the candy before it hardens. To make multi-colored candies, pour small amounts of different colored syrups. Clean molds immediately after use with hot water and a brush. Never use soap on clay; it absorbs. For wooden molds, scrub with salt and warm water. Store molds in a dry place. In Africa, you can find clay molds in pottery villages. You can also carve simple shapes into wood. Practice with simple shapes like circles or squares. This method adds a personal touch and reduces packaging waste. Always test a small batch first. If the candy sticks, oil the mold more. This is a great way to make candies for sale..Trim()

Packaging Candies in Banana Leaves and Recycled Paper

Packaging protects candy and makes it attractive. Banana leaves and recycled paper are cheap, eco-friendly, and available. For banana leaves, cut into squares about 15cm x 15cm. Wipe clean with a damp cloth, then pass over a flame quickly to make them flexible and sterile. Place 2-3 candies in the center, fold the leaf over, and tie with a strip of leaf or twine. For recycled paper, use clean newspaper, brown paper bags, or old magazines. Cut into squares. Wrap candies like a parcel: place candy in center, fold opposite corners, then roll. Secure with a small piece of tape or string. For a professional look, add a label with the candy name and date. You can also make small paper cones for hard candies. For caramels, twist the ends of wax paper. If you don't have wax paper, use banana leaf. To prevent sticking, dust candies with a little cornstarch or powdered sugar before wrapping. Store wrapped candies in a cool, dry place. For selling, use a clean basket or box. Avoid plastic if possible; it's expensive and pollutes. Banana leaves can be composted. Recycled paper can be reused. This packaging adds value and appeals to customers who care about the environment. Always ensure hands are clean when packaging. If candies are sticky, wrap individually. For a nice finish, add a small dried flower or a cinnamon stick. Practice folding to make it quick. This method is traditional in many African markets and can be a selling point..Trim()

Roasting Cocoa Beans in a Clay Oven or Pan

Roasting develops the chocolate flavor and loosens the bean shells. Start with 2 kg of dried cocoa beans. If using a clay oven, light a wood fire and let it burn down to embers. Spread the beans in a single layer on a metal tray or clay pot. Place the tray over the embers, about 15 cm above the heat. Stir every 2 minutes with a wooden spoon. Roast for 20-30 minutes. The beans are ready when they smell like dark chocolate and the shells crack easily. If using a pan on a stove, use medium heat and stir constantly. You will hear popping sounds. Do not let the beans burn. For a light roast, stop when the beans are light brown. For a dark roast, wait until they are dark brown but not black. After roasting, pour the beans onto a clean cloth or mat to cool for 10 minutes. Rub them with your hands to remove the shells. This is called winnowing. You can also winnow by putting beans in a basket and tossing them in the wind. The shells are lighter and blow away. You should get about 1.6 kg of nibs from 2 kg of beans. Store the nibs in a dry container. Roasting is the most important step for flavor. Practice with small batches until you can judge by smell and sound..Trim()

Winnowing and Grinding Cocoa Nibs with a Mortar and Pestle

After roasting and cooling, you need to separate the nibs from the shells. This is winnowing. Put the roasted beans in a clean cloth and rub them vigorously. The shells will flake off. Then, use a basket or a bowl. Toss the beans in the air. The wind blows the light shells away while the heavier nibs fall back. Do this on a windy day or use a fan. Repeat until you have mostly nibs. For 2 kg of beans, you should get about 1.6 kg of nibs. Now grind the nibs. Use a large mortar and pestle, preferably made of wood or stone. Put 1 cup of nibs in the mortar. You want a smooth, shiny liquid. This is called cocoa mass or cocoa liquor. It will be warm from friction. If it gets too thick, add a little cocoa butter or warm it slightly. For a finer grind, you can pass the mass through a sieve and grind again. This manual grinding is hard work but gives you pure chocolate. You can also use a meat grinder or a grain mill if available, but the mortar and pestle is traditional and effective. Store the cocoa mass in a cool place..Trim()

Refining Cocoa Mass with Sugar and Cocoa Butter

Now you turn cocoa mass into chocolate. You need sugar and cocoa butter. For a dark chocolate, use 60% cocoa mass, 30% sugar, and 10% cocoa butter. For milk chocolate, use 40% cocoa mass, 40% sugar, 15% milk powder, and 5% cocoa butter. Weigh your ingredients with a scale. Sugar should be fine and dry. It takes time and effort. If you have a mechanical grinder, use it. Otherwise, grind in a mortar, then pass through a fine sieve and grind again. The chocolate will become thicker as you refine. Conching Chocolate by Hand for Smooth Texture

Conching removes bitterness and makes chocolate smooth. After refining, the chocolate is still gritty and acidic. Conching means stirring and aerating the chocolate for a long time. Traditionally, this is done by hand with a wooden spoon or a stone roller. Put your refined chocolate in a warm bowl or a stone metate. Keep it at 45-50Â°C. Stir continuously with a wooden spoon. For a small batch of 1 kg, conch for at least 6 hours. If you have a mechanical mixer, use it on low speed. But hand conching is possible. Tempering and Pouring Chocolate into Bars

Tempering gives chocolate a snap and shine. It involves heating and cooling to specific temperatures. You need a thermometer. Chop your conched chocolate into small pieces. After making bars, you need to cure and store them properly. Curing means letting the chocolate rest for a few days to develop flavor. Wrap each bar tightly in foil or plastic. Calculating Cost per Batch of Chocolate

To know if you are making a profit, you must first know exactly what each batch of chocolate costs you. This is called cost per batch. Start by listing every ingredient you use: cocoa beans, sugar, milk powder, cocoa butter, lecithin, and any flavourings like vanilla or nuts. For each, write down the quantity used in one batch and the price you paid for that quantity. For example, if a 1 kg bag of sugar costs 1,200 KES and you use 500 g per batch, your sugar cost is 600 KES. Do this for all ingredients. Add them up to get total ingredient cost. Next, add packaging: wrappers, foil, boxes, labels. If you use 50 wrappers at 5 KES each, that's 250 KES. Then add fuel or electricity for roasting, grinding, and tempering. If you use a gas cylinder that costs 2,500 KES and it lasts for 10 batches, that's 250 KES per batch. Add labour if you pay someone, or estimate your own time. If you pay a helper 300 KES per batch, include that. Finally, add a small amount for equipment wear and tear, say 100 KES per batch. Sum everything: ingredients + packaging + fuel + labour + equipment = total cost per batch. For a typical 2 kg batch of milk chocolate, this might be 3,500 KES. Now divide by the number of bars or pieces you get. If you make 20 bars, each bar costs 175 KES. This is your break-even cost. You must sell above this to profit.
