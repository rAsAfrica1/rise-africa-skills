Module 1 · Introduction to Rice Milling

Overview of Rice Milling Industry

Rice milling is the process of removing the husk and bran layers from paddy rice to produce edible white rice. In Africa, rice is a staple food for millions, but local milling often falls short. Many farmers still use traditional methods like pounding in wooden mortars, which breaks grains and yields low quality. Modern rice milling involves a series of machines: destoners, huskers, polishers, and graders. A typical small-scale mill in Nigeria or Tanzania can process 1-2 tonnes of paddy per hour, costing between $5,000 and $20,000. The industry is growing because governments are pushing for local processing to reduce imports. For example, Nigeria imports over 2 million tonnes of rice annually, but local milling can cut that. However, challenges include poor infrastructure, unreliable electricity, and lack of skilled operators. A successful mill needs a steady supply of paddy, proper drying, and regular maintenance. In rural areas, mobile mills on trucks are becoming popular, bringing services closer to farmers. The key message: rice milling is a viable business if you manage quality and costs. Start small, learn the machines, and connect with farmers. With Africa's population rising, demand for well-milled rice is guaranteed. This module will equip you with the basics to enter this industry..Trim()

Rice Grain Structure and Composition

To mill rice well, you must understand the grain's structure. A paddy grain consists of the hull (husk), bran layers, endosperm, and germ. The hull is the outer protective covering, making up about 20% of the grain weight. It is not edible and must be removed. Under the hull are several bran layers, rich in vitamins, minerals, and oil. The bran constitutes about 8-10% of the grain. The endosperm is the white, starchy part we eat, accounting for 70-80% of the grain. The germ is the embryo, about 2-3% of the grain, containing healthy oils and nutrients. When milling, we remove the hull and bran to get white rice. But over-milling removes too much bran and germ, reducing nutrition and causing breakage. In Africa, many consumers prefer parboiled rice, where paddy is soaked, steamed, and dried before milling. This process pushes nutrients from the bran into the endosperm, making the rice more nutritious and less sticky. Understanding composition helps you set milling degrees. For example, to produce 70% white rice, you must remove 30% as husk and bran. If you mill too little, rice is brown and spoils faster. If you mill too much, you get broken grains and lose weight. So, adjust your machines based on grain moisture and variety. Local varieties like Nerica have different structures, so test before milling..Trim()

Milling Terminology and Definitions

In rice milling, you'll hear terms like paddy, husk, bran, milled rice, head rice, and broken rice. Paddy is the raw rice grain with its hull. Husk is the outer shell removed during husking. Bran is the brown layer under the hull, removed during polishing. Milled rice is white rice after bran removal. Head rice refers to whole grains, while broken rice are fragments. The milling yield is the percentage of head rice obtained from paddy. A good mill yields 60-65% head rice. A destoner removes stones and dirt. A husker removes the hull. A polisher or whitener removes the bran. A grader separates head rice from broken. A rice miller must also know about moisture content: ideal paddy moisture for milling is 14%. If too dry, grains break; if too wet, machines clog. Another term is 'degree of milling', which describes how much bran is removed. Light milling leaves some bran, giving brown rice; heavy milling gives white rice. In Africa, many small mills use a single-pass machine that combines husking and polishing, but this often breaks grains. Better setups use multiple passes. You'll also hear 'parboiling', which is a pre-milling treatment. And 'milling recovery', which is the ratio of milled rice to paddy. Knowing these terms helps you communicate with technicians and buy the right equipment. For example, when buying a mill, ask for its 'head rice yield' specification. This vocabulary is your foundation for practical work..Trim()

Global Rice Production and Consumption

Rice is the third most produced cereal globally, after maize and wheat. In 2022, world paddy production was about 780 million tonnes. Asia dominates, with China and India producing over half. Africa produces about 30 million tonnes of paddy, with Nigeria, Egypt, and Madagascar as top growers. However, Africa consumes more than it produces, importing about 15 million tonnes annually. This gap is an opportunity for millers. Global consumption is rising, especially in Africa where population grows at 2.5% per year. Rice is preferred for its ease of cooking and versatility. In West Africa, jollof rice is a staple; in East Africa, pilau. The demand for high-quality, locally milled rice is increasing because imported rice is expensive and often lower quality. For example, a tonne of imported rice costs $500, while locally milled can be $400. But local rice must compete on quality: consumers want clean, unbroken grains. So, millers who produce consistent quality can capture the market. Also, climate change affects production: droughts in Asia raise prices, making local milling more profitable. In Africa, organizations like the African Development Bank support rice value chains. As a miller, you should track global prices and local harvests. If you mill during harvest season, you get paddy cheaply. The key takeaway: Africa's rice deficit is your business case. Focus on reducing imports by milling local paddy efficiently..Trim()

Role of Milling in Rice Value Chain

The rice value chain includes production, harvesting, threshing, drying, milling, packaging, and marketing. Milling is the critical middle step that transforms paddy into edible rice. Without proper milling, paddy is worthless for most consumers. A miller adds value by removing husk and bran, improving taste, and extending shelf life. In Africa, many farmers sell paddy to traders who export it or mill poorly. By setting up a local mill, you keep value in the community. For example, a farmer might sell paddy at $200 per tonne, but milled rice sells for $400 per tonne. The miller captures that $200 difference minus costs. Milling also creates jobs: operators, maintenance staff, and baggers. It supports other businesses like transport and packaging. However, the miller must coordinate with farmers to ensure a steady supply. Contract farming, where you agree to buy paddy at a set price, is common in Tanzania and Ghana. Also, by-products like husk and bran have value: husk can be used as fuel or animal bedding; bran is used in animal feed. So, a well-run mill wastes nothing. In the value chain, milling is the link between agriculture and food security. If milling is inefficient, the whole chain suffers. Therefore, invest in training and quality control. Your role is not just to mill, but to improve the entire chain by providing quality rice that meets consumer needs..Trim()

Quality Parameters of Milled Rice

Quality determines the price of milled rice. Key parameters include head rice percentage, broken rice percentage, moisture content, foreign matter, and color. Head rice is whole grains; the higher, the better. Premium rice has over 60% head rice. Broken rice is sold cheaper, often for flour or animal feed. Moisture content should be 12-14% for safe storage. Too high, and rice grows mold; too low, and it cracks. Foreign matter like stones, dust, and husk must be less than 0.5%. Color should be uniform white, without yellow or red grains. In Africa, consumers also prefer aromatic varieties like Basmati or local scented rice. To achieve quality, you need proper drying, cleaning, and milling. Use a destoner to remove stones, a husker that doesn't break grains, and a polisher that removes bran evenly. Test your rice with simple tools: a moisture meter (costing $50-$200), a grain grader, and visual inspection. Also, avoid over-milling: it reduces head rice and nutrients. For export, you must meet stricter standards, like those of the FAO. In local markets, quality still matters: a bag of clean, head rice sells faster. So, train your workers to sort and grade. Remember, quality is not an accident; it comes from careful control at every step. Start by milling small batches and testing. As you improve, your reputation and profits grow..Trim()

Module 2 · Rice Varieties and Grain Characteristics

Classification of Rice Varieties

Rice is broadly classified into two species: Oryza sativa (Asian rice) and Oryza glaberrima (African rice). In Africa, O. glaberrima is native to West Africa and is known for its hardiness, but it shatters easily and has lower yield. Most commercial rice in Africa is O. sativa, which includes indica and japonica types. Indica rices are long, slender grains that cook fluffy and separate; they are grown in the lowland tropics and are the most common in Africa. Japonica rices are short, round grains that cook sticky; they are grown in temperate regions and high-altitude areas like Ethiopia and Madagascar. A third group, javanica, is intermediate. You can also classify by ecology: lowland (paddy) rice grows in flooded fields; upland rice grows on dry land, often in rotation with other crops. Lowland rice yields more but needs water control. Upland rice yields less but is important for food security in hilly areas. Another classification is by grain length: long (over 6.6 mm), medium (5.5â6.6 mm), and short (less than 5.5 mm). Grain length affects milling: long grains break more easily. Also, glutinous (waxy) rice is sticky when cooked and is used for special foods. Aromatic rices like Basmati and Jasmine are long-grain and prized for smell. In Africa, local varieties like Nerica (New Rice for Africa) combine African and Asian traits for drought tolerance and higher yield. Knowing these classes helps you set your mill correctly: long-grain rice needs gentler handling to reduce breakage..Trim()

Physical Properties of Paddy

Paddy is rice still in its husk. Its physical properties determine how you mill it. Key properties include grain dimensions (length, width, thickness), weight (1000-grain weight), hardness, moisture content, and husk content. Length ranges from 5 to 10 mm, width from 2 to 4 mm. A typical 1000-grain weight is 20â30 grams. Hardness affects breakage: harder grains resist cracking. Moisture content is critical: ideal for milling is 12â14%. Too dry (below 12%) makes grains brittle and they shatter; too wet (above 14%) makes them sticky and clog the mill. You can test moisture with a simple moisture meter or by biting a grainâif it shatters, it's too dry; if it chews, it's too wet. Husk content is about 20% of paddy weight. After milling, you get about 60â65% white rice, 20% husk, 10% bran, and 5% germ and losses. Grain shape matters: long slender grains break more easily than short round ones. Also, chalkiness (white opaque spots) weakens grains and lowers milling yield. You can check chalkiness by holding grains against light. Foreign matter like stones, straw, and dirt should be removed before milling to prevent damage. In Africa, many farmers thresh by hand, leaving more foreign matter, so cleaning is essential. Use a simple screen or winnowing to remove light matter. Proper storage keeps moisture stable; store paddy in dry, ventilated bags off the floor. These physical properties guide your mill settings: adjust roller clearance based on grain size, and set husker speed to avoid cracking..Trim()

Chemical Composition of Rice Kernels

A rice kernel is mostly starch (about 75â80% of dry weight), with protein (7â8%), fat (2â3%), and small amounts of fibre, vitamins, and minerals. Starch is the main energy source. It comes in two forms: amylose and amylopectin. Amylose makes rice firm and separate; amylopectin makes it sticky. Long-grain indica rices have high amylose (22â30%), while short-grain japonica have low amylose (0â20%). This affects cooking and milling: high-amylose rice tends to break more during milling because it is less flexible. Protein content is higher in the bran and germ, so white rice has less protein than brown rice. Fat is mostly in the bran and germ; it can go rancid if stored too long, so bran should be removed quickly. Vitamins and minerals (B vitamins, iron, zinc) are concentrated in the bran and germ. Milling removes these, which is why white rice is less nutritious. In Africa, where rice is a staple, this matters for nutrition. You can reduce nutrient loss by milling less (e.g., brown rice) or by fortifying white rice. The chemical composition also affects milling: high-fat rice can clog rollers if not cleaned. High-moisture rice has more free water, making it softer and harder to mill without breakage. To test composition simply, you can't do lab tests, but you can observe: rice with a lot of bran (darker) has more nutrients. Aromatic rice has volatile compounds (like 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline) that give smell. These compounds are lost during milling if you over-polish. So, for aromatic varieties, mill gently and avoid excessive polishing..Trim()

Differences Between Indica and Japonica Rice

Indica and japonica are the two major subspecies of Asian rice. Indica rices are grown in tropical and subtropical lowlands, including most of Africa. They have long, slender grains, and when cooked, they are fluffy and separate. Japonica rices are grown in temperate regions and high altitudes, like Ethiopia and Madagascar. They have short, round grains and cook sticky and moist. Indica plants are usually taller, tiller more, and are less resistant to cold. Japonica plants are shorter, have fewer tillers, and tolerate cold better. In milling, indica grains are longer and more prone to breaking because they are slender. Japonica grains are shorter and rounder, so they break less. Indica rice usually has higher amylose, making it firmer; japonica has lower amylose, making it stickier. This affects milling: indica may need slower roller speeds to reduce breakage. Japonica may need less polishing because it is already sticky. In Africa, indica is dominant because it suits the climate. However, Nerica varieties are interspecific crosses between African rice (O. glaberrima) and Asian rice (O. sativa), often with indica traits. They combine drought tolerance with higher yield. When you mill, check the grain type: if it's long and slender, treat it as indica; if short and round, treat it as japonica. Adjust husker clearance: indica needs wider clearance to avoid cracking; japonica can take tighter clearance. Also, cooking quality differs: consumers in West Africa prefer long-grain indica for dishes like jollof rice, while some East African markets prefer japonica for sticky dishes. Know your market to choose varieties and milling settings..Trim()

Impact of Variety on Milling Yield

Milling yield is the amount of white rice you get from paddy. It varies by variety. Typically, you get 60â65% head rice (whole grains) and 5â10% broken grains, plus bran and husk. But some varieties give higher yields. For example, long-grain indica rices often have lower head rice yield because they break more easily. Short-grain japonica rices tend to have higher head rice yield because they are rounder and stronger. Nerica varieties can give good yields if milled properly. Other factors: grain hardness, chalkiness, and moisture content. Hard, translucent grains mill better than soft, chalky ones. Chalky grains break easily. Moisture content: if too dry, grains shatter; if too wet, they mash. So, adjust moisture to 12â14% before milling. Also, the milling process itself: a two-step process (husking then polishing) gives higher yield than a one-step. In Africa, many small mills use single-pass machines, which cause more breakage. You can improve yield by using a rubber-roll husker instead of a steel husker, and by setting the roller clearance correctly. For long-grain rice, use a clearance of about 0.5â0.8 mm; for short-grain, 0.3â0.5 mm. Also, screen the paddy to remove immature grains, which break easily. Immature grains are lighter and can be removed by winnowing. By choosing the right variety and milling carefully, you can increase head rice yield by 5â10%. That means more income. For example, if you mill 100 kg of paddy, a good yield gives 60 kg head rice; a poor yield gives 50 kg. At $1 per kg, that's $10 difference. So, variety matters..Trim()

Grain Grading and Specifications

Grain grading sorts milled rice by quality. It affects price. Grades are based on head rice percentage, broken rice percentage, foreign matter, discoloration, and moisture. In many African markets, grades are: Grade 1 (premium): at least 95% head rice, no foreign matter, no discolored grains. Grade 2: 80â94% head rice, minimal foreign matter. Grade 3: 60â79% head rice, some broken and foreign matter. Below that is off-grade. To grade, you need simple tools: a sieve to separate broken from head rice, a scale to weigh, and your eyes. Take a 100-gram sample. Remove foreign matter (stones, straw). Weigh the head rice (grains at least 75% of original length). Calculate percentage. For example, if you have 85 grams head rice, that's 85%. Also check discoloration: yellow or black grains lower grade. Moisture should be 12â14%; test with a moisture meter or by feel. In Africa, many millers sell to local traders who set their own standards. But for export or formal markets, you need to meet standards like those of the West African Rice Development Association (WARDA) or international ISO. For example, Grade 1 rice must have less than 0.1% foreign matter. To improve grade, you can use a destoner to remove stones, a paddy separator to remove immature grains, and a color sorter (if available) to remove discolored grains. Even without machines, you can hand-pick. Grading also involves labeling: put rice in clean bags, label with grade, weight, and date. This builds trust and gets better prices. Remember: consistent grading leads to repeat customers..Trim()

Pre-cleaning Equipment and Operations

Pre-cleaning removes straw, leaves, empty grains, and dirt before paddy enters the mill. In Africa, most small mills use a combination of a vibrating screen and a destoner. The vibrating screen has two or three layers with different hole sizes: top layer 10-12 mm for large trash, middle layer 5-6 mm for smaller trash, and bottom layer 2-3 mm for sand. A fan blows light materials away. For a 1-ton-per-hour mill, the screen area should be at least 1.5 square meters. The screen angle is usually 10-15 degrees, and the vibration speed is 300-400 rpm. Feed paddy evenly using a hopper with a gate; uneven feeding reduces efficiency. Check screens daily for holes or tears; replace worn screens immediately. Clean the machine after every 8 hours of operation. In rural areas, pre-cleaning can also be done manually with a sieve or winnowing, but this is slow and less effective. A simple motorized pre-cleaner costs about $500-$1,500 and pays for itself by reducing wear on downstream equipment. Always wear a dust mask when cleaning the machine. After pre-cleaning, paddy should have less than 1% foreign matter. Test by taking a 1 kg sample and hand-sorting. If trash exceeds 1%, adjust the screen or feed rate. Pre-cleaning also removes stones larger than 5 mm, but smaller stones need a destoner. Remember: pre-cleaning protects your husker and polisher from damage and improves rice quality. De-stoning and Magnetic Separation

De-stoning removes stones and heavy particles that can damage the husker and end up in final rice. A destoner works by fluidizing a bed of paddy on a vibrating deck while air blows upward. Stones, being heavier, sink and move up the inclined deck, while paddy floats and moves down. The deck angle is typically 5-10 degrees, and vibration is 400-600 rpm. Air velocity must be adjusted so paddy just fluidizes; too much air blows paddy away, too little lets stones stay mixed. For a 1-ton-per-hour mill, the destoner deck should be about 1 meter wide and 1.5 meters long. Feed paddy at a constant rate; sudden surges reduce separation. Check the stone discharge outlet every 30 minutes to ensure stones are being removed. Magnetic separation uses magnets to remove iron particles like nails, wire, and screws. Install a permanent magnet or electromagnet in the chute before the husker. A good magnet can catch particles as small as 0.5 mm. Clean the magnet every 4 hours; iron buildup reduces its strength. In Africa, many mills use a simple bar magnet mounted at an angle. Test magnetic strength with a small nail; if it doesn't stick, replace the magnet. After de-stoning and magnetic separation, paddy should have less than 0.1% stones and zero iron particles. Take a 1 kg sample and check by hand. If stones remain, reduce feed rate or increase air. If iron remains, add a second magnet. These steps are critical because a single stone can break a husker rubber roll, costing $50-$200 to replace. A nail can spark and cause a fire. So always run de-stoning and magnetic separation before husking..Trim()

Paddy Husking and Separation

Husking removes the outer hull from paddy to produce brown rice. In Africa, most small mills use a rubber roll husker. Two rubber rolls spin in opposite directions at different speeds: one fast (about 1000 rpm) and one slow (about 800 rpm). The speed difference creates a shearing action that pulls the hull off. The gap between rolls is critical: too wide and paddy passes unhusked; too narrow and grains break. Set the gap to about 0.5-0.8 mm for long grain, 0.4-0.6 mm for short grain. Adjust while running: feed a handful of paddy and check the output. You want at least 90% husked with less than 5% broken. After husking, a separator separates brown rice from unhulled paddy and hulls. The mixture goes over a vibrating screen with holes sized to let brown rice through but retain unhulled paddy. Unhulled paddy is returned to the husker. Hulls are blown away by a fan and can be used for fuel or animal bedding. For a 1-ton-per-hour mill, the husker needs a 5-7.5 kW motor. Rubber rolls wear out after 80-120 hours of operation; replace them when the surface becomes smooth or cracked. In rural areas, some use a steel huller, but it breaks more grains. Rubber rolls are better for quality. After husking, brown rice should have less than 2% unhulled paddy. Check by taking a 100 g sample. If unhulled paddy is high, narrow the roll gap slightly. Paddy Drying and Tempering

Paddy must be dried to 12-14% moisture before milling to prevent broken grains and mold. In Africa, sun drying is common: spread paddy in a thin layer (5-10 cm) on a concrete floor or tarpaulin. Stir every 30 minutes. On a sunny day, it takes 1-2 days to dry from 20% to 14%. Use a moisture meter to check; if you don't have one, bite a grain: it should be hard but not shatter. For larger quantities, use a mechanical dryer. A batch dryer with a 1-ton capacity costs about $2,000-$5,000. Set drying air temperature to 40-45Â°C for seed paddy, 50-60Â°C for milling paddy. Higher temperatures cause cracks. Drying too fast also causes cracks; aim for a drying rate of 0.5-1% moisture per hour. After drying, temper paddy by letting it rest in a bin for 12-24 hours. In humid regions, use a fan to circulate air. After tempering, check moisture again; it should be uniform. If you skip tempering, broken grains can increase by 10-20%. Module 5 · Rice Whitening and Polishing

Principles of Rice Whitening

Rice whitening is the process of removing the bran layers and germ from brown rice to produce white rice. In a typical milling setup, you first remove the husk to get brown rice, then you whiten it. The bran layers are made of several thin layers: pericarp, seed coat, nucellus, aleurone layer, and germ. Whitening removes these layers by abrasion or friction. Abrasive whitening uses a rotating stone or emery wheel that scrapes off the bran. Friction whitening uses a steel roller that rubs grains against each other and a screen, creating pressure that shears off bran. In Africa, most small to medium mills use friction whitening because it is gentler and breaks fewer grains. The key principle is to apply just enough pressure and time to remove bran without cracking the rice kernel. You control pressure by adjusting the clearance between the roller and the screen, and the flow of rice through the machine. Too little pressure leaves bran; too much pressure breaks grains and generates heat. Heat is the enemy: it can cause rice to crack and become chalky. A good whitener will remove bran evenly and keep broken grains below 5%. For a beginner, start with a friction whitener set at low pressure, then gradually increase until you see mostly white rice with few brown streaks. Always check the bran coming out: it should be fine and consistent, not chunky. Remember, whitening is not just about making rice white; it is about doing it efficiently with minimal loss. In Africa, where rice is a staple, even a 2% improvement in head rice yield means more food and income. So learn to listen to the machine: a steady hum means good operation; a grinding noise means too much pressure. Practice with small batches until you get it right..Trim()

Degree of Milling and Its Measurement

Degree of milling (DOM) is a measure of how much bran has been removed from brown rice. It is usually expressed as a percentage of bran removed or as a numerical score. In simple terms, if you remove all bran, you get 100% DOM, which is fully white rice. But in practice, you rarely remove every trace because over-milling wastes rice and breaks grains. Most markets prefer 8-10% DOM, which means about 8-10% of the brown rice weight is removed as bran. To measure DOM, you can use several methods. The simplest is visual comparison: take a sample of your milled rice and compare it to a standard chart or a sample of known DOM. For more accuracy, use a milling meter, which measures the reflectance of light from the rice surface. A whiter surface means higher DOM. Another method is chemical: treat a sample with a dye that stains bran, then count stained grains. But for a small mill in Africa, visual and weight-based methods are practical. Weigh a known quantity of brown rice, mill it, then weigh the white rice and bran. The weight loss divided by the original brown rice weight gives you the DOM. For example, if you start with 1 kg brown rice and end with 900 g white rice and 100 g bran, DOM is 10%. Keep records: if your DOM is too low (less than 6%), rice looks brown and customers reject it. If too high (over 12%), you lose yield and rice may crack. Aim for 8-10% for local markets. Also, check for even milling: some grains may be over-milled while others are under-milled. This happens if the machine is not set right. Adjust pressure and flow to get uniform whiteness. Remember, DOM affects cooking quality: under-milled rice takes longer to cook and has a chewy texture; over-milled rice cooks fast but can be sticky. So measure and adjust regularly..Trim()

Polishing and Glazing Processes

After whitening, rice still has a rough surface and some bran dust. Polishing and glazing are finishing steps that make rice shiny, smooth, and more attractive to customers. Polishing removes the remaining bran and smooths the grain surface. It is done in a polishing machine, which is similar to a whitener but with softer rollers and less pressure. Some polishers use a mixture of water and air (mist polishing) to help remove bran without breaking grains. Glazing, on the other hand, coats the rice with a thin layer of glucose or talc to give it a glossy appearance. In many African markets, glazed rice is preferred because it looks premium. However, glazing with talc is banned in some countries due to health concerns. A safer glazing method uses a fine mist of water and then a gentle tumbling action, which brings out the natural shine of the rice. The process: after whitening, rice goes to a polisher where it is rubbed against a screen or a brush. The friction removes fine bran particles. Then, in a glazing drum, a small amount of water (about 0.5-1% of rice weight) is sprayed onto the rice as it tumbles. The water helps to seal the surface and create a shine. Sometimes, a tiny amount of edible oil (like groundnut oil) is added to enhance gloss. But be careful: too much water can cause rice to swell or mold. So use a fine mist and keep the rice moving. For small mills, a simple polisher with a brush roller works well. After polishing, let the rice cool and dry before packaging. Polishing increases the market value of rice, but it also adds cost. So only polish if your customers demand it. In Africa, where many consumers buy rice by appearance, polishing can make your product stand out. But always prioritize cleanliness and safety over shine..Trim()

Mist Polishing and Water Polishing

Mist polishing and water polishing are two methods to improve the appearance of white rice by adding a small amount of moisture during the polishing stage. In mist polishing, a fine mist of water is sprayed onto the rice as it passes through the polisher. The water helps to soften the bran remnants, making them easier to remove, and also gives the rice a smoother surface. Typically, you use about 0.2 to 0.5 liters of water per 100 kg of rice. The key is to use a very fine mist, not a stream. If you add too much water, the rice becomes wet, sticky, and may clog the machine or develop mold. In water polishing, a larger amount of water is used, often in a separate machine called a water polisher. Here, rice is mixed with water (about 1-2% by weight) and tumbled, which washes away bran and creates a shiny surface. After water polishing, the rice must be dried immediately to safe moisture content (12-14%) to prevent spoilage. In Africa, mist polishing is more common in small mills because it requires less equipment and less drying. Water polishing is used in larger mills that have dryers. Both methods require clean water. Never use dirty water; it will contaminate the rice. Also, the water should be potable. The benefits: polished rice looks better, cooks more evenly, and has less bran dust. But there are risks: if the rice is too wet, it can crack during drying, reducing head rice yield. So monitor moisture carefully. A simple moisture meter is a good investment. If you don't have one, squeeze a few grains: they should be hard, not soft. After polishing, spread the rice in a thin layer to air-dry or use a mechanical dryer. In humid African climates, drying can be challenging, so mist polishing is safer. Practice with small batches to find the right amount of water. Remember, the goal is to enhance appearance without compromising quality..Trim()

Bran Removal and Collection

Bran is the outer layer of the rice grain that is removed during whitening and polishing. It is a valuable by-product, rich in oil, protein, and vitamins. In a rice mill, bran removal and collection are important for two reasons: first, to keep the milling area clean and safe; second, to earn extra income from selling bran as animal feed or for oil extraction. During whitening, bran is produced as fine particles. These particles are carried by air from the milling chamber to a cyclone or a bag filter. A cyclone is a cone-shaped device that uses centrifugal force to separate bran from air. The bran falls to the bottom and is collected in bags. A bag filter uses cloth bags to trap bran while letting clean air out. For small mills, a simple cyclone with a collection bag is adequate. You must ensure that the bran collection system is working well; if bran escapes into the air, it creates dust, which is a health hazard and can cause explosions if ignited. So regular maintenance is key. Empty the bran bags frequently; do not let them overfill. Bran should be stored in a dry place, as it spoils quickly due to high oil content. If you plan to sell bran, pack it in woven polypropylene bags (50 kg) and store on pallets away from moisture. In Africa, bran is in demand for cattle, goats, and poultry feed. You can also use bran to make rice bran oil, but that requires advanced technology. For a small mill, selling raw bran is profitable. The quantity of bran produced depends on the degree of milling: for 10% DOM, you get about 10 kg bran per 100 kg brown rice. So if you mill 1 ton of brown rice, you get about 100 kg bran. At a market price of $0.10 per kg, that's $10 extra income. So don't throw bran away; collect and sell it. Also, keep the collection area clean to avoid contamination with stones or other debris. A well-maintained bran collection system improves mill efficiency and safety..Trim()

Optimizing Whitening for Quality

Optimizing whitening means getting the best quality white rice with the highest yield and lowest energy cost. Quality here refers to whiteness, head rice yield (whole grains), and freedom from defects. To optimize, you need to control several factors: the type of rice, moisture content, machine settings, and operator skill. First, rice variety matters: long-grain rice tends to break more than short-grain, so adjust pressure accordingly. Moisture content of brown rice should be 12-14% for best milling. If too dry, grains crack; if too wet, bran sticks and milling is uneven. Test moisture with a meter or by biting a grain: it should be hard but not brittle. Second, machine settings: the clearance between the roller and screen, the rotational speed, and the feed rate. Start with the manufacturer's recommendation, then fine-tune. For a friction whitener, a clearance of 10-12 mm is typical. Increase pressure gradually until you achieve desired DOM (8-10%). Check broken grains every 15 minutes: if more than 5% are broken, reduce pressure. Third, keep the machine clean and well-maintained. Worn rollers or screens cause uneven milling and breakage. Replace them as needed. Fourth, train operators to listen and watch: a steady sound and uniform rice output indicate good operation. Fifth, consider multi-pass milling: instead of one heavy pass, use two or three lighter passes. This reduces breakage and heat. For example, first pass removes 5% bran, second pass another 3%, third pass 2%. This gentle approach yields more head rice. Finally, monitor energy consumption: whitening uses a lot of electricity. Optimize by avoiding over-milling and keeping machines at peak efficiency. In Africa, where power can be unreliable, a diesel generator may be needed, so fuel cost matters. By optimizing, you can increase head rice yield by 5-10%, which translates to more income. Remember, quality rice sells faster and at a higher price. So take the time to adjust and test. Keep records of settings and results to learn what works best for your rice..Trim()

Sifting and Grading Equipment

Sifting and grading equipment separates rice by size and removes impurities. In Africa, common tools include rotary sifters, vibrating screens, and manual sieves. A rotary sifter has a rotating drum with perforated screens; it spins at 200-300 RPM, separating broken rice from head rice. For small mills, a vibrating screen with 2-3 decks is affordable. The top deck might have 4 mm holes to remove large stones, the middle deck 2 mm holes to separate head rice, and the bottom deck 1 mm holes for small brokens. Capacity ranges from 500 kg/hour to 2 tons/hour. Locally fabricated screens from Uganda or Nigeria cost $500-$2000. Operating tips: adjust screen angle to 10-15 degrees for optimal flow; clean screens every 4 hours to prevent clogging; replace worn screens when holes enlarge by 10%. Safety: lock out power before maintenance. In rural areas, women often use hand sieves with 2 mm mesh to grade rice for local markets. This manual method handles 20-50 kg/hour but is labor-intensive. For better efficiency, a pedal-operated sifter can double output. Regular maintenance includes checking belt tension and lubricating bearings weekly. Remember, proper sifting reduces broken rice in final product, increasing market value by 10-20%..Trim()

Length and Thickness Grading

Length and thickness grading separates rice grains into uniform sizes. This is crucial because buyers pay more for consistent grain length. The main equipment is a length grader, often a disk or indent cylinder separator. An indent cylinder has small pockets that lift shorter grains as it rotates; longer grains stay in the cylinder and exit separately. Typical cylinder speed is 30-50 RPM. For thickness grading, a thickness grader uses slotted screens: thin grains fall through slots, while thick grains pass over. In Africa, local artisans in Tanzania and Nigeria make simple length graders using wire mesh with 6 mm and 8 mm slots. A motorized unit costs $1,500-$3,000 and processes 1-2 tons/hour. Manual length grading using a slotted sieve can achieve 100 kg/hour. Adjustments: set indent cylinder pockets to match desired length (e.g., 7 mm for long grain). For thickness, slot width should be 1.8-2.0 mm for standard rice. Maintenance: clean pockets daily with a brush; check for worn slots every 100 hours. Calibration: test with a sample of known grain sizes. Safety: wear gloves when handling screens. In West Africa, length grading is often done after destoning to ensure uniform product for export. Proper grading reduces broken rice during milling and improves cooking quality. Aim for 90% uniformity in head rice length..Trim()

Destoners and Gravity Separators

Destoners and gravity separators remove stones and other heavy impurities from rice. A destoner uses a vibrating deck with a porous surface and upward airflow. Sampling Techniques for Paddy and Rice

Sampling is the first step in quality control. If you test a bad sample, you get wrong results. For paddy, take samples from different parts of the bag or truck. Use a probe or scoop to take at least 5 samples from each lot. Mix them together to make a composite sample. For a 50 kg bag, take 200-300 grams. For a truckload, take 1 kg from 10 different spots. In Africa, many mills use simple tools: a metal scoop, a clean bucket, and sample bags. Avoid sampling from the top only; grain settles. Take from bottom and middle too. If you see insects or mold, note it. Send samples to a lab if you can. Otherwise, test on-site. Remember: a good sample represents the whole lot. Never mix old and new samples. Keep samples in a cool, dry place. For export, follow buyer's sampling plan. For local sales, a simple composite sample works. Train workers to sample correctly. One bad sample can reject a whole truck. So do it right..Trim()

Moisture Content Measurement

Moisture content (MC) is the percentage of water in paddy or rice. Too high MC (>14%) causes mold and insects. Too low (<12%) makes rice brittle and breaks during milling. For paddy, ideal MC for milling is 12-14%. For milled rice, store at 12-13%. How to measure? The best tool is a moisture meter. In Africa, many mills use a digital moisture meter (e.g., Kett or Dickey-john). They cost $200-$500. If you don't have one, use the salt method: mix 10g of rice with 20g of dry salt in a jar. Shake for 2 minutes. If salt sticks to the rice, MC is too high (>14%). If it doesn't, MC is okay. Another method: bite a grain. If it cracks easily, MC is low. If it is soft, MC is high. For accurate results, use a meter. Calibrate it weekly with a standard. Take 3 readings and average. For paddy, test before buying. For rice, test after milling. If MC is high, dry in the sun on a tarpaulin for 2-3 hours. Turn every 30 minutes. If MC is low, add moisture by spraying water and mixing, then rest for 24 hours. Always record MC in a logbook. Milling yield is the percentage of milled rice you get from paddy. Head rice recovery is the percentage of whole grains after milling. Physical and Chemical Quality Tests

Physical tests check appearance, size, and defects. Chemical tests check for contaminants. For physical tests, take 100g of rice. Spread on a tray. If you find defects, re-clean or re-mill. For export, follow ISO 7301 standard. For local market, aim for clean, uniform rice. Simple tools: a sieve, a scale, and a notebook. Cooking quality determines if buyers will come back. Cook a sample: take 100g rice, wash twice, add 200ml water (1:2 ratio). Boil for 15-20 minutes. Energy Consumption in Rice Mills

In a typical small to medium rice mill in Africa, energy is the second-largest cost after paddy purchase. Most mills run on diesel generators, grid electricity (where available), or a mix. A 2-ton per hour mill usually has a 30-50 kW motor for the huller, a 15-20 kW motor for the polisher, and smaller motors for elevators, blowers, and conveyors. Total connected load can be 80-120 kW. If you run a diesel generator, you consume about 0.3 liters of diesel per kWh generated. So running an 80 kW load for 8 hours uses roughly 192 liters of diesel per day. At $1.20 per liter, that's $230 daily just for fuel. Grid electricity is cheaper per kWh but often unreliable, forcing you to keep a generator on standby. The main energy consumers are the huller (40-50% of total), the polisher (20-30%), and the blowers and conveyors (15-20%). Lighting and office loads are minor but can be reduced with LEDs. To cut energy costs, start by measuring. Install a simple kWh meter on your main panel. Record daily energy use for two weeks. Note when you run at full load versus idle. Many mills waste energy by running motors at no load during breaks. Simple actions like switching off conveyors when not needed, keeping belts tight, and sharpening huller blades can reduce energy per ton by 10-20%. Also, consider the cost per ton of paddy milled. If you mill 16 tons per day and spend $230 on diesel, that's $14.4 per ton just for energy. Your total milling cost might be $30-40 per ton. So energy is a big lever. Track it weekly..Trim()

Energy Auditing and Efficiency Measures

An energy audit is a simple walk-through of your mill to find where energy is wasted and how to fix it. You don't need a professional auditor to start. Begin by listing all motors and their rated power (kW). Note their running hours per day. Use a clamp meter (cost: $50-100) to measure actual current drawn by each motor. Compare actual kW to rated kW. If a 30 kW motor draws 35 kW, it's overloaded; if it draws 15 kW, it's oversized or not working hard. Check power factor: low power factor (below 0.8) means you pay for reactive power. Install capacitors to correct it. Next, look at mechanical efficiency. Worn belts, misaligned pulleys, and lack of lubrication increase friction. A loose belt can waste 5-10% of motor energy. Sharpen or replace huller blades every 200-300 hours of operation. Dull blades increase energy use by 15-25%. Also, check the aspirator and blower systems. Leaks in ducting waste air and energy. Seal them. For lighting, replace incandescent and fluorescent tubes with LED. A 100W incandescent can be replaced by a 15W LED, saving 85% with the same brightness. Use occupancy sensors in storage areas. For larger mills, consider variable frequency drives (VFDs) on motors that run at partial load, like conveyors. A VFD can cut energy use by 20-50% on those motors. Finally, schedule milling during off-peak hours if your utility offers lower rates. In many African countries, off-peak power is 30-50% cheaper. Keep an energy logbook: record daily kWh, diesel liters, and tons milled. Calculate specific energy consumption (kWh per ton). Aim to reduce it by 5% each quarter. For a mill that spends $200/day on diesel, that's $40-60 saved daily. Payback can be 3-5 years. Biomass is even more promising for rice mills because you already have rice husk.
