Module 2 · Wheat Kernel Anatomy and Composition

Structure of the Wheat Kernel

The wheat kernel, also called a grain, is a small seed that contains three main parts: the bran, the germ, and the endosperm. Understanding these parts is the first step to milling good flour. The bran is the hard outer layer that protects the seed. It makes up about 14-15% of the kernel weight. The bran is rich in fibre, vitamins, and minerals, but it does not mill into fine flour easily. The germ is the tiny embryo at the bottom of the kernel. It is about 2-3% of the weight and contains oil, vitamins, and proteins. The germ can go rancid quickly if not stored properly, so many millers remove it to make flour with a longer shelf life. The endosperm is the largest part, about 82-84% of the kernel. It is mostly starch and protein, and it is what we grind into white flour..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, wheat kernels are usually smaller and harder than those grown in Europe or North America. For example, a typical kernel from Ethiopia or Kenya weighs about 30-40 milligrams. When you inspect wheat, you can see the creaseâa deep groove along one side of the kernel. This crease traps dirt and small stones, so cleaning is very important before milling. The brush end, opposite the germ, has fine hairs called brush. These hairs can also hold dust..Trim()

.Trim()

To see the structure yourself, take a few wheat kernels and soak them in water for 24 hours. Then cut them lengthwise with a sharp knife. You will see the bran as a dark outer layer, the endosperm as a white starchy area, and the germ as a small yellow-brown part at the bottom. This simple test helps you understand what your mill is doing when it separates bran from endosperm..Trim()

.Trim()

When you mill, you want to break the kernel so that the endosperm breaks into small pieces while the bran stays in larger flakes. This makes it easier to sift out the bran. If you mill too hard, the bran shatters into tiny bits that mix with the flour, making it darker and speckled. So knowing the kernel structure helps you adjust your mill settings for better flour..Trim()

Chemical Composition of Wheat

Wheat kernels are made of water, carbohydrates, proteins, fats, minerals, and vitamins. The exact amounts vary with variety, growing conditions, and storage. On average, a dry wheat kernel contains about 70-75% carbohydrates, 10-15% protein, 2-3% fat, 1-2% minerals (ash), and 12-14% water. These numbers matter to millers because they affect how the wheat mills and how the flour behaves..Trim()

.Trim()

Carbohydrates are mainly starch, which is the energy store of the seed. Starch is what makes flour thicken and gel when heated. The protein in wheat is special because it can form gluten when mixed with water. Gluten gives dough its elasticity and strength. Hard wheats, like those grown in the highlands of Ethiopia and Kenya, have more protein (12-14%) than soft wheats (8-10%). That is why hard wheats are used for bread and soft wheats for cakes and biscuits..Trim()

.Trim()

Fat is mostly in the germ. Even though it is only 2-3% of the kernel, fat can go rancid and give flour a bad smell. That is why whole wheat flour has a shorter shelf life than white flour. Minerals, measured as ash, are mostly in the bran. Ash content tells you how much bran is in the flour. White flour has low ash (0.4-0.6%), while whole wheat flour has high ash (1.5-2.0%)..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, wheat is often grown in areas with high temperatures and drought. This can reduce protein content and make the grain harder. For example, wheat from Sudan or northern Nigeria may have lower protein but still mill well if handled correctly. To check protein, you can send a sample to a lab, but a simple field test is to chew a few kernels: hard, glassy kernels usually have more protein than soft, floury ones..Trim()

.Trim()

Understanding composition helps you decide how to mill. High-protein wheat needs more water during tempering (adding water before milling) to soften the bran and toughen the endosperm. Low-protein wheat may need less water. By knowing what is in your wheat, you can adjust your mill and get the flour you want..Trim()

Wheat Classes and Varieties

Wheat is not just one crop. There are many classes and varieties, each with different milling and baking qualities. In Africa, the most common classes are hard red winter, hard red spring, soft red winter, durum, and soft white. These names tell you about the kernel's hardness, colour, and the season it is grown..Trim()

.Trim()

Hard red winter wheat is grown in places with cold winters, like the highlands of Ethiopia and Kenya. It has high protein and is good for bread. Hard red spring wheat is grown in cooler areas and also has high protein. Soft red winter wheat has lower protein and is used for cakes, biscuits, and pastries. Durum wheat is the hardest and is used for pasta like macaroni and spaghetti. Soft white wheat is low in protein and is used for noodles and flatbreads..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, local varieties are also important. For example, in Ethiopia, farmers grow improved varieties like 'Kakaba' and 'Danda'a', which are hard wheats with good bread-making quality. In Kenya, 'Kenya Nyati' and 'Kenya Fahari' are popular. In South Africa, 'SST' varieties are common. These varieties are bred for drought tolerance, disease resistance, and good milling quality..Trim()

.Trim()

When you buy wheat for milling, ask for the class and variety. If you want to make bread, choose hard wheat with 12-14% protein. For cakes, choose soft wheat with 8-10% protein. If you mix them, you get medium protein, which is good for all-purpose flour. You can also blend different varieties to get the protein level you need..Trim()

.Trim()

A simple test to tell hard from soft wheat: take a kernel and bite it. Hard wheat is vitreous (glassy) and shatters; soft wheat is opaque and crushes easily. Another test is to cut the kernel: hard wheat looks glassy inside, soft wheat looks floury. Knowing your wheat class helps you set your mill and predict how the flour will bake..Trim()

Wheat Grading and Specifications

Wheat grading is a system to judge the quality of a batch of wheat. It looks at factors like moisture, test weight, foreign matter, broken kernels, and damaged kernels. In Africa, many countries follow national grading standards, often similar to those used in Europe or the USA. For example, Kenya has grades like Grade 1, Grade 2, and Grade 3, based on test weight and defects..Trim()

.Trim()

Moisture content is critical. If wheat is too wet (above 14%), it can grow mould and rot. If it is too dry (below 10%), it becomes brittle and shatters during milling, making too much bran powder. The ideal moisture for safe storage is 12-13%. You can check moisture with a moisture meter, but a simple field method is to bite a kernel: if it is hard and cracks, it is dry; if it is soft and chewy, it is too wet..Trim()

.Trim()

Test weight is the weight of a fixed volume of wheat, usually expressed in kilograms per hectolitre (kg/hl). High test weight (above 76 kg/hl) means plump, heavy kernels with more endosperm and less bran. Low test weight (below 70 kg/hl) means thin, shrivelled kernels with less flour. In Africa, a good wheat should have a test weight of at least 75 kg/hl..Trim()

.Trim()

Foreign matter includes stones, dirt, seeds, and chaff. Grade 1 wheat should have less than 1% foreign matter. Broken kernels and damaged kernels (sprouted, insect-damaged, or diseased) should also be low. Too many broken kernels make flour specky and reduce yield..Trim()

.Trim()

To grade wheat, you need a sample, a scale, a sieve, and a moisture meter. Take a 1 kg sample, remove foreign matter by hand or sieve, and weigh the clean wheat. Then calculate percentages. If you are buying wheat, always ask for a grading certificate or test the wheat yourself. Good grading means better flour and less waste..Trim()

Impact of Kernel Properties on Milling

The properties of the wheat kernel directly affect how it mills and the quality of the flour. Hardness, size, moisture, and protein content are the most important. Hard wheat is harder to grind and requires more energy, but it produces flour with good bread-making quality. Soft wheat grinds easily but gives weaker flour. If you mill hard wheat too finely, you can damage the starch and make the flour sticky..Trim()

.Trim()

Kernel size matters. Large kernels have more endosperm and less bran, so they give higher flour yield. Small kernels have more bran relative to endosperm, so yield is lower. In Africa, wheat kernels are often smaller due to drought stress. You can adjust your mill to handle smaller kernels by reducing the gap between the stones or rollers. If the gap is too wide, the kernels pass through without breaking; if too narrow, you get too much bran powder..Trim()

.Trim()

Moisture content affects milling too. Dry wheat shatters, creating fine bran particles that are hard to sift out. Wet wheat gums up the mill and can clog screens. The ideal moisture for milling is 14-15% for hard wheat and 13-14% for soft wheat. This is achieved by tempering: adding water to the wheat and letting it sit for 12-24 hours. Tempering makes the bran tough and the endosperm soft, so they separate cleanly..Trim()

.Trim()

Protein content influences gluten strength. High-protein wheat needs more water in tempering to develop gluten properly. Low-protein wheat needs less. If you mill low-protein wheat for bread, you may get a flat loaf. You can blend it with high-protein wheat to improve it..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, damaged kernels (sprouted, insect-damaged) produce poor flour. Sprouted kernels have high enzyme activity that breaks down starch, making sticky dough. Always remove damaged kernels before milling. By understanding these properties, you can adjust your mill and tempering to get the best flour from your wheat..Trim()

Hands-on Wheat Inspection

Hands-on inspection is the best way to judge wheat quality before you buy or mill. You do not need a laboratory. With a few simple tools and your senses, you can check moisture, test weight, foreign matter, and kernel condition. This lesson shows you how..Trim()

.Trim()

First, collect a representative sample. Take small handfuls from different parts of the bag or truck. Mix them together. You need about 1 kg for a good test. Look at the sample: good wheat should be uniform in colour, with no mould, no insects, and no bad smell. If you see black spots, it may be diseased. If it smells musty, it is mouldy..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, check moisture. Bite a few kernels. If they are hard and crack between your teeth, moisture is likely below 13%. If they are soft and chewy, moisture is above 14%. For a more accurate test, use a moisture meter. In Africa, you can buy small moisture meters for about $50-$100. If you don't have one, use the bite test and also squeeze a handful: if kernels stick together, it's too wet..Trim()

.Trim()

Check test weight. Fill a 1-litre container with wheat, level it off, and weigh it. A good test weight is 750-800 grams per litre (75-80 kg/hl). If it weighs less than 700 grams, the wheat is light and will give low flour yield. You can also use a 1-pint container and convert..Trim()

.Trim()

Remove foreign matter. Spread a sample on a clean surface and pick out stones, dirt, seeds, and chaff. Weigh the clean wheat and calculate the percentage of foreign matter. Grade 1 wheat should have less than 1%. Also look for broken kernels and damaged kernels. Count them in a 100-kernel sample. More than 5% broken means poor quality..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, do a simple milling test. Grind a small amount in a coffee grinder or hand mill. Feel the flour: it should be smooth, not gritty. If it is gritty, the bran is not separated well. By doing these checks, you can avoid buying bad wheat and get better flour..Trim()

Roller Mill Components and Operation

A roller mill is the heart of a flour mill. It breaks wheat kernels open and scrapes the endosperm from the bran. The main parts are the feed hopper, feed roll, grinding rolls (usually two pairs), and the gap adjustment. In Africa, many mills use roller mills from Turkey, China, or Europe. The rolls are made of chilled cast iron or steel, and they have a spiral groove pattern called corrugation. The number of grooves per inch (or per centimeter) and the spiral angle determine how the wheat is cut. For first break, you want a coarse corrugation, like 10 to 12 grooves per inch, with a spiral angle of 4 to 6 percent. For later breaks, finer corrugation, like 20 to 28 grooves per inch. The two rolls in a pair turn in opposite directions. One turns faster than the other. The speed ratio is called the differential. For break rolls, a differential of 2.5:1 is common; for reduction rolls, 1.25:1. The gap between the rolls is critical. For first break, set the gap to about 0.5 to 0.6 mm. You measure it with a feeler gauge. If the gap is too wide, the wheat passes through uncut. If too narrow, you get too much bran powder. The feed roll controls the flow of wheat into the grinding zone. It turns slower than the grinding rolls and ensures an even curtain of grain. The whole mill is driven by an electric motor, often 30 to 75 kW per pair of rolls. In many African mills, the rolls are changed every 3 to 6 months, depending on tonnage. A typical 100-tonne-per-day mill might have 8 to 12 roller mills, each with two or four rolls. The operator must check the temperature of the rolls; if they get too hot, the flour quality drops. You can feel the roll temperature by handâif it's too hot to touch, stop and check the gap or the feed rate..Trim()

Plansifters and Purifiers

After the roller mill, the ground material goes to a plansifter. A plansifter is a stack of rectangular sieves that separate particles by size. It shakes back and forth, and the particles fall through different screens. In a typical mill, a plansifter has 8 to 16 sections, each with a different mesh size. The top screens are coarser, like 20 mesh (about 0.8 mm openings), and the bottom screens are finer, like 100 mesh (0.15 mm). The material that stays on top of a screen is called 'overs' and goes to the next break or reduction roll. The material that passes through is 'throughs' and may go to a purifier or to the flour bin. A purifier is a machine that separates pure endosperm from bran and germ using a combination of sieving and air. It has a sloping, vibrating screen and a fan that blows air upward. The lighter bran particles are lifted by the air and carried away, while the heavier endosperm falls through the screen. In Africa, purifiers are often used in semolina mills for pasta, but also in flour mills to get cleaner flour. A common purifier has a capacity of 1 to 2 tonnes per hour. The screen mesh is usually 10 to 14 mesh. The air speed is adjusted so that bran is lifted but endosperm is not. You can test by putting a sample on the screen and watching: if too much endosperm is blown away, reduce the air. The plansifter is driven by a motor with an eccentric weight, creating a gyratory motion. The speed is around 200 to 250 rpm. The sieves need to be cleaned regularly. In many African mills, workers brush the screens every shift to prevent blocking. If the screens are blocked, the sifting efficiency drops, and flour gets mixed with bran. A simple check: take a handful of material from the overs and rub it between your fingers. If you feel fine flour, the screen is passing too much. Adjust the tension or replace the screen..Trim()

Bran Finishers and Detachers

Bran finishers and detachers are machines that recover flour still attached to bran. After the break rolls, the bran particles often carry small chunks of endosperm. A bran finisher beats the bran to shake off the endosperm. It has a horizontal rotor with beaters that rotate at high speed inside a cylindrical screen. The bran enters one end and is beaten against the screen. The endosperm falls through the screen, and the bran exits the other end. A typical bran finisher has a rotor speed of 800 to 1200 rpm and a screen with 0.5 to 1.0 mm openings. Capacity is about 1 to 3 tonnes per hour. In many African mills, bran finishers are used after the last break roll. The recovered flour is called 'finisher flour' and is usually lower quality, so it might be added to the feed flour or to the main flour depending on the market. A detacher is similar but gentler. It is used after the reduction rolls to break up lumps of flour that form when the rolls are set too close. A detacher has a rotor with pins or blades that turn at 500 to 800 rpm. It doesn't have a screen; it just breaks the lumps. The material then goes to the plansifter. You can tell if a detacher is needed if you see lumps in the flour after the reduction roll. In a typical 100-tonne mill, you might have 2 to 4 bran finishers and 4 to 6 detachers. Maintenance is simple: check the beaters for wear every month. Worn beaters reduce efficiency. Also check the screen for holes. A hole as small as 2 mm will let bran through and contaminate the flour. You can patch small holes with a piece of screen and wire, but it's better to replace the screen. In Africa, where spare parts can be hard to get, many mills keep a spare screen and a set of beaters on hand..Trim()

Pneumatic Conveying Systems

Pneumatic conveying moves flour, bran, and semolina through pipes using air. In a modern mill, you rarely see bucket elevators for flour; instead, a fan blows air through a pipe, and the material is carried along. There are two main types: positive pressure and negative pressure (vacuum). Positive pressure is common for moving flour from the mill to the packing bin. A blower pushes air at 15 to 25 meters per second through a pipe. The material is fed into the pipe via a rotary valve. The air speed must be high enough to keep particles suspended but not so high that it damages the flour. For flour, a speed of 18 to 22 m/s is typical. The pipe diameter is usually 100 to 150 mm. The system needs a cyclone or a filter receiver at the end to separate the flour from the air. In Africa, many mills use a simple cyclone and a rotary valve to discharge the flour. Negative pressure systems are used for dusty areas, like the intake of wheat or the collection of bran. A fan pulls air through the pipes, and the material is sucked into a cyclone. The advantage is that if there is a leak, air is pulled in, not blown out, so dust stays inside. The disadvantage is that the fan must handle the material, so it wears faster. For a 100-tonne mill, you might have a central vacuum system with a 30 kW fan. The pipes should be as straight as possible; every bend adds resistance. Use long-radius bends, not sharp elbows. Also, keep the pipe size consistent. If you reduce the diameter, the air speed increases and can cause blockages. A common problem in African mills is moisture. If the flour is too damp, it sticks to the pipe walls and causes blockages. Keep the flour moisture below 14%. You can check by squeezing a handful; if it holds shape, it's too wet. Clean the system daily: open the inspection hatches and remove any buildup..Trim()

Maintenance and Roll Changes

Roller mill rolls wear out. The corrugations become dull, and the gap becomes uneven. In a busy African mill, you might need to change rolls every 3 to 6 months. The first sign is poor extraction: you get less flour and more bran in the break system. You can check by measuring the gap with a feeler gauge. If the gap is more than 0.1 mm wider than the setting, the rolls are worn. Also, look at the flour: if it feels gritty, the rolls are not cutting properly. To change a roll, you need to lock out the power, remove the guards, and disconnect the feed roll. Then you remove the old roll and install a new one. The new roll must be balanced and aligned. Use a dial indicator to check runout; it should be less than 0.05 mm. The gap is set with a feeler gauge and locked. After changing rolls, run the mill empty for 10 minutes to check for vibration. Then feed a small amount of wheat and check the stock. You also need to maintain the rest of the mill. Grease the bearings every 500 hours. Check the belts and chains for tension. Clean the plansifter screens daily. In many African mills, there is no dedicated maintenance team, so the operator does it. Keep a logbook: record the date, the roll number, and the gap setting. This helps you predict when the next change is due. For a 100-tonne mill, a full roll change might take 4 to 6 hours per roll pair. Spare rolls are expensive, so many mills send worn rolls to a specialist for re-corrugation. In Africa, there are companies in South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria that re-corrugate rolls. The cost is about 30% of a new roll. Always keep at least one spare roll pair on site. And train your staff: a well-trained operator can double the life of a roll by setting the gap correctly and feeding evenly..Trim()

Automation and Control Systems in Milling

Automation in milling uses sensors, computers, and controllers to run the mill efficiently. In Africa, many small mills still run manually, but larger mills are adopting automation. The most common system is a PLC (Programmable Logic Controller) that controls the roller mills, plansifters, and conveyors. Sensors measure the feed rate, the roll gap, the temperature, and the motor load. For example, a sensor on the feed roll tells the PLC how much wheat is entering. If the flow drops, the PLC speeds up the feed roll. If the motor load gets too high, the PLC opens the gap to prevent a jam. A typical automated mill has a central control room with a computer screen showing the whole flow. The operator can start and stop machines with a mouse. One key automation is the gap control on the roller mills. Instead of turning a handwheel, a motor adjusts the gap based on the load. This keeps the gap constant even as rolls wear. Another is the moisture control. A sensor measures the moisture of the wheat before the first break. If it's too dry, a valve adds water. The target moisture is 15.5 to 16.5% for hard wheat, 14.5 to 15.5% for soft wheat. In Africa, automation can be a challenge because of power fluctuations. Many mills install voltage stabilizers and backup generators. Also, dust and heat can damage electronics. So control panels are often placed in air-conditioned rooms. For a small mill, you don't need full automation. You can start with a simple variable frequency drive (VFD) on the main motors. A VFD lets you adjust the speed of the rolls and the fans, saving energy. It also gives a soft start, reducing wear. A VFD for a 75 kW motor costs about $2,000 to $3,000. Many African millers find that a VFD pays for itself in six months through energy savings. Finally, keep records. Even a simple logbook of run hours, gaps, and temperatures helps you spot problems before they stop production..Trim()

Whole Wheat and Graham Flour Production

Whole wheat flour is made by grinding the entire wheat kernelâbran, germ, and endospermâso nothing is removed. To produce it, start with clean, dry wheat (moisture below 14%). High-Gluten and Low-Gluten Flours

Gluten is the protein that gives bread its structure and chew. High-gluten flour contains 12â14% protein, while low-gluten flour has 6â9%. Organic flour is milled from wheat grown without synthetic pesticides or fertilizers. To be certified organic, the wheat must be grown on land free from prohibited substances for at least 3 years. Gluten-Free Flours and Blends

Gluten-free flours are made from grains, seeds, and nuts that do not contain gluten. Common ones include rice flour, maize flour, millet flour, sorghum flour, teff flour, and cassava flour. Mill Production Planning and Scheduling

Production planning starts with knowing your mill's capacity. A small roller mill in Kenya might process 500 kg of wheat per hour. If you run one 8-hour shift, that's 4,000 kg per day. Your weekly plan should match orders. For example, if a bakery needs 200 bags per week, you need 3 days of milling. Use a simple wall chart or a notebook to write down orders, wheat stock, and milling days. Many African millers use a 'first in, first out' rule for wheat. Schedule harder wheat first because it takes more power. Also, group similar flour types to reduce changeover time. Changeover from white flour to whole wheat takes about 30 minutes for cleaning. So, if you have multiple orders, mill all white flour first, then whole wheat. Power availability is a big issue. In Nigeria, many millers run generators during grid outages. Schedule milling during times when electricity is stable, often early morning. Keep a generator on standby but only run it when needed to save fuel. Labor: assign one person to feed wheat, one to monitor the mill, and one to bag flour. Train them to watch for jams. A jam can stop production for 20 minutes. So, preventive checks every hour.
