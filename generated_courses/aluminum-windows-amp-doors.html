Why Aluminum is Popular in African Construction

Aluminum windows and doors are everywhere in Africa, from Lagos to Nairobi to Johannesburg. The main reason is that aluminum does not rust like steel or rot like wood. In humid coastal cities like Mombasa or Accra, steel windows can rust through in two or three years. Aluminum, on the other hand, forms a thin protective oxide layer that stops corrosion. Common Window and Door Types in African Homes

When you start working with aluminum, you will mostly build four types of windows and three types of doors. Knowing these will help you talk to customers and plan your materials. The most common window is the sliding window. It has two or three panels that slide horizontally on a track. A standard sliding window for a bedroom is 1.2 meters wide by 1.2 meters high. The third type is the awning window, which opens outward from the top. It is good for rain protection because you can leave it open during light rain. Module 2 · Measuring and Site Assessment

How to Measure Windows and Doors Accurately

To measure a window or door opening for aluminum fabrication, you need the width and height of the rough opening (the hole in the wall where the frame will sit). Use a steel tape measure (5m or 8m) and always measure in millimeters. For width, measure at three points: top, middle, and bottom of the opening. Write down the smallest number. For height, measure left, center, and right. Again, take the smallest. For example, if top width is 1200mm, middle is 1195mm, bottom is 1198mm, the width is 1195mm. This ensures the frame fits even if the wall is not perfectly straight..Trim()

.Trim()

For doors, also measure the diagonal from top-left to bottom-right and top-right to bottom-left. If the two diagonals differ by more than 6mm, the opening is out of square and you must note that. You will need to either adjust the frame or plan for packing. Always measure from the inside face of the wall to the inside face of the opposite wall. Do not measure from the outside because plaster or cladding can change the thickness..Trim()

.Trim()

If the opening has a sill or threshold, measure from the top of the sill to the top of the opening. For windows, measure from the bottom of the opening (where the frame will rest) to the top. Never assume the opening is a perfect rectangle; old buildings in Africa often have uneven walls. Use a spirit level to check if the sill is level. If not, measure at the highest point of the sill and note the difference..Trim()

.Trim()

Record all measurements in a notebook or on your phone. Take a photo of the opening with the tape measure visible for reference. Double-check every number before leaving the site. A 5mm mistake can mean a window that does not fit, costing you time and money. Remember, aluminum frames cannot be easily cut and re-welded on site, so accuracy is critical..Trim()

Using a Tape Measure and Laser Measure

A steel tape measure is your basic tool. Choose one with a blade width of at least 16mm and a length of 5m or 8m. The hook at the end should be loose so it can slide for inside and outside measurements. When measuring, hook the end on one side of the opening and extend the tape to the other side. Keep the tape straight and taut. Read the measurement at the point where the tape meets the opposite edge. For accuracy, look directly at the mark, not at an angle. Most tapes show millimeters and centimeters; always use millimeters to avoid confusion..Trim()

.Trim()

A laser measure is faster and more accurate for long distances. Tools like Bosch GLM 50 or Leica DISTO are common. Turn it on, place the base against one side of the opening, and press the button. The laser dot hits the opposite side, and the distance appears on the screen. For width, measure from left to right. For height, measure from bottom to top. Laser measures can also calculate area and volume, but for windows and doors you only need length. They work well in bright sunlight if you use a target plate. Always check the battery before going to site..Trim()

.Trim()

When using a tape, avoid the common mistake of reading the wrong side of the tape. Some tapes have imperial on one side and metric on the other. Stick to metric. If you use a laser, make sure the device is set to millimeters, not inches. Also, laser measures can be fooled by reflective surfaces like glass or polished tiles. In those cases, place a piece of white paper at the target point..Trim()

.Trim()

For both tools, measure each dimension at least twice. If the two readings differ by more than 2mm, take a third reading and use the average. Record the numbers immediately. In Africa, dust and heat can affect tape measures; keep them clean and dry. A laser measure costs more but saves time on large sites. For a beginner, start with a good tape measure and upgrade later..Trim()

Checking for Square, Level, and Plumb

Square means the opening has 90-degree corners. Level means horizontal surfaces are flat, not sloping. Plumb means vertical surfaces are straight up and down. These three checks are essential before you order an aluminum frame. If the opening is out of square, the frame will not fit properly, and you will have gaps or difficulty opening the window..Trim()

.Trim()

To check square, measure the two diagonals of the opening. For a window 1200mm wide and 1500mm high, the diagonal should be the same both ways. Use a tape measure from the top-left corner to the bottom-right corner, then from top-right to bottom-left. If the difference is more than 6mm, the opening is out of square. Note the difference and decide if you need to pack the frame or adjust the wall..Trim()

.Trim()

To check level, use a spirit level (600mm or 1200mm long). Place it on the sill or floor where the frame will sit. If the bubble is not centered, the surface is not level. Mark the high point. For a door, check the floor threshold. If it slopes, water may run in. You may need to use packers under the frame to make it level..Trim()

.Trim()

To check plumb, hold the spirit level vertically against the left and right sides of the opening. The bubble should be centered. If not, the wall is leaning. This is common in old buildings. You can either fix the wall or make the frame slightly smaller and pack it. Always check plumb at the top, middle, and bottom of each side..Trim()

.Trim()

Use a plumb bob (a weight on a string) for tall openings. Hang it from the top corner and see if the string touches the bottom corner. If it does not, the wall is out of plumb. Record all deviations. In your fabrication order, specify that the frame must be made to the smallest dimensions and that packers will be used. Never assume the opening is perfect. In Africa, many walls are built by hand, so deviations are normal. Your job is to measure and document them..Trim()

Assessing Wall Types: Block, Brick, and Concrete

The wall type determines how you fix the aluminum frame and what tools you need. In Africa, you will encounter three main types: concrete block (also called cement block or cinder block), clay brick, and reinforced concrete. Each has different hardness and drilling requirements..Trim()

.Trim()

Concrete blocks are large, hollow, and relatively soft. They are common in residential buildings. To fix a frame, you drill with a masonry bit (6mm or 8mm) and use plastic wall plugs and screws. The blocks can crumble if you drill too fast. Use a hammer drill on low speed. For a window, you typically fix through the frame into the wall every 600mm. If the block is hollow, use expanding anchors or chemical anchors for better grip..Trim()

.Trim()

Clay bricks are smaller, solid, and harder than blocks. They are often used in older buildings. Drilling requires a hammer drill and a carbide-tipped bit. Bricks can crack if you drill too close to the edge. Keep at least 50mm from the edge. Use screws and plugs, or for heavy doors, use anchor bolts. Brick walls are usually straighter than block walls, but they can still be out of plumb..Trim()

.Trim()

Reinforced concrete is the hardest. It contains steel rebar. You cannot drill through rebar with a standard masonry bit. You need a hammer drill with a concrete bit, and you must avoid rebar. Use a rebar detector or drill slowly and feel for resistance. If you hit rebar, move the hole 50mm to the side. For fixing, use heavy-duty anchors or cast-in inserts. Concrete walls are often found in high-rise buildings and are very strong but difficult to modify..Trim()

.Trim()

Before drilling, test the wall with a small hammer. A dull sound means hollow block; a sharp sound means solid brick or concrete. Also check for plaster thickness. Plaster can be 10-20mm thick and may hide the true wall type. Always wear safety glasses and a dust mask when drilling. In Africa, dust from concrete and brick is a health hazard. Use a vacuum or wet the area to reduce dust..Trim()

Documentation is the bridge between the site and the factory. If you write measurements on a scrap of paper and lose it, you waste time and money. Use a standard form or a notebook with pre-printed columns. For each window or door, record: location (e.g., 'Master bedroom, north wall'), opening width (smallest), opening height (smallest), diagonal difference, wall type, and any notes (e.g., 'sill not level, 10mm slope')..Trim()

.Trim()

Take clear photos. Use your phone. Take one photo of the whole opening, one close-up of the tape measure showing the width, and one showing the height. Include a reference object like your hand or a pencil for scale. Name the photos with the location and date. For example, 'MasterBedroom_Window_2025-03-15.jpg'. Store them in a folder on your phone or cloud. If you have a site plan, mark each opening with a number and use that number in your notes..Trim()

.Trim()

Create a simple sketch for each opening. Draw the shape (rectangle, arch, etc.) and write the dimensions on the lines. Note which way the window or door opens (inward, outward, sliding). For doors, note the hinge side (left or right) and whether it is a single or double door. For windows, note if it is fixed, casement, sliding, or awning. This information is critical for fabrication..Trim()

.Trim()

After measuring, review your notes with a colleague or your supervisor. Read the numbers aloud. Check that the smallest width and height are clearly marked. If you have a laser measure, download the data to your phone or write it down immediately. Never rely on memory. In Africa, network coverage can be poor, so keep a paper backup..Trim()

Finally, give a copy of your documentation to the fabricator. Include your contact number in case they have questions. A well-documented job reduces errors and speeds up installation. Remember, the fabricator is not on site; they only know what you tell them. So be precise and complete..Trim()

Common Measuring Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Mistake 1: Measuring only once. Always measure at least twice. If the numbers differ, measure a third time. For example, if you get 1200mm and 1203mm, take the smaller (1200mm) and note the difference. A single measurement can be off by 5mm if the tape slips..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 2: Using the wrong units. Mixing inches and millimeters causes major errors. In Africa, building codes use metric. Always use millimeters. If you have an imperial tape, ignore the inch side. Write 'mm' after every number..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 3: Not checking for square. Many installers assume the opening is square. If you don't check diagonals, the frame may not fit. Always measure both diagonals. If they differ by more than 6mm, note it and plan to pack the frame..Trim()

.Trim()

Mistake 4: Measuring from the wrong surface. For a window, measure from the inside face of the wall, not from the plaster or tiles. Plaster can be uneven. If you measure from the plaster, the frame may be too small. Remove any loose plaster or trim before measuring..Trim()

Understanding Alloy Grades and Tempers

Aluminum alloy grades and tempers determine the strength, corrosion resistance, and workability of your profiles. The most common alloy for architectural aluminum is 6063, with temper T5 or T6. Alloy 6063 is magnesium-silicon based, offering good extrudability and moderate strength. Temper T5 means the profile is cooled from the extrusion press and artificially aged to increase strength. T6 is solution heat-treated and artificially aged, giving higher strength. In Africa, you'll often see profiles marked with these grades. For windows and doors, 6063-T5 is standard. Tempers range from soft (O) to hard (T6). Soft tempers are easy to bend but dent easily. Hard tempers are strong but may crack if bent. Corner Joints: Crimping, Screwing, and Welding

Corner joints are the backbone of any aluminum window or door. In Africa, where power can be unreliable, most workshops use mechanical joints: crimping and screwing. Crimping is fast and strong for standard windows. You need a crimping tool (manual or pneumatic), a hammer, and a steel block. First, cut your aluminum profiles at 45 degrees using a mitre saw. Clean the cut ends with a file. Insert a corner cleat (usually aluminum or plastic) into both profile ends. Then, using the crimping tool, press the outer walls of the profile into the cleat. Do this on both sides of the joint. For a stronger joint, add two self-tapping screws (usually 4.8mm x 25mm) through the profile into the cleat. Screwing alone is common for doors and heavy windows. Pre-drill with a 3.5mm bit to avoid cracking the aluminum. Welding aluminum is rare in small workshops because it requires a TIG welder and argon gas, which is expensive. But for high-end projects, welding gives a seamless finish. If you weld, use 4043 filler rod and clean the joint with a stainless steel brush before welding. After welding, grind the joint smooth. For most African workshops, crimping and screwing are the go-to methods. Always check the joint for squareness using a carpenter's square. A good corner joint should have no gaps and be perfectly 90 degrees. Test by trying to twist the frame; if it moves, tighten or add more screws. Remember, a weak corner will cause the whole window to sag, leading to problems with opening and closing..Trim()

Using Cleats and Corner Connectors

Cleats and corner connectors are the hidden heroes of aluminum assembly. They hold the frame together and keep it square. There are three main types: corner cleats (also called corner keys), T-connectors, and mullion connectors. For standard windows, you use a corner cleat that fits inside the hollow profile. These come in different sizes, so match the cleat to your profile's internal dimensions. In Africa, you'll find cleats made of aluminum, nylon, or zinc alloy. Nylon is cheap but can break under heavy load; aluminum is stronger. To install, cut your profiles at 45 degrees, then slide the cleat into both ends. You may need to tap it gently with a rubber mallet. Once in, crimp or screw as described in Lesson 1. For doors, use a heavier cleat with a screw port. T-connectors join a vertical and horizontal profile at 90 degrees, like where a window mullion meets the frame. These often have a flat plate that screws into both profiles. Mullion connectors are used when you have multiple windows side by side. They allow you to join two frames without a visible gap. Always apply a small amount of silicone sealant inside the joint before assembly to prevent water leaks. After joining, check for alignment: the faces of the profiles should be flush. If not, loosen and adjust. A common mistake is using a cleat that's too small, which leads to a loose joint. Always buy cleats from a reputable supplier and keep them dry to avoid corrosion. In coastal areas, use stainless steel screws to prevent rust..Trim()

Applying Sealants and Gaskets

Sealants and gaskets keep water and air out of your aluminum windows and doors. In Africa, heavy rain and dust make this step critical. You need two main products: silicone sealant and rubber gaskets (also called weatherstrips). Silicone sealant comes in cartridges and is applied with a caulking gun. Use neutral-cure silicone, not acetic-cure (which smells like vinegar and can corrode aluminum). Before applying, clean the surfaces with a cloth and alcohol to remove dust and oil. Cut the nozzle at a 45-degree angle to control the bead. For corner joints, apply a bead of silicone inside the joint before assembling, then another bead on the outside after assembly. Smooth it with a wet finger or a plastic spatula. Gaskets are rubber strips that fit into grooves on the profile. They come in different shapes: EPDM, silicone, or PVC. EPDM is best for African sun because it resists UV. To install, cut the gasket slightly longer than needed, then press it into the groove with a roller or a flat screwdriver. Don't stretch it; let it sit for a few minutes to relax. For sliding windows, use a fin gasket on the frame and a brush gasket on the sash. For casement windows, use a bubble gasket on the frame. After installation, test by spraying water on the window from a hose. If water leaks inside, add more sealant or replace the gasket. Remember, a good seal saves energy and prevents mold. In dusty areas, check gaskets every six months and replace if cracked..Trim()

Assembling Sliding Window Frames

Sliding windows are the most common in African homes because they save space and allow good ventilation. Assembling them requires precision. Start by cutting your frame profiles: the outer frame (top, bottom, and sides) and the sash profiles (the moving panels). Use a mitre saw set at 45 degrees for the outer frame corners. For the bottom track, you need a profile with a raised rail to guide the rollers. Assemble the outer frame first using corner cleats and screws. Use a smaller cleat for sashes. Install rollers on the bottom of each sash; typically two rollers per sash, positioned at the ends. The rollers should be adjustable so you can raise or lower the sash later. Then, slide the sashes into the frame. The inner sash goes in first, then the outer sash. Assembling Casement Window Frames

Casement windows open outward like a door. They are popular in Africa for their tight seal against rain and wind. Assembly starts with the outer frame, which is a rectangle joined at 45 degrees with cleats and screws. The frame must be perfectly square. Next, assemble the sash (the opening part). The sash is also a rectangle, but it has a rebate to hold the glass. Cut the sash profiles at 45 degrees and join with cleats. Then, install the hinges. Casement hinges are usually friction stays (also called restrictors) that allow the window to open to different angles. Mark the hinge positions on the frame and sash: typically, hinges go 100mm from the top and bottom of the sash. Drill holes and screw the hinges in place. Make sure the sash opens and closes smoothly. If it rubs, adjust the hinge arms. Next, install the handle and locking mechanism on the opposite side. For security, use a multi-point lock. Then, install the glass. Casement windows use a glazing bead (a small profile that snaps into the sash) to hold the glass. Apply a bead of silicone around the glass before snapping the bead in place. Door frames are bigger and heavier than window frames, so they need stronger joints. For hinged doors, the outer frame is assembled with corner cleats and screws, but you must use larger cleats and more screws. The frame is usually fixed to the wall with anchor bolts. The door leaf (the moving part) is assembled similarly, but it needs a reinforced corner joint because it will carry the weight of the door. Use a corner bracket or a heavy-duty cleat. For hinged doors, install three hinges: top, middle, and bottom. The middle hinge carries most of the weight. Use stainless steel hinges for coastal areas. For sliding doors, the assembly is different. The outer frame has a top track and a bottom track. The bottom track must be strong enough to support the door's weight, so use a heavy-duty profile with a stainless steel rail. The door panels are assembled like sliding window sashes but larger. Install rollers on the bottom of each panel; use at least two rollers per panel, rated for the door's weight. For heavy doors, use four rollers. For security, use a mortise lock or a hook lock. Test the door by sliding it back and forth; it should not wobble. If it does, adjust the rollers or add a floor guide. Types of Glass Used in Africa: Clear, Tinted, and Tempered

In Africa, the three most common types of glass for windows and doors are clear, tinted, and tempered. Clear glass is the cheapest and most basic. It lets in maximum light but offers no heat or UV protection. In hot climates, clear glass can make rooms unbearably hot, so many builders use tinted glass instead. Tinted glass has a metallic coating or body tint that reduces solar heat gain and glare. Common tints are bronze, grey, and green. Bronze and grey are popular in West and East Africa for their heat rejection. Tinted glass costs about 20-30% more than clear but can cut cooling costs..Trim()

.Trim()

Tempered glass is heat-treated to be 4-5 times stronger than ordinary glass. When it breaks, it shatters into small, blunt pieces instead of sharp shards, making it much safer. In many African countries, building codes require tempered glass for doors, low windows (below 500mm from floor), and any glass that people might walk into. Tempered glass cannot be cut or drilled after tempering, so all fabrication must be done before the tempering process. It costs roughly double the price of clear glass..Trim()

.Trim()

For most residential windows in Africa, 4mm or 5mm clear or tinted glass is sufficient. For larger windows or areas with high wind loads, use 6mm. For doors and safety-critical areas, use 10mm or 12mm tempered glass. Always check local building regulations. When ordering, specify thickness, tint, and whether tempering is required. Ask your supplier for a certificate of conformity for tempered glass. Remember that laminated glass (two layers bonded with a plastic interlayer) is also used in some applications for security and sound insulation, but it is not as common as the three types above. Choose based on climate, budget, and safety needs..Trim()

Measuring and Cutting Glass Safely

Measuring glass accurately is critical. Use a steel tape measure, not a cloth tape, because cloth stretches. Measure the opening in the aluminum frame where the glass will sit. For a window with glazing beads, measure the width and height between the rebates (the inner edges where the glass rests). Subtract 3-5mm from each dimension to allow for expansion and to make installation easier. For example, if the opening is 800mm wide, cut glass to 795-797mm. Always measure twice and write down the numbers. Use a pencil to mark the glass. Never use a pen; ink can be hard to remove..Trim()

Cutting glass requires a flat, clean surface. Use a glass cutter with a carbide wheel. Lubricate the wheel with a drop of light oil. Hold the cutter at a 90-degree angle to the glass and score a single, smooth line from edge to edge. Do not score twice over the same line; that dulls the wheel and creates a weak cut. After scoring, place a thin rod or pencil under the glass along the score line. Press down gently on both sides to snap the glass. For thick glass (6mm and above), you may need to tap the underside of the score line with the ball end of the cutter to help the break run..Trim()

Safety is paramount. Always wear safety glasses, gloves, and long sleeves. Glass shards are razor-sharp. Work in a well-ventilated area. Never cut glass near children or pets. If you must cut laminated glass, you need to cut both sides and use a solvent to soften the plastic interlayer. In Africa, many small workshops use manual glass cutters; practice on scrap pieces first. Always dispose of glass waste in a designated bin, never in regular trash..Trim()

Glazing beads are the strips that hold the glass in the aluminum frame. They can be made of aluminum, PVC, or rubber. The most common in Africa are aluminum beads with a rubber gasket. For casement windows, ensure the glass does not interfere with the opening mechanism. For sliding windows, make sure the glass does not rub against the track. Using Rubber and Silicone Seals

Rubber and silicone seals are essential to keep water, dust, and air out of your windows and doors. Rubber seals, also called gaskets or weatherstrips, are usually made of EPDM (ethylene propylene diene monomer) rubber, which resists UV, ozone, and temperature extremes. They come in various profiles: U-channel, wedge, and bulb. For glazing, a U-channel gasket is common. It fits around the glass edge and then into the aluminum frame. Silicone sealant is a caulk-like material that cures into a flexible rubber. It is used to fill gaps and create a watertight seal. In Africa, silicone is widely available in cartridges and comes in clear, black, white, and bronze..Trim()

Glass is fragile and expensive. In Africa, where roads can be rough, transporting glass without breakage requires planning. Always carry glass vertically, never flat. If you don't have a rack, lean the glass against a solid surface at a slight angle (about 10-15 degrees from vertical) and secure it with straps. Pad the glass with cardboard, foam, or blankets. Never lift glass by the corners; it can twist and break. Carry glass vertically with the bottom edge supported. When setting glass down, place it on a clean, flat surface with padding. Do not set it on concrete or gravel. In a vehicle, place the glass behind the seats or in the bed, secured so it cannot slide. Avoid potholes and speed bumps if possible. In rainy season, cover glass with a tarp to prevent water spots. When unloading, have one person guide the glass while another supports it. Never drop glass, even from a small height. Store glass in a dry, secure area on site, away from traffic. Use a glass rack with felt-lined slots. If glass is stored outside, keep it off the ground and covered to prevent damage from debris and weather..Trim()

Glazing Sliding and Casement Windows

Glazing a sliding window requires attention to the track and rollers. First, remove the sliding panel from the frame. Lay it flat on a padded surface. The glass is held in the panel by glazing beads, usually on the inside. Remove the old beads with a putty knife or a flat screwdriver. Clean the frame and check the rollers. Replace any worn rollers. Cut the new glass to size, allowing 3-5mm clearance. Install the rubber gasket around the glass edge, then place the glass into the panel. Reinstall the beads, starting with the long sides. Tap them gently with a rubber mallet. Apply silicone sealant along the bead edges for a watertight seal. Do not seal the drainage holes at the bottom of the panel. Reinstall the panel into the track and adjust the rollers so the panel slides smoothly and locks properly..Trim()

For casement windows, the glass is held in the sash, which opens outward or inward. Remove the sash from the frame by unscrewing the hinges. Lay the sash flat. Remove the old beads and glass. Clean the sash. Cut new glass to fit, again allowing 3-5mm clearance. Install the rubber gasket, then the glass, then the beads. For casement windows, the glass is usually held by beads on the inside, but some designs have beads on the outside. Check your specific window. After glazing, apply silicone sealant around the glass on the inside. Reattach the sash to the frame. Check that the window opens and closes freely. Adjust the hinges if needed. For both types, ensure the glass does not come into contact with any metal parts; the rubber gasket should separate them. Test for leaks by spraying water on the window from outside. If water enters, reapply silicone. Always wear safety glasses when glazing. In Africa, many sliding windows use 4mm or 5mm glass, while casement windows often use 6mm for better wind resistance. Calculating Material Costs per Window or Door

To price any window or door, you first need to know exactly what it costs you in materials. Start by measuring the frame. For a standard 1.2m x 1.5m window, you need aluminum profiles: top, bottom, left, right, and a middle horizontal bar. Each profile length is cut from standard 6-meter bars. Calculate total linear meters: for this window, the perimeter is (1.2+1.5)*2 = 5.4m, plus the middle bar 1.2m = 6.6m. But you must add 10% for cutting waste, so buy 7.3m. Aluminum bars cost about $12 per meter in many African cities, so 7.3m x $12 = $87.60. Next, glass: for a 1.2x1.5 window, you need two panes of 0.6x0.7m (since the middle bar splits it). Glass costs $25 per square meter, so area = 0.6*0.7*2 = 0.84 sqm, cost = $21. Add rubber seals: 6.6m at $0.50/m = $3.30. Add screws, handles, and hinges: about $8. Pricing Your Products for Profit
