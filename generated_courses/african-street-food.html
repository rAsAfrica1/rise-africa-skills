Module 1 · The Business of African Street Food

Why Street Food Works in Africa

Street food works in Africa because it solves three problems at once: people need to eat cheaply, they need to eat quickly, and they need to eat food they trust. A plate of rice and stew, chapati and beans, grilled maize, suya, mandazi, or akara costs far less than a restaurant meal and takes minutes to serve. In most African cities, a large share of workers earn daily wages and eat at least one meal away from home. That is your customer, and they buy every single day, not once a month..Trim()

.Trim()

Your advantages as a small operator are real. Rent is low because you trade from a stall, a table, a cart, or a roadside spot. Labour is often just you, or you plus one helper. You buy ingredients fresh from the same market every morning, so you know your costs. You can change your menu the same day if prices move. A restaurant must plan weeks ahead; you can decide at 6am what you are cooking today..Trim()

.Trim()

Start with one dish done well. A trader selling only grilled chicken with kachumbari can build a name faster than one selling ten dishes badly. Customers return for consistency: same taste, same portion, same price, same time. Write your recipe down in a notebook so a helper can repeat it exactly..Trim()

.Trim()

Cash is immediate. Street food is a cash business, so money comes in the same day you spend it. That is powerful if you manage it: buy in the morning, sell by afternoon, count profit at night. The danger is spending the day's takings before you restock tomorrow. Keep a separate tin or mobile money wallet for restock money and never touch it for personal use..Trim()

.Trim()

Location and timing decide more than cooking skill. Find where people already walk: bus stages, market gates, construction sites, school exits, hospital entrances. Be there before they are hungry and stay until the rush ends. If you sell breakfast, be ready by 5:30am. If you sell evening snacks, start at 4pm. Street food is not a hobby; it is a daily rhythm you must keep..Trim()

The Money: Cash Flow, Margins, Daily Targets

Cash flow means money moving in and out of your business. In street food, cash flow is daily. You spend in the morning on ingredients and fuel, and you collect during the day. If you run out of cash at 7am, you cannot trade. So your first rule is: never spend tomorrow's stock money on today's personal needs..Trim()

.Trim()

Margin is what is left after you subtract the cost of making one plate. If a plate of beans and chapati sells for 100 shillings and the ingredients, fuel and serving cost 60 shillings, your gross margin is 40 shillings per plate. That 40 shillings must cover transport, licences, your helper, and your own income. Know this number for every dish you sell..Trim()

.Trim()

Daily targets turn hope into a plan. Say your monthly costs are 30,000: rent 8,000, licence 2,000, transport 6,000, helper 10,000, gas and charcoal 4,000. Divide by 26 trading days: you must earn about 1,154 shillings per day just to break even. If your margin per plate is 40 shillings, you must sell at least 29 plates a day before you make any profit. To earn 15,000 profit a month, add 577 shillings a day, which is about 15 more plates. Now you have a real target: 44 plates a day..Trim()

.Trim()

Track three numbers every evening in a small exercise book: total sales, total spending on stock and fuel, and cash in hand. Subtract to get today's profit. Do this for two weeks and you will see your patterns: which days are strong, which dishes sell, where money leaks..Trim()

.Trim()

Use mobile money to separate business and personal money. Many traders in Kenya, Ghana, Nigeria and Tanzania use M-Pesa, MoMo or similar wallets. Keep one wallet for business and one for home. When you need stock, withdraw only the stock amount. This single habit prevents the most common failure in street food: a busy week with no profit because the money was eaten..Trim()

Licensing, Permits and Trading Space

Every African city has rules about where you can trade and what you must pay. Ignoring them is expensive: fines, confiscated goods, or being moved off a good pitch by force. The exact rules differ by country and city, so visit your local municipal or county office and ask directly. Do not rely on rumours from other traders..Trim()

.Trim()

Usually you will need some combination of: a business permit or single business permit, a health or food handler's certificate, a medical certificate for you and your helpers, and a daily or monthly market fee for the space you use. In many places a food handler's certificate requires a simple medical check, sometimes a typhoid test, and a short hygiene course. The cost is often modest, but the check can take a week, so start early..Trim()

.Trim()

Trading space has three common types. A designated market stall is legal and safer but may cost more and have less traffic. A roadside pitch near a busy stage is cheap and busy but may be informal and risky. A mobile cart lets you move to where the customers are but may require a specific permit. Ask which category your spot falls under before you invest in equipment..Trim()

.Trim()

Keep your papers with you, laminated or in a plastic sleeve. Inspectors do not want long explanations; they want to see a valid permit. Renew before it expires, not after. A lapsed permit can cost you a full day of sales plus a fine..Trim()

.Trim()

Work with other traders. Form or join a small traders' association if one exists. Groups negotiate better with local government, share security, and can buy stock in bulk at lower prices. If a new fee appears, ask for it in writing. Pay only against an official receipt, and keep every receipt in one envelope. These small records protect you when a dispute arises and prove your business is legitimate when you want a loan or a bigger space later..Trim()

Choosing Your Pitch: Foot Traffic and Timing

A good pitch is not just a busy place; it is a busy place at the right hour with people who are ready to buy. Before you commit, stand at the spot for two or three days and count. Count people passing between 6am and 9am, then 12pm and 2pm, then 5pm and 8pm. Note who they are: office workers, market sellers, students, drivers, construction workers. Different crowds want different food and different prices..Trim()

.Trim()

Match your menu to the crowd. Near a bus stage in the early morning, sell fast, filling, cheap food: tea, mandazi, chapati, boiled eggs, or porridge. Near offices at lunch, sell a full plate that can be eaten in fifteen minutes. Near a school at 4pm, sell snacks at low prices: samosas, roasted maize, groundnuts, ice blocks. Near a construction site, sell large portions at fair prices; workers need energy and value..Trim()

.Trim()

Timing is money. If your customers arrive at 6am, being open at 7am means you missed them. Prepare the night before: chop vegetables, soak beans, marinate meat, clean your equipment. Arrive early, set up fast, and be ready before the first customer. The first hour often decides the day..Trim()

.Trim()

Check the practical details of the pitch. Is there water nearby for washing hands and utensils? Is there shade or shelter for rain? Is there secure space to leave your cart or table overnight? Is the ground level and clean? Is there a toilet your customers can use? These details affect how long customers stay and whether they return..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, respect the neighbours. Do not block doorways, drains, or roads. Keep your area clean and take your waste with you. A pitch you keep tidy is a pitch you keep longer. If another trader already has a strong spot, do not fight over it; find the second-best spot nearby and earn your own customers with better service, faster speed, and consistent taste..Trim()

Costing a Plate: Ingredients, Fuel, Labour

Costing a plate means knowing exactly what it costs you to make and serve one portion. Without this number you are guessing, and guessing leads to working hard for no profit. Do it once properly for each dish, then update it whenever market prices change..Trim()

.Trim()

Start with ingredients. Buy the amount you use in one cooking session and record the price. If you cook 50 plates from 5kg of rice costing 400, 2kg of beans costing 300, 1 litre of oil costing 250, and vegetables costing 200, your ingredient total is 1,150. Divide by 50 plates: 23 per plate for ingredients. Do the same for every item, including salt, spices, onions, and tomatoes. Small items add up..Trim()

.Trim()

Add fuel. Charcoal, firewood, gas, or electricity is a real cost. If a bag of charcoal costs 1,200 and lasts three days, and you cook 50 plates a day, that is 1,200 divided by 150 plates, which is 8 per plate. Add water, if you buy it, and any packaging: paper, plastic bags, or leaves..Trim()

.Trim()

Add labour, even if it is your own. If you pay a helper 500 a day and you sell 50 plates, that is 10 per plate. If you work alone, still put a value on your time, for example 800 a day, so you can see whether the business can pay you. If it cannot, your prices are too low or your costs are too high..Trim()

.Trim()

Add a share of fixed costs: licence, rent, transport, and equipment wear. If these total 1,000 a day across 50 plates, that is 20 per plate. Now add everything: 23 ingredients + 8 fuel + 10 labour + 20 fixed = 61 per plate. If you sell at 100, your profit is 39 per plate. If you sell at 80, profit is 19. This is why a 20-shilling price change can make or break you..Trim()

.Trim()

Review your costing every week. When tomato or oil prices jump, either adjust your portion, find a cheaper supplier, or raise your price slightly. Write the new costing in your book so you always know your true profit per plate..Trim()

The Records That Keep You Profitable

Records are not paperwork for its own sake; they are how you see your business clearly. You do not need a computer. One exercise book, one pen, and five minutes every evening are enough to run a profitable street food business..Trim()

.Trim()

Keep four simple records. First, a daily sales sheet: date, dishes sold, quantity of each, total sales. Second, a purchases sheet: what you bought, from whom, how much, and for what. Third, a cash book: money in, money out, cash in hand at close. Fourth, a stock note: what is left over, what spoiled, what you need tomorrow. These four pages tell you everything..Trim()

.Trim()

At the end of each week, add up your sales and your purchases. Subtract to get your gross profit. Then subtract your fixed costs for the week: rent, licence, transport, helper, fuel. What remains is your real profit. If it is negative, you are subsidising the business from your own pocket and must change something: raise prices, cut a slow dish, buy cheaper, or sell more..Trim()

.Trim()

Records also help you spot waste. If you buy 10kg of tomatoes and 2kg spoil every week, you are losing money. If your best-selling dish is chapati and your worst is samosa, drop the samosa and make more chapati. Without records, you would keep making both and wonder why you are tired and poor..Trim()

.Trim()

Records give you power. When you want a loan from a sacco, a microfinance group, or a bank, they will ask for evidence of income. A trader with six months of clean records looks serious; a trader with none looks risky. Records also help if you hire a helper, because you can show them exactly how the business runs and what targets to hit..Trim()

.Trim()

Start tonight. Write down today's sales, today's spending, and what is left in your tin. Do it again tomorrow. After thirty days you will know your business better than most people who have traded for years. That knowledge is your real capital..Trim()

Module 2 · Foundations: Fire, Oil and Dough

Setting Up a Street Kitchen

Start with a spot that has three things: foot traffic, shade, and a water source within 50 metres. In most African cities, you need a permit from the local council. Visit the municipal offices and ask for the 'hawker's licence' or 'street trading permit'. In Nairobi, it costs about 2,000 KES per year; in Accra, around 100 GHS per quarter. Budget for it. Your stall must be stable. Use a wooden table at least 1 metre long and 70 cm high. If you fry, place your oil drum or gas cooker on a separate metal stand, never on the same table where you chop vegetables. Keep a 2-metre gap between the cooking fire and where customers stand. For storage, use two plastic crates with lids: one for dry goods (flour, salt, spices), one for tools (knives, spoons, tongs). Never store food on the ground. A simple three-sided windbreak made from corrugated iron sheets will protect your flame and keep dust off your food. For washing, bring two 20-litre jerrycans: one with clean water and soap, one for rinsing. A small basin and a bottle of liquid soap cost less than 500 KES. Set up a handwashing station at the front of your stall so customers see you wash. Finally, arrange your workspace in a line: raw ingredients on the left, cooking in the centre, finished food and serving on the right. This prevents cross-contamination and speeds up service. Test your setup at home before you go live. Time yourself: you should be able to serve your first customer within 15 minutes of arriving..Trim()

Charcoal, Gas and Wood: Choosing Your Heat

Charcoal is the most common street fuel in Africa because it is cheap, widely available, and gives high heat. A 50 kg sack of charcoal in Kampala costs about 80,000 UGX and lasts 5-7 days if you cook 6 hours a day. Charcoal burns at 600-800°C, perfect for deep frying and grilling. But it produces carbon monoxide, so never use it in a closed room. Gas (LPG) is cleaner and faster to control. A 6 kg cylinder costs around 15,000 NGN in Lagos and lasts 10-12 days if you fry 4 hours daily. Gas burns at 1,000°C and you can turn it off instantly, saving fuel. However, refilling can be unreliable in rural areas. Wood is free in many villages but burns slower and produces more smoke. Use dry hardwood, not softwood, to avoid creosote that flavours food badly. For a beginner, start with charcoal: it is forgiving and you can see the heat. Buy a 'jiko' (Kenya) or 'coal pot' (Ghana) for 500-1,500 KES. For gas, buy a single-burner stove with a hose and regulator. Always check the hose for cracks before lighting. For wood, build a three-stone fire and keep a bucket of sand nearby to control flames. Whichever you choose, never leave a fire unattended. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder, 1 kg) or a bucket of sand within arm's reach. Learn to judge heat by holding your palm 15 cm above the coals: if you can hold it for 3 seconds, it is medium; 1 second, high. This skill saves fuel and prevents burnt food..Trim()

Oil for Frying: Types, Temperature, Reuse

Use vegetable oils with a high smoke point: palm oil (smoke point 235°C), groundnut oil (225°C), sunflower oil (225°C), or canola (205°C). Avoid olive oil for deep frying�??it is expensive and smokes at 190°C. In West Africa, red palm oil gives colour and flavour to puff-puff and plantain, but it foams. Let it settle for 10 minutes after heating before frying. In East Africa, sunflower oil is common and cheap. Buy in 5-litre jerrycans to save money: 5 litres of sunflower oil in Dar es Salaam costs about 25,000 TZS. Heat oil to 170-180°C for most doughnuts and fritters. If you don't have a thermometer, drop a 1 cm cube of bread: it should brown in 60 seconds. If it browns in 30 seconds, the oil is too hot (200°C+); if it takes 90 seconds, too cool. Reuse oil only 3-4 times. Each time, strain it through a fine mesh or cloth to remove crumbs. Store in a dark, cool place. When oil turns dark, smells rancid, or foams excessively, throw it away. Never mix old oil with new. To dispose, let it cool, pour into a sealed container, and give to a soap maker or waste collector�??never down the drain. For safety, keep a lid nearby to smother flames if oil catches fire. Never use water on an oil fire. A simple oil thermometer costs 5,000 KES and pays for itself in one week by preventing burnt batches..Trim()

The Dough Toolkit: Mixing, Resting, Shaping

You need five tools: a large bowl (plastic or stainless steel, 5-litre), a wooden spoon or your clean hands, a measuring cup, a clean cloth, and a flat surface for kneading. For most street doughs�??mandazi, puff-puff, doughnuts�??use all-purpose flour. In Nigeria, you might use cassava flour for 'akara' but that's a batter, not a dough. Start with a basic ratio: 500 g flour, 1 teaspoon instant yeast, 1 teaspoon salt, 2 tablespoons sugar, 250 ml warm water (not hot�??hot water kills yeast). Mix dry ingredients first. Add water slowly, stirring with a spoon until it forms a shaggy mass. Then knead on a floured surface for 8-10 minutes until smooth and elastic. If it sticks, add flour one tablespoon at a time. Resting is critical: cover with a damp cloth and leave in a warm place for 1-2 hours until doubled in size. In cold weather, put the bowl near your fire but not too close. After resting, punch down the dough to release air. Shape into balls (50 g each for mandazi) or rings. For puff-puff, use a spoon to drop small balls into oil. Always shape with clean, oiled hands to prevent sticking. If you want to save time, make the dough the night before and keep it in a cool box with ice packs. It will rise slowly and be ready by morning. Never skip resting�??it makes the dough light and digestible. Test a small piece first: fry one ball. If it floats and browns in 2 minutes, your oil is ready..Trim()

Food Safety on the Street

Street food must be safe or you will lose customers and get sick yourself. The biggest risks are bacteria from raw meat, dirty water, and dirty hands. Keep raw meat and vegetables separate. Use two cutting boards: red for meat, green for vegetables. If you can't afford two, cut vegetables first, then meat, then wash the board with soap and hot water. Cook meat to 75°C�??no pink inside. For fried foods, oil at 175°C kills most bacteria in 2 minutes. Never leave cooked food at room temperature for more than 2 hours. If you prepare ahead, keep hot food above 60°C and cold food below 5°C. In hot African climates, bacteria double every 20 minutes. So serve immediately or reheat thoroughly. Cover all food with mesh lids or clean cloths to keep flies off. Flies carry typhoid and cholera. Buy a fly trap or use a fan to create a breeze. Wash your hands with soap for 20 seconds after using the toilet, handling raw meat, or touching money. Keep a bottle of hand sanitiser (70% alcohol) for quick clean-ups. Never smoke or chew while cooking. Wear a clean apron and tie back long hair. If you have diarrhoea or a cut on your hand, stay home or wear gloves. Clean your stall at the end of each day: scrape tables, sweep the ground, and wash utensils in hot soapy water. A clean stall attracts more customers�??they can see you care..Trim()

Water, Cleanliness and Hand Hygiene

Water is the most common source of sickness in street food. Never use tap water directly unless you know it is treated. In most African cities, tap water is chlorinated but may still contain bacteria. Boil water for 1 minute, then cool it in a clean covered container. Or use a filter (ceramic or biosand) or add 2 drops of household bleach (5% sodium hypochlorite) per litre, wait 30 minutes. For washing hands, use running water�??not a bowl where everyone dips. A simple 'tippy-tap' made from a 5-litre jerrycan with a hole and a string costs nothing and saves water. Fill it with clean water and hang it at your stall. Add soap. Wash hands for 20 seconds: palms, back of hands, between fingers, under nails. Rinse. Dry with a clean cloth or air dry. For washing utensils, use three basins: wash with soap and hot water, rinse with clean water, then sanitise with a bleach solution (1 tablespoon bleach per 4 litres water) for 1 minute. Air dry on a clean rack�??do not wipe with a dirty cloth. For dishes, use a separate basin from handwashing. Change water every 2 hours or when it looks dirty. Store clean water in a covered container with a tap or ladle�??never dip a cup into it. If you use a bucket, keep a dedicated scoop that only touches water. Finally, keep your fingernails short and clean. Dirt under nails carries bacteria. Wash your hands before you start cooking, after handling money, after using the toilet, and after touching your face or phone. A bar of soap and a jerrycan cost less than 500 KES but can save you from a week of sickness and lost sales..Trim()

Jollof Rice: The Full Method

Jollof rice is the backbone of many street food stalls. To make it for 20 servings, you need 4 cups of long-grain parboiled rice, 6 large tomatoes, 2 red bell peppers, 1 large onion, 3 tablespoons tomato paste, 1/3 cup groundnut oil, 2 teaspoons curry powder, 1 teaspoon thyme, 1 teaspoon salt, 1 teaspoon black pepper, 2 bay leaves, 4 cups chicken stock, and 1 cup water. Blend tomatoes, peppers, and half the onion into a smooth puree. Slice the rest of the onion. Heat oil in a large pot, fry sliced onion until golden, add tomato paste and fry for 2 minutes. Add the puree and fry on medium heat for 10 minutes until it reduces and darkens. Stir in curry, thyme, salt, pepper, and bay leaves. Add washed rice and stir to coat. Add stock and water; the liquid should be 1 inch above the rice. Bring to a boil, then reduce heat to low, cover tightly, and cook for 25 minutes. Do not stir during cooking. After 25 minutes, check if rice is tender; if not, add 1/4 cup water and cook 5 more minutes. Fluff with a fork. For street service, portion into 250g servings and keep warm in a cooler or insulated container. Jollof can be made in large batches and holds well for 4 hours. Sell at 500-800 Naira per plate depending on your location..Trim()
