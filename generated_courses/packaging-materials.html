History and Evolution of Packaging

In the 11th century, Yoruba merchants used tightly woven raffia bags to transport kola nuts across the Sahel. By the 15th century, Swahili traders on the East African coast packed spices and ivory in wooden crates lined with dried banana leaves. Types of Paper and Paperboard

Paper and paperboard are everywhere in packaging. In Africa, common types include kraft paper, newsprint, and paperboard like SBS (solid bleached sulfate) and recycled chipboard. Kraft paper is strong and tear-resistant, often used for bags and wrapping. It comes in weights from 30 to 120 gsm (grams per square meter). Newsprint is cheap and used for inner wrapping, but it's weak and not water-resistant. Paperboard is thicker, usually above 200 gsm. SBS is white and food-safe, used for cosmetics and medicine boxes. Recycled chipboard is grey or brown, used for cereal boxes and shoe boxes. In many African markets, locally made paperboard from waste paper is common. For example, in Kenya, companies like Chandaria Industries recycle waste paper into paperboard for packaging. When choosing, consider strength, printability, and moisture resistance. For dry goods like tea leaves, a 300 gsm kraft paperboard works well. For frozen foods, you need polyethylene-coated board to resist moisture. Always check the caliper (thickness) in micrometers; typical folding boxboard is 300-500 microns. If you're starting a small packaging business, you can buy paperboard sheets from local suppliers or import from South Africa or Egypt. Test by folding a sample: if it cracks, it's not suitable for creasing. Remember, the right paper type saves money and protects your product..Trim()

Manufacturing Process of Paperboard

Paperboard is made from wood pulp or recycled paper fibers. The process starts with pulping: wood chips or waste paper are cooked with chemicals (like sodium hydroxide) or mechanically ground to separate fibers. In Africa, many small mills use waste paper, which is soaked and pulped in a hydrapulper. The pulp is then refined to improve fiber bonding. Next, the pulp slurry is poured onto a moving wire screen, where water drains and fibers form a mat. This mat is pressed between rollers to squeeze out more water, then dried over heated cylinders. For multi-layer board, different pulps are combined: top layer for printing, middle for bulk, bottom for strength. The dried board is then calendered (pressed between smooth rollers) to improve surface smoothness. Finally, it's cut into sheets or rolls. Key parameters: consistency (fiber to water ratio) is 0.5-1% at the wet end; drying temperature is 100-120Â°C. In Nigeria, local mills like Bel Papyrus produce paperboard from recycled fibers. If you're setting up a small operation, you can buy pre-pulped fiber or start with a simple Hollander beater. Quality control checks include basis weight (gsm), moisture content (should be 6-8%), and thickness. Moisture is critical: too dry, the board is brittle; too wet, it won't print well. Always store paperboard in a dry place, off the floor, to prevent warping. This process turns waste into valuable packaging, creating jobs and reducing imports..Trim()

Corrugated Board: Structure and Grades

Corrugated board is made of a fluted (wavy) layer sandwiched between flat linerboards. The flutes give strength and cushioning. Common flute types: A-flute (4.8 mm high, good cushioning), B-flute (3.2 mm, good crush resistance), C-flute (4.0 mm, a balance), E-flute (1.6 mm, thin, good for printing), and F-flute (0.8 mm, very thin). In Africa, B and C flutes are most common for shipping boxes. The structure can be single-wall (one flute), double-wall (two flutes), or triple-wall. Grades are based on linerboard weight: for example, 125 gsm liner with 120 gsm fluting gives a standard box. Bursting strength (measured in kPa) and edge crush test (ECT) determine quality. A typical single-wall box has an ECT of 4-6 kN/m. For heavy loads like bananas, use double-wall with ECT above 7 kN/m. Corrugated board is made on a corrugator: liner and medium are glued with starch adhesive. In Kenya, packaging companies like Bobmil Industries produce corrugated boxes for agriculture. When buying, ask for the flute type and ECT rating. To test, stand on a box: if it crushes easily, it's weak. Store corrugated boxes flat, away from moisture, as humidity reduces strength by up to 50%. For small businesses, you can buy corrugated sheets and assemble boxes manually. This is a low-cost way to package goods for local markets..Trim()

Paperboard Converting: Cutting, Creasing, and Folding

Converting paperboard into boxes involves cutting, creasing, and folding. Cutting is done with a die-cutter: a sharp steel rule bent into the shape of the box net. The die is pressed onto the board, cutting through. For small-scale, you can use a hand die or a laser cutter. Creasing creates a line where the board folds. A creasing rule (blunt edge) presses a groove into the board. The groove depth should be about 50% of board thickness. For a 400-micron board, crease width is 1.2 mm. If the crease is too shallow, the board cracks; too deep, it weakens. Folding is then done by hand or machine. For straight-line boxes, a folder-gluer applies glue and folds. In Africa, many small workshops use manual creasing tools and bone folders. Key steps: first, print the board (if needed). Then die-cut and crease in one pass. Remove waste (the skeleton). Fold along creases and glue the side seam. For lock-bottom boxes, no glue is needed; tabs interlock. Test the fold: it should be clean, no cracking. If cracking occurs, adjust crease width or use a softer board. For short runs, you can use a tabletop die-cutter from China, costing around $500. For larger runs, partner with a local converter. Always wear gloves when handling cut boards. Store finished boxes flat to prevent distortion. This process adds value to plain board, allowing you to sell custom boxes to bakeries, pharmacies, and retailers..Trim()

Printing and Coating on Paperboard

Printing on paperboard makes your package attractive and informative. Common methods: offset lithography (for high-quality, large runs), flexography (for corrugated, fast), and digital printing (for short runs, variable data). In Africa, flexo is popular for corrugated boxes because it's cheap and fast. Offset is used for folding cartons. Before printing, the board may be coated: clay coating gives a smooth, white surface for better ink adhesion. Water-based coatings protect against moisture and scuffing. For food packaging, use food-grade inks and coatings. Key parameters: ink viscosity (for flexo, 20-30 seconds in a Zahn cup), drying temperature (60-80Â°C). In Nigeria, printers like Printpak use offset for beverage cartons. If you're small-scale, start with digital printing: a desktop printer can print on pre-cut sheets, but you need a flatbed printer for thick board. Alternatively, screen printing works for simple designs. Always test print a sample: check for smudging, color accuracy, and adhesion. Use a tape test: press tape on the print, pull off; if ink comes off, it's not cured. Coatings can be applied online (during printing) or offline. For water resistance, apply a polyethylene or wax coating. But note: waxed board is not recyclable. For eco-friendly, use starch-based coatings. After printing, the board is die-cut and folded. Ensure the printed design aligns with the creases. This adds professionalism and can increase sales..Trim()

Testing and Quality Control for Paper Packaging

Testing ensures your paper packaging meets requirements. Key tests: basis weight (gsm) â weigh a 10x10 cm sample and multiply by 100. Thickness (caliper) â use a micrometer, measure at 5 points. Moisture content â use a moisture meter; should be 6-8%. For strength, burst test (Mullen) measures resistance to rupture; typical values: 200-400 kPa for folding boxboard. Edge crush test (ECT) for corrugated: cut a small sample, compress between plates; ECT in kN/m. For printing, check ink adhesion with tape test. For creasing, fold and inspect for cracks. In Africa, many small producers skip testing, leading to failures. Simple tools: a kitchen scale for basis weight, a caliper from a hardware store, and a homemade burst tester using a bicycle pump and pressure gauge. For corrugated, you can approximate ECT by stacking boxes and adding weight until failure. Record results in a logbook. If board fails, check storage: humidity above 60% weakens paper. Also, test after converting: boxes should withstand a drop from 1 meter with no damage. For food packaging, ensure no toxic inks. Always keep samples from each batch. If you buy from suppliers, ask for test certificates. In Kenya, KEBS has standards for paper packaging. Meeting them opens doors to supermarkets. Quality control saves money by preventing returns and recalls..Trim()

Types of Metals: Steel, Aluminum, and Alloys

In metal packaging, the three main materials you will work with are steel, aluminum, and their alloys. Steel is strong and cheap, made from iron and carbon. Tinplate is steel coated with a thin layer of tin to stop rust. It is used for food cans, paint tins, and bottle caps. Tin-free steel (TFS) uses chromium instead of tin; it is cheaper and good for beer and beverage cans. Aluminum is lightweight, does not rust, and conducts heat well. It is perfect for drink cans, aerosol cans, and foil trays. Alloys mix metals to improve properties. For example, aluminum 3004 is used for can bodies because it is strong and easy to shape. In Africa, you can source steel from local scrap or import from South Africa, Egypt, or Nigeria. Aluminum is recycled locally in many countries. When choosing, consider cost, corrosion resistance, and weight. Steel is heavier but cheaper; aluminum is lighter but costs more. For food, use tinplate or coated steel. For drinks, aluminum is best. Always check the metal grade: for cans, look for 'tinplate' or 'aluminum 3004'. Test with a magnet: steel sticks, aluminum does not. This simple test helps you sort metals at the workshop..Trim()

Can Manufacturing: Two-Piece and Three-Piece

Can manufacturing has two main methods: two-piece and three-piece. In three-piece cans, you make a body by rolling a rectangular sheet into a cylinder and welding the side seam. Then you attach a top and bottom end. This is common for food cans like tomato paste or corned beef. The body is usually tinplate, and the ends are also tinplate. The weld is done with a copper wire electrode; you must control the current to avoid burn-through. In two-piece cans, you start with a circular blank of aluminum or steel. A press draws it into a cup, then a second draw stretches it into a tall can. There is no side seam, so no leaks. This is how beverage cans are made. The bottom is formed in the same piece. The top end is seamed on later. For a small-scale operation in Africa, three-piece can making is more practical because you can use a manual roller and a spot welder. Two-piece requires expensive deep-drawing presses. However, you can buy pre-formed two-piece cans and fill them. Key tools: sheet metal shears, roller, welder, seamer. For three-piece, cut body blanks to size, roll into cylinder, weld seam, then flange both ends. For two-piece, you need a press. Always test for leaks by filling with water and checking for drips. In Africa, many small canneries use imported two-piece cans and focus on filling and seaming..Trim()

Coating and Lining Technologies

Metal cans need a coating or lining to protect the food or drink from the metal, and to protect the metal from acids. Without a lining, acidic foods like tomatoes or pineapple will react with the tinplate, causing off-flavors and rust. The most common linings are epoxy, phenolic, and acrylic. Epoxy is used for acidic foods and beverages. Phenolic is for meats and fish. Acrylic is for beer and some fruits. You can apply linings by spraying, roller coating, or electrocoating. In small workshops, spray coating is easiest. First, clean the metal with a solvent like acetone. Then spray a thin, even coat of food-grade epoxy. Cure it in an oven at 200Â°C for 10-15 minutes. The coating must be 5-10 microns thick. Too thin, it fails; too thick, it cracks. For cans, you also need to coat the seams and ends. Always use food-safe coatings that meet FDA or equivalent standards. In Africa, you can buy epoxy resin from chemical suppliers in Kenya, Nigeria, or South Africa. Test the coating by filling the can with a saltwater solution and leaving it for 48 hours. If you see rust or blisters, the coating is bad. Also, check for pinholes by holding the can up to a light. A good lining is smooth and shiny, with no gaps..Trim()

Ends, Easy-Open Ends, and Seaming

Can ends are critical for sealing and opening. There are two main types: standard ends and easy-open ends (EOE). Standard ends require a can opener. Easy-open ends have a scored line and a tab that you pull to open. The tab is riveted to the end. To make an EOE, you start with a coated aluminum or tinplate sheet. You stamp out a circular blank, then form the curl and the countersink. The score line is made with a sharp die that cuts partway through the metal. The tab is made from a separate piece and attached with a rivet. For small-scale production, you can buy pre-made EOEs and seam them onto your cans. Seaming is the process of attaching the end to the can body. You use a seaming chuck and rolls. The end is placed on the can, then the first operation roll curls the edge of the end around the can flange. The second operation roll tightens the seam. The seam must be airtight and leak-proof. A good seam has a thickness of about 1.2 mm and a width of 2.5 mm. You can check with a seam micrometer. In Africa, manual seaming machines are available for small canneries. Aerosol and Specialty Metal Packaging

Aerosol cans are used for products like insect spray, paint, deodorant, and cooking spray. They are made of tinplate or aluminum. The can must withstand internal pressure of 3-6 bar. Aerosol cans have a concave bottom for strength and a valve on top. The valve is crimped onto the can. The propellant is a gas like butane or propane, mixed with the product. For small-scale production in Africa, you can buy empty aerosol cans and a filling machine. The filling process: first, the product is put into the can, then the valve is crimped, then the propellant is injected through the valve. You need a pressure tester to check for leaks. Specialty metal packaging includes tubes for toothpaste or ointments, trays for food, and drums for chemicals. Aluminum tubes are made by impact extrusion: a slug of aluminum is punched into a die to form a tube. The tube is then coated inside and outside. For drums, steel is rolled and welded. In Africa, metal drums are often reconditioned and reused for chemicals or water. Always check for corrosion and clean thoroughly. For aerosols, safety is key: never fill near a flame, and use proper ventilation. Test every batch for pressure and leaks. Corrosion and Compatibility Testing

Corrosion is the enemy of metal packaging. It happens when metal reacts with oxygen, water, or acids. For food and drinks, you must test that the product does not corrode the can and that the can does not spoil the product. Common tests include: salt spray test (ASTM B117), which exposes the can to a salt mist for 24-72 hours to see if rust forms. For compatibility, you fill the can with the actual product and store it at 40Â°C for 3 months. Then you check for off-flavors, color changes, and metal dissolution. You can also do a simple test: fill the can with the product, seal it, and leave it at room temperature for 2 weeks. Open it and look for rust, pitting, or a metallic taste. For acidic products like tomato sauce, use a coated can. For salty products, use tinplate with a lacquer. In Africa, you can do a basic corrosion test by burying a can in wet soil for a week; if it rusts, the coating is poor. Always keep records of your tests. If a can fails, check the coating thickness, the seam, and the metal grade. A good can should last at least 2 years on the shelf. Remember: corrosion is faster in hot, humid climates, so store cans in a cool, dry place. Test each new batch of cans and each new product formula..Trim()

Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) for Packaging

Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is a method to measure the environmental impact of a package from cradle to grave. For a small African producer, a simplified LCA can be done using free tools like the OpenLCA software or the online calculator from the Sustainable Packaging Coalition. Start by listing all stages: raw material extraction (e.g., sugarcane for bioplastic), production, transport, use, and disposal. For each stage, note energy, water, and waste. For example, 1 kg of PET plastic requires about 80 MJ of energy and emits 3 kg CO2e, while 1 kg of glass requires 15 MJ but is heavier to transport. In Africa, transport distances are often long, so weight matters. A case study: a Kenyan juice company switched from glass bottles (500g each) to PET (30g each) and cut transport emissions by 70%. But PET is rarely recycled in many African countries, so end-of-life impact is high. Use LCA to compare options: for a 1-litre bottle, local reusable glass may have lower impact than imported bioplastic if it is reused 20 times. To do a quick LCA, use the free app 'LCA Calculator' or the 'Packaging Impact' tool from EarthShift. Collect data from your suppliers: ask for energy use per kg of material.
