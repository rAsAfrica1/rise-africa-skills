Module 1 · Introduction to Street Food and Take Away

The Global Street Food Phenomenon

Street food is a global industry worth over $100 billion, and Africa is one of its fastest-growing markets. From Lagos to Nairobi, Cairo to Cape Town, street vendors serve millions of meals daily. In Nigeria, the popular 'suya' skewers and 'jollof rice' plates generate thousands of naira per day for small operators. In Kenya, 'smokie pasua' and 'chapati' wraps sell for as little as 50 shillings each, yet vendors can earn a decent living. The phenomenon is driven by affordability, convenience, and taste. Customers want quick, cheap, delicious food that fits their busy lives. Why is street food so popular in Africa? Urbanization plays a big role. As cities grow, workers need fast meals during commutes. Think of 'mandazi' in East Africa, 'puff-puff' in West Africa, or 'brik' in Tunisia. These are not just snacks; they are cultural staples. Tourists also seek authentic street food experiences, adding another revenue stream..Trim()

Key Differences: Street Food vs. Take Away

Many people use 'street food' and 'take away' interchangeably, but they are different business models. Street food is typically prepared and sold from a stall, cart, or kiosk, often with no seating. Customers eat while standing or walking. Take away (or takeout) refers to food prepared in a fixed kitchen (restaurant, cloud kitchen, or home) and packaged for customers to eat elsewhere. The key differences are location, setup cost, menu flexibility, and regulations. Street food requires a mobile or semi-permanent pitch, while take away needs a licensed kitchen space. Street food often has lower overheads but higher exposure to weather and location restrictions. Take away can operate from a rented shop or even a home kitchen with delivery apps..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, both models thrive. A 'mama put' in Lagos is street food: she cooks in a large pot, serves on plates or in nylon bags, and customers eat nearby. A take away shop in Nairobi might sell 'nyama choma' packed in foil containers for customers to carry home. The equipment differs: street food needs a portable stove, a table, and a cooler; take away needs a commercial kitchen with refrigeration, packaging stations, and possibly a POS system. Your choice depends on capital, location, and target customer. If you have $200, start with street food. If you have $2,000, consider a small take away..Trim()

.Trim()

Another difference is menu. Street food menus are usually short and focused: one or two signature items. Take away menus can be broader because you have more storage and cooking space. However, take away faces more competition from restaurants and delivery apps. Street food competes on price and convenience. For example, a street vendor selling 'rolex' (chapati with eggs) in Kampala can make 500 UGX profit per unit, while a take away shop selling the same item might charge double but pay rent and staff. Your decision should be based on your skills, capital, and local demand. Many successful operators start as street food and later open a take away outlet..Trim()

Costing and Pricing Strategies

To make a profit, you must know your costs and price correctly. Start by calculating your cost per plate. List all ingredients: for a plate of 'jollof rice' with chicken, you might spend 300 NGN on rice, 200 NGN on chicken, 100 NGN on oil and spices, and 50 NGN on packaging. That's 650 NGN per plate. Add overheads: gas, transport, permits, and your time. If you sell 50 plates a day, and your daily overhead is 2,000 NGN, that's 40 NGN per plate. So total cost is 690 NGN. Now, decide your profit margin. Street food typically marks up 30-50%. So you could sell at 900-1,000 NGN. Check competitors: if others sell at 800 NGN, you need to either reduce costs or add value (e.g., bigger portion, free drink)..Trim()

Office workers may pay more for convenience and quality; students want cheap and filling. You can have two sizes: small at 500 NGN, large at 800 NGN. Track your sales daily. If you sell out too early, you priced too low. If you have leftovers, you priced too high or made too much. Adjust weekly..Trim()

Setting Up a Legal Street Food Business

Starting a legal street food business in Africa requires navigating licenses, permits, and local bylaws. The first step is to register your business name. In Nigeria, you can register with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) as a sole proprietorship for about 10,000 NGN. In Kenya, register with the Business Registration Service for 1,000 KES. In Ghana, the Registrar General's Department charges around 50 GHS. This gives you a legal identity and allows you to open a business bank account. Next, obtain a food handler's permit from your local health authority. This involves a medical exam and a fee (often 1,000-5,000 NGN, 500-1,000 KES, etc.). You also need a trading license from the local government, which varies by location. In some cities, you must also get a stall allocation from the market association or municipal council..Trim()

.Trim()

Location is critical. Not all areas allow street vending. In Lagos, some streets are restricted; you might need to rent a space in a registered market. In Nairobi, the county government has designated vending zones. Check with your local ward office. If you operate from a fixed kiosk, you may need a change of use permit for the land. If you are mobile, you might need a mobile vendor's license. Always keep your permits visible at your stall. Inspectors can ask for them at any time. Fines for operating illegally can be high (e.g., 50,000 NGN in Lagos) and your goods may be confiscated..Trim()

Taxes: You must pay taxes if your income exceeds the threshold. In many countries, small businesses with turnover below a certain amount (e.g., 25 million NGN in Nigeria) are exempt from VAT but may pay a presumptive tax. Keep records of all transactions. Consider joining a vendor association; they can help with advocacy and bulk purchasing. Also, get a mobile money account (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, etc.) to accept digital payments. This increases sales and provides a record. Finally, insure your business if possible. A simple public liability insurance costs little and protects you if a customer gets sick. Legal setup may seem like a hassle, but it protects you from closures and builds customer trust. Start the process early; don't wait until you are caught..Trim()
