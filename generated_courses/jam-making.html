History and Types of Jams, Jellies, and Preserves

Jam making has deep roots in Africa. Long before sugar was widely available, communities preserved fruits by boiling them with honey or tree saps. In North Africa, figs and dates were cooked into thick pastes. In West Africa, mangoes, guavas, and pineapples were turned into sweet spreads. European colonists later introduced cane sugar and glass jars, but the basic idea remained: cook fruit with something sweet to make it last..Trim()

.Trim()

Today, there are three main types of fruit spreads. Jam is made from crushed or chopped fruit cooked with sugar until thick. It often contains seeds and pieces of fruit. Jelly is made from fruit juice only, so it is clear and smooth. Preserves are similar to jam but contain whole or large pieces of fruit, like whole strawberries or chunks of pineapple. Marmalade is a type of preserve made from citrus fruits, usually with shreds of peel..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, we have amazing fruits for each type. For jam, use soft fruits like mango, papaya, or banana. For jelly, use high-pectin fruits like guava, sour plum, or citrus. For preserves, use firm fruits like pineapple, pawpaw, or whole small figs. You can also mix fruitsâmango and ginger, or pineapple and coconutâto create unique flavours..Trim()

.Trim()

The key difference between these spreads is texture and fruit preparation. Jam is chunky, jelly is clear, and preserves have large fruit pieces. All three rely on the same basic chemistry: fruit, sugar, acid, and pectin. Once you understand that balance, you can make any of them. In this module, we will focus on jam because it is the easiest for beginners and forgiving if you make small mistakes..Trim()

.Trim()

Start by thinking about what fruits are in season near you. A ripe mango in Accra or a guava in Nairobi will make better jam than an underripe fruit shipped from far away. Your first batch doesn't need fancy equipmentâa heavy pot, a wooden spoon, and clean jars will do. The goal is to capture the taste of African fruit at its peak and make it last for months..Trim()

You don't need a factory to make good jam. A few basic tools will get you started. The most important is a heavy-bottomed pot. Next, you need a long wooden spoon or a heat-resistant spatula. Wood is best because it doesn't get hot and it won't scratch your pot. You'll also need a ladle for filling jars. Jars are critical. Use glass jars with new lids. In Africa, you can reuse jars from bought products like mayonnaise or pasta sauce, but you must sterilize them properly (see Lesson 5). Lids should be new or in perfect conditionâany rust or dents will prevent a seal. You'll also need a large pot for sterilizing jars and processing filled jars. This is called a water bath canner. Any deep pot with a rack at the bottom works; the rack keeps jars from touching the bottom and cracking..Trim()

.Trim()

Other useful items: a sharp knife and cutting board for preparing fruit, a measuring cup, and a timer. A blender or potato masher can help crush fruit, but you can also do it by hand. All equipment must be clean and dry before use. Wash everything with hot soapy water, rinse well, and let air dry. Avoid using plastic utensils if the jam is very hotâthey can melt. Understanding Pectin: Natural vs. Commercial

Pectin is a natural substance found in the cell walls of fruits. It's what makes jam thicken and set. Without enough pectin, your jam will be runny like syrup. With too much, it becomes rubbery. Understanding pectin is the difference between success and failure..Trim()

.Trim()

Some fruits are high in pectin: guava, sour plum, citrus peel, green apples, and unripe mangoes. Others are low: ripe mangoes, strawberries, pineapple, and papaya. If you use low-pectin fruits, you need to add commercial pectin or mix with high-pectin fruits. Commercial pectin comes as a powder or liquid. Powdered pectin is added to fruit before sugar; liquid pectin is added after sugar and a boil. Always follow the recipe that comes with your pectinâit's tested and reliable..Trim()

.Trim()

Natural pectin levels change as fruit ripens. Underripe fruit has more pectin; overripe fruit has less. That's why jam recipes often call for a mix of ripe and slightly underripe fruit. For example, if you make mango jam, add 20% underripe mango to boost pectin. You can also make your own pectin stock: simmer green apples or citrus peels in water, strain, and use the liquid in place of some water in your recipe..Trim()

.Trim()

Testing for pectin: Mix 1 teaspoon of fruit juice with 1 teaspoon of rubbing alcohol in a small glass. If a jelly-like blob forms, pectin is high. If only small specks form, it's low. This test is quick and uses materials you might have at home. In Africa, where commercial pectin can be expensive or hard to find, this knowledge is valuable. You can rely on natural pectin by choosing the right fruits and adding lemon juice (acid helps pectin set)..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember: pectin needs acid and sugar to gel. Even high-pectin fruits need a little lemon or lime juice. The ratio is roughly 1 tablespoon of lemon juice per 1 kg of fruit. Too little acid and the jam won't set; too much and it tastes sour. With practice, you'll learn to judge by feel. Start with recipes that use commercial pectin for consistent results, then experiment with natural pectin as you gain confidence..Trim()

The Role of Sugar, Acid, and Temperature

Sugar does more than sweeten jam. It preserves the fruit by drawing out water, which bacteria and mould need to grow. It also helps pectin form a gel. But too much sugar can crystallize or make the jam overly stiff. The right balance is usually 60-65% sugar by weight in the final jam. For a simple recipe, use 1 part sugar to 1 part prepared fruit (by weight). If the fruit is very sweet, reduce sugar slightly; if it's tart, you might increase it a little..Trim()

.Trim()

Acid, usually from lemon or lime juice, is essential. It brings out the fruit's flavour, helps pectin set, and prevents spoilage. Without acid, jam can taste flat and may not thicken. Use fresh lemon juiceâabout 1 tablespoon per 1 kg of fruit. Bottled lemon juice works in a pinch, but fresh is better. For citrus fruits, you may not need extra acid because they are already acidic. Taste your fruit mixture before cooking and adjust: it should be slightly tart, not sour..Trim()

.Trim()

Temperature is the third key. Jam sets at 104-105Â°C (219-221Â°F) at sea level. At higher altitudes, the setting point is lower. In many African cities at high elevation, like Addis Ababa or Nairobi, water boils at a lower temperature, so you need to adjust. A candy thermometer is the most accurate way to check. If you don't have one, use the wrinkle test: put a small plate in the freezer, drop a teaspoon of hot jam on it, let it cool for a minute, then push it with your finger. If it wrinkles, it's ready. If it runs, keep boiling and test again every 2 minutes..Trim()

.Trim()

Cooking time matters too. Boil the jam rapidly, stirring often to prevent burning. The mixture should bubble thickly. A batch of 2 kg fruit usually takes 20-30 minutes to reach setting point. Don't walk awayâjam can scorch quickly. Once set, remove from heat immediately. Skim off any foam with a metal spoon. Then ladle into sterilized jars. The combination of sugar, acid, and temperature is what turns fruit into a shelf-stable spread. Master these three, and you can make jam from any fruit..Trim()

Food Safety and Sterilization Basics

Food safety is not optional when making jam. Improperly sterilized jars or lids can lead to mould, yeast, or dangerous bacteria like Clostridium botulinum, which causes botulism. Even though jam is acidic, you must follow safe practices. The good news: sterilization is simple and cheap..Trim()

.Trim()

First, wash your hands, all utensils, and work surfaces with hot soapy water. Rinse well. Then sterilize your jars. The easiest method is boiling: place clean jars in a large pot, cover with water, and boil for 10 minutes. Keep them in the hot water until you're ready to fill. Lids should be placed in a separate pot of hot (not boiling) water for a few minutesâboiling can damage the sealing compound. Alternatively, you can wash jars in a dishwasher on a hot cycle, but boiling is more reliable..Trim()

.Trim()

After filling jars with hot jam, wipe the rims with a clean, damp cloth to remove any stickiness. Any jam on the rim will prevent a proper seal. Place the lid on and screw the band finger-tightânot too tight, because air needs to escape during processing. Then process the filled jars in a boiling water bath. This step is crucial. Put jars on a rack in a deep pot, cover with at least 2 cm of water, and boil for 10 minutes (add 1 minute for every 300 meters above sea level). Then turn off heat and let jars sit in the water for 5 minutes before removing..Trim()

.Trim()

As jars cool, you'll hear a 'pop' soundâthat means the lid has sealed. After 24 hours, check the seal: press the centre of the lid. If it doesn't move, it's sealed. If it pops up and down, it's not sealed. Store unsealed jars in the fridge and use within 2 weeks. Sealed jars can last a year in a cool, dark place..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, where electricity may be unreliable, proper sealing is even more important. Never use jars with cracks or chipped rims. Never reuse lids that are rusty or bent. If you see any mould on the surface of jam after opening, discard the whole jarâdon't just scrape it off. Mould can produce invisible toxins. Follow these steps, and your jam will be safe and delicious..Trim()

Overview of Fruit Selection and Seasonality

The best jam starts with the best fruit. In Africa, you have access to an incredible variety of fruits, many of which are perfect for jam. The key is to choose fruit that is ripe but not overripe. Overripe fruit has less pectin and may have mould or off-flavours. Underripe fruit is too tart and lacks sweetness. The ideal is fruit that is fully coloured, slightly soft, and fragrant..Trim()

.Trim()

Seasonality matters. Buying fruit in season means it's cheaper, tastier, and more nutritious. In West Africa, mango season runs from March to July. In East Africa, mangoes peak from December to February. Guavas are often available year-round but are sweetest in the rainy season. Pineapples are best in the dry season. Papaya is available all year but tastes best in warm months. Strawberries, if you can find them, are usually a cool-season crop. Plan your jam making around these cycles..Trim()

.Trim()

When you buy fruit, look for bruises or soft spots. A few bruises are fineâjust cut them out. But avoid fruit with mould or a fermented smell. Wash fruit thoroughly under running water. For fuzzy fruits like peaches or guavas, rub the skin gently to remove dirt. For firm fruits like pineapple, cut off the top and bottom, then peel. Remove all seeds and pits. For mangoes, cut the cheeks off the pit and scoop out the flesh..Trim()

.Trim()

You can also use frozen or dried fruit when fresh is out of season. Frozen fruit is often picked at peak ripeness and works well for jam. Dried fruit, like dates or figs, can be rehydrated and cooked into a rich spread. But fresh local fruit is always best. If you have a garden or know a farmer, ask for 'second-grade' fruitâit's cheaper and perfect for jam..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, consider mixing fruits to balance flavour and pectin. High-pectin fruits like guava can be combined with low-pectin fruits like mango. A classic African combination is mango and ginger, or pineapple and coconut. Experiment with small batches. Keep notes on what worked. Over time, you'll develop your own seasonal jam calendar, making the most of what each month offers..Trim()

Choosing the Right Fruits for Jam

The best jam starts with the right fruit. In Africa, you have amazing choices: mango, papaya, pineapple, guava, banana, and berries like strawberries in cooler areas. For jam, you need fruits that are ripe but not overripe. Overripe fruits have less natural pectin and more water, which makes jam runny. Underripe fruits have more pectin but less flavor. The sweet spot is fully ripe but still firm. If you press a mango, it should give slightly but not be mushy..Trim()

.Trim()

Not all fruits set equally. High-pectin fruits like guava, green apples, and citrus peels set easily. Medium-pectin fruits include mango, papaya, and pineapple. Low-pectin fruits like strawberries, bananas, and peaches need added pectin or a mix with high-pectin fruits. A simple test: mix 1 tablespoon of fruit juice with 1 teaspoon of methylated spirits. If a jelly-like blob forms, pectin is high. If it's stringy, medium. If nothing, low..Trim()

.Trim()

Choose fruits at your local market. Buy in bulk when in season for cheaper prices. For example, mango season in West Africa means you can get a crate for a low price. Wash fruits well, peel if needed (like pineapple and papaya), and remove seeds or pits. For guava, you can leave seeds if you like, but many prefer to strain them out. For berries, remove stems..Trim()

.Trim()

Prepare fruit by cutting into small pieces. For firm fruits like pineapple, dice into 1 cm cubes. For soft fruits like mango, mash lightly. You want about 1 kg of prepared fruit to make about 4-5 jars of jam. Remember, fruit weight after peeling and cutting is what matters. If a recipe calls for 1 kg of fruit, that means 1 kg of clean, ready-to-cook fruit..Trim()

.Trim()

Use a stainless steel or enamel pot. Aluminum or copper can react with acids and give a metallic taste. A wide pot helps water evaporate faster. A wooden spoon for stirring. A blender or potato masher for mashing. Always taste your fruit first. If it's very sweet, you may reduce sugar. If it's tart, you may need more sugar. Adjust to your taste..Trim()

Sugar Varieties and Their Impact on Set

Sugar does more than sweeten jam. It preserves, helps set, and adds texture. In Africa, you'll find white sugar, brown sugar, honey, and sometimes palm sugar. White granulated sugar is the most common and gives a clean taste. It's best for jams where you want fruit flavor to shine. Brown sugar adds a caramel taste and darker color. It can make jam taste richer, but it can overpower delicate fruits like papaya. Honey is natural but expensive. It can be used, but it adds its own flavor and can make jam darker. Palm sugar is used in some regions and gives a unique smoky sweetness..Trim()

.Trim()

The amount of sugar matters. Traditional jam uses equal weight of sugar to fruit. For 1 kg of fruit, use 1 kg of sugar. This high sugar helps set and preserve. But many modern cooks prefer less sugar. If you reduce sugar, you must add pectin or cook longer. A good rule: never reduce sugar below 50% of fruit weight if you want a good set without added pectin. For low-sugar jam, use special pectin like Pomona's..Trim()

.Trim()

Sugar also affects set. Sugar binds with water, making it less available for pectin to form a gel. Too little sugar, and the jam won't set. Too much sugar, and it can crystallize. The right balance is key. When you cook jam, sugar also helps preserve by reducing water activity, which stops mold and bacteria. That's why traditional jams last longer without refrigeration..Trim()

.Trim()

To test if you have enough sugar, use a refractometer. A good jam has 65% sugar content. Without a refractometer, use the plate test: put a drop of jam on a cold plate. If it wrinkles when you push it, it's ready. If it runs, cook longer. Remember, sugar is not just for taste; it's a functional ingredient. Choose your sugar based on the fruit and your desired outcome. For a bright mango jam, use white sugar. For a deep guava jam, brown sugar works well. Always measure by weight, not volume, for accuracy..Trim()

Acid Sources: Lemon Juice, Citric Acid, and Vinegar

Acid is critical in jam making. It does three things: helps pectin set, balances sweetness, and preserves color and flavor. Without acid, jam can be too sweet and may not set properly. In Africa, you have easy access to lemons, limes, and vinegar. Citric acid powder is also available in some markets..Trim()

.Trim()

Lemon juice is the most common acid. One tablespoon of lemon juice per 1 kg of fruit is a good starting point. For low-pectin fruits like strawberries, use 2 tablespoons. Lemon juice adds a fresh taste. Lime juice works similarly and is popular in tropical regions. Vinegar, especially white vinegar, can be used if lemons are scarce. Use 1 tablespoon of vinegar per 1 kg of fruit. But vinegar has a strong taste, so use it sparingly. Apple cider vinegar is milder but adds color..Trim()

.Trim()

Citric acid powder is concentrated. Use 1/4 teaspoon per 1 kg of fruit. It has no flavor, so it's good for delicate fruits. Dissolve it in a little water before adding. Too much acid can make jam taste sour and can also weaken pectin if overused. So measure carefully..Trim()

.Trim()

Acid also affects color. For example, guava jam without acid turns brown. With lemon juice, it stays pink. Acid also helps set by lowering pH. Pectin needs a pH between 2.8 and 3.5 to gel. Most fruits are in this range, but some like bananas and figs are low-acid. For those, add extra lemon juice. A simple pH test strip can help, but taste is often enough. If jam tastes flat, add a little acid..Trim()

.Trim()

When adding acid, do it at the beginning of cooking. That way it can interact with pectin. If you add at the end, it may not set as well. Also, acid can cause sugar to crystallize if added late. So always follow recipes. For a typical African fruit jam, start with 1 tablespoon lemon juice per 1 kg fruit. Adjust after tasting. Remember, acid is your friend for a perfect set and bright flavor..Trim()

Pectin Types: Liquid, Powder, and Pomona's

Pectin is a natural substance in fruits that makes jam gel. Some fruits have enough; others need added pectin. There are three main types you can buy: liquid pectin, powdered pectin, and Pomona's Universal Pectin. Each works differently..Trim()

.Trim()

Liquid pectin is easy to use. It's added at the end of cooking, after sugar. It sets quickly. Use one pouch (about 85 ml) for 1 kg of fruit. Stir it in, boil for 1 minute, then jar. Liquid pectin gives a softer set, good for jams you want to spread easily. It doesn't require extra acid because it's already balanced. But it's often more expensive and has a shorter shelf life..Trim()

.Trim()

Powdered pectin is the most common. It's added at the beginning, mixed with fruit or sugar. It needs acid and sugar to set. Use 1 tablespoon per 1 kg of fruit. For low-sugar jams, use special low-sugar pectin. Powdered pectin gives a firmer set. It's cheaper and lasts longer. But it can clump if not mixed well. Always mix with a little sugar before adding..Trim()

.Trim()

Pomona's Universal Pectin is different. It's a citrus pectin that sets with calcium, not sugar. That means you can use very little sugar or even honey. It comes with a calcium water packet. You mix the pectin with sugar, then add calcium water to the fruit. Use 1 teaspoon pectin and 1 teaspoon calcium water per 1 kg fruit. Pomona's is great for low-sugar or sugar-free jams. It's more expensive but very flexible. It's available online in many African countries..Trim()

.Trim()

When using pectin, follow the package instructions. Too much pectin makes jam rubbery. Too little makes it runny. If you're using natural high-pectin fruits like guava, you may not need added pectin. Test with the alcohol method. For a beginner, start with powdered pectin for regular jams and Pomona's for low-sugar. Always check the expiry date. Old pectin loses strength. Store in a cool, dry place..Trim()

Using Spices, Herbs, and Flavor Extracts

Spices, herbs, and extracts turn plain fruit jam into something special. In Africa, you have a wealth of flavors: ginger, cinnamon, cloves, nutmeg, vanilla, cardamom, and even chili. Use them to complement fruit, not overpower it. Start with small amounts; you can always add more, but you can't take it out..Trim()

.Trim()

Ginger is popular in mango and pineapple jam. Use 1 teaspoon of freshly grated ginger per 1 kg of fruit. Cinnamon pairs well with apple, pear, and banana. Use 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon per 1 kg. Cloves are strong; use 2-3 whole cloves per 1 kg, and remove before jarring. Nutmeg is good with guava and papaya; use 1/4 teaspoon ground. Cardamom is great with citrus and berry jams; use 3-4 pods, crushed. Vanilla extract adds warmth; use 1 teaspoon per 1 kg, added at the end of cooking to preserve flavor..Trim()

.Trim()

Herbs like rosemary, mint, and basil can be used. Rosemary with strawberry or lemon jam is refreshing. Use 1 sprig per 1 kg, steep during cooking, then remove. Mint with pineapple or mango is cooling. Use 1 tablespoon chopped fresh mint, added at the end. Basil with strawberry or peach is surprising and delicious. Use 1 tablespoon chopped fresh basil at the end..Trim()

.Trim()
