Identifying Clay Deposits in Your Region

Start by asking local potters, brickmakers, or farmers where they find clay. In many African regions, clay appears along riverbanks, in termite mounds, or where roads cut through hills. Look for areas where the soil is sticky when wet and cracks when dry. You can also check for changes in vegetationâclay soils often support different plants than sandy soils. Once you suspect a deposit, collect samples from different spots within a 10-meter area. Use a hoe and shovel to dig 50 cm down to reach less weathered material. In Africa, common clay colors are reddish-brown (iron-rich), gray, and white (kaolin). Red clay is good for tiles and bricks; white clay is better for ceramics..Trim()

Test the clay's behavior by making a small ball and letting it dry in the shade. If it cracks badly, it may be too sandy or too plastic. Mix a little water to form a thick paste and rub it between your fingersâgritty means sand, smooth means fine clay. Finally, check local regulations. In some countries, digging clay on community land requires permission from elders or local government. Always ask before you dig. With practice, you'll identify good deposits within a few hours of walking your area..Trim()

Testing Clay Quality: Plasticity, Shrinkage, and Firing

To turn local clay into tiles or ceramics, you must test three key properties: plasticity, shrinkage, and firing behavior. Start with a simple plasticity test. Take 100 grams of dry clay, add water slowly, and knead until it forms a smooth, workable ball. Roll it into a coil 1 cm thick. Next, test shrinkage. Make a rectangular bar 10 cm long, 2 cm wide, and 1 cm thick. Mark a 10 cm line on it with a ruler. Let it air-dry in the shade for 3 days. Measure the line again. Raw clay contains stones, roots, and sand that ruin tiles. Cleaning starts with drying. Spread clay on a concrete floor or tarpaulin in the sun for 2-3 days.
