Nutritional Basics: Carbohydrates, Proteins, and Fats

Breakfast cereals are mainly carbohydrates, but they also contain proteins and fats. Carbohydrates provide energy. In cereals, starch is the main carbohydrate. Whole grains like millet and sorghum have complex carbs that digest slowly, keeping you full. Refined grains like white maize lose fiber and nutrients. Major Cereal Grains: Wheat, Corn, Rice, Oats, and Barley

Cereal grains are the foundation of breakfast cereals. In Africa, the most common are maize (corn), rice, wheat, sorghum, millet, and oats. Each has different properties. Maize is cheap and widely available; use white or yellow maize meal for extruded cereals. Rice, especially broken rice, is used for puffed rice. Wheat is used for flakes and farina. Oats are used for rolled oats and granola. Barley is used for malted cereals and flakes. When selecting grains, check for moisture content (should be below 14% to prevent mould), foreign matter (stones, dust), and insect damage. Buy from local farmers or millers to reduce cost. For small-scale production, you can start with maize, rice, and wheat. Store grains in dry, ventilated rooms on pallets, away from walls. Use within 6 months. Clean grains by winnowing or sieving before milling. For puffed cereals, grains need to be uniform in size. For flakes, grains are cooked, dried, and rolled. In Africa, traditional grains like millet and sorghum can be used for nutritious, gluten-free cereals. Test each batch for hardness and taste. A simple test: chew a few grains; they should be dry and crunchy, not soft or musty. Always source from reliable suppliers and inspect deliveries. Remember, quality of grain determines quality of final product..Trim()

Sweeteners: Sugar, Honey, and Syrups

Sweeteners add taste, texture, and colour to breakfast cereals. Common sweeteners are white sugar, brown sugar, honey, glucose syrup, and molasses. In Africa, you can also use local honey, palm sugar, and cane syrup. Sugar is the cheapest and most used. For coating cereals, use fine granulated sugar. Honey gives natural flavour and moisture; use raw or processed honey. Syrups like glucose or corn syrup prevent crystallisation and add shine. When using sweeteners, measure accurately. Fats and Oils in Cereal Production

Fats and oils add richness, crispiness, and help bind ingredients. Common oils are vegetable oil, palm oil, coconut oil, and groundnut oil. Fortification: Vitamins and Minerals

Fortification adds vitamins and minerals to replace those lost during processing. Common fortificants: iron, zinc, vitamin A, B vitamins (thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, folic acid), and vitamin C. In Africa, fortification is important to fight malnutrition. Use premix blends from reputable suppliers. For 1 kg of cereal, add according to supplier instructions; typically 0.1â0.5 g premix. For vitamin A, use heat-stable forms. Store premix in cool, dark place; use within expiry. Test uniformity by taking samples from different parts of batch. Over-fortification is toxic; never exceed recommended levels. Under-fortification wastes money. For small-scale, buy pre-fortified flour or use fortified premix. Label clearly: 'Fortified with vitamins and minerals'. In some African countries, fortification is mandatory for maize and wheat flour. Check local regulations. For traditional grains like millet, fortification can be done. Use a simple scale (0.01 g accuracy). Keep records of batch numbers and amounts. Train workers on safe handling; wear masks and gloves. Sourcing and Quality Control of Ingredients

Sourcing quality ingredients at good prices is key to profit. Buy directly from farmers, millers, or wholesalers. In Africa, local markets and cooperatives offer fresh grains. For sweeteners, oils, and flavours, use reputable suppliers. Always inspect deliveries: check for moisture, insects, stones, and off-smells. For grains, test moisture with a simple moisture meter or bite test (should crack, not squash). For oils, smell for rancidity. For sweeteners, check for crystallization or fermentation. Establish relationships with suppliers; negotiate bulk discounts. Keep records of supplier names, dates, and batch numbers. Set a quality standard: e.g., moisture <14%, foreign matter <1%, no live insects. Reject poor batches. Store ingredients properly: grains on pallets in dry room, oils in dark containers, sweeteners airtight. Use FIFO (first in, first out). Train workers on inspection. For small-scale, do simple tests: float grains in water (bad grains float), sieve for stones. For fortificants, buy from certified suppliers. Quality control saves money by preventing spoiled batches. Test final product for taste, texture, and appearance. Keep samples for reference. In hot climates, use preservatives and proper packaging. Aim for consistent quality to build customer trust. Start with a checklist for each ingredient. Review suppliers annually. Good sourcing and QC reduce waste and increase sales..Trim()

Cleaning and Conditioning of Grains

Before you mill any grain into breakfast cereal, you must clean it. Dirty grain ruins flavor, wears out your mill, and can make people sick. Start with a simple winnowing basket or a mechanical aspirator to blow away chaff, dust, and light seeds. For small-scale operations, a 500 kg/hour destoner costs about $1,200. After cleaning, condition the grain. Conditioning means adjusting moisture so the bran and germ separate easily during milling. Roller milling is the heart of flake production. You need a roller mill with two smooth steel rollers, each about 20-30 cm in diameter and 30-50 cm long. HACCP Principles in Cereal Production

HACCP stands for Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points. It is a food safety system that prevents problems before they happen. In your cereal production, you will use HACCP to keep your product safe from harmful bacteria, chemicals, and physical objects. The first step is to identify where hazards could occur. Write down every step in your process, from receiving raw materials to packing the final product. Then, for each step, ask: what could go wrong here? Next, determine the critical control points (CCPs). These are steps where you can prevent, eliminate, or reduce a hazard to a safe level. For example, roasting or toasting your cereal at 150Â°C for 10 minutes can kill most bacteria. That is a CCP. You must set critical limits: the minimum temperature and time needed. Monitor these limits regularly. If the temperature drops below 150Â°C, you must stop and fix it. Keep records of all monitoring. You also need corrective actions: what will you do if something goes wrong? For instance, if the roaster fails, you might re-roast the batch or discard it. Finally, verify that your HACCP plan works by testing your final product for bacteria and checking records. Clean all equipment with hot water and detergent after each batch. Use a sanitizer like chlorine solution (100 ppm) to kill bacteria on surfaces. Store your finished cereal in airtight containers. If you use plastic bags, make sure they are food-grade and sealed properly. Avoid storing near walls or on the floor, as dampness can seep in. In hot, humid African climates, spoilage happens fast. Packaging keeps your cereal safe from moisture, insects, and bacteria. If the seal is weak, air and water can get in, and the cereal will spoil quickly.
