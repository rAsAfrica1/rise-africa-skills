Module 1 · Sourcing Local Ginger and Turmeric

Identifying fresh ginger and turmeric at local markets

When you walk into a local market in Lagos, Accra, or Nairobi, you'll see ginger and turmeric piled in baskets or spread on sacks. To pick the best, start with your eyes. Fresh ginger should have smooth, light brown skin. Avoid ginger that looks wrinkled or has dark spots. Turmeric should be bright orange inside when you break a small piece. The outside skin is usually brownish-orange. If the turmeric looks pale yellow or white inside, it's old and weak..Trim()

Next, use your hands. Pick up a piece of ginger. It should feel heavy for its size and firm, not soft or spongy. Squeeze gently: if it gives way, it's rotting. Turmeric fingers should also be firm. Break a small piece to check for moisture and strong smell. Fresh turmeric has a peppery, earthy smell. Ginger should smell sharp and citrusy. If there's no smell, it's been sitting too long..Trim()

Look for mould. White or black fuzzy patches mean the root is spoiled. Also check for tiny holes or sawdust-like powder, which are signs of pests like weevils or beetles. A few small spots can be cut away, but if mould covers more than a small area, reject the whole piece. In hot climates, roots spoil fast, so buy from sellers who keep their stock in the shade and rotate it daily..Trim()

.Trim()

Ask the seller when the roots were harvested. The best ginger and turmeric come from farms within a few days' travel. If they say it's from last month, be cautious. You can also test freshness by scratching the skin with your fingernail. If it's dry and papery, it's old. If it's moist and reveals bright colour underneath, it's fresh. Buy small quantities first, take them home, and see how they perform in your juice or tea. That way you learn to judge quality by results, not just appearance..Trim()

Negotiating bulk prices with farmers in your area

Buying directly from farmers can cut your costs by 30-50% compared to market retailers. Start by finding farmers who grow ginger or turmeric. Ask at local agricultural extension offices, or visit nearby farming communities. In many African countries, ginger is grown in areas like Kaduna in Nigeria, or the Volta Region in Ghana. Turmeric is common in Uganda and Malawi. Once you find a farmer, visit their farm. See the crop yourself. That builds trust and lets you check quality before harvest..Trim()

When you're ready to negotiate, know the current market price per kilogram. Check with two or three local traders to get a baseline. Then offer the farmer a price slightly below retail but above what a middleman would pay. For example, if retail is 2,000 naira per kg, a middleman might pay 800 naira. You could offer 1,200 naira. That's a win for both. Be clear about quantity: "I will buy 50 kg every month." That consistency is valuable to farmers..Trim()

Negotiation is not just about price. Talk about payment terms. Can you pay on delivery? Can you pay half upfront? Farmers often need cash for transport or labour. Offering a deposit shows you're serious. Also discuss harvest dates. If you buy right after harvest, prices are lower. Ginger and turmeric are usually harvested in the dry season, from November to February in many parts of Africa. Plan your bulk purchases then..Trim()

Checking for mould, pests, and quality signs

Mould and pests can destroy your entire stock and make your products unsafe. When you receive ginger or turmeric, inspect every sack. Start by smelling the roots. A musty, earthy smell means mould. Fresh roots smell sharp and clean. Then look closely. Mould appears as white, grey, or black fuzz. It often grows in cuts or bruises. If you see mould on more than a few pieces, reject the whole batch. Mould produces toxins that can make people sick, and you can't wash them away..Trim()

.Trim()

Pests are easier to spot. Look for tiny holes in the skin. These are made by beetles or weevils. You might also see fine powder, which is frass (insect waste). Shake the roots over a white cloth. If powder falls off, there are pests. Also check for sticky residue, which can be from scale insects. A few pests can be removed by washing and drying, but if the infestation is heavy, don't buy. Pests multiply fast in warm storage..Trim()

.Trim()

Quality signs go beyond mould and pests. Good ginger is firm, heavy, and has smooth skin. It should snap when you break it, not bend. Turmeric should be hard and have a bright orange interior. If it's soft or has a dull colour, it's old. Also check for shrivelling. Shrivelled roots have lost moisture and flavour. They weigh less, so you're paying for water loss. Look for cuts and bruises. These are entry points for mould. Small cuts can be trimmed, but large ones mean the root is damaged..Trim()

.Trim()

When you buy, ask to see the inside of a few roots. Cut them open with a small knife. Ginger should be pale yellow, not brown or grey. Turmeric should be deep orange, not pale. If the inside is discoloured, the roots are old or diseased. Finally, trust your senses. If something looks or smells off, don't buy it. It's better to pay a bit more for quality than to lose money on spoiled stock..Trim()

Storing roots to prevent spoilage in hot climates

In hot, humid African climates, fresh ginger and turmeric can spoil in a week if stored poorly. The key is to control moisture, heat, and air. Never leave roots in a plastic bag in the sun. That creates condensation and mould. Instead, store them in a cool, dark, dry place. A ventilated basket or a wooden crate works well. If you have a fan, keep air moving. Ideal temperature is 10-15Â°C, but most homes don't have that. So use the coolest spot you have, like a shaded corner or a lower shelf..Trim()

.Trim()

For short-term storage (1-2 weeks), keep roots whole and unwashed. Dirt protects them. Spread them in a single layer on a dry surface. Don't pile them high. Check daily and remove any soft or mouldy pieces immediately. For longer storage (1-3 months), you can use sand. Get clean, dry sand. Put a layer of sand in a box, then a layer of roots, then more sand. Keep the box in a dark, cool place. The sand absorbs moisture and keeps roots firm. This method works well for ginger and turmeric..Trim()

.Trim()

Another option is drying. Slice roots thinly and dry them in the sun or a solar dryer. In 2-3 days, they become hard and brittle. Store dried slices in an airtight jar in a dark cupboard. They last 6-12 months. You can grind them into powder later. If you have a refrigerator, wrap roots in a paper towel, then place in a perforated plastic bag. They last 3-4 weeks. But don't freeze fresh roots unless you plan to blend them immediately; freezing makes them mushy..Trim()

.Trim()

Always check your stock weekly. Look for mould, soft spots, or pests. If you find mould on one root, remove it and check the rest. Mould spreads quickly. Also, keep roots away from onions and potatoes, which release gases that speed up spoilage. Finally, rotate your stock: use older roots first. Label your storage dates. That way you never lose money to spoilage..Trim()

Finally, keep records. Write down each supplier's name, contact, price, and quality notes. Over time, you'll know who is best for which season. That knowledge saves you money and stress..Trim()

Calculating cost per kilogram for your budget

Knowing your cost per kilogram is essential for pricing your shots and teas. Without it, you might sell at a loss. Start by recording every purchase. Write down the date, supplier, total weight, and total price. For example, you buy 25 kg of ginger for 30,000 naira. Your cost per kg is 30,000 Ã· 25 = 1,200 naira. But that's not the final cost. You also have transport, maybe 2,000 naira. And you lose some weight to spoilage or trimming. If you lose 10%, your usable weight is 22.5 kg. So your true cost per kg is (30,000 + 2,000) Ã· 22.5 = 1,422 naira. Always calculate after spoilage..Trim()

For turmeric, same method. If you buy 10 kg for 15,000 naira, cost per kg is 1,500 naira. Add transport of 1,000 naira. Assume 5% loss, so usable weight is 9.5 kg. True cost = (15,000 + 1,000) Ã· 9.5 = 1,684 naira. Now you know your break-even. If you use 50g of ginger per bottle of shot, that's 0.05 kg. At 1,422 naira per kg, the ginger in one bottle costs 71 naira. Add turmeric, lemon, honey, bottle, and labour, and you get your total cost per bottle..Trim()

.Trim()

Use a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone. Record every expense: roots, transport, packaging, gas, electricity, labour. At the end of the month, add them up and divide by the number of bottles sold. That's your actual cost per unit. Compare it to your selling price. If you're not making at least 30% profit, adjust your price or find cheaper suppliers. Don't forget to include your own time. Many small businesses fail because they don't pay themselves..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, review your costs every month. Prices change with seasons. During harvest, ginger might be 800 naira per kg. Off-season, it could be 2,000 naira. Buy in bulk during harvest and store properly. That lowers your average cost. Also, negotiate transport. Can you share a truck with other buyers? Can you pick up yourself? Small savings add up. By knowing your cost per kilogram, you can set prices confidently and grow your business..Trim()

Module 2 · Basic Equipment for Small-Scale Production

Choosing affordable blenders and juicers for your kitchen

For ginger and turmeric shots you need a machine that can break tough fibrous roots into a smooth pulp. A 1000W to 1500W blender with a glass or thick plastic jug is the sweet spot. In most African cities you will find these between 150,000 and 400,000 shillings or the equivalent in naira, cedis or rand. Brands like Kenwood, Philips, Ramtons and Sonik are common and spare parts are available. Avoid the very cheap 300W blenders sold in open markets; they burn out after two or three kilos of ginger..Trim()

.Trim()

A juicer is optional at the start. A slow masticating juicer gives more juice and less foam, but costs two to three times a blender. A centrifugal juicer is faster but struggles with turmeric because the roots are hard and stringy. If your budget is tight, buy one good blender first and add a juicer when daily production passes 15 litres. Many successful producers never buy a juicer; they blend with water and press through cloth..Trim()

.Trim()

Check three things before paying. First, the blade: stainless steel, four or six prongs, thick not thin. Second, the motor base: it should feel heavy and have a reset button underneath. Third, the jug seal: a rubber gasket that fits tightly, because turmeric stains and leaks. Ask the seller to blend a piece of raw carrot in front of you. If it stalls, walk away..Trim()

.Trim()

Buy from a shop that gives a written receipt and a warranty of at least six months. Keep the box and manual. Register the warranty by phone or WhatsApp the same day. Second-hand blenders can work if you test them with carrot and listen for grinding noises in the motor. A good blender plus a spare jug will carry you through your first year of production..Trim()

Using manual graters and presses when electricity is unreliable

Load shedding, fuel prices and rural locations mean you cannot depend on electricity every day. Manual tools keep production moving and cost very little. The three essentials are a stainless steel box grater, a wooden or plastic ginger press, and a strong cotton cloth for squeezing. A good box grater with fine and coarse sides costs under 10,000 shillings or 5,000 naira in most markets..Trim()

.Trim()

To grate ginger and turmeric by hand, wash and scrub the roots, cut them into 5cm pieces, and rub them against the fine side of the grater over a wide bowl. Turmeric will stain your hands and the grater, so wear gloves or rub your hands with oil before starting. One person can grate about 2kg of ginger in 45 minutes. That yields roughly 1.2 litres of juice after pressing, depending on the water you add..Trim()

.Trim()

For pressing, you have three options. A simple wooden press costs little and works like a garlic press for larger roots. A lever press with a metal plate is faster and costs more. The cheapest method is the cloth twist: put the grated pulp in a clean cotton bag, twist the top tightly, and squeeze with both hands over a bowl. Two people can twist a stronger cloth for more yield. Add 200ml of clean water per kilo of pulp to help release the juice..Trim()

.Trim()

Keep manual tools clean and dry. Stainless steel does not rust easily but the wooden handles can grow mould. Wash with hot water and vinegar after every use, then dry in the sun. Store the grater hanging, not flat on a wet surface. If you produce every day, buy two graters so one can dry while you use the other. Manual work is slower but it never stops because of a power cut..Trim()

Selecting food-grade bottles and jars from local suppliers

Your packaging protects the product and carries your brand. For ginger and turmeric shots, the best bottle is food-grade PET plastic or glass with a tight screw cap. Glass is heavier and more expensive but does not react with the acidic juice. PET is lighter, cheaper and safer for delivery by boda boda or okada. Both are acceptable if they are new and food-grade..Trim()

.Trim()

In most African cities you can find suppliers in the industrial area or the main market. A 250ml PET bottle with cap costs between 60 and 150 shillings or 30 to 80 naira when bought in packs of 100. A 500ml glass bottle costs 300 to 700 shillings or 150 to 350 naira. Always ask for "virgin" or "new" material, not recycled. Recycled plastic can release chemicals into the acidic juice and give a plastic taste..Trim()

.Trim()

Check the cap liner. A good cap has a rubber or foam liner inside that seals against the rim. Without it, the juice leaks and spoils faster. Fill one bottle with water, close it, shake hard for ten seconds and turn it upside down over a cloth. If water comes out, reject that batch of caps. Also smell the bottle: it should have no chemical or petrol smell..Trim()

.Trim()

For jars, choose wide-mouth glass jars with metal lids for dry products like turmeric powder or tea blends. Sterilise them before filling. Buy from a supplier who sells to other food businesses, not from a general plastic shop. Ask for an invoice and keep one sample bottle from each batch so you can trace a problem. Label with product name, ingredients, production date and best-before date..Trim()

Sanitising equipment with boiling water and vinegar

Clean equipment is the difference between a product that lasts two weeks and one that spoils in three days. You do not need expensive chemicals. Boiling water and white vinegar kill most bacteria, yeasts and moulds that attack fresh juice. This method works for blenders, jugs, knives, chopping boards, spoons, cloths, bottles and caps..Trim()

.Trim()

Start by washing everything with warm water and dish soap to remove pulp and oil. Rinse well. Then pour boiling water over the surfaces or soak small items in a bowl of boiling water for two minutes. For blenders, half-fill the jug with boiling water, add two tablespoons of white vinegar, and run the machine for 30 seconds. Empty and repeat with clean boiling water only. Do not put cold glass into boiling water; warm it first or it will crack..Trim()

.Trim()

For bottles and caps, make a sanitising bath: 5 litres of hot water plus 250ml of white vinegar. Soak bottles for two minutes, then drain upside down on a clean rack. Caps need only 30 seconds because heat can warp the plastic. Cloths and straining bags should be boiled for five minutes in a pot with a spoon of vinegar, then dried in direct sunlight..Trim()

.Trim()

Vinegar is cheap and available in every market. Buy the clear white type, not flavoured. Keep a separate bucket and brush only for sanitising. Wash your hands with soap before touching clean equipment. Never dry bottles with a dirty cloth; air-dry them upside down. If you have a gas cooker, boil water in the morning and keep it in a thermos so you can sanitise throughout the day without reheating..Trim()

Setting up a clean production corner in your home

You do not need a factory. A clean corner of your kitchen, a spare room or a covered veranda can work if you control dirt, pests and cross-contamination. Choose a space at least 2 metres by 2 metres with a table, a water source nearby and a door or curtain you can close. Keep it away from toilets, rubbish bins and animal areas..Trim()

.Trim()

Your production table should be stainless steel or a smooth plastic-topped table that you can wipe. Wood is hard to sanitise. Cover it with a clean plastic sheet if needed. Arrange your space in three zones: a dirty zone for washing and peeling roots, a clean zone for blending and pressing, and a packing zone for filling bottles. Never let dirty roots touch the packing zone. Use different coloured cloths for each zone: red for dirty, blue for clean, green for packing..Trim()

.Trim()

You need a hand-washing station with soap, a jerrycan with a tap, and a bucket for waste water. Keep a small bin with a lid for peelings and empty it daily. A shelf above the table holds clean bottles upside down. A small fridge is ideal for storing finished shots, but if you do not have one, use a cool box with ice packs and sell within 48 hours..Trim()

.Trim()

Light and ventilation matter. Work near a window or under a bright bulb so you can see dirt. Keep the floor dry. Cover your hair, wear a clean apron and remove jewellery. Do not eat, smoke or keep pets in the production corner. At the end of each day, wash the floor, wipe surfaces with vinegar water, and leave the space clean for the next morning..Trim()

Maintaining and repairing common equipment faults

Blenders, juicers and presses fail in predictable ways. Learning to fix small faults saves money and keeps production running. The most common blender problem is a burnt smell or the motor stopping under load. This usually means you are overfilling the jug or blending dry roots without water. Switch off, unplug, remove half the contents, add 200ml of water and try again. If the motor has a reset button underneath, press it..Trim()

.Trim()

A leaking jug is usually a worn rubber gasket or a loose blade assembly. Remove the base, take out the gasket, wash it and check for cracks. If cracked, buy a replacement at the same shop where you bought the blender. If the blade is loose, tighten the nut underneath with a spanner, but do not overtighten or you will crack the plastic. A dull blade can be sharpened by a metalworker for a small fee, but replacement blades are often cheaper..Trim()

.Trim()

Juicers clog when turmeric fibres wrap around the spinning basket. Switch off, unplug, and clean the basket with a stiff brush every 2kg of roots. Never put your hand inside a juicer that is plugged in. Manual presses need oil on the screw thread every week and a check that the metal plate is not bent. Wooden presses should be dried in the shade, not direct sun, or they crack..Trim()

.Trim()

Keep a small repair kit: screwdrivers, pliers, a spanner, electrical tape, spare gaskets and a spare blade. Keep a notebook of faults and fixes. If a motor sparks or smokes heavily, stop using it and take it to a qualified technician. Do not open the motor housing yourself. A well-maintained blender should last two to three years of daily use..Trim()

Washing roots thoroughly to remove soil and bacteria

Ginger and turmeric roots come out of the ground covered in soil, which can carry harmful bacteria like Salmonella and E. coli. To wash properly, start by filling a large basin with clean water. Add 1 tablespoon of salt and 2 tablespoons of white vinegar per 5 litres of water. This solution helps kill bacteria. Then rinse under running clean water. If the water is very dirty, repeat the process with fresh water. For a final sanitising step, soak the roots in a solution of 1 teaspoon of household bleach (unscented, 3.5% concentration) per 4 litres of water for 2 minutes, then rinse again with clean water. Module 4 · Making Classic Ginger Shots

Peeling and chopping ginger for maximum juice yield

To get the most juice from your ginger, start with fresh, firm roots. Look for ginger that is heavy for its size, with smooth skin and no mould. In many African markets, you can buy ginger by the 'mudu' or small heap. For a 500ml bottle of shots, you need about 250g of fresh ginger. Peeling: use a small spoon to scrape off the skin. A spoon removes less flesh than a knife, so you waste less. If the ginger is very knobbly, break it into smaller pieces first. You can also soak ginger in water for 10 minutes to loosen dirt, then scrub with a clean brush. Chopping: cut ginger into thin slices or small cubes, about 1cm thick. Thinner pieces blend more easily and release more juice. Remove any soft or discoloured spots. For maximum yield, freeze the chopped ginger for 2 hours before blending. Freezing breaks down cell walls, so you get more juice. If you don't have a freezer, soak chopped ginger in cold water for 30 minutes, then drain. This also helps. A good ratio is 1 part ginger to 2 parts water by weight. So for 250g ginger, use 500ml clean water. Blend in a strong blender or food processor. If you only have a manual grater, grate the ginger finely, then squeeze the pulp through a clean cloth. Always work on a clean surface. Wash your hands, knives, and cutting board with soap and water before starting. This prevents contamination and makes your shots last longer. Yield: 250g ginger + 500ml water gives about 600ml of strained juice. That fills two 300ml bottles. With practice, you can increase yield by blending twice: blend, strain, then blend the pulp again with a little water. This second pressing can add 10-15% more juice. Blending ginger with water and straining for shots

After peeling and chopping, you are ready to blend. Use a blender with a strong motor. If you have a small blender, work in batches. The basic recipe: for every 250g of chopped ginger, add 500ml of clean drinking water. Do not use hot water, as it can kill beneficial compounds and make the shot taste cooked. Cold or room temperature water is best. Blend on high speed for 2 to 3 minutes. Stop and scrape down the sides with a spatula, then blend again for 1 minute. The mixture should look like a thick smoothie. If it is too thick, add a little more water, 50ml at a time. Now strain. You need a fine-mesh strainer, a clean muslin cloth, or a new clean cotton shirt. Place the cloth over a large bowl. Pour the blended mixture into the cloth. Gather the corners and twist to squeeze out all the liquid. Squeeze hard. The dry pulp left behind can be composted or used in cooking. For a clearer shot, strain twice: first through a coarse sieve, then through a fine cloth. Some people like a little pulp for texture, but for bottling, clear juice is better because it lasts longer. Yield: from 250g ginger and 500ml water, you should get about 500-600ml of strained juice. If you get less, your ginger was dry or old. Taste the juice. It should be spicy and strong. If it is too mild, you can add more ginger next time. If it is too strong, dilute with a little water. For a 300ml bottle, you need about 250ml of this juice, leaving space for lemon and other additions. Always work in a clean kitchen. Sterilise your bottles by boiling them in water for 10 minutes, then let them dry upside down. This step is critical for shelf life. Without sterilising, your shots may spoil in 2-3 days. Calculating costs and setting profitable prices

To make money from your ginger and turmeric shots, you must know exactly what each bottle costs you. Start by listing every ingredient: ginger, turmeric, lemons, honey, and water. For example, if you buy 1 kg of ginger for 50 birr and use 200g per batch, that's 10 birr. Turmeric: 100g at 30 birr per kg = 3 birr. Lemons: 5 lemons at 5 birr each = 25 birr. Honey: 200ml at 100 birr per litre = 20 birr. Water: negligible. Total ingredients: 58 birr. Next, add packaging: bottles, caps, labels. If a bottle costs 15 birr and you fill 10 bottles, that's 150 birr. So total direct costs = 208 birr for 10 bottles, or 20.8 birr per bottle. Now add overheads: electricity for blending, transport to market, and your time. If you spend 2 hours per batch and want to earn 50 birr per hour, that's 100 birr, making total cost 308 birr, or 30.8 birr per bottle. To set a profitable price, aim for at least 50% markup. So selling price = 30.8 * 1.5 = 46.2 birr. Round to 50 birr per bottle. Check competitors: if others sell at 40 birr, you need to reduce costs or add value (e.g., organic label). Always calculate per batch, not per bottle, to avoid mistakes. Use a notebook or a simple phone app like Excel or Google Sheets to track. Review costs monthly because prices change. If ginger price rises, adjust your selling price or find a cheaper supplier. Never guess your costsâguessing leads to losses..Trim()

Reinvesting profits to buy better equipment

When you start selling ginger and turmeric shots, you might use a hand blender and manual juicer. That's fine, but as sales grow, you'll need better equipment to save time and increase output. Reinvesting profits means taking a portion of your earnings and buying tools that make your work faster and more consistent. For example, if you make 500 birr profit in a month, don't spend it all on personal needs. Set aside at least 30% (150 birr) for equipment. A good quality blender (like a NutriBullet or similar) costs around 1,500 birr. Save for 10 months, or buy a used one for 800 birr. A stainless steel juicer might cost 2,000 birr. Start with the most impactful item: a high-speed blender reduces blending time from 30 minutes to 5 minutes per batch. That means you can make more bottles per day. Another useful tool is a bottle filling funnel and a capping machine. A manual capping machine costs about 500 birr and ensures bottles are sealed tightly, preventing leaks. Also consider a small refrigerator for storage if you don't have oneâit keeps shots fresh longer. Buy equipment gradually, one item at a time, and only after you have consistent sales. Don't go into debt for equipment. Use profits only. Keep a separate savings container or mobile money account for equipment. Track how much you save weekly. Once you buy a new tool, calculate how much time or money it saves. For instance, if a blender saves 2 hours per day, and you value your time at 50 birr per hour, that's 100 birr saved daily. The equipment pays for itself in 8 days. That's a smart reinvestment..Trim()

Keeping separate business and personal money

One of the biggest mistakes small business owners make is mixing business and personal money. You buy personal airtime with business cash, or you use business profit to pay school fees without recording it. This makes it impossible to know if your business is truly profitable. To avoid this, open a separate bank account or mobile money account for your business. In many African countries, you can open a free business account with a local bank or use services like M-Pesa, Airtel Money, or MTN MoMo. Use that account only for business income and expenses. When you sell shots, deposit the money into the business account. When you buy ginger, pay from that account. Pay yourself a fixed salary, say 500 birr per week, and transfer it to your personal account. That salary is your personal money. Any remaining profit stays in the business account for reinvestment. Keep a simple record book or use a free app like Wave or QuickBooks. Write down every transaction: date, description, money in, money out. At the end of each month, calculate total sales minus total expenses. That's your profit. If you took 2000 birr as salary, but profit was only 1500 birr, you are overpaying yourself. Adjust. Separate money also helps you save for taxes, licenses, and emergencies. It builds trust with customers and suppliers who see you as professional. Planning for seasonal demand and festivals

Demand for ginger and turmeric shots is not the same all year. During cold and flu season (rainy months), sales rise because people want immune boosters. In many African countries, June to August (cold season) and December (festive) are busy. During these times, you might sell 3 times your normal volume. So, starting two months before, increase production. Buy extra ginger and turmeric when prices are low (after harvest, usually November to January). Store them properly: ginger in a cool, dry place, turmeric in airtight containers. You can also freeze peeled ginger for up to 3 months. Second, create special festival packages. For example, a 'Festive Immunity Pack' with 6 bottles at a discount (e.g., 250 birr instead of 300). Promote on WhatsApp, Facebook, and local radio. Third, hire temporary helpers for the busy period. Train them in advance. Fourth, manage cash flow. During peak, you earn more, so save a portion for the slow season. Slow months (like March to May) may have low sales. Use savings to cover costs and pay yourself. Also, during slow times, focus on marketing and finding new customers. Offer subscriptions: weekly delivery of 5 bottles at a small discount. That gives steady income. Finally, track sales from last year to predict this year. Keep a simple chart. If you sold 500 bottles in December last year, plan for 600 this year. Being prepared means you never run out of stock or miss a sales opportunity..Trim()
