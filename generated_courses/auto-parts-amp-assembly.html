Module 2 · Basic Tools and Safety in Auto Assembly

Essential Hand Tools for Auto Assembly: Spanners, Screwdrivers, and Pliers

As an auto assembly technician, you will use three main hand tools daily: spanners, screwdrivers, and pliers. Spanners (also called wrenches) come in two common types: open-end and ring. Open-end spanners are flat with a U-shaped jaw, while ring spanners have a closed loop that grips all six sides of a bolt. For most assembly work, use the correct size spanner that fits snugly. A 10mm spanner is common for small bolts, 13mm for medium, and 17mm for larger ones. Never use an adjustable spanner (shifting spanner) for final tightening because it can slip and round off bolt heads. Always pull the spanner toward you rather than pushing, so if it slips, your knuckles won't hit sharp metal..Trim()

.Trim()

Screwdrivers come in flat-head and Phillips (cross-head) types. Choose the size that matches the screw slot exactly. A screwdriver that is too small will damage the screw head, making it hard to remove later. For auto assembly, you'll often need a Phillips #2 for interior trim screws and a flat-head 6mm for hose clamps. When using a screwdriver, keep the shaft in line with the screw and apply firm pressure while turning to prevent slipping..Trim()

.Trim()

Pliers are used for gripping, bending, and cutting. Combination pliers have a gripping jaw and a cutting edge. Needle-nose pliers reach into tight spaces. Locking pliers (vise grips) can hold parts together temporarily. Always wear safety glasses when cutting wire with pliers, as small pieces can fly. In African workshops, tools are often shared, so after use, wipe them clean with a rag, apply a drop of oil to moving parts, and return them to the toolbox. A rusty tool is a weak tool. Buy good quality brands like King Tony or Teng Tools if you can afford them; they last longer than cheap copies. Remember: the right tool for the right job saves time and prevents injuries..Trim()

Using a Torque Wrench Correctly for Bolted Connections

A torque wrench is a special tool that tightens bolts to a specific tightness, called torque, measured in Newton-meters (Nm) or foot-pounds (ft-lb). Over-tightening can stretch or break bolts; under-tightening can cause parts to loosen and fall off. For example, a typical wheel nut on a small car requires about 110 Nm, while a cylinder head bolt might need 40 Nm plus a 90-degree turn. Always check your vehicle's workshop manual for exact torque values. Never guess..Trim()

.Trim()

Before using a torque wrench, set it to the desired value by turning the handle until the scale reads correctly. Some wrenches have a locking collar; twist it to lock the setting. When tightening, pull smoothly and steadily until you feel or hear a click. That click means you've reached the set torque. Do not continue pulling after the click, as this can damage the wrench. Use the torque wrench only for final tightening. First, snug the bolt with a regular spanner, then finish with the torque wrench..Trim()

.Trim()

After use, always release the tension by turning the setting back to zero. Store the wrench in its case, away from moisture and heat. Do not use it as a breaker bar or hammer. In shared workshops, torque wrenches are often dropped or misused; treat it like a precision instrument. If you suspect it's out of calibration, have it checked. A good torque wrench costs between $50 and $200, but it prevents costly engine damage. For African assembly work, where roads can be rough, correct torque on suspension and wheel bolts is critical for safety..Trim()

Personal Safety Gear: Gloves, Goggles, and Overalls

Personal safety gear is your last line of defense against injury. In auto assembly, you must wear gloves, safety goggles, and overalls. Gloves protect your hands from cuts, burns, and chemicals. Use nitrile gloves when handling fluids like oil, coolant, or battery acid. For general assembly, leather or mechanic's gloves give better grip and protect against sharp edges. Never wear loose gloves near rotating parts like fans or belts; they can get caught and pull your hand in. Check gloves for holes before each use..Trim()

.Trim()

Safety goggles or glasses protect your eyes from flying metal chips, sparks, and fluid splashes. When grinding, cutting, or using a hammer, always wear goggles. Even a small rust particle can cause a serious eye infection. In dusty African workshops, a dust mask is also wise when sanding or cleaning with compressed air. Overalls (coveralls) keep your clothes clean and protect your skin from hot surfaces and chemicals. Choose a cotton or flame-resistant overall that fits well but allows movement. Avoid loose sleeves or dangling jewelry that can catch on machinery..Trim()

.Trim()

Your footwear matters too: steel-toe boots protect your toes from falling tools or heavy parts. Never wear sandals or open shoes in the workshop. Keep your safety gear clean and inspect it regularly. Replace worn-out items immediately. In many African settings, safety gear may be scarce, but you can improvise: a clean cloth wrapped around your face as a dust mask, or sunglasses (though not ideal) for eye protection. However, always prioritize buying proper gear when you can. Your health is worth more than any shortcut..Trim()

Safe Handling of Batteries and Fluids

Car batteries contain sulfuric acid and lead, both dangerous. When handling a battery, wear gloves and goggles. Never lean over a battery while charging, as it can release explosive hydrogen gas. Keep sparks, flames, and cigarettes away. To remove a battery, always disconnect the negative (-) terminal first, then the positive (+). When installing, connect positive first, then negative. This prevents accidental short circuits. Use a battery carrier or both hands to lift; batteries are heavy (15-20 kg) and can drop on your feet. If acid touches your skin, flush with plenty of water for 15 minutes and seek medical help. If it gets in your eyes, rinse with clean water immediately and go to a clinic..Trim()

.Trim()

Fluids like engine oil, coolant, brake fluid, and fuel also require care. Always store them in labeled containers, never in soda bottles. Clean up spills immediately with sand or absorbent rags. Brake fluid damages paint and is toxic; wear gloves. Coolant (antifreeze) tastes sweet but is deadly if swallowed; keep away from children and animals. When draining oil, use a proper drain pan and funnel. Never pour used oil down a drain or on the ground; collect it for recycling. In many African cities, used oil can be sold to recycling companies..Trim()

.Trim()

Fuel is highly flammable. Work in a well-ventilated area, no smoking, and have a fire extinguisher nearby. When siphoning fuel, use a proper pump, never your mouth. Store fuel in approved containers only. After handling any fluid, wash your hands with soap and water. Remember, a clean workspace and careful habits prevent accidents and protect the environment..Trim()

Organizing Your Workspace for Efficiency

A cluttered workspace slows you down and causes accidents. Organize your area using the 5S method: Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize, and Sustain. Start by sorting tools: keep only what you need for the job. Remove broken or unused items. Set in order means every tool has a home. Use a shadow board (a board with outlines of tools) or labeled drawers. For example, keep all spanners on a rack ordered by size, from 8mm to 24mm. Screwdrivers in a holder, pliers on a hook. This way, you can grab the right tool without searching..Trim()

.Trim()

Shine means clean your workspace after every job. Wipe up oil spills immediately to prevent slips. Sweep the floor. A clean shop is a safe shop. Standardize means creating a routine: at the end of each day, spend 10 minutes returning tools and cleaning. Sustain means keeping it up. In shared African workshops, where multiple people use the same space, organization is even more critical. Label everything with a marker or paint. Use old cans or plastic containers as organizers. A simple pegboard on the wall can hold many tools..Trim()

.Trim()

Also, arrange your workbench so that frequently used tools are within arm's reach. Keep heavy items on lower shelves. Ensure good lighting, especially in dark corners. If working outdoors, use a tarpaulin to protect from sun and rain. A well-organized workspace reduces frustration, saves time, and prevents injuries. Preventing Theft and Loss of Tools in a Shared Workshop

In shared workshops, tools often disappear. This costs money and time. To prevent theft and loss, start with a sign-out system. Create a simple notebook or chalkboard where each person writes their name, the tool taken, and the time. When they return it, they sign it back in. This creates accountability. If your workshop has a lockable cabinet, use it for expensive tools like torque wrenches and diagnostic scanners. For everyday tools, use a shadow board with outlines; a missing tool is immediately visible..Trim()

.Trim()

Mark your tools with your name or a unique color of paint or tape. This makes it harder for someone to claim your tool as theirs. If you can afford it, buy tools with your initials engraved. In a shared space, never leave tools lying on the floor or on a workbench overnight. At the end of the day, do a quick inventory: count your spanners, screwdrivers, and pliers. If something is missing, ask around immediately. Most thefts are opportunistic; if someone knows you check daily, they are less likely to take..Trim()

.Trim()

For power tools, keep them in a locked cage or room. Use a chain and padlock through the handles if no cabinet is available. In some African workshops, a trusted tool manager is appointed. This person is responsible for issuing and collecting tools, and they receive a small fee. This system works well. Also, build a culture of respect: when someone returns a tool, thank them. When someone loses a tool, they should replace it. This discourages carelessness. Finally, insure your tools if possible. Some microfinance banks offer small business insurance. Losing a $200 torque wrench can set you back weeks; prevention is far cheaper..Trim()

Identifying Shock Absorbers, Struts, and Control Arms

Identifying Clutch Plate, Pressure Plate, and Release Bearing

When you open a manual gearbox clutch housing, you will see three main parts: the clutch plate (also called friction plate), the pressure plate, and the release bearing (throw-out bearing). The clutch plate is a round disc with a hole in the centre and friction material on both sides. It has a splined hub that slides onto the gearbox input shaft. A new clutch plate for a Toyota Hilux measures about 225mm in diameter and 8mm thick. The pressure plate is a heavy metal cover with a diaphragm spring inside. It bolts to the flywheel with 6 to 8 bolts, usually 12mm or 14mm heads. The release bearing is a round bearing that slides on the gearbox input shaft sleeve. It has a smooth face that presses against the pressure plate fingers. In Africa, you will often see worn clutch plates with rivets exposed. If the friction material is less than 1mm above the rivets, replace it. The pressure plate fingers should be flat; if they are worn or bent, replace the pressure plate. The release bearing should spin smoothly without noise. Spin it by hand; if it grinds or feels rough, replace it. Always replace all three parts together as a kit. A clutch kit for a Nissan Hardbody costs around 1500 to 2500 rand in South Africa or 8000 to 12000 naira in Nigeria. Buying separate parts is false economy because a worn release bearing will quickly damage a new pressure plate. When identifying parts, check for part numbers stamped on the pressure plate and clutch plate. Take a photo with your phone before removal. Clean the parts with brake cleaner, not petrol, because petrol leaves oil residue that causes slipping. Store new clutch parts flat and dry. Never touch the friction surface with oily hands. In many African workshops, you can find reconditioned clutch plates, but they are risky. A reconditioned plate may have new friction material glued on, but the hub springs may be weak. For a daily driver, buy new. For a farm truck that does low speed work, a reconditioned kit might last, but check the rivets and springs carefully..Trim()

Diagnosing Clutch Slipping and Juddering

Clutch slipping means the engine revs but the vehicle does not accelerate properly. You will notice it most in 4th or 5th gear when climbing a hill or accelerating hard. To test, drive at 30 km/h in 3rd gear, then press the accelerator hard. If the engine rpm rises quickly but the speed does not increase, the clutch is slipping. Another test: engage handbrake, put gearbox in 3rd gear, and slowly release the clutch while pressing the accelerator. If the engine does not stall, the clutch is slipping. Common causes: worn friction material, oil on the clutch plate from a leaking rear main seal or gearbox input shaft seal, or a faulty pressure plate. If oil is the cause, you must fix the leak and replace the clutch plate. A slipping clutch will eventually fail completely. Do not delay repair. Juddering (shaking) when you release the clutch is another problem. It feels like the vehicle hops or vibrates. Causes include: worn or warped flywheel, loose engine mounts, worn clutch plate hub springs, or a bent release fork. Check engine mounts by prying with a barâthey should not move much. Check the flywheel for hot spots or cracks. If the flywheel is warped, it must be machined or replaced. A worn clutch plate with broken springs will cause judder. Also, if the release bearing is not sliding smoothly on the input shaft sleeve, it can cause judder. In Africa, dusty roads accelerate wear. Always use genuine or good quality clutch kits. A cheap clutch may slip within 10,000 km. When diagnosing, ask the driver about driving habits: riding the clutch, resting foot on the pedal, or holding the vehicle on a hill with the clutch instead of the handbrake. Understanding Automatic Transmission Basics

An automatic transmission (autobox) shifts gears without a clutch pedal. It uses a torque converter, planetary gearsets, clutches, and bands. The torque converter is a fluid coupling that connects the engine to the gearbox. It has a turbine, impeller, and stator. The impeller spins with the engine, throwing transmission fluid onto the turbine, which turns the gearbox input shaft. The stator multiplies torque at low speeds. Automatic transmission fluid (ATF) is the lifeblood. Check the ATF level with the engine running in Park or Neutral, on level ground. The dipstick has marks for cold and hot. Use only the specified ATF typeâusually Dexron III or Toyota Type T-IV. Never mix types. For a Toyota Hilux automatic, the gearbox takes about 7 to 8 litres. If the fluid is dark brown or smells burnt, change it. A simple drain and refill replaces only 3 to 4 litres, so repeat three times with a short drive between to exchange most fluid. Common problems: slipping, harsh shifting, or no movement. Slipping in an automatic often means low fluid, worn clutches, or a faulty solenoid. Harsh shifting can be caused by a bad vacuum modulator or dirty valve body. In Africa, many automatic gearboxes fail due to overheating in traffic and lack of fluid changes. If the gearbox has a cooler, check for leaks. If you are not trained, do not open an automatic gearboxâit is precise and clean work. For basic maintenance, you can change the fluid and filter (if accessible). The filter is usually inside the pan; remove the pan (10mm bolts), replace the filter, and refit with a new gasket. Torque pan bolts to 8-10 Nm. If the vehicle has a CVT (continuously variable transmission), use only CVT fluid. Never tow a heavy load with a slipping automatic. If the transmission has a limp mode (stuck in one gear), scan for codes with an OBD2 scanner. Common codes: P0730 (incorrect gear ratio), P0740 (torque converter clutch circuit). Many African mechanics avoid automatics, but with basic care they can last 200,000 km. Learn to check fluid and recognize symptoms. If in doubt, refer to a specialist..Trim()

Reading Tyre Sizes and Choosing the Right Tyres

Look at the sidewall of any tyre and you will see a code like 195/65R15 91H. The first number, 195, is the tyre width in millimetres. The second number, 65, is the aspect ratio: the height of the sidewall is 65% of the width. The R means radial construction. The 15 is the rim diameter in inches. The 91 is the load index, and H is the speed rating. For example, a 91 load index means each tyre can carry 615 kg. If your car weighs 1,500 kg, four tyres at 91 give you 2,460 kg total capacity, which is safe. Never fit a tyre with a lower load index than the manufacturer recommends..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, road conditions vary from smooth tarmac to rough gravel. For mixed use, choose a tyre with a slightly higher aspect ratio, like 70 or 75, because the taller sidewall absorbs potholes better and protects your rims. A lower profile tyre (like 55) looks sporty but is easily damaged on bad roads. Also check the treadwear rating: a number like 400 means the tyre lasts longer than one with 200. Traction rating A is best for wet roads; temperature rating A resists overheating..Trim()

.Trim()

When buying used tyres, check the DOT code: the last four digits show the week and year of manufacture. For example, 2319 means the tyre was made in the 23rd week of 2019. Tyres older than six years should be avoided, even if the tread looks good, because rubber hardens and cracks. Also inspect for uneven wear, bulges, or exposed cords. A matching set of four tyres of the same size and brand is ideal, but at minimum, replace tyres in pairs on the same axle..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, always inflate to the pressure listed on the driver's door jamb or fuel cap, not the maximum on the tyre sidewall. For a typical sedan, that is around 30-35 PSI. Under-inflation causes overheating and rapid wear; over-inflation reduces grip. Check pressures weekly when tyres are cold..Trim()

Changing a Tyre Safely with a Jack and Spanner

Before you start, park on a flat, hard surface away from traffic. Turn on hazard lights and apply the handbrake. If you have wheel chocks, place them on the wheel diagonally opposite the one you are changing. For a manual car, engage first gear; for an automatic, put it in Park. Loosen the wheel nuts slightly with the spanner while the tyre is still on the ground. Do not remove them yet..Trim()

.Trim()

Locate the jacking point under the car, usually a reinforced metal seam near each wheel. Your owner's manual shows exact spots. Place the jack head under that point and turn the handle clockwise until the tyre just lifts off the ground. Never put any part of your body under the car when it is only supported by a jack. If you have a jack stand, place it under a strong chassis point as a backup..Trim()

.Trim()

Now remove the wheel nuts completely and pull the wheel straight off. If it is stuck, kick the tyre edge or use a rubber mallet. Mount the spare wheel, aligning the bolt holes. Screw on the nuts by hand until snug. Lower the jack until the tyre touches the ground but still bears no weight. Then tighten the nuts in a star pattern: tighten one, then the one opposite, and so on. This ensures the wheel sits flat. Finally, lower the car fully and tighten the nuts firmly with the spanner. Check tightness again after 50 km..Trim()

.Trim()

Common mistakes: using a scissor jack on soft ground (it sinks), over-tightening nuts with a foot (strips threads), or forgetting to re-check torque. A torque wrench set to 110 Nm is ideal for most cars. If you have no torque wrench, tighten firmly by hand but do not jump on the spanner. Always replace the spare with a properly inflated one, and repair the punctured tyre as soon as possible..Trim()

Balancing Wheels and Understanding Wheel Weights

Wheel balancing stops vibrations that occur at certain speeds, usually 80-120 km/h. An unbalanced wheel causes the steering wheel to shake, uneven tyre wear, and stress on bearings. Balancing is done on a special machine that spins the wheel and measures heavy spots. The machine tells the operator where to attach small lead or zinc weights on the rim. These weights come in grams, typically 5g to 50g, and are either clip-on (for steel rims) or stick-on (for alloy rims)..Trim()

.Trim()

When a tyre is mounted on a rim, the heavy spot of the tyre (marked with a yellow dot) should be aligned with the light spot of the rim (often the valve stem). This reduces the amount of weight needed. After balancing, the machine spins the wheel again to confirm it is within 5 grams of perfect. If not, the operator adds or moves weights. A properly balanced wheel needs no more than 60g total weight. If it needs more, the tyre or rim may be defective..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many roadside tyre shops have balancing machines, but not all are calibrated. Ask the operator to show you the machine reading before and after. If you see a pile of old weights on the rim, insist they remove all old weights first. Also, after off-road driving, mud can stick to the rim and throw off balance. Wash your wheels regularly. If you feel vibration after hitting a pothole, a wheel weight may have fallen off. Re-balance that wheel..Trim()

.Trim()

Dynamic balancing (spin balancing) is better than static (bubble balancing) because it corrects both up-down and side-to-side imbalance. Always balance all four wheels when you buy new tyres. For a small extra cost, it saves your suspension and gives a smoother ride..Trim()

Performing Basic Wheel Alignment Checks

Wheel alignment ensures your wheels point in the correct direction. The three main angles are camber (vertical tilt), caster (steering axis tilt), and toe (horizontal direction). Toe is the most critical for tyre wear. If your front wheels point slightly inward (toe-in) or outward (toe-out), the tyres scrub sideways and wear quickly. You can check toe at home with a simple string method. Park on level ground with wheels straight. Tie a long string to a fixed point at the rear of the car, stretch it along the side to the front, and measure the distance from the string to the front and rear of the front tyre. The difference should be small, typically 0-3 mm toe-in..Trim()

.Trim()

A more accurate DIY check uses a measuring tape. Measure the distance between the inner edges of the front tyres at the front and at the rear of the tyres. For most cars, the front measurement should be 1-3 mm less than the rear (toe-in). If the difference is more than 5 mm, you need alignment. Also check the steering wheel: if it is off-centre when driving straight, alignment is off. Another sign is uneven tyre wear: feathered edges on the inside or outside of the tread..Trim()

.Trim()

Many alignment problems come from worn suspension parts: tie rod ends, ball joints, or control arm bushings. Before paying for alignment, have a mechanic check these. If they are loose, alignment will not hold. In Africa, potholes and rough roads knock alignment out frequently. Get it checked every 10,000 km or after a hard impact. A professional alignment with a computerised machine costs about 20-50 USD in most cities. It is worth it to save tyres that cost 100 USD each..Trim()

.Trim()

If you cannot afford professional alignment, at least set toe roughly using the string method. But camber and caster require shims or adjustment bolts, so see a specialist. Always test drive after alignment on a straight road; the car should not pull to one side..Trim()

Repairing Tubeless Tyre Punctures

Tubeless tyres can be repaired if the puncture is in the tread area, not the sidewall. A simple repair uses a plug kit, available at any auto parts shop for a few dollars. First, locate the puncture by spraying soapy water on the tyre and looking for bubbles. Mark the spot. Remove the object (nail, screw) with pliers. If the object is still in, do not remove it until you are ready to plug..Trim()

.Trim()

Use a reaming tool to clean and roughen the hole. Insert the reamer into the hole and twist it to remove dirt and make the hole slightly larger. Then take a plug strip (a sticky rubber string) and thread it through the eye of the insertion tool. Push the tool into the hole until about 2 cm of the plug remains outside. Pull the tool out quickly, leaving the plug in the hole. Cut off the excess plug flush with the tread. Inflate the tyre to the correct pressure and check for leaks with soapy water..Trim()

.Trim()

This plug repair is a temporary fix. For a permanent repair, take the tyre to a shop where they will remove the tyre from the rim and apply a patch from the inside. This is called a patch-plug combination. It costs more but lasts the life of the tyre. Never repair a puncture in the sidewall or shoulder; the tyre must be replaced. Also, if the tyre has been driven flat, the inside may be damaged; have it inspected..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many roadside repairs use only plugs. That is fine for emergencies, but if you plan to drive at high speed or long distance, get an internal patch. Keep a plug kit and a small compressor in your car. Learn to do it yourself: it takes 10 minutes and can save you a long walk..Trim()

Sourcing Affordable Tyres and Rims

New tyres are expensive, but you can find good deals if you know where to look. Start with local tyre shops that sell used tyres, often called 'second-hand' or 'part-worn'. They may have tyres with 50-70% tread left for half the price. Check the DOT code for age and inspect for damage. Avoid tyres with uneven wear or patches. Also, ask for 'take-offs' from car dealers or fleet operators who replace tyres in sets. These are often high-quality brands at low prices..Trim()

.Trim()

Online marketplaces like Jumia, OLX, or Facebook Marketplace can have good deals, but you must inspect in person. Never pay before seeing the tyre. For rims, steel rims are cheaper and easier to repair; alloy rims look better but bend more easily on potholes. If you need a rim, check scrapyards for matching models. A used steel rim might cost 10-20 USD, while a new alloy can be 100 USD or more..Trim()

.Trim()

Consider retreaded tyres for off-road or slow-speed use. Retreading adds a new tread to a used casing. They are cheaper but not recommended for high-speed driving. In many African countries, retreads are common for trucks and buses. For a car, only use retreads if you drive mainly on rough roads at low speeds. Always buy in pairs or sets of four for even wear..Trim()

.Trim()

When negotiating, ask for a discount for cash or for buying multiple tyres. Check the manufacturing date: tyres older than six years are unsafe even if unused. Also, ensure the load index and speed rating match your car's requirements. Finally, factor in the cost of fitting, balancing, and new valve stems. A cheap tyre that costs extra to mount may not be a bargain. Build a relationship with a trusted tyre dealer who will give you honest advice..Trim()
