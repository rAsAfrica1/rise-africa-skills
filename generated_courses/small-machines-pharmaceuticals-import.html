Module 1 · The Import Business Opportunity

Why Importing Works for African Entrepreneurs

Importing works in Africa because local manufacturing is often too expensive or limited. For example, a small maize mill in Tanzania costs $2,500 locally, but the same machine from China costs $800 plus $400 shipping and 25% import duty, totaling $1,400. You save $1,100. Similarly, a box of 1000 paracetamol tablets from India costs $15, while locally it might cost $30. The demand is high: Nigeria alone needs over 500 million tablets yearly. You don't need a factory; you need a phone, a supplier, and a clearing agent. Start with one product, like a manual brick-making machine or a first-aid kit. Use Alibaba to find suppliers, and Payoneer to pay them. The profit margin can be 50-100%. The key is to start small, test the market, and reinvest. Importing fills the gap between what Africans need and what they can afford..Trim()

Machines vs Pharmaceuticals: Two Different Games

Machines and pharmaceuticals are both importable, but they follow different rules. Machines like sewing machines, water pumps, or grinding mills are durable goods. You can buy one unit for $500, sell it for $900, and the customer uses it for years. The risks are lower: no expiry, less regulation, and you can inspect before shipping. Pharmaceuticals, like antibiotics or painkillers, are fast-moving but highly regulated. A box of amoxicillin may cost $2 but expires in 2 years. You need a drug license, cold storage, and strict record-keeping. In Kenya, the Pharmacy and Poisons Board requires registration; in Nigeria, NAFDAC. Without it, your goods get seized. Machines require less paperwork but higher shipping costs due to weight. Pharmaceuticals need air freight for temperature control, which is expensive. Start with machines if you're new: lower legal barriers. Once you have capital and a licensed pharmacist partner, add pharmaceuticals. Never mix the two in one shipment..Trim()

Understanding the China-Africa Trade Corridor

The China-Africa trade corridor is the busiest route for African importers. China exports over $150 billion to Africa yearly. The main ports are Shanghai, Ningbo, and Guangzhou. Goods travel by sea to ports like Mombasa (Kenya), Lagos (Nigeria), and Durban (South Africa). A 20-foot container from China to Mombasa costs about $2,500 and takes 30-40 days. From Guangzhou to Lagos, it's $3,000 and 35 days. You can also use air freight for urgent items: $5 per kg, 5-7 days. The key players are freight forwarders, who handle shipping and customs. Use platforms like Alibaba and Made-in-China to find suppliers. Payment is often via bank transfer or Payoneer. Watch out for scams: always use Trade Assurance on Alibaba. The Chinese government supports this trade with the Belt and Road Initiative, which has improved ports and roads. For you, the corridor means access to cheap machines and medicines. Start by ordering a sample, then scale up..Trim()

The Roles of Pakistan, India, and Russia

Pakistan, India, and Russia are key players in African imports, especially for pharmaceuticals and machines. India is the world's largest generic drug supplier. A strip of 10 paracetamol tablets from India costs $0.10; in Africa, it sells for $0.50. India also exports small machines like sewing machines and water pumps. Pakistan specializes in surgical instruments and generic medicines; a basic surgical kit costs $20 in Pakistan and sells for $50 in Africa. Russia exports industrial machines and some pharmaceuticals, but less common. For Africa, India is the easiest to work with: English-speaking suppliers, Alibaba presence, and direct flights. Pakistan offers competitive prices on surgical goods. Russia can supply heavy machines but has language and payment barriers. To import from India, use IndiaMART or Alibaba. For Pakistan, try TradeKey. Payment via bank transfer or Western Union. Always check licenses: Indian suppliers need a drug license for pharmaceuticals. Start with India for medicines, Pakistan for surgical tools, and Russia for specialized machines..Trim()

Your Role: Importer, Distributor, or Reseller

You can enter the import business in three roles: importer, distributor, or reseller. As an importer, you buy directly from overseas suppliers and handle shipping and customs. Example: you buy 100 water pumps from China at $50 each, pay $500 shipping and 20% duty, total cost $6,500. You sell to distributors at $80 each, earning $8,000, profit $1,500. As a distributor, you buy from an importer in bulk and sell to retailers. You need less capital but lower margins. As a reseller, you buy from a distributor and sell to end-users, often online. Start as a reseller to learn the market. Use Jumia or Kilimall to test demand. Then become a distributor by partnering with an importer. Finally, import directly. Each role requires different licenses: importers need an import license and tax ID; distributors need a business permit; resellers can operate informally. Choose based on your capital and risk tolerance. Most successful African entrepreneurs start as resellers and grow..Trim()

The Legal Boundaries You Must Never Cross

Importing has legal boundaries that can land you in jail or cost you your business. First, never import counterfeit or expired pharmaceuticals. In Nigeria, NAFDAC seizes fake drugs and fines up to $10,000. Second, never smuggle goods to avoid duty. Customs in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa use scanners and risk profiles. If caught, you pay 3 times the duty and may face prosecution. Third, never import restricted items like certain chemicals or weapons without a license. Fourth, always declare the correct value. Under-declaring is fraud. Fifth, for pharmaceuticals, you must have a license from the national drug authority. In Ghana, it's the FDA; in Tanzania, TMDA. Without it, your goods are destroyed. Sixth, respect intellectual property: don't import fake branded machines. Use official suppliers. Finally, keep records for at least 5 years. Tax authorities can audit you. Follow these rules, and you build a sustainable business. Break them, and you lose everything..Trim()

Understanding MOQs, FOB, and CIF Pricing

MOQ stands for Minimum Order Quantity. It's the smallest number of units a supplier will sell. For small machines, MOQs range from 1 to 10 units. For pharmaceuticals, MOQs can be 1,000 to 10,000 tablets. Always negotiate MOQâmany suppliers will lower it for a higher price per unit. For example, if MOQ is 5 units at $500 each, you might get 2 units at $600 each. FOB (Free On Board) means the supplier delivers goods to the port in China (e.g., Shanghai, Shenzhen) and loads them onto the ship. You pay for freight, insurance, and import duties. CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) means the supplier pays for shipping and insurance to your port (e.g., Lagos, Mombasa). CIF is easier for beginners but often 10-20% more expensive. For a $5,000 machine, FOB might be $5,000 + $800 freight = $5,800; CIF might be $6,200. Always get both quotes. For Africa, sea freight takes 30-45 days from China to West Africa, 20-30 days to East Africa. Air freight is faster (5-7 days) but 5-10 times more expensiveâonly for urgent, high-value items. When comparing quotes, ensure they specify: FOB port, shipping line, transit time, and destination port. Also, check if the price includes export clearance and local charges. For pharmaceuticals, ensure the supplier provides a Certificate of Analysis (COA) and MSDS. Always ask for a proforma invoice (PI) that details: unit price, MOQ, total price, FOB/CIF terms, payment terms, and delivery time. Compare at least 3 PIs. Remember: the cheapest FOB price might not be the best if the supplier is far from the port (extra inland freight). Use a freight forwarder to get the best rates. Payment Methods: T/T, Letters of Credit, Escrow

T/T (Telegraphic Transfer) is a bank wire transfer. It's the most common payment method for Chinese suppliers. Typically, you pay 30% deposit, then 70% before shipping. L/C (Letter of Credit) is a bank guarantee. Your bank pays the supplier only when shipping documents are presented. It's safer for large orders (above $10,000) but expensive: banks charge 1-3% of the order value, plus document fees. L/C requires precise documentsâany discrepancy can delay payment. For African importers, L/C is common for orders above $20,000. Escrow (like Alibaba's Trade Assurance) is the safest: Alibaba holds your payment until you confirm receipt. Understanding the Harmonized System

The Harmonized System (HS) is a standardized numerical method of classifying traded products. It is used by customs authorities worldwide to identify products for duty and tax purposes. Every product you import has an HS code, which is a string of numbers. The first six digits are the same in all countries that use the HS (over 200 countries). The next digits can vary by country. For example, a diesel engine might be 8408.90 in one country and 8408.90.00 in another. The HS is maintained by the World Customs Organization (WCO). It is updated every five years; the current version is HS 2022. As an importer, you must know the HS code of your product because it determines the duty rate, any restrictions, and the documents required. In Africa, most countries use the HS but have their own national tariff schedules. For instance, Nigeria uses the ECOWAS Common External Tariff (CET), which is based on HS 2017. Kenya uses the EAC CET. Ghana uses the ECOWAS CET. These tariffs list the duty rate for each HS code. You can find your country's tariff online or at the customs office. The HS is organized into 21 sections, 97 chapters (chapter 77 is reserved for future use). Chapters are grouped by product type: live animals (01-05), vegetable products (06-14), machinery (84-85), vehicles (86-89), etc. Pharmaceuticals are in Chapter 30. When you import, you must declare the HS code on your customs declaration. If you misclassify, you may pay the wrong duty or face penalties. So, it's crucial to get it right. Start by understanding the structure: the first two digits are the chapter, the next two are the heading, and the next two are the subheading. For example, 3004.90 is a subheading for medicaments. In the next lessons, we'll show you how to find the exact code for your product and calculate your taxes..Trim()

Finding the correct HS code can be tricky, but with the right tools and method, you can do it. First, gather details about your product: what it is, what it's made of, its function, and its packaging. For example, a portable generator: is it diesel or petrol? What is its power output? These tools let you search by keyword or browse chapters. Start with the chapter that seems most relevant. For machinery, look at Chapters 84 and 85. Chapter 84 covers mechanical appliances, boilers, and engines. Chapter 85 covers electrical machinery. For pharmaceuticals, Chapter 30. Once you find a potential code, read the description carefully. The HS uses a hierarchy: Section, Chapter, Heading, Subheading. You must match your product to the most specific description. For example, if you import a laptop, it's not just 'machinery' (Chapter 84) but specifically 8471.30 for portable digital computers. If you import a solar panel, it's 8541.40. VAT and Other Levies

When importing, you pay more than just import duty. Value Added Tax (VAT) is a consumption tax applied to most goods and services. In many African countries, VAT is charged on the CIF value plus import duty. So, if your goods cost $10,000 CIF and duty is $1,000, VAT is calculated on $11,000. VAT rates vary: Nigeria 7.5%, Kenya 16%, Ghana 15% (plus NHIL and GETFund levies). However, some goods are exempt or zero-rated. In Kenya, pharmaceuticals and agricultural inputs are VAT-exempt. In Nigeria, basic food items and medical products may be exempt. In Ghana, some machinery for manufacturing may be exempt. You must check your country's VAT Act. Besides VAT, there are other levies. In Nigeria, there's a 0.5% ECOWAS Trade Liberalisation Scheme (ETLS) levy, and a 0.5% Comprehensive Import Supervision Scheme (CISS) levy. In Kenya, there's a 2% Import Declaration Fee (IDF) and a 1.5% Railway Development Levy (RDL). In Ghana, there are NHIL (2.5%), GETFund (2.5%), and COVID-19 levy (1%). These add up. So, your total tax can be significant. To calculate, use this formula: Total Taxes = Import Duty + VAT + Other Levies. For example, in Kenya, if CIF is $10,000, duty 10% = $1,000, IDF 2% = $200, RDL 1.5% = $150, VAT 16% on ($10,000+$1,000) = $1,760. Total taxes = $3,110. That's a lot! So, you must factor these into your landed cost. Also, some countries offer exemptions or reduced rates for certain imports, like machinery for export processing zones. Check if you qualify. To pay, you usually pay through the customs system or a bank. Keep all receipts. If you overpay, you can claim a refund, but it's a hassle. So, get it right the first time. Use a customs broker to help you compute and pay the correct taxes. They can also advise on exemptions. Remember, VAT is often recoverable if you're VAT-registered and sell the goods, but for many small importers, it's a cost. So, plan your pricing accordingly..Trim()

Calculating Your True Landed Cost

Landed cost is the total cost of a product once it has arrived at your door, including the product cost, shipping, insurance, customs duties, taxes, and other fees. Many importers make the mistake of only looking at the product price and shipping, then get shocked by customs charges. To calculate your true landed cost, follow these steps. First, determine the FOB (Free On Board) price from your supplier. This is the cost of the goods plus loading onto the ship at the port of origin. Second, add international freight and insurance. Freight can be air or sea. For sea, a 20-foot container from China to Nigeria might cost $3,000-$5,000. Insurance is usually 0.5% of FOB. This gives you CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight). Third, calculate import duty. Use the HS code and your country's duty rate. Duty = CIF x Duty Rate. Fourth, calculate VAT. VAT = (CIF + Duty) x VAT Rate. Fifth, add other levies like IDF, RDL, ETLS, etc. Sixth, add port charges, clearing agent fees, transportation to your warehouse, and any demurrage if delays. Also, consider bank charges for foreign exchange and letters of credit. Once you sum all these, you get the landed cost. For example, importing a machine: FOB $10,000, freight $1,000, insurance $50, CIF $11,050. Duty at 10% = $1,105. VAT at 16% on $12,155 = $1,945. IDF 2% = $221, RDL 1.5% = $166. Clearing agent $500, transport $200. Total landed cost = $11,050 + $1,105 + $1,945 + $221 + $166 + $500 + $200 = $15,187. That's 52% more than FOB! So, you must price your product with this in mind. To make a profit, your selling price must exceed landed cost plus your operating expenses and desired margin. Use a spreadsheet to compute this for each product. Also, consider exchange rate fluctuations; they can significantly affect your cost. Always get a recent quote from your freight forwarder and customs broker. Tools like the online landed cost calculators from freight forwarders can help. But verify the rates with your local customs. In Africa, it's wise to add a 10-15% buffer for unexpected charges. By knowing your true landed cost, you can negotiate better with suppliers, choose the most cost-effective shipping method, and set profitable prices..Trim()

SONCAP for Nigeria

SONCAP stands for Standards Organisation of Nigeria Conformity Assessment Programme. It is mandatory for most regulated products entering Nigeria, including small machines and pharmaceuticals. Without a SONCAP certificate, your goods will be stuck at the port and you may pay hefty demurrage fees. The process starts with a Product Certificate (PC), which you obtain before shipping. The PC is issued after testing your product in an accredited lab. It is valid for three years if you keep importing the same product from the same factory. For each shipment, you then apply for a SONCAP Certificate (SC) using the PC. The SC is what customs officials check. To get the PC, you need to submit test reports, invoices, and a Request for Certificate (RFC) to a SONCAP-accredited agency like SGS, Intertek, or Bureau Veritas. They will review your documents and may inspect the goods at the factory. Fees for a PC range from $500 to $1,500 depending on product complexity. The SC costs about 0.5% of the FOB value, with a minimum of $200. Timelines: PC takes 5-10 working days; SC takes 2-3 days after submitting the PC and shipping documents. Be careful: fake SONCAP certificates are common. Always verify the certificate number on SON's website. Also, ensure your product meets Nigerian standards, like NIS 123 for electrical appliances. If your product fails, you must fix it and retest. Budget for these costs and time in your import plan. Many importers fail because they skip the PC and try to get the SC directly, which is impossible. Start early: get your PC before you even order goods. That way, you avoid delays. For pharmaceuticals, you also need NAFDAC registration, which is separate but often runs parallel. In summary, SONCAP is a two-step process: PC then SC. Use accredited agencies, verify certificates, and factor in 2-4 weeks total. This protects your business and ensures smooth clearance..Trim()

PVoC for Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda

PVoC stands for Pre-Export Verification of Conformity. It is required for regulated products entering Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda. The goal is to ensure goods meet local standards before they leave the exporting country. For small machines and pharmaceuticals, you must get a Certificate of Conformity (CoC) from an accredited agency like SGS, Intertek, or Bureau Veritas. The process: first, check if your product is regulated. In Kenya, for example, most electrical goods, toys, and chemicals are regulated. You then contact an agency and submit a Request for Certification (RFC) with proforma invoice, test reports, and product details. The agency reviews and may inspect the goods at the factory. If all is well, they issue a CoC. Without a CoC, your goods will be rejected at the port, and you may have to re-export them at your own cost. Costs: for a 20-foot container of small machines, expect $500-$1,200 for inspection and certification. The fee is based on FOB value, typically 0.5% to 1%, with a minimum of $300. Timelines: 5-10 working days for document review, plus inspection scheduling. In Kenya, PVoC is mandatory for all regulated products. Tanzania has a similar program called PVoC, and Uganda uses PVoC as well. Each country has its own list of regulated products, so check the respective standards body: KEBS for Kenya, TBS for Tanzania, and UNBS for Uganda. A common mistake is assuming one CoC works for all three countries. It does not. You need a separate CoC for each destination. Also, some products are exempt, like raw materials for manufacturing, but you must prove that. For pharmaceuticals, you also need registration with the national drug authority, like PPB in Kenya. To save time, work with an agency that has offices in your country and in the destination country. They can guide you on specific requirements. Always verify the agency is accredited by the destination country's standards body. For example, in Kenya, only agencies recognized by KEBS can issue CoCs. Budget 2-3 weeks for the entire process. If you ship without a CoC, you risk confiscation and fines. So, plan ahead. Get your test reports ready early, and ensure your supplier cooperates with the inspection. NRCS for South Africa

Product Certificate and Certificate of Conformity

In pre-shipment inspection, two key documents are the Product Certificate (PC) and the Certificate of Conformity (CoC). They are often confused, but they serve different purposes. The PC is a long-term approval for a specific product from a specific factory. It is issued after testing and possibly factory inspection. The PC is valid for 1-3 years, depending on the country. For example, in Nigeria's SONCAP, the PC is valid for 3 years. The CoC, on the other hand, is shipment-specific. It confirms that the goods in a particular shipment conform to the approved PC or to the relevant standards. You need a CoC for each shipment. In Kenya's PVoC, the CoC is issued per shipment. In South Africa, the CoC is also per shipment, based on the LOA. So, the PC is like a driver's license, and the CoC is like a vehicle inspection sticker for each trip. To get a PC, you need to submit test reports from an accredited lab, product specifications, and a factory audit report if required. The cost for a PC ranges from $500 to $2,000. The CoC costs about 0.5% of the FOB value, with a minimum of $200-$300. Timelines: PC takes 1-3 weeks; CoC takes 2-5 days after submitting shipping documents. A common mistake is to think that a PC alone is enough for customs clearance. It is not. You must have a CoC for each shipment. Another mistake is to use a PC from a different factory. The PC is tied to the factory, so if you change manufacturers, you need a new PC. Also, ensure that the test reports are recent, usually within 1-2 years. For pharmaceuticals, the PC may be replaced by a marketing authorization or registration certificate. Always check the specific requirements of the destination country. To streamline, work with an agency that can handle both PC and CoC. They can guide you on the required tests and documentation. Keep copies of all certificates and CoCs. Customs officials may ask for them. In summary, PC is for the product, CoC is for the shipment. Both are essential for smooth import. Budget for both and start early. Module 10 · Customs Clearance and Documentation

The Documents You Need for Every Shipment

Every import shipment into Africa requires a core set of documents. Missing one can delay your goods for weeks and cost you demurrage fees of $50-$200 per day. The essential documents are: a Commercial Invoice, Packing List, Bill of Lading (for sea) or Air Waybill (for air), Certificate of Origin, and an Import Declaration (like the ID in Nigeria or the IDF in Kenya). For pharmaceuticals, you also need a Drug Registration Certificate and an Import Permit from the national drug regulator, such as NAFDAC in Nigeria or PPB in Kenya. For machines, you may need a Pre-Export Verification of Conformity (PVoC) certificate from an inspection company like SGS or Bureau Veritas. Always prepare three original copies of each document: one for customs, one for your clearing agent, and one for your records. In Africa, many borders now use electronic systems like the Single Window in Ghana or the Tradenet in South Africa. You must upload scanned copies before the ship arrives. A common mistake is sending original documents by regular mail; use DHL or FedEx to ensure tracking and speed. Also, check that the names and addresses on all documents match exactly. A mismatch between the invoice and the bill of lading will trigger a customs query and cost you at least $100 in amendment fees. Start by creating a checklist for each shipment and tick off every document before the cargo leaves the supplier..Trim()

Commercial Invoice and Packing List

The Commercial Invoice is the seller's bill to you. It must show: seller and buyer names and addresses, invoice number and date, detailed description of goods (including brand, model, and serial numbers for machines), quantity, unit price, total value, currency (usually USD), Incoterms (like FOB or CIF), and country of origin. For pharmaceuticals, include batch numbers and expiry dates. Customs uses this to calculate duty. Under-declaring value to pay less duty is illegal and can lead to seizure and fines of 50% of the goods' value. The Packing List shows how goods are packed: number of cartons, weight per carton, dimensions, and contents of each package. It helps customs officers locate items for inspection. For machines, list each component separately. For pharmaceuticals, list each drug, strength, and quantity per box. Both documents must be in English or the official language of the destination country. Use a spreadsheet to create these documents; templates are available from trade portals like the International Trade Centre's TradeMap. Always keep digital and hard copies. When you send them to your clearing agent, use PDF format to prevent editing. A common error is using generic descriptions like 'spare parts' or 'medicine' â be specific: '10 units of Bosch GWS 20-180 angle grinder, 180mm, 2000W' or '500 boxes of Coartem 20/120mg tablets, 6x4 tablets per box'. Specificity speeds up clearance and reduces the chance of customs asking for a physical inspection, which can add 3-5 days..Trim()

Bill of Lading and Air Waybill

The Bill of Lading (B/L) is a contract between the shipper and the shipping line. It serves as a receipt, a document of title, and a contract of carriage. For sea freight, you need an Original B/L or a Telex Release. The Original B/L must be endorsed by the shipper and presented to the shipping line to collect your cargo. A Telex Release is faster: the shipper surrenders the original at origin, and you get a release at destination. Always confirm with your supplier which type they will provide. The B/L shows: shipper, consignee (you or your clearing agent), notify party, vessel name, port of loading, port of discharge, container number, seal number, and description of goods. Check that the consignee name matches your import license exactly. For air freight, the Air Waybill (AWB) is not a document of title; it is a receipt and contract. The airline releases cargo to the consignee named on the AWB without requiring an original document. This makes air freight faster but riskier if you have not paid your supplier. Always use a freight forwarder to handle B/L and AWB. In Africa, major ports like Mombasa, Lagos, and Durban have online systems to track containers. For example, in Kenya, you can use the Kenya Ports Authority's online portal. If your B/L is delayed, you can request a Letter of Indemnity from your bank, but this costs 1-2% of the cargo value. Keep all B/L and AWB copies for at least 5 years for tax audits..Trim()

Certificate of Origin

The Certificate of Origin (COO) proves where your goods were manufactured. It is essential for customs to determine duty rates under trade agreements. For example, under the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), goods originating in member countries may enjoy reduced or zero tariffs. To benefit, you need a valid COO from a recognized chamber of commerce or customs authority in the exporting country. For machines from China, you need a COO issued by the China Council for the Promotion of International Trade (CCPIT). For pharmaceuticals from India, a COO from the Indian Chamber of Commerce. The COO must show: exporter, consignee, country of origin, description of goods, and a declaration signed by the exporter and stamped by the issuing authority. Some countries require a legalized COO from their embassy in the exporting country; this costs about $50-$100 and takes 3-5 days. In Africa, many customs authorities now accept electronic COOs through the ASYCUDA World system. However, always carry a hard copy. A wrong or missing COO can result in you paying the full general duty rate, which can be 20-35% instead of 0-10%. For example, a machine classified under HS code 8467 (tools) may have a 5% duty with a COO from a trade partner, but 20% without. Always verify the HS code with your customs agent before shipment. Also, check if your country has a specific form, like the EUR.1 for European goods or the Form A for GSP. Keep the COO with your customs file..Trim()

Import Permits and Licenses

Before you import, you must obtain the necessary permits and licenses. For general goods, you need an Import License or Importer Registration Certificate from your country's customs authority. In Nigeria, this is the Form M and the Importers Certificate. In Kenya, it is the Import Declaration Form (IDF) and a PIN certificate. For pharmaceuticals, you need a Drug Import Permit from the national drug regulator: NAFDAC in Nigeria, PPB in Kenya, FDA in Ghana, or SAHPRA in South Africa. This permit requires you to submit the product's Certificate of Pharmaceutical Product (CPP), GMP certificate, and samples for testing. The process takes 2-6 weeks and costs $200-$1000 per product. For machines, some countries require a Pre-Export Verification of Conformity (PVoC) from agencies like SGS, Intertek, or Bureau Veritas. This inspection happens in the exporting country and costs 0.5-1% of the FOB value. You also need a Tax Identification Number (TIN) and sometimes a customs bond. For controlled goods like generators or used machines, you may need an environmental permit. Always check with your clearing agent before you order. A common mistake is importing without a permit, leading to seizure and fines. A customs clearing agent is your local expert who handles documentation, duty payment, and cargo release. In Africa, you cannot clear cargo yourself at most ports; you must use a licensed agent.
