Understanding the Menstrual Cycle

Menstruation is a natural monthly process that happens to girls and women from puberty (around age 10-15) until menopause (around age 45-55). Each month, the body prepares for a possible pregnancy by thickening the lining of the uterus. If no pregnancy occurs, this lining breaks down and leaves the body through the vagina as blood and tissue. This is called a period. Anatomy of a Disposable Pad

A disposable pad has five main layers. From top to bottom: top sheet, acquisition layer, absorbent core, back sheet, and adhesive. The top sheet touches your skin. It is made of non-woven polypropylene or cotton. It must be soft and allow liquid to pass through quickly. The acquisition layer, also called the distribution layer, is usually made of polyester or viscose. It spreads liquid evenly so the core can absorb it fast. The absorbent core is the heart of the pad. It contains cellulose fluff pulp and super absorbent polymer (SAP). Cellulose comes from wood pulp, often from pine or eucalyptus trees. SAP is a powder that can absorb up to 300 times its weight in water. In a typical pad, SAP makes up 5 to 10 grams. The core is wrapped in tissue paper to keep the SAP from shifting..Trim()

The back sheet is a thin film of polyethylene (PE) or polypropylene (PP). It is waterproof and keeps liquid from leaking onto your underwear. Some back sheets are breathable, made of microporous film, to reduce sweating. The adhesive is a hot-melt glue that sticks the pad to your panty. It is usually applied in stripes or dots. Wings, if present, have their own adhesive to wrap around the panty..Trim()

The top sheet is the layer that touches your skin. It must be soft, non-irritating, and allow liquid to pass through quickly. Adhesives and Wings: Design and Function

Adhesives keep the pad in place. Disposable pads use hot-melt adhesive, which is a thermoplastic glue that is solid at room temperature but melts when heated. Wings are extensions on the sides of the pad that fold under the panty. They have adhesive on them to secure the pad. Wings prevent the pad from shifting and help stop side leaks. There are two types: straight wings and contoured wings. Straight wings are simple rectangles; contoured wings are curved to fit the panty better. Most pads have wings that are 4 to 6 cm long. The adhesive on wings is usually a repositionable adhesive, so you can adjust the pad. Some pads have wingless designs, but they tend to move more..Trim()

For reusable pads, you do not use adhesive. Instead, you use snaps, buttons, or Velcro. Snaps are plastic or metal fasteners that press together. They are durable and washable. You can buy snap pliers and snaps from craft stores. Velcro is easier but can collect lint and lose grip over time. Buttons are cheap but require buttonholes. When designing wings, make sure they are long enough to wrap around the panty and overlap. A wing length of 6 cm works for most panties. The adhesive should cover at least 70% of the wing area. If you are making disposable pads, test the adhesive by sticking a pad to a cotton panty, then pulling it off. It should not leave residue or tear the fabric. For reusable pads, test the snaps by washing and drying the pad 20 times. They should still snap securely. In summary, adhesives and wings are critical for comfort and leak protection. Module 4 · Manufacturing Processes for Disposable Pads

Raw Material Preparation

Before you start making pads, you need to gather and prepare your raw materials. For disposable pads, the core materials are: absorbent fluff pulp (wood pulp), super absorbent polymer (SAP), non-woven fabric for the top sheet, polyethylene film for the back sheet, and adhesive glue. In Africa, you can source fluff pulp from local paper mills or import from suppliers in South Africa or Egypt. SAP is imported from countries like China or India. Non-woven fabric can be purchased from textile factories in Nigeria or Kenya. You will also need release paper for the adhesive, and wrapping film for packaging..Trim()

.Trim()

Once you have your materials, you must prepare them. Fluff pulp comes in large rolls or bales. You need to break it down into fluffy fibers using a hammer mill or a grinder. This increases its absorbency. SAP comes as a powder; you must store it in a dry place because it absorbs moisture from the air. Non-woven fabric and polyethylene film need to be cut to the correct width for your pad size. For a standard pad, the top sheet and back sheet are about 7 cm wide for a panty liner, or 10-12 cm for a regular pad..Trim()

.Trim()

You also need to prepare your workspace. Set up a clean, dry area with tables for laying out materials. Use gloves and masks to avoid contamination. Measure and cut materials in batches to save time. For example, cut 100 top sheets and 100 back sheets at once. Keep SAP in an airtight container. Weigh the SAP you need per pad: typically 0.5 to 1 gram for a regular pad. Fluff pulp per pad: about 3 to 5 grams. Prepare adhesive by checking its viscosity; if too thick, warm it slightly. Always keep a record of batch numbers and dates for quality control. Proper preparation reduces waste and ensures smooth production..Trim()

Forming and Layering

Forming and layering is where you build the pad's absorbent core and assemble the layers. The core is made of fluff pulp mixed with SAP. First, fluff the pulp using a hammer mill or a grinder until it's soft and fluffy. Then, mix in SAP powder. A typical ratio is 1 part SAP to 5 parts fluff pulp by weight. For a regular pad, use about 4 grams of fluff and 0.8 grams of SAP. Mix them thoroughly in a mixing drum or by hand with gloves. This mixture is your absorbent core..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, you need to form the core into a rectangular shape. Use a mold or a simple wooden frame to shape the core. The core should be about 6-8 cm wide and 20-25 cm long for a regular pad. Press it lightly to hold its shape. If you don't have a mold, you can spread the mixture evenly on a flat surface and cut it to size later. But for consistency, a mold is better..Trim()

.Trim()

Now, layer the pad. Start with the back sheet: polyethylene film, cut to the same size as the core plus 1 cm margin all around. Place the core on top of the back sheet. Then cover with the top sheet: non-woven fabric, also cut to size. The top sheet should be slightly larger than the core to allow for sealing. If you are making a winged pad, the wings are extensions of the top sheet and back sheet on the sides. You will attach them later. For now, focus on the basic three layers: back sheet, core, top sheet..Trim()

.Trim()

After layering, you need to bond the layers together. You can use a heat sealer or a simple iron with a cloth over the pad to melt the non-woven and polyethylene slightly, creating a seal. Alternatively, use adhesive glue along the edges. Make sure the layers are aligned and flat. Any wrinkles will cause leaks. Work on a clean table, and do one pad at a time. For small-scale production, you can layer 10-20 pads at once and then bond them. Check each pad for even distribution of the core. A well-formed pad should be smooth and uniform..Trim()

Cutting and Shaping

After forming and layering, you need to cut and shape the pads to their final size and contour. Disposable pads come in various shapes: rectangular, contoured (hourglass), and with wings. For a basic rectangular pad, you simply cut the layered stack to the desired length and width. Use a sharp rotary cutter or a die-cut press for accuracy. For a regular pad, the final dimensions are typically 22-25 cm long and 7-8 cm wide. For a panty liner, 15-18 cm long and 5-6 cm wide. If you don't have a die-cut machine, you can use a template made of wood or cardboard and cut with a sharp utility knife on a cutting mat. Always cut on a clean, hard surface..Trim()

.Trim()

For contoured pads, you need to shape the sides to fit the body. The contour is narrower at the center and wider at the ends. You can create a template with the desired shape, then trace it onto the layered pads and cut. Alternatively, use a die-cut machine with a custom die. The contoured shape reduces bulk and improves comfort. For winged pads, you cut the basic shape first, then cut wings on the sides. Wings are typically 3-4 cm long and 2-3 cm wide, extending from the sides of the pad. They are used to wrap around the underwear. Cut wings as part of the top sheet and back sheet, ensuring they are sealed together..Trim()

.Trim()

After cutting, you need to seal the edges to prevent the core from leaking out. Use a heat sealer or a hot iron with a protective cloth to seal the edges of the pad. You can also use adhesive glue along the edges. For a clean finish, trim any excess material. Check each pad for sharp edges or loose fibers. A good cut and shape ensures the pad fits well and performs its function. For small-scale production, you can cut 50-100 pads per hour with a rotary cutter. Always wear gloves to avoid cuts and keep the cutting area clean. Store cut pads in a dust-free container until the next step..Trim()

Adhesive Application and Wing Attachment

Adhesive application is critical for keeping the pad in place. You need two types of adhesive: one for the back of the pad to stick to the underwear, and one for the wings if they are present. Use a hot melt adhesive glue, which is applied in a thin line or pattern. For the back sheet, apply a strip of adhesive about 1-2 cm wide along the center of the pad's back. You can use a glue gun or a glue applicator machine. For small-scale production, a glue gun works fine. Apply the glue in a zigzag or dot pattern to allow flexibility. The adhesive should be pressure-sensitive, meaning it sticks when pressed. After applying, cover the adhesive with release paper (silicone-coated paper) to prevent it from sticking to other surfaces before use..Trim()

.Trim()

For winged pads, you need to attach wings to the sides. Wings are usually extensions of the top sheet and back sheet, but if they are separate, you can attach them by heat sealing or gluing. Cut wing pieces from non-woven fabric and polyethylene film. Place them on the sides of the pad, aligning with the edges. Seal them using a heat sealer or glue. Ensure the wings are securely attached and can fold around underwear. The wings also need adhesive on their back side to stick to the underwear. Apply a small strip of adhesive on each wing and cover with release paper..Trim()

.Trim()

After applying adhesive, test a few pads to ensure they stick well but don't tear the underwear. The adhesive should not be too strong or too weak. Store pads in a cool, dry place because heat can melt the adhesive. For quality control, check that the release paper is easy to peel off and that the adhesive remains on the pad, not on the paper. If you are using a glue gun, keep the nozzle clean to avoid clogs. Work in a well-ventilated area. For medium-scale production, consider a hot melt glue applicator with a nozzle that applies a consistent pattern. This speeds up production and ensures uniformity. Always wear gloves and avoid skin contact with hot glue..Trim()

Packaging and Sterilization

Packaging protects the pads from contamination and makes them easy to carry and store. For disposable pads, each pad is usually wrapped individually in a thin plastic film or paper wrapper. You can use a flow wrap machine or manually wrap each pad. For manual wrapping, cut a piece of wrapper about 12 cm x 20 cm. Place the pad in the center, fold the wrapper over, and seal the edges with a heat sealer or tape. Ensure the wrapper is sealed on all sides to keep out dust and moisture. For bulk packaging, place 8-10 individually wrapped pads in a larger bag or box. Label the package with the brand name, pad type, quantity, and manufacturing date. Use a printing stamp or sticker..Trim()

.Trim()

Sterilization is not always required for disposable pads, but if you want to ensure they are free from bacteria, you can use gamma irradiation or ethylene oxide (EtO) sterilization. These are industrial processes and may not be available for small-scale producers. Alternatively, you can use UV light sterilization, but it is less effective. For most small-scale producers, the key is to maintain a clean production environment. Use gloves, masks, and clean equipment. Avoid touching the absorbent core with bare hands. If you cannot sterilize, focus on hygienic packaging: wrap pads immediately after production, and store in a clean, dry place..Trim()

.Trim()

For sterilization, if you have access to a gamma irradiation facility, send your packaged pads for treatment. The typical dose is 25 kGy. This kills bacteria and viruses. EtO sterilization uses ethylene oxide gas, but it requires aeration to remove residues. UV sterilization can be done with a UV lamp, but it only sterilizes surfaces. For small-scale, you can use a simple sterilization method: place wrapped pads under a UV lamp for 30 minutes on each side. However, this is not a substitute for proper industrial sterilization. Always test for sterility by sending samples to a lab. For packaging, use food-grade plastic or paper. Avoid recycled materials that may contain contaminants. Proper packaging and sterilization ensure the pads are safe for use..Trim()

Quality Control in Manufacturing

Quality control (QC) is essential to ensure every pad meets standards for absorbency, comfort, and safety. Start by inspecting raw materials: check fluff pulp for lumps, SAP for clumping, and non-woven for holes. Reject any material that is contaminated or damaged. During production, check each step. After forming, weigh the core to ensure it has the right amount of fluff and SAP. For a regular pad, the core should weigh 4-5 grams. After cutting, check dimensions: length and width within 0.5 cm tolerance. After adhesive application, test the stickiness by pressing a pad onto a piece of fabric and pulling gently. It should stick but not tear the fabric..Trim()

.Trim()

After packaging, perform final QC on a sample of pads. Test absorbency by pouring 5 ml of saline solution (or water with a little salt) onto the center of the pad. It should absorb within 10 seconds and not leak. Test the top sheet for dryness: press a tissue on the pad after absorption; the tissue should remain dry. Check the wings for secure attachment. Inspect the wrapper for seals and tears. If any pad fails, identify the cause and correct the process. Keep records of tests and defects. For small-scale production, test at least 5 pads per batch. For larger batches, test 10% or more..Trim()

.Trim()

Maintain a clean workspace. Sterilize equipment regularly. Train workers on hygiene and QC procedures. Use a checklist for each production run. Common defects include uneven core, weak adhesive, and poor sealing. Address these by adjusting the mixing, adhesive amount, or heat sealing temperature. For example, if the core is uneven, mix fluff and SAP more thoroughly. If adhesive is weak, increase the amount or change the glue. If seals are poor, increase heat or pressure. Document everything. This helps you trace problems and improve. Quality control is not just about rejecting bad pads; it's about building a reputation for reliable products. In Africa, where access to affordable pads is limited, quality is key to gaining trust and growing your business..Trim()

Module 5 · Manufacturing Processes for Reusable Pads

Fabric Selection: Cotton, Bamboo, and Microfiber

Choosing the right fabrics is the first step to making a reusable pad that is comfortable, absorbent, and long-lasting. The top layer that touches the skin should be soft and breathable. Cotton is the best choice: it is cool, allows air to flow, and does not irritate the skin. Look for 100% cotton, preferably unbleached or naturally dyed. Flannel cotton is also good because it is soft and absorbent. Bamboo is another option; it is very soft, naturally antibacterial, and more absorbent than cotton. However, bamboo can be more expensive and harder to find in local markets. Microfiber is a synthetic fabric that absorbs liquid quickly and holds it well, but it should not be used directly against the skin because it can cause irritation and does not breathe. Instead, use microfiber as an inner absorbent layer..Trim()

.Trim()

For the core absorbent layers, you need fabrics that soak up liquid fast and hold it without leaking. Cotton fleece, bamboo fleece, or microfiber terry are excellent. You can also use old towels or flannel sheets, but make sure they are clean and not worn thin. For the waterproof backing, you need a fabric that stops liquid from passing through. Polyurethane laminate (PUL) is the best choice: it is thin, waterproof, and breathable. Alternatively, you can use a waterproof nylon or polyester fabric, but PUL is more comfortable. Avoid plastic sheets as they do not breathe and can cause rashes..Trim()

.Trim()

When buying fabrics, always wash them first to shrink them and remove any chemicals. Pre-washing also makes the fabric softer. In Africa, you can find suitable fabrics at local markets, fabric shops, or online suppliers. Cotton and bamboo are often sold as meters or yards. For a single reusable pad, you need about 20 cm by 30 cm of each fabric type. A typical pad has three layers: a soft top layer, one or two absorbent layers, and a waterproof backing. You can add more absorbent layers for heavy flow. Always test a small piece of fabric by dropping water on it to see how quickly it absorbs and whether it leaks through. This simple test saves you from making pads that fail..Trim()

Pattern Making and Sizing

A pattern is a template you use to cut fabric into the shape of a pad. You can make patterns from cardboard, old X-ray film, or thick plastic. Start by drawing the shape of a pad on paper. A standard reusable pad is about 24 cm long and 8 cm wide at the center, with wings that extend 4 cm on each side. For heavy flow, make it longer (28 cm) and wider (10 cm). For light flow or panty liners, use 18 cm by 6 cm. You can also make different sizes for day and night. Remember that the pad should fit comfortably in the underwear without bunching..Trim()

.Trim()

To make a pattern, draw half the pad shape on a folded piece of paper, then cut it out and unfold it to get a symmetrical shape. Use a ruler to keep lines straight. For the wings, draw curves that will wrap around the underwear. You can add a snap or button later. Once you have a paper pattern, trace it onto your chosen pattern material. Cut it out carefully. Label the pattern with the size and the type of fabric (e.g., 'Top layer', 'Absorbent', 'Backing')..Trim()

.Trim()

When cutting fabric, place the pattern on the fabric and pin it down. Use sharp scissors or a rotary cutter. Cut two pieces for the top layer (if you want a folded edge) and two for the backing, plus one or two absorbent layers. Always cut on the grain of the fabric to prevent stretching. If you are making many pads, you can stack fabric layers and cut multiple pieces at once. But be careful to keep the pattern aligned..Trim()

.Trim()

Sizing is important for comfort and leak protection. Measure the underwear you will wear with the pad. The pad should be long enough to cover the crotch area and extend a little at the front and back. The wings should be long enough to wrap around the gusset and snap together. Test the pattern by making a sample pad with cheap fabric first. Wear it for a few hours to see if it fits well. Adjust the pattern as needed. Sewing a reusable pad requires a basic sewing machine or a needle and thread if you are hand-sewing. A sewing machine makes the work faster and stronger. Use a straight stitch for most seams and a zigzag stitch for edges to prevent fraying. If you have a serger, it is ideal for finishing edges. Start by sewing the absorbent layers together. Stack one or two absorbent layers (e.g., cotton fleece) and sew them along the edges with a zigzag stitch. This keeps them from shifting. If you want extra absorbency, you can quilt the layers by sewing lines across them..Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Insertion of Absorbent Layers

The absorbent layers are the heart of a reusable pad. They determine how much liquid the pad can hold and how dry the top layer feels. You can use natural fabrics like cotton, bamboo, or hemp, or synthetic microfiber. For a typical pad, you need 2 to 4 layers of absorbent fabric, depending on the flow. For light flow, use 2 layers; for heavy flow, use 4 layers. Each layer should be cut to the same shape as the pad body, but slightly smaller so they fit inside the pad without sticking out..Trim()

.Trim()

When inserting absorbent layers, you can either sew them directly into the pad or make them removable. Sewn-in layers are simpler and less likely to shift. To sew them in, stack the layers on top of the waterproof backing, then cover with the top layer and sew all around. Removable layers are inserted into a pocket, which makes drying faster and allows you to adjust absorbency. To make a pocket, sew the top layer and backing together leaving one end open, then insert the absorbent layers. You can also make separate absorbent inserts that you place inside the pocket. This is useful for heavy flow days when you need extra protection..Trim()

.Trim()

If you use microfiber, place it in the middle, not against the skin. Microfiber absorbs quickly but can cause irritation if it touches the skin. Also, microfiber can be bulky, so use it sparingly. Natural fibers like cotton and bamboo are more breathable and comfortable against the skin. You can combine fabrics: a layer of microfiber for quick absorption, then a layer of cotton for holding liquid. This gives you the best of both..Trim()

.Trim()

Before sewing, test the absorbency of your layers by pouring a small amount of water (about 30 ml) onto them. See how quickly it absorbs and whether it leaks through. If it leaks, add more layers or use a more absorbent fabric. Also, consider the drying time. More layers take longer to dry, especially in humid climates. In Africa, where sun drying is common, thinner layers dry faster and are less likely to develop mold. Always wash and dry absorbent layers thoroughly before use. You can also pre-shrink them by washing in hot water and drying on high heat. This prevents the pad from shrinking after sewing..Trim()

Finishing and Fastening Methods

Finishing touches make your reusable pad look professional and function well. After sewing all layers, you need to secure the edges and add fastening. The most common fastening is a snap button, but you can also use Velcro (hook and loop tape) or even buttons and buttonholes. Snaps are durable, easy to use, and do not snag. You can buy snap pliers and snap sets from craft shops or online. To attach a snap, you need to make a small hole in the wing, then press the snap parts together with the pliers. Alternatively, you can sew on snaps by hand. Velcro is cheaper and easier to find, but it can wear out over time and may catch on other fabrics in the wash. If you use Velcro, sew the hook side on one wing and the loop side on the other..Trim()

.Trim()

Another finishing method is to add a layer of topstitching around the entire pad. This reinforces the edges and gives a neat look. Use a contrasting thread color if you like. You can also add a decorative stitch, but keep it functional. For the wings, you can shape them into a curve that wraps around the underwear. Make sure the wings are long enough to overlap by at least 2 cm when fastened. Test the fastening by putting the pad on underwear and adjusting the fit. The pad should stay in place without shifting..Trim()

.Trim()

If you are making pads for sale, consider adding a label with care instructions. You can sew a small tag on the back with washing symbols. Also, consider the color and pattern of the top fabric. Dark colors hide stains better, but light colors show if the pad is clean. Many women prefer dark or patterned fabrics for the top layer. For the backing, you can use a solid color. You can also add a layer of fleece or flannel on the back for extra softness against the underwear..Trim()

.Trim()

International Standards for Sanitary Pads

When you make sanitary pads, you need to meet certain international standards so your product is safe and effective. The most common standard is ISO 9001, which is about quality management. It means you have a system to consistently produce good pads. Another key standard is ISO 13485, specifically for medical devices. In many countries, sanitary pads are considered medical devices because they touch the body and manage blood. ISO 13485 requires strict control of materials, production, and traceability. For absorbency, there is ISO 9073-6, which tests how much liquid a pad can hold. Leakage resistance is covered by ISO 9073-8. These tests use a standard solution that mimics menstrual fluid. You don't need a big lab to start. You can use simple tools like a graduated cylinder, a stopwatch, and a kitchen scale. For example, to test absorbency, cut a pad, place it in a funnel, and pour 10 ml of saline solution (0.9% salt water) slowly. Wait 5 minutes, then weigh the wet pad. The difference in weight gives you the absorbency in grams. International standards also require that materials are non-toxic and don't cause irritation. The WHO and UNICEF have guidelines for menstrual products in low-resource settings. They recommend that pads be free from harmful chemicals and have good absorbency (at least 10 ml for regular pads). In Africa, many countries adopt these standards through their national bureaus, like KEBS in Kenya or SON in Nigeria. Meeting these standards can open doors to bigger markets, including NGOs and government tenders. Start by documenting your process, testing each batch, and keeping records. Testing for Absorbency and Leakage

Absorbency and leakage are the two most important performance tests for sanitary pads. If a pad leaks, it's useless. You can test these at your workshop with simple tools. For absorbency, you need a way to measure how much liquid a pad can hold. Use a syringe or graduated cylinder to measure 10 ml of saline solution (9 grams of salt in 1 litre of water). Place the pad on a flat surface, then slowly pour the solution onto the center. Wait 5 minutes, then place a pre-weighed dry paper towel under the pad. If the paper towel gets wet, the pad leaked. To measure absorbency, weigh the pad before and after. The difference in grams is the amount absorbed. A regular pad should absorb at least 10 ml; a heavy pad should absorb 15-20 ml. For leakage, you can do a simple test: put the pad on a piece of blotting paper, pour 10 ml of saline, and press with a 1 kg weight for 1 minute. Check if liquid seeps to the blotting paper. Another method is to wear the pad with a coloured liquid and see if it leaks through the sides or back. Skin Irritation and Biocompatibility Testing

Sanitary pads touch sensitive skin, so they must not cause irritation or allergic reactions. Biocompatibility means the pad is compatible with the human body. The main concerns are the top sheet (the layer that touches the skin) and any chemicals or fragrances used. In international standards, biocompatibility is tested using ISO 10993. This involves tests like skin irritation and sensitization. You can do a simple patch test with volunteers.
