Module 1 · Understanding Natural Hair

Hair Types and Texture Classification

Natural hair in Africa is incredibly diverse, ranging from tight coils to loose waves. The most common classification system uses numbers 1 to 4, with subtypes a, b, and c. Type 4 hair, which is dominant across the continent, has three subtypes: 4a (defined S-pattern coils), 4b (Z-shaped bends with less definition), and 4c (tightly packed zigzag coils with minimal definition). Many people have a mix of types on different parts of their head. To identify your type, wash your hair with a gentle shampoo like Shea Moisture Jamaican Black Castor Oil, apply a leave-in conditioner, and let it air-dry without touching. Then observe the pattern: if you see clear S-shaped curls, you likely have 3c or 4a; if you see sharp angles and few curls, you have 4b or 4c. Tools like a curl chart from a local salon can help. In Africa, hair type often correlates with climate: drier regions like the Sahel tend to have tighter 4c hair, which protects the scalp from sun and dust. Knowing your type guides product choice: 4c hair needs heavy butters like shea or cocoa butter, while 3c hair does well with lighter creams. Avoid using typing to limit yourself; hair changes with health, hormones, and care. Use this knowledge to choose the right comb (wide-tooth) and styling method (e.g., two-strand twists for 4b/c). Remember, no type is better than another; each has unique beauty and needs..Trim()

Porosity and Density: What They Mean

Porosity refers to how well your hair absorbs and retains moisture. It is determined by the condition of the hair cuticle, the outer layer. Low porosity hair has tightly closed cuticles, making it hard to absorb water but easy to retain once wet. High porosity hair has raised cuticles, absorbing water quickly but losing moisture just as fast. To test porosity at home, drop a clean strand of shed hair into a glass of water. If it floats for a few minutes, you have low porosity; if it sinks slowly, medium; if it sinks immediately, high. In Africa, high porosity is common due to sun damage, harsh shampoos, or heat styling. For low porosity, use heat when deep conditioning: apply a conditioner like ORS Olive Oil, cover with a plastic cap, and sit under a hooded dryer for 15 minutes. For high porosity, layer products: water-based leave-in, then oil (e.g., baobab or castor), then a butter to seal. Density is how many strands grow per square inch. High density means thick hair; low density means you can see scalp easily. Test by parting your hair in four sections and looking at the scalp. High density needs more product and time to style; low density needs lighter products to avoid weighing down. Both porosity and density affect how you wash, condition, and style. For example, high porosity low density hair should avoid heavy butters that clump. Use this knowledge to build a routine that works for you, not against you..Trim()

The Science of Curl Patterns

Curl patterns are determined by the shape of the hair follicle and the way proteins (keratin) bond inside the hair. A round follicle produces straight hair; an oval follicle produces waves; a flat follicle produces curls. In African hair, the follicle is often kidney-shaped, creating tight coils. The curl pattern is also affected by the number of disulfide bonds, which are chemical links between keratin proteins. These bonds can be broken by heat or chemicals (like relaxers) and reformed to change the pattern. Understanding this helps you care for your curls without damaging them. For example, combing dry hair stretches the bonds and causes breakage; always detangle wet hair with conditioner and a wide-tooth comb like a seamless comb from a local beauty supply. The curl pattern also influences how sebum (natural oil) travels down the hair shaft. In straight hair, oil reaches the ends easily; in curly hair, oil stays near the scalp, leaving ends dry. That is why moisturizing ends daily with a water-based spritz and sealing with oil is crucial. In Africa, humidity can cause frizz by breaking hydrogen bonds in the hair; using a gel like Eco Styler can help define curls. Avoid excessive heat: temperatures above 300Â°F (150Â°C) permanently alter curl pattern. Instead, use a diffuser on low heat or air-dry. Knowing the science empowers you to choose safe styling methods like braid-outs, twist-outs, or Bantu knots that work with your curl pattern, not against it..Trim()

Identifying Your Hair's Unique Needs

Every head of natural hair has unique needs based on genetics, environment, and history. Start by assessing your hair's current state: is it dry, brittle, or limp? Does it break easily? Use the 'snap test': take a shed hair and stretch it. If it snaps quickly, you need protein; if it stretches and doesn't bounce back, you need moisture. In Africa, sun exposure and dust can dry out hair, so you may need more frequent moisturizing. Consider your lifestyle: if you exercise daily, you may need to co-wash (wash with conditioner only) more often. If you wear wigs or weaves, your natural hair needs a breathable cap and regular moisture. Your hair's needs also change with seasons: during the rainy season, use lighter products; during the dry season, use heavier butters. Keep a simple routine: cleanse, condition, moisturize, seal, and protect. For example, a 4c high porosity hair might need: wash with African Black Soap, deep condition with a protein-free conditioner, leave-in with aloe vera juice, seal with shea butter, and style in flat twists. A 3c low porosity hair might need: wash with a clarifying shampoo monthly, deep condition with heat, use a light leave-in, and seal with jojoba oil. Pay attention to how your hair responds. If it feels greasy, reduce oils; if it feels dry, add water-based moisture. Visit a natural hair salon for a consultation if unsure. Your hair's unique needs are not a problem to fix but a language to learn. Listen to your hair, and it will tell you what it wants..Trim()

Understanding Children's Hair Needs

Children's hair is not just smaller adult hair. It is finer, more porous, and more sensitive. The scalp of a child produces less oil until puberty, so it dries out faster. In many African communities, children's hair is often braided tightly or left uncovered, leading to breakage. You need to treat it with extra care. Start by knowing your child's hair type: is it fine and soft, or thick and coarse? Fine hair needs lighter products and less manipulation. Thick hair can handle heavier butters and oils. Porosity matters too: if hair absorbs water quickly and dries fast, it is high porosity and needs sealing with heavy oils like castor. If water beads up, it is low porosity and needs lighter oils like coconut. Also consider the child's age. A toddler's hair is still developing, so avoid harsh chemicals and tight styles. From ages 2 to 5, focus on gentle cleansing and protective styles. From 6 to 12, you can introduce more styling but still avoid relaxers. In Africa, sun exposure and dust can dry hair. Always cover hair with a satin-lined cap or scarf when going outside. Remember, a child's hair grows about 1 cm per month, so patience is key. Never compare your child's hair to others. Each head is unique. Your goal is healthy hair, not length. Feed the hair from inside: ensure the child eats protein, iron, and vitamins. Malnutrition shows in hair. So, a balanced diet of beans, greens, and eggs supports growth. Finally, teach children to love their hair. Negative comments can harm self-esteem. Gentle Cleansing and Conditioning

For older children, every 7 to 10 days is enough. Start by wetting hair thoroughly with warm water. Apply a small amount of shampoo to your palms, then massage into the scalp only, not the length. Detangling Kids' Hair Without Tears

Detangling is the number one cause of tears and hair breakage. The key is to work in small sections, use the right tools, and never rush.
