Overview of the Citrus Industry

The global citrus industry is worth over $15 billion annually. It includes fresh fruit, juice, and by-products like pectin and essential oils. Major producers are Brazil, China, the USA, and Mediterranean countries. In Africa, South Africa is the largest producer, followed by Egypt, Morocco, and Nigeria. South Africa exports about 1.5 million tonnes of citrus per year, mainly to Europe, the Middle East, and Asia. Egypt exports mostly oranges to Russia and Saudi Arabia. Other African countries like Ghana, Kenya, and Tanzania are growing their industries, but they face challenges: poor infrastructure, limited cold storage, and strict export standards. The industry is organized into grower associations, like the Citrus Growers' Association of Southern Africa (CGA) and the Egyptian Citrus Exporters Association. These groups help with market access, research, and training. For a new farmer, the industry offers opportunities in both local and export markets. However, to export, you must meet quality standards: fruit size, color, and absence of pests. You also need certification like GlobalG.A.P. The local market is less demanding but prices are lower. Many African farmers sell to informal markets, but there is growing demand from supermarkets and juice companies. The industry also faces threats: citrus greening disease (HLB) has devastated orchards in Asia and America, and it is now in Africa. Climate change is causing erratic rainfall and heatwaves. To succeed, you must stay informed, join a cooperative, and practice good orchard management. The future of citrus in Africa is bright if farmers adopt modern techniques and work together..Trim()

Module 2 · Citrus Botany and Physiology

Anatomy of Citrus Trees

A citrus tree has three main parts: roots, trunk, and canopy. The roots anchor the tree and absorb water and nutrients. Most feeder roots are in the top 30 cm of soil, spreading 1.5 times the canopy width. The trunk supports the canopy and transports water and nutrients. The canopy consists of branches, leaves, and fruit. Leaves are the factory: they capture sunlight for photosynthesis. Citrus leaves are evergreen, living 2-3 years. They are thick and waxy to reduce water loss. Flowers are small, white, and fragrant, appearing in spring. Fruit develops from the ovary after pollination. In Africa, common citrus types include sweet orange, mandarin, lemon, lime, and grapefruit. Grafting is common: a scion (fruit variety) is joined to a rootstock (e.g., rough lemon, trifoliate orange). Rootstocks affect tree size, disease resistance, and soil adaptation. For example, rough lemon rootstock is vigorous and suited to sandy soils, while trifoliate orange is dwarfing and tolerates cold. Knowing anatomy helps you prune, train, and diagnose problems. For instance, yellow leaves with green veins indicate iron deficiency, common in alkaline soils. Pruning opens the canopy for light and air, reducing disease. Remove water sprouts (vertical shoots) and suckers from the rootstock. In Africa, many farmers grow citrus in backyard orchards or small plots. Proper spacing (e.g., 5m x 5m for oranges) ensures good airflow. Understanding tree parts is the first step to good management..Trim()

Photosynthesis and Respiration in Citrus

Photosynthesis is how citrus trees make food. Leaves use sunlight, carbon dioxide (CO2), and water to produce sugars and oxygen. The process occurs in chloroplasts, mainly in the palisade cells of leaves. For every 6 molecules of CO2 and 6 of water, the tree makes 1 molecule of glucose and 6 of oxygen. Glucose is used for growth, fruit production, and stored as starch. Respiration is the opposite: trees break down sugars to release energy for growth and maintenance. This happens day and night, using oxygen and releasing CO2. The balance between photosynthesis and respiration determines net growth. If photosynthesis is low (e.g., due to shade, drought, or nutrient deficiency), growth and yield suffer. Citrus leaves are adapted to capture light efficiently. They have a waxy cuticle to prevent water loss and stomata (pores) on the underside for gas exchange. Stomata close when it's hot or dry to conserve water, which also reduces CO2 intake. In Africa, high temperatures and drought stress can limit photosynthesis. To maximize photosynthesis, ensure trees get full sun (at least 6 hours daily), adequate water (25-50 mm per week), and nutrients like nitrogen and magnesium (central to chlorophyll). Pruning to open the canopy improves light penetration. Avoid overhead irrigation in the evening, which can promote disease. Mulching conserves soil moisture and moderates temperature. Remember: healthy leaves = more sugar = more fruit. Monitor leaf color: dark green indicates good chlorophyll; yellowing signals problems. Test soil and leaf tissue for nutrients. With good management, a mature orange tree can produce 100-200 kg of fruit per year..Trim()

Growth Cycles and Phenology

Citrus trees have distinct growth cycles: flushes of new leaves, flowering, fruit set, and dormancy. In tropical Africa, growth can be continuous, but in subtropical areas (e.g., South Africa, Mediterranean North Africa), trees have 2-4 flushes per year, mainly in spring and summer. Flushes are periods of rapid shoot growth. New leaves are tender and light green, hardening over weeks. Flowering occurs after a period of cool temperatures (below 20Â°C) or drought stress, depending on variety. For example, oranges need 2-3 months of cool weather to induce flowering. In Kenya, flowering often happens at the start of the long rains (March-May). Fruit development takes 6-12 months, depending on type. Lemons and limes can flower and fruit year-round. Phenology is the timing of these events. It affects management: fertilize before flushes, irrigate during fruit set, and harvest at correct maturity. In Africa, climate variability can disrupt cycles. Drought delays flowering; too much rain during bloom reduces pollination. Keep records of flowering and harvest dates to plan operations. Pruning after harvest encourages new flush. Pests like citrus leafminer attack new flush. Monitor weekly. Use pheromone traps for fruit flies. Apply foliar sprays of zinc and boron during flush for healthy growth. A well-managed tree can have 3 flushes per year, each supporting future fruit. Understanding phenology helps you synchronize inputs with tree needs, leading to higher yields and better fruit quality..Trim()

Flowering and Fruit Development

Flowering is the start of fruit production. Citrus flowers are small, white, and fragrant. They can be perfect (male and female parts) or staminate (male only). Pollination is mostly by insects (bees) or wind. Some varieties, like mandarins, can set fruit without pollination (parthenocarpy). Flowering is triggered by cool temperatures (15-20Â°C) or water stress. In Africa, many farmers induce flowering by withholding irrigation for 4-6 weeks, then watering heavily. After pollination, the ovary develops into fruit. Initially, many small fruits form, but most drop (June drop) due to competition. Only 1-5% of flowers become mature fruit. Fruit growth follows a sigmoid curve: slow, fast, then slow. It takes 6-12 months to mature. For oranges, 7-9 months; lemons, 5-7 months. Fruit size and quality depend on water, nutrients (especially potassium), and sunlight. During development, ensure consistent moisture; drought causes fruit drop and small size. Apply potassium sulfate (e.g., 200g per tree per year) to improve fruit size and sweetness. Pest control is critical: fruit flies lay eggs in young fruit, causing rot. Use bait sprays or traps. Harvest when fruit reaches maturity: color change (e.g., green to orange), juice content (e.g., 40% for oranges), and sugar-acid ratio. In Africa, market demand often favors early harvest, but immature fruit is sour and reduces repeat sales. Test with a refractometer (Brix) and acid titration. Proper handling after harvest extends shelf life. With good management, a mature tree can produce 50-150 kg of fruit per season..Trim()

Root Systems and Nutrient Uptake

Citrus roots are shallow, with most feeder roots in the top 30 cm of soil. They spread horizontally up to 1.5 times the canopy diameter. Roots absorb water and nutrients, and anchor the tree. They also store carbohydrates. Root health is crucial: compacted or waterlogged soils suffocate roots. In Africa, many soils are sandy or clayey, affecting root growth. Sandy soils drain fast but leach nutrients; clay soils hold water but can become waterlogged. Improve soil with organic matter (compost, manure) to enhance structure and nutrient holding. Citrus needs 12 essential nutrients: macronutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S) and micronutrients (Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl). Nitrogen (N) promotes leaf and shoot growth. Phosphorus (P) supports root and flower development. Potassium (K) improves fruit size, color, and juice quality. Calcium (Ca) strengthens cell walls. Magnesium (Mg) is central to chlorophyll. Micronutrients like zinc and iron are needed in small amounts but are often deficient in African soils, especially alkaline ones. Nutrient uptake is affected by soil pH. Citrus prefers pH 5.5-6.5. At higher pH, iron, zinc, and manganese become unavailable, causing yellowing. At lower pH, aluminum toxicity can occur. Test soil annually. Apply fertilizers based on soil test and leaf analysis. For a mature orange tree, apply 1-2 kg of NPK 2:1:1 per year, split into 3-4 applications. Foliar sprays of zinc and manganese during flush correct deficiencies quickly. Over-fertilizing can burn roots and pollute groundwater. Use organic sources like manure (10-20 kg per tree per year) to improve soil health. Mulching conserves moisture and reduces weeds. Proper irrigation (drip or basin) ensures nutrients dissolve and reach roots. Healthy roots = healthy tree..Trim()

Hormonal Regulation in Citrus

Hormones are chemical messengers that control citrus growth and development. The main hormones are auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, abscisic acid, and ethylene. Auxins promote root formation and apical dominance (suppress side shoots). Gibberellins stimulate shoot elongation and delay fruit aging. Cytokinins promote cell division and delay leaf senescence. Abscisic acid (ABA) closes stomata during drought and promotes seed dormancy. Ethylene ripens fruit and promotes leaf and fruit drop. In citrus, hormones regulate flowering, fruit set, and drop. For example, gibberellic acid (GA3) sprays can reduce fruit drop and improve rind quality. Auxins like 2,4-D can prevent pre-harvest drop but must be used carefully to avoid residues. Ethylene is used commercially to degreen lemons and oranges. In Africa, smallholders can use simple techniques: pruning alters auxin distribution, promoting branching. Water stress increases ABA, inducing flowering. Fruit thinning (removing excess young fruit) reduces competition and increases size, mediated by hormones. Hormones also interact with nutrients. For instance, nitrogen promotes cytokinin synthesis, boosting growth. Zinc deficiency reduces auxin production, causing little leaf and dieback. Understanding hormones helps you manipulate trees for better yield. Use GA3 (10-20 ppm) at fruit set to reduce June drop. Apply ethephon (an ethylene releaser) to accelerate fruit ripening for early market. But always follow label instructions. Overuse can harm trees and environment. Integrated management combines pruning, irrigation, nutrition, and judicious hormone use. For African farmers, low-cost options like pruning and water management are often sufficient. Record observations: note when natural drop occurs, and plan interventions. With practice, you can use hormones to your advantage..Trim()

Seed Selection and Rootstock Varieties

When you grow citrus from seed, the tree may not be the same as the parent, and it will take many years to fruit. That is why farmers use rootstocks. A rootstock is the bottom part of the tree that provides the roots. The top part, called the scion, is the variety you want fruit from. Choosing the right rootstock is key. In Africa, common rootstocks include Rough Lemon, which is vigorous and drought-tolerant but susceptible to some diseases; Sour Orange, which is hardy and tolerates calcareous soils but is sensitive to tristeza virus; and Cleopatra Mandarin, which is resistant to tristeza and produces good quality fruit. For sandy soils, Rough Lemon is often used. For heavy soils, Sour Orange may be better. Always buy rootstock seeds from a certified supplier to avoid diseases. Sow seeds in a nursery bed with well-drained soil, rich in organic matter. Plant seeds 1-2 cm deep, spaced 5 cm apart. Water regularly. Seeds germinate in 2-4 weeks. When seedlings are 15-20 cm tall, they are ready for grafting. Remember, rootstock choice affects tree size, fruit quality, and pest resistance. Talk to extension officers or other farmers to know what works in your area. Always keep records of your rootstock and scion sources..Trim()

Grafting Techniques for Citrus

Grafting joins a scion (the fruiting variety) to a rootstock. The most common method for citrus is the cleft graft, done when the rootstock is 1-2 cm thick. First, cut the rootstock horizontally about 20-30 cm above the soil. Make a vertical slit about 3 cm deep in the center of the cut surface. Prepare the scion: take a healthy shoot of the desired variety, about 10-15 cm long, with 2-3 buds. Cut the bottom of the scion into a wedge shape, about 3 cm long. Insert the wedge into the slit so the cambium layers (the green layer under the bark) of scion and rootstock meet on at least one side. Wrap the union tightly with grafting tape or polythene strip to hold it and prevent drying. Cover the scion and union with a plastic bag to keep humidity high. Place the grafted plant in a shaded area. After 2-3 weeks, check for new growth. Remove the bag gradually. Grafting is best done during active growth, usually at the start of the rainy season. Tools needed: sharp knife, grafting tape, plastic bags, and pruning shears. Keep tools clean with alcohol to avoid disease. Success rate can be 70-90% if done properly. Grafting allows you to grow many trees with the same good fruit quality and disease resistance..Trim()

Budding Methods and Timing

Budding is another way to propagate citrus. It involves inserting a single bud from the desired variety into the rootstock. The most common method is T-budding or shield budding. Do this when the rootstock is about 1 cm thick, usually 6-12 months after sowing. The best time is when the bark slips easily, which is during active growth, often in the rainy season. To T-bud, make a T-shaped cut on the rootstock about 15-20 cm above the ground. Cut a bud from the scion with a piece of bark (the shield) about 2-3 cm long. Insert the shield under the bark flaps of the T-cut. Wrap with budding tape, leaving the bud exposed. After 2-3 weeks, check if the bud is green and plump. If it is, cut the rootstock about 10 cm above the bud to force the bud to grow. Remove any shoots from the rootstock below the bud. Budding is faster and uses less scion wood than grafting. It also allows you to bud many rootstocks quickly. Keep the nursery free of weeds and pests. Water regularly but avoid waterlogging. With good care, the bud will grow into a new shoot, which will become the main trunk. Budding is a skill that requires practice, but it is very rewarding..Trim()

Nursery Setup and Care

A citrus nursery should be in a sunny spot with good drainage. Prepare beds 1 meter wide and 10-20 meters long, raised 15-20 cm above ground. Mix soil with compost or well-rotted manure at a ratio of 3:1. Sterilize the soil by solarization: cover the bed with clear plastic for 4-6 weeks to kill pests and diseases. Alternatively, use fumigants if available. Sow seeds or place potted rootstocks. If using pots, use black polythene bags of 20-30 cm diameter filled with the soil mix. Space pots 30 cm apart. Water daily, preferably in the morning. Fertilize with a balanced NPK (e.g., 17-17-17) every 4-6 weeks, about 1 teaspoon per plant. Control weeds by hand weeding. Watch for pests like aphids, leaf miners, and mites. Use neem oil or recommended pesticides. For diseases like citrus canker, remove affected leaves and apply copper-based fungicide. Shade young seedlings with a shade net (50% shade) for the first 2-3 months. Harden them by gradually removing shade. Keep records of sowing, grafting, and treatments. A well-managed nursery gives healthy, vigorous seedlings ready for transplanting in 12-18 months..Trim()

Transplanting Seedlings to Field

Transplant citrus seedlings when they are 12-18 months old, 60-80 cm tall, and have a stem diameter of at least 1 cm. Choose a site with full sun and well-drained soil, pH 5.5-6.5. Prepare the field by clearing weeds and ploughing. Dig planting holes 40 cm wide and 40 cm deep, spaced 4-6 meters apart for standard trees, or 2-3 meters for dwarf varieties. Mix topsoil with 1-2 buckets of compost or manure and 100 g of phosphate fertilizer. Place the seedling in the hole, ensuring the graft union is 15-20 cm above the soil. Backfill and firm the soil. Water immediately with 10-20 liters. Mulch around the base with dry grass to conserve moisture. Stake the tree if needed. Transplant at the start of the rainy season to ensure adequate moisture. After planting, water every 2-3 days for the first month, then reduce to weekly. Protect from livestock and pests. Apply fertilizer after 2-3 months. Keep the area weed-free. With proper care, trees will start bearing fruit in 3-5 years. Transplanting is stressful for the plant, so handle roots carefully and avoid damaging the graft union..Trim()

Diseases like citrus greening (HLB), tristeza, and canker can destroy your orchard. The best way to avoid them is to plant certified disease-free seedlings. Certification means the nursery follows strict rules to produce clean plants. In many African countries, the government or authorized agencies inspect nurseries and issue certificates. Module 5 · Soil and Nutrient Management

Soil Testing and Analysis

Soil testing is the first step to growing healthy citrus. Without it, you are guessing what your trees need. In Africa, many soils are old and weathered, often lacking nitrogen, phosphorus, and organic matter. A soil test tells you exactly what is missing and what is too much. You can get a soil test done at a government agricultural office, a university lab, or a private company. In Kenya, for example, the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organisation (KALRO) offers soil testing services. In Nigeria, you can contact the Soil Science Society of Nigeria or local agricultural development programmes. The cost is usually between $10 and $30 per sample..Trim()

.Trim()

To take a good sample, use a clean bucket and a soil auger or a spade. Walk in a zigzag pattern across your orchard. Collect soil from 15 to 20 spots at a depth of 0 to 20 cm, avoiding areas near trees, fences, or where you have applied fertilizer recently. Mix all the soil in the bucket, then take about 500 grams (one pound) of the mixed soil and put it in a clean bag. Label it with your name, farm, and the date. Send it to the lab..Trim()

.Trim()

The lab will test for soil pH, organic matter, nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients like zinc and boron. For citrus, the ideal pH is between 5.5 and 6.5. If your pH is below 5.0, the soil is too acidic; you may need to add lime. If it is above 7.0, it is too alkaline; you may need to add gypsum or elemental sulfur. The lab report will give you recommendations for fertilizer. For example, if your soil test shows low phosphorus (below 10 ppm), you might need to apply 200 kg of triple superphosphate per hectare. Always follow the lab's advice, but also consider your budget and local availability of fertilizers..Trim()

.Trim()

Soil testing should be done every two to three years. If you are planting a new orchard, test before planting so you can correct problems early. Keep records of your test results and what you applied. This helps you track changes and make better decisions. Remember, a soil test is an investment, not an expense. It saves you money by preventing over-fertilization and poor yields..Trim()

Fertilizer Types and Application Methods

Citrus trees need regular feeding to produce good fruit. Fertilizers come in many forms: inorganic (mineral) and organic (manure, compost). In Africa, common inorganic fertilizers include urea (46% N), ammonium nitrate (34% N), triple superphosphate (46% P2O5), and muriate of potash (60% K2O). These are sold in 50 kg bags. Organic fertilizers like poultry manure, cattle manure, and compost are also valuable. They add nutrients and improve soil structure..Trim()

.Trim()

The amount of fertilizer you apply depends on the age of the tree and the soil test results. For a young tree (1-3 years), apply 100-200 grams of nitrogen per year, split into two or three applications. For a mature fruiting tree (5+ years), apply 500-1000 grams of nitrogen per year, plus phosphorus and potassium. A common recommendation in Kenya is to apply 500 g of NPK 17:17:17 per mature tree per year, split into two doses. But always adjust based on your soil test..Trim()

.Trim()

Application methods matter. Broadcasting fertilizer on the surface is easy but less effective because some nutrients are lost to the air or runoff. For citrus, the best method is to apply fertilizer in a ring around the tree, at the drip line (the edge of the canopy). Dig a shallow trench 5-10 cm deep, spread the fertilizer evenly, then cover with soil. This puts nutrients where the roots can reach them. For young trees, apply in a circle 30-60 cm from the trunk. Never put fertilizer directly against the trunk; it can burn the bark..Trim()

.Trim()

Timing is also important. Apply fertilizer at the start of the rainy season, when roots are actively growing. In East Africa, this is usually March-April and September-October. Avoid applying during heavy rains, as nutrients can wash away. For organic manure, apply 10-20 kg per mature tree per year, spread under the canopy. Manure should be well-rotted to avoid burning roots and to kill weed seeds. You can also use foliar sprays for quick correction of deficiencies, but they are not a substitute for soil application. Always read the label and use protective gear when handling fertilizers..Trim()

Micronutrient Deficiencies and Corrections

Citrus trees need small amounts of micronutrients like zinc, boron, iron, manganese, and copper. In African soils, zinc and boron deficiencies are very common, especially in sandy or highly weathered soils. A deficiency can reduce fruit yield and quality. You can identify deficiencies by looking at the leaves. Zinc deficiency causes small, narrow leaves with yellow patches between green veins (mottled). New leaves are most affected. Boron deficiency causes thick, brittle leaves, and fruit may be lumpy or have gum pockets. Iron deficiency shows as yellow leaves with green veins, starting on new leaves. Manganese deficiency looks similar but affects older leaves first..Trim()

.Trim()

To correct zinc deficiency, spray the leaves with zinc sulfate. Mix 5 grams of zinc sulfate per liter of water (about 0.5%) and spray the canopy until it drips. Do this 2-3 times per year, especially during flushing. You can also apply zinc sulfate to the soil at 50-100 grams per mature tree. For boron deficiency, spray with borax or boric acid at 1-2 grams per liter. Be careful: too much boron is toxic. Do not exceed 2 grams per liter. For iron deficiency, spray with ferrous sulfate at 5 grams per liter. In alkaline soils, iron is less available, so foliar sprays are better than soil application. For manganese, spray with manganese sulfate at 2-5 grams per liter..Trim()

.Trim()

Before spraying, confirm the deficiency with a leaf tissue test. Collect leaves from the middle of the canopy, 4-6 months old, from non-fruiting branches. Send them to a lab. The lab will tell you the exact nutrient levels. For example, zinc should be 25-100 ppm in dry leaf tissue. If it is below 25 ppm, you need to act. In many African countries, micronutrient fertilizers are available as pre-mixed foliar sprays. For example, in South Africa, products like 'Zinc Plus' or 'Boron Plus' are common. In Kenya, you can find 'Masterfol' or 'Agro-Zinc'. Always follow the label instructions..Trim()

.Trim()

Prevention is better than cure. Keep soil pH in the right range (5.5-6.5) because micronutrients are most available at that pH. Add organic matter regularly; it helps chelate micronutrients and makes them available to roots. Avoid over-liming, which can cause iron and manganese deficiencies. If you see symptoms, act quickly. A timely spray can save your crop..Trim()

Organic Matter and Composting

Organic matter is the backbone of healthy soil. It improves soil structure, holds water, and feeds beneficial microbes. In Africa, many soils are low in organic matter (below 1%). Citrus trees grow best when organic matter is 2-3% or higher. You can increase organic matter by adding compost, manure, or mulch. Compost is made from plant residues, animal manure, and kitchen waste. It is cheap and easy to make on the farm..Trim()

.Trim()

To make compost, choose a shaded area near your orchard. Build a pile 1.5 meters wide and 1 meter high. Use alternating layers: 15 cm of dry materials (maize stalks, straw, dry leaves) and 10 cm of green materials (grass clippings, kitchen waste, fresh manure). Add a handful of wood ash or a little soil to each layer. Keep the pile as moist as a wrung-out sponge. Turn the pile every two weeks to add air. In 2-3 months, the compost will be dark, crumbly, and smell earthy. You can also use a compost pit or a bin. A pile of 1 cubic meter will produce about 300-500 kg of compost..Trim()

.Trim()

Apply compost under the canopy of each tree, 10-20 kg per mature tree per year. Spread it evenly from the trunk to the drip line, but keep it 10 cm away from the trunk to prevent rot. You can also use compost as a mulch, which suppresses weeds and conserves moisture. In dry areas, mulch is very important. Use dry grass, straw, or banana leaves as mulch, 5-10 cm thick. Do not use fresh manure as mulch; it can burn roots and attract flies..Trim()

.Trim()

Another way to add organic matter is to grow cover crops and green manures (see next lesson). You can also apply vermicompost (worm castings) or compost tea. Compost tea is made by soaking compost in water for 24-48 hours, then straining and spraying on leaves or soil. It adds beneficial microbes. In Kenya, some farmers use 'bokashi' compost, which is fermented with effective microorganisms. Whatever method you choose, remember that organic matter is a long-term investment. It takes time to build, but the benefits last for years..Trim()

Cover Crops and Green Manures

Cover crops are plants grown to cover the soil, not for harvest. Green manures are cover crops that are ploughed into the soil to add nutrients. For citrus orchards, cover crops are very useful. They reduce erosion, suppress weeds, improve soil structure, and add organic matter. In Africa, good cover crops include cowpea, lablab, pigeon pea, sunhemp, and mucuna. These are legumes that fix nitrogen from the air. Grasses like sorghum, millet, or rye can also be used, but they do not fix nitrogen..Trim()

.Trim()

To plant a cover crop, clear the weeds and broadcast the seed at the start of the rainy season. For cowpea, use 20-30 kg of seed per hectare. For lablab, 25-30 kg per hectare. Rake the soil lightly to cover the seed. The cover crop will grow between the trees. When it flowers or before it sets seed, cut it down and leave it on the surface as mulch, or plough it into the soil. If you plough it in, wait 2-3 weeks before planting or fertilizing to allow decomposition. Green manure crops can add 50-100 kg of nitrogen per hectare. That is like applying 100-200 kg of urea..Trim()

.Trim()

Cover crops also help with pest management. Some, like marigold, repel nematodes. Others, like cowpea, attract beneficial insects. In citrus, nematodes are a big problem. Marigold (Tagetes spp.) can be planted as a cover crop to reduce nematode populations. You can also use cover crops to improve soil aeration. Deep-rooted crops like pigeon pea break up hardpan. After harvest, their roots decay and create channels for water and air..Trim()

.Trim()

One caution: do not let cover crops compete with young citrus trees for water and nutrients. Keep a clear area of at least 1 meter around the trunk. For mature trees, competition is less of a problem. In dry areas, cover crops may use too much water. In that case, use mulch instead. You can also grow cover crops only during the rainy season and then terminate them. If you use a legume cover crop, you may need to inoculate the seed with rhizobium bacteria before planting. This ensures good nitrogen fixation. Inoculants are available from agricultural suppliers. Always choose cover crops that are adapted to your region and rainfall pattern..Trim()

Fertigation Principles

Fertigation is the application of fertilizers through the irrigation system. It is common in commercial citrus production, especially where drip irrigation is used. Fertigation saves labour, reduces fertilizer waste, and delivers nutrients directly to the root zone. In Africa, fertigation is used in South Africa, Morocco, and Egypt, and increasingly in Kenya and Nigeria. To use fertigation, you need a drip irrigation system, a fertilizer tank, and soluble fertilizers. Common soluble fertilizers include potassium nitrate, calcium nitrate, and monoammonium phosphate (MAP)..Trim()

.Trim()

The first principle is to choose the right fertilizers. Not all fertilizers are soluble. Urea is highly soluble, but triple superphosphate is not. For fertigation, use fertilizers that dissolve completely and do not clog emitters. You also need to consider compatibility. For example, calcium nitrate and phosphate fertilizers should not be mixed in the same tank because they form a precipitate (calcium phosphate) that clogs drippers. Instead, inject them separately or at different times. A good practice is to inject calcium nitrate first, then flush the lines, then inject phosphate..Trim()

.Trim()

The second principle is to calculate the right concentration. Too much fertilizer can burn roots; too little is ineffective. A common rule is to apply 100-200 ppm of nitrogen in the irrigation water. For a mature citrus tree, you might apply 500-1000 grams of nitrogen per year, split into weekly or biweekly doses during the growing season. For example, if you irrigate 1000 liters per tree per week, and you want 150 ppm N, you need 150 grams of N per 1000 liters. If using calcium nitrate (15.5% N), you need about 970 grams of calcium nitrate per 1000 liters. Always check the label for nutrient content..Trim()

.Trim()

The third principle is timing. Fertigate when the tree needs nutrients most: during flowering, fruit set, and fruit enlargement. In the rainy season, reduce fertigation because rain provides some nutrients and can leach them away. In the dry season, fertigation is very effective. Always start with a short irrigation cycle to wet the soil, then inject fertilizer, then flush with clean water for 10-15 minutes to prevent clogging. Monitor your system regularly. Check emitters for clogging and clean filters. Use a pressure gauge to ensure proper pressure. If you are new to fertigation, start with a small area and test. You can also use a simple venturi injector, which is cheap and works with low pressure. With practice, fertigation can increase yields by 20-30% compared to broadcasting..Trim()

Module 6 · Water Management and Irrigation

Water Requirements of Citrus Trees

Citrus trees need consistent moisture to produce good fruit, but the exact amount depends on tree age, soil, and climate. A mature orange tree in a warm area uses about 40 to 60 litres of water per day during peak summer. In cooler months, that drops to 15 to 25 litres. Young trees just planted need 5 to 10 litres every 2 to 3 days. The roots take up most water in the top 60 cm of soil, so you do not need to soak deeply beyond that. Sandy soils hold little water, so you may need to irrigate every day or two. Clay soils hold more but drain slowly, so water every 3 to 4 days. A simple test: dig a hole 30 cm deep near the tree. If the soil feels like a wrung-out sponge, moisture is good. If it crumbles dry, irrigate. If it drips water, you are overwatering. In Africa, many farmers use the 'bucket method' for young trees: punch a small hole in a 20-litre bucket, fill it, and let it drip slowly near the trunk. This gives about 20 litres over 4 to 6 hours. For mature trees, a common practice is to build a basin around the tree (1.5 m diameter) and fill it with 60 to 80 litres once a week in dry season. Always water in the early morning or late afternoon to reduce evaporation. Mulching with dry grass or crop residues around the base reduces water loss by up to 30%. Avoid wetting the trunk directly to prevent rot. Signs of too little water: leaves curl inward, fruit drops, and new growth stops. Signs of too much: yellowing leaves, root rot, and fungus on the trunk. Adjust based on what you see..Trim()

Drip Irrigation Design and Maintenance

Drip irrigation saves water by delivering it directly to the roots, drop by drop. For a small citrus orchard of 50 trees, you can build a simple system for under $200 using locally available materials. You need a water source (tank or borehole), a filter, a main line (32 mm PVC or black polyethylene), and drip lines (16 mm) with emitters spaced 60 cm apart. Each emitter puts out 2 to 4 litres per hour. For a mature tree, run 4 emitters per tree, each for 2 hours daily. That gives 16 to 32 litres per tree per day. Place emitters 30 cm from the trunk, under the canopy edge where feeder roots are densest. Water Quality and Salinity Management

Not all water is good for citrus. High salt, high sodium, or high chloride can damage trees. Citrus is moderately sensitive to salinity. Drought is a reality in many parts of Africa. But you can keep citrus alive and productive with smart strategies. First, reduce water loss. Common Weeds in Citrus Orchards

Weeds compete with citrus trees for water, nutrients, and sunlight, reducing yields by up to 30%. In African orchards, common grassy weeds include couch grass (Cynodon dactylon), which spreads via rhizomes and is drought-tolerant, and star grass (Cynodon nlemfuensis), often found in moist areas. Broadleaf weeds like blackjack (Bidens pilosa) and pigweed (Amaranthus spp.) grow quickly and host pests like aphids. Sedges such as purple nutsedge (Cyperus rotundus) thrive in wet, poorly drained soils and are hard to control because they produce underground tubers. Parasitic weeds like striga (Striga asiatica) attack citrus roots in some regions, stunting growth. Identify weeds early: couch grass has creeping stems and finger-like seed heads; blackjack has yellow flowers and sticky seeds; nutsedge has triangular stems and umbel-like flower clusters. Weeds are worst in young orchards where tree canopies don't shade the ground. In mature orchards, shade suppresses many weeds, but aggressive species still emerge. In Africa, many farmers use local knowledge: some weeds like amaranth are edible and can be harvested, but be careful not to let them go to seed. Mechanical and Manual Weed Control

Mechanical and manual methods are the backbone of weed control in African citrus orchards, especially where herbicides are expensive or unavailable. Hand hoeing is common but labor-intensive: one worker can clear about 0.1 hectare per day. Use a sharp hoe to cut weeds at the soil surface, avoiding deep digging that damages citrus feeder roots. For larger areas, a slasher or machete can knock down tall weeds, but you must repeat every 4-6 weeks during the rainy season. Mowers (brush cutters) are faster: a petrol-powered brush cutter can clear 0.5 hectare per day, but keep the blade 5 cm above the soil to avoid root injury. In row middles, a tractor-mounted rotary mower can cover 2 hectares per day, but avoid mowing close to tree trunks. Cultivation with a disc harrow or tine cultivator is effective in dry season, but in wet soils it can compact the soil and spread rhizomatous weeds like couch grass. For small orchards, hand pulling is best for weeds near the trunk, especially vines that climb trees. Timing is critical: control weeds before they flower and set seed. Mulching is one of the best ways to suppress weeds, conserve moisture, and improve soil health in citrus orchards.
