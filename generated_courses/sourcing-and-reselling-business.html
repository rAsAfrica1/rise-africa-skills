Module 1 · Introduction to Sourcing and Reselling

What is Sourcing and Reselling?

Sourcing and reselling is the business of buying products at a low price and selling them at a higher price for profit. In Africa, this can be done both online and offline. For example, you might buy used clothing from a wholesaler in Nairobi for KES 100 per piece and resell each piece for KES 300 on Facebook Marketplace or at a local market. The key is to find products that are in demand and available at a low cost. Common sourcing channels include local markets (e.g., Balogun Market in Lagos), wholesale suppliers, online platforms like Alibaba, and even clearance sales. Reselling can be done through social media (WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram), e-commerce sites (Jumia, Kilimall), or physical stalls. The profit margin depends on your ability to negotiate, find unique items, and market effectively. Start small: with as little as $50 (about 20,000 Naira or 5,000 KES), you can test a product. Reinvest profits to grow. This business requires no formal degree, but you must be willing to learn about products, pricing, and customer service. It's a flexible way to earn income, especially for young people in Africa where jobs are scarce. Remember, success comes from consistency and understanding your customers' needs..Trim()

The Reselling Business Model Explained

The reselling business model is simple: you buy low, sell high. But to make it work, you need to understand the flow. Step 1: Identify a product. Look for items that are easy to source and have steady demand. For example, phone accessories like chargers and earbuds are always in demand in African cities. Step 2: Find a supplier. You can buy from local wholesalers, online marketplaces like Alibaba or AliExpress, or even from local manufacturers. For instance, in Ghana, you can source shea butter from women's cooperatives and resell it online. Step 3: Set your price. Calculate your total cost (product + shipping + packaging + platform fees) and add a profit margin. A common margin is 30-50%. If a phone charger costs you GHS 10, sell it for GHS 15-20. Step 4: Market and sell. Use WhatsApp status, Facebook groups, or Jumia to reach customers. Take clear photos and write honest descriptions. Step 5: Deliver and get paid. Use mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo) or cash on delivery. Step 6: Reinvest. Use profits to buy more stock or expand to new products. This model works because it requires low capital and can be done part-time. But challenges include competition, fake suppliers, and shipping delays. To succeed, keep records of all transactions and always test new products with small quantities before buying in bulk..Trim()

Key Platforms and Marketplaces Overview

As a reseller in Africa, you need to know where to buy and where to sell. For buying, Alibaba and AliExpress are popular for electronics, clothing, and accessories, but shipping can take weeks and customs fees apply. Local options include wholesale markets like Kantamanto in Accra, Balogun in Lagos, or Eastleigh in Nairobi. For selling, social media is king. WhatsApp Business lets you create a catalog and chat with customers directly. Facebook Marketplace and Instagram Shops are great for reaching local buyers. E-commerce platforms like Jumia (Nigeria, Kenya, Egypt), Kilimall (Kenya), and Takealot (South Africa) allow you to list products and handle payments, but they charge commissions (typically 10-20%). For used items, OLX and Jiji are popular in Nigeria, Kenya, and Ghana. Payment platforms: M-Pesa (Kenya), MTN Mobile Money (Ghana, Uganda), and Flutterwave for online payments. When choosing a platform, consider fees, ease of use, and your target audience. For example, if you sell fashion to young women, Instagram and TikTok are better than Jumia. Always read platform policies to avoid account bans. Start with one buying source and one selling platform, master it, then expand. Use tools like Canva for free product graphics and Google Trends to check demand..Trim()

Legal and Tax Basics for Resellers

Many resellers in Africa operate informally, but as you grow, you must understand legal and tax basics. First, business registration: In Nigeria, register with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) as a sole proprietorship for about NGN 10,000. In Kenya, register with eCitizen for KES 1,000. In Ghana, register with the Registrar General's Department. Registration gives you a business name, legal protection, and access to bank accounts. Second, taxes: In most African countries, small businesses with turnover below a threshold (e.g., NGN 25 million in Nigeria) are exempt from VAT, but you may still need to pay income tax on profits. Keep records of all sales and expenses. Use a simple notebook or free app like Wave or QuickBooks. Third, licenses: Some products require permits, e.g., food items need health certificates, and cosmetics need NAFDAC approval in Nigeria. Fourth, consumer protection: Always provide receipts and honor returns to build trust. Fifth, import duties: If you import goods, you must pay customs duties. For example, in Kenya, importing clothes attracts 25% duty plus VAT. To avoid trouble, declare goods accurately. Finally, join a reseller association for support. While these steps may seem tedious, they protect you from fines and help you scale. Start by registering your business name and keeping basic records..Trim()

Setting Up Your Business Entity

Choosing a business entity is a key step. In Africa, the most common for resellers are sole proprietorship, partnership, and limited liability company (LLC). Sole proprietorship is easiest and cheapest: you register your business name, and you are the sole owner. Profits are taxed as personal income. In Nigeria, CAC registration costs about NGN 10,000; in Kenya, KES 1,000 via eCitizen. Partnership involves two or more people sharing profits and liabilities. It's good if you have a trusted partner, but get a written agreement. LLC (called Limited Company in Nigeria, Private Limited Company in Kenya) is more complex and costly but limits personal liability. For example, in Ghana, registering a limited company costs about GHS 500. For most starting resellers, sole proprietorship is sufficient. To set up: 1) Choose a business name (check availability). 2) Register online or at the registrar's office. 3) Get a Tax Identification Number (TIN). 4) Open a business bank account (some banks require registration). 5) Obtain any necessary permits. You also need a business phone number and email. Use a separate bank account for business to track finances. Consider a mobile money merchant account for easy payments. Don't skip registration; it builds customer trust and lets you access loans. If you plan to sell on Jumia or Kilimall, they often require a registered business. Start simple, then upgrade as you grow..Trim()

Creating a Business Plan for Reselling

A business plan is your roadmap. For reselling, keep it simple: one page is enough. Start with your goal: e.g., 'To earn $500 per month within 6 months by reselling phone accessories in Nairobi.' Then outline: 1) Product: What will you sell? Choose 1-2 categories. Research demand using Google Trends or asking in Facebook groups. 2) Target market: Who are your customers? E.g., students, young professionals. 3) Sourcing: Where will you buy? List suppliers, prices, and lead times. 4) Pricing: Calculate costs and set a profit margin. Example: buy earbuds at KES 200, sell at KES 400. 5) Marketing: How will you reach customers? WhatsApp groups, Instagram ads, flyers. 6) Operations: How will you handle orders, delivery, and returns? 7) Finances: Start-up capital needed, projected sales, and expenses. Use a simple spreadsheet. 8) Risks: Competition, supplier issues, theft. Plan how to mitigate. 9) Milestones: Monthly targets. Review your plan monthly and adjust. A good plan helps you stay focused and convinces lenders or partners. You can find free templates online (e.g., from SCORE or local business development centers). Write it in English or your local language. Share it with a mentor for feedback. Remember, a plan is a living documentâupdate it as you learn..Trim()

Module 2 · Market Research and Niche Selection

Identifying Profitable Product Categories

Start by looking at what people already buy in your local market and online. In Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, and Ghana, high-demand categories include phone accessories, fashion (especially African print), home goods, beauty products, and small electronics. For example, phone cases and chargers sell fast on Jumia and Kilimall. But profit depends on sourcing cost. A phone case bought for 100 KES in Nairobi's River Road can sell for 500 KES online. That's a 400% markup. Look for products with a markup of at least 2x. Avoid heavy or fragile items because shipping costs eat profit. Also consider seasonal demand: during December, toys and gifts spike. Use local classifieds like Jiji or OLX to see what's in demand. Check Facebook Marketplace and WhatsApp groups. A simple method: visit a local market, note prices, then compare to online prices. If you can buy low and sell high, that's a profitable category. Start with one category, like phone accessories, and test. Don't spread yourself thin. Once you find a winner, double down. Remember, profit is not just about price; it's about turnover. A product that sells 10 times a month at 200 KES profit each is better than one that sells once at 1000 KES profit. So focus on fast-moving items. Also, consider your access to suppliers. If you can get a wholesale discount, that's a competitive edge. For example, in Lagos, Computer Village is a hub for electronics. In Accra, Circle Odawna is good for fashion. Use these local advantages. Finally, think about what you can store and ship easily. Small, light, non-breakable items are best. So start by listing 5-10 product ideas, then research each one's selling price and sourcing cost. Pick the one with the best margin and demand..Trim()

Terapeak is a powerful tool for eBay sellers, but it's not free. It costs about $10 per month, but you can use it to see what sold on eBay in the last 90 days. It shows you the exact number of sales, average selling price, and sell-through rate. For example, you might find that 'iPhone 11 screen protector' sold 1,200 units in 90 days at an average of $8. That tells you demand is high. But Terapeak is not the only tool. For African marketplaces, use Jumia's 'Best Sellers' page and Kilimall's 'Top Rated' section. These are free. Also, use Google Trends to see if interest is rising or falling. For example, search 'African print dress' in Ghana; if the trend is up, that's a good sign. Another free tool is eBay's 'Sold Items' filter. Just search a product, then filter by 'Sold' and 'Completed'. You'll see actual sold prices. This is gold. Analyzing Competition and Demand

Before you buy stock, check how many other sellers are offering the same product. On Jumia, search for your product. If there are 50 listings, competition is high. If there are 5, it's low. But low competition might mean low demand. So you need to balance. A good sign is when there are a few sellers but they have many reviews and sales. That means demand exists. For example, on Jumia Kenya, a search for 'wireless earbuds' might show 30 sellers, but the top 3 have sold over 500 units each. That's a competitive but profitable market. You can still enter if you differentiate: offer a better warranty, faster delivery, or a bundle. Also, check the quality of listings. If many have poor photos and descriptions, you can beat them with a professional listing. Demand analysis: look at 'sold' counts on eBay or 'orders' on Jumia. If a product has 100+ sales per month, demand is strong. But also check seasonality. For example, Christmas lights sell only in December. So don't stock up in June. Another method: use Facebook groups. Post a question like 'Where can I buy [product]?' If you get many responses, demand is there. Also, check local markets. If you see many stalls selling the same item, it's either high demand or oversupply. Observe how fast they restock. If they restock daily, demand is high. If weekly, moderate. Also, consider your unique advantage. Maybe you can source from a factory in China via Alibaba and sell at a lower price. Or you can offer free delivery within Nairobi. Competition is not always bad; it validates the market. But avoid markets where big players like Jumia or Kilimall dominate with rock-bottom prices. You can't compete on price alone. So find a niche where you can add value. For example, sell phone cases with African designs that big retailers don't offer. That reduces direct competition. Always ask: can I realistically get sales? If yes, proceed..Trim()

Products with many reviews and high ratings are likely selling well. Another method: use Terapeak (if you have it) to see exact sales data. Choosing Your Primary Reselling Platform

Not all platforms are equal. In Africa, the main ones are Jumia, Kilimall, Konga (Nigeria), Takealot (South Africa), and Facebook Marketplace. Each has pros and cons. Jumia is the biggest, with millions of visitors. But competition is fierce, and fees can be high. For example, Jumia charges a commission of 8-15% depending on category, plus shipping fees. Kilimall is smaller but has lower fees and less competition. It's good for beginners. Konga is strong in Nigeria, but you need a local bank account. Takealot dominates South Africa, but it's hard to get approved as a third-party seller. Facebook Marketplace is free and has huge traffic, but it's informal and you handle shipping yourself. For a beginner, I recommend starting with Facebook Marketplace and WhatsApp groups because they are free and easy. You can list items, chat with buyers, and arrange delivery. Once you have consistent sales, move to Jumia or Kilimall. But note: Jumia requires you to have a registered business and a tax ID in some countries. So check the requirements. Also, consider your product category. If you sell fashion, Instagram and Facebook are great. If you sell electronics, Jumia is better. If you sell handmade crafts, Etsy is an option, but shipping from Africa is expensive. Another platform is Bidorbuy in South Africa. Also, consider using your own website with Shopify or WooCommerce, but that requires marketing. For most beginners, the best strategy is to start on social media, then expand to a marketplace. Remember, each platform has its own rules. On Jumia, you must ship within 24 hours. On Facebook, you set your own terms. So choose based on your capacity. Also, consider payment methods: Jumia pays weekly, Facebook is cash on delivery or mobile money. So pick a platform that fits your cash flow. Start with one, master it, then add another. Creating a Niche Strategy for Long-Term Success

A niche is a focused product category where you become an expert. Instead of selling everything, you sell one thing well. For example, instead of 'phone accessories', sell only 'phone cases for Samsung Galaxy'. This helps you stand out and build a loyal customer base. To create a niche strategy, first pick a category you know or love. If you're into fashion, focus on African print clothing. If you're into tech, focus on refurbished laptops. Then, research the sub-niches. For African print, you could specialize in men's shirts, women's dresses, or kids' clothing. Choose one that has demand but not too much competition. Use the tools from earlier lessons to validate. Next, define your target customer. Are they young professionals in Lagos? Or tourists looking for souvenirs? This affects your pricing and marketing. Then, create a brand. Even a simple name and logo on your packaging makes you look professional. Offer excellent customer service: respond quickly, handle returns fairly. This builds trust. Also, source consistently. Find reliable suppliers, maybe from local markets or Alibaba. For example, if you sell African print bags, find a tailor in your city who can produce them in bulk. This gives you a unique product that big retailers don't have. Then, market your niche on social media. Join Facebook groups related to your niche, post photos, and engage. Use Instagram with hashtags like #AfricanPrintBags. Over time, you become the go-to person. Also, consider expanding within your niche: add matching shoes or jewelry. But don't dilute your focus. A niche strategy also means you can charge higher prices because you're specialized. For example, a generic phone case sells for 500 KES, but a custom-designed one with a niche theme sells for 1500 KES. So think long-term: build a brand, not just a store. Reinvest profits into better packaging, ads, and inventory. Track your metrics: which products sell best, which ads convert. Adjust accordingly. With a niche, you can dominate a small market and grow steadily..Trim()

Tracking Income and Expenses

As a reseller, every naira, shilling, or cedi that comes in and goes out must be recorded. Without tracking, you are guessing. Start with a simple notebook or a free spreadsheet like Google Sheets. Create columns for date, description, income (money received), and expenses (money spent). For example, on 1st March you buy 20 phone cases at 500 each from a supplier in Lagos, spending 10,000. That is an expense. On 5th March you sell 15 cases at 1,200 each, earning 18,000. That is income. Record every single transaction, even small ones like transport to the market or airtime for calls to customers. If you use a smartphone, apps like Wave or Kippa are designed for small businesses in Africa and can send reminders. At the end of each week, total your income and expenses. This habit shows you exactly where your money goes. Many resellers fail because they mix personal and business cash. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for your business. Use a dedicated SIM card for business calls. When you track properly, you can answer simple questions: Did I make a profit last month? Which product sells best? Where can I cut costs? Without tracking, you might feel busy but be losing money. Set a fixed time each day, say 8 PM, to update your records. It takes 10 minutes. Do it consistently. After one month, you will have real data to guide your decisions. Remember, tracking is not for tax alone; it is for your own survival and growth. Start today with a notebook and pen if you have nothing else. The goal is to know your numbers..Trim()

Calculating Profit and Loss

Profit is what remains after you subtract all costs from your sales. Loss is when costs exceed sales. To calculate profit, you need two numbers: total revenue (all money from sales) and total expenses (all money spent to buy and sell goods). For example, you sell 50 bags at 2,000 each, revenue = 100,000. Your expenses: buying bags at 1,200 each = 60,000, transport = 5,000, packaging = 2,000, airtime and data = 3,000. Total expenses = 70,000. Profit = 100,000 - 70,000 = 30,000. That is your gross profit. But you also have overheads like rent for storage, electricity, or wages for a helper. Subtract those to get net profit. If net profit is negative, you have a loss. Many resellers forget to include their own time or transport. For instance, if you spend 2,000 on bus fare to meet a supplier, that is an expense. Track every cost. Use a simple formula: Profit = Sales - (Cost of Goods Sold + Operating Expenses). Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) is what you paid for the items you sold. If you bought 100 bags but only sold 50, COGS is for 50 bags only, not 100. The remaining 50 are inventory, not an expense yet. This is a common mistake. Also, calculate profit per item: selling price minus buying price. If you buy at 1,200 and sell at 2,000, profit per bag is 800. Then multiply by number sold. This helps you decide which products to focus on. If a product gives low profit, drop it. Review your profit and loss weekly or monthly. Write it down. Compare months to see if you are improving. If you are making a loss, find out why: Are prices too low? Are expenses too high? Adjust quickly. Profit is not optional; it is the reason you are in business..Trim()

Managing Cash Flow

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. You can be profitable on paper but still run out of cash to pay suppliers or transport. This happens when customers owe you money or when you spend too much on stock that sits unsold. For example, you have 50,000 in sales but 40,000 is on credit from customers who will pay next month. Meanwhile, you need 30,000 to buy new stock today. You have no cash. To manage cash flow, follow these rules: First, collect payments immediately. If you sell on credit, set clear terms: payment within 7 days. Follow up politely but firmly. Second, don't overstock. Buy only what you can sell in 2-3 weeks. Use the 50/30/20 rule: 50% of cash for restocking, 30% for expenses, 20% as reserve. Third, negotiate credit from your suppliers. Ask for 14 days to pay after delivery. This gives you time to sell and collect cash before paying. Fourth, keep a cash flow forecast. On a sheet, list expected cash in (from sales) and cash out (for purchases, transport, rent) for the next 4 weeks. If you see a shortfall, arrange a small loan from a microfinance bank or a family member early. Fifth, separate personal and business cash. Never use business cash for personal needs like school fees. Pay yourself a small salary. Tools like Kippa or Wave can help track cash. Also, mobile money statements show your balance. Review daily. If you have surplus cash, save it for slow months. Cash flow problems kill more reselling businesses than lack of profit. Stay on top of it..Trim()

Tax Deductions for Resellers

Tax deductions reduce the amount of income tax you pay. As a reseller, you can deduct ordinary and necessary expenses used to run your business. In many African countries, small businesses with turnover below a threshold (e.g., 25 million Naira in Nigeria) may pay a simplified tax or be exempt, but you still need records. Common deductions include: cost of goods sold (what you paid for items you resold), transport to buy or deliver goods, packaging materials, airtime and data for business calls, rent for storage or shop, salaries for helpers, and fees for accounting software. For example, if your revenue is 500,000 and you spent 300,000 on goods, 20,000 on transport, 10,000 on packaging, your taxable profit is 170,000. Without deductions, you would pay tax on 500,000. Keep receipts for every expense. A receipt can be a handwritten note from a supplier, a mobile money transfer screenshot, or a store invoice. If you buy from an open market without receipts, write the details in your record book and ask the seller to sign. In some countries, you can deduct a portion of your home rent if you use part of your home as an office. Also, depreciation on equipment like a laptop or scale can be deducted over time. To claim deductions, you must file your tax returns. Many revenue authorities now allow online filing. If you are unsure, visit a local tax office or ask a business association. Do not ignore tax; penalties can be high. But do not overpay by missing deductions. A simple spreadsheet can track deductible expenses.
