Module 1 · Introduction to Crocodile Farming

History and Global Industry of Crocodile Farming

Crocodile farming started in the 1960s in countries like Zimbabwe and South Africa, mainly to produce high-quality skins for luxury goods. Before farming, wild crocodiles were hunted almost to extinction. Today, legal farming supplies most crocodile skins worldwide. Global production is around 1.5 million skins per year, with major farms in Africa (Zimbabwe, South Africa, Zambia, Kenya), Asia (Thailand, Indonesia), and Australia. Africa produces about 30% of the world's crocodile skins, mostly Nile crocodile. The industry is worth over $500 million annually. In Africa, countries like Zimbabwe have around 50 farms, and South Africa has over 60. A single high-quality belly skin can sell for $300 to $800. Meat is also sold locally and exported. By-products like teeth, claws, and bones are used for jewelry and traditional medicine. Farming reduces pressure on wild populations and provides rural jobs. For a new farmer, the key is to understand that this is a long-term business: crocodiles take 3 to 5 years to reach market size. Start small with 20 to 50 hatchlings, and learn as you grow. Contact your national wildlife authority for permits before you buy any animal..Trim()

Species Selection: Saltwater, Nile, and Other Crocodilians

The two main species farmed in Africa are the Nile crocodile (Crocodylus niloticus) and the saltwater crocodile (Crocodylus porosus). Nile crocodiles are native to most of sub-Saharan Africa and are well-suited to warm climates. They grow to 4-5 meters, but farmed ones are usually harvested at 1.5-2 meters. Saltwater crocodiles are bigger and more aggressive, and they need brackish water. They are farmed in Australia and Southeast Asia, but in Africa, Nile crocodile is the best choice. Other species like the African slender-snouted crocodile and dwarf crocodile are not commercially farmed because they grow slowly and have less valuable skins. When choosing stock, buy from a registered farm or licensed breeder. Never take eggs or animals from the wild without a permit. A good Nile crocodile hatchling costs $20 to $50. You need a ratio of one male to three females for breeding. Check that the animals are healthy: clear eyes, no wounds, and active. Ask for records of parentage to avoid inbreeding. In Africa, Nile crocodile farms are common in Zimbabwe, South Africa, Zambia, and Kenya. If you are in West Africa, the Nile crocodile is still the best option. Avoid mixing species in the same pen, as they can fight and interbreed. Start with at least 10 females and 3 males if you plan to breed..Trim()

Legal and CITES Regulations for Crocodile Farming

Crocodile farming is heavily regulated because all crocodile species are listed under CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species). Nile crocodile is in Appendix I or II depending on the country. In most African countries, you need a permit from the national wildlife authority, such as Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority, South African National Parks, or Kenya Wildlife Service. To start a farm, you must apply for a captive breeding permit, which requires an approved facility, quarantine area, and a management plan. You also need CITES permits for any export or import of live crocodiles, skins, or meat. Each shipment must have a CITES export permit from your country and an import permit from the destination country. The permits cost $100 to $500 per shipment. You must tag each skin with a CITES non-detriment finding tag. Illegal trade carries heavy fines and prison. In Africa, many farms are involved in the CITES registered captive breeding program, which allows legal trade. Keep detailed records of births, deaths, and sales. Invite wildlife officers for inspections. It is also important to follow local animal welfare laws. Without proper permits, your products cannot be sold internationally. Work with a lawyer or consultant who knows wildlife law. The process can take 6 to 12 months, so start early..Trim()

Market Overview: Skins, Meat, and By-Products

The main product from crocodile farming is the skin, especially the belly skin. High-quality skins are used for luxury handbags, shoes, belts, and watch straps. The price depends on size, pattern, and quality. A 1.5-meter Nile crocodile belly skin can sell for $300 to $800. Classic belly skins with large scales are most valuable. Meat is the second product. Crocodile meat is white, low in fat, and high in protein. It sells for $10 to $20 per kilogram in local markets and up to $30 per kilogram in export markets. In Africa, restaurants in tourist areas and cities buy crocodile meat. By-products include teeth, claws, bones, and skulls, which are sold as curios, jewelry, or traditional medicine. A set of teeth can fetch $20 to $50. The global crocodile skin market is dominated by luxury brands like HermÃ¨s and Louis Vuitton. Africa exports about 300,000 skins per year. The main markets are Europe, Asia, and the USA. To succeed, you need to produce consistent quality. Skins must be properly salted or chrome-tanned. Many African farmers sell raw skins to tanneries in South Africa or Zimbabwe. You can also sell directly to international buyers if you have CITES permits. Meat is often sold locally to avoid export regulations. Start by contacting local tanneries and restaurants to understand demand. Join a farmer association to get better prices..Trim()

Basic Biology and Behavior of Crocodiles

Crocodiles are reptiles, so they are cold-blooded. They cannot control their body temperature internally. They need to bask in the sun to warm up and shade or water to cool down. Their ideal body temperature is 30-33Â°C. If it gets too cold, they stop eating and grow slowly. If too hot, they can die. In Africa, you need a climate where temperatures stay above 20Â°C most of the year. Crocodiles are carnivores. In the wild, they eat fish, birds, and mammals. On a farm, you feed them chopped fish, chicken, and red meat. They eat 5-10% of their body weight per week. They have strong jaws and sharp teeth. They swallow food in chunks. They can go weeks without food but will fight if hungry. Crocodiles are territorial and aggressive, especially males. They need space. A grow-out pen should allow at least 1 square meter per crocodile. They are also cannibalistic; smaller ones can be eaten by larger ones. So you must sort by size. Breeding happens once a year. Females lay 30-60 eggs in a nest. Incubation is 80-90 days at 28-32Â°C. The temperature determines the sex: lower temperatures produce females, higher produce males. Hatchlings are 25-30 cm long. They grow about 30 cm per year. They can live 50-70 years. Handle them with care using a noose pole and secure their jaws with tape..Trim()

Site Selection and Climate Requirements

Choosing the right site is critical. You need a location with a warm climate, plenty of water, and good security. Crocodiles need temperatures between 25Â°C and 35Â°C. In Africa, this means lowveld areas like the Zambezi Valley, Limpopo, or coastal regions. Avoid highlands where it gets cold. You need a reliable water source, such as a borehole, river, or municipal supply. Water quality must be good: pH 6.5-8.5, low chlorine. You will need to change water regularly. The site should be away from residential areas to avoid conflicts and smell. At least 500 meters from homes and 1 km from schools. The soil should be well-drained, like sandy loam, to prevent muddy pens. You need a slope of 1-2% for drainage. Security is vital: crocodiles can escape and hurt people. Use strong fences: 2-meter high chain-link with a concrete base. Add a double gate. You also need a building for incubating eggs and a quarantine area. Power supply for pumps and heaters is helpful. Roads must be accessible for feed and skin transport. Land size: for 100 crocodiles, you need about 0.5 hectares including pens, buildings, and buffer. Start with a small pen of 10m x 5m for 50 hatchlings. Get a topographic map and soil test. Consult local wildlife authority for site approval. Budget $5,000 to $10,000 for basic infrastructure..Trim()

Module 2 · Facility Design and Construction

Designing Pens and Enclosures for Different Life Stages

Crocodiles need different spaces as they grow. Hatchlings (up to 3 months) are kept indoors in plastic tubs or concrete tanks of 1m x 1m, with 20-30 cm of water. Provide a dry basking area using a brick or sloping concrete. Stock 20 hatchlings per square meter. Juveniles (3-12 months) go into larger pens: 2m x 3m for 10-15 animals, with a land-to-water ratio of 1:1. Use concrete walls 60 cm high, smooth to prevent injury. Sub-adults (1-3 years) need 4m x 6m pens with 30 cm water depth and a 2m x 2m dry area. Stock 1 animal per 1.5 square meters. Adults (3+ years) require 10m x 20m enclosures with a 3m x 4m pool, 50 cm deep, and a dry area of at least 20 square meters. Stock 1 adult per 10 square meters. Always slope the floor 2% toward a drain for easy cleaning. In Africa, use locally available materials: concrete blocks, clay bricks, or rammed earth stabilized with cement. Roof pens with shade cloth or corrugated iron to prevent overheating. Ensure walls extend 1m above ground and 50 cm below to prevent digging. Add a 1m high smooth barrier at the top to prevent climbing. Keep pens close to a water source and access road. For all stages, provide hiding spots using PVC pipes or stacked bricks. Regularly check for sharp edges and repair cracks to avoid injury and escapes..Trim()

Water Systems: Supply, Drainage, and Filtration

Water quality is critical for crocodile health. You need a reliable supply: borehole, river, or municipal water. For a medium farm (100 adults), plan for 5000 liters per day. Store water in a 10,000-liter tank on a 3m stand for gravity flow. Pipes: use 50mm PVC for mains, 25mm for pen inlets. Each pen should have a tap and a float valve to maintain water level. Drainage: slope pen floors 2% to a 110mm PVC drain pipe that leads to a central collection pit. The pit should be 2m x 2m x 1m, lined with concrete. From there, pump water to a filtration system. Filtration: use a three-stage system: 1) Sedimentation tank (2m x 2m x 1m) where solids settle; 2) Mechanical filter with gravel and sand layers (0.5m deep); 3) Biological filter with plastic bio-balls or lava rocks to break down ammonia. For small farms, a simple sand filter and daily water exchange (20% of volume) works. Test water weekly for pH (6.5-8.0), ammonia (<0.5 mg/L), and temperature (28-32Â°C). In Africa, use locally made filters: plastic drums filled with gravel, sand, and charcoal. Clean filters monthly. Recycle water by pumping from the collection pit back to the sedimentation tank. Always have a backup water source, like a rainwater harvesting system from roofs. Install a sump pump in the collection pit to handle overflow during rains. Keep drainage channels covered with grates to prevent escapes and debris..Trim()

Heating and Temperature Control Systems

Crocodiles are cold-blooded; they need 28-32Â°C to digest food and grow. In Africa, ambient temperatures can drop at night or in winter, so heating is essential. For hatchling rooms, use infrared heat lamps (100W) suspended 50 cm above the basking area. One lamp per 2 square meters. For juvenile and adult pens, use submerged water heaters (1000W per 10,000 liters) to keep water at 30Â°C. Alternatively, use black plastic pipes on the roof to solar-heat water. In cooler regions like South Africa's highveld, install a solar water heating system with a 200-liter tank and collector panels. For large farms, a biomass heater (burning wood or agricultural waste) can heat water in a boiler, circulated through pipes under pen floors. Insulate walls and roofs with polystyrene or thatch to reduce heat loss. Use thermostats to control heaters and avoid overheating. Monitor temperature with digital thermometers placed in water and on land. Provide shade in summer to prevent temperatures above 35Â°C. In hot climates, use evaporative cooling: wet sacks on walls or misting systems. For power outages, have a backup generator or use gas heaters. Security Fencing and Containment Protocols

Crocodiles are strong and can climb, dig, and push. Security fencing must be robust. Use a perimeter fence of 2m high chain-link (50mm mesh) with a 1m overhang angled inward at the top. Bury the fence 50 cm deep and line with concrete to prevent digging. For pens, walls should be 1.5m high for adults, made of concrete or brick, with a smooth inner surface. Add a 30cm wide smooth lip at the top to prevent climbing. Gates should be double-door: an outer gate and an inner gate, never both open at once. Use heavy-duty hinges and locks. Inside pens, have a catch pen or crush for safe handling. Containment protocols: staff must wear boots and use catch poles. Never work alone. Count animals daily. Inspect fences weekly for damage, especially after storms. Keep a toolkit with wire, pliers, and concrete for quick repairs. In Africa, consider using local materials: wooden poles treated with used oil, or metal drums filled with concrete as posts. For small farms, a simple 1.2m high wall of concrete blocks with a smooth render works. Always have a secondary barrier: a 1m high fence 1m away from the main pen to catch escapees. Train staff in escape response: use nets and shields, never bare hands. Keep a record of all incidents. Install warning signs. For night security, use solar-powered motion lights. In areas with theft risk, add a guard house and patrols. Remember, a crocodile can lunge up to 2m, so stay beyond that distance when feeding..Trim()

Quarantine and Isolation Facilities

New or sick crocodiles must be isolated to prevent disease spread. Build a quarantine facility at least 20m from main pens. It should have 4-6 small pens, each 2m x 2m, with individual water and drainage. Use concrete walls and floors for easy disinfection. Each pen has a separate tap and drain that goes to a dedicated septic tank, not the main system. Stock 1-2 animals per pen. Before introducing a new crocodile, clean and disinfect the pen with 2% sodium hypochlorite (bleach) and let it dry. Quarantine new animals for 60 days. During this time, observe daily for signs of disease: lethargy, loss of appetite, skin lesions, or abnormal feces. Wear dedicated boots and gloves for each pen, and change them between pens. Feed quarantined animals last. For sick animals, have an isolation pen with a heat source (30Â°C) and easy access for treatment. Keep a veterinary kit: antibiotics, antiseptics, and faecal test kits. In Africa, use locally available disinfectants like lime (calcium hydroxide) for floors. For small farms, a single quarantine pen can be used, but must be cleaned thoroughly between animals. Always dispose of dead animals by burning or deep burial (2m deep) away from water sources. Record all treatments and movements. After quarantine, if healthy, move animals to main pens. If disease is suspected, consult a vet. Quarantine pens should have a footbath at the entrance with disinfectant. Never mix age groups in quarantine. Keep the area free of rodents and wild birds..Trim()

Nutritional Requirements for Hatchlings, Juveniles, and Adults

Hatchlings (0-6 months) need a diet high in protein and energy for rapid growth. Juveniles (6-24 months) grow slower but still need 35-40% protein. Transition to chopped fish, poultry offal, and a grower pellet (32-36% protein). Feed 3-5% of body weight daily. A 5kg juvenile eats 150-250g per day. Adults (over 2 years) are maintained for meat or breeding. Protein needs drop to 28-32%. Use whole fish, meat scraps, and a maintenance pellet. Feed Formulation and Commercial Diets

You can mix your own feed to cut costs, but it must meet protein, fat, and mineral needs. Commercial crocodile diets are available from feed companies in South Africa, Zimbabwe, and Kenya. Common Diseases and Parasites in Crocodiles

Crocodiles are tough, but in captivity they get stressed and sick. The most common killers on African farms are bacterial infections from dirty water. If you see a crocodile floating sideways or not eating for two days, isolate it immediately. Use a 10% salt bath for 30 minutes daily for three days.
