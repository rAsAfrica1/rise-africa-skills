Module 1 · Introduction to Banana Farming

Global Banana Production and Trade Overview

Bananas are the world's most exported fruit. In 2023, global production hit about 135 million tonnes, with India, China, and Indonesia leading. But the biggest exporters are Ecuador, the Philippines, Costa Rica, and Colombia. Africa produces a lot but exports very little. Countries like Uganda, Rwanda, and Cameroon grow bananas mostly for local food. Uganda alone produces around 10 million tonnes yearly, but most is eaten locally. The global trade is dominated by one variety: the Cavendish, which makes up 47% of all exported bananas. It is grown in large plantations in Latin America and the Philippines. These bananas are harvested green, shipped in refrigerated ships at 13.3Â°C, and ripened with ethylene gas in destination countries. The journey from farm to supermarket takes 3-4 weeks. For African farmers, the export market is tough to enter because of strict quality standards and high shipping costs. But there is a growing regional market. In East Africa, Kenya imports bananas from Uganda. In West Africa, Ghana and Ivory Coast export to Europe. The key takeaway: Africa is a production giant but a trade minnow. That means opportunities exist to process bananas into flour, chips, wine, and juice for local and regional markets. Also, fair trade and organic certification can help smallholders access premium export niches. To compete, you need to focus on quality, consistency, and volume. Start small, learn the market, and consider joining a cooperative to meet buyer demands..Trim()

Types of Bananas: Dessert, Cooking, and Plantain

Not all bananas are the same. There are three main types: dessert bananas, cooking bananas, and plantains. Dessert bananas are sweet and soft. The most common is the Cavendish, which you see in supermarkets. It is eaten raw. In Africa, other dessert types like Sukali Ndizi in Uganda and Apple bananas in Kenya are popular. Cooking bananas, often called matoke in East Africa, are starchy and must be cooked. They are boiled, steamed, or roasted. Examples include East African Highland bananas (Matoke) and Bluggoe. Plantains are larger, firmer, and starchy. They can be boiled, fried, or roasted. In West Africa, plantains are a staple. They are used to make dodo (fried plantain), alloco, and chips. The choice of type depends on your market. Dessert bananas fetch higher prices in urban areas. Cooking bananas are for local food markets. Plantains have a long shelf life and are good for processing into flour and chips. Each type has different growing needs. Dessert bananas need well-drained soil and wind protection. Cooking bananas tolerate poorer soils. Plantains need more water. Pests and diseases also differ. Black Sigatoka affects all, but Fusarium wilt (Panama disease) is worse on Cavendish. For a beginner, start with a type that has a ready market. If you are near a city, grow dessert bananas. If you are in a rural area, grow cooking bananas or plantains for local sales. Always plant at least two varieties to spread risk..Trim()

Banana Farming Systems: Monoculture vs. Intercropping

You can grow bananas alone (monoculture) or with other crops (intercropping). Monoculture means a field of only bananas. It is easier to manage, spray, and harvest. But it has risks. Pests and diseases spread fast. Soil nutrients get depleted quickly. You need to apply fertiliser regularly. Monoculture is common on large plantations in Ecuador and the Philippines. In Africa, it is used by commercial farms in Kenya and South Africa. Intercropping means growing bananas with other crops like beans, maize, coffee, or cocoa. This is the traditional African way. It provides food and income while the bananas mature. It also improves soil health. Legumes like beans fix nitrogen. The shade from bananas helps crops like coffee. But intercropping makes management harder. You must manage different fertiliser and water needs. Pests may attack one crop and spread. For smallholders, intercropping is often better. It spreads risk and gives quick returns. For example, in Uganda, farmers grow bananas with coffee and beans. In Nigeria, plantains are intercropped with cocoyam and maize. If you choose monoculture, plant windbreaks and use drip irrigation. If you intercrop, choose compatible crops. Avoid tall crops that compete for light. Good companions are beans, groundnuts, and sweet potatoes. Space bananas at 3m by 3m. Plant intercrops between rows. Always rotate intercrops to avoid pest build-up. Remember, bananas are heavy feeders. Whether monoculture or intercropping, you must feed the soil with manure or compost every 3-4 months..Trim()

Economic Importance and Market Demand

Bananas are a cash crop and a food security crop. In Africa, over 100 million people depend on bananas for food and income. Uganda produces about 10 million tonnes yearly, worth over $1 billion. Kenya's banana industry is worth $400 million. In Ghana, plantains and bananas contribute significantly to GDP. The demand for bananas is growing. Urban populations want affordable, nutritious food. Bananas are rich in potassium, vitamin B6, and fibre. They are also gluten-free. The market has several segments. Fresh bananas for local markets. Processed products like flour, chips, wine, and juice. Export markets for premium dessert bananas. Each has different prices. In local markets, a bunch of cooking bananas sells for $2-$5. A kilogram of dessert bananas sells for $0.50-$1.50. Processed products earn more. Banana flour sells for $2-$4 per kg. Banana chips sell for $3-$5 per kg. But processing requires equipment and certification. For a beginner, start with fresh sales. Identify a market: schools, hospitals, hotels, or open-air markets. Talk to traders. Join a cooperative to aggregate volume. The biggest challenge is post-harvest loss. Up to 40% of bananas are lost due to poor handling. Invest in crates, not sacks. Harvest at the right maturity. For cooking bananas, harvest when full but green. For dessert, harvest when slightly yellow. Transport quickly to market. With good practices, you can earn a steady income. Consider value addition later..Trim()

Challenges and Opportunities in Banana Farming

Banana farming is not easy. You face pests, diseases, drought, and market problems. The worst disease is Panama disease (Fusarium wilt TR4). It kills Cavendish bananas and stays in the soil for decades. It has wiped out farms in Asia and is now in Africa (Mozambique, 2013). Black Sigatoka is a fungal disease that reduces yields by 30-50%. Weevils and nematodes attack the roots and corms. Drought and poor soils reduce yields. In Africa, many farmers lack clean planting material. They use suckers from infected fields. That spreads disease. Market access is another challenge. Prices fluctuate. Transport is expensive. Post-harvest losses are high. But there are opportunities. The demand for bananas is rising. You can grow disease-resistant varieties like FHIA hybrids. You can use tissue culture plantlets, which are clean and high-yielding. You can intercrop to spread risk. You can add value by making flour, chips, or wine. You can join a cooperative to access training, credit, and markets. Organic and fair trade certification can earn you premium prices. Climate-smart practices like mulching and drip irrigation help. The key is to start small, learn, and reinvest. Many African governments and NGOs offer support. For example, in Uganda, the National Banana Research Programme provides clean suckers. In Kenya, the Banana Growers Association helps with marketing. Do not go it alone. Seek advice from extension officers. Attend farmer field schools. With the right knowledge, banana farming can be profitable and sustainable..Trim()

Overview of the Banana Value Chain

The banana value chain starts with production and ends with the consumer. Each step adds value and cost. Step 1: Input supply. You need suckers, fertiliser, manure, and tools. Buy from certified nurseries. Step 2: Production. You plant, weed, prune, and harvest. This takes 9-12 months for the first harvest. Step 3: Harvesting and post-harvest handling. Harvest with a sharp knife. Leave a stalk. Handle bunches carefully. Do not throw them. Use crates for transport. Step 4: Transportation. Move bananas to market quickly. Use trucks or motorcycles. Avoid direct sun. Step 5: Aggregation and trading. Traders buy from farmers and sell to wholesalers. Prices are often set by traders. To get better prices, join a cooperative. Step 6: Processing. Some bananas are processed into flour, chips, wine, or juice. This requires a processing facility and food safety certification. Step 7: Retailing. Supermarkets, open markets, and kiosks sell to consumers. Each step has challenges. Farmers often get the smallest share. For a $1 banana, the farmer might get $0.20. Traders and retailers get the rest. To increase your share, move up the chain. Process your own bananas. Sell directly to consumers. Use digital platforms like WhatsApp and Facebook to market. In Africa, the value chain is mostly informal. But formal chains are growing. Supermarkets like Shoprite and Carrefour source from local farmers. They need consistent quality and volume. If you can meet their standards, you can get better prices. Start by mapping your local value chain. Who buys? What price? What quality? Then find your niche..Trim()

Module 2 · Banana Plant Biology and Growth Cycle

Anatomy of the Banana Plant: Pseudostem, Corm, and Leaves

The banana plant is not a tree but a giant herb. Its 'trunk' is a pseudostem, made of tightly packed leaf sheaths. The pseudostem can grow up to 30 feet tall and 2 feet thick, depending on the variety. At the base is the corm, a true stem that is underground and stores food. The corm produces suckers (new shoots) and roots. Roots are fibrous and shallow, mostly in the top 30 cm of soil, so avoid deep digging near the plant..Trim()

.Trim()

Leaves emerge from the center of the pseudostem. Each new leaf pushes up through the center, and as it expands, it splits along the veins to resist wind. A healthy plant has 8-12 functional leaves at flowering. Leaves are the factory for photosynthesis. If leaves are damaged by wind, disease, or insects, fruit yield drops. In Africa, common leaf diseases include black sigatoka (black streaks) and banana bunchy top virus (dark green streaks on leaf edges)..Trim()

.Trim()

Practical tip: Count leaves regularly. If a plant has fewer than 6 healthy leaves at flowering, remove it and let a sucker replace it. When pruning, use a sharp machete or knife, and clean it with 10% bleach between plants to avoid spreading disease. In dry areas like northern Nigeria or Senegal, mulch around the base with dry grass to keep roots cool and moist..Trim()

.Trim()

The pseudostem is not wood; it is 90% water. That means the plant needs plenty of water. In Uganda, farmers often plant bananas in trenches or basins to catch rainwater. In Kenya, they use drip irrigation. A mature plant needs about 25 mm of water per week. Without enough water, the pseudostem shrinks and leaves collapse..Trim()

.Trim()

When you understand the anatomy, you can manage the plant better. For example, if the pseudostem is weak, prop it with a forked stick to prevent it from falling under the weight of the bunch. If the corm is exposed, cover it with soil. Healthy roots mean a healthy plant..Trim()

The Banana Growth Cycle: Vegetative to Reproductive

The banana growth cycle has two main phases: vegetative and reproductive. The vegetative phase starts when a sucker emerges from the corm. For the first 3-4 months, the plant grows leaves and roots. It produces a new leaf every 7-10 days in warm weather. In cooler highlands, it may take 15-20 days. The pseudostem thickens as leaf sheaths accumulate..Trim()

.Trim()

After 4-6 months, the plant enters the reproductive phase. The growing point inside the pseudostem changes from producing leaves to producing a flower. This is called 'flower initiation.' You cannot see it from outside, but the plant stops making new leaves and the center becomes swollen. About 2-3 months later, the flower emerges from the top. This is the 'shooting' stage..Trim()

.Trim()

From shooting to harvest takes another 3-4 months, depending on temperature. In the lowlands of Ghana or CÃ´te d'Ivoire, the whole cycle from sucker to harvest can be 10-12 months. In the highlands of Ethiopia or Cameroon, it can take 18-24 months. The total heat units matter: bananas need about 1,500-2,000 growing degree days (base 14Â°C) to complete a cycle..Trim()

.Trim()

Practical management: During vegetative growth, remove excess suckers so the main plant gets all nutrients. Keep only one sucker for the next cycle (the 'follower'). During reproductive growth, ensure consistent water. Drought at flowering causes small bunches and poor fruit fill. After harvest, cut the pseudostem down to about 1 meter and split it to speed decomposition. The corm will produce new suckers for the next ratoon crop..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many farmers grow bananas in mixed systems with coffee or beans. That is fine, but bananas need full sun for at least 6 hours a day. Shade reduces yield. Also, do not plant bananas in waterlogged areas; roots rot in 2-3 days of flooding. If your area floods, plant on raised beds or mounds..Trim()

Photosynthesis and Nutrient Uptake in Bananas

Photosynthesis is how banana plants make food. Leaves capture sunlight and combine carbon dioxide (CO2) from the air with water (H2O) from the soil to make sugars. This happens in the green parts of the leaf. A healthy banana leaf can be 2 meters long and 0.5 meters wide. The more leaf area, the more food the plant makes. That food goes to the bunch, the corm, and new suckers..Trim()

.Trim()

Bananas are heavy feeders. A mature plant removes about 200-250 kg of nitrogen (N), 30-50 kg of phosphorus (P2O5), and 300-400 kg of potassium (K2O) per hectare per year. Potassium is the most important for fruit size and sweetness. In Uganda, farmers use kitchen ash (rich in potassium) around the base. In Kenya, they apply 200 g of NPK 17-17-17 per plant every 3 months. In Nigeria, poultry manure is common: apply 5-10 kg per plant per year, split into two doses..Trim()

.Trim()

Nutrient uptake happens through roots. Banana roots are shallow and have root hairs that absorb water and nutrients. The best time to apply fertilizer is when the soil is moist, not dry. After applying, cover with mulch to reduce evaporation. Do not put fertilizer directly against the pseudostem; it can burn. Spread it in a ring 30-60 cm from the base..Trim()

.Trim()

Signs of deficiency: Yellow leaves (nitrogen), purple leaf edges (phosphorus), and yellow-orange leaf margins (potassium). If you see these, act quickly. In sandy soils, nutrients leach away, so apply little and often. In clay soils, apply less frequently. Test your soil every 2 years if possible. Many African extension services offer soil testing for a small fee..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember: Photosynthesis needs water. If the soil is dry, stomata (tiny leaf pores) close, and CO2 cannot enter. So irrigation and mulching are just as important as fertilizer..Trim()

Flowering, Bunch Development, and Fruit Maturation

Flowering starts when the flower bud emerges from the top of the pseudostem. This is called 'shooting.' The flower is a large, purple, torpedo-shaped bud. It opens gradually, revealing rows of flowers. Female flowers are at the base and become fruit. Male flowers are at the tip and fall off. Each row is called a 'hand,' and each hand has 10-20 'fingers' (bananas). A bunch can have 5-15 hands, depending on variety and nutrition..Trim()

.Trim()

After pollination (which is not needed for most cultivated bananas), the ovaries swell into fruit. The bunch grows for 60-90 days. During this time, the plant needs plenty of water and potassium. If water is short, fingers are small and may not fill. In Ghana, farmers bag bunches with perforated polythene bags to protect from insects and sunburn. In Uganda, they use dry banana leaves. Bagging also speeds up maturity by 1-2 weeks..Trim()

.Trim()

Fruit maturation: Bananas are harvested when they are mature but still green. Maturity is judged by the age of the bunch (e.g., 80-100 days after shooting) or by the disappearance of angular ridges on the fingers. For local markets, harvest when fingers are 3/4 full. For export, harvest earlier. In East Africa, the 'Kampala' variety is harvested at 90 days. In West Africa, 'Agbagba' is harvested at 100-110 days..Trim()

.Trim()

After harvest, the bunch is cut into hands or clusters. The main pseudostem is cut down, and a new sucker is selected to replace it. Do not leave the old pseudostem standing; it harbors weevils. Cut it into pieces and spread on the ground..Trim()

.Trim()

Practical tip: To get bigger bunches, remove the 'bell' (male bud) about 2 weeks after shooting. This stops the plant from wasting energy on male flowers. Also, remove the last 1-2 hands if they are small; this directs nutrients to the upper hands. This is called 'dehanding' or 'thinning'..Trim()

Suckering and Ratoon Management

Suckers are new shoots that grow from the corm. They are the future of your banana farm. A healthy plant produces 3-5 suckers per year. You must manage them: too many suckers compete with the mother plant for water and nutrients. The goal is to have one mother plant, one follower (for the next harvest), and maybe one young sucker. Remove all others..Trim()

.Trim()

How to remove a sucker: Use a sharp, clean spade or machete. Cut the sucker at its base, below the soil, being careful not to damage the mother corm. In dry areas, leave the cut sucker on the ground as mulch. In wet areas, remove it to prevent disease. Do this every 2-3 months. In Uganda, farmers call this 'desuckering.'.Trim()

.Trim()

Choosing a follower: Select a sucker that is 30-60 cm tall, with broad leaves and a thick base. It should be on the opposite side of the mother's bunch to balance weight. Do not choose a sucker that is too small (less than 20 cm) or too large (more than 1 meter). A good follower will produce a bunch in 10-12 months..Trim()

.Trim()

Ratoon management: After harvest, the mother plant is cut down. The follower becomes the new mother. This cycle can continue for 5-10 years if managed well. Each ratoon crop usually yields more than the first, up to a point. After 5-6 cycles, yields decline due to soil exhaustion and pest buildup. Then you should replant with fresh corms or tissue-culture plantlets..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many farmers practice 'mat' management: they keep 3-4 plants per mat (one mother, one follower, one young sucker, and one new sucker). This ensures continuous production. But do not overcrowd; each mat needs 2-3 meters spacing. If you see weevil damage (holes in corm, yellowing leaves), apply ash or neem cake around the base. Also, rotate with beans or maize to break pest cycles..Trim()

Factors Affecting Growth: Temperature, Light, and Humidity

Bananas grow best in warm, humid conditions. The ideal temperature range is 26-30Â°C. Below 16Â°C, growth stops. Above 38Â°C, leaves may scorch and fruit may sunburn. In Africa, lowland areas like the Congo Basin and coastal Kenya are ideal. Highlands above 1,600 meters are too cold for most varieties, though some (like 'Huti') tolerate cooler nights. Frost kills banana leaves; if you farm in a frost-prone area, plant on slopes with good air drainage..Trim()

.Trim()

Light: Bananas need full sun, at least 6-8 hours per day. Shade reduces photosynthesis and yield. In mixed cropping, plant bananas as the main crop and shade-tolerant crops (like cocoa or coffee) at the edges, not directly under bananas. In home gardens, avoid planting near tall trees. If you must plant in partial shade, choose a variety that tolerates it, like 'Pisang Awak'..Trim()

.Trim()

Humidity: Bananas need 60-80% relative humidity. Dry air (below 50%) causes leaf edges to dry and fruit to crack. In dry regions like the Sahel, irrigate and mulch heavily. Wind is also a factor: strong winds shred leaves and topple plants. Plant windbreaks like casuarina or grevillea on the windward side. In the Caribbean, they use banana windbreaks. In Africa, farmers in the Rift Valley use maize or sorghum as windbreaks..Trim()

.Trim()

Rainfall: Bananas need 100-150 mm of rain per month. If rainfall is less, irrigate. In Ethiopia, farmers use furrow irrigation. In South Africa, drip irrigation is common. During dry spells, water twice a week, 20-30 liters per plant. Do not let the soil dry out completely. But also avoid waterlogging: bananas die in standing water within 3-4 days. If your soil is heavy clay, plant on ridges..Trim()

.Trim()

Practical summary: Choose a site with full sun, good drainage, and wind protection. Test your soil pH; bananas prefer 5.5-7.0. Add lime if too acidic. Keep temperature, light, and humidity in mind when selecting varieties. For example, 'Cavendish' needs high humidity, while 'Bluggoe' tolerates drier conditions..Trim()

Planting Techniques: Hole Preparation and Spacing

Planting holes for bananas should be dug at least 60 cm wide and 60 cm deep. In dry areas, make them 80 cm deep to reach moist soil. Separate topsoil (first 30 cm) from subsoil. Optimal Planting Seasons and Timing

In Africa, the best time to plant bananas is at the start of the rainy season. For most of West Africa, this is April to June. Avoid planting in the middle of a dry season unless you can water every 2-3 days. Also avoid planting right before a known dry spell. Mulching is covering the soil around banana plants with dry organic material. It conserves moisture, suppresses weeds, and adds nutrients as it rots. Desuckering is removing unwanted suckers from the banana mat so the mother plant and one or two followers get all the water and nutrients. Suckers are shoots that grow from the underground corm. Principles of Organic Banana Production

Organic banana production avoids synthetic chemicals and relies on natural processes. In Africa, this means using compost, manure, and cover crops instead of urea or NPK fertilizers. For example, a farmer in Uganda can apply 10-15 kg of well-rotted cattle manure per banana mat each year, spread 30 cm from the pseudostem. This feeds the plant slowly and improves soil structure. Instead of pesticides, organic farmers use neem extract, wood ash, or insecticidal soap. A simple neem spray: soak 1 kg of crushed neem seeds in 10 liters of water for 24 hours, strain, and add 5 ml of liquid soap per liter. Spray weekly on leaves to control aphids and caterpillars. Organic farming also means no genetically modified organisms (GMOs) and no synthetic hormones. Crop rotation with beans or groundnuts fixes nitrogen and breaks pest cycles. Mulching with dry grass or banana leaves conserves moisture and suppresses weeds. A key principle is biodiversity: plant different crops together, like bananas with coffee or beans, to confuse pests and attract beneficial insects. Organic certification requires a 3-year transition period during which you must follow all organic rules. During transition, yields may drop slightly, but premiums can be 20-30% higher. In Kenya, organic bananas sell for KES 80-100 per kg versus KES 50-60 for conventional. Start small: convert one acre at a time. Keep records of all inputs and practices. Soil Health and Composting

Healthy soil is the foundation of organic banana farming. Bananas need deep, well-drained soil with pH 5.5-7.0. In many African farms, soils are depleted of organic matter. To fix this, make compost. Organic Pest and Disease Control

Organic banana farming controls pests and diseases without synthetic chemicals. The main pests are banana weevils, nematodes, and aphids.
