Module 1 · Introduction to Steel Fabrication

Overview of Steel Fabrication Industry

Steel fabrication is the process of cutting, bending, and assembling steel to build structures, machines, and everyday items. In Africa, this industry is growing fast. From the Dangote Refinery in Nigeria to the Standard Gauge Railway in Kenya, steel fabricators are needed everywhere. You will find work in welding shops, construction sites, mining companies, and manufacturing plants. For example, a small workshop in Ghana might make steel gates, window frames, and roof trusses for local homes. A larger company in South Africa might build mining equipment or bridges. The demand for steel fabricators is high because Africa is investing in infrastructureâroads, railways, buildings, and pipelines. As a fabricator, you could earn between $300 and $1,500 per month depending on your skills and location. You can start with simple tools: a welding machine, angle grinder, measuring tape, and a workbench. Many successful fabricators started as apprentices in small shops. Whether you work for a company or start your own, steel fabrication offers a clear path to a stable income and respect in your community..Trim()

Steel Types and Properties

Not all steel is the same. The two main types you will work with are mild steel and high-carbon steel. Mild steel, also called low-carbon steel, contains about 0.05% to 0.25% carbon. It is soft, easy to weld, and bend. Most gates, tables, and building frames use mild steel. High-carbon steel has 0.6% to 1.5% carbon. It is harder and stronger but more brittle and harder to weld. You will find it in tools like chisels, hammers, and blades. Another common type is stainless steel, which contains chromium (at least 10.5%). It resists rust and is used in kitchens, hospitals, and food processing plants. In Africa, stainless steel is popular for handrails and water tanks because it lasts long in humid conditions. You also need to know about steel shapes. Common profiles include flat bars, round bars, square tubes, rectangular tubes, angle iron (L-shaped), channel iron (C-shaped), and I-beams. Each has a specific use. For example, a 50mm x 50mm angle iron is great for making frames. A 100mm x 50mm rectangular tube works for trailer chassis. When you buy steel, you will see markings like S275 or S355. These indicate the yield strength in megapascals (MPa). S275 means it can withstand 275 MPa of stress before bending permanently. For most general fabrication, S275 is fine. Always ask your supplier for the steel grade and check for rust or bends before buying. Knowing your steel types helps you choose the right material for the job, saving money and preventing failures..Trim()

Fabrication Processes Overview

Steel fabrication follows a clear sequence: measuring, cutting, shaping, joining, and finishing. First, you measure and mark. Use a steel measuring tape, a scriber, and a try square. Accuracy mattersâa 1mm error can ruin a whole project. Next, cutting. You can use a hacksaw for small pieces, but for speed, use an angle grinder with a cutting disc or a chop saw. For thick steel, a plasma cutter or oxy-acetylene torch works well. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. After cutting, you shape the steel. Bending can be done with a hydraulic press brake or manually with a hammer and anvil for small parts. You might also need to drill holes using a drill press or hand drill with cobalt bits. Then comes joining. Welding is the most common method. Arc welding (stick) is cheap and portableâperfect for site work. MIG welding is faster and cleaner for thin steel. You can also use bolts, rivets, or screws for parts that need to be disassembled. Finally, finishing. Grind down sharp edges and weld spatter with a grinding disc. Then clean the steel and apply a primer and paint to prevent rust. In Africa, red oxide primer is common because it is affordable. For outdoor items, use a topcoat of enamel or epoxy paint. Each step requires practice. Start with simple projects like a steel stool or a small gate. As you get better, you can build trailers, staircases, and roof trusses. Remember: measure twice, cut once. That old rule saves time and material..Trim()

Safety in Steel Fabrication

Steel fabrication is dangerous if you ignore safety. Every year, thousands of workers get injured from cuts, burns, eye damage, and falls. You must protect yourself. First, wear the right gear. Safety glasses or a face shield are non-negotiable when grinding or cutting. Welding requires a welding helmet with a dark lens (shade 10 or higher). Wear leather gloves, a leather apron, and steel-toe boots. Never wear loose clothing or open shoes. Second, manage fire risks. Welding and grinding create sparks that can ignite flammable materials. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby and clear the work area of oil, paint, and paper. In small African workshops, it is common to have a bucket of sand or water for emergencies. Third, handle tools safely. Before using an angle grinder, check the disc for cracks and make sure the guard is in place. Never remove the guard. When using a chop saw, clamp the steel securely. For welding, ensure proper ventilationâwelding fumes contain harmful gases. Work outdoors or use a fan to blow fumes away. Fourth, prevent electric shock. Check welding cables for damage and never weld in wet conditions. Use a residual current device (RCD) if available. Fifth, practice good housekeeping. Keep the floor clear of scraps, cables, and tools. Store gas cylinders upright and secure them. If you work at height, use a harness and scaffold. Finally, know first aid. Keep a first aid kit with bandages, burn cream, and eye wash. If you get a cut, clean it and cover it. For eye injuries, flush with water and see a doctor. Safety is not optionalâit is part of the job..Trim()

Reading Fabrication Drawings

Fabrication drawings are the language of the trade. They show you exactly what to build, what size, and how to join it. You must learn to read them. A typical drawing has three views: front, top, and side. These are called orthographic projections. Each line has meaning: solid lines show visible edges, dashed lines show hidden edges, and center lines show holes or symmetry. You will also see dimensions with arrows and numbers. For example, a dimension of 1200 means 1200mm (1.2 meters). Always check the unitsâmost drawings use millimeters. Next, look for the title block. It is usually at the bottom right. It tells you the project name, scale, material, and date. The scale might be 1:10, meaning the drawing is one-tenth of real size. You will also find a bill of materials (BOM) that lists all parts, quantities, and sizes. For example, a BOM might say: 4 pieces of 50x50x5 angle iron, 1200mm long. That is your cutting list. Weld symbols are also important. A triangle with a line means a fillet weld. The number next to it shows the weld size. A flag symbol means weld on site. If you see a circle at the joint, it means weld all around. In Africa, many small shops use hand-drawn sketches, but learning standard symbols helps you work with engineers and bigger companies. Practice by reading drawings of simple items like a steel door or a table. Measure the drawing, then measure the real thing. Soon, you will be able to build from a drawing without asking questions..Trim()

Career Paths and Opportunities

Steel fabrication opens many doors. You can start as a helper or apprentice, then become a welder, fabricator, or supervisor. With experience, you can specialize: structural steel for buildings, pipe welding for oil and gas, or ornamental ironwork for homes. In Africa, the mining sector hires many fabricators to maintain equipment. Construction companies need fabricators for rebar, columns, and beams. You can also work in shipbuilding, automotive repair, or agricultural machinery. If you prefer self-employment, start a small workshop. With a good reputation, you can get contracts from local builders, schools, and churches. Another path is to become a welding inspector or teacher. Certification like AWS (American Welding Society) or CSWIP (Certification Scheme for Welding and Inspection Personnel) can boost your income. In many African countries, vocational training centers offer short courses. You can also learn online through YouTube channels like Welding Tips and Tricks. To grow, save money to buy better tools: a MIG welder, a plasma cutter, a magnetic drill. Network with other fabricators and join local associations. Always deliver on time and keep your work clean. Word of mouth is powerful. Many successful fabricators in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa started with one welding machine and now employ ten people. Your career in steel fabrication can take you from a small shop to large projects. The demand is thereâyou just need skill and determination..Trim()

Carbon Steel Grades and Applications

Carbon steel is the backbone of fabrication in Africa. It is affordable, weldable, and strong. The main grades you will see are A36, 1018, 1045, and A572. A36 is a low-carbon steel with a yield strength of 250 MPa. It is used for structural beams, columns, and base plates. In Nigeria, Ghana, and Kenya, A36 is common in building frames and machine bases. 1018 is a low-carbon steel with good machinability. It is used for shafts, pins, and bolts. 1045 is a medium-carbon steel with higher strength (yield around 530 MPa) but it is harder to weld. It is used for gears, axles, and tools. Stainless Steel and Alloy Steels

Stainless steel is not just one material. It is a family of steels with at least 10.5% chromium. The chromium forms a thin oxide layer that resists rust. In Africa, stainless is used in food processing, hospitals, and coastal areas where salt eats ordinary steel. The most common grades are 304 and 316. 304 has 18% chromium and 8% nickel. It is good for kitchen sinks, dairy tanks, and architectural trim. 316 has 16% chromium, 10% nickel, and 2% molybdenum. It resists chlorides better, so it is used for marine parts, chemical tanks, and medical implants. 316 is more expensive but lasts longer in salt air..Trim()

Alloy steels are carbon steels with added elements like chromium, nickel, molybdenum, or vanadium to improve strength, toughness, or wear resistance. Examples include 4140 (chromoly), 4340, and tool steels like D2. 4140 is used for shafts, gears, and connecting rods. It has good strength and toughness. 4340 is used for aircraft landing gear and high-stress parts. D2 is used for cutting dies and knives because it wears well. These steels are harder to weld. They often need preheat and post-weld heat treatment..Trim()

Material Test Reports (MTRs)

A Material Test Report (MTR) is a document that tells you what your steel is made of. It is like a birth certificate for steel. It shows the chemical composition and mechanical properties. The chemical composition includes carbon, manganese, phosphorus, sulfur, silicon, and other elements. The mechanical properties include yield strength, tensile strength, elongation, and sometimes hardness. The MTR also shows the heat number, which traces the steel back to the mill. Without an MTR, you do not know if the steel is A36, 1045, or something else. In Africa, many suppliers do not provide MTRs. You must ask. If they cannot provide one, find another supplier or send a sample to a lab..Trim()

How to read an MTR: Look for the grade. It should say A36, 1018, 304, etc. Check the heat number. Check the yield strength. For A36, yield must be at least 250 MPa. For 1045, yield is around 530 MPa. Check the carbon content. A36 has 0.25% max carbon. 1045 has 0.45% carbon. High carbon makes welding difficult. Check the elongation. A36 has 20% min elongation. If elongation is low, the steel is brittle. Check the phosphorus and sulfur. They should be low (0.04% max for A36). High phosphorus and sulfur cause cracking..Trim()

Steel Surface Preparation

Before you weld, paint, or galvanize steel, you must prepare the surface. Dirt, rust, mill scale, oil, and paint cause poor welds and early corrosion. In Africa, where humidity and salt are high, surface preparation is critical. The first step is to remove oil and grease. Use a solvent like acetone or a strong detergent. Wipe with a clean rag. Do not use petrol or diesel; they leave a film. For light rust, use a wire brush or sandpaper. For heavy rust, use a grinder with a grinding wheel or a needle gun. Mill scale is the dark grey layer on new steel. It must be removed before welding because it causes porosity and lack of fusion. Use a grinder or sandblasting..Trim()

If you wait, rust will start again. For small jobs, use a grinder with a flap disc. A 115 mm angle grinder with a 40-grit flap disc is good. For corners, use a wire cup brush. Always wear safety glasses, gloves, and a dust mask..Trim()

Material Handling and Storage

Steel is heavy and can be dangerous. A 6-meter length of 50 x 50 x 5 mm angle weighs about 22 kg. A 6-meter length of 100 x 50 mm channel weighs about 56 kg. A 6-meter length of 200 x 100 mm I-beam weighs about 150 kg. You cannot lift these alone. Use a crane, forklift, or at least two people. In Africa, many workshops use manual handling. If you must lift, bend your knees, keep your back straight, and hold the steel close to your body. Wear gloves and steel-toe boots. Never lift steel over your head. Never stand under a suspended load..Trim()

Store steel off the ground. Use wooden sleepers or concrete blocks. Steel on the ground picks up moisture and rusts. Stack steel by size and grade. Do not mix stainless with carbon steel. Stainless will pick up carbon contamination and rust. Use plastic or wood separators. Store flat bar and angle on racks. Store pipe and round bar on racks with supports to prevent bending. Store plate vertically or on a flat rack. Do not stack plate on the ground. Use a rack with arms..Trim()

Reading and Interpreting Blueprints

A blueprint is the map of the steel piece you will build. In a typical African workshop, you might receive a hand-drawn sketch or a printed drawing. Learn to read three views: front, top, and side. Each line tells you something: thick solid lines are visible edges, dashed lines are hidden edges, and centre lines are dash-dot. Dimensions are usually in millimetres. For example, a bracket might be 150 mm long, 50 mm wide, and 6 mm thick. Look for the title block in the bottom right corner: it shows the part name, material (e.g., mild steel), and scale (1:1, 1:2, or 1:10). If the scale is 1:2, multiply every dimension by 2 to get real size. Check for notes like 'weld all around' or 'grind flush'. Always verify dimensions by adding them up: if a plate has two holes 40 mm from each end and is 200 mm long, the distance between holes is 120 mm. If the drawing is unclear, ask your supervisor before cutting. In many African workshops, blueprints are reused, so check for revisions. A good habit is to sketch the part on a piece of paper or on the steel itself, marking key points. This helps you catch mistakes early. Remember, a misread dimension can waste expensive steel. So take your time, use a ruler and pencil, and double-check every number..Trim()

Measuring Tools and Techniques

You need accurate measurements to avoid gaps and misalignment. The basic tools are a steel rule (150 mm or 300 mm), a measuring tape (3 m or 5 m), a try square, a combination square, and a vernier caliper for precise work. In Africa, you can buy these at hardware stores in cities like Lagos, Nairobi, or Johannesburg. Always keep your tape clean and avoid dropping it. When measuring, place the rule's zero mark exactly on the edge. For a 90-degree check, use a try square: press the stock against the edge and see if the blade touches the other side evenly. If light shows, the corner is not square. To measure the inside of a pipe, use a caliper or an inside micrometer. For long distances, use a tape with a helper to hold the end. A common mistake is reading from the 10 mm mark instead of zero; always check. When marking, use a scriber or soapstone, not a pencil, because pencil lines rub off. For repeated measurements, make a story stick: a straight piece of wood or steel with marks for each length. This speeds up work. In hot climates, metal expands, so measure early in the morning if precision matters. Finally, calibrate your tape by comparing it with a known standard, like a steel rule. If it's off by more than 0.5 mm over 1 m, replace it..Trim()

Layout for Cutting and Drilling

Layout means drawing the shape and hole positions on the steel before cutting or drilling. Start by cleaning the surface with a wire brush or rag. Use a scriber or soapstone to mark the outline. For cutting, you need a clear line: use a centre punch to mark hole centres, then scribe a circle around it. For a 10 mm hole, mark the centre with a punch, then use a divider set to 5 mm radius to scribe the circle. For straight cuts, use a steel rule and scriber to draw a line, then use a try square to ensure it's perpendicular. Add a cutting allowance: for oxy-fuel cutting, add 2-3 mm; for angle grinder, add 1-2 mm. So if you need a 100 mm plate, mark 102 mm. For drilling, mark the hole centres with a centre punch and a hammer. Use a dot punch for small holes. If you have multiple identical parts, make a template from thin plywood or steel. In African workshops, a common method is to use a chalk line for long cuts on sheets. For example, to cut a 2 m sheet, snap a chalk line, then cut with a grinder. Always double-check dimensions before cutting. After cutting, file the edges to remove burrs. Remember, layout is the most critical step: a mistake here wastes material and time. So measure twice, mark once..Trim()

Marking with Soapstone and Scribe

Soapstone and scribe are your main marking tools. Soapstone is a soft stone that leaves a white mark on steel. It's cheap and works well on dark or rusty surfaces. Use it for rough layout, like marking cut lines on a beam. A scribe is a sharp-pointed steel tool that scratches a fine line. Use it for precise work, like hole positions. To use soapstone, rub it on the steel to create a flat edge, then sharpen it to a point with a file. Hold it like a pencil and draw. For scribe, hold it at a slight angle and drag it along a straightedge. You can also use a scriber with a magnet to hold it. In Africa, soapstone is common in markets; a piece costs very little. Always keep your scribe sharp; a dull point gives a wide, inaccurate line. When marking, use a hammer and centre punch to make a small indentation at hole centres. This guides the drill bit. For curves, use a divider or trammel points. A trammel is a beam with two adjustable points; set the distance, then scribe an arc. For large circles, use a piece of string and a nail. After marking, go over the lines with a permanent marker if you need them to last. But remember, soapstone lines can be wiped off, so mark just before cutting. If you're working in bright sun, soapstone may be hard to see; use a scribe or a marker. Always clean the surface before marking to ensure the line is clear..Trim()

Templates and Jigs

A template is a pattern you use to mark many identical parts. A jig holds the workpiece and guides the tool. Both save time and ensure accuracy. For example, if you need 50 identical brackets, make a template from 3 mm plywood or aluminium. Trace the shape onto steel, then cut. For drilling, a drill jig is a plate with hardened bushings that guide the drill bit. In African workshops, you can make simple jigs from scrap steel. For a hole jig, weld a piece of flat bar with a hole to a clamp. For cutting, a straight edge clamped to the work acts as a jig for a grinder or torch. To make a template, first make one perfect part, then use it to mark others. Trace around it with a scriber. For holes, use a transfer punch: a punch that fits the hole and has a centre point to mark the mating part. When making a jig, ensure it is rigid and the bushings are replaceable. A common jig is a welding jig to hold parts at 90 degrees. Use angle iron and clamps. Templates and jigs reduce errors and speed up production. They are essential for repetitive work, like making window frames or gates. In many African metal shops, jigs are made from wood or steel, whatever is available. Always check your template after a few uses; it can wear. Store templates flat to prevent warping..Trim()

Tolerance and Allowances

Tolerance is the acceptable variation in a dimension. Allowance is the extra material you leave for machining or fitting. For example, if a shaft must fit a hole, you might make the shaft 0.05 mm smaller than the hole. Press Brake Operations

A press brake is a machine that bends metal sheets by pressing them between a punch and a die. In Africa, many small workshops use manual press brakes, but CNC press brakes are becoming more common. Before you start, check the machine's tonnage. For example, bending a 2 mm mild steel sheet 1 meter long requires about 20 tons. Always wear gloves and safety glasses. Set the back gauge to the bend line. The back gauge is a stop that positions the sheet. For a 90-degree bend, use a 88-degree punch and die set to allow for springback. The die opening should be 8 times the sheet thickness. So for 2 mm sheet, use a 16 mm die. Clamp the sheet firmly. Step on the pedal to bring the punch down. The bend happens quickly. Release the pedal and remove the part. For multiple bends, use a stop or a fixture to keep consistency. In hot climates, metal can expand; work in the morning when it's cooler. Always check the first piece with a square and protractor. Adjust the ram if needed. Common mistakes include using the wrong die, not accounting for springback, and bending against the grain. Practice on scrap metal first. Keep your hands clear of the punch area. Never reach into the machine while it's running. Regular maintenance includes checking hydraulic fluid and lubricating the guides. With practice, you can bend complex shapes like boxes and channels. Remember, safety first: never operate a press brake alone, and always have a foot pedal guard..Trim()

Roll Bending and Plate Rolling

Roll bending, also called plate rolling, shapes metal into curves using a machine with three or four rolls. This is how tanks, pipes, and curved roofs are made. Hot and Cold Forming

Hot forming and cold forming are two ways to shape steel. Cold forming is done at room temperature. It includes bending, rolling, and pressing. It is faster and gives a better surface finish, but it requires more force and can cause cracking in thick or brittle steel. Hot forming involves heating the steel to a high temperature, usually between 800Â°C and 1200Â°C, making it easier to shape. In Africa, blacksmiths have used hot forming for centuries. Springback and Compensation

Springback is the tendency of metal to return partially to its original shape after bending. This happens because steel is elastic. When you bend a sheet to 90 degrees, it may spring back to 92 or 93 degrees. To get an accurate bend, you must compensate for springback. The amount of springback depends on the material, thickness, and bend radius. For mild steel, springback is about 1 to 2 degrees. For stainless steel, it can be 3 to 5 degrees or more. To compensate, you can overbend. For example, if you need a 90-degree bend and springback is 2 degrees, set the press brake to bend to 88 degrees. The metal will spring back to 90. Another method is to use a bottoming die, where the punch forces the metal into the die, reducing springback. For roll bending, springback means the rolled cylinder will open up slightly. To compensate, roll the plate a little tighter than needed. For pipe bending, springback causes the bend angle to be less than desired. You can overbend by a few degrees. A simple way to test springback is to bend a sample piece and measure the angle after release. Do this for each material and thickness. In Africa, where material may vary, always test. Use a protractor or angle gauge. For critical bends, you can use a hydraulic press with a gauge to control the depth. Remember, springback increases with higher strength steel. If you are bending aluminum, springback is less. Practice on scrap. Keep a record of springback for common jobs. This saves time and material. Never assume; always measure. With experience, you can predict springback and adjust your machine settings. This is a key skill for any fabricator..Trim()

Forming Defects and Remedies

Forming defects can ruin your work and waste material. Common defects include cracking, wrinkling, springback, and distortion. Cracking happens when the metal is bent too sharply or when it is brittle. To fix, increase the bend radius or anneal the metal first. To prevent, use a mandrel or fill the pipe with sand. For plate rolling, wrinkling can happen if the rolls are not parallel. Adjust the rolls and re-roll. Springback we discussed earlier; compensate by overbending. Distortion is when the part twists or warps after bending. This can happen due to uneven heating or cooling in hot forming, or due to stress in cold forming. To remedy, stress-relieve the part by heating it to 600Â°C and cooling slowly. Another defect is orange peel, where the surface becomes rough like an orange. This happens when bending a coarse-grained material. Use a finer-grained material or anneal. In Africa, where materials may be recycled or of unknown quality, defects are common. Always test a sample before production. Use the right tool for the job. Keep your dies and rolls clean and lubricated. If a defect occurs, don't hide it; analyze the cause. Check the material, the machine settings, and the technique. Sometimes, a simple adjustment like slowing down the bend speed can help. For cracking, you can also grind a small radius on the bend line. Remember, prevention is better than cure. Fixture and Positioner Setup

When you assemble steel, you need to hold parts in the right place before welding. That's what fixtures and positioners do. A fixture is any device that holds parts securely during assembly. Alignment and Squaring

Alignment means making sure parts are in the correct position relative to each other. Squaring means ensuring corners are at 90 degrees. In steel fabrication, misalignment can cause big problems. A gate that is out of square won't close properly. A frame that is twisted will be difficult to weld and may fail. To check alignment, use a tape measure, a spirit level, and a plumb bob. For squaring, measure the diagonals of a rectangular frame. If the diagonals are equal, the frame is square. For example, if you're building a 2 m x 1 m frame, both diagonals should be 2.236 m (using Pythagoras). Measure from corner to corner. If one diagonal is longer, the frame is out of square. To correct it, use a come-along or a port-a-power to pull the long diagonal shorter. Alternatively, use a heavy hammer to adjust the corners. Always check square before tacking. In many African workshops, a simple water level (a clear plastic tube filled with water) works well for leveling over long distances. For vertical alignment, use a plumb bob or a spirit level. Tolerance is the allowable variation in dimensions. In steel fabrication, you can't be perfect, but you must stay within acceptable limits. For angles, use a digital protractor or a combination square. Always calibrate your tools. A tape measure can stretch or have a bent hook. Check it against a known standard. Bolted connections are used when you need to assemble on site or when welding is not practical. Bearing bolts transfer load through the bolt shank bearing against the hole. Friction grip bolts clamp the plates together so load is transferred by friction. Riveting and Adhesive Bonding

Riveting and adhesive bonding are alternative joining methods to welding and bolting. Riveting was once common in steel structures but is now mostly used in sheet metal and aerospace. However, in some African workshops, riveting is still used for repairs or for thin-gauge work. There are two main types: solid rivets and blind rivets (pop rivets). Solid rivets require access to both sides and are hammered or squeezed. Blind rivets can be installed from one side using a rivet gun. For steel fabrication, use steel or stainless steel rivets. Aluminum rivets are weaker. To install a blind rivet, drill a hole the same diameter as the rivet. Insert the rivet into the hole and use a rivet gun to pull the mandrel. The mandrel snaps off, leaving the rivet head. Rivets are good for vibration resistance but not as strong as bolts. For adhesive bonding, you use structural adhesives like epoxy or acrylic. These are useful for joining dissimilar materials or for sealing. Surface preparation is critical. Clean the surfaces with a degreaser, then abrade with sandpaper or a grinder. Apply the adhesive according to the manufacturer's instructions. Clamp the parts together and leave to cure. Cure time can be from minutes to hours depending on the adhesive. But it requires clean conditions and often heat for curing. In a typical workshop, you can use a heat gun or oven. For riveting, you need a rivet gun, drill, and rivets. For adhesive bonding, you need clamps, mixing sticks, and applicator guns. Both methods have their place. Riveting is fast and doesn't require skilled welders. Adhesive bonding can join thin sheets without distortion. However, neither is a substitute for welding in high-load structural applications. Use them for non-critical joints, panels, or repairs. Always test a sample joint before full assembly. And remember, safety: rivet guns can be dangerous, and adhesives can be toxic. Wear gloves and eye protection. Module 8 · Finishing and Surface Treatment

Grinding and Surface Preparation

Before you paint or coat steel, you must prepare the surface. Dirt, rust, mill scale, and oil will make any finish fail. Start by inspecting the steel. Look for rust (red or brown), mill scale (dark grey flaky layer), weld spatter, and sharp edges. Use a wire brush or scraper to remove loose rust and dirt. For heavier rust, use a chipping hammer. Then, use an angle grinder with a grinding wheel (typically 115mm or 230mm diameter) to grind down welds, smooth edges, and remove mill scale. Hold the grinder at a 15-30 degree angle and move it steadily. Don't press too hard; let the tool do the work. For corners and tight spots, use a die grinder or a file. After grinding, switch to a sanding disc (40-80 grit) to smooth the surface. Finally, clean the surface with a solvent like acetone or methylated spirits to remove oil and dust. In Africa, where humidity and heat can be high, work in the shade and avoid leaving bare steel overnight. A properly prepared surface should look uniformly grey or white metal, with no shiny spots (which indicate oil) and no dark patches (rust). This step takes time but ensures your coating lasts for years..Trim()

Shot Blasting and Sandblasting

Shot blasting and sandblasting are methods to clean steel by firing abrasive particles at high speed. Sandblasting uses silica sand, while shot blasting uses steel shot or grit. Both remove rust, mill scale, and old paint, leaving a rough profile that helps coatings stick. In Africa, sandblasting is common but dangerous: silica dust causes silicosis. Always wear a respirator, goggles, and heavy gloves. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. For small jobs, a portable sandblaster (like a pressure pot) works with a compressor delivering at least 100 PSI. Hold the nozzle 300-500mm from the surface and move in steady passes. For large structures, use a shot blasting machine with a closed cabinet or a centrifugal wheel system. After blasting, the steel should be a uniform grey-white. Blast to SA 2.5 or SA 3 standard (near-white or white metal). Immediately apply a primer within 4 hours to prevent flash rust, especially in humid coastal areas like Mombasa or Lagos. If you don't have equipment, you can hire a mobile blasting service. Remember: never use sand from a river or beach without testing for salt; salt causes rust. Use clean, dry abrasive. Safety first: no blasting near people or animals..Trim()

Painting and Coating Systems

Painting steel is not just about colour; it's about protection. A good coating system has three parts: primer, intermediate coat, and topcoat. Primer sticks to the steel and prevents rust. Use a zinc-rich primer for harsh environments, or an epoxy primer for general use. Apply by brush, roller, or spray. For brush, use a 50mm or 75mm natural bristle brush. For spray, use a conventional or airless sprayer. Mix paint thoroughly and thin only as recommended. Apply the primer at 75-100 microns dry film thickness (DFT). Let it dry (usually 4-6 hours at 25Â°C). Then apply an intermediate coat, often epoxy or polyurethane, at 100-150 microns DFT. Finally, the topcoat (polyurethane or enamel) gives colour and UV resistance, at 50-75 microns DFT. Total DFT should be 200-300 microns. In Africa, use paints that resist heat and humidity. Avoid painting in direct sun or when humidity is above 85%. Check with a wet film gauge and dry film gauge. Clean tools with thinner. Store paint away from heat. If you see runs, sags, or brush marks, sand and recoat. A good paint job lasts 5-10 years..Trim()

Galvanizing and Metalizing

Galvanizing coats steel with zinc to prevent rust. There are two main types: hot-dip galvanizing and metalizing (also called zinc spraying). Hot-dip galvanizing involves dipping prepared steel into molten zinc at about 450Â°C. The zinc reacts with the steel to form a tough alloy layer. This is done in a factory, so you must send your steel to a galvanizing plant. The coating is typically 50-100 microns thick and lasts 20-50 years, even in coastal areas. For large structures like transmission towers or boat trailers, hot-dip is best. Metalizing, on the other hand, can be done on-site. You use a wire arc or flame spray gun to melt zinc wire and spray it onto the steel. The surface must be blasted to SA 2.5 first. The zinc coating is 100-200 microns thick, but it's porous, so you must seal it with a thin coat of epoxy or polyurethane. Metalizing is good for repairs or for very large objects that can't be dipped. In Africa, hot-dip galvanizing plants are in major cities like Johannesburg, Nairobi, and Lagos. Metalizing equipment is portable but expensive. Both methods require safety gear: respirator, gloves, and face shield. Never weld galvanized steel without removing the zinc first; the fumes are toxic..Trim()

Powder Coating

Powder coating is a dry finishing process. Instead of liquid paint, you spray electrostatically charged powder onto a grounded metal part. The powder sticks, then you bake it in an oven at 180-200Â°C for 10-20 minutes. The powder melts and forms a smooth, hard film. Powder coating is tougher than wet paint and comes in many colours. It's great for furniture, fencing, and automotive parts. To powder coat, first clean and pretreat the steel. Blast or phosphate the surface. Then hang the part on a conveyor or rack. Use a powder spray gun (electrostatic) to apply the powder. Aim for 60-100 microns thickness. Too thin and you'll see bare spots; too thick and it may orange-peel. After spraying, cure in an oven. In Africa, powder coating is common in cities like Cairo, Durban, and Accra. Small shops use batch ovens; large factories use conveyor lines. If you don't have an oven, you can't powder coat properly. Some try to use a heat gun, but results are poor. Safety: wear a dust mask and avoid breathing powder. Keep the area clean; powder is flammable. Powder coating is not good for parts that will be welded later; the coating will burn. Also, it's hard to touch up; you need to re-coat the whole part..Trim()

Quality Inspection of Finishes

After finishing, you must inspect the coating to ensure it will protect the steel. Start with a visual check. Look for runs, sags, drips, brush marks, holidays (missed spots), and foreign particles. For adhesion, use a cross-hatch or pull-off test. Cross-hatch: cut a grid with a knife, apply tape, and pull. If paint comes off, adhesion is poor. Pull-off: glue a dolly, pull with a gauge, and note the force. For galvanizing, check thickness with a magnetic gauge and look for bare spots. For powder coating, check for orange peel, pinholes, and colour match. Also check for cure: wipe with MEK (methyl ethyl ketone) â if the coating softens, it's undercured. Document all results. In Africa, humidity and heat can affect curing, so test often. If a coating fails, you must remove it and redo. Quality inspection saves money in the long run..Trim()

Estimating and Costing

Estimating and costing is how you figure out what to charge for a steel fabrication job so you make a profit and don't go broke. Start by measuring the job. For a simple gate, measure width, height, and count bars. If you use 20mm square tubing, each meter weighs about 1.2 kg. Steel costs around $1.20 per kg in many African cities. So a 5-meter gate frame using 12 meters of tubing needs 14.4 kg, costing about $17. Add hinges, paint, and consumables like welding rods and grinding discs. A 5kg box of rods costs $8 and you might use half. Paint: 1 liter covers 5 square meters and costs $6. A gate has about 3 square meters of surface, so $4 of paint. Now labor: if you pay a helper $10 per day and the job takes 2 days, that's $20. Add your own time at $15 per day for 2 days = $30. Overhead: electricity for welder, rent, wear on tools. Estimate 10% of material and labor. Total material: $17 + $4 + $4 = $25. Labor: $50. Overhead: $7.50. Total cost: $82.50. Now add profit. A 30% markup gives $107.25. Round to $110. Always add 10% contingency for mistakes or price changes. Keep a notebook of all costs per job. After the job, compare actual to estimate. If you spent more on grinding discs, adjust next time. In Africa, steel prices change often due to imports. Call your supplier for current prices before quoting. Never quote from memory. Use a simple calculator and write everything down. For bigger jobs like staircases, break into sections: stringers, treads, handrails. Estimate each separately. Remember transport: hiring a truck to deliver the gate might cost $20. Include that. If you forget, you lose profit. Also, charge for design time if you draw plans. A good estimate wins the job and keeps you in business..Trim()

Production Planning and Scheduling

Production planning means deciding what work to do, in what order, and when, so you meet deadlines without chaos. Start with a job list. Write each project, its deadline, and how many hours it needs. Shop Safety and OSHA Regulations

Shop safety is not optional. In steel fabrication, you face burns, cuts, eye injuries, and electric shock. OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration) sets rules that many countries adopt. Even if your country doesn't enforce them, follow them to stay alive and productive. First, personal protective equipment (PPE). Always wear safety glasses when grinding, cutting, or chipping. A grinding disc can shatter and blind you. Wear a welding helmet with at least shade 10 for arc welding. Wear leather gloves when handling steel. Wear steel-toe boots. Wear ear plugs when grinding for long periods. Wear a respirator when painting or welding galvanized steel (which releases toxic zinc fumes). Second, fire safety. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry chemical, ABC type) within 10 meters of welding. Never weld near paint, thinners, or gas cylinders. Keep a bucket of sand or water for small fires. Third, electrical safety. Inspect welder cables for cracks. Never weld in wet conditions. Use a ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) if possible. Turn off welder when changing electrodes. Fourth, machine safety. Use guards on grinders. Never remove the guard. Clamp workpieces securely. Keep hands away from the blade. Fifth, housekeeping. Keep floors dry and free of scrap. Trip hazards cause falls. Store gas cylinders upright and chained. Sixth, ventilation. Weld in a well-ventilated area or use a fume extractor. Manganese fumes cause Parkinson's-like symptoms. Seventh, training. Every worker must know where the first aid kit is and how to use it. Have a first aid kit with burn gel, bandages, and eye wash. Eighth, emergency plan. Know the nearest clinic and have a phone number. In Africa, many shops lack formal OSHA enforcement, but you can self-regulate. Post safety rules on the wall. Hold a 5-minute safety talk every Monday. Lead by example: wear your PPE. If you see unsafe act, stop it. A safe shop has fewer accidents, less downtime, and better morale..Trim()
