History of barbering in Africa: From traditional grooming to modern shops

In many African communities, grooming was never just about hair. Before colonial borders, barbers were often the same people who performed scarification, healed minor wounds, and prepared bodies for burial. In West Africa, the Yoruba had specialised guilds of hairdressers called 'Onidiri' who used combs carved from bone and razors made from sharpened iron. In East Africa, Maasai warriors used ochre and animal fat to style their hair, with elders shaping locks using wooden clips. These were not 'barbershops' but community stations for identity, status, and hygiene..Trim()

.Trim()

When European colonial rule and later independence brought new tools, the trade shifted. By the 1950s, clippers and straight razors arrived in cities like Lagos, Nairobi, and Accra. Young men who had learned traditional cutting began opening small kiosks near markets. The 1980s and 1990s saw a boom: electric clippers became affordable, and barbershops became social hubs for men to discuss politics, football, and business. Today, a barber in Kibera or Soweto might cut 20 heads a day using a Wahl clipper and a Kinyozi chair..Trim()

.Trim()

Modern African barbering is a blend of old and new. You still see traditional razors for shape-ups, but also digital tools like trimmers with 0.5mm guards. The best barbers respect the history: they know that a clean shave was once a rite of passage. They also know that their shop is a safe space. In many towns, the barber is the first to hear about a new job, a wedding, or a funeral. That trust is your foundation..Trim()

.Trim()

Your business starts with understanding this legacy. You are not just cutting hair; you are continuing a craft that built communities. When you greet a client, you are joining a line of African groomers who served chiefs, warriors, and workers. That history gives you pride and a responsibility to keep standards high..Trim()

A barber in an African community wears many hats. First, you are a technician: you shape hair, beards, and hairlines with precision. But you are also a confidant, a counsellor, and sometimes a mediator. In many towns, the barbershop is where men go to talk about problems they cannot discuss at home. Keep your fingernails short and scrubbed. If you have a beard, keep it trimmed. Clients notice. In a hot African climate, you will sweat. Keep a small towel and a change of shirt in the shop. Health and safety: Preventing skin infections and bloodborne diseases

As a barber, you face real health risks. Clients can carry ringworm, impetigo, lice, scabies, and even HIV or hepatitis B. Your job is to prevent transmission. First, know the signs. Ringworm looks like a circular, scaly patch on the scalp. Impetigo causes red sores that ooze. If you see these, do not cut. Politely tell the client to see a clinic. Offer to reschedule after treatment. Do not feel bad; you are protecting yourself and others..Trim()

.Trim()

Bloodborne diseases are the most serious. HIV and hepatitis B can spread through razor cuts. Always use a new blade for every shave. Never reuse a blade, even on the same client. If you accidentally cut a client, stop immediately. Put on gloves, clean the wound with antiseptic, and cover it. Then disinfect all tools that touched blood. If you cut yourself, wash the wound with soap and water, then apply an antiseptic. Go for HIV testing within 72 hours if you suspect exposure. Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is available at many clinics in Africa and can prevent infection if started early..Trim()

.Trim()

Use barriers. Wear disposable gloves when you have any cuts on your hands. For shaving, use a fresh blade and a clean towel per client. Never share towels. Wash towels in hot water with detergent and dry them in the sun. The sun's UV rays kill many germs. For clippers, use a blade guard and disinfect between clients. Keep a sharps container for used blades. A simple plastic bottle with a screw cap works. When full, seal it and take it to a health centre for disposal..Trim()

.Trim()

Educate your clients. Tell them that a clean barber is a safe barber. Show them you use a new blade. Let them see you disinfect your clippers. This builds confidence. Also, get vaccinated against hepatitis B. The vaccine is safe and often free at public clinics. In Kenya, the hepatitis B vaccine is part of the routine immunisation schedule. If you are not vaccinated, go this week. Your health is your business..Trim()

Separating personal and business finances

Many barbers in Africa mix their personal money with the shop's money. They take cash from the till for groceries, pay for clippers from the same pocket, and at the end of the month, they don't know if the business made a profit. This is the first habit to break. Open a separate bank account for your barbershop. If you don't have a registered business, you can still open a second personal account and use it only for business. In Kenya, M-Pesa has a 'Business' option where you can register a till number; in Nigeria, use a separate bank account with your business name. The goal is simple: all income from clients goes into that account, and all business expenses come out of it. Pay yourself a fixed salaryâsay, 30% of profitâinto your personal account. This makes it clear if the shop is actually making money. Use a notebook or a free app like Wave or Keeper to record every transaction. At the end of each day, count the cash, compare it to your records, and deposit the business portion. This discipline will save you from unexpected tax problems and help you see where your money goes. When you apply for a loan or grant later, lenders will ask for business records; without separation, you have none. Start today: open that account, deposit today's earnings, and pay yourself a small salary. It's the foundation of growth..Trim()

Budgeting for supplies and overheads

A budget is just a plan for your money. For a barbershop, your main costs are supplies (clippers, blades, shaving cream, aftershave, capes, towels) and overheads (rent, electricity, water, airtime, transport). To budget, first track your spending for one month. Write down every single expenseâeven a 50 naira recharge card. At the end of the month, add up each category. For example, you might find you spend 20,000 naira on blades, 15,000 on shaving cream, 10,000 on electricity, and 50,000 on rent. Now set targets: next month, buy blades in bulk from a wholesaler to get a discountâmaybe 10% off. For overheads, negotiate rent if you pay quarterly, or find a shop with lower rent but good foot traffic. Use a simple envelope system: label envelopes for 'Supplies', 'Rent', 'Electricity', 'Savings', and put the budgeted cash in each. When an envelope is empty, you stop spending in that category. For digital payments, use a budgeting app like Mint or a spreadsheet on your phone. Review your budget every month. If you overspend on supplies, figure out whyâmaybe you're losing blades too fast because you're not cleaning them properly. Adjust next month. A good rule: aim to keep supplies under 15% of your revenue and overheads under 30%. That leaves 55% for profit and savings. This simple habit will make your shop stable and ready to grow..Trim()

Saving and reinvesting profits

Profit is what's left after you pay all expenses and yourself a salary. Many barbers spend all profit on personal wants, leaving nothing for the business. To grow, you must save a portion and reinvest. A common rule is 50/30/20: 50% of profit reinvested in the business, 30% saved for emergencies, 20% for personal rewards. Reinvesting can mean upgrading equipment: a professional Wahl clipper costs about 50,000 naira but lasts years and gives cleaner cuts. Or you could buy a generator to avoid power cuts. Track your return on investment: if you buy a new clipper for 50,000 and it helps you serve 5 more clients per day at 500 naira each, that's 2,500 extra per dayâyou recover the cost in 20 days. Savings protect you from emergencies like a broken chair or sudden rent increase. Without savings, you might borrow at high interest. Accessing loans and grants for small businesses

To grow, you may need extra money for equipment, a new shop, or training. Loans and grants are two options. Grants are free money you don't repay, but they are competitive.
