After your adult flies mate in the cage, females will lay eggs in the small holes of a cardboard egg-carton or a piece of corrugated plastic. You need to collect these eggs every 24 hours. Module 6 · Waste Management and Substrate Formulation

Types of Organic Waste for BSF

Black soldier fly larvae (BSFL) eat a wide range of organic waste, but not all waste is equal. In Africa, the best waste streams are those that are consistently available, free or cheap, and safe for the larvae. Common examples include market waste (rotten tomatoes, mango peels, banana skins, cabbage leaves), kitchen waste from hotels and restaurants (rice, ugali, beans, fish bones, meat scraps), and agro-industrial by-products (brewery waste, palm kernel cake, cottonseed meal, molasses). Animal manure from pigs, poultry, and cattle is also excellent, but it must be fresh and not mixed with sawdust or bedding. Avoid waste that is high in salt, oil, or soap, such as leftover fries or dishwater. Also avoid citrus peels in large amounts because the limonene can slow larval growth. A good rule: if it rots and smells like food, BSFL can probably eat it. But test small batches first. .Trim()

.Trim()

Collect waste daily to prevent it from rotting too much. In many African cities, you can partner with markets, hotels, and food processors to collect their waste for free or for a small fee. For example, a market in Accra or Nairobi may produce 500 kg of fruit and vegetable waste per day. You can collect it in 50 kg sacks or 200-liter drums. Use a wheelbarrow or pickup truck for transport. Always wear gloves and boots when handling waste. .Trim()

.Trim()

Before feeding, sort out plastics, glass, metal, and other non-organic items. A simple sorting table with a mesh bottom helps. Also remove any large bones or hard seeds that larvae cannot chew. For manure, mix it with other waste to reduce moisture and odor. A good starter mix is 70% fruit and vegetable waste, 20% manure, and 10% brewery waste. This gives a balanced diet. .Trim()

.Trim()

Start with a small trial: feed 10 kg of waste to 1 kg of larvae. Observe how fast they eat and if they seem healthy. If they avoid certain waste, remove it. Keep records of what you feed and how much. Over time, you will learn which local waste works best. Remember, consistency is key: larvae grow best when they get the same type of feed regularly..Trim()

Pre-processing Waste for Feeding

Fresh waste is too big and too wet for young larvae. Pre-processing makes it easier for them to eat and reduces odors and pests. The main steps are: sorting, chopping, draining, and sometimes fermenting. .Trim()

.Trim()

First, sort out non-organic materials. Use a sorting table or a clean tarp. Remove plastics, glass, metals, and any toxic items like batteries. Then chop the waste into small pieces, about 1-2 cm. You can use a machete, a manual chaff cutter, or a motorized shredder. A shredder costs about $200-$500, but a machete works fine for small-scale. Chop hard items like potato peels, maize cobs, and bones. .Trim()

.Trim()

Next, drain excess liquid. Waste that is too wet (above 70% moisture) can drown larvae and cause bad smells. Spread the chopped waste on a slanted concrete slab or a mesh screen to let water drip out. You can also mix in dry materials like rice husks, sawdust, or shredded paper to absorb moisture. Aim for a moisture level like a wrung-out sponge: when you squeeze a handful, only a few drops come out. .Trim()

.Trim()

For waste that is very smelly or likely to attract flies, ferment it for 2-3 days. Fermentation kills pathogens and makes the waste more digestible. To ferment, put chopped waste in a sealed drum or plastic bag. Add a handful of sugar or molasses to speed up the process. After 2-3 days, it will smell sour, not rotten. Then feed it to the larvae. .Trim()

.Trim()

If you are using manure, you must pre-process it too. Fresh manure can be too hot (high ammonia) and may contain harmful bacteria. Mix manure with equal parts of dry plant waste and let it compost for 1 week. Turn it every 2 days. This reduces ammonia and makes it safe for larvae. .Trim()

.Trim()

Always wear gloves and a mask when pre-processing waste. Wash your tools and hands after. A simple setup: a chopping board, a machete, a 200-liter drum for fermentation, and a mesh screen for draining. This can be set up for less than $50. With practice, you can pre-process 100 kg of waste in 1-2 hours..Trim()

Balancing Carbon and Nitrogen

Just like a garden compost pile, BSF larvae need a balanced diet of carbon (C) and nitrogen (N). Carbon comes from dry, fibrous materials like rice husks, sawdust, shredded paper, maize cobs, and dry leaves. Nitrogen comes from wet, protein-rich materials like fruit and vegetable waste, manure, fish scraps, and brewery waste. The ideal C:N ratio for BSF larvae is between 15:1 and 25:1. If the ratio is too high (too much carbon), larvae grow slowly because they lack protein. If it is too low (too much nitrogen), the waste becomes too wet, smells like ammonia, and can kill larvae. .Trim()

.Trim()

How do you know the ratio? You can estimate by volume. A simple rule: mix 3 parts wet waste (nitrogen) with 1 part dry waste (carbon) by volume. For example, for every 3 buckets of fruit and vegetable waste, add 1 bucket of rice husks or sawdust. This gives a C:N ratio around 20:1. If you are using manure, which is already balanced, add only a little dry material to control moisture. .Trim()

.Trim()

You can also use a simple test: squeeze a handful of the mixed substrate. If it drips more than a few drops, add more dry material. If it is crumbly and dry, add more wet waste. The mix should feel like a damp sponge. .Trim()

.Trim()

Different wastes have different C:N ratios. For example, fruit waste has a C:N of about 20:1, while poultry manure is about 10:1. Sawdust is about 500:1, so use it sparingly. A good starter mix: 50% fruit and vegetable waste, 30% manure, 20% brewery waste. This gives a balanced diet. .Trim()

.Trim()

If you notice larvae crawling out of the substrate, it may be too wet or too high in ammonia. Add dry material and mix well. If larvae are small and slow, add more nitrogen-rich waste like fish scraps or manure. Keep records of your mixes and larval growth. Over time, you will learn the right balance for your local waste. .Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, balancing C:N is not just about growth; it also reduces odors and pests. A well-balanced substrate ferments properly and does not attract houseflies. So take the time to mix your waste correctly..Trim()

Avoiding Toxic Substances

BSF larvae are tough, but certain substances can kill them or contaminate your harvest. The most common toxins are pesticides, heavy metals, and cleaning chemicals. In Africa, many farmers use pesticides on crops, and these residues can end up in market waste. For example, tomatoes sprayed with insecticides like cypermethrin can kill larvae. Always ask your waste supplier if they use chemicals. If you are not sure, wash the waste with clean water and let it drain before feeding. Or better, source from organic farms or known clean suppliers. .Trim()

.Trim()

Heavy metals like lead, mercury, and cadmium can come from batteries, old paint, and industrial waste. Never feed waste that contains these. Also avoid waste from tanneries, hospitals, and electronic waste dumps. These can contain pathogens and toxins that accumulate in larvae and then in the animals or people who eat them. .Trim()

.Trim()

Cleaning chemicals like bleach, soap, and disinfectants are also toxic. Do not pour bleach or soapy water into your larvae bins. If you clean your bins, rinse them well with clean water and let them dry before adding new waste. Also avoid waste that has been treated with lime or other preservatives. .Trim()

.Trim()

Some plants are toxic to BSF. For example, leaves of cassava, castor, and jatropha contain cyanide or other toxins. Do not feed these in large amounts. Small amounts may be okay, but it is safer to avoid them. Also avoid moldy waste, as molds can produce mycotoxins that harm larvae. .Trim()

.Trim()

If you suspect toxicity, you will see larvae dying or trying to escape the bin. Remove the waste immediately and replace it with fresh, safe waste. Wash the bin with clean water. If many larvae die, you may need to start a new colony. .Trim()

.Trim()

To be safe, always test a small batch of new waste before feeding it to your main colony. Put 100 larvae in a small container with the new waste. If they survive and grow for 3 days, it is safe. If they die, do not use that waste. This simple test can save your whole farm..Trim()

Reducing Odors and Pests

A well-managed BSF farm should not smell bad or attract pests. Odors come from anaerobic decomposition (when waste is too wet and compacted) and from overfeeding. Pests like houseflies, rats, and ants are attracted to rotting waste and spilled feed. Here is how to prevent both. .Trim()

.Trim()

First, control moisture. The substrate should be like a wrung-out sponge, not dripping wet. If it is too wet, add dry materials like rice husks, sawdust, or shredded paper. Mix well. Also, make sure your bins have drainage holes at the bottom to let excess liquid out. Collect the liquid (leachate) in a container and use it as fertilizer for plants. .Trim()

.Trim()

Second, do not overfeed. Only give as much waste as the larvae can eat in 1-2 days. If waste sits for more than 3 days, it will start to rot and smell. A good rule: 1 kg of larvae can eat about 2-3 kg of waste per day. So if you have 10 kg of larvae, feed 20-30 kg of waste daily. Adjust based on how fast they consume. .Trim()

.Trim()

Third, keep the area clean. Remove any spilled waste immediately. Keep the surrounding area free of weeds and tall grass, which harbor pests. Use a tarp or concrete floor under your bins to make cleaning easier. .Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, use physical barriers. For ants, place the legs of your bins in containers of water or apply used engine oil around the legs. For rats, use traps or keep a cat. For houseflies, use fly screens or netting over the bins. You can also hang fly traps. .Trim()

.Trim()

Fifth, consider using beneficial microbes. You can buy effective microorganisms (EM) or make your own from rice water and molasses. Spraying a diluted solution on the substrate can reduce odors and speed up decomposition. .Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, harvest regularly. When larvae are ready to pupate, they will crawl out of the substrate. Collect them and process them. Do not let them turn into flies inside the bin, as adults do not eat and will die, but they can attract other flies. .Trim()

.Trim()

If you follow these steps, your farm will be odor-free and pest-free. This is important for keeping good relations with neighbors and for producing clean, safe larvae..Trim()

Scaling Up Waste Processing

When your BSF colony grows, you will need to process more waste. Scaling up requires planning, space, and equipment. Start small and grow gradually. A good target: process 100 kg of waste per day within 6 months. .Trim()

.Trim()

First, secure a reliable waste supply. Talk to multiple sources: markets, hotels, restaurants, food processing plants, and farms. Aim for at least 3 sources so you are not dependent on one. Sign simple agreements if possible. For example, a hotel might give you 50 kg of food waste daily in exchange for a small fee or free larvae for their chickens. .Trim()

.Trim()

Second, design a waste processing area. You need space for sorting, chopping, draining, and fermenting. A concrete slab of 5m x 5m is enough for 100 kg/day. Build a shed with a roof to protect from rain and sun. Use a shaded area for fermentation drums. .Trim()

.Trim()

Third, invest in labor-saving tools. A motorized shredder (cost: $300-$800) can chop 100 kg of waste in 15 minutes. A cement mixer (cost: $200-$500) can mix waste and dry materials quickly. A wheelbarrow and shovels are essential. You may also need a small truck or tricycle for transport if you collect from far. .Trim()

.Trim()

Fourth, train workers. You will need 2-3 people for 100 kg/day: one to collect and sort, one to chop and mix, one to feed and monitor. Pay them fairly and provide gloves, boots, and masks. .Trim()

.Trim()

Fifth, manage your larvae production. To process 100 kg of waste per day, you need about 30-50 kg of larvae. You can produce this by having multiple breeding cages and nursery bins. Keep records of waste in and larvae out. .Trim()

.Trim()

Sixth, find markets for your products. Larvae can be sold as chicken, fish, or pig feed. Frass (larva manure) is a good fertilizer. Leachate can be sold as liquid fertilizer. .Trim()

.Trim()

Scaling up is a business. Keep track of costs and income. A simple notebook is fine. Reinvest profits into better equipment. With good planning, you can turn waste into wealth..Trim()

Determining Optimal Harvest Time

Harvesting black soldier fly larvae at the right time gives you the best quality and quantity. If you harvest too early, larvae are small and yield is low. If you wait too long, larvae turn into pupae or even adult flies, and they stop eating. The ideal time to harvest is when larvae are in their fifth instar (the last stage before pupation). At this stage, they are about 2 cm long, plump, and light cream to pale yellow in colour. They have stopped feeding and are looking for a dry place to pupate. In warm African climates (25â30Â°C), this stage is reached about 14 to 18 days after the eggs hatch. In cooler conditions, it may take up to 21 days. Check your larvae every day after day 12. Look for larvae that are crawling out of the feeding substrate and moving towards the edges or up the walls of your bin. This is a clear sign they are ready. Another sign is that the substrate becomes more liquid and smells less like rotting food, because the larvae have consumed most of it. You can also use a simple sieve to check size: if most larvae are longer than your fingernail (about 1.5â2 cm), they are ready. Do not wait for all larvae to become black or dark brown; those are prepupae and are also good for harvest, but they have less fat. For maximum protein and fat, harvest when they are still white or cream. Plan to harvest in the morning when it is cooler, as larvae are less active. Use a shovel or gloved hands to scoop out a handful and inspect. If you see many small larvae still, wait 2â3 more days. If you see many dark, hard cases (pupae), you have waited too long. Regular observation is key. Keep a notebook to record the date you first saw larvae and the date you harvest, so you can predict future harvests. In Africa, where temperatures are often high, development is faster, so check earlier than you might expect..Trim()

Separating Larvae from Substrate

After harvesting, you need to separate the larvae from the remaining substrate (frass, uneaten food, and bedding). This can be done in several ways, depending on your scale. For small-scale farmers (a few kilograms per day), the simplest method is to use a sieve. Get a sieve with a mesh size of about 2â3 mm. Put a handful of the harvested mixture into the sieve and shake it gently over a bucket. The frass and small particles fall through, while the larvae remain on top. You may need to pick out any large lumps of substrate by hand. Another method is water flotation. Put the mixture in a bucket of water. The larvae will float, while the heavier frass and food particles sink. Skim the larvae off the top with a strainer or your hands. This method is fast but uses water, and the larvae become wet, so you must dry them quickly to prevent spoilage. For medium-scale farmers (10â50 kg per day), a mechanical shaker or a rotating drum sieve can save time. You can build a simple shaker using a wooden frame and a mesh screen, powered by hand or a small motor. For large-scale operations, commercial separators are available, but they are expensive. A low-cost option is to use a cement mixer with a mesh screen inside; as it rotates, the frass falls out and larvae stay in. After separation, the frass is a valuable fertiliser. You can sell it or use it on your farm. Do not throw it away. The larvae should be collected in a clean container. If you used water flotation, spread the larvae on a screen or cloth to drain and dry for 30 minutes before further processing. Always wear gloves and a mask when separating, as the dust from frass can be irritating. Work in a well-ventilated area. If you are separating by hand, it can take 1â2 hours for 10 kg of mixture. With practice, you will get faster. Remember to clean your equipment after each use to prevent disease and odours..Trim()

After separating larvae from substrate, they are still dirty and alive. You must clean and kill them quickly to prevent them from pupating or spoiling. Cleaning removes any remaining frass, bacteria, and dirt. Killing stops enzymatic activity that causes spoilage and makes them safe to process. Start by rinsing the larvae in clean water. Put them in a large bowl or bucket and add enough water to cover them. Stir gently with your hand or a spoon. The larvae will sink, and dirt will float. Pour off the dirty water and repeat 2â3 times until the water runs clear. Do not leave them in water for more than 10 minutes, as they can drown and become mushy. After rinsing, you need to kill them. The most common method is blanching: bring a large pot of water to a rolling boil. Add the larvae and boil for 2â3 minutes. This kills them instantly and also cleans them further. Do not overcook, or they become tough and lose nutrients. Another method is freezing: spread the larvae on a tray and put them in a freezer for at least 4 hours. Freezing is slower but preserves nutrients well. However, freezing requires electricity, which may not be available in rural areas. A third method is sun-drying: spread larvae thinly on a mat and dry under strong sun for 2â3 days. This kills them slowly and may allow some to escape. For most African farmers, boiling is the best because it is quick, cheap, and does not require electricity. After boiling, drain the larvae in a colander and spread them on a clean cloth or screen to cool and dry. You can now proceed to drying or milling.
