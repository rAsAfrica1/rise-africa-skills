Module 3 · Scalding, Dehairing, and Singeing the Carcass

Heating water to the correct temperature for scalding

Scalding a pig is the key step that loosens the hair so it can be scraped off easily. The water must be hot enough to work but not so hot that it cooks the skin. The ideal temperature is between 60Â°C and 65Â°C (140Â°F to 149Â°F). If you don't have a thermometer, use the elbow test: dip your elbow into the water. It should feel very hot but not scalding. Another test is to dip a small area of the pig's back for a few seconds; if the hair comes off easily when you rub it, the temperature is right. If the water is too cool, the hair will be hard to remove. If it's too hot, the skin will turn pale and start to cook, making it tough and prone to tearing. .Trim()

.Trim()

To heat water, use a large pot or a metal drum over a fire or gas burner. For a whole pig, you'll need 50 to 100 litres of water, depending on the size of the pig. Start with clean water. Bring it to a boil, then let it cool slightly or mix with cold water to reach the right temperature. Stir the water well before testing. .Trim()

.Trim()

In many African settings, people use a large drum cut in half or a built-in concrete tank. If you're using a drum, make sure it's clean and free of rust. You can also add a little wood ash to the water; this helps break down the hair and makes scraping easier. But don't add too muchâabout one handful per 20 litres is enough. .Trim()

.Trim()

Always have a source of cold water nearby to adjust the temperature if it gets too hot. Keep a thermometer if possible, but learn the feel test. With practice, you'll know by touch. Remember, the goal is to scald, not boil. Work quickly once the pig is in the water, as the temperature will drop. If you're scalding more than one pig, reheat the water between animals. .Trim()

.Trim()

Safety note: Never leave the fire unattended, and keep children away from the hot water. Use gloves and long sleeves to protect yourself from splashes..Trim()

Immersion and scraping techniques for removing hair

Once the water is at the right temperature, it's time to immerse the pig. If you have a large enough container, lower the whole pig into the water. If not, you can scald one half at a time. Hold the pig by the legs or use ropes to lower it carefully. Make sure the water covers the area you want to scald. Leave the pig in the water for 2 to 5 minutes. The time depends on the pig's size and hair thickness. For a small pig, 2 minutes may be enough; for a large one, up to 5 minutes. .Trim()

.Trim()

After soaking, pull the pig out and place it on a clean surface, like a wooden pallet or a tarp. Immediately begin scraping the hair off with a sharp knife or a specialized scraper. A dull knife will tear the skin, so keep it sharp. Use a pulling motion, not pushing, to avoid cutting the skin. Scrape in the direction of the hair growth. You'll see the hair and a thin layer of dirt come off. Rinse the scraper often in clean water. .Trim()

.Trim()

Work quickly because the hair becomes harder to remove as the pig cools. If you're scalding half at a time, do the first half, then flip the pig and scald the other half. For hard-to-reach areas like the head, ears, and between the legs, use a smaller knife or even a spoon to scrape. Be careful around the eyes and snout. .Trim()

.Trim()

After scraping, check for any remaining hair. If there are patches, you can re-dip that area in hot water for a few seconds and scrape again. But don't over-scald. Once the hair is mostly off, rinse the pig with clean water to remove loose hair and dirt. .Trim()

.Trim()

In many African communities, people work together: one person holds the pig, another scrapes, and a third keeps the water hot. This teamwork makes the job faster. Remember to keep the work area clean and dispose of hair properly to avoid attracting flies..Trim()

Using a blowtorch or fire to singe remaining hair

After scraping, there will still be fine hairs and sometimes stubborn patches. Singeing removes these and also helps to kill bacteria on the skin. You can use a blowtorch, a gas torch, or even a fire made with dry grass or wood. If using a blowtorch, keep the flame at a medium setting and hold it about 15-20 cm away from the skin. Move the flame back and forth quickly so you don't burn the skin. You want to singe the hair, not cook the meat. .Trim()

.Trim()

If you don't have a blowtorch, you can use a fire. Build a small fire with dry grass or straw. Once it's burning well, pass the pig over the flames, turning it so all sides get exposed. Be careful not to let the pig catch fire. You can also use a metal sheet to reflect heat onto the pig. Another method is to use a hot iron rod or a piece of metal heated in the fire and run it over the skin. .Trim()

.Trim()

Singeing should be done in a well-ventilated area, as the burning hair produces a strong smell and smoke. Wear gloves and keep a bucket of water nearby in case of emergencies. After singeing, the skin will be blackened in spots. That's normal. You'll scrape off the burnt layer in the next step. .Trim()

.Trim()

In some African traditions, singeing is done with a blowtorch fueled by kerosene or gas. If you use a blowtorch, check the hose and connections for leaks before lighting. Never point the torch at yourself or others. Keep children away. .Trim()

.Trim()

Singeing not only removes hair but also gives the skin a nice golden color and a crispy texture after cooking. It's an important step for a clean, professional finish..Trim()

Scraping and washing the skin for a clean finish

After singeing, the pig's skin will have burnt hair and a black layer. You need to scrape this off to get a clean, white skin. Use a sharp knife or a metal scraper. Work on a clean surface. Scrape firmly but carefully, using short strokes. You'll see the black layer come off, revealing the clean skin underneath. Rinse the scraper often in water. .Trim()

.Trim()

For tough spots, you can dip the scraper in water or use a little ash as an abrasive. Ash also helps to remove any remaining hair and neutralizes odors. After scraping, wash the entire pig with clean water. You can use a brush to scrub the skin, especially in crevices like the armpits, groin, and between the toes. .Trim()

.Trim()

If you want an extra clean finish, you can wash the pig with a mixture of water and lime juice or vinegar. This helps to remove any lingering smell and brightens the skin. Rinse thoroughly with clean water. .Trim()

.Trim()

After washing, dry the pig with a clean cloth or let it air dry. The skin should be smooth and free of hair. Any remaining hair can be removed with a razor or a small knife. Check the skin for any cuts or bruises. .Trim()

.Trim()

In many African markets, a clean, well-singed pig fetches a better price. Take your time with this step. A clean carcass is not only more appealing but also safer to eat because dirt and bacteria are removed. .Trim()

Finally, inspect the skin for any wounds or abscesses. If you find any, cut them out and discard. Wash your hands and tools thoroughly after handling raw meat..Trim()

Removing hooves and lower legs

Removing the hooves and lower legs is done after the skin is cleaned. You can do this with a sharp knife, a meat saw, or a cleaver. First, locate the joint between the hoof and the leg. It's usually a visible line where the hair changes or where the leg narrows. Place the pig on its back or side. .Trim()

.Trim()

If using a knife, make a cut around the leg just above the hoof, cutting through the skin and tendons. Then bend the hoof backward to expose the joint. Cut through the joint with a knife or saw. For larger pigs, a saw is easier. If you have a cleaver, you can chop through the bone with a firm strike. .Trim()

.Trim()

After removing the hooves, you may want to remove the lower legs up to the knee or hock. This is optional but often done for easier handling. Cut through the joint at the knee. Again, use a saw or cleaver for the bone. .Trim()

.Trim()

In some African cultures, the hooves and lower legs are cleaned and cooked as delicacies, often in soups or stews. If you plan to use them, wash them thoroughly and remove any hair or dirt. You can singe them again if needed. .Trim()

.Trim()

If you're not using them, dispose of them properly. Don't leave them lying around, as they can attract flies and rodents. .Trim()

.Trim()

Always use a stable cutting surface, like a sturdy table or chopping block. Keep your knives sharp and cut away from your body. Wear gloves if you have them. After removing the hooves, wash the legs with clean water and continue with the next steps of processing. .Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, clean cuts make the carcass look better and reduce the risk of contamination..Trim()

Inspecting the skin for wounds or abscesses

Inspecting the skin is a crucial step for food safety. After the pig is scalded, scraped, and singed, look over the entire skin carefully. You're looking for wounds, abscesses, bruises, or any signs of disease. A healthy pig skin should be smooth, pale, and free of lumps or discharges. .Trim()

.Trim()

Start at the head and work your way down. Check the ears, eyes, snout, and mouth. Look for cuts, sores, or swelling. Then move to the neck, shoulders, back, and sides. Feel the skin with your hands; you might detect lumps that you can't see. Abscesses are pockets of pus that can be felt as soft or firm lumps under the skin. If you find one, cut it out with a clean knife, making sure to remove all the infected tissue. Discard it properly. .Trim()

.Trim()

Also check the belly, groin, and legs. Look for ticks, lice, or other parasites. If you see ticks, remove them with tweezers and dispose of them. Wash the area with disinfectant. .Trim()

.Trim()

If you find many abscesses or signs of serious disease, like widespread redness or foul smell, the meat may not be safe to eat. In that case, consult a veterinarian or discard the carcass. .Trim()

.Trim()

In African settings, where pigs may be raised in free-range conditions, wounds from fighting or accidents are common. Minor cuts can be trimmed away. But always err on the side of caution. .Trim()

.Trim()

After inspection, wash the carcass again with clean water. This step ensures that only healthy meat goes to market or to your family's table. It also protects you and your customers from foodborne illnesses. .Trim()

.Trim()

Keep records of any abnormalities you find, as this can help you improve your pig husbandry practices..Trim()

Module 4 · Evisceration: Removing Internal Organs Safely

Making the initial cut without piercing intestines

After you have bled and scalded the pig, hang it on a gambrel or sturdy branch with the head down. Use a sharp 6-inch boning knife or a skinning knife. Stand slightly to the side so you are not directly in front of the belly. Make a shallow cut from the anus down to the sternum, but only through the skin and the thin layer of fat. Do not cut deep. The intestines lie just under the abdominal wall, so a cut deeper than 1 cm can puncture them. Use the tip of the knife, not the whole blade. Hold the knife like a pencil for control. Start at the anus: cut around it in a circle, about 2 cm away from the opening, to free it from the surrounding skin. Then slit downward, using two fingers of your free hand to guide the blade and keep the intestines away. If you feel resistance, stop and use your fingers to push the guts back. A common mistake is to cut too fast. Work slowly. If you accidentally nick the intestines, the smell will be terrible and the meat can be contaminated. If that happens, immediately wash the area with plenty of clean water and cut away any soiled meat. In rural areas, many people use a machete, but that is dangerous because it is too big. A small, sharp knife is safer. Keep a bucket of clean water nearby to rinse your hands and the knife. After the initial cut, you should see the white membrane (peritoneum) covering the organs. You will cut through that next, but first make sure the skin is fully open from anus to sternum. This first cut sets the stage for the whole evisceration. Take your time..Trim()

Removing the digestive tract and separating edible organs

Once the belly is open, you will see the intestines, stomach, liver, heart, and lungs. Start by pulling the intestines out gently. They are long and coiled. Use both hands to lift them out and place them in a clean bucket or on a clean tarp. Do not pull hard or you will tear them. Next, locate the stomach. It is a large, sac-like organ on the left side. Cut it free at the esophagus and at the duodenum (the first part of the small intestine). Put the stomach with the intestines for later processing. Now find the liver. It is a large, dark red organ on the right side, just behind the diaphragm. Cut the ligaments that hold it to the diaphragm and the vena cava. Be careful not to cut into the gallbladder, a small green sac attached to the liver. If it breaks, wash the liver immediately with clean water. Set the liver aside. The heart is in the chest cavity, enclosed in a sac called the pericardium. Cut the sac open and pull the heart out. Cut the major blood vessels close to the heart. The kidneys are embedded in fat on the back wall of the abdomen. They are bean-shaped and dark red. Use your fingers to scoop them out, leaving the fat. The lungs are light pink and spongy. They are not usually eaten in many African communities, but some people use them for dog food. Cut them out and set aside for disposal. Separate the edible organs (liver, heart, kidneys) into one clean container, and the stomach and intestines into another for tripe. Keep everything clean and cool. Work quickly, especially in hot weather..Trim()

Cleaning and preserving the liver, heart, and kidneys

After you have removed the liver, heart, and kidneys, rinse them with clean water. Do not soak them in water for a long time, as that makes them lose flavour and nutrients. For the liver, cut away any large blood vessels or bile ducts. If the gallbladder was broken, wash the liver with a weak solution of salt water (1 tablespoon of salt per litre) to remove bitterness. Pat dry with a clean cloth. For the heart, cut it open and remove any blood clots from the inside. Rinse well. For the kidneys, cut them in half lengthwise and remove the white core (the ureter and blood vessels). Soak them in salt water for 30 minutes to reduce the strong urine smell, then rinse again. To preserve these organs, you can refrigerate them at 4Â°C for up to 3 days, or freeze them at -18Â°C for up to 3 months. If you have no electricity, you can preserve them by salting: rub them generously with coarse salt and hang them in a cool, dry, well-ventilated place for 2-3 days. Then store in a cloth bag. Another method is to cook them immediately into a stew or soup. In many African markets, fresh liver and kidneys are sold quickly, so if you plan to sell, keep them in a cooler with ice. Always use clean containers and wash your hands before handling cooked or raw organs. Never leave them at room temperature for more than 2 hours. Proper cleaning and preservation prevent food poisoning and waste..Trim()

Handling the stomach and intestines for tripe

Tripe is a popular food in many African countries, but it requires thorough cleaning. Start with the stomach. Cut it open lengthwise and empty out any undigested food. Rinse with plenty of water. Then turn it inside out. The inner lining has a rough texture. You can scrape it with a blunt knife or a piece of broken tile to remove the slimy layer. Some people use wood ash or lime (calcium hydroxide) to help loosen the slime. Rub the ash or a small amount of lime on the stomach, then rinse. Be careful with lime; it can burn your skin, so wear gloves. For the intestines, you need to separate the small intestine from the large intestine. The small intestine is thinner and more coiled. Cut them into manageable lengths (about 50 cm). To clean, you can use a stick or a long, thin bamboo to push the contents out, or you can squeeze the contents out by hand. Then rinse with running water. Turn the intestines inside out by using a thin stick to push one end through. Wash again with salt and lemon juice or vinegar to remove odour. Some people soak them in salt water for 1 hour. After cleaning, boil the stomach and intestines in water with salt, onions, and spices for 1-2 hours until tender. In West Africa, tripe is often cooked in a spicy pepper soup. In East Africa, it is fried with onions and tomatoes. Always clean tripe thoroughly; if not, it can cause diarrhoea. Store cleaned tripe in the fridge for up to 2 days or freeze for later use..Trim()

Splitting the carcass along the spine with a saw
