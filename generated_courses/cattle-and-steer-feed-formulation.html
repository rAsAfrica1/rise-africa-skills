Overview of beef cattle industry and feeding systems

In Africa, beef cattle are raised in three main systems: pastoral, mixed crop-livestock, and commercial feedlot. Pastoral systems dominate in the Sahel, Horn of Africa, and parts of East and Southern Africa, where cattle graze natural pastures and migrate with rainfall. Mixed systems are common in highlands like Ethiopia and Kenya, where cattle eat crop residues (maize stover, sorghum stalks) plus some grazing. In West Africa, feedlot operators often use cottonseed cake and urea-treated straw. .Trim()

Opportunities lie in improved forages (e.g., Brachiaria hybrids), crop residue treatment (urea or lime), and local feed mixing. As a feed formulator, your goal is to match nutrient supply to cattle needs at the lowest cost. Digestive anatomy and physiology of ruminants

Cattle are ruminants with a four-compartment stomach: rumen, reticulum, omasum, and abomasum. The rumen is a large fermentation vat (100â150 liters in mature cattle) containing billions of microbesâbacteria, protozoa, and fungi. These microbes break down fibrous feeds like grass and straw that humans cannot digest. The reticulum traps foreign objects and helps regurgitation. The omasum absorbs water and minerals. The abomasum is the true stomach, secreting acid and enzymes. .Trim()

When a cow eats, feed is chewed, swallowed, and enters the rumen. Microbes ferment carbohydrates into volatile fatty acids (VFAs)âacetate, propionate, and butyrateâwhich provide 70â80% of the cowâs energy. Protein is broken down to ammonia and microbial protein. The cow then regurgitates a bolus (cud), chews it again, and swallows. This rumination stimulates saliva production, which buffers rumen pH. A healthy rumen pH is 6.0â7.0. When cattle eat too much grain, pH drops (acidosis), causing off-feed, laminitis, and even death. .Trim()

Feed formulators must respect the rumen. Sudden diet changes disrupt microbes; always transition over 7â10 days. Provide effective fiber (long hay, straw) to stimulate chewing and saliva. Aim for 30â40% neutral detergent fiber (NDF) in the diet. Non-protein nitrogen (NPN) like urea can be used, but only up to 1% of dry matter, and never in low-energy diets. The rumen also synthesizes B vitamins and vitamin K, so these are rarely needed in feed. .Trim()

In Africa, where roughages are often low quality, understanding rumen function is critical. For example, urea-treated straw increases digestibility from 45% to 60% by providing ammonia to microbes. Nutrient requirements: energy, protein, minerals, vitamins

Cattle need six nutrient classes: water, carbohydrates, fats, proteins, minerals, and vitamins. Energy is the first limiting nutrient. It is measured in total digestible nutrients (TDN) or megajoules (MJ) of metabolizable energy. A 300 kg growing steer gaining 1 kg/day needs about 12 MJ ME per day. A 500 kg lactating cow needs 80â100 MJ ME. Energy comes from carbohydrates: sugars, starch, and fiber. In Africa, maize, sorghum, and molasses are common energy sources. .Trim()

Protein is measured as crude protein (CP). Rumen microbes need degradable protein (RDP) to grow; the cow needs undegradable protein (UDP) for muscle and milk. A growing steer needs 12â14% CP; a lactating cow 16â18%. Common protein meals: cottonseed cake (40% CP), soybean meal (44% CP), sunflower cake (30% CP), and fishmeal (60% CP). Salt (sodium chloride) is often used as a carrier. .Trim()

Feed quality and safety considerations

Feed quality determines animal performance and profitability. Key quality parameters: moisture (should be below 12% for grains, 15% for hay), crude protein, energy (TDN or ME), fiber (NDF and ADF), and ash. For roughages, color and smell indicate quality: good hay is green and sweet-smelling; moldy hay is brown and musty. For grains, test for broken kernels, foreign matter, and insects. In Africa, aflatoxin contamination in maize and groundnut cake is a major risk. Aflatoxin B1 above 20 ppb can cause liver damage, reduced growth, and milk residues. Using NRC and BR-CORTE tables for nutrient requirements

To feed cattle correctly, you need to know how much energy, protein, minerals, and vitamins they require each day. Two widely used references are the NRC (National Research Council) tables from the United States and the BR-CORTE tables from Brazil. Both provide requirements based on animal weight, age, sex, and expected growth rate. For example, a 250 kg growing steer gaining 1 kg per day may need 2.5 kg of TDN (total digestible nutrients) and 0.7 kg of crude protein according to NRC. BR-CORTE, designed for tropical cattle like Zebu, often gives slightly different values because it accounts for heat stress and lower-quality forages. In Africa, where many cattle are Zebu or crosses, BR-CORTE is often more accurate. To use these tables, first identify your animal's body weight and target daily gain. Then find the corresponding row in the table for energy (TDN or ME) and protein (CP or MP). Adjust for environmental factors like temperature if needed. For instance, if you are in a hot region like northern Kenya, increase energy requirements by 10-15% because cattle pant and sweat, using extra energy. Always use a scale or weigh tape to get accurate weights. If you don't have a scale, use a heart girth tape: measure the circumference just behind the front legs and the body length from shoulder to pin bone, then apply the formula: weight (kg) = (heart girth cm)^2 x body length cm / 10840. Once you have the requirements, compare them to the nutrient content of your available feeds. If your feed is deficient, you can add supplements like cottonseed cake or molasses. Remember, these tables are guides; observe your cattle's body condition and adjust. A body condition score of 3 (on a 1-5 scale) is ideal for breeding, while growing steers should be around 3.5. Keep records of feed intake and weight gain to fine-tune your rations. For smallholder farmers, the BR-CORTE tables are often more accessible and tailored to tropical breeds. You can find them online or in extension manuals. Always use the most recent version, as requirements are updated with new research..Trim()

Determining dry matter intake in cattle

Dry matter intake (DMI) is the amount of feed (excluding water) that a cow or steer eats in a day. It is the foundation of any ration because if the animal doesn't eat enough, it won't get the nutrients it needs, no matter how good the feed is. DMI is affected by body weight, feed quality, and environmental conditions. As a rule of thumb, cattle consume 2% to 3% of their body weight in dry matter daily. For example, a 300 kg steer on good quality forage might eat 6 to 9 kg of dry matter. However, if the forage is low quality (high fiber, low protein), intake drops to maybe 1.5% of body weight. To determine DMI accurately, you can use prediction equations. The NRC (2000) equation for growing cattle is: DMI (kg/day) = (Body weight^0.75 x (0.2435 x NEg - 0.0466 x NEg^2 - 0.1128)) / NEg, where NEg is net energy for gain in Mcal/kg. That's complex, so a simpler field method is to offer a known amount of feed and weigh the leftovers. For example, give 10 kg of hay and if 2 kg is left, DMI is 8 kg. But this only works if you can weigh feed. In Africa, many farmers use visual estimates: a standard 20-litre bucket holds about 5 kg of chopped maize stover, but this varies. Better to use a hanging scale. Another method is to use the 'fill unit' system: 1 kg of concentrate equals 1 fill unit, while 1 kg of straw equals 3 fill units. A 300 kg steer can consume about 8 fill units per day. So if you feed 2 kg of concentrate (2 fill units), you can feed 2 kg of straw (6 fill units) to reach 8. This helps balance rations. Environmental heat also reduces DMI: for every 1Â°C above 25Â°C, intake drops by about 0.5%. So in hot climates, feed during cooler hours (early morning, evening) to maximize intake. Also, ensure fresh water is always available; cattle drink 3-5 litres per kg of dry matter consumed. High-protein feeds and good palatability increase intake. Monitor body condition and adjust. If cattle are losing weight, they are not eating enough. Use a simple record sheet to track daily feed offered and refusals. Over time, you'll learn your herd's typical intake. Remember, DMI is the first thing to check when formulating a ration: if you formulate for 8 kg DMI but the animal only eats 6 kg, the nutrient density must be higher. So always start with a realistic DMI estimate..Trim()

Energy systems: TDN, ME, NE for maintenance and gain

Energy is the most important nutrient for cattle, driving growth, milk production, and body maintenance. In feed formulation, we use three main energy systems: TDN (Total Digestible Nutrients), ME (Metabolizable Energy), and NE (Net Energy). TDN is the simplest and most common in Africa. It is expressed as a percentage or in kg. For example, maize grain has about 80% TDN, while maize stover has only 50%. A 250 kg steer gaining 1 kg/day needs about 2.5 kg of TDN per day. TDN is calculated from digestible crude protein, fiber, nitrogen-free extract, and fat (fat is multiplied by 2.25 because it has more energy). However, TDN overestimates energy for low-quality feeds because it doesn't account for energy lost in urine and methane. That's where ME and NE come in. ME is the energy available after digestion and urine losses. It is measured in megacalories (Mcal) per kg of dry matter. For example, good quality lucerne hay has about 2.2 Mcal ME/kg, while poor grass hay has 1.6 Mcal ME/kg. NE is the energy actually used by the animal for maintenance (NEm) and gain (NEg). The relationship is: NE = ME x efficiency (0.6 for maintenance, 0.4 for gain). So if a feed has 2.0 Mcal ME/kg, it provides 1.2 Mcal NEm and 0.8 Mcal NEg. To formulate, you need to meet both maintenance and gain requirements. Maintenance is the energy needed just to keep the animal alive; gain is for growth. For a 300 kg steer, maintenance requirement is about 5.5 Mcal NEm/day, and to gain 1 kg, you need an additional 3.5 Mcal NEg/day. So total NE requirement is 9.0 Mcal/day. If your feed has 1.2 Mcal NEm and 0.8 Mcal NEg per kg, you would need 5.5/1.2 = 4.6 kg for maintenance and 3.5/0.8 = 4.4 kg for gain, total 9 kg dry matter. But that assumes the feed is balanced. In practice, you mix feeds to match the energy density. For example, combine 2 kg maize (1.6 Mcal NEm/kg) with 6 kg hay (0.9 Mcal NEm/kg) to get 2*1.6 + 6*0.9 = 8.6 Mcal NEm, close to the 9.0 needed. Always check that protein and minerals are adequate. Energy deficiencies cause poor growth, weight loss, and infertility. Excess energy makes cattle fat, which is costly and can cause calving problems. Use TDN for quick field calculations, but for precise rations, use ME and NE. Most African extension materials provide TDN values for local feeds, so start there..Trim()

Protein systems: CP, DIP, UIP, metabolizable protein

Protein is essential for muscle growth, milk production, and overall health. In cattle nutrition, we talk about crude protein (CP), degradable intake protein (DIP), undegradable intake protein (UIP), and metabolizable protein (MP). CP is simply the total nitrogen content multiplied by 6.25. For example, cottonseed cake has 40% CP, while maize stover has only 4%. But not all CP is used equally. DIP is the portion broken down by rumen microbes. These microbes need DIP to grow and digest fiber. UIP (also called bypass protein) escapes the rumen and is digested in the small intestine. MP is the actual protein absorbed by the animal, coming from both microbial protein and UIP. A common mistake is to feed only CP without considering DIP and UIP. For a growing steer, you need about 12-14% CP in the diet. But if all of it is DIP, the microbes may not have enough energy to use it, and excess DIP is wasted as urea in urine. Adjusting requirements for environmental factors

Cattle requirements change with the environment. Heat, cold, humidity, wind, and mud all affect how much energy and feed an animal needs. In Africa, heat stress is the biggest issue. When temperatures rise above 25Â°C, cattle eat less and use more energy to cool themselves. For every 1Â°C above 25Â°C, dry matter intake drops by about 0.5%, and maintenance energy requirement increases by about 2%. For example, a steer in northern Nigeria where it is 35Â°C would need 20% more energy for maintenance and would eat 5% less feed. To adjust, you must increase the energy density of the diet. If the normal diet has 60% TDN, you might need 70% TDN. You can do this by adding more maize or molasses. Also, feed during cooler hours (early morning or evening) to encourage intake. Provide shade and plenty of water. In cold environments, like the highlands of Ethiopia, cattle need more energy to stay warm. Below 15Â°C, maintenance energy increases by 1% per degree drop. For a 300 kg steer at 5Â°C, that's a 10% increase. So you might add 0.5 kg of maize per day. Humidity also matters: high humidity makes heat stress worse because cattle cannot pant effectively. Wind helps cool cattle, so in hot, windy areas, heat stress is less. Mud in wet seasons increases energy needs by up to 30% because cattle expend energy walking through it. In such conditions, provide a dry resting area and increase energy by 10-15%. Altitude affects water intake and can cause stress; at high altitudes, cattle may need more energy for breathing. When adjusting, always use the NRC or BR-CORTE environmental adjustment factors. For example, NRC has an equation: NE requirement = maintenance NE x (0.8 + 0.2 x (20 - temperature)/10) for cold, and for heat, multiply by 1.1 to 1.3. A simpler rule: for each 5Â°C above 25Â°C, increase energy by 10%. For each 5Â°C below 15Â°C, increase by 5%. Minerals like potassium and sodium are lost in sweat, so provide extra salt and a good mineral mix. Always monitor body condition and adjust based on actual performance. If cattle are losing weight, they need more energy. If they are too fat, reduce. Keep records of temperature and feed intake to learn your local patterns. Proximate analysis: moisture, ash, protein, fat, fiber

Proximate analysis is the basic set of tests that tells you what a feed is made of. It measures six things: moisture, ash, crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, and nitrogen-free extract (NFE). In Africa, you can send samples to labs at universities like Makerere, Nairobi, or Ibadan, or use portable kits. For moisture, weigh a sample, dry it at 105Â°C for 24 hours, and reweigh. If you start with 100 g and end with 88 g, moisture is 12%. High moisture (above 14%) causes mould. Ash is what's left after burning at 550Â°C; it shows mineral content. A feed with 10% ash has a lot of dirt or minerals. Crude protein is measured by the Kjeldahl method: digest with acid, distill, and titrate. If you get 3.2% nitrogen, multiply by 6.25 to get 20% crude protein. That's good for soybean meal but low for fish meal. Crude fat is extracted with ether. A feed with 5% fat gives energy. Crude fiber is the tough part; high fiber (above 18%) means low digestibility for cattle. NFE is calculated by difference: 100% minus moisture, ash, protein, fat, and fiber. For a typical maize grain: 12% moisture, 1.5% ash, 9% protein, 4% fat, 2% fiber, so NFE is 71.5%. This tells you maize is energy-rich. Always test your own ingredients because African feeds vary widely. A farmer in Kenya found his hay had 8% protein, but a lab in Tanzania showed 5% for the same grass species. Proximate analysis is cheap and fast, and it's the first step to balancing a ration..Trim()

In vitro and in situ digestibility techniques

Digestibility tells you how much of a feed the animal can actually use. In vitro means 'in glass' â you simulate digestion in a lab. The most common method is the Tilley and Terry two-stage technique. You take 0.5 g of dried, ground feed, put it in a tube with rumen fluid from a slaughtered cow (or a fistulated cow), and incubate at 39Â°C for 48 hours. Then you add acid-pepsin and incubate another 24 hours. What's left is undigested. If you started with 0.5 g and 0.35 g disappeared, dry matter digestibility is 70%. That's good for alfalfa but poor for straw. In situ means 'in place' â you put feed samples in nylon bags (porosity 50 microns) and insert them into the rumen of a live cow through a fistula. You remove bags at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours. The weight loss over time gives you a degradation curve. For a farmer, you can estimate how fast a feed breaks down. For example, maize silage might have 60% digestibility at 48 hours, while cottonseed hulls only 30%. In Africa, fistulated cows are available at research stations like ILRI in Nairobi or IITA in Ibadan. If you don't have access, you can use the in vitro method with rumen fluid from a local abattoir. These techniques help you decide if a cheap feed like brewers' grains is worth buying. If in vitro digestibility is below 50%, it's poor for cattle. Above 65%, it's excellent. NIRS is a fast, non-destructive method that uses light to predict feed composition. You shine near-infrared light (wavelengths 780â2500 nm) onto a ground feed sample. The light absorbed by the sample creates a spectrum, which is like a fingerprint of the feed's chemical bonds. A computer compares this spectrum to a library of known samples and predicts moisture, protein, fat, fiber, and even digestibility. Anti-nutritional factors and mycotoxin screening

Anti-nutritional factors (ANFs) are natural compounds in feeds that reduce digestion or harm the animal. Common ones in African feeds include tannins in sorghum and acacia leaves, trypsin inhibitors in raw soybeans, gossypol in cottonseed, and phytate in maize bran. Tannins bind proteins and make them unavailable. A feed with more than 5% tannins is poorly digested. You can test tannins with the Folin-Denis method: extract with water, add reagent, and read colour at 725 nm. For trypsin inhibitors, use the casein digestion assay. Raw soybeans have high inhibitors; heating at 120Â°C for 20 minutes destroys them. Gossypol is toxic to cattle; free gossypol should be below 0.02% in the diet. You can test with the aniline method. Mycotoxins are toxins from moulds, especially aflatoxin, fumonisin, and zearalenone. In Africa, aflatoxin is a big problem in groundnuts, maize, and cottonseed. Aflatoxin B1 above 20 ppb in feed is unsafe for dairy cattle. You can screen with ELISA kits (cost about $5 per test) or send to labs like the Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service (KEPHIS). For a quick farm test, use a black light: aflatoxin glows greenish-yellow under UV. But this is not quantitative. Always dry feeds to below 12% moisture and store in dry, ventilated areas. If you suspect mycotoxins, add a binder like bentonite or yeast cell wall to the ration. For ANFs, proper processing (soaking, cooking, roasting) reduces them. Interpreting feed analysis reports

A feed analysis report can look confusing, but it's just a table of numbers. The first thing to check is the moisture content. If it's above 14%, the feed may spoil. Next, look at crude protein (CP). For a growing steer, you want 12â16% CP. For a dry cow, 8â10% is enough. If the report says 9% CP, that feed is low for milk production. Then check crude fiber (CF). High CF (above 18%) means low energy. For dairy, you want CF below 18% but not too low, or the rumen gets upset. Ash tells you mineral content. If ash is above 10%, there may be dirt or excess minerals. Fat should be 3â5% for energy. If fat is above 8%, it can cause digestive problems. ADF should be 20â30%. If NDF is 50%, the cow will eat less. You also get TDN or ME. For a 400 kg steer gaining 1 kg per day, he needs about 70% TDN. If your feed has 60% TDN, you must supplement with maize. Always compare the report to standard values for that ingredient. For example, cottonseed meal should have 40% CP, 1% fat, 15% fiber. If your report says 30% CP, it's been diluted. Look at the standard error or confidence intervals if given. Also check for anti-nutritional factors like tannins or mycotoxins. Ionophores and their effects on feed efficiency

Ionophores are feed additives that change the bacteria in the rumen to improve feed efficiency. They are not antibiotics for treating disease; they are used continuously at low doses. Common ionophores include monensin (Rumensin), lasalocid (Bovatec), and salinomycin. In Africa, monensin is the most widely available, often imported from South Africa, Europe, or China. For cattle, the typical inclusion rate is 20â30 grams per tonne of feed for monensin, providing about 200â300 mg per head per day. For steers on high-grain diets, this can improve feed conversion by 5â10% and reduce bloat. Ionophores work by favouring Gram-positive bacteria that produce propionate, a energy source, while suppressing Gram-negative bacteria that produce methane and ammonia. This shift means more energy from the same feed. For a 300 kg steer eating 8 kg of feed per day, a 7% improvement saves about 0.56 kg of feed daily. Over a 120-day finishing period, that's 67 kg of feed saved per animal. To use ionophores, buy a pre-mix (e.g., Rumensin 200) and mix thoroughly into the total mixed ration (TMR) using a feed mixer. Start with a low dose and build up over 5â7 days to avoid feed refusal. Never exceed 33 g/tonne for monensin, as toxicity can cause heart failure. Do not feed to horses or dogs. In Africa, where feed costs are high, ionophores are one of the cheapest tools to improve profitability. Always store in a dry place and use within 6 months. Check local regulations; in some countries, ionophores require a veterinary prescription..Trim()

Probiotics, prebiotics, and direct-fed microbials

Probiotics, prebiotics, and direct-fed microbials (DFMs) are natural additives that support gut health and digestion. Probiotics are live beneficial bacteria, such as Lactobacillus and Bacillus species. Prebiotics are non-digestible fibres that feed these good bacteria, like mannan-oligosaccharides (MOS) from yeast cell walls. DFMs are specific strains of live microbes, often yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) or bacteria. In Africa, yeast culture (e.g., Yea-Sacc) is popular because it is stable in heat and improves fibre digestion. For cattle, typical inclusion rates are 10â20 g per head per day for yeast, or 5â10 g for bacterial DFMs. These additives help during stress periods: weaning, transport, or high-grain feeding. They reduce diarrhoea, improve feed intake, and increase weight gain by 3â5%. For example, a feedlot in Kenya using Saccharomyces cerevisiae at 15 g/head/day saw a 4% improvement in average daily gain (ADG) over 90 days. Probiotics are not drugs; they are sold as feed supplements. You can buy them as powders or pastes. Mix into the feed or top-dress. Store in a cool, dry place; heat above 40Â°C kills live yeast. Prebiotics like MOS are often included at 2â5 g/head/day. They bind harmful bacteria like E. coli and prevent them from attaching to the gut. For small-scale farmers, using fermented feeds like silage naturally provides some probiotics. But commercial DFMs give consistent results. Always check expiry dates and buy from reputable suppliers. In Africa, look for products registered by your national drug authority. These additives are safe and leave no residues, making them ideal for organic or export-oriented beef..Trim()

Buffers and neutralizers for high-grain diets

When cattle eat a lot of grain, the rumen becomes acidic, leading to acidosis, off-feed, and even death. Buffers and neutralizers help keep rumen pH stable. Common buffers are sodium bicarbonate (baking soda), magnesium oxide, and sodium sesquicarbonate. Sodium bicarbonate is the most used: include 0.5â1% of the total dry matter (DM) in the diet. For a steer eating 10 kg DM, that's 50â100 g per day. Magnesium oxide is stronger; use 0.2â0.4% of DM (20â40 g/day). Buffers work by neutralizing excess acid and maintaining pH above 6.0. In high-grain finishing diets (70â90% grain), acidosis is a major risk. Symptoms include loose manure, reduced feed intake, and laminitis. In Africa, where maize and sorghum are common grains, buffers are essential. For example, in South African feedlots, sodium bicarbonate is routinely added at 0.8% of DM. To use, mix the buffer thoroughly into the TMR. Start with a lower level and increase as grain increases. Do not exceed 1.5% sodium bicarbonate, as it can cause alkalosis and reduce intake. Magnesium oxide is often used in combination with bicarbonate for better results. Another option is sodium bentonite, a clay that absorbs toxins and slows starch fermentation. Use 1â2% of DM. Buffers are cheap and effective. A 50 kg bag of sodium bicarbonate costs about $30 in many African countries, enough for 500â1000 head-days. Always provide free-choice salt and clean water. Monitor manure consistency and feed intake daily. If cattle are off-feed, reduce grain and increase forage. Buffers are not a substitute for proper step-up feeding; they are a safety net..Trim()

Enzymes and feed processing aids

Enzymes are proteins that break down feed components, making them easier to digest. In cattle, the most common feed enzymes are cellulases, xylanases, and amylases. Cellulases break down fibre (cellulose) into sugars, improving forage digestion. Xylanases break down hemicellulose, also in fibre. Amylases break down starch. For cattle, adding enzymes can improve feed efficiency by 2â5%, especially with low-quality forages. Typical inclusion rates: 0.5â2 kg per tonne of feed for a commercial enzyme blend. To use, mix the enzyme powder with a small amount of feed, then blend into the TMR. Do not mix with hot ingredients; heat above 60Â°C destroys enzymes. Store in a cool, dry place. Growth promotants are substances that increase weight gain and feed efficiency. They include hormonal implants (e.g., trenbolone acetate, estradiol) and beta-agonists (e.g., zilpaterol). Starter diets for newly received calves

When calves arrive at your farm after transport, they are stressed, often dehydrated, and may have been off feed for hours. The first 24 hours are critical. Provide free-choice hay and fresh water with electrolytes. Do not offer grain immediately. After 12-24 hours, introduce a starter diet. A good starter for a 200 kg calf should contain 14-16% crude protein, 2-3% fat, and 15-20% fiber. Use locally available ingredients: maize meal (energy), soybean meal or cottonseed cake (protein), wheat bran (fiber), and molasses (palatability). A simple mix: 40% maize, 25% soybean meal, 20% wheat bran, 10% molasses, 5% mineral-vitamin premix. Mix well and offer small amounts (0.5 kg per calf) several times a day. Increase gradually over 7-10 days to 2-3 kg per calf per day. Always provide clean water. In Africa, drought-stressed calves may need extra protein; consider adding 1% urea to the concentrate if natural protein is scarce, but never feed urea to weak calves. Monitor calves for scours; if it occurs, reduce grain and add fiber. Weigh calves weekly to track growth. A well-managed starter phase can achieve 0.5-0.7 kg daily gain. Use a feed trough with dividers to prevent bullying. If calves are very young or light, consider a creep feed with 18% protein. Always consult a local extension officer for ingredient availability and price. Remember, starter diets are not just about nutrients; they are about getting calves eating and healthy. Clean feed troughs daily to prevent mold. This phase sets the foundation for the entire feeding period..Trim()

Growing and finishing diets for steers and heifers

Growing and finishing diets are designed to put on weight efficiently. Growing phase (200-350 kg) targets 0.7-1.0 kg daily gain with 12-14% protein. Record feed intake and weight gain to calculate feed conversion ratio (FCR). A good FCR is 6:1 to 8:1. If FCR is poor, check for parasites and adjust diet. Always ensure consistent feed quality. A lactating cow diet: 45% maize, 20% cottonseed cake, 15% wheat bran, 10% molasses, 5% mineral premix, 5% limestone. Provide 2-3 kg concentrate per 5 kg milk. Always offer free-choice mineral blocks, especially with selenium and copper. Principles of least-cost diet formulation

Least-cost formulation is a method to find the cheapest mix of feed ingredients that meets the nutritional needs of cattle. It works by solving a mathematical problem: you have a list of available ingredients with their nutrient contents and prices, and you want to choose amounts of each to minimize total cost while meeting requirements for energy, protein, minerals, etc. For example, in Kenya, maize costs 35 KES/kg and provides 3.3 Mcal/kg of metabolizable energy (ME), while wheat bran costs 22 KES/kg and provides 2.8 Mcal/kg ME. A least-cost program will decide how much of each to use to meet an energy target of 2.6 Mcal/kg for a growing steer. The key principle is that no single ingredient is always cheapest; it depends on nutrient requirements and prices. The method uses linear programming, a mathematical technique that considers all constraints simultaneously. Constraints include minimum and maximum levels of ingredients (e.g., at least 15% maize for palatability, at most 20% cottonseed cake due to gossypol). The program finds the combination that satisfies all constraints at lowest cost. This is more accurate than hand calculations because it can handle many ingredients and nutrients at once. In Africa, where feed costs can be 70% of livestock production, least-cost formulation can save 10-20% on feed bills. For instance, a farmer in Tanzania using local sunflower cake and maize bran could reduce cost from 800 TZS/kg to 650 TZS/kg. To start, you need a list of ingredients, their nutrient profiles (from lab analysis or feed tables), and current market prices. Then you set the nutrient requirements for your cattle class (e.g., 12% crude protein for maintenance). The software does the rest. Remember, least-cost does not mean lowest quality; it means meeting requirements at minimum cost. Always check that the final mix is palatable and safe. With practice, you can adjust constraints to use more local ingredients and reduce reliance on imported soy or maize..Trim()

Sensitivity analysis and shadow prices

Sensitivity analysis shows how changes in prices or nutrient requirements affect the least-cost formula. Shadow prices (also called dual values) tell you how much the total cost would change if you relax a constraint by one unit. For example, if the shadow price for crude protein is 5 KES per 0.1% increase, it means that raising the protein requirement from 12% to 12.1% would increase cost by 5 KES per kg of feed. This helps you decide which nutrients are most expensive to increase. In Brill or Excel Solver, you can generate a sensitivity report after solving. It lists shadow prices for each constraint and allowable ranges for prices. For instance, if maize price increases by 2 KES/kg, the report shows whether the optimal mix changes. If the price increase is within the 'allowable increase', the mix stays the same but cost rises; if beyond, you need to re-formulate. In Africa, where prices fluctuate due to drought or import costs, sensitivity analysis is crucial. Suppose you formulate a diet with 50% maize at 35 KES/kg. If maize price rises to 40 KES/kg, you can check the report: maybe you can replace some maize with wheat bran (22 KES/kg) without hurting performance.
