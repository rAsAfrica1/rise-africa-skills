Module 1 · Introduction to Agricultural Tool Making

Overview of Agricultural Tools and Their Uses

Agricultural tools are the backbone of farming in Africa. From the simple hoe to the ox-drawn plough, each tool has a specific job. In this lesson, you will learn about common tools and their uses. The hoe is the most basic tool, used for digging, weeding, and making ridges. There are two main types: the short-handled hoe for close work and the long-handled hoe for heavier tasks. The machete (panga) is used for clearing bush, harvesting, and cutting. The axe is for felling trees and splitting firewood. The sickle is for harvesting grains like rice and wheat. The plough, often pulled by oxen or donkeys, turns soil to prepare seedbeds. The harrow breaks clods and levels the soil. The planter drops seeds at the right spacing. The wheelbarrow carries heavy loads. In Africa, many farmers still use hand tools because they are affordable and easy to maintain. But knowing the right tool for the job saves time and energy. For example, using a hoe to harvest rice is slow and damages the grain; a sickle is much better. Similarly, a machete is not for digging. You must match the tool to the task. Also, tools are made from different materials: iron for blades, wood for handles. Understanding these basics will help you when you start making or repairing tools. As you go through this module, keep in mind that a well-made tool can last for years if properly cared for. In the next lessons, you will learn safety, maintenance, and how to make your own tools..Trim()

Safety Practices in the Tool Making Workshop

Safety is not optional in a tool making workshop. Every year, many accidents happen because people ignore simple rules. In this lesson, you will learn how to protect yourself and others. First, always wear protective gear. Safety glasses or goggles protect your eyes from flying metal chips when grinding or chipping. Leather gloves protect your hands from sharp edges and hot metal. Closed-toe shoes or boots protect your feet from dropped tools or hot sparks. An apron or overalls protect your clothes and skin. Second, keep your workshop clean and organized. A cluttered floor causes trips and falls. Keep tools in their places. Sweep up metal shavings and wood dust regularly. Third, use tools correctly. A wrench is not a hammer. A screwdriver is not a chisel. Using the wrong tool can cause injury and damage the tool. When using power tools like grinders or drills, check the cords for damage. Never use a power tool in wet conditions. Fourth, handle hot metal with tongs or pliers, never with bare hands. Quench hot metal in water or oil safely, away from your body. Fifth, have a first aid kit nearby and know how to use it. For burns, cool with clean water. For cuts, apply pressure and cover. Sixth, work in a well-ventilated area. Fumes from welding, soldering, or painting can be harmful. If you are welding, use a welding helmet and fire-resistant clothing. Seventh, never work alone if you can avoid it. If you must, tell someone where you are and what you are doing. Finally, learn to recognize fatigue. Tired people make mistakes. Take breaks. By following these practices, you can work safely and productively. Remember, a safe workshop is a productive workshop..Trim()

Basic Hand Tools and Their Maintenance

Hand tools are the foundation of any workshop. In this lesson, you will learn about common hand tools and how to keep them in good condition. The most important tools for tool making are: hacksaw, files, hammers, chisels, punches, pliers, wrenches, screwdrivers, and measuring tools like rulers and squares. A hacksaw cuts metal. Use a blade with 14 to 18 teeth per inch for general work. Keep the blade tight and replace it when dull. Files shape and smooth metal. Use a single-cut file for sharpening and a double-cut for fast removal. Clean files with a file card to remove metal chips. Store files separately to avoid dulling. Hammers come in different weights. A 2-pound ball-peen hammer is good for general work. Check the handle for cracks and the head for looseness. If the head is loose, replace the handle or wedge it tight. Chisels cut metal. Keep the cutting edge sharp with a file or grinder. Never use a chisel with a mushroomed headâgrind it back to shape. Punches mark or drive pins. Use the right size for the job. Pliers grip, bend, and cut wire. Keep the pivot oiled. Wrenches tighten and loosen nuts and bolts. Use the correct size to avoid rounding the nut. Screwdrivers must match the screw head. A worn tip will slip and damage the screw. Measuring tools like steel rules and try squares must be kept clean and rust-free. For all tools, rust is the enemy. After use, wipe them with an oily rag. Store them in a dry place. If rust appears, remove it with steel wool and oil. Sharp tools are safe tools because they require less force. Dull tools slip and cause accidents. So, sharpen your tools regularly. A few minutes of maintenance each day will save you hours of work and money. In the next lesson, you will learn how to read tool designs..Trim()

Reading and Interpreting Tool Designs

Before you make a tool, you need to understand its design. A design is a drawing or plan that shows the shape, size, and parts of a tool. In this lesson, you will learn how to read simple tool designs. Designs can be sketches on paper or technical drawings with dimensions. The most common views are the front view, top view, and side view. These show the tool from different angles. Dimensions are given in millimetres (mm) or inches. For example, a hoe blade might be 200 mm long, 150 mm wide, and 3 mm thick. The handle might be 1200 mm long and 30 mm in diameter. You need to know how to read these numbers. A scale drawing shows the tool smaller or larger than real size. For example, a scale of 1:2 means the drawing is half the real size. You must multiply the drawing measurements by 2 to get the real size. Lines on a drawing have meaning. Solid lines show visible edges. Dashed lines show hidden edges. Centre lines show the middle of round parts. Arrowheads show dimensions. You also need to understand symbols for materials. For example, a pattern of diagonal lines might mean steel. A wavy pattern might mean wood. Sometimes, notes are written on the drawing, like 'weld here' or 'grind smooth'. In Africa, many tool designs are passed down by tradition, but modern designs can improve performance. For instance, a hoe blade can be made from an old car spring, which is tough and wears well. The design must consider the local materials available. You also need to think about the user: a handle should fit the hand comfortably. A blade should be balanced. When you can read a design, you can make a tool that works. You can also modify designs to suit your needs. Practice by copying simple designs and making them. Start with a simple tool like a garden trowel. Measure, draw, and then build. This skill will serve you well..Trim()

Selecting Materials for Agricultural Tools

The quality of a tool depends on the materials used. In this lesson, you will learn how to choose the right materials for agricultural tools. The main materials are metals, wood, and sometimes plastic or rubber. For blades and cutting edges, you need steel that can be hardened. High-carbon steel is best because it can be sharpened and holds an edge. Old car springs, files, and leaf springs are good sources of high-carbon steel. Mild steel is softer and not good for cutting edges, but it can be used for handles or brackets. To test if a piece of steel is high-carbon, touch it to a grinding wheel. If it gives off long, yellow sparks, it is high-carbon. If the sparks are short and red, it is mild steel. For handles, you need strong, straight-grained wood that does not split easily. Good choices include hickory, ash, and locally available hardwoods like mahogany or mvule. Avoid softwoods like pine; they break easily. The wood must be dry to prevent shrinking and loosening. You can also use metal or fibreglass handles, but wood is traditional and easy to replace. For fasteners, use bolts, rivets, or welding. Bolts allow you to replace parts. Rivets are permanent. Welding is strong but requires skill. For the connection between blade and handle, a tang that goes into the handle is common. It should be long enough to prevent wobbling. You can also use a socket or a ferrule. When selecting materials, consider the local climate. In wet areas, wood rots quickly, so treat it with oil or paint. In dry areas, wood cracks, so choose a species that resists cracking. Also, consider the cost. Recycled materials are cheap and good for the environment. For example, an old hoe blade can be reforged into a new tool. Always inspect materials for cracks, rust, or defects. A weak material will fail when you need it most. By choosing wisely, you make tools that last. In the next lesson, you will learn how to organize your workshop for efficient work..Trim()

Workshop Organization and Workflow

A well-organized workshop saves time and prevents accidents. In this lesson, you will learn how to set up your workshop and plan your work. First, choose a location. It should be dry, well-lit, and have good ventilation. If possible, have a separate area for dirty work like grinding and welding, and a clean area for measuring and assembly. The floor should be concrete or packed earth, not slippery. Second, arrange your tools and materials. Keep frequently used tools on a wall board or in a rack within easy reach. Store materials by type and size. Label shelves or bins. Keep a clear space for assembly. Have a sturdy workbench. A good height is 900 mm from the floor. The bench should have a vice mounted securely. Third, organize your workflow. When making a tool, follow a logical sequence: measure, mark, cut, shape, drill, assemble, and finish. Keep the work area clean as you go. Put tools back after use. This prevents losing them and keeps the area safe. Fourth, manage waste. Have separate containers for metal scraps, wood scraps, and rubbish. Metal scraps can be recycled or used for small parts. Fifth, maintain your equipment. Check power tools before use. Lubricate machines. Replace worn parts. Sixth, plan your day. Write a list of tasks. Prioritize. If you are making many tools, do similar tasks together, like cutting all the blades first, then shaping them. This is called batch production and it saves time. Seventh, think about safety in your layout. Keep fire extinguishers and first aid kits accessible. Ensure good lighting to avoid mistakes. Eighth, keep a small inventory of common materials so you don't run out. By organizing your workshop, you can work faster and safer. A clean, well-ordered workshop is a sign of a professional tool maker. Practice these habits daily..Trim()

Module 2 · Basic Metalworking Skills

Measuring and Marking Out Metal

Before you cut, drill, or bend any metal, you must measure and mark it accurately. In tool making, a mistake of 2 mm can mean a wasted piece of steel. Start with a steel rule (300 mm or 600 mm) and a scriberâa sharp, hardened steel pin with a point. For agricultural tools like hoe blades or mower parts, you often work from a drawing or a sample. Place your metal on a flat surface, like a steel plate or a heavy bench. Use a try square to check that edges are straight and at 90 degrees. If the edge is rough, file it first.\n\nTo mark a line, apply layout dye (or chalk, or even a permanent marker) to the metal. This makes scribed lines visible. Hold the steel rule firmly against the edge, and scribe a line by pulling the scriber onceâdo not go back and forth. For circles, use a pair of dividers or a trammel. Set the distance between the points to the radius you need. For example, to mark a 10 mm hole, set dividers to 5 mm. Place the fixed point at the center punch mark, then swing the dividers to scratch the circle.\n\nCenter punching is critical. A center punch is a hardened steel rod with a pointed tip. Place the punch on your intersection mark, hold it vertical, and hit it once with a hammer (1 kg to 2 kg). This creates a small dent that guides your drill bit later. For accurate work, use an automatic center punchâit has a spring inside and you just press down.\n\nAlways double-check your measurements before cutting. Use a caliper or micrometer for precise work. A vernier caliper can measure to 0.02 mm. For most agricultural tools, Â±0.5 mm is acceptable. Remember: measure twice, cut once. In rural workshops, where replacing metal is costly, this rule saves time and money.\n\nFinally, keep your marking tools clean and dry. Rust on a scriber or rule ruins accuracy. Store them in a wooden box or cloth roll. Practice on scrap metal until you can scribe a straight line and punch a center mark without slipping..Trim()

Cutting Metal with Hacksaws and Shears

Cutting metal is a core skill for making tools. You will use a hacksaw for thick bars and pipes, and tin snips or shears for thin sheet metal. Start with a hacksaw: it has a frame and a blade with 14, 18, or 24 teeth per inch (TPI). For general work on mild steel, use 18 TPI. For thin-walled pipe or sheet, use 24 TPI to avoid snagging. For thick solid bar, use 14 TPI.\n\nMount your work in a bench vise. Mark your cut line with a scriber and center punch. Start the cut by drawing the saw backward a few times to make a groove. Then use full strokes, pushing forward and pulling back. Apply light pressure on the forward stroke only. Let the saw do the workâpushing too hard breaks teeth. Keep the blade lubricated with a few drops of engine oil or water. A good cut should take 30 to 60 seconds for a 25 mm steel bar.\n\nIf the blade wanders, don't twist it. Stop, reposition the saw in the groove, and continue. For long cuts, rotate the blade 90 degrees in the frame to cut deeper. When you are almost through, slow down and support the offcut to prevent it from falling and bending the blade.\n\nFor sheet metal up to 1.5 mm thick, use tin snips. There are straight, left-cutting, and right-cutting snips. For straight cuts, use straight snips. For curves, use left or right snips depending on the direction. Mark your line, then clamp the sheet to a bench. Start at an edge and cut with short, firm strokes. Do not close the snips completelyâthis creates a jagged edge. For thicker sheet (up to 2 mm), use a bench shear or a nibbler.\n\nAlways wear gloves and safety glasses. Cut edges are razor-sharp. After cutting, file the edges to remove burrs. In African workshops, a hacksaw and a pair of good snips are often the only cutting tools available, so master them. Practice on scrap metal until your cuts are straight and square..Trim()

Filing and Shaping Metal Edges

After cutting, metal edges are rough and sharp. Filing removes burrs, squares edges, and shapes curves. You need a set of files: a flat file for straight edges, a half-round for curves, and a round file for holes. For mild steel, use a single-cut file for finishing and a double-cut file for rapid removal. File lengths range from 100 mm to 300 mm. Start with a 200 mm bastard cut file for general work.\n\nSecure your work in a vise at about elbow height. Hold the file with one hand on the handle and the other on the tip. For flat filing, push the file forward and slightly down, using the full length of the file. Apply pressure on the forward stroke only; lift the file on the return. Do not rock the fileâkeep it flat. For draw filing (finishing), hold the file at 90 degrees to the edge and push it sideways. This produces a smooth surface.\n\nTo square an edge, file until the edge is flat and at 90 degrees to the face. Check with a try square. For curved shapes, use a half-round file. Clamp the work so the curve is accessible. File with a rocking motion, following the curve. For holes, use a round file. Turn the file slightly as you push.\n\nFile cards (wire brushes) clean the file teeth. Clogged files scratch instead of cut. Clean your file every few minutes. Chalk on the file reduces clogging when filing soft metals like aluminum.\n\nFor agricultural tools, filing is used to sharpen hoe blades, machetes, and axes. A sharp edge is a filed edge. But for tool making, filing is for shaping and fitting. After filing, deburr all edges. A smooth edge prevents cuts and improves fit. In rural Africa, a good file can last years if kept dry and clean. Store files separately to avoid dulling. Practice on scrap steel until you can file a flat edge and a smooth curve..Trim()

Drilling Holes in Metal

Drilling holes is essential for bolting, riveting, and fitting parts. You need a drillâeither a hand drill or an electric drill. For accuracy, use a drill press. But in many African workshops, a portable electric drill (500 W to 800 W) is common. Use high-speed steel (HSS) drill bits. For mild steel, a bit with a 118-degree point angle works well. Common sizes: 3 mm, 5 mm, 6.5 mm, 8.5 mm, 10 mm.\n\nFirst, mark the hole center with a center punch. This prevents the bit from wandering. Apply a few drops of cutting oil (or engine oil) to the punch mark. Start with a small pilot bit (3 mm) to create a guide hole. Then switch to the final size. Run the drill at a moderate speed: for a 6 mm bit in steel, about 600 to 800 RPM. Too fast burns the bit; too slow chips the edge.\n\nHold the drill perpendicular to the surface. If using a hand drill, use both hands and keep steady pressure. Let the bit cutâdo not force it. Clear chips by withdrawing the bit occasionally. For deep holes, withdraw the bit every few millimeters to clear swarf. Use a brush, not your fingers, to remove chips.\n\nFor large holes (over 12 mm), drill a pilot hole first, then use a step drill or a hole saw. For precision, clamp the work to a drill press table. If the bit squeals, reduce speed and add oil. If it smokes, stop and let it cool.\n\nAfter drilling, deburr the hole with a larger drill bit or a countersink. This removes sharp edges. For tapping (next lesson), the hole size must match the tap. For a 6 mm bolt, drill a 5 mm hole for tapping. Always wear safety glasses. In agriculture, you will drill holes for plow bolts, mower blades, and tool handles. Practice on scrap metal until your holes are straight and clean..Trim()

Threading and Tapping

Threading creates screw threads on a rod (external) or inside a hole (internal). For tool making, you will mostly tap holes to accept bolts. You need a tap set (taper, plug, and bottoming taps) and a tap wrench. For external threads, you need a die and die stock. Common thread sizes: M6, M8, M10, M12. In Africa, both metric and imperial (Whitworth) threads are used. Know which one you need.\n\nTo tap a hole, first drill the correct tap drill size. For M6, use a 5.0 mm drill; for M8, 6.8 mm; for M10, 8.5 mm; for M12, 10.2 mm. Clamp the work securely. Start with a taper tap (first tap). Insert it into the hole, keeping it straight. Turn it clockwise with light pressure until it bites. Then turn one full turn forward, then half a turn back to break chips. Apply cutting oil generously.\n\nAfter the taper tap goes through, use the plug tap (second tap) to cut threads closer to the bottom. For blind holes, use a bottoming tap. Never force a tapâif it binds, back it out, clear chips, and add oil. A broken tap is very difficult to remove.\n\nFor external threads, use a die. Clamp the rod in a vise. Chamfer the end with a file. Start the die straight, turn clockwise, and reverse every half turn. Use oil. For a 10 mm rod, you need a 10 mm die. The rod diameter should be slightly less than the die size (e.g., 9.8 mm for M10).\n\nIn agriculture, threading is used for bolts, clamps, and adjustable linkages. Practice on scrap metal. Always clean threads with a wire brush. A well-tapped hole holds a bolt securely. Remember: the tap drill size is critical. Too small, and the tap breaks; too large, and the threads strip..Trim()

Basic Joining Techniques: Riveting and Bolting

Joining metal parts is how you assemble tools. Two common methods are riveting and bolting. Riveting is permanent; bolting is removable. For agricultural tools, both are used. You need rivets (solid or blind), a rivet gun or hammer, a bucking bar, and a drill. For bolting, you need bolts, nuts, washers, and wrenches.\n\nFor solid riveting, drill a hole slightly larger than the rivet shank (e.g., 4.1 mm for a 4 mm rivet). Insert the rivet through the parts. Place the head on an anvil or bucking bar. Use a ball-peen hammer to flatten the protruding shank into a second head. Start with light taps around the edge, then work toward the center. For a 4 mm rivet, the shank should protrude about 1.5 times the diameter (6 mm).\n\nFor blind rivets (pop rivets), use a rivet gun. Drill a hole matching the rivet size. Insert the rivet into the gun, place it in the hole, and squeeze the handle until the mandrel snaps. Blind rivets are fast and work from one side. They are good for sheet metal.\n\nBolting is stronger and can be disassembled. Use graded bolts (8.8 or 10.9) for high stress. For a 10 mm bolt, drill an 11 mm hole. Place washers under the nut and bolt head. Tighten with a spanner or socket. For vibration, use a lock washer or thread-locking compound. In Africa, many farmers use bolts to attach plow shares, mower blades, and tool handles.\n\nAlways match rivet or bolt material to the base metal to avoid galvanic corrosion. Stainless steel rivets in aluminum can cause corrosion. For outdoor tools, use galvanized or stainless bolts. After joining, check that the parts are tight and aligned. Practice on scrap metal until your joints are strong and neat. Remember: a good joint is as strong as the metal around it..Trim()

Making Watering Cans and Buckets

A good watering can is a farmer's daily tool. You can make one from a 5-litre plastic jerrycan or a metal bucket. For a plastic jerrycan, cut the top off with a hacksaw, leaving a handle. For the spout, use a 30 cm length of 12 mm PVC pipe. Drill a hole in the side of the can near the bottom, insert the pipe, and seal it with silicone or epoxy. The rose (sprinkler head) can be made from a tin can lid punched with a 2 mm nail in a grid pattern. Attach it to the spout with wire or tape. For a metal bucket, you can solder a spout made from a piece of galvanised pipe. Alternatively, make a simple bucket from a 20-litre oil tin. Clean it thoroughly with soap and water. Cut the top off with a chisel and hammer, leaving a rim. Attach a handle made from 6 mm rebar bent into a U-shape, or use heavy wire. For a watering can, punch holes in the bottom of a tin can and attach it to a wooden handle. In Africa, where materials are scarce, recycling is key. Fabricating Drip Irrigation Components

Drip irrigation saves water by delivering it directly to plant roots. You can make a simple drip system using 16 mm black polyethylene (PE) pipe, available in most African farm supply shops. Start with a main line from your water source. Use a knife or pipe cutter to cut the pipe. For emitters, you can use micro-tubes: cut 30 cm lengths of 4 mm spaghetti tubing. Insert one end into a hole punched in the main line with a 4 mm punch. The other end goes to the plant. To regulate flow, tie a knot in the tube or insert a small plastic barb. Alternatively, make emitters from old bicycle inner tubes: cut small strips and puncture a pinhole. For a 100-metre row of tomatoes, you need about 200 emitters spaced 50 cm apart. You can also make a DIY drip line by punching holes directly in the 16 mm pipe every 30 cm. But holes can clog; use a filter made from a cloth bag or a 200-mesh screen. Connect the main line to a bucket or tank using a 13 mm barbed connector and a rubber washer. Gravity feed works if the tank is at least 1 metre above the field. For larger areas, use a small 12V pump. In dry areas like the Sahel, drip irrigation can increase yields by 50% with half the water. Maintain your system by flushing the lines weekly. Use vinegar to clear mineral deposits. With a punch, barb fittings, and end caps, you can assemble a drip kit for a 100 mÂ² garden in a day. Always use a filter to prevent clogging. Building Sprinkler Systems

Sprinkler systems mimic rainfall and are good for leafy greens, carrots, and pasture. A simple homemade sprinkler can be made from a 2-litre plastic bottle. Puncture many small holes (1 mm) in the sides and bottom using a hot needle. Attach the bottle to a garden hose with a clamp. When water flows, it sprays in a circle. For a larger area, use a 20-litre bucket with holes drilled in the lid, placed on a raised platform. Connect a hose to the bucket and let gravity do the work. For a more efficient system, make a rotating sprinkler from a bicycle wheel. Weld or bolt a 1-metre length of 15 mm galvanised pipe to the hub. Making Hand Pumps and Treadle Pumps

Hand pumps and treadle pumps lift water from shallow wells (up to 10 metres) and are vital where electricity is absent. A simple hand pump can be made from PVC pipe and a rubber plunger. Use a 50 mm PVC pipe as the cylinder. Cut a rubber disc from an old tyre inner tube to fit snugly inside the pipe; this is the plunger. Attach the plunger to a 10 mm steel rod. The rod goes up and down through a cap on the pipe. At the bottom, install a one-way valve (a flapper made from a rubber sheet and a hinge). When you pull up, water is drawn in; when you push down, the valve closes and water is pushed up. For the handle, use a metal lever attached to the rod. A treadle pump is more efficient for irrigation. It uses two cylinders and a treadle (foot pedal) mechanism. Build a frame from 40 mm angle iron. The cylinders are 100 mm PVC pipes. Connect the pistons to a crank shaft made from a bicycle axle. The treadle is a wooden plank pivoted in the middle. As you step, the crank turns, moving the pistons up and down. A well-made treadle pump can lift 1 litre per stroke and irrigate 0.2 hectares. Use leather or rubber cups for pistons. Seal all joints with Teflon tape or rubber gaskets. Test for leaks by pumping water and checking for air bubbles. These pumps are cheap to build (under $50) and easy to maintain. In many African countries, treadle pumps have doubled incomes for smallholder farmers. Always install a foot valve to keep water in the pipe. Regular maintenance extends the life of your irrigation tools and saves water. For watering cans and buckets, check for leaks and cracks. Seal small holes with silicone or beeswax. In all cases, keep a maintenance log. Train all users on proper operation. A well-maintained drip system can last 10 years; a neglected one fails in one season. Use the right tools: a pipe cutter, spanners, and a wire brush. Store equipment under cover when not in use. Making Maize Shellers

A maize sheller saves hours of hand-shelling. Start with a wooden or metal drum, 200-litre oil drum works well. Cut a rectangular opening 25 cm by 15 cm on the side. Inside, weld or bolt 8 to 12 metal teeth (10 mm round bars, 5 cm long) in a spiral pattern along the drum's inner surface. The shaft runs through the centre on two bearings (pillow block bearings, 25 mm bore). Attach a hand crank or a pulley for a 1-2 HP electric motor. The clearance between the teeth and the drum wall should be 2-3 cm for dry maize. For wet maize, increase to 4 cm to avoid crushing. Build a hopper on top (30 cm x 30 cm) and a discharge chute at the bottom. Test with 5 kg of dry maize: you should shell 20-30 kg per hour by hand, or 100-150 kg per hour with a motor. Adjust the teeth if kernels break too much. Use hardened bolts for teeth to last longer. Grease the bearings every 50 hours of use. Fabricating Grain Mills and Grinders

Grain mills turn maize, millet, or sorghum into flour. The simplest is a plate mill: two cast iron or steel discs, 20-25 cm diameter, with grooves cut radially. One disc is stationary, the other rotates. The gap between them adjusts from 0.5 mm (fine flour) to 2 mm (coarse meal). Use a 1.5-3 HP electric motor or a 5-7 HP diesel engine. Build a wooden or metal frame to hold the discs and motor. A hopper feeds grain into the centre of the rotating disc. The centrifugal force pushes grain outward, grinding it between the plates. For a hammer mill, use a rotor with 4-8 swinging hammers (10 mm thick steel) inside a drum. A 3 mm screen at the bottom controls particle size. Hammer mills are better for dry grains and can also crush groundnuts or cassava chips. For either type, ensure the drive belt is tight and pulleys aligned. Use a 1:3 ratio (motor pulley 5 cm, mill pulley 15 cm) to get 800-1200 RPM for plate mills. Test with 1 kg of maize: you should get 20-50 kg per hour. Wear safety glasses and ear protection. Clean the mill after each use to prevent mould..Trim()

Making Threshing Machines
