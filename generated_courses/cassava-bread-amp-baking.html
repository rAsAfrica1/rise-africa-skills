Module 1 · Cassava Basics for Bakers

Identifying fresh cassava roots in local markets

When you buy cassava roots at the market, you need to pick ones that are fresh and good for baking. Fresh cassava roots are firm and hard. Press the root with your thumb; it should not leave a dent. The skin should be brown and rough, with no soft spots or mould. Avoid roots that are wet or have a sour smell. A fresh root snaps cleanly when you break it. The inside should be white or creamy white, with no black lines or discoloration. If you see black streaks, the root is old or diseased. Size matters: medium-sized roots, about 2 to 3 inches in diameter, are best. Very thick roots can be woody and fibrous. Also, look at the ends: they should be clean and dry, not mushy. In many African markets, cassava is sold in piles. Ask the seller to cut a small piece for you to check. If the inside is white and smells fresh, it's good. If it smells like almonds (bitter) or is yellowish, reject it. Buy cassava that was harvested recently, ideally within 2 days. In hot weather, cassava spoils fast. So, buy from a seller who has a fresh supply. If you can, buy directly from a farmer. You can also test by scratching the skin: fresh cassava skin is hard to scratch, while old cassava skin peels off easily. Another trick: fresh cassava is heavy for its size. Pick up two roots of the same size; the heavier one has more water and is fresher. After buying, use it within 2 to 3 days. If you must store it, keep it in a cool, dark place. Remember, cassava that is not fresh will give you poor-quality flour and bread. So, take your time to choose well..Trim()

Understanding sweet vs bitter cassava varieties

Cassava comes in two main types: sweet and bitter. The difference is in the amount of cyanide, a natural poison. Sweet cassava has low cyanide and can be eaten after peeling and cooking. Bitter cassava has high cyanide and must be processed carefully to remove the poison. In Africa, sweet cassava is often eaten boiled or fried, while bitter cassava is used for flour, gari, and fufu. For baking, you can use both, but you must process bitter cassava properly to make it safe. How to tell them apart? Sweet cassava roots usually have a light brown skin and white flesh. They taste slightly sweet when raw. Bitter cassava roots often have a darker skin and yellow or creamy flesh. They taste very bitter and leave a burning sensation in your mouth. Never taste raw bitter cassava to check; it can be dangerous. Instead, ask the seller or farmer. They know their varieties. Common sweet varieties in Africa include 'TME 419' and 'TMS 30572'. Bitter varieties include 'TMS 91/02324' and 'TMS 92/0326'. If you are not sure, treat it as bitter and process it well. For baking, sweet cassava is easier because you can peel, wash, and grate it directly. Bitter cassava needs extra steps: soaking, pressing, and fermenting to remove cyanide. The cyanide is in the water, so you must squeeze it out. A simple test: grate a small piece, squeeze the juice, and put a drop on a piece of paper. If it turns blue with a cyanide test kit (available at some agro-shops), it's bitter. But most home bakers don't have kits. So, rely on local knowledge. In many villages, people know which variety is sweet or bitter. Always ask. If you use bitter cassava without proper processing, you can get sick. So, choose sweet cassava for simple baking, or learn to process bitter cassava safely..Trim()

How to peel and wash cassava safely

Peeling cassava is simple, but you must do it safely because the raw root contains cyanide. Start by cutting off both ends of the root with a sharp knife. Then, make a shallow cut along the length of the root, through the skin only. Use your thumb to lift the skin and peel it off. If the skin is thick, use a knife to help. Work on a clean surface, like a wooden board or a mat. Wear gloves if you have them, because the raw juice can irritate your skin. If you don't have gloves, wash your hands with soap after peeling. Never touch your eyes or mouth while peeling. After peeling, cut the root into smaller pieces, about 2 to 3 inches long. Remove any woody core or black spots. Then, wash the pieces in plenty of clean water. Use a large bowl or basin. Rub the pieces with your hands to remove dirt and starch. Change the water at least twice. The water will become milky white. That's the starch. For sweet cassava, you can now grate or boil it. For bitter cassava, you must soak the pieces in water for 24 to 48 hours, changing the water every 6 hours. This removes cyanide. After soaking, drain and squeeze the pieces to remove excess water. You can use a clean cloth or a sieve. Then, wash again with clean water. Always wash your hands, knives, and boards with soap and hot water after handling raw cassava. Do not let children or animals eat the peelings. Dispose of peelings in a pit or burn them. If you feel dizzy or sick while peeling, stop and get fresh air. Cyanide gas can be released when peeling bitter cassava. So, work in a well-ventilated area, like outside or near an open window. With practice, peeling 5 kg of cassava takes about 30 minutes. Stay safe and clean..Trim()

Tools needed for cassava processing on a budget

You don't need expensive machines to start processing cassava for baking. With a few basic tools, you can do everything by hand. First, a sharp knife. A kitchen knife or machete works. Keep it sharp to peel easily. Second, a cutting board or a clean flat stone. Third, a large bowl or basin for washing. Plastic basins are cheap and available in any market. Fourth, a grater. You can use a traditional hand grater, or make one by punching holes in a tin can. But a proper box grater is better. In many African markets, you can buy a cassava grater for a low price. Fifth, a clean cloth or a mesh bag for squeezing. A strong cotton cloth works well. Sixth, a sieve or a basket for draining. Seventh, a drying tray. You can use a clean mat, a wooden board, or a metal sheet. For drying in the sun, a raised platform keeps it clean. Eighth, a mortar and pestle for pounding if you want flour. Or you can take your dried cassava to a local mill. Ninth, a scale for measuring. A simple kitchen scale is fine. Tenth, storage containers. Use airtight jars or plastic buckets with lids. For fermenting bitter cassava, you need a large pot or bucket with a lid. For pressing, you can use a simple wooden press made from two boards and a rope, or just use heavy stones. All these tools can be found locally or made by a carpenter or welder. You don't need electricity. Start small: a knife, basin, grater, and cloth are enough to make cassava flour for your family or small bakery. As your business grows, you can invest in a motorized grater or a hydraulic press. But for now, keep it simple and cheap. Clean your tools after every use to avoid contamination..Trim()

Storing fresh cassava to avoid spoilage

Fresh cassava roots spoil quickly, often within 2 to 3 days after harvest. To avoid spoilage, you must store them properly. The best way is to use them immediately. But if you must store, keep them in a cool, dark, and dry place. Do not leave them in the sun. In hot African climates, cassava can rot in one day if left outside. One method is to bury the roots in slightly moist sand or soil. This keeps them fresh for up to one week. Use a shaded area, like under a tree or in a shed. Another method is to wrap the roots in dry banana leaves or newspaper and keep them in a basket. This works for 3 to 4 days. Do not wash the roots before storing; washing removes the natural protective layer and speeds up spoilage. Only wash just before peeling. If you have a refrigerator, you can store peeled cassava in a plastic bag in the vegetable compartment for up to 5 days. But many bakers don't have fridges. Another option is to process the cassava into flour or dough and store that instead. Dried cassava flour can last for 6 months in an airtight container. To dry, grate or slice the cassava and sun-dry for 2 to 3 days until brittle. Then mill and store. For fresh roots, check them daily. Remove any that show mould or soft spots. One bad root can spoil the others. So, sort them regularly. If you buy in bulk, only buy what you can use in 3 days. In markets, cassava is often sold in large bundles. If you buy a bundle, share with other bakers or process it quickly. You can also freeze grated cassava. Squeeze out excess water, pack in plastic bags, and freeze. It lasts for 3 months. Thaw before use. Remember, spoilage is the biggest loss for cassava bakers. So, plan your purchases and storage carefully..Trim()

Health and safety when handling raw cassava

Raw cassava contains cyanide, a poison. Sweet cassava has small amounts, but bitter cassava has a lot. You must handle it safely to avoid getting sick. The first rule: never eat raw cassava. Always peel, wash, and cook or process it. The cyanide is in the juice. So, when you peel or grate, do it in a well-ventilated area. Open windows or work outside. Cyanide gas can be released, especially from bitter cassava. If you feel dizzy, headache, or nausea, stop and move to fresh air. Wear gloves if possible. If you don't have gloves, wash your hands with soap and water immediately after handling. Do not touch your face, eyes, or mouth with unwashed hands. Keep children away from raw cassava and peelings. Do not let animals eat the peelings; they can get sick too. Dispose of peelings by burning or burying deep. For bitter cassava, you must remove cyanide by soaking, fermenting, and pressing. Soak grated or sliced cassava in water for 24 to 48 hours, changing water every 6 hours. Then squeeze out all the water. The cyanide goes into the water. After processing, cook the cassava thoroughly. Boiling, frying, or baking destroys any remaining cyanide. If you are making flour, ensure the cassava is completely dried and milled. Dry it in the sun for 2 to 3 days. A simple test for cyanide: take a small piece of processed cassava and smell it. If it smells like bitter almonds, it may still have cyanide. Cook it more. Never skip the processing steps for bitter cassava. Also, keep your work area clean. Wash knives, boards, and bowls with soap and hot water. Do not use the same cloth for raw cassava and other foods. If you feel sick after handling cassava, drink plenty of water and seek medical help. Prevention is easy: follow the steps, and you will be safe..Trim()

Module 2 · From Cassava to Flour

Grating cassava using manual and motorized graters

Grating is the first step in turning fresh cassava roots into flour. You must grate the roots within 24 hours of harvest, or they will spoil. Start by peeling the roots with a sharp knife, removing both the outer bark and the inner rind. Wash the peeled roots in clean water to remove dirt and sand. Cut them into chunks that fit your grater..Trim()

For manual grating, you can use a traditional wooden grater with a perforated metal sheet, or a simple hand grater. For larger batches, a motorized grater is better. A small diesel or electric grater can process 100-200 kg per hour. You can buy one or build one using a engine and a drum with sharp teeth..Trim()

Pressing and fermenting cassava mash for flour

After grating, the cassava mash contains about 60-70% water. You must press it to remove excess liquid and reduce cyanide. The traditional method is to put the mash in a woven bag (like a jute sack) and press it under heavy stones or a lever press for 1-2 days. A simple screw press or hydraulic jack press can shorten this to 3-4 hours. Press until the mash feels firm and crumbly, with less than 40% moisture..Trim()

Fermentation happens during pressing. The natural bacteria and enzymes in the mash break down starches and detoxify cyanide. For flour, ferment for 12-24 hours at room temperature (25-30Â°C). In cooler weather, it may take 36-48 hours. You can tell it's ready when the mash smells slightly sour, like fermented dough, and feels dry. If it smells rotten or has mold, throw it away..Trim()

If you want a milder taste, ferment for a shorter time (6-12 hours). For a stronger sour taste, ferment up to 3 days. In West Africa, many people prefer a sour note in their cassava flour, while in East Africa, a milder flour is common. Adjust to your market..Trim()

After pressing, break up the mash with your hands or a wooden paddle. It should be crumbly, not sticky. If it's still wet, press more. Drying is critical: if the flour is not dry enough, it will mold within weeks. The target moisture content is 10-12%. Milling dried cassava into fine flour

Milling turns the dried cassava mash into fine flour. You can use a manual mill (like a corn mill) or a motorized hammer mill or disc mill. Before milling, ensure the dried mash is completely dry. If it's even slightly damp, it will clog the mill and produce lumpy flour. Break large pieces with a pestle or hammer. Sieving cassava flour for consistent texture

Sieving removes coarse particles and ensures your flour is fine and uniform. This is important for baking because coarse flour makes dense, gritty bread. Use a sieve with a mesh size of 0.5 mm to 1 mm. A standard kitchen flour sieve works, or you can make one with a wooden frame and wire mesh. For larger batches, use a rotary sifter or a mechanical shaker..Trim()

Place the sieve over a clean bucket or tray. Pour the milled flour into the sieve in small amounts. Shake the sieve side to side or tap it gently. The fine flour falls through, while coarse particles remain on top. Do not press the flour through with your hands, as this can push coarse bits through. If the flour is damp, it will clog the sieve. In that case, dry it further in the sun for a few hours..Trim()

The coarse particles you remove can be re-milled. Put them back into the mill and grind again. You can repeat this until all flour passes through the sieve. After sieving, you should have a soft, powdery flour. Test by rubbing a pinch between your fingers; it should feel like talcum powder, not sand. If it's still gritty, sieve again with a finer mesh. Packaging and storing cassava flour for baking

Packaging protects your flour from moisture, insects, and contamination. Use clean, dry containers: plastic buckets with tight lids, heavy-duty plastic bags, or woven polypropylene sacks with inner plastic liners. For retail, small 1 kg or 2 kg bags are popular. You can seal plastic bags with a heat sealer or tie them tightly. Label each package with the date and type of flour (e.g., 'Cassava Flour, Fermented, Best Before: [date]')..Trim()

Before packaging, ensure the flour is completely cool and dry. Any warmth will cause condensation inside the package, leading to mold. Fill containers to the top to reduce air space. If using sacks, double-bag them: an inner plastic bag and an outer woven sack. Store in a cool, dry place, away from direct sunlight and strong odors. Module 12 · Growing Your Cassava Baking Business

Reinvesting profits into better equipment

When you start selling cassava bread, you might be using a borrowed oven, a hand grater, and a wooden spoon. That works, but to grow you need to put some of your profit back into tools that save time and improve quality. A good rule: after each sale, set aside 20% of your profit into a 'equipment fund'. For example, if you make 50,000 Naira profit in a month, put 10,000 Naira aside. After five months, you have 50,000 Naira. That can buy a used but working electric grater, which can grate 20 kg of cassava in 10 minutes instead of two hours by hand. .Trim()

.Trim()

Start with the tool that bottlenecks you most. If you spend hours grating, buy a grater. If your oven is small and you can only bake 10 loaves at a time, save for a larger oven. A local welder can make a simple drum oven for 80,000 to 150,000 Naira. That oven can bake 30 loaves at once, tripling your output. .Trim()

.Trim()

Also consider a generator if power cuts stop your baking. A small 2.5 kVA generator costs around 150,000 Naira and can run a mixer and oven. But don't buy everything at once. Make a list of equipment in order of importance. Price each item at your local market or from online sellers like Jumia. Then work towards the first item. .Trim()

.Trim()

When you buy equipment, keep it clean and maintained. A grater that rusts will spoil your cassava. Oil moving parts weekly. Store your oven under a roof. Good tools last years and make your work easier, so you can bake more and earn more. Remember, reinvesting is not spendingâit's building your future..Trim()

Adding new cassava products to your line

Cassava bread is just the beginning. You can add other cassava-based products to increase sales and use up leftover dough or peel. For example, cassava chin-chin: cut small squares of cassava dough, fry them in hot oil, and sell as snacks. A cup of chin-chin sells for 200 Naira, and you can make 20 cups from 5 kg of cassava flour. That's 4,000 Naira from the same cassava you used for bread. .Trim()

.Trim()

Another product is cassava flour for baking. Many people want to buy ready-made cassava flour to bake at home. You can mill dry cassava into fine flour, package it in 1 kg bags, and sell for 800 Naira per bag. If you mill 50 kg, you get about 45 kg of flour after sieving, earning 36,000 Naira. .Trim()

.Trim()

You can also make cassava fufu or gari, but those are common. Instead, try cassava cakes or muffins. Use your bread recipe but add sugar, eggs, and flavorings like vanilla or banana. Bake in small cups and sell for 150 Naira each. They are popular for breakfast or with tea. .Trim()

.Trim()

Start with one new product. Test it with your existing customers. Ask them what they think. If it sells well, add another. Keep your costs low by using the same oven and staff. For example, when you bake bread, put the chin-chin in the oven after bread is done, using residual heat. This saves fuel. .Trim()

.Trim()

Packaging matters. Use clean, clear plastic bags or paper wraps with your business name and phone number. A simple sticker or stamp is enough. New products bring new customers who might also buy your bread. And they increase your income without much extra work..Trim()

Finding partners and suppliers for growth

To grow, you need reliable suppliers of cassava and other ingredients, and partners who can sell your products. Start with cassava. If you buy from the same farmer every week, you can negotiate a lower price. For example, a farmer might sell fresh cassava roots at 30,000 Naira per tonne if you buy regularly, instead of 35,000 Naira for one-off. That saves 5,000 Naira per tonne. Find farmers at local markets or through agricultural extension officers. Visit their farms to check quality. .Trim()

.Trim()

For other ingredients like sugar, salt, yeast, and oil, buy in bulk from wholesalers. A 50 kg bag of sugar costs less per kg than buying 1 kg packets. Team up with other bakers to buy a full bag and share the cost. This is a simple partnership. .Trim()

.Trim()

You can also partner with local shops, schools, or offices to sell your bread. For example, a school might agree to buy 50 loaves every week for their students' breakfast. You deliver fresh every morning. In return, you give them a small discount, like 5%. That's a steady income. .Trim()

.Trim()

Another partner is a transport rider. If you don't have a vehicle, make a deal with a motorcycle or tricycle rider to deliver your products for a fixed fee per trip. For example, 1,000 Naira per delivery within 5 km. They get regular work, you get reliable delivery. .Trim()

.Trim()

Always put agreements in writing, even if simple. Write the quantity, price, and delivery date. Both parties sign. This prevents misunderstandings. Good partners help you grow faster because you can focus on baking while they handle supply and sales..Trim()

Applying for small business grants or loans

When you need money to grow, grants and loans can help. A grant is free money you don't repay. A loan is money you borrow and pay back with interest. Start by looking for grants for small businesses in your country. In Nigeria, for example, the YouWin! program and the Tony Elumelu Foundation offer grants. In Kenya, the Youth Enterprise Development Fund. Search online for 'small business grants for bakers in [your country]'. Also ask at your local government office or business association. .Trim()

.Trim()

To apply, you need a simple business plan. Write one page: what you sell, how much you earn, what you need the money for, and how it will increase your profit. For example: 'I bake 100 loaves a week and earn 40,000 Naira profit. I need 200,000 Naira to buy a larger oven. With it, I can bake 300 loaves and earn 120,000 Naira profit.' Include photos of your products and a list of your equipment. .Trim()

.Trim()

For loans, microfinance banks are good for small amounts. They might lend 50,000 to 500,000 Naira. Interest rates vary, but expect 5-10% per month. Only borrow if you are sure you can repay. Calculate: if you borrow 100,000 Naira at 5% monthly for 6 months, you repay about 120,000 Naira total. Your extra profit from the loan must be more than that. .Trim()

.Trim()

Keep records of all sales and expenses. Lenders want to see that you are serious. A simple notebook is fine. Write down every sale and every purchase. Also, save a small amount each week for repayment. .Trim()

.Trim()

Hiring and training more staff

When orders increase and you can't bake alone, it's time to hire. Start with one person. A good first hire is someone who can help with mixing, grating, and cleaning. You can pay a daily wage, like 2,000 to 3,000 Naira per day, depending on your area. Or a monthly salary of 30,000 to 50,000 Naira. .Trim()

.Trim()

Hire someone you trust. Ask for references from previous employers. If they have no experience, that's okay if they are willing to learn. Train them yourself. Show them how to measure ingredients correctly. For example, 1 kg of cassava flour needs 500 ml of water. Let them practice while you watch. Correct mistakes gently. .Trim()

.Trim()

Write down simple steps for each task. For example: 'Step 1: Wash hands. Step 2: Weigh 2 kg cassava flour. Step 3: Add 1 litre water and mix for 5 minutes.' Post these steps on the wall. This helps your staff remember and reduces errors. .Trim()

.Trim()

As you grow, you may need a second person for sales and delivery. That person can go to markets or shops while you bake. Pay them a small base salary plus a commission for each new customer they bring. For example, 500 Naira per new shop that buys regularly. .Trim()

.Trim()

Treat your staff well. Provide clean drinking water, a place to rest, and pay on time. If they work hard, give a bonus at the end of the month. Happy staff stay longer and work better. Also, train them on food safety: wash hands, wear clean aprons, cover hair. This keeps your products safe and your business reputable. With good staff, you can take a day off and still earn..Trim()

Setting goals for the next 12 months

To grow, you need clear goals. Write them down. For example: 'In 12 months, I will sell 200 loaves per week and earn 80,000 Naira profit per week.' Make your goals specific, measurable, and realistic. Don't say 'I want more money.' Say 'I will increase sales by 20% every three months.' .Trim()

.Trim()

Break your 12-month goal into smaller steps. Every month, set a target. For example, Month 1: buy a used grater. Month 2: add chin-chin to my product line. Month 3: find two new shops to sell my bread. Month 4: hire one helper. Month 5: apply for a loan. Month 6: increase bread production by 50 loaves per week. And so on. .Trim()

.Trim()

Keep a simple chart on your wall. Mark each goal when you achieve it. This motivates you. Also, review your progress every month. If you didn't meet a goal, ask why. Maybe you were sick, or a supplier increased prices. Adjust your plan. Don't give up. .Trim()

.Trim()

Set financial goals too. Aim to save 10% of your profit each month. After 12 months, you'll have a cushion for emergencies or new equipment. Also, aim to reduce waste. If you throw away 5 kg of cassava dough each week, that's 20 kg per month. At 500 Naira per kg, you lose 10,000 Naira. So set a goal to reduce waste to 1 kg per week. .Trim()

.Trim()

Share your goals with a trusted friend or mentor. They can encourage you. Celebrate small wins: when you buy that grater, treat yourself. But then set the next goal. With clear goals, you turn your baking from a job into a growing business..Trim()
