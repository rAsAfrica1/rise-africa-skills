What is Charcoal?

Charcoal is a black, porous material made by heating wood or other organic matter in the absence of oxygen. This process, called pyrolysis, drives off water and volatile compounds, leaving behind almost pure carbon. In Africa, traditional charcoal is made by slowly burning wood under a mound of earth or in a metal kiln. For example, a common method in Ghana uses a earth mound that takes 3-5 days to carbonize 1 tonne of wood, yielding about 250 kg of charcoal. But for drawing, you need a different kind: artist charcoal, which is made from willow, vine, or other soft woods, heated in a sealed retort at around 400Â°C for several hours. The result is a lightweight, brittle stick that leaves a rich black mark on paper..Trim()

That means you can cover a large area quickly, which is why it's great for sketching and shading. It also comes in different hardnesses: soft charcoal is very black and smudgy, while hard charcoal is compressed and holds a sharper point. History of Charcoal in Art

Charcoal has been used for drawing for thousands of years. Some of the earliest known charcoal drawings are on cave walls in Europe and Africa. In Africa, the Apollo 11 Cave in Namibia has charcoal drawings dating back 26,000 years, depicting animals and human figures. These ancient artists used charred sticks from their fires, much like you might use a burnt branch today. But charcoal as a fine art medium really took off during the Renaissance in Europe, when artists like Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo used it for preparatory sketches. They valued it for its speed and expressiveness..Trim()

Charcoal vs. Other Drawing Media

When you draw, you choose a medium based on the effect you want. Charcoal is different from graphite pencil, ink, and pastel. Let's compare them practically. Graphite pencil, the common HB or 2B, is made of graphite and clay. It gives a precise, gray line that is hard to smudge. It's great for detailed work, but it can't produce the deep, velvety blacks of charcoal. Charcoal, on the other hand, is pure carbon and produces a matte black that is much darker. A single stroke of soft charcoal can be as dark as many layers of graphite. However, graphite erases cleanly, while charcoal leaves a stain even after erasing..Trim()

.Trim()

Ink, whether from a pen or brush, is liquid and permanent. It gives sharp, high-contrast lines and is excellent for calligraphy and comics. But ink cannot be blended or smudged easily, and it doesn't offer the range of tones that charcoal does. Pastel, which is pigment mixed with a binder, is similar to charcoal in that it's soft and blendable, but it comes in colors. Charcoal is monochromeâblack, white, and graysâwhich forces you to focus on value and form. That's why many art teachers start students on charcoal before moving to color..Trim()

In terms of tools, charcoal is cheaper than most media. In many African markets, you can buy a box of 12 charcoal sticks for the price of a single graphite pencil. You also need less equipment: a kneaded eraser, a blending stump, and paper with some tooth (texture). Graphite requires sharpeners and erasers that don't smudge. Ink requires pens and brushes that need cleaning. Charcoal is low-maintenance..Trim()

Basic Charcoal Safety and Handling

Charcoal is generally safe, but you must handle it with care. The main risks are dust and skin irritation. Charcoal dust is fine and can be inhaled, which over time may irritate your lungs. In a small studio in Accra or Lagos, where ventilation might be poor, this is a real concern. Always work in a well-ventilated area. Open a window or work outdoors if possible. If you're using charcoal powder, wear a dust mask (a simple N95 or even a cloth mask helps). Avoid blowing on your drawing to remove dust; instead, tap it off gently or use a drafting brush..Trim()

Charcoal can also dry out your skin. The dust absorbs natural oils, leading to cracked fingertips. If you have sensitive skin, wear thin cotton gloves or apply a barrier cream before drawing. Wash your hands with soap and water after each session. Don't eat or drink while working, as you might ingest charcoal dust. Also, be careful with fixative sprays. These are often aerosol cans containing solvents. Use them outdoors or in a spray booth, and hold your breath while spraying. A cheaper alternative is hairspray, but it's not archival and can yellow over time. For student work, it's fine..Trim()

Storage is important. Charcoal sticks are brittle. Keep them in a rigid box with foam or cloth padding. If you make your own charcoal, ensure it's fully cooled before handling. Buy artist-grade charcoal from art supply stores; in Africa, look for brands like Derwent, Faber-Castell, or local suppliers like Kenafric in Kenya. Overview of Charcoal Types

Not all charcoal is the same. For drawing, you'll encounter several types, each with different properties. The main categories are willow charcoal, vine charcoal, compressed charcoal, charcoal pencils, and charcoal powder. Let's go through them. Willow charcoal is made from willow branches. It's soft, light, and produces a gray-black line that erases easily. It's perfect for laying in initial sketches because you can adjust it without leaving heavy marks. Vine charcoal is similar but made from grapevines; it's slightly harder and darker. Both are fragile and come in thin sticks. In Africa, you might find locally made charcoal from acacia or eucalyptus, but these are often harder and more suitable for cooking than drawing. For art, stick to imported or specialized brands..Trim()

Compressed charcoal is made from charcoal powder mixed with a binder like gum arabic and pressed into sticks. It's much harder and darker than willow or vine. It comes in different grades: soft, medium, and hard. Soft compressed charcoal is very black and smudgy; hard is less messy and holds a point. You can sharpen it with sandpaper. This is the type most artists use for bold, dark areas. Charcoal pencils are compressed charcoal encased in wood, like a regular pencil. They come in grades from 2B to 6B, with higher numbers being softer and darker. They're great for details and controlled lines. You can sharpen them with a knife or sharpener, but be carefulâthe lead is brittle..Trim()

Charcoal powder is pure charcoal ground into a fine dust. You apply it with a brush, a sponge, or your finger. It's excellent for covering large areas quickly, like backgrounds. You can also use it to tone your paper before drawing. A little goes a long way. Finally, there's white charcoal, which is actually not charcoal but a white pigment (often calcium carbonate) mixed with a binder. It's used for highlights on toned paper. It doesn't erase like black charcoal..Trim()

Module 2 · Willow Charcoal

Properties of Willow Charcoal

Willow charcoal is made from the wood of willow trees (Salix species), which grow in many parts of Africa, especially in wetter highland areas like Kenya's Rift Valley and parts of Ethiopia. To make it, willow branches are cut, stripped of bark, and heated in a sealed container without oxygen (a process called pyrolysis) until they turn into pure carbon. The result is a soft, lightweight stick that is easy to break and leaves a rich, dark mark. Unlike compressed charcoal, which is made from charcoal powder mixed with a binder, willow charcoal is natural and contains no additives. This means it behaves differently: it is softer, dustier, and produces a velvety, matte black line..Trim()

.Trim()

Willow charcoal comes in various thicknesses, from thin 2-3 mm sticks to thick 10-15 mm sticks. Thin sticks are great for detailed lines, while thick sticks cover large areas quickly. The charcoal is very brittle, so store it in a rigid box with tissue or cloth padding to prevent breakage. In Africa's humid climates, moisture can make willow charcoal crumble; keep it in an airtight container with a few grains of silica gel or dry rice to absorb dampness..Trim()

.Trim()

One key property is its excellent blendability. Because it is soft, it smudges easily with a finger, a paper stump, or a cloth. This makes it ideal for creating smooth gradients and atmospheric effects. However, it also means your drawing can become messy if you rest your hand on the paper. Use a spare sheet under your hand to avoid unintended smudges. Willow charcoal also erases more easily than compressed charcoal, but it stains the paper slightly, leaving a grey residue. On rough paper, it catches the texture and creates a grainy look; on smooth paper, it glides and produces solid dark tones..Trim()

.Trim()

Willow charcoal is also very economical. A bundle of 20-30 sticks can cost as little as 500 Kenyan shillings or 2000 Nigerian naira, and you can make your own by burning willow branches in a metal tin with a small hole. It is a favourite for sketching because it allows rapid, expressive work. It is not as dark as compressed charcoal or charcoal powder, but its range from light grey to deep black is perfect for studies and finished drawings alike. In hot, dry conditions, it may become slightly harder, so store it away from direct sun. Overall, willow charcoal is a versatile, forgiving medium that suits beginners and professionals..Trim()

Making Marks with Willow Charcoal

To make marks with willow charcoal, you need the right surface and a few simple tools. Use paper with a bit of tooth (texture), like 120-150 gsm cartridge paper or Ingres paper. Smooth printer paper will make the charcoal slide and produce thin, scratchy lines. Tape your paper to a wooden board or a flat table with masking tape so it doesn't move. Break a thick willow stick in half to create a chisel edge, and sharpen a thin stick with sandpaper for detail. Keep a kneaded eraser, a soft brush, and a cloth nearby..Trim()

.Trim()

Hold the charcoal like a pencil for controlled lines, or like a brush for broad strokes. For a fine line, use the sharpened tip and press lightly. For a bold line, drag the side of the stick. Vary pressure: light pressure gives grey, heavy pressure gives black. Try different angles: vertical lines for structure, diagonal for energy, and circles for shading. Practice making a page of marks: straight lines, curves, dots, and hatches. In Africa, many learners start by drawing local subjects like a calabash, a clay pot, or a baobab tree. Use quick strokes to capture the shape, then refine..Trim()

.Trim()

Willow charcoal responds well to speed. Fast strokes create broken, textured lines; slow strokes create solid, dark lines. You can also twist the stick as you draw to get a varied line. For a broad area, lay the stick flat and sweep it across the paper. To make a gradient, gradually reduce pressure as you move. If the charcoal feels too scratchy, rub it on a piece of scrap paper first to wear down the sharp edges..Trim()

.Trim()

Experiment with different hand positions. Drawing from the wrist gives small marks; drawing from the shoulder gives large, expressive marks. Stand up if possible, so you can move your whole arm. This is how you get lively, confident lines. Remember that willow charcoal is fragile: don't press too hard or it will snap. If it breaks, don't throw it away; use the pieces for different effects. After making marks, blow away dust or brush it gently with a soft brush. Avoid wiping with your hand, as it will smudge. With practice, you will develop a personal vocabulary of marks that can suggest texture, light, and form. This is the foundation for all charcoal drawing..Trim()

Creating Gradients with Willow Charcoal

Gradients are smooth transitions from dark to light, and willow charcoal excels at them because it smudges so easily. To create a gradient, start by laying down a dark area of charcoal using the side of a thick stick. Then, use a paper stump, a soft cloth, or your finger to blend the charcoal outward, gradually reducing pressure as you move into the lighter area. Work in one direction to avoid muddying. For a very smooth gradient, use a dry brush to lightly sweep the charcoal dust across the paper..Trim()

A common exercise is the value scale: draw a rectangle and divide it into five or six boxes. Fill the first box with the darkest charcoal you can make (press hard, layer several times). Leave the last box white. In the middle boxes, create even steps of grey by blending. This teaches you to control pressure and blending. In Africa, where sunlight is strong, observing how shadows fall on objects like a mango or a woven basket can help you see subtle value changes. Practice making a gradient from black to white on a strip of paper, then try a spherical object like an orange, with a highlight, mid-tone, and shadow..Trim()

Willow charcoal is not as dark as compressed charcoal, so for very dark areas, you may need to build up layers. Let each layer sit for a moment, then blend. If you want a crisp edge, use a kneaded eraser to lift out highlights after blending. For a soft edge, blend with a clean finger. Remember that blending with your finger adds natural oils that can make the charcoal less erasable, so use a paper stump for archival work. Also, work from light to dark: start with light tones and gradually add darker layers. This prevents the drawing from becoming too dark too quickly..Trim()

With patience, you can achieve a full range of tones from the faintest grey to deep black, all with a single stick of willow charcoal. This skill is essential for realistic shading and atmospheric perspective..Trim()

Erasing and Lifting Willow Charcoal

Erasing and lifting are not just for correcting mistakes; they are creative techniques that add highlights and texture. Willow charcoal is relatively easy to erase because it sits on the surface of the paper. You can use a kneaded eraser, a hard vinyl eraser, or even a piece of bread (a traditional method in many African art schools). A kneaded eraser is best because it can be shaped to a point for fine lines or flattened for broad areas. To lift charcoal, gently press the eraser onto the paper and pull up. Don't rub, as that can damage the paper and grind charcoal into the fibres..Trim()

.Trim()

For a soft highlight, use a kneaded eraser to dab at the area. For a sharp highlight, shape the eraser into a wedge and draw a line. You can also use a brush to sweep away charcoal dust, which lightens the area without erasing. A stiff brush, like a stencil brush, can create texture by removing charcoal from the peaks of the paper. Another method is to use a clean cloth or tissue to wipe out large areas, such as clouds or light reflections. In Africa, many artists use a piece of soft white bread rolled into a ball to lift charcoal; the bread picks up the charcoal without smudging..Trim()

.Trim()

If you want to erase completely back to white paper, use a vinyl eraser. However, willow charcoal often leaves a grey stain, especially on rough paper. To minimize staining, use a light touch and avoid pressing the charcoal too hard into the paper. If you need a pure white highlight, you can use white chalk or pastel on top, but that changes the medium. For most sketches, a slight stain is acceptable and can add depth..Trim()

.Trim()

Practice erasing on a test piece: make a dark patch, then use different erasers to see the effects. Try lifting out a window in a dark building, or a sun in a sky. You can also use masking tape to create crisp edges: tape off an area, draw over it, then remove the tape to reveal a clean edge. Remember that erasing works best when the charcoal is freshly applied. If it has been sitting for days, it may have set slightly. Always keep your eraser clean by kneading it; if it gets too dirty, stretch it and fold it. With practice, erasing becomes a powerful tool for creating light and form..Trim()

Combining Willow Charcoal with Other Media

Willow charcoal is versatile and can be combined with many other media to create mixed-media drawings. One of the most common combinations is with white chalk or white pastel pencil. This is called a deux-couleurs technique: you work on toned paper (such as grey or tan) with black charcoal for shadows and white for highlights. This method is fast and effective for portraits and still lifes. In Africa, you can find toned paper or make your own by mixing a little acrylic or coffee with water and brushing it onto cartridge paper. Let it dry before drawing..Trim()

.Trim()

Another combination is willow charcoal with graphite pencil. Graphite gives sharp, precise lines, while charcoal provides soft, smoky tones. Use graphite for fine details like whiskers or thin branches, and charcoal for shading. Be careful: graphite is shiny and charcoal is matte, so they can look mismatched. Use graphite sparingly and blend charcoal over it to unify. You can also combine willow charcoal with coloured soft pastels. The charcoal provides a strong dark, while pastels add colour. Work charcoal first, then pastel on top. Avoid over-blending, or colours will become muddy..Trim()

Willow charcoal also works well with water. If you brush water over a charcoal drawing, it will dissolve and create a wash, similar to ink. This is called a charcoal wash. Use a soft brush and clean water. You can create gradients and soft edges by wetting the charcoal and spreading it. However, once wet, the charcoal is permanent and cannot be erased. Introduction to Powdered Charcoal

Powdered charcoal, also called charcoal dust or charcoal powder, is exactly what it sounds like: charcoal that has been crushed into fine particles. Unlike solid sticks or lumps, powdered charcoal is loose and can be applied in many ways. In Africa, powdered charcoal is often a byproduct of making charcoal or is produced by crushing leftover pieces. You can make your own by taking charcoal lumps and grinding them with a mortar and pestle, or by placing them in a strong cloth bag and crushing them with a hammer or stone. For a finer powder, sift it through a kitchen sieve. Store your powder in an airtight container, like a glass jar with a tight lid, to keep it dry and free from dirt..Trim()

.Trim()

Powdered charcoal is incredibly versatile. It can be used for drawing, shading, and creating textures. Because it is loose, you can apply it with your fingers, a brush, a cloth, or even a sponge. It is also easy to blend and smudge, making it perfect for soft gradients and atmospheric effects. However, it can be messy, so work on a covered surface and wear old clothes. In many African markets, you can buy charcoal powder cheaply from vendors who sell charcoal for cooking. If you cannot find it, simply make your own from charcoal sticks..Trim()

.Trim()

One key property of powdered charcoal is its intensity. A little goes a long way. You can create dark, rich blacks or light, subtle greys depending on how much you apply and how much you blend. It is also erasable to some extent; you can lift it with a kneaded eraser or a piece of bread. This makes it forgiving for beginners. Applying Powdered Charcoal

Applying powdered charcoal is simple but requires control. Start with a dry, clean surface. You can work on paper, cardboard, or even primed canvas. For best results, use paper with some texture, like watercolor paper or charcoal paper. If you are using thin paper, tape it down to a board to prevent curling. Pour a small amount of powder into a shallow dish or saucer. Never dip your fingers directly into the main container; that contaminates the powder. There are several ways to apply the powder. The most direct is with your fingers. Dip a finger into the powder and rub it onto the paper. You can create broad strokes or small dabs. For finer control, use a brush. A soft brush, like a makeup brush or a large watercolor brush, picks up powder and lets you dust it onto the surface. To create darker areas, apply more powder and press it into the paper with your finger or a blending tool. To lighten, simply use less powder or blend it out. Powdered charcoal is excellent for creating a variety of textures. Because it is loose, you can manipulate it in many ways to mimic rough surfaces, soft clouds, or grainy earth. Blending is the key to smooth transitions and realistic shading with powdered charcoal. Unlike pencil, powdered charcoal blends very easily, which can be both a blessing and a curse. Another method is to use a workable fixative, which allows you to continue drawing after it dries. This is useful if you want to build layers. For a final fix, use a non-workable fixative. Types of Charcoal Paper

Charcoal paper comes in three main types: laid, wove, and textured. Laid paper has parallel lines from the manufacturing process, giving a ribbed surface that grabs charcoal dust and creates a broken, linear texture. Wove paper is smoother and uniform, good for detailed work but holds less charcoal. Textured paper, often called 'tooth,' has a pronounced grain that catches charcoal particles, allowing for rich darks and expressive marks. Texture and tooth are the two properties that determine how charcoal interacts with paper. Hatching and Cross-Hatching

Hatching is the simplest way to create value with charcoal. You draw parallel lines close together to make a darker area, or spread them apart for a lighter area. Use a stick of compressed charcoal or a charcoal pencil. Hold it like a pencil for control, or like a brush for looser marks. On newsprint or cartridge paper, start with light pressure. .Trim()

Cross-hatching adds a second set of lines at an angle, usually 90 degrees, over the first set. This builds darker values without pressing harder. For example, draw vertical lines, then horizontal lines over them. The overlap creates a grid that reads as a mid-tone. Add a third set at 45 degrees for deep shadows. Use hatching for quick sketches of market scenes, trees, or faces. Keep your lines consistent in direction. For a rounded object like a calabash, curve the lines to follow the form.
