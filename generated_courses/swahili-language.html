Greetings and Introductions

In Swahili, greetings are more than just wordsâthey are the key to opening doors in East Africa. Whether you are in Nairobi, Dar es Salaam, or Kampala, starting with a proper greeting shows respect and builds trust. The most common greeting is "Jambo!" which means "Hello!" but it is often used with tourists. For a more authentic feel, use "Habari?" (How are you?) and respond with "Nzuri" (Good) or "Salama" (Peaceful). Another essential is "Shikamoo" to elders, meaning "I hold your feet" as a sign of respect; the elder replies "Marahaba." For peers, "Mambo?" (What's up?) and "Poa" (Cool) are popular among youth. When introducing yourself, say "Jina langu ni..." (My name is...). To ask someone's name, say "Jina lako ni nani?" If you want to know where someone is from, ask "Unatoka wapi?" and answer with "Natoka..." followed by your country. For example, "Natoka Kenya." A handshake is common, but wait for a woman to extend her hand first. In rural areas, you might be greeted with "Hujambo?" (Are you fine?) and you reply "Sijambo" (I am fine). Remember, taking time to greet properly is never wasted; it is an investment in relationships. The Swahili Alphabet and Pronunciation

Swahili uses the Latin alphabet, but with a few tweaks. There are 26 letters, but some are pronounced differently than in English. The good news: Swahili is phoneticâevery letter is pronounced exactly as it is written. Vowels are pure and short: a (ah), e (eh), i (ee), o (oh), u (oo). So "mama" is mah-mah, not may-may. Consonants are mostly like English, but watch out for these: 'g' is always hard as in "go," never soft; 'j' is pronounced like the 'j' in "jump"; 'ch' is like 'ch' in "church"; 'ny' is like the 'ny' in "canyon"; and 'ng' at the beginning of a word is like the 'ng' in "sing" but with a hard 'g' soundâpractice saying "ng'ombe" (cow). The letter 'r' is rolled slightly, similar to Spanish. There are no silent letters. Stress usually falls on the second-to-last syllable: "ki-TA-bu" (book), "ha-BA-ri" (news). To practice, say these words aloud: "asante" (thank you), "karibu" (welcome), "safari" (journey), "lala" (sleep). A common mistake is to pronounce vowels as diphthongs; keep them short and crisp. For example, "bei" (price) is beh-ee, not bay. Listen to Swahili radio or music to train your ear. Try recording yourself and compare with native speakers. With consistent practice, you'll master the sounds quickly..Trim()

Basic Numbers 1-10

Numbers are essential for shopping, bargaining, and telling time. In Swahili, numbers 1 to 10 are straightforward. Here they are: 1 - moja, 2 - mbili, 3 - tatu, 4 - nne, 5 - tano, 6 - sita, 7 - saba, 8 - nane, 9 - tisa, 10 - kumi. Notice that 'moja' starts with 'm', and 'mbili' has a slight hum at the startâpractice saying 'm' and 'b' together. When counting objects, you use different prefixes depending on the noun class, but for now, just learn the basic forms. To say "one book," you say "kitabu kimoja" (ki- prefix for kitabu). But for numbers above one, the number itself often stays the same: "vitabu vitatu" (three books). For money, you'll hear "shilingi moja" (one shilling) or "shilingi kumi" (ten shillings). Common Polite Expressions

Politeness is highly valued in Swahili culture. Using the right expressions shows respect and opens hearts. Start with "Tafadhali" (Please) and "Asante" (Thank you). If someone helps you, say "Asante sana" (Thank you very much). The response is "Karibu" (You're welcome) or "Hakuna matata" (No problem). When you need to interrupt or get attention, say "Samahani" (Excuse me/Sorry). If you accidentally bump someone, "Samahani" works, but for a more sincere apology, say "Nisamehe" (Forgive me). To say "I'm sorry" for a mistake, use "Pole" (pronounced po-leh) when someone is in trouble or has had a misfortune; it means "Sorry" in a sympathetic way. For example, if someone trips, you say "Pole." When leaving, "Kwaheri" (Goodbye) is standard, but "Tutaonana" (See you later) is friendlier. To say "Good morning," use "Habari ya asubuhi?" (News of the morning?) and reply "Nzuri" (Good). In the evening, "Habari ya jioni?" For a polite request, start with "Naomba..." (I request...). For example, "Naomba maji" (I request water). To say "You're welcome" after thanks, you can also say "Karibu tena" (Welcome again). When someone says "Asante" for a gift, you say "Karibu" or "Bila shaka" (Of course). Practice these in daily interactions: at the market, in a matatu, at a restaurant. A simple "Tafadhali" and "Asante" will make people smile and treat you warmly. Remember, tone mattersâspeak softly and sincerely..Trim()

Self-Introduction: Name and Origin

Introducing yourself confidently is the first step to making friends in Swahili. Start with a greeting: "Jambo!" or "Habari!" Then say "Jina langu ni..." (My name is...). For example, "Jina langu ni Amina." To ask someone's name, say "Jina lako ni nani?" (What is your name?). If you want to be more formal, "Unaitwa nani?" (What are you called?). After they answer, you can say "Nimefurahi kukutana nawe" (I am happy to meet you). Next, share where you are from: "Natoka..." (I come from...). For example, "Natoka Nigeria" or "Natoka Tanzania." To ask "Where are you from?" say "Unatoka wapi?" If you want to know where someone lives, ask "Unaishi wapi?" (Where do you live?) and answer "Ninaishi..." (I live in...). You can also mention your profession: "Mimi ni mwalimu" (I am a teacher) or "Mimi ni mwanafunzi" (I am a student). To ask about someone's work, say "Unafanya kazi gani?" (What work do you do?). A full introduction might sound like: "Habari! Jina langu ni John. Natoka Uganda. Mimi ni mhandisi. Nimefurahi kukutana nawe." Practice this with a friend, swapping roles. In business settings, you might add your company: "Ninafanya kazi katika kampuni ya..." (I work at ... company). Remember to use "na" for "and" and "lakini" for "but" as you expand. Also, note that in Swahili, the verb comes with the subject prefix, so "natoka" means "I come from," "unatoka" means "you come from." Practice by recording yourself and listening for fluency. Soon, you'll introduce yourself like a native..Trim()

Cultural Tips: Greeting Etiquette

Greeting etiquette in Swahili culture is rich and varies across regions. In East Africa, greetings are not rushed; they are a ritual that shows respect and builds community. Always greet before asking for anything. A common sequence: "Shikamoo" to an elder, who replies "Marahaba." Then "Habari yako?" (How are you?) and reply "Nzuri." For peers, "Mambo?" and "Poa." Handshakes are common, but in some Muslim areas, men and women do not shake hands; instead, a woman may place her hand on her chest and nod. In rural areas, you might see people bow slightly. When greeting a group, greet each person individually, starting with the eldest. If you are eating, greet those present but don't interrupt a meal; wait until they finish. In business, a handshake is appropriate, but always greet everyone in the room before sitting. Titles matter: use "Bwana" (Mr.) or "Bi" (Ms.) before a surname. For elders, "Mzee" (elder) or "Mama" (mother) is respectful. When someone greets you, respond warmly and ask about their family: "Habari ya familia?" (How is the family?). This shows you care. If you are visiting a home, bring a small gift and greet everyone. In Swahili, there is a saying: "Mtoto umleavyo ndivyo akuavyo" (As you raise a child, so they grow). Greeting is part of raising respectful children. Also, avoid using left hand to give or receive items; use right hand or both. When leaving, say "Kwaheri" to each person. These small gestures go a long way. Practice with locals, and they will appreciate your effort..Trim()

Days of the Week and Months

In Swahili, the days of the week are based on a seven-day cycle, but the week starts on Saturday. The days are: Jumamosi (Saturday), Jumapili (Sunday), Jumatatu (Monday), Jumanne (Tuesday), Jumatano (Wednesday), Alhamisi (Thursday), and Ijumaa (Friday). Notice that most days start with 'Juma' which means 'week'. Jumamosi means 'first day of the week', Jumapili means 'second day', and so on. Alhamisi and Ijumaa are borrowed from Arabic. To say 'today is Monday', you say 'Leo ni Jumatatu'. 'Tomorrow is Tuesday' is 'Kesho ni Jumanne'. 'Yesterday was Sunday' is 'Jana ilikuwa Jumapili'. Cultural Notes: Family Structure

In African Swahili-speaking communities, family structure is often extended, not nuclear. A typical household may include grandparents, parents, children, uncles, aunts, and cousins. In rural Tanzania, 60% of households are extended families. The eldest male, often 'baba mkubwa' (big father) or 'mzee', makes key decisions. Respect is shown through language: use 'shikamoo' to elders, reply 'marahaba'. Children are raised by the community: 'Mtoto ni wa jamii' (A child belongs to the community). This means neighbors can discipline or praise a child. In Swahili, kinship terms reflect this: 'baba mdogo' (small father) means uncle, 'mama mdogo' (small mother) means aunt. Marriage is often arranged or approved by families. Bride price ('mahari') is common: in Kenya, it can range from 10 cows to 100,000 KES. In Tanzania, it might be 5 to 20 cows. The groom's family pays to the bride's family. This creates a bond between families. Polygamy is practiced in some Muslim communities, with up to 4 wives allowed under Islamic law. But monogamy is common among Christians. In urban areas like Nairobi or Dar es Salaam, nuclear families are becoming more common, but ties to the village remain strong. During holidays like Eid or Christmas, families gather. The 'shamba' (farm) is a family asset. Elders are cared for by children; nursing homes are rare. To show respect, use plural for elders: 'wazee' not 'mzee' when addressing. For example, 'Wazee, habari za asubuhi?' (Elders, good morning?) Also, family names carry meaning: 'Juma' means born on Friday, 'Amina' means trustworthy. When introducing your family, mention your tribe: 'Mimi ni Mswahili' (I am Swahili). Common Verbs: Daily Actions

In Swahili, everyday actions are expressed with verbs that you will use constantly. Start with these ten: kula (to eat), kunywa (to drink), kulala (to sleep), kuamka (to wake up), kwenda (to go), kuja (to come), kufanya kazi (to work), kusoma (to read/study), kuandika (to write), and kupika (to cook). Notice that all infinitives begin with 'ku-'. To make a simple command or a present tense statement, you remove 'ku-' and add the correct subject prefix. For example, 'Ninakula' means 'I eat' or 'I am eating'. The prefix 'ni-' means 'I'. For 'you' (singular), use 'u-': 'Unakula' = you eat. For 'he/she', use 'a-': 'Anakula' = he/she eats. For 'we', use 'tu-': 'Tunakula' = we eat. For 'you' (plural), use 'm-': 'Mnakula' = you all eat. For 'they', use 'wa-': 'Wanakula' = they eat. Practice these with each verb. To make the negative, change the prefix: 'si-' for I, 'hu-' for you, 'ha-' for he/she, 'hatu-' for we, 'ham-' for you all, 'hawa-' for they. So 'Sili' = I don't eat. 'Hakunywi' = he doesn't drink. Present Tense Verb Conjugation

Swahili present tense is formed by combining a subject prefix, the tense marker '-na-', and the verb stem. The formula is: Subject Prefix + 'na' + Verb Stem. For example, 'kupika' (to cook) becomes 'ninapika' (I cook), 'unapika' (you cook), 'anapika' (he/she cooks), 'tunapika' (we cook), 'mnapika' (you all cook), 'wanapika' (they cook). The subject prefixes are: ni- (I), u- (you singular), a- (he/she), tu- (we), m- (you plural), wa- (they). Note that for 'I' and 'we', the prefix is 'ni-' and 'tu-', but before vowels they may change slightly; however, with '-na-' they remain consistent. For negative present, replace '-na-' with '-i-' and change the subject prefix for some persons: si- (I), hu- (you), ha- (he/she), hatu- (we), ham- (you all), hawa- (they). So 'sipiki' (I don't cook), 'hupiki' (you don't cook), 'hapiki' (he/she doesn't cook), 'hatupiki' (we don't cook), 'hampiki' (you all don't cook), 'hawapiki' (they don't cook). The verb stem may change slightly in the negative; for example, 'kula' becomes 'li' in 'sili' (I don't eat). Practice with common verbs: 'kusoma' (to read) -> 'ninasoma' (I read), 'kusikiliza' (to listen) -> 'unasikiliza' (you listen), 'kuandika' (to write) -> 'anaandika' (he writes). To ask questions, simply use the same form with a rising intonation: 'Unasoma?' (Are you reading?). To answer yes, say 'Ndiyo, ninasoma.' No: 'Hapana, sisomi.' Use these patterns to talk about what you do regularly. For example, 'Kila siku, ninaamka saa mbili, ninasali, na ninakunywa chai.' (Every day, I wake up at 8, pray, and drink tea.) With practice, conjugation becomes automatic..Trim()

Simple Past Tense

In Swahili, the simple past tense is formed by inserting the infix -li- between the subject prefix and the verb root. For example, 'I ate' is nilikula (ni- I, -li- past, -kula eat). 'You ate' is ulikula. 'He/she ate' is alikula. The subject prefixes are: ni- (I), u- (you singular), a- (he/she), tu- (we), m- (you plural), wa- (they). So 'we ate' is tulikula, 'you all ate' is mlikula, and 'they ate' is walikula. To make the negative past, change -li- to -ku- and add the negative prefix ha- before the subject prefix, except for 'I' which becomes si-. For example, 'I did not eat' is sikula (si- negative I, -ku- past negative, -kula eat). 'You did not eat' is hukula. 'He did not eat' is hakula. Practice with common verbs: kunywa (to drink) -> nilinywa (I drank), kusoma (to read) -> nilisoma (I read), kuandika (to write) -> niliandika (I wrote). Use these in daily African contexts: 'Nilikula ugali na sukuma wiki jana' (I ate ugali and collard greens yesterday). 'Alisoma kitabu cha Kiswahili' (He read a Swahili book). 'Tulinywa chai asubuhi' (We drank tea in the morning). To ask questions, use the same structure but with a rising intonation or question word. For example, 'Ulikula?' (Did you eat?) or 'Ulifanya nini jana?' (What did you do yesterday?). Remember that time expressions like jana (yesterday), juzi (day before yesterday), and asubuhi (morning) often appear with past tense. Practice by saying five things you did yesterday using -li-. For instance, 'Niliamka saa mbili asubuhi' (I woke up at 8 AM), 'Nilikunywa chai' (I drank tea), 'Nilifanya kazi shambani' (I worked in the farm), 'Nilipika wali' (I cooked rice), 'Nililala saa nne usiku' (I slept at 10 PM). This tense is essential for telling stories and reporting events. In African markets, you might hear 'Nilinunua mahindi kwa shilingi elfu tano' (I bought maize for 5000 shillings). Keep practicing with verbs you use daily..Trim()

Talking About Past Events

When talking about past events in Swahili, you need to combine the past tense with specific time markers and details. Start by identifying the event and when it happened. Use jana (yesterday), juzi (day before yesterday), wiki iliyopita (last week), mwezi uliopita (last month), or mwaka uliopita (last year). For example, 'Jana nilikwenda sokoni' (Yesterday I went to the market). To add more information, use the past tense consistently. 'Nilinunua nyanya, vitunguu, na pilipili' (I bought tomatoes, onions, and peppers). 'Nilikutana na rafiki yangu' (I met my friend). 'Tulizungumza kwa saa moja' (We talked for one hour). To describe a series of past events, use the past tense for each verb. 'Nilifika nyumbani, nilipumzika, kisha nilipika chakula' (I arrived home, rested, then cooked food). For negative past events, use the -ku- infix with negative prefixes. 'Sikuenda kazini jana kwa sababu nilikuwa mgonjwa' (I did not go to work yesterday because I was sick). 'Hakufika kwenye mkutano' (He did not arrive at the meeting). To ask about past events, use question words like lini (when), nini (what), wapi (where), and nani (who). 'Ulifanya nini jana?' Making Predictions and Plans

In Swahili, you use the future tense -ta- to make predictions and plans. Predictions are about what you think will happen, while plans are about what you intend to do. For predictions, you can use words like labda (maybe), pengine (perhaps), or bila shaka (without doubt). For example, 'Labda mvua itanyesha kesho' (Maybe it will rain tomorrow). 'Bila shaka timu yetu itashinda' (Without doubt our team will win). 'Pengine bei zitapanda' (Perhaps prices will rise). For plans, you often use the future tense with time expressions. 'Kesho nitakwenda kazini' (Tomorrow I will go to work). 'Wiki ijayo tutaanza mradi mpya' (Next week we will start a new project). 'Mwaka ujao nitasafiri kwenda Uganda' (Next year I will travel to Uganda). To express intention, you can also use the verb 'kusudi' (to intend) or 'kupanga' (to plan). 'Nakusudia kufungua duka' (I intend to open a shop). 'Tunapanga kujenga nyumba' (We plan to build a house). In African business contexts, making plans is crucial. For example, a farmer might say, 'Nitapanda mahindi mwezi ujao' (I will plant maize next month). A trader might say, 'Nitaagiza bidhaa kutoka Dubai' (I will order goods from Dubai). Time Expressions: Yesterday, Tomorrow

Time expressions are essential for using past and future tenses correctly. In Swahili, the most common time expressions are jana (yesterday) for past and kesho (tomorrow) for future. Other past time expressions include juzi (day before yesterday), wiki iliyopita (last week), mwezi uliopita (last month), mwaka uliopita (last year), asubuhi (morning), mchana (afternoon), jioni (evening), and usiku (night). For future, we have kesho kutwa (day after tomorrow), wiki ijayo (next week), mwezi ujao (next month), mwaka ujao (next year), and also asubuhi, mchana, jioni, usiku but with future context. When using these expressions, the tense of the verb must match. For past, use -li-: 'Jana nilikwenda sokoni' (Yesterday I went to the market). For future, use -ta-: 'Kesho nitakwenda sokoni' (Tomorrow I will go to the market). Note that some time expressions like asubuhi, mchana, jioni, usiku can be used with both past and future depending on the tense. For example, 'Asubuhi nilikunywa chai' (In the morning I drank tea) vs 'Asubuhi nitakunywa chai' (In the morning I will drink tea). To be more specific, you can combine: 'Jana asubuhi' (Yesterday morning), 'Kesho jioni' (Tomorrow evening). Also, 'saa' is used for clock time: 'saa mbili asubuhi' (8 AM), 'saa nne usiku' (10 PM). In African contexts, people often use these expressions in daily conversations. For example, a farmer might say, 'Jana nililima shamba, kesho nitapanda mbegu' (Yesterday I plowed the field, tomorrow I will plant seeds). A trader might say, 'Wiki iliyopita niliuza bidhaa nyingi, wiki ijayo nitaagiza zaidi' (Last week I sold many goods, next week I will order more).
