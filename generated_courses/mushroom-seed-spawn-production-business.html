Identifying Common Contaminants: Molds, Bacteria, and Viruses

In mushroom spawn production, contaminants are your biggest enemy. You need to spot them early. The most common are molds like Trichoderma (green mold), Aspergillus (black mold), and Penicillium (blue-green mold). These appear as fuzzy or powdery patches on grain or substrate. Trichoderma starts white but turns green within 2-3 days. Bacteria often show as slimy, wet spots, sour smells, or cloudy liquid in jars. Viruses are harder to see; they cause slow growth, deformed mushrooms, or sudden death of mycelium. In Africa, high humidity and warm temperatures speed up contamination. For example, in Nigeria or Kenya, spawn runs often get contaminated within 24-48 hours if sterile technique is poor. Use your nose: a sour or rotten smell means bacteria. Use your eyes: any color other than white (the mycelium) is suspect. Green, black, orange, or pink patches are molds. Also watch for 'wet spot' bacteria that make grain look greasy. Viruses are rare in small-scale spawn but can come from infected cultures. Always isolate any jar that looks different. A simple magnifying glass or phone camera zoom helps. Remember: healthy mycelium is white, thick, and smells like fresh mushrooms. Anything else is a red flag. Train yourself to check jars daily. Early detection saves entire batches..Trim()

Preventive Measures: Sterile Technique and Hygiene

Prevention is cheaper than cure. In spawn production, you must work clean. Start with a clean room or still air box (SAB). A simple SAB can be made from a plastic storage box with two arm holes. Diagnosing Slow or Failed Colonization

When your spawn jars or bags sit for days without white mycelium spreading, something is wrong. First, check temperature. Most mushroom species colonize best between 24-27Â°C. In many African regions, ambient temperatures can exceed 30Â°C, which slows or kills mycelium. Use a thermometer and if needed, move bags to a cooler room or use a fan. Second, examine moisture. If the substrate is too dry, mycelium stalls. You want field capacity: squeeze a handful and only a few drops come out. If too wet, anaerobic bacteria grow. Third, verify spawn viability. If you used old or heat-stressed spawn, it may be dead. Break open a jar: healthy spawn smells like mushrooms and is fully white. If it smells sour or is discolored, discard. Fourth, check gas exchange. Mycelium needs oxygen. If you sealed bags too tightly or filter patches are clogged, colonization slows. Ensure you have a breathable filter or loose lid. Fifth, look for contamination. Green mold (Trichoderma) or black spots mean competitors. If contamination is minor, you can try to transfer healthy mycelium to fresh substrate, but often it's best to bury the block in a garden bed and start over with sterilized equipment. Finally, consider substrate composition. Some agricultural wastes like sawdust need supplementation with bran (10-20% by weight) to provide nitrogen. Without it, growth is slow. For beginners, start with pasteurized straw (chopped to 2-5 cm) which colonizes quickly. Keep records: date, temperature, substrate type. Solving Common Contamination Outbreaks

Contamination is the number one killer of spawn businesses. The most common culprits are green mold (Trichoderma), black pin mold, and bacteria. If you see green spores, act immediately. If you get bacterial contamination (sour smell, wet spots), it often comes from too much water or insufficient pasteurization. Fix by reducing moisture and increasing pasteurization time.
