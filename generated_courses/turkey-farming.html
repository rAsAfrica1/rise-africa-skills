Overview of the Turkey Industry

Turkeys are not just for holidays. In Africa, turkey farming is a growing business because turkeys grow fast, taste good, and fetch high prices. Turkey Breeds and Varieties

Choosing the right breed is like choosing the right tool for a job. Different turkey breeds have different sizes, growth rates, and temperaments. In Africa, you'll find both local and imported breeds. The most common commercial breed is the Broad Breasted White. It grows fast, has lots of white meat, and is ready for market in 16-20 weeks. But it needs high-quality feed and good housing. The Broad Breasted Bronze is similar but has dark feathers and is hardier in free-range systems..Trim()

For small-scale farmers, heritage breeds like the Norfolk Black, Bourbon Red, and Narragansett are good choices. They grow slower (24-28 weeks) but are tough, good foragers, and can survive on less feed. They also have a richer flavor. In Africa, local turkeys (often called indigenous or village turkeys) are small but very hardy. They weigh 4-8 kg and are resistant to many local diseases. However, they lay fewer eggs (20-40 per year) compared to commercial breeds (80-100 eggs per year)..Trim()

When choosing a breed, consider your market. If you sell to hotels and supermarkets, they want large, white-meat birds. Go for Broad Breasted White. If you sell to local markets or want organic meat, heritage or local breeds work well. You can also crossbreed to get the best of both: hardiness from local breeds and size from commercial ones. For example, crossing a local hen with a Broad Breasted White tom can produce offspring that grow faster and survive better..Trim()

Turkey Farming Systems

There are three main ways to raise turkeys: free-range, semi-intensive, and intensive. Each has pros and cons. Free-range is the cheapest. You let your turkeys roam during the day and lock them up at night. They eat insects, grass, and kitchen scraps. This works well in rural areas with plenty of land. But growth is slow, and predators like hawks, snakes, and dogs can kill your birds. You also need to provide supplemental feed (maize, soy, and vitamins) to keep them healthy. Free-range is good for 20-50 birds..Trim()

.Trim()

Semi-intensive is a mix. You keep turkeys in a fenced area with a simple shelter, and you feed them balanced rations. They get some exercise and sunshine, which keeps them healthy. This system is good for 50-200 birds. You need about 2 square meters per bird in the pen. Provide feeders and drinkers. In Africa, many farmers use this system because it balances cost and production. You can use locally available materials like bamboo, mud bricks, and thatch for housing..Trim()

.Trim()

Intensive system is for commercial farms. Turkeys are kept indoors in large sheds with controlled temperature, ventilation, and lighting. This allows you to raise thousands of birds. But it's expensive: you need automatic feeders, drinkers, and heating. Feed conversion is best in this system, but disease spreads fast if biosecurity is poor. You must vaccinate and disinfect regularly. In South Africa, large farms use intensive systems. In Nigeria, some farms in Oyo and Ogun states use intensive houses for 1,000+ birds..Trim()

.Trim()

For beginners, start with semi-intensive. It's affordable and manageable. Build a simple house with wire mesh walls and a corrugated iron roof. Use wood shavings as bedding. Change bedding weekly. Provide 24-hour clean water. Feed a starter mash for 0-8 weeks, grower mash for 9-16 weeks, and finisher mash for 17-20 weeks. Always keep records of feed, mortality, and sales. With good management, you can make a profit in your first cycle..Trim()

Regulations and Certifications

Rules keep you out of trouble. In most African countries, you need permits to farm turkeys. In South Africa, you must register with the Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development if you keep more than 100 birds. You also need a veterinarian to issue health certificates before selling. In Nigeria, the Nigerian Agricultural Quarantine Service (NAQS) regulates poultry movement. You need a movement permit to transport turkeys across states. In Kenya, the Kenya Veterinary Board requires farmers to keep records of vaccinations and treatments..Trim()

.Trim()

Biosecurity is not just a good idea; it's often the law. You must prevent diseases like avian influenza. This means controlling who enters your farm, disinfecting boots and vehicles, and isolating new birds for 30 days. If there is an outbreak, you must report it to the authorities. In South Africa, the Animal Diseases Act requires you to report suspected avian influenza immediately. Failure to do so can lead to fines or imprisonment..Trim()

Certifications can boost your sales. Organic certification is valuable. To be certified organic, you must follow strict rules: no synthetic pesticides, no antibiotics, and access to outdoors. In Africa, organizations like Ecocert and Ceres certify organic farms. It takes 2-3 years to get certified, and it costs money, but organic turkey meat sells for 30-50% more. Another certification is HACCP (Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point) for food safety. This is important if you sell to supermarkets or export..Trim()

This requires strict biosecurity, traceability, and residue monitoring. It's a long process, but it opens big markets. Start small: sell locally first. Get a business permit, pay taxes, and keep records. Join a farmer cooperative to share costs of certification. The rules may seem heavy, but they protect your birds and your business..Trim()

Getting Started: Planning Your Farm

Planning is the difference between profit and loss. Before you buy a single poult, sit down and write a plan. First, decide your scale. Start with 50-100 birds. You can expand later. You need a budget. For 100 birds, expect to spend $1,200-$1,500. This covers poults, feed, vaccines, housing, and bedding. If you don't have that cash, start with 20 birds. Many farmers start with 10 and grow slowly..Trim()

Choose a site. Your farm should be on well-drained land, away from swamps and other poultry farms. You need clean water and electricity (or solar). Build a house that protects from rain, wind, and predators. A simple structure: wooden poles, wire mesh walls, and a corrugated iron roof. The floor should be concrete or packed earth with wood shavings. Allow 0.5 square meters per bird in the brooder and 2 square meters per bird after 8 weeks. You also need a storage room for feed and a quarantine area for sick birds..Trim()

Buy feed from a trusted mill. You can mix your own feed to save money: maize (60%), soybean meal (30%), fishmeal (5%), and minerals (5%). But get a nutritionist to help. Vaccinate on schedule: Newcastle disease (day 1, week 3, week 8), fowl pox (week 4), and coccidiosis (in feed). Keep records of everything: feed, mortality, sales, and expenses..Trim()

Start small, learn fast, and reinvest your profits. Turkey farming is a marathon, not a sprint. With good planning, you can build a successful business..Trim()

Module 2 · Turkey Housing and Facilities

Designing Turkey Barns

When designing a turkey barn in Africa, start with the climate. Turkeys need protection from rain, wind, and predators, but also good airflow. A simple open-sided barn works well in most tropical regions. Face the long side away from prevailing winds and towards the morning sun. Use local materials like wooden poles, bamboo, or eucalyptus for framing. For walls, you can use mud bricks, timber offcuts, or wire mesh covered with tarpaulin that can be rolled up. The floor should be concrete or packed earth raised at least 15 cm above ground level to prevent flooding. A concrete floor is best for easy cleaning and biosecurity. Size matters: allow 0.3 to 0.5 square meters per adult turkey. For a flock of 100 birds, that's a barn of 30 to 50 square meters. Height should be at least 2.5 meters at the eaves and 3.5 meters at the ridge to allow hot air to rise. Install a solid roof of corrugated iron sheets or thatch. Thatch is cooler but flammable; iron is durable but needs insulation. A simple trick: paint the roof white to reflect heat. Include a feed storage room and a footbath at the entrance. For free-range systems, provide a fenced run with shade trees. Always build on a slight slope for drainage. In areas with strong winds, brace the structure with diagonal supports. Remember, turkeys are curious and can fly short distances, so cover the run with netting if needed. A well-designed barn reduces disease and stress, leading to better growth and egg production..Trim()

Bedding and Litter Management

Litter is the material spread on the barn floor to absorb moisture, provide cushioning, and keep birds clean. In Africa, you can use rice husks, wood shavings, chopped straw, sawdust, or dried grass. Avoid using wet or moldy materials. Spread litter 5 to 10 cm deep for adult turkeys. In brooder areas for poults, start with 3 to 5 cm and add more as needed. The goal is to keep litter dry and loose. Wet litter causes ammonia buildup, which damages turkeys' respiratory tracts and eyes. Ammonia smells sharp; if you can smell it, it's too high. Stir the litter daily with a rake to aerate it. Remove caked or wet spots immediately and replace with fresh dry litter. Depending on flock density and climate, you may need to top up litter every week. In humid regions, add a thin layer of agricultural lime or superphosphate to reduce ammonia. For every 10 square meters, use about 1 kg of lime. Replace all litter between batches: remove old litter, scrub the floor with disinfectant, and let it dry before adding new litter. Compost old litter with manure; it makes excellent fertilizer. Never use litter from other poultry without composting, as it can carry disease. For poults, use soft, fine litter to prevent leg injuries. Keep litter away from feed and water to reduce spoilage. Good litter management is one of the cheapest ways to keep your turkeys healthy..Trim()

Ventilation Systems

Turkeys need fresh air to breathe and to remove moisture, heat, and ammonia. In Africa, most turkey barns use natural ventilation. This means designing the barn with open sides, ridge vents, and adjustable curtains. The minimum ventilation rate for adult turkeys is about 0.5 to 1 cubic meter per minute per bird in hot weather. For a 100-bird flock, that's 50 to 100 cubic meters per minute. Achieve this by having openings on at least two opposite walls. The total open area should be at least 10% of the floor area. For example, a 30 square meter barn needs 3 square meters of openings. Use wire mesh to keep out birds and rodents. Install roll-up curtains on the sides so you can control airflow. On hot days, open them fully; on cold or windy days, close them partially. Ridge vents allow hot air to escape. Make them 30 cm wide and cover with a raised roof cap to keep rain out. In very hot regions, add ceiling fans or a simple tunnel ventilation system: fans on one end pull air through the barn. For small flocks, a couple of 50-watt fans can help. Avoid drafts directly on poults; they chill easily. For brooding, use a small fan to move air gently but not directly on the birds. Monitor air quality by smell and by checking litter moisture. If you see condensation on walls or ceiling, increase ventilation. Good ventilation reduces respiratory diseases and heat stress, which are major killers in African turkey farming..Trim()

Heating and Cooling

Poults (baby turkeys) cannot regulate their body temperature for the first few weeks. They need supplemental heat. In Africa, you can use a brooder lamp (infrared or incandescent) or a charcoal stove with a safety guard. For 50 poults, one 250-watt infrared lamp hung 45 cm above the litter is enough. Watch the poults: if they huddle under the lamp, they are cold; if they spread away, they are too hot. For adult turkeys, heat stress is a bigger problem. Turkeys have dark feathers and no sweat glands. When temperatures exceed 30Â°C, they pant and reduce feed intake. Provide shade, increase ventilation, and use cooling methods. A simple evaporative cooling system: hang wet gunny sacks or jute bags in front of open windows. As air passes through, it cools. You can also spray the roof with water in the afternoon. Misting systems with fine nozzles can lower air temperature by 5-8Â°C. For small flocks, a fan blowing over a tray of water works. Ensure plenty of cool drinking water; add electrolytes if birds are panting heavily. In cold regions like the highlands, you may need to heat adult turkeys during cold nights. Use a safe heater or a well-ventilated charcoal stove. Never use open fires without ventilation; carbon monoxide kills. Insulate the roof with local materials like thatch or grass to reduce heat gain and loss. Always monitor temperature daily..Trim()

Lighting Programs

Light controls turkey growth, egg production, and behavior. For meat turkeys (broilers), you can use continuous light for the first 3 days to help poults find feed and water. After that, use 23 hours of light and 1 hour of darkness to prevent panic. From week 4, reduce to 18 hours light and 6 hours dark. This allows rest and reduces stress. For breeder turkeys, lighting is critical for egg production. Hens need 14 to 16 hours of light daily to lay. If natural daylight is less than 12 hours (common in some seasons), use artificial light. A single 40-watt bulb per 20 square meters is enough. Hang bulbs 2 meters high. Use a timer to keep a consistent schedule. Never decrease light hours for laying hens; that stops egg production. For growing pullets (future layers), start with 8 hours of light and gradually increase to 14 hours by 20 weeks. In Africa, many farmers rely on natural daylight. If you are near the equator, daylight is about 12 hours year-round, so you may need to add 2-4 hours of artificial light in the evening. Use energy-saving LED bulbs; they are cheap to run. For brooding, provide 24 hours of light for the first week, then reduce. Bright light can cause cannibalism in turkeys, so use dim light (10-20 lux) after the first week. You can dim by using lower-watt bulbs or painting bulbs. Always provide a dark period so turkeys can rest. A simple light program improves feed conversion and egg numbers. Keep a record of your lighting schedule and adjust as needed..Trim()

Biosecurity Infrastructure

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out of your turkey farm. It starts with the physical setup. Build a perimeter fence around the barn area. Use thorny bushes or wire mesh to keep out wild birds and animals. At the entrance, have a footbath with disinfectant. Use a mixture of 1 part bleach to 10 parts water, or commercial disinfectant. Change it daily. Provide boots or shoes that stay on the farm. Visitors should not enter the barn unless necessary; if they do, they must wear clean boots and coveralls. Keep different age groups of turkeys separate. Never mix poults with adults. If you have multiple barns, work from youngest to oldest. Quarantine new birds for 30 days before adding to the flock. Keep feed in closed containers to prevent rodents. Control flies and beetles, which can carry disease. Clean and disinfect feeders and drinkers daily. Remove dead birds immediately and bury them deep or burn them. Have a separate sick bay for any turkey showing signs of illness. Use a footbath at each barn entrance, not just the main gate. For free-range systems, rotate grazing areas to reduce parasite buildup. Vaccinate against common diseases like Newcastle and fowl pox, following local veterinary advice. Keep records of all treatments and deaths. Train all workers on biosecurity rules. A simple sign-in log for visitors helps track potential disease sources. Remember, biosecurity is cheaper than treating disease. One outbreak can wipe out your flock. Invest in basic infrastructure: a fence, footbaths, and separate storage. These small steps save money and lives..Trim()

Common Turkey Diseases

Turkeys in Africa face several diseases that can wipe out your flock quickly if you don't act fast. The most common is Newcastle disease, which causes sneezing, coughing, twisting of the neck, and sudden death. It spreads through droppings, contaminated feed, and even your shoes. There is no cure, so vaccination is your only protection. Another killer is fowl pox, which shows as scabs on the head, legs, and mouth. It spreads slowly but can kill young poults. Blackhead disease is a big problem where turkeys share space with chickens. It causes yellow droppings, drooping wings, and darkening of the head. Chickens carry the parasite but don't get sick, so never keep turkeys and chickens together. Coccidiosis is caused by a tiny parasite in dirty water and wet litter. It leads to bloody droppings and weakness. Treat with amprolium in drinking water for 5 days. CRD (chronic respiratory disease) causes rattling breath and swollen eyes; use tylosin or doxycycline as directed by a vet. To prevent these, keep your turkey house dry, clean feeders daily, and isolate any new birds for 3 weeks. In Africa, many farmers lose birds because they don't recognize early signs. Check your flock every morning: look for birds that stand apart, have pale combs, or refuse to eat. If you see these signs, call a vet or agricultural extension officer immediately. Remember, dead birds should be buried deep or burned, never eaten or sold. With good management, you can reduce disease outbreaks by up to 80%..Trim()

Vaccination Protocols

Vaccination is the cheapest insurance for your turkeys. In Africa, the most important vaccines are for Newcastle disease (ND), fowl pox, and sometimes fowl typhoid. Start with ND vaccine at day 1 for poults, given as a drop in the eye or nostril. Repeat at 3 weeks and again at 8 weeks. After that, booster every 3 months if you keep breeders. For fowl pox, vaccinate at 4 weeks by wing web stab. Use a two-pronged needle to prick the wing web; check for scabs 7-10 days later to confirm it worked. Store vaccines in a cooler with ice packs, never in direct sunlight. Reconstitute only with the provided diluent, and use within 2 hours. Use clean syringes and boil them for 10 minutes before use. Never use disinfectants on syringes because they kill the vaccine. For Newcastle, you can also use a drinking water route, but add skim milk powder (2g per litre) to protect the virus. Withdraw water for 2 hours before giving the vaccine so birds are thirsty. Give enough waterers so every bird drinks. After vaccination, birds may look slightly sick for a day; that's normal. Keep records: date, vaccine name, batch number, and number of birds. In many African countries, the government or NGOs provide free ND vaccines. Contact your local veterinary office. Parasites are silent thieves that steal your turkeys' feed and health. The two main types are internal (worms) and external (lice, mites, fleas). Biosecurity Practices

Biosecurity means keeping germs out of your turkey farm. It is the cheapest way to prevent disease. Start with a fence around your compound to keep out wild birds and visitors. Wild birds carry Newcastle and avian influenza. Use a footbath at the entrance of each pen. Fill it with 2% sodium hypochlorite (bleach) or 1% formalin. Change the solution daily. If you must keep both, house them in separate buildings at least 50 metres apart. Quarantine new birds for 3 weeks in a separate pen. Watch for signs of illness before mixing. Clean and disinfect feeders and drinkers daily. Use a brush and soap, then rinse with bleach solution. Store feed in sealed containers to keep out rats and wild birds. Compost manure away from the turkey house. Limit visitors; if they must come, give them boots and a coat. After handling birds, wash your hands with soap. Recognizing Sick Birds

You must spot sick turkeys early to save them and the flock. A healthy turkey is alert, active, and has bright eyes and red wattles. It eats and drinks well, and its droppings are firm with a white cap. A sick turkey often stands apart with drooped wings and a dull, pale head. It may close its eyes or have a runny nose. Check the legs and feet for swelling or scabs, a sign of fowl pox or bumblefoot. Selecting Breeding Stock

Selecting the right breeding stock is the foundation of a profitable turkey farm. Start by choosing birds that are healthy, vigorous, and free from deformities. In Africa, local breeds like the indigenous turkey are hardy and disease-resistant, but they may grow slower. For meat production, consider crossbreeds such as the Norfolk Black or Bronze. Look for broad-breasted birds with strong legs, as they must support heavy weight during mating. Select hens that lay regularly and have a calm temperament; aggressive hens may injure chicks. For toms (males), choose those with a large, upright fan, bright caruncles, and active strutting behavior. Avoid birds with crooked keels, slipped wings, or pasty vents. A good rule: select from parents that have a proven record of high hatchability and fast growth. In Africa, where feed costs are high, prioritize feed efficiency. Weigh birds at 16-20 weeks; keep those above average weight. Keep a ratio of 1 tom to 8-10 hens to ensure fertile eggs without overworking males. Buy stock from reputable hatcheries or established farmers, not from live bird markets where disease risk is high. Quarantine new birds for 3 weeks before mixing. Record the source, age, and performance of each bird. Cull any bird that shows signs of illness, poor growth, or low egg production. Remember, good genetics plus good management equals success. Start with 10-20 hens and 2-3 toms for a small-scale farm. Over time, replace older birds (after 2-3 years) with their best offspring to maintain vigor..Trim()

Mating Systems

Mating systems determine how you pair toms and hens to produce the next generation. The simplest is pen mating: keep 1 tom with 8-10 hens in a confined area. This works well for small farms but can lead to inbreeding if you keep related birds. To avoid this, rotate toms every 2 years or bring in new blood. Another system is flock mating, where multiple toms run with a larger flock. This mimics natural mating but you cannot control which tom fathers which poults. For genetic improvement, use artificial insemination (AI). AI allows you to use one superior tom to fertilize many hens, and it prevents injuries from heavy toms mounting hens. In Africa, AI is gaining popularity because it reduces the number of males needed. To collect semen, train the tom to mount a dummy or a hen, then massage the vent. Collect 0.2-0.5 ml per ejaculation, dilute with saline, and inseminate hens within 30 minutes. Inseminate each hen every 7-10 days. For natural mating, ensure the pen floor is not slippery; provide deep litter (wood shavings or sand) to prevent leg injuries. Watch for over-mating: hens with bare backs or wounds need rest. Separate them for a week. Rotate breeding groups every 6 months to maintain fertility. If you keep a tom for more than 2 years, fertility drops. Replace him with a young, vigorous tom. Always keep records of which tom mated which hens so you can trace parentage. In hot climates, mate in early morning or evening to avoid heat stress. Provide fresh water and shade. A good mating system balances genetic diversity, fertility, and bird welfare..Trim()

Egg Production and Management

A healthy turkey hen lays 60-100 eggs per year, mostly between 30 and 54 weeks of age. In Africa, egg production can be lower due to heat and poor nutrition. To maximize egg numbers, provide a laying diet with 16-18% protein and 3-4% calcium. Use crushed oyster shells or limestone as calcium sources. Hens need 14-16 hours of light daily; if natural daylight is short, use a bulb (40 watts per 20 square meters) to extend light. Collect eggs at least 3 times a day to prevent breakage and dirty eggs. Nest boxes should be dark, clean, and lined with soft bedding like dry grass. One nest box per 4-5 hens. Eggs for hatching should be stored at 15-18Â°C and 75% humidity for no more than 7 days. Turn them twice daily if stored longer. Do not wash hatching eggs; instead, gently wipe with a dry cloth. If eggs are dirty, use a fine sandpaper. In hot climates, store eggs in a cool room or evaporative cooler. Never store eggs near strong smells like kerosene or onions, as they absorb odors. Keep a record of lay: date, number of eggs, and any abnormalities. Soft-shelled or thin-shelled eggs indicate calcium deficiency or disease. If a hen lays outside the nest, confine her to the nest box for a few days. Broody hens stop laying; if you want eggs, break broodiness by moving the hen to a wire-bottom cage for 3-5 days. For hatching, select eggs that are well-shaped, average size, and free of cracks. Discard very small or double-yolked eggs. With good management, you can get 70-80% fertile eggs and 60-70% hatchability..Trim()

Incubation and Hatching

Turkey eggs take 28 days to hatch. You can use a natural hen (a broody turkey or chicken) or an incubator. For small flocks, a broody hen is cheap and effective. Set 8-12 eggs under a broody hen at night. She will turn them and keep them warm. Provide her with food and water nearby. For larger scale, use a forced-air incubator. Infertile eggs look clear; dead embryos show a blood ring. Remove them to prevent contamination. In Africa, power outages are common. Use a backup generator or a solar-powered incubator. If power fails, wrap the incubator in blankets and do not open it for up to 2 hours. After hatching, leave poults in the incubator until they are dry and fluffy (12-24 hours). Provide clean water with vitamins and a starter feed of 28% protein. Dip beaks in water to teach drinking. Keep bedding dry. Hatch rate depends on egg quality, incubation hygiene, and temperature control. Sanitize incubator before and after each hatch. With practice, you can achieve 80% hatch of fertile eggs..Trim()

Genetic Improvement

Genetic improvement means selecting the best birds to breed so that each generation is better than the last. Start by defining your goal: do you want faster growth, more eggs, or better disease resistance? In Africa, disease resistance and heat tolerance are often more important than maximum growth. Choose a selection method. Individual selection: pick birds based on their own performance (e.g., weight at 20 weeks). Family selection: pick birds whose parents and siblings also perform well. This is better for traits like egg production. For growth, weigh birds at 8, 16, and 24 weeks. Keep the heaviest 20% as breeders. For egg production, count eggs per hen from 30 to 45 weeks. Keep hens that lay above average. Use a breeding card for each bird: record ID, hatch date, weight, eggs laid, and health. Avoid inbreeding by not mating close relatives (father-daughter, brother-sister). Introduce new blood every 3-4 years from a different farm or hatchery. Crossbreeding can give hybrid vigor: cross two different breeds (e.g., local hen with Broad Breasted White tom) to get faster-growing offspring. But keep pure lines if you want to sell breeding stock. In hot climates, select for smaller body size and good feed conversion. Cull aggressively: any bird with deformities, poor growth, or chronic illness should not breed. Over time, you can improve growth by 5-10% per generation. Keep records for at least 3 generations to see trends. Join a farmer group to exchange breeding stock and ideas. Remember, genetic improvement is slow but permanent. Good nutrition and health unlock genetic potential..Trim()

Record Keeping for Breeding

Record keeping is the backbone of a successful breeding program. Without records, you cannot know which birds are performing well. Start with a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on a phone. For each bird, assign a unique ID (e.g., leg band or wing tag). Record: hatch date, sex, parent IDs, weight at 8, 16, and 24 weeks, date of first egg, number of eggs laid per week, egg weight, fertility, hatchability, and health treatments. For mating, record which tom was with which hens and dates. For incubation, record set date, number of eggs set, candling results, hatch date, and number of poults hatched. Use a calendar to track dates. In Africa, where many farmers operate without electricity, use a chalkboard or a waterproof notebook. Keep records in a safe, dry place. At the end of each season, calculate key figures: average eggs per hen, fertility rate (fertile eggs / eggs set), hatch rate (poults hatched / fertile eggs), and growth rate (weight gain per day). Compare these to previous seasons. Identify your best 10% of birds and use them as breeders next year. Cull the bottom 20%. Share records with buyers to prove quality. For example, a buyer will pay more for poults from a hen that lays 90 eggs per year than from one that lays 50. Records also help you spot problems early: a drop in fertility may mean a tom is too old or overheated. A drop in egg production may mean disease or poor feed. Keep records for at least 3 years to see genetic trends. Train your workers to record daily. Use simple codes: 'E' for egg, 'H' for hatched, 'D' for dead. Good records turn a hobby into a business..Trim()

Budgeting and Cost Analysis

A budget is a plan for your money. For turkey farming, you need to know your costs before you start. Let's say you want to raise 100 turkeys from day-old poults to 20 weeks. So keep mortality low.\n\nTo analyse costs, divide them into fixed costs (housing, equipment) and variable costs (feed, poults, meds). Insurance and Contracts

Insurance protects you from big losses. For turkey farms, you can insure the birds, the housing, and equipment. Understanding Turkey Behavior

Turkeys are social birds with a clear pecking order. Observe your flock daily to learn normal behavior. Healthy turkeys are active, curious, and vocal. They dust-bathe to clean feathers and reduce parasites. They stretch wings, run, and flap. When you approach, they should be alert but not panicked. Turkeys are ground-dwelling but can fly short distances; clip wings if needed. They roost at night; provide perches. Males (toms) strut, puff up, and gobble to attract hens. Hens cluck softly. Aggression occurs when overcrowded or when new birds are introduced. Separate injured birds. Turkeys are curious and will investigate anything shiny; keep metal objects away to prevent hardware disease. They learn routines; feed at same times daily. Use this to move them calmly.
