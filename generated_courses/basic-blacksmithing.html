Overview of Blacksmithing History and Traditions

Blacksmithing in Africa goes back over 2,000 years. The Haya people of Tanzania were smelting iron by 500 BCE, and the Nubians of Sudan were making iron weapons even earlier. In West Africa, the Yoruba and Igbo had sophisticated forge traditions, with blacksmiths often holding spiritual status. The smith was not just a toolmaker but a community leader, mediator, and keeper of secrets. In many cultures, blacksmiths were believed to have supernatural powers because they could transform rock into tools..Trim()

.Trim()

Traditional African forges were simple but effective. They used charcoal made from local hardwoods, and bellows made from goatskin or clay pots. The smith would heat iron in a clay furnace, then hammer it on a stone anvil. Techniques like forge welding, twisting, and drawing out were passed down orally. In places like Mali, the Bambara blacksmiths (known as numu) still practice these methods today, making everything from farm hoes to ceremonial staffs..Trim()

.Trim()

Colonialism disrupted many of these traditions. Cheap imported steel and manufactured goods flooded African markets in the 19th and 20th centuries, pushing many blacksmiths out of work. But the skills never disappeared. Today, there is a revival. Artisans in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa are combining old techniques with modern tools to create high-value products like custom knives, furniture, and architectural ironwork. Organizations like the African Blacksmiths Association are connecting smiths across the continent..Trim()

For you as a beginner, learning this history matters because it shows that blacksmithing is not a dead craft. It is a living tradition that has adapted for centuries. The same basic principles�??heat, hammer, shape�??that worked for your ancestors still work today. By learning to forge, you are joining a lineage that stretches back thousands of years. Essential Blacksmithing Terminology

Before you light your first fire, you need to speak the language. The forge is the firebox where you heat metal. It can be made from a car brake drum, a metal bucket lined with clay, or a simple pit. The tuyere (pronounced 'tweer') is the pipe that blows air into the fire. Your air source can be a hand-cranked blower, a hair dryer, or a bellows made from tire inner tube. The hammer is your main tool. A cross-peen hammer (one flat face, one wedge-shaped) is best for beginners. It weighs 1�??2 kg. Tongs hold hot metal. The quench is the rapid cooling that hardens steel. The forge fire needs fuel: charcoal or coal. Key processes: drawing out means making metal longer and thinner. Upsetting means making it shorter and thicker. Bending is exactly what it sounds like. Setting Up a Basic Home Forge

You can build a working forge for under $50. Start with the firepot. A car brake drum or a heavy metal bucket works well. Cut a hole in the side near the bottom for the tuyere. Line the inside with a mixture of clay and sand (3 parts clay, 1 part sand) to insulate it. Let it dry for two days. The tuyere can be a 2-inch steel pipe. Connect it to an air source: a hand-cranked blower from an old sewing machine, a hair dryer on cool setting, or a bellows made from a tire inner tube and two wooden boards..Trim()

For the anvil, find a piece of railroad track (at least 30 cm long) or a large sledgehammer head. Mount it on a sturdy log or a steel stand so the top is at knuckle height (about 75 cm from the ground). You can also use a heavy piece of flat steel plate. The anvil does not need to be fancy; it just needs to be heavy and hard. A 10 kg sledgehammer head buried in a log works fine for small work..Trim()

Your hammer should be 1�??2 kg. A cross-peen hammer is versatile. Get one pair of flat-jaw tongs that fit your metal stock. You will also need a wire brush, a file, and a bucket of water (slack tub). For fuel, use hardwood charcoal. You can make your own by burning wood in a pit covered with dirt, or buy it from local charcoal sellers. Avoid softwood charcoal; it burns too fast. If you can get bituminous coal, it gives more heat but produces smoke and clinker..Trim()

Forge Safety: Fire, Burns, and Ventilation

Blacksmithing is safe if you respect three things: fire, heat, and fumes. First, fire. Your forge can reach 1,200°C. Never leave it unattended. Keep a bucket of water and a bucket of sand within arm's reach. Sand smothers fuel fires; water is for quenching hot metal and cooling burns. Do not wear synthetic clothing (polyester, nylon) because it melts and sticks to skin. Wear cotton or wool. Tie back long hair. Remove dangling jewelry. Keep the area around the forge clear of dry leaves, paper, and rags. If you work indoors, you must have a chimney or hood that pulls smoke outside. A simple hood can be made from a metal drum cut in half and mounted above the forge, connected to a pipe that exits through a wall or roof..Trim()

.Trim()

Second, burns. Hot metal does not glow red until about 500°C. Below that, it looks like cold metal but will still burn you badly. Always assume metal near the forge is hot. Use tongs, never your bare hands. Wear leather gloves when handling hot stock, but not when hammering�??gloves can slip and cause you to lose your grip. Forge burns are serious. If you get burned, cool the area under running water for 10 minutes, then cover with a clean cloth. Do not apply butter or oil. Seek medical help for large or deep burns..Trim()

.Trim()

Third, ventilation. Burning charcoal and coal produces carbon monoxide (CO), an invisible, odorless gas that kills. Never work in a closed room without proper airflow. Even outdoors, do not stand directly over the fire and breathe the smoke. Coal smoke contains sulfur and other toxins. If you feel dizzy, headachy, or nauseous, move to fresh air immediately. Set up a fan to blow smoke away from your face, but not so strong that it blows heat away from your metal. A simple rule: if you can smell smoke strongly, you need more ventilation..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, keep a first aid kit nearby. Include burn gel, bandages, and eyewash. Wear safety glasses every time you hammer�??scale (hot flakes) flies off. Ear protection is wise when hammering for long periods. And never forge when you are tired or distracted. Most accidents happen when you rush. Take breaks, drink water, and stay focused..Trim()

Personal Protective Equipment and Clothing

Your body is your most important tool. Protect it. Start with eyewear. Safety glasses or goggles are non-negotiable. Hot scale, sparks, and metal fragments fly when you hammer. A simple pair of clear safety glasses costs $5 and can save your sight. If you wear prescription glasses, get over-glasses or side shields. Never use sunglasses; they are not impact-rated..Trim()

.Trim()

For clothing, wear cotton or wool long sleeves and long pants. Synthetic fabrics melt and cause severe burns. A leather apron is ideal�??it stops sparks and hot metal from reaching your chest and lap. You can buy one for $20�??$40 or make one from thick leather. Avoid loose clothing that can catch on tools or the anvil. Wear closed-toe leather boots. Steel-toe is best. Never wear sandals or open shoes. Hot metal drops can land on your feet. Also, tuck your pants over your boots or wear leather spats to keep sparks out..Trim()

.Trim()

Hands: You need two types of gloves. For forging (hammering), wear thin leather gloves that allow dexterity. They protect from heat and blisters but let you grip the hammer. For handling hot metal and moving the fire, wear thick leather gloves (welding gloves). Never use gloves with synthetic liners. When you are done, check gloves for holes. Replace them. For your ears, use foam earplugs or earmuffs if you will be hammering for more than 30 minutes. The ringing sound of steel on steel can damage hearing over time..Trim()

.Trim()

Hair and jewelry: Tie back long hair. Remove rings, watches, bracelets, and necklaces. Metal jewelry conducts heat and can catch on tools. If you have a beard, keep it trimmed or wear a face shield when grinding. For your lungs, a simple dust mask is useful when grinding or brushing scale, but it does not protect against carbon monoxide. That requires ventilation. Finally, keep a leather welder's jacket or a heavy cotton shirt for welding or forge welding. The key is layers: you can always remove a layer if you get too hot, but you cannot add skin. Invest in good PPE. It costs less than a hospital visit..Trim()

Module 2 · The Forge and Fire Management

Understanding Forge Types: Coal, Gas, and Propane

A forge is simply a controlled fire that gets hot enough to make metal soft. In Africa, the most common forge is a coal forge, often made from a car wheel rim, a metal bucket, or a simple clay mound. Coal forges burn charcoal or mineral coal. Charcoal is made by burning wood slowly with little air; it lights easily and is cheap, but burns fast. Mineral coal burns hotter and longer but needs a good air blast from a hand-cranked blower or electric fan. A coal forge needs a firepot (a shallow bowl) and a tuyere (a pipe that brings air into the fire). You can build one from scrap: a brake drum works well as a firepot, and a length of pipe with a hair dryer or hand blower provides air..Trim()

.Trim()

Gas forges run on propane or butane. They are cleaner and easier to light, but the fuel must be bought. A basic gas forge is a metal drum or pipe lined with ceramic fibre or firebrick to hold heat. A burner (often a simple pipe with a small hole for air) mixes gas and air, and the flame swirls inside the chamber. Gas forges heat metal without smoke or ash, so they are good in towns where coal smoke is a problem. However, they can be more expensive to run and require a steady supply of gas cylinders..Trim()

.Trim()

Propane is the same as cooking gas in many countries. A 9 kg cylinder will run a small forge for about 8 to 10 hours. You need a regulator and a hose rated for gas. Never use a homemade regulator or a leaking hose. Gas forges must have a way for exhaust gases to escape, or you risk carbon monoxide poisoning. Always work outdoors or in a well-ventilated shed..Trim()

.Trim()

Choose your forge based on what you have. If you have scrap metal and wood to make charcoal, build a coal forge. If you live in a city with gas supply, a gas forge may be easier. Many smiths start with coal and later add a gas forge for smaller jobs. The key is to learn to control the fire, no matter what fuel you use..Trim()

Building and Maintaining a Coal Fire

To build a coal fire, start with a clean firepot. Remove old clinker (the glassy waste that blocks air) and ash. Place a handful of dry kindling or paper in the bottom, then add small pieces of charcoal or coal. Light the kindling from the top, and turn on the air blast gently. As the fire catches, add more coal around the edges, leaving a hollow in the centre. This hollow is where you will put your metal. The fire should be bright orange-yellow, not dull red..Trim()

.Trim()

Once the fire is going, you need to keep it fed. Add coal little by little around the outside, not directly on the hot centre. Green coal (new coal) smokes and gives off yellow smoke, which is normal. As it heats, it turns to coke and burns clean. Use a poker to break up clinker and keep air passages open. The fire should be about the size of a melon for most work. If you need a bigger fire, add more coal and increase air. If the fire gets too big, stop adding coal and let it burn down..Trim()

.Trim()

Maintaining the fire means watching the colour. A good forging fire is bright yellow-orange. If it is dark red, you need more air. If it is white and sparking, it is too hot and you are burning the metal. The metal should be placed in the fire at the same depth each time, usually about 2-3 cm below the surface of the coal. Rotate the metal so it heats evenly. Never leave metal in the fire while you do other tasks; it can burn..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many smiths use charcoal instead of coal. Charcoal burns faster, so you need to add it more often. A charcoal fire is ready when the coals are glowing red with a little ash on top. You can use a hand-powered bellows made from a plastic bag or a wooden box to provide air. Keep the bellows or blower working steadily; too much air blows ash into the metal, too little makes a cold fire. After work, let the fire die down, then clean out the firepot and dispose of ash safely..Trim()

Controlling Fire Temperature and Size

Controlling the fire is the most important skill in blacksmithing. Temperature is controlled by the amount of air you blow into the fire and the amount of fuel. More air means more oxygen, which makes the fire hotter. But too much air can burn the metal or blow sparks. For most forging, you want a bright orange heat, around 1,100 to 1,200 degrees Celsius. That is hot enough to make steel soft like clay. If you need to weld, you need a white heat, around 1,300 degrees Celsius. You can judge temperature by colour: dark red is about 600°C, bright red 900°C, orange 1,100°C, yellow 1,200°C, white 1,300°C..Trim()

.Trim()

To increase temperature, add more air by turning up the blower or pumping the bellows faster. You can also add more coal around the centre. To decrease temperature, reduce air or spread the coal out. The size of the fire also matters. A small fire concentrates heat, good for small parts like hooks. A large fire spreads heat, good for long pieces like tongs. You can shape the fire by piling coal into a mound for a small hot spot, or spreading it flat for a wide heat..Trim()

.Trim()

Use a damper or a valve on your air supply to make fine adjustments. If you have an electric blower, a simple switch or a speed control works. With a hand-cranked blower, you control speed by how fast you turn. Always keep a bucket of water nearby to cool the fire if it gets too hot. You can also sprinkle water on the coal around the edge to keep it from burning too fast..Trim()

.Trim()

Practice by heating a piece of scrap steel. Watch the colour change as you increase air. Learn to hold a steady heat. In Africa, where fuel may be scarce, controlling the fire also saves money. A well-controlled fire uses less coal or charcoal. Remember, a forge fire is like a living thing: it needs food (fuel), air, and attention. With practice, you will know exactly how much air and fuel to use for each job..Trim()

Using a Gas Forge: Setup and Adjustment

A gas forge is simpler to light than a coal forge, but you must set it up safely. First, place the forge on a stable, fireproof surface like concrete or bare earth. Never put it on wood or near flammable materials. Connect the gas hose to the burner and the regulator to the gas cylinder. Use soapy water to check for leaks: if bubbles form, tighten the connection. Open the cylinder valve slowly. Then open the burner valve a little and light the burner with a long match or spark lighter. Never lean over the forge when lighting; keep your face and hands to the side..Trim()

.Trim()

Once lit, adjust the air intake on the burner. Most burners have a sliding sleeve that controls air. If the flame is yellow and lazy, it needs more air. If it is blue and roaring, it is good. A proper flame is blue with a slight roar. If the flame blows out, turn off the gas, wait a minute, and try again with less gas. The forge chamber should heat up to orange in about 5 minutes. You can adjust the gas flow with the regulator or a needle valve. More gas means more heat, but also more fuel use..Trim()

.Trim()

To control temperature, you can adjust the gas and air. For most forging, you want the inside of the forge to be bright orange. If it is too hot, the metal will sparkle and burn. If too cold, the metal will not soften. You can also use a firebrick to block part of the opening to keep heat in. A gas forge is best for small to medium pieces. For long pieces, you may need to open both ends or use a larger forge..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, propane is available in many towns, but it can be expensive. A 9 kg cylinder lasts about 8 hours at full blast. To save gas, turn down the burner when you are not heating metal. Always turn off the gas at the cylinder when finished. Let the forge cool before moving it. Check the hose and regulator regularly for cracks. Never use a gas forge indoors without proper ventilation. Carbon monoxide is invisible and deadly..Trim()

Fire Safety and Emergency Procedures

Fire safety starts before you light the forge. Clear the area around your forge of dry grass, paper, rags, and any flammable liquids. Keep a bucket of water, a bucket of sand, and a fire extinguisher within arm's reach. In Africa, many workshops are outdoors or in open sheds, so wind can carry sparks. Work with your back to the wind if possible, and never light a forge near thatched roofs or fuel stores. Wear cotton or leather clothing, not synthetic, because synthetic melts and sticks to skin. Leather boots, gloves, and safety glasses are essential..Trim()

.Trim()

When lighting a coal forge, use dry kindling and never use petrol or paraffin to start the fire. That is a common and deadly mistake. If the fire gets out of control, do not panic. Turn off the air blast first. That will cool the fire quickly. Then spread the coals with a rake or shovel. If flames spread to nearby materials, use the water or sand. For a gas forge, if you smell gas, turn off the cylinder immediately. Do not light a match or switch on a light. Open all doors and windows. If the gas has been leaking, let it air out before trying to light..Trim()

.Trim()

Burns are the most common injury. For a minor burn, cool it with clean running water for 10 minutes, then cover with a clean cloth. Do not use butter or oil. For a serious burn, get medical help. If you get metal in your eye, do not rub it. Cover the eye and go to a clinic. Always keep a first aid kit with bandages, antiseptic, and burn gel..Trim()

Grip and Stance for Effective Striking

Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, left foot slightly forward if you are right-handed. Your anvil should be at knuckle height when you stand straight. In Africa, many smiths work on a simple anvil set on a hardwood stump or a concrete block. Adjust the height by adding or removing wooden planks under the stump until the anvil face meets your knuckles. For a right-handed smith, grip the hammer handle about 2 cm from the end with your right hand. Wrap your fingers around the handle, but keep your thumb alongside your index finger, not wrapped over. This prevents blisters and gives you control. Your left hand holds the workpiece with tongs, not your bare hand. Keep your left elbow close to your body for stability. Your right arm should be relaxed, not tense. The hammer head should rest on the anvil face when you start. When you lift the hammer, use your shoulder and elbow, not just your wrist. A common mistake is to grip too tightly. Think of holding a small bird: firm enough so it doesn't escape, but not so tight that you crush it. Your stance should allow you to step forward or back slightly as you work. Keep your knees slightly bent. Your eyes should focus on the spot where you want the hammer to hit, not on the hammer itself. Practice this stance without striking: just lift the hammer and bring it down slowly to the anvil face. Do this 20 times. Then add a gentle tap. In many African workshops, apprentices spend the first week just practicing grip and stance before they are allowed to strike hot metal. This builds muscle memory. Remember, a good grip and stance reduce fatigue and improve accuracy. If you feel pain in your wrist or elbow, adjust your grip or stance. The hammer should feel like an extension of your arm, not a separate tool..Trim()

The Three Basic Hammer Blows

There are three fundamental hammer blows: the tap, the forging blow, and the full blow. The tap is a light, controlled strike used for fine adjustments, straightening, or setting a piece before a heavier blow. Use only wrist movement for a tap. The hammer head travels about 10 cm. The forging blow is the workhorse. It uses elbow and shoulder, with a moderate swing. The hammer head travels about 30 cm. This blow moves metal efficiently without exhausting you. The full blow is a heavy strike for drawing out thick stock or moving large masses of metal. It uses your whole body: you rise slightly on your toes, swing from the shoulder, and let the hammer's weight do the work. The hammer head travels 45 cm or more. For a full blow, grip the handle firmly but not tightly. Let the hammer bounce slightly after impact. In African smithies, you often see a smith using a full blow to draw out a piece of scrap leaf spring into a knife blade. That requires many full blows. But for finishing, they switch to taps. Practice each blow on cold metal first. Set a piece of mild steel on the anvil. Strike it 10 times with a tap, then 10 times with a forging blow, then 5 times with a full blow. Listen to the sound: a tap gives a light ping, a forging blow a solid thud, a full blow a deep ring. The sound tells you if you are hitting flat. If the hammer face is not parallel to the anvil face, you will get a dull sound and a dented workpiece. Always keep your wrist straight at impact. Bend your elbow, not your wrist. After each blow, return the hammer to the same starting position. Consistency is key. In many African training programs, apprentices practice these three blows on a block of wood or a piece of soft steel for hours before they touch hot metal. That is how you build control..Trim()

Accuracy and Consistency Practice

Accuracy means hitting the same spot every time. Consistency means using the same force and rhythm. To practice, take a piece of mild steel, 1 cm thick and 20 cm long. Place it on the anvil. Mark a spot with chalk. Strike that spot 20 times with a forging blow. Then check the mark. If your hammer marks are scattered, you need to adjust your stance and grip. A good drill is to strike a line of 10 marks, each 1 cm apart, using taps. Then do the same with forging blows. In Africa, many smiths use a piece of hardwood as a practice anvil because it is quieter and cheaper. But for real accuracy, use your actual anvil. Set up a target: a small dot of paint on a scrap plate. Strike it 50 times. Count how many times you hit within 5 mm. Aim for 40 out of 50. Another drill: place a coin on the anvil and strike it lightly with a tap. The coin should not fly off. This teaches controlled force. For consistency, practice a rhythm: strike, lift, strike, lift, at a steady pace. Use a metronome app on your phone set to 60 beats per minute. Strike once per beat. Do this for 2 minutes. Rest. Repeat. You will notice that your accuracy improves when you are not tired. So take breaks. In many African workshops, apprentices practice accuracy by forging a simple nail: they must draw out the point, shape the head, and cut it off, all with consistent blows. If the nail is crooked, their accuracy is off. Another method: strike a piece of lead. Lead deforms easily and shows every hammer mark. After 10 strikes, you can see exactly where you hit. Use lead to diagnose your accuracy. Remember, accuracy comes from repetition. Do 100 strikes a day for a week. You will see improvement..Trim()

Using the Anvil's Horn and Face

An anvil has two main working areas: the face and the horn. The face is the flat, hardened top surface. Use it for flat forging, drawing out, and bending. The horn is the pointed, curved end. Use it for bending curves, forming rings, and drawing out tapered points. In Africa, many anvils are made from a piece of railway track or a heavy steel block. If your anvil has no horn, you can use a piece of round bar held in a vise as a substitute. To use the face, place your workpiece flat on it. Strike with a forging blow. The face should be smooth but not polished. A polished face makes the hammer slide. A slightly rough face grips the metal. To use the horn, place your workpiece over the curve. Strike the metal where it touches the horn. This bends it. For example, to make a hook, heat a 1 cm round bar. Place it over the horn at the point where you want the bend. Strike the bar just behind the horn with a forging blow. The bar will curve around the horn. Move the bar and strike again to continue the curve. For a tight ring, use the tip of the horn. For a gentle curve, use the base of the horn. In many African smithies, the horn is used to make traditional hoes, sickles, and bracelets. Practice by bending a 30 cm piece of 8 mm round bar into a U-shape. Heat it to orange. Place the center on the horn. Strike the ends down with a forging blow. Then flip it and strike the curve to smooth it. Another use of the horn is drawing out a taper. Place the workpiece on the horn at an angle. Strike the end. The horn acts as a fulcrum, and the metal thins. Always keep the horn clean. Dirt or scale will mark your work. After using the horn, brush it with a wire brush. The face and horn are your main shaping tools. Master them, and you can make almost anything..Trim()

Avoiding Common Hammering Mistakes

The most common mistake is gripping the hammer too tightly. This causes blisters, fatigue, and poor control. Remember: firm but relaxed. Another mistake is hitting with a bent wrist. Your wrist should be straight at impact. If you bend your wrist, the hammer face will not be parallel to the anvil, and you will dent the workpiece. A third mistake is looking at the hammer instead of the workpiece. Your eyes should be on the spot where you want to hit. The hammer will follow your gaze. A fourth mistake is using too much force for light work. Many beginners try to move metal with full blows when a forging blow would do. This wastes energy and can damage the metal. A fifth mistake is not returning the hammer to the same starting position after each blow. This leads to inconsistent strikes. A sixth mistake is standing too far from the anvil. You should be close enough that your hammer arm can swing freely without hitting your body. A seventh mistake is using a hammer that is too heavy. A 1 kg hammer is good for most work. A 2 kg hammer is for heavy drawing out. If you are tired, switch to a lighter hammer. An eighth mistake is ignoring the sound. A dull thud means you are hitting flat or the metal is too cold. A clear ring means a good strike. In many African workshops, apprentices are taught to listen. A ninth mistake is striking cold metal. Cold metal can crack or damage your hammer. Always work at the right temperature: orange for mild steel, yellow for high carbon. A tenth mistake is not maintaining your hammer. A loose head is dangerous. Check it every day. If the handle is cracked, replace it. A well-maintained hammer gives better control. Finally, avoid striking the anvil face directly with a full blow. That can chip the anvil. Always have metal between the hammer and the anvil. Practice these corrections, and your hammering will improve quickly..Trim()

Building Stamina and Rhythm

Blacksmithing is physical work. You need stamina to swing a hammer for hours. Start with a 1 kg hammer. Swing it 50 times in a row. Rest for 1 minute. Do 3 sets. Increase to 100 swings per set after a week. In Africa, many smiths work from sunrise to noon, then rest during the heat. Build your stamina gradually. A good rhythm is essential. Rhythm means a steady, repeatable pattern of strikes. It conserves energy and improves accuracy. Use a metronome app set to 60 beats per minute. Strike once per beat. Do this for 5 minutes. Then increase to 80 beats per minute. Practice with a forging blow. You will find that a steady rhythm helps you last longer. Another drill: strike the anvil face with a tap, then a forging blow, then a tap, then a forging blow. Repeat 20 times. This builds coordination. For rhythm, work with a partner. One strikes, the other turns the metal. You must match the rhythm. In many African smithies, two smiths work together: one with a heavy sledge, one with a smaller hammer. They strike alternately. This is called a striking team. You can practice this by having a partner strike a piece of wood while you strike the anvil. Listen to each other. To build stamina, also work on your core strength. Do planks and squats. A strong core protects your back. Eat well before work: a meal with rice, beans, and vegetables gives lasting energy. Drink water often. Dehydration causes fatigue. In the heat, drink 500 ml every hour. Take breaks every 30 minutes. Stretch your arms and back. Many African smiths have a stool near the anvil. When they feel tired, they sit and do light taps. That is fine. The goal is to work steadily, not to exhaust yourself. After a month of daily practice, you will be able to swing a hammer for 2 hours with only short breaks. That is the level you need for professional work..Trim()

Understanding Steel: Carbon Content and Alloys

Steel is iron mixed with a small amount of carbon. The carbon content decides how hard the steel can become when heat treated. Low-carbon steel (mild steel) has less than 0.3% carbon. It is easy to forge and weld but will not harden much when quenched. You find it in rebar, car parts, and cheap angle iron. Medium-carbon steel (0.3�??0.6% carbon) is a good all-rounder. It hardens enough for many tools but stays tough. Old leaf springs from trucks and cars are often 5160 steel, a medium-carbon alloy with chromium. High-carbon steel (0.6�??1.5% carbon) is what you want for knives, chisels, punches, and axes. It can get very hard but also brittle if not tempered. Files, old saw blades, and coil springs are good sources. Alloy steels add other elements: chromium for wear resistance, vanadium for toughness, molybdenum for heat resistance. In Africa, you often work with recycled steel. Learn to identify it: test with a grinder �?? high-carbon steel throws bright yellow sparks that burst into many small forks; mild steel gives few dull orange sparks. A file test: a new file will skate over hardened high-carbon steel but bite into mild steel. When you buy steel from a scrap yard, ask for old agricultural equipment, truck axles, or plow discs �?? these are often medium to high carbon. Keep a few known samples in your shop for comparison. Never assume; always test. The carbon content also affects forging temperature: high-carbon steel burns at a lower temperature than mild steel, so watch your fire. With practice, you will learn to judge by feel and spark..Trim()

Critical Temperatures and Color Indicators

In blacksmithing, you judge temperature by the color of the steel in dim light. The critical temperature is where steel changes its internal structure and becomes ready for hardening. For most high-carbon steels, this is between 760°C and 870°C. In color terms, that is a bright cherry red to orange-red. A common mistake is to quench too cold. If the steel is only dull red (around 600°C), it will not harden. If it is bright yellow or white (over 1000°C), you are burning the carbon and the steel will be weak. Learn to work in a shaded area or at dusk. A magnet is a useful tool: steel becomes non-magnetic at its critical temperature (around 770°C for simple carbon steels). Heat a piece and touch it with a magnet. When it stops sticking, you are at the right temperature for quenching. This is a reliable trick in any light. For African smiths using charcoal or coal forges, the fire may be uneven. Quenching: Oil, Water, and Air

Quenching is the rapid cooling of hot steel to make it hard. The speed of cooling matters: faster cooling makes harder steel but also more brittle. Water is the fastest, then oil, then air. For most simple high-carbon steels (like 1080 or 5160), oil is the best choice. Warm oil (around 50�??60°C) cools more evenly than cold oil. You can use used motor oil, canola oil, or transmission fluid. Never use water on high-carbon steel unless you know it is a water-hardening grade (like W1). Water can crack the steel. For medium-carbon steels, oil is also good. Air-hardening steels (like A2) are rare in basic smithing; they harden by cooling in still air. But for most African workshops, oil is your friend. Set up a quench tank: a metal pipe or bucket filled with at least 4 liters of oil. The oil should be deep enough to fully submerge the tool. After quenching, steel is very hard but also brittle �?? it can crack or shatter like glass. Tempering removes some of that brittleness while keeping most of the hardness. The file test is the simplest way to check if your steel is hard. A new, sharp file is harder than most knife steels. If it bites, re-harden and quench again. Remember that a file will also skate on work-hardened steel or on a piece that is not fully hardened but has a hard surface. You can also use a set of hardness testing files (like those from a machinist supply) that are calibrated to different Rockwell C scales, but they are expensive. Heat Treating Simple Tools

Now you will heat treat three common tools: a cold chisel, a center punch, and a simple knife. For each, the process is similar but the details differ. Start with a cold chisel made from 5160 or 1080 steel. Forge the cutting edge to a 60-degree angle. Normalize: heat to cherry red and let it cool in air. Then harden: heat just the first 5 cm of the cutting end to non-magnetic (bright cherry red). Quench only that end in warm oil, moving it in a figure-eight. Hold for 10 seconds, then check with a file. It should skate. Principles of Forge Welding

Forge welding is the ancient art of joining two pieces of metal by heating them to a white-hot temperature and hammering them together. In Africa, blacksmiths have used this technique for centuries to make tools, weapons, and art. To succeed, you need to understand the principle: when steel or iron reaches a specific temperature range�??around 1,200°C to 1,400°C (2,200°F to 2,600°F)�??the metal becomes plastic and, when pressed or hammered, the atoms on the surfaces can bond. This is not melting; it's a solid-state weld. The key is clean surfaces, the right heat, and speed..Trim()

.Trim()

In your forge, you'll know the metal is ready when it glows a bright yellow-white, almost sparkler-like. If you see tiny sparks shooting off, that's a sign it's burning�??too hot. You want the metal to look like a glowing lemon. At this temperature, you have only a few seconds to act before it cools. That's why preparation and speed are critical..Trim()

.Trim()

African forges often use charcoal or coal, which can reach these temperatures with a good air blast. You'll need a tuyere (air pipe) and a steady hand on the bellows or blower. The principle is simple: heat, clean, flux, hammer. But each step must be precise. If the metal is too cold, it won't weld; too hot, it burns and becomes useless. Practice judging the color in different lighting�??shade your eyes or work in a dimly lit area to see the true glow..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, forge welding is not just about heat; it's about pressure. A single, firm hammer blow can fuse the metal, but you must strike while the metal is at welding heat. Many beginners fail because they hesitate. With practice, you'll develop a rhythm: heat, brush, flux, heat again, then quick, confident hammer strikes. This skill will allow you to create strong joints without electricity or modern welding machines, using only your forge, hammer, and anvil..Trim()

Preparing Surfaces for Welding

Before you even think about heating metal, you must prepare the surfaces. Dirty, rusty, or scaled metal will not weld. The surfaces must be clean, bare, and shaped to fit together tightly. Start by using a wire brush or a file to remove all rust, dirt, and old scale. If you have a grinder, use it, but a hand file works too. In many African workshops, a simple wire brush and a piece of broken grinding stone are used. Scrub until you see shiny metal..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, shape the ends. For a basic lap weld, you can simply overlap two flat bars. But for a stronger joint, you'll want to scarf the ends�??taper them so they fit together like puzzle pieces. A scarf joint increases surface area and helps the weld hold. To make a scarf, heat the end and hammer it to a taper, then bend it slightly so the two pieces interlock. .Trim()

After shaping, clean the surfaces again. Any scale that formed during shaping must be removed. Then, apply flux immediately before heating. Flux, such as borax, melts and coats the metal, preventing oxidation (scale) from forming at welding heat. Without flux, the oxygen in the air will create a layer of iron oxide that stops the weld. You can buy borax at any market or pharmacy; it's cheap and effective. Some blacksmiths also use fine sand or clay, but borax is best..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, make sure your hammer and anvil are clean. A dirty hammer face can introduce impurities. Have your tools ready: hammer, anvil, tongs, wire brush, and flux. Using Flux: Borax and Commercial Fluxes

Borax is a white powder that melts at around 743°C (1,370°F) and forms a glassy coating on the hot metal. Practice Welds: Scarf Joints and Lap Welds

The two most common forge welds are the lap weld and the scarf weld. The lap weld is simplest: you overlap two pieces of metal and weld them together. .Trim()

The scarf weld is stronger and more traditional. You taper the ends of both pieces so they fit together like a V or a tongue-and-groove. This increases surface area and distributes stress. To make a scarf, heat the end and hammer it to a gradual taper, then bend it slightly. When you bring the two pieces together, they should interlock. Weld as usual: heat, flux, and hammer. .Trim()

Practice with scrap metal first. Cut a few pieces of mild steel bar, about 1 cm thick. Try a lap weld on one pair, and a scarf weld on another. Use borax as flux. Keep your forge hot and work quickly. After welding, test the joint by bending it in a vise or hammering it. A good weld will not break; it will bend. If it breaks, examine the break: if it's clean, the metal didn't reach welding heat; if it's dirty, you didn't clean or flux enough..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, forge welding takes practice. Don't be discouraged if your first attempts fail. In Africa, apprentices often spend weeks perfecting this skill. Start with small pieces and simple joints. Even experienced blacksmiths sometimes have welds fail. The key is to diagnose the problem and correct it. Here are common issues and solutions. First, if the weld doesn't stick at all, the metal likely wasn't hot enough. Forge welding requires a bright yellow-white heat. If the metal was orange or red, it's too cold. .Trim()

Second, if the weld sticks but breaks easily, the surfaces were dirty or not fluxed properly. Scale, rust, or dirt prevents bonding. .Trim()

Third, if the metal burns (sparks fly off), it's too hot. Burnt metal is ruined�??it becomes brittle and cannot be welded. Solution: watch the color carefully. Remove the metal as soon as it reaches bright yellow. Don't let it sit in the fire. Use a lower air blast if necessary..Trim()

.Trim()

Fifth, if the joint is weak, you may not have used enough pressure or the joint design was poor. For a lap weld, make sure you hammer the entire overlap, not just the edges. Selecting Steel for Blades

For a blade that holds an edge and survives hard use, you need steel with enough carbon to harden. In most African markets, the easiest source is an old leaf spring from a truck or car.
