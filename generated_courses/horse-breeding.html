Module 1 · Equine Reproductive Anatomy and Physiology

Anatomy of the Mare's Reproductive Tract

The mare's reproductive tract is a series of tubes and chambers that you must know if you are to breed horses successfully. Start at the vulva, the external opening below the anus. It should be small and vertical, with good muscle tone. A sunken or gaping vulva allows air and bacteria in, causing infections that cost you foals. Next comes the vagina, about 15-20 cm long, where the stallion deposits semen. Then the cervix, a thick muscular ring that seals the uterus when the mare is pregnant. You can feel it through the rectum during palpation. The uterus is Y-shaped, with two horns where the embryo implants. In a mare, the uterus is about 20-25 cm long. The oviducts are narrow tubes that catch the egg from the ovary. The ovaries are bean-shaped, about 5-7 cm long, and they release eggs during the breeding season. In Africa, many mares are small indigenous breeds like the Barb or Dongola. Their tracts are smaller, so use a smaller speculum and be gentle. To examine a mare, use a clean plastic sleeve, lubricant, and a gentle rectal palpation. You can also use a speculum and a flashlight to see the cervix. A common problem in Africa is poor hygiene during breeding, leading to endometritis. Always wash the mare's vulva with mild soap and warm water before breeding. Also, watch for urine pooling in older mares, which can kill sperm. If you see urine dribbling, have a vet check. Proper anatomy knowledge helps you time breeding, detect pregnancy early, and prevent infections. Remember, a healthy mare's tract is your first step to a live foal..Trim()

Anatomy of the Stallion's Reproductive Tract

The stallion's reproductive tract is simpler than the mare's but just as important. The testicles are the main organs, located in the scrotum between the hind legs. In a mature stallion, each testicle is about 8-12 cm long and 5-6 cm wide. They produce sperm and testosterone. The epididymis sits on top of each testicle, a coiled tube where sperm mature and are stored. From there, the vas deferens carries sperm up into the body. The accessory glands include the seminal vesicles, prostate, and bulbourethral glands. They produce seminal fluid that nourishes and transports sperm. The penis is muscular and retracts into the sheath when not erect. During breeding, the stallion's penis becomes erect, and he ejaculates 50-100 ml of semen, containing billions of sperm. In Africa, many stallions are kept in hot, dusty conditions. Heat stress can lower sperm quality. Provide shade and cool water. Also, check for injuries to the penis or sheath, common in thorny bush. To collect semen, use an artificial vagina (AV) heated to 45-50Â°C, with a clean liner and lubricant. A mare in heat or a phantom mare can be used. Handle the stallion calmly; rough handling causes injuries. After collection, examine the semen under a microscope. Motility should be above 60% for good fertility. If you see pus or blood, consult a vet. Regular exercise and good nutrition, including minerals like zinc and selenium, keep sperm healthy. Remember, a stallion's job is to breed many mares, so his reproductive health directly affects your herd's productivity..Trim()

The Estrous Cycle and Hormonal Regulation

The mare's estrous cycle is unique among farm animals. It lasts about 21 days, but only 5-7 days are spent in heat (estrus). The rest is diestrus, when she is not receptive. The cycle is controlled by hormones from the brain and ovaries. The hypothalamus releases GnRH, which tells the pituitary to release FSH and LH. FSH causes follicles to grow on the ovaries. As follicles grow, they produce estrogen, which makes the mare show signs of heat: squatting, urinating, and winking the vulva. When estrogen peaks, LH surges, causing ovulation. The egg is released about 24-48 hours after the LH peak. After ovulation, the follicle becomes a corpus luteum (CL), which produces progesterone. Progesterone prepares the uterus for pregnancy and stops the mare from showing heat. If she does not conceive, the CL regresses after about 14 days, progesterone drops, and a new cycle begins. In Africa, the long daylight hours of summer trigger the breeding season. Mares often stop cycling in winter (anestrus). To manage breeding, you can use lights to extend daylight to 16 hours, starting in late winter. Also, monitor heat signs twice daily. A simple teaser stallion can help. If you want to breed, do it every other day during heat until ovulation. Palpate the ovaries to feel follicles. A follicle of 35-40 mm is close to ovulation. Hormone treatments like prostaglandin can shorten diestrus, and hCG can induce ovulation. But use them only under veterinary advice. Understanding the cycle helps you time breeding and reduce wasted covers..Trim()

Spermatogenesis and Semen Production

Spermatogenesis is the process of making sperm in the stallion's testicles. It takes about 60 days from start to finish. The testicles contain tiny tubes called seminiferous tubules. Inside, germ cells divide and mature into sperm. This process needs cooler temperatures than body heat. That's why the testicles hang outside the body in the scrotum. In hot African climates, keep stallions in shade and avoid working them hard during the hottest part of the day. A stallion can produce 2-6 billion sperm per ejaculate. But quality matters more than quantity. A good ejaculate has at least 60% motile sperm and 70% normal morphology. To collect semen, use an artificial vagina (AV) as described earlier. Collect into a clean, warm container. Evaluate immediately: look at volume, color, and motility under a microscope. Normal semen is milky white. If it's watery or yellow, there may be a problem. Dilute semen with extenders if you need to store it. For fresh breeding, use within 1-2 hours. For cooled storage, keep at 5Â°C and use within 24-48 hours. In Africa, many breeders use fresh semen because cooling equipment is scarce. If you breed naturally, ensure the mare is in heat and clean. One stallion can breed 30-40 mares per season if managed well. But overuse leads to poor sperm quality. Give stallions one day rest between breedings. Also, provide a balanced diet with enough protein (12-14%) and minerals. Vitamin E and selenium are important. Regular exercise keeps them fit. If sperm counts are low, check for infections or injuries. A vet can test for bacteria. Remember, spermatogenesis is continuous, so any stress today affects sperm quality two months later..Trim()

Seasonal Effects on Reproduction

Horses are seasonal breeders. In Africa, the breeding season is influenced by daylight, not temperature. Mares come into heat when daylight increases, usually in spring and summer. In equatorial regions, daylight is fairly constant, so mares may cycle year-round. But in southern Africa, with longer summer days, mares are more fertile from October to March. In North Africa, the season is from February to June. Stallions also show seasonal changes: sperm production is higher in spring and summer. To maximize fertility, plan breeding for the optimal season. If you want foals born in early summer, breed in late spring. But remember, gestation is 11 months (about 340 days). So breeding in November gives a foal in October. In hot climates, avoid breeding in the hottest months because heat stress reduces sperm quality and can cause early embryonic death. Provide shade, fans, and cool water. Also, ensure good nutrition; dry season grazing is low in nutrients. Supplement with hay, concentrates, and minerals. If you want to breed out of season, use artificial lighting. Put mares under lights for 16 hours a day, starting 60-90 days before you want them to cycle. A simple fluorescent light in the stable works. But this costs money, so many African breeders stick to natural seasons. Another factor is the rainy season. Mud and wet conditions can cause hoof problems and infections. Keep breeding areas dry. Also, monitor for ticks and other parasites, which can affect fertility. By understanding seasons, you can plan when to breed, when to foal, and how to manage your horses for best results..Trim()

Endocrine Control of Reproduction

The endocrine system uses hormones to control reproduction. In horses, the key hormones are GnRH, FSH, LH, estrogen, progesterone, prostaglandin, and oxytocin. The hypothalamus in the brain releases GnRH in pulses. This stimulates the pituitary gland to release FSH and LH. FSH causes follicles to grow on the ovaries. As follicles grow, they produce estrogen. Estrogen makes the mare show heat and prepares the reproductive tract for breeding. When estrogen is high, it triggers a surge of LH, which causes ovulation. After ovulation, the ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum (CL) and produces progesterone. Progesterone prepares the uterus for a pregnancy and prevents the mare from showing heat. If the mare does not conceive, the uterus releases prostaglandin, which destroys the CL. Then progesterone drops, and a new cycle starts. If she conceives, the embryo sends a signal to prevent prostaglandin release, so the CL stays and progesterone remains high. In Africa, stress, poor nutrition, and disease can disrupt these hormones. For example, a mare with heavy parasite load may have irregular cycles. To manage reproduction, you can use hormone treatments. Prostaglandin can be given to bring a mare into heat quickly. hCG can be used to induce ovulation when a follicle is ready. But these should be used under veterinary guidance. Also, regular monitoring of heat signs and palpation helps you know when to breed. Keep records of each mare's cycle. Good nutrition, especially energy and protein, supports hormone production. Minerals like selenium and vitamin E also help. By understanding hormones, you can solve breeding problems and improve success rates..Trim()

Basic Principles of Inheritance

Every foal inherits half its genes from the sire and half from the dam. These genes come in pairs, one from each parent. Some genes are dominant, meaning a single copy is enough to show the trait. Others are recessive, meaning two copies are needed. For example, the gene for black coat color is dominant over chestnut. If a black horse carries a hidden chestnut gene, it can still pass chestnut to its foal. This is why two black horses can produce a chestnut foal if both carry the recessive chestnut allele..Trim()

In Africa, many breeders use simple inheritance to predict foal traits. Suppose you have a bay stallion that is heterozygous for black (Ee) and a chestnut mare (ee). The stallion produces sperm with either E or e; the mare always gives e. So each foal has a 50% chance of being bay (Ee) and 50% chestnut (ee). You can use a Punnett square to work this out on paper. No lab needed..Trim()

.Trim()

Understanding inheritance helps you avoid surprises. If you want a foal with a specific coat color, choose parents wisely. For racing or work, inheritance also affects speed, stamina, and temperament, but these are polygenicâmany genes each with small effect. You cannot predict them as easily. Still, selecting parents with good performance records improves your odds..Trim()

.Trim()

In practice, keep simple records: sire, dam, and foal traits. Over time, you will see patterns. For example, if a stallion consistently throws foals with crooked legs, he may carry a recessive defect. That is when you need genetic testing, which we cover later. For now, master the basics: dominant vs recessive, homozygous vs heterozygous, and how to use a Punnett square. These tools cost nothing and work anywhere..Trim()

Coat Color Genetics and Prediction

Coat color in horses is controlled by a few major genes. The extension gene (E) determines black vs red. E = black, e = red (chestnut). The agouti gene (A) restricts black to the points, creating bay. A = bay, a = black. So a horse with E and A is bay; E and no A is black; e and any A is chestnut. Other genes like cream (Cr) dilute colors: one copy turns bay to buckskin, chestnut to palomino; two copies turn bay to perlino, chestnut to cremello. In Africa, cream dilutes are popular for their striking looks..Trim()

.Trim()

To predict foal color, list each parent's genotype. For example, a buckskin stallion (Ee Aa Crcr) bred to a chestnut mare (ee aa crcr) can produce: bay, black, buckskin, palomino, and chestnut. The probabilities depend on the exact alleles. You can calculate them with a 3-gene Punnett square, but many breeders use free online calculators like the 'Horse Color Calculator' by Animal Genetics. It works offline if you save the page..Trim()

.Trim()

Practical tip: test your breeding stock. A simple DNA test for E, A, and Cr costs about $30 per horse from labs like UC Davis or VetGen. In Africa, you can send hair samples (with roots) to these labs; results come in 2-3 weeks. Knowing genotypes prevents guessing. For example, if you want a palomino foal, you need a chestnut mare (ee) and a stallion that carries cream and is not black-based (e.g., palomino or cremello). A cremello stallion (CrCr) bred to any chestnut mare produces 100% palomino foals. That is a powerful tool..Trim()

.Trim()

Record every foal's color at birth. Over years, you will see which matings produce desired colors. Color does not affect performance, but it affects market price. In many African markets, a flashy palomino or buckskin sells for 20-30% more than a plain bay. So use genetics to your advantage..Trim()

Inbreeding and Linebreeding Strategies

Inbreeding means mating closely related horses, like father to daughter or brother to sister. Linebreeding is a milder form, mating horses that share a common ancestor but are not as closely related, such as half-cousins or grandparent to grandoffspring. Both increase homozygosityâthe pairing of identical genes. This can fix desirable traits, but also brings together harmful recessive genes. In horses, inbreeding depression causes reduced fertility, smaller foals, and weaker immune systems..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, where registered stock can be expensive and scarce, linebreeding is common. A smart strategy is to linebreed to a proven ancestor, like a champion stallion, but keep the inbreeding coefficient below 10%. You can calculate this coefficient with the formula: sum over all common ancestors of (0.5)^(n1+n2+1), where n1 and n2 are the number of generations from the sire and dam to the common ancestor. For example, mating a half-brother and half-sister (common sire) gives 12.5% inbreeding. That is too high for most breeds. Aim for less than 6.25% (equivalent to first cousins)..Trim()

.Trim()

Practical steps: Use pedigree software like Breeders Assistant or free tools like the 'Inbreeding Calculator' on the Horse Genetics website. Enter the pedigree up to 5 generations. If the coefficient is above 10%, find an unrelated mate. Alternatively, use outcrossing: bring in a horse from a different line, even if it is less prestigious. In Kenya, some breeders rotate stallions every two years to avoid inbreeding..Trim()

Monitor foals from linebred matings. If you see defects like parrot mouth, contracted tendons, or heart problems, stop that pairing. Keep records. Linebreeding can work, but only with strict culling and genetic testing. Never breed a carrier of a recessive disorder to another carrier, even if they are only distantly related..Trim()

Pedigree Evaluation and Performance Records

A pedigree is a family tree of a horse, usually showing 3 to 5 generations. It tells you the ancestors' names, colors, and sometimes race or show records. In Africa, many pedigrees are incomplete, especially for local breeds. But even a simple record helps. Start by writing down the sire, dam, and their parents. Then add any known performance: race wins, pulling capacity, fertility, or endurance. For example, a stallion that won 5 races at 1600m is more valuable for breeding sprinters than one that never raced..Trim()

.Trim()

When evaluating a pedigree, look for consistency. If a sire consistently throws foals that win at 1200m, that is a strong signal. Check the dam's produce record: how many foals has she had, and how many performed well? A dam with 5 foals and 3 winners is excellent. Use a simple index: (number of winners / number of foals) x 100. Above 50% is good. Also check for genetic disorders in the pedigree, like HYPP (hyperkalemic periodic paralysis) in Quarter Horses or SCID (severe combined immunodeficiency) in Arabians. If a sire is a carrier, test your mare before breeding..Trim()

Genetic Disorders and Testing

Horses can inherit several genetic disorders. The most common are: HYPP (hyperkalemic periodic paralysis) in Quarter Horses and related breeds, causing muscle tremors and weakness; SCID (severe combined immunodeficiency) in Arabians, where foals lack an immune system and die within months; HERDA (hereditary equine regional dermal asthenia) in Quarter Horses, causing fragile skin; and GBED (glycogen branching enzyme deficiency) in Quarter Horses, which is fatal. Other disorders include PSSM (polysaccharide storage myopathy) and MH (malignant hyperthermia). In Africa, these may be less common in local breeds, but imported bloodlines can carry them..Trim()

.Trim()

Testing is simple: pull 20-30 mane hairs with roots, place them in a paper envelope, and mail to a lab like UC Davis (USA) or Animal Genetics (UK). Costs range from $25 to $50 per test. For African breeders, shipping may take 1-2 weeks, and results come by email. You can also use in-country labs in South Africa (e.g., Vetdiagnostix) or Kenya (e.g., ILRI). Always test breeding stock for disorders common in their breed. For example, test all Arabians for SCID, all Quarter Horses for HYPP, HERDA, GBED, and PSSM1..Trim()

.Trim()

If a horse tests positive as a carrier (heterozygous), it does not have the disease but can pass it on. Never breed two carriers together. If you have a carrier mare, breed her to a clear stallion. Then each foal has a 50% chance of being clear and 50% carrier. Test the foals before breeding them. If a horse tests affected (homozygous), do not breed it. In some cases, affected horses can be managed (e.g., HYPP with diet), but they should not be bred..Trim()

.Trim()

Keep records of all test results. Share them with buyers. In Africa, where pedigree verification can be weak, a clear genetic test adds value. A stallion with a 'clear' certificate for all major disorders can command a higher stud fee. Testing is an investment, not a cost..Trim()

Genomic Selection in Horse Breeding

Genomic selection uses DNA markers across the entire genome to predict a horse's breeding value for complex traits like speed, stamina, and fertility. Unlike single-gene tests, it looks at thousands of SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphisms) simultaneously. This technology is advanced but becoming cheaper. In 2024, a horse genomic test costs about $200-$300 from labs like Neogen or Weatherbys. For African breeders, it may be out of reach for many, but it is worth understanding..Trim()

.Trim()

The process: collect a hair or blood sample, send to a lab, and receive a genomic estimated breeding value (GEBV) for traits of interest. For example, a GEBV for racing speed might be +2.5, meaning the horse is predicted to be 2.5 units faster than average. You can use GEBVs to select young horses before they train, saving years of feeding and training. In South Africa, some Thoroughbred breeders use genomic selection to pick yearlings. In Kenya, the technology is not yet common, but you can access it through international labs..Trim()

.Trim()

For a small breeder, the practical approach is to combine genomic data with traditional pedigree and performance records. If you cannot afford genomic testing, use progeny testing: breed a stallion to 10 mares, then evaluate the foals' performance. After 3-4 years, you have a good idea of his genetic merit. This is slower but free. Alternatively, collaborate with other breeders to share costs. A group of 10 breeders can each contribute $30 to test a shared stallion..Trim()

.Trim()

Genomic selection is not a magic bullet. It works best for traits with high heritability, like height and racing speed (heritability ~0.3-0.5). For low-heritability traits like fertility (heritability ~0.1), it is less reliable. Always use genomic predictions as one tool among many. In Africa, where environmental factors (nutrition, disease) vary greatly, genetics alone will not guarantee success. But over time, selecting for genetically superior animals will improve your herd. Start with simple tests, then upgrade as your budget allows..Trim()

A breeding soundness examination (BSE) checks if a stallion can breed successfully. In Africa, where veterinary services may be limited, you can do a basic BSE yourself before calling a vet. Module 6 · Breeding Methods and Techniques

Natural Cover and Pasture Breeding

Natural cover is the simplest and cheapest breeding method: a stallion runs with mares and mates them when they are in heat. In pasture breeding, you turn a healthy stallion out with 10 to 20 mares in a large camp of at least 10 hectares. The stallion will detect mares in oestrus by smell and behaviour, and mate them several times over 2 to 3 days. This method suits free-ranging herds in Africa because it needs little labour and no special equipment. However, you must manage risks. Young or old stallions may injure mares or themselves. A stallion can cover 30 to 40 mares in a season, but keep the ratio lower (1:10) for better conception. Provide plenty of water and shade; heat stress reduces fertility. Watch for kicks and bites. If a mare does not conceive after two cycles, remove her for examination. Record mating dates by observing the stallion's activity daily. Pasture breeding can spread venereal diseases like contagious equine metritis (CEM), so test all horses before joining. Also, inbreeding is a danger: rotate stallions every 2 years or use unrelated bloodlines. For smallholders, natural cover is practical, but you must monitor body condition and deworm regularly. A well-managed pasture breeding programme can achieve 80-90% conception rates..Trim()

Hand Mating and In-Hand Breeding

Hand mating gives you control over the breeding process. You bring a mare in heat to a stallion while both are held by handlers. Use a breeding hobble or twitch to keep the mare still if she resists. The stallion is led to the mare's left side; he mounts, and intromission lasts 10 to 30 seconds. After dismounting, the mare's tail is held aside to prevent urine backflow, and she is rested. This method allows you to record exact dates, use a stallion on many mares (up to 50 per season), and prevent injuries. In Africa, hand mating is common on stud farms near cities. You need a safe breeding shed with non-slip floor, 4 x 6 metres, and two experienced handlers. Tease the mare first with a teaser stallion to confirm oestrus. Breed every 48 hours while she is in heat. If the mare is a maiden or has a history of difficult breeding, use a breeding roll or a twitch. After mating, wash the mare's vulva with mild disinfectant to reduce infection. Keep a record: date, stallion, mare, and any problems. Hand mating reduces disease transmission compared to pasture breeding but requires more labour. Costs include handler wages and facility upkeep. Conception rates can reach 85-90% if timing is correct. Always check the stallion's penis for injuries or discharge before and after mating..Trim()

Artificial Insemination: Fresh and Cooled Semen

Artificial insemination (AI) lets you breed many mares to one stallion without transport. Fresh semen is collected, diluted, and inseminated within 1 to 2 hours. Cooled semen is extended with skim milk or a commercial extender, chilled to 5Â°C, and shipped in a transport container; it remains fertile for 24 to 48 hours. To collect semen, use an artificial vagina (AV) heated to 45-50Â°C, with a mare in heat as a mount. The stallion ejaculates into the AV; the gel fraction is filtered out. The cervix is located and the semen deposited 2-3 cm into the uterus. Proper hygiene prevents endometritis. Pregnancy rates with fresh semen: 70-85%; with cooled semen: 50-70%. Frozen semen allows you to store stallion genetics for years and breed mares across continents. The process: collect fresh semen, extend with a cryoprotectant (e.g., egg yolk + glycerol), package into 0.5 ml straws, and freeze in liquid nitrogen vapour. Storage is at -196Â°C in a cryogenic tank. In Africa, frozen semen is expensive but valuable for rare breeds. To inseminate, thaw straws in a water bath at 37Â°C for 30 seconds. Embryo Transfer Techniques

Embryo transfer (ET) allows a valuable mare to produce multiple foals per year. The donor mare is bred, and 6 to 8 days later, the embryo is flushed from her uterus and transferred to a recipient mare. In Africa, ET is used for sport horses and rare breeds. Steps: 1) Synchronise donor and recipient mares' cycles using prostaglandin (e.g., cloprostenol) and progesterone. 2) Breed donor at oestrus. 3) On day 7 after ovulation, flush the uterus with 1-2 litres of sterile saline via a catheter; collect fluid and filter to find the embryo. 4) Evaluate embryo under a microscope; good embryos have 32-64 cells. 5) Transfer to a recipient mare that is 1-2 days behind in cycle. Use a sterile pipette to place the embryo into the uterine horn. Success rates: 50-70% of flushed embryos result in pregnancy. Costs: US$500-1500 per attempt, plus recipient mare upkeep. You need a veterinarian with ET training. Smallholders can use ET by sending donor mare to a clinic. Recipient mares should be healthy, 3-10 years old, and have good uterine tone. After transfer, monitor for pregnancy at 14 days via ultrasound. If unsuccessful, repeat. ET can help you get foals from a mare that cannot carry a pregnancy due to injury or age..Trim()

Advanced technologies like Ovum Pick-Up (OPU) and Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection (ICSI) are used when natural breeding or AI fails. The oocytes are then matured in a lab for 24 hours. ICSI involves injecting a single sperm directly into the oocyte using a micromanipulator. The resulting embryo is cultured for 7-10 days and then transferred to a recipient mare. This is useful for stallions with poor sperm quality or frozen semen with low motility. In Africa, these services are available at a few referral centres in South Africa, Kenya, and Egypt. Costs are high: US$2000-5000 per attempt, plus shipping of oocytes or embryos. Success rates: 20-30% live foal rate per OPU-ICSI cycle. For smallholders, it is not practical unless you have a high-value mare. However, you can learn the basics: OPU requires a trained vet, an ultrasound with a follicular aspiration kit, and a lab for ICSI. If you cannot afford it, focus on improving nutrition and management to boost natural fertility. Advanced tech is a tool for elite genetics, not everyday breeding..Trim()

Early Pregnancy Diagnosis Methods

Knowing whether your mare is pregnant as early as possible saves money and prevents accidents. In Africa, where vet access may be limited, you can use several practical methods. The first is teasing: from day 14 after covering, bring the mare near a stallion daily. A pregnant mare will usually refuse him, pin her ears, and kick. This is free but not 100% reliable; some mares show false heat. A better method is rectal palpation by a skilled technician. From day 18 to 21, a trained hand can feel a small, fluid-filled swelling in the uterus. By day 35, you can feel the fetus. This requires a vet or experienced AI technician, but it is cheap and quick. Ultrasound is the gold standard. A portable scanner with a 5 MHz linear probe can detect a pregnancy as early as day 14. You see a black fluid-filled circle (the embryonic vesicle) in the uterus. By day 25, you can see the heartbeat. In many African countries, mobile vet services offer ultrasound for a fee. Blood tests for equine chorionic gonadotropin (eCG) can be done from day 40, but they are less common. Urine tests are not reliable in horses. For small-scale breeders, the best plan is: tease daily for 21 days, then have a vet do rectal palpation or ultrasound at day 30. If positive, start proper care. If negative, rebreed on the next cycle. Early diagnosis prevents feeding a non-pregnant mare expensive supplements and allows you to manage a pregnant one correctly..Trim()

Ultrasound Monitoring of Fetal Development

Ultrasound is the most powerful tool for monitoring pregnancy in mares. It uses sound waves to create a picture of the uterus and fetus. In Africa, portable ultrasound machines are increasingly available through veterinary clinics or AI services. You should scan at key intervals. First scan at day 14-16: confirm pregnancy by seeing a black, fluid-filled vesicle. Also check for twins. Twins are dangerous because mares rarely carry both to term; one or both die, and the mare may abort. If you see two vesicles, your vet can pinch one or give a drug to reduce it. Second scan at day 25-30: you should see the embryo with a heartbeat. The heartbeat is a flickering motion. If no heartbeat, the pregnancy is not viable. Third scan at day 45-60: check for fetal sex (if desired) and ensure the fetus is developing normally. The placenta should be attached properly. After day 60, scans are less frequent unless problems arise. A healthy fetus grows steadily. By day 90, you can see the skeleton. By day 150, the fetus looks like a small horse. Ultrasound also helps detect uterine fluid, which may indicate infection or imminent abortion. In hot African climates, dehydration can reduce image quality, so ensure the mare is well hydrated before scanning. Always use a clean probe and lubricant. If you cannot access ultrasound, rectal palpation at day 30 and 60 can still give useful information, but ultrasound gives you early warning of problems..Trim()

Endocrine Changes During Pregnancy

Hormones are chemical messengers that control pregnancy. In the pregnant mare, several key hormones change. Understanding them helps you know what is normal and when to worry. Progesterone is essential to maintain pregnancy. It is produced by the corpus luteum on the ovary for the first 35-40 days. After that, the placenta takes over. If progesterone drops too low, the mare may abort. In Africa, where stress from heat, poor nutrition, or transport can affect hormones, monitoring progesterone via blood tests can be useful. However, tests are not always available. A practical sign of low progesterone is a mare that shows signs of heat (oestrus) during early pregnancy. If that happens, call a vet; she may need progesterone injections. Equine chorionic gonadotropin (eCG) is produced by the placenta from day 40 to 120. It helps form accessory corpora lutea that produce more progesterone. High eCG levels can cause ovarian cysts, which are harmless. Oestrogens increase from day 60, peaking around day 200. They help relax the cervix and prepare the uterus. Prolactin rises near foaling to prepare milk production. Relaxin softens the pelvic ligaments. In late pregnancy, cortisol rises to trigger labour. For the African breeder, the main takeaway: if a mare shows heat after day 40, or has a bloody discharge, suspect hormone imbalance. Ensure she gets good nutrition and low stress. Regular vet checks are best, but if not possible, keep her calm and well-fed..Trim()

Nutritional Requirements of the Pregnant Mare

A pregnant mare needs extra feed, but not too much. Overfeeding can cause problems like laminitis or a difficult birth. Underfeeding leads to weak foals. In Africa, where good pasture may be seasonal, you must adjust the diet. For the first 5 months of pregnancy (early gestation), the mare needs only about 10% more energy than a dry mare. If she is on good grass or hay, that is enough. Provide free-choice mineral salt, especially calcium and phosphorus. From month 6 to 8 (mid-gestation), increase energy by 20%. Add 0.5 to 1 kg of a balanced concentrate per day. Good sources in Africa include maize, sorghum, and cottonseed cake. Avoid moldy feed; it can cause abortion. From month 9 to 11 (late gestation), the fetus grows fast. Increase energy by 30-40%. A 500 kg mare may need 2-3 kg of concentrate daily, plus good hay. Protein should be 12-14% of the diet. Add 30-50 grams of calcium and 20-30 grams of phosphorus daily. If you cannot afford commercial supplements, crushed bone meal or limestone flour can help. Provide fresh water always; a pregnant mare drinks 40-60 litres per day. In hot climates, she may drink more. Add salt to feed (30-50 g/day) to encourage drinking. Also ensure she has access to shade. Overweight mares are at risk of foaling problems, so monitor body condition: you should feel ribs but not see them. Keep her active with light exercise.
