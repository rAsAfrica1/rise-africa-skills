What is First Aid and Why It Matters

First aid is the immediate help you give to someone who is injured or suddenly ill, using simple skills and everyday items. In Africa, where ambulances can take 30 minutes or more to arrive in rural areas, your actions in the first 10 minutes often determine whether a person lives or dies. Legal and Ethical Considerations: Good Samaritan Laws and Consent

The Chain of Survival and Emergency Medical Services

The Chain of Survival is a sequence of actions that gives a person the best chance of surviving a cardiac arrest or major emergency. In Africa, the chain has five links: 1) Early recognition and calling for help, 2) Early CPR, 3) Early defibrillation (if available), 4) Advanced medical care, and 5) Post-resuscitation care. Personal Safety and Scene Assessment

Before you help anyone, you must ensure the scene is safe for you. If you become a victim, you cannot help others. Look for dangers: traffic, fire, smoke, live electrical wires, gas leaks, unstable buildings, or violent people. In African markets, be aware of crowds, animals, and criminal activity. Initial Assessment: ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation)

The ABCs are the foundation of first aid. A stands for Airway, B for Breathing, C for Circulation. Check them in order. First, ensure the airway is open. If someone is unconscious, tilt their head back gently and lift the chin. Look inside the mouth for vomit, blood, or objects. If you see something, sweep it out with a finger only if it is loose and visible. Do not push it deeper. For a choking conscious person, give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades, then 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver)..Trim()

.Trim()

Next, check breathing. Look for chest rise, listen for air movement, and feel for breath on your cheek for 10 seconds. If they are not breathing, start CPR immediately. For adults, give 30 chest compressions (push hard and fast at least 5 cm deep, 100â120 per minute) then 2 rescue breaths. If you are untrained or unwilling, do hands-only CPR (compressions only). For children, use one hand, and for infants, two fingers. Continue until help arrives or the person breathes..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, check circulation. Look for severe bleeding. If blood is spurting or pooling, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth or gauze. Do not remove the cloth if it soaks throughâadd more on top. If bleeding is from an arm or leg, elevate it above the heart. Use a tourniquet only if pressure fails and bleeding is life-threatening. For shock, lay the person flat, raise legs 30 cm, and keep them warm with a blanket..Trim()

Module 2 · CPR and AED for Adults

Recognizing Cardiac Arrest and Agonal Breathing

Cardiac arrest happens when the heart suddenly stops pumping blood. In Africa, it often occurs outside hospitals, in homes, markets, and workplaces. Every minute without CPR reduces survival by about 10%. You must act fast. The first step is to check for responsiveness. Tap the person firmly on the shoulder and shout, 'Are you okay?' If they do not respond, they may be in cardiac arrest. Next, check for normal breathing. Look at the chest for 5 to 10 seconds. A person in cardiac arrest may have no breathing or only gasping. Gasping, also called agonal breathing, looks like occasional snoring, gurgling, or slow, irregular gasps. It is not normal breathing. Many people mistake gasping for normal breathing and delay CPR. Do not be fooled. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, assume cardiac arrest. In Africa, common causes include heart attacks, electrocution, drowning, and severe allergic reactions. Business owners and community members should be trained to recognize these signs. Remember: unresponsive + no normal breathing = start CPR. Call for help immediately. If you are alone, use your mobile phone to call emergency services on speaker while you begin CPR. In many African countries, emergency numbers vary: 112 in Kenya, 10111 in South Africa, 199 in Nigeria. Know your local number. Do not waste time checking for a pulse; it is unreliable even for trained professionals. The key is to recognize the absence of normal breathing and act. Time is heart muscle. Every second counts..Trim()

Hands-Only CPR Technique for Adults

Hands-only CPR is CPR without rescue breaths. It is recommended for untrained bystanders or those unwilling to give breaths. It is simple, effective, and easy to remember. For adults, hands-only CPR can double or triple survival chances. Here is how to do it. First, ensure the scene is safe. Then, tap the person and shout. If no response and no normal breathing, call for help. If someone else is around, ask them to call emergency services and find an AED. If you are alone, call on speakerphone and start CPR. Position the person on their back on a firm, flat surface. Kneel beside their chest. Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, between the nipples. Place your other hand on top and interlock your fingers. Keep your arms straight, shoulders directly above your hands. Push hard and fast. Compress at least 5 cm (2 inches) deep. Push at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute. That is about 2 compressions per second. Allow the chest to come back up fully after each compression. Do not stop until help arrives, an AED is ready, or you are too exhausted to continue. In Africa, many people fear causing harm, but doing nothing is worse. Hands-only CPR is safe and does not require mouth-to-mouth. It is perfect for busy markets, offices, and community gatherings. Practice on a pillow or a CPR manikin if available. The rhythm of the song 'Stayin' Alive' by the Bee Gees is about 100 beats per minuteâuse it to keep pace. Remember: push hard, push fast, and do not stop..Trim()

Chest Compressions: Depth, Rate, and Recoil

Chest compressions are the heart of CPR. They manually pump blood to the brain and vital organs. To be effective, you must get three things right: depth, rate, and recoil. Depth: For an average adult, push down at least 5 cm (2 inches) but not more than 6 cm (2.4 inches). If you are a small person, use your body weight to help. Too shallow and blood flow is poor; too deep and you may break ribs. Rib fractures are a risk, but they are better than death. Rate: Aim for 100 to 120 compressions per minute. That is faster than one per second. Count out loud: 'One and two and three and...' or use a metronome app. Many smartphones have free metronome apps. The song 'Stayin' Alive' has the correct beat. Recoil: After each compression, let the chest come all the way back up. Do not lean on the chest. This allows the heart to refill with blood. If you do not allow full recoil, blood flow decreases. In Africa, where ambulances may be far away, high-quality compressions are even more critical. You may need to continue for 10, 20, or 30 minutes. Switch with another rescuer every 2 minutes to avoid fatigue. Fatigue leads to shallow compressions. If you are alone, keep going as long as you can. Remember: 5 cm deep, 100-120 per minute, full recoil. Practice on a firm surface. Do not be afraid to push hard. The life you save could be a family member, a colleague, or a customer..Trim()

Rescue Breaths: When and How to Provide

Rescue breaths, also called mouth-to-mouth, provide oxygen to a person who is not breathing. In adult CPR, the current recommendation is to start with 30 chest compressions before giving 2 rescue breaths. This is called the 30:2 ratio. However, if you are untrained or uncomfortable, hands-only CPR is still effective. Rescue breaths are especially important for drowning, electrocution, and drug overdose, where lack of oxygen is the main problem. Here is how to give them. First, open the airway. Place one hand on the forehead and two fingers under the chin. Tilt the head back gently. This lifts the tongue away from the back of the throat. Pinch the nose shut with your thumb and index finger. Take a normal breath, not a deep one. Seal your mouth over the person's mouth. Blow steadily for about 1 second. Watch for the chest to rise. If it rises, give a second breath. If it does not rise, re-tilt the head and try again. Do not blow too hard or too fast; it can cause stomach inflation. After 2 breaths, immediately resume compressions. Continue cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths. In Africa, concerns about diseases like HIV and TB may make you hesitant. Use a CPR face shield or pocket mask if available. If not, you can still do hands-only CPR. The risk of transmission is very low. If you are unwilling or unable to give breaths, do not stop compressions. Something is better than nothing. Remember: 30 compressions, 2 breaths, repeat..Trim()

Using an AED: Pad Placement and Safety

An AED, or Automated External Defibrillator, is a portable device that checks the heart rhythm and can send an electric shock to restart the heart. It is safe and easy to use, even for untrained people. AEDs are increasingly available in African airports, malls, and large offices. If you see one, use it. First, turn on the AED. It will give you voice prompts. Expose the person's chest. Dry it if wet. Remove any medication patches. If the person has a lot of chest hair, shave it quickly with a razor from the AED kit. Place one pad on the upper right side of the chest, just below the collarbone. Place the other pad on the lower left side, below the nipple, on the side of the chest. The pads have pictures to guide you. Plug the connector into the AED if not already connected. The AED will analyze the heart rhythm. Do not touch the person during analysis. If a shock is advised, the AED will charge. Shout 'Clear!' and make sure no one is touching the person. Press the shock button. Immediately resume chest compressions after the shock. If no shock is advised, continue CPR. The AED will re-analyze every 2 minutes. Keep the pads on. In Africa, AEDs may be rare, but if one is available, it greatly improves survival. Do not be afraid to use it. It will not shock a normal heart. Remember: turn on, attach pads, clear, shock if advised, resume CPR..Trim()

Team CPR and Switching Roles

Cardiac arrest is a team effort. When more than one rescuer is available, you can provide better CPR with less fatigue. The key is to switch roles every 2 minutes, or sooner if tired. High-quality compressions require energy. After 2 minutes, even a strong person starts to push less deeply. So, plan your team. One person does compressions. Another gives rescue breaths. A third calls emergency services and finds an AED. A fourth can manage the crowd and keep time. If you have only two rescuers, one does compressions while the other gives breaths. Switch every 2 minutes. The switch should be quickâless than 5 seconds. To switch smoothly, the rescuer doing compressions says, 'I'm getting tired, switch on my next count.' Then, at the end of a cycle, the new rescuer kneels on the opposite side, places hands on the chest, and continues. The old rescuer moves to the breathing position. If you are alone, you cannot switch, but you can call for help. In African communities, teamwork is natural. Use it. Appoint a timekeeper with a phone timer. Rotate roles. Keep encouraging each other. After the ambulance arrives, give a clear handover: what happened, when CPR started, how many shocks, and any changes. Remember: 2-minute switches, clear communication, and support. Team CPR saves more lives than solo CPR. Practice with your colleagues, family, or community group. You never know when you will need it..Trim()

Types of Bleeding: Arterial, Venous, Capillary

Bleeding is classified by which blood vessels are damaged. Knowing the difference helps you act fast. Capillary bleeding is the most common: small cuts, scrapes, or abrasions. Blood oozes slowly and is usually bright red. It often stops on its own or with light pressure. Venous bleeding comes from veins, which carry blood back to the heart. It flows steadily and is dark red. Because veins are under low pressure, bleeding can be controlled with direct pressure, but it can still be serious if a large vein is cut. Arterial bleeding is the most dangerous. Arteries carry oxygen-rich blood from the heart under high pressure. Blood spurts in pulses, matching the heartbeat, and is bright red. This type of bleeding can quickly become life-threatening..Trim()

Classification of Burns: First, Second, Third Degree

Burns are classified by how deep they damage the skin. First-degree burns affect only the outer layer (epidermis). The skin is red, painful, and dry, like a mild sunburn. Second-degree burns go deeper into the dermis, causing blisters, severe pain, and a wet, shiny appearance. Third-degree burns destroy all skin layers and may damage nerves, fat, muscle, or bone. The skin may be white, brown, charred, or leathery, and the area may be painless because nerves are destroyed. In Africa, common causes include open fires for cooking, hot water, paraffin stoves, and workplace accidents. To estimate burn size, use the palm of the victim's hand (including fingers) as about 1% of their total body surface area (TBSA). For example, if a burn covers 5 palms, it is roughly 5% TBSA. Seek emergency care if a burn is larger than 3% TBSA (about 3 palms) in children, or larger than 10% in adults, or if it involves the face, hands, feet, genitals, or major joints. Also get help for any third-degree burn, electrical burn, chemical burn, or if the victim has trouble breathing. Remember: never apply butter, oil, toothpaste, or mud to a burnâthese trap heat and cause infection. Cool the burn with clean, cool running water for at least 20 minutes as soon as possible. For third-degree burns, do not cool with water if the person is shivering or if the burn is largeâkeep them warm and get urgent help. CPR and AED Skills Practice

When someone collapses and is not breathing, every second counts. In this lesson, you will practice CPR and using an AED on a manikin. Work on a firm, flat surface like a table or the ground.
