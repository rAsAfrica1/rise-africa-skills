Basic Botany and Growth Stages of Soybean

Soybean is an annual legume with a taproot system that can reach 1.5 meters deep, though most roots are in the top 30 cm. The stem is erect, hairy, and grows 0.5-1.5 meters tall. Leaves are trifoliate (three leaflets) and alternate on the stem. Flowers are small, white to purple, and self-pollinated. Pods are 3-7 cm long, containing 2-4 seeds. Seeds are round to oval, with colors ranging from yellow, green, brown, to black. The root system forms nodules with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Bradyrhizobium japonicum), which convert atmospheric nitrogen into plant-available forms..Trim()

.Trim()

Growth stages are divided into vegetative (V) and reproductive (R) stages. Vegetative stages: VE (emergence), VC (cotyledon), V1 (first trifoliate), V2 (second trifoliate), up to V(n) where n is the number of trifoliate leaves. Reproductive stages: R1 (beginning flowering), R2 (full flowering), R3 (beginning pod), R4 (full pod), R5 (beginning seed), R6 (full seed), R7 (beginning maturity), R8 (full maturity). Understanding these stages helps with timing of fertilizer, irrigation, and pest control..Trim()

.Trim()

At R7, leaves start turning yellow and pods turn brown. Harvest at R8 when seeds rattle in the pod and moisture is about 14-16%. In Africa, many farmers harvest too early or too late, leading to losses. Timely harvest ensures high seed quality..Trim()

Module 2 · Understanding Soybean Varieties

Determinate vs. Indeterminate Growth Habits

Soybean plants grow in two main ways: determinate and indeterminate. Determinate varieties stop growing taller once they start flowering. They put all their energy into filling pods. These are often shorter, bushy plants, maturing in a shorter window. Indeterminate varieties keep growing while they flower and set pods. They get taller, produce more leaves, and can yield more over a longer season. In Africa, most commercial varieties are indeterminate because they adapt to varying rainfall. For example, in Nigeria, the variety 'TGX 1448-2E' is indeterminate and yields about 2.5 tonnes per hectare with good management. Determinate types like 'Doko' are common in shorter-season areas of northern Ghana. The choice affects your planting date and spacing. Indeterminate types need wider spacing (75 cm between rows) to allow air movement and reduce disease. Determinate types can be planted closer (50 cm). Also, indeterminate soybeans are more prone to lodging if you apply too much nitrogen. Since soybean fixes its own nitrogen, avoid nitrogen fertilizer; instead, inoculate seed with rhizobia. For a smallholder, indeterminate varieties are often better because they can recover from early stress like drought. But if your rainy season is short (under 100 days), choose determinate. Always check with your local extension officer for the best growth habit for your area. Knowing this helps you plan harvest and avoid losses..Trim()

Maturity Groups and Selection Criteria

Soybean varieties are grouped by how many days they take to mature, called maturity groups (MG). Groups range from 000 (very early, 75-85 days) to X (very late, 160+ days). In Africa, most recommended varieties fall in MG III to MG VII. For example, in Zambia, 'Magoye' is MG IV (110-120 days). In Uganda, 'Maksoy 3N' is MG V (120-130 days). Choosing the right maturity group is critical. If you plant a late variety in a short rainy season, pods won't fill before dry weather. If you plant an early variety in a long season, you lose yield potential. Start by finding your local rainfall pattern: how many days of reliable rain do you get? Then pick a variety that matures 10-15 days before the rains normally end. For a 120-day season, choose MG IV (110 days). For 150 days, MG V or VI. Also consider day length: near the equator, day length is stable, so maturity is mostly driven by temperature. In southern Africa, longer days in summer can delay flowering. Tools like the 'Soybean Maturity Group Map' from IITA help. Ask your seed dealer for the maturity group number. If they don't know, avoid that variety. Also look at yield potential: a good variety yields 2-3 tonnes per hectare with proper spacing and inoculant. But don't chase highest yield if it matures too late. A slightly lower-yielding early variety that finishes before drought is better than a high-yielding late one that fails. Always test a new variety on a small plot before planting your whole farm..Trim()

GMO, Non-GMO, and Organic Varieties

Soybean varieties come in three main types: GMO (genetically modified), non-GMO (conventional), and organic. GMO soybeans have genes inserted for traits like herbicide tolerance (e.g., Roundup Ready) or insect resistance. In Africa, GMO soybeans are grown commercially only in South Africa. Most other African countries do not allow GMO soybean planting, though some import GMO soy for animal feed. Non-GMO varieties are bred through traditional crossing. They are the standard in Nigeria, Zambia, Ghana, and Kenya. Organic soybeans are non-GMO and grown without synthetic pesticides or fertilizers. They fetch premium prices in export markets, sometimes 20-30% more. But organic yields are often lower (1-1.5 tonnes per hectare) because weeds and pests are harder to control. For a smallholder, non-GMO is usually best: seed is cheaper, you can save seed (though not recommended for hybrids), and local markets accept it. If you want to sell to organic buyers, you need certification, which costs money and takes 2-3 years. GMO seed is expensive and you cannot save it legally. Also, some countries like Kenya require labeling of GMO foods. Before planting, check your country's biosafety laws. In most of Africa, planting GMO soybean without approval is illegal. So stick to non-GMO unless you have a contract with a buyer who wants GMO and you are in a country where it's legal. For organic, start with a small plot, use compost and manual weeding, and find a certified buyer first. Never mix GMO and non-GMO seed in the same field if you want to sell as non-GMO..Trim()

Seed Quality and Certification

Good seed is the foundation of a good soybean crop. Poor seed can cut yields by 50% or more. Quality seed has four key attributes: high germination (at least 85%), genetic purity (true to variety), physical purity (no weed seeds or stones), and freedom from disease. In Africa, always buy certified seed from a registered dealer. Look for the certification tag from your national seed authority, like NASC in Nigeria, SCCI in Zambia, or KEPHIS in Kenya. The tag shows the variety name, lot number, germination percentage, and test date. If a seller cannot show a tag, walk away. You can also do a simple germination test at home: count 100 seeds, plant them in a tray of soil, keep moist, and count how many sprout after 7 days. If less than 85 sprout, don't use that seed for planting. Certified seed costs more (about $1-2 per kg) but is worth it. Saved seed from your previous crop often has lower germination and may carry diseases like soybean mosaic virus. If you must save seed, select from healthy plants, dry it well to 12% moisture, and store in a cool, dry place with ash or neem leaves to deter insects. Never use seed from a field with disease. Also, check seed size: larger seeds have more food reserves and emerge stronger. But don't buy based on size alone. Always ask for the variety name and maturity group. For organic production, look for organic-certified seed, which is harder to find but available from some cooperatives. Remember: cheap seed is expensive in the long run..Trim()

Regional Adaptation and Yield Potential

Soybean varieties perform differently across Africa's diverse climates. A variety that yields 3 tonnes per hectare in the highlands of Ethiopia may yield only 1 tonne in the lowlands of Mozambique. Regional adaptation means matching a variety to your local rainfall, temperature, soil, and day length. In West Africa, IITA has released many varieties like 'TGX 1835-10E' (yields 2.5 t/ha, matures in 100 days) for the savannas. In East Africa, 'Maksoy' series from Uganda yields 2-2.5 t/ha in 110-120 days. In southern Africa, 'Magoye' is popular in Zambia and Zimbabwe for its drought tolerance, yielding 1.5-2 t/ha. Yield potential is the maximum yield a variety can achieve under ideal conditions. But your actual yield depends on management: planting date, spacing, weeding, and pest control. For example, planting at 60 cm x 10 cm with 2 seeds per hole gives about 333,000 plants per hectare. That density can produce 2.5 t/ha if you control weeds early. If you plant late or skip weeding, yield drops to 0.5 t/ha. Also, soybean needs phosphorus and potassium. Apply 100 kg/ha of NPK 20-10-10 at planting if your soil is poor. But don't apply nitrogen; soybean fixes its own. Inoculate seed with rhizobia (e.g., Biofix) for best results. Check with your local agricultural research station for the best varieties for your district. They often have trial data. Never assume a variety from another region will work for you. Test it first on a small plot. With good management, even a 1.5 t/ha variety can be profitable..Trim()

Choosing the Right Variety for Your Farm

Choosing the right soybean variety is a decision that affects your entire season. Start by answering four questions: (1) How long is my rainy season? (2) What is my market? (3) What are my pests and diseases? (4) What seed can I afford? For rainfall, count the reliable rainy days. If you have 100-120 days, choose an early maturity group (MG III or IV) like 'Doko' or 'TGX 1448-2E'. If you have 150+ days, choose MG V or VI like 'Maksoy 3N'. For market, if you sell to a processor for oil or feed, any non-GMO variety works. If you sell to organic exporters, you need organic-certified seed. If you sell to a GMO-accepting buyer in South Africa, you can plant GMO. For pests, if you have high soybean rust, choose resistant varieties like 'TGX 1835-10E'. For nematodes, choose resistant varieties like 'UG 5'. For seed cost, certified seed is best but if money is tight, buy a small amount and multiply it: plant 5 kg on 0.2 ha, harvest 100 kg, and plant that next season. But never save seed from hybrid varieties. Also consider your labor: indeterminate varieties need more spacing and may need staking if they lodge. Determinate types are easier to manage. Finally, talk to other farmers in your area. What did they plant last year? What yielded well? Visit a demonstration plot at your extension office. Try at least two varieties on small plots side by side. Measure yield from each. Then scale up the winner. That is the surest way to choose the right variety for your farm..Trim()

Module 6 · Water Management and Irrigation

Water Requirements at Different Growth Stages

Soybeans need different amounts of water at each stage of growth. From planting to emergence, the seed needs enough moisture to swell and sprout, but not so much that it rots. A light irrigation of 15-20 mm after planting is enough if the soil is dry. During the vegetative stage (from emergence to flowering), the plant grows leaves and roots. It uses about 3-4 mm of water per day. In dry conditions, you may need to irrigate every 7-10 days with 25-30 mm. The most critical stage is flowering and pod formation. Water stress now can cut yields by 50% or more. The plant needs 5-6 mm per day. If rainfall is low, irrigate every 5-7 days with 30-35 mm. During pod fill, water helps fill the seeds. Too little water causes small, shriveled beans. Aim for 4-5 mm per day. As the plant matures and leaves turn yellow, water needs drop to 2-3 mm per day. Stop irrigation once pods are full and leaves start dropping to avoid mold. In Africa, where rains can be unpredictable, knowing these stages helps you plan. For example, in Nigeria's Guinea Savannah, soybean flowering often coincides with a dry spell in August. A farmer with a small pump can save the crop by applying 30 mm at that time. Use a rain gauge or a simple tin can to measure rainfall. If you get less than 25 mm in a week, consider irrigation. Remember, soybeans are more drought-tolerant than maize, but they still need consistent moisture for good yields. Sandy soils hold less water, so irrigate more often but with less water each time. Clay soils hold more, so you can wait longer but avoid waterlogging. Always check soil moisture by squeezing a handful of soil: if it crumbles, it's too dry; if it forms a ball, it's good; if it drips, it's too wet..Trim()

Irrigation Systems: Drip, Sprinkler, Furrow

Choosing the right irrigation system depends on your field size, water source, and budget. Drip irrigation is the most efficient. It delivers water directly to the plant roots through small tubes with emitters. It saves up to 50% water compared to other methods. For soybeans, use drip lines spaced 0.5-0.75 m apart, with emitters every 30 cm. Each emitter releases 2-4 liters per hour. You can run it for 1-2 hours every day or every other day. Drip is good for small plots (up to 1 hectare) and where water is scarce. It also reduces weeds because only the crop gets water. However, drip systems can clog if water is dirty, so use a filter. Sprinkler irrigation mimics rainfall. You can use hand-moved sprinklers or permanent systems. For soybeans, sprinklers should apply 25-30 mm per application. Space sprinklers 12 m apart and run them for 2-3 hours to get that amount. Sprinklers are good for larger fields (2-5 hectares) and sandy soils. But they can spread diseases if leaves stay wet, so irrigate in the morning so leaves dry before evening. Also, wind can cause uneven watering. Furrow irrigation is the cheapest and most common in Africa. You make small channels between crop rows and let water flow. For soybeans, plant on ridges and make furrows 0.5-0.75 m apart. Water flows down the furrow and soaks into the soil. You need a slope of 0.5-2% for water to flow. Apply water until the furrow is full for about 30 minutes, then move to the next. Furrow uses more water (up to 40% loss) and can cause erosion, but it requires no special equipment. In many parts of Africa, farmers use buckets or watering cans for small plots. That is labor-intensive but works for 0.1 hectare. You can also use a treadle pump to lift water from a well or river. No matter the system, always maintain it: check for leaks, clean filters, and repair broken parts quickly. The best system is one you can afford and manage well..Trim()

Scheduling Irrigation Based on Soil Moisture

Irrigating at the right time is as important as how much you apply. You don't want to guess; you want to know when the soil is dry. The simplest tool is your hand. Dig 15 cm into the soil near the plant roots. Take a handful and squeeze it. If it forms a ball that holds together but doesn't drip, the moisture is good. If it crumbles, it's too dry and you need to irrigate. If it drips water, it's too wet and you should wait. Do this test every 2-3 days during flowering and pod fill. Another low-cost tool is a soil moisture meter. You can buy a simple probe for $20-50. It gives a reading of wet, moist, or dry. Insert it 15-20 cm deep at several spots in the field. If most spots read dry, irrigate. For more precision, use a tensiometer. It measures how tightly water is held in the soil. Install it at 30 cm depth. When the reading is above 50 centibars (cb), it's time to irrigate. Below 30 cb, soil is wet enough. Tensiometers cost $50-100 but last for years. You can also use the feel method with a shovel: dig a small hole, feel the soil at 15 cm and 30 cm. If both are dry, irrigate. For soybean, the critical times to check are: just after planting, at flowering, and at pod fill. In these stages, don't let soil moisture drop below 50% of field capacity. In sandy soils, check more often because they dry quickly. In clay soils, check less often but be careful not to overwater. A simple rule: if you get less than 25 mm of rain in a week, check soil moisture. If dry, apply 25-30 mm of water. Keep a record of rainfall and irrigation dates in a notebook. This helps you learn your field's pattern. In Africa, where rains are erratic, scheduling is key to saving water and getting a good harvest. You can also use a rain gauge made from a tin can to measure rainfall. Place it in an open area and check daily. If you know a dry spell is coming, irrigate before the soil gets too dry. This reduces stress on the plants..Trim()

Drainage Management to Prevent Waterlogging

Soybeans hate waterlogged soil. Too much water in the root zone stops the roots from taking up nutrients and can cause root rot. Waterlogging for just 3-5 days during flowering can cut yields by 30-50%. In Africa, heavy rains can flood fields, especially in low-lying areas or on clay soils. To prevent this, you need good drainage. The first step is to make raised beds or ridges. Plant soybeans on ridges 20-30 cm high. This keeps the roots above the water table. If you use furrow irrigation, make sure the furrows drain well. At the end of each furrow, dig a shallow ditch to carry excess water away. For flat fields, create a system of open drains. Dig ditches 30-50 cm deep and 30 cm wide along the edges and through the field every 10-15 meters. These drains collect surface water and lead it to a lower area or a stream. In small plots, you can use a spade to make shallow channels. Another method is subsoiling. If you have a tractor, use a subsoiler to break up compacted soil layers 30-40 cm deep. This allows water to soak in faster. For farmers without tractors, use a fork jembe or hoe to loosen soil. Also, add organic matter like compost or manure. It improves soil structure and helps water drain. If your field is in a valley, consider making a diversion ditch on the uphill side to redirect runoff. In areas with heavy rains, plant on the contour (across the slope) to slow water flow. Check your field after every heavy rain. If you see standing water for more than a day, improve drainage immediately. You can also plant cover crops like cowpea or mucuna in the off-season to improve soil. Remember, soybeans are more sensitive to waterlogging than maize. If you see yellowing leaves and stunted growth, it might be waterlogging. Act fast: open drains and avoid irrigating until the soil dries. In the dry season, you can use the same drains for irrigation if needed. Good drainage also reduces disease like root rot and charcoal rot. So invest in drainage before planting..Trim()

Drought Stress Mitigation Techniques

Drought can hit soybeans at any stage, but flowering and pod fill are the most damaging. When plants don't get enough water, they wilt, leaves curl, and pods fail to fill. But you can reduce the damage. First, choose drought-tolerant varieties. In Africa, varieties like 'TGX' lines from IITA are bred for drought tolerance. Ask your extension officer for recommendations. Second, plant early when rains are reliable. If you plant with the first rains, the crop has more time to grow before a dry spell. Third, use mulch. Spread crop residues like maize stalks or grass between rows. A 5 cm layer of mulch reduces evaporation by up to 70%. It keeps soil cool and moist. You can also use plastic mulch, but it's expensive. Fourth, practice conservation tillage. Don't plow too much; leave residues on the surface. This helps water infiltrate and reduces runoff. Fifth, if you have irrigation, apply water at critical stages. Even one good irrigation at flowering can save the crop. If you don't have irrigation, you can use a bottle drip system: bury a plastic bottle with small holes near the plant roots and fill it with water. It slowly releases water. For larger areas, use a treadle pump to lift water from a well. Sixth, reduce plant density. If drought is expected, plant fewer seeds per hectare. Plants will have more water each. Seventh, control weeds. Weeds compete for water. Keep the field clean. Eighth, apply potassium fertilizer. Potassium helps plants regulate water. Use 50 kg/ha of muriate of potash. Ninth, if drought hits, you can spray a kaolin clay solution on leaves. It reflects sunlight and reduces water loss. Mix 30 g of kaolin per liter of water and spray. Tenth, harvest early if drought is severe. If pods are filling but plants are dying, you can harvest and use the beans for seed or feed. Don't wait for total loss. Finally, keep records. Note when drought occurs and how you responded. This helps you plan next season. In Africa, climate change is making droughts more common, so these techniques are essential. Even small actions can make a big difference..Trim()

Water Conservation Practices

Water is precious, especially in Africa where rainfall is often scarce or unpredictable. Conserving water means getting more crop per drop. Start with your soil. Add organic matter like compost, manure, or crop residues. Every 1% increase in soil organic matter can hold an extra 20,000 liters of water per hectare. So make compost from kitchen waste, animal dung, and crop residues. Apply 5-10 tons per hectare before planting. Mulching is another key practice. Cover the soil with a 5-10 cm layer of dry grass, straw, or leaves. This reduces evaporation, keeps soil temperature stable, and suppresses weeds. You can also use living mulch like cowpea or groundnut between soybean rows, but be careful not to compete for water. Next, improve your irrigation method. If you use furrow, convert to surge irrigation: apply water in pulses (on and off) to reduce runoff and deep percolation. For sprinklers, irrigate early morning or late evening when evaporation is low. Avoid irrigating on windy days. For drip, use timers or check emitters regularly to avoid overwatering. Harvest rainwater. Build small dams, ponds, or tanks to catch runoff from roofs and fields. A 10,000-liter tank can irrigate 0.1 hectare for a week. You can also dig trenches or swales along the contour to capture rainwater. These are shallow ditches that hold water and let it soak in. In dry areas, use zai pits: dig planting holes 20-30 cm deep and wide, fill with manure, and plant. The pits capture water. Another practice is to reduce tillage. No-till or minimum tillage leaves residues on the surface, which improves water infiltration and reduces evaporation. If you use a plow, don't overdo it. Also, fix leaks in your irrigation system. A single dripping emitter can waste 20 liters per day. Check pipes, joints, and emitters weekly. Train your family or workers on water-saving habits. For example, use a bucket instead of a hose to water seedlings. Finally, plant drought-tolerant varieties and adjust planting dates to match rainfall. In many parts of Africa, the rainy season is getting shorter, so every drop counts. By combining these practices, you can reduce water use by 30-50% while maintaining yields. Remember, conservation is not just for dry times; it's a year-round habit..Trim()

Major Insect Pests: Aphids, Beetles, Caterpillars

Soybean in Africa is attacked by three main groups of insects: aphids, beetles, and caterpillars. Aphids are tiny (1-3 mm), soft-bodied, and cluster on young leaves and stems. They suck sap, causing leaves to curl, yellow, and stunt growth. They also transmit viruses. Check weekly from 2 weeks after planting. A heavy infestation is when you find 50 or more aphids per plant on 20% of plants. In cool highlands, aphids can wipe out 40% of yield if untreated..Trim()

.Trim()

Beetles include blister beetles, bean leaf beetles, and flea beetles. Blister beetles are 1-2 cm long, black or grey with stripes. They chew leaves and flowers. Bean leaf beetles are red or yellow with black spots and chew round holes in leaves. Flea beetles are small (2-3 mm) and jump when disturbed; they cause shot-hole damage. A threshold for bean leaf beetles is 5 beetles per plant during pod fill. Blister beetles also release a blistering chemical, so wear gloves when handling them..Trim()

.Trim()

Caterpillars are the larvae of moths and butterflies. The most damaging are pod borers (Helicoverpa and Spodoptera species), armyworms, and loopers. They chew leaves, bore into pods, and eat seeds. Armyworms march in groups and can defoliate a field in 2 days. Scout by shaking plants over a white cloth or bucket. Threshold: 2-3 large caterpillars per row meter, or 20% defoliation before flowering. Pod borers are worse after flowering; 1 larva per plant can reduce yield by 10%..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, these pests are worse in warm, dry spells and where soybeans follow soybeans. Rotate with maize or sorghum to break cycles. Plant early to avoid peak pest populations. Keep field edges clean because pests often start there. Learn to identify each pest correctly; a photo guide from your extension officer helps. Early action saves the crop..Trim()

Scouting Techniques and Economic Thresholds

Scouting means walking your soybean field regularly to count pests and damage. Do it at least once a week from emergence to pod fill. Walk in a zigzag or W pattern, stopping at 10 spots per hectare. At each spot, inspect 5 plants. Look at the whole plant: upper and lower leaves, stems, flowers, and pods. Use a hand lens (10x) to see small pests like aphids and mites. Carry a notebook and record date, pest, number per plant, and damage level..Trim()

.Trim()

For aphids, count aphids on 3 leaves per plant (top, middle, bottom). Economic threshold: 50 aphids per leaf on 20% of plants. For beetles, count beetles per plant. Threshold for bean leaf beetle: 5 per plant during pod fill. For caterpillars, shake plants over a white cloth (1m x 1m) and count dislodged larvae. Threshold: 2-3 large caterpillars per row meter, or 20% defoliation before flowering. For pod borers, inspect 20 pods per plant; threshold is 1 larva per plant or 5% pod damage..Trim()

.Trim()

Economic threshold (ET) is the pest level at which the cost of control equals the value of yield loss. If pest numbers are below ET, do not spray; you save money and protect beneficial insects. If above ET, act immediately. Example: if a farmer expects 2 tonnes per hectare and soybeans sell for 500 USD per tonne, the crop is worth 1000 USD. If caterpillars at 3 per meter cause 10% loss (100 USD), and spraying costs 30 USD, then spraying is profitable. But if pest numbers cause only 2% loss (20 USD), spraying loses money..Trim()

.Trim()

Keep scouting records to see trends. After rain, pest numbers often drop. After dry spells, they rise. Share records with neighbours to warn them. Train two people per farm to scout so you always have data. Use a smartphone app like 'Pest Scout' if available, or a simple notebook. Scouting takes 30-60 minutes per hectare but prevents crop loss..Trim()

.Trim()

.Trim()

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Strategies

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) combines many methods to keep pests below economic thresholds with the least harm to people and the environment. IPM is not a single action but a year-round plan. It starts with good crop husbandry: plant certified, pest-resistant soybean varieties like 'TGx' lines that resist aphids and pod borers. Rotate soybeans with cereals like maize or sorghum every season to break pest life cycles. Plant early and at recommended spacing (60 cm between rows, 5 cm within rows) to reduce humidity that favours aphids..Trim()

.Trim()

Sanitation is key. Remove crop residues after harvest because they harbour beetles and caterpillars. Plough deeply to bury pupae. Keep field edges free of weeds like blackjack and pigweed, which host aphids and beetles. Use trap crops: plant a border of cowpea or sunflower around your soybean. Pests attack the trap crop first, then you can spray or destroy just that border. Intercrop soybean with maize or sorghum; this confuses pests and increases beneficial insects..Trim()

.Trim()

Monitor weekly using scouting and economic thresholds. Only spray when thresholds are reached. Choose selective insecticides like Bt or insecticidal soap first, and broad-spectrum chemicals only as a last resort. Rotate chemicals with different modes of action to prevent resistance. For example, if you used lambda-cyhalothrin this season, use chlorantraniliprole next season. Never mix chemicals unless the label says it is safe..Trim()

.Trim()

After harvest, evaluate what worked. Share with neighbours. IPM reduces costs by 30-50% compared to calendar spraying. It also protects bees, which pollinate soybeans and increase yield by 10-15%. After harvest, soybean seed can be attacked by storage pests like cowpea weevil (Callosobruchus maculatus), grain moths, and flour beetles. These pests bore into seeds, reducing quality and germination. A heavy infestation can destroy 50% of stored seed in 6 months. Prevent them before storage. Dry soybean to 12% moisture or less. Use a moisture meter; if not available, bite a seed: it should crack, not be soft. Clean the storage area: remove old grain, dust, and cobwebs. Repair cracks and holes..Trim()

.Trim()

Common Soybean Diseases: Rust, Blight, Root Rot

Soybean rust is the most feared disease in Africa. It starts as tiny brown or reddish-brown spots on the underside of leaves. These spots have raised pustules that release spores. In humid conditions, rust can spread across a field in 7 to 10 days and cause 30% to 80% yield loss. Scout your field twice a week from flowering onwards. Blight comes in two main forms: bacterial blight and frogeye leaf spot. Bacterial blight causes angular, water-soaked spots that turn brown with a yellow halo. Determining Harvest Maturity

Soybean harvest maturity is not just about the calendar. You need to check the crop itself. The best time to harvest is when 95% of the pods are brown or tan and the seeds rattle inside. The leaves have mostly dropped, and the stems are dry but not brittle. If you wait too long, pods split open and seeds fall to the ground. If you harvest too early, seeds are green, small, and have poor oil content. In Africa, where many farmers grow soybean in rain-fed conditions, maturity can vary by field and even within a field. Walk your field and sample a few plants from different spots. Squeeze a pod: if the seed is hard and makes a sound, it's ready. In northern Nigeria, harvest often starts in late October to early November; in Zambia, it's May to June. Plan to harvest as soon as the crop is ready, because delays increase losses. If you have a lot of acreage, you may need to harvest in stages. Also, check the weather forecast: if rain is coming, harvest immediately, even if slightly immature, because wet pods lead to mold and poor quality. For smallholders, hand harvesting with sickles is common, but it's slow. If you can, hire a combine harvester or a contractor. The key is to monitor your crop daily once it starts turning brown. Don't rely on neighbours; every field is different. Use a simple moisture tester or the bite test. Harvest when the majority of pods are dry and seeds are firm. Combine Settings and Harvest Efficiency

If you use a combine harvester, correct settings are critical to avoid losses. Start with the reel speed: it should be about 10-20% faster than the ground speed. If the reel is too fast, it will beat the pods and cause shattering. For soybean, the cylinder speed should be around 400-600 rpm, and the concave clearance should be wide enough to avoid cracking seeds but narrow enough to thresh all pods. A good starting point is 10-15 mm clearance. The fan speed should be adjusted so that chaff is blown out but seeds are not. Too much air blows seeds out the back; too little leaves chaff in the grain tank. Check the grain tank sample: if you see cracked seeds, reduce cylinder speed or increase clearance. If you see unthreshed pods, tighten clearance or increase speed. Ground speed should be slow enough to allow the header to pick up all pods, but fast enough for efficiency. In Africa, many farmers use small combine harvesters or contractors. Before harvesting, level the field and remove obstacles. Make sure the header is set low to catch low pods, but not so low that it picks up soil. For soybean, the header height should be about 5-10 cm above the ground. If the crop is lodged, you may need to adjust the reel to lift the plants. Always monitor losses behind the combine: place a 1m x 1m sheet on the ground and count seeds. More than 4-5 seeds per square foot means you are losing too much. Adjust settings accordingly. Also, check for header loss: pods left on the stem. If you find many, slow down or adjust the reel. Minimizing Harvest Losses

Harvest losses in soybean can be as high as 20% if you are not careful. The main causes are shattering, header loss, and threshing loss. Shattering happens when pods are overly dry and brittle. To reduce it, harvest when moisture is 13-15%, not lower. If you must harvest at lower moisture, do it in the early morning or late evening when humidity is higher, reducing shattering. For hand harvesting, use a sharp sickle and cut close to the ground, then gently place plants on a tarpaulin. Don't throw them. For combine harvesting, adjust the reel speed and height. Also, ensure the header is not too low to avoid picking up stones and soil. Another major loss is from lodging (plants fallen over). If your crop is lodged, you may need to harvest in one direction or use a pickup reel. In Africa, many smallholders harvest by hand and then thresh by beating with sticks. This can cause seed damage and loss. Instead, use a small thresher or a mechanical thresher. If beating, do it on a clean tarpaulin and gently. After threshing, winnow to remove chaff. Losses also occur during transport: use tight bags and don't overload. Store in a dry place. To measure losses, use a 1m x 1m frame. Count seeds on the ground before harvest (pre-harvest loss), during harvest (header loss), and after (threshing loss). Aim for total losses below 5%. For every 1% loss, you lose about 20 kg per hectare if yield is 2 tons. So, 5% loss is 100 kg. That's money lost. Simple steps: harvest at right moisture, adjust combine, use tarpaulins, handle gently. Also, train your workers. Drying and Storage of Soybeans

After harvest, soybean moisture is often 15-18%. For safe storage, you need to dry it to 12% or less. If you store at higher moisture, mold grows and seeds rot. In Africa, sun drying is common. Spread beans on a clean tarpaulin or concrete floor in thin layers (5-10 cm) and stir every hour. It takes 2-3 days of good sunshine to reach 12%. If you have a moisture meter, test daily. Without a meter, bite a seed: it should shatter, not be soft. Another method is using a solar dryer or a mechanical dryer. For small quantities, a simple solar dryer with a black plastic sheet can work. Never dry on bare ground; it picks up soil and stones. Also, avoid drying on roads where vehicles may run over. Once dry, clean the beans by winnowing or using a sieve to remove chaff, dirt, and broken seeds. Then store in clean, dry bags. Use new or well-cleaned jute or polypropylene bags. Never use bags that previously held fertilizer or chemicals. Store in a cool, dry, well-ventilated place. Raise bags on pallets or wooden planks to avoid moisture from the floor. Keep away from walls. Stack bags no more than 10 high to avoid crushing. If you have many bags, leave space between stacks for air circulation. For long-term storage, you can use hermetic bags (like PICS bags) or metal silos. These prevent insects and mold. Check stored beans every month for signs of insects or moisture. If you see weevils, you can sun-dry again or use approved storage insecticides like Actellic. But always follow safety instructions. In humid areas, consider using airtight containers. Remove all stones, dirt, and plant parts. Use a sieve or winnowing. Broken seeds come from rough threshing or poor combine settings. Adjust your thresher to minimize breakage. Damaged seeds include those with insect holes, mold, or discoloration. These reduce quality and can cause rejection. After drying, take a sample to a local extension officer or buyer to test. Many cooperatives have moisture meters and grading charts. If you are selling to a processor, they may have specific requirements. For example, some want large seeds for food, others want high oil content for crushing. Know your market. Also, avoid mixing old and new beans; it lowers quality. Store different varieties separately. When bagging, label with date and variety. If you are in a group, consider collective grading and marketing to get better prices. In Nigeria, the Nigerian Agricultural Quarantine Service sets standards; in Kenya, KEBS. But even without formal grading, you can improve by cleaning and drying properly. Remember, a clean, dry product always fetches more. Also, never use chemicals directly on the grain unless approved and correctly applied. Transportation and Marketing Preparation

After harvest, drying, and grading, you need to transport and market your soybean. Proper preparation ensures you get the best price. First, package your beans in clean, strong bags. Cost of Production and Budgeting

To make money from soybeans, you must know your costs. Start by listing every expense for one hectare. In many African countries, seed costs about $30-$50, inoculant $10, and fertilizer (if used) $100-$200. Land preparation with a tractor might cost $50-$100, while manual labour could be $80. Planting, weeding, and harvesting labour can total $150-$300. Add transport and bags: $50. Total costs often range from $400 to $700 per hectare. .Trim()

.Trim()

Next, estimate your yield. With good management, you can get 1.5 to 2.5 tonnes per hectare. If you sell at $400 per tonne, 2 tonnes gives $800 revenue. Subtract $600 costs, and your profit is $200. But if you only get 1 tonne, you lose money. That's why budgeting matters. .Trim()

.Trim()

Write your budget before planting. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Excel or Google Sheets. List all costs and expected income. Compare different scenarios: what if price drops to $300? What if yield is 1.8 tonnes? This helps you decide whether to plant, and how much to invest. .Trim()

.Trim()

Remember, costs vary by country and season. Always use local prices. Talk to other farmers and extension officers. Keep receipts for everything. At the end of the season, compare your actual costs to your budget. You will learn where you overspent and where you can save. Good budgeting turns farming from gambling into a business..Trim()

Record Keeping and Financial Analysis

Record keeping is the difference between a farmer who guesses and a farmer who knows. You need three simple records: a cash book, a labour record, and a harvest record. In your cash book, write down every shilling or naira you spend and receive. Date, description, amount. For example: '10 May 2025, bought 20kg soybean seed, 45,000 shillings.' At harvest, record bags harvested and sold. .Trim()

.Trim()

At the end of the season, calculate your profit. Total income minus total expenses. But also look deeper. Calculate your cost per kilogram: total costs divided by total kilograms harvested. If you spent 600,000 shillings and harvested 2,000 kg, your cost per kg is 300 shillings. If you sell at 400 shillings, you make 100 shillings profit per kg. .Trim()

.Trim()

Financial analysis also means checking your break-even point. How many kilograms must you sell to cover all costs? Break-even = total costs divided by selling price per kg. If costs are 600,000 and price is 400, break-even is 1,500 kg. Anything above that is profit. .Trim()

.Trim()

Use a simple notebook or a free spreadsheet. Many farmer cooperatives offer record-keeping training. Keep records for at least three seasons. You will see trends: which fields yield more, which costs are rising, and when you make the most profit. With good records, you can get loans from banks or microfinance institutions because you can prove your farm is profitable. Without records, you are invisible to lenders..Trim()

Market Analysis and Price Trends

Soybean prices change with seasons and demand. In many African markets, prices are lowest right after harvest (June-August in East Africa, October-December in West Africa) because everyone is selling. Prices rise 3-6 months later when stocks run low. For example, in Uganda, soybean might sell for 1,800 UGX/kg at harvest and 2,500 UGX/kg six months later. .Trim()

.Trim()

To analyze the market, talk to buyers before you plant. Who buys soybeans? Processors (for oil, animal feed, soy milk), exporters, and local traders. Ask them: What price will you offer at harvest? What quality do you need? Do you offer contracts? Also check online platforms like Esoko, M-Farm, or local commodity exchanges. Radio programs often announce market prices. .Trim()

.Trim()

Track prices weekly in a notebook. After one season, you will see a pattern. You can then decide to store your soybeans and sell later for a higher price. But storage costs money and risks pests. Only store if the price difference covers storage costs and you have good storage. .Trim()

.Trim()

Another strategy is to sell in smaller quantities throughout the year. If you harvest 2 tonnes, sell 500 kg at harvest to cover urgent costs, then sell the rest monthly. This smooths your income. Also, consider processing: a kilogram of soy flour sells for three times the price of raw soybeans. But that requires equipment and certification. Start by understanding raw bean prices, then explore value addition..Trim()

Direct Marketing and Value-Added Products

Direct marketing means selling straight to consumers or processors, skipping middlemen. You can sell to local schools, hospitals, restaurants, or feed mills. For example, a school might buy 100 kg of soybeans per month for porridge. A feed mill might buy 5 tonnes per season. Visit them with a sample and a price. Offer consistent quality and timely delivery. .Trim()

.Trim()

Value-added products increase your profit. Raw soybeans might sell for $400/tonne. But roasted soy flour can sell for $1,200/tonne. Soy milk sells for $0.50 per litre, and from 1 kg of beans you can make 8 litres, earning $4 from $0.40 worth of beans. Other products: soy oil, soy cake (animal feed), and soy-based snacks. .Trim()

.Trim()

To start, learn simple processing. Roast soybeans in a pan or oven, grind into flour using a mill. Package in sealed bags with a label: your farm name, weight, and production date. For soy milk, soak beans overnight, grind, boil, and filter. Add sugar or flavour. Sell at local markets, churches, or through WhatsApp groups. .Trim()

.Trim()

You need food safety. Wash hands, use clean water, and sterilise bottles. In many countries, you need a health certificate to sell processed foods. Visit your local bureau of standards for guidance. Start small: process 10 kg per week. Reinvest profits to buy better equipment. Direct marketing and value addition can double or triple your income from the same soybean crop..Trim()

Contracts and Risk Management

A contract is a written agreement to sell soybeans at a set price, quantity, and time. For example, a processor might contract you to deliver 2 tonnes of soybeans in September at $450 per tonne. This protects you from price drops. But if prices rise to $600, you miss out. So read contracts carefully. .Trim()

.Trim()

Before signing, check: Who bears transport costs? What quality standards (moisture content, foreign matter)? What happens if you fail to deliver? Penalties can be harsh. Always get a copy. If you cannot read, ask a trusted extension officer or lawyer to explain. .Trim()

.Trim()

Risk management means preparing for problems. Weather risk: drought or too much rain. Use drought-tolerant varieties, plant early, and practice conservation agriculture. Pest risk: armyworms, aphids. Scout weekly and use integrated pest management. Price risk: store and sell later, or use contracts. Financial risk: don't borrow more than you can repay. .Trim()

.Trim()

Diversify. Don't plant all your land with soybeans. Grow maize, beans, or vegetables. If soybean prices fall, you still have income. Also, join a farmer group. Groups can negotiate better contracts, share storage, and access insurance. .Trim()

.Trim()

Keep a risk plan: list the top three risks and your actions. For example: 'If drought, I will use my savings to buy water for irrigation.' 'If price drops below $350, I will store and sell in three months.' Simple planning reduces stress and losses..Trim()

Farm Insurance and Government Programs

Farm insurance pays you if disaster strikes. In Africa, index-based insurance is common. It pays out if rainfall is below a certain level, or if yields in your area fall. For example, you pay a premium of $50 per hectare. If rainfall is 30% below normal, you get $200. You don't need to prove individual loss. .Trim()

.Trim()

Where to find insurance? In Kenya, Kilimo Salama; in Nigeria, NAIC; in Ghana, Ghana Agricultural Insurance Pool. Ask your cooperative or extension officer. Premiums vary: 5-10% of expected revenue. If you expect $800 revenue, premium might be $40-$80. It's worth it if you cannot afford a total loss. .Trim()

.Trim()

Government programs can help. Many countries subsidise fertiliser, seed, or equipment. For example, Nigeria's Anchor Borrowers' Programme gives loans at low interest to soybean farmers. Ethiopia's Agricultural Transformation Agency provides extension and market linkage. Check with your local agriculture office. .Trim()

.Trim()

Also look for matching grants. The African Development Bank and USAID fund projects that help farmers buy processing equipment. You often need to contribute 30-50% and the grant covers the rest. .Trim()

.Trim()

To access these, you need records and a business plan. That's why Module 12 lessons on budgeting and record keeping matter. Keep your documents ready. Register your farm as a business if possible. Join a cooperative because programs often work through groups. With insurance and government support, you can take bigger risks and grow your soybean business..Trim()
