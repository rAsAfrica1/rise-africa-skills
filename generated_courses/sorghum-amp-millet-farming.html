Spacing recommendations for sorghum: 75cm x 25cm and variations

Spacing is one of the most important decisions you make at planting. For sorghum, the standard recommendation in many African conditions is 75 cm between rows and 25 cm between plants within the row. If you plant too close, plants compete for water and nutrients, and you get tall, thin stalks with small heads. If you plant too wide, you waste land and get more weeds..Trim()

In higher rainfall areas or with short, early-maturing varieties, you can tighten to 75 cm x 20 cm (about 66,000 plants/ha) or 60 cm x 25 cm (about 66,000 plants/ha). The key is to match plant population to available water and soil fertility..Trim()

.Trim()

When planting, use a rope and pegs to mark rows. A simple string with knots every 75 cm helps you keep rows straight. For within-row spacing, you can use a stick cut to 25 cm. Always plant on the contour or across the slope if your land is not flat, to reduce erosion..Trim()

.Trim()

With correct spacing, you give each plant enough room to grow strong and produce a heavy head..Trim()

If you plant too sparsely, you allow weeds to take over and you harvest less grain per hectare..Trim()

Planting depth and seed treatment against termites

Planting depth is critical for germination. For sorghum and millet, plant seeds 2-5 cm deep. In sandy soils, plant deeper (4-5 cm) because the surface dries out quickly. Thinning and gap filling after emergence

Thinning and gap filling are simple but powerful practices to maximize yield. After your sorghum or millet emerges, you will often have too many plants per hill because you dropped multiple seeds. For millet, thin to two plants per station if you planted in hills, or one plant if you planted in rows. Thinning also reduces competition for water and nutrients, and it allows better airflow, reducing disease..Trim()

.Trim()

Gap filling means replanting where seeds did not germinate or where seedlings died. Gaps reduce yield because you have fewer plants than planned. Check your field 1-2 weeks after emergence. Water the replanted spot if possible. If you have extra seedlings from a nursery or from thinned plants, you can transplant them carefully, but sorghum and millet do not transplant as well as some crops. Replanting is usually better. Do gap filling early, within 2 weeks of emergence, so the new plants catch up with the rest..Trim()

.Trim()

Thinning and gap filling together ensure you have the right plant population. A uniform stand uses sunlight, water, and nutrients efficiently. It also makes weeding and harvesting easier. If you skip thinning, you may get many small heads instead of fewer large ones. If you skip gap filling, you lose yield from empty spaces. So take the time to walk your field and do these tasks. Using zai pits or planting basins in dry areas

In dry areas with less than 500 mm rainfall, zai pits (also called planting basins or tassa) are a traditional but powerful technique to capture water and improve soil fertility. A zai pit is a small, shallow basin dug into the soil, about 20-30 cm in diameter and 10-15 cm deep. You dig these pits before the rains, usually during the dry season. Space them 75-80 cm apart in rows that are also 75-80 cm apart. That gives about 15,000-20,000 pits per hectare. In each pit, you place a handful of manure or compost, then plant 3-5 seeds of sorghum or millet after the first rains. The pit collects rainwater and keeps it around the roots, so plants survive dry spells..Trim()

.Trim()

Zai pits are especially good for degraded, crusted soils where water runs off. The pits break the crust and allow water to infiltrate. The organic matter in the pit feeds the plants and improves soil structure over time. In the Sahel, farmers who use zai pits with manure have increased yields from almost nothing to 500-1000 kg/ha even in dry years. You can also add a small amount of wood ash or termite soil to the pit to deter termites and add nutrients. After planting, you may need to protect the pits from animals with thorn branches until plants are established..Trim()

.Trim()

Maintenance: Each year, you can reuse the same pits, adding fresh manure. Over time, the soil in and around the pits becomes more fertile. If you have a plow, you can make ridges and plant on the ridge, but zai pits are better for dry, flat land. They are labor-intensive to dig, but the payoff is worth it. You can also use a hoe to make planting basins in a grid. For millet, you can plant in the same pits. In very dry areas, combine zai pits with mulching to reduce evaporation. With zai pits, you turn a dry, barren field into a productive one. It is a climate-smart technique that has been proven across Africa..Trim()

Understanding NPK needs for sorghum and millet

Sorghum and millet need three main nutrients from the soil: nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Think of them as building blocks: nitrogen makes leaves green and tall, phosphorus builds strong roots and grain, and potassium helps the plant resist drought and disease. A 50 kg bag of compound fertilizer like NPK 15-15-15 contains 15% of each nutrient. Your crop also needs smaller amounts of calcium, magnesium, sulphur, and zinc, but these are usually present in African soils unless the land is very sandy or worn out..Trim()

.Trim()

A good sorghum crop yielding 2 tonnes of grain per hectare removes about 40 kg of nitrogen, 20 kg of phosphorus (P2O5), and 30 kg of potassium (K2O) from the soil. Millet yields less, around 1 tonne per hectare, and removes about 20 kg N, 10 kg P2O5, and 15 kg K2O. If you harvest and remove the stalks for fuel or animal feed, you take away even more nutrients. That is why continuous farming without fertilizer slowly starves the soil..Trim()

.Trim()

In most African smallholder fields, nitrogen is the biggest limiting nutrient. You will see stunted, yellowing plants. Phosphorus is often second, causing purple stems and poor root growth. Potassium deficiency is less common but appears as yellow or brown edges on older leaves. A simple soil test from your local agricultural extension office can tell you exactly what your soil lacks. If you cannot test, a general recommendation for sorghum and millet is to apply 100-150 kg per hectare of NPK 15-15-15 at planting, and then top-dress with 50-100 kg per hectare of urea or ammonium sulphate..Trim()

.Trim()

Remember that fertilizer is not a substitute for organic matter. Manure, compost, and crop residues improve soil structure, hold water, and feed beneficial microbes. Combining organic and mineral fertilizers gives the best results. Start with what you can afford: even a few handfuls of NPK per planting hole can double your yield on poor soil. The key is to apply nutrients where the roots can reach them, not broadcast them where weeds steal them..Trim()

When and how to apply basal fertilizer at planting

Basal fertilizer is the fertilizer you put in the soil at planting time. It gives young seedlings a strong start before their roots can search for nutrients. For sorghum and millet, the best basal fertilizer is a compound containing nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, such as NPK 15-15-15 or NPK 20-10-10. Phosphorus is especially critical at this stage because it drives root development. Without it, seedlings stay weak and never catch up..Trim()

.Trim()

Apply basal fertilizer at the rate of 100 to 150 kg per hectare. If you plant in rows 75 cm apart and 25 cm between hills, that is about 53,000 planting hills per hectare. So 100 kg per hectare means roughly 2 grams per hill, which is about a bottle cap full. You can measure with a small tin or a calibrated cup. Do not guess wildly; too much fertilizer can burn seeds and kill them. Too little wastes money..Trim()

.Trim()

The method matters. Open your planting hole or furrow, drop the seed, then place the fertilizer 5 to 8 cm to the side and 5 cm below the seed. Never let fertilizer touch the seed directly. If you mix fertilizer with the seed, it will absorb moisture, release salts, and destroy germination. In dry areas, you can apply fertilizer in a band along the row and cover it with soil before planting. This is called banding. It concentrates nutrients near the roots and reduces loss to weeds..Trim()

.Trim()

Timing is also important. Apply basal fertilizer when the soil is moist, either just before planting or at planting. If the soil is bone dry, wait for rain. Fertilizer applied to dry soil may not dissolve and can be blown away by wind. In sandy soils, which are common in the Sahel and parts of East Africa, nutrients leach quickly. Split your basal application: put half at planting and the other half two weeks later. In heavier clay soils, one application at planting is usually enough..Trim()

.Trim()

Finally, combine basal fertilizer with organic matter. Put a handful of well-rotted manure or compost in each planting hole before you add the mineral fertilizer. The organic matter holds water and nutrients, and it feeds soil life. This simple practice can increase your yield by 30% or more compared to mineral fertilizer alone..Trim()

Top dressing with urea or ammonium sulphate at knee height

Top dressing means adding nitrogen fertilizer after the crop is already growing. For sorghum and millet, the best time is when the plants reach knee height, about 30 to 45 cm tall, usually 3 to 5 weeks after planting. At this stage, the crop is rapidly growing leaves and stems, and it needs a big supply of nitrogen. Without top dressing, you may get a tall plant with a small head or no head at all..Trim()

.Trim()

The two common top-dressing fertilizers are urea (46-0-0) and ammonium sulphate (21-0-0). Urea is stronger, so you need less of it. Ammonium sulphate also contains sulphur, which is good for sandy soils. Apply 50 to 100 kg per hectare of urea, or 100 to 200 kg per hectare of ammonium sulphate. For a small plot, 50 kg of urea per hectare is about 1 gram per planting hill. Use a bottle cap or a small spoon to measure accurately..Trim()

.Trim()

How you apply matters. Broadcast the fertilizer evenly between the rows, not on the leaves. If granules land on the leaves, they can burn them, especially when the leaves are wet. The best method is to place the fertilizer in a band 10 cm from the plant base and then cover it lightly with soil. This is called side-dressing. Covering prevents nitrogen from escaping into the air as ammonia gas, especially with urea. If you broadcast and do not cover, you can lose up to 30% of the nitrogen..Trim()

.Trim()

Timing with rain is critical. Apply top dressing just before a light rain or when the soil is already moist. After applying, if you have a hoe, lightly cultivate the soil to mix the fertilizer in and kill weeds. .Trim()

One warning: do not top-dress too late. If you apply urea after the plant has started flowering, it will mostly go to leaves and stalks, not grain. You will get a lot of green matter but poor yield. Knee height is the sweet spot. For a small farm, you can also split the top dressing: half at knee height and half one week later. Making and using liquid organic fertilizers (manure tea)

Liquid organic fertilizer, often called manure tea, is a cheap and powerful way to feed your sorghum and millet. It is made by soaking animal manure or compost in water, which extracts nutrients into a liquid that plants can absorb quickly. You can make it on your farm with materials you already have. It is especially useful for top dressing when you cannot afford urea, or when you want to give your crop a quick boost..Trim()

.Trim()

To make manure tea, you need a container (a drum, bucket, or old water tank), fresh animal manure (cow, goat, chicken, or rabbit), and water. Rainwater harvesting techniques: tied ridges, contour bunds, and dams

In dryland Africa, every drop of rain counts. Tied ridges are a simple technique: after ploughing, you make ridges 20-30 cm high and 60-90 cm apart, then tie them with cross-ridges every 2-3 meters to form basins. These basins catch rainwater and hold it long enough to soak into the soil. This can increase yields by 30-50% in dry years. Contour bunds are earth banks built along the contour of a slope, spaced 10-20 meters apart. They slow runoff and let water infiltrate. For small dams, you can dig a farm pond 2-3 meters deep, lined with clay or plastic, to store water for supplemental irrigation. In areas with 500-800 mm annual rainfall, a 10x10 meter pond can collect enough water for 1-2 hectares of sorghum during a dry spell. Always dig ponds at the lowest point of your field and keep them fenced from livestock. Maintenance is key: repair ridges and bunds after each season, and desilt ponds before the rains. Timing first and second weeding for sorghum and millet

Weeding at the right time is the cheapest way to double your sorghum or millet yield. The first weeding must be done when the crop is 2 to 3 weeks old, or when the weeds are still small and before they compete for water and nutrients. .Trim()

The goal is to remove any weeds that survived the first weeding and to stop new flushes of weeds from setting seed. Using hand hoes, oxen weeders, and selective herbicides safely

For most smallholder sorghum and millet farmers in Africa, the hand hoe is the main weeding tool. Recognizing maturity signs for sorghum and millet

Knowing when to harvest is critical. If you harvest too early, grain is moist and shrivels; too late, grain shatters and birds eat it. For sorghum, maturity is 90-120 days after planting, depending on variety. Check the hard dough stage: grain is firm, not doughy, and a thumbnail pressed into it leaves a slight dent. The plant leaves turn yellow and dry, but the grain itself should still be slightly soft. For millet, maturity is 75-90 days. The seed head turns from green to brown or grey. Grain at the bottom of the panicle matures first, so test by pressing a few grainsâthey should be hard and not milky. Another sign: when you shake the panicle, mature grain rattles. In Africa, many farmers use bird activity as a sign: when birds start flocking to the field, it's time to harvest. But don't wait too long. For sorghum, if you wait until the whole plant is brown, the grain may shatter. For millet, if the panicle is fully brown and dry, grain can fall off. A simple test: bite a grain. If it cracks between your teeth and is not soft, it's ready. Also, check moisture: grain should be below 20% moisture for harvest. You can use a moisture meter, but if you don't have one, squeeze a handful of grainâif it sticks together, it's too wet. Harvest when 90% of the grains on the panicle are mature. Clean grain fetches a higher price. Buyers reject grain with stones, chaff, or broken kernels. After threshing, you must clean and winnow. For long-term storage, a granary protects grain from pests, moisture, and theft. A simple improved granary can be built with local materials. Keeping simple farm records: costs, yields, and sales

.Trim()

.Trim()

Calculating profit and loss for your sorghum and millet enterprise

Profit is what remains after you subtract all your costs from your total sales. If your costs are higher than your sales, you have a loss. To calculate profit for your sorghum or millet, you need to know your total revenue and total costs for the season..Trim()

.Trim()

Total revenue is the money you received from selling your grain, and also from selling fodder, seed, or any other product. For example, if you sold 20 bags of sorghum at 3,000 KES per bag, that's 60,000 KES. If you also sold 5 bags of millet at 4,000 KES, that's 20,000 KES. Total revenue = 80,000 KES..Trim()

.Trim()

Total costs include all variable costs: seeds, fertiliser, pesticides, hired labour, transport, and bags. Also include fixed costs like land rent or depreciation on tools. Suppose you spent 10,000 KES on seed, 15,000 KES on fertiliser, 8,000 KES on labour, 2,000 KES on transport, and 5,000 KES on rent. Total costs = 40,000 KES. .Trim()

But be careful: some costs are hidden. Did you use your own labour? If you worked 100 hours, that labour has value. If you hired someone, you would pay them. So even if you don't pay yourself, you should estimate the cost of your own labour. Also, if you used manure from your own animals, that has value too. .Trim()

To make better decisions, calculate profit per acre. If you planted 2 acres of sorghum and made 40,000 KES profit, that's 20,000 KES per acre. Compare that to other crops. .Trim()

Module 12 · Scaling Up and Building a Sorghum/Millet Business

Expanding land through renting or buying

To grow your sorghum or millet business, you need more land. In most African countries, land is rented or bought through local leaders or family. Start by renting. A hectare of good farmland rents for about $50 to $150 per season in countries like Nigeria, Kenya, or Tanzania. That is cheaper than buying, which can cost $500 to $5,000 per hectare depending on location and water access. Before you rent, test the soil. Dig a hole 30 cm deep. If the soil is sandy and pale, add compost. If it is dark and holds moisture, it is good. Also check water sources: a borehole or river nearby reduces irrigation costs. Always sign a written agreement with the landowner, witnessed by two people. Include the rent amount, duration (at least 2 seasons), and what happens if you improve the land (like building a fence). For buying, save 30% of your profits each year. In 3 years, you can buy 1 hectare. Buy land with clear title or a customary certificate. Avoid disputed land. After acquiring land, plant a cover crop like cowpeas to add nitrogen. Rotate sorghum with legumes every other season to keep the soil healthy. If you rent, negotiate a 5-year lease to justify investing in soil fertility. Remember, expanding too fast without labour or equipment leads to losses. Expand by 1 hectare per year, not 5..Trim()

Hiring and training farm labour

When your farm grows beyond 2 hectares, you cannot do all the work alone. You need hired labour. In Africa, farm labour is paid daily or per task. A casual worker earns $3 to $8 per day depending on the country and season. For sorghum and millet, you need labour for planting, weeding, bird scaring, harvesting, and threshing. Hire 2 workers per hectare during peak times. For example, weeding 1 hectare takes 4 people 2 days. Pay them fairly and on time to keep them motivated. Train your workers on specific tasks. Show them how to plant seeds 2 cm deep and 30 cm apart. Demonstrate how to identify pests like stem borers. Use a simple checklist: each worker must weed 10 rows per day. Provide tools: hoes, machetes, and gloves. For threshing, train 2 workers to use a mechanical thresher safely. Pay a bonus of $5 per 100 kg of clean grain to encourage quality. Keep records: name, days worked, amount paid. Use a notebook or a free app like FarmLogs. Treat workers with respect. Provide clean drinking water and a shade tree for breaks. Happy workers stay, reducing training costs. Consider hiring a permanent supervisor for $50 to $100 per month. This person manages daily tasks and reports to you. With good labour, your yields can increase by 30% because weeding and harvesting are done on time..Trim()

Investing in equipment: threshers, mills, and storage

Manual threshing of sorghum and millet is slow and loses grain. A mechanical thresher saves time and money. A small motorized thresher costs $300 to $800 in most African cities. It threshes 500 kg per hour, compared to 20 kg per hour by hand. That means you can process 5 tonnes in 10 hours instead of 250 hours. Rent one first to test. If you grow more than 5 hectares, buy one. For milling, a hammer mill costs $500 to $1,500. It turns grain into flour for local markets. You can charge neighbors $0.10 per kg to mill their grain, earning extra income. Storage is critical. Poor storage leads to weevils and mold. Use hermetic bags like Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS) bags. A 100 kg PICS bag costs $2 to $3 and keeps grain safe for 6 months without chemicals. For larger quantities, build a metal silo. A 1-tonne silo costs $200 to $400. Keep grain moisture below 12% before storage. Test by biting a grain: if it cracks, it is dry enough. Store in a cool, dry place on pallets. Never store on the floor. Check monthly for insects. If you see weevils, sun-dry the grain for 2 days. With proper equipment, you reduce post-harvest losses from 30% to less than 5%. That extra grain is pure profit. Start with a thresher, then a mill, then storage as your volume grows..Trim()
