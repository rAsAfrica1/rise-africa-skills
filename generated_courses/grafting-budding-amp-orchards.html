Identifying the rootstock, scion, and bud in local fruit trees

When you graft, you join two different trees into one. The bottom part, with the roots, is the rootstock. The top part, which you cut from a good fruiting tree and join on, is the scion. A bud is a small growing point taken from the scion tree and inserted into the rootstock. In a mango tree in your compound, the rootstock is the seedling grown from a seed with strong roots. The scion is a piece of branch from a tree that gives sweet fruit. The bud is a single eye cut with a small shield of bark..Trim()

.Trim()

You can find these parts on any local fruit tree. Look at a mature avocado. The trunk below the graft union is the rootstock. Feel for a slight swelling or scar about 20-30 cm above the soil where the original graft was made. Above that line is the scion, the part that produces the fruit you eat. On a citrus tree, the rootstock is often rough lemon or rough lemon-type seedling, and the scion is the sweet orange or tangerine. The buds are the small points along the green young branches, sitting in the leaf axils..Trim()

.Trim()

To identify a bud, look for a small, slightly raised eye where a leaf joins the stem. On mango, buds are plump and green when active. On citrus, they are tiny and may be hidden under the leaf stalk. Use a sharp knife or a budding knife to cut a bud with a thin slice of wood and bark. The bud is the future branch. The rootstock supplies water and minerals. The scion supplies the fruit quality. When you understand these three parts, you can plan your grafting work..Trim()

.Trim()

In Africa, many farmers use local rootstocks because they survive drought and poor soil. For example, in Kenya, mango rootstock is often a local seedling that tolerates heat. The scion comes from a named variety like Apple or Ngowe. The bud is taken from the same scion tree. Always label your trees so you know which part is which. A simple paint mark at the graft union helps. If you cannot tell the rootstock from the scion, you may cut the wrong part and lose the tree..Trim()

.Trim()

Practice on a branch of a guava or citrus. Cut a small branch and look for the bud eyes. Then trace down to the older wood. This is the rootstock side if the tree was grafted. With time, you will see the difference clearly. This skill is the foundation of all grafting and budding work..Trim()

How the cambium layer works and why it's key to grafting success

The cambium is a thin layer of living cells just under the bark. It is only a few cells thick, but it is the engine of growth in stems. When you cut a branch, the cambium is the green or white slippery layer between the bark and the wood. In grafting, the cambium of the scion must touch the cambium of the rootstock. If they do not touch, the graft fails. This is the single most important rule..Trim()

.Trim()

Think of the cambium as a sheet of tiny bricks that can divide. When it divides, it makes new wood on the inside and new bark on the outside. When you wound a tree, the cambium around the wound starts to grow and form a callus. In a graft, the callus from the scion and the rootstock meet and join. This is called graft union. The union is strong only if the cambium layers are aligned. You need at least one side of the cambium to match, but matching both sides is better..Trim()

.Trim()

To see the cambium, cut a young branch of mango or citrus. Peel back the bark gently with your thumb. The slippery, wet layer you see is the cambium. It is easy to damage, so never touch it with dirty hands or let it dry out. When you make a grafting cut, work quickly. The cambium dies if it dries. In dry African conditions, you may need to wrap the graft with polythene tape to keep it moist..Trim()

.Trim()

The cambium also explains why the size of the scion and rootstock matters. If the scion is much thinner than the rootstock, you can still align one side. But if the scion is thicker, you may need to shave the rootstock to match. For budding, you slip a bud with a small piece of cambium under the bark of the rootstock. The cambium of the bud must touch the cambium of the rootstock. This is why you cut a T-shaped slit and slide the bud in..Trim()

.Trim()

In practice, keep your knife sharp and clean. A blunt knife crushes the cambium. Use a grafting knife or a new razor blade. Make one clean cut. Do not saw. After joining, tie the union tightly with budding tape or polythene strips. The tape holds the cambium together until it heals. Check for cambium contact by looking at the cut surface. You want a smooth, wet line. If it is dry or brown, recut. With practice, you will feel the cambium and know it is right..Trim()

Grafting works best when the tree is actively growing. Active growth means the cambium is dividing and sap is flowing. In mango, avocado, and citrus, you can see active growth by looking at the new leaves and shoots. In mango, active growth shows as new reddish-brown leaves that turn green. The buds swell and new shoots appear. This usually happens at the start of the rains or after a dry spell. In East Africa, mango flushes two to three times a year. The best time to graft is when the flush is just starting, not when it is finished..Trim()

.Trim()

Avocado growth is different. Avocado grows in flushes too, but the leaves are pale green and soft. The buds are large and green. Active growth in avocado often happens in warm, wet months. In Kenya, avocado grafting is done in March to May and September to November when the weather is mild and humid. Avoid grafting when the tree is flowering or when it is too dry. Citrus is ever-growing in warm areas. It flushes several times a year. Look for new shoots with light green leaves and small thorns. The cambium is active when the bark slips easily. The difference between grafting and budding in simple terms

Grafting and budding are two ways to join a scion to a rootstock. In grafting, you use a piece of branch with several buds. In budding, you use a single bud. That is the main difference. Both methods join the cambium layers, but the size of the scion differs. Grafting is used when the rootstock and scion are about the same thickness, or when you want a strong, quick union. Budding is used when the rootstock is bigger than the bud, and when you want to save scion wood..Trim()

.Trim()

In grafting, you cut the rootstock and the scion to fit together. Common methods are cleft grafting, whip grafting, and side grafting. For example, in mango, cleft grafting is common. You cut the rootstock stem and split it, then insert a wedge-shaped scion with 2-3 buds. You tie it and cover with a polythene bag to keep humidity. In budding, you make a T-shaped cut in the rootstock bark, lift the bark, and slide in a bud with a small shield of bark. You then tie it. The bud stays dormant or grows into a new shoot..Trim()

.Trim()

Budding is faster and uses less scion wood. One bud can make one new tree. A single branch can provide many buds. Grafting uses more wood but gives a stronger union faster. In citrus, budding is the standard method. In avocado, grafting is more common because the wood is easier to handle. In mango, both are used, but grafting is often preferred for large-scale nurseries..Trim()

.Trim()

The choice also depends on the season. Budding requires the bark to slip, which happens during active growth. Grafting can be done when the bark is not slipping, as long as the cambium is active. In dry areas, grafting with a polythene cover is more reliable because it keeps moisture. Budding is more delicate and needs careful handling..Trim()

.Trim()

For a beginner, start with budding on citrus because the bark slips easily and the bud is small. Then try grafting on mango. Always use a sharp knife. For budding, a budding knife with a special bark lifter is helpful. For grafting, a grafting knife is fine. Whichever method you choose, the cambium must touch. The difference is only in the size of the scion and the technique. Both give you a new tree with the fruit you want..Trim()

How sap flow and season affect graft take

Sap flow is the movement of water and nutrients in the tree. When sap is flowing, the cambium is active and the graft can heal. When sap is slow, the graft may dry out or fail. Sap flow depends on the season, the weather, and the health of the tree. In Africa, the best time to graft is when the sap is rising, usually at the start of the rains. During this time, the tree is waking up and making new growth. The cambium is dividing, and the graft union can form quickly..Trim()

.Trim()

In the dry season, sap flow slows down. The tree may drop its leaves and go dormant. Grafting then is risky. The scion may dry out before the union forms. If you must graft in the dry season, you need to water the rootstock regularly and cover the graft with a polythene bag to keep humidity. In very hot areas, shade the graft. In the rainy season, sap flow is high, but too much rain can cause rot. Avoid grafting in heavy rain. A light rain or humid weather is ideal..Trim()

.Trim()

Temperature also affects sap flow. In cold highland areas, sap flow starts later. In warm lowlands, it starts earlier. For example, in the Kenyan highlands, avocado grafting is best in March to May and September to November. In the hot coastal lowlands, mango grafting can be done in May to July and October to December. Know your local climate. Talk to other farmers. Keep records of when your grafts take..Trim()

.Trim()

Sap flow also depends on the rootstock. A healthy, well-watered rootstock has good sap flow. A stressed rootstock has poor sap flow. Before grafting, water the rootstock well for a week. Do not graft on a sick tree. The scion also needs to be fresh. Collect scion wood in the morning when the tree is full of sap. Wrap it in moist paper or banana leaf. Use it within a day or two. If you store it, keep it cool and moist..Trim()

.Trim()

After grafting, sap flow continues to move through the union. If the graft takes, you will see new shoots from the scion in 2-4 weeks. If the scion turns brown and shrivels, the graft failed. Do not give up. Try again in the next season. With practice, you will learn the best time for your area. Sap flow is your guide. Watch the tree, and it will tell you when to graft..Trim()

Selecting rootstocks for drought tolerance in semi-arid areas

In semi-arid Africa, choosing the right rootstock is the difference between a mango tree that survives and one that dies in the first dry season. Identifying high-yielding mother trees in your community

Your first job is to find a mother tree that gives you the best possible scion wood. A mother tree is any healthy tree you take cuttings from. It must be high-yielding, disease-free, and true to type. In your community, start by asking farmers which mango, avocado, or citrus tree gives the most fruit every year. Look for signs of anthracnose (black spots on leaves and fruit), gummosis (sticky sap on the trunk), or viral diseases like tristeza in citrus. Do not take scion wood from a tree with these problems. The tree should be at least five years old and in full production. Young trees may not have stable genetics. Also, avoid trees that have been heavily pruned or stressed by drought. The best mother trees are vigorous, with strong new growth. Look for branches that are exposed to sunlight, as they produce healthier wood. Timing is critical. Cut scion wood when the tree is dormant or just before bud break. Module 12 · Turning Your Orchard into a Business

Calculating costs and setting profitable prices

To make money from your orchard, you must know your costs. Start by listing all expenses for one year. For a small mango orchard of 50 trees, you might spend: seedlings (50 x $2 = $100), land prep ($50), fertilizer ($80), pesticides ($40), watering ($60), tools ($50), and labour for planting and pruning ($200). Total costs = $580. If each tree produces 100 fruits after 3 years, that's 5,000 fruits. To find cost per fruit, divide $580 by 5,000 = $0.12. But you also need to cover your time and risk. Add 30% profit, so price per fruit = $0.12 x 1.3 = $0.16. If you sell at $0.20, you make good profit. For grafted seedlings, costs include rootstock, scion, pots, soil, water, and labour. Say you produce 200 seedlings: rootstock ($0.20 each = $40), scion (free from your trees), pots ($0.10 each = $20), soil ($10), water ($5), labour ($50). Total = $125. Cost per seedling = $0.63. Add 50% profit = $0.95. Sell at $1.00. Always check market prices: in Kenya, a grafted avocado seedling sells for $1.50-$3.00. So you can price competitively. Remember to include transport to market. If you take 100 fruits to market and pay $5 transport, that adds $0.05 per fruit. So your final price must cover all costs plus profit. Keep a notebook and write every expense. At the end of the season, compare your actual costs to your estimates. Adjust prices next season. Never sell below cost, or you will lose money. Also consider value addition: dried fruit, juice, or jam can fetch higher prices. But start simple: calculate, set a price, and track if you are making profit..Trim()

Keeping simple records for your orchard

Records are your memory. Without them, you cannot know if you are making a profit or loss. Use a simple notebook or a cheap exercise book. Divide it into sections: expenses, sales, and observations. Every time you spend money, write the date, what you bought, and the amount. For example: '5 May 2025 - bought 2 kg fertilizer - $4'. Every time you sell, write: '10 June 2025 - sold 50 mangoes - $10'. At the end of each month, add up all expenses and all sales. Subtract expenses from sales to get profit. If you have a smartphone, use a free app like 'Farm Records' or 'MyOrchard' or even a simple spreadsheet like Google Sheets. But paper works fine. Also record tree performance: which trees produce more, which have pests, when they flower. This helps you plan. For grafted seedlings, record how many you grafted, how many survived, and how many you sold. If you sell to a cooperative, record the date, quantity, and payment. Keep receipts for everything. If you buy from a shop, ask for a receipt. If you sell at market, write your own receipt. At the end of the year, you can see your total income and total costs. This helps you set prices for next year and apply for loans. Banks and grants want to see records. Even a simple notebook shows you are serious. Keep records for at least 3 years. Store them in a dry place. Review them monthly. This simple habit can double your profit because you will stop wasting money..Trim()

Social media is free and reaches many people. Use WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram. Start with WhatsApp: create a broadcast list of farmers, neighbours, and market traders. Send clear photos of your seedlings and fruits. Write short messages: 'Grafted mango seedlings, $1.50 each, ready for planting. Call 0712345678'. Post twice a week. On Facebook, join groups like 'Kenya Farmers', 'Nigeria Agriculture', 'Uganda Fruit Growers'. Post photos with prices and your location. Use hashtags: #GraftedSeedlings #MangoFarming #OrchardBusiness. On Instagram, post short videos of you grafting or harvesting. Show the quality. Respond to comments quickly. Offer delivery for bulk orders. For fruit, post when it is in season: 'Sweet ripe mangoes, $0.20 each, 100 available'. Use WhatsApp status to show daily harvests. Ask customers to refer friends. You can also use TikTok: short clips of your orchard with music. Keep it simple. Always include your phone number. If you have a smartphone, take good photos in daylight. For seedlings, show the graft union and healthy leaves. For fruit, show size and colour. You can also create a simple poster using free apps like Canva and share it. Offer a discount for 10 or more seedlings. Partner with local agro-vets to display your posters. Social media is not just for selling; it builds trust. When people see your work, they will buy. Remember to reply to every message. Even if you are busy, a quick 'Thank you, I will call you' helps. Track which posts bring sales. Spend 30 minutes daily on social media. Forming a cooperative to sell in bulk

A cooperative is a group of farmers who work together to sell more and get better prices. To form one, find 10-30 farmers in your area who grow similar crops. Hold a meeting and agree on a name and rules. Choose a chairman, secretary, and treasurer. Write a simple constitution: how to join, fees, and how profits are shared. Register with the government if possible. In Kenya, register with the Ministry of Cooperatives; in Nigeria, with the Corporate Affairs Commission. Registration costs about $50-$100. Once formed, you can collect fruits from all members and sell as one big lot. For example, if each farmer has 50 mangoes, 20 farmers have 1,000 mangoes. A buyer will pay more for 1,000 than for 50. You can also buy inputs like fertilizer and seedlings in bulk at a discount. For grafted seedlings, a cooperative can produce 5,000 seedlings and sell to NGOs or government projects. Set a quality standard: only sell healthy, pest-free fruits. Choose one person to negotiate with buyers. Keep records of each member's contribution. Pay members after deducting cooperative costs (transport, packaging). Hold monthly meetings to plan. Cooperatives also help access loans: banks trust groups more than individuals. You can open a group bank account. Start small: begin with 5 farmers and grow. Avoid conflicts by writing everything down. If a member breaks rules, have a penalty. A cooperative can also share knowledge: one member learns grafting and teaches others. This builds community. In many African countries, cooperatives get better prices and have ready markets. So talk to your neighbours today..Trim()

Accessing loans and grants for small-scale farmers

You need money to expand your orchard. Loans must be repaid, grants are free. Start with grants: many NGOs and government programs support small farmers. In Kenya, check the Youth Enterprise Development Fund, Women Enterprise Fund, and Agricultural Finance Corporation. In Nigeria, the Bank of Agriculture and NIRSAL. In Ghana, the MASLOC. Also look at international NGOs like Heifer International, One Acre Fund, and Send a Cow. They offer training and small grants. To apply, you need a simple business plan, records, and a group. Many grants target women and youth. For loans, start with SACCOs (Savings and Credit Cooperatives) in Kenya, or village savings groups. Interest rates are lower than banks. Banks may ask for collateral, but group loans are easier. For example, a group of 10 farmers can get a loan of $1,000 and share. Use the loan for seedlings, fertilizer, or irrigation. Only borrow what you can repay. Calculate: if you borrow $500 at 10% interest, you repay $550. Your orchard must generate at least $600 profit to be safe. Keep records to show the bank you can repay. Also consider mobile money loans like M-Shwari or Tala, but interest is high. Avoid them for farming. Grants often require a proposal: write why you need the money, how you will use it, and how it will help the community. Ask a local extension officer for help. Apply to many sources. Be patient; it can take months. Never pay a bribe. If you get a grant, use it exactly as promised. This builds trust for future funding..Trim()

Creating a 1-year business plan for your orchard

A business plan is a roadmap. For your orchard, write a simple one-page plan. Start with your goal: 'To produce 500 grafted mango seedlings and 2,000 mangoes in 2026, earning $1,000 profit.' Then list your resources: land size, number of trees, tools, and labour. Next, plan activities month by month. January: buy rootstock and scion, prepare nursery. February: grafting. March: watering and weeding. April: apply fertilizer. May: pest control. June: harvest first fruits. July: sell seedlings. August: prune. September: harvest main crop. October: sell fruits. November: record keeping and review. December: plan next year. For each activity, estimate costs and income. For example, seedlings: 500 x $1.50 = $750 income; costs $300; profit $450. Fruits: 2,000 x $0.20 = $400 income; costs $150; profit $250. Total profit $700. Add a marketing plan: use WhatsApp and local markets. Add a risk plan: what if drought? You will install drip irrigation. What if pests? You will spray neem oil. Keep the plan simple. Write it in a notebook. Review it every month. Adjust as needed. Show it to a loan officer or grant committee. A good plan shows you are serious. It also keeps you focused. At the end of the year, compare actual results to the plan. Learn from mistakes. Then write a better plan for the next year. This is how you grow from a small orchard to a real business..Trim()
