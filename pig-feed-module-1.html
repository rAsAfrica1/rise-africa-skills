Pig Feed Formulation / Module 1 of 12

Module 1

🐷 Swine Nutrition Fundamentals

The foundations every pig feeding decision rests on. Covers how the pig's single-stomach digestive system actually works, how energy is measured and paid for, how lysine and the other amino acids drive lean growth, which vitamins and minerals matter and why availability differs from the number on the bag, and how to run a drinking system that does not quietly cap your feed intake.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Trace feed through the four sections of the pig's digestive tract and name what is absorbed in each
  • Distinguish digestible, metabolisable and net energy and state which one your ration should be balanced on
  • Explain why lysine, not crude protein, is the number that drives lean growth
  • List the fat-soluble and water-soluble vitamins pigs need and the published level for each class
  • State the calcium and phosphorus requirements for each class of pig and the ratio between them
  • Explain how water supply limits feed intake and therefore growth and milk yield

Lesson 1.1~12 min

Digestive System of Pigs

In this lesson
  • Trace feed through the four sections of the pig's digestive tract and name what is absorbed in each
  • Explain why the same ingredient is digested differently by a 10 kg weaner and a 70 kg finisher
  • Match high-fibre local ingredients to the classes of pig that can actually use them

The pig has a single stomach. That one fact explains most of what you can and cannot feed it. Unlike a cow or a goat, a pig has no rumen full of microbes standing between the feed and the animal, so a pig cannot live on grass, maize stover or banana leaves. It needs feed that its own enzymes can break down, and it competes with people for many of those ingredients. Understanding the tract section by section is what lets you use cheap local by-products without wrecking growth.

Feed travels through four working sections:

  1. Mouth and oesophagus. Chewing is brief; particle size is set mostly by your hammer mill screen, not by the pig. Grind too coarse and nutrients pass through undigested; grind to dust and you invite stomach ulcers and dust waste.
  2. Stomach. This is an acid vat. In an unsupplemented diet the reference standards put stomach pH in the 4.5 to 5.5 range, and adding formic acid pulls it down to about 3.5 to 4.5. That acidity does two jobs: it starts protein breakdown, and it kills swallowed bacteria. A young weaner does not yet produce enough acid, which is exactly why post-weaning diarrhoea is so common and why acidifiers work.
  3. Small intestine. This is where the money is made. Starch, protein and fat are broken down by the pig's own enzymes and absorbed here. Enzyme systems are not fixed: newborn piglets are built for milk, and the intestinal maltase and isomaltase that handle grain starch develop over the weeks after weaning, which is why the reference standards taper lactose out of nursery diets in stages rather than all at once.
  4. Large intestine. Here bacteria ferment whatever the small intestine could not digest, producing volatile fatty acids. Butyrate, one of those acids, is the main energy source for the cells lining the large intestine. This section is a bonus, not a substitute for the small intestine.

Age changes everything. Apparent total tract digestibility of fibre varies with source, roughly 50 to 70 percent for cellulose and 60 to 75 percent for soy fibre, but young pigs under about 15 kg score much lower because their enzyme systems are immature, while pigs over about 60 kg digest fibre far better. The practical rule that falls out of this is the fibre ceiling. The standards allow roughly 2 to 4 percent crude fibre in weaner diets, 4 to 6 percent in grower and finisher diets, 6 to 8 percent in gestating sow diets, and 3 to 4 percent in lactation diets where energy density matters more.

One more digestion fact drives a large share of your mineral bill. Phosphorus in plant feeds is locked in phytate, and roughly 70 to 80 percent of it is unavailable to the pig. The pig has no useful phytase of its own. That is why the number on a feed tag and the number the pig actually absorbs are different things, and why the industry measures standardised total tract digestible phosphorus rather than total phosphorus.

Worked example. A farmer in Ibadan, Nigeria, has cheap brewers' spent grain and rice bran available and wants to push both into every ration. Suppose his supplier tags read 14 percent crude fibre for the dried brewers' grain and 11 percent for the rice bran, and his maize-and-soya base sits at 3 percent crude fibre. Use your own tags; these are illustrative. If he includes 20 percent brewers' grain and 15 percent rice bran, the fibre contribution is:

  • Brewers' grain: 0.20 x 14 = 2.8 points
  • Rice bran: 0.15 x 11 = 1.65 points
  • Base at 65 percent: 0.65 x 3 = 1.95 points
  • Total = 2.8 + 1.65 + 1.95 = 6.4 percent crude fibre

At 6.4 percent that mix is above the 4 to 6 percent grower ceiling and far above the 2 to 4 percent weaner ceiling, but it sits neatly inside the 6 to 8 percent gestating sow window. So the same cheap by-products that would stunt his weaners are close to ideal for his dry sows, where fibre also provides satiety. Sorting ingredients by which pig can digest them, rather than by price alone, is the whole skill.

Do this today: write down every feed ingredient on your farm in one column, then beside each one write which class of pig it is safe for (weaner, grower, finisher, gestating sow, lactating sow) using the crude fibre ceilings in this lesson and the crude fibre figure on the supplier's tag. Mark any ingredient with no tag as 'test before use'.

Lesson 1.2~12 min

Energy Requirements for Pigs

In this lesson
  • Distinguish digestible, metabolisable and net energy and state which one your ration should be balanced on
  • Look up the correct energy density for each weight class and check a ration against it
  • Calculate whether adding fat to a finisher diet pays at your local fat price

Energy is the largest single cost in pig feeding and the one most producers never measure. Pigs eat to meet an energy target. Give them a thin, bulky diet and they will try to eat more of it, fail, and grow slowly. Give them a dense diet and they will eat less bulk and still grow. Everything else in the ration is balanced relative to energy.

There are three energy systems and they are not interchangeable:

  • Digestible energy (DE) is what is absorbed. In practice DE runs roughly 5 to 10 percent higher than metabolisable energy.
  • Metabolisable energy (ME) subtracts losses in urine and gas. Most feed tags and most requirement tables you will meet in Africa use ME in kcal per kg.
  • Net energy (NE) subtracts the heat produced during digestion and metabolism. It is the most accurate system because it accounts for the fact that fat, starch and protein cost different amounts to convert into body tissue. The reference standards put grower diets at about 2.35 to 2.65 Mcal NE/kg and finisher diets at about 2.46 to 2.73 Mcal NE/kg.

Use ME for day to day work unless your supplier gives you NE values, but understand that a diet high in protein looks better on ME than it truly is, because protein carries a heavy heat cost.

Published energy densities by weight class:

  1. Weaner, 5 to 11 kg: 3,300 to 3,400 kcal ME/kg. High density, because a small stomach cannot take volume.
  2. Grower, 12 to 26 kg: 3,200 to 3,350 kcal ME/kg.
  3. Early finisher, 27 to 60 kg: 3,200 to 3,350 kcal ME/kg.
  4. Mid finisher, 60 to 100 kg: 3,150 to 3,300 kcal ME/kg.
  5. Late finisher, 100 to 130 kg: 3,100 to 3,250 kcal ME/kg.

Notice the density falls as the pig grows. A big pig can eat volume, so you do not need to pay for concentrated energy, and cheaper bulkier ingredients become viable.

Maintenance comes first. The standards express maintenance net energy as 100 x bodyweight in kg raised to the power 0.75, and give two published worked results: a 50 kg pig needs about 1,495 kcal NE per day for maintenance alone, and a 100 kg pig about 2,371 kcal. Read that carefully. Doubling the pig's weight did not double its maintenance cost, but it did add roughly 876 kcal a day that produces no growth whatsoever. Every day a pig sits in your pen not growing, you are paying that bill.

Worked example. A producer in Mbeya, Tanzania, finishes 60 pigs per batch. His finisher ration is a maize and cassava blend that he estimates at 3,050 kcal ME/kg, below the 3,150 to 3,300 window for the 60 to 100 kg phase. He is considering adding 3 percent palm oil. The standards state that adding 1 percent fat improves feed conversion by about 0.04 points.

  • Expected FCR improvement: 3 x 0.04 = 0.12 points
  • His current FCR over the 60 to 100 kg phase: 3.20. New FCR: 3.08
  • Feed to move a pig from 60 to 100 kg at 3.20 FCR: 40 kg gain x 3.20 = 128 kg feed
  • At 3.08 FCR: 40 x 3.08 = 123.2 kg feed. Saving 4.8 kg of feed per pig
  • Base ration cost, illustrative local price: 0.42 USD/kg. Feed saved = 4.8 x 0.42 = 2.02 USD per pig
  • Cost of the oil: 3 percent of 123.2 kg = 3.7 kg per pig. At a local palm oil price of 1.30 USD/kg that is 4.81 USD, but the oil replaces 3.7 kg of base ration worth 1.55 USD, so the net extra cost is 4.81 - 1.55 = 3.26 USD per pig.

The oil costs 3.26 and returns 2.02, so at these prices it loses him 1.24 USD per pig, or 74 USD per batch. Substitute your own prices. The calculation flips in his favour the moment palm oil drops below roughly 0.95 USD/kg, or if the extra energy also lifts daily gain enough to shorten the batch and free the pen sooner. That second effect is real but harder to price, which is why you measure it on your own farm before committing.

Do this today: find the energy figure on the tag or supplier sheet for the feed you are giving your heaviest pigs, write it beside the published window for that weight class from this lesson, and note in one sentence whether your ration is above, inside, or below the target.

Lesson 1.3~12 min

Protein and Amino Acid Requirements

In this lesson
  • Explain why lysine, not crude protein, is the number that drives lean growth
  • Read the published SID lysine requirement for any class of pig and compare it to a ration
  • Calculate the lysine shortfall in a ration and cost the cheapest way to close it

Crude protein is a laboratory shortcut. It measures nitrogen and multiplies it by a factor. It tells you nothing about whether the protein contains the specific amino acids a pig needs to build muscle. A ration can carry 18 percent crude protein and still starve the pig of lysine, and a ration at 15 percent crude protein with the right amino acid balance can outperform it while costing less and polluting less.

Pigs need ten essential amino acids from feed. Lysine is almost always the first limiting one in cereal-based diets, which is why the entire industry formulates on lysine first and expresses every other amino acid as a percentage of lysine. This is the ideal protein concept, and it is the single most useful idea in practical pig nutrition.

The measure used is SID lysine: standardised ileal digestible lysine, the lysine actually absorbed before the large intestine, expressed as a percentage of the diet. Published requirements fall steadily with age:

  1. Creep and early nursery, 5 to 7.5 kg: 1.30 to 1.40 percent SID lysine
  2. Late nursery, 7.5 to 11.5 kg: 1.20 to 1.30 percent
  3. Grower, 12 to 26 kg: 1.08 to 1.25 percent
  4. Early finisher, 27 to 60 kg: 1.00 to 1.10 percent
  5. Mid finisher, 60 to 100 kg: 0.80 to 0.95 percent
  6. Late finisher, 100 to 130 kg: 0.70 to 0.85 percent
  7. Gestating sow: 0.50 to 0.60 percent, at roughly 12 to 13 percent total protein
  8. Lactating sow: 0.77 to 1.34 percent depending on feed intake, targeting about 60 g SID lysine per day, at 14 to 16 percent crude protein
  9. Mature boar: 0.62 percent, at about 13 to 14 percent protein

The other amino acids follow lysine at fixed ratios. For growers the published ratios are methionine plus cysteine at 56 to 58 percent of lysine, threonine at 62 to 64 percent, and tryptophan at 18 to 19 percent. Note that the mature boar breaks the pattern with markedly higher ratios: methionine plus cysteine at 70 percent and threonine at 74 percent of lysine.

Two errors cost African producers real money. The first is feeding one ration from weaning to slaughter. A pig at 110 kg fed a 1.20 percent lysine diet is being fed almost twice what it needs, and expensive protein is being burned as energy and excreted as nitrogen. The second is the opposite: cutting protein in young pigs to save money, which caps lean growth permanently in the phase where feed conversion is at its best.

Worked example. A producer in Kumasi, Ghana, sends his grower ration for analysis and gets 0.90 percent SID lysine. His pigs are 12 to 26 kg, where the requirement is 1.08 to 1.25 percent. He targets the mid-point, 1.15 percent.

  • Shortfall: 1.15 - 0.90 = 0.25 percentage points, which is 2.5 g of SID lysine per kg of feed
  • He mixes one tonne at a time, so he needs 2.5 g x 1,000 kg = 2,500 g, or 2.5 kg of extra SID lysine per tonne
  • Option A, synthetic L-lysine HCl. This product is about 78 percent lysine, so he needs 2.5 / 0.78 = 3.2 kg per tonne. At an illustrative local price of 3.20 USD/kg that is 10.24 USD per tonne, which is about 0.01 USD per kg of feed.
  • Option B, more soybean meal. Substituting soybean meal for maize raises lysine but also raises crude protein and cost across the whole ration, and in his market soybean meal was 0.72 USD/kg against maize at 0.38. Displacing 80 kg of maize with 80 kg of soybean meal per tonne costs 80 x (0.72 - 0.38) = 27.20 USD per tonne.

Synthetic lysine closes the gap at roughly a third of the cost, and it does so without dragging up crude protein. That is the answer most of the time in cereal-based African rations, provided the rest of the amino acid pattern is checked afterwards so that methionine or threonine does not simply become the new limiting factor. As Module 3 lesson 3 sets out, once lysine is fixed you work down the ratio list in order.

Do this today: find the crude protein figure on your grower feed tag, then write beside it the SID lysine figure. If the tag does not state lysine, telephone or message the supplier and ask for it in writing, and record their answer or their refusal.

Lesson 1.4~11 min

Vitamin Requirements

In this lesson
  • List the fat-soluble and water-soluble vitamins pigs need and the published level for each class
  • Recognise the field signs of the three vitamin shortages most common on smallholdings
  • Cost a vitamin premix per pig and decide whether a stress-level top-up is justified

Vitamins are the cheapest insurance in pig farming and the most commonly skipped. A premix is a small fraction of ration cost, yet skipping it or buying an expired one silently costs growth, immunity and litter size. Vitamins split into two groups: fat-soluble A, D, E and K, which the pig stores in liver and fat; and water-soluble B vitamins and choline, which are not stored in useful quantity and must arrive in every day's feed.

Published requirements, per kg of complete diet:

  1. Vitamin A: weaners 2,000 to 2,200 IU, growers 1,600 to 2,000 IU, finishers 1,300 to 1,600 IU, sows and boars 2,000 to 4,000 IU. Deficiency shows as reduced growth, poor coat quality and reduced immunity.
  2. Vitamin D3: growing pigs from 5 to 135 kg need 150 to 220 IU, but sows and boars need 800 IU, a fourfold jump.
  3. Vitamin E: weaners 30 to 44 IU, growers 22 to 30 IU, finishers 11 to 22 IU, sows and boars 44 IU.
  4. Vitamin K as menadione: 0.50 mg/kg for every class. It prevents bleeding disorders and is rarely short in a standard diet.
  5. Choline: weaners 0.60 g/kg, growers 0.50, finishers 0.30 to 0.50, and sows and boars 1.00 to 1.25 g/kg. The sow figure is roughly double because colostrum choline supports piglet neurological development. Deficiency causes fatty liver and reduced reproductive performance.
  6. B vitamins, standard levels per kg: thiamine 1.0 mg, riboflavin 3.0 mg, niacin 10 mg, pantothenic acid 10 mg, pyridoxine 1.5 mg, cobalamin 0.01 mg, folic acid 0.3 mg, biotin 0.05 mg.

Three practical points matter more than memorising the table.

First, vitamin E under stress. The standard growing-pig level is 11 to 16 IU/kg, but under immune challenge or heat stress the published stress level is 44 IU/kg and upwards, with research going to 100 to 200 IU/kg. Vitamin E works as a pair with selenium through the glutathione peroxidase system, so raising one without the other wastes money. Module 9 lesson 6 covers the deficiency picture in detail; here the point is simply that the requirement is not one fixed number.

Second, vitamin D after weaning. Young pigs show reduced blood 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 status after weaning even when dietary D3 looks adequate, because stress interferes with absorption. Research from 2020 onward shows the metabolite form, 25(OH)D3 at 2 to 5 micrograms per kg, improves immune response, gut barrier function and bone mineralisation compared with D3 alone. It carries a cost premium and is best justified in gilt development or premium programmes rather than across the whole herd.

Third, storage kills vitamins. Vitamins degrade with heat, light, moisture and time, and trace minerals in the same premix accelerate the process. Buy premix in quantities you will use inside about three months, keep it sealed, cool and dark, and never use a bag past its date.

Worked example. A producer in Lilongwe, Malawi, mixes 2 tonnes of grower feed a month and buys a vitamin-trace mineral premix at an illustrative 4.50 USD/kg, included at 2.5 kg per tonne.

  • Premix per tonne: 2.5 kg x 4.50 = 11.25 USD
  • Cost per kg of finished feed: 11.25 / 1,000 = 0.011 USD
  • A grower eating 60 kg of feed through the phase: 60 x 0.011 = 0.68 USD per pig
  • Across 240 pigs a year: 240 x 0.68 = 163 USD

Now compare that with one avoidable loss. If skipping premix costs him even four extra deaths a year at an illustrative 45 USD per weaned pig, that is 180 USD, more than the entire annual premix bill, before counting slower growth in every surviving pig. He also checks whether his supplier's premix already contains selenium and vitamin E at breeding-stock levels, because if it is a grower premix at 22 to 30 IU vitamin E, his sows at 44 IU are short and he needs either a second premix or a top-up.

Do this today: take your vitamin premix bag outside, photograph the label and the expiry date, and write down the vitamin A, D, E and selenium levels it declares. Compare each one against the figures in this lesson for the class of pig you are feeding it to, and note any that fall short.

Lesson 1.5~11 min

Mineral Requirements and Availability

In this lesson
  • State the calcium and phosphorus requirements for each class of pig and the ratio between them
  • Explain why available phosphorus differs from total phosphorus and how phytase changes the sum
  • Calculate the feed cost saving from adding phytase to a home-mixed ration

Minerals divide into macro minerals, needed in percentages, and trace minerals, needed in parts per million. Get calcium and phosphorus wrong and you get broken legs, poor growth and a wasted mineral bill. Get the trace minerals wrong and you get problems that look like disease.

Calcium and phosphorus by class, as percentages of the diet:

  1. Weaner, 5 to 11 kg: total calcium 0.85, total phosphorus 0.70, STTD phosphorus 0.45 to 0.63, ratio about 1.2:1
  2. Grower, 12 to 26 kg: calcium 0.70, total phosphorus 0.60, STTD phosphorus 0.35, ratio held between 1:1 and 1.5:1
  3. Finisher 27 to 60 kg: calcium 0.65, STTD phosphorus 0.32
  4. Finisher 60 to 100 kg: calcium 0.55, STTD phosphorus 0.28
  5. Finisher 100 to 130 kg: calcium 0.46, STTD phosphorus 0.26
  6. Gestating and lactating sow: calcium 0.85 to 0.95, total phosphorus 0.41 to 0.62, STTD phosphorus 0.30 to 0.35 in gestation and 0.38 to 0.42 in lactation, with lactating sows needing at least 22 g STTD phosphorus a day
  7. Mature boar: calcium 0.80, available phosphorus 0.40

The ratio matters as much as the levels. Wide calcium to phosphorus ratios interfere with phosphorus absorption, and ratios above about 1.5:1 are flagged as problematic in growing pigs. Dietary calcium above 1.0 percent also reduces zinc absorption. The common African field error is over-liming: a producer buys cheap limestone, throws in an extra scoop for "strong bones", and unintentionally blocks the phosphorus and zinc he already paid for.

Availability is the second half of the story. Because 70 to 80 percent of plant phosphorus is phytate-bound, the industry measures STTD phosphorus rather than total. Microbial phytase, added at 500 to 2,500 FTU per kg of diet, releases phytate phosphorus and typically improves available phosphorus by 0.10 to 0.20 percentage points depending on dose and substrate.

Trace mineral requirements you should know:

  • Iron: 60 to 200 mg/kg for nursing piglets, 80 to 100 for weaners, 40 to 100 for growers, 40 to 80 for finishers, 80 for sows and boars
  • Copper: nutritional requirement 5 to 6 ppm; nursery programmes commonly run 15 to 20 ppm; pharmacological range is 125 to 250 ppm and toxicity risk begins above 250 ppm
  • Zinc: 80 to 100 ppm baseline for growers and finishers, 100 to 125 ppm for sows and boars
  • Manganese: 2 to 4 mg/kg for growing pigs but 25 mg/kg for sows and boars
  • Selenium: 0.15 to 0.30 mg/kg for growing pigs, 0.20 to 0.30 for sows, 0.30 to 0.50 for boars
  • Iodine: 0.14 to 0.20 mg/kg at every stage

Worked example. A producer in Kigali, Rwanda, mixes 3 tonnes of grower feed a month. His formulation currently uses dicalcium phosphate at 12 kg per tonne to hit 0.35 percent STTD phosphorus. He adds phytase at 500 FTU/kg, which the standards say releases roughly 0.10 percentage points of available phosphorus.

  • 0.10 percentage points of a tonne is 0.10 / 100 x 1,000 kg = 1.0 kg of available phosphorus per tonne
  • If his dicalcium phosphate supplies about 18 percent phosphorus, replacing 1.0 kg of phosphorus needs 1.0 / 0.18 = 5.6 kg of dicalcium phosphate
  • Dicalcium phosphate at an illustrative 0.95 USD/kg: 5.6 x 0.95 = 5.32 USD saved per tonne
  • Phytase at 500 FTU/kg typically costs, illustratively, 1.80 USD per tonne of feed
  • Net saving: 5.32 - 1.80 = 3.52 USD per tonne, which across 36 tonnes a year is 127 USD

He must then re-balance calcium, because removing dicalcium phosphate removes calcium too, and he must not let the ratio drift above 1.5:1. Substitute your own prices; the method is what transfers.

Lesson 1.6~12 min

Water and Drinking Systems

In this lesson
  • Explain how water supply limits feed intake and therefore growth and milk yield
  • Measure drinker flow rate and drinker-to-pig ratios on your own farm
  • Adjust water and electrolyte management during hot weather to protect sow feed intake

Water is the nutrient nobody budgets for and the one that fails most often. The reference standards are blunt about it: water access must be unlimited, and dehydration reduces feed intake. A pig that cannot drink will not eat, and a pig that will not eat does not grow, does not milk and does not conceive. On many African smallholdings the single cheapest performance gain available is a working drinker.

Note honestly what the standards do and do not give you. They state that water must be unlimited and that restriction cuts intake, but they do not publish a litres-per-pig-per-day table. So rather than repeat a number you cannot verify, measure your own system. Two measurements tell you nearly everything.

The first is flow rate. Hold a bucket under a nipple, open it fully, and time exactly sixty seconds. Measure the volume with a marked jug. Do this for every drinker on the farm and write the result on the pen card. Drinkers block with scale, sediment and biofilm, and a nipple delivering a trickle will hold back a whole pen of pigs even though it looks like it is working. The second is the ratio of pigs to functioning drinkers, counted per pen. If a pen shows queuing, pushing at the drinker, or pigs drinking at night when the dominant animals sleep, you do not have enough drinking points regardless of what the design said.

Water quality matters as much as quantity. Common problems on smallholdings are:

  • Biofilm inside pipes and tanks, which shelters bacteria and gives water a smell pigs avoid
  • Sediment from unfiltered borehole or river water blocking nipples
  • Warm water in black tanks standing in the sun, which pigs drink less of
  • Contamination from a tank with no lid, open to birds, dust and rodents
  • Troughs shared with dung, which links directly to the gut problems Module 9 lesson 1 teaches you to spot

Heat is where water and nutrition meet hardest. The standards report that lactating sows above roughly 29 degrees Celsius reduce feed intake by 15 to 30 percent against thermoneutral conditions, creating an acute energy deficit at exactly the moment their litters need milk. The published nutritional responses are to raise energy density from about 3,362 to 3,500 or more kcal ME per kg by adding fat, to raise sodium by 0.1 to 0.2 percentage points and potassium by 0.1 percentage points to support appetite signalling, and to consider betaine at 0.1 to 0.2 percent for osmotic balance. Every one of those depends on the sow being able to drink freely. Fat and salt without water make things worse, not better.

Worked example. A producer in Dodoma, Tanzania, runs 10 finishing pens with one nipple each and cannot understand why growth stalls in the hot months. He times every nipple with a bucket and finds four delivering a strong stream, three a moderate stream, and three barely dribbling. He strips the three worst and finds sediment and scale.

  • Cost of three replacement nipples at an illustrative 2.50 USD each: 7.50 USD
  • Cost of an in-line mesh filter at the tank outlet: 18 USD
  • Cost of a plastic lid for the header tank: 12 USD
  • Total: 37.50 USD, plus one afternoon of labour

Set that against his herd. He finishes 130 pigs a year. If restricted water was costing even 30 grams of daily gain across a 90-day finishing period, that is 2.7 kg per pig, and at an illustrative 2.30 USD per kg liveweight that is 6.21 USD per pig, or 807 USD a year. The repair pays for itself several times over in the first month, which is why water is checked before anyone starts arguing about ration formulas.

Make it a routine, not a project. Add drinker flow to the daily pen walk, flush and scrub tanks on a fixed date each month, and never let a hot week pass without confirming that the pigs which need water most, the lactating sows, are the ones getting it first.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Why can a pig not be raised on grass and crop residues the way a goat can?

The pig is monogastric. Without a rumen it relies on its own enzymes, so highly fibrous feeds pass through largely undigested, especially in young pigs.

2. Approximately how much of the phosphorus in plant-based feed ingredients is unavailable to the pig?

Roughly 70 to 80 percent of plant phosphorus is bound in phytate. This is why standardised total tract digestible phosphorus, not total phosphorus, is the formulation basis.

3. A ration calculates out at 6.4 percent crude fibre. Which class of pig is it best suited to?

Gestating sow diets tolerate roughly 6 to 8 percent crude fibre and benefit from the satiety. Weaners are capped at 2 to 4 percent and lactating sows at 3 to 4 percent.

4. Which section of the pig's digestive tract absorbs most of the starch, protein and fat?

The small intestine is where the pig's own enzymes break down and absorb the bulk of digestible nutrients. The large intestine only ferments what escapes.

5. Why do nursery diets taper lactose out in stages rather than removing it at once?

The piglet starts life equipped for milk sugar. Starch-digesting enzyme capacity builds over the nursery period, so lactose is reduced in step with that development.

6. Which energy system accounts for the heat produced during digestion and metabolism?

Net energy subtracts the heat increment, which is why it ranks fat, starch and protein more accurately than ME does.

7. What is the published energy density window for a pig in the 100 to 130 kg late finishing phase?

Energy density falls as pigs grow, from 3,300-3,400 in the weaner phase to 3,100-3,250 in late finishing, because appetite and gut capacity increase.

8. By roughly how much does each 1 percent of added dietary fat improve feed conversion?

The published figure is about 0.04 FCR points per 1 percent added fat, which is the number you use to decide whether oil is worth its price.

9. Why does maintenance energy matter so much to a producer's profit?

A 100 kg pig burns roughly 2,371 kcal NE a day simply staying alive. Slow growth means paying that bill repeatedly for no output.

10. A ration for 12 to 26 kg growers tests at 2,900 kcal ME/kg. What is the likely result?

The published window is 3,200 to 3,350 kcal ME/kg. Pigs eat to an energy target, and when the diet is too dilute their gut capacity limits intake before the target is met.

11. Why is crude protein a poor guide to a ration's true value for growth?

Crude protein is nitrogen multiplied by a factor. Two rations at the same crude protein can differ enormously in lysine and the other essential amino acids.

12. What does SID stand for in SID lysine?

Standardised ileal digestible lysine is the lysine absorbed before the large intestine, which is the fraction the pig can actually use for growth.

13. A ration tests at 0.90 percent SID lysine and the target for the pigs eating it is 1.15 percent. What is the shortfall per kilogram of feed?

0.25 percentage points equals 0.25 g per 100 g, which is 2.5 g per kilogram of feed, or 2.5 kg per tonne.

14. Which class of pig has the highest published SID lysine requirement as a percentage of the diet?

Early nursery pigs need 1.30 to 1.40 percent SID lysine, the highest of any class, because lean growth potential relative to feed intake peaks then.

15. In most cereal-based rations, closing a small lysine gap is cheapest by which route?

Synthetic lysine closes a specific gap without raising overall crude protein or ration cost the way a large soybean meal substitution does.

16. Which vitamin requirement rises roughly fourfold from growing pigs to breeding stock?

Growing pigs need 150 to 220 IU/kg of vitamin D3 while sows and boars need 800 IU/kg, so breeding animals need their own premix specification.

17. Vitamin E works as a system with which mineral?

Vitamin E and selenium work together through the selenium-dependent glutathione peroxidase system. Raising one without the other limits the benefit.

18. What is the published vitamin K requirement for pigs?

Menadione is required at 0.50 mg/kg across all classes and is rarely deficient in standard diets.

19. Why should vitamin premix be bought in quantities used within a few months?

Vitamin activity falls during storage, and the trace minerals sharing the bag accelerate the loss, so old premix delivers less than the label states.

20. Which deficiency picture matches vitamin A shortage?

The reference standards list reduced growth, poor coat quality and reduced immunity as the deficiency signs for vitamin A.

21. Why does high dietary calcium reduce the value of the phosphorus in a ration?

Ratios wider than about 1.5:1 impair phosphorus absorption in growing pigs, and calcium above 1.0 percent also depresses zinc absorption.

22. What does adding microbial phytase at 500 to 2,500 FTU/kg typically achieve?

Phytase releases phytate-bound phosphorus, improving available phosphorus by roughly 0.10 to 0.20 percentage points depending on dose and substrate.

23. What is the published manganese requirement difference between growing pigs and sows?

Growing pigs absorb manganese efficiently and need only 2 to 4 mg/kg, while sows and boars require 25 mg/kg for bone and reproductive function.

24. A late finisher at 110 kg requires what level of dietary calcium?

Calcium requirement falls with weight, from 0.85 percent in weaners down to 0.46 percent in the 100 to 130 kg phase.

25. Which trace mineral requirement stays constant at every stage of life?

Iodine is required at 0.14 to 0.20 mg/kg across all growth stages, for thyroid hormone synthesis.

26. What is the first consequence of restricted water supply in pigs?

Dehydration reduces feed intake. Because pigs eat to an energy target, water restriction shows up first as a growth and milk problem, not an obvious thirst problem.

27. How much can feed intake fall in lactating sows above about 29 degrees Celsius?

The published range is a 15 to 30 percent decline versus thermoneutral conditions, which creates an acute energy deficit during lactation.

28. Which is the correct nutritional response to heat stress in a lactating sow?

Because she eats less bulk, energy must be concentrated, and modest sodium and potassium increases support appetite. All of this requires unlimited water.

29. What is the practical way to find a partly blocked nipple drinker?

A blocked nipple often still appears to work. Timed flow measurement against the other drinkers is the only reliable field test.

30. Why is a lid on the header tank worth its small cost?

An open tank is a direct contamination route into every drinker on the farm, undermining the gut health the rest of the feeding programme depends on.

Module 1 capstone

Build a Nutrition Baseline File for your own farm. Step 1: write out every ration you currently feed, ingredient by ingredient, with the exact inclusion percentage and the price per kilogram you last paid at your local market or agro-dealer. Step 2: for each ration, copy the published requirement figures for that class of pig from this module (energy in kcal ME/kg, SID lysine percent, calcium, phosphorus, and the main vitamins and trace minerals) onto the same page so requirement and ration sit side by side. Step 3: collect the analysis tags or supplier declarations for your three largest ingredients and record crude protein, crude fibre, and any energy figure the supplier states; where no tag exists, write 'unknown' rather than guessing. Step 4: measure the flow rate of every drinker on the farm with a bucket and a watch, and record which ones are blocked, dripping or dry. Step 5: list the three biggest gaps between what your pigs need and what you can prove you are giving them, and put a price on closing each one using real local ingredient and premix prices. Step 6: keep this file and update it every time an ingredient price moves more than about 10 percent, because every later module in this course builds calculations on top of it.
