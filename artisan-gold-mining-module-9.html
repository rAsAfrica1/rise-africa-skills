1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Gold recovery is the step where you finally get paid for all your hard digging. In artisan mining, you might have ore that contains only 2 to 5 grams of gold per tonne. If you lose even half of that during recovery, you are throwing away money. On a 10-gram-per-tonne day, losing 5 grams means losing about $300 at today's prices. That is why recovery methods matter. They determine whether your family eats well or struggles.

In Africa, many miners use mercury or simple panning. Mercury is dangerous and often illegal. Panning works but is slow and misses fine gold. This module teaches you safer, more efficient methods that capture more gold. You will learn to use gravity concentration, sluice boxes, and clean-up techniques that work with local materials. Whether you are in Tanzania, Ghana, Burkina Faso, or Zimbabwe, these methods apply.

Recovery is not just about tools. It is about understanding your ore. Some gold is free and coarse, easy to catch. Some is fine and locked in sulphides. You need to know what you have. A simple test: pan a sample and see how much gold you get. If you see fine flakes, you need a different approach. If you see nuggets, gravity methods work well.

This module will take you through the entire process. First, we cover why recovery matters and how to assess your ore. Then we look at the tools you need, most of which you can make or buy locally. Next, we go step by step through a reliable method using a sluice box and pan. We then discuss common mistakes and how to fix them. Safety is critical, so we cover that too. Finally, you will do a practical exercise to test your skills.

By the end, you will be able to recover more gold with less effort and less risk. You will also reduce your environmental impact. Remember, every gram counts. Let's get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To recover gold efficiently, you need the right tools. Most can be made locally or bought at a mining supply shop. The core tools are: a gold pan, a sluice box, a classifier sieve, a shovel, a bucket, a snuffer bottle, and a magnet. You also need a water source, either a stream or a drum of water. For mercury-free recovery, you will not need mercury, but you might use borax for smelting later.

A gold pan is your most basic tool. In Africa, you can buy a plastic or metal pan for $5 to $15. A 14-inch pan is good for beginners. You can also make a wooden pan, but plastic is lighter and lasts longer. The sluice box is a wooden or metal channel with riffles (ridges) that catch gold. You can build one from local timber and rubber matting. A typical sluice is 1.2 meters long, 30 cm wide, and 10 cm deep. The riffles are placed at intervals to create dead zones where gold settles.

A classifier sieve is a bucket with a mesh bottom. It helps break up clay and separate large rocks. Use a 5 mm mesh for most ores. A shovel and bucket are standard. A snuffer bottle is a plastic bottle with a tube to suck up fine gold. You can make one from a 500 ml bottle and a straw. A magnet helps remove black sand (magnetite) from your concentrate.

For safety, you need gloves, boots, and eye protection. If you use mercury, you need a respirator, but we recommend avoiding mercury. Instead, use a retort if you must, but better to use gravity only. For smelting, you need a crucible, borax, and a furnace. A simple charcoal furnace works.

Water is essential. You need a steady flow for the sluice. If you are far from a stream, use a pump or carry water in drums. A 200-litre drum can process about 100 kg of ore if you recycle water. Always have a settling pond to catch muddy water. This prevents pollution and lets you reuse water.

Gather these tools before you start. Borrow or share with other miners to reduce costs. A good sluice box can be shared among a group. Keep your tools clean and in good repair. A worn pan or torn mat will lose gold.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to recover gold using a sluice box and pan. This method works for alluvial and hard rock ore after crushing. You will need about 20 litres of water per minute for the sluice.

Step 1: Crush and classify. If your ore is hard rock, crush it to sand size (less than 5 mm). Use a hammer or stamp mill. Then classify through a 5 mm sieve to remove large rocks. For alluvial, just dig and screen. You want a consistent feed size.

Step 2: Set up your sluice. Place it at a slight angle, about 10 to 15 degrees. The water should flow fast enough to wash lighter material but slow enough for gold to settle. Test with a handful of gravel: if it washes away too fast, reduce the angle. If it clogs, increase angle. Put a mat or carpet under the riffles to catch fine gold.

Step 3: Feed the sluice. Shovel ore into the sluice at a steady pace. Do not overload. A good rate is one shovel every 10 seconds. Let the water wash the material through. Use a rake or stick to break up clumps. Keep the feed consistent. If you see gold sitting on top of the riffles, stop and clean up.

Step 4: Clean up the sluice. After processing 100 to 200 kg of ore, stop feeding. Wash down the riffles and mat into a bucket. Do this carefully. All the concentrate goes into the bucket. You should have about 5 to 10 litres of concentrate.

Step 5: Pan the concentrate. Take a pan and put a small amount of concentrate in it. Add water and swirl. Tilt the pan slightly and let the lighter material wash off. Gold is heavy and stays at the bottom. Repeat until you have only black sand and gold. Use a magnet to remove black sand. Then use a snuffer bottle to suck up the gold. Put it in a safe container.

Step 6: Weigh and store. Weigh your gold on a small scale. Record the amount. Store it in a sealed container. If you have fine gold, you can smelt it with borax to make a button. For smelting, mix gold with borax (1:1) and heat in a crucible until molten. Pour into a mold.

This method recovers 80 to 95% of free gold. For fine gold, you may need to repeat panning or use a shaker table. Practice makes perfect.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced miners make mistakes that lose gold. Here are the most common and how to fix them.

Mistake 1: Too much water flow. If water flows too fast, it washes gold out of the sluice. Fix: reduce the angle or slow the water. Test with a pan: if you see gold in the tailings, your flow is too high. Adjust until tailings are clean.

Mistake 2: Overloading the sluice. Shoveling too fast buries the riffles and gold cannot settle. Fix: feed slowly and steadily. One shovel every 10 seconds is a good rule. If the sluice clogs, stop and clean.

Mistake 3: Not classifying. Large rocks create turbulence and let gold escape. Fix: always screen your ore to less than 5 mm. Use a classifier sieve. This also makes panning easier.

Mistake 4: Losing fine gold during panning. Fine gold floats or washes away if you are not careful. Fix: use a pan with deep sides. Add a drop of dish soap to break surface tension. Pan slowly and use a snuffer bottle to catch fine flakes. Never pour off water quickly.

Mistake 5: Using mercury unsafely. Mercury is toxic and illegal in many countries. It also loses fine gold if not used properly. Fix: avoid mercury. Use gravity methods only. If you must use it, do so in a retort and wear protective gear. Better to invest in a shaker table.

Mistake 6: Not cleaning the sluice properly. Gold hides in the mat and corners. Fix: clean the sluice thoroughly after each run. Wash all matting into a bucket. Check corners and riffles. Do not leave any concentrate behind.

Mistake 7: Ignoring black sand. Black sand (magnetite) can trap fine gold. Fix: use a magnet to remove black sand before final panning. Then pan the remaining gold. If gold is still trapped, use a blue bowl or spiral wheel.

Mistake 8: Poor water management. Muddy water reduces visibility and clogs the sluice. Fix: use a settling pond. Recycle water. Change water often if it gets too muddy.

Mistake 9: Not testing your ore. You might be processing low-grade ore and wasting time. Fix: pan a sample first. If you see no gold, move to another spot. Test different areas.

Mistake 10: Giving up too soon. Recovery takes practice. Fix: keep learning. Watch videos, ask other miners, and improve your technique. Every mistake is a lesson.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional in gold recovery. You are dealing with heavy equipment, water, and sometimes chemicals. Follow these guidelines to stay safe and protect your community.

Personal protective equipment (PPE): Always wear gloves, boots, and eye protection. When crushing ore, wear a dust mask to avoid silicosis. If you use mercury, wear a respirator and work outdoors. But remember, mercury is dangerous. Avoid it if possible. Instead, use borax for smelting. Borax is safer but still use gloves and eye protection.

Water safety: Never work alone near water. Use a buddy system. If you are using a pump, check hoses for leaks. Keep electrical equipment away from water. If you work in a stream, be aware of flash floods. Watch the weather. Do not work in fast-flowing water.

Chemical safety: If you use mercury, never touch it with bare hands. Store it in a sealed container. Never burn mercury amalgam in an open pan; use a retort. The fumes are highly toxic. Better yet, switch to mercury-free methods. Cyanide is even more dangerous and should never be used by artisans. It is illegal in most African countries.

Environmental best practice: Do not dump tailings into rivers. Use a settling pond. This prevents pollution and protects fish and downstream users. Recycle water as much as possible. Reclaim your mining site after you finish. Fill holes and replant vegetation. This keeps the land usable for farming.

Health and hygiene: Wash your hands before eating or smoking. Do not eat in the work area. Keep children away from the site. Pregnant women should not handle mercury or chemicals. If you feel dizzy or sick, stop work and get fresh air.

Community relations: Inform local leaders about your activities. Get permission to mine. Avoid conflicts with farmers and other miners. Share water sources fairly. If you use mercury, you risk poisoning your neighbors. Be a responsible miner.

Emergency preparedness: Have a first aid kit on site. Know the nearest clinic. For mercury exposure, seek medical help immediately. For injuries, stop bleeding and clean wounds. Always have a phone or means of communication.

Best practices: Keep your site clean and organized. Store tools safely. Maintain your equipment. Train your team on safety. Use the buddy system. Never take shortcuts. A safe mine is a productive mine. Remember, your health and the environment are more valuable than gold.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is time to put your knowledge into practice. This exercise will test your ability to recover gold using a sluice box and pan. You will need: 20 kg of ore, a sluice box, a pan, a classifier, a bucket, a shovel, water, and a snuffer bottle. Work in a team of two or three.

Step 1: Prepare your site. Set up your sluice near a water source. Make sure the angle is about 10-15 degrees. Place a mat under the riffles. Fill a bucket with water for panning. Put on your PPE.

Step 2: Classify your ore. Use the classifier to screen the 20 kg of ore to less than 5 mm. Remove any large rocks. You should have about 18 kg of classified material.

Step 3: Run the sluice. Feed the ore slowly into the sluice. Use one shovel every 10 seconds. Let the water wash it through. Watch for gold on the riffles. If you see gold, stop and clean up. Otherwise, process all 18 kg.

Step 4: Clean up. Stop the water. Wash the riffles and mat into a bucket. Collect all concentrate. You should have about 2-3 litres of concentrate.

Step 5: Pan the concentrate. Take a small amount and pan it. Use a slow swirling motion. Remove black sand with a magnet. Use the snuffer bottle to collect gold. Weigh your gold. Record the weight.

Step 6: Calculate recovery. If you know the grade of your ore (e.g., 2 g/tonne), you can calculate recovery. For 20 kg, expected gold is 0.04 g. If you recovered 0.03 g, your recovery is 75%. If you recovered 0.035 g, recovery is 87.5%. Aim for over 80%.

Step 7: Repeat and improve. If recovery is low, check for mistakes. Was the water flow too fast? Did you overload? Did you clean thoroughly? Adjust and try again with another 20 kg. Compare results.

Step 8: Document your results. Write down the weight of ore, gold recovered, and any issues. This helps you track improvement. Share your findings with your team.

This exercise should take about 2-3 hours. It is a safe, mercury-free way to recover gold. Practice until you consistently achieve high recovery. Remember, the goal is to recover every gram. Good luck!

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Gold Recovery Methods for Artisan Miners

This practical reference consolidates the field knowledge an artisan miner needs to recover gold efficiently, safely, and profitably. It is written for small-scale operators across Africa from the ashanti goldfields of Ghana and the greenstone belts of Zimbabwe to the coppergold zones of Zambia and the artisanal workings of Tanzania, Burkina Faso, Mali, and the DRC. The goal is business-first: recover more gold per tonne of ore, waste less mercury and cyanide, reduce injuries, and turn a subsistence activity into a viable enterprise.

Gold recovery is the stage where value is actually realised. Poor recovery destroys margins built on hard labour and diesel costs. A miner who recovers 55% of contained gold instead of 85% loses nearly a third of revenue enough to erase profit entirely. The methods below cover gravity concentration (panning, sluicing, shaking tables), amalgamation, and the safe handling of concentrates, with emphasis on what works at the village level.

RED SAFETY WARNING Mercury & Cyanide

NEVER burn mercury amalgam in the open or inside a home. Mercury vapour is odourless, invisible, and causes permanent brain and kidney damage. Always use a closed retort with a condenser and capture the mercury for reuse.

NEVER handle cyanide without training, gloves, goggles, and a respirator. Cyanide is lethal a pinch can kill. Keep it locked away from children, food, and water sources.

NEVER discharge tailings or mercury-contaminated water into rivers, streams, or farmland. This poisons entire communities downstream. Use settling ponds and follow the Minamata Convention guidelines.

Step-by-Step Practical Method

  1. Crush and grind ore. Reduce to sand-size (ideally <2 mm) to liberate gold. Use a stamp mill or ball mill; wet grinding reduces dust and silicosis risk.
  2. Concentrate by gravity. Run slurry through a sluice box or shaking table. Gold (specific gravity 19.3) settles behind riffles while lighter gangue washes away.
  3. Pan the concentrate. Use a gold pan with a gentle circular motion. Tilt and wash off lighter material until only black sand and gold remain.
  4. Amalgamate (if used). Add a small amount of mercury to the concentrate in a closed container. Gold forms an amalgam. Squeeze through a cloth to remove excess mercury.
  5. Retort the amalgam. Heat in a closed retort. Mercury vapour condenses and is collected; gold sponge remains. Never burn in the open.
  6. Smelt the gold. Melt the sponge with borax in a crucible to produce a gold button or bar for sale.
  7. Treat tailings. If economic, leach tailings with cyanide in a controlled vat only with proper training and permits.
  8. Record and sell. Weigh gold, record recovery rate, and sell to a licensed dealer for fair price. Keep business records for reinvestment.

Common Mistakes and Fixes

  • Mistake: Overloading the sluice. Fix: Feed slowly; let water do the work.
  • Mistake: Using too much mercury. Fix: Use only 12 g per gram of gold; excess mercury is waste and poison.
  • Mistake: Burning amalgam in open air. Fix: Always use a retort; capture and reuse mercury.
  • Mistake: Skipping tailings check. Fix: Re-pan tailings; often 2030% of gold is lost.
  • Mistake: Selling unrefined gold cheaply. Fix: Smelt into a bar and get an assay before selling.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Wear PPE: gloves, goggles, respirator, boots.
  2. Grind ore to <2 mm for maximum liberation.
  3. Use gravity first it is cheap and mercury-free.
  4. Use a retort for all amalgam burning.
  5. Never discharge tailings into water bodies.
  6. Keep cyanide locked and away from children.
  7. Record gold weight and recovery rate daily.
  8. Sell to licensed dealers for fair prices.
