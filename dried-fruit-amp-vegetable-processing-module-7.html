Lessons

1. How to Check Moisture Content Without a Meter

Without a moisture meter, you can still tell if your dried fruit or vegetables are ready for storage. The goal is to reach 1020% moisture for most dried produce (e.g., mango, tomato, banana). Here are three reliable field tests.

First, the squeeze test. Take a piece and squeeze it firmly in your fist. For most fruits, it should feel leathery and spring back slightly, not squish like fresh fruit. For vegetables like tomatoes, it should be brittle but not crumbly. If juice runs out, it's not dry enough. If it snaps like a cracker, it may be over-dried (still safe, but less tasty).

Second, the salt test. Put a tablespoon of dry salt in a small clean jar. Add a few pieces of dried produce. Shake well and let sit for 5 minutes. If the salt clumps or sticks to the produce, moisture is still too high. If the salt stays free-flowing, moisture is likely below 20%. This works because salt absorbs moisture.

Third, the weight-loss method. Weigh a small sample (e.g., 100g) on a kitchen scale. Dry it further in your solar dryer or oven at 60C for 1 hour. Weigh again. If the weight drops by more than 2g, the original sample still had too much moisture. Repeat until weight change is less than 1g per hour. This gives a rough moisture percentage: (weight loss / original weight) x 100.

For accuracy, always test a few pieces from different parts of the batch. If any fail, return the whole batch to the dryer for another 12 hours. In humid climates, dry to the lower end (1012%) to prevent spoilage. In dry areas, 1520% is fine. Record your results in a notebook so you learn how long each crop takes under your local conditions.

2. Preventing Mold, Yeast, and Bacterial Growth

Mold, yeast, and bacteria need three things to grow: moisture, warmth, and time. Your job is to break that chain. In dried foods, moisture is the main culprit. If your product has more than 20% moisture, mold will appear within days. Yeast causes fermentation (alcohol smell), and bacteria can make food unsafe.

Start by drying thoroughly. Use the tests from Lesson 1. Then cool the product completely before packaging. Warm dried fruit creates condensation inside the container, which feeds microbes. Spread it on a clean tray in a dry room for 3060 minutes until it reaches room temperature.

Next, control the storage environment. Keep containers sealed and store them in a cool, dark place below 25C. In hot African climates, a dark cupboard or insulated box works. Avoid storing near walls that get sun. If you have a refrigerator, use it for long-term storagebut first ensure the product is in airtight packaging to prevent moisture from entering.

Add natural antimicrobials where appropriate. For example, a light dusting of salt on dried vegetables (like tomatoes) inhibits bacteria. For fruits, a dip in lemon juice (citric acid) before drying lowers pH and slows mold. You can also use food-grade desiccant packets (silica gel) in containersbut never let them touch the food directly.

Finally, practice strict hygiene. Wash hands, clean all surfaces, and never dry produce near animals or garbage. Mold spores are everywhere; you cannot eliminate them, but you can deny them the moisture they need. If you see any fuzzy spots, smell alcohol, or feel slime, throw the whole batch away. Do not try to salvage by cutting off moldtoxins spread invisibly.

3. Safe Storage Containers and Packaging Materials

The container you choose determines how long your dried products stay safe. The best containers are airtight, food-grade, and opaque. Avoid open bowls, paper bags, or cloth sacksthey let in moisture, insects, and dust.

For small-scale sales, use clean glass jars with rubber gaskets or metal lids. Plastic buckets with airtight lids (food-grade HDPE, marked with a #2 or #5 recycling symbol) work well for bulk. Never use containers that previously held chemicals, oil, or painteven after washing, residues can leach into food. If you use plastic, make sure it is thick and not scratched, as scratches harbor bacteria.

For packaging individual portions, consider: polyethylene bags (thick, food-grade) heat-sealed or tied tightly; vacuum-sealed bags (best but expensive); or laminated foil pouches (block light and moisture). If selling at local markets, simple clear plastic bags with a twist tie are acceptable if you double-bag and squeeze out air. But for longer shelf life, invest in a impulse sealer (around $3050) and use 0.08mm thick bags.

Always fill containers to the top to reduce air space. Add a small desiccant packet (silica gel) if you live in a humid area. Label with the date and product name. Store in a cool, dark place. For reusable jars, wash with hot soapy water, rinse, and dry completely before refilling. Never mix old and new batches in the same container without cleaning first. If you see condensation inside the container, your product was not dry enough or the container is not airtightre-dry and repackage.

4. Labeling and Traceability for Your Products

A good label does two things: it tells customers what they are buying, and it helps you track problems. For local sales, you need at minimum: product name (e.g., 'Dried Mango Slices'), net weight (e.g., '100g'), your business name and phone number, and the date of production. If you use any additives (like sulphur dioxide or citric acid), list them. For export or larger markets, you may need batch numbers and expiry dates.

Traceability means you can trace a product back to the day and batch it was made. Use a simple code: write the date as YYYYMMDD (e.g., 20250315) and add a batch letter (A, B, C for different drying runs that day). For example, '20250315-A'. Keep a notebook or spreadsheet with: date, product, raw material source (farm or market), drying method, moisture test result, and any issues. If a customer complains, you can check that batch and see if others had the same problem.

Labeling also builds trust. Handwritten labels are fine if they are neat and legible. Use permanent marker on stickers or directly on the container. Include a 'Best Before' datetypically 612 months for properly dried and stored products. For fruits with higher moisture (1520%), use 6 months; for very dry vegetables (10%), 12 months. If you sell in bulk, attach a label to the sack or provide a small card.

Finally, keep a sample of each batch (about 50g) in a sealed jar for at least 3 months. If a problem arises, you can test the sample. This simple habit can save your reputation. Remember, traceability is not just for big companiesit protects your small business too.

5. Cleaning and Sanitizing Your Drying Equipment

Dirty equipment is the fastest way to ruin a good batch. Mold and bacteria hide in cracks, corners, and screens. Clean after every use, and sanitize at least once a week if you dry daily.

Start with dry cleaning. Remove all food pieces and dust with a brush or vacuum. Never use water on electrical parts. For solar dryers, take out trays and screens. Scrape off stuck bits with a plastic scrapernot metal, which can scratch surfaces.

Then wash with hot soapy water. Use a food-safe detergent (dish soap is fine). Scrub trays, racks, and the inside of the drying chamber with a soft brush or sponge. Pay attention to corners and seams. Rinse with clean water. For wooden trays, do not soakwipe with a damp cloth and dry immediately to prevent warping and mold.

Sanitize with a bleach solution. Mix 1 tablespoon of plain unscented bleach (5% sodium hypochlorite) per 1 liter of water. Spray or wipe all food-contact surfaces. Let sit for 2 minutes, then rinse with clean water and air-dry. For metal parts, you can also use 70% alcohol (isopropyl or ethanol) sprayed on and left to evaporateno rinse needed. Vinegar (white) is a mild sanitizer but not as effective as bleach; use it for daily wiping.

Dry all parts completely before reassembling. Moisture left in equipment grows mold. Store clean trays in a dust-free cupboard. Never use the same cloth for cleaning floors and food surfaces. Wash your hands before handling clean equipment. If you see any black spots or persistent smell, replace the partit is not worth the risk.

6. Meeting Basic Food Safety Standards for Local Sales

Even for local markets, you must follow basic food safety rules to protect your customers and your business. In most African countries, local authorities (city council, health department) have simple requirements for small food producers. Check with your local officemany offer free advice.

The core rules: keep clean, separate raw and dried, cook (dry) thoroughly, keep at safe temperatures, and use safe water. For dried products, the key points are: dry to safe moisture (1020%), store in clean airtight containers, and prevent contamination from pests, chemicals, and dirty hands. Your drying area should be away from toilets, animals, and garbage. Use clean water for washing raw produceif uncertain, boil and cool it first.

Personal hygiene is non-negotiable. Wash hands with soap before touching food. Wear clean clothes and tie back hair. Cover any cuts with waterproof plasters. Do not dry food if you have diarrhea or a skin infection. Keep fingernails short and clean. Visitors should not handle food.

For sales, you may need a simple food handler's permit. In many places, this involves a short course and a medical check. The cost is usually low (e.g., $520). Display your permit if required. Also, label your products as described in Lesson 4. If you sell to shops, they may ask for a certificate of analysis from a labbut for local markets, a self-declaration of good practices often suffices.

Finally, keep records: what you dried, when, moisture test results, and any cleaning done. If a customer gets sick, you can show you followed safe practices. This protects you legally and builds trust. Remember, food safety is not just about rulesit is about respecting your customers' health.

<h2>Practical Reference: Quality Control &amp; Food Safety for Dried Fruit and Vegetable Processing</h2>

<p>For a dried fruit and vegetable business, quality control is not a paperwork exercise it is the difference between repeat customers and a ruined consignment. A single mouldy batch sold at a market stall can destroy trust built over months. This practical reference gives you the checks, thresholds, storage rules and record-keeping habits that keep your product sellable, from the drying rack to the buyer's hand.</p>

<p>Work through this module alongside the six lessons: moisture testing without a meter, preventing microbial growth, safe packaging, labelling and traceability, cleaning your equipment, and meeting basic food safety standards for local sales. Everything here is designed for small and medium processors working with solar dryers, cabinet dryers or open-air racks in African conditions high humidity, variable power, and informal market channels.</p>

<h3>1. Moisture Content: The Single Most Important Number</h3>

<p>Moisture content determines shelf life. Too wet, and mould and yeast will grow within days. Too dry, and you lose weight, texture and profit. Without a moisture meter, you can still get a reliable field estimate using the <strong>oven or salt test method</strong> and the <strong>cut-and-squeeze test</strong>.</p>

<p><strong>Cut-and-squeeze test (field estimate):</strong> Cut a piece across the thickest part. Squeeze firmly between thumb and forefinger. For most dried fruit (mango, banana, pineapple), no free moisture should appear and the piece should spring back slowly. For vegetables (tomato, onion, pepper, leafy greens), the piece should snap or crumble rather than bend. If it bends like leather, it is still too wet.</p>

<p><strong>Salt jar / oven method (semi-quantitative):</strong> Weigh a 10 g sample, dry it in a low oven (around 70 C) or in your solar dryer for several hours, then reweigh. Weight loss original weight 100 = approximate moisture percentage. Repeat twice and average the results. This is not laboratory-grade, but it is good enough to catch a batch that is 510 % too wet.</p>

<h3>2. Microbiological Control: Mould, Yeast and Bacteria</h3>

<p>Mould, yeast and bacteria need three things: moisture, nutrients and warmth. Your job is to remove at least one. Drying removes moisture; correct storage keeps it out; hygiene removes nutrients and contamination.</p>

<p><strong>Practical rules:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Dry to the lower end of the range if you are storing for more than 3 months.</li>
<li>Cool the product completely before packing. Warm product creates condensation inside the bag.</li>
<li>Never mix a wet batch with a dry batch. One wet piece can re-wet a whole container.</li>
<li>Use clean, dry hands or gloves. Human hands carry <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em> and faecal bacteria.</li>
<li>Discard any batch with visible mould, off-smell or sliminess. Do not "cut off the mouldy part" mould hyphae and mycotoxins can spread invisibly.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:0;font-weight:bold;color:#6b1414;"> SAFETY WARNING Aflatoxins and mould</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#6b1414;">Mould on dried chilli, groundnut, maize or tomato can produce <strong>aflatoxins</strong>, which are invisible, heat-stable and can cause liver cancer. Do not taste, sell or feed mouldy product to animals. If you see green, black or white fuzz, or smell a musty odour, <strong>discard the entire batch</strong> and clean the drying area. Never attempt to "wash off" or "re-dry" mouldy product for sale.</p>
</div>

<ul>
<li>Use food-grade materials only. Never use cement bags, fertiliser sacks or pesticide containers.</li>
<li>Heat-seal where possible. A simple impulse sealer (or a candle and pliers) gives a better seal than a knot.</li>
<li>Fill to leave minimal headspace; squeeze out air before sealing.</li>
<li>Store away from sunlight, fuel, pesticides and strong-smelling goods.</li>
<li>Keep off the floor on pallets or shelves. Use rodent-proof and insect-proof storage.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. Labelling and Traceability</h3>

<p>Even for informal markets, a simple label builds trust and protects you. At minimum, include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Product name (e.g. "Sun-dried Mango Slices")</li>
<li>Net weight in grams or kilograms</li>
<li>Your business name and phone number</li>
<li>Production date and best-before date</li>
<li>Batch or lot number (e.g. "B-2025-07-14-01")</li>
<li>Storage instructions ("Store cool and dry, away from sunlight")</li>
<li>Country of origin</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Traceability</strong> means you can trace one finished pack back to the farm, harvest date and drying batch and forward to the buyer. Keep a simple notebook or spreadsheet with: date received, supplier, weight in, weight out, drying time, moisture check, packaging date, batch number, buyer. This is what allows you to recall a problem batch without discarding your whole stock.</p>

<h3>5. Cleaning and Sanitising Your Drying Equipment</h3>

<p>Residue from previous batches feeds mould and bacteria. Clean after every production run.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Dry-clean first:</strong> scrape and brush off dried residue from trays, racks and dryer walls.</li>
<li><strong>Wash:</strong> use warm water with food-safe detergent. Rinse thoroughly.</li>
<li><strong>Sanitise:</strong> use a dilute chlorine solution (1 teaspoon of 5 % bleach per 1 litre of clean water) or food-grade sanitiser. Let contact for at least 1 minute, then air-dry.</li>
<li><strong>Sun-dry:</strong> UV from sunlight adds a natural sanitising effect for trays and racks.</li>
<li><strong>Pest control:</strong> inspect for insects, rodent droppings and cobwebs. Seal cracks in walls and floors.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Meeting Basic Food Safety Standards for Local Sales</h3>

<p>Most African countries have a national food safety authority for example, NAFDAC (Nigeria), KEBS (Kenya), Ghana Standards Authority, Tanzania Bureau of Standards, or the relevant Ministry of Health. Even where enforcement is light, aligning with basic standards opens doors to supermarkets, hotels, NGOs and export buyers.</p>

<p><strong>Core practices:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Train all staff in handwashing, clean clothing, no jewellery, no eating in the processing area.</li>
<li>Keep a clean water supply. If unsure, boil or filter water used for washing and sanitising.</li>
<li>Separate raw and dried product areas to prevent cross-contamination.</li>
<li>Record everything: incoming material, drying logs, moisture checks, cleaning logs, dispatch.</li>
<li>Use a simple HACCP-style approach: identify hazards (mould, pests, chemicals, glass, metal), set controls, monitor, correct, and verify.</li>
<li>Get your product tested at a local laboratory once or twice a year for moisture, aflatoxin (for chilli, groundnut, maize) and microbial counts.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Moisture:</strong> Test every batch with cut-and-squeeze and, where possible, oven/salt method. Target 1020 % depending on crop.</li>
<li><strong>Cool before packing:</strong> Never pack warm product condensation causes mould.</li>
<li><strong>Food-grade packaging only:</strong> Heat-seal, minimal headspace, no reused chemical sacks.</li>
<li><strong>Label every pack:</strong> Product, weight, business name, phone, dates, batch number.</li>
<li><strong>Clean after every run:</strong> Dry-clean, wash, sanitise, sun-dry trays and racks.</li>
<li><strong>Store cool, dry, dark:</strong> Off the floor, away from fuels, pesticides and strong odours.</li>
<li><strong>Discard mouldy batches:</strong> Never sell, taste or feed mouldy product aflatoxin risk.</li>
<li><strong>Keep records:</strong> Incoming, drying, moisture, cleaning, dispatch for traceability and recall.</li>
</ol>
