1. Daily Cash Handling and Record Keeping for Small Shops

Running a small shop or market stall in Africa means handling cash every single day. Whether you sell tomatoes in Kampala, phone credit in Lagos, or second-hand clothes in Nairobi, the way you manage money determines whether your business grows or collapses. This lesson gives you practical systems for handling cash and keeping records that work even when you have no electricity, no smartphone, or no formal accounting training. Start with the three-container system. Get three small containers, tins, or envelopes. Label them: Business Cash, Personal Cash, and Change Float. Every morning before you open, decide how much money goes into your Change Float. This is the money you use to give customers change. In many African markets, customers pay with large notes and expect change immediately. If you use your business cash for change, you lose track of what is profit and what is float. Keep the float separate. At the end of the day, count your float. It should be the same amount you started with, plus or minus small differences from coins. If it is short, investigate immediately. A missing 500 shillings today becomes 5,000 shillings next week if you ignore it. The second container is Business Cash. This is all the money you receive from sales. The third is Personal Cash. This is money you take out for food, rent, school fees, or transport. The rule is simple: never take money directly from the Business Cash container for personal use without recording it as a withdrawal. Many small businesses fail because the owner treats the till as a personal wallet. You buy lunch from the till, pay a boda boda from the till, and by evening you cannot tell whether you made a profit or a loss. The three-container system forces you to see the difference. Now for record keeping. You do not need a computer. You need a notebook and a pen. Get a hardcover exercise book. Draw lines to create columns: Date, Item Sold, Quantity, Unit Price, Total Sale, Expenses, and Cash on Hand. Every sale, no matter how small, gets written down. If you sell one sweet for 100 shillings, write it. If you sell a whole sack of maize, write it. At the end of the day, add up your total sales and total expenses. Subtract expenses from sales to get your daily profit. Then count your cash. It should match your Cash on Hand column. If it does not match, you have a problem. Maybe you forgot to record a sale. Maybe you gave credit and forgot to write it. Maybe money was stolen. The notebook will show you the truth. In many African markets, traders resist writing down small sales because it slows them down. But consider this: a trader in Kumasi who sells provisions discovered after one month of record keeping that she was losing 15 percent of her revenue to unrecorded credit and small thefts. Once she started writing everything down, her profit increased by 20 percent in two months. The notebook pays for itself. For those who have a basic phone, use the calculator and SMS notes. You can send yourself a text message with the day's total sales and expenses. At the end of the week, compile these into your notebook. If you have a smartphone, there are free apps like Kobo Collect or simple Excel templates, but do not let technology become an excuse. A pen and notebook work everywhere. Credit sales are a major challenge in African small shops. Many customers want to buy on credit and pay later. This is normal in community-based trading, but it can destroy your business if you do not manage it. Create a credit page in your notebook. Write the customer's name, the date, the items taken, the amount owed, and the agreed payment date. When they pay, cross it out and write the payment date. Never give credit without writing it down. If a customer refuses to wait while you write, do not give them credit. Your memory is not a bank. At the end of each week, review your credit page. Follow up with customers who are overdue. Do it politely but firmly. In many African cultures, direct confrontation is avoided, so use a gentle reminder: 'I saw your name in my book. I know you will pay when you can. Can we agree on a date?' This works better than anger. Also, set a credit limit per customer. If someone already owes you 10,000 shillings and wants more credit, say no. Offer to sell for cash instead. You can also introduce a small incentive for early payment, like a 2 percent discount if they pay within three days. This encourages faster repayment. Another critical practice is separating business money from personal money at the bank or mobile money level. If you use M-Pesa, Airtel Money, or a bank account, open a separate account for the business. Do not mix personal and business transactions. In many African countries, mobile money is the primary way to save and transfer money. Use it to deposit your daily profit. Even 1,000 shillings a day adds up to 30,000 shillings a month. That is capital for restocking. When you deposit, write the deposit in your notebook as an expense or transfer. This keeps your records clean. Finally, do a weekly review. Every Sunday evening or Monday morning, sit down with your notebook. Add up the week's sales, expenses, and profit. Compare this week to last week. Are sales going up or down? Which items sell best? Which items sit on the shelf? Adjust your stock buying accordingly. If you sell more sugar than rice, buy more sugar. If customers ask for a product you do not have, write it in a 'wanted' list and try to source it. This simple review turns your shop from a daily hustle into a real business. Remember, in small shop and market trading, cash is king, but records are the kingdom. Without records, you are guessing. With records, you are managing. Start today. Get three containers, a notebook, and a pen. Write every sale. Count your cash every night. Review every week. Your business will thank you.

2. Pricing and Profit Margins in Competitive African Markets

Pricing is one of the hardest decisions for small shop and market traders in Africa. Set your prices too high, and customers walk to the next stall. Set them too low, and you work all day for nothing. This lesson teaches you how to calculate costs, set prices that attract customers, and protect your profit margins even when competition is fierce. First, understand the difference between cost, price, and profit. Cost is what you pay to buy or make the product. Price is what the customer pays you. Profit is price minus cost. If you buy a crate of soda for 20,000 shillings and sell each bottle for 1,000 shillings, and there are 24 bottles, your total sales are 24,000 shillings. Your profit is 4,000 shillings. That is a 20 percent margin. But wait, you also paid transport to bring the crate from the wholesaler. Maybe that was 2,000 shillings. Now your profit is only 2,000 shillings, or about 8 percent. If you also paid a market fee of 500 shillings, your profit drops to 1,500 shillings. This is why many traders think they are making money but actually are not. You must calculate all your costs. There are two types of costs: direct and indirect. Direct costs are the cost of the goods you sell. Indirect costs include transport, market fees, packaging, airtime for business calls, and even a portion of your rent if you have a permanent stall. Add all these together to get your true cost per item. For example, if you sell rice by the cup, calculate the cost of the whole bag, plus transport, plus the cost of the plastic bags you use, plus a small amount for your stall rent. Divide by the number of cups in the bag. That is your true cost per cup. Only then can you set a price that gives you a real profit. Now, how do you set prices in a competitive market? Start by checking your competitors. Walk around the market or the street where you trade. What are they charging for the same or similar items? If you are the only one selling a particular item, you have more freedom. But if there are five other traders selling the same tomatoes, you must be competitive. However, do not simply match the lowest price. If someone is selling at a loss, they will soon be out of business. You need to find your unique advantage. Maybe you sell in smaller quantities that are more affordable for daily-wage earners. Maybe you offer credit to trusted customers. Maybe you are open earlier or later than others. Maybe you package your goods more attractively. These differences allow you to charge a slightly higher price or attract more customers. A common mistake is to compete only on price. In many African markets, customers value relationships, trust, and convenience. If you greet customers by name, remember their preferences, and always give correct change, they will pay a small premium to buy from you. A trader in Accra who sells eggs found that by offering a free plastic bag for every dozen and by always having change, she could charge 50 pesewas more per dozen than the trader next to her. Customers appreciated the service. So your pricing strategy should include value-added services. Another key concept is the break-even point. This is the number of items you must sell to cover all your costs. If your total daily costs are 10,000 shillings for rent, transport, and market fees, and you make 500 shillings profit per item, you must sell 20 items just to break even. Anything above 20 is your true profit. Knowing this helps you set daily sales targets. If you are halfway through the day and have only sold 10 items, you know you need to push harder or adjust your display. For perishable goods like vegetables, fruits, and fresh fish, pricing is even more critical. You must sell before they spoil. Many traders use a two-tier pricing strategy. In the morning, sell at full price. By afternoon, reduce the price to clear stock. By evening, sell at cost or even slightly below cost to avoid total loss. This is better than throwing away spoiled goods. But you must plan so that you do not rely on evening discounts for your profit. Buy only what you can sell at full price, and use the discount as a safety net, not a strategy. In some African markets, haggling is expected. Customers will ask for a lower price. You need to build a small buffer into your starting price so you can give a discount and still make a profit. For example, if your true cost is 800 shillings and you want a 25 percent profit, your target price is 1,000 shillings. But you set your starting price at 1,200 shillings. When a customer haggles, you can drop to 1,000 shillings and they feel they have won. If they pay the full 1,200 shillings, you make even more. But be careful: do not set the starting price so high that you scare customers away. Study your market. In some places, haggling is minimal, and a fixed price is respected. In others, it is a social ritual. Adapt to your local context. Record keeping is essential for pricing. If you do not know your costs, you cannot set prices. Use the notebook system from the previous lesson. Track every purchase and every sale. At the end of the month, calculate your average profit margin per product category. You may discover that some products are not worth selling. For example, a shop owner in Dar es Salaam found that she was making only 5 percent on sugar but 40 percent on cooking oil. She reduced her sugar stock and increased oil. Her overall profit went up. Also, review your prices regularly. Inflation in many African countries means that the cost of goods rises. If you do not adjust your prices, your profit margin shrinks. But raising prices can lose customers. The solution is to raise prices in small increments and explain why. 'The wholesale price has gone up, so I have to adjust a little. But for you, my loyal customer, I will keep it as low as possible.' This honesty builds trust. Finally, consider bulk pricing and loyalty pricing. Offer a lower price per unit if a customer buys a larger quantity. For example, sell one bar of soap for 500 shillings, but three bars for 1,400 shillings. This encourages larger purchases and moves stock faster. For your best customers, offer a loyalty card or a small discount after ten purchases. This keeps them coming back. In the competitive world of African small shops and market trading, pricing is not just about numbers. It is about understanding your costs, your customers, and your competition. Calculate your true costs. Know your break-even point. Add value. Adjust for inflation. Record everything. If you do these things, you will not just survive. You will thrive.

3. Stock Management and Avoiding Dead Stock

Stock is the lifeblood of any small shop or market stall. If you have the right goods at the right time, you make sales. If you have too much of the wrong goods, your money is tied up in items that do not move. This lesson teaches you practical stock management techniques for African markets, where supply chains can be unpredictable and cash flow is tight. The first rule of stock management is: know what you have. Many traders keep stock in their heads. They think they know how many tins of milk are left, but when a customer asks for three, they discover they only have two. This leads to lost sales and unhappy customers. You need a simple stock card system. Get a notebook or a set of index cards. For each product, write the name, the date you received it, the quantity received, the quantity sold, and the balance. Every time you sell, update the card. Every time you restock, update the card. At the end of the day, do a quick physical count of your top-selling items. Compare the count to your card. If they do not match, investigate. Maybe you forgot to record a sale. Maybe a customer took something without paying. Maybe you gave credit and did not write it down. The stock card is your early warning system. The second rule is: do not overstock. It is tempting to buy a large quantity when the wholesaler offers a discount. But if you cannot sell it quickly, your money is stuck. In many African markets, cash flow is more important than bulk discounts. If you spend all your cash on 100 bags of rice and it takes three months to sell them, you have no money to buy other fast-moving items like cooking oil, soap, or sugar. You also risk the rice getting wet, infested with weevils, or stolen. A better approach is to buy smaller quantities more frequently. Yes, you may pay a slightly higher unit price, but you keep your cash moving. You can also negotiate with wholesalers for credit or consignment. Some wholesalers will give you goods on credit if you have a good relationship. You pay after you sell. This is common in many African markets. Build trust with your suppliers by paying on time and being honest. The third rule is: identify dead stock quickly. Dead stock is inventory that has not sold in 30 days. It could be a new product that customers did not like, a seasonal item that is out of season, or a product that has been replaced by a cheaper competitor. Dead stock is money sitting on your shelf doing nothing. Every month, review your stock. Mark items that have not sold. Ask yourself: why is this not moving? Is the price too high? Is the packaging damaged? Is it the wrong season? Can I bundle it with a popular item? If you cannot find a way to sell it, discount it and clear it. Even selling at cost is better than holding it forever. You can also trade it with another trader for something that sells. For example, if you have too many umbrellas at the end of the rainy season, trade them with a trader who sells shoes for a few pairs of shoes that you can sell. Barter among traders is common in African markets and can solve dead stock problems. The fourth rule is: use the FIFO method. FIFO stands for First In, First Out. This means you sell the oldest stock first. If you have two bags of maize flour, one from last week and one from today, sell the older one first. This prevents spoilage and expiration. For perishable goods like vegetables, fruits, and dairy, FIFO is critical. Arrange your display so that older items are at the front. When you restock, put new items at the back. Train anyone who helps you in the shop to follow this rule. A common mistake is to put new stock in front because it looks better. That leads to old stock rotting at the back. The fifth rule is: protect your stock. In many African markets, theft, pests, and weather are real threats. If you have a permanent stall, invest in a lockable cabinet or a metal box for high-value items like phone credit, batteries, or cosmetics. If you have a temporary stall, pack up your stock every night and take it home. Do not leave it in the market. For food items, use airtight containers to keep out rats, cockroaches, and moisture. Raise your stock off the ground on pallets or bricks to protect from floods and dampness. If you sell clothes or fabrics, keep them in plastic bags or a closed trunk to prevent dust and insects. The sixth rule is: track your stock turnover. Stock turnover is how many times you sell and replace your stock in a given period. If you sell 100 bottles of soda per week and you keep 50 bottles in stock, your turnover is two times per week. High turnover is good because it means your money is working hard. Low turnover means your money is sleeping. Calculate turnover for your main products. If a product has low turnover, either reduce the quantity you buy or stop selling it. Use the cash to buy products with high turnover. The seventh rule is: plan for seasons and events. In Africa, demand changes with seasons, holidays, and events. During Ramadan, demand for dates, cooking oil, and sugar goes up. During Christmas, demand for rice, chicken, and new clothes increases. During the rainy season, demand for umbrellas, raincoats, and hot drinks rises. During school opening, demand for exercise books, pens, and uniforms spikes. Smart traders plan ahead. They buy extra stock two to three weeks before the season. They also reduce stock of items that do not sell during that season. Keep a seasonal calendar in your notebook. Mark the months and the products that sell best. Over time, you will build a reliable buying plan. The eighth rule is: involve your customers. Ask your customers what they want. If many people ask for a product you do not have, write it down. Try to source it. If you can be the only shop in your area that stocks a particular item, you will attract customers. But do not buy a large quantity until you test demand. Buy a small amount first. If it sells quickly, buy more. If it sits, do not buy it again. Finally, remember that stock management is about discipline. It is easy to buy what looks good or what a wholesaler pressures you to buy. It is harder to buy only what your customers will buy. But that discipline is what separates a struggling trader from a successful one. Count your stock. Record every sale. Review your dead stock. Use FIFO. Protect your goods. Plan for seasons. If you do these things, your stock will always be your friend, not your enemy.

4. Customer Care and Building Trust in African Markets

In African small shops and market stalls, customer care is not just about being polite. It is about building relationships that turn one-time buyers into loyal customers who come back every day and tell their friends about you. This lesson gives you practical ways to attract, serve, and keep customers in the African context. The first principle is: greet every customer. In many African cultures, greeting is not optional. It is a sign of respect. A customer who walks into your shop and is not greeted feels invisible. They may leave and never return. Even if you are busy with another customer, make eye contact and say, 'Welcome, I will be with you shortly.' If you are sitting, stand up. If you are eating, stop eating. Give the customer your full attention. A simple greeting in the local language goes a long way. In Kenya, 'Habari' or 'Sasa.' In Tanzania, 'Mambo.' In Nigeria, 'How you dey.' In Ghana, 'Ete sen.' In South Africa, 'Sawubona.' Use the greeting that fits your area. The second principle is: know your customers by name. This sounds simple, but many traders do not do it. When a customer buys from you, ask their name. Write it down if you need to. The next time they come, greet them by name. 'Good morning, Mama Ngozi. How is the family?' This personal touch makes customers feel valued. It also makes them less likely to go to a competitor. A trader in a busy Nairobi market started writing down the names of her regular customers and their usual purchases. Within three months, her sales increased by 30 percent because customers felt a personal connection. The third principle is: listen more than you talk. When a customer asks for something, listen carefully. Do not interrupt. If they are describing a problem, let them finish. Then repeat back what you heard to make sure you understand. 'So you need a soap that is gentle on your baby's skin. Is that right?' This shows that you care about their needs, not just about making a sale. It also helps you recommend the right product. If you do not have what they need, say so honestly. Do not try to sell them something else just to make a sale. They will remember your honesty. The fourth principle is: give correct change and fair prices. There is nothing that destroys trust faster than shortchanging a customer. Always count the change back to the customer. Say the amount out loud. 'Your item is 1,500 shillings. You gave me 2,000. Your change is 500 shillings.' This prevents mistakes and builds confidence. Also, do not charge different prices to different customers for the same item unless there is a clear reason, like a bulk discount. If a customer discovers that you charged them more than your neighbor, they will feel cheated. In many African markets, word travels fast. A reputation for fairness is one of your most valuable assets. The fifth principle is: handle complaints well. No business is perfect. Sometimes a customer will be unhappy. Maybe a product was defective. Maybe they were overcharged by mistake. Maybe they had to wait too long. How you handle the complaint matters more than the complaint itself. Listen without getting defensive. Apologize sincerely, even if you are not sure you were wrong. Then offer a solution. Replace the product. Refund the money. Give a small discount on their next purchase. The goal is to turn an unhappy customer into a loyal one. Studies from around the world show that customers who have a complaint resolved well become more loyal than customers who never had a problem. This is true in Africa too. A customer whose issue is handled with respect will tell others, 'Even when there was a problem, they treated me well.' The sixth principle is: offer credit wisely. In many African communities, credit is a normal part of trading. Customers buy now and pay later. This can build strong loyalty, but it can also destroy your business if not managed. Only give credit to customers you trust. Start with small amounts. If they pay on time, increase the limit slowly. If they do not pay, stop giving credit. Be clear about the payment date. Write it down. Remind them politely before the date. If they are late, follow up gently. Do not embarrass them in front of others. A private conversation is better. 'I know you are good for it. Can you pay by Friday?' This preserves their dignity and your relationship. The seventh principle is: keep your shop clean and organized. Customers judge your business by its appearance. If your stall is dirty, if products are covered in dust, if there is trash on the ground, customers will assume your goods are low quality. Clean your stall every morning. Arrange products neatly. Make sure labels are visible. If you sell food, keep it covered and protected from flies. If you sell clothes, fold them neatly. A clean shop attracts more customers and allows you to charge slightly higher prices. The eighth principle is: be consistent. Open your shop at the same time every day. Close at the same time. If customers know they can rely on you, they will plan their shopping around your schedule. If you are sometimes open and sometimes closed, they will go to someone who is always there. Consistency also applies to your prices and your quality. Do not sell good quality one week and poor quality the next. Customers notice. The ninth principle is: go the extra mile. Small gestures make a big difference. If a customer buys a heavy item, help them carry it to their car or to the bus stop. If a customer is short by a few shillings, let it go occasionally. If a customer is sick, ask about their health. If they have a baby, compliment the baby. These small acts of kindness cost you almost nothing but create strong emotional bonds. In many African cultures, relationships are more important than transactions. A customer who feels a personal connection to you will not leave you for a slightly cheaper price elsewhere. The tenth principle is: ask for feedback. At the end of a sale, ask, 'Is there anything else you need?' or 'How did you find the product?' This shows that you care about their experience. If they give you feedback, act on it. If they say your prices are too high, consider adjusting. If they say you never have a certain item, try to stock it. Customers appreciate being heard. Finally, remember that customer care is not a one-time event. It is a daily practice. It is the accumulation of small actions: a smile, a greeting, a fair price, a quick service, a sincere apology. In the competitive world of African small shops and market trading, you cannot always compete on price. But you can always compete on care. Build trust. Build relationships. Build a business that customers love.

5. Managing Risks and Growing Your Small Shop Business

Every small shop and market trader in Africa faces risks. Theft, fire, flooding, illness, price shocks, and sudden changes in the market can wipe out years of hard work. But with the right strategies, you can reduce these risks and grow your business steadily. This lesson covers practical risk management and growth strategies for African small shops and market stalls. Start with theft. Theft is one of the biggest risks. It can come from customers, from people passing by, or even from people you trust. To reduce theft, keep your cash in a secure place. Do not keep large amounts of cash in your stall. Use mobile money or a bank account to deposit excess cash daily. If you must keep cash, use a locked metal box and never leave it unattended. For high-value items like phone credit, batteries, or cosmetics, keep them behind the counter or in a locked cabinet. If you have employees, do not let one person handle both sales and cash alone. Rotate duties. Count the cash together at the end of the day. Install a simple lock on your stall. If you have a permanent shop, consider a metal door and a padlock. If you have a temporary stall, pack up everything every night. Do not leave goods overnight. The second risk is fire. Many markets in Africa have suffered devastating fires. To reduce fire risk, do not use candles or open flames near your stock. If you use a generator, keep it outside and away from flammable materials. Do not overload electrical sockets. Keep a bucket of sand or water near your stall. If you can afford it, buy a small fire extinguisher and learn how to use it. The third risk is flooding. In many African cities, markets flood during the rainy season. Raise your stock off the ground. Use pallets, bricks, or wooden shelves. Keep important items on the highest shelf. If you know a flood is coming, move your stock to higher ground or to a safe place. Do not wait until the water is inside your stall. The fourth risk is illness. If you are sick, you cannot run your business. Take care of your health. Eat well. Drink clean water. Get enough rest. If you can afford it, have a small health insurance or savings for medical emergencies. Also, train someone you trust to run the shop if you are away. This person should know your prices, your suppliers, and your record-keeping system. If you are hospitalized for a week, your business should not collapse. The fifth risk is price shocks. In many African countries, the prices of goods can rise suddenly due to inflation, fuel shortages, or currency devaluation. To protect yourself, do not keep all your money in cash. Keep some in mobile money, some in a bank, and some in stock. But do not overstock either. Find a balance. Also, build relationships with multiple suppliers. If one supplier raises prices too high, you can buy from another. Do not rely on a single wholesaler. The sixth risk is credit default. If you give credit, some customers will not pay. To reduce this risk, keep credit small and short-term. Do not give credit to strangers. Do not give credit for large amounts. If a customer defaults, stop giving them credit immediately. Do not let one bad customer destroy your business. Now, let us talk about growth. Many small shop owners want to grow but do not know how. Growth does not mean moving to a big shop overnight. It means increasing your sales and profit step by step. The first growth strategy is: increase your product range slowly. Add one or two new products at a time. Test them. If they sell well, keep them. If they do not, stop. Do not add ten new products at once. You will tie up your cash in dead stock. The second growth strategy is: increase your customer base. How do you get new customers? You can advertise. In African markets, word of mouth is powerful. Ask your happy customers to tell their friends. You can also use a small signboard with your shop name and a special offer. If you have a smartphone, use WhatsApp status or Facebook to post your products and prices. Many traders in Africa now use WhatsApp groups to take orders and announce new stock. This is free and effective. The third growth strategy is: increase your average sale. This means getting each customer to buy more. You can do this by bundling products. For example, sell a 'breakfast pack' with tea leaves, sugar, and milk. Or sell a 'baby pack' with soap, oil, and diapers. You can also place small, inexpensive items near the counter to encourage impulse buys. Sweets, biscuits, and airtime are good for this. The fourth growth strategy is: open a second location. Once your first shop is stable and profitable, you can open a second stall in a different market. But do not do this until you have a trusted person to run the first shop. You cannot be in two places at once. Also, make sure you have enough capital to stock both locations. The fifth growth strategy is: add services. If you sell phone credit, you can also offer phone charging. If you sell food, you can offer delivery for a small fee. If you sell clothes, you can offer basic tailoring or hemming. Services bring in extra income and attract customers who need multiple things. The sixth growth strategy is: save and reinvest. Many traders spend all their profit on personal needs. To grow, you must save a portion of your profit every day or every week. Even 10 percent is enough. Put this savings into a separate account. Use it to buy more stock, improve your stall, or take advantage of a bulk discount. Over time, this savings will become your growth capital. The seventh growth strategy is: learn continuously. Attend training sessions if they are available. Talk to other traders. Ask them what works for them. Read simple business books or listen to business programs on the radio. The more you learn, the better your decisions. Finally, remember that growth is not always linear. There will be good months and bad months. Do not give up during the bad months. Review your records. Cut unnecessary expenses. Focus on your best-selling products. Keep your customers happy. And always keep a small emergency fund for unexpected shocks. In the world of African small shops and market trading, risk is real, but it can be managed. Growth is possible, but it requires discipline. Protect your cash. Protect your stock. Protect your health. Build relationships. Save and reinvest. Learn and adapt. If you do these things, your small shop will not just survive. It will grow into a thriving business that supports you, your family, and your community.

<h3>1. Daily Cash Handling: The Three-Pile Method</h3>

<p>Many small shops in African markets fail not because they lack customers, but because cash leaks. Money comes in, money goes out for household needs, and by evening the trader cannot tell whether the day was profitable. The three-pile method solves this without a bank account.</p>

<p>Each evening, count all cash on hand. Divide it into three piles:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Pile 1 Float:</strong> Money for tomorrow's change and small restock. Usually 2030% of daily cash.</li>
<li><strong>Pile 2 Restock:</strong> Money reserved for the next market run or supplier payment. Usually 4050%.</li>
<li><strong>Pile 3 Draw:</strong> Household money and personal expenses. Usually 2030%.</li>
</ul>

<p>The percentages are a starting point, not a rule. The important discipline is that Pile 3 is counted, not grabbed. When a trader grabs from the till without counting, profit disappears. ILRI's work on small-scale livestock and retail traders in East Africa found that businesses which separated business and household cash were significantly more likely to survive beyond three years (ILRI, 2018).</p>

<p>Record each pile in a simple notebook or phone note. At the end of the week, add up Pile 3. If it is more than 30% of total cash, the business is being drained. Either sales must rise or household draw must fall.</p>

<h3>2. Stock Turn: The Number That Tells You What to Buy</h3>

<p>Stock turn (also called inventory turnover) measures how many times you sell through your average stock in a period. For a small shop, the formula is:</p>

<p><strong>Stock Turn = Cost of Goods Sold Average Stock Value</strong></p>

<p>If your cost of goods sold for the month is 400,000 KES and your average stock value is 100,000 KES, your stock turn is 4. That means you sell through your entire stock four times a month roughly once a week. This is healthy for fast-moving goods like sugar, soap, cooking oil and airtime.</p>

<p>Slow stock turn is the silent killer of small shops. A trader who buys 20 bags of rice because the price was good, then sells only 2 bags a week, has locked cash in stock for 10 weeks. That cash cannot buy the fast-moving items customers actually want. The table below shows typical stock turn benchmarks for common small-shop categories in African markets.</p>

<h3>3. Pricing for Profit in Competitive Markets</h3>

<p>In competitive African markets, price is often set by what the customer will pay, not by what the trader wants. But cost must be covered. The minimum viable price is:</p>

<p><strong>Minimum Price = (Cost of Item + Transport + Packaging + Any Loss) (1 Target Margin)</strong></p>

<p>If a trader buys a crate of soda for 10,000 KES, pays 500 KES transport, and expects 2% breakage loss, the true cost is 10,700 KES. If the target margin is 20%, the minimum selling price is 10,700 0.8 = 13,375 KES. Selling at 13,000 KES looks like a profit but is actually below target after waste.</p>

<h3>4. The Weekly Record Routine</h3>

<p>A record system that takes more than 15 minutes a day will not survive. The routine below is designed for a trader with a notebook and a basic phone.</p>

<p><strong>Daily (5 minutes):</strong> Write total sales, total cash out for restock, and household draw. Count cash on hand. Note any stock that finished.</p>

<p><strong>Weekly (15 minutes):</strong> Add daily sales. Count remaining stock value at cost. Calculate stock turn for top 5 items. Check Pile 3 draw as percentage of sales.</p>

<p><strong>Monthly (30 minutes):</strong> Calculate gross profit (sales minus cost of goods sold). Compare to previous month. Identify the top 3 and bottom 3 items by profit. Decide what to stop buying and what to buy more of.</p>

<p>MSD Animal Health and university extension services across Africa have published similar simple record templates for small-scale agrovets and livestock input traders. The principle is the same: if it is not written down, it did not happen.</p>

<p style="margin: 0; font-weight: bold; color: #b91c1c; font-size: 16px;"> SAFETY WARNING FOOD, CHEMICALS AND CASH HANDLING</p>
<p style="margin: 8px 0 0 0; color: #7f1d1d;">If your shop sells food, agricultural chemicals, veterinary drugs or any product that enters the body or touches skin, you must follow national safety rules. Never store pesticides, veterinary medicines or chemicals next to food, drinks or cooking oil. Never repackage chemicals into food or drink containers. Wash hands after handling cash, stock or chemicals. Keep a first-aid kit and know the nearest health facility. For food: check expiry dates weekly, keep raw and cooked items separate, and discard any stock that shows spoilage, mould or pest damage. Selling spoiled food or mislabelled chemicals can kill customers, close your business and lead to prosecution. WHO and national food safety authorities publish free guidance use it.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Count cash every evening</strong> and split into Float, Restock and Draw piles before sleeping.</li>
<li><strong>Write daily sales and expenses</strong> in a notebook or phone note no exceptions, even on busy days.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate stock turn weekly</strong> for your top 5 products. If days on hand is double the benchmark, stop buying.</li>
<li><strong>Use the true cost formula</strong> (cost + transport + loss) before setting any selling price.</li>
<li><strong>Keep household draw below 30%</strong> of daily cash. If it rises, fix it within the week.</li>
<li><strong>Check expiry dates and spoilage weekly</strong> for all food, drink and chemical stock.</li>
<li><strong>Separate chemicals from food</strong> at all times different shelf, different area, clearly labelled.</li>
<li><strong>Review monthly profit by product</strong> and cut the bottom 3 items that lock cash without profit.</li>
</ul>
