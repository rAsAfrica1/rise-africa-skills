1. Milling sorghum and millet into flour using local mills

Milling turns your sorghum and millet grain into flour that sells for three to four times the price of raw grain. A 50 kg bag of sorghum might fetch $20, but milled into fine flour it can bring $60 or more. The first step is cleaning: winnow to remove chaff, then pick out stones and dirt. You can use a simple vibrating sieve or just a woven tray. Next, you need to decide between whole grain flour and decorticated flour. Decorticated (pearled) flour is lighter and preferred for many dishes, but you lose about 15-20% of the grain as bran. To decorticate, you can use a hand-pounder or a small dehuller machine. Many local mills offer dehulling as a service.

For milling itself, you have three main options: the traditional grinding stone (slow but free), a diesel-powered hammer mill (fast, costs $0.05-$0.10 per kg), or an electric plate mill. A hammer mill with a 10-15 horsepower engine costs around $1,500-$3,000 new, but you can find used ones for $800. The mill should be set to produce fine flour for tuwo or ugali, or coarse meal for porridge. Always mill in small batches and sift the flour through a fine mesh (like a 250-micron sieve) to remove any remaining grit. Store the flour in clean, dry sacks or airtight containers. Whole grain flour keeps for 2-3 months if kept cool and dry; decorticated flour lasts longer, up to 6 months. Never mill wet grain â it will clog the mill and spoil the flour. If you mill for others, charge by the kilogram or by the basin. A common rate is $0.10 per kg for milling service. This can be a steady income between harvests.

2. Making traditional foods: tuwo, ugali, fura, and dambu

These four foods are staples across Africa and can be made at home or for sale. Tuwo is a smooth, stiff porridge from northern Nigeria, made from sorghum or millet flour. To make tuwo: boil water, then slowly add flour while stirring with a wooden paddle (called a 'muciya'). Use about 1 part flour to 3 parts water. Keep stirring over low heat for 10-15 minutes until the mixture pulls away from the pot. Turn it out and shape into balls. Serve with soup. A large pot of tuwo can feed 10 people and costs about $1.50 to make, selling for $5.

Ugali is the East African version, often from millet or maize. It is firmer. Boil water, add flour gradually, and stir until thick. For a family of six, use 1 kg flour and 3 litres water. Cook for 5-8 minutes. Ugali is usually served with sukuma wiki or meat stew.

Fura is a millet-based dough ball from northern Nigeria and Niger. It is made from millet flour, sometimes mixed with sorghum. You knead the flour with water and a little ginger and pepper, then shape into balls and boil them. After boiling, you can mash them with yogurt or milk to make 'fura da nono', a popular drink. A batch of 20 fura balls costs about $2 to make and sells for $8.

Dambu is a steamed millet or sorghum dish, common in northern Nigeria. You mix flour with water to form a crumbly dough, then steam it for 30-40 minutes until cooked. It is often mixed with oil, pepper, and vegetables. Dambu is cheap to make â about $1 per kg â and sells for $3 per kg. All these foods require clean hands, clean water, and proper cooking to avoid spoilage. If you sell them, wrap them in clean leaves or plastic and sell fresh. They can also be dried and packaged for longer shelf life.

3. Producing malt for local brewing and beverages

Malt is germinated, dried grain. Sorghum and millet malt are used for brewing traditional beers like burukutu, pito, and dolo, and for making malted drinks like 'maltina' or local porridge. To make malt, start with good quality grain. Clean it and soak in water for 12-24 hours. Then drain and spread the grain in a thin layer (5-10 cm) on a clean floor or in a tray. Keep it moist and warm (25-30Â°C). Stir every few hours. After 2-4 days, the grain will sprout. You want the sprout to be about 1-2 cm long â not too short, not too long. At this point, stop germination by drying the grain. You can sun-dry for 2-3 days, or use a solar dryer. The dried malt should be crisp and easy to crush.

For brewing, the malt is ground and mixed with water. The enzymes in the malt convert starch to sugar. For 20 litres of traditional beer, you need about 2 kg of malt. The process: mix malt with warm water (60-70Â°C), let it rest for 1-2 hours, then boil with hops or other bittering agents, cool, and ferment with yeast for 2-3 days. For non-alcoholic malt drinks, you can roast the malt lightly for flavour, then grind and steep in hot water, strain, and sweeten.

Malt production is a good business. A 50 kg bag of grain costs $20. After malting, you get about 40 kg of malt (20% loss). Malt sells for $1.50-$2 per kg, so 40 kg brings $60-$80. You can also sell malt to local brewers. The key is consistent quality: uniform germination, proper drying, and clean storage. Store malt in dry sacks away from moisture and pests. It keeps for 3-6 months.

4. Packaging flour and grains for retail sale

Packaging is what turns a raw commodity into a branded product that can sit on a shelf. For flour, you need airtight, moisture-proof bags. Common options: clear plastic bags (0.5 kg, 1 kg, 2 kg) sealed with a heat sealer, or woven polypropylene bags (5 kg, 10 kg, 25 kg) with an inner liner. A heat sealer costs $30-$50. For small retail, 1 kg and 2 kg bags are best. You can print labels with your brand name, weight, production date, and expiry date. A simple label can be printed at a local print shop for $0.05 per label.

For grains, you can sell whole grain in 1 kg, 5 kg, or 50 kg bags. Clean the grain well â remove stones, broken kernels, and dust. You can also polish the grain to make it look more attractive. Packaging grains in clear bags lets customers see the quality. Always include a small sachet of desiccant (like silica gel) in larger bags to prevent moisture. For long storage, use vacuum sealing if available.

Hygiene is critical. Work on a clean table. Wear gloves and a hairnet. Weigh accurately â customers trust consistent weights. A simple digital scale (5 kg capacity) costs $20-$30. Seal bags immediately after filling. Store packaged products in a cool, dry place away from sunlight. If you sell in local markets, you can also offer bulk discounts. A 1 kg bag of sorghum flour might sell for $1.50, while a 5 kg bag sells for $6.50. The packaging cost per kg is about $0.10-$0.20. Good packaging increases shelf life and makes your product look professional. Many buyers, especially in cities, will pay more for well-packaged flour.

5. Starting a small-scale milling business: costs and profits

A small-scale milling business can be started with as little as $2,000. The main equipment is a hammer mill or plate mill. A good used diesel hammer mill with a 10-15 HP engine costs $800-$1,500. A new one costs $2,000-$3,000. You also need a sieve, weighing scale, sealing machine, and initial stock of grain. If you have a building, you can use it. If not, rent a small shop for $50-$100 per month. You will need a milling operator â you can do it yourself or hire one for $100-$150 per month. Fuel or electricity costs: diesel is about $1.20 per litre, and milling 100 kg of grain uses about 2-3 litres. So fuel cost per kg is roughly $0.03. Maintenance and spare parts: set aside $50 per month.

Letâs calculate profit. Assume you mill 500 kg per day, 25 days a month = 12,500 kg. If you charge $0.10 per kg for milling service, thatâs $1,250 revenue. But if you buy grain, mill it, and sell flour, your margins are higher. Buy 12,500 kg of sorghum at $0.40 per kg = $5,000. Milling cost (fuel, labour, maintenance) = $0.05 per kg = $625. Packaging = $0.15 per kg = $1,875. Total cost = $7,500. Sell flour at $1.20 per kg = $15,000. Gross profit = $7,500 per month. Thatâs before rent and other overheads. Even at half that volume, you can make $2,000-$3,000 profit monthly.

Start small: mill for others first to learn the market. Then expand into your own branded flour. Keep records of all costs and sales. Reinvest profits into better equipment. Many successful millers start with one machine and grow to three or four within two years. Location matters â near a market or busy road. Also consider offering dehulling and cleaning services for an extra fee. With good management, a milling business can be very profitable.

6. Quality control and food safety for processed products

Quality control means making sure your flour, malt, or packaged grain is safe, clean, and consistent. Start with the raw grain. Never use mouldy, insect-damaged, or wet grain. Aflatoxin is a serious danger in groundnuts and sometimes in grains. To avoid it, dry grain properly to below 12% moisture. You can test moisture with a simple moisture meter ($100-$200) or by the salt-and-bottle method: put grain in a bottle with dry salt, shake, and if salt sticks, grain is too wet. Clean grain by winnowing and sieving. Remove all stones, dust, and broken kernels.

During processing, hygiene is key. Wash your hands, wear clean clothes, and keep the mill clean. After milling, sift the flour to remove any grit. Store flour in clean, dry containers. For packaging, use food-grade bags. Never use bags that held chemicals or cement. Label with production and expiry dates. For flour, shelf life is 2-3 months for whole grain, 6 months for decorticated. For malt, 3-6 months. Keep products away from sunlight, moisture, and pests. Use airtight containers and consider adding a desiccant.

Food safety also means avoiding contamination. Keep animals out of the processing area. Use clean water if you wash equipment. If you produce traditional foods like fura or dambu, cook them thoroughly and sell fresh. For longer shelf life, dry them properly and package. Regularly clean your mill: after each use, brush out leftover flour. Check for insects. If you find weevils, discard the affected batch and clean the mill with hot water and vinegar. Train your workers on hygiene. A simple quality control checklist can help: check grain moisture, remove foreign matter, clean equipment, seal packages, and label correctly. Following these steps will build customer trust and reduce waste. Good quality means repeat customers and better prices.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Adding Value to Sorghum and Millet

Across the Sahel, East Africa, and Southern Africa, sorghum and millet are staple cereals with a quiet but powerful commercial advantage: they process well at very small scale. A single operator with a hammer mill, a clean drying floor, and reliable packaging can turn a 50 kg bag of grain worth roughly US$20â30 into retail flour worth US$60â120, depending on market positioning. This lesson gives you the practical numbers, process steps, and safety rules to run that business profitably.

Value addition is not just about grinding grain. It is about selling a consistent, safe, shelf-stable product that customers trust enough to buy repeatedly. The four main product routes are: (1) milled flour for retail and institutional buyers, (2) traditional foods for local markets and events, (3) malt for brewing and beverages, and (4) cleaned/packaged whole grain for premium and export-adjacent markets. Each has different equipment, margins, and regulatory requirements.

Lesson 1: Milling Sorghum and Millet into Flour

Milling is the core value-addition step. Sorghum and millet have harder endosperm than maize in some varieties, so you need a mill that can handle the load without overheating the flour. The most common small-scale setup in Africa is a hammer mill (5â20 HP diesel or electric) or a plate mill (disc mill). Hammer mills produce finer, more uniform flour and are better for retail; plate mills are cheaper but require more passes and produce more heat.

Key practical points: clean grain before milling (destoning removes grit that damages plates and teeth); condition grain to 14â16% moisture for easier separation of bran; and sift after milling to grade flour into fine (retail), medium (institutional), and bran (animal feed). Bran is a sellable by-product, not waste.

Lesson 2: Making Traditional Foods â Tuwo, Ugali, Fura, and Dambu

These foods are the fastest route to cash flow because they require minimal equipment and sell daily. Tuwo (Northern Nigeria) is a stiff porridge from sorghum or millet flour, usually sold with soups. Ugali (East Africa) is a stiff maize or millet porridge; millet ugali commands a premium in health-conscious urban markets. Fura (Northern Nigeria/Niger) is a fermented millet dough ball, rolled and sold with nono (fermented milk). Dambu is a steamed, crumbly millet or sorghum dish, often sold as a snack or side.

For commercial production, the critical controls are fermentation time and temperature, cooking consistency, and packaging for same-day sale. Fura and dambu have short shelf lives (24â48 hours without refrigeration), so sell through fixed outlets or pre-orders rather than long distribution chains.

Lesson 3: Producing Malt for Local Brewing and Beverages

Sorghum and millet malt is used for traditional beers (e.g., burukutu, dolo, pito), commercial clear beers, and non-alcoholic malt drinks. The process: steep grain in water (24â48 hours), germinate in beds or drums (3â5 days), kiln/dry (50â60Â°C for light malt, higher for dark), then rootlet removal and storage. Malt commands 2â4Ã the price of raw grain, but requires strict moisture and temperature control to avoid mould and aflatoxin.

Lesson 4: Packaging Flour and Grains for Retail Sale

Packaging is where margin is won or lost. Retail buyers pay for convenience, trust, and shelf life. Use food-grade laminated or high-density polyethylene (HDPE) bags with a moisture barrier. Common sizes: 500 g, 1 kg, 2 kg, 5 kg, 10 kg, 25 kg. Label with product name, net weight, production date, batch code, best-before date, and contact details. In many countries, fortified flour labelling and standards apply â check with your national standards body (e.g., KEBS, SON, NAFDAC, TBS).

Lesson 6: Quality Control and Food Safety

Sorghum and millet are vulnerable to aflatoxin, especially in humid storage. Aflatoxin is a carcinogen and a trade barrier. Controls: dry grain to <13% moisture before storage; use hermetic bags or clean, dry, raised pallets; test with rapid kits (e.g., GIPSA or local lab); reject mouldy grain; never blend mouldy grain into food products. Also control foreign matter (stones, metal, glass), insects, and microbial contamination through cleaning, hygiene, and proper packaging.

â  SAFETY WARNING â AFLATOXIN AND MILL DUST

Aflatoxin-contaminated grain cannot be made safe by milling, cooking, or fermentation. If grain smells musty, shows green-yellow mould, or tests above your national limit (commonly 10â20 ppb for food), do not process it for human consumption. Divert only to approved industrial uses or destroy it. Separately, hammer mill dust is explosive and harmful to lungs: install dust extraction, wear N95 masks, and keep ignition sources away from milling areas.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Test grain moisture before buying â reject above 13% for storage.
  • Clean and destone grain before milling to protect equipment and quality.
  • Calibrate scales and sifters weekly; record extraction rates.
  • Label every package with batch code, production date, and best-before date.
  • Test for aflatoxin at intake and before release; keep records for 12 months.
  • Train staff on hand hygiene, pest control, and safe chemical handling.
  • Track cost per kg of flour monthly; review pricing against input costs.
  • Secure at least two repeat buyers (shops, schools, institutions) before scaling.
