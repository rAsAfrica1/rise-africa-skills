Lessons

1. Calculating the cost of one cassava bread loaf

To know if you are making a profit, you must first know exactly how much it costs to make one loaf of cassava bread. This is called the cost per loaf. You need to add up all the ingredients and other expenses that go into making a batch, then divide by the number of loaves you produce.

Start by listing every ingredient you use for one batch. For example, to make 20 loaves, you might use: 10 kg cassava flour (at 500 naira per kg = 5,000 naira), 1 kg sugar (600 naira), 250 g yeast (800 naira), 200 g salt (100 naira), 1 litre oil (1,200 naira), and 5 litres water (usually free or 50 naira). Add these: 5,000 + 600 + 800 + 100 + 1,200 + 50 = 7,750 naira for ingredients.

Next, add other costs that change with production: firewood or gas (1,500 naira), packaging bags (20 bags at 50 naira = 1,000 naira), and transport to buy ingredients (500 naira). Total variable costs = 7,750 + 1,500 + 1,000 + 500 = 10,750 naira.

Now add fixed costs that you pay regardless of how much you bake, like rent (2,000 naira per day), your own labour (say 3,000 naira per day), and equipment depreciation (500 naira per day). Total fixed costs = 5,500 naira. So total cost for the batch = 10,750 + 5,500 = 16,250 naira. Divide by 20 loaves: 16,250 20 = 812.50 naira per loaf. That is your cost per loaf.

If you sell each loaf for 1,000 naira, you make 187.50 naira profit per loaf. But if you sell at 800 naira, you lose 12.50 naira per loaf. Always calculate your cost per loaf before setting your price. Keep a notebook and record every expense, even small ones. As your business grows, you can recalculate monthly because prices of ingredients change.

2. Setting a profitable price for your market

Once you know your cost per loaf, you can set a price that covers costs and gives you a profit. But your price must also be one that customers in your area are willing to pay. Start by checking what other bakers charge for similar bread. If most sell at 900 naira, you cannot charge 1,500 naira unless your bread is much bigger or better.

Use this simple formula: Price = Cost per loaf + Desired profit. If your cost is 812.50 naira and you want 200 naira profit, price at 1,012.50 naira. Round to 1,000 naira for easy change. But if the market price is 900 naira, you need to either reduce your costs or accept lower profit. For example, you could buy cassava flour in bulk to get a discount, or use less packaging.

Consider your customers. Are they school children with small budgets? Then make smaller loaves at 500 naira. Are they office workers? They might pay 1,200 naira for a large, well-packaged loaf. You can also offer different sizes: small, medium, large. This way you serve more people and increase total sales.

Do not forget to factor in spoilage. If you bake 20 loaves but only sell 18, the cost of the 2 unsold loaves must be covered by the 18 sold. So your effective cost per sold loaf is higher. To avoid this, start with a small batch and increase as you learn demand. Also, test your price: sell at one price for a week, then adjust. Keep records of how many you sell at each price. The right price is the one that sells all your bread and leaves you a profit. Remember, pricing is not permanent; review it every month as costs change.

3. Understanding profit margins and break-even

Profit margin is the percentage of your selling price that is profit. It tells you how healthy your business is. To calculate: Profit margin = (Profit Selling price) 100. If you sell a loaf for 1,000 naira and your profit is 187.50 naira, margin = (187.50 1,000) 100 = 18.75%. That means for every 100 naira a customer pays, you keep 18.75 naira. A margin below 10% is risky; aim for 20-30% if possible.

Break-even is the number of loaves you must sell to cover all your costs, making neither profit nor loss. Use this formula: Break-even loaves = Total fixed costs (Selling price per loaf Variable cost per loaf). From our earlier example: fixed costs = 5,500 naira, selling price = 1,000 naira, variable cost per loaf = 10,750 20 = 537.50 naira. So contribution per loaf = 1,000 537.50 = 462.50 naira. Break-even = 5,500 462.50 = 11.9, so you must sell at least 12 loaves to break even. If you sell 20, you make profit on the extra 8 loaves.

Knowing your break-even helps you set daily targets. If you must sell 12 loaves just to cover costs, you know selling only 10 means a loss. You can then decide to reduce fixed costs (e.g., bake from home instead of renting a shop) or increase sales. Also, track your profit margin over time. If it drops, find out why: are ingredient prices up? Are you wasting more? Adjust quickly.

Keep a simple record: for each day, write total sales, total costs, and profit. At the end of the week, calculate your average margin. If it is low, try raising prices slightly or cutting waste. Remember, profit is not what you put in your pocket immediately; set aside a portion for savings and reinvestment.

4. Record keeping for daily sales and expenses

Good records are the difference between a business that grows and one that fails. You do not need a computer; a simple exercise book and a pen work perfectly. At the start of each day, write the date at the top of a new page. Make two columns: one for money coming in (sales) and one for money going out (expenses).

For sales, record every sale as it happens. If you sell 5 loaves at 1,000 naira each, write '5 loaves x 1,000 = 5,000'. If you sell on credit, write the customer's name and amount owed. At the end of the day, add up all sales. For expenses, write down everything you spend: cassava flour, sugar, firewood, transport, even a bottle of water for yourself. Include small amounts like 100 naira for a matchbox. These add up.

At the end of each day, calculate your daily profit: Total sales Total expenses = Profit. If profit is negative, you made a loss. Write that down too; it helps you see problems early. Also, keep separate records for large purchases like an oven or a bicycle for delivery. These are capital expenses, not daily costs.

Every week, review your records. Add up total sales, total expenses, and total profit for the week. Compare with previous weeks. Are sales going up? Are expenses rising faster than sales? If so, find out why. Maybe you are buying flour in small quantities at a higher price. Adjust.

At the end of the month, calculate your overall profit. Then decide how much to save, how much to reinvest, and how much to take as your own pay. Keep your records in a safe, dry place. They are proof of your business for loans or grants. Even if you cannot write well, use symbols or ask a family member to help. The key is consistency: record every day, no matter how small the sale.

5. Reducing waste in cassava processing

Waste eats your profit. In cassava bread baking, waste can happen at every step: peeling, grating, pressing, baking, and selling. By reducing waste, you lower your cost per loaf and increase profit without raising prices. Start by buying good quality cassava roots. Avoid roots that are soft, bruised, or already spoiling. If you process your own cassava, peel carefully to remove only the outer skin, not the white flesh. A good peeler can save up to 10% of the root.

During grating and pressing, ensure your equipment is clean and working well. If the press is not tight, you lose starch in the water. Collect that water and let the starch settle; you can use it in other products or sell it. When baking, measure ingredients accurately. Too much flour or water can ruin a batch. Use a scale if possible, or consistent cups. Do not overbake; burnt loaves cannot be sold. Set a timer and check the oven.

Packaging waste is also costly. If you use plastic bags, buy in bulk to reduce cost per bag. Reuse clean bags or use paper wraps if cheaper. Train your workers to handle bread gently to avoid breaking. Store unsold bread properly to keep it fresh for the next day; you can sell day-old bread at a discount instead of throwing it away.

Track your waste. For one week, weigh or count what you throw away: peels, spoiled dough, burnt loaves, unsold bread. Calculate the cost of that waste. For example, if you throw away 2 loaves per day at 812.50 naira each, that is 1,625 naira lost daily. Over a month, that is nearly 50,000 naira. Imagine what you could do with that money. Set a target to reduce waste by half in one month. Simple changes like better storage, staff training, and accurate measurement can make a big difference. Less waste means more profit for you.

6. Saving for equipment and business growth

To grow your cassava bread business, you need better equipment: a larger oven, a mechanised grater, a pressing machine, or a delivery bicycle. These cost money, but you can save for them gradually from your daily profits. The key is to pay yourself a small salary and set aside a fixed amount as savings every day or week. Even 200 naira per day adds up to 6,000 naira in a month, 72,000 naira in a year.

Open a separate savings account or use a safe box at home. If you use a bank or mobile money, choose one with low fees. Set a goal: for example, save 100,000 naira for a new oven. Calculate how long it will take: if you save 500 naira per day, you reach 100,000 naira in 200 days (about 7 months). Write this goal where you can see it daily.

Do not wait until you have all the money to grow. You can start with small improvements: buy a second baking tray, or a better scale. These increase your capacity little by little. Reinvest a portion of your profit each month. For example, if you make 30,000 naira profit in a month, save 10,000 naira for equipment, 10,000 naira for ingredients, and take 10,000 naira as your pay.

Consider joining a savings group (like a rotating savings club, called 'esusu' in Nigeria or 'chama' in Kenya). Members contribute regularly and take turns receiving the lump sum. This can help you buy equipment faster without a bank loan. Also, keep records of your savings and equipment purchases. When you buy a new machine, note how it reduces your cost per loaf or increases your daily output. That is growth.

Finally, be patient. Growing a business takes time. Avoid taking loans with high interest unless you are sure the equipment will pay for itself quickly. With consistent saving and smart reinvestment, you will soon have a bigger, more profitable bakery.

<h2>Practical Reference: Costing, Pricing, and Profit in Cassava Bread Baking</h2>

<p>This practical reference gives you the numbers, formulas, and records you need to run a cassava bread bakery as a business, not just a kitchen. Cassava bread (often called <em>gluten-free cassava loaf</em> or <em>cassava flour bread</em>) is gaining demand across Africa because it is locally sourced, gluten-free, and cheaper than wheat-based bread when cassava flour is processed well. But demand alone does not make profit. You must know your cost per loaf, set a price that covers all costs and leaves a margin, track daily sales and expenses, and reduce waste. This module walks you through each step with Africa-focused examples and templates.</p>

<h3>Lesson 1: Calculating the Cost of One Cassava Bread Loaf</h3>
<p>Your cost per loaf has two parts: <strong>variable costs</strong> (ingredients, packaging, fuel per loaf) and <strong>fixed costs</strong> (rent, equipment depreciation, salaries) spread across all loaves. The formula is:</p>
<p><strong>Cost per loaf = Variable cost per loaf + (Total fixed costs Number of loaves produced)</strong></p>
<p>Example: You produce 200 loaves per day. Fixed costs per day = 20,000 NGN (rent, labour, depreciation). Fixed cost per loaf = 20,000 200 = 100 NGN. Variable cost per loaf = 280 NGN. Total cost per loaf = 380 NGN.</p>

<h3>Lesson 2: Setting a Profitable Price for Your Market</h3>
<p>Price must cover total cost plus profit, but it must also be competitive. Use <strong>cost-plus pricing</strong> as your floor, then check the market. If your total cost is 380 NGN and you want a 30% margin, price = 380 1.30 = 494 NGN. Round to 500 NGN. If competitors sell at 450 NGN, you must either reduce costs or differentiate (better taste, larger loaf, gluten-free certification, branded packaging).</p>

<h3>Lesson 3: Understanding Profit Margins and Break-Even</h3>
<p><strong>Gross profit margin = (Selling price Variable cost) Selling price 100.</strong> Using 500 NGN price and 280 NGN variable cost: margin = (500280)/500 100 = 44%. <strong>Net profit margin = (Selling price Total cost) Selling price 100</strong> = (500380)/500 100 = 24%.</p>
<p><strong>Break-even point (loaves) = Total fixed costs (Selling price Variable cost per loaf).</strong> If fixed costs = 20,000 NGN/day and contribution per loaf = 500280 = 220 NGN, break-even = 20,000 220 = 91 loaves per day. You must sell at least 91 loaves to cover all costs; every loaf after that adds 220 NGN to profit.</p>

<h3>Lesson 4: Record Keeping for Daily Sales and Expenses</h3>
<p>You cannot manage what you do not measure. Keep a simple daily log with these columns: date, loaves baked, loaves sold, unsold, selling price, cash received, ingredient expenses, fuel, labour, packaging, other expenses, and daily profit. Use a notebook or a free spreadsheet app on your phone. At the end of each week, total your sales and expenses. At month-end, calculate your actual cost per loaf and compare it to your estimate. Adjust prices or reduce costs if margins fall below 20%.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Cassava Processing:</strong> Raw cassava contains cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin and lotaustralin) that can cause cyanide poisoning if not properly processed. Always use <strong>well-processed, detoxified cassava flour</strong> from a reputable supplier. If you process your own cassava, follow the full procedure: peeling, soaking, fermenting, pressing, drying, and milling. Never use raw or under-processed cassava flour in bread. Symptoms of cyanide poisoning include dizziness, headache, nausea, and difficulty breathing. Seek immediate medical help if these occur. Store cassava flour in a cool, dry place away from moisture and pests.
</div>

<h3>Lesson 5: Reducing Waste in Cassava Processing</h3>
<p>Waste is lost profit. Common waste points in cassava bread baking include: over-mixing, over-baking, unsold loaves, spoiled flour, and poor packaging. Practical steps: (1) Bake in batches based on your daily sales record, not guesswork. (2) Use a digital scale for consistent loaf sizes. (3) Store cassava flour in airtight containers away from moisture. (4) Turn unsold loaves into breadcrumbs or croutons for resale. (5) Train staff on proper measuring and oven timing. (6) Track waste daily in your record book. A 5% reduction in waste can increase net profit by 1015%.</p>

<h3>Lesson 6: Saving for Equipment and Business Growth</h3>
<p>Set aside a fixed percentage of daily profit at least 10% into a separate savings account or mobile money wallet. Use this fund for: a larger oven, a dough mixer, a delivery bicycle, better packaging, or a second sales point. In many African countries, you can join a <strong>Village Savings and Loan Association (VSLA)</strong> or <strong>SACCO</strong> to access low-interest loans. The African Development Bank and FAO support cassava value chain financing through programmes like <em>Feed Africa</em> and <em>Agri-PPP</em>. Keep your records clean they are your collateral when applying for credit.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Calculate cost per loaf weekly using actual ingredient, fuel, and labour costs.</li>
<li> Set price at total cost + 2535% margin; check competitor prices monthly.</li>
<li> Know your break-even number of loaves per day and track it.</li>
<li> Record every sale and expense daily no exceptions.</li>
<li> Weigh ingredients and loaves to control portion and waste.</li>
<li> Store cassava flour in airtight, dry containers; use FIFO (first in, first out).</li>
<li> Save at least 10% of daily profit for equipment and growth.</li>
<li> Review monthly profit margin; adjust price or costs if below 20%.</li>
</ul>
