1. Understanding Goat Digestive System

Goats are ruminants, meaning they have a four-chambered stomach: rumen, reticulum, omasum, and abomasum. The rumen is the largest chamber and acts as a fermentation vat where billions of microbes break down fibrous plant material. This allows goats to digest roughages like grass, hay, and leaves that humans cannot eat. The reticulum helps sort feed and trap foreign objects. The omasum absorbs water and minerals, and the abomasum is the true stomach where acid and enzymes digest protein.

A healthy rumen has a pH between 6.0 and 7.0. When goats eat too much grain, the pH drops, causing acidosis. Symptoms include off-feed, diarrhea, and lethargy. To keep the rumen healthy, provide plenty of long-stem roughage and introduce grains gradually. Goats also chew cud, regurgitating feed to chew again and mix with saliva, which buffers rumen acid.

In Africa, where browse (shrubs and trees) is often abundant, goats thrive because they are browsers, not grazers. They prefer to eat leaves, twigs, and shrubs at knee to shoulder height. This makes them ideal for mixed farming systems. However, sudden diet changes can disrupt the rumen microbes. Always transition over 710 days.

Practical tip: Observe your goats for 30 minutes after feeding. Healthy goats chew cud while resting. If you see bloating on the left side, it may be frothy bloat from lush legumes. Drench with 20 ml vegetable oil and walk the goat. To check rumen motility, place your fist on the left flank and feel for contractionsshould be 12 per minute. If none, call a vet.

Water is critical: a goat drinks 48 liters per day. Without water, rumen microbes stop working. Provide clean water at all times. Also, provide a salt lick or mineral block to supply sodium and other minerals. Remember, the digestive system is the engine of the goat; keep it running smoothly with consistent, high-fiber feed.

2. Hay and Forage Selection

Hay is dried forage, and it forms the backbone of goat diets, especially in dry seasons. Good hay is greenish, leafy, and smells sweet. Bad hay is brown, dusty, or moldynever feed moldy hay because it causes respiratory problems and abortions. In Africa, common hays include Rhodes grass, Guinea grass, and legume hays like lucerne (alfalfa) or desmodium. Legume hays are higher in protein (1520%) than grass hays (610%).

When selecting hay, check for seed heads. Early-cut hay (before seed heads form) is more nutritious. Late-cut hay is stemmy and low in protein. For goats, chop long hay to 510 cm to reduce waste. Store hay in a dry, raised shed on pallets to prevent moisture. A bale of hay weighs 1525 kg and can feed one goat for 1015 days if supplemented.

Forage can also be fresh. Goats love browse: leaves from acacia, moringa, leucaena, and gliricidia. These are high in protein (1825%). Plant fodder trees along boundaries. Cut and carry fresh leaves daily. But avoid toxic plants like lantana, oleander, and nightshade. If you are unsure, let a goat nibble a small amount and observe for 24 hours.

In dry seasons, you can make silage from maize stalks or sorghum. Chop, pack into a pit or plastic drum, and seal tightly. Silage ferments and lasts for months. It is moist and acidic, so introduce gradually. A 200-liter drum holds about 100 kg of silage, enough for 5 goats for 2 weeks.

Practical feeding plan: Give goats free-choice hay or browse. A 40-kg goat eats 11.5 kg of dry matter daily. If hay is low quality, supplement with 200300 g of concentrate. Always provide hay in a rack to keep it off the ground and reduce parasite eggs. Rotate pastures to prevent worm buildup. Remember, forage quality determines how much grain you need to buyinvest in good hay and fodder trees to cut costs.

3. Grain and Concentrate Feeding

Grains and concentrates are energy and protein supplements. Common grains in Africa include maize, sorghum, millet, and oats. These are high in starch (6070%) and provide quick energy. However, too much grain causes acidosis. The rule: never feed more than 1% of body weight as grain per day. For a 40-kg goat, that is 400 g maximum, split into two meals.

Concentrates also include oilseed cakes like cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, and groundnut cake. These are 3040% protein and are excellent for growing kids and lactating does. Mix your own ration: 50% maize, 30% cottonseed cake, 15% wheat bran, 4% mineral premix, 1% salt. This gives about 16% protein. Feed 200500 g per day depending on the goat's needs.

Introduce grains slowly over 710 days. Start with 50 g per day and increase by 50 g every two days. Always feed roughage first, then grain. This stimulates rumen microbes and reduces acidosis risk. If goats show diarrhea or go off-feed, reduce grain and give hay.

Processing grains improves digestion. Crush or grind maize and sorghum coarsely. Whole grains may pass undigested. Soaking grains for 12 hours also softens them. For small herds, a simple hand grinder works. For larger herds, a hammer mill is ideal.

Store grains in airtight containers away from moisture and rodents. Aflatoxin from moldy maize is deadly. Never feed moldy grain. Also, avoid feeding urea-treated straw to goats unless trainedurea poisoning is fatal.

Practical tip: Use a weighing scale. Many farmers guess and overfeed. A plastic cup holds about 200 g of maize. For a lactating doe, feed 500 g concentrate plus free-choice hay. For a dry doe, 200 g is enough. Adjust based on body condition. Remember, grain is expensiveuse it strategically to fill nutrient gaps, not as the main feed.

4. Mineral and Vitamin Supplements

Minerals and vitamins are needed in small amounts but are critical for health, growth, and reproduction. In many African soils, minerals like phosphorus, copper, and selenium are deficient. Goats on poor forage will show signs: poor growth, brittle bones, rough coat, and infertility. A good mineral supplement prevents these problems.

The most important minerals for goats are calcium, phosphorus, sodium, copper, selenium, and zinc. The ideal calcium-to-phosphorus ratio is 2:1. Forages are often high in calcium but low in phosphorus. Provide a mineral block or loose mineral mix. A common recipe: 50% dicalcium phosphate, 30% salt, 10% limestone, 5% trace mineral premix, 5% molasses for palatability. Offer free-choice in a wooden box. Goats will eat 1020 g per day.

Vitamins A, D, and E are essential. Green forage provides vitamin A precursors (carotene). Sunlight provides vitamin D. Vitamin E is in fresh plants. In dry seasons, supplement with a vitamin premix. For pregnant does, inject vitamin A, D, E (2 ml per 50 kg) 4 weeks before kidding to prevent kid mortality.

Copper is critical but toxic in excess. Goats need 1020 ppm in the diet. In areas with copper deficiency, use a copper bolus (2 g) every 6 months. Selenium deficiency causes white muscle disease in kids. Inject 1 ml selenium (with vitamin E) at birth and repeat at 4 weeks in deficient areas.

Salt (sodium chloride) is often forgotten. Provide a salt lick at all times. Goats need 510 g per day. Without salt, they eat less and produce less milk.

Practical tip: Make a mineral box from a plastic container nailed to the wall. Fill with loose mineral mix. Check weekly and refill. Do not mix minerals into grain unless you know the exact requirementsoverdosing can kill. If goats ignore the mineral, they may not need it; if they crave it, they are deficient. Always provide clean water nearby. In areas with high rainfall, use a covered mineral feeder to prevent leaching.

5. Feeding Different Age Groups

Feeding goats correctly by age ensures healthy growth and productivity. Newborn kids (03 days) must receive colostrum within the first hour of birth. Colostrum is rich in antibodies and energy. Feed 200 ml per kid every 4 hours for the first 24 hours. If the doe rejects the kid, use frozen or pooled colostrum from another doe. Never feed cow colostrumit lacks specific antibodies for goats.

From day 4 to weaning (34 months), kids need milk. If dam-raised, they nurse 46 times daily. For bottle-fed kids, give 500800 ml of goat milk per day, split into 3 feedings. Introduce solid feed at 2 weeks: offer fine hay, chopped leaves, and a creep feed (18% protein). Creep feed is a mix of maize, soybean meal, and minerals. Feed 50100 g per day, increasing to 300 g by weaning. Always provide clean water.

Weaners (36 months) are growing fast. They need 1416% protein. Feed 300500 g concentrate plus free-choice hay. Also provide browse. Monitor body condition; thin weaners need more grain. Separate males and females to prevent early pregnancy.

Yearlings (612 months) are almost mature. Feed 200400 g concentrate plus hay. Pregnant does need extra: 300500 g concentrate in late pregnancy. Flush does (increase feed) 3 weeks before breeding to boost ovulation. Lactating does need 500800 g concentrate plus hay and water. A doe producing 2 liters of milk daily needs 1.5 kg dry matter and 18% protein.

Bucks need 200300 g concentrate to maintain condition. Overfeeding bucks causes urinary calculi. Ensure calcium:phosphorus ratio is 2:1 and provide plenty of water.

Practical tip: Use a simple feeding chart. Weigh feed with a kitchen scale. For kids, use a bottle with a nipple. For groups, use feed troughs with dividers to prevent bullying. Always provide the best feed to pregnant and lactating doesthey are the future of your herd.

6. Body Condition Scoring and Adjusting Rations

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a hands-on method to assess fat and muscle. It helps you adjust feed without expensive scales. Use a scale of 1 to 5: 1 = emaciated, 2 = thin, 3 = ideal, 4 = fat, 5 = obese. Feel the backbone, ribs, and hip bones. If bones are sharp and prominent, score 2. If smooth and covered, score 3. If you cannot feel bones, score 4 or 5.

Score your goats every month. For does, score at breeding, mid-pregnancy, and weaning. Ideal BCS for breeding is 3.03.5. For lactating does, 2.53.0. For bucks, 3.0. Kids should be 2.53.0. If a goat scores below 2, increase feed. If above 4, reduce grain and increase hay.

To adjust rations, first check forage quality. If hay is stemmy, add 200 g concentrate. If goats are thin on good hay, they may have wormsdeworm and then increase feed. For a thin doe, feed 500 g concentrate plus free-choice hay. For a fat doe, feed only hay and 100 g concentrate until BCS drops to 3.

Use a simple tool: a livestock weighing tape. Measure heart girth just behind the front legs and use a chart to estimate weight. For goats, heart girth in cm minus 10 gives approximate weight in kg. For example, 70 cm girth = 60 kg. This helps calculate feed amounts.

Practical tip: Keep records. Note BCS, feed given, and any changes. Adjust graduallysudden changes cause acidosis. If many goats are thin, check for parasites, dental problems, or insufficient feed. If many are fat, you are wasting money. Aim for BCS 3: goats are healthy, fertile, and productive. Remember, BCS is the best low-cost tool for feeding management.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Goat Feeding and Nutrition in African Conditions

This reference sheet is written for the working goat farmer and agripreneur. It is not a textbook chapter. It is a decision-support tool you can print, laminate, and keep in the feed store or the goat house. Every recommendation here assumes you are running a commercial or semi-commercial herd in an African context variable rainfall, seasonal feed gaps, limited access to manufactured concentrates, and a market that pays for condition, not sentiment. Your job is to convert feed into weight, milk, and kids at the lowest cost per kilogram of gain. That is the only metric that matters.

Goats are not small cattle. They are browsers with a digestive system built for variety, and they will out-perform cattle on marginal land if and only if you respect how that system works. The practical reference below covers the digestive logic, feed selection, concentrate use, mineral supplementation, age-specific feeding, and body condition scoring as a management routine. Use it with the other lessons in this module.

SAFETY WARNING: Acidosis and Urea Toxicity

Never introduce concentrates suddenly. A goat that has been on forage alone and is suddenly given 500g of maize will develop rumen acidosis within hours. Symptoms: off-feed, bloated, diarrhoea, lying down, rapid breathing. Death can occur within 24 hours.

Urea treatment of straw: If you treat maize stover with urea, use 4-5 kg urea per 100 kg dry straw, dissolve in 40-50 litres of water, and seal for 21 days. Never feed urea-treated straw to kids under 3 months. Never allow goats to drink the urea solution directly. Keep urea locked away from children and animals.

Mycotoxin risk: Never feed mouldy groundnut haulms, cotton seed cake, or maize. Aflatoxin causes liver damage, reduced milk, and abortion. If it smells musty or shows black/green mould, burn it or compost it do not feed it.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Weigh or condition-score your goats monthly. You cannot manage what you do not measure. A BCS of 2.5-3.5 is the target for breeding does; below 2.0 means emergency supplementation.
  2. Feed 60-70% forage, 30-40% concentrate by DM. Forage is the foundation. Concentrate is a supplement, not a replacement. This ratio keeps the rumen healthy and your costs down.
  3. Provide clean water at all times. A lactating doe drinks 6-10 litres per day. Water restriction reduces intake and milk yield faster than feed restriction.
  4. Offer free-choice mineral licks. Use a commercial goat mineral or a 50:50 mix of limestone and salt. Phosphorus is the first limiting mineral in most African forages.
  5. Change feed over 7-10 days. Replace 1/4 of the old ration with new feed every 2-3 days. This gives rumen microbes time to adapt.
  6. Store feed off the ground on pallets. Moisture above 12% causes mould. A dry, ventilated store saves more money than any feed additive.
  7. Feed at the same times daily. Goats are creatures of habit. Split feed into two meals morning and evening to reduce waste and improve digestion.
  8. Record feed intake and cost per goat per day. If you do not know your cost per kg of gain, you cannot price your animals profitably. Track it weekly.
