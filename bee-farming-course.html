Bee Farming

A complete 6-month program for African beekeeping — baited top-bar hives, evening inspections done safely with defensive bees, the capped-comb harvest, and honey that sells at retail-jar prices.

⏱️ 6 Months (4–8 hrs/week)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? Beekeeping runs on the flowering calendar: this program takes you from empty hive to your first honey harvest across one build-up-and-flow season — siting and baiting hives, catching colonies, managing them through the build-up, and harvesting when the flow peaks. The bees do the work; your job is to give them a home, protection, and to take the crop at the right moment.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each month → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen at the apiary, at dusk, in a suit.

⚠️ Read first: African bees demand respect

  • African honey bees (Apis mellifera scutellata and relatives) defend far more fiercely than European bees. They attack in numbers, pursue further (100–300 m+), and mass stings can kill. This is manageable — millions of African beekeepers work them safely — but ONLY with full protective kit, a working smoker, evening/night work, and correct apiary siting.
  • Site hives at least 200–300 m from homes, roads, schools, penned livestock and water points — a tethered animal near an agitated apiary can be stung to death. Screens of bush/fence force flight paths above head height.
  • Anyone with a known sting allergy must not work bees — and every apiary session needs an escape plan, a closed vehicle or building in reach, and never working alone at night.
  • Never spend before the Iron-Clad Price Check — 3 quotes for hives, suits and smokers.
MONTH 1 — Bee Biology, Products & Planning ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Why bees are the lightest-touch farm business

  • No feed bills, no daily labour: a colony forages 3+ km in every direction and feeds itself; a well-run hive needs attention hours per MONTH, not per day.
  • Income stack: honey (10–25 kg/hive/year typical for African top-bar/local conditions, more on strong flows), beeswax, propolis, and pollination value for your own crops (+20–30% yields on many fruits and legumes).
  • Honey never spoils, stores without electricity, and always sells — the ideal remote/rural cash crop.

Lesson 1.2 — Inside the colony

CasteCountRole & lifespan
Queen1Lays up to 1,500–2,000 eggs/day; lives 1–3 years; her pheromone holds the colony together
Workers10,000–60,000All female: nurse → builder → guard → forager; ~6 weeks in season
Drones100s (seasonal)Males; mate with queens; evicted before dearth
  • Development: egg 3 days → larva → capped pupa; worker emerges day 21, queen day 16, drone day 24 — these numbers let you read any comb.
  • The year: build-up on early flowers → the honey FLOW (main flowering) → dearth (dry/cold, colony lives on stores) → repeat. Your entire calendar hangs on knowing your local flow months.

Lesson 1.3 — Reading the flowering calendar

  • Great African bee forage: acacias, msasa/miombo woodland, eucalyptus, citrus and mango, sunflower, moringa, aloes (dearth rescue), cotton and many crop weeds.
  • Build your local calendar: which of these flower in which months within 3 km of your site — elders and existing beekeepers know it by heart; write it down.
  • One hive's flight range covers ~2,800 hectares — you're not farming your plot, you're harvesting the landscape.

Lesson 1.4 — Planning the enterprise

  • Starter scale: 5–10 hives — enough for real honey volume, few enough to learn on; expect 60–70% occupation in year one from baiting.
  • Check the rules: some countries register apiaries/honey sales; forestry areas may need permits for hive placement — one office visit now.
  • Budget priority order: protective suit and smoker FIRST (never compromised), then hives, then processing kit.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Map every major bee-forage plant within 1 km of your intended site and draft the 12-month flowering calendar. (2) Visit a working beekeeper — observe an evening inspection from a safe distance and record their flow months and yields. (3) Price suits, smokers and hives from 3 suppliers.

MONTH 2 — Hives, Equipment & the Apiary Site ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Hive types for Africa

HiveCostStrengthsLimits
Kenyan Top Bar (KTB)Low–medium ($15–40 local build)Simple carpentry, easy comb-by-comb inspection, ideal African starterComb honey (crushed), less honey than frames per colony
Langstroth (frames)High ($60–150+)Highest yields, extractable frames reused, commercial standardPrecision parts, extractor needed
Traditional log/bark/potNear zeroCheap, bees love themDestructive harvest, no inspection, low quality honey
  • Recommended path: start with KTBs (build 5 from one plan), add Langstroths as income grows. KTB spec that matters: top bars exactly 32 mm wide (the natural comb spacing African bees build).

Lesson 2.2 — The kit

  • Full suit (overall + zipped hood/veil), thick gloves, gumboots with trousers OVER them — African bees find every gap; tape yours.
  • Smoker: fuel with dry cow dung, sacking or dried leaves; smoke masks alarm pheromone and triggers honey-gorging (calmer bees). Light it BEFORE suiting up; a dead smoker mid-hive is how bad stories start.
  • Hive tool (or flat chisel), bee brush/feather, bucket with lid for cut comb, torch with RED light for night work (bees don't fly to red).

Lesson 2.3 — Siting the apiary (safety is a location decision)

  • 200–300 m minimum from homes, paths, roads, schools, penned/tethered animals and shared water; behind a 2 m screen (hedge, shade-cloth, maize) so flight lines rise above people.
  • Morning sun, midday shade (heat melts comb), wind protection, water within 500 m (or provide a dripping container — bees NEED water and will otherwise find the neighbour's trough).
  • Hives on stands 60–100 cm high with greased legs or ant guards — ants, honey badgers and hive beetles are the robbers; wire-strap lids against badgers where they occur.
  • Space hives 1–2 m apart, entrances facing away from your working side.

Lesson 2.4 — Baiting: getting bees for free

  • African swarming seasons fill empty hives on their own: rub the inside with beeswax and propolis, add a few drops of lemongrass oil (mimics scout pheromone), and hang/place hives before swarm season.
  • Best bait sites: 2–3 m up a tree edge facing morning sun; move occupied hives to the apiary at night after 2–3 weeks of settling.
  • Alternatives: buy a nucleus colony from a beekeeper, or transfer a wild colony (advanced — do it first with an experienced keeper).
✅ Iron-Clad Price Check: a local carpenter building 5 KTBs from your plan usually beats shop prices by half — 3 quotes on hives, suit and smoker.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 2): (1) Build or commission 5 KTB hives (32 mm bars) and one stand per hive. (2) Prepare and place all 5 as baited hives before/into swarm season. (3) Practise the full suit-up + smoker-lighting drill until the smoker stays lit 30 minutes.

MONTH 3 — Working the Bees: Inspections & Colony Reading ▾

Lesson 3.1 — The inspection protocol (African edition)

  • When: late evening/dusk, calm warm weather, never before rain or in wind; never in daytime near people or animals.
  • Approach from behind/side, 3–5 good smoker puffs at the entrance, wait 60–90 seconds, then work under the lid with smoke ahead of your hands.
  • Move like honey: slow, no bumps, no crushed bees (crushing releases alarm pheromone = escalation). 10–15 minutes maximum; long openings chill brood and enrage guards.
  • If the colony boils out at you: close up calmly, walk (don't run flailing) through bushes to break pursuit, and try another evening — some colonies are hot; requeening or splitting calms lines over time.

Lesson 3.2 — Reading the combs

  • Healthy brood nest: solid slabs of pearly larvae and tan capped brood in the centre bars, pollen arcs around them, white capped honey outside — the "target" pattern.
  • Queen-right without seeing her: EGGS (tiny standing rice grains, one per cell) mean she laid within 3 days. No eggs + emergency queen cells = queenless; scattered "shotgun" brood = failing queen or disease.
  • Stores check: outer bars heavy with capped honey = good; light hive going into dearth = mark for feeding or early harvest restraint.

Lesson 3.3 — The colony's calendar decisions

  • Build-up: give space ahead of the bees (empty bars toward the brood nest in a KTB) — crowded colonies swarm away with half your workforce.
  • Swarm signs: queen cells like peanut shells on comb edges, bearding at the entrance — split the colony (take 3–4 brood bars + bees into a new hive) and you've turned swarming into a free second colony.
  • Absconding (the African specialty — the WHOLE colony leaves): triggered by heat, ants, beetles, hunger, frequent disturbance or harvesting too hard. Every management rule in this course is partly an anti-absconding rule.

Lesson 3.4 — Dearth feeding (rescue only)

  • Feed 1:1 sugar syrup in an internal/entrance feeder only when stores are critically light in dearth — never during the flow (it adulterates honey) and never open-feeding (it starts robbing wars between hives).
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 3): (1) Conduct two full evening inspections with a mentor or after the video drill — log queen-right status, brood pattern, stores for every hive. (2) Practise finding eggs on a comb photo set or live comb. (3) Write each hive's record card: occupation date, temperament (calm/hot), strength, stores.

MONTH 4 — Pests, Diseases & Colony Protection ▾

Lesson 4.1 — The African enemies list

EnemyDamageDefence
Ants (safari/sugar)Overrun and abscond a hive overnightGreased/oiled stand legs, ash rings, clear vegetation bridges
Small hive beetleLarvae ferment combs ("slime-out")Strong colonies, no abandoned comb, squash adults at inspections
Wax mothDestroys stored/weak-colony combStrong colonies; freeze or sun-seal stored comb
Honey badgerRips hives open at nightStrong stands, wire straps, hanging hives where badgers are common
Birds (bee-eaters), toads, spidersForager lossesUsually tolerable; clear toad cover under entrances
Humans (theft, pesticides)Hives stolen; foragers poisonedHidden/marked hives, community agreements; talk to neighbouring sprayers — spray evenings, bees home

Lesson 4.2 — Diseases and the African advantage

  • African honey bees largely tolerate varroa mites and resist many brood diseases that devastate other continents — the flip side of their defensiveness. You mostly manage PESTS, not pathogens.
  • Still watch for: foul-smelling, sunken, perforated brood cappings (foulbrood — report to bee inspector/vet where present; don't trade combs), chalky white mummies at the entrance (chalkbrood — requeen, ventilate).
  • Hygiene rules: never move combs between apiaries carelessly, never buy old combs, sterilise the hive tool between sick and healthy hives (smoker flame works).

Lesson 4.3 — The strong-colony doctrine

  • Every pest and disease answer is the same answer: a STRONG colony defends itself — beetles patrolled, moths evicted, robbing repelled.
  • Keep colonies strong by: space ahead of growth, stores never robbed to zero at harvest, young productive queens (splits/requeen every 1–2 years), and minimal well-timed disturbance.
  • Unite weak colonies (newspaper method: stack with a paper sheet between; they merge as it's chewed through) rather than nursing two losers into the dearth.

Lesson 4.4 — The apiary safety audit

  • Quarterly walk-through: stand legs greased? screens intact? lids strapped? water point flowing? no comb litter (beetle nursery)? neighbours still happy? first-aid + antihistamine in the kit? escape route clear?
  • Sting protocol: scrape (don't pinch) stings out fast — venom pumps for a minute; smoke the sting site (masks the "sting here" pheromone); many stings or any breathing/face swelling = clinic immediately.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 4): (1) Do the full apiary audit checklist and fix the three worst findings. (2) Grease/ash every stand and strap every lid. (3) Meet your crop-spraying neighbours and agree evening-spray windows; log the agreement.

MONTH 5 — The Honey Harvest ▾

Lesson 5.1 — When to harvest

  • Harvest at the END of the flow, evening, good weather — and take only combs that are 75%+ CAPPED: capping is the bees' certificate that moisture is below ~18–19% and the honey is ripe. Uncapped "green" honey ferments in the bucket.
  • The golden restraint rule: leave the brood nest untouched and leave the colony 30–40%+ of its stores (more entering dearth) — greedy harvesting = absconded hives and no crop next year.
  • A KTB harvest: smoke, lift honey bars from the far end, brush bees off each comb back into the hive, cut comb into the lidded bucket, close within 15 minutes.

Lesson 5.2 — Processing comb honey (no machines needed)

  • Crush-and-strain: cut/ crush capped comb → drain through a clean cloth/fine sieve into a food-grade bucket, 24–48 h in a warm room → settle 2–3 days → skim foam → bottle. Never heat above ~40°C (kills enzymes, ruins flavour and the "raw" premium).
  • Hygiene = shelf life: everything washed and DRY (water in honey → fermentation), no smoking indoors over open honey, lids sealed tight (honey pulls moisture from air).
  • Langstroth path: uncap and spin frames in an extractor — combs return to hives and the next crop comes faster.

Lesson 5.3 — Wax: the second crop

  • The strained crushings are beeswax: melt in a water bath (NEVER direct flame — wax is flammable), strain through cloth, cool into blocks.
  • Wax sells to candle-makers, cosmetics producers, and back to beekeeping as foundation/bait — typically 8–10% of honey weight and often a better price per kg than honey.

Lesson 5.4 — Quality that commands premiums

  • Grade as you bottle: clear strained honey (premium jars), cloudy first-strain (cooking grade), cut-comb squares in clear tubs (the top-price product — zero processing!).
  • Label: pure raw honey, apiary location, harvest month, weight — and NEVER blend in sugar syrup; adulteration destroys the reputation the whole business stands on.
  • Crystallised honey is NOT spoiled — it's proof of purity; warm the jar in warm water to re-liquefy and teach your customers the same.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 5): (1) Harvest your capped combs by the protocol (or shadow a mentor's harvest) — log kg per hive and % left for the bees. (2) Crush-and-strain, settle, and bottle your first batch with full labels. (3) Render the wax into a clean block and weigh it.

MONTH 6 — Selling, Economics & Your Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — The honey market ladder

  • Bulk to packers/middlemen: easiest, lowest price ($1.50–3/kg typical).
  • Own-label retail jars: 2–4× bulk price via shops, pharmacies, salons, church and office networks — the jump worth making immediately.
  • Premium niches: raw/comb honey to health buyers, single-flower honeys (miombo, citrus), gift packs at festivals; beeswax to candle/cosmetic makers.
  • Services income: pollination placement for horticulture farms, colony/nuc sales to new beekeepers, and training — experienced keepers earn as much teaching as harvesting.

Lesson 6.3 — Growing the apiary for free

  • Splits from your strongest colonies each build-up + baited hives every swarm season = 30–50% annual colony growth at zero purchase cost.
  • Out-apiaries: agreements with farmers (they get pollination, you get sites) let one beekeeper run 50+ hives across a district.

Lesson 6.4 — Final project: your bankable business plan

  • Assemble from six months of hive cards and money records: apiary site plan with safety distances, flowering calendar, hive count and growth-by-splits plan, harvest results and honey grades, buyer list with prices, risk plan (absconding, theft, pesticide events, sting emergencies), 3-year expansion to out-apiaries.
  • Submit with photos for your rAs certificate — the same document fits honey-buyer contracts and forestry/NGO beekeeping schemes.

📖 Glossary

  • Flow / dearth — main flowering surplus season / flowerless survival season.
  • KTB — Kenyan Top Bar hive; combs hang from 32 mm bars.
  • Queen-right — colony has a laying queen (eggs present).
  • Swarming — colony reproduction: old queen leaves with half the bees.
  • Absconding — the WHOLE colony deserting (African speciality).
  • Split — dividing a strong colony into two.
  • Nuc — nucleus: a small starter colony.
  • Capped honey — wax-sealed ripe honey (<~18–19% moisture).
  • Crush-and-strain — comb honey processing without machines.
  • Propolis — bee resin sealant; a sellable product.
