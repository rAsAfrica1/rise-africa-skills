1. Step-by-Step System Wiring Diagram for a Small Business

To wire a small business solar system, you need a clear plan. Start by drawing your components: solar panels, charge controller, battery bank, inverter, and AC loads. For a typical shop with a fridge, lights, and phone charging, you might use four 250W panels (1000W total), a 60A MPPT charge controller, four 200Ah deep-cycle batteries (24V system), and a 3kVA pure sine wave inverter. Mount panels facing north (in southern Africa) at a 20-30 degree tilt. Use 4mm solar cable for panel to controller, and 25mm for battery to inverter. Connect panels in series-parallel to match controller voltage. Run cables through conduit to protect from sun and rain. Connect charge controller to battery bank first, then panels. Connect inverter to battery with thick cables and a 150A DC breaker. Wire AC output to a distribution board with circuit breakers for each load. Ground the panel frames and inverter case. Label all cables. Test with a multimeter before powering loads. Always use fuses or breakers on every positive line. A common mistake is undersizing cables, causing voltage drop and heat. For a 3kVA inverter, use at least 25mm cable for a 1-2 meter run. Tighten all connections firmly; loose connections cause fires. If you are unsure, hire a qualified solar technician to inspect your work.

2. Connecting Inverter to Battery Bank and AC Loads

Connecting your inverter correctly is critical for safety and performance. First, ensure your battery bank is wired for the correct voltageusually 12V, 24V, or 48V. For a 24V system, connect two 12V batteries in series (positive to negative) to make 24V, then parallel additional pairs for more capacity. Use 25mm or thicker cables for battery-to-inverter connections. Install a DC circuit breaker (rated for at least 1.5 times your inverter's max current) on the positive cable. For a 3kVA 24V inverter, max current is about 125A, so use a 150A breaker. Connect the negative cable last to avoid sparks. Tighten terminals with a wrenchloose connections overheat. Now for AC loads: the inverter's AC output goes to a small distribution board. Install a 16A or 20A MCB for each circuit (lights, sockets, fridge). Use 2.5mm copper cable for AC wiring. Connect the inverter's earth terminal to a ground rod or your building's earth. Never connect the inverter's AC output to the grid unless it's a hybrid inverter designed for that. For off-grid, simply plug your loads into the inverter's AC sockets or wire them to the board. Test with a multimeter: check AC voltage (should be 220-240V) and polarity. If the inverter beeps or shows fault, disconnect and check connections. Always turn off the inverter before working on AC wiring.

3. Setting Up a Hybrid System with Grid or Generator Backup

A hybrid system lets you use solar first, then battery, then grid or generator as backup. This is ideal for areas with unreliable grid power. You need a hybrid inverter (e.g., 5kVA) that can take DC from solar and batteries, and AC from grid or generator. Connect solar panels to the hybrid inverter's PV input (check max voltage, often 500V). Connect battery bank to the battery terminals. Connect grid or generator to the AC input terminals. The inverter will automatically switch between sources. Set priorities: solar > battery > grid. For a small business, set the inverter to use solar during the day, battery at night, and grid only when battery is low (e.g., below 20%). Use a generator of at least 1.5 times the inverter's continuous power to handle surge. Install a changeover switch if you want manual control, but hybrid inverters have automatic transfer. Program the inverter via its LCD or app: set battery type (e.g., lithium or lead-acid), charging current (e.g., 30A), and grid charging times. Ensure the generator is off when grid is present to avoid backfeed. Ground the system properly. Test by simulating a grid outage: turn off grid breaker, see if inverter switches to battery within 20 milliseconds. If it fails, check settings. Always use surge protection on AC input.

4. Using Changeover Switches and Breakers Safely

Changeover switches and breakers protect you and your equipment. A changeover switch (manual or automatic) lets you switch between grid, generator, and inverter output. For a small business, install a manual changeover switch rated for your total load (e.g., 63A). Connect grid to one input, generator to another, and inverter output to the third. The output goes to your distribution board. Never connect two sources at oncethat's why changeover switches have mechanical interlock. For breakers: use DC breakers for solar and battery circuits (rated for DC voltage, e.g., 250V DC). AC breakers (MCBs) for grid and load circuits. Size DC breakers at 1.25 times the expected current. For a 60A charge controller, use an 80A DC breaker. For battery-to-inverter, use a 150A DC breaker for 3kVA. Install breakers in a dry, accessible place. Label each breaker clearly: 'Solar In', 'Battery', 'Inverter Out', 'Grid'. Test breakers monthly by switching off and on. Never bypass a breaker. Use fuses for extra protection on small wires. Always turn off the main breaker before working on wiring. If a breaker trips, find the fault before resetting. Use a multimeter to check for shorts. In Africa, power surges are common, so use surge protection devices (SPDs) on AC input. A 40kA SPD costs about $30 and can save your inverter.

5. Testing Your System Before Powering Your Equipment

Before you plug in your fridge or computer, test the system step by step. First, check all connections are tight and correct. Use a multimeter to measure battery voltage: a 24V bank should read 25.6V when fully charged. Check solar panel open-circuit voltage: for four 250W panels in series, expect around 150V DC. Connect panels to charge controller and check it starts charging. Next, test the inverter without loads: turn it on, measure AC output voltage (should be 230V 5%). Check frequency (50Hz). If the inverter has a display, look for error codes. Now, test with a small load like a 100W bulb. It should light steadily. If it flickers, check connections. Then, test with a larger load like a 500W drill. Monitor battery voltageit should not drop below 22V for a 24V system. If it does, your battery may be undersized. Test the changeover switch: switch from inverter to grid and back, ensuring no interruption to critical loads. Test breakers: trip each one to confirm it cuts power. Check earth leakage: use a socket tester to ensure correct polarity and earth. Finally, run the system for 24 hours with normal loads, checking for overheating cables or unusual noises. Record voltages and currents. If anything is wrong, fix before connecting sensitive equipment. Always wear safety glasses and gloves.

6. Labelling and Documenting Your Solar Installation

Good labelling and documentation save time and prevent accidents. Start by labelling every cable at both ends: 'PV+', 'PV-', 'Battery+', 'Battery-', 'AC Out', 'Grid In'. Use durable labels or heat-shrink tubing with printed text. Label breakers and switches clearly: 'Main DC', 'Inverter AC Out', 'Solar Breaker'. Create a system diagram showing all components, cable sizes, breaker ratings, and battery wiring. Note the date of installation and battery water topping schedule (if lead-acid). Keep a logbook: record daily energy production (kWh), battery voltage in the morning, and any faults. Take photos of the wiring before closing panels. For the client, provide a simple user manual: how to turn on/off, what to do if breaker trips, and emergency contacts. In Africa, where technicians may be scarce, clear documentation helps anyone service the system. Use a permanent marker or label maker. Avoid paper labels that fall off. Also, document the settings of your hybrid inverter: battery type, charging current, grid charging times. If you sell the business, this documentation adds value. Store a copy in a plastic sleeve near the system. Update it whenever you change anything. A well-documented system is safer, easier to troubleshoot, and more reliable.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Integrating Inverter, Battery, and Solar Panels

For a small African business a maize mill, a barbershop, a cold room, a phone-charging kiosk, or a tailoring workshop the solar system is only as good as the wiring that ties it together. A correctly integrated inverter, battery bank, and PV array will run your loads reliably for years. A poorly integrated one will trip breakers, cook batteries, or burn out your equipment often within weeks.

This practical reference walks you through the physical and electrical integration of a small hybrid system. It assumes you already have your components sized (see previous modules) and are now ready to connect them. Every step here is written for the African context: variable grid quality, generator backup, high ambient temperatures, and the need for systems that a local technician can service.

1. Step-by-Step System Wiring Overview

The integration follows a simple energy flow: PV array charge controller (or inverter's built-in MPPT) battery bank inverter AC distribution loads. In a hybrid system, the grid or generator feeds in through a changeover switch or the inverter's AC input.

2. Connecting Inverter to Battery Bank and AC Loads

Battery-to-inverter wiring is where most small African installations fail. The DC current is high (a 3 kW inverter at 24 V draws over 125 A), so cable sizing and torque matter more than anything else.

Steps:

  1. Confirm the inverter's DC input voltage matches the battery bank (12 V, 24 V, or 48 V). Never mix.
  2. Use the shortest possible cable run. Keep inverter within 12 m of the battery bank.
  3. Size cables for the full surge current, not just continuous. Use the manufacturer's table.
  4. Install a DC-rated fuse or breaker on the positive cable, close to the battery terminal.
  5. Connect battery positive last, and use a torque wrench to the manufacturer's spec (typically 46 Nm for M8 terminals).
  6. For AC loads, run from the inverter's AC output through a distribution board with individual breakers per circuit.

3. Setting Up a Hybrid System with Grid or Generator Backup

In most African urban and peri-urban settings, the grid is unreliable frequent outages, voltage sags, and surges. A generator may fill the gap. A hybrid inverter handles both automatically, but only if wired correctly.

Key integration points:

  • AC input: Grid or generator feeds the inverter's AC input terminal. The inverter uses this to charge batteries and pass through to loads.
  • Generator sizing: Must be at least 1.5 the inverter's charger rating, or the generator will stall when the charger kicks in.
  • Grid quality: If grid voltage regularly drops below 180 V or spikes above 260 V, install an AVR or a voltage protection relay before the inverter.
  • Priority settings: Configure the inverter to prioritise solar battery grid generator, so you minimise fuel and grid costs.

4. Using Changeover Switches and Breakers Safely

A changeover switch lets you manually select between inverter output and grid/generator supply. In many small businesses, this is the cheapest way to add backup without a fully automatic hybrid inverter.

Rules for safe changeover wiring:

  • Use a break-before-make changeover switch. A make-before-make switch will short grid and inverter together a fire risk.
  • Mount the switch in a lockable enclosure, clearly labelled "GRID" and "SOLAR".
  • Never switch under load if the switch is not rated for it. Turn off the main breaker first.
  • Install an RCD (residual current device) on the AC output to protect against earth leakage critical in damp or dusty environments.
  • Use DC-rated breakers on the PV and battery circuits. AC breakers will arc and fail on DC.

SAFETY WARNING DC ARC AND ELECTROCUTION HAZARD

PV arrays and battery banks cannot be "switched off" at the source. A PV array generates voltage whenever there is light, and a battery bank stores lethal energy 24/7. Never work on DC wiring without first isolating the array at the DC breaker, disconnecting the battery positive terminal, and verifying with a multimeter. DC arcs do not self-extinguish like AC arcs they sustain and cause fires. Use only DC-rated breakers, fuses, and MC4 connectors. Wear insulated gloves and use insulated tools. In high-temperature African conditions, check terminal torque every 6 months loose DC connections are the number one cause of solar fires.

5. Testing Your System Before Powering Your Equipment

Never connect your business loads on day one. Test in stages:

  1. PV open-circuit voltage: Measure each string. Confirm it matches the charge controller's max input voltage.
  2. Battery voltage: Check each battery and the full bank. Confirm polarity before connecting the inverter.
  3. Insulation resistance: Use a megohmmeter on the DC and AC circuits to check for leakage to earth.
  4. Inverter no-load test: Power the inverter with no AC loads. Confirm output voltage (230 V 5%) and frequency (50 Hz 1%).
  5. Progressive load test: Add loads one at a time a light, then a fan, then a fridge. Watch for voltage drop and inverter alarms.
  6. Changeover test: Simulate a grid outage. Confirm the switch transfers cleanly and the inverter carries the load.
  7. Thermal check: After 30 minutes at full load, check terminal temperatures with an IR thermometer. Anything above 60 C is a problem.

6. Labelling and Documenting Your Installation

Good documentation is what separates a professional installation from a hobby project. It also protects your investment when you sell the business or hand over to a technician.

  • Label every cable at both ends: "PV+ String 1", "Battery+", "AC Out Fridge Circuit".
  • Post a single-line diagram inside the enclosure door.
  • Record: panel model and serial numbers, battery install date, inverter settings, breaker ratings.
  • Keep a maintenance log: terminal torque checks, water top-ups (for flooded batteries), cleaning dates.
  • Store a digital copy WhatsApp it to yourself or email it. Paper gets lost.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Battery bank voltage matches inverter DC input rating
  • DC fuse/breaker installed on positive cable within 30 cm of battery
  • All DC cables sized for surge current, torque-checked to spec
  • Changeover switch is break-before-make and clearly labelled
  • RCD installed on AC output circuit
  • PV open-circuit voltage verified against controller max input
  • Progressive load test completed before connecting business equipment
  • Single-line diagram posted and all cables labelled
