1. Names of Shops and Markets

In Zimbabwe, when you go shopping, you'll visit different types of shops. A 'chitoro' is a small general store, often found in townships. A 'musika' is an open-air market where many vendors sell fresh produce, clothes, and household items. A 'supermarket' or 'kambani' is a larger store like OK Zimbabwe or Pick n Pay. For specific items, you might go to a 'bhutchery' (butchery) for meat, a 'bhekari' (bakery) for bread, or a 'pharmacy' (kihemisi) for medicine. In rural areas, you might find 'mishonga' (traditional markets) or weekly markets like 'musika weSvondo' (Sunday market).

When asking for a shop, say 'Ndiri kutsvaga chitoro che...' (I am looking for a shop of...). For example, 'Ndiri kutsvaga chitoro chezvipfeko' (I am looking for a clothes shop). If you want to know where the market is, ask 'Musika uri kupi?' (Where is the market?). Common answers: 'Uri kumberi' (It's ahead), 'Uri kuseri' (It's behind), 'Uri pedyo nebhangi' (It's near the bank).

In cities like Harare, you might visit Mbare Musika, the largest market. In Bulawayo, there's Egodini Market. In Mutare, Sakubva Musika. These are great places to practice your Shona. Vendors will appreciate your effort. Remember: 'Ndapota' means please, and 'Ndatenda' means thank you. Use them often. Also, 'Mangwanani' (good morning), 'Masikati' (good afternoon), and 'Manheru' (good evening) are polite greetings when entering a shop.

If you need to find a specific item, say 'Ndiratidzei...' (Show me...). For example, 'Ndiratidzei mupunga' (Show me rice). Or 'Ndinoda kutenga...' (I want to buy...). If you don't see what you want, ask 'Mune ... here?' (Do you have ...?). For instance, 'Mune chingwa here?' (Do you have bread?). The vendor might reply 'Ehe, tine' (Yes, we have) or 'Aiwa, hatina' (No, we don't have).

When you're ready to pay, go to the 'kheshi' (cashier) or 'kubhadhara' (payment point). In markets, you pay the vendor directly. Always carry small notes and coins because change can be scarce. A 'dollar' is 'dhora', and 'cents' are 'masendi'. Knowing these terms will make your shopping smoother. Practice these phrases daily, and soon you'll navigate any Zimbabwean market with confidence.

2. Bargaining and Asking Prices

Bargaining is common in Zimbabwean markets, but not in supermarkets or formal shops where prices are fixed. In a 'musika' (market), you can negotiate. Start by asking 'Imarii?' (How much?) or 'Zviri marii?' (How much is it?). The vendor will say a price. If it's too high, you can say 'Inodhura' (It's expensive) or 'Ndipeiwo discount' (Give me a discount). A common phrase is 'Ndikumbireiwo' (Please reduce for me). You can also say 'Ndipeiwo pamutengo wakaderera' (Give me a lower price).

To make a counter-offer, say 'Ndinokupa ...' (I will give you ...). For example, if they ask $10, you might say 'Ndinokupa $7' (I'll give you $7). The vendor might accept or say 'Aiwa, hongu $9' (No, okay $9). You can meet in the middle. If you're buying multiple items, ask for a bulk discount: 'Ndiri kutenga zvinhu zvitatu, ndipeiwo discount' (I'm buying three items, give me a discount).

In Shona, numbers are important. 1 = poshi, 2 = piri, 3 = tatu, 4 = ina, 5 = shanu, 6 = tanhatu, 7 = nomwe, 8 = sere, 9 = pfumbamwe, 10 = gumi. For prices: $1 = dhora rimwe, $2 = madhora maviri, $5 = madhora mashanu, $10 = madhora gumi. Cents: 50c = masendi makumi mashanu. Practice counting: 'madhora matatu nemasendi makumi mashanu' = $3.50.

When bargaining, be polite and smile. A good opener is 'Mangwanani, imarii iyi?' (Good morning, how much is this?). If you think the price is fair, say 'Ndinoitora' (I'll take it). If not, say 'Ndichaona' (I'll see) and walk away slowly; sometimes the vendor will call you back with a better price. Don't be aggressive. Zimbabweans value respect. Use 'Ndapota' (please) and 'Ndatenda' (thank you).

In rural areas, barter is sometimes possible. You might trade maize for vegetables. But usually, cash is preferred. US dollars are widely accepted, but you might get change in bond notes or coins. Always check your change. If you feel cheated, say 'Mandiitira chitsotsi' (You cheated me) but only if true. Better to agree on price before paying. With practice, you'll become a skilled bargainer.

3. Currency and Payment Methods

Zimbabwe uses a multi-currency system. The US dollar (USD) is the most common, but you'll also see South African rand (ZAR), Botswana pula (BWP), and British pounds (GBP) in some areas. Since 2019, the Zimbabwe dollar (ZWL) is also used, but it loses value quickly. Most transactions are in USD. Coins: 1c, 5c, 10c, 25c, 50c, and $1 coins. Notes: $1, $2, $5, $10, $20, $50, $100. In Shona, 'dhora' is dollar, 'masendi' is cents. 'Mari' means money. 'Mari yemuchina' is foreign currency.

Payment methods: Cash is king. Always carry small notes because vendors rarely have change. In supermarkets, you can pay by 'card' (kadhi) - debit or credit. EcoCash is a popular mobile money service. To use EcoCash, dial *151# on your phone. You can send money to a vendor's EcoCash number. The vendor will ask 'Une EcoCash here?' (Do you have EcoCash?). If yes, you say 'Ehe, ndine' (Yes, I have). Then you transfer the amount. Other mobile money: OneMoney, Telecash. For online payments, some accept PayPal or bank transfer, but rare in markets.

When paying, say 'Ndiri kubhadhara nemari' (I am paying with cash) or 'Ndiri kubhadhara necard' (I am paying by card). If you need change, ask 'Mune change here?' (Do you have change?). If not, you might need to buy something small to break a large note. In markets, vendors often share change among themselves. Be patient.

Prices are usually quoted in USD. For example, a loaf of bread is $1, a litre of milk $1.50, a dozen eggs $2.50. In Shona: 'chingwa' = bread, 'mukaka' = milk, 'mazai' = eggs. If you want to know the price, ask 'Imarii chingwa?' (How much is bread?). The answer might be 'Dhora rimwe' (One dollar). If you have a $20 note, say 'Ndine madhora makumi maviri' (I have $20). The vendor might say 'Handina change' (I don't have change). Then you can ask 'Mungandipa change here?' (Can you give me change?).

Always keep your money safe. Use a wallet or purse. Don't flash large amounts. In crowded markets, be aware of pickpockets. If you're robbed, shout 'Mbavha!' (Thief!). But prevention is better. Learn to count your change in Shona: 'poshi, piri, tatu...' This helps you avoid being short-changed. With these tips, you can handle money confidently in Zimbabwe.

4. Clothing and Sizes

When shopping for clothes in Zimbabwe, you'll need to know terms for clothing and sizes. 'Zvipfeko' means clothes. 'Hembe' is shirt, 'bhurukwa' is trousers, 'siketi' is skirt, 'dhirezi' is dress, 'juzi' is jersey or sweater, 'bhachi' is jacket, 'kabhudhi' is coat. For shoes: 'shangu' is shoes, 'maoko' is sandals, 'bhutsi' is boots. 'Ngowani' is hat, 'sokisi' is socks, 'gurovhosi' is gloves. Underwear: 'chizvaro chemukati' (men's), 'bhurugwa remukati' (women's).

Sizes: Small = 'diki', Medium = 'pakati', Large = 'hombe', Extra Large = 'hombe zvikuru'. But in Zimbabwe, sizes are often in numbers: 32, 34, 36 for trousers; S, M, L, XL for shirts. For shoes: size 6, 7, 8, etc. Children's sizes: 'diki' for small, 'hombe' for large. When asking for a size, say 'Ndinoda size ...' (I want size ...). For example, 'Ndinoda size 32 yebhurukwa' (I want size 32 trousers). If you need to try on, ask 'Ndingaedza here?' (Can I try?). The vendor might show you a fitting room: 'Imba yekuedza'.

In markets, clothes are often second-hand (mabhero). You can find great deals. Inspect items for tears or stains. Ask 'Iyi yakanaka here?' (Is this good?). If there's a defect, say 'Iyi ine chikanganiso' (This has a problem). You can bargain for a lower price. For new clothes, go to shops like Edgars, Topics, or Truworths. Prices are fixed there.

When buying, know your measurements. For shirts, collar size: 15, 15.5, 16. For women's dresses: 8, 10, 12, 14. In Shona, 'hukuru' means size. 'Hukuru hwepamusoro' is large size. If unsure, ask the vendor 'Ndiyani hukuru hwangu?' (What is my size?). They might measure you with a tape measure: 'tepisi'. Common phrases: 'Ndiratidzei hembe' (Show me a shirt), 'Ndinoda imwe ruvara' (I want another colour). Colours: 'chena' (white), 'tema' (black), 'tsvuku' (red), 'bhuruu' (blue), 'green' (girinhi), 'yero' (yellow).

Always check the price before trying on. Ask 'Imarii iyi?' (How much is this?). If you like it, say 'Ndinoitora' (I'll take it). If not, 'Ndichaona' (I'll see). In Zimbabwe, it's polite to greet the vendor first: 'Mangwanani, ndiratidzei hembe' (Good morning, show me a shirt). Thank them: 'Ndatenda'. With these terms, you can shop for clothes confidently.

5. Quantities and Measurements

In Zimbabwean markets, you often buy in specific quantities. Knowing how to ask for amounts is essential. 'Zvimedu' means pieces. 'Chidimbu' is a piece. For example, 'Ndinoda zvidimbu zviviri zvechingwa' (I want two pieces of bread). 'Hafu' is half, 'kota' is quarter. 'Ndinoda hafu yekilo' (I want half a kilo). 'Kilo' is kilogram. 'Gumi' is ten, 'makumi maviri' is twenty. For liquids: 'litre' = 'rita'. 'Ndinoda malita maviri emukaka' (I want two litres of milk).

Common measurements: 1 kilogram (kg) = 1000 grams. 'Giremu' is gram. 1 litre (L) = 1000 millilitres. 'Mirita' is millilitre. In markets, vendors use scales: 'chikero'. They might say 'Saka, 2kg?' (So, 2kg?). You confirm: 'Ehe, 2kg'. For vegetables, you might buy a 'bundle' = 'chitsama'. For example, 'chitsama chemuriwo' (a bundle of vegetables). 'Bhegi' is bag. 'Ndinoda bhegi remupunga' (I want a bag of rice). 'Bhegi' can be 10kg, 25kg, or 50kg. Ask 'Bhegi rakapisai?' (How big is the bag?).

When buying fruits, you can say 'Ndinoda maapuro matatu' (I want three apples). 'Maapuro' is apples, 'mabanana' is bananas, 'maorenji' is oranges. For tomatoes: 'madomasi'. 'Ndinoda madomasi mashanu' (I want five tomatoes). For onions: 'hanyanisi'. 'Ndinoda hanyanisi mbiri' (I want two onions). If you want a specific amount, say 'Ndinoda 500g yehanyanisi' (I want 500g of onions). The vendor will weigh it.

In rural areas, traditional measurements are used. 'Denga' is a tin (about 2kg). 'Ngoma' is a bucket (about 10kg). 'Chikwama' is a small sack. For maize: 'mbeu' is seed, but for buying, 'chibage' is maize. 'Ndinoda denga rechibage' (I want a tin of maize). For groundnuts: 'nzungu'. 'Ndinoda ngoma yenungu' (I want a bucket of groundnuts). These traditional measures are still common.

Always ask for a receipt if you need one: 'Ndipeiwo risiti' (Give me a receipt). In markets, receipts are rare, but in supermarkets, they are standard. If you're buying in bulk, ask for a discount: 'Ndiri kutenga zvakawanda, ndipeiwo discount' (I'm buying a lot, give me a discount). Learn these terms, and you'll get the right amount every time.

6. Polite Requests When Shopping

Politeness is crucial in Shona culture. When shopping, always use polite phrases. Start with a greeting: 'Mangwanani' (Good morning), 'Masikati' (Good afternoon), 'Manheru' (Good evening). Then say 'Ndapota' (Please) when making a request. For example, 'Ndapota ndiratidzei hembe' (Please show me a shirt). 'Ndapota ndipeiwo chingwa' (Please give me bread). 'Ndapota ndibatsireiwo' (Please help me).

To ask for something, use 'Ndingakumbireiwo...' (May I ask for...). For example, 'Ndingakumbireiwo mvura' (May I ask for water). Or 'Ndingakumbireiwo peni' (May I ask for a pen). If you need assistance, say 'Ndingabatsirwa here?' (Can I be helped?). The vendor might reply 'Hongu, ndinobatsira' (Yes, I help). Then explain what you need. Use 'Ndinoda' (I want) but soften it with 'Ndapota': 'Ndapota, ndinoda kutenga chingwa' (Please, I want to buy bread).

When you receive something, say 'Ndatenda' (Thank you). For more emphasis, 'Ndatenda zvikuru' (Thank you very much). If someone helps you, say 'Mwaita basa rakanaka' (You did a good job). If you're just looking, say 'Ndiri kungoona' (I'm just looking). If you decide not to buy, say 'Aiwa, ndatenda' (No, thank you). Never be rude. Zimbabweans appreciate polite customers and may give you a better price.

In markets, vendors might call out to you: 'Sisi! Bhura! Uya utenge!' (Sister! Brother! Come buy!). You can respond politely: 'Ndiri kungoona chete' (I'm just looking). Or 'Ndichadzoka' (I'll come back). If you're not interested, 'Aiwa, ndatenda' is fine. If you need to ask a question, say 'Ndine mubvunzo' (I have a question). For example, 'Ndine mubvunzo: Imarii iyi?' (I have a question: How much is this?).

At the end of the transaction, say 'Ndatenda, muswera' (Thank you, have a good day). The vendor will reply 'Ndatendawo' (Thank you too) or 'Fambai zvakanaka' (Go well). If you're with a friend, you can say 'Tichaonana' (See you later). These small courtesies go a long way. Practice them daily. Even if your Shona is limited, a smile and 'Ndapota' and 'Ndatenda' will make your shopping experience pleasant. Remember, respect is the key to successful interactions in Zimbabwe.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Shopping and Money in Shona

This practical reference supports the Rise AFRICA Skills module on Shopping and Money within the Shona Language course. It is designed for African agribusiness operators, market traders, and value-chain actors who buy inputs, negotiate with suppliers, sell produce, and manage cash in Zimbabwe and the wider Shona-speaking region. The focus is on usable language: the words and phrases that move a transaction from greeting to payment.

Zimbabwe's informal economy is a major commercial engine, and much of it runs through open markets, tuckshops, and small retail outlets. According to the International Labour Organization, informal employment accounts for around 80 per cent of total employment in Zimbabwe, with women heavily represented in wholesale and retail trade (ILO, 2018). For agribusiness, this means the ability to ask prices, bargain, specify quantities, and confirm payment methods in Shona is not a cultural nicety it is a direct commercial skill.

1. Names of Shops and Markets

Knowing what to call a place is the first step. Shona uses different nouns for formal shops, open markets, and informal stalls. In agribusiness supply chains, you will often move between a chitoro (tuckshop) for packaged inputs and a musika (market) for bulk produce.

2. Bargaining and Asking Prices

Bargaining (kutaurirana or kudzikisa mutengo) is normal in open markets but less so in formal shops. A business-first approach is to open politely, ask the price, then make a counter-offer based on volume. In agribusiness, volume leverage is your strongest bargaining tool.

Safety Warning Cash and Mobile Money Fraud

Zimbabwe has recorded rising cases of mobile money fraud and counterfeit banknotes in busy markets. Never share your EcoCash or OneMoney PIN with a buyer, seller, or agent. Confirm the transaction SMS on your own handset before releasing goods. For large agribusiness transactions, use a registered bank or a licensed mobile money agent, and insist on a written receipt (risiti). The Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe advises the public to verify agent codes and report suspicious transactions to their bank or mobile money provider immediately.

3. Currency and Payment Methods

Zimbabwe operates a multi-currency system. The Zimbabwe Gold (ZiG) was introduced in April 2024 as the local currency, alongside the United States dollar and other foreign currencies. In practice, agribusiness transactions may be quoted in USD, ZiG, or South African rand, so always confirm the currency before agreeing a price.

Common payment methods include cash (USD or ZiG), EcoCash, OneMoney, bank transfer, and point-of-sale cards. In rural markets, cash and mobile money dominate. For input purchases, ask whether the seller accepts kubhadhara neEcoCash (payment by EcoCash) or requires cash.

4. Clothing and Sizes

When buying workwear, overalls, gumboots, or protective clothing, size vocabulary matters. Shona speakers often use English size terms, but knowing the local phrasing helps.

  • Hembe shirt / top
  • Bhurukwa trousers
  • Bhutsu shoes / boots
  • Siketi skirt
  • Ngowani hat
  • Hombodo overalls / work suit
  • Saizi yangu ndeye... My size is...
  • Ndapota ndipe saizi hombe. Please give me a larger size.
  • Iyi idiki / ihombe. This is too small / too big.

5. Quantities and Measurements

Agribusiness runs on quantities. Whether you are buying 50 kg of maize seed or selling 200 kg of tomatoes, you need clear measurement language. Shona uses both traditional and metric terms.

  • Kilogramu kilogram (kg)
  • Giramu gram (g)
  • Litita litre (L)
  • Tonhi tonne
  • Chitsva a bunch (e.g., of vegetables)
  • Dengu a large basket / sack measure
  • Mukombe a cup / small measure
  • Ndinoda makirogiremu makumi mashanu. I want 50 kilograms.
  • Ipa huremu hwakarurama. Give accurate weight.
  • Chikero chiri kupi? Where is the scale?

Always verify weight on a visible scale (chikero). In grain and fertiliser trade, under-weighing is a common dispute. Ask for the scale to be zeroed before weighing.

6. Polite Requests When Shopping

Politeness opens doors and improves prices. Shona uses ndapota (please), ndinokumbira (I request), and respectful address forms. In business, a calm, respectful tone often secures a better deal than aggressive bargaining.

  • Ndapota, ndipe... Please give me...
  • Ndinokumbira risiti. I request a receipt.
  • Ndapota, ndiratidze... Please show me...
  • Ndinokumbira kuti mundibatsire. I request that you help me.
  • Maita basa. Thank you (respectful).
  • Ndokumbirawo kuona zvimwe. May I also see others.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Greet first: Start with Mhoroi or Mangwanani before asking prices it builds rapport and often improves the deal.
  2. Confirm currency: Ask Imarii? and clarify whether the price is in USD, ZiG, or rand before agreeing.
  3. State volume early: Mention bulk quantity (ndinoda kuwanda) to unlock discounts.
  4. Verify weight: Insist on seeing the chikero (scale) zeroed before grain or fertiliser is weighed.
  5. Protect your PIN: Never share mobile money PINs; confirm the SMS on your own phone before releasing goods.
  6. Ask for a receipt: Use Ndinokumbira risiti for all business transactions, especially inputs and bulk sales.
  7. Know your sizes: For workwear and PPE, state saizi yangu clearly and check fit before paying.
  8. Close politely: End with Maita basa it maintains the trading relationship for future deals.
