1. Common Faults and Diagnostic Techniques

When your solar pump stops working, start with the simplest checks. First, look at the controller display. Many controllers show error codes like E01 for low voltage or E04 for dry run. Write down the code and check the manual. If there is no display, use a multimeter to measure the voltage at the pump terminals. A 24V system should read between 26V and 30V in full sun. If it reads below 22V, the problem is likely in the array or wiring.

Next, check the water source. A clogged intake screen or a fallen dam level can cause the pump to run dry. Remove the pump from the dam and inspect the intake. Clean any mud, leaves, or debris. In many African dams, silt builds up quickly during the rainy season, so check monthly. Also, listen to the pump. A humming sound without water flow often means a stuck impeller or a broken shaft. A grinding noise points to worn bearings.

Use a clamp meter to measure current. For a 1kW pump, expect around 4A at 250V DC. If current is zero, you have an open circuit. If current is very high, you have a short or a seized pump. Check all connections for corrosion. In humid areas, terminals corrode fast. Clean them with sandpaper and apply petroleum jelly. Test the float switch or pressure switch with a multimeter in continuity mode. A faulty switch is a common fault.

Finally, check the solar array. Look for shadows from trees or dust. Even a small shadow on one panel can cut output by 50%. Clean panels with a soft cloth and water. Measure open-circuit voltage of each panel. A 250W panel should read about 37V open-circuit. If one panel reads much lower, it is faulty. By following these steps, you can find most faults in under an hour.

2. Troubleshooting Low Water Output

If your pump runs but delivers less water than before, first measure the actual flow. Use a bucket and a stopwatch. For example, if you expect 10,000 litres per day but only get 4,000, you have a problem. Check the sun conditions. A cloudy day can reduce output by 70%. Compare with a sunny day. If output is still low, check the array tilt. Panels should face north in the southern hemisphere (most of Africa) and be tilted at an angle equal to your latitude. For Nairobi (1S), tilt 5-10; for Johannesburg (26S), tilt 26. A wrong tilt can cut daily output by 20-30%.

Next, inspect the pump intake and impeller. Sand and silt wear impellers quickly. Remove the pump and open the pump end. If impeller blades are worn thin or broken, replace them. In many African dams, fine clay particles act like sandpaper. Install a sediment trap or a pre-filter. Also, check for leaks in the pipes. A small leak on the suction side can suck air and reduce flow. Tighten all joints and replace cracked pipes. Use a pressure gauge to check for blockages. A clogged filter or non-return valve can restrict flow.

Check the controller settings. Some controllers have a maximum power point tracking (MPPT) setting that may be off. Reset to factory defaults or adjust according to the manual. If the controller has a speed setting, ensure it is at 100%. Also, verify that the float switch is not stuck in a partially closed position. Test by bypassing the switch temporarily. If flow increases, replace the switch.

Finally, measure the voltage and current at the pump while running. If voltage is low (below 200V for a 240V system), the array is undersized or there is voltage drop in the cables. Use thicker cables for long runs. For a 50m run at 10A, use at least 6mm copper cable. If current is low but voltage is normal, the pump may be worn. In that case, replace the pump end or the whole pump. Regular maintenance every 6 months prevents most low-output problems.

3. Electrical Fault Finding: Open Circuits, Shorts, Ground Faults

Electrical faults stop pumps dead. You need a digital multimeter and a clamp meter. Start by isolating the array from the controller. Measure the open-circuit voltage (Voc) of the array. For a 24V system, Voc should be around 44V for two 250W panels in series. If Voc is zero, you have an open circuit. Check each panel individually. A broken wire or a blown fuse is common. Look for rodent damagerats love to chew DC cables. Repair with proper MC4 connectors and heat-shrink tubing.

If Voc is normal but the pump does not run, check for a short circuit. Set your multimeter to continuity or resistance mode. With the array disconnected, measure resistance between the positive and negative cables at the controller. It should read infinite (open). If it reads near zero, you have a short. Trace the cables and look for pinched or cut insulation. In dam environments, water can enter junction boxes and cause shorts. Seal all boxes with silicone and use IP68 connectors.

Ground faults are dangerous. Measure resistance between the positive cable and earth ground (a metal stake in the ground). It should be infinite. If you read any resistance, you have a ground fault. This often happens when a cable is damaged and touches the metal pump housing or a wet surface. Disconnect the pump and test again. If the fault disappears, the pump motor is grounded. Replace the pump. If the fault remains, the cable is damaged. Use a megohmmeter (insulation tester) for better accuracy. A good reading is above 1 megohm.

Also, check the controller's internal fuses. Many controllers have a 20A fuse. Test with a multimeter. A blown fuse indicates a short or overload. Never replace with a higher rating. After fixing a fault, test the system with a low-voltage source first, like a 12V battery, to avoid damaging the controller. Always turn off the array before working on wiring. In Africa, many faults come from poor connections due to heat and humidity. Use dielectric grease on all terminals and re-tighten every 6 months.

4. Pump Performance Degradation and Solutions

Over time, every pump loses performance. A new pump might deliver 20,000 litres per day; after two years, it might only give 12,000. The main causes are wear, corrosion, and scaling. In African dams, water often contains sand, silt, and minerals. These act like sandpaper on impellers and seals. First, measure the pump's flow and head. Use a pressure gauge at the outlet and a bucket to measure flow. Compare with the pump's original curve. If flow is down by more than 20%, it's time for maintenance.

Open the pump and inspect the impeller. Worn impellers have rounded edges or cracks. Replace the impeller and wear rings. For a typical 1kW pump, an impeller kit costs $50-$100. Also, check the mechanical seal. A leaking seal lets water into the motor, causing rust and short circuits. Replace seals every 2 years. In sandy water, use a silicon carbide seal for longer life. Clean the motor windings with electrical contact cleaner and dry them in the sun before reassembly.

Scaling from calcium and magnesium is common in boreholes and some dams. Scale reduces the impeller clearance and blocks cooling passages. Descale with a weak acid solution (5% citric acid) for 30 minutes, then rinse. Never use strong acid. For solar pumps, also check the controller's maximum power point tracking. If the controller is old, it may not track well. Upgrading to a modern MPPT controller can recover 10-15% of lost output. Also, check the array for degradation. Solar panels lose about 0.5% per year. After 10 years, a 250W panel might only produce 230W. Clean panels regularly and replace any with cracked glass or discoloration.

Finally, adjust the pump's operating point. If the dam level has dropped, the pump may be running at a higher head, reducing flow. Lower the pump or add a booster pump. For a dam, use a floating pump or a submersible on a adjustable stand. Regular maintenance every 6 monthscleaning, tightening, and lubricatingcan double the pump's life. Keep a log of flow and pressure to spot degradation early.

5. Optimizing Array Tilt and Orientation

Solar panels make most power when sunlight hits them straight on. In Africa, the sun is always in the north for southern hemisphere countries, and in the south for northern hemisphere countries. For most of sub-Saharan Africa, panels should face north. The correct tilt angle is your latitude. For Nairobi (1S), tilt 5-10; for Harare (17S), tilt 17; for Cape Town (34S), tilt 34. If you are in West Africa, like Ghana (8N), face south and tilt 8. A wrong orientation can cut daily output by 20-40%.

You can adjust tilt seasonally. In summer, the sun is higher, so use a lower tilt (latitude minus 10). In winter, use a higher tilt (latitude plus 10). For a fixed system, set tilt at latitude. But if you rely on the pump year-round, seasonal adjustment gives up to 15% more water. Use a simple inclinometer or a smartphone app to measure tilt. Mark the frame with paint so you can return to the same angle. For a dam pump, you may need to mount panels on a ground rack, not on a roof, to allow adjustment.

Avoid shading at all costs. Even a small shadow from a tree branch or a pole can drop output by 50%. In the morning and afternoon, shadows are long. Walk around your array at 9am, noon, and 3pm. Cut any trees or move the array. Also, clean panels weekly in dusty areas. Dust from the Sahel can reduce output by 1% per day if not cleaned. Use a soft brush and water. Never clean hot panels with cold waterthey can crack.

For a dam pump, you can also use a tracking system. A single-axis tracker follows the sun east to west and can increase output by 25-30%. But trackers cost more and need maintenance. For small systems, manual adjustment is cheaper. If you have multiple panels, wire them in series to match the controller's voltage. For a 24V system, use two 250W panels in series. For a 48V system, use four. Always check the controller's maximum input voltage. In Africa, high temperatures reduce panel voltage by about 0.4% per C. So a 250W panel rated at 37V might only give 33V at 50C. Account for this when sizing cables and controllers.

6. Data Logging and Performance Analysis

You cannot improve what you do not measure. Start a simple logbook. Every day, write down: date, time, weather (sunny, cloudy, rainy), water output (litres), pump run hours, and any error codes. Use a 1-litre jug to calibrate a larger container. For example, fill a 200-litre drum and time it. Then you can estimate daily output. If you have a flow meter, even better. A basic mechanical flow meter costs $30 and fits in the pipe. Record the reading at the start and end of each day.

For electrical data, use a cheap data logger. A USB voltage and current logger costs $50. Connect it between the array and controller. It will record voltage, current, and power every minute. Download the data weekly to a laptop or phone. Look for patterns. If power drops every afternoon, it might be due to shading or a hot controller. If voltage is low in the morning, the array may be undersized. Compare daily energy (watt-hours) with water output. A good system might use 5 kWh to pump 20,000 litres. If energy is high but water is low, the pump is inefficient.

Analyze your data monthly. Calculate the performance ratio: actual water output divided by expected output. Expected output = (array power peak sun hours pump efficiency) / (head 9.81). For a 1kW array, 5 peak sun hours, 50% efficiency, and 20m head, expected output = (1000 5 0.5) / (20 9.81) = 12.7 m/day. If you get 8 m, your performance ratio is 0.63. That means you are losing 37% to faults, dirt, or wrong tilt. Investigate the biggest losses first.

Use the data to schedule maintenance. If flow drops by 10% in a month, clean the panels and check the intake. If current spikes, check for a short. If voltage is low on sunny days, test each panel. Keep logs for at least a year to see seasonal changes. In Africa, the rainy season brings clouds and silt. Your log will tell you when to expect lower output. Share your data with other farmers. Rise Africa Skills can help you set up a simple Excel sheet or a free app like PumpLogger. With good data, you can optimize your system and avoid costly breakdowns.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Troubleshooting & Performance Optimization for Solar Pumping Systems

For an African agribusiness, a solar pump is not just equipment it is the engine of irrigation, livestock watering, and fish farming. When it underperforms, the cost is measured in wilted crops, lost market windows, and wasted capital. This practical reference consolidates the fault-finding logic you need to keep water flowing, using field-proven diagnostics and optimization techniques. It assumes a typical sub-Saharan installation: a photovoltaic array, a variable-frequency drive (VFD) or maximum power point tracking (MPPT) controller, a submersible or surface pump, and a dam, borehole, or river source.

1. Common Faults and Diagnostic Techniques

Most solar pump failures in African field conditions fall into three buckets: electrical, hydraulic, and solar resource. The field diagnostic sequence should always follow the energy path from array to controller to pump to water. Start with a visual inspection: burnt connectors, rodent damage, corroded terminals, and cracked panels are common in rural installations. Then use a clamp meter to measure DC voltage and current at the array and controller input. A healthy array under full sun should produce within 10% of its rated open-circuit voltage. If voltage is present but current is near zero, suspect an open circuit or a failed MPPT. If current is high but voltage collapses, suspect a short or ground fault.

For hydraulic faults, measure static water level and dynamic water level while the pump runs. A pump that runs but delivers no water often has a clogged intake screen, a broken drop pipe, or a worn impeller. In sandy or silty dams, intake clogging is the number one cause of low output. Use a pressure gauge on the discharge line to check head. If the pump is running at high pressure but low flow, the impeller or check valve is likely worn.

2. Troubleshooting Low Water Output

Low output is the most common complaint. The diagnostic tree is simple: is the pump receiving enough power, and is the water path clear? First, check solar irradiance. A panel that is dirty, shaded, or misaligned can lose 2040% of its output. Second, check the controller's MPPT tracking some low-cost controllers lose efficiency at high temperatures. Third, check the pump itself. A submersible pump with a worn impeller may still run but deliver half its rated flow. In Africa, where pumps often run 810 hours per day, impeller wear is accelerated by abrasive silt. Always install a pre-filter or settling basin if your dam has high sediment load.

3. Electrical Fault Finding: Open Circuits, Shorts, Ground Faults

Electrical faults are the most dangerous and the most misdiagnosed. An open circuit means no current flows often a broken wire, a blown fuse, or a loose MC4 connector. A short circuit means current takes a low-resistance path, often causing overheating or fire. A ground fault means current leaks to earth, which can electrify the pump casing or the water itself. Use an insulation resistance tester (megohmmeter) to check the pump motor and cable insulation. A reading below 1 M indicates a ground fault. Always isolate the array before testing. In Africa, lightning-induced surges are a leading cause of controller failure install surge protection devices (SPDs) on both DC and AC sides.

SAFETY WARNING ELECTRICAL HAZARD

Solar arrays cannot be switched off like a grid supply they generate voltage whenever exposed to light. Before opening any junction box, disconnect the array and verify zero voltage with a multimeter. Never work on a pump controller or motor while the array is connected. Ground faults can electrify water and pump casings, causing lethal shock. Use insulated tools, wear rubber gloves, and ensure the system is properly earthed. If you are not a qualified electrician, do not attempt electrical fault finding call a certified technician.

4. Pump Performance Degradation and Solutions

Performance degradation is gradual and often ignored until the pump fails. The main causes are impeller wear, motor winding degradation, cable insulation aging, and controller drift. In a dam-based system, silt and debris accelerate wear. The solution is a maintenance schedule: inspect intake screens weekly, flush the pump monthly, and test insulation resistance quarterly. If flow drops by more than 15% from baseline, schedule a pump pull and inspection. In many African contexts, replacing a worn impeller is far cheaper than replacing the entire pump keep spare impellers and seals on hand.

5. Optimizing Array Tilt and Orientation

Array tilt is the cheapest performance upgrade you can make. In Africa, most installations are fixed-tilt, but seasonal adjustment can boost annual yield by 515%. For a site at 15N (e.g., Senegal, Mali), the optimal tilt is roughly latitude for year-round, but 1015 higher in winter and 1015 lower in summer. For a site at 15S (e.g., Zambia, Zimbabwe), the logic reverses. Orientation should always be true north in the southern hemisphere and true south in the northern hemisphere. Avoid east-west orientation unless you have a tracking system. Shading is the silent killer even a single shaded cell can reduce string output by 30%. Keep vegetation cleared and mount panels at least 1 m above ground to avoid dust and animal damage.

6. Data Logging and Performance Analysis

You cannot optimize what you do not measure. A simple data logger even a low-cost voltage and current logger can transform your maintenance from reactive to predictive. Track daily water output, pump runtime, array voltage, and current. Compare against baseline. A 10% drop in flow over one month is a warning sign. In Africa, where grid power is unreliable, solar data logging also helps you prove system performance to financiers or cooperatives. Use a simple spreadsheet or a free tool like the Global Solar Atlas to compare expected yield against actual. If your pump produces less than 80% of expected, investigate immediately.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Visual first: Check for burnt connectors, rodent damage, cracked panels, and water ingress.
  • Measure voltage and current at array and controller with a clamp meter under full sun.
  • Test insulation resistance with a megohmmeter below 1 M means ground fault.
  • Check intake screen and impeller monthly; clean or replace as needed.
  • Adjust array tilt seasonally 1015 change can boost output by 515%.
  • Log daily water output and compare to baseline; investigate >10% drop.
  • Install surge protection on DC and AC sides to prevent lightning damage.
  • Keep spare impellers, seals, and fuses on site downtime costs more than parts.
