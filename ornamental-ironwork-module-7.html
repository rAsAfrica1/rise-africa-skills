1. Introduction and Why It Matters

A gate is often the first thing a visitor sees at a home or business. In many African communities, a well-built metal gate signals security, status, and craftsmanship. For you as an ornamental ironworker, gate construction is a core skill that can earn steady income. Unlike a simple fence, a gate must open and close smoothly, withstand daily use, and look good. This module will teach you how to design and build gates that are strong, safe, and attractive.

Why focus on gates? Because every compound, shop, and school needs at least one. In cities like Lagos, Nairobi, or Accra, demand for custom gates is high. A basic single swing gate can sell for 50,000 to 150,000 Naira (or equivalent) depending on size and design. With the right skills, you can produce 23 gates per month and build a reputation. But poor work leads to sagging hinges, rust, and unhappy customers. So learn it right.

This module covers six lessons: introduction, tools and materials, step-by-step method, common mistakes, safety, and a practical exercise. You will learn to measure, cut, weld, and finish a gate from start to finish. We assume you have basic welding and metalworking skills, but even if you are new, follow along carefully. By the end, you will be able to design a simple single swing gate and build it to last.

Remember: a gate is not just a barrier; it is a product you sell. Quality work brings referrals. Take pride in every joint and every weld. Let's get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To build a gate, you need the right tools and materials. Without them, you will waste time and produce weak work. Here is a practical list for a basic single swing gate, 3 feet wide and 6 feet tall.

Tools: A welding machine (arc welder, 200250 amps) with electrodes (2.5mm or 3.2mm, e.g., E6013). An angle grinder with cutting and grinding discs. A tape measure (5m), try square, spirit level, and chalk or marker. A hacksaw for small cuts. A drill with metal bits for hinges. A hammer, chisel, and files. Clamps to hold pieces. Safety gear: welding helmet, gloves, apron, boots, and goggles.

Materials: Mild steel square tubes (e.g., 25mm x 25mm x 2mm) for the frame. Flat bars (25mm x 5mm) for decorative scrolls or hinges. Round bars (10mm or 12mm) for pickets. Hinges (weld-on or bolt-on, 23 per gate). A latch or lock. Primer and paint (anti-rust). Concrete mix for posts (if installing).

Where to buy: In most African cities, visit a steel merchant or scrap market. For example, in Kano, Nigeria, the Sabon Gari market has steel. In Nairobi, check Industrial Area. Buy from a trusted supplier to avoid weak steel. Always measure twice and cut once. Keep a small notebook to record your cut list. Pre-cut all pieces before welding to save time. With these tools and materials, you are ready to build.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to build a simple single swing gate. We assume a finished size of 3ft wide x 6ft tall.

Step 1: Design and measure. Sketch your gate. Decide on frame, pickets, and decoration. Measure the opening where the gate will hang. Allow 1/2 inch clearance on each side and bottom for movement. So if opening is 37 inches wide, gate should be 36 inches wide. Height: 72 inches.

Step 2: Cut pieces. For the frame, cut two vertical tubes 72 inches long, and two horizontal tubes 36 inches long. For pickets, cut round bars 60 inches long (leaving space for top and bottom rails). You need about 7 pickets spaced 4 inches apart. Cut flat bars for scrolls if desired.

Step 3: Build the frame. Lay the tubes on a flat surface (a welding table or concrete floor). Use clamps to hold the rectangle square. Check with a try square. Weld the corners. Grind smooth.

Step 4: Add pickets. Mark positions on the top and bottom rails. Tack weld each picket in place. Check spacing and vertical alignment. Then weld fully.

Step 5: Attach hinges and latch. Weld one hinge leaf to the gate frame and the other to the post. For a 3ft gate, use two hinges (top and bottom). For heavier gates, use three. Weld a latch or lock box on the opposite side.

Step 6: Finish. Grind all welds. Clean with a wire brush. Apply primer, then two coats of paint. Let dry.

Step 7: Install. Set posts in concrete, ensuring they are plumb. Hang the gate, check swing. Adjust if needed. Lubricate hinges. Your gate is ready.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced welders make mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them.

Mistake 1: Gate not square. If the frame is not square, the gate will not close properly. Fix: Always check diagonals. Measure from top-left to bottom-right and top-right to bottom-left. They must be equal. If not, adjust before welding.

Mistake 2: Weak welds. Poor penetration causes cracks. Fix: Use correct amperage (e.g., 90120 amps for 2.5mm electrode). Clean metal before welding. Practice on scrap.

Mistake 3: Hinges placed wrong. If hinges are too far apart or misaligned, gate will sag. Fix: Align hinges perfectly. Use a level. For a 6ft gate, top hinge about 6 inches from top, bottom hinge about 6 inches from bottom. Weld securely.

Mistake 4: No allowance for expansion. Metal expands in heat. If gate fits too tight, it will jam. Fix: Leave 1/4 to 1/2 inch gap around gate.

Mistake 5: Poor rust protection. Bare steel rusts quickly, especially in humid or coastal areas. Fix: Apply primer and at least two coats of outdoor paint. Use galvanized steel if budget allows.

Mistake 6: Ignoring safety. Welding without helmet or gloves causes injury. Fix: Always wear PPE. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby.

Mistake 7: Not testing. Always test gate swing before final installation. Fix: Hang gate temporarily, open and close. Adjust as needed. Learn from each mistake. A good gate is a series of small correct decisions.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional. As an ornamental ironworker, you face hazards: burns, eye damage, cuts, and electrical shock. Follow these rules to stay safe and produce quality work.

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE): Always wear a welding helmet with proper shade (shade 10 for arc welding). Use leather gloves, apron, and steel-toe boots. Wear safety glasses when grinding or cutting. Use earplugs if noise is loud. Never weld in shorts or open shoes.

Workshop safety: Keep your work area clean. Remove flammable materials. Ensure good ventilationwelding fumes are toxic. Use a welding screen to protect others. Check electrical cords for damage. Ground your welder properly. Keep a fire extinguisher and first aid kit nearby.

Best practices: Plan your work. Measure twice, cut once. Use jigs or clamps for repeatability. Keep your tools sharp and maintained. Weld on a flat, fireproof surface. Grind welds smooth for a professional finish. Paint immediately after cleaning to prevent rust.

Ergonomics: Lift with your legs, not your back. Use a trolley for heavy steel. Take breaks to avoid fatigue.

Environmental care: Dispose of metal scraps responsibly. Recycle where possible. Avoid breathing zinc fumes from galvanized steelwear a respirator.

By following these practices, you protect yourself and your customers. A safe worker is a productive worker. Remember, no gate is worth an injury.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to build a gate. This exercise will guide you through making a small single swing gate, 2 feet wide x 4 feet tall. Use scrap or new steel.

Materials: 20mm square tubes for frame (2 pieces 48 inches, 2 pieces 24 inches). 10mm round bars for pickets (5 pieces 36 inches). 2 hinges, 1 latch. Primer and paint.

Tools: Welder, grinder, tape measure, square, clamps, drill, safety gear.

Steps:
1. Cut all pieces to length. Clean ends.
2. Lay frame tubes on flat surface. Clamp square. Check diagonals. Weld corners.
3. Mark picket positions on top and bottom rails: space evenly, about 4 inches apart. Tack weld pickets. Check vertical. Weld fully.
4. Grind welds smooth.
5. Attach hinges to one side: top hinge 4 inches from top, bottom hinge 4 inches from bottom. Weld. Attach latch on other side.
6. Clean with wire brush. Apply primer, then two coats of paint. Let dry.
7. Install on a post (you can use a wooden post for practice). Check swing.

Evaluation: Does gate open and close smoothly? Are welds strong? Is it square? Is paint even? Take a photo for your portfolio.

Time: 46 hours. Cost: about 5,00010,000 Naira (or equivalent) for materials. This small gate teaches all key skills. Repeat until confident. Then move to larger gates.

<h2>Practical Reference: Gate Design and Construction</h2>

<p>This practical reference is the workshop companion to the Gate Design and Construction module. It is written for the working ornamental ironworker, the small fabrication business owner, and the apprentice who wants to move from "I can weld" to "I can quote, build, and deliver a gate that earns repeat business." In the African market where security, status, and durability drive demand a gate is rarely just a gate. It is a shop's business card, a client's first line of defence, and a product that must survive heat, dust, humidity, and heavy daily use.</p>

<p>Use this lesson alongside your tools and materials notes and your step-by-step method. The goal here is to give you the numbers, standards, and checks that keep you profitable and safe on real jobs.</p>

<h3>1. Reading the Job Before You Cut Steel</h3>

<p>Every gate starts with a site assessment. Before quoting, measure the opening (width, height, and diagonals to check squareness), confirm the wall or pillar type (concrete, block, brick, or steel post), note the ground slope, and identify the swing direction and hinge side. A gate that binds after installation is almost always a measuring or foundation problem, not a welding problem.</p>

<p>For African conditions, also record: prevailing wind direction (for large sheeted gates), dust and rain exposure, and whether the client wants a pedestrian wicket gate integrated. A double gate with a wicket is a strong seller for residential compounds because it separates vehicle and foot traffic.</p>

<h3>3. Spacing, Clearance, and Security Standards</h3>

<p>Bar spacing is a design and security decision. For security gates, vertical bars should be close enough to prevent a hand or tool passing through typically 100120 mm centres for residential, and 80100 mm for high-security applications. For child safety on pool or play-area gates, spacing must not allow a 100 mm sphere to pass (a common international benchmark).</p>

<p>Ground clearance matters in African conditions: allow 2550 mm between the gate bottom and the finished ground level to accommodate dust, gravel, and minor ground movement. On sloped driveways, a rising or sliding gate may be required instead of a swing gate.</p>

<strong style="color:#c00"> SAFETY WARNING WELDING AND GRINDING</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0">Welding galvanised or painted steel releases toxic fumes (zinc oxide, lead, and solvents). Always grind back coatings to bare metal before welding, work in a well-ventilated area or outdoors, and wear a P2/P3 respirator not just a dust mask. Arc eye and flash burns are permanent injuries: use a proper welding helmet with the correct shade (minimum shade 10 for arc welding), and screen the work area so passers-by and apprentices are not exposed. Keep a dry powder or CO fire extinguisher within 3 metres of the welding station. Never weld near fuel, paint thinners, or LPG cylinders.</p>
</div>

<h3>4. Business-First Quoting and Pricing</h3>

<p>An ornamental gate is a manufactured product with a service component. Price it that way. A simple cost model for an African workshop:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Material cost:</strong> steel mass (from Table 1) local price per kg, plus consumables (electrodes, discs, paint, hinges, lock).</li>
<li><strong>Labour:</strong> cutting, welding, grinding, finishing estimate hours honestly; most small gates take 816 hours for a two-person team.</li>
<li><strong>Overheads:</strong> power, gas, wear on machines, workshop rent add 1525%.</li>
<li><strong>Transport and installation:</strong> never absorb this; it is a separate line item.</li>
<li><strong>Margin:</strong> 2540% on residential work is normal; higher for custom design and tight deadlines.</li>
</ul>

<p>A common mistake is quoting per gate rather than per kilogram plus labour. When steel prices move and in many African markets they move sharply per-gate pricing destroys your margin. Quote with a validity period (e.g. 14 days) and state that steel price fluctuations may adjust the final invoice.</p>

<h3>5. Finishing for African Conditions</h3>

<p>Corrosion is the number one killer of ornamental gates in coastal and humid regions. A three-stage finish is the minimum: (1) remove all mill scale, rust, and welding spatter; (2) apply a zinc-rich primer or hot-dip galvanise if budget allows; (3) topcoat with a UV-stable enamel or 2-pack polyurethane. In coastal cities such as Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Lagos, and Durban, hot-dip galvanising plus topcoat can double gate life compared with paint alone.</p>

<p>For inland, dusty environments, choose a matte or satin finish gloss shows dust and scratches. Black, charcoal, and bronze remain the best-selling colours across the continent, with white and green popular in East African residential estates.</p>

<h3>6. Installation Checks That Prevent Callbacks</h3>

<p>Most gate failures are installation failures. Before you leave site:</p>

<ul>
<li>Check the gate swings freely through its full arc without binding.</li>
<li>Confirm the gate rests level and the latch engages without lifting.</li>
<li>Verify posts are plumb and the gate does not sag when opened 90.</li>
<li>Test the lock and handle from both sides.</li>
<li>Check ground clearance is consistent across the opening.</li>
<li>Touch up any paint damaged during installation.</li>
<li>Demonstrate operation to the client and hand over a simple care note.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Measure opening width, height, and diagonals confirm square before cutting.</li>
<li>Estimate gate weight from Table 1 and select hinges and posts accordingly.</li>
<li>Set vertical bar spacing to 100120 mm (80100 mm for high security).</li>
<li>Include a diagonal brace on every leaf over 1.2 m wide to prevent sag.</li>
<li>Allow 2550 mm ground clearance; more on sloped or gravel driveways.</li>
<li>Grind coatings off before welding; wear a P2/P3 respirator and shade 10+ helmet.</li>
<li>Apply zinc-rich primer plus UV-stable topcoat; galvanise for coastal jobs.</li>
<li>Quote per kg plus labour with a 14-day validity never a flat per-gate price.</li>
</ol>
