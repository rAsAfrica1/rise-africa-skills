1. Reading Bar Bending Schedules and Cutting Lists on African Sites

Welcome to Module 5, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on the single most important document a steel fixer will ever hold: the bar bending schedule, often called the BBS, and its close cousin the cutting list. On many sites across Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, South Africa, Tanzania and Uganda, the BBS is the difference between profit and loss. If you cut steel wrongly you cannot weld it back. Steel is money. Every metre you waste is money taken from your employer or from your own pocket if you are a subcontractor. So let us learn to read these documents like a professional. A bar bending schedule is a table that tells you, for every structural element, the bar mark, the bar diameter, the shape code, the number of bars required, the length of each bar, and the total weight. The shape code is a standard reference, usually to BS 8666 or a local adaptation, that shows you how the bar must be bent. For example shape code 00 is a straight bar. Shape code 11 is an L shape. Shape code 21 is a U shape. Shape code 51 is a closed link or stirrup. You must memorise the common shape codes because on site nobody will give you a picture for every bar. The cutting list is a simplified version, often prepared by the foreman, that groups bars by diameter and total length so the steel yard can prepare stock. Your first task every morning is to compare the BBS with the latest revision of the structural drawings. Revisions are dangerous. A drawing may be marked Rev C while your BBS is Rev B. If you cut to the old revision you will produce hundreds of bars that are too short or too long. In Africa, where drawing distribution can be slow and internet is not always reliable, revision control is a daily discipline. Keep a small notebook. Write the revision number and date on every page you use. Before cutting any bar, check three things: the bar mark, the diameter, and the total length including hooks and bends. A common mistake is to forget the bend deductions. When you bend a bar, the steel stretches on the outside and compresses on the inside. The true length of the bar before bending is not simply the sum of the straight lengths. You must subtract a deduction for each bend. For a 90 degree bend the deduction is typically 2 times the diameter. For a 135 degree bend it is 3 times the diameter. For a 180 degree hook it is 4 times the diameter. These figures vary slightly with code and bender, so always confirm with your engineer. If you ignore deductions your links will be too big and will not fit inside the column or beam. That means recutting, and recutting means waste. Now let us talk about practical African context. Many sites do not have a dedicated steel yard with a covered cutting area. You may be cutting on bare ground under the sun. In that case, organise your steel by diameter and bar mark. Use wooden sleepers to keep bars off the ground so they do not rust or pick up mud. Paint or tag the ends of cut bars with the bar mark using a marker or a metal tag. Do not rely on memory. In a busy site with fifty workers, memory fails. Another Africa-specific issue is the supply of steel. Imported steel may come in 12 metre lengths. Local steel may come in 11.8 or 12.2 metres. Always measure the actual stock length before you plan your cuts. If you assume 12 metres and the stock is 11.8, your cutting plan will fail. Use a cutting optimisation method. The simplest is to list all required lengths for one diameter and try to combine them so that offcuts are minimised. For example if you need twenty bars of 3.5 metres and fifteen bars of 2.2 metres, you can cut one 12 metre bar into three 3.5 metre pieces plus one 1.5 metre offcut. That offcut can be used for links or spacers. Never throw away an offcut longer than 500 millimetres. Keep a rack for offcuts sorted by diameter. A good steel fixer saves his employer up to ten percent of steel cost through smart cutting. That is a business skill, not just a trade skill. Finally, learn to calculate weight. Steel weight is approximately 0.006165 times diameter squared, in kilogrammes per metre, when diameter is in millimetres. So a 12 millimetre bar weighs about 0.888 kilogrammes per metre. A 16 millimetre bar weighs about 1.578 kilogrammes per metre. A 20 millimetre bar weighs about 2.466 kilogrammes per metre. This matters because steel is bought by weight, not by length. If you know the weight you can check deliveries and prevent theft or short supply. In summary, the BBS and cutting list are your daily tools. Respect revisions. Calculate bend deductions. Measure actual stock. Optimise cuts. Save offcuts. Know your weights. Do these things and you will be a steel fixer who is trusted with responsibility, and responsibility leads to better pay and more work.

2. Fixing Columns, Beams and Slabs: Sequence and Quality Control

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 5. Now that you can read a bar bending schedule, we move to the actual fixing of reinforcement in columns, beams and slabs. The sequence of work matters as much as the quality. If you fix in the wrong order you will block yourself in and have to undo good work. In Africa, where labour is often available but supervision is thin, a steel fixer who knows the correct sequence becomes a leader on site. Let us start with columns. A column is a vertical member that carries load from beams and slabs down to the foundation. The main bars, also called longitudinal bars, run vertically. The links, also called stirrups or ties, wrap around the main bars. The first step is to check the starter bars coming out of the foundation or the slab below. These must be in the correct position, the correct diameter, and the correct length of lap. A lap is the overlap between two bars that transfers force from one to the other. Lap length depends on diameter, concrete grade, and whether the bar is in tension or compression. A common rule of thumb is forty times the diameter for tension laps in normal concrete, but always follow the engineer's drawing. If the starter bars are bent or misplaced, you must straighten them carefully or drill and grout new bars. Never cut a starter bar short without approval. Next, set the vertical bars. Use a column cage or a template at the top to hold the bars at the correct spacing. The spacing is measured centre to centre. Use a steel tape, not a piece of wood. Plumb the bars with a spirit level or a plumb bob. A column that is out of plumb by more than the tolerance, often ten millimetres in three metres, will cause problems for the formwork and the finished structure. Then fix the links. Links must be spaced correctly, usually closer together near the top and bottom of the column where shear forces are highest. The spacing is given on the drawing. Start from the bottom and work up. Each link must be tied to the main bars with binding wire. Use a figure of eight tie or a double tie so the link cannot slip. The hooks of the link must be bent into the concrete, not sticking out. For a closed link the hook is usually 135 degrees with a ten diameter extension. In seismic areas the hook must be 135 degrees. In non-seismic areas 90 degrees may be allowed, but check. Now beams. A beam is a horizontal member that carries load from the slab to the columns. Beams have top bars, bottom bars, and links. Sometimes they also have side bars or skin reinforcement. The bottom bars carry tension in the middle of the span. The top bars carry tension over the supports. So the top bars must be continuous over the column and must have the correct lap or anchorage into the column. The sequence is: first place the bottom bars, then the links, then the top bars. Or you can assemble the cage on the ground and lift it into the formwork. On many African sites the cage is assembled on the ground because it is easier and safer. If you assemble on the ground, make sure the ground is level and clean. Use spacers to keep the bars at the correct cover. Cover is the distance from the outside of the concrete to the outside of the reinforcement. Cover protects the steel from rust and fire. Typical cover is twenty five millimetres for beams and slabs, forty millimetres for columns, and fifty millimetres for foundations in contact with ground. In coastal areas or where there is salt, cover must be increased. Use plastic spacers or concrete blocks, not stones or wood. Stones can crack and wood can rot. Now slabs. A slab is a flat horizontal member that carries floor or roof load. Slabs have bottom bars in two directions, top bars over supports, and sometimes top bars in the middle for cantilevers. The bottom bars are usually the main bars and run in the shorter direction. The distribution bars run in the longer direction. The sequence is: first mark the positions on the formwork, then place the bottom bars, then the distribution bars, then the top bars and chairs. Chairs are supports that hold the top bars at the correct height. Without chairs the top bars will be pushed down during concrete pouring and will not work. This is one of the most common defects in African construction. I have seen slabs where the top bars were sitting on the bottom bars, giving zero effective depth. That slab will crack. So always use enough chairs. Space them at about one metre in both directions. Tie the bars at every intersection, or at least every second intersection. Use binding wire that is annealed and soft. Do not use rusty wire. The wire should be cut with a hook, not with your teeth. Quality control is your responsibility. Check the following before you call the engineer for inspection: bar diameter and spacing, lap lengths, link spacing, cover, chairs, and cleanliness. Remove any mud, oil, or loose rust. If the steel is rusty, wire brush it. If it is oily, wipe it with solvent. Concrete does not stick to oil. Also check that the bars are not too close together. If the gap between bars is less than the aggregate size plus twenty millimetres, the concrete cannot flow. In that case you must adjust the spacing or use smaller aggregate. Finally, think about safety. Steel fixing is dangerous. Wear gloves, boots, and a helmet. Be careful when lifting cages. Use a crane or a team lift, not your back. Keep the site tidy. A tidy site is a safe site. In summary, columns first, then beams, then slabs. Starters, verticals, links. Bottoms, links, tops. Mark, place, tie, chair. Check cover, lap, spacing. Clean and safe. Do this and your work will pass inspection the first time, which saves time and money for everyone.

3. Lapping, Anchorage and Development Length in Practice

Welcome to Lesson 3. In this lesson we go deep into lapping, anchorage and development length. These are the technical heart of steel fixing. If you get them wrong the structure can fail, even if everything looks neat. In Africa, where many projects are designed by engineers in cities but built in rural areas, the steel fixer must understand the principles, not just copy a drawing. Let us start with the basic idea. Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension. Steel is strong in both. So we put steel where the concrete will crack. But steel only works if it is bonded to the concrete. Bond is the grip between the steel surface and the concrete. If the bond fails, the bar slips and the concrete cracks. Lapping is when we overlap two bars to transfer force from one to the other. Anchorage is when we embed a bar into concrete so it can develop its full strength. Development length is the length of embedment needed to develop the yield strength of the bar. The formula for development length depends on bar diameter, concrete strength, steel strength, and the position of the bar. A bar in the top of a beam has poorer bond because of bleeding and settlement, so its development length is longer. A bar with a epoxy coating has poorer bond. A bar in lightweight concrete has poorer bond. In normal conditions, a common rule is forty times the diameter for tension lap in grade thirty concrete with high yield steel. But this is only a rule of thumb. Always use the drawing. Now let us talk about lap types. There are three main types: contact lap, non-contact lap, and welded lap. Contact lap is when the two bars touch. Non-contact lap is when they are separated by a small distance, usually not more than four times the diameter or fifty millimetres. Welded lap is when they are welded, but this is rare in Africa because welding rebar requires skilled welders and can weaken the steel if not done correctly. In most cases we use contact lap or non-contact lap. The lap length must be measured along the bar, not along the centreline of the structure. So if you have a vertical bar and a starter bar, the lap is the vertical distance of overlap. Do not measure diagonally. Also, stagger your laps. If all the bars in a column lap at the same level, that section is weaker. Stagger the laps so that only half the bars lap at any one section. The stagger distance is usually the lap length plus a bit. For example if the lap is six hundred millimetres, stagger by one metre. Now anchorage. At the end of a bar, you need an anchorage. A straight bar can be anchored by extending it into a column or a beam. A bent bar can be anchored with a hook. A hook is a bend of 90 degrees or 135 degrees with a straight extension. The extension length is usually twelve times the diameter for a 90 degree hook and ten times the diameter for a 135 degree hook. But again, check the drawing. In Africa, many small projects use standard hooks because they are easy to make. But a hook is not a substitute for proper development length. If the drawing says a bar must extend one metre into the support, a hook does not reduce that length. The hook only helps to anchor the bar after the straight length is provided. Now let us talk about practical problems. Problem one: bars are cut too short. This happens when the steel fixer does not read the BBS correctly or when the cutting list is wrong. If a bar is too short, you cannot simply weld another piece on unless the engineer approves. Welding can create a brittle zone. The correct solution is to cut a new bar. Problem two: laps are not staggered. This happens when the foreman does not plan the bar lengths. You can fix it by recutting some bars, but that wastes steel. Better to plan from the start. Problem three: bars are not clean. Mud, oil, and loose rust reduce bond. Clean the bars before fixing. Problem four: bars are not tied properly. A bar that is not tied will move during concrete pouring. Use enough binding wire. Tie at every intersection or at least every second intersection. Problem five: cover is not maintained. Use spacers. In Africa, plastic spacers are sometimes stolen or not available. You can make concrete spacers on site using a small mould. Make them the same strength as the concrete. Do not use stones. Problem six: laps are in the wrong place. Laps should be in zones of low stress, not at mid-span of a beam or at the base of a column. The drawing will show the lap zones. Follow them. Now let us talk about development length in a practical way. Imagine you are fixing a beam. The bottom bars must extend into the column by a certain length. That length is the development length. If the column is too small, you may need to use a hook or a mechanical anchor. Mechanical anchors are couplers or headed bars. They are expensive but sometimes necessary. In Africa, mechanical couplers are becoming more common on large projects because they save steel and reduce congestion. But they require special equipment and training. If you are not trained, do not use them. Finally, let us talk about quality control. Before concrete is poured, check every lap and anchorage. Measure the lap length with a tape. Check the stagger. Check the hooks. Check the cover. Take photographs. In Africa, where disputes can arise later, photographs are your evidence. Keep a record. Sign the inspection sheet. If the engineer asks for a change, get it in writing. Do not accept verbal instructions for structural changes. In summary, lapping and anchorage are about bond. Bond needs length, cleanliness, and cover. Stagger your laps. Use hooks correctly. Maintain cover. Tie properly. Check and record. Do these things and your structure will be safe and durable for many years.

4. Site Organisation, Safety and Productivity for Steel Fixers

Welcome to Lesson 4. In this lesson we move from technical skills to site organisation, safety and productivity. In Africa, steel fixing is often done by teams of casual workers under a foreman. The difference between a profitable project and a loss-making project is organisation. A well-organised team fixes more steel, wastes less, and has fewer accidents. Let us start with the steel yard. The steel yard is where you store, cut, and bend steel. It should be close to the work area but not in the way of concrete trucks or cranes. It should be on firm, level ground. If possible, it should be covered to protect steel from rain. Rust is the enemy of bond. In coastal areas, salt in the air accelerates rust. So keep steel off the ground on wooden sleepers. Sort steel by diameter. Use racks or bins. Label each rack with the diameter and the bar mark. Keep a chalkboard or a whiteboard with the day's cutting list. At the end of the day, count the remaining stock and compare with the records. This prevents theft, which is a major problem on some sites. Now let us talk about cutting. Cutting can be done with a hacksaw, a bolt cutter, a shear, or an angle grinder. For large diameters, use a shear or a cut-off saw. Always wear eye protection. Sparks and metal chips can blind you. When cutting with an angle grinder, make sure the disc is rated for metal and is not cracked. Do not remove the guard. Do not cut towards yourself. For bending, use a bar bending machine or a manual bender. Manual benders are common in Africa because they are cheap and do not need electricity. But they require strength and skill. Make sure the bender is bolted to a firm base. Use the correct size pins for the bar diameter. If the pins are too small, the bar will kink. If they are too large, the bend radius will be wrong. The bend radius matters because a tight bend can crack the steel. The minimum bend radius is usually two times the diameter for small bars and three times for large bars. Now let us talk about safety. Steel fixing has many hazards. Cuts from sharp ends. Back injuries from lifting. Falls from heights. Eye injuries from flying chips. Electrical hazards from tools. Heat stress from working in the sun. To manage these, you need a safety plan. Start with personal protective equipment. Gloves. Boots. Helmet. Eye protection. High visibility vest. Hearing protection when using noisy tools. In hot climates, wear light clothing that covers your skin. Drink water often. Take breaks in the shade. Heat stroke is a killer. Next, housekeeping. Keep the work area tidy. Do not leave bars sticking up where someone can trip or fall onto them. Bend over the ends of vertical bars or put a wooden cap on them. This is called mushrooming. It prevents impalement. I have seen a worker fall onto a vertical bar and die. It is a real risk. Do not ignore it. Next, lifting. Steel bars are heavy. A twelve metre bar of twenty five millimetre diameter weighs about thirty seven kilogrammes. Do not lift it alone. Use two people or a machine. When lifting a cage, use a crane with a proper sling. Do not use binding wire as a sling. It will break. Do not stand under a suspended load. Next, access. If you are working at height, use a scaffold or a platform. Do not climb on the reinforcement. It is not a ladder. It can collapse. If you must walk on the reinforcement, use a walkway board. Next, electricity. If you use an angle grinder or a welding machine, check the cables. Do not use damaged cables. Use a residual current device. Earth the machine. Do not work in wet conditions with electrical tools. Now let us talk about productivity. Productivity is not about working faster. It is about working smarter. The first rule is preparation. Before you start, make sure you have the drawings, the BBS, the cutting list, the tools, the binding wire, the spacers, and the bars. If you spend the first hour looking for tools, you lose money. The second rule is teamwork. Divide the work into tasks. One person cuts. One person bends. Two people fix. One person ties. Rotate tasks to prevent fatigue. The third rule is sequence. Fix in the correct order. Do not jump around. Do not start a new column before you finish the one you are on. The fourth rule is quality. Do it right the first time. Rework costs more than doing it slowly and correctly. The fifth rule is communication. Talk to the foreman. If something is wrong, report it. If you need a tool, ask. If you are tired, rest. Do not suffer in silence. Now let us talk about records. Keep a daily log. How many bars fixed? How many hours worked? How many bars wasted? How many accidents? This data helps you improve. It also helps you get paid. If you are a subcontractor, you need records to claim for variations. If the engineer changes the drawing, you need to record the extra work. In Africa, where payment can be delayed, records are your protection. Finally, let us talk about training. If you are a foreman, train your team. Teach them to read the BBS. Teach them to tie properly. Teach them to use spacers. Teach them safety. A trained team is faster and safer. Do not keep knowledge to yourself. Share it. In summary, organise your yard. Cut and bend safely. Wear your PPE. Keep the site tidy. Lift with help. Use proper access. Prepare before you start. Work as a team. Follow the sequence. Do it right the first time. Communicate. Keep records. Train others. These are the habits of a professional steel fixer. They will make you valuable on any site in Africa.

5. Inspection, Testing and Handover of Reinforcement Works

Welcome to Lesson 5, the final lesson of Module 5. In this lesson we cover inspection, testing and handover of reinforcement works. This is the stage where your work is checked by the engineer, the consultant, or the client. If you pass inspection, you get paid and you get more work. If you fail, you lose time and money. So let us learn how to prepare for inspection and how to hand over cleanly. The first step is self-inspection. Do not wait for the engineer to find your mistakes. Check your own work. Use a checklist. The checklist should include: bar diameter, bar spacing, bar mark, lap length, lap stagger, link spacing, link hooks, cover, chairs, tie wire, cleanliness, and safety. Walk through the area and check each item. Use a tape measure. Use a spirit level. Use a plumb bob. Take photographs. If you find a mistake, fix it before the engineer arrives. The second step is documentation. Keep a file with the approved drawings, the BBS, the cutting list, the delivery notes, the mill certificates, and the inspection records. Mill certificates are important because they prove the steel grade. In Africa, some suppliers sell grade forty steel as grade sixty. If you do not check, you may be fixing weak steel. The mill certificate shows the chemical composition and the mechanical properties. If you cannot get a mill certificate, ask for a test. A tensile test can be done at a laboratory. It costs money but it is cheaper than a failure. The third step is the inspection itself. The engineer will come with a checklist. He will check the dimensions, the spacing, the laps, the cover, and the cleanliness. He may ask you to cut a bar to check the diameter. He may ask you to remove a spacer to check the cover. Be cooperative. Do not argue. If he finds a defect, write it down. Ask for a clear instruction. Do not guess. If he asks for a change that is not on the drawing, ask for a written instruction. This protects you from blame later. The fourth step is testing. In some projects, the engineer will ask for a pull-out test. A pull-out test checks the bond between the steel and the concrete. It is done by embedding a bar in concrete and pulling it out. The test is expensive and is usually done on a sample basis. In most African projects, pull-out tests are rare. But if they are required, make sure the bars are clean and the concrete is properly cured. The fifth step is handover. Handover is when you give the completed reinforcement to the next trade, usually the formwork carpenter or the concrete pourer. Before handover, make sure the area is clean. Remove all offcuts, wire, and tools. Make sure the bars are secure. Make sure the spacers are in place. Make sure the chairs are in place. Make sure the cover is correct. Make sure the laps are correct. Make sure the links are correct. Then sign the handover sheet. The handover sheet should list the date, the area, the bar marks, the quantity, and any defects. Both you and the receiver should sign. Keep a copy. The sixth step is protection. After handover, the reinforcement must be protected until concrete is poured. Do not let workers walk on the top bars. Do not let them drop tools on the bars. Do not let them weld near the bars. Do not let them use the bars as a ground for welding. Welding current can damage the steel. If concrete is not poured immediately, cover the bars with a tarpaulin to protect from rain. If the bars rust, clean them before pouring. The seventh step is the pour. During the pour, you should have a steel fixer standing by. His job is to watch the reinforcement. If a bar moves, he must fix it. If a spacer falls, he must replace it. If the concrete covers the bars, he must stop the pour. This is called a watching brief. Do not leave the site during the pour. Many defects happen during the pour because nobody is watching. The eighth step is post-pour inspection. After the concrete is poured and cured, you may be asked to inspect the exposed reinforcement. This is rare because the reinforcement is covered. But if there is a defect, you may need to chip out the concrete and repair the bar. This is expensive. So prevent defects before the pour. The ninth step is record keeping. Keep all inspection records, test results, handover sheets, and photographs. These records are your proof of quality. If there is a dispute later, you can show that you did the work correctly. In Africa, where projects can be delayed and memories fade, records are essential. The tenth step is learning. After every project, review what went well and what went wrong. Did you waste steel? Did you have accidents? Did you pass inspection first time? Did you get paid on time? Use this review to improve your next project. In summary, self-inspect. Document. Cooperate with the engineer. Test if required. Handover cleanly. Protect the work. Watch the pour. Keep records. Learn from experience. These are the habits of a professional steel fixer. They will make you trusted, respected, and well paid. This concludes Module 5. You now have the knowledge to read a bar bending schedule, fix columns, beams and slabs, lap and anchor bars, organise a site safely, and hand over your work to a high standard. Go and apply these skills on your next project. Your work will speak for you.

<p>The diagram above shows the three elements a steel fixer works on most often. The critical points are: main bars must be continuous through the joint where the schedule requires it; links must be spaced correctly and hooked around the main bars; and cover must be maintained so the steel is protected from moisture and chlorides. In coastal cities such as Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Luanda and Lagos, inadequate cover is one of the leading causes of premature corrosion and structural distress.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #b03a2e; background:#fdecea; padding:14px; margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#b03a2e;">SAFETY WARNING HANDLING AND CUTTING REBAR</strong>
<p style="margin-top:8px;">Rebar is heavy, has sharp cut ends and deformed ribs that can tear gloves and skin. On African sites where mechanical handling is limited, the following risks are common and must be controlled:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Manual handling injuries:</strong> A 12 m length of 25 mm bar weighs approximately 46 kg. Two workers lifting without coordination cause back and shoulder injuries. Use two-person lifts with a clear count, or mechanical aids where available.</li>
<li><strong>Cut and impalement injuries:</strong> Exposed cut ends at ground level are a leading cause of foot and leg puncture wounds. Cap or bend over all vertical starter bars before leaving the workface.</li>
<li><strong>Eye injuries from cutting:</strong> Angle grinder and bar cutter operations throw metal fragments. Wear safety glasses or a face shield at all times.</li>
<li><strong>Hand-arm vibration and cuts from grinders:</strong> Limit continuous grinder use and inspect discs for damage before every cut.</li>
<li><strong>Chemical exposure:</strong> Some release agents, form oils and epoxy coatings contain solvents and amines that cause dermatitis and respiratory irritation. Read the safety data sheet (SDS), wear nitrile gloves, and ensure ventilation when working in enclosed formwork.</li>
<li><strong>Structural collapse during fixing:</strong> Never remove props or temporary supports to gain access. Never stand on unsupported reinforcement cages.</li>
</ul>
<p>Personal protective equipment (PPE) is not optional: hard hat, safety boots, gloves, high-visibility vest and eye protection are the minimum on any active steel fixing workface.</p>
</div>

<h3>Practical Fixing Sequence for Columns, Beams and Slabs</h3>

<p>The sequence below is the one that consistently passes inspection and avoids rework on African sites where supervision may be stretched.</p>

<p><strong>1. Columns first.</strong> Set out column starter bars from the grid lines. Check verticality with a plumb bob or spirit level. Fix column links at the spacing shown on the bar bending schedule, starting with the first link within 50 mm of the slab or foundation top. Ensure every link hook engages a main bar and is bent into the core, not outward. Tie with 1.6 mm annealed wire at every fourth intersection, or every intersection at laps and corners.</p>

<p><strong>2. Beams next.</strong> Position beam bottom bars on chairs or spacers to maintain cover. Place links over the bottom bars, then feed the top bars through. Check that top bars are continuous over supports where the schedule requires it, and that laps are staggered as shown. Tie top bars to links before releasing the cage. Check the link spacing at the ends of beams shear is highest near supports, so links are often closer there.</p>

<p><strong>3. Slabs last.</strong> Place bottom bars first, then chairs, then top bars. Maintain the specified cover using purpose-made spacers or approved alternatives. Check bar spacing against the schedule at three points along each span. Tie at every second intersection in the body of the slab and every intersection at edges, around openings and at laps.</p>

<p><strong>4. Openings and penetrations.</strong> Never cut main bars to make way for services without written approval from the engineer. Trim bars and add trimmer bars as detailed. This is one of the most common causes of inspection failure on African sites.</p>

<p><strong>5. Final check before pour.</strong> Walk the whole area with the bar bending schedule in hand. Confirm bar diameter, spacing, lap length, cover and link spacing. Confirm all ties are tight and no bars are displaced. Confirm starter bars for the next level are present and correctly positioned. Only then call for inspection.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Bar bending schedule and latest structural drawings on site and understood by the foreman.</li>
<li>Bar diameters checked against the schedule never assume a bar is the right size by eye.</li>
<li>Lap lengths measured and staggered as specified; laps tied at both ends and at mid-length.</li>
<li>Link/stirrup spacing checked at three points per element, with closer spacing at supports where required.</li>
<li>Cover maintained with approved spacers or chairs; no steel touching formwork.</li>
<li>All cut ends at ground level capped or bent over; no exposed vertical starter bars left unprotected.</li>
<li>Ties tight and correct no loose wires that could be dislodged during the pour.</li>
<li>Openings and penetrations trimmed and reinforced as detailed; no main bars cut without approval.</li>
<li>Inspection requested only after the crew has completed its own check against the schedule.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Business Note: Why First-Time Inspection Matters</h3>

<p>On a typical African commercial or residential project, a failed reinforcement inspection can delay the pour by half a day to two days. The direct cost includes idle concrete pump, idle labour, and in some cases a cancelled concrete delivery. The indirect cost is worse: a contractor with a reputation for failed inspections is less likely to be invited to tender for the next phase. Training the crew to self-check against the bar bending schedule before calling the engineer is the single highest-return habit a steel fixing business can build.</p>
