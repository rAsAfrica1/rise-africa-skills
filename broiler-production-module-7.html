1. Why Growth Monitoring Matters for African Broiler Farmers

Growth monitoring is the heartbeat of any profitable broiler enterprise. In Africa, where feed costs can consume 65 to 75 percent of total production expenses, every gram of feed that does not convert into saleable meat is money lost. This lesson explains why systematic growth monitoring is not a luxury for large commercial farms but a survival tool for small and medium scale producers from Nigeria to Kenya, Ghana to South Africa. When you weigh your birds regularly, you move from guessing to knowing. You know whether your flock is on track, whether your feed is delivering value, and whether your management is working. Without monitoring, you are farming blind. The first principle is simple: what gets measured gets managed. A farmer who weighs a sample of birds every week can detect problems two to three weeks before they become visible to the naked eye. A drop in growth rate often precedes disease, poor ventilation, or feed quality issues. Early detection means early correction, and early correction protects your margins. Consider a typical scenario in Oyo State, Nigeria. A farmer buys 500 day old chicks at 1,200 naira each. Feed costs 28,000 naira per 25 kilogram bag. If the flock reaches market weight of 2.2 kilograms in 42 days with a feed conversion ratio of 1.7, the business is profitable. But if the feed conversion ratio drifts to 2.0 because of poor monitoring, the farmer loses over 100,000 naira on that batch alone. That is the difference between expanding to 1,000 birds next cycle and shutting down. Growth monitoring also helps you negotiate better. When you have records, you can prove to feed suppliers, veterinarians, and buyers that your operation is professional. Banks and microfinance institutions in countries like Uganda and Tanzania increasingly ask for production records before approving agricultural loans. Your growth charts become your business case. There are three core metrics every broiler farmer must track. First is average body weight, measured weekly from a random sample of at least 5 percent of the flock. Second is weekly feed intake, measured by recording how much feed is consumed each day. Third is feed conversion ratio, calculated by dividing total feed consumed by total weight gained. These three numbers tell you almost everything about flock performance. In Africa, many farmers face specific challenges that make monitoring even more critical. Heat stress during the hot season reduces feed intake and slows growth. Poor quality feed, sometimes adulterated with sawdust or sand, destroys feed conversion. Disease outbreaks such as Newcastle and Gumboro can wipe out weeks of progress. Regular weighing helps you separate these problems. If feed intake is normal but growth is slow, the issue is likely feed quality or disease. If feed intake drops and growth slows, heat stress or ventilation problems are likely. If feed intake rises but growth stays flat, you may have a feed conversion problem linked to genetics or health. Practical monitoring does not require expensive equipment. A simple hanging scale or a digital kitchen scale accurate to 10 grams is enough. A notebook and pen work perfectly for record keeping. Many farmers now use basic mobile phone apps or spreadsheets to track data. The key is consistency. Weigh on the same day each week, at the same time of day, before feeding. Use a random sample, not the biggest or smallest birds. Record everything. The business case for growth monitoring is strongest when you calculate your own feed conversion ratio and compare it to industry benchmarks. In well managed African farms, broilers should achieve a feed conversion ratio of 1.5 to 1.8 by 42 days. If your ratio is above 2.0, you are leaving money on the table. Every 0.1 improvement in feed conversion ratio saves roughly 5 to 7 percent on feed costs. For a farmer spending 500,000 naira on feed per cycle, that is 25,000 to 35,000 naira saved. Multiply that across six cycles per year and you have real money. Growth monitoring also improves your decision making on when to sell. Birds that are growing well but have not yet reached target weight should be kept. Birds that have plateaued should be sold immediately to avoid wasting feed. Without monitoring, farmers often keep birds too long, pushing feed conversion ratio above 2.5 and destroying profitability. Finally, growth monitoring is a risk management tool. In areas with disease pressure, early detection of slow growth allows you to isolate and treat before the whole flock is affected. In areas with feed price volatility, monitoring helps you adjust feeding strategies. In areas with market price fluctuations, monitoring helps you time your sales. The African broiler industry is growing rapidly, and competition is increasing. Farmers who monitor growth will outcompete those who do not. This module will teach you exactly how to set up a monitoring system, how to calculate feed conversion ratio, how to interpret the numbers, and how to take corrective action. By the end of this module, you will have the skills to turn your flock records into profit. Remember, the birds cannot tell you what is wrong, but the numbers can. Start weighing today.

2. Setting Up a Simple Growth Monitoring System on Your Farm

A growth monitoring system does not need to be complicated or expensive. In fact, the best systems in African broiler production are simple, consistent, and used every single day. This lesson walks you through setting up a practical monitoring system that works for farms of 100 to 10,000 birds. The goal is to collect accurate data with minimal labour and use that data to make better decisions. The first step is to define your sampling method. You cannot weigh every bird, so you must weigh a representative sample. For flocks under 500 birds, weigh at least 25 birds. For flocks of 500 to 2,000 birds, weigh 50 birds. For flocks above 2,000 birds, weigh 100 birds. Always select birds randomly. Do not pick the biggest birds because they look good, and do not pick the smallest because you feel sorry for them. Walk through the house and catch birds at different locations, different feeders, and different drinkers. This gives you a true average. The second step is to establish a weighing schedule. Weigh your birds on the same day every week. For broilers, the most important weighing days are day 7, day 14, day 21, day 28, day 35, and day 42. If you are raising birds to 49 days, add day 49. Weigh early in the morning before the first feeding. This ensures the crop is empty and the weight reflects true body mass. Use a hanging scale with a weighing cone or a digital scale with a small crate. For very young chicks, a kitchen scale works well. Record the weight of each bird or the total weight of the sample. The third step is to record feed intake accurately. You need to know exactly how much feed you put into the house and how much is left over. Weigh feed bags before opening them. If you use bulk feed, use a calibrated bucket or scale. Record feed given every day. At the end of each week, calculate total feed consumed by subtracting leftover feed from feed delivered. Do not guess. Guessing leads to wrong feed conversion ratios and wrong decisions. The fourth step is to calculate average body weight and weekly weight gain. Average body weight is the total weight of your sample divided by the number of birds weighed. Weekly weight gain is this week average weight minus last week average weight. For example, if your birds weighed 450 grams on day 14 and 850 grams on day 21, the weekly gain is 400 grams. This number tells you if growth is accelerating, stable, or slowing. The fifth step is to calculate feed conversion ratio. The formula is total feed consumed divided by total weight gained. For a weekly calculation, use feed consumed during the week divided by weight gained during the week. For a cumulative calculation, use total feed consumed since day one divided by total live weight produced since day one. Cumulative feed conversion ratio is the most important number because it reflects overall efficiency. The sixth step is to keep records in a way that you can actually use. A simple notebook with columns for date, age in days, average weight, feed consumed, and feed conversion ratio is enough. Many farmers in Kenya and Ghana now use free mobile apps like Kuku Track or simple Google Sheets templates. The key is to write down the numbers immediately, not at the end of the week when you have forgotten. The seventh step is to set targets and compare. Use industry benchmarks as a guide. In tropical conditions, a typical broiler should weigh about 180 to 200 grams at day 7, 450 to 500 grams at day 14, 900 to 1,000 grams at day 21, 1,400 to 1,600 grams at day 28, 1,900 to 2,100 grams at day 35, and 2,300 to 2,600 grams at day 42. Feed conversion ratio should be around 1.0 to 1.2 at day 14, 1.3 to 1.5 at day 28, and 1.6 to 1.8 at day 42. These are not absolute rules because genetics, feed quality, and climate vary. But they give you a reference point. The eighth step is to train your workers. If you have farm hands, they must understand why weighing matters and how to do it correctly. A worker who weighs the same three birds every week will give you false data. A worker who forgets to record feed will ruin your feed conversion ratio calculation. Invest time in training. Show them how to catch birds gently, how to read the scale, and how to write clearly. Pay them a small bonus if data is complete and accurate each week. The ninth step is to review your data weekly. Every Sunday evening, sit down with your records. Calculate your feed conversion ratio. Compare it to last week and to your target. Ask three questions: Are the birds growing as expected? Is feed conversion improving or worsening? What might explain any change? Write down your conclusions. This weekly review is where monitoring turns into management. The tenth step is to act on what you learn. If growth is below target, check feed quality, water availability, temperature, and disease. If feed conversion ratio is rising, check for feed wastage, rodent damage, or health problems. If growth is above target, consider whether you can reduce feed costs or sell earlier. Monitoring without action is just record keeping. Monitoring with action is profit. In Africa, many farmers operate with limited resources. You may not have a computer or a smartphone. That is fine. A notebook and a pencil work. You may not have a digital scale. A hanging scale from a local market works. You may not have a veterinarian nearby. Your records will help you describe the problem accurately when you do get help. The most important thing is to start. Do not wait for perfect conditions. Start with 25 birds. Start with a notebook. Start today. As you build your monitoring system, you will find that it becomes easier and faster. You will also find that your decisions become sharper. You will know when to buy feed, when to sell birds, and when to change your management. That is the power of a simple growth monitoring system.

3. Calculating and Interpreting Feed Conversion Ratio

Feed conversion ratio is the single most important number in broiler production. It tells you how efficiently your birds are turning feed into meat. In Africa, where feed is the largest cost, improving feed conversion ratio is the fastest way to increase profit. This lesson explains exactly how to calculate feed conversion ratio, how to interpret it, and how to use it to make better business decisions. The formula is simple: feed conversion ratio equals total feed consumed divided by total weight gained. For example, if your flock consumed 1,000 kilograms of feed and gained 600 kilograms of live weight, the feed conversion ratio is 1,000 divided by 600, which equals 1.67. This means it took 1.67 kilograms of feed to produce 1 kilogram of chicken. Lower is better. A feed conversion ratio of 1.5 is better than 1.8. A feed conversion ratio of 2.0 is poor and usually means you are losing money. There are two ways to calculate feed conversion ratio: weekly and cumulative. Weekly feed conversion ratio uses feed consumed during one week divided by weight gained during that same week. This helps you spot problems quickly. Cumulative feed conversion ratio uses total feed consumed since day one divided by total live weight produced since day one. This is the number you use to judge overall flock performance. Both are useful. Weekly tells you what is happening now. Cumulative tells you where you stand overall. To calculate feed conversion ratio accurately, you must measure two things correctly: feed intake and weight gain. Feed intake is the total amount of feed the birds actually eat. This is not the same as the amount of feed you buy. Some feed is wasted, spilled, or eaten by rodents. To measure feed intake, weigh feed before putting it in the feeders. At the end of the week, weigh any leftover feed. Subtract leftover from delivered. The difference is feed intake. If you use automatic feeders, calibrate them regularly. If you use manual feeders, fill them at the same time each day and do not overfill, because overfilling leads to spillage. Weight gain is the difference between average body weight at the end of the week and average body weight at the start of the week. Multiply average weight gain by the number of birds to get total weight gain. For example, if you have 500 birds and average weight gain is 400 grams, total weight gain is 200,000 grams or 200 kilograms. If feed intake for the week was 340 kilograms, weekly feed conversion ratio is 340 divided by 200, which equals 1.7. Now let us interpret feed conversion ratio. A feed conversion ratio below 1.5 is excellent. This is achievable with high quality feed, good genetics, and excellent management. A feed conversion ratio of 1.5 to 1.7 is good. Most well managed African farms fall in this range. A feed conversion ratio of 1.7 to 1.9 is average. You are making money but there is room for improvement. A feed conversion ratio of 1.9 to 2.2 is poor. You are likely losing money or barely breaking even. A feed conversion ratio above 2.2 is very poor. You need to identify and fix the problem immediately. Several factors affect feed conversion ratio. Genetics is one. Some breeds convert feed more efficiently than others. Feed quality is another. Feed with poor amino acid balance, low energy, or contaminants will have a higher feed conversion ratio. Health status is critical. Sick birds eat but do not grow, so feed conversion ratio rises. Heat stress reduces feed intake and increases maintenance energy needs, worsening feed conversion ratio. Stocking density matters. Overcrowded birds cannot access feed and water properly, so feed conversion ratio rises. Feed wastage from poor feeder design or overfilling also raises feed conversion ratio. In Africa, specific challenges affect feed conversion ratio. Aflatoxin contamination in maize and groundnuts is common in many regions. Even low levels of aflatoxin reduce growth and increase feed conversion ratio. Heat stress during the hot dry season is a major issue. Birds pant, eat less, and grow slower. Poor quality protein sources, such as fishmeal with high ash content or soybean meal that is underprocessed, reduce feed conversion efficiency. Disease pressure from Newcastle, Gumboro, and coccidiosis is high in many areas. All of these factors must be managed to keep feed conversion ratio low. To improve feed conversion ratio, start with the basics. Provide clean, cool water at all times. Water is the most important nutrient. A bird that is dehydrated will not eat or grow properly. Ensure proper ventilation to remove ammonia and excess heat. Use good quality feed from a reputable supplier. If you mix your own feed, test your ingredients for aflatoxin and ensure proper amino acid balance. Control disease with a strict vaccination programme and good biosecurity. Reduce feed wastage by using well designed feeders and not overfilling them. Keep rodents and wild birds out of the house. Maintain proper stocking density. For broilers, 10 to 12 birds per square metre is ideal in tropical conditions. Harvest at the right time. Keeping birds beyond their optimal market weight increases feed conversion ratio because older birds use more feed for maintenance and less for growth. Feed conversion ratio also has a direct financial impact. Let us calculate. Suppose you have 1,000 broilers. You sell them at 2.5 kilograms live weight. Total live weight is 2,500 kilograms. If your feed conversion ratio is 1.7, you need 4,250 kilograms of feed. If feed costs 500 naira per kilogram, your feed cost is 2,125,000 naira. If your feed conversion ratio is 2.0, you need 5,000 kilograms of feed, costing 2,500,000 naira. The difference is 375,000 naira. That is the cost of poor feed conversion ratio. Now imagine you have 10,000 birds. The difference becomes 3,750,000 naira. This is why feed conversion ratio matters so much. A small improvement in feed conversion ratio translates into huge savings. To improve feed conversion ratio by 0.1, you need to focus on the factors you can control. You cannot change genetics easily, but you can change feed quality, health, housing, and management. Many African farmers have improved feed conversion ratio from 2.0 to 1.7 simply by fixing waterers, reducing stocking density, and vaccinating properly. That is a 15 percent improvement in feed efficiency. It is achievable. Track your feed conversion ratio every week. Write it down. Compare it to your target. If it rises, investigate immediately. If it falls, figure out what you did right and do it again. Feed conversion ratio is not just a number. It is a mirror that reflects the quality of your management. Use it to improve, and your profits will follow.

4. Using Growth Data to Make Better Business Decisions

Growth data is not just for the farm. It is a business tool. In African broiler production, farmers who use growth data to make decisions consistently outperform those who rely on intuition. This lesson shows you how to turn weekly weights and feed conversion ratios into decisions about feeding, selling, buying, and expanding. The first decision area is feed management. Your growth data tells you whether your feed is delivering value. If your birds are growing below target but eating normally, your feed quality may be poor. If they are eating less and growing less, you may have a health or heat stress problem. If they are eating more but growing the same, your feed conversion ratio is worsening, and you need to investigate feed wastage or disease. Use your data to decide whether to continue with your current feed or switch suppliers. In many African markets, feed quality varies widely. Some suppliers dilute feed with fillers. Your growth data will expose this. If you switch feed and growth improves, you have evidence to justify the change. If growth does not improve, you have saved yourself from wasting money on a more expensive feed. The second decision area is selling timing. Broilers have an optimal market window. If you sell too early, you leave money on the table because birds have not reached their full weight. If you sell too late, feed conversion ratio worsens and you lose money on feed. Your growth data helps you identify the optimal selling day. For most African markets, the target is 2.2 to 2.5 kilograms live weight. If your birds reach this weight at day 42, sell at day 42. If they reach it at day 38, sell at day 38. Do not hold them just because you think they might grow more. Every extra day adds feed cost without proportional weight gain. Use your weekly weights to project when birds will reach target weight. If they are growing faster than expected, plan to sell earlier. If they are growing slower, plan to sell later, but only if feed conversion ratio is still acceptable. The third decision area is buying day old chicks. Your growth data from previous batches tells you which hatchery or breed performs best on your farm. If you consistently get better feed conversion ratio from one supplier, buy from that supplier. If one breed grows faster but has poorer feed conversion ratio, calculate which is more profitable. Sometimes a slower growing breed with better feed conversion ratio is more profitable than a faster growing breed with poor feed conversion ratio. Your data will show you. The fourth decision area is expansion. Before you expand from 500 to 1,000 birds, you need to know if your current operation is efficient. If your feed conversion ratio is above 2.0, expanding will only multiply your losses. Fix your efficiency first. If your feed conversion ratio is 1.7 or better and your mortality is below 5 percent, you are ready to expand. Your growth data gives you the confidence to invest. The fifth decision area is pricing and negotiation. When you sell birds, buyers often try to negotiate based on appearance. If you have data showing your birds reached 2.5 kilograms at day 42 with a feed conversion ratio of 1.6, you can justify a premium price. You can also use your data to negotiate better prices with feed suppliers. If you buy 100 bags of feed per cycle, show them your records and ask for a bulk discount. The sixth decision area is labour and management. If your growth data shows that performance drops when a particular worker is on duty, you have a training or supervision issue. If performance improves after you change a management practice, you have evidence to keep doing it. Use data to reward workers who achieve good feed conversion ratio. A bonus of 5,000 naira for a feed conversion ratio below 1.7 is cheap compared to the savings. The seventh decision area is risk management. If your growth data shows a sudden drop in weight gain, you can act before the problem spreads. If you see a pattern of poor growth during the hot season, you can invest in cooling systems or adjust feeding times. If you see a pattern of poor growth after a particular feed delivery, you can test that feed for aflatoxin. Data turns surprises into manageable risks. Let us look at a real example from a farm in Uganda. The farmer had 800 broilers. At day 21, average weight was 750 grams instead of the target 900 grams. Feed intake was normal. The farmer checked waterers and found that half of them were blocked. Birds were spending time trying to drink instead of eating. After fixing the waterers, growth improved. By day 42, average weight was 2.3 kilograms and feed conversion ratio was 1.75. Without monitoring, the farmer would not have known there was a problem until day 35 or 40, and by then the loss would have been irreversible. Another example from Ghana. A farmer noticed that feed conversion ratio was rising from 1.6 to 1.9 between day 28 and day 35. He investigated and found that rats were eating feed at night. He spent 200 cedis on rat control and saved over 2,000 cedis in feed. The data paid for itself many times over. In Nigeria, a farmer used growth data to negotiate a contract with a restaurant. The restaurant wanted a consistent supply of 2.5 kilogram birds every week. The farmer showed his growth records and proved he could deliver. He got a premium price and a guaranteed market. That is the power of data. To use growth data effectively, you must review it regularly. Do not just collect data and file it. Look at it every week. Ask what it is telling you. Make one decision based on it. Then check if that decision improved performance. This cycle of measure, decide, act, and review is the foundation of profitable farming. In Africa, where margins are thin and risks are high, this discipline is not optional. It is the difference between a farm that survives and a farm that thrives. Start using your growth data today. Your bank account will thank you.

5. Troubleshooting Poor Growth and High Feed Conversion Ratio

Even with good management, problems happen. Broilers may grow slower than expected or feed conversion ratio may rise. In Africa, the causes are often related to heat, disease, feed quality, or management. This lesson teaches you how to troubleshoot poor growth and high feed conversion ratio step by step. The first step is to confirm the problem. Do not panic based on one weighing. Check your data. Is average weight below target for two consecutive weeks? Is feed conversion ratio above 1.9? If yes, you have a problem. If it is a single bad week, it may be a measurement error or a temporary stress. Recheck your scale, your sample, and your feed records. The second step is to check water. Water is the most common cause of poor growth that farmers overlook. Birds need clean, cool water at all times. If waterers are dirty, birds drink less. If water is too warm, birds drink less. If waterers are too few or too low, birds cannot access water. Check waterers twice a day. Clean them. Make sure there is one waterer for every 80 to 100 birds. In hot weather, add extra waterers. If you use nipple drinkers, check the pressure and flow rate. A bird that does not drink enough will not eat enough, and growth will suffer. The third step is to check feed. Is the feed fresh? Is it mouldy? Does it smell bad? Is it the right formulation for the age of the birds? Broilers need starter feed for days 1 to 14, grower feed for days 15 to 28, and finisher feed for days 29 to market. If you feed the wrong formulation, growth and feed conversion ratio will suffer. Check for feed wastage. Are feeders overfilled? Are there holes in the bags? Are rodents eating the feed? Weigh feed going in and feed left over. If feed intake is normal but growth is poor, the feed may be low quality. Send a sample to a laboratory if possible. In many African countries, laboratory testing is available through agricultural universities or feed companies. The fourth step is to check temperature and ventilation. Heat stress is a major cause of poor growth in Africa. When temperatures rise above 30 degrees Celsius, broilers reduce feed intake and growth slows. Feed conversion ratio worsens because birds use energy to pant instead of grow. Check for signs of heat stress: panting, wings spread, birds crowding around waterers. Improve ventilation by opening vents, using fans, or adding roof insulation. In extreme heat, consider cooling the roof with water or using foggers. Cold stress is also a problem for young chicks. If chicks are huddled together, they are cold. Adjust brooder temperature. The fifth step is to check for disease. Sick birds eat less and grow slower. Look for symptoms: ruffled feathers, droopy wings, diarrhoea, coughing, sneezing, or sudden deaths. Common diseases in African broiler production include Newcastle, Gumboro, coccidiosis, and chronic respiratory disease. Check your vaccination programme. Are you vaccinating on time? Are you using the correct dosage? Are you storing vaccines properly? If you suspect disease, consult a veterinarian immediately. Do not guess. The sixth step is to check stocking density. Overcrowding causes stress, reduces feed and water access, and worsens feed conversion ratio. For broilers in tropical conditions, 10 to 12 birds per square metre is ideal. If you have more, you need more space or you need to sell some birds early. The seventh step is to check for parasites. Internal parasites like worms and external parasites like mites and lice can reduce growth. Deworm your birds if necessary. Consult a veterinarian for the right product and dosage. The eighth step is to check for toxins. Aflatoxin is a common problem in African feed. It comes from mouldy maize, groundnuts, or other ingredients. Even low levels reduce growth and increase feed conversion ratio. If you suspect aflatoxin, change your feed source or use a toxin binder. Toxin binders are available from feed companies and veterinary suppliers. The ninth step is to check your records. Are you measuring correctly? Are you weighing the same birds every week? Are you recording feed accurately? Are you calculating feed conversion ratio correctly? A simple mistake in record keeping can make you think you have a problem when you do not. The tenth step is to make a plan. Once you identify the cause, act. If it is water, fix the waterers. If it is feed, change the feed. If it is heat, improve ventilation. If it is disease, treat it. If it is density, reduce it. Write down what you did and when. Then monitor the results. Did growth improve? Did feed conversion ratio fall? If yes, you solved the problem. If no, go back to step one and check again. In Africa, troubleshooting is a skill that separates successful farmers from struggling ones. The successful farmer does not wait for a crisis. They monitor, they notice small changes, and they act early. They also learn from every batch. They keep records of what worked and what did not. They share knowledge with other farmers. They invest in training. They build relationships with veterinarians, feed suppliers, and extension officers. Poor growth and high feed conversion ratio are not inevitable. They are signals that something is wrong. Your job is to find out what and fix it. With practice, you will become faster and more accurate at troubleshooting. You will also become more profitable. Remember, the birds cannot talk, but your data can. Listen to it.

<h2>Practical Reference: Growth Monitoring Tools, Targets and Benchmarks for African Broiler Farms</h2>

<p>This module is a practical reference you can keep on the farm office wall or on your phone. It brings together the numbers, tables and checklists that turn growth monitoring from a vague idea into a daily management routine. The focus is on decisions that affect money: when to sell, when to change feed, when to call a vet, and when to cut losses.</p>

<p>Broiler farming in Africa operates on thin margins. Feed alone accounts for 6575% of total production cost in most commercial systems, so every gram of feed that does not convert into saleable meat is money lost. Growth monitoring is the earliest warning system you have. A bird that is 10% below target body weight at day 21 will almost never recover the full deficit, and the feed already consumed cannot be recovered. Catching the problem on day 7 or day 14 is worth far more than catching it on day 28.</p>

<h3>Why Weigh Birds, Not Just Look at Them</h3>

<p>Visual assessment is unreliable. Experienced stockmen consistently overestimate flock uniformity and average weight when they rely on eye alone. A digital kitchen scale accurate to 1 gram, a small plastic bucket, and a notebook will outperform the best-trained eye. Weigh a representative sample of 510% of the flock, or at least 20 birds per house, on the same day each week, at the same time of day, before feeding. Record individual weights, not just the average, because uniformity (coefficient of variation) tells you as much about flock health and future performance as the mean does.</p>

<h3>How to Use These Tables on Your Farm</h3>

<p>Weigh your sample birds on days 7, 14, 21, 28, 35 and 42. Compare your average to the target in Table 1. If your birds are more than 5% below target, investigate immediately. The most common causes in African conditions are: feed restriction (either deliberate or accidental through feeder space shortage), heat stress reducing intake, coccidiosis or other gut disease, poor-quality feed with low energy or protein, and water shortage. Each of these has a different fix, so diagnose before you spend money.</p>

<p>Calculate your FCR at every weighing: total feed consumed (kg) divided by total live weight gain (kg). Compare to Table 2. If your FCR is 0.2 points above the benchmark for your system, you are losing roughly USD 0.09 per kg of live weight on a 1,000-bird flock averaging 2 kg, that is USD 180 per cycle. Over six cycles a year, that is over USD 1,000. This is the business case for growth monitoring.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:14px; margin:20px 0; font-family:Arial, sans-serif;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b; font-size:15px;"> SAFETY WARNING HEALTH AND CHEMICALS</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#1a1a1a;">Never use antibiotics or coccidiostats without a veterinary prescription and a confirmed diagnosis. Misuse of antimicrobials in poultry contributes to antimicrobial resistance, which is a direct threat to human health in Africa. Observe all withdrawal periods before slaughter selling birds with drug residues is illegal in most countries and harms consumers. Store all chemicals, vaccines and medicated feed in a locked cabinet away from children, food and feed. Wear gloves and a mask when handling disinfectants, and wash hands thoroughly after contact. If you suspect disease, isolate affected birds and contact a qualified veterinarian or your nearest veterinary extension officer before treating. Do not use human medicines on poultry.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Weigh weekly:</strong> Sample 510% of birds (minimum 20) on the same day and time each week, before feeding.</li>
<li><strong>Record individual weights:</strong> Not just the average uniformity matters as much as mean weight.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate FCR at every weighing:</strong> Total feed in (kg) total live weight gain (kg).</li>
<li><strong>Compare to targets:</strong> Use Table 1 for weight and Table 2 for FCR benchmarks by system.</li>
<li><strong>Act within 24 hours:</strong> If birds are &gt;5% below target, investigate feed, water, heat, disease and stocking density immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Check feeder and drinker space:</strong> At least 5 cm of feeder space and 2 cm of drinker space per bird at 21 days, increasing with age.</li>
<li><strong>Monitor water intake:</strong> A sudden drop in water consumption is the earliest sign of disease or heat stress.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a simple ledger:</strong> Record feed purchases, bird weights, mortality and sales. This is your business intelligence.</li>
</ul>
