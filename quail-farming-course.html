Course Overview

Why quail? No other common livestock reaches productive age as fast: Japanese quail start laying at 6–7 weeks old (versus 18–20 weeks for chickens) and are ready for meat sale even sooner. This means a quail enterprise turns capital into income faster than almost any other livestock option, with lower space and feed requirements than chickens — ideal for someone starting with very limited capital or space.

Structure: 4 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Quail startle easily and can injure themselves against cage tops. Sudden loud noise or fast movement near the cage can cause quail to jump upward violently and hit their heads — soft netting or padded cage tops are not optional, they prevent real injuries and deaths.
  • Quail are far more sensitive to temperature swings as chicks than most poultry. Brooder temperature control in the first 2 weeks is the single biggest factor in chick survival — more so than for chicken chicks.
  • Check your local regulations before scaling up quail egg or meat sale. Some regions have specific licensing for quail products separate from chicken egg/poultry regulation — confirm requirements before committing to a larger commercial scale.
MONTH 1 — Quail Biology & Housing ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Why quail move so fast

  • Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica) are the standard farmed quail species — small (around 100–140 g adult weight), reach laying age at 6–7 weeks, and can lay an egg nearly every day once mature, similar to a high-producing layer hen but on a much faster overall cycle.
  • Meat-type quail reach market weight at around 5–6 weeks, meaning a full production cycle from chick to market bird is often complete within the time a chicken chick is still growing toward laying age.
  • This speed comes with a trade-off: quail are more sensitive to management mistakes (temperature, stress, nutrition) than chickens, so consistent daily care matters more, not less, than with slower-growing poultry.

Lesson 1.2 — Cage and housing design

  • Quail are typically raised in cages rather than open floor housing — cages keep birds off their own droppings (reducing disease and keeping eggs clean) and use vertical space efficiently, allowing high stocking density in a small footprint.
  • Cage floors need small enough mesh to prevent leg injury but still allow droppings to fall through; egg-collection cages often have a slight slope so eggs roll gently to a collection tray.
  • Cage tops must be soft (netting, padded material) or high enough to prevent head injury from the quail's characteristic startle-jump reflex.

Lesson 1.3 — Stocking density and space

  • A common stocking guideline is roughly 6–8 adult quail per square foot (about 65–85 per square metre) of cage floor for layers — overcrowding reduces egg production and increases stress-related pecking.
  • Separate cages by purpose: breeding stock, laying flock, and grow-out meat birds each have different space and management needs and shouldn't be mixed in one cage.
  • Ensure good ventilation and airflow around cages — quail are sensitive to ammonia buildup from droppings in poorly ventilated spaces, which affects both health and egg production.

Lesson 1.4 — Site and environment

  • Site the quail house away from strong wind, direct hot sun, and loud/sudden noise sources — quail's easily-startled nature means the housing environment itself is a management tool, not just shelter.
  • Maintain fairly stable temperature for adult quail (they tolerate a range but perform best avoiding extremes) — extreme heat particularly reduces egg-laying rate.
  • Keep the area secure from predators (cats, rats, snakes) — quail's small size makes them vulnerable, and cage design should account for this from the start.

MONTH 2 — Incubation & Brooding ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Egg selection for hatching

  • Select clean, correctly shaped, medium-sized eggs from healthy, mature breeding stock — misshapen or very small/large eggs hatch poorly and shouldn't be set even if convenient in quantity.
  • Store hatching eggs no more than about 7–10 days before setting, pointed end down, at a cool (not cold) temperature — hatchability drops the longer eggs are stored before incubation.
  • Bring stored eggs to room temperature before placing in the incubator — sudden temperature change from cold storage directly into a hot incubator reduces hatch rates.

Lesson 2.2 — Incubation conditions

  • Quail eggs incubate at around 37.5°C with 45–50% humidity for the first 14 days, then increased humidity (around 65%) for the final 3–4 days before hatch — incubation is typically 16–18 days total, notably shorter than chicken eggs (21 days).
  • Turn eggs several times a day (or use an automatic turner) up until 2–3 days before expected hatch, when turning should stop to allow chicks to position correctly for hatching.
  • Monitor incubator temperature and humidity consistently — quail eggs are unforgiving of incubation errors, and a small sustained temperature deviation can significantly reduce hatch rate.

Lesson 2.3 — Hatching and the first 24 hours

  • Resist the urge to help chicks out of the shell unless clearly necessary after a significant delay — most healthy chicks hatch unassisted, and premature help can cause injury or bleeding.
  • Leave newly hatched chicks in the incubator until fully dry and fluffed (usually several hours) before moving to the brooder — wet chicks moved too early chill quickly.
  • Have the brooder pre-warmed and ready before hatching begins — chicks need immediate warmth once moved, and a cold brooder is a common cause of early chick loss.

Lesson 2.4 — Brooding quail chicks

  • Brooder temperature starts around 37–38°C in week 1, reduced gradually by about 3°C per week until reaching ambient temperature by around week 4 — quail chicks are more temperature-sensitive than chicken chicks and chill faster if the brooder is too cool.
  • Use a shallow, secure brooder with fine mesh sides (quail chicks are tiny and can escape through gaps that would contain chicken chicks) and a non-slip floor surface to prevent leg splay injuries.
  • Provide chick-sized feeders and waterers to prevent drowning — quail chicks can drown in water containers designed for larger chicks, so use shallow, chick-appropriate designs with marbles or a screen to prevent full immersion.

MONTH 3 — Feeding & Production Management ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Feeding by growth stage

StageTarget CP%Notes
Starter (0–3 weeks)24–28%Highest protein of any common poultry starter feed
Grower (3–6 weeks)20–22%Transition toward adult ration
Layer ration20–24%Higher protein than chicken layer feed; calcium raised for eggshell
Meat/broiler-type finisher22–24%Higher energy for rapid finishing to market weight

Lesson 3.2 — Formulating or sourcing quail feed

  • Quail need notably higher protein than chicken feed at every stage — standard chicken feed is usually insufficient for quail without supplementation, especially for layers and starters.
  • Where commercial quail feed isn't available locally, chicken starter/grower feed can be supplemented with additional protein (fish meal, soybean meal) to reach quail's higher targets — see the Animal Feed Making course for full formulation guidance.
  • Feed particle size matters for very young quail chicks — crumble or fine mash suits their tiny beaks better than standard-size pellets or mash meant for chickens.

Lesson 3.3 — Managing the laying flock

  • Provide consistent lighting (roughly 14–16 hours of light per day) for optimal laying rate — inconsistent light patterns reduce and disrupt egg production.
  • Collect eggs at least twice daily — quail eggs are small and can be damaged or soiled if left too long in the cage, and consistent collection also reduces egg-eating behaviour if it develops.
  • Monitor laying rate per bird over time — a sustained drop signals stress, disease, nutrition problems, or the bird approaching the end of peak lay and worth culling from the breeding/laying flock.

Lesson 3.4 — Meat production management

  • Meat-type quail are typically grown separately from the laying flock, on a higher-energy finishing ration, and processed at 5–6 weeks when they reach target market weight.
  • Reduce stress in the final growing weeks — startled or stressed birds burn energy that would otherwise go toward weight gain, directly affecting final market weight and feed efficiency.
  • Plan processing/harvest dates against hatch dates in batches — staggered hatching produces a continuous supply for market rather than one large batch all ready at once.

MODULE 4 — Health, Markets & the Quail Business ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Common health issues

  • Watch for respiratory signs (gasping, discharge), reduced feed/water intake, and drop in egg production — these are the earliest and most reliable signs of a developing health problem in a quail flock.
  • Maintain a basic vaccination and biosecurity routine appropriate to local disease pressure, coordinated with a local veterinary/extension service familiar with quail or general poultry health.
  • Prevent cannibalism/pecking (a known risk in crowded or stressed quail flocks) through correct stocking density, adequate feeder/waterer space, and prompt removal of any injured bird before others target it.

Lesson 4.2 — Biosecurity basics

  • Limit visitor access to the quail house, disinfect footwear/equipment moving between different bird groups, and quarantine any new stock before introducing to the existing flock.
  • Control rodents and wild bird access around the housing — both can carry disease into a quail flock and are drawn to spilled feed if housekeeping is poor.
  • Clean and disinfect cages and equipment between batches, particularly brooders, to break any disease cycle before the next hatch group arrives.

Lesson 4.3 — Markets: eggs and meat

  • Quail eggs occupy a growing niche market — sold individually or in small trays to health-conscious consumers, specialty grocers, and restaurants, often at a meaningful premium per egg compared with chicken eggs relative to size.
  • Quail meat is sold to restaurants (particularly those offering game or specialty dishes), individual consumers seeking a novel or premium protein, and sometimes through direct online/social media orders given the product's niche appeal.
  • Live breeding stock and started (point-of-lay) birds are a further income stream, sold to other farmers entering quail production — similar in principle to the breeding-stock sales seen in other fast-cycle livestock enterprises.

Lesson 4.4 — Final project: your bankable business plan

  • Assemble: cage/housing plan and capacity, incubation and brooding protocol, feeding plan by stage, laying/meat production schedule, biosecurity protocol, target markets (eggs, meat, breeding stock) with pricing, and a staged growth plan given quail's fast production cycle.
  • Submit with your hatch rate, laying rate and growth records for your rAs certificate — quail plans that document consistent hatch and lay rates are exactly what niche buyers and lenders look for in a fast-turnover livestock business.

📖 Glossary

  • Coturnix japonica — the Japanese quail, the standard farmed quail species.
  • Point-of-lay — a bird raised close to laying age, ready to start production.
  • Brooder — a heated enclosure for raising newly hatched chicks.
  • Startle-jump reflex — quail's tendency to jump upward suddenly when startled, a risk for head injury against hard cage tops.
  • Biosecurity — practices that prevent disease introduction and spread within a flock.
  • CP% (crude protein) — the standard measure of protein content in feed.
