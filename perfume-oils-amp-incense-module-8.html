1. Calculating Cost per Unit for Oils and Incense

To price your products profitably, you must first know exactly what each unit costs you to make. For perfume oils, start by listing every ingredient: carrier oils (like jojoba or sweet almond), fragrance oils or essential oils, and any additives like vitamin E. Suppose you buy 1 litre of jojoba oil for 5,000 KES. If you use 10 ml per 30 ml bottle, that's 50 KES of jojoba. Your fragrance oil costs 8,000 KES for 500 ml; using 5 ml per bottle costs 80 KES. The bottle, cap, and label cost 60 KES. So total cost per unit = 50 + 80 + 60 = 190 KES. For incense, calculate cost per stick or per bundle. If you make 100 sticks from 500 g of charcoal powder (cost 800 KES), 100 g of binder (200 KES), and 20 ml of fragrance oil (600 KES), total materials = 1,600 KES. Add 100 KES for bamboo sticks. That's 1,700 KES for 100 sticks, so 17 KES per stick. If you sell bundles of 20 sticks, cost per bundle = 340 KES. Always include a small percentage for waste or spillagesay 5%. So for oils, add 5% of 190 = 9.5 KES, making unit cost 199.5 KES. Round up to 200 KES. For incense, add 5% to 17 = 0.85 KES, so 17.85 KES per stick. Keep a notebook or a simple Excel sheet listing all raw materials and their costs. Update prices whenever you buy new supplies. This simple step prevents you from selling at a loss. Many beginners forget to include the cost of small items like pipettes or gloves, but those add up. Track everything. Once you know your true cost per unit, you can set a price that covers costs and leaves profit.

2. Setting Wholesale and Retail Prices

Once you know your cost per unit, you can set two prices: wholesale (for bulk buyers like shops) and retail (for individual customers). A common rule is to double your cost for wholesale and triple it for retail. Using the perfume oil example: cost per unit = 200 KES. Wholesale price = 400 KES. Retail price = 600 KES. For incense sticks: cost per stick = 17.85 KES. Wholesale price per stick = 36 KES (if sold in bundles of 20, that's 720 KES per bundle). Retail price per stick = 54 KES (or 1,080 KES per bundle of 20). But these are just starting points. Check what competitors charge. In Nairobi, a 30 ml perfume oil might retail for 500800 KES. If your quality is higher, you can charge more. For wholesale, offer a discount for larger orders. For example, if a shop buys 50 bottles, give them 350 KES per bottle instead of 400. That encourages bigger orders. Also consider your market: tourists might pay more, while local repeat customers expect fair prices. Always communicate your prices clearly. Write them on a price list. For retail, you can set a recommended price and let resellers add their own markup. For example, you sell wholesale at 400 KES, and suggest they retail at 600 KES. That gives them a 200 KES profit per bottle. For incense, if you sell a bundle of 20 sticks wholesale at 720 KES, suggest retail at 1,080 KES. This makes your product attractive to shops. Remember, your prices must cover all costs and leave you a profit. If you sell wholesale at double cost, your profit margin is 50% (since cost is half of selling price). That's a good target. Adjust based on what the market will bear. Test different prices at local markets and see what sells best.

3. Accounting for Packaging, Labor, and Overhead

Many small producers forget to include packaging, labor, and overhead in their costs, and end up losing money. Let's break these down. Packaging: For perfume oils, you need bottles, caps, labels, and maybe a box. A 30 ml glass bottle with cap might cost 50 KES, a label 10 KES, and a small gift box 30 KES. That's 90 KES per unit. If you use a cheaper plastic bottle (20 KES) and no box, packaging drops to 30 KES. For incense, packaging might be a cellophane bag and a sticker: 5 KES per bundle. Labor: Even if you work alone, your time has value. If you make 20 perfume oils in 2 hours, and you want to earn 200 KES per hour, that's 400 KES labor for 20 units = 20 KES per unit. For incense, if you make 100 sticks in 1 hour and want 150 KES per hour, that's 1.5 KES per stick. Overhead: These are ongoing costs like rent, electricity, internet, and transport. If your monthly overhead is 10,000 KES and you produce 500 units per month, overhead per unit = 20 KES. For incense, if you produce 2,000 sticks, overhead per stick = 5 KES. Now add these to your material cost. Perfume oil: materials 200 + packaging 90 + labor 20 + overhead 20 = 330 KES per unit. Incense stick: materials 17.85 + packaging 0.25 (5 KES per bundle of 20) + labor 1.5 + overhead 5 = 24.6 KES. Now your wholesale price should be at least double that: 660 KES for oil, 49 KES per stick (or 980 KES per bundle). Retail triple: 990 KES for oil, 74 KES per stick (1,480 KES per bundle). These numbers show why ignoring these costs leads to underpricing. Keep a simple notebook to track all expenses, even small ones like glue or tape.

4. Offering Discounts and Bulk Deals

Discounts and bulk deals can boost sales, but you must structure them so you still make a profit. First, know your break-even point. If your total cost per perfume oil is 330 KES, you cannot sell below that. For bulk deals, offer a lower price per unit but require a minimum order. For example, retail price is 990 KES. For orders of 1019 bottles, offer 10% off: 891 KES each. For 2049 bottles, 20% off: 792 KES each. For 50+ bottles, 30% off: 693 KES each. Check that even at 30% off, you still cover cost (330 KES) and make profit (363 KES per bottle). For incense, if cost per stick is 24.6 KES, retail 74 KES. Bulk: 10 bundles (200 sticks) at 10% off = 66.6 KES per stick; 50 bundles at 20% off = 59.2 KES per stick; 100 bundles at 30% off = 51.8 KES per stick. All are above cost. You can also offer free delivery for bulk orders within your city. Another strategy: 'Buy 5 get 1 free' for retail. If you sell 5 at 990 KES, you get 4,950 KES. Giving one free costs you 330 KES, so total revenue 4,950 for 6 units, average 825 KES per unit. That's still profitable (cost 330). But be careful: free items should not exceed your profit margin. Always calculate. Use a simple formula: Discounted price = Original price (1 discount rate). Ensure discounted price > total cost. Also, consider seasonal discounts during holidays or for loyal customers. Keep records of all discounts given so you can see if they increase sales enough to justify lower margins. Many African markets respond well to 'wholesale price' when buying in bulk, so label your deals clearly.

5. Tracking Expenses with a Simple Notebook or App

Tracking every expense is the only way to know if you're making a profit. You don't need fancy software; a simple notebook works. Divide a page into columns: Date, Item, Cost, Quantity, Total. For example: 1/5/2025, Jojoba oil, 5,000 KES, 1 litre. 2/5/2025, Fragrance oil, 8,000 KES, 500 ml. 3/5/2025, Bottles, 1,500 KES, 30 bottles. At the end of each week, add up all expenses. Also record sales: Date, Product, Quantity, Price per unit, Total. At month's end, subtract total expenses from total sales to get profit. If you prefer digital, use a free app like 'Expense Manager' or 'Wave' (for invoicing). Even Google Sheets on your phone works. Create a simple spreadsheet with two tabs: Expenses and Sales. For expenses, list categories: Raw Materials, Packaging, Labor, Overhead, Transport, Miscellaneous. For sales, list each transaction. The key is consistency: record every purchase and sale, no matter how small. A 50 KES pack of stickers matters. At the end of each month, calculate your cost per unit again using actual expenses. You might find that your cost per unit has changed. Adjust your prices accordingly. Also, track your inventory: how many bottles or sticks you have left. This helps you plan purchases and avoid overstocking. Many African entrepreneurs use a 'cash book' where they note all money in and out. That's enough. If you have a smartphone, apps like 'Keeper' or 'QuickBooks Self-Employed' can help, but a notebook is fine. The goal is to know your numbers so you can make smart decisions.

6. Determining Profit Margins for Growth

Profit margin is the percentage of each sale that is profit. It tells you how much room you have to grow. Calculate it as: (Selling Price Total Cost) / Selling Price 100. For perfume oil with total cost 330 KES and retail price 990 KES: profit = 660 KES. Margin = 660/990 100 = 66.7%. For wholesale at 660 KES: profit = 330 KES, margin = 50%. For incense stick cost 24.6 KES, retail 74 KES: profit = 49.4 KES, margin = 66.8%. Wholesale at 49 KES: profit = 24.4 KES, margin = 49.8%. These are healthy margins. But you need to consider volume. If you sell 100 oils retail, profit = 66,000 KES. If you sell 200 wholesale, profit = 66,000 KES. Same profit, but more work. So decide your strategy: high margin, low volume (retail) or lower margin, high volume (wholesale). For growth, aim for a net profit margin of at least 2030% after all expenses. Use your profit to reinvest: buy better packaging, more raw materials, or market your products. For example, if you make 50,000 KES profit in a month, save 30% (15,000 KES) for business growth, and take 70% as your income. As you grow, you can negotiate better prices for supplies, reducing your cost per unit and increasing margin. Also, track your profit margin monthly. If it drops, find out why: maybe raw material prices increased, or you gave too many discounts. Adjust prices or cut costs. Remember, profit is not just for spending; it's for growing your business. Set a goal: e.g., increase profit margin by 5% in three months by buying in bulk. With consistent tracking and smart pricing, you can build a sustainable perfume and incense business.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Pricing & Costing Perfume Oils and Incense

This practical reference is written for African perfume oil and incense makers who want to price confidently, protect their margins, and grow from a kitchen-table operation into a registered, bankable business. Whether you are blending attar-inspired roll-ons in Mombasa, pressing frankincense resin in Somaliland, or hand-rolling agarbatti in Nairobi's Kariobangi, the arithmetic is the same. What changes is your cost base, your packaging, your labour, and the channels you sell through. Get the numbers right and you can quote a wholesale buyer without fear. Get them wrong and you will work harder for less money.

The single biggest mistake small African fragrance businesses make is pricing from the competitor's shelf tag instead of from their own cost sheet. A 10 ml roll-on selling for KES 500 in a mall is not your benchmark your benchmark is your own cost per unit plus a margin that funds growth, stock, and the next batch of raw materials. This lesson walks through the full costing chain, from a single bottle of oil to a 50-piece wholesale carton.

1. Calculating Cost per Unit for Oils and Incense

Cost per unit is the foundation of every price you will ever quote. It is not what you "feel" a bottle is worth it is the total money and time that left your hands to put that bottle on the shelf. For perfume oils, the dominant cost is usually the fragrance concentrate and the carrier (jojoba, sweet almond, or fractionated coconut oil). For incense, it is resin or charcoal, binder, and bamboo sticks.

Work in batches. If you make 100 10 ml roll-ons in one session, add up every cost in that session and divide by 100. Do this once honestly and you will never guess again.

2. Setting Wholesale and Retail Prices

Once you know your cost per unit, pricing becomes a rule, not a guess. The rule used across African informal and formal trade is simple: wholesale is a smaller multiple of cost, retail is a larger multiple. Wholesale buyers take volume and pay faster, so they earn a discount but never below your cost plus a thin margin.

3. Accounting for Packaging, Labour, and Overhead

These three items quietly destroy margins if ignored. Packaging in Africa is often the second largest cost after the fragrance itself imported glass and pumps can double your unit cost. Labour is the cost most often forgotten by owner-operators; if you do not pay yourself, you are subsidising your customers. Overhead includes rent, electricity, water, internet bundles for WhatsApp orders, and the annual single business permit.

Practical rules:

  • Packaging: Buy in bulk from local suppliers where quality allows. A reusable refill model (customer keeps the bottle, buys 10 ml refill pouches) cuts packaging cost by up to 60%.
  • Labour: Set a realistic hourly rate for yourself even KES 200/hour is better than zero. Add 15% for statutory burdens if you employ anyone.
  • Overhead: Allocate a fixed monthly amount across expected monthly units. If monthly overhead is KES 15,000 and you make 1,000 units, add KES 15 per unit.
SAFETY WARNING FLAMMABLE & REGULATORY

Perfume oils and incense contain high concentrations of flammable alcohol, solvents, and resins. Never store bulk stock near open flames, generators, or cooking areas. In many African markets (Kenya KEBS, Nigeria NAFDAC, Ghana FDA, South Africa NRCS), cosmetic and fragrance products require registration before retail sale. Selling unregistered cosmetic products can attract fines, product seizure, or closure. Keep a Material Safety Data Sheet (MSDS) for every raw material, label allergens, and store concentrates in a cool, ventilated room away from children. Do not price a product so low that you cannot afford compliant packaging and labelling non-compliance is a cost, not a saving.

4. Offering Discounts and Bulk Deals

Discounts are a tool, not a habit. The rule: a discount must be funded by either a lower cost (larger batch, cheaper packaging) or a higher volume that still covers your fixed costs. Never discount below your wholesale floor unless you are clearing dead stock.

  • Volume tiers: 5% off at 10 units, 12% off at 25, 20% off at 50+.
  • Bundle deals: "Buy 3 roll-ons, get 1 incense pack free" moves slow stock without cutting the headline price.
  • Payment terms discount: 3% off for payment within 7 days. This protects your cash flow, which matters more than the discount itself.
  • Festival pricing: Ramadan, Christmas, and wedding season justify premium gift packaging at full price do not discount during peak demand.

5. Tracking Expenses with a Simple Notebook or App

You do not need accounting software to start. You need consistency. A single ruled exercise book with four columns Date, Description, Money In, Money Out is enough for the first year. Record every purchase the same day, including transport (boda boda, matatu) and airtime.

When you outgrow the notebook, move to a free app. Recommended options that work offline or on low data in Africa:

  • Wave Accounting free, works on Android, supports M-Pesa-style cash entries manually.
  • Kakebo / Money Manager simple expense trackers, no internet needed.
  • Google Sheets a shared sheet with your supplier and sales agent keeps everyone honest.

At the end of every month, total your Money Out and compare it to your Cost per Unit calculation. If actual spending is higher, your cost sheet is wrong fix it before you quote again.

6. Determining Profit Margins for Growth

Profit margin is what is left after every cost, including your own labour. There are two numbers to watch:

  • Gross margin = (Price Cost per unit) Price. Aim for 50%+ on retail, 35%+ on wholesale.
  • Net margin = (Total sales Total expenses) Total sales. Aim for 1525% in year one, growing to 30%+ as you scale.

Reinvest at least 30% of net profit into raw material stock, packaging, and a small marketing budget. In the African fragrance market, the businesses that survive are the ones that can quote a wholesale order of 500 units without borrowing at high interest because they know their numbers cold.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Calculate cost per unit for every product, every batch never guess.
  2. Include your own labour at a real hourly rate; do not work for free.
  3. Allocate overhead monthly across expected production volume.
  4. Set wholesale at 1.62.0 cost; retail at 2.54.0 cost.
  5. Record every expense the same day notebook or app, but consistent.
  6. Discount only when funded by volume or cost savings, never below cost.
  7. Check regulatory registration (KEBS, NAFDAC, FDA, NRCS) before retail sale.
  8. Review gross and net margin monthly; reinvest at least 30% of profit.
