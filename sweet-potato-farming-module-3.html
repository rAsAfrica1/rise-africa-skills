1. Evaluating Soil Texture, pH, and Fertility

Sweet potatoes grow best in loose, well-drained soil. To check texture, grab a handful of moist soil and squeeze it into a ball. If it holds shape but crumbles when you poke it, it's loam ideal. If it forms a sticky ribbon, it's clay too heavy. If it won't hold together, it's sand too loose. For sweet potatoes, you want sandy loam or loam. In Africa, many soils are sandy or clayey; you can improve them with organic matter.

Soil pH affects nutrient availability. Sweet potatoes prefer pH 5.8 to 6.2, but they tolerate 4.5 to 7.5. You can test pH with a simple kit from an agro-dealer (cost about $5$10) or by sending a sample to a district agricultural office. To raise pH (make less acidic), add wood ash or lime. To lower pH, add elemental sulfur or pine needles. But don't guess test first.

Fertility: sweet potatoes need moderate nitrogen, high phosphorus, and high potassium. A common recommendation is 200 kg/ha of NPK 6-10-20 or similar. But without a soil test, you might waste money. Look for signs: pale leaves mean nitrogen deficiency; purple leaves mean phosphorus deficiency; yellow edges mean potassium deficiency. Also, add compost or manure 5 to 10 tons per hectare to boost organic matter and nutrients.

In Africa, many soils are degraded. Rotate with legumes like beans or groundnuts to fix nitrogen. Avoid fields where sweet potatoes or other root crops grew last season to prevent pest buildup. If you must reuse a field, apply extra compost. Remember, healthy soil = healthy roots. A simple test: dig a hole 30 cm deep; if you see earthworms and dark soil, it's fertile. If hardpan or pale soil, you need amendments.

Finally, check drainage. Sweet potatoes rot in waterlogged soil. Dig a 30 cm hole, fill with water, and see how long it takes to drain. If still there after 2 hours, choose another site or make raised beds. Good texture, correct pH, and adequate fertility set the foundation for a bumper harvest.

2. Choosing the Right Field: Drainage and Slope

Sweet potatoes hate wet feet. Waterlogged soil causes root rot, especially during heavy rains. Choose a field that slopes gently 2% to 5% is ideal. This allows excess water to run off without causing erosion. If your land is flat, you can still grow sweet potatoes, but you must make raised beds or ridges. Avoid low-lying areas where water collects after rain. In many parts of Africa, valleys and depressions flood; keep sweet potatoes on higher ground.

Check drainage by digging a 30 cm deep hole and filling it with water. If water drains within 12 hours, drainage is good. If it takes 4+ hours, it's poor. For poor drainage, either install drainage ditches (30 cm deep around the field) or plant on ridges 3040 cm high. Ridges also warm the soil earlier in the season, which sweet potatoes like.

Slope matters for erosion. On slopes above 8%, plant across the slope (contour) and use vetiver grass or other barriers to slow runoff. In steep areas, terracing works but is labor-intensive. For most smallholders, a gentle slope with contour ridges is sufficient. Avoid steep slopes because heavy rain can wash away topsoil and expose roots.

Also consider aspect: in hot, dry regions, a north-facing slope (in southern hemisphere) or south-facing (northern hemisphere) gets less direct sun, reducing heat stress. In cooler highlands, a sunnier slope warms soil faster. Wind can also damage vines; plant windbreaks like napier grass or maize if strong winds are common.

Finally, check previous land use. Avoid fields that were recently in sweet potatoes or other root crops (carrots, cassava) due to shared pests like sweet potato weevil. Also, avoid fields with heavy weed pressure, especially couch grass. If you must use such a field, fallow it or plant a cover crop like mucuna for one season. A good field is one that drains well, has gentle slope, and hasn't had sweet potatoes for at least 2 years.

3. Land Clearing and Primary Tillage

Start by clearing the field. Remove bushes, trees, and rocks. For small plots, use a machete or slasher. For larger areas, a brush cutter or tractor-mounted slasher saves time. Cut vegetation close to the ground and remove it or burn it (but burning loses nutrients; better to compost or use as mulch). If there are perennial weeds like couch grass or spear grass, dig out the roots they will regrow if left. In Africa, many farmers use hand hoes (jembe) for clearing; it's labor-intensive but effective for small plots.

Primary tillage breaks up compacted soil and buries weeds. The goal is to loosen soil to 2030 cm depth. You can use a hand hoe, ox-drawn plough, or tractor plough. For hand tillage, dig with a jembe, turning soil over. Do this when soil is moist but not wet if it sticks to your tool, it's too wet. Tillage is best done at the start of the rains, so you can plant soon after. In dry areas, till after the first rains to avoid hard clods.

If using an ox-plough, plough to 1520 cm depth. A tractor disc plough can go deeper (2530 cm). Avoid ploughing too deep if subsoil is poor you might bring up infertile clay. After ploughing, let the soil rest for a week or two to allow weeds to germinate, then do secondary tillage to kill them. This is called stale seedbed technique.

For no-till or minimum tillage, you can plant sweet potatoes into undisturbed soil if it's loose and well-drained. But most African soils benefit from some tillage to break hardpans. A hardpan is a compacted layer 1520 cm down; test with a metal rod if it won't penetrate, you need to break it. Use a chisel plough or hand hoe to fracture it.

Remember, primary tillage should be done once. Over-tilling destroys soil structure and organic matter. After tillage, the field is ready for secondary tillage and bed formation.

4. Secondary Tillage and Bed Formation

Secondary tillage refines the seedbed. After primary ploughing, use a hand hoe or harrow to break clods and level the soil. The goal is a fine tilth soil particles like breadcrumbs so sweet potato roots can expand easily. Do this when soil is dry enough to crumble. If you're using a tractor, a disc harrow works well. For hand tools, a rake or hoe is fine. Remove any remaining weeds and rocks.

Sweet potatoes are usually grown on ridges or mounds. Ridges are long, raised beds; mounds are circular hills. Ridges are better for drainage and mechanization. Make ridges 3040 cm high and 6080 cm apart. For mounds, make them 30 cm high and 60 cm wide. The spacing depends on your variety and soil fertility. On poor soil, closer spacing (60 cm) gives more plants per hectare; on fertile soil, wider (80100 cm) allows bigger roots.

To form ridges, use a hand hoe or ox-drawn ridger. Start by marking lines with a string. Then pull soil from both sides to form a ridge. In dry areas, make ridges flat-topped to conserve moisture; in wet areas, make them peaked to shed water. If you have a tractor, a ridger attachment makes quick work. For small plots, a hoe is fine it takes about 23 days to ridge 1 hectare by hand.

Ridges should run across the slope (contour) to reduce erosion. If the field is flat, run them north-south for even sun exposure. Add compost or manure into the ridge before planting mix it into the top 15 cm. This concentrates nutrients where roots will grow. You can also add a handful of wood ash per ridge for potassium.

After forming ridges, let them settle for a few days before planting. If it rains heavily, ridges may slump; repair them. A well-formed ridge warms up faster, drains excess water, and gives roots room to swell. This is critical for a good harvest.

5. Soil Amendments: Organic Matter and Compost

Sweet potatoes respond well to organic matter. It improves soil structure, holds water, and feeds beneficial microbes. The best amendment is compost decomposed plant and animal waste. Make compost by layering green materials (kitchen scraps, grass clippings) with brown materials (dry leaves, straw) and a little manure. Turn it every 2 weeks. In 23 months, you get dark, crumbly compost. Apply 510 tons per hectare (about 12 kg per square meter) before planting. Mix it into the top 15 cm of soil.

If you don't have compost, use well-rotted manure. Cow, goat, or poultry manure works, but poultry is high in nitrogen use sparingly (23 tons/ha) to avoid excessive vines. Fresh manure can burn roots; let it age for at least 3 months. In Africa, many farmers keep livestock; collect manure and store it under cover to prevent nutrient loss from rain and sun.

Green manures are another option. Plant legumes like mucuna, lablab, or cowpeas in the off-season, then plough them under before they set seed. They add nitrogen and organic matter. For example, mucuna can produce 100150 kg of nitrogen per hectare. This is a cheap way to fertilize. Also, crop residues like maize stalks can be chopped and incorporated, but they take longer to decompose.

Wood ash is a good source of potassium and raises pH. Apply 12 handfuls per ridge (about 500 kg/ha). But don't overdo it too much ash can make soil too alkaline. If you have access to bone meal, it adds phosphorus. Rock phosphate is slow-release but effective in acidic soils.

Remember, organic matter is not a quick fix. It builds soil health over seasons. Start with whatever you have even a small amount helps. Apply amendments 24 weeks before planting so they can integrate. Avoid applying fresh manure just before harvest, as it can introduce pathogens. Healthy soil grows healthy sweet potatoes.

6. Testing Soil and Interpreting Results

Soil testing takes the guesswork out of farming. You can use a simple DIY kit or send samples to a lab. For a DIY kit, buy from an agro-dealer (cost $10$20). It tests pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. To take a sample, collect soil from 1015 spots across the field, digging 15 cm deep. Mix them in a clean bucket, then take a subsample of about 500g. Air-dry it, then follow kit instructions. For lab testing, contact your district agricultural office many offer low-cost or free tests.

Interpret pH first. Sweet potatoes prefer 5.86.2. If pH is below 5.0, add lime (24 tons/ha) or wood ash. If above 7.0, add elemental sulfur or organic matter like pine needles. Lime takes 23 months to act, so apply before the season. For nitrogen, a reading of 0.10.2% is adequate. If low, add compost or a nitrogen fertilizer like urea (100 kg/ha). But too much nitrogen gives leafy vines and small roots.

Phosphorus should be 1020 ppm (parts per million). If low, add bone meal or triple superphosphate (100 kg/ha). Potassium should be 100200 ppm. If low, add wood ash or potassium sulfate (50100 kg/ha). Remember, these are general guidelines your local extension officer can give specific rates for your area. In many African soils, phosphorus is the most limiting nutrient.

Also test for organic matter. A simple test: put a tablespoon of soil in a jar with water, shake, and let settle. Organic matter floats. If less than 2%, add compost. Earthworms are a good sign if you see none, soil is degraded.

Keep records of your test results and amendments. Over time, you'll see what works. If you can't test, observe your plants: yellow leaves = nitrogen; purple = phosphorus; brown edges = potassium. But testing is more accurate. Use results to make a fertilizer plan. For example, if pH is 5.5 and phosphorus is low, apply lime and bone meal. Then plant. Healthy soil = high yields.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Site Selection and Soil Preparation for Sweet Potato

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) is one of Africa's most reliable cash and food crops, but yield differences between farms often come down to one thing: where you plant and how you prepare the soil before the first vine touches the ground. A well-chosen, well-prepared field can return 1525 tonnes per hectare with minimal inputs, while a poorly drained or compacted site can halve that figure regardless of how good your planting material is. This reference lesson gives you the field-level decisions that protect your investment, reduce post-harvest losses, and keep buyers coming back.

Why Site Selection Is a Business Decision

Sweet potato roots are the product. They are underground, they are bulky, and they are unforgiving of waterlogged soils, hardpans, and low pH. Every decision at site selection stage directly affects:

  • Marketable yield root size, shape, and uniformity determine whether you sell to processors, supermarkets, or open markets.
  • Input costs poor soils require more fertilizer, more compost, and more labour to correct.
  • Harvest and transport costs slope and field access affect how quickly you can get roots to market before they spoil.
  • Risk exposure flooding, erosion, and drought stress are site-specific and largely avoidable.

Evaluating Soil Texture, pH, and Fertility

Sweet potato performs best on well-drained sandy loams and loamy sands. These soils warm quickly, allow roots to expand without obstruction, and are easy to harvest. Heavy clays produce misshapen, cracked roots and hold water that causes rot. Pure sands drain too fast and leach nutrients, but can work with heavy organic amendment.

You can assess texture in the field using the ribbon test: moisten a handful of soil, squeeze it between thumb and forefinger, and try to form a ribbon. If it forms a long ribbon (>5 cm), it is clay-heavy and needs organic matter and possibly raised beds. If it barely holds together, it is sandy and needs compost and split fertilizer applications.

Soil pH should ideally be 5.86.7. Sweet potato tolerates acidity better than many crops, but below pH 5.2, phosphorus locks up and aluminium toxicity can stunt roots. Above pH 7.5, micronutrients like zinc and boron become less available. Lime raises pH; elemental sulphur or organic matter can lower it slowly.

Choosing the Right Field: Drainage and Slope

Sweet potato will not tolerate standing water for more than 2448 hours. Waterlogging causes root rot, souring, and complete crop loss in severe cases. Choose fields that:

  • Drain freely look for slight elevation, natural surface drainage, or sandy subsoil.
  • Have a gentle slope 15% is ideal. Flat fields risk ponding; steep slopes (>10%) erode and make harvesting difficult.
  • Are not in low-lying valley bottoms these collect water and cold air.
  • Have good access trucks and motorbikes must reach the field edge during harvest, especially in the rainy season.

In flood-prone areas, use raised beds or ridges 3040 cm high. In dry areas, sunken beds or flat planting conserve moisture. Match your bed system to your rainfall pattern.

Land Clearing and Primary Tillage

Clear the field of bushes, stumps, and perennial weeds at least 34 weeks before planting. Perennial weeds like couch grass (Cynodon dactylon) and nut sedge (Cyperus spp.) will regrow and compete aggressively. Slash, then remove roots and rhizomes by hand or with a hoe. Burning is discouraged because it destroys organic matter and soil life, though controlled burning of cleared material is sometimes used on acidic soils.

Primary tillage should break the hardpan to at least 30 cm depth. Use a hand hoe, ox-drawn plough, or tractor. On smallholdings, double-digging (removing topsoil, breaking subsoil, replacing topsoil) is effective but labour-intensive. A single pass with a chisel plough or subsoiler is the most efficient option where available.

Secondary Tillage and Bed Formation

Secondary tillage creates a fine, uniform seedbed. Break clods, level the surface, and incorporate organic matter. Then form beds or ridges:

  • Ridges (3040 cm high, 6080 cm apart) best for wet areas and heavy soils.
  • Raised beds (2030 cm high, 1 m wide) good for commercial production and mechanized harvesting.
  • Flat planting suitable only for well-drained, light soils in dry areas.

Beds should run along the contour on sloping land to reduce erosion. Leave alleys or drainage channels between beds to manage water.

Soil Amendments: Organic Matter and Compost

Organic matter is the single most valuable amendment for sweet potato. It improves structure, water-holding capacity, nutrient exchange, and soil biology. Apply 510 tonnes per hectare of well-decomposed compost or manure, incorporated into the top 1520 cm during secondary tillage.

Fresh manure should be composted for at least 46 weeks to kill pathogens and weed seeds. Poultry litter is nutrient-rich but should be used sparingly (23 t/ha) to avoid salt burn. Green manures like cowpea, lablab, or mucuna can be grown and incorporated before planting, adding nitrogen and biomass.

Inorganic fertilizer, if used, should be applied based on soil test results. A typical recommendation is 200300 kg/ha of NPK 10-10-20 or similar, split between planting and 46 weeks after planting. Avoid high-nitrogen fertilizers late in the season, which promote vines at the expense of roots.

SAFETY WARNING Lime and Fertilizer Handling

Agricultural lime, dolomite, and inorganic fertilizers can cause serious eye and skin irritation. Always wear gloves, eye protection, and a dust mask when spreading lime or fertilizer. Never apply lime and ammonium-based fertilizer at the same time wait at least 2 weeks between applications to avoid ammonia loss. Store all chemicals away from food, water sources, and children. Wash hands and equipment thoroughly after use.

Testing Soil and Interpreting Results

Before you invest in seed, labour, and fertilizer, spend a small amount on a soil test. Options range from free or low-cost government extension services to commercial labs and simple field test kits.

What to test:

  • pH (most critical)
  • Organic matter content
  • Available phosphorus and potassium
  • Nitrogen (or estimate from organic matter)
  • Micronutrients (zinc, boron) if deficiency symptoms appear

How to sample: Take 1520 sub-samples from across the field in a zigzag pattern, 020 cm deep. Mix them in a clean bucket, quarter the mixture, and send about 500 g to a lab. Avoid sampling near fences, animal pens, or compost heaps.

Interpreting results: A lab report will give you values and often a recommendation. Focus on pH first, then phosphorus and potassium. If pH is below 5.5, lime before planting. If phosphorus is low, apply compost and rock phosphate or triple superphosphate. If potassium is low, apply muriate of potash (KCl) or wood ash. Always calibrate recommendations to your local soil type and rainfall.

For farmers without lab access, simple field kits (pH strips, NPK colour kits) give rough but useful guidance. Extension officers can also help interpret results and adjust recommendations for local conditions.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test soil pH and texture before clearing aim for pH 5.86.7 and sandy loam texture.
  2. Choose a field with good drainage and 15% slope; avoid valley bottoms and flood-prone areas.
  3. Clear perennial weeds and remove roots 34 weeks before planting.
  4. Break the hardpan to at least 30 cm depth using a hoe, plough, or subsoiler.
  5. Incorporate 510 t/ha compost or well-decomposed manure during secondary tillage.
  6. Form ridges or raised beds 3040 cm high, running along the contour on slopes.
  7. Apply lime if pH < 5.5 and wait at least 2 weeks before planting.
  8. Keep records of soil test results, amendments, and yields to refine your practice each season.
