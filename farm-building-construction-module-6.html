1. Roof Design and Pitch for Farm Buildings

Roof design starts with pitch, which is the angle of the roof. In Africa, most farm buildings use a pitch between 15 and 45 degrees. For thatch, you need at least 45 degrees so water runs off fast. For corrugated metal, 15 degrees is enough, but if you are in a high-rainfall area like the coast, go 20 degrees or more. A simple way to set pitch is using the 1:4 rule: for every 4 units horizontal, rise 1 unit. For example, if your building is 6 metres wide, the roof rises 1.5 metres from wall to ridge. You can mark this with a line level and string.

Gable roofs are easiest to build and cheapest. They have two sloping sides meeting at a ridge. Hip roofs are stronger in high winds but take more skill. For farm buildings, gable is usually best. The overhang should be at least 600 mm on all sides to keep rain off walls. In windy areas, keep overhang to 400 mm to reduce uplift.

Rafters are the main sloping members. Use timber that is dry and treated. For a 4metre span, use 50 x 150 mm rafters spaced at 800 mm centres. Purlins run across rafters to hold roofing sheets. For metal sheets, purlin spacing is 900 mm to 1200 mm. For tiles, use 300 mm to 400 mm. Always use galvanised nails or screws. In termite areas, treat all timber with used engine oil or buy treated poles.

Good design also considers ventilation. Leave a gap at the ridge or use vented ridge caps. This lets hot air escape, keeping the building cooler. Finally, plan for rainwater harvesting: slope the roof toward gutters. A 6 m x 10 m roof can collect 60,000 litres of water per year in a 1000 mm rainfall area. That is enough for livestock or irrigation.

2. Installation of Corrugated Metal Roofing

Corrugated metal sheets are the most common roofing in Africa. They come in lengths up to 3 metres, but you can order custom lengths to avoid overlaps. Use 0.4 mm to 0.5 mm thick sheets for farm buildings. Thinner sheets dent easily. You need: roofing sheets, purlins (50 x 75 mm timber), galvanised screws with rubber washers, a drill or hammer, a hacksaw or tin snips, a measuring tape, and a chalk line.

Start at the bottom edge (eave) and work up. Lay the first sheet square with the gable end. Overlap sheets by one corrugation if the roof slope is steep, or two corrugations if slope is less than 20 degrees. For end laps, overlap 150 mm for slopes under 20 degrees, and 100 mm for steeper. Fix screws at every second corrugation on the purlins. Drive screws straight and tight enough to compress the rubber washer, but not so tight that you dent the sheet. On the ends, use screws every corrugation.

Cut sheets with tin snips or an angle grinder with a metal cutting disc. Never cut with a hammer and chisel it damages the zinc coating. After cutting, paint the cut edges with zinc-rich paint or bitumen to prevent rust. Walk on the sheets only on the purlins, and wear soft-soled shoes. In hot sun, metal gets very hot wear gloves.

For a 6 m x 10 m roof at 20 degrees pitch, you need about 80 square metres of roofing. If sheets are 0.8 m wide and 3 m long, each covers 2.4 square metres, so order 34 sheets. Add 10% for waste. Use 6 screws per square metre, so about 480 screws. Buy screws with neoprene washers they last longer. Finally, check for leaks after the first rain and tighten any loose screws.

3. Roofing with Tiles and Shingles

Tiles and shingles give a neat look and last 3050 years, but they are heavier and need stronger roof structure. Common types in Africa are clay tiles, concrete tiles, and bitumen shingles. Clay tiles are made locally in many countries. They weigh about 45 kg per square metre, so your trusses must be strong. Use 50 x 150 mm rafters at 600 mm centres, and purlins at 300400 mm centres.

For clay or concrete tiles, start at the eave and work up. Each tile overlaps the one below. Fix tiles with galvanised nails or clips two per tile in windy areas. Cut tiles with a tile cutter or angle grinder with diamond blade. At the ridge, use ridge tiles bedded in mortar (1 part cement to 3 parts sand). At hips and valleys, use special tiles or lead flashing. For bitumen shingles, you nail them to a plywood or OSB deck. Use 6 nails per shingle. In hot climates, shingles can soften; choose lighter colours to reflect heat.

Tiles are heavy, so you need a strong foundation and walls. A 6 m x 10 m roof with tiles adds about 2.7 tonnes of weight. Make sure your trusses are designed for this. If you are unsure, consult a carpenter. Also, tiles need a steeper pitch at least 22 degrees for clay tiles, 15 degrees for concrete tiles, and 20 degrees for shingles. Lower pitch causes water to blow back under the tiles.

Maintenance: replace broken tiles immediately. Check for moss and algae, especially in humid areas. You can clean with a stiff brush and water. Avoid walking on tiles they break easily. Use a roof ladder. In areas with hail, tiles can crack; consider metal sheets instead. But for a permanent farmhouse or store, tiles are a good investment.

4. Waterproofing and Flashing Details

Waterproofing stops water from entering at joints, valleys, and penetrations. Flashing is the material used usually galvanised steel, aluminium, or PVC. The most common leak points are: valleys (where two roof slopes meet), ridges, eaves, chimneys, vents, and wall junctions. At valleys, use a valley flashing at least 600 mm wide, laid under the roofing. For metal roofs, use a pre-formed valley tray. For tiles, use lead or aluminium flashing. Fix with roofing nails and seal with roofing mastic.

At the ridge, use ridge caps. For corrugated metal, buy curved ridge pieces that match the profile. Overlap them by 100 mm and fix with screws. For tiles, bed ridge tiles in mortar. In high-rain areas, add a ridge vent to allow air out but keep rain out. At eaves, install a drip edge an L-shaped metal strip that goes under the roofing and over the fascia. This directs water into the gutter and prevents it from rotting the fascia board.

Around chimneys or vent pipes, use a boot flashing (a rubber or metal collar) that slides over the pipe and seals to the roof. For square penetrations, build a curb (a raised box) and flash around it. Use self-adhesive bitumen tape for small repairs, but it is not permanent. For flat roofs, use torch-on bitumen membrane or EPDM rubber. Apply a primer first, then roll out the membrane and seal overlaps with a torch or adhesive.

Always use good quality sealants: polyurethane or silicone, not cheap acrylic. Clean the surface before applying. In Africa, intense UV degrades sealants quickly, so inspect every year before the rains. Replace cracked or pulled flashing. A small leak can rot timber and ruin stored crops. Spend time on flashing it is the difference between a dry building and a wet one.

5. Gutters and Downspouts for Rainwater Management

Gutters collect rainwater from the roof and channel it to downspouts, which direct it to storage tanks or away from the building. In Africa, rainwater harvesting is vital. A 100 square metre roof in a 800 mm rainfall area can collect 80,000 litres per year. That is enough to water 100 head of cattle for a month or irrigate a small garden. Gutters are usually PVC, galvanised steel, or aluminium. PVC is cheapest and easy to work with.

Size gutters based on roof area. For up to 50 square metres, use 100 mm diameter gutter. For 50100 square metres, use 125 mm. For larger, use 150 mm. Slope gutters toward downspouts at 1:500 (2 mm per metre). Use a string line to set the slope. Fix gutters with brackets every 800 mm. At corners, use pre-made angles or seal joints with PVC cement. At the downspout, use a drop outlet. Downspouts should be 75100 mm diameter. Place one downspout for every 1012 metres of gutter. In heavy rain areas, use two.

Install a leaf guard or gutter mesh to keep out leaves and debris. Clean gutters twice a year before and after the rainy season. A blocked gutter overflows and damages walls and foundations. For downspouts, direct water into a tank or a soakaway pit. A soakaway is a hole filled with stones and gravel, 1 m deep and 1 m wide, that lets water soak into the ground. Keep soakaway at least 3 metres from the building foundation.

If you store water, use a first-flush diverter to discard the first dirty water. A simple diverter is a pipe with a cap that you open manually. For tanks, use food-grade plastic or concrete. Cover tanks to prevent mosquito breeding. In dry areas, every drop counts. A well-designed gutter system pays for itself in one season.

6. Roof Insulation and Ventilation

In hot African climates, a metal roof can reach 70C, making the building unbearable. Insulation and ventilation work together to keep the space cooler. Insulation slows heat transfer; ventilation removes hot air. The cheapest insulation is a reflective foil (aluminium foil) laid under the roofing sheets. It reflects radiant heat. Install it over the purlins before fixing sheets. For better insulation, use 50 mm thick polyurethane or polystyrene boards, but they are expensive. A low-cost option is to use a ceiling of woven grass mats or plasterboard with 100 mm of sawdust or vermiculite on top.

Ventilation is critical. Hot air rises and must escape. Install ridge vents along the ridge. For metal roofs, use vented ridge caps or a continuous ridge vent. At the eaves, leave a 25 mm gap between the wall and the roof, covered with insect mesh. This allows cool air to enter and hot air to exit. In still air, add a turbine vent or solar-powered extractor fan. For livestock buildings, aim for 0.1 square metres of vent area per 10 square metres of floor area.

In cold highland areas, insulation keeps heat in. Use 100 mm of fibreglass or rockwool in the ceiling. Seal gaps to prevent draughts. But never block ventilation completely moisture from animals and crops causes condensation and rot. Use a vapour barrier on the warm side of the insulation. In thatched roofs, ventilation is natural through the thatch, but you still need a ridge vent.

Maintain insulation: check for tears or dampness. Wet insulation is useless. If you use foil, make sure it is not torn during installation. For existing roofs, you can add insulation from below, but it is easier during construction. A well-insulated and ventilated roof reduces heat stress on animals, improves milk yield in dairy cows, and protects stored feed from spoilage. It is worth the extra cost.

<h2>Practical Reference: Roofing Systems for Farm Buildings in Africa</h2>

<p>Roofs are the single most expensive structural element on most African farm buildings, typically accounting for 2540% of total construction cost. For agribusiness operators, the roof is not merely shelter it determines whether stored grain stays below 13% moisture, whether poultry litter remains dry enough to prevent ammonia burn, whether a workshop's tools rust, and whether a dairy parlour passes inspection. A poorly specified roof can destroy a season's margin in one rainy season. A well-specified one can last 30 years and cut rainwater harvesting dependency on boreholes. This reference distils design data, installation tolerances, and cost benchmarks for the six core roofing lessons in this module.</p>

<h3>1. Roof Design and Pitch: The Cost-Critical Decision</h3>

<p>Pitch is the cheapest insurance you can buy. In African monsoon and highland zones, the design storm intensity commonly reaches 60120 mm/hour. A roof below 15 with corrugated galvanised iron (CGI) will back-feed water under laps during wind-driven rain; below 10 it will pond and fail within two seasons. For thatch, the practical minimum is 45; for clay tile, 30; for concrete tile, 22.5 (or 17.5 with double underlay). Each 5 of pitch adds roughly 812% to sheet area and 46% to total roof cost but it removes the need for sealants, secondary underlay, and repeat call-backs.</p>

<p>Design span drives purlin spacing. On a 6 m gable with CGI, purlins at 900 mm centres are standard; at 1,200 mm the sheet deflects under foot traffic and fatigues at the fastener line. Wind uplift is the silent killer on open-sided livestock sheds always specify cyclonic washers and a minimum 3 fasteners per sheet per purlin in coastal and high-wind zones such as Mombasa, Beira, and Dakar.</p>

<h3>2. Installation of Corrugated Metal Roofing</h3>

<p>CGI remains the workhorse of African farm buildings because it is light (4.55 kg/m), locally rolled in most capitals, and installable by a two-person crew. The failure mode is rarely the sheet it is the fastener and the lap. Always lap end-to-end by 150 mm minimum (200 mm in exposed sites) and side-lap by one corrugation. Fasten through the crest, not the trough, using 65 mm umbrella-head screws with EPDM washers. Over-driving the screw crushes the washer and guarantees a leak within 12 months.</p>

<p>Cut sheets with a nibbler or hand snips. Angle grinders burn off the zinc coating along the cut edge and initiate rust within weeks a mistake that costs a full re-roof. Touch up all cuts and scratches with zinc-rich paint. On-site rolling is now common in Kenya, Tanzania, and Nigeria, cutting transport cost by 3040% for spans over 8 m.</p>

<h3>3. Roofing with Tiles and Shingles</h3>

<p>Tiles and shingles are capital-heavy but lifecycle-cheap. A concrete-tile roof on a 150 m poultry house costs roughly 2.5 CGI upfront but lasts 40+ years with near-zero maintenance, and its thermal mass keeps internal temperature 35C lower directly improving feed conversion ratio in broiler production. Clay tiles are produced locally in Uganda, Rwanda, and Zimbabwe and support regional value chains.</p>

<p>The installation discipline is underlay first: a breathable membrane (not polythene) across the rafters before battens. Never mortar-bed ridge tiles on a timber roof thermal movement cracks the mortar. Use dry-fix ridge systems with foam or mechanical clips. Shingles (bitumen or stone-coated steel) suit low-pitch verandas and office blocks but are vulnerable to UV degradation in the Sahel; specify SBS-modified bitumen rated for 40C+ surface temperature.</p>

<h3>4. Waterproofing and Flashing Details</h3>

<p>Ninety percent of roof leaks occur at penetrations and junctions, not in the field of the roof. Flashing must be designed, not improvised. Key details: chimney and vent pipe flashings with 150 mm vertical leg and 100 mm apron; valley flashing in 0.6 mm coated steel or self-adhesive bitumen membrane; and wall abutment flashing stepped into mortar joints at every course.</p>

<p>For flat or low-pitch sections over feed stores, use a two-layer torch-on membrane (3 mm + 4 mm) with 100 mm side laps and 150 mm end laps, laid perpendicular to the fall. Prime the substrate and never torch over wet concrete trapped moisture blisters the membrane within one hot season.</p>

<h3>5. Gutters and Downspouts: Rainwater as a Business Asset</h3>

<p>On a 200 m roof in a 800 mm annual rainfall zone, you can harvest roughly 140,000 litres per year after 12% losses. At a borehole or tanker cost of KES 38 per litre in dry regions, that is a real revenue line. Size gutters using the formula: gutter cross-section (cm) = roof plan area (m) rainfall intensity (mm/hr) 1,500. For a 200 m roof at 100 mm/hr, that gives ~13 cm a 125 mm half-round gutter. Downspouts: one 75 mm pipe per 50 m of roof; one 110 mm pipe per 100 m.</p>

<h3>6. Roof Insulation and Ventilation</h3>

<p>Under a bare CGI roof in West African conditions, surface temperature can reach 70C, radiating 812C of heat into the building below. For poultry, dairy, and seed storage, that is a direct production loss. Two low-cost interventions: (a) a radiant barrier aluminium foil laminated to the underside of the sheet or laid over purlins cuts radiant heat gain by up to 60%; (b) a ventilated ridge with eaves inlets sized at 1:300 of floor area provides continuous stack ventilation. In high-rainfall zones, close the eaves with insect mesh to keep out birds and rodents, which are the leading cause of feed contamination in stored grain.</p>

<strong style="color:#b03a2e;"> SAFETY WARNING Working at Height and Hot Work</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Roof work is the leading cause of fatal construction accidents on African farms. Before any roof installation or repair: (1) erect edge protection or a fall-arrest harness anchored to a certified point; (2) never walk on wet, dusty, or newly-painted CGI it is effectively frictionless at 15+; (3) keep a 10 m clear zone below and stop work when wind exceeds 30 km/h; (4) for torch-on membranes, maintain a fire watch for 60 minutes after the last torch and keep two 9 kg dry-powder extinguishers on the roof; (5) never work alone a spotter on the ground is mandatory. Train every crew member on harness use before first ascent.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Pitch check:</strong> Confirm CGI 15, tile 22.5, thatch 45 before ordering materials.</li>
<li><strong>Purlin spacing:</strong> 900 mm for CGI on farm spans; 345 mm battens for concrete tile.</li>
<li><strong>Fasteners:</strong> 65 mm umbrella-head screws with EPDM washers, through crest, not over-driven.</li>
<li><strong>Laps:</strong> 150 mm end lap minimum (200 mm in exposed coastal sites); one corrugation side lap.</li>
<li><strong>Flashing:</strong> 150 mm vertical leg, 100 mm apron at every penetration; step-flash wall abutments.</li>
<li><strong>Gutters:</strong> Size to 13 cm per 200 m at 100 mm/hr; one downspout per 50100 m.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilation:</strong> Ridge vent + eaves inlets at 1:300 of floor area; insect mesh on all openings.</li>
<li><strong>Safety:</strong> Harness, edge protection, fire watch for torch-on, and no lone working.</li>
</ol>
