Goat & Sheep Farming

A complete 6-month program — hardy breeds, raised housing, the FAMACHA worm war, tight kidding seasons and festival-timed sales — from first flock to compounding herd.

⏱️ 6 Months (8–12 hrs/week)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? Six months takes a goat or sheep flock through one full rhythm: buying right, a 5-month pregnancy (~150 days), and kids/lambs on the ground being grown for the festive market. Small stock are the fastest, lowest-capital route into livestock — and the most punished by one enemy: worms. This course is built around beating that enemy and selling into the price peaks.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each month → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen at the kraal and the sale pens.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Internal worms kill more goats and sheep in Africa than every other disease combined. Month 4 teaches the FAMACHA eye check — five seconds per animal that replaces guesswork with survival.
  • PPR ("goat plague") is a notifiable disease — rapid spread, high deaths, fever + mouth sores + diarrhoea + pneumonia together. Report immediately; vaccination campaigns exist in many countries.
  • Never spend before the Iron-Clad Price Check: 3 quotes for animals, fencing and feed — small stock margins are made at buying time.
MONTH 1 — Small Stock Science, Breeds & Planning ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Why goats and sheep are the smart start

  • Low entry cost: one cow's price buys 5–8 goats — and a flock multiplies faster (twins are normal; a doe can kid 3 times in 2 years).
  • Fast cycle: gestation ~150 days (5 months); kids/lambs reach sale weight (25–35 kg) at 6–9 months.
  • Drought insurance: goats browse bushes and trees that cattle cannot use; they stay alive on land where cattle fail.
  • The market never sleeps: religious festivals (Eid, Christmas, Easter), ceremonies and funerals create year-round cash demand with sharp price peaks (Month 6).

Lesson 1.2 — Goats vs sheep: know the difference

Lesson 1.3 — Breeds that pay in Africa

  • Boer goat: the meat benchmark — blocky, fast-growing (bucks 100+ kg); needs decent feeding and worm control.
  • Kalahari Red: Boer-class meat with better heat/hardiness; red coat resists sunburn.
  • Indigenous goats (Mashona, Small East African, WAD): small but supremely hardy, fertile, disease-tolerant — the ideal foundation females.
  • Dorper sheep: Africa's meat sheep — no shearing (sheds), fast growth, drought-hardy.
  • Indigenous fat-tailed sheep (e.g. Sabi, Maasai types): walk far, survive drought, tolerate worms better than exotics.
  • The classic profit cross: indigenous does/ewes (toughness, fertility) × Boer or Dorper male (growth and size on every kid/lamb).

Lesson 1.5 — Judging an animal at the sale

  • Age by teeth like cattle: 2 permanent incisors ≈ 1–1.5 yrs, 4 ≈ 2 yrs, 6 ≈ 2.5–3 yrs, full mouth ≈ 4 yrs; worn gums = old stock someone is offloading.
  • Health signs: bright eyes, glossy coat, firm pellet droppings, no limping, no bottle-jaw (soft swelling under the jaw = severe worms), udder soft and even on females.
  • FAMACHA preview: pull down the lower eyelid — deep red/pink is healthy; pale pink to white means dangerous worm anaemia. Never buy pale-eyed animals.

(2) Walk your land and list the browse trees/shrubs goats could use — photograph five. (3) Decide goats, sheep or mixed — and write your three reasons with the fencing implication.

MONTH 2 — Housing, Fencing & Flock Setup ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Housing that keeps small stock alive

  • The killers housing must beat: damp ground (worms + foot rot + pneumonia), night predators, theft and rain-chill on newborns.
  • The African best-practice goat house: a raised slatted floor 0.8–1 m off the ground — droppings and urine fall through, hooves stay dry, worm reinfection drops sharply, and cleaning is a broom job.
  • Space: 1.5–2 m² per adult inside; kidding/lambing pens 2 m² per female; shade and a rain roof are non-negotiable, full walls are not (ventilation beats warmth everywhere except the newborn pen).
  • Slat gap 1.5–2 cm: wide enough for pellets, too narrow for small hooves.

Lesson 2.2 — Fencing: the goat test

  • Rule: if a fence holds water, it will hold a goat. Practical target: 1.2–1.4 m high, tight mesh (diamond/veld mesh) or 6–8 well-strained wires with close spacing at the bottom.
  • Sheep need less height but more predator-proofing: tight bottom wires or mesh against jackals and dogs; night kraaling remains the cheapest predator answer.
  • Living fences (sisal, thorn brush reinforcement) cut costs; electric strands on existing fences stop chronic escapees.

Lesson 2.3 — Equipment on a budget

  • Raised hay rack/feeder: goats and sheep waste (and worm-contaminate) any feed placed on the ground — a rack pays for itself in one season.
  • Water troughs cleaned daily; a footbath tray (zinc sulphate or copper sulphate solution) at the house entrance where foot rot is a problem.
  • Handling: one small catch pen and a hanging scale or girth tape; hoof shears; marker crayons; a herd book.

Lesson 2.4 — Founding the flock

  • Starter flock that works: 10 indigenous/cross does or ewes (young, 2–4 tooth, eyelids red) + 1 quality Boer/Dorper male — bought from at least two different sources for genetic spread.
  • Buy females BEFORE the breeding season you plan; buy the male last and best — he is half of every kid.
  • Arrival protocol: quarantine 3 weeks, dose for worms, check/trim hooves, dip or pour-on for external parasites, then join the flock.
✅ Iron-Clad Price Check: mesh fencing, timber/slats and the animals — 3 quotes each. Fencing is the biggest capital item in small stock; mesh prices vary wildly between suppliers.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 2): (1) Design your raised house to scale for 12 animals + 2 kidding pens using the space numbers. (2) Build or fully cost the house, fence line and hay rack. (3) Assemble the buying file: quotes for 10 females + 1 male from at least 3 sellers.

MONTH 3 — Feeding & Browse Management ▾

Lesson 3.1 — What the flock must get

  • Intake: 3–4% of body weight in dry matter daily — a 30 kg goat needs roughly 1–1.2 kg DM (browse, grass, hay, crop residues combined).
  • Protein: growing kids/lambs and lactating females need 12–16% CP in the diet; dry adults maintain on 8–10%.
  • Minerals: a salt + phosphorus + trace-mineral lick available year-round; goats denied minerals chew bones, plastic and soil.
  • Clean water daily — pellet-dry droppings and low milk are the first signs of water shortage.

Lesson 3.2 — Browse: the free protein bank

  • Tree leaves and pods commonly run 12–25% protein — higher than most grass ever reaches. Acacia pods, mulberry, leucaena, moringa, pigeon pea and sesbania are flock superfoods.
  • Cut-and-carry: lopping branches into the rack feeds the flock without fence-breaking; coppiced trees regrow for repeat harvests.
  • Leucaena caution: excellent protein but limit to ~30% of the diet (mimosine toxicity at high levels).
  • Plant a fodder bank this rainy season: 50–100 multipurpose trees around kraals and boundaries = dry-season insurance forever.

Lesson 3.3 — Supplementation that pays

  • Flushing: feed females an extra 200–400 g/day of energy/protein (maize + cake, or good pods) for 3–4 weeks before the male joins — more twins, measurably.
  • Late pregnancy (last 6 weeks): 70% of foetal growth happens now; supplement 200–300 g/day or lose birth weights and milk.
  • Fattening for the festive market: 250–500 g/day concentrate for 6–10 weeks on selected sale animals — Month 6 does the maths.
  • Crop residues (groundnut haulms, bean straw, maize stover) stored dry at harvest are the backbone of dry-season feeding.

Lesson 3.4 — The grazing/browsing plan against worms

  • Worm larvae climb only the bottom 5–10 cm of wet grass — every feeding decision that keeps mouths OFF short wet grass is a worm-control decision.
  • Rotate kraaling areas and browse routes; rest paddocks 4–6+ weeks; browse (heads-up feeding) is naturally low-worm.
  • Never force hungry flocks onto short green regrowth after rain — that lawn is a larval minefield.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 3): (1) Build the feed budget: flock kg × 3.5% × 90 dry-season days vs your stored residues — close the gap on paper. (2) Cut-and-carry test: rack-feed browse for one week and record which species the flock prefers. (3) Plant or pot at least 20 fodder tree seedlings.

MONTH 4 — Health: Winning the Worm War & Beyond ▾

Lesson 4.1 — FAMACHA: the five-second life-saver

  • The deadliest worm (barber's pole worm, Haemonchus) sucks blood — so anaemia shows in the eyelid colour before the animal collapses.
  • The check: roll down the lower eyelid and score the inner colour — deep red (healthy) → red-pink → pink (watch) → pale pink (DOSE) → white (dose + emergency feeding, may need vet).
  • Check every animal every 2 weeks in the rainy season, monthly in the dry — and dose ONLY the pale ones.
  • Why selective dosing? Blanket-dosing the whole flock every month breeds super-worms resistant to every drench — the disaster already crippling many regions. Targeted dosing keeps the drugs working for years.

Lesson 4.2 — The rest of the health map

ProblemSignsPrevention / action
Coccidiosis (young stock)Dark/bloody diarrhoea in kids around weaningDry clean pens, raised floors; treat early (sulpha drugs per vet)
Foot rot (mainly sheep)Limping, foul smell between clawsTrim hooves, footbath, cull chronic cases, avoid wet kraals
Orf (sore mouth)Scabs on lips/gums of kidsSelf-limiting in ~3 weeks; feed soft; it infects humans — gloves!
Heartwater (tick-borne)Fever, nervous signs, sudden deathDip/pour-on on local schedule; vet fast if seen
PneumoniaCoughing, fast breathing after cold wet nightsDry draught-free housing; vet-directed antibiotics early
Pulpy kidney / enterotoxaemiaSudden death of the BEST-fed kids/lambsVaccinate (multiclostridial) — cheap and essential where feeding well
PPR — notifiableFever + mouth sores + diarrhoea + pneumonia together, spreading fastREPORT immediately; vaccination campaigns exist in many countries

Lesson 4.3 — The flock health calendar

  • Fortnightly (rainy season): FAMACHA all animals; dose pale ones only, recording which drench was used (rotate drench families yearly).
  • Quarterly: hoof inspection/trim; external parasite pour-on or dip per local tick pressure.
  • Yearly: multiclostridial vaccination (pulpy kidney/tetanus group) for all, 4–6 weeks pre-kidding for females so immunity passes in colostrum; PPR vaccination where campaigns run.
  • Always: record every treatment and respect withdrawal periods before slaughter sales.

Lesson 4.4 — The daily 60-second check

  • Count them, watch them walk, watch them chew: a small-stock animal that stands apart, ears down, not chewing cud is already sick — small stock hide illness until nearly too late.
  • Droppings check: firm pellets good; clumped soft = watch; scouring = act today.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 4): (1) FAMACHA-score your whole flock (or a neighbour's) and chart the scores. (2) Trim four animals' hooves correctly. (3) Write and laminate your 12-month health calendar with the vet office's local tick/PPR guidance and emergency number.

MONTH 5 — Breeding, Kidding & Raising the Crop ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Planning the breeding season

  • Work backwards from the money: kids/lambs sell best at festivals — count back ~150 days gestation + 6–9 months growth to set the joining date.
  • Controlled joining (male in for 6 weeks = two heat cycles) gives one tight kidding batch: one vaccination, one weaning, one sale group.
  • Flush females (Month 3), check the male's feet/teeth/testicles beforehand, and mark his brisket with raddle/crayon so served females show colour — your pregnancy register writes itself.

Lesson 5.2 — Kidding and lambing management

  • Move heavily pregnant females to clean kidding pens; most deliver unaided within an hour of hard straining — two front feet and a nose is normal.
  • Intervene with clean help if straining exceeds ~1 hour without progress, or only a head/tail shows.
  • The first-hour checklist: clear the nose, let the mother lick (bonding), navel in iodine, and colostrum within 1–2 hours — small stock newborns chill and fade fast.
  • Twins and triplets: ensure BOTH teats work and the small one drinks; a foster or bottle plan saves the triplet.

Lesson 5.3 — From birth to weaning

  • Week 1: kids in dry draught-free pens while mothers browse nearby; watch navels and joints (navel-ill from dirty pens).
  • From week 2–3: creep feed (a pen gap only youngsters fit through) with 16–18% CP starter — creep-fed kids wean 20–30% heavier.
  • Castrate non-breeding males early (first 2 weeks, rubber ring or burdizzo done properly); tag and record every birth.
  • Wean at 3–4 months by full separation; wean onto the BEST grazing/browse plus the fortnightly FAMACHA — weaners are the worm war's front line.

Lesson 5.4 — Flock structure and culling

  • Each year keep the best young females as replacements (target: eyelids red all season, twins born and raised, good udder).
  • Cull hard: empty two seasons, chronic limpers, repeat pale-eyes, bad mothers — sold in good condition they fund replacements.
  • Change the male every 2–3 years (or run two) to avoid father-daughter matings that shrink the flock's size and vigour.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 5): (1) Build the backwards calendar: target sale festival → joining date → kidding window → weaning → fattening start. (2) Prepare two kidding pens and a newborn kit (iodine, clean towels, torch, bottle + teat). (3) Start the birth register: dam, sire, date, sex, weight, twin status.

MONTH 6 — Fattening, Festival Marketing & Your Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Selling into the peaks

  • Small stock prices are event-driven: Eid al-Adha (live rams/bucks command their year's top prices), Christmas/New Year, Easter, weddings and ceremonies — a sale calendar IS a price strategy.
  • Eid buyers want intact (uncastrated), healthy, unblemished males of good size — plan a batch specifically for it.
  • Channels: farm-gate live sales (ceremony buyers pay premiums for choice), sale pens/auctions, butcheries (price per kg), and standing orders with restaurants for chevon/mutton.

Lesson 6.2 — The 8-week fattening program

  • Select sale males 8–10 weeks before the target date; pen with shade, water, roughage plus 300–500 g/day concentrate stepped up over a week.
  • Expected gain: 100–150 g/day on-farm, so 8 weeks adds roughly 6–8 kg — often the difference between two whole price bands at the festival pen.
  • Dressing percentage: 45–55% — a 35 kg live goat yields a 16–19 kg carcass; know this before any butchery negotiation.

Lesson 6.4 — Milk and other income streams

  • Dairy goats (Saanen/Toggenburg crosses) give 1–3 L/day — goat milk and cheese command health-market premiums (see rAs Cheese Making).
  • Manure from raised houses is bagged, weed-seed-poor fertiliser that vegetable growers buy.
  • Skins, and breeding-stock sales (your best young males to other farmers) out-earn slaughter prices.

Lesson 6.5 — Final project: your bankable business plan

  • Assemble from six months of records: flock plan and breed strategy, housing/fencing done or costed, feed-security calendar, the health calendar with FAMACHA logs, the festival sale calendar with real local prices, risk plan (drought, theft, PPR), 3-year flock-growth projection.
  • Submit with photos and records summary for your rAs certificate — the same document works for livestock schemes, banks and partners.

📖 Glossary

  • Doe/ewe — female goat/sheep; buck/ram — male; kid/lamb — young.
  • FAMACHA — eyelid-colour anaemia score guiding selective worm dosing.
  • Browse — tree/shrub leaves and pods eaten by goats.
  • Flushing — pre-mating feed boost for more twins.
  • Creep feeding — youngsters-only feeder behind a small gap.
  • Raddle — marker on the male's brisket that colour-marks served females.
  • Bottle-jaw — under-jaw swelling from severe worm anaemia.
  • Drench — oral worm medicine; drench resistance — worms surviving it.
  • PPR — peste des petits ruminants, notifiable "goat plague".
  • Dressing % — carcass ÷ live weight (45–55%).
