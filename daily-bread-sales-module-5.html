1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Baking is the heart of your bakery business. It turns your prepared dough into the golden, aromatic bread that customers buy. Without proper baking, even the best dough becomes a waste of money and time. In Africa, where many bakeries rely on charcoal, wood, or gas ovens, managing the oven well is critical. You need to understand that baking is not just about putting dough in an oven; it's about controlling temperature, time, and steam to get consistent results.

Why does this matter? Because your customers expect the same quality every day. If your bread is sometimes burnt, sometimes pale, or sometimes raw inside, they will go to another bakery. Consistent baking builds trust and repeat sales. Also, fuel is expensive. If you bake at the wrong temperature, you waste fuel and may lose a whole batch. In many African communities, bread is a daily staple. Your ability to bake well directly affects your income and your family's well-being.

Good oven management also reduces accidents. Burns from hot ovens or fires from uncontrolled flames are common in small bakeries. By learning proper techniques, you protect yourself and your workers. Moreover, efficient baking means you can produce more loaves in less time, increasing your daily bread sales. This module will guide you through the tools, methods, mistakes, safety, and practice needed to master baking and oven management. Whether you use a simple mud oven or a modern gas oven, the principles remain the same. Let's get started on turning your dough into profit.

2. Tools and Materials

To bake bread, you need more than just an oven. You need the right tools to load, monitor, and unload your bread safely and efficiently. First, you need a reliable oven. In Africa, common types include wood-fired brick ovens, charcoal ovens, gas ovens, and electric ovens. Each has its own temperature control method. For wood-fired ovens, you'll need firewood or charcoal, a poker, and a way to measure temperature, such as an oven thermometer or a simple trick like sprinkling flour to see if it browns.

Next, you need baking trays or pans. These should be sturdy and fit your oven. If you're baking round loaves directly on the oven floor, you might use a peel (a long-handled flat shovel) to slide them in and out. For loaves in pans, you need a tray lifter or thick gloves. A timer is essential; you can use a clock or a phone timer. A spray bottle with water is useful for creating steam, which gives bread a crispy crust. You'll also need a cooling rack to let bread cool after baking.

For safety, have oven mitts or thick cloths, a fire extinguisher or sand bucket, and a first-aid kit. A thermometer is non-negotiable; guessing temperature leads to inconsistent results. If you use a gas oven, check the gas hose and regulator regularly for leaks. For wood ovens, keep a metal ash bucket. Finally, a notebook to record baking times, temperatures, and results helps you improve. Investing in these tools may cost a little, but they save money by preventing burnt batches and accidents. Remember, in baking, preparation is half the battle.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps for successful baking. First, preheat your oven. For most bread, you need 200Â°C to 220Â°C (400Â°F to 425Â°F). If using a wood oven, light the fire and let it burn until the oven is hot, then push the coals to the side or remove them. For gas ovens, set the dial and wait 15-20 minutes. Use an oven thermometer to confirm. Second, prepare your loaves. Shape your dough and let it proof (rise) until it's ready. Score the top with a sharp knife to allow expansion.

Third, load the bread. If using a peel, dust it with flour or cornmeal, place the loaf on it, and slide it into the oven. If using pans, place them on the oven rack. Leave space between loaves for heat circulation. Fourth, create steam. For a crispy crust, spray water into the oven (avoiding the glass) or place a pan of hot water on the bottom rack. Steam helps the bread rise fully before the crust hardens. Fifth, set your timer. Most loaves bake in 25-40 minutes, depending on size. Rotate the bread halfway through for even baking.

Sixth, check for doneness. Tap the bottom of a loaf; it should sound hollow. The internal temperature should be around 90Â°C (195Â°F) for soft bread. If not done, bake 5 more minutes. Seventh, remove bread and cool on a rack. Don't slice hot bread; it continues to cook and may become gummy. Finally, clean your oven after it cools to prevent buildup. Record your times and temperatures for next time. With practice, you'll develop a rhythm. Remember, every oven behaves differently, so adjust based on your observations.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced bakers make mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Oven not hot enough. If bread comes out pale and dense, your oven was too cool. Fix: Preheat longer and use a thermometer. For wood ovens, add more fuel and wait. Mistake 2: Oven too hot. Bread browns too fast outside but raw inside. Fix: Lower temperature by opening the oven door briefly or reducing fuel. Next time, start with lower heat and increase.

Mistake 3: No steam. Bread has a thick, dull crust. Fix: Add a pan of water or spray the oven walls (not the glass) with water before closing. Mistake 4: Overcrowding. Bread touches and steams instead of bakes, resulting in pale sides. Fix: Bake in batches, leaving 2-3 cm between loaves. Mistake 5: Not rotating. One side burns. Fix: Rotate trays halfway through baking. Mistake 6: Underbaking. Bread is doughy in the center. Fix: Use a thermometer to check internal temp (90Â°C) or tap the bottom. Bake longer if needed.

Mistake 7: Burning due to sugar or toppings. If your bread has sugar or seeds, they can burn. Fix: Lower temperature by 10-20Â°C or cover loosely with foil. Mistake 8: Ignoring oven hot spots. Some ovens have areas that are hotter. Fix: Learn your oven's quirks and place bread accordingly, or use a baking stone for even heat. Mistake 9: Not cleaning. Burnt bits smoke and affect flavor. Fix: Clean after each use. Mistake 10: Using wet dough. Too much water makes bread spread. Fix: Adjust hydration or use a bit more flour. By anticipating these issues, you'll waste less and sell more.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in the bakery is not optional. Burns, fires, and injuries can ruin your business. Always wear protective gear: oven mitts, long sleeves, and closed shoes. Never use wet cloths to handle hot pans; they conduct heat and cause steam burns. Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand near the oven. For gas ovens, check for leaks with soapy water; if bubbles form, tighten connections or call a technician. Never use a gas oven if you smell gas; ventilate and turn off the supply.

For wood or charcoal ovens, manage the fire carefully. Keep flammable materials away. Use a metal ash bucket and never dispose of ashes in plastic bins. Ensure good ventilation to avoid carbon monoxide buildup. When loading bread, use a peel or tongs; never reach into a hot oven with bare hands. Keep children and pets away from the baking area. Establish a routine: preheat, bake, cool, clean. Clean your oven regularly to prevent grease fires and maintain efficiency.

Best practices include: maintaining a consistent baking schedule, recording temperatures and times, and training all workers on safety. Store fuel safely away from heat. Use a thermometer to avoid guesswork. Cool bread on racks, not on the floor. Keep your workspace tidy to prevent slips. In Africa, many bakeries operate in shared spaces; ensure your oven area is well-marked. Finally, have a first-aid kit and know basic burn treatment: cool with running water for 10 minutes, cover with a clean cloth, and seek medical help if severe. Your safety ensures your business continues.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. This exercise will help you apply what you've learned about baking and oven management. You will bake a simple white bread loaf and monitor your oven. Gather your tools: dough (made from 500g flour, 300ml water, 10g yeast, 10g salt, 10g sugar), oven, thermometer, timer, baking tray, cooling rack, and oven mitts. Preheat your oven to 200Â°C. If using a wood oven, burn wood until the oven reaches that temperature, then remove coals.

Shape your dough into a loaf and place it on a greased tray. Score the top. Once the oven is ready, load the bread and set your timer for 30 minutes. After 15 minutes, rotate the tray. Also, create steam by placing a small pan of hot water in the oven. At 25 minutes, check the bread: tap the bottom; if it sounds hollow, it's done. If not, bake 5 more minutes. Remove and cool on a rack. Record the actual baking time and any issues (e.g., uneven browning).

After cooling, evaluate: Is the crust golden? Is the inside soft? If not, adjust next time. For example, if the bottom burned, place the tray higher. If the bread is pale, increase temperature by 10Â°C. Repeat this exercise three times, changing one variable each time (e.g., temperature, steam, position). Keep notes in a notebook. This practice builds your skill and confidence. Remember, consistent baking leads to consistent sales. Share your results with fellow bakers to learn together.
