Module 5

🐠 Broodstock and Reproductive Nutrition

Feed your breeders as a separate class of fish. This module covers the nutrient specifications a broodstock diet must hit, what maternal nutrition does to egg and larval quality, the conditioning feeding calendar from recovery through to the spawning trigger, and how to keep breeders producing for years instead of one season.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • State the crude protein, lipid, fatty acid and vitamin levels a broodstock diet must reach for your species
  • Link each broodstock nutrient to the specific egg or larval outcome it changes
  • Lay out the five conditioning phases with their durations, diets and feeding rates
  • Specify the recovery diet and explain what each nutrient is rebuilding
  • Work backwards from your fingerling market to date every conditioning phase
  • Identify the nutritional causes of premature broodstock failure

The six lessons

1. Reproductive Nutrition Requirements 2. Egg Quality and Hatchability 3. Conditioning for Spawning 4. Post-Spawn Recovery Feeding 5. Nutritional Conditioning Before Breeding Season 6. Longevity and Health in Broodstock
Lesson 5.1~11 min

Reproductive Nutrition Requirements

In this lesson
  • State the crude protein, lipid, fatty acid and vitamin levels a broodstock diet must reach for your species
  • Calculate the nutrient gap between your current feed and a proper broodstock specification
  • Adjust vitamin E upward as dietary lipid rises so that high-fat broodstock feed does not oxidise

A breeding fish is not a big grow-out fish. A grow-out fish is building muscle, and muscle is mostly protein and water. A female preparing to spawn is building eggs, and an egg is a package of protein, fat, fat-soluble vitamins and phospholipid that must keep a larva alive for days before it can feed itself. The nutrient mix that builds cheap muscle will not build good eggs, which is why feeding breeders on grow-out pellet quietly caps what a hatchery can produce.

Start with the species numbers. Broodstock crude protein by species is 30-40 percent for Nile tilapia, 35-40 percent for African catfish, 30-35 percent for channel catfish and 28-35 percent for common carp. Set against grow-out levels of 25-32 percent for tilapia, 28-35 percent for African catfish, 25-28 percent for channel catfish and 25-30 percent for carp, you can see that the protein lift is real but modest. Most farmers over-focus on protein here. The bigger change is fat.

Broodstock lipid across all four species is 12-18 percent of the diet against 5-8 percent at grow-out. That is roughly a doubling. Inside that fat, the fraction that matters most is EPA plus DHA, the long-chain omega-3 fatty acids, which must reach 1.5-2.5 percent of the diet for tilapia and 1.5-2.0 percent for African catfish, against only 0.3-0.8 percent and 0.5-1.0 percent respectively at grow-out. Freshwater fish can convert some dietary linolenic acid into EPA and DHA, but conversion efficiency is only 5-15 percent for EPA and 2-5 percent for DHA. That conversion is too slow and too lossy to fill an ovary. This is the one place in fish feeding where paying for real fish oil rather than substituting vegetable oil is defensible.

Phospholipid, usually supplied as soy lecithin, should be 3-5 percent of a broodstock diet against the incidental 0.5-1 percent that comes along in plant meals. Phospholipid forms the structure of egg yolk and larval nerve tissue.

Now the vitamins. Vitamin C rises from 100 mg/kg to 200-300 mg/kg, and vitamin E from 100-150 mg/kg to 200-400 mg/kg. The vitamin E figure is not arbitrary; it scales with lipid. Polyunsaturated fats oxidise, and oxidation in a high-fat feed destroys the very fatty acids you paid for. A rule you can apply: if you push dietary lipid above 10 percent or EPA plus DHA above 1 percent, move vitamin E to the top of the 200-400 mg/kg band.

Work out your own gap. Say you feed a 30 percent protein, 7 percent lipid tilapia pellet to your breeders. Against a target of 35 percent protein and 14 percent lipid, you are 5 percentage points short on protein and 7 short on lipid. To close the protein gap in a 10 kg batch, you need 0.5 kg more protein. Black soldier fly larvae meal at 40-45 percent protein contributes about 0.42 kg of protein per kilogram added, so roughly 1.2 kg of BSFL meal replacing 1.2 kg of pellet moves you close, and it brings 25-35 percent lipid with it, which also helps the fat gap. Add oil for the rest: 7 percentage points of lipid on 10 kg is 0.7 kg of oil, less the roughly 0.35 kg the BSFL meal already brought, so about 350 ml of oil.

One caution on energy. The optimum protein-to-energy ratio for growing fish is 8-10 kcal digestible energy per gram of protein. Broodstock diets run richer than that on purpose, but a very fatty diet fed for months to fish that are not spawning simply deposits body fat, which reduces fertility rather than raising it. High lipid belongs in the conditioning phases, not year-round. Lesson 5 sets out the calendar.

Lesson 5.2~11 min

Egg Quality and Hatchability

In this lesson
  • Link each broodstock nutrient to the specific egg or larval outcome it changes
  • Measure hatch rate and deformity rate on your own farm as a nutrition indicator
  • Rank vitamin and lipid interventions by the size of the hatchability gain they deliver

Hatch rate is a nutrition report card written three weeks after you fed the feed. If a batch of eggs goes cloudy and fungal, if larvae hatch but never swim up, if too many fry are bent or short-bodied, the cause usually sits in the broodstock diet rather than in the incubation water.

Here is what the documented gains attach to, one nutrient at a time.

  1. Lipid at 12-18 percent instead of 6-8 percent gives 20-40 percent more viable larvae and raises egg survival 10-20 percent. Fat is the larva's fuel between hatching and first feeding.
  2. EPA plus DHA at 1.5-2.5 percent raises spawn frequency 10-20 percent and improves swim-up success, because these fatty acids drive prostaglandin synthesis and build larval nervous tissue.
  3. Vitamin C at 200-300 mg/kg cuts egg mortality 20-30 percent. The vitamin is deposited in the egg and primes the larva's immune system before it has one of its own.
  4. Vitamin E at 200-400 mg/kg lifts fecundity 10-15 percent and reduces the count of abnormal eggs.
  5. Phospholipid at 3-5 percent improves larval pigmentation and lifts survival 10-15 percent.
  6. Balanced vitamin supplementation overall pulls the deformity rate down from 5-10 percent to 1-3 percent.

Taken together, proper conditioning gives 20-40 percent more eggs per female, 10-20 percent better hatch rate, 15-25 percent better larval survival from yolk sac to free feeding, and 10-15 percent faster early larval growth.

Two storage traps destroy these gains before the fish ever eat. First, plain ascorbic acid loses 50-90 percent of its potency within two to four weeks at ambient temperature. If your broodstock premix uses unstabilised vitamin C, the 250 mg/kg on the label may be 50 mg/kg by the time the bag is half empty. Stabilised forms, ascorbyl phosphate or ascorbyl palmitate, retain 60-95 percent at six months and cost only USD 0.02-0.05/kg of feed more. On a broodstock diet that is the cheapest insurance you can buy. Second, biotin. Raw egg white contains avidin, which binds biotin and creates deficiency. Farmers who supplement breeders with raw egg, a common practice around catfish hatcheries, are risking exactly that; cook it or leave it out.

Worked example, Uganda. A hatchery in Wakiso District strips 30 African catfish females a season, averaging about 40,000 eggs each, so roughly 1,200,000 eggs. Their measured hatch rate is 55 percent and their survival from hatch to two-week fry is 40 percent, giving 1,200,000 x 0.55 x 0.40 = 264,000 fry. They condition properly for one season: protein to 38 percent, lipid to 15 percent, vitamin C to 250 mg/kg in stabilised form, vitamin E to 300 mg/kg. Applying the lower end of the documented gains, hatch rate rises 10 percent relative, so 55 becomes 60.5 percent, and larval survival rises 15 percent relative, so 40 becomes 46 percent. New output is 1,200,000 x 0.605 x 0.46 = 334,000 fry. That is 70,000 extra fry. At a conservative farm-gate USD 0.03 each, USD 2,100 of extra revenue. The extra feed cost across an eight-week conditioning of a 45 kg broodstock herd is in the region of USD 300-400.

Measure it yourself, because averages are not your farm. For each spawn record four numbers: estimated egg count, eggs that develop eyes, larvae that swim up and start feeding, and the count of visibly deformed fry in a sample of 100. Keep those four numbers per batch for a year. When you change the broodstock diet you will see the change in the second or third spawn, not the first, because the eggs for the current spawn were already being built on the old diet. Module 11 Lesson 4 covers what to feed the larvae once they are yours.

Lesson 5.3~11 min

Conditioning for Spawning

In this lesson
  • Lay out the five conditioning phases with their durations, diets and feeding rates
  • Calculate the kilograms of feed each phase requires for your own broodstock biomass
  • Recognise the physical signs that a female has moved from phase one to phase two

Conditioning is a sequence, not a diet. The nutrient specification from Lesson 1 is applied in stages, each with its own duration, protein and lipid level and feeding rate, and skipping a stage costs you either eggs or breeders. Module 11 Lesson 3 walks through the calendar as a hatchery planning exercise; this lesson is about the arithmetic of the ration in each phase.

The five phases:

  1. Pre-conditioning, 4-8 weeks. Maintain 28-30 percent crude protein and 6-8 percent lipid. Feed at 60-80 g per kg of body weight per day. Objective: baseline health and recovery from the previous spawn.
  2. Conditioning phase one, 4-6 weeks. Move to 35 percent protein and 12 percent lipid, with phytoplankton or algae if you have green water. Feed at 80-100 g/kg/day. Objective: gonad development; the females begin to swell visibly.
  3. Conditioning phase two, 2-4 weeks. Move to 38-40 percent protein and 15-18 percent lipid, adding live food if you can get it. Feed at 100-120 g/kg/day. Objective: maturation and yolk accumulation; the belly becomes full and opaque.
  4. Trigger, 3-7 days. Keep the same high-lipid diet, add live Artemia or chironomids if available, and feed to appetite once or twice daily. The spawning cue itself is environmental: a temperature drop, a photoperiod change, or relief of crowding.
  5. Recovery, 2-4 weeks. Drop to 30-32 percent protein and 8-10 percent lipid at maintenance feeding. Lesson 4 covers this phase in full.

Now the arithmetic, worked for a real herd. A farmer near Kumasi, Ghana, keeps 24 Nile tilapia broodfish averaging 350 g, so 24 x 0.35 = 8.4 kg of broodstock biomass.

Pre-conditioning at 70 g/kg/day: 8.4 x 0.07 = 0.59 kg of feed a day. Over 42 days that is 24.8 kg. Phase one at 90 g/kg/day: 8.4 x 0.09 = 0.76 kg a day. Over 35 days that is 26.6 kg. Phase two at 110 g/kg/day: 8.4 x 0.11 = 0.92 kg a day. Over 21 days that is 19.4 kg. Trigger at roughly the same rate for 5 days: 4.6 kg.

Total for the run-up: about 75 kg of feed. The first 25 kg is ordinary grow-out-grade feed and can be bought at USD 0.60-0.80/kg, call it USD 17. The remaining 50 kg must be broodstock grade. Bought in at USD 1.50-3.00/kg that is USD 75-150. Upgraded at home from grow-out pellet plus fish meal or BSFL meal, oil and premix, expect USD 1.00-1.40/kg, so USD 50-70. Either way the whole conditioning run costs under USD 170 for a herd that should produce tens of thousands of fry.

Three practical rules that decide whether the plan works.

First, feed the rate to actual biomass, not to a number you wrote down two months ago. Reweigh a sample of five breeders at the start of each phase; a well-conditioned female gains weight, and a ration set for 8.4 kg is under-feeding a herd that is now 9.5 kg.

Second, split the daily ration into at least two feeds. At 110 g/kg/day the fish are eating 11 percent of body weight in dry matter terms per day, which is a heavy load; delivered in one drop, much of it goes uneaten and straight into the ammonia budget.

Third, watch the water. A high-protein, high-lipid ration at a high feeding rate is the fastest way to push ammonia past 0.05 mg/L in a small conditioning pond or hapa. If ammonia rises above 0.1 mg/L or dissolved oxygen falls below 5 mg/L, cut the ration 20-30 percent that day and increase exchange. Module 9 has the management detail.

Lesson 5.4~11 min

Post-Spawn Recovery Feeding

In this lesson
  • Specify the recovery diet and explain what each nutrient is rebuilding
  • Time the return to conditioning so the next spawn is not compromised
  • Cost the difference between a rushed second spawn and a properly recovered one

A female that has just spawned has moved a large fraction of her body reserves into eggs. She is depleted of lipid, low on fat-soluble vitamins, physically bruised from stripping or from spawning behaviour, and immunologically exposed. What happens in the next two to four weeks decides whether she spawns well again or becomes a passenger.

The recovery specification is 30-32 percent crude protein and 8-10 percent lipid, fed at maintenance level, for two to four weeks. Notice that it is not a drop back to grow-out feed. Protein stays a little above grow-out because tissue repair is protein work, and lipid stays above grow-out because the reserves that were exported into the eggs have to be replaced before the next round of vitellogenesis can even start. Feeding a recovering female a 28 percent, 6 percent lipid pellet simply extends her recovery.

Three things to get right in the recovery window.

First, vitamin C. Spawning and handling are the two biggest stress events in a broodfish's year and they usually arrive together. Fish given adequate vitamin C show about 50 percent fewer infections. Because the vitamin is water-soluble and not stored, protection has to come from the feed on the day, not from a reserve. Keep the recovery diet at the broodstock level of 200-300 mg/kg rather than dropping to the grow-out 100 mg/kg, and make sure it is a stabilised form given that recovery feed is often the tail end of an old bag.

Second, feed intake. A stripped or spawn-exhausted fish may not eat for the first day or two. Offer a small ration, observe, and remove what is not taken within twenty minutes. Uneaten high-lipid feed in a recovery hapa fouls water fast, and fouled water on a stressed fish is how you lose breeders. If a fish has not eaten by day three, look for fungal infection at the vent or on abraded skin.

Third, do not restart conditioning early. The temptation on a farm with fingerling orders to fill is to push a female straight from spawning back into a 38 percent, 18 percent lipid diet. Two to four weeks of proper recovery is the documented window, and the pre-conditioning phase of four to eight weeks exists precisely to carry the fish from recovery to the next conditioning cycle. Compressing this is the most common reason hatcheries see their second and third spawns of a season come out smaller than the first.

Worked example, Tanzania. A hatchery near Mwanza runs 20 tilapia females of 400 g, so 8 kg. Recovery at 70 g/kg/day is 8 x 0.07 = 0.56 kg a day, and over 21 days that is 11.8 kg of feed. A recovery-grade diet made from grow-out pellet plus a little oil and the broodstock premix costs about USD 0.95/kg, so the entire recovery phase costs roughly USD 11. Compare that to the cost of getting it wrong. If rushing recovery drops the next spawn from 40,000 to 30,000 viable eggs per female across 20 females, that is 200,000 eggs lost; even at a 50 percent hatch and 40 percent survival that is 40,000 fewer fry, worth USD 1,200 at USD 0.03. Eleven dollars of feed against twelve hundred dollars of fry.

Finally, use the recovery window to do the handling work you would otherwise do at a worse time. Weigh the fish and record it, check for lesions and fin damage, treat wounds, and cull any female who has lost condition badly or failed to spawn twice running. A broodstock pond gets managed once a cycle; recovery is the moment when the fish are already being handled and the loss of a day's feeding matters least.

Lesson 5.5~11 min

Nutritional Conditioning Before Breeding Season

In this lesson
  • Work backwards from your fingerling market to date every conditioning phase
  • Buy and store broodstock ingredients before the season rather than during it
  • Protect high-lipid conditioning feed from rancidity in a hot climate

The nutrient specifications and the phase durations only turn into fry if they are put on a calendar. A conditioning programme is thirteen to twenty-one weeks long from the start of pre-conditioning to the trigger. If you begin planning when the fingerling orders arrive, you are already three months late.

Back-plan from the market. Suppose your buyers want stocking-size fingerlings in October, and it takes you about eight weeks from hatch to a saleable fingerling. You need a spawn in early August. Working backwards from an early-August trigger: phase two of three weeks takes you to mid-July, phase one of five weeks to early June, and pre-conditioning of six weeks to late April. So the decisions about feed, ingredients and which fish to breed have to be made in March. Write those four dates on the wall of the feed store.

The second reason to plan early is temperature. Nile tilapia grow rapidly above 25 C and are optimal at 25-30 C; African catfish are optimal at 20-28 C. Conditioning a herd through a cool spell wastes feed, because the fish eat less and convert it worse. If your dawn water temperature drops below 20 C for part of the year, place the conditioning window where the water is warm and use the cool season for pre-conditioning and recovery, when feeding rates are low anyway.

Third, buy ingredients ahead. The broodstock inputs that matter are the ones that are hardest to find at short notice: a good fish meal or BSFL meal for protein, oil for lipid, soy lecithin for phospholipid, and a broodstock-grade vitamin and mineral premix with stabilised vitamin C. Premix is the item most often missing on a small farm. Budget the two premixes at roughly USD 0.03-0.05/kg of feed each, plus USD 0.02-0.05/kg for stabilised vitamin C. On a 75 kg conditioning run those add up to about USD 8, which is trivial money that nonetheless has to be spent before the season, not during it.

Worked example, Zambia. A farmer at Kafue holds 30 African catfish broodfish averaging 1.1 kg, so 33 kg of biomass, and plans a single conditioning run. His feed requirement across pre-conditioning at 70 g/kg/day for 42 days, phase one at 90 g/kg/day for 35 days and phase two at 110 g/kg/day for 21 days works out as: 33 x 0.07 x 42 = 97 kg, plus 33 x 0.09 x 35 = 104 kg, plus 33 x 0.11 x 21 = 76 kg. Total 277 kg. Roughly 100 kg is maintenance grade and 180 kg conditioning grade. At USD 0.70/kg and USD 1.20/kg respectively, that is USD 70 plus USD 216, so USD 286 for the season. He orders the fish meal, the oil and both premixes in March, in one purchase, and stores them dry.

Storage is the last piece and the one that quietly ruins conditioning programmes. A conditioning feed at 15-18 percent lipid is a rancidity risk in a hot store. Rice bran, if you use it, oxidises within two to three weeks unless it has been heat-stabilised, and stabilised bran lasts only two to three months. Vitamin A loses about 50 percent of potency over six months and vitamin E loses 30-50 percent. Practical rules: mix conditioning feed in batches no larger than two to three weeks of consumption, keep it off the floor and out of the sun, buy oil in small containers so a big one is not left half-open for months, and never make a high-lipid batch before you need it just because ingredients happen to be available.

One more decision belongs in the pre-season plan: which fish breed. Selecting, holding and replacing breeders, and the enterprise economics of selling fingerlings, are covered in Module 11.

Lesson 5.6~11 min

Longevity and Health in Broodstock

In this lesson
  • Identify the nutritional causes of premature broodstock failure
  • Set an ingredient safety standard for feed going to breeders
  • Build a simple broodstock record that shows when to replace a fish

A good breeding fish is an asset that should work for several seasons. Losing one early is expensive in a way that is easy to miss, because the cost is not the fish, it is the fry she would have produced. Most premature broodstock failure on small farms comes from three nutritional causes: chronic overfeeding of energy, contaminated ingredients, and vitamin shortfalls that never become visible enough to act on.

Start with energy. The optimum protein-to-energy ratio is 8-10 kcal of digestible energy per gram of protein. Below 8, the fish deposits fat rather than tissue. A broodstock diet deliberately runs richer during conditioning, but if a herd is held on a 15 percent lipid diet all year, the result is fatty fish with fatty livers and declining fertility. Choline deficiency compounds this: with under 1,000-2,000 mg/kg, fish develop fat deposition and liver lipidosis. Plant meals supply useful choline, so a diet built on soya cake and bran is less exposed than one built on high-fat animal ingredients, but the underlying answer is the calendar from Lesson 5. High lipid in the conditioning windows, moderate lipid the rest of the year.

Second, ingredient safety. Broodstock feed concentrates risk because the same fish eat the same feed for years and because toxins move into eggs. Nile tilapia are the most aflatoxin-sensitive of the farmed species covered here, with a safe level below 10 ppb generally and a working standard of under 5 ppb for tilapia feeds against under 20 ppb for channel catfish. Aflatoxin B1 is common in maize and groundnut cake at 5-50 ppb in tropical conditions, exactly the ingredients a smallholder is most likely to use. Fumonisin in maize argues for keeping maize under 20 percent of the feed. Practical standard for breeders: buy grain from a source you can inspect, reject any batch with visible mould, caking or a musty smell, keep maize inclusion modest, and never send doubtful ingredients to the broodstock pond just because the fish are not being sold. Module 10 Lesson 4 covers mycotoxin management in detail.

Third, the quiet vitamin failures. Look at your breeders when you handle them. Cloudy eyes or cataracts point to methionine or riboflavin; depigmentation points to methionine; skeletal curvature points to tryptophan; soft, deformed skeletons in the fry point to vitamin D3, which fish cannot make from sunlight at all and must get from feed at 1,000-2,000 IU/kg; fin erosion and gill problems point to lysine. Poor wound healing after handling points to vitamin C or pantothenic acid. A fish that shows any of these is telling you the premix is absent, old or under-dosed.

Worked example, Nigeria. A hatchery near Ibadan holds 50 African catfish breeders and replaces about 40 percent of them every year, buying in 20 replacement fish at USD 8 each, so USD 160 annually, and losing the production of each fish that leaves before its time. After separating the breeders onto a proper diet with a full premix, moving to maize under 20 percent of the mix, and switching to stabilised vitamin C, the replacement rate falls to 20 percent. That is 10 fewer fish bought, USD 80 saved directly, but the real gain is that a mature, well-conditioned female produces more and better eggs than a first-season replacement, so the herd's output rises without any increase in fish numbers.

Keep a broodstock book. One line per fish or per tagged group: date acquired, weight at each handling, spawn dates, estimated egg number, hatch rate, and any lesion or treatment. Replace a fish when she has failed to spawn twice in succession, when weight has fallen between handlings despite full feeding, or when her hatch rates have dropped clearly below the herd average. That is a nutrition and management decision made on evidence rather than on guesswork, and it is the difference between a breeding herd and a pond full of old fish.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Compared with grow-out feed, the largest proportional change in a broodstock diet is:

Lipid roughly doubles from 5-8 percent to 12-18 percent, while protein rises only a few percentage points.

2. EPA plus DHA in a broodstock diet should reach about:

Grow-out needs 0.3-0.8 percent; reproduction needs 1.5-2.5 percent because conversion from plant omega-3 is inefficient.

3. Conversion of dietary linolenic acid to DHA in freshwater fish runs at roughly:

DHA conversion is 2-5 percent and EPA 5-15 percent, far too slow to supply an ovary.

4. Vitamin E is raised to 200-400 mg/kg in broodstock feed mainly to:

Polyunsaturated fats peroxidise; without extra vitamin E the fatty acids you paid for are destroyed in the bag.

5. Feeding a 12-18 percent lipid diet to breeders all year round tends to:

High lipid belongs in the conditioning phases; fed continuously it produces fat fish rather than fecund fish.

6. Raising broodstock vitamin C to 200-300 mg/kg reduces egg mortality by about:

Vitamin C is deposited in the egg and primes larval immunity, cutting egg mortality 20-30 percent.

7. Deformity rate in fry from well-supplemented broodstock falls to:

Balanced vitamin supplementation moves deformity from the usual 5-10 percent down to 1-3 percent.

8. Unstabilised ascorbic acid in a feed bag loses:

This is why broodstock feeds must use ascorbyl phosphate or ascorbyl palmitate.

9. Feeding raw egg to broodstock risks deficiency of:

Avidin in raw egg white binds biotin and blocks its absorption.

10. After changing the broodstock diet, you should expect to see egg quality change:

The eggs for the current spawn were already built on the previous diet, so the effect appears one or two spawns later.

11. Feeding rate during conditioning phase two is:

Phase two runs at 100-120 g per kg body weight per day on a 38-40 percent protein, 15-18 percent lipid diet.

12. A herd of 24 tilapia averaging 350 g fed at 90 g/kg/day needs a daily ration of:

24 x 0.35 = 8.4 kg biomass; 8.4 x 0.09 = 0.76 kg of feed per day.

13. The spawning trigger itself is delivered by:

The diet stays high-lipid through the trigger; the cue is environmental, not nutritional.

14. Pre-conditioning lasts:

Four to eight weeks at maintenance restores fish before the high-nutrient push begins.

15. If ammonia in the conditioning hapa passes 0.1 mg/L you should:

Heavy high-protein rations drive ammonia; cutting feed and exchanging water protects both fish and eggs.

16. The post-spawn recovery diet should contain:

Recovery sits between grow-out and conditioning: enough protein for repair and enough lipid to rebuild reserves.

17. Recovery feeding should last:

Two to four weeks at maintenance, then four to eight weeks of pre-conditioning before the next push.

18. Vitamin C is kept at broodstock level during recovery because:

With no body store, protection depends on the feed eaten that day, and spawning plus handling is peak stress.

19. A fish that has not eaten by day three after spawning should be:

Prolonged refusal after handling usually signals infection on damaged tissue, not simple stress.

20. Rushing a female back into conditioning most commonly shows up as:

Reserves were never rebuilt, so the following spawns come out with fewer and poorer eggs.

21. A full conditioning programme from pre-conditioning to trigger runs about:

Four to eight weeks pre-conditioning, four to six in phase one, two to four in phase two, plus the trigger.

22. Conditioning should be scheduled so that the high-feeding phases fall:

Below optimum the fish eat less and convert worse, so conditioning feed is wasted.

23. Conditioning feed at 15-18 percent lipid should be mixed in batches of:

High-lipid feed goes rancid in a hot store, and rancid feed destroys the fatty acids conditioning depends on.

24. For a herd of 33 kg fed at 110 g/kg/day for 21 days, phase two needs:

33 x 0.11 = 3.63 kg per day, and 3.63 x 21 = about 76 kg.

25. The input most often missing on a small farm when conditioning starts is:

Premix has to be bought ahead; it costs little but cannot be improvised from local ingredients.

26. The species most sensitive to aflatoxin among those farmed here is:

Tilapia feeds should stay under about 5 ppb aflatoxin B1 against under 20 ppb for channel catfish.

27. Fish differ from land animals in that they:

Fish lack the skin pathway, so all 1,000-2,000 IU/kg of vitamin D3 must come from the diet.

28. Holding broodstock on a 15 percent lipid diet all year tends to cause:

High lipid belongs in the conditioning windows; year-round it produces fat, infertile fish.

29. Cloudy eyes or cataracts in a breeder point most directly to:

Cataracts and depigmentation are classic methionine signs, and riboflavin deficiency also causes eye cataracts.

30. A fish should be considered for replacement when she:

Replacement is an evidence decision from the broodstock record, not an age rule.

Module 5 capstone

Build and cost a complete twelve-month broodstock feeding plan for your own farm. Start by separating and weighing your breeders: count them, weigh at least five of each sex, and calculate total broodstock biomass in kilograms. Record what you currently feed them, its crude protein and lipid if the label states it, and what you pay per kilogram. Then write out the five phases from this module with dates on them, working backwards from the month your fingerling buyers want stock: pre-conditioning of four to eight weeks at 28-30 percent protein and 6-8 percent lipid fed at 60-80 g per kg body weight per day, conditioning phase one of four to six weeks at 35 percent protein and 12 percent lipid at 80-100 g/kg/day, conditioning phase two of two to four weeks at 38-40 percent protein and 15-18 percent lipid at 100-120 g/kg/day, the three to seven day trigger, and two to four weeks of recovery at 30-32 percent protein and 8-10 percent lipid. Calculate the kilograms of feed each phase needs for your actual biomass and the cost of each phase at your local ingredient prices, both for a bought broodstock diet at USD 1.50-3.00/kg and for a diet you upgrade yourself from grow-out pellet. Finally, estimate the fry the plan should produce, apply the documented gains of 20-40 percent more eggs and 15-25 percent better larval survival to your own baseline, value the extra fry at your local price, and write down the payback.
