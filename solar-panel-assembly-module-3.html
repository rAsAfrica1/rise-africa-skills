1. Preparing Tab Ribbons and Flux Application

Tab ribbons are thin strips of tinned copper that connect solar cells together. They come in rolls, usually 2mm wide and 0.15mm thick for standard 5-inch cells. You need to cut them to length: for a 5-inch cell, cut about 12cm for the front and 12cm for the back. Use sharp scissors or wire cutters. Wipe the ribbon with a clean cloth and isopropyl alcohol (or local spirit) to remove oil and dirt. Then apply flux. Flux is a chemical that cleans metal and helps solder flow. You can use rosin flux paste or liquid flux. Apply a thin layer with a small brush or cotton swab. Too much flux leaves sticky residue; too little means solder won't stick. In Africa, where humidity and dust are common, keep flux covered when not in use. Work on a clean, flat surface like a wooden board or ceramic tile. Wear safety glasses and gloves. Good preparation prevents weak joints later. Practice cutting and fluxing 10 ribbons before soldering. A common mistake is touching the ribbon with bare fingers after cleaningâoils from your skin ruin the flux. Always hold ribbons with tweezers or clean gloves. Store cut ribbons in a dry container. If you are using lead-free solder, use a flux designed for it; leaded solder works with rosin flux. Never mix fluxes. For best results, flux both the ribbon and the cell's contact pads. Let the flux dry for 30 seconds before soldering. This step takes patience but ensures strong electrical connections.

2. Soldering Tab Ribbons to Solar Cell Front Contacts

The front of a solar cell has thin silver lines called fingers and wider busbars. You will solder the tab ribbon across the busbar. Set your soldering iron to 350Â°C for leaded solder or 380Â°C for lead-free. Use a chisel tip, about 3mm wide. Place the cell on a heat-resistant mat. Align the ribbon along the busbar, leaving about 5mm overhang at each end. Hold the ribbon with one hand using tweezers, and the iron with the other. Touch the iron to the ribbon and busbar together for 2 seconds, then feed a small amount of solder (0.5mm diameter) into the joint. Do not press hardâlet the heat do the work. Move the iron slowly along the busbar, soldering in 2cm sections. You should see the solder turn shiny and flow smoothly. If it looks dull or lumpy, you are moving too fast or the iron is too cold. After soldering, inspect the joint: it should be smooth and concave. Let the cell cool for 1 minute before handling. In dusty workshops, cover the cell to prevent contamination. Practice on broken cells first. A good solder joint has low resistance and will not overheat. Remember, solar cells are fragileâtoo much heat cracks them. If you hear a crack, stop and replace the cell. Keep your iron tip clean by wiping it on a damp sponge every few joints. This ensures good heat transfer.

3. Soldering Bus Ribbons to Solar Cell Back Contacts

The back of a solar cell has a full aluminum or silver coating, often with solder pads. You will solder wider bus ribbons (usually 4mm to 5mm wide) to these pads. These ribbons carry higher current, so they must be thick. Cut bus ribbons to length, about 15cm for a 5-inch cell. Clean and flux the back pads just like the front. Set your iron to the same temperature as before. Place the bus ribbon on the pad, leaving 1cm overhang. Solder from one end to the other, pressing gently. Use a larger solder wire (1mm) for faster melting. The back of the cell is more robust than the front, but still avoid excessive heat. Solder in 3cm sections, allowing 5 seconds between sections to let the cell cool. You should see a bright, smooth solder fillet along the entire ribbon. If the solder balls up, add a little flux and reheat. After soldering, check for bridges between adjacent padsâthey cause short circuits. Use a multimeter to test continuity. In Africa, where power may be unstable, use a temperature-controlled iron; a simple iron can overheat. Practice on scrap cells. A good back solder joint will not peel off when you tug gently on the ribbon. Let the cell cool completely before flipping it over. Store soldered cells in a dry place. Never solder in a windy areaâthe solder will cool too fast and create cold joints.

4. Using a Soldering Iron with Temperature Control

A temperature-controlled soldering iron is essential for solar panel assembly. Unlike a simple plug-in iron, it lets you set the exact temperature. For solar cells, use 350Â°C for leaded solder (63/37 tin-lead) and 380Â°C for lead-free solder (SAC305). Higher temperatures damage cells; lower temperatures cause cold joints. Buy a station with a digital display, like a Hakko FX-888D or a Chinese equivalent. In Africa, where voltage fluctuates, use a surge protector or a voltage stabilizer. Always let the iron heat up for 5 minutes before use. Clean the tip with a damp sponge or brass wool before each joint. Tin the tip by melting a small amount of solder on itâthis protects the tip and improves heat transfer. When soldering, hold the iron like a pen, at a 45-degree angle. Do not drag the tip; lift it straight up after each joint. If the tip becomes black or pitted, replace it. A good tip lasts about 3 months with daily use. Use a stand to rest the iron safely. Never leave it unattended. After work, turn off the iron and let it cool before storing. For field work, consider a battery-powered iron like the TS100, which runs on 24V. It reaches 400Â°C and is portable. But for workshop use, a station is better. Calibrate your iron every month using a thermocouple. If you don't have a thermometer, test on a scrap cell: if solder melts in 2 seconds, the temperature is right. Keep your workspace well-ventilated to avoid inhaling flux fumes.

5. Inspecting Solder Joints for Quality

After soldering, inspect every joint. A good solder joint is shiny, smooth, and concave (like a small fillet). It should look like a tiny volcano. A bad joint is dull, lumpy, or ball-shaped. Use a magnifying glass (10x) or a smartphone camera zoom. Check for cracks, which look like thin dark lines. Also look for bridgesâsolder connecting two busbars that should be separate. Use a multimeter in continuity mode to test for shorts. For each cell, measure the resistance between the front busbar and the back pad. It should be less than 0.1 ohms. If higher, re-solder. Tug gently on the ribbon with tweezers; it should not move. If it lifts, the joint is cold. Cold joints happen when the iron is too cold or you move the ribbon before the solder solidifies. In dusty environments, cover the cell after inspection. Keep a log of each cell's resistance. In a 60-cell panel, one bad joint can reduce output by 10%. So inspect all 60. Use a bright LED light to see reflections. A good joint reflects light evenly. Practice by making 10 joints and grading them: 1 (bad) to 5 (perfect). Aim for 4 or 5. If you find a bad joint, mark it with a marker and repair it later. Never ignore a suspicious joint. In Africa, where panels face harsh sun and heat, weak joints fail faster. So quality inspection is critical. After inspection, store cells in a clean, dry box.

6. Repairing Cold or Weak Solder Joints

Cold joints look dull and grainy. They have high resistance and will overheat. To repair, first clean the joint with isopropyl alcohol. Apply fresh flux. Set your iron to 350Â°C (leaded) or 380Â°C (lead-free). Touch the iron to the joint and reheat for 2-3 seconds. Add a tiny amount of fresh solder. The old solder should melt and blend. Remove the iron and let it cool naturallyâdo not blow on it. If the joint still looks bad, remove all old solder with a desoldering pump or solder wick. Then re-solder from scratch. For a weak joint where the ribbon lifts, you may need to add more solder. Hold the ribbon down with tweezers while soldering. If the cell itself is cracked, the joint cannot be repairedâreplace the cell. After repair, re-inspect and test resistance. It should be less than 0.1 ohms. If not, repeat. In Africa, where replacement cells may be scarce, repair is a valuable skill. Practice on scrap cells. Keep a repair kit: flux, solder, wick, pump, and extra ribbons. Never use acid flux for repairsâit corrodes. Use rosin flux only. If you repair a joint more than twice, discard the cell. Too much heat damages the cell's internal connections. After repair, let the cell cool for 5 minutes before handling. Store repaired cells separately and label them. A well-repaired joint is as good as new. But prevention is better: use correct temperature and clean tips from the start.

Step-by-step practical procedure

1. Preparing tab ribbons and flux application. Cut tab ribbon to length (typically 2Ã cell width plus 10 mm overhang). Clean ribbon with isopropyl alcohol (IPA) to remove oxidation. Apply a thin, even layer of flux to the cell busbar using a flux pen or small brush. Do not flood â excess flux causes splatter and residue.

2. Soldering tab ribbons to front contacts. Place the ribbon over the silver busbar. Press the iron tip flat against the ribbon for 2â3 seconds, feeding a small amount of solder at the tip-ribbon interface. The solder should flow smoothly and form a concave fillet. Lift the iron straight up â do not drag.

3. Soldering bus ribbons to back contacts. Flip the cell carefully using a vacuum pen or soft tweezer. Align bus ribbon with back contact pads. Solder in the same manner, ensuring full wetting across the pad. Keep the cell flat to avoid stress cracks.

4. Using a temperature-controlled iron. Set the station to 330â350 Â°C for SnPb or 350â370 Â°C for SAC305. Verify with a tip thermometer weekly. Use a chisel tip (3â5 mm) for best heat transfer. Replace oxidized or pitted tips â they cause cold joints.

6. Repairing cold or weak joints. Re-flux the joint, apply the iron for 1â2 seconds to reflow, and add a small amount of fresh solder. Do not overheat â if the joint does not reflow cleanly on the second attempt, scrap the cell. Repeated heating damages the silver contact and the silicon.
