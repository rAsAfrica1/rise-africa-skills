Lessons

1. Target Identification and Validation

In drug discovery, a 'target' is a molecule in the bodyusually a proteinthat a drug can interact with to produce a therapeutic effect. For example, the target for the malaria drug chloroquine is a protein in the parasite's digestive vacuole. To find a target, scientists study diseases at the molecular level. In Africa, common targets include the Plasmodium falciparum parasite for malaria, Mycobacterium tuberculosis for TB, and HIV reverse transcriptase for AIDS. They use tools like genome sequencing (e.g., Illumina machines) and bioinformatics databases (e.g., NCBI) to identify genes and proteins unique to the disease. For instance, the H3Africa initiative has sequenced thousands of African genomes to find targets for diseases like sickle cell and trypanosomiasis.

Once a target is identified, it must be validatedproving that hitting it actually changes the disease. In the lab, researchers use techniques like CRISPR-Cas9 to knock out the gene in cells and see if the disease stops. For example, knocking out the gene for a key enzyme in the malaria parasite can kill it. They also use RNA interference (RNAi) to reduce protein levels. In animal models, they test drugs that block the target. For TB, researchers use mice infected with M. tuberculosis to see if inhibiting a target like InhA reduces bacterial load.

Validation is crucial because many targets fail. For instance, a target might be involved in the disease but also in normal body functions, causing side effects. In Africa, limited lab infrastructure can be a challenge, but collaborations with global partners like the Medicines for Malaria Venture (MMV) provide access to high-throughput screening facilities. Local scientists can also use low-cost models like zebrafish to study targets. For example, at the University of Cape Town, researchers use zebrafish to study TB targets.

After validation, the target is ready for lead discovery. Remember, a good target is one that is specific to the disease, accessible to drugs, and safe to modulate. This step saves years and millions of dollars by killing bad targets early. In Africa, focusing on diseases like malaria, TB, and neglected tropical diseases ensures that drug discovery addresses local health needs.

2. Lead Compound Discovery and Optimization

Once a target is validated, the next step is to find a 'lead compound'a molecule that interacts with the target and shows potential as a drug. Leads can come from natural sources, like plants used in traditional medicine. For example, the anti-malarial artemisinin was isolated from Artemisia annua (sweet wormwood), a plant used in Chinese medicine. In Africa, researchers screen local plants: the bark of the African cherry (Prunus africana) is used for prostate issues, and the leaves of the neem tree have antimicrobial properties. They use extraction methods like maceration or Soxhlet extraction, then test fractions in bioassays.

Another approach is high-throughput screening (HTS), where thousands of compounds are tested against the target using robotic systems. For example, the H3D centre at the University of Cape Town screens thousands of compounds against malaria and TB targets. They use 384-well plates and automated liquid handlers. Compounds can also be designed using computer-aided drug design (CADD). Software like Schrdinger or MOE allows scientists to model how a molecule fits into the target's active site. In Africa, limited computing power can be a barrier, but cloud-based tools like Google Colab help.

Once a hit is found, it must be optimized. This means modifying the chemical structure to improve potency, selectivity, and safety. Medicinal chemists make analogssmall changes like adding a methyl groupand test them. For example, the antimalarial drug mefloquine was optimized from quinine. They use structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies to understand which parts of the molecule are important. They also check 'drug-likeness' using Lipinski's Rule of Five: molecular weight <500, logP <5, hydrogen bond donors <5, acceptors <10. Many African natural products fail these rules, so chemists synthesize simpler versions.

Optimization also considers pharmacokinetics: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME). For instance, a compound might be potent in a test tube but not absorbed in the gut. They use Caco-2 cell assays to predict absorption and liver microsomes to test metabolism. In Africa, contract research organizations (CROs) like Synexa Life Sciences in South Africa offer these services. The goal is to produce a lead with good efficacy and safety, ready for preclinical testing. This process can take 2-3 years and test hundreds of compounds.

3. Preclinical Testing: In Vitro and In Vivo

Before a drug can be tested in humans, it must undergo preclinical testing to assess safety and efficacy. This starts with in vitro studiestests done in test tubes or cell cultures. For example, to test a new anti-TB drug, researchers grow Mycobacterium tuberculosis in liquid culture (e.g., Middlebrook 7H9 broth) and add the drug at various concentrations. They measure the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) that stops bacterial growth. For malaria, they use Plasmodium falciparum cultures and measure parasite viability with a colorimetric assay like SYBR Green. These tests are done in biosafety level 2 or 3 labs, which are available at institutions like KEMRI in Kenya and NICD in South Africa.

In vitro tests also check toxicity to human cells. For instance, they use human liver cells (HepG2) to see if the drug damages the liver. They measure cell viability using MTT assay. If the drug kills cancer cells but not normal cells, it's promising. They also test for hERG channel inhibition, which can cause heart arrhythmias. This is done with patch-clamp electrophysiology or fluorescent assays. In Africa, some labs have these capabilities, but many rely on partners in Europe or the US.

Next comes in vivo testingin live animals. The most common are mice and rats. For malaria, they infect mice with Plasmodium berghei and treat them with the drug. They measure parasitemia (percentage of infected red blood cells) and survival. For TB, they use mice infected with M. tuberculosis and count colony-forming units (CFUs) in lungs. They also test in non-human primates for drugs that need closer human resemblance, but this is rare in Africa due to cost and ethics. Instead, they use zebrafish for early toxicity and efficacy studies. For example, at the University of Pretoria, zebrafish are used to screen for TB drugs.

Preclinical testing also includes pharmacokinetic studies: how the drug is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted. They give the drug to animals and measure blood levels over time using HPLC or mass spectrometry. They determine half-life, clearance, and bioavailability. For example, a drug might be given orally and intravenously to compare. They also conduct toxicology studies: giving high doses to animals to find the maximum tolerated dose and observe side effects. These studies follow Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) guidelines. In Africa, GLP-certified labs are few, but some exist in South Africa and Egypt. The data from preclinical testing is used to design human trials and is submitted in an Investigational New Drug (IND) application.

4. Investigational New Drug (IND) Application Process

After preclinical testing, a company or research institution must file an Investigational New Drug (IND) application with a regulatory authority before testing in humans. In the US, this is the FDA; in Africa, it's national regulators like SAHPRA (South Africa), NAFDAC (Nigeria), or PPB (Kenya). The IND includes all preclinical data: chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC); pharmacology; toxicology; and a detailed clinical trial protocol. The goal is to show that the drug is safe enough to test in humans. For example, if a drug caused severe liver damage in mice, the IND would likely be rejected.

The IND process starts with a pre-IND meeting with the regulator to discuss the plan. Then, the applicant submits the IND, which must be reviewed within 30 days in the US. If the FDA doesn't object, the trial can proceed. In Africa, timelines vary; SAHPRA aims for 90 days, but delays are common. The IND also requires an Investigational Review Board (IRB) or Ethics Committee approval to protect human subjects. In Africa, ethics committees like the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology review protocols.

Key sections of an IND include: 1) Animal pharmacology and toxicology data, 2) Manufacturing information (how the drug is made, stability, purity), 3) Clinical protocol (phase I design, dosing, patient selection), and 4) Investigator's brochure (summary of all known information). For example, for a new antimalarial, the IND would include in vitro MIC data, mouse efficacy, and rat toxicity studies. It must also detail how the drug will be formulated (e.g., tablet, injection) and how it will be packaged and labeled.

In Africa, many countries have streamlined IND processes for priority diseases like malaria and TB. The African Medicines Agency (AMA) is being established to harmonize regulations. However, challenges remain: limited regulatory capacity, slow reviews, and high fees. To navigate this, local sponsors often partner with international organizations like the European and Developing Countries Clinical Trials Partnership (EDCTP) or the US NIH. For example, the IND for the antimalarial drug artesunate was filed by a Chinese company, but African researchers contributed data. Once the IND is approved, clinical trials can begin, starting with Phase I.

5. Clinical Trial Phases: I, II, III, and IV

Clinical trials are conducted in four phases to test a drug's safety and efficacy in humans. Phase I trials are the first-in-human studies, usually involving 20-80 healthy volunteers. The main goal is safety: to determine the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and identify side effects. They often use dose escalation, starting with a very low dose and increasing gradually. For example, a Phase I trial of a new malaria drug might give single ascending doses to healthy adults in a hospital setting. In Africa, Phase I units exist at sites like the Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI) and the University of Cape Town's Clinical Research Centre. These trials last several months and require intensive monitoring.

Phase II trials test the drug in patients with the disease, typically 100-300 participants. They assess efficacy and further safety. For instance, a Phase II trial for a new TB drug might enroll patients with drug-resistant TB and measure sputum culture conversion after 8 weeks. They also determine the optimal dose. Phase II can be divided into IIa (proof of concept) and IIb (dose-finding). In Africa, Phase II trials for malaria and HIV are common, often funded by EDCTP or the Gates Foundation. For example, the Phase II trial of the antimalarial drug KAF156 was conducted in multiple African countries.

Phase III trials are large, randomized, controlled trials involving 1,000-3,000 patients. They compare the new drug to the standard treatment or placebo. The goal is to confirm efficacy and monitor side effects. For example, a Phase III trial of a new HIV drug might compare it to efavirenz-based therapy, measuring viral suppression at 48 weeks. These trials are expensive and take years. In Africa, Phase III trials require strong infrastructure: trained staff, reliable supply chains, and community engagement. Sites like the Africa Health Research Institute in South Africa and the Medical Research Council in Uganda have conducted major Phase III trials.

After approval, Phase IV trials (post-marketing surveillance) monitor long-term safety in the general population. They can detect rare side effects that weren't seen in earlier phases. For example, the antimalarial drug mefloquine was found to cause neuropsychiatric effects in some patients during Phase IV. In Africa, pharmacovigilance systems are weak, but efforts like the African Vaccine Regulatory Forum (AVAREF) are improving. Phase IV studies can also test new indications or use in special populations, like pregnant women. Conducting clinical trials in Africa is essential because disease burdens are high, and results may differ due to genetic and environmental factors. However, ethical considerations like informed consent and community engagement are paramount.

6. New Drug Application (NDA) and Approval Pathways

After successful Phase III trials, a company submits a New Drug Application (NDA) to a regulatory agency for marketing approval. In the US, it's the FDA; in Africa, it's national regulators like SAHPRA, NAFDAC, or PPB. The NDA is a massive document containing all data from preclinical and clinical studies, manufacturing details, and proposed labeling. The goal is to prove that the drug is safe and effective for its intended use. For example, an NDA for a new antimalarial would include data from Phase I-III trials showing it reduces parasitemia and improves survival compared to standard treatment.

The review process can take 6-12 months in the US, often longer in Africa. The FDA may convene an advisory committee of experts to vote on approval. If approved, the drug can be marketed. In Africa, regulatory agencies are strengthening, but many rely on approvals from stringent authorities like the FDA or EMA. The African Medicines Agency (AMA) aims to harmonize reviews, but it's not yet fully operational. For example, SAHPRA has a robust review process and often serves as a reference for other African countries.

There are expedited pathways for drugs that address unmet medical needs. In the US, these include Priority Review (6 months), Breakthrough Therapy, Accelerated Approval, and Fast Track. For example, the antimalarial drug tafenoquine was granted Priority Review. In Africa, similar pathways exist but are less formalized. For TB and malaria, the WHO prequalification program is crucial: it assesses drugs for quality, safety, and efficacy, allowing procurement by UN agencies. For example, generic antiretrovirals for HIV are prequalified by WHO, making them affordable in Africa.

After approval, the company must continue to monitor safety (Phase IV) and comply with manufacturing standards (Good Manufacturing Practice, GMP). In Africa, local manufacturing is growing: Aspen Pharmacare in South Africa produces antiretrovirals, and Universal Corporation in Kenya makes antimalarials. However, most active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) are imported from India or China. The NDA also includes a risk evaluation and mitigation strategy (REMS) if needed. For example, a drug with serious side effects might require patient monitoring. Ultimately, approval brings a drug to market, but access and affordability remain challenges in Africa. Partnerships like the Clinton Health Access Initiative (CHAI) help negotiate lower prices.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Drug Discovery and Development in the African Pharmaceutical Context

This practical reference distils the operational realities of taking a molecule from a validated target to an approved product, with an explicit focus on what African manufacturers, contract research organisations (CROs), regulators, and investors can realistically do today. The global average cost of developing one new chemical entity is widely cited at over US$2.5 billion, with timelines of 1015 years. For African firms, the strategic entry points are rarely "discover a novel blockbuster from scratch." They are: (1) generic and hybrid applications under the WHO Prequalification and Stringent Regulatory Authority pathways; (2) repurposing and reformulation of off-patent molecules for neglected tropical diseases and endemic conditions; (3) contract manufacturing and clinical trial services for global sponsors; and (4) regional regulatory harmonisation through the African Medicines Agency (AMA) and the African Medicines Regulatory Harmonisation (AMRH) initiative.

1. Target Identification and Validation

For African research institutions, validated targets with strong regional relevance include Plasmodium falciparum enzymes (e.g., dihydrofolate reductase, PfDHFR), Mycobacterium tuberculosis InhA, and Trypanosoma brucei kinases. Validation requires genetic knockdown, chemical probes, and animal model confirmation. Business reality: target validation is the hardest step to fund locally. Most African biotech start-ups partner with global academic centres or leverage open-source target databases such as the TDR Targets database (WHO/TDR) and ChEMBL. The commercial value at this stage is IP filing a composition-of-matter patent through ARIPO (African Regional Intellectual Property Organization) or OAPI costs far less than US or EPO filings and gives regional protection across member states.

2. Lead Compound Discovery and Optimization

High-throughput screening (HTS) is capital-intensive; most African labs instead use fragment-based screening, natural product libraries (e.g., from the ICIPE in Kenya or the University of Ibadan's natural products collection), and computational docking. Lead optimisation balances potency, selectivity, metabolic stability, and critically for Africa heat stability and low-cost synthesis. A molecule that requires cold-chain storage or imported chiral building blocks is commercially fragile. Local synthesis routes using commodity reagents available from Sasol, Dangote, or regional chemical distributors reduce cost of goods sold (COGS) by 3050%.

3. Preclinical Testing: In Vitro and In Vivo

Preclinical work must establish safety pharmacology (hERG, CNS, respiratory), ADME, and toxicology (repeat-dose, genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, reproductive). African CROs such as those in South Africa (e.g., BioAnalytical Services, Synexa Life Sciences) and Egypt offer GLP-compliant bioanalysis and some toxicology. Where local GLP capacity is absent, outsourcing to India or Europe is the norm. The cost of a full IND-enabling package ranges from US$15 million depending on the indication.

4. Investigational New Drug (IND) Application Process

In the US, an IND is submitted to FDA and becomes effective 30 days later if no clinical hold is issued. In Africa, equivalent pathways exist under national regulators (SAHPRA in South Africa, NAFDAC in Nigeria, PPB in Kenya) and increasingly under the AMA's continental framework. The AMRH has harmonised technical requirements for clinical trial applications across the East African Community, Southern African Development Community, and Economic Community of West African States. A typical IND/CTA dossier includes: chemistry, manufacturing and controls (CMC); pharmacology and toxicology; previous human experience; and the clinical protocol and investigator brochure.

5. Clinical Trial Phases IIV

Africa hosts roughly 24% of global clinical trials, yet carries over 20% of the global disease burden. This mismatch is a commercial opportunity: sponsors need sites, investigators, and patients. Phase I units operate in South Africa, Kenya, Uganda, and Egypt. Phase IIIII trials for HIV, TB, malaria, and now non-communicable diseases are increasingly sited in Africa. Phase IV (post-marketing) is where African pharmacovigilance systems National Pharmacovigilance Centres reporting to the WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring generate real-world evidence that can support label expansion and local market access.

6. New Drug Application (NDA) and Approval Pathways

An NDA (US) or Marketing Authorisation Application (EU) is a full dossier. For African manufacturers, the pragmatic routes are:

  • Abridged / generic: bioequivalence against an approved reference product.
  • WHO Prequalification: mandatory for UN procurement (Global Fund, Gavi, UNICEF).
  • Article 58 (EMA): scientific opinion for medicines used only outside the EU.
  • Regional reliance: SAHPRA, NAFDAC, and PPB increasingly accept approvals from Stringent Regulatory Authorities or WHO Listed Authorities.
  • AMA: once fully operational, continental marketing authorisation will reduce duplicated dossiers across 55 states.

SAFETY WARNING Clinical Trial Conduct and Pharmacovigilance

Never initiate first-in-human dosing without a documented, regulator-authorised IND/CTA, an approved protocol, ethics committee clearance, and a functioning serious adverse event (SAE) reporting system. In Africa, additional safeguards are mandatory: informed consent must be provided in the participant's language; community engagement and traditional leadership consent must be documented; and data must be submitted to the national pharmacovigilance centre and the WHO global database (VigiBase). Failure to report SAEs within statutory timelines (typically 24 hours for deaths and life-threatening events) can result in trial suspension, criminal liability, and irreparable damage to the site's ability to attract future sponsors. Counterfeit or substandard investigational product is a persistent regional risk verify every batch through the manufacturer's certificate of analysis and, where available, the WHO Medical Product Alert system.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Validate the commercial case before the science: confirm there is a procurement buyer (Global Fund, Gavi, national essential medicines list) or a private market willing to pay.
  2. Secure IP early: file a provisional patent via ARIPO or OAPI before publishing or presenting at conferences.
  3. Map the regulatory route: decide between full NDA, abridged generic, WHO PQ, or Article 58 before committing to preclinical spend.
  4. Build GLP/GCP capacity or partner: identify a CRO with auditable quality systems; budget for external audits.
  5. Engage the national regulator and ethics committee early: pre-submission meetings reduce rejection cycles by months.
  6. Design for African logistics: heat stability, simplified dosing, and locally sourced excipients reduce COGS and cold-chain risk.
  7. Establish pharmacovigilance from day one: budget for a qualified person for pharmacovigilance (QPPV) and VigiBase reporting.
  8. Plan for scale-up and tech transfer: secure a manufacturing partner with WHO GMP certification before Phase III completion.
