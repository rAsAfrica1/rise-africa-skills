1. Determining Harvest Maturity

Knowing when to harvest potatoes is critical for maximum yield and quality. In Africa, most potato varieties mature 90 to 120 days after planting, depending on altitude and climate. But don't just count daysâlook at the plants. When leaves turn yellow and stems fall over, the crop is nearing maturity. For storage potatoes, wait until the vines have completely died back and the skin is thick (set). For immediate sale or consumption, you can harvest earlier when vines just start to yellow, but yield will be lower.

A simple field test: dig up a few sample plants from different spots. Rub the skin of a tuber with your thumb. If the skin slips off easily, the potatoes are immature and not ready for storage. If the skin stays firm, they are mature. Also check tuber sizeâmost marketable tubers should be at least 5 cm in diameter. In Kenya's highlands, farmers often harvest when 80% of vines have dried. In Nigeria's Jos Plateau, harvest timing may be 100-120 days.

Weather matters. Harvest when soil is moist but not waterlogged. Dry soil makes digging hard and increases bruising. If heavy rain is coming, harvest early to avoid rot. For seed potatoes, harvest slightly earlier to avoid disease spread. Always test a small area first. If you harvest too early, potatoes have thin skins, store poorly, and have lower starch. Too late, they may sprout in the ground or be damaged by pests. Use a calendar and visual cues together. Record planting date and variety. For example, 'Unica' matures in 90 days, 'Dutch Robijn' in 110 days. Adjust for your region. When in doubt, dig a plant and check skin set. That's your best guide.

2. Mechanical and Manual Harvesting Techniques

Harvesting potatoes can be done by hand or with machines. In most of Africa, manual harvesting with hand hoes or forks is common, especially on small plots. For manual harvesting, start at the edge of the field. Use a fork or hoe to lift the soil carefully, 20-30 cm away from the plant to avoid spearing tubers. Lift the plant and shake off soil. Pick tubers by hand, leaving small ones and damaged ones. Work in teams: one person digs, others collect into baskets or buckets. Avoid throwing potatoesâbruises lead to rot. A good worker can harvest 200-300 kg per day manually.

Mechanical harvesters are used on larger farms. A tractor-drawn potato digger lifts the soil and tubers onto a chain, separating soil. Then workers pick tubers from the ground. For best results, adjust the digger depth to 15-20 cm. If soil is too dry, irrigate lightly a day before harvest to reduce clods and bruising. Mechanical harvest is fasterâone hectare can be done in a few hoursâbut requires investment. In South Africa and some commercial farms in Kenya, mechanical harvesters are common. However, they can damage tubers if not calibrated.

After digging, leave potatoes in the field for 1-2 hours to dry skin, but not in direct sun for long, as they turn green (glycoalkaloids). Collect into crates or bags. Never drop potatoes from a height. Use padded buckets. For manual harvesting, a fork with rounded tines is better than a sharp hoe. In sandy soils, hand pulling works if soil is loose. In clay soils, wait for moderate moisture. Always harvest when soil temperature is coolâearly morning or late afternoon. This reduces bruising. After harvest, move potatoes to a shaded area quickly. Proper technique reduces losses by up to 20%.

3. Vine Killing and Skin Set

Vine killing (also called haulm destruction) is done 2-3 weeks before harvest to mature the tubers and thicken the skin. This is essential for storage potatoes. When vines are killed, the plant stops growing, and the skin sets, reducing damage during harvest. In Africa, small-scale farmers often do this by cutting vines with a machete or slashing. For larger farms, chemical desiccants like diquat or glyphosate are used, but these may be expensive or unavailable. Mechanical vine killing with a roller or flail is another option.

Why kill vines? It prevents tuber growth, reduces disease spread (especially late blight), and allows skin to set. If you harvest without vine killing, skins are thin and slip off, leading to bruising and rot. For seed potatoes, vine killing also prevents virus transmission by aphids. Timing: kill vines when tubers reach desired size, usually 2-3 weeks before planned harvest. For example, if you want to harvest on day 100, kill vines on day 80. After killing, leave the field for 14-21 days. The skin will thicken and adhere to the flesh.

How to kill vines manually: cut stems at ground level with a sharp hoe or knife. Do not pull vines, as this can uproot tubers. For chemical desiccation, spray when leaves are dry and no rain is expected for 6 hours. Use recommended rates: diquat at 2-3 L/ha in 200 L water. Wear protective gear. After vine killing, reduce irrigation to avoid regrowth. Check skin set by rubbing a tuberâif skin stays firm, it's ready. If not, wait a few more days. In hot climates, skin set may take longer. In cool highlands, 14 days is enough. Proper vine killing can increase storage life by 2-3 months. It also makes harvesting easier because vines are not in the way.

4. Sorting, Grading, and Packing

After harvest, potatoes must be sorted and graded to get better prices and reduce losses. Sorting means removing damaged, diseased, green, or misshapen tubers. Grading means separating by size and quality. In Africa, common grades: Grade 1 (large, uniform, no defects), Grade 2 (medium, minor defects), and Grade 3 (small or damaged, for processing or home use). Use simple tools: a wooden or metal sizing gauge with holes of 45 mm, 55 mm, and 65 mm. Or use manual sorting tables where workers pick out bad potatoes.

Start by spreading potatoes on a clean tarpaulin in a shaded area. Never sort on bare groundâsoil and stones cause bruises. Remove any tubers with cuts, soft spots, greening, or sprouting. Green potatoes contain solanine, which is toxic; discard them. Also remove rocks and debris. For seed potatoes, grade strictly: only healthy, medium-sized (35-55 mm) tubers. For market, Grade 1 tubers should be 55-80 mm, firm, and clean. Grade 2 can be 45-55 mm. Small tubers under 35 mm are often used for animal feed or processing.

Packing: Use clean, ventilated crates or sacks. For local markets, 50 kg sacks are common, but they cause bruising when stacked. Better to use 10-20 kg crates or mesh bags. For export, use 25 kg cartons with ventilation holes. Label with grade, variety, and date. Store in a cool, dark place. Avoid overfillingâleave 5 cm space. In Kenya, farmers use wooden crates for transport to Nairobi markets. In Nigeria, raffia baskets are used but can damage potatoes. Plastic crates are best but costly. If using sacks, fill only 3/4 and do not drop. Proper sorting and grading can increase income by 30-50% because buyers pay more for uniform, clean produce. It also reduces storage rot. Always sort before storage, not after.

5. Storage Conditions for Potatoes

Proper storage extends the life of potatoes for months. The ideal storage conditions: temperature 4-8Â°C for table potatoes, 2-4Â°C for seed potatoes, and 10-15Â°C for processing potatoes. In Africa, many farmers lack cold storage, so they use traditional methods. For short-term storage (1-3 months), a dark, cool, ventilated room works. In highlands like Ethiopia or Kenya, ambient temperatures can be 10-15Â°C, which is okay for a few weeks. But for longer storage, you need to manage temperature and humidity.

Humidity should be 90-95% to prevent weight loss. If too dry, potatoes shrivel. If too wet, rot. Use a hygrometer. For small-scale storage, build a potato clamp: a pile of potatoes covered with straw and soil, with a ventilation chimney. This keeps temperature stable. Or use a ventilated shed with slatted wooden floors. Never store potatoes in direct sunlight or in plastic bagsâthey sweat and rot. Also, store in the dark to prevent greening. If you have electricity, a cold room is best. Set at 4Â°C for table potatoes. But don't store below 2Â°Câsweetening occurs. For seed potatoes, store at 2-4Â°C to break dormancy slowly.

Ventilation is key. Air must circulate to remove heat and gases. Stack crates with gaps. Don't pile more than 2 meters high. Inspect regularly for sprouts, rot, or rodents. Remove rotten tubers immediately. For long-term storage, use sprout inhibitors like chlorpropham (CIPC), but this is not available everywhere and has restrictions. In Africa, many farmers use neem leaves or other traditional methods to reduce sprouting. Store only healthy, mature, cured potatoes. Curing (holding at 15-20Â°C and high humidity for 10-14 days) heals cuts and thickens skin. After curing, lower temperature gradually. With good storage, you can sell when prices are high, increasing profit. Losses can be reduced from 30% to under 10%.

6. Reducing Post-Harvest Losses

Post-harvest losses in potatoes can be 20-50% in Africa due to poor handling, storage, and transport. To reduce losses, follow these steps: First, harvest at correct maturity and avoid bruising. Use proper tools and handle potatoes like eggs. Second, cure potatoes before storage. Curing heals wounds and reduces rot. Spread potatoes in a shaded, ventilated area at 15-20Â°C and 90% humidity for 10-14 days. Third, sort and gradeâremove damaged tubers. Fourth, store in optimal conditions: cool, dark, ventilated. Fifth, transport carefully. Use crates, not sacks, and avoid rough roads. If using sacks, pad the truck bed with straw.

Common causes of losses: greening (exposure to light), sprouting (warm storage), rot (bruising and moisture), and weight loss (low humidity). To prevent greening, store in darkness or use opaque covers. To prevent sprouting, keep cool (4Â°C) or use sprout inhibitors. To prevent rot, avoid bruising and ensure good ventilation. Also, control pests like rodents and insects. Use traps or cats. For transport, load during cool hours. Avoid mixing with ethylene-producing fruits (like bananas) which cause sprouting.

In Africa, simple technologies help: solar-powered cold rooms, evaporative coolers (charcoal coolers), and improved clamps. For example, a charcoal cooler can keep potatoes at 10-15Â°C in hot climates. Train workers on gentle handling. Use clean containers. Never drop potatoes from a height. For market, sell in smaller quantities to reduce storage time. If you have surplus, process into chips or flour to add value. Record losses to identify problems. By following these practices, you can reduce losses to less than 10%, meaning more food and income. Remember, every potato saved is money in your pocket. Start with careful harvesting and never neglect storage. Post-harvest care is as important as growing the crop.

Practical Reference: Harvesting & Post-Harvest Handling of Potatoes in Africa

For African potato growers, the money is not made in the field â it is protected in the store. Across Kenya, Nigeria, Ethiopia, Rwanda, Egypt, and South Africa, potato is a high-value cash crop, yet post-harvest losses commonly run between 15% and 40% depending on variety, handling, and storage. A 30% loss on a 10-tonne harvest is 3 tonnes of saleable crop gone â roughly the difference between profit and loss for a smallholder or emerging commercial farm. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the decision tools to harvest at the right time, handle tubers with minimal bruising, and store them so they reach the market with quality and weight intact.

1. Determining Harvest Maturity

Harvest too early and you get low yield, thin skins, and poor storage life. Harvest too late and you risk rots, greening, and field losses to pests. Two maturity signals matter:

  • Vine maturity: For maincrop potatoes, harvest 2â3 weeks after vines die back naturally or are killed (see Lesson 3).
  • Skin set: Rub a tuber with your thumb. If the skin does not slip and the tuber does not "skuff" easily, skin set is adequate.
  • Specific gravity / dry matter: For processing or chips, target 1.080â1.095 specific gravity; for table potatoes, 1.060â1.080 is acceptable.

In East African highlands (Kenya, Rwanda, Ethiopia), the main season harvest runs OctoberâJanuary; in Egypt and South Africa, harvest windows differ by region. Always harvest when soil moisture is moderate â not waterlogged, not bone dry.

2. Mechanical and Manual Harvesting Techniques

Manual harvesting (hand forks, hoes) dominates smallholder systems across Africa, but is labour-intensive and bruise-prone. Mechanical harvesting with tractor-drawn potato diggers or harvesters is used on commercial farms in Egypt, South Africa, and parts of Kenya and Nigeria. Key rules for both:

  • Adjust harvester depth so the share runs 2â5 cm below the deepest tuber â avoid spearing.
  • Reduce drop heights to less than 30 cm at every transfer point; bruising is invisible but costly.
  • Harvest in dry, cool conditions â ideally early morning or late afternoon. Avoid harvesting when soil is wet.
  • Use padded crates or baskets, never throw tubers into a heap.

3. Vine Killing and Skin Set

Vine killing (also called desiccation or haulm destruction) stops tuber bulking and triggers skin set. Methods include:

  • Mechanical: Flail or roll vines (common in Kenya, South Africa).
  • Chemical: Apply approved desiccants (e.g., diquat where registered) following national label rules.
  • Manual: Cutting vines by hand on small plots.

Skin set takes 7â21 days depending on temperature and variety. A well-set skin is your first line of defence against storage rots and weight loss.

4. Sorting, Grading, and Packing

Sorting removes rots, greened tubers, and stones. Grading sorts by size and quality for specific markets. Packing must protect tubers from light (to prevent greening) and from impact.
