1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Pattern making is the first step in metal casting. A pattern is a replica of the part you want to cast, but slightly larger to allow for shrinkage and machining. Without a good pattern, you cannot make a good mold, and without a good mold, you cannot cast a good part. In Africa, where recycling scrap metal is common, a well-made pattern lets you turn old engine blocks and broken tools into new products like cooking pots, plough parts, or machine components. This module teaches you how to make patterns using local materials and simple tools.

Patterns are usually made from wood, metal, or plastic. In many African foundries, hardwood like mahogany or iroko is used because it resists warping and is easy to shape. You will learn to select the right wood, cut it to size, and finish it so it pulls cleanly from the sand. The pattern must be strong enough to withstand repeated ramming of sand. It also needs a smooth surface to leave a good impression.

A pattern must include allowances: shrinkage allowance (because metal shrinks when it cools), draft allowance (taper so it can be removed from the sand), and machining allowance (extra material for finishing). For example, if you cast aluminium, shrinkage is about 1.3%, so a 100 mm pattern becomes 101.3 mm. You will learn to calculate and apply these allowances.

Why does this matter? Because a poorly made pattern wastes time, metal, and fuel. In a small foundry in Nigeria or Kenya, every failed casting costs money. By mastering pattern making, you reduce defects like misruns, sand inclusions, and dimensional errors. You also gain the skill to make patterns for any part you need, from a simple pot to a complex gear. This module gives you the foundation to become a skilled pattern maker, a trade that is in demand across Africa's growing manufacturing sector.

2. Tools and Materials

To make patterns, you need a set of basic tools and the right materials. Most tools can be bought locally or made by a blacksmith. You will need: a try square, steel rule, marking gauge, mortise gauge, tenon saw, coping saw, wood chisels (6 mm, 12 mm, 25 mm), mallet, smoothing plane, spokeshave, rasps and files, sandpaper (80, 120, 220 grit), and a bench vice. For measuring, a vernier caliper is useful but not essential. You also need a workbench and a set of clamps. In many African workshops, these tools are shared, so learn to keep them sharp and oiled to prevent rust.

For materials, hardwood is the best choice. Mahogany, iroko, teak, and camphor are common in West and Central Africa. They are dense, stable, and take a smooth finish. Softwoods like pine are easier to work but wear out quickly. You can also use metal patterns (aluminium or cast iron) for long production runs, but they are harder to make without a lathe. For small patterns, you can use plastic resin or even hard wax. In some areas, you can find pattern makers who use compressed sawdust mixed with glue, but this is not as durable.

You also need finishing materials: shellac, varnish, or paint. These seal the wood so it does not absorb moisture from the sand. A sealed pattern releases better and lasts longer. For draft, you will file or plane a taper of 1 to 3 degrees on vertical faces. For fillets (rounded corners), you can use leather, wood filler, or wax. Remember, the pattern must be slightly larger than the final part to allow for shrinkage. For aluminium, add 1.3% to all dimensions; for cast iron, add 1%; for brass, add 1.5%. Use a calculator or a shrinkage rule (a special ruler that already accounts for shrinkage).

Safety is important: wear eye protection when chiseling or sanding, and use a dust mask when sanding hardwood. Keep your tools sharp; a dull tool is more dangerous than a sharp one. Store patterns in a dry place to prevent warping. With these tools and materials, you are ready to start making your first pattern.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to make a simple pattern, such as a flat plate with a boss (a raised circular pad). First, study the drawing of the part. Note all dimensions, then add shrinkage allowance. For aluminium, multiply each dimension by 1.013. For example, a 200 mm length becomes 202.6 mm. Also add machining allowance if the part will be machined: typically 2-3 mm on surfaces that will be cut. Write down the pattern dimensions.

Second, select a piece of hardwood that is straight-grained and free of knots. Cut it slightly larger than the pattern dimensions using a tenon saw. Plane one face flat and true, then use a try square to mark the edges. Plane the edges square to the face. This is your reference surface. Next, mark the outline of the pattern on the wood using a marking gauge and rule. For the boss, mark a circle with a pair of dividers or a compass. Cut away waste wood with a coping saw, staying about 1 mm away from the line. Then use chisels and rasps to shape to the line. For the boss, you can turn it on a lathe if available, or shape it with a spokeshave and file.

Third, apply draft. On all vertical faces, file or plane a taper of 1 to 3 degrees. You can make a simple draft gauge by cutting a block at the desired angle. Check with a bevel protractor. For the boss, taper its sides too. Next, round all internal corners with fillets. You can glue in a piece of leather or use wood filler shaped with a file. Fillets prevent sand from breaking off and causing defects.

Fourth, sand the pattern smooth, starting with 80 grit and finishing with 220 grit. Remove all scratches. Then seal the pattern with two coats of shellac or varnish, sanding lightly between coats. This protects the wood from moisture and makes it release easily from the sand. Finally, mark the pattern with its part number and the shrinkage allowance used. Store it in a dry place. Now you can use it to make a mold. Remember to check the pattern for accuracy before casting; a small error in the pattern becomes a big error in the casting.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced pattern makers make mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them. First, forgetting shrinkage allowance. If you make a pattern exactly to the final dimensions, the casting will be too small. Fix: always add the correct shrinkage allowance. For aluminium, add 1.3%; for cast iron, 1%; for brass, 1.5%. Use a shrinkage rule or calculate. If you already made the pattern, you can sometimes use it for a smaller part, but often you must start again.

Second, insufficient draft. Without draft, the pattern sticks in the sand, and you damage the mold when removing it. Fix: file or plane a taper of at least 1 degree on all vertical faces. If you already made the pattern, you can carefully file the draft, but be careful not to change dimensions too much. For future patterns, always apply draft before finishing.

Third, rough surface. A rough pattern leaves a rough casting and may cause sand to stick. Fix: sand progressively from 80 to 220 grit, then seal with shellac or varnish. If the pattern is already sealed and rough, sand lightly and reseal.

Fourth, warped pattern. Wood can warp if not dried properly or stored in damp conditions. Fix: use well-seasoned hardwood, seal all surfaces, and store flat in a dry place. If warped, you may need to make a new pattern.

Fifth, incorrect fillets. Sharp internal corners can cause cracks in the casting. Fix: add fillets using leather, wax, or wood filler. Shape them smoothly. If you forgot, you can add fillets after the pattern is made, but it may be tricky.

Sixth, no core prints. If your casting needs a hole, you need core prints on the pattern to support the core. Fix: add core prints (extra projections) at the ends of the hole. They should be slightly larger than the core. If you forgot, you can sometimes drill and insert dowels, but it's better to plan ahead.

Seventh, using softwood. Softwood patterns wear out quickly and can break during ramming. Fix: use hardwood or metal for production runs. If you must use softwood, reinforce with metal edges or use it for only a few castings. By avoiding these mistakes, you save time and material.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Pattern making involves sharp tools and wood dust, so safety is critical. Always wear safety glasses when chiseling, sawing, or sanding. Wood chips can fly into your eyes. Wear a dust mask when sanding, especially with hardwoods like iroko, which can cause allergic reactions. Use ear protection when using power tools, though most pattern making in Africa is done by hand. Keep your workbench clean and free of clutter. Clamp your work securely before cutting or chiseling. Never cut towards your body; always cut away from yourself. Keep chisels and planes sharp; a dull tool requires more force and can slip.

Best practices start with planning. Study the drawing carefully and calculate all allowances before cutting wood. Use a pencil and rule to mark clearly. Work to a tolerance of 0.5 mm for most patterns. Check dimensions with a caliper or rule at each stage. Make a full-size drawing on paper or plywood to use as a template. This helps you avoid errors. For complex patterns, make a prototype from cheap wood or cardboard first.

Choose the right material for the job. For one-off castings, hardwood is fine. For hundreds of castings, consider making a metal pattern. Always seal patterns to protect against moisture. Store patterns in a dry, ventilated area, preferably on shelves, not on the floor. Label each pattern with its part number and shrinkage allowance. Keep a record book of patterns you have made, noting dimensions and any special features.

In the workshop, maintain a clean and organised space. Put tools back after use. Oil metal tools to prevent rust. Sweep sawdust regularly; it is a fire hazard. When using shellac or varnish, work in a well-ventilated area and avoid flames. Dispose of oily rags safely. Finally, learn from experienced pattern makers in your community. In many African cities, there are master craftsmen who can teach you tricks. Observe their methods and ask questions. By following these safety and best practices, you will produce high-quality patterns and avoid accidents.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to make a pattern yourself. We will make a simple flat plate with a boss, as described in the step-by-step lesson. This exercise will take about 4-6 hours. You will need: a piece of hardwood (e.g., mahogany) about 250 mm x 150 mm x 30 mm, your tools, and finishing materials. First, obtain a drawing of the part. If you don't have one, design a simple plate: 200 mm long, 100 mm wide, 20 mm thick, with a boss 50 mm diameter and 30 mm high in the centre. Add shrinkage allowance for aluminium (1.3%). So pattern dimensions: length 202.6 mm, width 101.3 mm, thickness 20.26 mm, boss diameter 50.65 mm, boss height 30.39 mm. Add 2 mm machining allowance on all surfaces that will be machined. Write these down.

Cut your wood to approximate size. Plane one face and one edge flat and square. Mark the outline of the plate and the boss. Cut away waste with a coping saw, staying 1 mm from the line. Use chisels and rasps to shape to the line. For the boss, you can use a lathe if available, or shape it with a spokeshave and file. Check dimensions frequently with a caliper. Apply draft: file a 2-degree taper on all vertical edges. You can make a draft gauge by cutting a block at 2 degrees. For the boss, taper its sides. Add fillets at the base of the boss using leather or wood filler. Sand the pattern smooth with 80, 120, and 220 grit. Seal with two coats of shellac, sanding lightly between coats.

Once finished, inspect your pattern. Check all dimensions, draft, and surface finish. Mark it with the part number and shrinkage allowance. Now, test your pattern by making a simple sand mold. Use a two-part flask and green sand. Ram the sand around the pattern, then remove it carefully. If the pattern releases cleanly and the mold cavity is good, your pattern is successful. If not, identify the problem (e.g., insufficient draft, rough surface) and fix it. This exercise gives you hands-on experience. Repeat it with different shapes, like a cylinder or a gear blank, to build your skills. Remember, practice makes perfect. In Africa, skilled pattern makers are valued, and this skill can lead to employment or your own workshop.

<h2>Practical Reference: Pattern Making for Foundry Work in Africa</h2>

<p>Pattern making is the bridge between an idea and a saleable metal casting. In African foundries from small jua kali workshops in Nairobi and Kumasi to established jobbing shops in Johannesburg and Lagos the pattern determines whether a casting fits, whether it sells, and whether the business makes a profit. This practical reference consolidates the key decisions, tools, materials, measurements, and checks you need to turn a drawing into a repeatable production pattern.</p>

<p>Treat this lesson as a shop-floor companion. It assumes you have completed the earlier lessons on tools, step-by-step method, and safety. Here we focus on the numbers, tables, and checks that experienced pattern makers and foundry owners use daily.</p>

<h3>1. What the Pattern Must Deliver</h3>

<p>A pattern is not just a copy of the part. It is a tool that must:</p>

<ul>
<li>Produce the correct final dimensions after shrinkage and machining.</li>
<li>Withdraw cleanly from the sand without breaking the mould.</li>
<li>Survive repeated use ideally hundreds or thousands of cycles.</li>
<li>Be economical in material and labour, because your margin depends on it.</li>
</ul>

<p>The pattern allowance is the financial heart of this work. Get it wrong and you scrap metal, sand, and time three of your most expensive inputs.</p>

<h3>5. Step-by-Step Practical Method</h3>

<p>Use this sequence on the shop floor. It assumes a wooden split pattern for a medium-run job.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Read the drawing and confirm the alloy.</strong> Mark shrinkage on every dimension before cutting any timber.</li>
<li><strong>Select and season the timber.</strong> Kiln-dried or air-dried hardwood to below 12% moisture. Wet timber warps and ruins the pattern.</li>
<li><strong>Mark out and cut the halves.</strong> Work from a centreline. Check squareness with a try square.</li>
<li><strong>Shape with planes, spokeshave, and rasps.</strong> Apply draft as you go do not leave it to the end.</li>
<li><strong>Add core prints</strong> if the casting has an internal cavity. Core print length typically equals the core diameter.</li>
<li><strong>Fit and align the halves</strong> with dowels. Check for a tight parting line with no light gap.</li>
<li><strong>Finish and seal.</strong> Sand smooth, apply shellac or polyurethane varnish. Two coats minimum.</li>
<li><strong>Mark identification.</strong> Part number, alloy, shrinkage, and date on the pattern. This prevents expensive mix-ups.</li>
<li><strong>Trial mould and cast.</strong> Measure the first casting and correct the pattern before full production.</li>
</ol>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-weight:bold; color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING WOOD DUST AND SOLVENTS</p>
<p style="margin:0; color:#7b241c;">Wood dust from hardwoods such as iroko and mahogany is a respiratory sensitiser and a suspected carcinogen. Always work with extraction or a fitted N95/FFP2 mask, not a cloth or surgical mask. Shellac, polyurethane, and thinners are flammable and toxic store them in a locked metal cabinet away from the furnace, and never use them near an open flame or hot metal. In many African workshops ventilation is poor; open doors and windows, and never sand or spray in an enclosed room. If you feel dizzy or your eyes sting, stop and get fresh air immediately.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Common Mistakes and Their Business Cost</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Forgetting shrinkage allowance.</strong> The casting comes out undersize and must be scrapped or welded both destroy margin.</li>
<li><strong>No draft angle.</strong> The pattern tears the mould on withdrawal, causing sand inclusions and rejected castings.</li>
<li><strong>Under-dried timber.</strong> Pattern warps after a few pulls; every subsequent casting is out of tolerance.</li>
<li><strong>Wrong core print size.</strong> Core shifts, wall thickness varies, and the customer rejects the batch.</li>
<li><strong>No identification marks.</strong> Patterns get mixed up; a whole day's production can be the wrong part.</li>
<li><strong>Skipping the trial casting.</strong> Errors are found only after a full production run the most expensive way to learn.</li>
</ul>

<h3>7. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li> Drawing read and alloy confirmed before any cutting.</li>
<li> Shrinkage allowance applied to every dimension.</li>
<li> Machining allowance added only where machining is planned.</li>
<li> Draft angle applied on all vertical faces (13).</li>
<li> Timber seasoned below 12% moisture content.</li>
<li> Halves aligned with dowels; parting line tight, no light gap.</li>
<li> Core prints sized correctly for internal cavities.</li>
<li> Pattern sealed, marked, and trial-cast before production.</li>
</ul>
